Sticking Doors and Drawers: Wood and Indoor Humidity

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A bedroom door that rubs the frame every July and rattles in its latch every January is usually not a bad door. It is wood doing what wood does as the air in your house gets wetter and drier. This lesson explains the swelling and shrinking, how to keep indoor humidity in a steady band, and how to tell humidity trouble from settling or a loose hinge.

Why wood swells and shrinks

Wood is hygroscopic. It trades water vapor with the air until it settles at a moisture content that matches the surrounding relative humidity (RH). The federal wood handbook calls that balance point the equilibrium moisture content, and it publishes a table for it. At 70°F the table gives about 6.2% moisture content at 30% RH, 9.2% at 50% RH, 11.0% at 60% RH and 13.1% at 70% RH. A land-grant extension fact sheet adds a real-house example: in a home with forced-air gas heat, winter equilibrium can fall to around 4%.

Below the point where the cell walls are full of water (the handbook says to use about 30% moisture content if you do not know it), wood changes size in a roughly straight-line way with moisture. The size change is not equal in every direction. The handbook says shrinkage across the growth rings (tangential) is about twice the shrinkage across the radius, and that shrinkage along the length of the grain averages only 0.1% to 0.2% from green to dry for most species. In plain terms: a board gets noticeably wider and thicker in humid weather but hardly longer.

That is why doors stick on the sides, not the top, and why a drawer front tightens in its opening but a drawer side slides the same way along its length. Rails and stiles of a door change little along their length, but a wide panel or a glued-up tabletop-style drawer front moves across its width.

Signs by season

  • Humid summer: doors rub at the latch side or bottom edge, drawers stop short or need a shove, cabinet doors stop closing flush, trim joints close up and paint can crack or bridge at the joint. Windows with wood sashes may bind.
  • Dry winter: gaps open between a door and its stop, doors rattle or will not stay latched, drawer fronts loosen, trim miters open, panels in raised-panel doors show an unpainted sliver at the edge, and floor boards open. See our hardwood floor lesson for that last one.
  • Both seasons, same spot: if the door sticks in winter too, the cause is probably not humidity (see settling below).

Keeping indoor RH in a steady band

The wood does not care about a single number so much as about swings. A federal environmental agency's mold guide recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60% and, ideally, between 30% and 50%, and says to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. That band also fits wood: by the handbook table, 30% to 50% RH at 70°F corresponds to about 6% to 9% moisture content, a much smaller swing than a house that goes from 20% in January to 70% in August.

  • Summer: run the air conditioner, which removes moisture as it cools, and keep drip pans and drain lines clear as the same guide advises. If the air still feels damp, especially in a basement, a dehumidifier helps. See Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage.
  • Winter: if the house is very dry, a humidifier can bring RH up to the low end of the band. A whole-house humidifier or a portable room humidifier both work, but more is not better.
  • Do not over-humidify in winter. Warm, humid indoor air meets cold window glass and turns to water. The mold guide says condensation can be a sign of high humidity and to dry it quickly and cut the moisture source. If your windows fog or run with water, turn the humidifier down. Our window condensation lesson explains how to pick a setting.
  • Measure it. The same guide says RH can be measured with a small humidity gauge from a hardware store. Put one in the room with the problem door, not by the humidifier.

Unfinished edges soak up moisture

Finish slows moisture exchange but does not stop it. A federal finishing-lab report on humidity protection says even the best films only slow vapor, that penetrating oils give little protection, and that coating every face, edge and end matters because uneven coating lets the bare side gain or lose moisture faster and promotes warping. It also says end grain admits a great deal of moisture. For a home, that means the bottom edge of a door, the top edge, the back of a cabinet door and the inside of a drawer front are the common bare spots. Our own field note: a door that sticks only after a wet spell often has an unpainted bottom edge or a cut-down edge that was never sealed.

Worked example (our own): a 30-inch oak panel

This is our own example, not a published case. We use published shrinkage values and our own assumptions to estimate how much a wide flat-sawn red oak panel (for example, a glued-up drawer front or a door panel) changes width from winter to summer.

Assumptions (ours): the panel is 30.00 inches wide at 6% moisture content, every board is flat sawn so the width runs in the tangential direction, the panel is free to move (not pinned by a rigid frame), and moisture changes evenly through the panel.

Published inputs: northern red oak tangential shrinkage from green to oven dry is 8.6% (federal handbook, chapter 4). The handbook says shrinkage is roughly linear below about 30% moisture content, so we use a 30-point span.

  1. Coefficient per 1 point of moisture content (our own derivation): 8.6% ÷ 30 = 0.287% of width per point, or 0.00287 per point.
  2. Moisture change: 10% − 6% = 4 points.
  3. Fractional width change: 0.00287 × 4 = 0.0115, about 1.15%.
  4. Width change: 30.00 in × 0.0115 = 0.344 in, about 11/32 in. The panel is about 30.34 in wide at 10%.

Check by a second route: 30 in × 0.086 × (4 ÷ 30) = 30 × 0.086 × 0.1333 = 0.344 in. Same answer. An extension fact sheet gives a similar relation using a 28% span and 8.9% tangential shrinkage for red oak; that gives 30 × 0.089 × (4 ÷ 28) = 0.381 in, so the answer is about 0.34 to 0.38 in depending on the published value.

What the moisture numbers mean: from the handbook table at 70°F, 6.2% matches 30% RH and 10% falls between 9.2% (50% RH) and 11.0% (60% RH). Straight-line interpolation (our own) puts 10% at about 54% RH. So this example is roughly a dry winter room at 30% RH against a humid summer room a little over 50% RH.

Along the grain: a 24-inch rail with the handbook's 0.1% to 0.2% green-to-dry range, scaled to 4 of 30 points, changes by 24 × 0.001 × (4 ÷ 30) = 0.003 in to 0.006 in. That is a few thousandths of an inch, which is why only the width matters at the door edge.

Reality check (ours): a quarter-sawn panel changes less, about half as much by the radial value, and a framed door panel floats in grooves for this reason. A real panel also lags behind the room by days or weeks, and a finish slows it further, so the full change is a ceiling, not a daily swing. A third of an inch is far more than a door clearance, which is why wide solid parts are built to float.

When to wait before planing a door

Our own rule of thumb: do not plane a door that sticks only in the humid months until the season changes. If you shave it tight in August, it will probably open a gap in January. Wait for drier weather, or bring the house RH down first with the air conditioner or a dehumidifier and see whether the door frees up. If you must fix it now, take off only the minimum, and seal every bare edge you cut. Planing is more reasonable when the door sticks in all seasons, the house RH is already in the 30% to 50% band, and the stuck spot is a known hard point.

When it is settling or a hinge, not humidity

These are our own field signs, not published figures. Suspect something other than humidity when:

  • the door sticks at the same corner all year, or sticks more after a house change such as new floor covering or repairs to a deck or porch;
  • the gap around the door is wedge-shaped or the reveal is uneven from top to bottom, and nearby doors also hang crooked;
  • drywall cracks run diagonally from the corners of the door or window openings;
  • the hinge screws spin or the door sags when you lift the knob, which points to a loose hinge or stripped screw holes;
  • a cabinet door that sags is hanging off a loose hinge plate, not a swollen panel.

Tighten hinge screws first. A loose or sagging door is a quick check and a cheap fix. If doors across the house keep going out of square or cracks grow, have a qualified builder or structural professional look at the foundation and framing.

Field card: Sticking Doors and Drawers: Wood and Indoor Humidity

Wood moves with the air. Steady RH keeps it still.

  • Wood trades moisture with air; across the grain it moves a lot, along the grain almost none.
  • Tangential shrinkage is about twice radial; along the grain only 0.1% to 0.2% green to dry.
  • Target indoor RH: below 60%, ideally 30% to 50%.
  • Summer: air conditioning, dehumidifier. Winter: humidifier only as needed; watch window glass.
  • Seal all edges and ends, including the bottom of the door.
  • Our example: 30 in flat-sawn red oak, 6% to 10% moisture content, changes about 0.34 in.
  • Wait for the season before planing a door that only sticks in humid weather.
  • Same spot all year or a sagging door points to settling or a hinge.
