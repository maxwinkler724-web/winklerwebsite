Transfer Grilles and Jump Ducts for Closed Bedroom Doors: Pressure and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Close a bedroom door and the room can start to feel stuffy, hot or cold, and the door may whistle. When a room gets supply air but has no return of its own, the air has nowhere to go. This lesson covers why that pressurizes the room, the 3 pascal target people cite, the four ways to give the air a path back, and how to size and check one.

Why a closed door pressurizes a room

A forced-air system moves air in a loop. Supply registers push air into rooms, and the return side pulls the same amount back to the blower. In many houses the return is one or two big grilles in a hallway or living area. With the bedroom door open, the supply air drifts out and finds the return. With the door shut, the bedroom keeps receiving air but has a hard time sending it back.

The Building Science Corporation (BSC) information sheet on transfer grilles puts it plainly: a bedroom with a closed door and no return path becomes pressurized, supply flow to it is choked off, and the common areas end up depressurized. The existing lesson Not Enough Return Air covers the whole-system picture, and Airflow and Static Pressure Basics explains pressure itself. Here we focus on the room.

A second cost shows up in the hallway. BSC's information sheet on undercut doors says higher pressure differences across the door increase air leakage, and it puts the extra space-conditioning energy at 10% to 20%. A pressurized bedroom pushes air out through cracks in the walls, and the starved common area pulls outside air in.

The 3 Pa target

A pascal (Pa) is a very small pressure unit. 1 Pa is about 0.004 inch of water column, so 3 Pa is about 0.012 inch w.c., the figure the sources print beside it.

  • BSC's transfer grille sizing report (RR-0005) states a maximum bedroom pressurization of 3 Pa with the door closed.
  • BSC's undercut-door sheet says to size the relief paths to keep the pressure difference across a closed door under 3 Pa.
  • The ENERGY STAR Certified Homes requirement, as the Building America Solution Center (a U.S. Department of Energy resource) lists it, is a rater-measured difference of 3 Pa or less, met by any combination of transfer grilles, jump ducts, dedicated return ducts or undercut doors.
  • The Solution Center guides give the test conditions: doors closed, air handler at its highest design fan speed, room measured against the main body of the house. Rooms with design airflow under 150 cfm should read within plus or minus 3 Pa. For design airflow of 150 cfm or more, plus or minus 5 Pa (0.020 inch w.c.) is given. The guides call these good targets even without ENERGY STAR certification.

These are program and research targets. Ask the authority having jurisdiction what applies to your job.

The four options

Dedicated return

A ducted return in the room is the most direct fix. BSC's sheet says bedrooms should have either a direct-ducted return or a transfer grille, and that an airtight central return with transfer grilles significantly outperforms a leaky traditional return system.

Through-wall transfer grille

An opening through the wall or above the door. BSC notes these have interior baffles and relieve pressure differences between spaces. The Solution Center describes an offset pair as one grille high on the bedroom wall, away from furniture, and one low on the hallway side, with air traveling through the wall cavity.

Jump duct

A short insulated flex duct, usually through the attic, from a ceiling register in the room to one in the common space. The Solution Center says 10-inch duct is typical and that both openings need the required free area, with all seams sealed. BSC's undercut sheet prefers transfer grilles when the attic is vented, so the air stays inside the conditioned space. If a jump duct must cross an unconditioned attic, the Solution Center says to insulate it to at least current code requirements.

Door undercut

BSC's information sheet says undercutting "rarely works" and should not be relied upon, though it still lists a 1 inch minimum undercut alongside a grille. The undercut sheet adds that carpet often blocks the gap. An undercut is a helper, not the whole answer.

Sizing by free area

Free area is the open space air can actually pass through, not the nominal size stamped on the grille. The sources give two rules of thumb that differ, so we show both.

  • Existing homes, ENERGY STAR guidance: at least 1 square inch of free opening per cfm of supply air to the room. The Solution Center guide says it means free area, not nominal grille size.
  • BSC RR-0005: size from the room's supply flow (add the rooms together for a suite) using an orifice equation, Q = 1.07 x A x the square root of the pressure difference, where Q is cfm, A is free area in square inches and the pressure is in Pa. At 3 Pa that works out to about 0.54 square inch of net free area per cfm. It assumes net free area is 0.75 of the grille's total area, and its table assumes a 32 inch door with a 0.5 inch undercut.

Sound and privacy

A bare hole in the wall carries conversation and light. BSC says transfer grilles have interior baffles to control sound and light transfer. The Solution Center's alternative is cutting two matching openings opposite each other and fitting sheet metal baffles offset inside each one: air gets through, light is blocked and sound is reduced. Neither source gives baffle dimensions, and baffles add resistance, so we size the free area through the baffled grille, using the maker's data. Grille makers publish free area for each size. BSC's report lists catalog examples, such as 51 square inches for a 10 x 6 grille and 121 for a 12 x 12.

Measuring room pressure

The Solution Center test is simple and needs a manometer that reads in Pa.

  1. Run the air handler on high.
  2. Close all interior doors.
  3. Connect the tubing to the manometer's input port and leave the reference port open.
  4. Stand in the hallway or the middle of the house, slip the tubing under each door, and record each room's pressure against the house. Note the sign: bedrooms are usually positive with the door closed.

If a room misses the target, add or enlarge a path and measure again with the door shut.

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

Take a bedroom with a stated supply of 120 cfm, closed door, 32 inch door and a 0.5 inch undercut as in the BSC table. Everything below is our own arithmetic using the BSC equation and the ENERGY STAR guidance.

Part 1. Transfer grille at 3 Pa.

  1. Our own: A = Q / (1.07 x square root of 3) = 120 / (1.07 x 1.732) = 120 / 1.853 = 64.8 square inches of net free area (the same as 0.54 x 120).
  2. Our own: undercut area = 0.5 in x 32 in = 16 square inches.
  3. Our own: the undercut alone gives 16 of 64.8, about 25%. To pass the whole load through the gap, it would have to be 64.8 / 32 = 2.0 inches high, which is not practical.
  4. Our own: remaining free area for a grille = 64.8 - 16 = 48.8 square inches.
  5. Our own: gross grille area at 0.75 net = 48.8 / 0.75 = 65.1 square inches. At 12 inches wide, height = 65.1 / 12 = 5.4 inches. That agrees with the BSC table, whose 100 and 125 cfm rows give 4.2 and 5.7 inches at 12 inches wide.
  6. Our own: for the 1 square inch per cfm guidance instead: 120 x 1 = 120 square inches of free area, roughly twice as much.

Part 2. Jump duct. Our own: with the 16 square inch undercut credited, 48.8 square inches is a round duct of diameter = square root of (4 x 48.8 / 3.1416) = 7.9 inches. Without any undercut credit, 64.8 gives 9.1 inches.

Part 3. A room measured at 6 Pa.

  1. Our own: 6 Pa is about 6 x 0.004 = 0.024 inch w.c.
  2. Our own: target for a room under 150 cfm is within 3 Pa, so 6 Pa is double the limit and fails.
  3. Our own: the equation says the free area the room is effectively using at 6 Pa for 120 cfm is 120 / (1.07 x 2.449) = 45.8 square inches.
  4. Our own: at 3 Pa the need is 64.8, a ratio of 64.8 / 45.8 = 1.41, which is the square root of 2. Adding about 19 square inches of net free area (about 25 square inches of gross grille) would be the first estimate. Then measure again. Real openings never match the equation exactly, so the manometer has the last word.

Field card: Closed-Door Pressure

Every cfm in needs a way out.

  • Closed door plus supply and no return pressurizes the room.
  • Cited target: 3 Pa or less (5 Pa for rooms at 150 cfm or more), doors closed, fan on high.
  • Options: ducted return, transfer grille, jump duct, undercut (weak alone).
  • Size by free area: 1 sq in per cfm (ENERGY STAR existing homes) or about 0.54 sq in per cfm at 3 Pa (BSC calculation).
  • Baffle for sound and light, and size by the baffled free area.
  • Test: manometer tubing under the door, reference port open, read the sign.
