EPA 608 Major Repair vs Other Repairs: What Counts as Major and Why It Changes Evacuation

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Before you open a refrigerant circuit, the federal rule asks one sorting question: is this a major repair or not? The answer decides how far down you must pull the refrigerant first. Exam writers love the question because the rule gives a short, checkable list.

Check the current rule text

Check the current rule text. We read the eCFR pages for 40 CFR 82.152 (definitions) and 82.156 (required practices) and an EPA service-practice page in 2026. Rules get amended and this lesson is study help, not legal advice. Before you rely on any detail, open the current text yourself.

What the definition says

In 82.152, "major maintenance, service, or repair" is one sentence with two alternative clauses. The first is any work that involves removal of the compressor, condenser, evaporator, or auxiliary heat exchange coil. The second is any work that involves uncovering an opening of more than four square inches of "flow area" for more than 15 minutes. Either clause alone makes the job major. The definition has no numbered paragraphs, so on an exam you quote it by wording, not by letter.

Two details trip people up. The first clause is about removal, not touching. Tightening a flare nut on a compressor does not remove it. The second clause is the one most people forget, and it is the only place the rule puts numbers on opening size and time. Also, 82.152 says connecting and disconnecting hoses and gauges to measure pressure, add refrigerant or recover refrigerant is not "opening" the appliance.

Why the label matters

Section 82.156(a) is the evacuation rule. In plain words: technicians evacuate refrigerant to the Table 1 levels using certified recovery equipment before opening an appliance, and must verify the level was reached. The table lists levels by pressure class, charge size and equipment age; we do not copy it here, so read the current table. You may evacuate the whole appliance or only the part being serviced if that refrigerant can be isolated to a system receiver.

Then comes the exception. Paragraph (a)(1) applies to repairs that are not major and are not followed by an evacuation to the atmosphere. For medium-, high- and very high-pressure appliances the requirement is to evacuate to no higher than 0 psig before opening. For low-pressure appliances the requirement flips: pressurize to no higher than 0 psig, covering openings if you cannot isolate, with limits on nitrogen use that depend on the refrigerant. The EPA service-practice page describes the same split. Oil changes get their own wording in (a)(1), at 5 psig. So "major" is the switch between the deep Table 1 vacuum and the 0 psig path.

Leaks have their own paragraph. Under (a)(2), if leaks make the Table 1 levels unattainable, you isolate leaking from non-leaking parts where possible, evacuate the non-leaking parts to Table 1, and take the leaking parts as low as you can without contaminating the refrigerant, never above 0 psig. For the machines and their care, see our recovery machine lesson; for low-pressure chillers, see Type III recovery.

Worked example: five invented jobs

Our own example. These five jobs are invented for practice. No pressures or sizes are given, because the sorting depends on wording, not numbers. Assumptions: each system holds a refrigerant charge that falls under the general 82.156(a) rule (not a small appliance or motor vehicle air conditioner); none is a low-pressure chiller; none has been found leaking except job 3; and for the small-opening jobs we assume the tech opens only a tube-sized connection, not a large port, for a short time. Those two small-opening assumptions are ours, so check them against the real opening and the clock on a real job.

Typical exam questions

  • Which of these is a major repair? Pick the answer that removes a compressor, condenser, evaporator or auxiliary heat exchange coil. A fan motor, a contactor or a drier is a distractor.
  • What must you do before opening on a non-major repair to a high-pressure appliance? Evacuate to no higher than 0 psig (a)(1).
  • What is different for a low-pressure appliance? You pressurize to no higher than 0 psig instead of evacuating.
  • Does connecting gauges count as opening? No, per 82.152.
  • What if leaks keep you from reaching the required level? Isolate, take non-leaking parts to the table level, take leaking parts as low as possible without contaminating, never above 0 psig.

Answer wording matters. The word "major" does not appear in any answer as a pressure number; it is a label that selects a rule. Practice the sorting with our core practice test.

Field card: Major vs other repairs

Listed part out, or a big opening open a long time, means major.

  • Major: removal of compressor, condenser, evaporator or auxiliary heat exchange coil.
  • Also major: opening over four square inches of flow area for over 15 minutes.
  • Major: evacuate to the Table 1 level in 82.156(a) and verify.
  • Not major, no later release: 0 psig before opening (low pressure: pressurize to 0 psig).
  • Leaks that block the level: isolate, then lowest attainable, never above 0 psig.
  • Hoses and gauges do not count as opening.
