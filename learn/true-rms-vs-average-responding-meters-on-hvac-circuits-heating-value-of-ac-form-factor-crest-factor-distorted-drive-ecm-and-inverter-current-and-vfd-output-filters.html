True-RMS vs Average-Responding Meters on HVAC Circuits

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Clamp a motor lead and the display says 10.0 amps. Is that the real current? With a plain sine wave, yes, on almost any meter. With the chopped-up current that drives, ECM motors and inverter compressors draw, the answer depends on how the meter was built. This lesson explains what RMS really means and how to avoid trusting a number that is off by a fifth or more.

What RMS means: the heating value of AC

An AC current swings positive and negative, so its simple average over a cycle is zero. That is useless for judging heat in a wire or a winding. The useful number is the DC current that would warm a resistor by the same amount. That equivalent value is the root-mean-square, or RMS, value: square the instantaneous current, average the squares, then take the square root. Heating depends on current squared, which is why this definition works. A university lab handout on AC meters describes RMS as the true effective value of a waveform and gives the sine relation as peak divided by the square root of 2, about 0.707 times peak.

How an average-responding meter works

Most low-cost meters do not compute squares. They flip the negative half over (rectify it), average the result, and multiply by a fixed constant. The same lab handout gives the average of a rectified sine as 2 times peak divided by pi, about 0.6366 times peak, and says the meter multiplies by 1.111 to display RMS. The ratio of RMS to rectified average is called the form factor, and for a pure sine it is about 1.11. The meter is correct only if the wave is a sine. Any other shape has a different form factor, so the fixed 1.111 multiplier gives the wrong answer. The handout shows this with a triangle wave (reads low) and a square wave (reads high).

A true-RMS meter instead calculates the heating value of whatever shape it sees, within its stated bandwidth and crest factor limit. That is why you will see true-RMS marked on the meter and in its manual.

Crest factor: how peaky the wave is

Crest factor is the ratio of a waveform's peak to its RMS value, as a national laboratory power quality report defines it. A sine is about 1.414. A current that flows only in short pulses around the voltage peaks has a much larger crest factor, because the peak is tall while the heating value stays modest. Every true-RMS meter has a maximum crest factor it can handle at full range; beyond it the input clips and the reading falls short. Check the meter's specification page rather than guessing.

Which HVAC loads distort the current

  • VFD input. A university survey of harmonic measurements notes that most drives use a six-pulse rectifier, so the input current flows as two pulses per half cycle and drops to zero between them when there is no line inductor. The same survey reports form factors (total RMS divided by fundamental RMS) of roughly 1.2 to 1.5 for nonlinear loads, against 1.0 for a linear load.
  • ECM blower and circulator motors, inverter compressors. These contain electronic power stages that draw current in a similar peaky way. We did not find a source giving exact waveform numbers for these products, so check what the equipment manual says and rely on a true-RMS reading.
  • Electronic loads. Control boards, power supplies and LED lighting use rectifiers with capacitors, which also draw pulsed current.

The federal energy department's guide to motor systems warns that poor motor and drive selection can cause power quality problems such as harmonics and low power factor, and it says a power meter can directly measure voltage, current and power factor at the motor control center. See power factor on HVAC motors for the power side of that story.

VFD output voltage and the low-pass filter mode

A drive makes its output by switching the bus voltage on and off thousands of times a second and varying the pulse widths. A university motor-drive lab handout describes that output as a switched square-wave pattern and says the motor itself acts as a low-pass filter, so the motor currents are nearly sinusoidal. It also says its power analyzer had to have the line filter and frequency filter turned on to deal with the PWM, and that the voltage probe should be connected at the drive end of the cable. A handheld meter has the same problem: the raw voltage is full of fast switching edges that confuse the input stage. Meters meant for drive work offer a low-pass filter setting that removes the switching content so the display shows the part that actually tracks the output frequency, which is what the motor responds to. Without it, readings can bounce or disagree with the drive display. Use only a meter rated for the voltage and category involved, and see measuring amp draw with a clamp meter and inverter drives and variable speed compressors for context.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Assumptions: all values are invented for teaching, not measurements of a real machine.

Part A, pure sine, 10 A RMS.

  1. Peak = 10 x 1.414 = 14.14 A.
  2. Average of the rectified wave = 0.6366 x 14.14 = 9.00 A.
  3. Form factor = RMS / average = 10 / 9.00 = 1.11. So an average-responding meter, which multiplies the average by 1.111, shows 9.00 x 1.111 = 10.0 A. Correct.

Part B, distorted current. Assume the true RMS is 10 A and an average-responding meter reads 8 A.

  1. Error = 8 - 10 = -2 A.
  2. Percent under-read = 2 / 10 x 100 = 20 percent low.
  3. Check: the meter's internal rectified average would be 8 / 1.111 = 7.2 A, so this assumed wave has a form factor of 10 / 7.2 = 1.39 rather than 1.11. That falls inside a peaky, drive-like shape, though we picked it as an assumption.
  4. Why it matters: heating goes with current squared, so the real heating is (10 / 8)^2 = 1.56 times what the 8 A reading suggests, or about 56 percent more heat in a conductor.

Practical checks

  • Read the meter label and manual: it should say true-RMS for AC, with a stated crest factor and bandwidth.
  • Compare the two meters on a known resistive load, such as a heater element, where both should agree. Disagreement on a drive or ECM circuit points to the average-responding meter.
  • For drive output, use the low-pass filter setting and compare to the drive's own display.
  • Do not clamp one conductor of a pair, and keep the jaws closed fully; a federal workplace safety archive memo notes clip-on meters measure current without breaking the circuit, which is safer than placing a meter in series.
  • Use the rating of the meter for the circuit. See megohmmeter testing for insulation checks and VFD bearing currents for another drive-related fault.

Field card: True-RMS vs Average-Responding Meters on HVAC Circuits

True RMS measures the heat; average-responding meters assume a sine.

  • RMS is the DC-equivalent heating value of an AC wave.
  • Sine: peak = 1.414 x RMS; rectified average = 0.9 x RMS; form factor 1.11.
  • Average-responding meters multiply the rectified average by 1.111, right only for a sine.
  • Drive inputs, ECMs, inverters and electronic loads draw peaky current with a high crest factor.
  • Use true-RMS for current on those loads and the low-pass filter mode for drive output voltage.
  • Our assumed example: 8 A read vs 10 A true is 20 percent low.
