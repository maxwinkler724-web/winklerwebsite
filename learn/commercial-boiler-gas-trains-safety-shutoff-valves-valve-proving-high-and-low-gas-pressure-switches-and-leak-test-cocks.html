Commercial Boiler Gas Trains: Safety Shutoff Valves, Valve Proving and Leak Test Cocks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A commercial burner does not trust one valve to keep gas out of a hot, closed firebox. It uses two safety shutoff valves, a way to test them, and switches that watch gas pressure. This lesson walks the parts in order and shows how the tests work.

Check your own burner first

Follow the maker. Gas train layouts and test steps differ by burner, boiler and jurisdiction. This page is general education built from maker, insurer and state pages read in 2026. The burner manual and the authority having jurisdiction decide the real procedure. Gas work needs the right license and training; we are not teaching you to bypass anything.

The parts, in flow order

The National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors lists a main burner gas train this way: a manual shutoff valve, a gas pressure regulator, a low gas pressure switch, the first safety shutoff valve, a vent valve, the second safety shutoff valve, a high gas pressure switch, a second manual shutoff valve, then the firing rate valve, with test valves and a drip leg also shown. A distributor's gas train overview we read puts a strainer or sediment trap ahead of the regulator to catch scale and dirt before it reaches the valves. Different makers move parts around, which is why the diagram says "one common layout." Here is what each part does.

  • Manual shutoff and strainer. The manual valve lets you isolate the train for service. The strainer protects the regulator and the shutoff valves from debris, because a speck on a valve seat is how a valve ends up leaking.
  • Regulator. It drops supply pressure to what the burner needs. See Gas Meter and Service Regulator Basics for who owns what upstream.
  • Low gas pressure switch. Per the National Board article, it detects pressure too low for the burner to run and locks out, with manual reset, so someone checks the gas supply.
  • Safety shutoff valves (SSOVs). Two valves in series open and close the fuel automatically. Fireye, a flame safeguard maker, says NFPA 85 and ASME CSD-1 require two valves below 5,000 MBh. The valves are normally closed and the flame safeguard opens them only when the sequence allows.
  • Vent valve. It is normally open and powered closed while running. The National Board says that when the SSOVs are closed it vents gas that leaks past the first one, so a leaking second valve does not feed the firebox.
  • High gas pressure switch. It trips when pressure is too high, usually from a regulator failure, and also has a manual-reset lockout (National Board).
  • Firing rate valve. A butterfly or modulating valve that sets how much gas the burner gets as load changes. It is not a safety shutoff.

Leak test cocks and how the test goes

A leak test cock is a small manual valve with a pipe plug, one after each SSOV. An insurer's procedure (AXA XL) walks through it, and Fireye describes the same bubble check. In outline, with the burner shut down:

  1. Close the downstream manual shutoff valve.
  2. Power the vent valve closed. Test the upstream SSOV first: remove the cap on its test cock, attach a hose, put the other end in a bucket of water, and open the cock. AXA XL says to keep the hose shallow, because gas pressure can be under 1 in. w.c.
  3. Bubbles mean that valve is leaking. Close and recap the cock.
  4. Open the upstream SSOV with the vent still closed and repeat at the second test cock for the downstream valve.
  5. Close the upstream valve, release the vent valve, and reopen the downstream manual valve.

How you power the valves depends on the control. Fireye says the flame safeguard is removed from its base, or the valves disconnected from it, and jumpers are placed only with power off. We are not giving you a jumper recipe; use the maker's.

What counts as a pass? Neither source we read gives a bubble rate. AXA XL's only criterion is "If bubbles appear in the bucket, the valve is leaking," and Fireye says to compare the count to the acceptable amount in the applicable code. Because we cannot source a number, we do not state one here. If your burner maker gives an allowable rate, use that.

AXA XL recommends recording leak tests monthly, and says boiler codes call for monthly testing; Fireye says NFPA 54, NFPA 85, NFPA 86 and ASME CSD-1 require at least yearly. Those differ, so ask which your jurisdiction and insurer enforce. If a valve leaks, AXA XL says to clean it internally and repair it as needed, and to keep records of test date and valve condition.

Proof of closure and valve proving

Proof of closure (POC) is a switch on the valve that confirms it is physically closed. Fireye says a debris-blocked seat will not make the switch, so the sequence will not start or will lock out. It adds that the switch should not be field-adjustable.

Valve proving tests the seats automatically using a gas pressure switch between the valves. Fireye describes the sequence, which can run before prepurge or after postpurge:

  1. Evacuation: the downstream valve opens about 5 seconds to vent the space between the valves.
  2. Evacuation test: about 25 seconds with the switch staying closed. Failing points to a leaking upstream valve.
  3. Pressurization: the upstream valve opens about 5 seconds.
  4. Pressurization test: about 25 seconds with the switch changing state. Failing points to a leaking downstream valve.

Fireye notes the times are adjustable, and that proving can replace a vent valve under NFPA 85 and other listed standards. For an Our own total with the default times: 5 + 25 + 5 + 25 = 60 seconds per proving cycle.

Switch setpoints, basics

We found no universal rule. A pressure switch maker's instructions say to set the dial to the trip pressure you need, check the real trip point with an accurate gauge upstream of the switch, calibrate in the final mounting position, and follow the equipment maker for application values. The same document says a horizontally mounted switch trips about 0.2 in. w.c. higher and an upside-down one about 0.2 in. w.c. lower. To test a low switch, turn its setpoint up until it trips; a high switch, turn it down, per that maker, and never simulate faults while the burner is firing.

Worked example: reading regulator pressures

The NIST conversion table gives 1 psi = 27.6799 in. of water; we round to 27.7. Assumptions (Our own): supply to the regulator is 2 psi, and the burner is set for 7 in. w.c. at the regulator outlet.

  1. Inlet in in. w.c.: 2 psi × 27.7 = 55.4 in. w.c.
  2. Outlet in psi: 7 ÷ 27.7 = 0.253 psi.
  3. Pressure the regulator removes: 55.4 − 7 = 48.4 in. w.c.

This is why a gauge reading in psi on one side and in. w.c. on the other must be converted before you compare. Convert first, then judge the switch setpoints.

What a tech logs

New Jersey's boiler bureau advisory quotes ASME CSD-1 as saying operability and set points of devices be recorded in the boiler log, defects be reported to the owner and corrected immediately, and that boilers be tested after maintenance or repair. So log: date, which valves you tested, leak or no leak, the switch setpoints as found and left, and any repairs. For the New Jersey rules, read NJ Boiler Post-Service Testing; we do not repeat them here.

Field card: Commercial Boiler Gas Trains

Two valves, a vent or proving, and a way to prove the seats.

  • Typical order: manual shutoff, strainer, regulator, low switch, SSOV, vent, SSOV, high switch, firing rate valve.
  • A leak test uses the cock after each SSOV, a hose and a bucket of water. Bubbles mean a leak.
  • Proof of closure confirms a valve is shut; valve proving tests the seats.
  • Convert psi to in. w.c. with 27.7 before comparing gauges.
  • Log tests and setpoints. Follow the burner maker.
