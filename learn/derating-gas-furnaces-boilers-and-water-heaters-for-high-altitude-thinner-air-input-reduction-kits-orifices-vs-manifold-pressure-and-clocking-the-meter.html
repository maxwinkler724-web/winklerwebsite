Derating Gas Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters for High Altitude

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A furnace nameplate says 100,000 Btu/h, but that number was earned at low elevation. Up in the mountains the air is thinner, and the same gas flow can burn dirty. This lesson explains why gas appliances get derated at altitude, what the common rule says, how makers handle it with kits and orifices, and how to prove the result by clocking the meter.

Why thinner air changes the burn

Air pressure falls as you climb. Using a standard-atmosphere pressure formula from a federal space agency education page, we worked out that air pressure at 5,000 ft is about 83 percent of the sea-level value, and about 93 percent at 2,000 ft. Fewer air molecules fit in each cubic foot of room, so each cubic foot of combustion air holds less oxygen. Our own calculation from that formula; the page gives the formula, not these two numbers.

A burner needs a fixed amount of oxygen for every cubic foot of gas. A federal laboratory research paper on gas appliances at altitude found that the safe gas rate, the most input an appliance can burn completely, drops by about 3 to 4 percent of its sea-level value for each 1,000 ft of elevation. It also found that carbon monoxide shows up at lower gas rates as elevation rises, and that flames lift off the burner ports at lower rates too. That is an old study of older appliance designs, so we use it for the cause, not for exact numbers on today's equipment.

Put those together and the risk is clear. If a technician sets up a furnace at 5,000 ft exactly as the sea-level nameplate says, the burner gets too little air for its gas. Combustion can turn incomplete, flames can lift off the ports, and carbon monoxide (CO) can form. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics for what CO does and why it matters.

The common rule: about 4 percent per 1,000 ft

For most gas appliances, the makers' instructions give the rule. One furnace maker's installation instructions, hosted on a parts distributor's site, say that above 2,000 ft the furnace must be derated 4 percent per thousand feet. Another maker's furnace manual says input must come down 4 percent for each 1,000 ft above sea level. The wording differs, so a technician must read the exact line for the exact model. Where the maker gives no other rule, we use 4 percent per 1,000 ft above 2,000 ft as our working figure.

The code side agrees on the 4 percent number. A state study guide for LP-gas appliance work quotes the national fuel gas standard: above 2,000 ft, input ratings must be reduced by 4 percent for each 1,000 ft of elevation, by a method the authority having jurisdiction permits, or as the manufacturer's instructions say. The IFGC (the model code many states adopt) also expects gas flow to be adjusted for altitude above 2,000 ft in its gas pipe sizing section, as city adoptions of that code show. Because the code gives the maker's instructions and the local authority an equal say, local rules and the data plate always win over a rule of thumb. Ask the local inspector which one applies.

The same ideas apply to boilers and water heaters. Their makers publish their own derate instructions, and some units ship ready for altitude. We could not confirm model-specific boiler or water heater numbers from open sources, so check the manual for the unit in front of you.

Maker kits and orifices

Most makers do not tell you to "turn the gas down." They sell high-altitude kits. A kit usually has smaller burner orifices, and sometimes a different pressure switch. One maker's literature says a pressure switch change is required at 5,000 ft, and another says a different switch is needed above 4,000 ft. Makers do not agree on the line, so use the chart for your model. The first maker's literature says an orifice change may or may not be needed depending on elevation and the gas heating value, and the second maker's manual notes that some gas suppliers derate the gas themselves, so ask the utility what it delivers.

Why smaller orifices? Gas flow through an orifice depends on the hole area. Our own arithmetic: to cut input to 88 percent, the hole area should be about 88 percent as large, which means a diameter about 94 percent as large (the square root of 0.88 is 0.938). That is a small change in drill size, which is why you use the kit's parts, not a guess.

Orifice change or manifold pressure?

Input also changes with manifold pressure, as the lesson on the square root of manifold pressure shows. It is tempting to get the 12 percent drop by lowering manifold pressure. Our own arithmetic shows why that is awkward: input goes with the square root of pressure, so 88 percent input needs 0.88 × 0.88 = 77 percent of the pressure. On a 3.5 in. w.c. setting that is about 2.7 in. w.c. One maker's manual says plainly not to derate by adjusting manifold pressure lower than the nameplate value. A technician should use the orifice route the maker published and keep the manifold pressure on the data plate value. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure with a Manometer for the reading itself.

Worked example (our own): 100,000 Btu/h furnace at 5,000 ft

Assumptions, all ours: natural gas; nameplate input 100,000 Btu/h; installed at 5,000 ft; the maker gives no other rule, so we use 4 percent per 1,000 ft above 2,000 ft; the utility's heating value is 1,000 Btu per cubic foot (a round teaching number; always ask your utility for the real value); a meter dial that reads 1 cubic foot per revolution.

  1. Feet above the threshold: 5,000 − 2,000 = 3,000 ft, which is 3 steps of 1,000 ft.
  2. Reduction: 3 × 4 percent = 12 percent.
  3. Derated input: 100,000 × (1 − 0.12) = 88,000 Btu/h.
  4. Gas flow needed: 88,000 ÷ 1,000 = 88 cubic feet per hour.
  5. Clock the meter with all other gas appliances off. Time one revolution of the 1 cubic foot dial: seconds per cubic foot = 3,600 ÷ 88 = 40.9 seconds.
  6. Check with the clock: input = 3,600 × 1 cu ft × 1,000 ÷ seconds. At 40.9 s this gives 88,000 Btu/h. A faster dial, such as 36 s, means 100,000 Btu/h, which is 13.6 percent over the derated number (100,000 ÷ 88,000 = 1.136).

If the clock shows over-input, do not just trust the kit label. Check the orifice size stamped on each orifice, the gas type, and the manifold pressure. If the clock is right but you are still unsure, put a combustion analyzer in the flue and look at CO and oxygen; Combustion Analyzer Readings shows how. The federal energy agency's published annual average heat content of delivered natural gas was about 1,037 Btu per cubic foot in 2025, so a real utility number can differ from our 1,000 round figure by several percent. Use the utility's number for the real job. For the full clocking steps, including tips for multi-dial meters and timing, see Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.

Field steps

  • Find the job elevation, not the nearest town's.
  • Read the maker's altitude table for the exact model and gas type.
  • Install the kit parts (orifices, pressure switch if listed) and fill in the label.
  • Set manifold pressure to the data plate value, not lower.
  • Clock the meter, compare to the derated input, and then run a combustion check.

Field card: Derating Gas Appliances for Altitude

Thin air, same gas: cut the input, then prove it.

  • Pressure is about 83 percent of sea level at 5,000 ft (our calculation).
  • Without a derate, expect incomplete burning and CO.
  • Common rule where the maker has none: 4 percent per 1,000 ft above 2,000 ft.
  • Use the maker's kit and table for your model; makers differ on pressure switch changes.
  • Do not derate by lowering manifold pressure below the data plate value.
  • Our example: 100,000 Btu/h at 5,000 ft is 88,000 Btu/h, 40.9 s per cubic foot at 1,000 Btu/cu ft.
  • Confirm with the meter clock and a combustion check.
