Labor and Delivery Room HVAC: LDRP Rooms, C-Section Rooms, Newborn Warming and Nitrous Oxide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A labor room is a patient room with an unusual job: the mother may want it cool, the newborn needs it warm, family wants to stay, and nitrous oxide may be in use. This lesson covers the air side of a labor, delivery, recovery and postpartum (LDRP) room, how the C-section room differs, and how to think about nitrous oxide in the room air.

Check the current standard

Check the current standard. Hospitals follow the edition adopted by their state, plus their own ventilation plan. We could read the cesarean row of a public ASHRAE Standard 170 addendum, but not the current labor/delivery/recovery or nursery rows, so the LDRP air-change number below is labeled as an example. Confirm design values with the facility engineer.

The LDRP room as a patient room

An LDRP room lets one patient labor, deliver, recover and stay. For the person who services the air it behaves like a patient room, not an operating room. An older state hospital rule we read (a table of ventilation rates for hospital areas, shown as plain text in the sources) lists the LDRP room at 2 outdoor and 6 total air changes per hour (ACH), with directional control not required. The same table lists a nursery suite at the same 2 and 6, positive pressure, 75 F, and 30 to 60 percent relative humidity. Treat these as an older reference, not the current standard.

The hard part is comfort. The World Health Organization's newborn guide says the delivery room should be warm, at least 25 C (77 F), with a range of 25 to 28 C (77 to 82 F) when the baby is dried and held skin to skin. It also says the room should be free of drafts from open windows and doors or from fans, and that in hot weather air conditioning or fans should be turned off or adjusted in the delivery room. A mother in labor often feels too warm at that setting. So the usual complaint is a conflict between two people in one room, and the fix is local control, not a cold supply stream across the bed.

Newborn warmers and drafts

Many rooms have a radiant warmer: a heater above a bassinet that holds the baby's skin near 36 to 37 C using a skin sensor, according to a neonatology reference. The same page notes that heater output can be affected by drafts, air conditioning, or people moving near the bed. Air that moves across the baby also pulls heat by convection. For us this means:

  • Keep supply diffusers away from the warmer and the bed. Use a pattern that does not throw air at either one (see the figure).
  • Do not add a fan or spot cooler to chase a comfort complaint without asking the clinical staff.
  • The warmer is also a heat source. It adds load to a small room, so check that the room thermostat is not sitting where warmer heat reads it.
  • Quiet matters: a noisy VAV box or a rattling grille near a sleeping family is a service call.

For tiny premature babies, see NICU HVAC, which covers isolette drafts and noise targets so we do not repeat them here.

C-section rooms are operating rooms

A cesarean delivery room is a surgical room. The public ASHRAE Standard 170 addendum we read lists it as positive pressure, at least 4 outdoor and 20 total ACH, no air recirculated by room units, MERV-16 filtration, 20 to 60 percent relative humidity, and 68 to 75 F. The addendum also requires a documented risk assessment before operational changes to space ventilation. The CDC environmental guideline gives the older general operating-room figures. Everything else, including laminar diffusers, setbacks and humidity history, is in Operating Room HVAC. One C-section wrinkle: the baby is warmed in the same room, so the cold OR must still give the newborn a warm spot. Ask clinical staff how they plan this before touching a setpoint.

Nitrous oxide in labor

A hospital trade article says self-administered nitrous oxide and oxygen is mainly used on maternity units in labor and delivery rooms, and that it needs the same risk assessment as a surgical suite. The article says a waste anesthesia gas disposal (WAGD) system must be used, which means collecting gas at the source and venting it outdoors. For dentistry the same hazards and fixes are in Dental Office HVAC.

NIOSH recommends 25 parts per million (ppm) as a time-weighted average during administration. Its alert says to vent scavenging exhaust outside, away from fresh air intakes, windows and walkways, never into a recirculating system, and to keep scavenging flow near 45 liters per minute (below 40 L/min may leak around the mask). It suggests ceiling fresh-air supply and exhaust at or near the floor. NIOSH also says to check for leaks every 3 months, and that if levels exceed 25 ppm you add airflow or outdoor air and then local exhaust with scavenging. Its hazard-control page adds that supply and exhaust should be well apart so air does not short-circuit.

Where the room pattern cannot hold levels down alone, the scavenging line does the main work, which is why NIOSH puts local collection after dilution in its order of steps.

Worked example: room airflow and a nitrous oxide leak

Everything here is our own arithmetic. The room size is our assumption, and 6 total ACH is our labeled example from the older table above, not a current requirement.

  1. Room volume: 340 sq ft x 10 ft = 3,400 cubic feet.
  2. Airflow at 6 ACH: 3,400 x 6 / 60 = 340 cfm. Outdoor air at 2 ACH: 3,400 x 2 / 60 = 113 cfm.
  3. Assume a leak of 1 liter per minute of nitrous oxide (our assumption; the real leak varies). 1 L/min = 0.0353 cfm.
  4. Steady state with well-mixed air: C = G / Q = 0.0353 / 340 = 0.000104, which is about 104 ppm. That is above the 25 ppm REL, so room dilution alone at 6 ACH is not enough if the leak went uncontrolled.
  5. To hold 25 ppm by dilution only: Q = 0.0353 / 0.000025 = about 1,410 cfm, or 1,410 x 60 / 3,400 = about 25 ACH.
  6. With scavenging that catches 70 percent (NIOSH says working systems cut nitrous oxide by more than 70 percent), the remaining leak is 0.0106 cfm, so C = about 31 ppm. At an assumed 90 percent capture it is about 10 ppm.

The model assumes perfect mixing and a constant leak, and it ignores the patient's breathing zone, where levels are higher. The point is the shape: concentration rises with the leak and falls with airflow, and only collecting gas at the source changes G. Do not treat the numbers as a measured result.

Family, visitors and noise

Extra people add heat and moisture, and open doors disturb room pressure. If the room uses directional control, a door held open by visitors can erase it; pressure monitors are covered in Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare. Keep terminal units quiet; a family trying to rest will notice a hiss the staff learned to ignore.

What to check on a service call

  • Is the diffuser throwing air at the bed or the warmer?
  • Is the room thermostat in a place the warmer or the bed lamp can heat?
  • Is the exhaust grille unblocked, and does a tissue test show air moving toward it?
  • Does the scavenging exhaust discharge outdoors, away from intakes?
  • Did the clinical or infection-prevention team approve any setpoint or airflow change in writing?

Field card: Labor and Delivery Room HVAC

Warm baby, cooler mother: solve it locally, never with a draft.

  • LDRP room is a patient room; the older state table we read lists 6 total / 2 outdoor ACH. Confirm the current rate.
  • WHO newborn guide: delivery room at least 25 C (77 F), no drafts, adjust fans and air conditioning.
  • C-section room: ASHRAE 170 addendum lists 20 total / 4 outdoor ACH, positive, 68 to 75 F.
  • NIOSH nitrous oxide REL: 25 ppm TWA during administration.
  • Scavenging about 45 L/min, vented outdoors, away from intakes, never into a recirculating system.
  • Dilution idea: C = G / Q. Example 1 L/min leak in 340 cfm is about 104 ppm.
  • Get written approval before changing airflow or setpoints.
