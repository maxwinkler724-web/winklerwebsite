Glycol Makeup Feeders on Hydronic and Snow-Melt Loops: Why No City-Water Auto Fill, Feeder Pressure Settings, Leak Tracking and Concentration Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A plain boiler loop tops itself up from the city main through a fill valve. A glycol loop should not. Every gallon of plain water that sneaks in thins the mix and lowers the freeze protection you added the glycol for. This lesson covers the small premixed feeder that replaces the city-water fill, how to set it, and how to use it as a leak indicator.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Backflow, glycol and waste rules differ by town and state. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026. Follow the equipment maker's instructions and the authority where you work.

Why a glycol loop should not have a city-water auto fill

A normal automatic fill valve holds the loop at a set pressure. If the pressure drops, it opens and lets in city water. On a water-only loop that is the right answer. On a glycol loop it quietly changes the fluid. The loop lost some mix, the valve replaced it with plain water, and the glycol share fell. Nobody sees it, because the pressure gauge still reads normal and the heat still works. The first sign can be a split pipe on a cold night.

The second problem is contamination of the drinking water. An EPA cross-connection control manual describes a case in which ethylene glycol from a cooling system's holding tank got into the potable lines at a medical center. A manual fill valve had been left partly open so the tank was fed from the potable supply, and when line pressure fell the glycol moved backward into the drinking water. Two dialysis patients died. The same manual lists a boiler corrosion inhibitor reaching a building's water supply through leaking check valves on boiler feed lines, and a 10,000 gallon solar storage tank with inhibitor chemicals and no backflow protection on its makeup line. A permanent link between a treated loop and the city main is a hazard even when everything works today. For the fill valve and backflow device on an ordinary water-only boiler, see Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer and Backflow Prevention on HVAC Makeup Water.

How a feeder package works

A glycol feeder is a small self-contained package. The parts, in the order the fluid moves:

  • Premixed tank. It holds glycol already blended to the same strength as the loop. The tank is usually at room pressure, with no connection to the city main. A low-level switch or float sends an alarm before the tank runs dry.
  • Pump. A small pump pushes fluid from the tank into the loop, against the loop's pressure.
  • Pressure switch. It senses loop pressure and starts the pump when pressure falls below its setting, then stops it when pressure is restored.
  • Check valve. It keeps loop fluid from flowing backward into the tank when the pump is off.

The package is piped into the loop near the expansion tank, so the switch sees real system pressure and the new fluid mixes into the flow. The diagram shows the layout, with the old city-water fill crossed out. The feeder is a makeup device, not a repair. It never replaces finding the leak.

Setting the feeder pressure against the loop fill pressure

The loop has a cold fill pressure, set with the expansion tank pre-charge. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure. The feeder has two settings that must agree with it. This is our own reasoning, so confirm the details in the maker's instructions.

  • Switch cut-in. Set it a little below the loop's normal cold fill pressure, and above the point where air would be pulled in or pumps would starve. If it is set at or above the normal pressure, the pump will run every time the loop warms and cools and push the pressure up with every start.
  • Pump capability. The pump must be able to push against the highest normal loop pressure, but the loop's relief valve setting must stay above anything the pump can reach.
  • Air first. A pressure drop from trapped air or a stuck air vent can look like a leak. Rule out air, see Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents.

Use the feeder as a leak indicator

A feeder that runs often is telling you something. Write down the tank level, or the date and the gallons added, each visit. A tank that drops a little through the season is normal. A tank that suddenly drops faster, or a pump that cycles often, means fluid is going somewhere: a weeping valve packing, a pinhole, or a relief valve that is opening. Because the tank is a fixed volume, you can also tell how many gallons the loop has lost between visits. A low-level alarm is useful, but the logged trend is what warns you early. Never let a busy feeder hide the leak. If it is feeding steadily, find the wet spot, especially under slabs and in boiler rooms.

Testing concentration and inhibitor

Check the mix with a refractometer made for the type of glycol in the loop, and check it after any top-up or repair. Read how to take and read a sample, and how freeze protection changes with percentage, in Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops. A refractometer only measures glycol strength. It does not tell you whether the corrosion inhibitor is still working. A U.S. Department of Energy technology alert on solar water heating says propylene glycol breaks down as its corrosion inhibitors wear out, and that the fluid should be replaced every 5 to 10 years (sooner if the system runs hot). A DOE-hosted conference abstract also lists inhibited glycols as the way to give both corrosion and freeze protection, and ties the right amount to the chosen concentration. Ask the fluid maker how to test inhibitor and what to do when it is low.

Propylene vs. ethylene glycol, in general terms

ATSDR describes ethylene glycol as a sweet-tasting antifreeze that can seriously harm the kidneys, nervous system, lungs and heart if a large amount is swallowed. It also says small accidental amounts, such as a taste from wet fingers, are not likely to cause serious illness. ATSDR describes propylene glycol as generally considered safe and notes that the FDA classifies it as generally recognized as safe for food use; unlike ethylene glycol, it does not form harmful crystals when it breaks down. A conference abstract on the DOE OSTI site says both glycols reduce heat transfer and increase pumping power compared with water, an effect that is small in higher-temperature heating loops and larger in cooling loops. Never mix the two types in one loop unless the maker says so, and match the refractometer scale to the one in use.

Backflow and code, stated generally

The EPA manual says backflow protection should match the degree of hazard, and that the right device depends on that hazard, pipe size, location and testing needs. It describes an air gap as protecting against both backsiphonage and backpressure, and a reduced pressure principle device as the highest level of protection, used for high-hazard uses. A glycol loop that is ever connected to city water is a hazard that local plumbing authorities usually treat seriously, which is why a feeder with a vented tank, a check valve and no hard link to the main is the usual approach. Some jurisdictions still require a device on any boiler-room water connection, and some require strict separation of glycol and potable water. Ask the local plumbing authority before you design it, and do not fill a glycol loop through a temporary hose from the city line.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These are assumptions, not field data. A loop and slab hold 100 gal in total, mixed at 40 percent glycol by volume, so it holds 40 gal of glycol and 60 gal of water. We treat volumes as simply adding together, which is close enough for a study problem.

A leak and a plain-water top-up. Suppose a leak loses 10 gal of mix and someone tops it up with 10 gal of plain water.

  1. Glycol lost: 10 gal x 0.40 = 4 gal.
  2. Glycol left: 40 - 4 = 36 gal, in the same 100 gal total.
  3. New concentration: 36 / 100 = 36 percent.

The loop now has less freeze protection than the 40 percent we designed for. We use the refractometer reading as the check, because the pressure gauge looks normal.

Restoring 40 percent by drain and add. Adding pure glycol alone would overfill the loop. So we drain some of the 36 percent mix and put the same volume of pure (100 percent) glycol back, keeping 100 gal total. Let x be the gallons drained and replaced.

  1. Glycol after the swap: 36 - 0.36x + 1.00x = 36 + 0.64x.
  2. Set that equal to the 40 gal target: 36 + 0.64x = 40, so 0.64x = 4.
  3. x = 4 / 0.64 = 6.25 gal of pure glycol.

Check: after draining 6.25 gal, the loop holds 93.75 gal at 36 percent, which is 33.75 gal of glycol. Add 6.25 gal of pure glycol: 33.75 + 6.25 = 40 gal in 100 gal, which is 40 percent. In practice the maker's concentrate may not be 100 percent and some glycol is premixed, so use the maker's strength, test again with the refractometer, and bleed air after refilling.

Disposal of old glycol

Do not pour old glycol on the ground or into a storm drain, ditch, septic system or sewer. ATSDR advises avoiding spilling or draining antifreeze onto the ground, noting that ethylene glycol breaks down in water and soil within several days to a few weeks and to keep open containers away from children. An EPA document on used antifreeze says used antifreeze can contain metals such as lead, cadmium and chromium at levels that make it a regulated hazardous waste, that it may never be dumped into a storm drain, sanitary sewer, ditch, dry well or septic system, and that the state or local environmental agency is the best source for compliance guidance. It also lists on-site and mobile recycling or an off-site recycler as options. Keep drained fluid in labeled, closed containers and ask your local waste authority or a licensed hauler.

Field card: Glycol makeup feeders

Plain water in, freeze protection out.

  • No city-water auto fill on a glycol loop. Use a premixed feeder.
  • Feeder: premixed tank, pump, pressure switch, check valve, low-level alarm.
  • Cut-in a little below the cold fill pressure. Relief valve stays above the pump.
  • Log the tank level at each visit. A faster drop means a leak. Rule out air first.
  • Refractometer for strength, a separate check for inhibitor.
  • Example (our own): 100 gal at 40 percent, 10 gal lost and replaced with water gives 36 percent. Drain 6.25 gal and add 6.25 gal pure glycol to return to 40.
  • Old glycol is waste. Ask the local agency. Never pour it out.
