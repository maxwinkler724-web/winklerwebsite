Boiler NOx Emissions: Low-NOx Burners, Flue Gas Recirculation and NJ Air Permit Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler tech spends most of the day on safety and efficiency, but the stack is also regulated. Nitrogen oxides (NOx) are one of the things a burner can be tuned wrongly for, and in New Jersey the owner's air permit decides what the limit is. This lesson explains how NOx forms, how low-NOx burners and flue gas recirculation hold it down, how to read it on an analyzer, and what the New Jersey and federal rules ask of the person doing the tune-up.

Check the owner's permit and NJDEP

The owner's permit governs. Rules, permit numbers and size cutoffs change. This page was built from agency and rule pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal advice. For any real boiler, read the air permit or general permit registration on file, and check the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection (NJDEP) for current rules before you rely on anything here.

How NOx forms in a burner

EPA's AP-42 chapter on natural gas combustion names three routes. Thermal NOx is the main one for gas. It forms in the hot flame zone, and AP-42 says its rate rises with oxygen concentration, peak temperature and time spent at that peak. Fuel NOx comes from nitrogen bound in the fuel; AP-42 calls it insignificant for natural gas, although it matters for some oils. Prompt NOx forms early in the flame from nitrogen in the air and is usually small, though AP-42 says it can become significant with ultra-low-NOx burners.

The practical takeaway for a gas boiler is that NOx control means making the flame cooler, or giving the nitrogen less oxygen at the hottest point, without letting the flame become unstable or smoky.

Low-NOx burners and flue gas recirculation

AP-42 describes low-NOx burners as burners that stage combustion so the flame runs cooler, either by staging the air or staging the fuel, and gives a reduction of roughly 40 to 85 percent from uncontrolled levels. Flue gas recirculation (FGR) sends some cooled flue gas back to the burner. AP-42 says the recycled gas dilutes the flame and lowers its temperature, with a smaller effect from lowering oxygen in the primary flame zone, and that FGR is normally paired with burners designed for it. Together they are listed at roughly 60 to 90 percent reduction.

An HPAC Engineering article on industrial low-NOx burners says most current burners use induced FGR, where the combustion air fan pulls flue gas into a mixing box. It gives typical recirculation of 5 to 15 percent of flue gas output, rising to 20 to 30 percent near 9 ppm, and notes that the extra flow adds fan horsepower and slightly lowers boiler efficiency. For a 100 MMBtu/hr gas boiler it gives example levels of 85 ppm uncontrolled, 42 ppm low-NOx and 26 ppm low-NOx with FGR, and says these are illustrations, not regulatory limits. Ultra-low-NOx is the informal name for designs aimed at single-digit ppm. The Sacramento air district's BACT summary for 5 to 20 MMBtu/hr boilers lists 9 ppm for low-NOx burners and 7 ppm for ultra-low-NOx burners, both at 3% O2; that is a California district's finding, not a New Jersey limit.

What it does to flame stability and turndown

Recirculated flue gas is inert, so it makes the flame harder to hold. AP-42 says FGR requires low-NOx burners designed to keep the flame stable with that added flow, and that low-NOx burners and FGR can lower combustion efficiency and raise CO when off design or badly tuned. The HPAC article adds that designs aimed at 30 to 9 ppm use staged gun arrangements with separately modulated valves, that burners at 9 ppm or lower take longer to settle after load changes, and that emission guarantees are generally given at 4:1 turndown. It warns owners not to assume the burner can run lower than that and still hold its NOx number. For the tech this means the controls matter: a drifting air damper, a sticky FGR damper or a dirty flame scanner can push a low-NOx burner into high CO or a nuisance lockout long before it shows as a NOx problem. See burner controls and O2 trim.

Why ppm is corrected to 3% O2

A ppm reading depends on how much extra air is in the stack. More excess air dilutes the sample and makes the same burner look cleaner. To compare fairly, limits are written at a reference O2, and gas boiler limits in New Jersey general permits use 3% O2. The correction is the same one that appears in federal rules. 40 CFR 60.4213 defines an equation that adjusts a measured concentration to 15 percent O2 as Cadj = Cd x 5.9 / (20.9 − %O2), where 5.9 is 20.9 minus 15 and %O2 is the measured dry O2. Swap the 15 for 3 and the constant becomes 17.9. N.J.A.C. 7:27-19.16 uses the same 20.9 / (20.9 − measured O2) factor when converting ppm to pounds per million Btu. Our own note: the engine rule uses 15%, so we are applying its form with a different reference O2, not quoting a boiler formula from it.

Worked example: correcting a reading to 3% O2 (Our own)

Assumptions (ours): a natural gas boiler; the analyzer reads NOx in ppm on a dry basis; the O2 reading is dry and taken at the same sample point and time; no sample dilution or leaks.

  1. Measured: 22 ppm NOx at 5.0% O2.
  2. Constant for 3% reference: 20.9 − 3 = 17.9.
  3. Measured term: 20.9 − 5.0 = 15.9.
  4. Corrected: 22 x 17.9 / 15.9 = 393.8 / 15.9 = 24.8 ppm at 3% O2.

The raw 22 ppm looked lower than 24.8 only because the stack had extra air. To compare with a pound-per-Btu number, use the N.J.A.C. 7:27-19.16 form with its stated constants (molecular weight 46 for NOx, F-factor 8,710 dscf/MMBtu for natural gas, divisor 387,000,000): 22 x 46 x 8,710 x (20.9 / 15.9) / 387,000,000 = about 0.030 lb/MMBtu. For context, NJDEP's General Permit GP-009 page lists a natural gas limit of 0.0350 lb/MMBtu, equal to 29.0 ppmvd at 3% O2, so this boiler would sit under that example limit. Your permit may say something else.

Reading NOx on an analyzer

  • New Jersey's tune-up procedure and the federal area source tune-up both call for NO or CO and O2 before and after the adjustment, with a portable analyzer allowed. Record the O2 along with every NOx and CO value.
  • Our own advice: sample where the permit or test method says, and keep the probe clear of air leaks, because leaks raise O2 and distort the correction. See combustion analyzer readings and stack economizers.
  • Read your analyzer's manual to see whether it reports NO, NO2 or total NOx, and which one the permit limit uses.
  • Take readings at stable load. Recheck after any change to air, FGR or fuel settings.

New Jersey basics: permits and tune-ups

Air permits. NJDEP offers general permits that boiler owners register for online rather than negotiating a custom permit. The NJDEP pages we read describe GP-006A for boilers and heaters each under 10 MMBtu/hr (with annual NOx tonnage caps, and no stated tune-up or stack test) and GP-009 for boilers from 10 up to 50 MMBtu/hr. GP-009 requires low-NOx burners, or low-NOx burners with FGR, an annual tune-up no sooner than 9 months after the last, and before-and-after NOx, CO and O2 readings. A 2018 NJDEP training presentation lists the general permit names slightly differently and gives other numbers, so permit numbers do change. Larger boilers generally go through a case-by-case permit; we did not find that cutoff stated on the pages we read.

NOx RACT, N.J.A.C. 7:27-19. Under section 19.2, industrial, commercial and institutional boilers are covered from 5 MMBtu/hr, whether or not the facility is a major NOx facility. Section 19.7 sets numeric limits in tables for larger units, such as 0.05 lb/MMBtu for natural gas only at 25 to under 100 MMBtu/hr (non-refinery), and requires an annual combustion adjustment for units of 5 MMBtu/hr and up, done in the same quarter each year. Section 19.16 says what the adjustment is: inspect and clean the burner, check the flame pattern, check the air-to-fuel control calibration, minimize NOx and CO, and measure NO, CO and O2 before and after. Records go in a log kept at least five years, and an annual report goes to NJDEP within 45 days after the adjustment. Do the work as a tech, but the owner signs for the permit.

The federal area source boiler rule

40 CFR 63 subpart JJJJJJ covers certain boilers at area sources of hazardous air pollutants. Gas-fired boilers are excluded (section 63.11195(e)), and the definition in 63.11237 allows liquid fuel only for curtailment, startup, or up to 48 hours a year of testing and training. For covered coal, biomass and oil boilers, the tune-up repeats at most every 25 months. Boilers with an oxygen trim system, seasonal boilers, limited-use boilers and small oil-fired units of 5 MMBtu/hr or less may use a 61-month cycle (section 63.11223). The steps are similar to New Jersey's, and CO and O2 are measured before and after. This rule is about CO and hazardous air pollutants, not a NOx limit.

Field card: Boiler NOx basics

Cooler flame, less NOx; the permit sets the number.

  • Gas boilers make mostly thermal NOx in the hot flame zone.
  • Low-NOx burners stage the flame; FGR dilutes and cools it.
  • ppm at 3% O2 = measured ppm x (20.9 − 3) / (20.9 − measured O2).
  • Always write down O2 with the NOx and CO readings.
  • NJ: annual combustion adjustment for boilers of 5 MMBtu/hr and up; log kept 5 years.
  • The owner's permit governs. Check NJDEP for current rules.
