Endoscopy Suite HVAC: Procedure Rooms, Scope Reprocessing Room Exhaust, Disinfectant Vapors, Drying Cabinets and Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An endoscopy suite looks like an ordinary clinic, but it holds two different air problems. One is infection risk in the room where a scope goes into a patient's lungs. The other is the chemical vapor in the room where scopes are cleaned. This lesson sorts them out for a facility tech, using public government and standards-committee documents.

Check the facility's design documents first

The design documents and the infection control team decide. We read public addenda, federal guidance and a federal field study, not the full standard tables, so this page does not give a code answer for any room. Find the row the project was designed to, and do not change pressure or airflow without the owner's approval.

Three kinds of room, three jobs

Bronchoscopy room. ASHRAE's public Addendum w to Standard 170-2021 keeps a minimum pressure difference of negative 0.01 in. of water (negative 2.5 Pa) between a bronchoscopy room and adjacent spaces with other functions. The Joint Commission says in an FAQ that bronchoscopy must be done in a negative pressure room under the ventilation table, and that this is a space provision from NFPA code, so a risk assessment alone does not remove it. Where a patient cannot be moved to such a room, the organization needs a written process that weighs airborne risk and mitigation, such as HEPA filtration. A separate ASHRAE interpretation of the 2021 edition adds that the standard does not dictate how a space is used day to day; the infection control risk assessment and operational plan do.

GI procedure room. An ACHR News article on the standard's updates notes that the 2008 edition listed endoscopy rooms as positive, the 2013 edition changed the pressure column to no requirement, and a room used for bronchoscopy must be negative to adjacent spaces. The addendum adds that when one space is planned for both bronchoscopy and GI endoscopy, the bronchoscopy parameters apply. So a "GI room" that also does bronchoscopy cases is a negative room.

Scope reprocessing room. ASHRAE's addendum names "endoscope cleaning" among exhaust streams that must not go through an energy recovery device with leakage potential. That is the one design rule we confirmed for this room. For air change targets, NIOSH is more specific; see the next section.

For bronchoscopy exhaust, the addendum treats it like airborne isolation exhaust: a discharge at least 10 ft above the service level, 30 ft from outdoor air intakes and openable windows, and ductwork under negative pressure, with a HEPA exception.

The reprocessing room: where the vapor is

A NIOSH health hazard evaluation of eight facilities using ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA) found that most designed those rooms for at least 10 air changes per hour with no recirculation, and most rooms were negative to adjacent areas. Its recommendations: use the disinfectant in a dedicated room; at least 10 air changes per hour, including at least 2 outdoor; no recirculation; supply diffusers and returns placed to avoid short-circuiting; negative pressure with air moving from clean toward dirty areas; and local exhaust at the vapor source. It cites an industrial hygiene association suggestion of 80 to 120 feet per minute average face velocity.

The same study found doors, pass-through windows and partitions often left open, which killed the pressure difference. In one facility the chemical sat in a hallway where negative pressure could not hold. Automated washers had built-in local exhaust that went outdoors or through a charcoal filter, and some returned filtered air to the workroom.

For glutaraldehyde, NIOSH's older hospital alert recommends local exhaust with a capture velocity of at least 100 feet per minute and at least 10 air changes per hour, and found a system with only 10 percent outside air inadequate. OSHA's hospital eTool also says 100 feet per minute, adds mobile soaking stations that enclose the tray and remove vapor, and states there is no OSHA permissible limit for glutaraldehyde. The NIOSH pocket guide lists a ceiling of 0.2 ppm (0.8 mg/m3); the eTool lists a recommended ACGIH ceiling of 0.05 ppm. Peracetic acid products were not covered by the sources we read; check the product's safety data sheet with the facility safety officer.

The CDC disinfection guideline says automated endoscope reprocessors reduce staff exposure to high-level disinfectants, and that OPA needs no activation or exposure monitoring.

Drying cabinets, humidity and recovery bays

The CDC guideline says to rinse the channels with alcohol, dry them with forced air before storage, and store scopes in a way that promotes drying, for example hung vertically. It cites studies of vertical storage in a ventilated cabinet with very low rates of bacterial growth, and says a room or cabinet should be used for processed scopes only. A federal ambulatory surgery tool says the temperature and humidity where scopes are stored should be monitored, without giving a range. Ask the infection control team what range and log they use.

We did not confirm humidity or air change targets for recovery bays from a primary source, so we leave them out. Look up the project's design row, then see the ambulatory surgery lesson for how a suite's humidity logs and pressure map fit together.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions, all ours: a reprocessing room of 150 sq ft with a 9 ft ceiling; we use the NIOSH 10 air changes per hour as the target; a 4 ft sink with a slot hood at an example 100 cfm per linear foot; a 75 cfm negative offset. The slot hood rate is an assumption, not a cited design value; the hood maker's data and the facility's design govern.

  1. Volume: 150 sq ft x 9 ft = 1,350 cu ft.
  2. Room air at 10 ACH: 1,350 x 10 / 60 = 225 cfm.
  3. Outdoor minimum at 2 ACH: 1,350 x 2 / 60 = 45 cfm.
  4. Hood: 4 ft x 100 cfm/ft = 400 cfm.
  5. The hood is part of the room exhaust, not extra. Total exhaust is 400 cfm, which is 400 x 60 / 1,350 = 17.8 ACH. The 10 ACH target is met by the hood alone.
  6. Supply = exhaust minus offset = 400 - 75 = 325 cfm, which is 14.4 ACH of supply. The 75 cfm difference is what holds the room negative.
  7. With no recirculation, all 325 cfm is outdoor air or air from a system the designer approved for it. Sensible load at an example 20 F difference: 1.08 x 325 x 20 = 7,020 Btu/h.

What a tech checks

  • Door closed when the room is unattended; smoke at the door gap pulls in.
  • Pressure monitor reads negative and alarms work.
  • Hood capture checked with a meter, per the lesson on measuring airflow.
  • Exhaust ducts reach outdoors; no energy recovery device with leakage potential on this stream.
  • Washer exhaust filters changed on schedule and local exhaust in the preventive maintenance plan.

Field card: Endoscopy Suite HVAC

Bronchoscopy and reprocessing rooms pull air in. Keep the doors shut.

  • Bronchoscopy room: at least negative 0.01 in. w.c. to adjacent spaces (ASHRAE addendum).
  • Room used for both GI and bronchoscopy follows the bronchoscopy parameters.
  • Reprocessing room per NIOSH: 10 ACH, 2 outdoor, no recirculation, negative.
  • Local exhaust at the sink: 100 fpm capture (NIOSH, OSHA eTool); 80 to 120 fpm face velocity suggested.
  • Our example: 225 cfm room air, 400 cfm hood, 325 cfm supply, 75 cfm offset.
  • No energy recovery with leakage on endoscope cleaning exhaust.
  • Humidity ranges come from the facility, not from us.
