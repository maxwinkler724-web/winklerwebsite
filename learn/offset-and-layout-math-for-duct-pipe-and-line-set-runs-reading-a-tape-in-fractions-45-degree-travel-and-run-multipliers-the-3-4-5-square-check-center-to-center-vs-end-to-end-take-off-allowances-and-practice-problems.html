Offset and Layout Math for Duct, Pipe and Line Set Runs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most rough-in mistakes are not skill mistakes, they are arithmetic mistakes: a fraction added wrong, a diagonal cut to the wrong length, a corner that was never square. This lesson covers the small set of layout math a helper uses daily: reading a tape in fractions, 45 degree offsets, a few other angle multipliers, the 3-4-5 check for square, and how to turn a centerline dimension into a cut length.

Reading a tape in fractions

An inch on a tape is cut in half again and again: halves, quarters, eighths, sixteenths, and on many tapes thirty-seconds. A college measuring handout describes the same scheme and the habit that goes with it: write dimensions as whole inches plus a fraction, and reduce the fraction (4/16 becomes 2/8, then 1/4). To find a mark such as 13/16, count thirteen sixteenths from the inch line, or count three sixteenths back from the next inch.

Adding is easier if you put everything over the same denominator first. Our own example: 7 5/8 + 3 3/16. Change 5/8 to 10/16, add the fractions (10/16 + 3/16 = 13/16) and the whole numbers (7 + 3 = 10), and you get 10 13/16 inches. Do the same for subtraction, borrowing one inch as 16/16 when you need to.

Offsets: set, run and travel

An offset moves a duct, pipe or line set sideways around an obstacle using two matching fittings. Trade training texts name the sides of the triangle: the offset (also called the set) is the distance between the two parallel centerlines, the run is the distance along the original direction, and the travel is the diagonal, the center-to-center length of the angled piece. One state-posted piping training excerpt calls the diagonal the hypotenuse and says the fitter works out the unknown sides with sine, cosine and tangent or a multiplier table. Be careful with words: some workbooks use "run" for the diagonal, so always check what a drawing or a coworker means before you cut.

The 45 degree rule and the other multipliers

At 45 degrees the triangle has two equal legs, so the run equals the offset. The diagonal is the longer side of a right triangle with two equal legs, which by the Pythagorean theorem is the square root of 2 (about 1.414) times a leg. So travel = offset x 1.414. A pipefitting workbook from a federal education database states the same thing using the cosecant of 45 degrees, 1.4142, and notes that its worked examples subtract two small weld gaps from the centerline length.

For other fitting angles, use the sine and tangent: travel = offset divided by sin(angle), run = offset divided by tan(angle). Our own calculations from those formulas, rounded to three places:

Notice the pattern: shallow angles give long runs and long travel, steep angles give short ones. A 22.5 degree offset of 4 inches needs about 9 5/8 inches of run, which is why flex duct and line sets are often swept gently while rigid pipe around a beam uses 45 or 60.

Squaring a layout with 3-4-5

A state education department lesson on squaring corners explains the check: a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 is a right triangle because 3 squared plus 4 squared (9 + 16) equals 5 squared (25). Any multiple works as long as all three sides use the same unit, so 36-48-60 inches is common on a jobsite. To test a corner, mark 36 inches along one line and 48 inches along the other from the corner. If the distance between the marks is exactly 60, the corner is square. Longer means the angle is wider than 90 degrees, shorter means narrower. Use bigger multiples for longer runs; the bigger the triangle, the smaller the effect of a tape-reading slip.

Center-to-center versus end-to-end

Drawings and offset math give you center-to-center distances, measured along the centerline from fitting center to fitting center. The piece you cut is measured end-to-end. The difference is the fitting take-off (also called take-out or makeup allowance): the distance from a fitting's centerline to the point where the pipe or duct stops inside it. The cut length is the center-to-center length minus the take-off at each end. The federal workbook above gives a formula for welded elbows that relates take-off to elbow radius and angle, and lists different values for different sizes and angles. In other words, take-off varies by fitting type, size and angle; for copper, plastic, grooved, and sheet-metal fittings, get it from the fitting maker's data or measure a fitting in your hand. Never reuse a number from a different fitting.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed inputs. A line set must jog 9 1/2 inches around a stud pocket using two 45 degree fittings. Assume each fitting has a take-off of 1 1/8 inches (an assumption for this example only; measure your actual fitting).

  1. Travel (center-to-center) = 9.5 x 1.414 = 13.43 inches, about 13 7/16.
  2. Run along the wall = 9.5 inches, since the angle is 45 degrees.
  3. Take-off at both ends = 2 x 1.125 = 2.25 inches.
  4. Cut length of the angled piece = 13.43 - 2.25 = 11.18 inches, about 11 3/16.

Dry-fit the pieces before committing, and mark the piece so you cut square and do not trim twice.

Six practice problems (our own)

These problems are our own. Use 1.414 for 45 degrees and the table above for other angles.

  1. Add 6 3/4 + 2 5/16 inches.
  2. A 45 degree offset is 6 inches. Find the travel.
  3. The travel of a 45 degree offset measures 17 inches center-to-center. What was the offset?
  4. A 22.5 degree offset is 4 inches. Find the travel.
  5. You mark 30 inches and 40 inches from a corner. What distance between the marks means the corner is square, and what does 50 1/4 tell you?
  6. Two fittings are 48 inches apart center-to-center, and each has an assumed take-off of 1 1/2 inches. Find the cut length of the straight piece between them.
Answers
  1. 6 12/16 + 2 5/16 = 8 17/16 = 9 1/16 inches.
  2. 6 x 1.414 = 8.48 inches, about 8 1/2.
  3. 17 / 1.414 = 12.02, so about 12 inches.
  4. 4 x 2.613 = 10.45 inches, about 10 7/16.
  5. 50 inches (a 3-4-5 multiple of 10). 50 1/4 is longer, so the angle is a little more than 90 degrees.
  6. 48 - (2 x 1.5) = 45 inches.

Field card: Offset and Layout Math

Draw it, calculate it, dry-fit it, then cut.

  • Common denominators first; reduce the fraction at the end.
  • 45 degrees: run = offset, travel = offset x 1.414.
  • 22.5 degrees: travel x 2.613, run x 2.414. 60 degrees: travel x 1.155, run x 0.577.
  • Square check: 3-4-5 or 36-48-60 inches; longer diagonal means wider than 90.
  • Cut length = center-to-center minus take-off at each end. Take-off varies by fitting.
