Wet-Coil Heat Transfer on Cooling Coils: Enthalpy, Condensate Film and Pressure Drop

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A dry coil cools air and nothing else. A cooling coil that runs below the air's dew point also pulls water out of it, and that changes the heat transfer, the capacity and the air pressure drop. This lesson covers why a wet coil is described by enthalpy, what the water film does, and how to check a wet coil in the field.

Why enthalpy and not just dry-bulb

On a dry coil, heat moves because the air is warmer than the fin surface. Temperature difference is the driver, and all of the heat is sensible. Once the fin surface is colder than the air's dew point, water vapor condenses on it. Each pound of vapor that condenses gives up its latent heat, so the air loses sensible heat by cooling and latent heat by drying, at the same time and through the same surface. Temperature alone cannot describe that. Enthalpy, the total heat content of the air, can. A coil performance model from a national laboratory states it plainly: it uses temperature as the driving potential in the dry section and enthalpy as the driving potential in the wet section. In that model the water side is treated as saturated air at the water temperature, so the wet section is driven by the gap in enthalpy between the room air and saturated air at the coil surface.

This is why the total-heat formula in the field is built on enthalpy: total Btu/h = 4.5 × cfm × (h entering − h leaving), where 4.5 comes from air density at standard conditions (0.075 lb/ft³) times 60 minutes per hour. For the field method and the sensible and latent split, see Measuring Air-Side Cooling Capacity with Enthalpy and Wet-Bulb Readings.

The condensate film: a little resistance, a lot of capacity

The water on the fins is one more layer between the air and the metal. A thin film is a small resistance compared with the air-side film that already dominates a coil (see Air-Side vs Refrigerant-Side Heat Transfer in a Coil). The laboratory coil model we read does not carry a separate film term at all; it folds the fin resistance into the air-side resistance and uses one enthalpy-based conductance for the wet section. We read that as a sign that, for design and diagnosis, the film is a minor item. What it buys is large. The latent heat taken from the vapor comes on top of the sensible heat, so a wet coil moves much more total heat than the same coil with the same temperature difference would move if dry. In the worked example below, about a quarter of the total comes from condensing water.

Why wet coils show more air pressure drop

Water clings to fins and bridges the gaps between them, so the open area for air shrinks and the air has to push through harder. A university study of condensate on plain-fin and slit-fin heat exchangers found that moisture on the surface generally raises the air-side pressure drop and significantly affects overall thermal-hydraulic performance. The study varied fin spacing, tube rows and surface coatings and measured how much condensate stayed on the coil. The size of the penalty depends on fin spacing, fin shape, coatings and air velocity, so we do not give one number here. Expect a wet coil to read noticeably higher than the same coil dry, and expect tight fin spacing to be hit harder. A federal research report on coil fouling also notes an earlier suggestion that a wet surface promotes dirt build-up on the air side, which is one more reason wet coils need clean filters.

Dry rows and wet rows of the same coil

A coil is not wet or dry as a whole. Air meets the first rows when it is warmest, and if the surface there is above the entering dew point those rows stay dry and work on temperature difference. Farther in, the surface falls below the dew point and the rest of the coil sweats. The laboratory model handles this by splitting the coil into a dry section and a wet section and iterating on the wet fraction until the surface at the boundary sits at the dew point. A coil with a hot entering airstream may be mostly dry, while a humid, lightly loaded day wets nearly all of it. That is also why one coil can show different pressure drop on different days with the same airflow.

Low airflow and frost

Less air across the coil makes the coil colder and the leaving air colder. In laboratory tests reported by a university research center, leaving air fell from 58.8 F at 425 cfm per ton to 47.0 F at 127 cfm per ton. Cutting airflow from 425 to 300 cfm per ton reduced sensible capacity by about 15 percent and raised latent capacity by about 7 percent; moisture removal peaked near 250 cfm per ton and dropped quickly below 200. The same paper warns that very low airflow can lead to coil icing, flood back and eventual compressor failure. Once frost forms, the picture changes: ice insulates the fins, blocks the fin gaps so airflow falls further, and the coil gets colder still. Latent capacity is no longer a bonus, because the moisture is now building up as ice. See AC Evaporator Coil Frozen: Ice on the Lines, Causes and Fixes.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with stated assumptions. Airflow is 1,200 cfm at standard air density. Entering air is 80 F dry bulb and 67 F wet bulb; leaving air is 57 F dry bulb and 56 F wet bulb. At sea-level pressure, a standard psychrometric calculation gives an enthalpy of about 31.4 Btu/lb for the entering air and about 23.8 Btu/lb for the leaving air. These are our own calculated values, so a printed chart read by eye may differ by a few tenths.

  1. Enthalpy difference: 31.4 − 23.8 = 7.6 Btu/lb.
  2. Total heat removed: 4.5 × 1,200 × 7.6 = 41,040 Btu/h, about 3.4 tons.
  3. Sensible part: 1.08 × 1,200 × (80 − 57) = 1.08 × 1,200 × 23 = 29,808 Btu/h.
  4. Latent part by difference: 41,040 − 29,808 = 11,232 Btu/h.
  5. Sensible heat ratio: 29,808 ÷ 41,040 = 0.73.

Check: the humidity ratio falls from about 0.01117 to 0.00931 lb water per lb dry air, a drop of 0.00186. At 5,400 lb of air per hour (4.5 × 1,200) that is about 10 lb of water per hour, roughly 1.2 gallons per hour into the drain pan. The usual latent shortcut (0.68 × cfm × grains) gives about 10,600 Btu/h, within about 5 percent of the 11,232 by difference; the gap comes from rounding and the approximate constants. If the sensible portion looks right and the latent part is zero, your wet bulb readings are wrong or the coil is not dehumidifying.

Field checks

  • Static pressure across the coil, dry vs wet. With the blower running and cooling off, record static before and after the coil, then run cooling until the coil is wet and steady and record again. The wet reading should be higher. A big jump, or a dry reading that is already high, points to dirty fins, a loaded coil or blocked fin gaps. Compare against the maker's coil data for your airflow.
  • Entering and leaving wet bulb. Read both with the same instrument, in a mixed airstream, after the system has run long enough to settle. Leaving wet bulb close to leaving dry bulb (as in the example) says the air left nearly saturated. A large gap means little dehumidification.
  • Airflow. Confirm airflow before you blame refrigerant. Low airflow gives a cold coil and a cold leaving temperature that can fool you into thinking capacity is high.
  • Look for frost. Frost on the coil face or suction line means shut cooling off, let the blower thaw it and find the cause.

Field card: Wet-Coil Heat Transfer on Cooling Coils: Enthalpy, Condensate Film and Pressure Drop

Wet coil: enthalpy drives it, the film is minor, the pressure drop is higher.

  • Dry coil: temperature difference drives sensible heat only.
  • Wet coil: enthalpy difference drives sensible plus latent heat together.
  • Total Btu/h = 4.5 × cfm × enthalpy difference; sensible = 1.08 × cfm × dT.
  • Water on the fins adds a little resistance and raises air pressure drop.
  • Low airflow cools the coil more and risks frost; check airflow first.
  • Compare static across the coil dry and wet; read entering and leaving wet bulb.
