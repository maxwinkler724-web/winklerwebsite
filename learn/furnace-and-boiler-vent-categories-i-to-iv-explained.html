Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Also in the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open any gas furnace or boiler manual and you will find a vent category, I through IV. It decides what pipe you may use, whether the vent can be shared, and where the condensate goes. If you have ever searched "category I vs category IV venting," this is the plain-language version.

The manual decides

The manual decides. This lesson explains the ideas. Vent work follows the appliance maker's installation instructions and the codes where you work, and the vent parts must be the ones the maker lists. Venting mistakes are carbon monoxide mistakes, so see Combustion and CO Safety Basics for what that risk looks like. We read manufacturer manuals, trade articles and a public training text for this page. We did not read the ANSI Z21.47 standard itself, so the category definitions below are as those sources describe them.

Two questions sort every appliance

The categories come from two questions. First, is the pressure inside the vent below room pressure (negative) or above it (positive)? Second, are the flue gases hot enough to stay dry, or cooled enough to make liquid water?

A public Skilled Trades BC text (TRU Open Press) defines the four categories by vent pressure and by flue loss, the share of fuel heat that leaves up the vent. Categories I and II have nonpositive vent pressure; III and IV have positive pressure. Categories I and III have a flue loss of 17 percent or more; II and IV have less than 17 percent. Trane's installer's guide for its condensing furnaces uses the same wording for Category IV: positive vent static pressure and a flue loss under 17 percent.

ACHR News columnist Eric Kjelshus describes the same grid in terms you can picture. Category I uses negative pressure, and the hot gases rise on their own. Category IV is positive pressure and condensing, which he ties to 90-percent-plus AFUE furnaces. He says Category II appliances are no longer manufactured. In practice, you will meet Category I and Category IV far more than the other two. For a deeper look at why a boiler condenses, see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing.

What it means for vent material

Category I. A trade-press article from Plumbing & Mechanical says a Category I system uses Type B double-wall vent, whose air space helps keep the gas warm and the draft working. PICKHVAC, a trade website, also lists a properly lined masonry chimney as an option, with a listed single-wall connector inside the served space.

Category III. The same trade article says Category III needs a fully sealed vent because the pressure is positive, and that it uses Category IV materials. PICKHVAC says plastic is not appropriate for Category III because of the higher temperatures, and names special stainless systems, often AL29-4C.

Category IV. Trane's guide lists PVC, CPVC and ABS, plus several polypropylene systems, and gives maximum temperatures in a table: PVC 158°F, CPVC 212°F, ABS 180°F and polypropylene 230°F. It says not to use cement on polypropylene. It also says Type 29-4C stainless steel must be used in place of PVC in some situations, for example where local codes require it. Whichever you use, it must be what the furnace maker lists. Dura-Vent's liner instructions say flatly that a new Category IV appliance is not permitted to be vented with B vent.

Why a condensing furnace cannot share a masonry chimney

A Category IV furnace is fan-powered and its gas is cool. Dura-Vent's instructions say condensing appliances have much lower exhaust temperatures, which create significant amounts of acidic condensate. Trane's guide calls the condensate somewhat acidic. A cool, wet, pushed gas stream does not belong in a Category I chimney, which is built around warm gas rising. Yates, whose article is cited below, describes condensate in an oversized or cool masonry chimney causing freeze-thaw damage to the masonry.

Trane's guide states that its furnaces are induced draft vented and must not be connected to any vent serving another appliance. PICKHVAC puts it as: a condensing furnace gets its own listed vent and termination. Its chimney section also says not to run its vent through a chimney serving wood-burning or oil furnaces, incinerators or any other gas appliance, and it has sealing and support rules for a chimney used as a pathway for the vent. It points to NFPA 54 for chimney routing. Follow the manual and the code.

The orphaned water heater

Many older homes vent the furnace and the water heater into one chimney. Replace the furnace with a 90-percent-plus unit and the furnace goes out a wall in plastic, while the water heater is left alone in a chimney sized for two appliances. Trane's guide warns that removing a furnace from a shared vent can leave the vent too large for the remaining appliances, and says the vent must be corrected using the National Fuel Gas Code tables. Contractor magazine author Dave Yates describes the result as a water heater that can back-draft, and an oversized, cool chimney that can condense and damage masonry. His fixes are a properly sized liner, or a direct-vent water heater with the chimney abandoned. PICKHVAC also recommends a properly sized aluminum or stainless liner for this case. The right answer on a real job comes from the code tables and the water heater's manual, and part of it is a draft and spillage check afterward. Technicians should treat every furnace swap as a venting job for the other appliance too.

Condensate

Condensing means the furnace makes water, and that water is a design feature to manage. Trane's guide calls for a drain pan under the furnace, a vent condensate trap at least 6 inches tall, and a drain pipe of at least 1/2 inch PVC or CPVC. If the drain crosses an unconditioned space, it asks for UL-listed heat tape. A blocked trap is also a common cause of a pressure switch that will not close; see Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis, and see Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation for where the pressure switch fits in the cycle.

Worked example: counting elbows against the table

Trane's guide gives maximum equivalent vent lengths in Table 10 and counts elbows as extra pipe. This example uses its upflow model UC1B080A9421 with 2-inch pipe in a direct-vent (two-pipe) setup, where the table limit is 50 feet. The limit applies to each pipe separately. Notes in the guide say one short-radius 90° elbow equals 8 feet of 2-inch pipe, one long-radius elbow equals 5 feet, and the termination elbow or tee counts too.

Suppose the vent run has 22 feet of straight pipe and 4 elbows, counting the termination elbow.

  1. With short-radius elbows: 4 × 8 ft = 32 ft of equivalent length.
  2. Add the straight pipe: 22 + 32 = 54 equivalent feet.
  3. Compare to the limit: 54 ft is more than 50 ft. This run is not allowed.

Two fixes are common. With long-radius elbows: 4 × 5 ft = 20 ft, and 22 + 20 = 42 equivalent feet. That is under 50 ft. Or move to 3-inch pipe, if the manual allows it for this model: Table 10 shows a 100-foot limit, and a short-radius 3-inch elbow equals 10 feet, so 22 + 4 × 10 = 62 ft, which is under 100 ft.

Check the model's own table first. Look for an altitude note too. Rheem's addendum for its 90+ furnaces, for example, subtracts length for each extra elbow and cuts allowed length 8 percent per 1,000 feet above 2,000 feet. Different makers handle elbows differently, and these numbers are one manual's example. Use the one that ships with the furnace.

Safe habits

Do not substitute pipe because it "looks the same." Do not skip the trap, the slope or the sealing. Do not leave a furnace or water heater running if you suspect spillage. After any vent change, check that every connected appliance drafts properly and confirm CO levels with an analyzer, as your maker and local rules require, and see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Field card: Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories

Pressure plus condensing decides the pipe.

  • I: negative pressure, non-condensing. II: negative, condensing (rare). III: positive, non-condensing. IV: positive, condensing.
  • Category IV furnaces make acidic condensate and need a maker-listed vent such as PVC, CPVC, polypropylene or stainless.
  • Never share a condensing furnace's vent with another appliance or a chimney flue; a chimney may serve only as a chase where the manual and code allow it.
  • Replacing a shared-vent furnace can orphan the water heater; resize or reline per NFPA 54 and the manual.
  • Count elbows as equivalent feet and stay under the table limit for your model.
  • Condensate needs a trap, a drain and freeze protection.
