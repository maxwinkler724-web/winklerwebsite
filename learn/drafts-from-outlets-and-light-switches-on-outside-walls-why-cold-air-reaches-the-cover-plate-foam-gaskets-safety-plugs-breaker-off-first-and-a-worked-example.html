Drafts From Outlets and Switches on Outside Walls: Gaskets, Plugs and Safe Sealing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Hold your hand over a light switch or outlet on an outside wall on a windy winter day and you may feel a thin stream of cold air. The device is not the problem. It sits in a hole cut into the wall, and the gaps around it let outdoor air reach the room. This lesson explains where the air comes from, which fixes are safe for a homeowner, and where the work stops and an electrician starts.

Why you feel cold air at the cover plate

A switch or outlet sits in a box set into the wall. The box is a hole through the drywall, and behind the drywall is a wall cavity that may connect to the attic, the crawl space or the outdoors. A national laboratory do-it-yourself guide names two common leak paths: gaps between the electrical box and the drywall, and unsealed holes where wires enter the box. It notes that air can move through those openings into the wall cavities, and that you can find the draft by feeling around the box with your hand.

Temperature and wind supply the push. Extension guidance describes the stack effect: warm air rises and leaves through gaps high in the house, and cold air is pulled in through gaps low in the house. Wind pressing on an outside wall can also drive air into openings on that side. The cover plate hides the gaps but does not seal them, so air finds its way through the plate edge and the screw openings. Read Home Insulation Types Explained to see why moving air can defeat insulation in the same cavity.

How much does it matter?

An extension fact sheet that lists where air leaks in an average house puts electrical outlets at about 2 percent of total leakage. A separate line for plumbing, electrical and HVAC penetrations is 17 percent. So outlets alone are a small slice, but a cheap and quick one to fix, and the same fact sheet says uncomfortable drafts and high bills often come from many gaps and cracks. It also says air sealing can often save 10 to 20 percent of home energy use. Outlets are one step in a longer job; see Drafty Doors and Windows and Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for the bigger leaks.

Worked example (our own): 20 outlets and switches

This is our own example, not a measurement. Assumptions: a house has 20 outlets and switches on outside walls, and each has an assumed open gap area of 0.5 square inch (the real number varies by box and workmanship).

  1. Total open area: 20 × 0.5 sq in = 10 sq in.
  2. A 3 inch by 3 inch hole: 3 × 3 = 9 sq in.
  3. Compare: 10 − 9 = 1 sq in more, and 10 ÷ 9 ≈ 1.11, so the 20 small gaps add up to about 11 percent more open area than the square hole. A square with 10 sq in of area is about 3.2 inches on a side (the square root of 10 is about 3.16).

The lesson: twenty gaps that each look too small to matter together equal a hole about the size of a hand in the wall. This example gives area only. It does not tell you how much air flows, because that depends on wind, temperature difference and the path inside the wall.

Step one: foam gaskets behind cover plates

Extension guides describe inexpensive foam gaskets that fit behind outlet and switch cover plates, and they list gaskets plus plugs for unused outlets as the simple fix for devices on outside walls. The national laboratory guide treats a gasket as optional and says to make sure the plate screws are long enough and the plate still sits flat.

  1. Turn the power off at the breaker for that circuit. The laboratory guide says to find the right breaker and confirm power is off with a voltage tester. It says that if you have no tester or cannot verify the power is off, do not go ahead.
  2. Unscrew the plate only. You do not need to pull the device from the box to fit a gasket.
  3. Fit the gasket over the device so its openings line up, then refit the plate and do not overtighten.
  4. Restore power and test the device.

If anything looks scorched, cracked, loose or warm, stop. See the electrician section below.

Step two: plugs in unused outlets

Plugs help in two ways. A plug in an unused outlet on an outside wall blocks air moving through the open slots. The federal consumer product safety agency also advises safety covers for all unused outlets where children are present. It says protectors should not be easy for a child to remove and should be large enough that a child cannot choke on them, and that replacement receptacles should be the tamper-resistant type. It also warns that children may push objects into an outlet that is not covered by a plate, so a missing or cracked cover plate should be replaced.

Caulk and foam: only where the instructions allow, never inside the box

Caulk is a good tool for fixed gaps; extension guides give limits of about 1/4 to 3/8 inch for the gap it can bridge. Our rule for this page is narrower. Do not put caulk or foam inside an electrical box, on wires or terminals, in screw holes, or on the device. Use sealant only on the outside edge of the room-side opening, and only where the product instructions and the device instructions allow it. The laboratory guide does describe sealing wire holes and the box perimeter with caulk or low-expanding foam after the power is off, but we treat work inside the box as electrician work, because sealant used wrongly can block a screw hole, cover a wire or keep a device from sitting flat. If you cannot tell what is allowed, stay with a gasket and a plug.

When to call an electrician

  • The outlet or switch is warm to the touch. The federal consumer product safety agency says unusually warm outlets or switches may indicate an unsafe wiring condition and advises you to stop using them until an electrician checks. It notes that some dimmer switches may be warm in normal use.
  • A device does not work, is loose in the wall, or shows damage.
  • You see bare wire, cracked boxes or burn marks, or smell burning.
  • You cannot confirm the power is off.
  • You want gaps sealed inside the box or the box replaced.

How this fits whole-house air sealing and comfort

When a family complains of cold rooms, outlet drafts are one small line in the air sealing list. Fix the largest openings first (attic bypasses, rim joists, ducts), then the small ones. Moving air and cold surfaces make a room feel cooler at the same thermostat setting, so a draft at a wall switch can lead someone to turn the heat up. Sealing leaks does not replace working equipment, and it must never block combustion air or ventilation. Field card: Drafts at outlets and switches

Breaker off, verify, gasket, plug, and stop if in doubt.

  • Leaks come from gaps at the box and drywall and open holes in the box.
  • Outlets are about 2 percent of leakage in an average house; many small gaps add up.
  • Turn off the breaker and confirm with a tester before removing a plate.
  • Use foam gaskets behind plates and plugs in unused outlets.
  • No caulk or foam inside the box; follow product instructions elsewhere.
  • Warm, damaged or unverifiable devices: call an electrician.
  • Our example: 20 × 0.5 = 10 sq in, versus 9 sq in for a 3 by 3 hole.
