HVAC Project Closeout Documents: O&M Manuals, As-Builts, TAB Reports, Warranties and Owner Training

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The crew leaves, the crane is gone, and the owner has a new rooftop unit. Three years later a different tech opens the roof hatch with no manual, no start-up report and no idea when the warranty started. This lesson covers the paperwork that prevents that, and how to file it so it can be found.

Why closeout paper matters

The DOE FEMP guide on commissioning for federal facilities says the O&M manual is where key baseline information is preserved. It describes how the equipment should be operated and sets out preventive maintenance procedures and schedules. It also says warranty details, vendor contacts and spare parts information belong with that documentation, and that commissioning checks that the systems and O&M documents left on site are complete. If you are the installing tech, you often fill these pages in. If you are the next tech, you live or die by them.

What a closeout package usually holds

Owners write their own lists, so the owner's specification wins. These are the pieces that show up again and again in the public requirements we read.

  • O&M manuals. One university standard asks for drawings and specifications organized by CSI number with labeled tabs, in one three-ring binder where possible. Its list includes the test and balance report, warranties and contractor training videos. A university BAS guideline asks for 11x17 hard copies of control diagrams and 8.5x11 copies of text such as sequences.
  • Approved submittals. The product data and shop drawings the engineer actually reviewed, so the model on the roof can be matched to what was approved. We include it from our own practice, not from a cited requirement.
  • Record (as-built) drawings. One university standard says the contractor's marked-up drawings must include all change orders, requests for information and other as-built information. Another requires each as-built sheet to be sealed and signed by a professional engineer. See the lesson on replacing a rooftop unit for why curb and duct changes need to be drawn.
  • Start-up reports. A commissioning report, as described on the WBDG commissioning documents page, includes completed installation, system readiness and start-up checklists, plus test and field observation reports.
  • Test and balance report. One university specification says to submit the TAB records before requesting the Substantial Completion inspection. Our TAB report lesson shows how to read one.
  • Commissioning report and systems manual. See the next section.
  • Warranty letters. With a start date written on them.
  • Spare parts and attic stock. Extra materials the owner keeps on site.
  • Owner training records. Agenda, sign-in sheet and any recordings.
  • BAS backups and credentials. Covered below.

Commissioning report versus systems manual

They are different documents. WBDG says the commissioning report should include an executive summary and a narrative of the commissioning process and results, along with review comments and their resolution, meeting minutes, corrective action reports, completed forms and logs, and the results of off-season and completion verification. The systems manual is the operating companion. WBDG recommends it include the owner's project requirements, basis of design, submittals, O&M requirements and manuals, training documents and the testing reports. It says the manual is ideally delivered in electronic, indexed (bookmarked) and hyperlinked form, with all documents available before training begins, and that final seasonal test results go into an updated manual. The VA commissioning process manual defines the systems manual as a system-focused composite document with the information required to operate the systems. For the testing side, see BAS commissioning checklists and functional testing.

Warranty letters and start dates

Do not assume the clock started the day you lit the unit. Public owner standards word it differently. One university specification says warranties normally start at Substantial Completion unless the contract says otherwise, and asks for each warranty as its own PDF with the date and term logged. Another university's standard says that when acceptance is delayed, the acceptance date starts the warranty period. A third university's page says Substantial Completion marks the start of the warranty period. So write the actual start date and term on every warranty letter, and ask which date the contract uses. The DOE FEMP guide also notes that condition testing is best done while warranties are still active, so hidden defects can be found before they expire. Calendar the expiration dates.

Spare parts and attic stock

One university specification asks for a list of each item with quantity, part number, manufacturer, contact information and current cost, labeled with maker and model, delivered to the location the owner picks, with a signed receipt. We found no public source giving a standard quantity for filters or belts, so quantities come from the owner's spec, not from us.

Owner training records

One university BAS guideline requires training to start no later than 30 days before scheduled construction completion, with a proposed schedule submitted at least 60 days before. A university closeout standard asks for a written agenda, recorded sessions delivered to the owner, and at least two weeks' notice of the training dates. Keep the sign-in sheet in the binder; it shows the owner was shown the system.

BAS passwords and backups

Public standards say less here than we would like. One university BAS guideline says not to use default passwords and to coordinate with the owner's BAS administrator, and lists password security procedures, graphics changes and software changes as training topics. Another requires the final database and program files to be loaded on the owner's server. Beyond that, the following is our own practice, not a cited requirement. Treat passwords as secrets: do not type them into the binder, an email or a PDF. Hand them to the owner's designated administrator in person or through the owner's approved password vault, and record in the binder only who received them and when. Keep an offline backup of the program, database and graphics on owner-controlled media, labeled with the date and software version.

Worked example: one rooftop unit replacement (ours, made up)

This is a made-up checklist labeled as our own. The job, owner and every date are invented. We assume a one-for-one rooftop unit swap on an existing curb adapter, a one-year parts warranty and a five-year compressor warranty (assumptions, not a maker's terms), and a contract that starts warranties at Substantial Completion. The roof crew calls the job done on a Friday. At closeout review the checklist looks like this.

ItemStatusWhat was missing
O&M manualPartialGeneric maker PDF only. No site page with unit tag, location, filter size, belt size or the control sequence.
Approved submittalPartialOnly the draft with comments. The version marked approved was never filed.
Record drawingsMissingCurb adapter and the moved condensate line were never drawn.
Start-up reportPartialCharge, amp draw and static pressure lines left blank.
TABMissingNo airflow check recorded after the swap.
Warranty letterPartialShows ship date, not the start date, and no expiration date.
Attic stockMissingOne spare filter set and one belt were promised, none left on site.
Training recordMissingOwner's tech was walked through it, but no sign-in sheet.
BASPartialPoint list given. No offline backup. Admin login still the default.

The warranty arithmetic, using our assumed terms: if Substantial Completion is June 6, 2026, the one-year parts warranty ends June 6, 2027 and the five-year compressor warranty ends June 6, 2031. If the letter only shows a May 20 ship date, the next tech has no way to know which date counts. That is why Tab 6 needs the start date written in, with both expiry dates calendared. Our own fix list: draw the curb change on the record set, fill and re-sign the start-up report, run and record a TAB check, relabel the warranty letter, deliver the attic stock with a signed receipt, collect the training sign-in, and hand the BAS backup and credentials to the owner's administrator.

Making it findable

  • One index at the front listing every tab, who supplied it and the date.
  • Use the unit tag from the drawings (for example RTU-3) in every file name and tab label. One university standard limits file names to 15 characters, so check the owner's rule first.
  • Keep a searchable PDF set that mirrors the binder. The same university standard asks for searchable PDFs.
  • Leave a laminated one-page unit sheet inside the unit door: tag, model, filter size, who to call, and where the binder lives. This is our own practice, not a requirement we found.
  • Update the binder when equipment is replaced, as DOE FEMP advises for O&M documentation. See retro-commissioning and operator rounds and logs for habits that keep records current.

Field card: HVAC closeout documents

If it is not in the binder, the next tech starts from zero.

  • Get the owner's closeout list first; it overrides ours.
  • Nine buckets: submittals, start-up, TAB, record drawings, commissioning, warranties, spares, training, BAS.
  • Write the warranty start date, term and expiry on the letter.
  • Record drawings must show the changes you actually made.
  • Sign-in sheet for every training session.
  • BAS passwords go to the owner's administrator, never into the binder.
  • Index the binder and mirror it as searchable PDFs.
