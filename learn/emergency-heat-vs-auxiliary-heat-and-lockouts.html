Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Customers see "AUX" glowing on the thermostat and call in a panic, or flip to "EM HEAT" in a cold snap and wonder why the bill doubles. Knowing the difference is half the diagnosis.

The two terms

Auxiliary heat is automatic backup that helps the heat pump when it cannot keep up. In an air handler with electric heat strips, the strips run along with the compressor. In a dual-fuel setup, the furnace may take over and the heat pump may shut off. Emergency heat is a manual mode that turns the compressor off and runs only the backup heat. Lennox describes it this way: emergency heat uses only the supplemental heat, while auxiliary heat uses it only when the heat pump cannot meet the demand. One article notes that thermostats and even technicians sometimes blur the two.

Why the AUX light is not always bad news

The AUX indicator means the backup heat is allowed to run. Technician-level articles list normal triggers: a big gap between room temperature and setpoint, outdoor temperatures below the point where the heat pump alone can carry the house, defrost cycles, and a timer when the setpoint is not reached after a set period (one article gives 10 to 20 minutes as an example; no manufacturer sheet we read gives a timer number, so check the control's own instructions). So AUX during a defrost cycle or a deep cold snap can be normal behavior.

What lockouts do

A lockout temperature is an outdoor temperature limit set at the thermostat or control. Careful: "compressor lockout" means two different things. On some thermostats it is a short restart delay (one White-Rodgers installer sheet describes it as an on/off setting that makes the thermostat wait 5 minutes before starting the compressor, default off). This lesson uses the other meaning, an outdoor-temperature limit. Two kinds appear in published guidance:

  • Aux lockout: backup heat is blocked when outdoor air is warmer than the setting. One article says common values are about 30 to 40 degrees F for typical systems, with cold-climate models sometimes in the 20s.
  • Compressor lockout: the heat pump compressor is blocked when it is colder than the setting, used mostly in dual-fuel systems. The same article gives roughly 10 to 25 degrees F as typical.

One concrete published spec: a Pacific Northwest utility program (Bonneville Power Administration, Performance Tested Comfort Systems) asks installers to set the compressor lockout to disabled or 5 degrees F or lower, and the aux heat lockout to 35 degrees F or lower. Its thermostat sheets (Honeywell T10, ecobee, Trane XL824) say the same. That is a program requirement, not a factory default or a universal rule, and the settings only appear when an outdoor sensor is enabled. If aux lockout is disabled, strip heat may run at any outdoor temperature. The same program's reference sheet says strip heat can be locked out by a Wi-Fi weather-station thermostat, an outdoor sensor, the outdoor board or a mechanical lockout. A smart-thermostat write-up gives a Nest example: aux heat lockout options from 35 to 90 degrees F, and a note that many installers use 20 to 30 degrees F for compressor lockout in poorly insulated homes. These are examples from specific products and opinion pieces. The right numbers depend on the equipment, the house and the manufacturer, so adjust a few degrees at a time and watch comfort and runtime.

When too much aux is a symptom

If backup heat runs constantly in mild weather, look past the thermostat:

  • Clogged filters, blocked returns, duct leaks or poor airflow.
  • Low charge or a compressor problem.
  • A bad outdoor temperature sensor that makes the control think it is colder than it is.
  • Aggressive settings: high aux lockout, short staging delays or big setbacks (one source suggests keeping setbacks small, about 0 to 3 degrees F, in heating season).
  • Frequent defrost caused by a dirty or blocked outdoor coil.
  • Wrong O/B setting, covered in the reversing valve lesson.

When emergency heat makes sense

Published guidance limits it to genuine problems such as compressor failure, severe icing or a repair period, and says to return the thermostat to normal heat afterward because it costs more to run. Another note warns that a thermostat that cannot learn the outdoor temperature may keep running the heat pump in extreme cold; in that case, a manual choice may be safer until it is fixed. Electric heat strips draw large current, so confirm breaker and wire sizes against the nameplate and follow lockout/tagout.

Field card: Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures

AUX helps the compressor. EM HEAT replaces it.

  • Aux heat is automatic and usually runs with the heat pump.
  • Emergency heat is manual and runs only the backup source.
  • Lockout temperatures limit when backup or compressor may run; values vary by system. "Compressor lockout" can also mean a 5-minute restart delay.
  • Constant aux in mild weather points to airflow, charge, sensor or settings.
  • Do not leave customers in EM HEAT after the repair.
