Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Every pound of steam that gives up its heat becomes a pound of hot water. Sending it back to the boiler instead of down the drain is one of the oldest and best energy-saving ideas in the steam trade.

Why it matters

The US Department of Energy's steam tip sheets point out that the heat in returned condensate can be more than 10 percent of the total energy in the steam in a typical system. Returned condensate is commonly 130 to 225 F, while cold makeup water is around 50 to 60 F. So bringing it back means less fuel to heat water, less makeup water, less treatment chemical and less water going to the sewer. In one tip-sheet example, a plant that returned much more condensate cut its makeup rate sharply and saved money on water and fuel.

The path the condensate takes

  • Radiators and equipment condense steam and release the water, often through traps.
  • Return piping carries it back, by gravity where pitch allows.
  • A receiver or condensate tank collects the water and a vent lets air and flash steam escape.
  • A pump, when needed, moves it to the boiler. A boiler feed unit pumps it in as the boiler needs it, controlled by the water level.
  • Makeup water is added to replace what is lost.

Why a small heat exchanger can flood (stall)

A maker-side note explains a problem on small steam-heated equipment. When the load drops, the steam pressure inside can fall to or below the pressure at the trap outlet. The trap can then no longer push condensate out, and water backs up into the steam space. One fix is a vacuum breaker on the steam inlet, which lets in air so gravity can drain the water to the trap. It is usually avoided on larger units because air in the steam space hurts heat transfer, and it does not help if the condensate is lifted after the trap, where a pump trap is the better choice. One maker bulletin describes a vent-and-breaker combination that opens at the end of each cycle so leftover condensate drains, mounted at the highest point of the steam chamber, but it gives no set opening pressure. No set pressures are given here either: use the maker's data for the exact equipment.

Feedwater, in plain terms

Feedwater is the water the boiler is fed: returned condensate plus makeup. It is better if it is hot, because cold water shocks the boiler and costs fuel to heat. It also needs to be clean. Dissolved oxygen and minerals cause corrosion and scale, and dirty water can foam or carry over into the steam piping and cause banging. Larger plants treat feedwater and sometimes use a deaerator to remove gases. Small heating boilers rely on keeping leaks low, using the manufacturer's water guidance and cleaning when needed.

Common problems

  • Failed traps let live steam into the return, which causes noise, wasted fuel and hot receivers.
  • Dripping hot condensate into a dry return can flash to steam and hammer, so trap and drip layout matter.
  • Contaminated condensate (from heat exchanger leaks, for example) may need to be diverted rather than returned.
  • Frequent makeup water means a leak. Track it with a meter.
  • Pump trouble: a hot condensate pump needs enough suction head to avoid vapor locking. Check the maker's requirements.
  • Return line corrosion from carbonic acid. Water treatment may help.

Condensate is hot enough to burn. Isolate and let it cool, and wear protective gear. Do not work on a pressurized receiver.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the exact equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints or wiring and piping steps.

Field card: Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics

Bring the heat back, and keep the water clean.

  • Returned condensate saves fuel, water and chemicals.
  • Condensate return path: traps, returns, receiver, pump, boiler.
  • Hot, clean feedwater is easier on the boiler.
  • High makeup water use signals leaks or bad traps.
  • Follow the maker's water treatment guidance.
