Duct Leakage Testing: Total vs Leakage to Outside and CFM25

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "duct leakage test CFM25," here is the short version: a tester seals every register, pressurizes the ducts to 25 pascals, and reads how much air the fan must supply to hold that pressure. That number is CFM25. Codes and programs then divide it by the home's floor area and compare it to a limit. This lesson explains the test, the two kinds of result, the limits, and the arithmetic.

Check your local code

Check your local code. Energy codes are adopted state by state, often with changes, and the limits below depend on the edition. This page was built from public code text, government guides and a tester manual read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. Confirm the rule with the building department that inspects the job.

What the test is

The Energy Conservatory's Duct Blaster manual and the Building America Solution Center (a Department of Energy program with Pacific Northwest National Laboratory) describe the same basic setup. A calibrated duct tester fan is connected to the duct system, usually at a return grille. Every other supply and return register is temporarily sealed with cardboard and tape or removable plastic. A digital manometer reads the pressure in the ducts. The tech turns the fan up until the ducts sit at 25 pascals (0.10 inch water column), and the airflow the fan needs to hold that pressure is the leakage. With the pressure attached to it, that flow is written "CFM25." Both sources say to keep a door or window open so the house pressure stays steady during the test.

Total leakage vs leakage to outside

There are two different questions. Total leakage counts every leak in the ductwork connected to the system, whether the air escapes into the conditioned house or outside it, according to Building America. It is the simple test above. Leakage to outside counts only air that escapes beyond the home's air barrier, such as into an attic or crawl space. Air leaking into the living space is not lost to the outdoors, so this test ignores it.

To isolate the outside leaks, a blower door pressurizes the house to 25 Pa relative to outside. The duct tester is then adjusted until the pressure between the ducts and the house reads zero. With no pressure difference, no air crosses between ducts and rooms, so the flow the fan supplies is going out through duct leaks to the outside. The Duct Blaster manual describes this in its leakage-to-outside chapter. For more on the blower door itself, see What Happens in a Home Energy Audit.

How results are written

A raw CFM25 number depends on house size, so it is divided by the conditioned floor area in units of 100 square feet. The result is "CFM25 per 100 sq ft of conditioned floor area." A 2,000 sq ft house is 20 of those units. ENERGY STAR also uses a fixed CFM25 cap, covered below. The limits apply to each duct system, not to the whole home, according to ENERGY STAR and Building America.

What the energy code says

The International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) residential chapter requires duct tightness to be verified by a test. The text offers two timings. A rough-in test is done after the duct connections are made, before drywall covers them. A post-construction test is done after the house is finished and covers the whole system, including the air handler cabinet.

  • 2012 and 2015 IECC. A table compiled by NEEP lists total leakage of 4 CFM25 per 100 sq ft at rough-in (3 if the air handler is not yet installed) and 4 per 100 sq ft post-construction. A Texas agency bulletin quoting the 2015 text agrees.
  • The 2009 edition was looser. The Department of Energy's code assistance page lists 6 at rough-in, and after construction either 12 total or 8 to outside.
  • Washington State's adopted 2021 text (a state-amended version) lists rough-in total leakage of 4 (3 without the air handler), and post-construction leakage to outdoors of 4 or total leakage of 4. All registers must be taped during the test.

Treatment of ducts inside the house differs between editions. The 2015 text quoted by the Texas bulletin says duct testing is not required if the air handler and all ducts are in conditioned space. The Washington 2021 text instead sets a looser total-leakage limit of 8 per 100 sq ft when all ducts and the air handler are fully inside the thermal envelope. It allows only 10 linear feet of return and 5 linear feet of supply outside it, and says ducts in crawl spaces do not qualify. Your jurisdiction may follow either approach, so check the adopted edition.

ENERGY STAR and RESNET

Programs add their own numbers. ENERGY STAR's National Rater Field Checklist says duct leakage is determined by a rater following ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380. Its rough-in total limit is the greater of 4 CFM25 per 100 sq ft or 40 CFM25. Its final total limit is the greater of 8 per 100 sq ft or 80 CFM25. Its leakage-to-outdoors limit is the greater of 4 per 100 sq ft or 40 CFM25. The "greater of" wording matters for small houses, where the fixed number is more forgiving. Building America adds that the outdoor test can be waived under ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380 when total leakage meets the lower 4 per 100 sq ft or 40 CFM25 threshold. These are program requirements, separate from the energy code.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

These numbers are invented for practice. A 2,000 sq ft house has one duct system. At post-construction, the total leakage test reads 160 CFM25. Use the 4 CFM25 per 100 sq ft post-construction limit from the IECC text above.

Step 1: floor area in units of 100 sq ft. 2,000 / 100 = 20.

Step 2: result per 100 sq ft. 160 / 20 = 8.0 CFM25 per 100 sq ft.

Step 3: allowed leakage. 4 x 20 = 80 CFM25.

Step 4: compare. 8.0 is above 4.0 and 160 is above 80, so the system fails the IECC limit by a factor of two. For contrast, the ENERGY STAR final limit is the greater of 8 x 20 = 160 or 80, so 160 CFM25 just meets that program's number. Same ducts, two different limits.

Step 5: seal and retest. The tech finds the worst leaks, seals them with mastic (see Sealing Duct Leaks) and retests. Say the retest reads 62 CFM25.

Step 6: recompute. 62 / 20 = 3.1 CFM25 per 100 sq ft. That is below 4.0, and 62 is below the 80 allowed, so the system passes with 80 - 62 = 18 CFM25 to spare.

Step 7: how much was saved. 160 - 62 = 98 CFM25 removed. 98 / 160 = 0.6125, about 61 percent less leakage.

How a tech uses the numbers

  • Pass or fail. Compare to the limit your inspector or program uses, and note which edition it came from.
  • Total first, then outside. A high total with a low outside number suggests the leaks are mostly into conditioned space. A high outside number points at ducts in attics, crawl spaces and cavities.
  • Prove the repair. Test before and after, as in the example, instead of judging by feel. Pair it with a static pressure check, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Know the layout. Where the ducts run changes which test and limit apply; see Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Field card: Duct Leakage Testing: Total vs Leakage to Outside and CFM25

Seal the registers, hold 25 Pa, read CFM25, divide by floor area.

  • CFM25 is duct leakage airflow measured with ducts at 25 Pa (0.10 in. w.c.).
  • Total leakage counts every leak. Leakage to outside needs a blower door and zero duct-to-house pressure.
  • Result = CFM25 / (floor area / 100).
  • IECC 2012 and 2015 tables: 4 per 100 sq ft at rough-in and post-construction (3 at rough-in without the air handler).
  • Exemptions and the ducts-inside limit differ by edition. Check the adopted code.
  • ENERGY STAR uses its own "greater of" limits and ANSI/RESNET/ICC 380.
  • Retest after sealing and record both numbers.
