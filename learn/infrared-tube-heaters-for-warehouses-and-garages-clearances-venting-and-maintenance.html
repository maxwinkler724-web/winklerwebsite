Infrared Tube Heaters for Warehouses and Garages: Clearances, Venting and Maintenance

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "infrared tube heater clearance to combustibles," the short answer is that every model has its own table in its own manual, and the numbers change with input size, tube position and reflector angle. This lesson explains how the heater works, how to read those tables, and how to check a stated installation against them.

Check the manual

Check the manual. This is general education built from maker manuals read in 2026, not a ruling on any building. The manual for the exact model, the label on the heater and the fuel gas code adopted where you work decide the answer. Gas heater installation is work for qualified, licensed people.

How a tube heater works

A gas-fired low-intensity infrared tube heater has a burner at one end of a long steel radiant tube. Hot combustion gases travel down the tube and heat it until it glows dull. A polished reflector above the tube pushes the infrared energy downward. At the far end, a fan either pushes gases through (positive pressure) or pulls them through (vacuum). Roberts-Gordon describes its CRV system as a vacuum pump pulling products of combustion through the tubes, with the burners allowed to fire only after a pressure switch proves that vacuum. Detroit Radiant describes its REV series as a positive-pressure, two-stage design with a built-in power exhauster, and says the heater has no pilot and cannot be lit by hand.

Because the energy is radiant, it warms floors, equipment and people it lands on rather than the air. That is why makers pitch these heaters for tall buildings, loading docks and doors that open often. Treat that as maker literature, not a guarantee: the heater still has to be sized and aimed.

Clearances to combustibles

A clearance is the minimum distance between the heater's hot surfaces and anything that can burn or be damaged. Detroit Radiant's general manual defines it as the distance between the tube surface or reflector and the combustible item. Makers publish a table with distances above, to the sides, below and at the ends, and the figures differ a lot:

  • Roberts-Gordon's CRV-B manual lists four distances per reflector or shield choice. For the level reflector on models 2 to 8 they are 4, 20, 48 and 20 inches, and for models 9 to 12 they are 4, 36, 60 and 36 inches. A shield or a different reflector changes every number. Its notes say some can be cut by half after 25 feet of tube downstream of the burner.
  • Roberts-Gordon's Caribe single-heater manual gives six distances for each of three sizes (30, 40 and 50 thousand Btu/h), measured from the reflector, and says the tilt distances are the same as horizontal.
  • Space-Ray's LTS/LTU manual gives sides, ceiling, below, ends and 45-degree front and rear. Below ranges from 40 inches for the smallest LTS models to 132 inches for the largest, and a footnote lowers it to 72 inches farther downstream from the control box.
  • Detroit Radiant's REV manual says to use the high Btu/h output when choosing a row, and gives different values for a 0-degree dual-pass setup and a 35-degree forward shield.

Do not carry one maker's numbers to another maker's heater. Space-Ray also notes that its clearances apply to materials that would be damaged by surfaces 90 degrees F above ambient.

Mounting height and angle

There are two different height ideas. One is a legal minimum: the manuals above say not less than 8 feet over the floor in public garages, and at least 10 feet above the highest wing or engine enclosure in aircraft hangars. Detroit Radiant also calls for a protective guard if mounted below 8 feet. The other is the maker's mounting height chart, which depends on size. Space-Ray lists, for example, 13 feet horizontal and 12 feet at 45 degrees for an LTU 90, and 20 and 19 feet for the LTU 225 and 250. Detroit Radiant's REV chart recommends 12 to 20 feet for the 100 thousand Btu/h models. Roberts-Gordon's Caribe manual warns that its clearance columns for C and E do not indicate required mounting height.

Tilting a heater (the manuals show angles up to 45 degrees) changes the tables. In Space-Ray's chart a 45-degree tilt lowers the minimum height by one foot. It does not remove the clearance rules.

Vehicles and stored materials

Every manual here makes the same points. Keep the marked clearances from vehicles parked underneath. Over a hoist, measure from the highest point of what is on the hoist. Where goods can be stacked under the heater, post a sign giving the maximum stacking height, next to the thermostat if there is one. Detroit Radiant's REV manual also says to post a clearance safety tag by the thermostat. Do not store or use explosive liquids or vapors near the heater.

Venting and combustion air

Tube heaters can be vented or unvented, and the manuals treat the two very differently. For the guard-rail on both, see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters in Closets and Basements and Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

  • Vented. Detroit Radiant uses 4 inch single-wall, 26 gauge or heavier pipe, a 20 foot maximum for most models and no more than two 90-degree elbows. Roberts-Gordon says its CRV system is Category III or IV, calls for 26 gauge single-wall corrosion-resistant pipe, silicone rated 550 degrees F at joints, and a downward pitch of 1/4 inch per 10 feet away from the pump.
  • Unvented. Detroit Radiant allows it with a factory vent cap and at least 4 cfm of ventilation per 1,000 Btu/h of installed input, and says not for residential use. Space-Ray gives the same 4 cfm per 1,000 figure with exhaust at the highest point and inlets below the heater. Roberts-Gordon says an unvented heater must be interlocked with enough building exhaust.
  • Combustion air. Detroit Radiant says indoor air needs at least 50 cubic feet per 1,000 Btu/h unless the building is unusually tight (under 0.40 air changes per hour), and says to duct outside air if chemicals, high humidity, contaminants or negative pressure apply. Space-Ray gives 1 square inch of opening per 1,000 Btu/h in a tight indoor area, or 1 square inch per 4,000 with outside air.

Annual maintenance

All the manuals call for a yearly inspection by a qualified person before the heating season. Space-Ray's list is the longest: check clearances and nearby stored goods, blow the dust off reflectors, check tubes for cracks or sag, check the intake and vent for debris and sealed joints, check gas lines for leaks, confirm the control box and tube are level, spin the blower wheel, check the electrode gap (3/16 inch) and ceramic, check suspension hardware and clean the burner and orifice. Detroit Radiant adds checking that the flame extends straight out and keeping both reflector sides clean. Turn off gas and power before servicing.

Common faults

  • Ignition lockout. Detroit Radiant's chart lists the symptom as glo-bar off after 45 seconds (15 for a spark igniter) with no ignition. Causes include a closed gas supply, a dirty orifice, a bad valve or wire, or inlet pressure over 14 in. w.c. Roberts-Gordon's Caribe locks out for an hour after three failed tries unless power is removed for five seconds. Space-Ray says to turn power off for 60 seconds, and if it still fails, five minutes.
  • Pressure switch will not close. Detroit Radiant lists a loose wire, a missing gasket, plugged tubing, a restricted exhaust, a wrong baffle position or a faulty switch, and says replace the switch, do not adjust it. The idea is the same as in Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis.

Worked example: checking a stated installation

Suppose a note says: "Space-Ray LTU 90, 90,000 Btu/h, mounted horizontally with the bottom at 12 ft; pallets stacked 6 ft high directly beneath." (These numbers are invented to practice with.) Using Space-Ray's tables:

  1. Mounting height. LTU 90 needs 13 ft horizontal, or 12 ft at 45 degrees. 12 minus 13 = minus 1 ft, so the stated horizontal install is 1 ft too low. Tilting to 45 degrees makes 12 minus 12 = 0, which just meets the chart.
  2. Below clearance. The table says 84 inches. 84 / 12 = 7 ft.
  3. Highest allowed stack. 12 ft minus 7 ft = 5 ft above the floor.
  4. Compare. The pallets are 6 ft, so 6 minus 5 = 1 ft too tall. Keep stacks under 5 ft, post a sign saying so, or raise the heater.
  5. Sides. The table says 52 inches, so 52 / 12 = 4.3 ft to the nearest combustible wall or rack on each side.
  6. Ceiling. The table says 6 inches above the tube, so 6 / 12 = 0.5 ft.

The installation as stated fails two checks. The table also depends on the maker's drawing for where height and clearance are measured, so confirm that on the label.

Field card: Infrared Tube Heaters

Read the model's own table, then check height, stacks and vents.

  • Burner, radiant tube, reflector, plus a fan that pushes or pulls the exhaust.
  • Clearance tables are per model, per input and per reflector or angle. Use the high Btu/h row.
  • Garages: at least 8 ft above the floor; hangars: 10 ft above highest wing.
  • Post a maximum stacking height sign by the thermostat.
  • Unvented: 4 cfm per 1,000 Btu/h of exhaust and supply, per two makers.
  • Yearly: reflectors, tubes, vent, gas lines, burner, electrode, hardware.
  • Lockout: check gas, orifice, igniter and sensor. Pressure switch: replace, do not adjust.
