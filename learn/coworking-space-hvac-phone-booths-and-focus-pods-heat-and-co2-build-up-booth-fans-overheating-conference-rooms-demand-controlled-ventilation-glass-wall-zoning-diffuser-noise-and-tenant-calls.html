Coworking Space HVAC: Phone Booths, Focus Pods, Swinging Occupancy and Tenant Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A shared workspace can hold 15 people at 9 a.m., 80 at noon and 10 at 6 p.m., and it now has small sealed boxes dropped into the open floor. This lesson covers what that does to heat, CO2 and noise, what a booth fan really changes, and what to check when a tenant calls.

Check the local rules first

Check the authority having jurisdiction. This page is general education. Fire code, sprinkler rules, electrical listing of pod fans and the adopted ventilation standard all depend on where the building is. Statements about fire and sprinkler questions below are generic on purpose. Ask the building's fire official before you change anything near a sprinkler head or a smoke detector.

Why swinging occupancy is the real problem

EPA's office ventilation fact sheet explains that outdoor air is set per person, so the airflow a room needs rises with its headcount. It also says inadequate outdoor air lets carbon dioxide and other pollutants build up, with drowsiness and headaches as possible results, and that the system should start several hours before people arrive. NIOSH adds that code-minimum ventilation may not protect people in crowded spaces. A shared floor is that case every day: the plan was drawn for an average, and the room is sometimes far from average. See Demand-Controlled Ventilation: CO2 Sensors in Offices and Meeting Rooms for the basics and Call Center and Dense Open Office HVAC for the open-floor side.

Booths and pods are small sealed boxes

A one-person phone booth or focus pod is built to block sound, and sound-tight usually means air-tight too. Inside is a person breathing out CO2 and giving off heat, plus often a laptop and a screen. The room's supply diffusers feed the open floor, not the box. Most booths therefore carry their own small fan. In our diagram below, the fan pulls room air in through a low opening and pushes it out through the top, back into the room. The fan does not bring in outdoor air. It only moves air between the pod and the room, so the pod stays about as good as the room, plus an increase set by the fan size. If the room is stale, the pod is stale.

Worked example: one person in a 70 cubic foot pod (our own)

Inputs. NIST researchers (Persily and de Jonge) tabulate CO2 generation for adults at 1.2 met, about desk work. For men aged 30 to under 40 the table gives 0.0046 L/s, and for women 0.0035 L/s. The paper says such averages are not accurate for a single person. We use the higher 0.0046 L/s. Assumptions, all ours: the pod holds 70 ft3 of air, which is 70 x 28.3 = about 1,980 L; the room air is at 600 ppm; the pod is perfectly mixed; leaks and the volume taken up by the body are ignored.

No fan (0 cfm).

  • Rise per second: 0.0046 L/s / 1,980 L = 0.0000023, which is 2.3 ppm per second.
  • Per minute: 2.3 x 60 = about 139 ppm per minute.
  • After 30 minutes: 139 x 30 = about 4,180 ppm above the room, so near 4,800 ppm total.
  • Time to climb 4,400 ppm to 5,000 ppm: 4,400 / 139 = about 32 minutes.

For scale, NIOSH lists a recommended limit of 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average, 30,000 ppm as a short-term limit, and 40,000 ppm as the immediately dangerous level. A real booth leaks, so this is a worst-case ceiling, not a prediction. It still shows how fast a tiny volume fills.

With a 20 cfm fan.

  • Convert generation: 0.0046 L/s / 0.472 = about 0.0097 cfm of pure CO2.
  • Steady rise: 0.0097 / 20 = 0.000487, which is about 490 ppm above the room.
  • Time constant: 70 ft3 / 20 cfm = 3.5 minutes, so the pod gets within a few percent of steady state in about 10 to 14 minutes.
  • Pod at steady state: 600 + 490 = about 1,090 ppm.

Why the fan matters. The fan turns an endless climb into a flat line near 1,100 ppm. It also limits how much the pod's heat can pile up. But the fan can only be as good as the air it takes in. If the room is at 1,200 ppm at noon, that pod sits near 1,700 ppm. Good pod air starts with good room ventilation.

Fans, sprinklers and fire code, generically

A pod is a room-like box inside a room. Local fire officials may ask whether the building's sprinkler coverage reaches the inside of an enclosed pod, whether a pod top blocks a spray pattern, and whether the pod's fan power and wiring are listed. Those answers depend on the adopted codes and the sprinkler design, and we did not check a code text for this lesson. As a tech, do not move a pod, block or cap a sprinkler head, or add a fan or duct to a pod. Report it to the building owner and let the fire official and the pod's installer settle it.

Conference rooms that overheat

Our own field logic: a conference room is quiet for hours, then 12 people and a wall display arrive at once. The room is often on one thermostat with a supply sized for the average. Glass walls add sun on one side and, on the other, no wall to hide a low supply. Typical causes of a hot room are a thermostat in the wrong spot, a VAV box minimum or damper stuck, a closed or crushed supply branch, or a zone sensor that sees the open floor. Fixes are usually its own zone, a sensor in the room, and a supply that can ramp up when people arrive. See Esports and Gaming Lounge HVAC for per-room zoning on a heavier load.

Demand-controlled ventilation with pods in the mix

A CO2 sensor on a wall or in the return reads the room, not the box. LBNL researchers writing for a state energy agency found that 47 percent of field sensors had errors above 75 ppm at 760 ppm, and that errors above 200 ppm were not unusual in new sensor models they tested in the lab. Few managers had recalibrated sensors after installation. NIOSH notes that fixed wall monitors can mislead and rooms are rarely well mixed. It suggests portable calibrated meters for baselines, with one possible target below 800 ppm and a look at causes when readings run above 110 percent of baseline. For pods, the practical move is to put a portable meter inside during a busy hour and compare with the room. A pod more than about 500 ppm above its room, with a working fan, deserves a look. That threshold is our own rule of thumb, based on the example above.

Diffuser noise and glass

A GSA acoustics guide gives mechanical background noise limits of NC 40 for open-plan space, NC 25 for meeting and training rooms and NC 20 for teleconference facilities, from the agency's design standard. The same guide warns that sound leaks through air conditioning components and ducting. In a glass-walled shared space the diffuser noise is often the easiest to fix: lower the airflow at the terminal, check for a damper partly closed near the diffuser, and keep a pod from sitting under a high-velocity grille. Glass reflects sound, so any whistle seems louder. Booths with a diffuser right over the top tend to draw calls.

Tenant service calls

  • "The pod is stuffy." Check the pod's fan runs and its intake is not blocked, then measure room CO2 and pod CO2.
  • "The conference room is hot." Check the zone sensor location, the VAV box, supply airflow and sun on the glass.
  • "There is a whistle." Look for a damper partly closed ahead of a diffuser and a diffuser that is too small for its airflow.
  • "It is cold by the window and hot by the pod." One thermostat is serving two very different loads.
