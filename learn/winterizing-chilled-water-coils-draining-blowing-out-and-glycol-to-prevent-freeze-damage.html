Winterizing Chilled Water Coils: Drain, Blow Out, Glycol

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chilled water coil that sits in an air handler with outdoor air is idle all winter, but it is not safe. If it holds water when a cold air stream hits it, the tubes can split, and you find out in spring. This lesson covers the three common ways to protect a coil that is shut down: drain it, blow it out, or fill it with glycol.

Check the maker and the drawings

Check the maker and the drawings. Each coil maker writes its own winter procedure, and the job's design may add pumps, valves or sequences. This lesson is general education built from the sources listed at the bottom. Your coil's installation manual and the site's written procedure win.

Why these coils freeze

A cooling coil is a long run of thin copper tube. Water in it has little volume compared to the cold air passing over the fins, so a cold stream can freeze it fast, and ice expands in a tube that has nowhere to go. A Trane catalog section on protecting coils says that when more outdoor air is brought in, the chance of air stratification goes up, and that a layer of freezing air moving through an air handler can damage unprotected hydronic cooling and heating coils. Aerofin, a coil maker, makes the same point: where outside air is tempered by mixing with warm return air, stratification may occur, and some of that air can still be cold enough to freeze the water in the coil.

Stratification means the air does not mix. Cold air rides along one side of the duct while warm air takes the other, so an average mixed-air reading can look fine while one patch of the coil is below freezing. The HPAC freezestat article says that predicting the coldest spot in an air stream is impossible, which is why a freezestat element is spread across the coil face. Our freezestat lesson covers that device in detail.

Outdoor air damper leakage is the other piece. We did not find a maker statement that quantifies how much leakage freezes a coil, so we will not give a number. What the sources do say is that the standard response to a cold condition is to stop the fan and close the outdoor air dampers, and Trane's engineers newsletter says designers should use dampers that seal tightly with minimal leakage. A damper that does not close tight lets winter air drift into a unit that is off, which matters most on a shut-down coil with water in it.

Why gravity draining is not enough

Opening the drain feels like the end of the job. The makers say it is not. Aerofin's coil protection bulletin states in capital letters that even though coils are drained, enough water may remain in them to cause freeze damage. Greenheck's coil manual says draining alone may leave water in the coil and tells you to use air or nitrogen pressure to blow out what is left. A serpentine coil is one long tube folded back and forth, with bends at both ends. Any bend or sag that sits lower than its neighbors can hold a pocket of water, and a pocket cannot run out against the air that must come in behind it. Greenheck also notes that its coils should be installed with tubes level, and that special drain headers are available when they cannot be.

No source we read gives a typical percentage of water that stays behind, so the example below uses a teaching value.

Blowing the coil out

Colmac Coil's manual lists the steps for a chilled water coil: drain it, blow out the remaining water droplets with compressed air, fill it with antifreeze at a concentration rated for the lowest temperature expected, then drain the antifreeze and put the drains and vents back. The Department of Energy page for a blow-out job on an air handler's chilled water coils describes the connection pattern: a blower on the upper return header connection and a drain at the lower supply header connection, so air pushes the water out of the low point.

The sources do not all agree on the air supply. Aerofin says using air from a compressed air tank is not recommended and calls for a blower run for at least half an hour, then a dryness check, light bumping of the coil during blowing, and a repeat if any water still comes out. Colmac and Greenheck name compressed air or nitrogen. Follow your coil maker, keep air pressure within what the coil and its fittings can take, and keep your body out of the line of the drain outlet.

Filling with glycol

Glycol lowers the freezing point of the water, and the water coil makers treat it as the fall-back that does not depend on getting every drop out. Aerofin recommends glycol antifreeze and says alcohol-type solutions are not recommended. Greenheck says glycol solutions or brines are the only freeze-safe media for low entering air conditions. Colmac says to use inhibited glycols, because uninhibited glycol can cause corrosion in copper tubes. Our glycol lesson covers ethylene versus propylene, refractometers and top-ups, so we keep this part short.

Two checks matter on a winter fill. First, test the concentration of what is actually in the coil, not what you poured into the bucket, because water left in the tubes dilutes it. Second, pick the target from the lowest expected temperature, not from the day you fill. Dow's guide separates freeze protection from burst protection. Burst protection lets slush form as long as there is room to expand, so it asks for less glycol than freeze protection does.

Vents, drains and valves

Every method above depends on connections that are easy to skip at install. A Trane catalog section on drain-the-coils protection says it needs vent and drain connections on every coil, plus shutoff valves to isolate each coil from the chillers. Greenheck supplies vent and drain connections on its water coils unless the order says otherwise, and its four-connection coils use the extra bottom connection as a manual drain. Colmac says to vent each coil at its highest point and to check that every vent and drain actually discharges a stream and is not blocked. On a job, that last check is worth doing in the fall, not when you are trying to drain in December.

Freeze-protection pumps and minimum flow

Another approach keeps the coil full and the water moving. A National Institutes of Health technical bulletin on 100 percent outside air units describes a pump in the bypass position on the cooling coil that runs when the air leaving the preheat coil falls below 40°F (adjustable), powered from an emergency source. It says pumps keep turbulent flow at reduced load and delay freezing if the preheat system fails, and that freezestats, typically set near 37°F, shut the unit down, start the circulating pump and need a manual reset. The bulletin gives no minimum flow value for hydronic coils, so we have none to offer. Look at the job's control sequence for what the designer chose. If the plan is to keep the loop circulating, remember that moving water only delays freezing; it does not replace a drained, blown-out or glycol-filled coil during a long shutdown. For the preheat coil side of this story, see preheat coils and face-and-bypass.

Worked example: a 25-gallon coil

Suppose a chilled water coil and its connected piping hold 25 gallons, and the plan is a 30 percent propylene glycol mix by volume. The numbers below are our own arithmetic.

Step 1, the mix. Our own: glycol = 0.30 × 25 gal = 7.5 gal. Water = 25 − 7.5 = 17.5 gal. Volumes are not perfectly additive in the real world, so this is a planning figure.

Step 2, the freeze point. Dow's Table 3 for DOWFROST propylene glycol lists 30.7 percent by volume at 9.2°F. A true 30 percent is a little weaker, so it freezes a little above that. Read the maker's table for the product in your loop. Dow's burst table also shows that about 29.3 percent covers burst protection to −10°F, which is a lower bar than freeze protection.

Step 3, trapped water. Our own, with a teaching value: after a gravity drain, 20 percent of the water stays in the tubes. 0.20 × 25 gal = 5 gal of plain water. At about 8.3 lb per gallon, that is roughly 42 lb of water in the tubes, held in pockets that can freeze.

Step 4, what it does to the fill. Our own: the empty-looking coil takes 20 gal, not 25. If you pour in 20 gal of a premixed 30 percent solution, you add 0.30 × 20 = 6 gal of glycol. The coil then has 6 gal glycol in 25 gal, or 6 ÷ 25 = 24 percent. Dow's table lists 25.5 percent at 14.7°F, so 24 percent freezes a little above that. To land on 30 percent, the 25 gal needs 7.5 gal of glycol in the 20 gal you add, so the premix must be 7.5 ÷ 20 = 37.5 percent. If you only checked the pail, you would believe the coil was protected to about 9°F when it is nearer 15°F or worse.

What 5 gallons means. Five gallons of trapped water is enough to damage a coil, and it also skews the glycol test. This is why the makers say to blow the coil out and why you test the concentration after the fill has circulated or been mixed.

What to inspect in spring

  • Look for leaks first. Check headers, returns and the fin pack for weeping, stains or ice marks before opening valves.
  • Pressure test. Colmac's manual calls for 100 psig with dry nitrogen, held 15 minutes to confirm there are no leaks, and a hydrostatic leak test on piping joints. Use the pressure your own coil maker lists.
  • Recover or flush the glycol. Colmac's procedure drains the antifreeze from the coil and says the recovered solution can be used on other coils. Handle and dispose of it per your site rules and the product's safety data sheet.
  • Refill and vent. Open the vents, close the drains, fill, then close the vents. Verify that every vent and drain flows and is not blocked, and re-tighten bolted connections once the system is at temperature.
  • Test the controls. Confirm the freezestat, damper and valve sequence before the next cold snap.

Field card: Winterizing Chilled Water Coils

Drained is not dry. Test what is in the coil.

  • Makers say a drained coil can still hold enough water to freeze and split.
  • Blow out the remainder (air or nitrogen per the maker), then fill with inhibited glycol or leave dry as the plan says.
  • Check that every coil has working vents, drains and isolation valves in the fall.
  • Trapped water dilutes glycol. Test the concentration in the coil.
  • Pump circulation delays freezing; it is not a long-shutdown plan.
  • Spring: check for leaks, pressure test per the maker, handle recovered glycol per the product sheet.
