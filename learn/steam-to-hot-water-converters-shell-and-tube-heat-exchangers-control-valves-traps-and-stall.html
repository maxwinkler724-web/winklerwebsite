Steam to Hot Water Converters: Control Valves, Traps and Stall

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many buildings with a steam supply make their heating hot water in a steam-to-water converter. It is a small shell-and-tube heat exchanger, but it has one habit that surprises new techs: at light load it can quit draining its own condensate. This lesson explains the parts, the numbers, and the fix.

How a converter heats the loop

In the converters we read about, steam fills the shell and the building water flows through the tubes. Columbia University's design guide spec calls for removable U-bend tube bundles with steam in the shell and water in the tubes. Steam gives up its latent heat as it condenses on the cool tube surface, and the condensate collects at the bottom. A temperature sensor on the water leaving the converter signals a modulating steam control valve. When the loop is hot enough, the valve throttles steam back; when the loop cools, it opens. Many sites also reset the supply water setpoint with outdoor temperature; see Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation. For the general idea of heat moving across tube walls, see Heat Exchanger Basics, and for steam supply pressure see PRV Station Basics.

Why throttling steam lowers the shell pressure

Steam condenses at one fixed temperature for each pressure. When the load drops, the control valve does not just pass less steam; the shell settles at a lower pressure and temperature, because less temperature difference is needed to move less heat. Spirax Sarco explains that if the water being heated stays below about 212 F, the shell can fall below atmospheric pressure. That is the root of stall.

What stall is

Spirax Sarco defines stall as a drop or stop in condensate flow that happens when steam pressure in the exchanger is equal to or less than the back pressure on the steam trap. TLV puts it the same way: the pressure difference across the trap goes negative. The trap has nothing pushing condensate through it, so the shell floods and the tubes lose heating surface.

The makers describe a cycle. Water cools off, the controller opens the valve, pressure rises, the pooled condensate finally drains, the water overheats, the valve closes, and the cycle repeats. Spirax Sarco lists poor temperature control, water hammer from steam meeting cooler pooled condensate, accelerated corrosion, and thermal stress on joints. TLV adds that the slugs can rupture thin tubes in shell-and-tube units.

A trap swap alone does not cure it. TLV says the fix is to raise the trap inlet pressure or lower the outlet pressure.

Fixes the makers describe

  • Drain by gravity. The Bell & Gossett manual for its straight-tube units says condensate return lines must not rise above the bottom of the exchanger and the trap must drain to an unpressurized return. Two university facility standards say the same: Washington University's requires gravity drainage with no lifting, and Columbia's calls for at least 12 in of vertical height from the trap discharge to the underside of the exchanger and no vertical lifts on low-pressure systems with control valves.
  • Vacuum breaker. Both university standards require one on the shell. A vacuum breaker lets air in when the shell would otherwise pull a vacuum, so the trap can still drain by gravity. Spirax Sarco places it on the steam inlet after the control valve and the trap about 0.5 to 1 m (about 1.6 to 3.3 ft) below the exchanger outlet. A Bell & Gossett article hosted by a distributor (Deppmann, 2017) adds that without one, trapped condensate can cause water hammer that damages the tube bundle.
  • Pump-trap. A pump-trap uses motive steam or air to push condensate out when shell pressure is too low. Spirax Sarco calls it generally the most effective choice where stall is likely, and TLV lists mechanical pump-traps as a way to raise inlet pressure. A Johns Hopkins steam specialties spec requires them on exchangers controlled by modulating valves.
  • Trap type. For heat exchangers draining to atmosphere, Spirax Sarco calls a float-and-thermostatic trap ideal, installed below the exchanger outlet, because it handles heavy and light loads and vents air. Armstrong's guidelines also describe F&T traps as responding immediately to condensate. Size the trap on the pressure at the trap, not the supply pressure ahead of the control valve (Bell & Gossett). Spirax Sarco adds that the trap must pass the stall load against the static head above its inlet, and that where back pressure comes from a lift or pressurized return a pump-trap may be required.

Relief valves, fouling and approach

Bell & Gossett says to install a properly sized relief valve on both sides, because volumetric expansion can occur during shutdown. Columbia's spec asks for a safety valve on the shell and a relief valve on the tube side placed before the isolation valves; Washington University requires relief valves on both sides discharging to a floor drain.

Approach here means the saturated steam temperature minus the leaving water temperature (our own definition for this lesson). Fouled tubes force the steam temperature up to deliver the same heat, so the approach grows at the same load. Columbia's spec uses a fouling factor of 0.0005 when selecting converters. Bell & Gossett says a rising pressure drop or falling performance signals that cleaning is needed. The same maker warns against cleaning tubes by blowing steam through single tubes, which overheats them. The plate-frame version of this idea is in Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach.

Tube leaks, testing and isolation

We did not find a maker procedure for locating a leaking tube or hydrotesting a converter in the sources we read, so this page does not teach one. What the sources do say: Bell & Gossett ties leaking tube joints to overheating and steam hammer damage; TLV says stall slugs can rupture thin tubes; and one Bell & Gossett newsletter warns that a pressure test can fail weak equipment, so isolate anything not meant to see test pressure. Follow the maker's instructions and your site procedure for testing.

For service, Bell & Gossett says to provide valves so each side can be isolated, shut off the hot medium first, remove all pressure and drain the unit before opening the heads, and never run a unit without its safety devices. Apply your site's lockout and tagging procedure to the steam, the water and the controls. The DOE steam tip sheet on returning condensate also reminds us why drainage matters beyond the converter: it says condensate holds more than 10% of the energy in a typical steam system.

Worked example: 200 gpm from 160 F to 180 F

All arithmetic below is Our own, using the 500 factor from the standard hydronic rule of thumb (8.33 lb per gal × 60 min per h × 1 Btu/lb-F, about 500) and steam properties from Armstrong's steam table, which is abstracted from Keenan and Keyes.

  1. Load. Q = 500 × 200 gpm × (180 − 160) = 500 × 200 × 20 = 2,000,000 Btu/h.
  2. Shell steam. Take 5 psig. The Armstrong table has no exact 5 psig row; the nearest is 20.0 psia (about 5.3 psig) at 227.96 F with latent heat 960.1 Btu/lb. We use 228 F and 960 Btu/lb.
  3. Steam flow. 2,000,000 ÷ 960.1 = about 2,083 lb/h (about 2,080).
  4. Full-load log-mean difference (simplified). Ends: 228 − 160 = 68 F and 228 − 180 = 48 F. LMTD = (68 − 48) ÷ ln(68/48) = 20 ÷ 0.348 = 57.4 F. Then UA = 2,000,000 ÷ 57.4 = about 34,800 Btu/h-F. We hold UA fixed, which ignores fouling and film changes.
  5. Light load. Same 200 gpm, outlet held at 180 F, return water now 175 F. Q = 500 × 200 × 5 = 500,000 Btu/h (25% of full). Needed LMTD = 500,000 ÷ 34,800 = 14.4 F.
  6. Steam temperature needed. Solve 5 ÷ ln[(Ts − 175)/(Ts − 180)] = 14.4. The ratio is e^(5/14.4) = 1.417, so Ts − 175 = 1.417 (Ts − 180), giving Ts = about 192 F.
  7. Shell pressure. The Armstrong table lists 193.2 F at 10 psia. So the shell sits near 10 psia, about 5 psi below atmosphere (roughly −5 psig, a vacuum).
  8. Back pressure. Suppose the trap discharges into a return that is open to atmosphere (0 psig) but lifts condensate 5 ft. EPA's conversion table gives 27.681 in. of water per psi, which is 2.31 ft per psi. Lift pressure = 5 ÷ 2.31 = 2.2 psi.
  9. Stall check (simplified). Differential = shell pressure − back pressure = −5 − (+2.2) = about −7 psi. It is negative, so the trap cannot drain by pressure. The 5 ft lift is what hurts; at full load the same lift is easily overcome (5 psig − 2.2 = +2.8 psi). With a trap below the shell and no lift, only the condensate head above the trap is available, and a vacuum breaker or pump-trap is needed. This is a simplified check, not a trap sizing method.

Field card: Steam to Hot Water Converters

Low load means low shell pressure, and low shell pressure can stop the drain.

  • Steam in the shell, water in the tubes; a modulating valve follows the outlet water temperature.
  • Load = 500 × gpm × dT. Steam lb/h = load ÷ latent heat at shell pressure.
  • Stall: shell pressure at or below trap back pressure. 1 psi is about 2.31 ft of water.
  • Fixes: drain by gravity with no lift, vacuum breaker, pump-trap, F&T trap sized for the stall load.
  • Relief valves on both sides. Rising approach or pressure drop points to fouling.
  • Isolate, depressurize, drain and lock out before opening heads.
