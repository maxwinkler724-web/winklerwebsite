Power Plant HVAC: Turbine Hall Ventilation and Support Rooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A power generating station is mostly a very large, very hot machine room with a few critical rooms hanging off it. The turbine hall needs a lot of outside air to carry heat away. The battery, switchgear and control rooms need clean, steady, well-controlled air. And in winter, the small instrument lines nobody thinks about are the first things to freeze. This lesson walks through each area and ends with a worked airflow example.

Why plants are different from office buildings

A station is built to turn fuel into electricity, and the building is a by-product. The federal workplace safety agency lists the hazards of the power generation industry on one page: heat, confined spaces, coal dust and natural gas explosions, boiler startup and shutdown explosions, steam and hot water releases, electrocution and falls. Ventilation and cooling work in a plant touches almost all of those, so you should treat every job as a plant job first and an HVAC job second: follow the site permit, lockout and entry rules before you open a panel or climb to a roof fan.

Facility techs usually are not designing the process. Your job is to keep the support systems alive: fans, louvers, dampers, coils, chillers, heat trace controls and their alarms. Knowing what each system protects tells you how urgent a failure is.

Turbine hall and generator building

Steam turbines, generators, hot piping, valves and lube oil systems all lose heat to the room. Transformers and generator auxiliaries add more. None of it is "cooling load" in the office sense, because there is no wish to hold 75°F. The aim is to keep the hall cool enough that people can work, motors and electronics stay inside their limits and the structure is not stressed.

A federal nuclear regulator document on one operating station shows the scale. The turbine building there is ventilated by a circulating air system with filtered outside air, hot-water heating coils in winter and evaporative coolers in summer. Its design conditions are a maximum indoor temperature of 105°F when it is 97°F outside and a minimum of 55°F at 5°F outside. Each unit's exhaust fans can move about 269,000 cfm, supply fans about 255,000 cfm, so the building runs at slight negative pressure. In cold weather, exhaust drops to a minimum of 62,500 cfm. Notice the pattern: big airflow in summer, a floor under it in winter, and heating coils so the incoming air does not freeze equipment.

Plain ventilation works because the hall has a large heat source and a lot of height. Cool outside air enters through low louvers, warms as it passes the equipment, rises and leaves through roof exhausters. Common field problems follow from it: closed or blocked low louvers (so roof fans starve and pull air through doors and leaks), dirty filters on supply fans, failed damper actuators.

Boiler building roof ventilators

The same idea applies over a boiler. Hot casing surfaces and steam leaks heat the air, which collects under the roof. Roof ventilators remove it. The workplace safety agency's heat page lists increased general ventilation, local exhaust at hot sources, insulating hot surfaces and fixing steam leaks as controls for heat. For you, that means roof ventilators are a safety system as well as a comfort item. Check that dampers open, that bird screens are not clogged, that the make-up air path is open, and report any steam or hot-water leak you see to the operators, rather than trying to ventilate it away.

Electrical and battery rooms

Switchgear and transformer rooms lose heat as electrical losses and need either filtered ventilation or mechanical cooling. See Cooling Electrical Rooms and Elevator Machine Rooms for how to size that.

Battery rooms need exhaust because charging can release gases. The regulator document above describes the battery rooms at that station as ventilated to remove heat and dangerous fumes, with redundant exhaust fans on the roof of the control bay and with one 250-volt battery room kept at negative pressure. The same document also notes fire dampers where ducts penetrate the battery room perimeter. It gives no hydrogen limit or air-change figure, and we do not give one here either. Use the site design documents and the lesson Battery Room Ventilation, and never bypass a fan interlock or alarm.

Control room cooling with backup

The control room is where the operators watch the whole station, so its cooling is built with spares. In the same regulator document, each air supply system for the control bay has two air handling or air conditioning units, each able to carry 100 percent of the load. Two 100 percent air-cooled chillers serve the control bay, with water-cooled condensing units as backup if both fail. The control room is held at slight positive pressure with a small makeup stream through filters, and an emergency ventilation system with two redundant trains starts on certain signals. For a tech, the lesson is to test the standby unit, not just the running one. A spare that has never been started is not a spare. The related lesson Plant Control Room HVAC covers this in more detail.

Coal and biomass dust areas

The workplace safety agency's hazards page cites a powerhouse explosion in which a secondary explosion came from coal dust accumulations, and it lists a bulletin on natural gas and coal dust explosions in power generating facilities. Our takeaway, not a design rule: fans, filters and ductwork in dust areas are not ordinary equipment. Do not add or change electrical equipment, heaters or fan motors there without the plant's hazardous-area approval, do not blow dust into the air with compressed air, and report dust built up on ducts, ledges or filters. The plant's own housekeeping and ignition-source rules decide what you may do.

Winter freeze protection for instruments and sample lines

Small lines freeze before big ones. The federal energy department's weatherization guide defines three measures that power plants use: heat tracing (a heat source applied to pipes, lines and equipment that must stay above freezing), thermal insulation, and windbreaks. It also lists temporary measures such as space heaters and draining nonessential water lines. A federal nuclear regulator guide says sensing lines should be designed and maintained so their fluids stay within the temperatures needed for their function, and that heat tracing keeps water piping from freezing in cold weather.

In practice, before winter test heat trace circuits and their controllers and alarms, confirm that insulation and weather enclosures are intact (a wet or torn jacket defeats the trace), and check that louvers and dampers that supply heated air to instrument shelters work. The cold weather lesson Cold Stress for HVAC Techs covers your own protection while you do that work.

Worked example (our own): ventilation airflow and air changes for a turbine hall

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design for any real plant. We use the common sensible heat relation for air at standard density, q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, where q is in Btu per hour and ΔT is in °F. It comes from 60 minutes per hour × 0.075 lb per cubic foot × 0.24 Btu per lb per °F, as shown in a university-hosted cooling load reference.

Assumptions: heat released to the hall air is 600 kW (turbine casing, piping, generator and transformer losses that reach the room air). The hall may rise 15°F above the outside air. The hall is 200 ft long, 100 ft wide and 60 ft high.

  1. Convert heat to Btu per hour using 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h: 600 × 3,412 = 2,047,200 Btu/h.
  2. Solve for airflow: cfm = q / (1.08 × ΔT) = 2,047,200 / (1.08 × 15) = 2,047,200 / 16.2 = about 126,400 cfm.
  3. Volume: 200 × 100 × 60 = 1,200,000 cubic feet.
  4. Air changes per hour: 126,400 cfm × 60 min/h = 7,584,000 cubic feet per hour; 7,584,000 / 1,200,000 = about 6.3 ACH.

Check: multiply back: 1.08 × 126,400 × 15 = 2,047,680 Btu/h, which matches within rounding. If the allowed rise were halved to 7.5°F, airflow would double to about 252,800 cfm and the hall would need about 12.6 ACH. Doubling the heat would do the same. Real halls also have solar and roof gains, a hotter outside design day, and thin air at altitude (the 1.08 constant falls when air is less dense), so the actual designer adds margin. Our example shows why: the airflow scales directly with heat and inversely with the allowed rise.

Field card: Power Plant HVAC: Turbine Hall Ventilation and Support Rooms

Heat out, clean air in, spares tested.

  • Airflow (cfm) = heat (Btu/h) / (1.08 × allowed rise in °F), at standard air density.
  • Air changes per hour = cfm × 60 / room volume in cubic feet.
  • Keep low louvers and make-up air paths open, or roof fans starve.
  • Battery room fans and interlocks are safety items. Never bypass them.
  • Test the standby control room unit, not just the running one.
  • In coal or biomass dust areas, follow the plant's hazardous-area and housekeeping rules.
  • Before winter, test heat trace, check insulation jackets and enclosures.
