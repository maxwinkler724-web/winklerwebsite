Cooling Coil Bypass Factor, Contact Factor and Apparatus Dew Point: Leaving Air Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Air crossing a cooling coil does not all get the same treatment. Some of it behaves as if it touched cold metal, and some of it behaves as if it never did. Bypass factor, contact factor and apparatus dew point (ADP) are the three terms that turn that picture into arithmetic. This lesson shows what they mean, what changes them, and how a tech can use them to judge a leaving-air reading.

What the three terms mean

Think of the air stream as two parts. One part acts as if it reached the effective coil surface temperature. The other part acts as if it passed through untouched and kept its entering condition. A national-lab documentation page for a bypass factor coil model describes it that way: a fraction at the same condition as the entering air, and the rest at the apparatus dew point. Nobody can see two streams, because they mix again just past the coil. The model is a simple way to predict the mixture.

  • Apparatus dew point (ADP) is the effective average surface temperature of the coil fins and tubes, as one research paper defines it. It is a model temperature, not a probe reading you can take on a fin.
  • Bypass factor (BF) is the fraction of the air that is unaffected by the coil, in the words of the same paper.
  • Contact factor (CF) is the other fraction, so CF = 1 − BF. We use that relation as the standard complement; the sources we read describe the two fractions together.

The same paper writes BF as a ratio, and the ratio works for temperature, humidity ratio or enthalpy: BF = (leaving − ADP) / (entering − ADP). Solve for leaving dry bulb and you get the working formula:

Leaving dry bulb = ADP + BF × (entering dry bulb − ADP)

A small BF means the leaving air lands close to the ADP. A large BF means it stays closer to the entering air.

What changes bypass factor

For a given coil, the biggest changer in the field is airflow. The documentation of a building energy simulation engineering reference states that for a given coil geometry the bypass factor is only a function of air mass flow rate, and the national-lab page makes the same point. The research paper adds the direction: a larger exchange surface lowers BF, and a higher air velocity raises it.

  • Face velocity. More air per square foot of face means each pound of air spends less time near the surface. BF rises. Slower air lets more of the stream touch, so BF falls. See Air Handler Cooling Coils: Face Velocity, Rows, Carryover, Drain Pans for how face velocity is figured and why limits exist.
  • Rows. A patent that quotes maker coil data states that the bypass factor reduces as rows in depth increase. More rows add surface and path length, so more of the air gets touched.
  • Fin spacing. A national-lab report on a coil model says wider fin spacing means fewer fins and less air-side heat transfer area. Combined with the paper above, less area means a higher BF. Fins packed close together do the opposite, though they also catch dirt faster and restrict airflow, as the face velocity lesson explains.

Our own plain reading of those sources: anything that adds surface or slows the air pushes BF down, and anything that removes surface or speeds the air pushes BF up. A dirty coil, a flattened fin comb and a missing baffle that lets air slip around the coil all behave like extra bypass in the field, because the air that reaches the coil is no longer evenly treated.

Why a low ADP is needed to remove moisture

Water vapor condenses only where the air meets a surface colder than its dew point. If the effective coil temperature stays above the entering air dew point, the coil is dry and the air only cools. A published research paper on coil design makes the same split: it classifies the coil as completely wet when the surface temperature at the inlet is below the dew point, and partly dry when condensation starts partway through. A low ADP is therefore the condition for latent removal, and the mixing relation shows how much of it reaches the leaving air: the leaving humidity ratio is also pulled toward the ADP humidity ratio, by the same contact fraction. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for the chart behind this, and Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load for why less airflow shifts capacity toward moisture.

Field data fit that story. A Florida university energy research center tested a residential system at 75 F and 60 percent relative humidity indoors. Leaving evaporator air temperature fell as airflow fell: 58.8 F at 425 cfm per ton, 54.1 F at 253 cfm per ton and 47.0 F at 127 cfm per ton. The same paper says latent capacity increases with reduced coil airflow, and that humidity removal peaks near 250 cfm per ton in those tests. Lower airflow lowers BF and colder coil conditions, so the leaving air lands nearer the ADP. Stay inside the maker's allowed airflow range; very low airflow is a freeze risk.

Worked example

Everything in this example is our own, with assumed inputs. We assume entering air at 80 F dry bulb and an ADP of 50 F. We assume the coil face is 3 sq ft, and the first condition is 1,200 cfm.

  1. Gap between entering air and ADP: 80 − 50 = 30 F.
  2. Face velocity: 1,200 cfm ÷ 3 sq ft = 400 fpm.
  3. Assume the coil runs at BF 0.10 at that airflow. Leaving dry bulb = 50 + 0.10 × 30 = 50 + 3.0 = 53.0 F. The contact factor is 1 − 0.10 = 0.90, and the dry-bulb drop is 80 − 53.0 = 27.0 F, which is 0.90 × 30.
  4. Now raise airflow to 1,500 cfm on the same coil. Face velocity = 1,500 ÷ 3 = 500 fpm. Assume BF rises to 0.15. We assume this number; a real coil's BF at a new airflow comes from the maker's data.
  5. Leaving dry bulb = 50 + 0.15 × 30 = 50 + 4.5 = 54.5 F. The drop is 80 − 54.5 = 25.5 F, which is 0.85 × 30. We also assume the ADP stays at 50 F for this comparison; in practice the ADP moves with the load and refrigerant conditions.

The leaving air is 1.5 F warmer at the higher face velocity. Sensible capacity is still higher because there is more air. Using the common sensible equation, Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature change (see the capacity lesson linked below for its limits): at 1,200 cfm, 1.08 × 1,200 × 27.0 = 34,992 Btu/h. At 1,500 cfm, 1.08 × 1,500 × 25.5 = 41,310 Btu/h. So the faster stream carries more sensible heat but gets less drying, because its leaving humidity ratio is also pulled less toward the ADP value.

Using it as a leaving-air sanity check

  1. Measure entering dry bulb and wet bulb and leaving dry bulb and wet bulb at stable conditions. Good wet-bulb technique matters; see Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field.
  2. Plot both states on a psychrometric chart and extend the line through them to the saturation curve. A building energy simulation reference describes finding the ADP in the same way: the line from the inlet state, at the slope of the coil process, meets the saturation curve at the apparatus dew point.
  3. Read the ADP and compute BF = (leaving − ADP) / (entering − ADP).
  4. Compare with what you expect for that coil. For example, entering 80 F, leaving 54.5 F and an ADP read as 50 F gives (54.5 − 50) / 30 = 0.15 (our own numbers).

Our own guidance on reading the result. If the ADP you get is well above the entering dew point, the coil was running dry and a latent complaint makes sense. If BF comes out much higher than a similar coil has shown before, suspect high airflow, a dirty or crushed coil, air slipping around the coil, or a probe that read stratified air at the coil outlet. A negative or impossible BF points at the readings, not the coil: re-check probe placement, wait for stable conditions, and re-measure. Use the maker's rated BF or leaving-air data when it is available; the numbers in this lesson are examples, not targets. If you want to turn the readings into capacity, see Measuring Air-Side Cooling Capacity with Enthalpy.

Field card: Bypass Factor and ADP

Leaving air = ADP + BF times the gap.

  • ADP is the coil's effective surface temperature; BF is the fraction of air that acts as if it never touched the coil.
  • CF = 1 − BF.
  • Leaving dry bulb = ADP + BF × (entering dry bulb − ADP).
  • More surface or slower air lowers BF; less surface or faster air raises it.
  • Moisture comes out only when the effective surface is below the entering dew point.
  • Check: plot entering and leaving on the chart, extend to saturation for the ADP, then compute BF.
  • Impossible BF means re-check probes and wait for stable readings.
