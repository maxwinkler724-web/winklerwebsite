Unit Conversions HVAC Techs Use Every Week: Inches of Water, Feet of Head, Btu, kW, Tons, CFM and Celsius

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most conversion mistakes in the field are not hard math. They come from dividing when you should multiply, or from carrying a number without its unit. This lesson collects the handful of conversions a tech uses every week, shows how to write the units into the math so the answer checks itself, and ends with six practice problems taken from real jobs.

Write the units in the math

Treat a unit like a number that can cancel. If you want psi and you start with inches of water column (in WC), the factor must have in WC on the bottom so it cancels: 3.5 in WC x (1 psi / 27.7 in WC) = 0.126 psi. The in WC cancel and psi is left. If your answer comes out with the wrong unit left over, you flipped the factor. Do this on paper or on the back of a glove until it is a habit. This is our own method note; it is ordinary dimensional analysis.

Pressure: inches of water column and feet of head to psi

NIST lists the inch of water (60 F) as 248.84 pascals and the psi as 6,894.757 pascals. Divide the second by the first and you get 27.7 in WC per psi. So psi = in WC / 27.7. Gas pressures are small in psi, which is why gas gauges and manometers read in WC. For gas work with a manometer, see Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer.

For water systems, NIST lists a foot of water (39.2 F) as 2,988.98 pascals, which works out to about 2.31 feet of water per psi. So psi = feet of head / 2.31, and each foot of head is about 0.433 psi. Head is a height, so it does not depend on pipe size. Pump curves are drawn in feet of head for that reason. Flow math for the same loops is in Hot Water Flow Rate Math.

Heat flow: Btu/h to kW and to tons

NIST gives 1 Btu per hour as 0.2930711 watt, so 1 kW is about 3,412 Btu/h. NIST also lists the ton of refrigeration as 12,000 Btu per hour (3.516853 kW). So tons = Btu/h / 12,000, and kW of heat = Btu/h / 3,412. One caution: this kW is heat moved, not electrical input. A 5-ton air conditioner moves about 17.6 kW of heat while its compressor and fans draw far less electricity. The formulas that connect heat to airflow and water flow are in HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas.

Airflow: CFM to liters per second

NIST lists 1 cubic foot per minute as 0.4719474 liter per second. Multiply CFM by 0.472. A common 400 CFM per ton rule of thumb (our own round number, not a standard) means a 3-ton system moving 1,200 CFM is moving about 566 L/s.

Temperature: a reading versus a difference

NIST gives the Fahrenheit-to-Celsius formula as (F - 32) / 1.8. That 32 only belongs to a single reading, because the two scales start at different places. NIST also says an interval of one Celsius degree is 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees. A temperature difference is an interval, so you divide by 1.8 and do not subtract 32. A 20 F split across a coil is 20 / 1.8 = 11.1 C. If you wrongly subtract 32 first you get (20 - 32) / 1.8 = -6.7 C, which is nonsense for a split. Rule: one reading, use 32; one difference, skip it.

Water: gallons to liters and pounds

NIST lists the U.S. gallon as 3.785412 liters. Fresh water is close to 1 kilogram per liter, and NIST lists the pound as 0.4535924 kg, so a gallon of water weighs a little over 8.3 pounds. Techs use 8.34 lb per gallon (our own rounding from those figures). The 8.34 is where the 500 in the hydronic formulas comes from: 8.34 lb/gal x 60 min/h is about 500 lb per gallon-per-minute-hour.

Vacuum: microns to inches of mercury

A micron on a vacuum gauge is one thousandth of a torr (our own note, using the usual millimeter-of-mercury equivalence). NIST lists the torr as 133.3224 pascals and the inch of mercury (32 F) as 3,386.38 pascals, so 1 torr is about 0.03937 in Hg. One micron is therefore about 0.00003937 in Hg, or in Hg = microns / 25,400. Gauge pressure scales are covered in Pressure Units for Refrigerant Work, and the evacuation target itself in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.

Our own worked example: six practice problems from the field

Try each one before you read the answer. Assumptions are stated in each problem.

  1. Gas manifold. A natural gas furnace manifold reads 3.5 in WC. What is that in psi? Answer: 3.5 in WC x (1 psi / 27.7 in WC) = 0.126 psi, about an eighth of a psi.
  2. A 5-ton unit. Assume 5 tons of cooling at the rated 12,000 Btu/h per ton. How many kW of heat? Answer: 5 x 12,000 = 60,000 Btu/h. 60,000 / 3,412 = 17.6 kW of heat moved (not electrical draw).
  3. A 20 F split. The air drops 20 F across a coil. What is that in Celsius? Answer: it is a difference, so 20 F / 1.8 = 11.1 C. No 32.
  4. Pump head. A circulator is rated 30 ft of head at the job's flow. What is that in psi? Answer: 30 ft / 2.31 ft per psi = 13.0 psi (30 x 0.433 = 13.0 as a check).
  5. 500 microns. A vacuum gauge reads 500 microns. What is that in inches of mercury? Answer: 500 / 25,400 = 0.0197 in Hg, about two hundredths of an inch.
  6. A 40-gallon tank. Assume the tank is full of water at about 8.34 lb/gal. How many pounds, and how many liters? Answer: 40 gal x 8.34 lb/gal = 333.6 lb, about 334 lb. 40 gal x 3.785 L/gal = 151.4 L.

More quick ones: 48,000 Btu/h is 48,000 / 12,000 = 4 tons, or 48,000 / 3,412 = 14.1 kW. 1,200 CFM is 1,200 x 0.4719 = 566 L/s. 75 F as a reading is (75 - 32) / 1.8 = 23.9 C. 7 in WC of inlet gas pressure is 7 / 27.7 = 0.253 psi.

Field card: HVAC unit conversions

Write the unit, cancel the unit.

  • psi = in WC / 27.7
  • psi = feet of head / 2.31
  • kW = Btu/h / 3,412; tons = Btu/h / 12,000
  • L/s = CFM x 0.4719
  • Reading: C = (F - 32) / 1.8. Difference: C = F / 1.8, no 32.
  • Water: 3.785 L and about 8.34 lb per gallon
  • Inches of mercury = microns / 25,400
