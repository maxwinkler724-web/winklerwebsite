Poultry and Livestock Barn Ventilation: Minimum Ventilation, Tunnel Fans, Ammonia and Heater CO Risk

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A poultry house is a long, tight box full of animals that give off heat, moisture and gas all day. Its ventilation has two very different jobs: in winter, bring in the smallest amount of air that keeps litter dry and the air clean; in summer, move a lot of air fast. This lesson walks through both, the controls that tie them together, and the heater and power risks a technician should respect.

Check the sources and the site

Check the manual. Extension guidance changes and varies by bird type, age and region. This page was built from university extension and government pages read in 2026 and is general education. Use the equipment maker's performance data and the grower's or integrator's own requirements for any real job.

Winter: minimum ventilation

In cold weather the fans are not there to cool anything. They are there to remove moisture and gas. University of Georgia Extension says minimum ventilation helps pull moisture out of the litter, which slows the microbes that make ammonia, and that it also limits carbon monoxide buildup. It recommends keeping ammonia below 25 ppm. University of Kentucky extension adds that readings above 25 ppm mean the minimum rate needs to go up, that you should sample about one foot off the floor one minute before the timer starts the fans, and that you should not trust your nose, because smell fades with exposure.

Heaters add to the load. University of Maryland Extension says each gallon of propane burned makes about 0.8 gallon (6.8 lb) of water, about 92,000 Btu of heat, and uses about 850 cubic feet per hour of fresh air. It suggests keeping relative humidity between 50% and 70%, and raising minimum ventilation as humidity nears 65%.

Because the fans run only part of the time, the inlets must work while the fans are on. Maryland Extension gives a target of 0.08 to 0.12 inch water column in a 40-foot-wide house with the minimum ventilation fans running, and says each 0.01 inch increase adds about 2 feet of throw so the incoming air rides the ceiling before it drops. Kentucky recommends five-minute timer cycles over ten-minute ones, because a ten-minute cycle can swing the temperature roughly 2 to 5 degrees F.

Static pressure and a tight house

If you have read Airflow and Static Pressure Basics, this is the same idea: the fans pull the house to a slightly lower pressure than outside, and that pressure difference is what makes air enter through the inlets at speed. A static pressure controller reads that difference and adjusts the inlets. If the house leaks, the controller cannot hold pressure, and air sneaks in through cracks and skips the inlets. A University of Arkansas Extension fan and pad maintenance publication (FSA8021) says an ideal house holds 0.20 inch with fans running and an older house should still reach 0.13, and that below 0.10 hot air gets pulled through cracks instead of the pad.

Summer: tunnel ventilation and wind chill

Tunnel ventilation turns the house into a wind tunnel. The sidewall inlets close, air comes in through the pad and tunnel inlet at one end, and fans at the other end pull it the length of the house. Moving air cools birds by wind chill. The Ontario agriculture ministry reports research on 7-week-old broilers in which 1 m/s air felt like still air about 1 degree C cooler, and 2 m/s about 3.7 degrees C cooler. It also says tunnel ventilation is not recommended below about 21 degrees C outside or for birds under 3 weeks old, and warns that air movement above 40 degrees C makes heat stress worse.

How fast should the air go? Older guidance aimed for about 400 feet per minute. Mississippi State University Extension (2024) says that is no longer enough for larger modern broilers: new houses should reach at least 600 to 700 feet per minute, and speed should be even across the house, since 600 everywhere beats 750 in the middle and 350 at the walls.

Worked example: how many tunnel fans?

The method comes from Auburn University and Ontario: house cross-section area times target air speed gives the airflow, then divide by what each fan actually delivers. Our own assumptions are marked.

  1. House (assumption, ours): 50 ft wide, 9 ft average ceiling height. Auburn says to use width times average ceiling height.
  2. Cross-section: 50 x 9 = 450 sq ft.
  3. Target speed: 600 ft/min, the low end of Mississippi State's 600 to 700 range.
  4. Airflow needed: 450 x 600 = 270,000 cfm.
  5. Fan rating (assumption, ours): 27,000 cfm each at 0.10 inch static pressure. This is a placeholder; use the rating on the actual fan's test sheet at 0.10 inch, because Mississippi State says to count fans at 0.10 inch and Auburn warns ratings fall as pressure rises, with pads adding pressure.
  6. Fans: 270,000 / 27,000 = 10.0 fans, so 10 fans just meet the target when everything is clean.
  7. Dirt margin (our check): Arkansas Extension says dirty shutters and blades can cut airflow 20% or more. Ten fans at 80% give 216,000 cfm, and 216,000 / 450 = 480 ft/min, short of 600.
  8. Add fans: 12 fans give 324,000 cfm clean, which is 720 ft/min. At 80% that is 259,200 cfm, or 576 ft/min, close to target even when dirty.

Ontario also notes that floor-raised birds occupy the lowest foot, which cannot be counted as usable airflow area, so a designer may refine this. This example only shows the arithmetic.

Pads and inlets

Evaporative cooling pads add resistance. Ontario says pad static pressure drop should be limited to roughly 0.03 to 0.09 inch, most makers suggest 250 to 350 feet per minute through the pad, and humidity above 80% stops birds from cooling by panting. Mississippi State says to keep pads wet, flush the line before spring, clean them with a garden hose, never with bleach, chlorine or pressure washing, and measure static pressure 20 to 30 feet past the pads: a high reading there points to dirty or mineral-clogged pads. It says static pressure should not exceed 0.12 inch with all tunnel fans running. The greenhouse version of this setup is in Greenhouse Heating and Ventilation.

Fans that lose airflow

Arkansas Extension says fans with loose belts can run 10 to 20 percent lower rpm than ones with tight belts, and a worn belt riding low in the pulley acts like a pulley about half an inch smaller. Mississippi State says dirty shutters can reduce airflow by as much as 30%, to replace belts about yearly, to inspect idlers and tensioners between flocks and to keep spare belts, motors and pulleys. See Belt Drives and Sheave Basics for how to check tension and wear.

Heater and CO risk

Brooders and furnaces burn fuel inside the house, and when combustion goes wrong they make carbon monoxide. University of Georgia Extension says CO is rarely detected in broiler houses but can come from unserviced propane brooders and furnaces, that a yellow flame can signal improper burning, and that units should be cleaned after each flock. It lists drowsiness below 200 ppm, toe picking and gasping at 600 to 1,000 ppm, and possible death above 1,500 ppm in about 1.5 hours. For people, the OSHA fact sheet gives a 50 ppm 8-hour limit, lists early symptoms such as headache, dizziness and nausea, and recommends maintained equipment, ventilation and CO monitors with audible alarms. A swine-room study in the Journal of Agricultural Safety and Health found room CO fell 44% after unvented heaters were replaced with vented ones. Combustion basics are in Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Alarms and backup power

Mechanically ventilated houses depend entirely on power. University of Kentucky extension recommends an alarm that signals when power is off and a standby generator that starts automatically, and says poultry survive only 20 to 30 minutes above 97 degrees F in still air. It says to test emergency systems regularly per the maker's instructions. Mississippi State says to run the generator weekly for at least 30 minutes, log hour-meter readings and keep the fuel tank at least half full. For the dairy side of the farm, see Dairy Farm Milk Cooling.

Field card: Poultry and Livestock Barn Ventilation

Winter removes moisture and gas. Summer removes heat.

  • Keep ammonia below 25 ppm; sample near floor level before the fans start.
  • Minimum ventilation: about 0.08 to 0.12 inch in a 40 ft house with fans running (Maryland Extension).
  • Tunnel target: 600 to 700 ft/min, fans counted at 0.10 inch (Mississippi State).
  • Fan count = width x average height x speed / fan cfm at the stated pressure; add margin for dirt.
  • Loose belts and dirty shutters cut airflow by 10 to 30 percent.
  • Yellow flame on a brooder: shut down, clean and check combustion.
  • Alarm for power loss, automatic generator, weekly exercise.
