Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

People spend most of their time indoors, so what is in the air matters. Heating and cooling equipment is part of the story, because it moves the air, filters it and sometimes brings in fresh air.

Where indoor pollutants come from

The U.S. EPA lists common sources of indoor pollution. They include combustion appliances, tobacco products, building materials and furnishings, household and cleaning products, heating, cooling and humidification equipment, excess moisture, and outdoor sources such as radon and outdoor air pollution. Heat and humidity can also raise the level of some pollutants.

Ventilation: the dilution idea

Ventilation controls how much outdoor air replaces indoor air. Outdoor air gets in through leaks and cracks, through open windows and doors, and through mechanical systems such as exhaust fans and air handlers. Fresh air dilutes indoor emissions and carries pollutants out. When too little outdoor air gets in, pollutants can build up to levels that affect health and comfort.

Common ventilation approaches

  • Natural. Windows, louvers and pressure differences.
  • Exhaust only. Fans in kitchens and bathrooms remove air, odors and moisture at the source.
  • Mechanical outside air. The air handler or a rooftop unit brings in a portion of fresh air. The share of outdoor air in the supply varies widely by building and use, and the required amount comes from the code and design for that building.
  • Economizers. Bring in extra outdoor air when it is cool or dry enough to save cooling energy. See Economizers Basics.
  • Energy recovery ventilators. Move heat, and sometimes moisture, between outgoing and incoming air so ventilation costs less energy.

Air changes per hour

One way to describe ventilation is air changes per hour, or ACH, which says how many times a room's air volume is replaced in an hour. Higher is more diluting. A general reference notes that recommendations differ greatly by use, from ordinary spaces to hospital rooms. The required amount for any building comes from the codes and standards that apply to it, not from a rule of thumb.

Filtration

Filters trap particles as air passes through. How well a filter works depends on particle size, filter density and depth, and the airflow. A clogged filter hurts more than comfort. It can reduce heat transfer, freeze a cooling coil, and in heating mode cause overheating. Better filters usually add resistance, so check that the blower can handle them. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Moisture is an air quality issue

Too much humidity, sweating ducts and wet drain pans support mold and odors. Controlling humidity is part of controlling air quality. Learn the physics in Psychrometrics for Technicians.

Pressure matters too

If a building supplies more air than it exhausts, it is under positive pressure and tends to keep outside contaminants out. Negative pressure pulls untreated air in through cracks. Combustion appliances need special attention here, because a space under negative pressure can interfere with venting. Read Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Safety note. Poor ventilation around fuel-burning equipment can allow carbon monoxide to build up. Do not shut off, block or reduce required outside air or exhaust to save energy or stop a draft. Follow the code, the design and the manufacturer's instructions.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It gives no setpoints, pressures or procedures.

Field card: Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics

Dilute, filter and control moisture.

  • Sources include combustion, materials, products, moisture and outdoor air.
  • Ventilation dilutes pollutants and carries them out.
  • Filters help but add resistance. Keep them clean and sized correctly.
  • Humidity control is part of air quality.
  • Do not reduce required outside air or exhaust. Codes and designs govern.
