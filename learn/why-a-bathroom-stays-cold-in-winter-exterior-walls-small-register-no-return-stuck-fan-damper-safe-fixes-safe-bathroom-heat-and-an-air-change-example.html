Why Your Bathroom Stays Cold in Winter: Safe Fixes and Safe Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Almost every house has one room that never warms up, and it is often the bathroom. The cause is usually a mix of cold surfaces, too little warm air arriving, and cold air sneaking in. This lesson sorts the causes, the safe checks a homeowner can do, which bathroom heat is safe, and when to call a pro.

Why the bathroom stays cold

Several things stack up, and most bathrooms have more than one.

  • Exterior walls and windows. A bathroom often sits on an outside wall with a window. Those surfaces are cold in winter, and your body loses heat toward them by radiation, so the room feels colder than the thermostat says. A university extension fact sheet on air sealing lists doors and windows at about 11% and 10% of the air leakage in an average home, and says air sealing can often save 10% to 20% on home energy.
  • A small or blocked supply register. Bathrooms are small, so the designer may have given them one small register, which delivers little heat on a cold day. A rug, towel or cabinet over it makes it worse. See the related lesson on blocked vents.
  • A long duct run. The bathroom may be at the far end of the duct system. Long runs lose heat to cold spaces and leak, which are common problems and are covered in the bonus room lesson below.
  • No return, so the door must be open. Air that goes in must get out. With no return in the bathroom, the room relies on a gap under the door or an open door. A shut door can leave the supply barely moving. This is our own explanation of how air flow works, not a quoted rule.
  • Exhaust fan with a stuck damper. A national-lab installation guide says the outdoor wall cap should include a damper that closes when the fan is not running, and that dampers must open freely. A damper stuck open, or a fan with none, lets cold air fall back into the room through the fan grille. That is our own reading of how a failed damper behaves.
  • Tile floors. Tile conducts heat away from bare feet fast, so it feels cold even if the air is fine. That is our own observation; the room air may be warmer than it feels.

Safe checks a homeowner can do

  1. Open the register. Make sure the damper lever or louvers are open and nothing blocks the register. Hold your hand over it while the system runs to feel for flow.
  2. Check the fan damper. With the fan off, look for cold air or a draft at the grille, and from outside see whether the wall cap flap hangs open or is stuck by dirt or ice. Never reach into a housing with power on. Turn the fan off at the switch, and if you must open it, shut off the breaker first. Cleaning a flap from the ground is fine; roof or ladder work is a job for a pro.
  3. Fix door gap problems. Leave the door open some, or trim the gap under it only if a pro agrees, so air can leave the room. If the door fits tight, ask a pro about a return path rather than cutting into the wall yourself. Do not weatherstrip the bottom of the door shut if the room has no return.
  4. Run the fan only as long as needed. The fan pulls warm air out and replaces it with cold air leaking in from outside the house. Use a timer or run it for the length of the shower plus a short time until the mirror clears. Our worked example below shows the cost of leaving it on. For fan sizing, see the related lesson.
  5. Weatherstrip. The university fact sheet advises installing or replacing weatherstripping around the whole door frame and the same for windows. Caulk gaps around the window trim, and check the cover plates on outside-wall switches. Our related lesson on drafty doors covers how.

Our own worked example: how much cold air one fan run pulls in

Our own example. Assumptions we chose: a bathroom 5 ft by 8 ft with an 8 ft ceiling; an exhaust fan rated 50 cfm (cubic feet per minute); the fan runs 20 minutes after a shower; the replacement air comes from outdoors at 30 degrees F; the room is held at 70 degrees F. These inputs are assumed, not measured. A real fan moves less than its rating when ducts are long, and the make-up air comes through leaks in the whole house, not only this room.

  1. Room volume: 5 x 8 x 8 = 320 cubic feet.
  2. Air changes per hour from the fan: 50 cfm x 60 minutes = 3,000 cubic feet per hour. 3,000 / 320 = about 9.4 air changes per hour.
  3. Air moved per run: 50 cfm x 20 minutes = 1,000 cubic feet. That is about 3.1 room volumes, all replaced by outdoor air that leaks in somewhere.
  4. Temperature difference: 70 - 30 = 40 degrees F.
  5. Heat to warm that air, using the standard sensible-heat relation for air, Btu per hour = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference (a university lecture-notes page gives this form): 1.08 x 50 x 40 = 2,160 Btu per hour while the fan runs.
  6. Over 20 minutes (one third of an hour): 2,160 x 20/60 = 720 Btu per run.

That is not a huge number, but it arrives all at once in a room with one small register, so the room drops fast. Each hour the fan runs adds another 2,160 Btu of load on the furnace. If the damper is stuck open, some cold air also falls in while the fan is off. We have no sourced figure for that leak, so treat the worked example as a lower bound.

Safe supplemental heat

Bathrooms mix water and electricity, so choose carefully.

  • Ceiling heat lamps and fan-heater units made for bathrooms are the usual fixed answer. Choose a unit marked by its maker as suitable for the bathroom, install it as the instructions say, and have a qualified electrician do any new wiring. We cannot cite a page for this guidance, so treat it as our own.
  • Plug-in portable space heaters do not belong near water. A federal consumer-safety agency news release says electric heaters should not be used in bathrooms, notes that most do not protect against electrocution if they contact water, and describes deaths when heaters fell or were pulled into water. A second agency page says to use extra caution with electric heaters in bathrooms and never put one near a tub or sink.
  • GFCI protection. The same news release recommends ground fault circuit interrupters, which can be installed in any wall outlet and shut off power within milliseconds if an appliance falls into water. A GFCI helps but is not permission to use a portable heater beside a tub.
  • Fire. A federal fire-administration page says to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heat sources, to turn heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed, and reports that portable heaters were 3% of home heating fires but 41% of fatal ones in its 2017 to 2019 data. Keep towels away from any heater.
  • Easy comfort wins. Use a bath mat and close the window blind (our own tips). A heated floor is a job for a pro (our own advice).

When to call a pro

  • The register delivers little air with all dampers open, or a room is cold in every season: ask a pro to measure airflow and look at the duct run.
  • You want a return path, a bigger register or duct changes.
  • A fan must be opened, ducted again or its wall cap replaced.
  • Any new heater needs wiring, or a GFCI outlet or breaker trips repeatedly.
  • You smell burning, see scorch marks, or feel a tingle from a metal fixture. Switch off power and call an electrician.

Field card: Why Your Bathroom Stays Cold in Winter: Safe Fixes and Safe Heat

Warm air in, cold surfaces and leaks out. Fix the leak before adding a heater.

  • Check the register is open and clear.
  • Check the fan damper; no cold draft with the fan off.
  • Keep a way for air to leave: open door or gap.
  • Run the fan only as long as needed.
  • Weatherstrip door and window.
  • Our own example: 320 cu ft room, 50 cfm fan, about 9.4 air changes per hour, 2,160 Btu per hour at a 40 degree F difference.
  • No portable heater near water. Fixed bathroom heaters on GFCI protection, wired by a pro.
