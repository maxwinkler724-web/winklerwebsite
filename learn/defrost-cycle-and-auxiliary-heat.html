Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a cold day the outdoor coil of a heat pump can grow a layer of frost. The defrost cycle is how the system melts it away, and auxiliary heat keeps the house from feeling it.

Why the outdoor coil frosts

In heating mode the outdoor coil is the cold side. It runs colder than the outside air so it can absorb heat. When the coil surface drops below freezing and the air carries moisture, frost forms. A thin layer is normal. A thick layer blocks airflow and cuts capacity, so the system needs a way to clear it.

What defrost does

To melt the frost, the control shifts the system toward cooling mode for a short time so hot refrigerant flows through the outdoor coil. The reversing valve changes position, and the outdoor fan is normally stopped so the heat goes into melting ice instead of blowing away. You may see some vapor or mist rise from the unit and hear a whoosh when the valve shifts; this is commonly seen, but if it looks like smoke or smells burnt, shut it off and call for service. Because the cycle briefly acts like cooling, the indoor coil can produce cool air during defrost, which people notice as a cold draft.

What starts and stops it

Manufacturers use different control schemes. Some units use timers combined with a coil temperature sensor. Others use more advanced logic that watches temperatures and run time. The defrost board contains timers and basic logic, as described in HVAC School's article. HVAC School's other defrost article describes two common schemes: time-and-temperature (a coil sensor plus a timer) and demand defrost (watching coil and outdoor air temperatures). How a particular unit decides to start and end defrost is a manufacturer detail, so read the control description for that model.

For a feel for the numbers, two trade articles give examples only. An Amana example uses a timer chosen by model at 30, 60, 90 or 120 minutes (60 is described as a good general choice). Defrost starts when the sensor says the coil is near freezing and the timer has run, and ends at a factory temperature of about 60 F or after a maximum time (stated as 12 minutes in that article). A Carrier example uses a pin-selected timer of 30, 60 or 90 minutes, with the defrost thermostat closed below 30 F when the timer expires and opening at about 65 F. A Trane demand-defrost example compares outdoor and coil sensors and gives no numbers. The two articles do not agree on a maximum time, and these figures are not confirmed against installation manuals. Check the manual for the unit in front of you.

Where auxiliary heat comes in

Auxiliary or backup heat is a second heat source, such as electric heat strips, a gas furnace or hot-water coil. It has two jobs. When the heat pump cannot keep up on a very cold day, the thermostat can call it in. During defrost, many systems also energize it to counteract the cooling effect and reduce cold-air complaints. Some contractors do not energize the backup on defrost when the backup is a gas furnace. Wiring usually runs through a W2 or similar terminal, and testing a defrost board often means forcing a defrost and confirming the second stage energizes. Details vary, and some systems with a furnace as backup are set up differently, so follow the wiring diagram.

Common problems, in general

  • Ice that never clears, or a unit that never defrosts, points to a failed sensor, board, reversing valve or low airflow: check airflow, the coil sensor, the board and the reversing valve, using the manufacturer's method.
  • Constant defrosting: a dirty coil or a poor sensor reading may confuse the control.
  • Backup heat running all the time: a thermostat setting, an outdoor lockout setting or a failure of the heat pump itself. Compare the electric bill clue, too, since strip heat costs more than heat pump heat.
  • Ice buildup around the base: blocked drainage or a unit sitting where water refreezes. Keep the base clear and drain-free.

Safety

Never chip ice off a coil with a tool; fins and tubing are easy to damage. Turn off and lock out power before working inside. Electric heat strips draw large current. The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern.

Field card: Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat

Frost is normal. Defrost and backup heat are how the system copes.

  • Heating mode makes the outdoor coil cold, so it can frost.
  • Defrost briefly reverses the cycle and stops the outdoor fan.
  • Backup heat often runs during defrost to soften the cold draft.
  • Start and stop logic differs by brand; read the control description.
  • Lock out power before touching anything.
