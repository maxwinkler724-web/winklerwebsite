How Long Should My AC or Furnace Run? Run Time vs Weather, Cycles Per Hour and When Trouble Shows

Last updated: 2026-11-02

There is no single right number of minutes. A heating or cooling system should run as long as the house needs it to, and that changes with the weather. A system that runs almost nonstop on the hottest afternoon of the year may be working exactly as designed, while one that clicks on and off every few minutes in mild weather may be too big. This lesson shows the arithmetic behind both and lists the simple checks a homeowner can make.

Run time follows load

Think of the equipment as a bucket that removes heat at a fixed rate, and the house as a tap that adds heat at a rate that changes with the weather. For an air conditioner, heat gain comes from sun, outdoor air leaking in, warm walls and roof, people and appliances. For a furnace the flow runs the other way: the house loses heat faster as it gets colder outside. The share of each hour the equipment must run is simply the load divided by the capacity. That is our own reasoning from the definitions, and the example below puts numbers on it.

Extension publications from Mississippi State University and the University of Florida say the equipment should be matched to a real load calculation (they name the national residential load method or a method based on it), not to square footage. Mississippi State adds that a properly sized unit runs for longer periods, which keeps the coil cold long enough to drain moisture and hold indoor humidity down. So "runs a lot on a hot day" and "sized correctly" are not opposites. For where the design weather numbers come from, see Outdoor Design Temperatures, and for the load calculation itself see Heat Load Calculation Intro.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, not measured data. Assumptions we chose for practice: a single-stage air conditioner with a steady capacity of 36,000 Btu/h (that is 3 tons, at 12,000 Btu/h per ton); the house heat gain stays constant through the hour; the thermostat cycles the unit in a perfect pattern; and we ignore start-up losses, duct losses and the split between sensible and moisture removal. Real houses are messier, but the pattern holds.

Run fraction = house heat gain / equipment capacity. Minutes per hour = run fraction x 60.

At 3 cycles per hour each cycle lasts 60 / 3 = 20 minutes, and the on-time is the run fraction times 20: 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes. On the hottest day the unit is on for the whole hour, so the cycle count falls to zero. The load rose by a factor of four from the mild day to the hottest day, and the run minutes rose by the same factor, from 15 to 60. Notice what that means: on the hottest day the unit has no spare capacity, so it holds the house temperature only if the thermostat setting is a realistic one. If the gain went above 36,000 Btu/h, the house would drift warmer even with the unit running nonstop.

Where "nearly nonstop" fits

Capacity is chosen from a design condition, a hot or cold day that is exceeded only a small share of the time (see Outdoor Design Temperatures). That is why a correctly sized system has little to spare on the worst few days a year and runs long stretches then. On most other days the load is lower and the equipment cycles. A furnace follows the same math with the load being the house heat loss and the capacity being the furnace output, so long runs on the coldest nights are expected too. Two-stage and variable-capacity equipment change the picture by running longer at lower output; see Single-Stage vs Two-Stage vs Variable Capacity.

How thermostat settings change starts per hour

A digital thermostat often has a cycle rate setting, in cycles per hour (CPH). Our Thermostat Cycle Rate Settings lesson explains, from maker documents, that this works as a ceiling on starts per hour near half load, not a schedule. The arithmetic is simple: cycle length = 60 / CPH, and on-time = run fraction x cycle length. Our own numbers for a half-load hour:

A lower setting means fewer starts and longer runs, but a wider swing in room temperature. The right value depends on the equipment type, so use the number in the equipment and thermostat instructions.

What very short cycles can mean

A federal right-sized air conditioner fact sheet (from the joint EPA and DOE efficiency program) says oversized units short cycle, running for shorter periods than they were engineered for, which adds wear. It also says efficiency is low at start-up and rises, peaking at about 10 minutes of operation; in its example, stretching run time from 5 to 9 minutes raises the energy efficiency ratio from 6 to 7, a 17 percent improvement. The same sheet says dehumidifying needs cycles long enough for water to condense and drain, and short cycles let some of it re-evaporate. Virginia Cooperative Extension describes the result as a house that is cool but clammy, and says people then often lower the thermostat, with each degree lowered raising cooling bills by 3 to 5 percent. A Florida Solar Energy Center paper reports the same direction: oversizing cuts run time, which reduces moisture removal and mixing of room air.

Oversizing is only one cause. Charge, airflow, controls and safeties can also stop a unit early. We cover those in AC Short Cycling: Causes, Oversizing, Charge, Thermostat and Safeties, Why Is My Furnace Short Cycling?, and for boilers Hot Water Boiler Short Cycling: Oversizing, Zoning and Fixes, so we do not repeat them here.

What never-ending runs can mean

  • It is the hottest or coldest stretch of the year. As the example shows, full-time running at the design extreme is normal if the house stays comfortable.
  • Airflow is restricted. The Department of Energy says to check an air conditioner filter more often if the unit runs constantly, and that dirt on the evaporator coil reduces airflow and heat absorption.
  • The outdoor unit cannot shed heat. The Department of Energy says to keep the area around the condenser free of debris and to trim plants back at least 2 feet.
  • The house is gaining more than it should. Open doors and windows, a very low thermostat setting or leaky ducts add load or waste capacity.
  • Something inside is not working. If the system runs for hours and the house still will not reach a comfortable temperature, that is the sign DOE gives for calling a technician.

Simple homeowner checks

  1. Filter. University of Florida and Mississippi State extension publications say to check filters monthly in peak season; the Department of Energy says to clean or replace an air conditioner filter every month or two while cooling. See Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit for fit and direction.
  2. Thermostat location. A thermostat in direct sun, in a draft or near a supply vent can misread the room and cause odd cycles; see Where to Put a Thermostat.
  3. Outdoor unit. Clear leaves, clippings and dryer vent output, and keep to the maker's clearances. Mississippi State and Florida extension say to follow clearances exactly and keep dryer vents at least 10 feet away so lint does not clog the coil.
  4. Vents and registers. A Department of Energy home heating guide says to keep vents, baseboard heaters and radiators unobstructed. Open the supply registers and keep furniture off returns.
  5. Note the pattern. Write down start and stop times for an hour on a mild day and on a hot day. Compare them with the table above.

When to call a pro

Call a technician when the system cannot bring the house to a comfortable temperature, when it short cycles after the checks above, or when the problem looks like refrigerant, ducts or electrical work. Mississippi State Extension recommends a licensed contractor for refrigerant charge work and for duct inspection and sealing, and says to ask for a written load calculation before any replacement. Homeowner checks should stop at the filter, outdoor coil debris and registers.

Field card: How long should my AC or furnace run?

Run fraction = load / capacity.

  • Run minutes per hour = (load / capacity) x 60.
  • Our example: 36,000 Btu/h unit, gains of 9,000, 18,000, 27,000, 36,000 give 15, 30, 45, 60 minutes per hour.
  • Cycle length = 60 / cycles per hour; at 3 per hour it is 20 minutes.
  • Nonstop on the worst day can be normal; very short runs in mild weather can mean oversizing.
  • Checks: filter, thermostat location, outdoor unit clear, vents open.
  • Cannot reach a comfortable temperature, or short cycles persist: call a pro.
