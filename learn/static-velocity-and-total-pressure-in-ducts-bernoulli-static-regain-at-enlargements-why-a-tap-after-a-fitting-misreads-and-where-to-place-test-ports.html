Static, Velocity and Total Pressure in Ducts: Static Regain and Tap Placement

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A manometer on a duct can show three different pressures, and mixing them up is how good technicians get bad numbers. This lesson sorts out static, velocity and total pressure, uses Bernoulli's idea to explain why static pressure goes up where a duct gets bigger, and shows why a static tap too close to a fitting can mislead you.

Three pressures, one sum

Moving air carries energy in two forms. Static pressure is the push on the duct walls, the one that inflates a flexible duct. Velocity pressure is the pressure that goes with the air's motion, and it exists only while air is moving in one direction. Total pressure is the sum: TP = SP + VP. University lecture notes on duct fluid mechanics give the same relation and the standard-air form for velocity pressure, VP = (V / 4005)² in inches of water column, where V is velocity in feet per minute. They also give the rearranged form V = 4005 × √VP, which is what a pitot traverse uses to turn a reading into fpm (see the pitot traverse lesson).

Static pressure is the one you read with a tap that is flat to the flow. Velocity pressure is never read directly with a plain tap. A pitot tube reads total pressure facing into the flow and static pressure at the side, and the difference is velocity pressure. That same set of notes illustrates it as TP minus SP equals VP.

Where Bernoulli comes in

Bernoulli's equation is an energy balance for a moving fluid. A national aeronautics laboratory describes it as relating pressure in a gas to local velocity and says it comes from conservation of energy. For duct work, the useful form is the one the university notes give, with real-world losses added: SP1 + VP1 = SP2 + VP2 + losses. If the air speeds up, velocity pressure rises, so static pressure must fall unless energy is being added. If the air slows down, velocity pressure falls and static pressure can rise. The pretend case with no losses is called ideal. Real fittings always lose some.

Static regain at an enlargement

When a duct gets bigger, the same airflow spreads over a larger area, so the velocity drops. Velocity pressure drops with the square of velocity. That lost velocity pressure does not vanish. Some of it converts back into static pressure, which is called static regain. The federal energy department's fan sourcebook describes the same idea for a fan outlet: kinetic energy in the airstream is converted to a static pressure increase. The rest of the drop is lost to turbulence in the fitting.

The opposite holds at a reduction. When a duct narrows, the air speeds up and velocity pressure rises. That energy comes out of static pressure, so static falls, and the fitting also adds its own loss on top. Static pressure going down through a fitting is not always a sign of a problem. Part of it is just the air speeding up.

Total pressure is different. In a straight run it drops slowly from friction. Across a fitting it drops by the fitting's loss. A national laboratory study of residential air distribution defines a fitting's local loss coefficient as the ratio of the total pressure loss across it to the velocity pressure, which is why velocity pressure shows up in every fitting calculation.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions. A rectangular duct enlarges so that air slows from 900 fpm to 600 fpm. Assume standard air, a smooth transition, and a fitting efficiency of 0.6. The 0.6 is our own assumed value for teaching, not a published rating for any fitting.

  1. Velocity pressure before: (900 / 4005)² = (0.2247)² = 0.0505 in. w.c.
  2. Velocity pressure after: (600 / 4005)² = (0.1498)² = 0.0224 in. w.c.
  3. Drop in velocity pressure: 0.0505 − 0.0224 = 0.0280 in. w.c. (using unrounded values, 0.02806).
  4. Ideal static regain equals that drop: about 0.028 in. w.c. The static pressure after the fitting would be 0.028 higher than before.
  5. With an assumed fitting efficiency of 0.6: 0.6 × 0.0281 = 0.0168, about 0.017 in. w.c. of actual regain.
  6. The rest, 0.0281 − 0.0168 = 0.0112, about 0.011 in. w.c., is lost in the fitting. Total pressure falls by that 0.011.

Check. We checked the velocity pressure a second way, from the energy form, VP = density × V² / 2. With the sourcebook's standard density of 0.075 lb/ft³, 900 fpm is 15 ft/s, and 0.075 × 15² / (2 × 32.174) = 0.262 lb/ft². One inch of water column is 5.202 lb/ft², so that is 0.0504 in. w.c., matching 0.0505. The same method at 600 fpm (10 ft/s) gives 0.0224.

What the numbers tell you. The whole effect is less than three hundredths of an inch. Because velocity pressure rises with the square of velocity, the effect grows quickly at higher speeds and is tiny at low ones.

Why a tap right after a fitting misreads

Just downstream of an elbow, transition or damper the flow is swirling and its speed is uneven across the duct. A static tap there does not see a clean static pressure. A peer-reviewed study of ventilation duct fittings from a university engineering department notes that pressure sensors are sometimes put at inappropriate sections, which can cause large measurement errors. In their own test rig they placed the downstream pressure ring about five times the duct dimension past the fitting, so the static pressure could recover fully. That distance is a researcher's choice for a laboratory rig, not a field rule. They also used rings with four taps, one on each duct face, to average out uneven readings.

The fan sourcebook makes a related point from the system side: uneven, swirling flow raises losses, and an elbow near a fan outlet can cut delivered flow noticeably. For the fan side of that story, see the fan system effect lesson.

What this means for test ports and transitions

  • Choose straight, quiet duct. Put a port in a straight section, away from elbows, transitions, dampers and takeoffs. Follow the equipment installation instructions where they give locations. For equipment readings, see the total external static lesson.
  • Compare like with like. A tap in a small, fast section and a tap in a large, slow section will not read the same static pressure even with no loss at all. Expect part of the difference to be velocity pressure.
  • Keep the tap flat to the flow. The notes we cite show the static port at the side of the probe, away from the impact opening, which is why a plain static tap should not face into the flow.
  • Size transitions from fitting data. How much velocity pressure comes back depends on the fitting, so use published loss data for the actual transition. The 0.6 above is only a teaching assumption.
  • Do not count on regain to fix a design. Treat any regain as a bonus, not a guarantee.

Field card: Static, Velocity and Total Pressure in Ducts: Static Regain and Tap Placement

TP = SP + VP. Static regain is velocity pressure turning back into static.

  • VP = (V / 4005)² in. w.c. at standard air, V in fpm.
  • Air slows in an enlargement: VP falls, SP can rise.
  • Air speeds up in a reduction: VP rises, SP falls.
  • Our example: 900 fpm is 0.050, 600 fpm is 0.022, ideal regain 0.028, about 0.017 at an assumed 0.6.
  • Do not tap right after a fitting or in swirling flow.
  • Tap flat to the flow, in a straight run, at the point the instructions name.
