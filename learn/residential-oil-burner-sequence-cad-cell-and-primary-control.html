Oil Burner Sequence: Cad Cell and Primary Control

Part of the Gas and Oil Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas furnace proves flame with a metal rod. A home oil burner proves flame with a small light sensor called a cad cell. This lesson follows one burner from the thermostat call to a steady flame, and shows how to read the cell with a meter.

Safety first. Oil burner service belongs to trained, qualified technicians. Learn the sequence here, but do not reset a locked-out burner over and over, bypass a control, or bleed a pump into a hot chamber. If you smell oil fumes or see smoke, get everyone outside and call for help.

The parts, in plain terms

A residential flame-retention burner is a motor with several jobs. Beckett's AF/AFG burner page lists the pieces: a fuel pump, a nozzle line that carries oil to the nozzle, electrodes fed by high voltage from an ignition transformer or a solid-state igniter, a blower that sends air through an air tube, and a flame-retention head matched to the appliance. The pump pushes oil to the nozzle, which breaks it into a fine mist. The electrodes make a spark across that mist. The blower supplies combustion air, and the retention head shapes the air so the flame stays stable. A cad cell, mounted so it can see the flame, tells the primary control whether anything is burning. Nozzle size and pump pressure are their own topic and are not covered here.

The cad cell

A cad cell is a cadmium sulfide sensor whose resistance depends on light. In the dark it resists current almost completely. When it sees the glow of an oil flame, its resistance falls. The control watches that resistance. Beckett's troubleshooting bulletin says a properly adjusted burner reads roughly 300 to 1000 ohms while running, and never more than 1600 ohms. The cell's checkout sheet from Resideo (Honeywell's maker brand) says the same cell should read below 1600 ohms with flame and above 20,000 ohms dark. A cell in the dark that still reads low is shorted. A cell in flame that reads high is dirty, misaimed, or looking at a poor flame.

The sequence of operation

Exact timings differ by model, so always read the control's own sheet. Here is the pattern for the Honeywell R7184, as its guides describe it:

  1. Call for heat. The thermostat and limit circuit close.
  2. Safe start check. The control confirms the cad cell sees no light. Honeywell's tech guide gives 2 to 6 seconds. If the cell sees a flame that should not be there, the burner will not start.
  3. Valve-on delay (some models). The motor and igniter start, but the oil valve waits. The R7184B adds a 15-second delay, which lets the blower clear the chamber and the spark settle before oil flows. Beckett's GeniSys 7505B has the same 15 seconds. Other models skip the delay and open the valve at once.
  4. Trial for ignition. The valve opens. Flame must be proven within the lockout time, which Honeywell says is 15, 30 or 45 seconds depending on the control. The GeniSys 7505 has a 15-second lockout.
  5. Ignition carryover. After the cell sees flame, the spark stays on 10 seconds (R7184) to steady the flame.
  6. Run. The cell keeps watching. If flame is lost, the R7184 waits 60 seconds and tries again. Three flame losses in a row without finishing a heat call, and it locks out.
  7. Off. At the end of the call the valve closes. Some models run the motor a little longer (motor-off delay) to finish burning the oil at the nozzle.

If flame is not proven when the trial ends, the control locks out: the valve closes, the motor and igniter stop, and an indicator flashes. Compare this with the gas steps in Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation and with Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics. For boilers, see Boiler Flame Safeguards, Purge and Lockout.

Why repeated resets are dangerous

During every trial for ignition the pump sprays oil. If the oil does not light, it pools in the combustion chamber and fills it with vapor. A later spark can then ignite a lot of vapor at once, which is the hard start called a puff-back (this is our plain-language explanation of the makers' warnings below). This is why the makers limit resets. Honeywell's R7184 sheet says the control can be reset only three times to limit unburned oil buildup, and its tech guide says restricted lockout exists to prevent repetitious resetting by the homeowner. Releasing it takes a 30-second button hold. Beckett's GeniSys manual says to troubleshoot before resetting from restricted lockout, to make sure the appliance is free of oil and oil vapor first, and that reset and service are for a qualified technician only. It also warns that failing to bleed the pump properly can cause unstable combustion, hot gas puff-back and heavy smoke. The takeaway for an apprentice or an owner: do not keep pressing reset. A burner that locks out again needs a qualified technician to find the cause. Soot and CO risks are covered in Combustion and CO Safety Basics and Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Worked example: reading the cell

A technician checks a burner that locks out. Per Beckett's bulletin, the burner must keep running while the meter is connected, so the cell leads are removed from F-F and a jumper (or a 1500-ohm resistor) is placed across the terminals before lockout. The technician then clips the meter across the cell leads once the flame is up.

Reading 1: 600 ohms. The limit is 1600 ohms. 1600 − 600 = 1000 ohms of margin, and 600 is about 38 percent of the limit (600 ÷ 1600 = 0.375). It also sits inside Beckett's typical 300 to 1000 range. On a Honeywell R7184, 600 falls in the 400 to 800 band, which the control shows as 2 flashes. Decision: the cell is fine. Look elsewhere for the lockout, such as ignition or air.

Reading 2: 2200 ohms. 2200 − 1600 = 600 ohms over the limit. Beckett says a reading above 1600 but not infinite means the cell is dirty or defective, the sighting is wrong, or the flame is poorly adjusted. Decision: wipe the lens, check the aim, and inspect the flame before replacing the cell.

Reading 3: infinite. That is an open circuit: a broken cell, a bad holder or wire, or a loose cell.

Afterward, remove the jumper, reconnect the cell leads, and with the burner off check that the dark reading is above 20,000 ohms. Never leave the jumper in place; it defeats the flame safeguard. If the jumper was not on before the control locked out, Beckett says to wait 3 to 5 minutes before trying again.

Field card: Oil Burner Sequence: Cad Cell and Primary Control

Spray, spark, prove flame, and never reset into a pool of oil.

  • Burner parts: pump, nozzle, electrodes, igniter, blower, retention head, cad cell.
  • Sequence: call for heat, safe start check, optional valve-on delay, trial for ignition, carryover, run.
  • Cad cell running: under 1600 ohms (typically 300 to 1000). Dark: over 20,000.
  • Above 1600 but not open: dirty, misaimed or poor flame. Infinite: open circuit.
  • Lockout times in the sheets read: 15, 30 or 45 seconds (R7184); 15 seconds (GeniSys 7505).
  • Makers limit resets to protect against unburned oil. Do not keep pressing reset.
  • Remove any test jumper afterward.
