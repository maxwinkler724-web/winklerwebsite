Boiler Pressure and Temperature Gauges: Reading PSI and Feet of Head, Setting the Altitude Pointer, Stuck Needles and Test-Gauge Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most hot water boilers carry one round gauge that shows three things: system pressure in psi, water temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, and an "altitude" reading in feet. It looks simple, and it is also the quickest way to tell whether a system is healthy. This lesson shows how to read it, how to set the altitude pointer, what normal looks like, and how to check and replace one safely.

What the three scales mean

State boiler rules treat the pressure gauge and the thermometer as required equipment on a hot water heating boiler. New York's low pressure boiler rule says each such boiler needs a pressure or altitude gauge connected to the boiler or its flow connection, plus a thermometer that shows the water temperature at or near the outlet (Part 4, sections 4-5.5 and 4-5.11). The North Dakota rules and the New Jersey pre-inspection checklist say the same, citing ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section IV paragraphs. On a combination gauge those jobs share one case.

  • PSI scale. Pressure at the gauge, in pounds per square inch.
  • Feet (altitude) scale. The same pressure expressed as feet of water. One foot of water is 0.433 psi, and one psi is 2.31 feet, according to the Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation formula table. So 10 psi and 23.1 feet are the same reading on two scales.
  • Temperature scale. Boiler water temperature in degrees Fahrenheit. Our own note: the high limit control, not this dial, is what holds the temperature down. See Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs.

Two pointers, one job each

The black needle moves by itself. It shows actual pressure on the psi scale and, at the same angle, the feet value on the altitude scale. The second pointer, usually a colored tab or a small hand you turn with a knob or by sliding it, does not move on its own. It is a marker. We set it to the cold fill pressure, so later we can see at a glance whether the system has drifted. (Our own explanation of how these gauges are used; follow the instructions printed with your gauge for the exact way to turn the marker.)

Why the altitude pointer matters

Water has to be pushed up to the highest radiator or coil. A Kansas State University repository article on hydronic system components defines fill pressure as the pressure needed to get water to the highest point in the system, uses 2.31 ft per psi, and adds a 5 psig margin so the top of the system does not go negative. If the needle falls toward the altitude pointer, or below it, the top of the system is losing its water cushion and air can be drawn in. Tank sizing and the fill valve are covered in Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure and Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer, so we only use the result here.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions: a three-story house, the gauge at the boiler in the basement, the highest radiator 25 ft above the gauge, a 30 psi relief valve, ordinary water, and a margin of 3 psi that we chose. These are our numbers, not a design.

  1. Static head: 25 ft x 0.433 psi per ft = 10.825 psi, about 10.8 psi.
  2. Add the margin: 10.8 + 3 = 13.8 psi. Round to a cold fill of 14 psi. (The university article's 5 psig margin would give about 16 psi, so the design or the maker's instructions decide the margin.)
  3. Altitude pointer: set it at 14 psi on the psi scale. On the feet scale that is 14 x 2.31 = 32.3, about 32 ft. It sits about 7 ft above the highest radiator, which is the margin.
  4. Check the top: 14 - 10.8 = 3.2 psi at the top radiator when cold. Positive, good.
  5. Hot reading of 20 psi: it is 10 psi below the 30 psi relief setting, about two-thirds (20 / 30 = 0.67) of it, and 6 psi above the cold fill. At the top radiator that is 20 - 10.8 = 9.2 psi. In feet, 20 x 2.31 = 46 ft.

Is 20 psi hot acceptable? The North Dakota boiler operator training presentation says pressure in a hot water system usually runs 10 to 20 psi for a boiler with a 30 psi relief valve, so 20 is at the high end of that range but inside it. What we would watch (our own judgment) is the trend: a pressure that settles at about the same hot number every cycle is normal, while one that creeps up toward the relief setting, or a cold reading that keeps falling below the pointer, points to a tank or fill problem. Start with Boiler Pressure Too High or Too Low, and for a valve that has already opened see Boiler Pressure Relief Valve Dripping or Discharging.

Normal cold and hot readings

Cold, the needle should sit at or a little above the altitude pointer. Warming the water makes it expand, so the needle rises as the boiler heats, and it falls back as the water cools. How far it rises depends on the expansion tank. New York's rule defines low pressure hot water boilers as those at or under 160 psi and 250 degrees Fahrenheit (section 4-1.2), which is far above household readings, so a house needle near the 30 psi relief setting is an alarm, not a normal day.

Gauge dial range

The dial is chosen against the relief valve. The New Jersey checklist and the North Dakota rules both say the scale should be graduated to not less than 1.5 nor more than 3.5 times the relief valve setting; New York requires at least 1.5 times. For a 30 psi relief valve that is a dial reaching from 45 psi up to 105 psi (1.5 x 30 and 3.5 x 30). New York also requires effective stops for the pointer at zero, and nonferrous tubing for gauge connections under one inch. A gauge that stops at 30 would be too small. If you replace a gauge, match the range and the connection to the original.

Wells versus direct immersion

The thermometer must read water temperature at or near the outlet (New York 4-5.11, New Jersey checklist HG-612). Two ways to do that, in our own words: a bulb in a dry well (a closed pocket) reads the water through the metal, and can usually be pulled without draining; a bulb in direct immersion touches the water, so the system must be drained down below it before it is removed. Which one you have depends on the gauge and the boiler tapping, so check the maker's instructions. A loose bulb in an empty well, or a well with no heat-conducting contact, can read low, so the dial may disagree with the pipe.

Stuck or lying needles

These are our own field checks, not measured values:

  • Needle will not return to the zero stop when the system is open to atmosphere and drained to below the gauge: the gauge is bad.
  • Needle never moves while the boiler heats and cools: the pressure passage may be plugged, or the isolating cock is closed. The isolating cock handle should be parallel to the pipe when open (New York 4-5.5).
  • Needle jumps or sticks: tap the case lightly once; if it moves, replace, do not trust it.
  • Temperature reads wrong: compare with a pipe thermometer at the same point, and check the bulb is seated in its well.

Checking against a test gauge

A lying gauge can hide a real overpressure. Compare it to a known good test gauge on a spare tapping (our own method): read both cold, then again hot, and note the difference at each. Use a recently calibrated test gauge; for how we confirm instruments, see Checking HVAC Meters and Gauges in the Field. If the two disagree beyond what the maker allows, replace the dial gauge. The gauge is an indicator; the relief valve is the safety device, so never lean on a gauge reading to excuse a leaking valve.

Replacing a gauge safely

  1. Shut the burner and boiler power off at the disconnect, and lock or tag it. OSHA's lockout/tagout rule, 29 CFR 1910.147(d)(5), calls for stored or residual energy to be relieved, disconnected, restrained or made safe, and for isolation to be verified before work starts. Hot pressurized water is stored energy.
  2. Close the fill valve and let the boiler cool until the water and the piping can be touched. Do not rely on the old gauge to say the pressure is gone, because it may be the one that is stuck.
  3. Drain down just below the gauge tapping, and open a vent so air can enter. Pressure must be at atmospheric before the gauge comes out.
  4. Turn the gauge by its wrench flat or hex, never by the case. Use a sealant suitable for hot water, and do not overtighten.
  5. Refill, vent, check for leaks, and reset the altitude pointer to the fill pressure you calculated.
  6. Compare to a test gauge, then run the boiler and watch the hot pressure and temperature reading for a full cycle.

Local rules decide who may do this work on a given boiler. Follow them and the maker's instructions.

Field card: Boiler Pressure and Temperature Gauges

The black needle moves; the altitude pointer is set.

  • 1 foot of water = 0.433 psi; 1 psi = 2.31 ft.
  • Cold fill = height to highest radiator x 0.433, plus a margin.
  • Our example: 25 ft x 0.433 = 10.8 psi, plus 3 = 14 psi = 32 ft.
  • Hot 20 psi against a 30 psi relief is inside the usual 10 to 20 psi range; watch the trend.
  • Dial range: 1.5 to 3.5 times the relief setting.
  • Replace only after lockout, cool-down, drain below the tapping, and zero pressure.
