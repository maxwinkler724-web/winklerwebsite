Ran Out of Heating Oil: How to Get the Heat Back Safely

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The house is cold, the burner clicks or hums, and the tank is empty. Delivery fixes the tank, but it does not always fix the furnace. Air gets into the fuel line when the tank runs dry, and the burner may not restart until a technician clears it. This lesson explains why, what you can safely check, and what to leave alone.

Safety first. If you smell oil fumes strongly, see smoke, or see oil on the floor near the burner, get everyone outside and call for help. Do not keep pressing the red reset button. Bleeding a pump and resetting a locked-out control are technician jobs.

Signs you ran out

The usual picture is a house that cools off with the thermostat calling for heat, a burner that tries to start and shuts off, or a burner that does not start at all. Before you assume the tank is empty, look at the tank gauge. Maine's heating oil checklist asks whether the fuel gauge is working correctly and not cracked, stuck, frozen or missing, and InspectApedia notes the same stuck-gauge possibility. A gauge that has not moved in weeks may be lying. A heating oil dealer or a technician can confirm the real level.

What the gauge reading means

Most home tanks have a float gauge with a dial or a clear window. It is an estimate, not a meter. InspectApedia says the gauge is not a precise instrument and, on a typical oval tank, is only close to accurate when the tank is full, half and empty. Its moderator calls the oil company when the gauge is at the quarter mark or lower. The Massachusetts Department of Energy Resources consumer tip sheet gives the same reason: dealers recommend not going below a quarter of a tank, because low oil can let sediment from the bottom of the tank be pulled into the fuel line and burner, which clogs filters and equipment and can lead to an extra service call.

Worked example: how fast a quarter tank can go

This example uses made-up round numbers. Suppose a 275-gallon tank reads one quarter. The nominal amount is 275 × 0.25 = 68.75 gallons. Suppose the burner has a 0.75 gallon-per-hour nozzle at 100 psi (see the nozzle lesson linked below). If it fired nonstop, which it does not, 68.75 ÷ 0.75 = about 91.7 hours, or 91.7 ÷ 24 = about 3.8 days. In a deep cold snap the burner runs a large share of the time, so the real reserve is a few days, and a gauge error on top of that makes the margin smaller. That is why a quarter tank means order now, not wait.

Why it will not just restart after delivery

The fuel pump pulls oil up the line. When the tank runs dry, air fills the line and pump. After the tank is refilled, that air stays. InspectApedia says air in the lines can keep the burner from running or can risk a puff-back, and that bleeding is usually needed after a complete run-out, a filter change or a line repair. Danfoss's BFPH fuel unit sheet says bleeding is only necessary on one-pipe systems and that a two-pipe system bleeds itself through the return line. Which kind of piping your home has is something a technician can read at the burner.

Why pressing reset again and again is dangerous

Each try to light sprays oil into the combustion chamber. If it does not light, the oil pools and fills the chamber with vapor, and a later spark can ignite all of it at once. That sudden, loud ignition is called a puff-back. The control makers write limits into their products for this reason.

  • Honeywell's R7184A instructions say the control can be reset only three times to limit unburned oil buildup in the combustion area. The count returns to zero after a heat call is completed, and release from restricted mode takes a 30-second button hold.
  • Beckett's GeniSys 7505 manual says reset and service are for a qualified technician only, to ensure the appliance is free of oil and oil vapor before starting or resetting, and to troubleshoot the system before resetting from restricted lockout. It also warns that failing to bleed the pump properly could result in unstable combustion, hot gas puff-back and heavy smoke, and not to let oil spray into a hot chamber while bleeding.

For a plain-language homeowner rule: if the burner locks out and the tank may have been dry, do not keep resetting it. Call for service. Our lesson on the oil burner sequence, cad cell and primary control walks through what the control is doing and why it locks out.

What to do, in order

  1. Check that the gauge is moving and that other causes are not in play. Confirm the thermostat is calling for heat.
  2. Order oil. How many gallons to ask for and what delivery minimums apply vary by dealer and by state, so ask your dealer.
  3. Do not repeatedly press reset. Tell the dealer the tank ran dry so they send a technician who can bleed the pump.
  4. Protect the pipes if the house is cold. See Heat Went Out in Winter: How to Keep Pipes From Freezing.
  5. Later, ask the technician about the filter and the tank condition. The New Jersey tank lesson linked below covers leaks and warning signs.

Sludge after a dry tank

Sediment settles on the tank bottom. The Massachusetts DOER sheet says running low can draw it into the fuel line and burner and clog filters. Ask the technician to look at the filter during the visit. The home heating oil tank lesson linked below explains how a tank's water and sludge problems tie to repeated burner trouble.

Technician section: after a run-dry

This is for apprentices and describes the idea, not a step list to improvise from. Follow the burner and fuel unit maker's instructions.

  • Bleeding at the pump. InspectApedia describes attaching a clear tube to the bleeder fitting, putting the other end in a clear jar, running the burner and opening the bleeder until bubbles stop. Danfoss's sheet says to open the bleeder no more than a half turn. Beckett's GeniSys manual says not to spray oil into a hot chamber while bleeding and to confirm the chamber has no oil or vapor first.
  • Filter, strainer and nozzle. Filter and strainer: Danfoss's sheet describes a pump strainer and suggests a quality filter in the supply line, so inspect both for sediment after a dry tank. Nozzle: replace only with the size and type the burner maker specifies; see the nozzle and pump pressure lesson for ratings.
  • Canister bleed screw. On a one-line filter canister, InspectApedia warns not to lose the small washer gasket under the screw, because a missing gasket can pull in air.
  • Verify. After bleeding, confirm a steady flame, a sensible cad cell reading and no oil in the chamber before leaving.

Field card: Ran Out of Heating Oil

Fill the tank, then fix the air.

  • Order oil at one quarter, as the Massachusetts energy office advises.
  • Float gauges are estimates and can stick.
  • Air in the line stays after refill. Bleeding is usually needed after a run-out.
  • One-pipe systems need bleeding; two-pipe units clear air through the return, per Danfoss.
  • Do not keep pressing reset. Honeywell's R7184A allows only three resets, to avoid unburned oil buildup.
  • Never spray oil into a hot chamber while bleeding.
  • Expect sediment and a clogged filter after a dry tank.
