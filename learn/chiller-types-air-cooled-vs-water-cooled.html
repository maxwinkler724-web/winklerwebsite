Chiller Types: Air-Cooled vs. Water-Cooled

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Every chiller makes cold water by moving heat out of a building. The big question is where the heat goes: into the outdoor air, or into a second loop of water that carries it to a cooling tower.

The job of a chiller

A chiller cools water, sometimes mixed with glycol, and pumps it to coils in air handlers and other equipment. The water picks up heat from the air, warms up, and returns to be cooled again. Inside the chiller, the usual vapor-compression cycle moves that heat to the condenser. How the condenser gets rid of the heat is what separates the two main types. See Chiller Plant Fundamentals for the whole loop.

Air-cooled chillers

The condenser sits outdoors with fans blowing air across coils, much like a very large condensing unit. The heat goes straight to the air. These are usually delivered as one factory-built package, so there is no cooling tower, no condenser-water pump and no condenser-water piping to install.

Water-cooled chillers

The condenser is a shell-and-tube heat exchanger, and a separate condenser-water loop carries the heat to a cooling tower. The chiller usually sits indoors. You now have more equipment to run and care for: the tower, condenser-water pumps, piping and a water treatment program. See Cooling Tower Basics.

Side by side, in general terms

  • Efficiency. A water-cooled condenser follows the outdoor wet-bulb temperature, which is lower than the dry-bulb temperature that an air-cooled condenser follows. That is why water-cooled plants are usually more efficient, though the advantage can shrink at night or in mild weather.
  • Maintenance. Air-cooled units have fewer systems to look after, mostly coil cleaning and fans. Water-cooled plants add tower upkeep, water treatment, condenser-tube cleaning and freeze protection.
  • Water use. Air-cooled uses no water. Open towers lose water to evaporation and need make-up water.
  • Size. One published comparison lists packaged air-cooled units from roughly 7.5 to 500 tons and water-cooled units from roughly 10 to 4,000 tons, so big loads tend to land on water-cooled machines.
  • Life. The same comparison gives a typical life of about 15 to 20 years for air-cooled and 20 to 30 years for water-cooled. Treat those as ballpark figures, since care and conditions change everything.

A third type: evaporative

In general practice, some chillers spray water over the condenser coil to help reject heat. This gets part of the water-cooled benefit outdoors without a remote tower, and it brings its own water and cleaning needs.

How to choose

There is no single right answer. Space, water cost and availability, climate, noise, backup power, local rules and the size of the load all push the decision. Water-scarce sites and cold climates often lean air-cooled. Large plants focused on energy use often lean water-cooled.

What to watch as a tech

  • Air-cooled: dirty or bent condenser coils, failed fans, and high ambient days that push head pressure up.
  • Water-cooled: fouled condenser tubes, poor water treatment, tower problems, and low condenser-water flow.
  • Either type: your pressure and temperature readings only mean something compared with the unit's own normal, so log them.

Safety

Chillers hold refrigerant under pressure and run on high voltage. Lock out and tag out before opening a panel or touching a rotating part, and never bypass a safety device to keep something running. Refrigerant work requires the proper certification.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the equipment in front of you and the rules where you work always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It gives no setpoints, and your site's numbers are the ones that count.

Field card: Chiller Types: Air-Cooled vs. Water-Cooled

Where the heat goes decides the type.

  • Air-cooled: heat goes to outdoor air, packaged, no tower, no water use.
  • Water-cooled: heat goes to a tower loop, usually more efficient, more to maintain.
  • Water-cooled condensing follows wet-bulb, air-cooled follows dry-bulb.
  • Big loads tend to favor water-cooled; water-scarce or cold sites tend to favor air-cooled.
  • Know your plant's normal readings and log them.
