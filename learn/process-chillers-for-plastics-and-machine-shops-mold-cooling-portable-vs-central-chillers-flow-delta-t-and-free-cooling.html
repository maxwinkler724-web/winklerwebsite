Process Chillers for Plastics and Machine Shops: Mold Cooling, Flow and Delta T

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An injection molding press makes plastic parts by pushing hot melt into a steel mold, and the part only becomes a part when the mold pulls the heat back out. A CNC machine tool has the opposite problem: motors and bearings make heat that must not warp the metal. A process chiller is the cold-water machine that serves both. This lesson covers how to size one from a stated load, what flow and delta T mean, and when outdoor air can do part of the job.

What the chiller is actually doing

In plastics, a trade article from Genemco says chillers pump coolant through mold channels to control mold temperature, which limits warping, sink marks and incomplete filling, and that they also cool hydraulic oil and other machine parts. In machine shops, a machining reference site says chillers keep spindle bearings and motor windings at a stable coolant temperature, because steel grows roughly 12 micrometers per meter for each degree Celsius, so a warm spindle changes the part you are cutting. The loop is the same idea in both: carry heat out in water, then dump it elsewhere. It is not building comfort cooling, but it uses the same refrigeration and hydronic skills.

Portable vs central

Conair's public article says portable chillers are typically 30 to 40 tons or less and suit a single machine or a process that needs a different temperature from the rest of the plant. A central chiller serves several large, variable loads that need water at about the same temperature, and the same article says one central unit is generally more efficient than several portables of the same total size. Advantage Engineering lists the practical differences: a portable chiller is meant to cool one use point, has a small reservoir and little thermal mass, and has no dedicated process pump or pump redundancy. A central chiller has a large reservoir (a "fluid flywheel"), a dedicated evaporator pump and a dedicated process pump, multiple refrigeration circuits and expandability. Installation is minimal for portables and extensive for central units.

Plastics Machinery Magazine adds a selection rule from the plant side: if 80 percent of the resins run within about 5 F of each other, a central system is worth considering. It also says most plants still keep a few portables for emergencies or for molds outside the central temperature range. Our reading is that this is a choice between simple and flexible (portable) and efficient and shared (central), not right and wrong.

Reservoir, pump and the loop

The diagram shows the loop. The chiller cools the water, the reservoir stores a buffer of cold water so the compressor does not short-cycle, the pump pushes water through a supply manifold to each machine, and a return manifold brings the warm water back. Many process loops are built with a vented reservoir open to the air, which is simple but lets in dust and air. A closed loop is sealed and pressurized and keeps the water cleaner. See the closed-loop treatment lesson linked below for inhibitors and bypass feeders.

Supply temperature and why glycol appears below 50 F

Advantage Engineering says a chiller typically runs a setpoint of 50 F or higher, and that straight water is limited to about 48 F; below that, use inhibited propylene glycol. Its chart pairs 25 to 47 F operation with at least 30 percent glycol (freeze point 10 F) and 10 to 24 F with 40 percent (freeze point minus 5 F). The percentages are the minimums fluid makers recommend for the corrosion and scale inhibitors to work. The same page says the refrigerant in the evaporator must run at least 10 F colder than the fluid setpoint, warns that more glycol than needed hurts heat transfer, and bans automotive antifreeze because its silicates coat heat transfer surfaces. For strength checks and refractometer use, see the brewery lesson linked below, which covers propylene glycol in more detail.

Colder water also costs capacity. Conair and Plastics Machinery Magazine both give the rating basis as 50 F leaving water, with roughly 2 percent more tonnage needed for every degree below it (and about 2 percent less above it).

Flow, delta T and the tons formula

Water carries about 500 Btu per hour for each gpm for each degree F of temperature change (Conair, and the Oak Ridge National Laboratory process cooling training deck, which also uses 12,000 Btu/hr per ton). Divide by 12,000 and you get the shop formula, which the ORNL deck, Advantage Engineering and Plastics Technology's card all give:

tons = gpm × delta T / 24

Delta T is return temperature minus supply temperature. Conair rates chillers for a 10 F rise. Advantage Engineering lists about 2.4 gpm per ton, which is the same as a 10 F rise, and a minimum of 4.8 gpm per ton (a 5 F rise) for blow molding. If glycol is in the loop, the ORNL deck says to use the glycol's own density and specific heat instead of water's, so the 500 factor is for plain water and flow will differ with glycol. Low delta T on a mold is normal: Plastics Technology's mold cooling article says the rise across a single circuit can be as low as a degree or two, which is why temperature sensors must be accurate. The chilled water pumps and delta T lesson linked below explains the plant-side version.

Worked example: four presses of polypropylene

Everything here is our own arithmetic on stated assumptions, and you should replace them with your plant's data.

  1. Assumption: four presses each process 50 lb/hr of polypropylene, so 200 lb/hr in total. Our own number.
  2. Resin factor: Plastics Technology's resin card lists polypropylene at 35 lb/hr per ton for mold cooling. 200 / 35 = 5.71 tons. Equivalent heat: 12,000 / 35 = about 343 Btu per lb (our own conversion; Advantage Engineering uses the same idea, 30 lb/hr of HDPE per ton).
  3. Margin: Conair says to add 10 to 20 percent. Assumption: 20 percent.
  4. Supply temperature: assumption of 45 F leaving water, 5 F below the 50 F rating, so add 5 × 2 = 10 percent. At 45 F the glycol chart calls for 30 percent propylene glycol.
  5. Total factor: 1 + 0.20 + 0.10 = 1.30 (we add the percents, as Conair describes them). 5.71 × 1.30 = 7.43 tons, so we would look at a 7.5 ton unit.
  6. Flow at 10 F delta T (assumption): gpm = 7.5 × 24 / 10 = 18 gpm. Check: 18 × 500 × 10 = 90,000 Btu/hr, and 90,000 / 12,000 = 7.5 tons.
  7. If the delta T were only 5 F: gpm = 7.5 × 24 / 5 = 36 gpm. That is double the flow for the same tons, so a bigger pump and bigger pipe.

This leaves out hydraulic oil coolers, the machines' own cooling needs, and pump heat. Those are extra loads you would add. The glycol flow correction was not worked, because our sources do not give a specific heat for the mix.

For the machine-shop side, the machining reference method is spindle kW times duty fraction plus 20 to 30 percent margin. A 10 kW spindle at 80 percent duty is 8 kW, about 2.3 tons, so it recommends 3 tons.

Chiller water, tower water and free cooling

These are different loops. The ORNL deck separates chilled water, which carries cooling to the process, from condenser water, which carries heat to the cooling tower. Genemco notes that a tower-assisted chiller can be more efficient than an air-cooled one at large scale. See the dry cooler vs open tower lesson linked below for the heat rejection side. Our own reasoning: tower water follows outdoor wet bulb, so it is generally too warm to hold a cold mold at 45 F in warm weather, which is why the chiller sits between them.

Free cooling uses cold outdoor air to take a share of the load. An Interplas Insights article describes a free cooler in series with a chiller: when outside air is colder than the returning fluid, a three-way valve sends fluid through a coil, the chiller works less, and the compressor, the most energy-hungry part, can idle. It says savings can be up to 80 percent but depend on weather and load. A New York State Energy Research and Development Authority case study of a molding plant lists a waterside economizer, used for free cooling tied to outdoor air temperature, among its process cooling upgrades. Filtration and scale

Mold channels are small. A Moldmaking Technology product article names dirt, rust and scale as the contaminants that clog them, and says clogged lines lead to costly maintenance and downtime. An inline filter with an indicator tells you when to clean it. The ORNL deck says a rising approach temperature at the same load points to fouling, which costs efficiency. Practically, that means treated water, the right inhibited glycol and a strainer in front of each mold.

Safety and checks

Lock out and relieve pressure before you open a loop. Check glycol strength with a refractometer rather than guessing. Confirm manufacturer data for flow and temperature range before you change setpoints. Your plant procedures come first.

Field card: Process chillers

Tons = gpm × delta T / 24.

  • Water carries about 500 Btu/hr per gpm per degree F.
  • Ratings assume 50 F leaving water and a 10 F rise; about 2 percent more capacity per degree below 50 F.
  • Below about 48 F supply, use inhibited propylene glycol, never automotive antifreeze.
  • Portable: one use point, small tank. Central: many loads, big tank, process pump.
  • Free cooling works when outdoor air is colder than the return fluid.
  • Rising approach or falling flow can mean fouled or plugged passages.
