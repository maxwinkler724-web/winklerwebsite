Electric Floor-Warming Mats Under Tile

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thin electric mat under bathroom or kitchen tile takes the chill off a cold floor. It is a simple device, but it is buried in mortar for the life of the floor, so the checks that matter all happen before the tile is set. This lesson covers what the mat does, how it is sized and protected, the ohm checks you can ask the installer to record, and what to do when it quits.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Every mat family has its own wattage, resistance table, sensor values and limits. We read two maker installation manuals (cited as plain text below, no brands) to show how the pieces fit. The papers that come with your mat win over this page, and so do the electrical code and your local inspector. This is general education, not an installation guide.

Comfort heat, not primary heat

Most mats under tile are meant to warm the floor you stand on. They are usually a comfort feature. Electric resistance heat turns all of its electricity into heat at the point of use, which the U.S. Department of Energy calls 100 percent efficient there, but it also says electric heat is often more expensive to run than heat from combustion appliances, because of how power is made and delivered. So a small mat that runs a few hours a day is a fair use. A mat asked to heat a whole cold room all day is a costly one. If you want floor heat as the main system, see our lesson on hot-water radiant floors and floor coverings instead; this page stays with electric mats.

Watts per square foot

Output is printed as watts per square foot of floor. One mat manual we read rates its mat at 12 watts per square foot, within 10 percent. A cable-and-spacing manual from another maker lists three spacings: 2-inch at 18 watts per square foot, 3-inch at 12, and 4-inch at 9. The closer the cable runs, the more watts each square foot of floor gets. Multiply the watts per square foot by the heated area and you have the connected load. Only the area that will be uncovered floor counts. The manuals give clearances to keep from walls, floor drains, toilet rings and other heat sources.

The floor sensor and the dedicated thermostat

A thin wire sensor is buried in the same mortar as the cable and tells the thermostat the floor temperature. Both manuals place it in the middle of two runs of cable, not touching either one, and several inches into the heated area. One suggests setting it in a conduit so it can be pulled out and replaced if it fails. The thermostat is a dedicated unit for the mat, not an ordinary wall thermostat. One manual limits each thermostat to 15 amps of combined load, and both want the mat on its own circuit. One of them says to keep the floor setting at or below 86 F (30 C).

Why the ground-fault protection is built in

The cable is energized wire sitting in wet-area mortar under your feet. If a nail, trowel or crack ever nicks the insulation, current can leak to ground through the mortar or tile. A ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) compares the current going out with the current coming back and opens the circuit when they differ by about 5 milliamperes, in as little as 1/40 of a second, according to OSHA. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends GFCIs in kitchens and bathrooms, and tells owners to test them once a month. One mat manual calls for a dedicated 20-amp circuit with a Class A GFCI or GFCI breaker, and recommends a thermostat with a 5 mA GFCI built in for wet areas such as bathrooms and kitchens. The other says never to put the heating system on a non-GFCI circuit. The National Electrical Code, Article 424, covers fixed electric heating equipment, and the code also has its own GFCI rules for bathrooms and kitchens. Your electrician and inspector apply the edition adopted where you live.

Ohm checks to ask the installer to record

You can ask for a simple log, with a signature and date, of readings taken with the power off. The manuals describe three points in time: before the mat is unrolled, after it is laid and before any mortar, and after the finished floor. One manual asks for four, adding a reading after the mortar. There are two kinds of reading.

  • Resistance of the heating wire. The installer measures across the two heating leads and compares the number to the one printed on the mat tag. The two sources we read allow a tolerance of 5 percent in one case and 10 percent in the other, so the tag and the maker paper decide what is acceptable.
  • Insulation check. Each heating lead is measured to the ground wire. A plain meter should read open circuit, and the cable-and-spacing manual prefers a 1000 V insulation tester, which reads open circuit on a good cable. That manual lists the insulation test as optional for the warranty and says a plain ohmmeter test can miss high-resistance leaks. Any other reading goes to the maker before tile goes down. Our insulation resistance lesson explains the meter.

A reading that drifts between steps is the point of the log. It shows the mat was fine until the mortar went on, or that it was fine until the tile was set, and it tells you who to call.

Never cut the heating cable

Both manuals are blunt: do not cut the heating cable. A mat is made to a set length and resistance. Cutting it changes the resistance, which changes the watts, and a splice buried in mortar is a hot spot waiting to fail. Only the plain lead wire may be shortened, per one manual. To turn a mat at a corner, installers cut the mesh backing between cable runs and leave the cable whole. A damaged mat is replaced, not repaired. Mortar must also cover the cable fully and cure before power is applied; one manual says cure time is typically one to four weeks.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This example is ours. Every input is an assumption, not a published figure for any mat.

  1. Assumptions: a 40 square foot mat, 12 watts per square foot, 120 V supply, the thermostat powered for 6 hours a day, the floor reaching its set point so the mat is actually on 50 percent of that time, 30 days a month, and a rate of $0.18 per kWh.
  2. Watts: 40 sq ft x 12 W/sq ft = 480 W.
  3. Amps: 480 W / 120 V = 4.0 A. That is well under the 15 amp per thermostat limit in one manual.
  4. Expected resistance, for the ohm log: R = V squared / watts = 120 x 120 / 480 = 30 ohms. A 10 percent tolerance would be 27 to 33 ohms. Your mat tag has the real number.
  5. Hours at full power: 6 h x 0.50 = 3 h per day.
  6. Daily energy: 0.48 kW x 3 h = 1.44 kWh.
  7. Monthly energy: 1.44 kWh x 30 = 43.2 kWh.
  8. Monthly cost: 43.2 kWh x $0.18 = $7.78 (about $7.78).
  9. Heat delivered: 480 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = about 1,638 Btu/h while on.

Under those assumptions the mat costs about $8 a month, which fits a comfort feature. Run it 16 hours a day at the same duty cycle and it is about $21 a month. Your rate and run time will differ, so substitute your own numbers.

When a floor warmer will not heat a cold room

The 1,638 Btu/h above is small. Suppose, as another assumption, that the room loses 3,000 Btu/h on a design cold day. The mat covers about half of that at best, and its heat has to rise through the tile and warm the air first. A bathroom with an uninsulated slab edge, a cold exterior wall or a leaky window will stay cold while the floor feels pleasant. Insulation under the mat, such as the optional underlayment in the drawing, helps the heat go up instead of down. If the room is cold, fix the room (insulation, air leaks, the main heat source) rather than asking the mat to carry it.

If it stops working

  1. Thermostat display or GFCI tripped. A maker troubleshooting guide says to check for loose connections, reset the GFCI, and, if it trips again, suspect a short or a damaged mat and call the maker or an electrician. Do not keep resetting it. Do not cut into the floor yourself.
  2. Floor sensor error. One guide says a low-temperature error ("LO") means the sensor is loose or broken, so reseat its wires at the thermostat. If that fails, the sensor has a resistance range printed in the manual (the one we read gives about 8,000 to 17,000 ohms across 85 F to 55 F). A "HI" reading calls for checking that only one sensor is connected. A sensor set in a conduit can be pulled and replaced, which is why one manual suggests installing it that way.
  3. Breaker trips, no heat, or odd smell. Turn the breaker off and call a qualified electrician. Compare new readings with the log you asked for.
  4. Nothing happens and no error. Check the thermostat is on, the setpoint is above the floor temperature, and the dedicated breaker is on.

Field card: Electric Floor-Warming Mats Under Tile

Test before, during and after, and never cut the cable.

  • Comfort heat, not primary heat. Electric resistance is often costly to run (DOE).
  • Connected load = watts per square foot x heated area. Our example: 40 x 12 = 480 W = 4.0 A at 120 V.
  • Sensor sits midway between two cable runs, not touching them.
  • GFCI protection is required by the maker manuals we read; GFCIs open at about 5 mA.
  • Ask the installer to log power-off resistance and insulation readings at each stage.
  • Never cut or splice the heating cable. Replace a damaged mat.
  • Tripping GFCI or a dead floor: do not keep resetting. Call a qualified electrician.
