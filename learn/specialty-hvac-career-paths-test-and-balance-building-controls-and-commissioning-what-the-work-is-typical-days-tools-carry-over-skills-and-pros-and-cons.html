Specialty HVAC Careers: Test and Balance, Controls and Commissioning

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most people picture an HVAC career as either service calls or installs. A third group of jobs sits next to both: measuring and setting how much air and water a building system moves, programming and fixing the controls that run it, and proving that the finished system does what the design says. This lesson describes those three specialties in plain terms so you can see how field skills carry over and what each path asks of you.

How to read this page

The three paths overlap, and employers use different job titles for them. The day-to-day descriptions below are our own general picture, not a rule, and your own market may differ. For the broader residential, light commercial and facilities picture, see HVAC Career Tracks Compared, and for first-year basics see How to Become an HVAC Technician. We do not cover pay or credentials here.

Test and balance technician

The work. A test-and-balance technician measures how much air moves through ducts, diffusers and fans, and how much water moves through pumps, coils and balancing valves, then adjusts dampers, valves and fan or pump settings until the flows match the design. Then they write it up. One university master specification we read says each air terminal is to be adjusted to 100 to 110 percent of design airflow, and it asks for a report with nameplate data, flows and pressures, and any deficiencies found, along with an instrument list. The same document requires calibrated instruments and says balancing is done after the water side has been flushed, cleaned and filled.

A typical day (our own description). Read the drawings and the design flows, set up on a system, take readings with a flow hood, a pitot tube or a differential pressure meter, adjust, re-measure, and record. Many days involve ceiling tiles, ladders and rooftops. Our lesson on duct traverses with a pitot tube shows one of the core measurements.

What carries over from service. Reading airflow and static pressure, knowing what a dirty coil or a collapsed flex duct does, using meters properly, and finding the real cause when numbers do not add up. The federal labor statistics handbook entry for heating, air conditioning and refrigeration technicians lists testing and balancing as one specialty a technician may focus on.

Building controls technician

The work. Controls techs program, check and troubleshoot the automation system that starts equipment, reads sensors and decides what to do. That means controllers, temperature, pressure and humidity sensors, actuators, wiring, and the network that ties them together. The labor statistics entry for HVAC technicians notes that modern systems have computerized parts and networking features that technicians must install, repair and program. A national laboratory page about its own campus describes staff using a building controls portal to run functional tests that validate the sequence of operation, check automatic fault diagnostics and verify trends. For background, see our Building Automation Basics.

A typical day (our own description). Chase an alarm or comfort complaint, open the trend log, compare a sensor to a handheld reference, check wiring and network connections, edit a sequence or setpoint, and test the change. Some days are at a laptop; others are in a mechanical room or above a ceiling.

What carries over from service. Low-voltage wiring, meter work, understanding how a damper, valve or compressor should respond, and reading a wiring diagram. New skills to build are logic and programming concepts, network basics and careful documentation.

Commissioning work

The work. A federal energy page describes commissioning as a quality-assurance process used to verify that a building performs according to the original design and intent and meets the needs of owners and occupants. A federal commissioning guide for facilities describes planning, investigating (including developing functional test plans and analyzing results), implementing fixes with retesting, and handing off with final documentation. A second federal guidance document says functional performance tests ensure that systems perform the function for which they were designed, and that a commissioning report lists outstanding or non-compliance issues.

A typical day (our own description). Review the design intent and sequences, walk the pre-start checklists, run a functional test (force a condition, watch the response), log what passed and what did not, then follow up until each issue is closed. Our BAS commissioning checklists lesson shows what that looks like on the controls side.

What carries over from service. Broad system knowledge, patience with troubleshooting, and the ability to explain a problem clearly to other trades. Commissioning also leans on the other two specialties, because balanced flows and working controls are part of what gets verified.

Tools at a glance

  • Test and balance: flow hood, pitot tube and manometer, anemometer, tachometer, clamp-on ammeter, hydronic differential pressure meter, thermometers.
  • Controls: laptop with the system software, network and signal testers, multimeter, handheld reference sensors, a loop calibrator.
  • Commissioning: checklists, data loggers, the same meters, and a documentation habit.

How people usually move in

These are common patterns, not requirements. Test and balance often starts by assisting an experienced tech on reading and adjusting. Controls often opens up when a service tech becomes the one everyone calls about sensors, wiring and the automation front end. Commissioning often draws people who already have field experience in several systems plus strong documentation habits. In each case, a few years of field experience in service or install is a common starting point.

Worked example (our own): a balancing tech's proportional check

Assumptions (ours, not from a real job): a branch has a design airflow of 400 cfm, and the tech's reading after setup is 360 cfm. A separate diffuser has a design of 150 cfm and is found at 95 percent of design.

  1. Branch percent of design = measured / design x 100 = 360 / 400 x 100 = 0.90 x 100 = 90 percent.
  2. Shortfall = 400 - 360 = 40 cfm, which is 10 percent of design.
  3. Against the 100 to 110 percent band in the university specification above, 90 percent is outside the band, so the tech opens the damper or looks for a restriction, then re-measures.
  4. Diffuser airflow = 0.95 x 150 = 142.5 cfm, which is 7.5 cfm short, and also below the 100 percent low end of that band.
  5. Check: 142.5 / 150 = 0.95. Check: 360 / 400 = 0.90.

Because ducts share a fan, raising one branch lowers the others, so the tech repeats the check across the system. The real tolerance on a job comes from its own specification.

Pros and cons

PathProsCons
Test and balanceClear measurable results; you see many building types; strong report-writing recordPhysical access work; depends on construction schedules; repetitive readings
Building controlsProblem-solving and logic; more desk and laptop time; skills fit modern systemsSteep learning of software and networks; on-call alarms; systems change often
CommissioningBig-picture view; teaches how whole systems fit; varied sitesHeavy paperwork; you find problems others made; travel and schedule pressure

These pros and cons are our own summary and will vary by employer.

Field card: Specialty HVAC Careers

Measure it, control it, prove it.

  • Test and balance: measure and set air and water flow to design, then report.
  • Controls: program, wire and troubleshoot sensors, controllers and networks.
  • Commissioning: verify with functional tests that systems do what the design says.
  • Percent of design = measured / design x 100. Example: 360 / 400 = 90 percent.
  • Field service skills carry into all three; add reports, logic or documentation.
