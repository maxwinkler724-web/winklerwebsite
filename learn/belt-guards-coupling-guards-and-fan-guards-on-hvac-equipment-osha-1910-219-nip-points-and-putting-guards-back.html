Belt, Coupling and Fan Guards on HVAC Equipment: OSHA 1910.219, Nip Points and Putting Guards Back

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A belt guard is the cheapest injury prevention on a rooftop or in a fan room, and it is the first thing that ends up leaning against the wall during a belt change. This lesson covers what the federal machine guarding rules ask of belt, shaft, coupling and fan guards, where the nip points are, and the order that gets the guard back on before anyone presses start.

Check your rules

Check your rules. This page reads the federal general industry standards for education. Your employer, your state plan and the equipment maker may ask for more. Follow the written procedure for the site you are on.

Two standards, two jobs

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 is the general machine guarding rule. It says guards must protect operators and other people in the machine area from hazards that include ingoing nip points and rotating parts, and that a guard must not be an accident hazard itself. A guard with a sharp edge or a loose corner that catches a sleeve fails that test. The same section, paragraph (a)(5), covers exposed fan blades: if the blade edge is less than 7 feet above the floor or working level, the blades must be guarded, and the guard openings may be no wider than one-half inch. OSHA instruction STD 1-12.1 and a 2003 interpretation letter both repeat the one-half inch limit. The letter applies it to cooling fans and fan blades in commercial air handling units at that height, and the instruction accepts concentric rings spaced no more than one-half inch apart if there are enough radial spokes and firm mounts to keep the guard from being pushed into the blades.

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.219 is the specific rule for mechanical power transmission: flywheels, shafts, pulleys, belts, gears, chains, collars and couplings. It sets the 7 foot line used on this page.

What 1910.219 asks for

  • Shafts. Exposed parts of horizontal shafting 7 feet or less from the floor or a working platform must be enclosed by a stationary casing or trough (paragraph c). Vertical and inclined shafts within 7 feet are enclosed too. Shaft ends may not stick out more than half the shaft diameter unless they have a nonrotating cap or safety sleeve, and unused keyways are filled or covered.
  • Pulleys. Pulleys with any part 7 feet or less from the floor or platform are guarded (paragraph d). A cracked or broken rim means the pulley is not used.
  • Belts. Where both runs of a horizontal belt are 7 feet or less from the floor, the guard reaches at least 15 inches above the belt or a standard height, and a belt with both runs 42 inches or less from the floor is fully enclosed (paragraph e). Vertical and inclined belts are enclosed with a guard. OSHA's directive on the words "enclosed" and "fully enclosed" says a belt that is guarded by its location, so nobody is exposed, needs no enclosure, and that for vertical and inclined belts only the portion 7 feet or less from the floor must be enclosed.
  • Small-belt exception. The standard leaves out some narrow belts running 250 feet per minute or less, such as single-strand V-belts up to 13/32 inch wide (paragraph a). Do not assume a belt qualifies; check the text and the actual belt speed.
  • Setscrews and keys. Projecting keys and setscrews on revolving parts are removed, made flush or covered, with exceptions for parts inside enclosures (paragraph h).
  • Couplings. A shaft coupling may not present a hazard from bolts, nuts, setscrews or the turning surface; bolts and nuts are acceptable if covered by safety sleeves or if they sit parallel to the shaft and do not extend past the flange (paragraph i). In practice that means a coupling on a pump or fan drive gets a guard over it, and the guard stays on when the motor is run.
  • Guard construction. Standard guards are expanded or perforated metal, wire mesh on an angle-iron frame, or solid sheet, free of burrs and sharp edges, and braced every 3 feet of height to a fixed part of the machine or building (paragraphs m and o).
  • Care of equipment. Power transmission equipment is inspected at intervals not exceeding 60 days, and pulleys are kept aligned so belts do not run off (paragraph p).

Nip points of belts and sheaves

OSHA's woodworking eTool describes in-running nip points, also called pinch points, as the places where parts move toward each other or where a moving part passes a stationary object. Belts and chains meeting pulleys or gears are named as examples. A hand, glove or sleeve is pulled in and crushed. On a belt drive the dangerous spots are the two places where the belt runs onto a sheave: one on the driver and one on the driven sheave, on opposite runs. Reverse the motor and the nip points swap, so treat every belt-to-sheave contact as a nip point. See Belt Drives and Sheave Basics for how the sheaves and belt work together.

Why guards come off, and why they go back on

A belt change, sheave swap or alignment check cannot be done through a closed guard, so the guard comes off. OSHA's lockout standard, 1910.147, applies to servicing when a person has to remove or bypass a guard or safety device. That is the rule behind a simple habit: lock out first, then take the guard off. The standard's sequence is to prepare, shut down in an orderly way, isolate the energy, apply the lock, relieve stored or residual energy, and verify the equipment is isolated. For the verify step on a motor, see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Stored energy on a fan matters here: a wheel can keep coasting or windmill from airflow after the motor is off, so restrain it before reaching in.

Before energy is restored, 1910.147(e) asks the authorized person to inspect the work area, remove nonessential items, confirm components are operationally intact, make sure everyone is out of harm's way and tell affected people. If the machine must be energized briefly for testing, paragraph (f)(1) gives the order: clear tools and materials, clear people, remove the locks, energize and test, then de-energize and reapply control measures before continuing. The guard is a component. A machine with its guard on the floor is not operationally intact.

Fan inlet guards, plenum fans and cooling towers

A plenum fan sits inside a cabinet or plenum, so it is protected by the housing, access doors and inlet screens. Those doors and screens are the guard. Do not run the fan with the door off or the inlet screen out. A cooling tower fan has the same issue at the fan deck and fan stack, and its driveshaft and coupling need their own guards. The fan blade rule in 1910.212(a)(5) uses the 7 foot line and the one-half inch opening, as described above. For the mechanical side of tower drives, see Cooling Tower Fan Drives, and for fan types, Centrifugal Fan Types. For glove choices around moving equipment, see Work Gloves for HVAC Techs.

Worked example (ours): belt change on an air handler with a 10 hp motor

This example is our own. We assume a 10 hp motor on a belt drive on a single air handler, with a shaft centerline mounted on a platform. Every dimension below is an assumption we chose to show the arithmetic; the maker's manual and your site procedure control the real job.

  1. Plan. Read the maker's belt change steps and note who must be told. Identify every energy source: the motor disconnect, any control power, and the fan wheel itself.
  2. Shut down and isolate. Stop the unit by its normal controls, open and lock the disconnect, and tag it.
  3. Verify. Test for voltage at the motor side of the disconnect with a meter you checked on a known source. Try the start control to confirm nothing moves.
  4. Restrain the wheel. Hold the fan wheel from turning on its own, per the maker's method, before reaching into the drive.
  5. Remove the guard. Keep the fasteners together and set the guard where it will not fall or be walked on.
  6. Change the belt. Back off the motor base, slip the belts off without prying over the sheave, inspect the sheaves for wear and rim damage, fit the new set, and tension and align per the maker.
  7. Check the 7 foot rule. Assume the exposed horizontal shaft is 6 feet above the platform. The rule applies to horizontal shafting 7 feet or less from the floor or platform. Arithmetic: 7 ft minus 6 ft = 1 ft, so the shaft is 12 inches inside the line and its exposed part needs a stationary casing or trough. A shaft at 7.5 feet would be above the line; a shaft at exactly 7 feet is still covered, because the wording is "7 feet or less." We also assume the sheaves are at or below the 7 foot line, so they stay inside the guard.
  8. Put the guard back. Every fastener in place, no sharp edges, no gaps that let a hand reach a nip point, and the guard rigid when you push on it.
  9. Clear the area. Remove tools and rags, check that the doors, screens and coupling or shaft guards are in place and that nobody is in the fan section.
  10. Remove your lock and test run. Tell affected people, remove your lock and tag, energize, and watch and listen from outside the guard for belt tracking and noise. If you must make another adjustment, shut down, lock out again and verify before the guard comes off.

This example gives the order; belt tension, sheave alignment limits and restraint method come from the equipment maker and are not stated here.

What a compliant guard looks like on the roof

  • It covers both sheaves and both belt runs, with no gap on any side where a hand can reach a nip point.
  • It is made of standard guard material with smooth edges, firmly braced, and does not rub the belt or sheave.
  • Fan guards have openings of one-half inch or less where the 7 foot rule applies.
  • It is fastened with the right fasteners, not tape or a single screw.

Field card: Belt, Coupling and Fan Guards

Lock out, verify, then pull the guard. Guard on before the lock comes off.

  • 1910.212(a)(5): fan blades under 7 feet from floor or working level are guarded, openings one-half inch or less.
  • 1910.219: shafts, pulleys, belts, chains and clutches 7 feet or less from the floor or platform are guarded.
  • Couplings: no exposed bolts, nuts or setscrews that can catch.
  • Nip points are where a belt runs onto a sheave. Treat both as live.
  • 1910.147 covers servicing that needs a guard removed.
  • No guard, no start. A removed guard is a lockout reason, not a shortcut.
