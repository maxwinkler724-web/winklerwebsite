Indoor Firing Range Ventilation: Lead Exposure, Airflow at the Firing Line, HEPA Exhaust and Filter Change Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An indoor firing range is a ventilation problem with a health goal: keep lead dust moving away from the people at the firing line and capture it before it reaches anyone else. This lesson explains the layout and numbers that NIOSH publishes, the OSHA lead limits, and the safe way to service the filters. It takes no position on firearms. It is about air.

Check the authority first

Check the authority. Range design is usually done by an engineer who follows the owner's safety program and local rules. This page is general education built from federal documents read in 2026. It is not design advice and not legal advice.

Where the lead comes from

NIOSH says impact with the bullet trap can generate lead dust, and dust that settles on floors and surfaces gets stirred up during cleaning. Ventilation is the main engineering control, but it works together with housekeeping, clothing and hygiene practices.

The airflow plan in plain terms

The idea is a slow, even "piston" of clean air that sweeps from behind the shooters, past the firing line and down range toward the bullet trap, where it is exhausted. Shooters stay upstream of the contamination.

  • Supply behind the shooters. NIOSH says to introduce supply air as far up range (toward the shooters' end) as possible, spread evenly from floor to ceiling and wall to wall. A perforated wall plenum has been shown to give uniform distribution at the firing line, and perforated radial diffusers mounted at ceiling height have been tested and shown effective. Diffusers that throw jets can create turbulence at the firing line. Nothing, such as target carriers or ammunition cabinets, should block the path between the supply air and the firing line.
  • Velocity at the firing line. NIOSH recommends a minimum of 50 feet per minute (fpm) and no more than 75 fpm along the firing line. If fall-out downrange is a concern, it also gives a minimum of 30 fpm downrange, evenly distributed.
  • Exhaust at or behind the bullet trap. That pulls the air, and the dust the trap generates, away from the shooters.
  • Slight negative pressure. NIOSH says exhaust should always exceed supply so the range stays under negative pressure. It gives no pressure number. It says the pressure should be just enough to produce inflow at openings such as doors and windows, which can be checked with a chemical smoke tube. For a general look at pressure relationships, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms.
  • Separate system. NIOSH says the range ventilation should be separate from the rest of the building's ventilation.

HEPA exhaust and filter change safety

NIOSH says the minimum exhaust filtration is HEPA or a MERV 18 to 19 rating. A true HEPA filter is certified to capture at least 99.97% of particles at 0.3 micron. NIOSH also calls for gaskets on the sides and face to prevent bypass, and for the filters to sit upstream of the exhaust fan. For how HEPA fits into other facilities, see Cleanroom HVAC, ISO Classes and HEPA Filters, and for general loading and pressure drop see One-Inch Filters vs Four-Inch Media Cabinets.

What is specific to a range:

  • Pre-filters load fastest, so they get changed more often than the HEPA stage. Change filters by the manufacturer's pressure-drop guidance, and check gauges regularly.
  • NIOSH says filter changes should be done by people trained in lead safety, wearing proper protective equipment. A loaded filter is full of lead, so treat it as hazardous until a waste test (TCLP) says otherwise.
  • NIOSH suggests interlocking filter access doors with the fans, and running the fans together.
  • The OSHA lead standard bars cleaning with compressed air and bars removing lead from clothing by blowing or shaking. Do not blow out, tap or shake a filter.
  • Housekeeping uses wet methods and an explosion-proof HEPA vacuum. NIOSH says dry sweeping should never be used in the range.

The same lead warning applies to the bullet trap: NIOSH says trap cleanup calls for NIOSH-certified respirators and full outer protective clothing. For the paint and old-boiler version of this hazard, see Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems.

OSHA lead limits

The general industry lead standard, 29 CFR 1910.1025 on eCFR, sets a permissible exposure limit (PEL) of 50 micrograms of lead per cubic meter of air as an 8-hour time-weighted average. The action level is 30 micrograms per cubic meter, which starts monitoring, medical surveillance and training duties. For shifts longer than 8 hours the limit is reduced by a formula in the standard. Its change room, shower and protective clothing sections also apply. NIOSH adds that without automatic monitoring, the range must measure ventilation effectiveness every 3 months and again within 5 days after any change.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Everything below is our own arithmetic. Only the 50 and 75 fpm limits and the 2,500 to 3,000 fpm duct velocities come from NIOSH. The room size, the supply fraction and the temperatures are assumptions we chose.

  1. Assumed range cross-section at the firing line: 20 ft wide by 10 ft high = 200 sq ft.
  2. Exhaust at 50 fpm: 200 sq ft x 50 fpm = 10,000 cfm. At 75 fpm: 200 x 75 = 15,000 cfm. So the NIOSH window is 10,000 to 15,000 cfm for this room.
  3. Supply (assumed 90% of exhaust, to keep the range negative): 0.90 x 10,000 = 9,000 cfm. The other 1,000 cfm enters as inflow through openings. At the high end: 0.90 x 15,000 = 13,500 cfm.
  4. Exhaust duct size: at 3,000 fpm, 10,000 cfm / 3,000 fpm = 3.3 sq ft of duct. At 2,500 fpm, 10,000 / 2,500 = 4.0 sq ft.
  5. Winter heating load for the supply air (assumed outdoor 10°F, supply heated to 65°F, so delta T = 55°F). Use Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T (see the heat transfer formulas lesson).
    At 50 fpm: 1.08 x 9,000 x 55 = 534,600 Btu/h.
    At 75 fpm: 1.08 x 13,500 x 55 = 801,900 Btu/h.

So even a modest 200 sq ft room needs over half a million Btu/h just to warm the air passing through it. This is a sensible-heat estimate only and ignores the building shell and equipment. Never size equipment from a study example.

Field card: Indoor Firing Range Ventilation

Clean air behind the shooters, negative pressure, HEPA at the exhaust.

  • NIOSH: 50 fpm minimum, 75 fpm maximum at the firing line; 30 fpm minimum down range if fall-out is a concern.
  • Supply high and far up range through a perforated wall or ceiling radial diffusers; no jets, no obstructions.
  • Exhaust at or behind the bullet trap; exhaust greater than supply; smoke tube at doors.
  • HEPA (or MERV 18 to 19) upstream of the fan with gasketed frames.
  • Filter changes by lead-trained staff with PPE; no compressed air, no shaking; treat loaded filters as hazardous waste until tested.
  • OSHA lead PEL 50 micrograms per cubic meter (8-hour average); action level 30.
  • Shower and change clothes before going home.

Educational summary only. Follow the range's safety program and local rules. Print this page to keep the card.

Take-home lead and family exposure

CDC's NIOSH guidance for families says children are the main concern, because lead dust carried home on clothes, shoes and hair can poison them. Its steps include taking work shoes off outside the home and storing them in a sealed bag, showering as soon as you get home if you cannot shower at work, wet-cleaning hard floors, using a HEPA vacuum on carpets and fabric, and telling a health care provider about the work, especially during pregnancy. NIOSH's range Alert adds that reusable outer clothing should be laundered by the employer, not at home, and that workers should keep street clothes and work clothes in separate lockers. The Alert also describes a case where carpet lead in student dorm rooms averaged 214 micrograms per gram versus 65 in other rooms, showing how far dust travels.
