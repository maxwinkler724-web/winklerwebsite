Boiler Feed Pumps and Condensate Receivers: Sizing, Pump NPSH, Hot Condensate and Vacuum Return Units

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler feed pump that rattles like gravel is usually pumping water that wants to be steam. This lesson covers what the receiver and pump are for, how makers size them, why near-boiling condensate flashes at the pump inlet, and where vacuum return units and pumping traps fit.

Transfer units and boiler feed units

Both are a receiver tank with one or more pumps, but they answer to different controls. Xylem's Domestic Pump application manual (TES-676B) says a condensate unit feeds the boiler from a float on its own receiver, while a boiler feed unit feeds the boiler from a level switch on the boiler. A condensate transfer unit only moves condensate from a low return to the boiler room or to a feed unit. The same manual calls the boiler feed unit preferable for all systems and says condensate units are practical only up to about 8,000 sq ft of equivalent direct radiation (EDR), because return surges can flood a boiler fed from a receiver float. Xylem's steam guide says to use a feed unit on boilers over 300,000 Btu/h. See Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics for the bigger picture of why condensate comes back at all.

How receiver size ties to steaming rate and return time

The receiver is a buffer. When the boiler draws water, condensate may still be on its way back through the piping. The receiver holds the difference. TES-676B gives these net storage figures for boiler feed receivers: five minutes for systems up to 30,000 sq ft EDR or about 200 boiler horsepower, at least ten minutes for larger ones, and at least fifteen for large single-story buildings or campuses where the return is slow. Xylem's other steam guide gives 10 to 20 minutes, with 10 as the minimum, and 15 or 20 for single-level buildings spread over a large area. A Bell & Gossett feed unit brochure rates its receivers on 10 minutes. TES-676B warns that an undersized feed receiver can overflow with returned condensate; a companion Xylem manual (TES-375B) notes this happens at shutdown, when everything left in the piping drains back.

Pump capacity: the multiplier

One boiler horsepower is the evaporation of 34.5 lb of water per hour at 212 F (TES-676B; the brochure uses the same figure). Makers size the feed pump above that rate. TES-676B lists 1.5 times boiler load for continuous duty, 2 times for intermittent duty, and 3 times where cavitation is a risk. The brochure sizes pumps for 2 times the system return rate. TES-375B says older standards used 3 times and newer units are generally rated at 2 times.

Feed pump control

On-off from boiler level. A level switch starts the pump at a low water level and stops it at a higher one. TES-676B says a boiler-mounted switch holds level within about one inch. Spirax Sarco's level-control guide points out the cost: cold feedwater arriving in bursts drops boiler pressure, and in its example with 80 C feedwater the burner may need to fire 40 percent higher with the pump on than off.

Continuous with a feedwater valve. The pump runs all the time and an automatic valve between pump and boiler meters the flow to match steam demand. Spirax Sarco says this smooths steam flow and level, and that a recirculation line keeps minimum flow through the pump. It costs more and is mainly seen on larger boilers.

Why hot condensate flashes in the suction

Water at 212 F is right at its boiling point at atmospheric pressure. Any drop in pressure turns some of it to vapor. TES-676B says cavitation is hot water flashing into steam as it enters the impeller. TLV's explainer adds that cavitation starts once available NPSH falls below the pump's required NPSH, and that hotter condensate and faster pumps make it worse. An air vent will not fix it, because the vapor forms inside the pump. For the math, see Pump NPSH Available vs Required and Pump Cavitation Basics.

Maker data gives usable limits. Pumps needing only 2 ft of NPSH can handle condensate at 210 F with the receiver at floor level, or 212 F with the receiver raised 2 ft (TES-375B). TES-676B's selection guide lists 200 F for its Type CC units, 210 F for CB and 212 F for CBE, and recommends a low-NPSH design above 195 F. The same guide lists only 160 F for its vacuum return unit models, and Bell & Gossett's VL manual says rated capacity assumes condensate no hotter than 160 F. Those are product limits, so read your nameplate and literature.

The discharge side matters too. TES-676B says a leaking discharge check can drain the riser, and when the pump restarts, steam pockets collapse and cause water hammer. Its table shows how much pressure is needed to keep condensate liquid: 0 ft for 212 F, 7.5 ft for 200 F, 24.3 ft for 155 F. See Flash Steam and Flash Tanks for what happens when hot condensate drops in pressure, and Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics for what happens to the gases.

Seals, vents and overflows

  • Seals. Bell & Gossett's VL manual says its units use mechanical shaft seals, warns not to run them dry, and asks for regular leak inspection. TES-676B says a pump left in a continuous "hand" position can run dry, and that boiler chemicals fed ahead of the suction shorten seal life, so treatment should not go into the receiver.
  • Vent. TES-676B wants the receiver vent the full size of the receiver connection, piped outdoors, so the tank never pressurizes. The VL manual says not to restrict a vent or run it over 20 ft high unless an overflow is provided.
  • Overflow. TES-676B says the overflow acts as a secondary vent and drains condensate if a pump fails or the make-up valve sticks. The VL manual says never plug it.

Vacuum return units

Older vacuum heating systems keep the return piping below atmospheric pressure so steam moves through radiators and air is pulled out. TES-676B describes a VLR unit, one pump handling both air and water, for systems up to about 20,000 sq ft EDR; above that it suggests separate air and water pumps. Another type combines a vacuum producer with a boiler feed unit, so the receiver stays under vacuum and some deaeration occurs. TES-375B says vacuum pumps are chosen on 5-1/2 in. Hg at 160 F and usually cut in at 3 in. Hg and out at 8 in. Hg. The VL manual gives the same switch settings for its lead pump. Lower pressure also lowers the boiling point, so a hot return is more likely to flash in a vacuum unit.

Pumping traps as an alternative

A pressure-powered pump, often called a pumping trap, uses motive steam or compressed air instead of a motor, so there is no impeller to cavitate. TLV says such a pump can remove the cavitation problem entirely. Spirax Sarco's sizing sheet says capacity rises with filling head, from about 0.7 of rated flow with no filling head to 1.0 at 6 in., 1.3 at 24 in. and 1.5 at 36 in., and that it needs a vented receiver above it plus the correct check valves. It computes back pressure as lift in feet times 0.433, plus pressure in the return line.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own example, not a design. Assumptions: a 100 BHP boiler running at full output (our assumption), intermittent-duty pump, and 500 lb/h of water per gpm, which is the 2 gpm per 1,000 lb/h conversion in TES-676B. Real water density changes with temperature, so a designer would check it.

  1. Evaporation rate: 100 BHP x 34.5 lb/h per BHP = 3,450 lb/h.
  2. Pump at 2 times (intermittent duty, TES-676B): 2 x 3,450 = 6,900 lb/h. In gpm: 6,900 / 500 = 13.8 gpm. At 1.5 times (continuous) it would be 5,175 lb/h, or 10.35 gpm.
  3. Boiler flow in gpm: 3,450 / 500 = 6.9 gpm.
  4. Receiver net storage at 5 minutes (TES-676B, up to 200 BHP): 6.9 x 5 = 34.5 gal. At the brochure's 10 minutes: 6.9 x 10 = 69 gal.

The tank must be bigger than the net figure, because the vent, overflow and inlet sit above the normal working level. Treat this as a check on the maker's selection table, not a substitute for it.

Field card: Boiler Feed Pumps and Condensate Receivers

Receiver buffers return lag; pump needs low NPSH for hot water.

  • Boiler feed unit: level switch on the boiler. Condensate unit: float on its receiver.
  • 1 BHP = 34.5 lb/h. Feed pump 1.5x continuous, 2x intermittent, 3x if cavitation risk (one maker).
  • Receiver: about 5 min up to 200 BHP, 10 min or more above (one maker); 10 to 20 min in another guide.
  • Hot water near 212 F needs a 2 ft NPSH pump and a flooded suction; vacuum units are rated around 160 F.
  • Vent full size, never plug the overflow, never run seals dry.
  • Pumping trap: no impeller, so no impeller cavitation.
