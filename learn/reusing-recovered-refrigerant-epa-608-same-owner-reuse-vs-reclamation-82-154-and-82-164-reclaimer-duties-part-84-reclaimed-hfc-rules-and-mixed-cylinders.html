Reusing Recovered Refrigerant: When It Can Go Back and When It Must Be Reclaimed

Last updated: 2026-11-03

You have a cylinder of refrigerant you just pulled out of a system. Can it go back in? Can it go into someone else's system? The federal answer turns on one question: does the refrigerant stay with the same owner, or does it change hands? This lesson walks through that split, what a reclaimer has to do, and why a mixed cylinder is such a problem.

Check the current rules. This page is general education built from federal regulation text and EPA pages read in 2026, not legal advice. Rules change. Confirm the current wording on eCFR and EPA before you rely on a detail, and ask your employer's compliance contact about gray areas.

Three words, one decision

Recover, recycle and reclaim are defined in 40 CFR 82.152. Recovering means taking refrigerant out of an appliance in any condition and storing it in an external container, with no testing or processing required. Recycling means cleaning it, typically by separating oil and passing it through filters, so it can be reused in equipment of the same owner. Reclaiming means reprocessing it to all of the specifications in Appendix A to subpart F and proving that with the lab methods in that appendix. Our recover, recycle and reclaim lesson covers the vocabulary; here we focus on the rules that decide which one a job needs.

Same owner: the refrigerant may go back

Paragraph (h) of 40 CFR 82.156 says refrigerant may be returned to the appliance it was recovered from, or to another appliance owned by the same person, without being recycled or reclaimed. The one carve-out is motor vehicle air conditioners and similar appliances, which have their own rules and are outside this lesson.

Notice what that paragraph does not say. It sets no purity test and does not require lab work. It is a permission about ownership, not a promise that the gas is good, and the equipment maker's instructions still apply. Our lesson on mixed and contaminated refrigerant shows how to check a cylinder before you trust it.

New owner: it must be reclaimed first

The other side of the line is 40 CFR 82.154(d), titled sale of used refrigerant. It says no one may sell, distribute, or offer to sell or distribute a class I or class II substance, or a non-exempt substitute, for use as a refrigerant if it consists wholly or partly of used refrigerant. The main exception is refrigerant that has been reclaimed by a person certified as a reclaimer under 40 CFR 82.164. The same paragraph lists a few narrow exceptions: used refrigerant from and for motor vehicle air conditioners that is recycled under the separate subpart for them, refrigerant already sealed in an appliance sold with a fully assembled circuit, transfers between a parent company and its subsidiaries, and transfers between a federal agency and facilities that agency owns.

Note that "distribute" is in the rule, so giving gas away does not escape it. Our practical test: if the gas leaves one owner's equipment and ends up in a different owner's equipment, a certified reclaimer comes in between.

What reclaiming actually requires

The purity specification is in the appendix titled Appendix A to subpart F of part 82. We will not reproduce its tables, but the parameters it covers show why this is a lab job and not a filter job:

  • Moisture, measured in the lab with a coulometric titration method on a liquid-phase sample.
  • High-boiling residue, by a volume or weight method.
  • Particulates, where visible dirt or rust is a failure.
  • Acidity, by titration.
  • Non-condensables such as air, by gas chromatography.
  • Other volatile impurities, including other refrigerants: not more than 0.5 percent by weight, also by gas chromatography.
  • Chlorides, by a turbidity pass or fail test.

What a reclaimer must do

Under 40 CFR 82.164(a), anyone reclaiming used refrigerant for sale to a new owner must meet the Appendix A specifications, verify every batch with the appendix methods, release no more than 1.5 percent of the refrigerant during reclamation, and dispose of reclamation waste lawfully. The owner or a responsible officer must certify to EPA that the company will meet those requirements, naming the company and listing its reclamation and analysis equipment (82.164(b)). Certifications cannot be transferred (82.164(c)). Violations can lead to suspension or revocation (82.164(e)).

Reclaimers also keep and report numbers (82.164(d)). They keep batch-by-batch analysis results, and they keep records of who sent them material and how much, by refrigerant type, for three years. By February 1 each year they report to EPA the previous year's total material received, the total of each refrigerant reclaimed, and the total mass of waste. See our lesson on 608 records for what you keep on your side.

Newer HFC rules: reclaimed refrigerant for certain servicing

EPA's 2024 Emissions Reduction and Reclamation rule added a second layer for HFCs in 40 CFR part 84, subpart C. Section 84.112 reads, in summary:

  • January 1, 2026: no one may sell, identify or report refrigerant as reclaimed if its regulated-substance part contains more than 15 percent virgin material by weight. Certified reclaimers must label the containers they fill with reclaimed substances and create an electronic certification record for each container, kept three years.
  • January 1, 2029: servicing or repairing equipment in three subsectors, supermarket systems, refrigerated transport and automatic commercial ice makers, must use reclaimed refrigerant meeting those standards.
  • February 14, 2027 and February 14, 2028: reclaimers, distributors and wholesalers selling reclaimed refrigerant for those uses must report to EPA, covering calendar years 2026 and 2027.

EPA's fact sheet and reporting page match these dates. This lesson does not try to say how the 2029 servicing requirement treats refrigerant returned to the same system it came from. Read section 84.112 and EPA's frequent questions page before relying on any shortcut in those three subsectors. EPA's rule page also notes a May 2026 proposal about leak repair for some transport units; it is a proposal, and it is about leak repair, not about the dates above.

Why mixed or contaminated refrigerant is a problem

  • It fails the specification. Over 0.5 percent of another volatile substance fails the Appendix A limit, and air above the non-condensable limit fails too. A cylinder that fails cannot be sold as reclaimed.
  • It cannot be separated easily. A reclaimer can filter and distill some contaminants, but splitting two refrigerants is hard, so mixed material is often handled as waste. Some reclaimers refuse it or set minimum quantities, so ask about intake terms.
  • It cannot be vented. Under 82.154(a)(3), knowingly releasing refrigerant after it was recovered is a violation of the venting prohibition. Cleaning up your own mistake by opening the valve is not allowed. See our venting prohibition lesson.
  • It harms the next system. Air, moisture, acid and the wrong gas shorten equipment life and change pressures and capacity. That is general trade knowledge, not a quote from the rule.

The habits that prevent it are the dull ones: one refrigerant per recovery cylinder, labeled before you connect, hoses purged, and a record of what went in. Cylinder choice matters too; see refillable and recovery cylinders and disposable cylinders.

The decision path

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions, all ours and invented for practice: a company owns a small office building with one old rooftop unit and one newer one, both charged with the same HFC refrigerant. A contractor has a tech with 608 certification on each job, uses a recovery machine, and keeps one labeled recovery cylinder per refrigerant. Nothing here involves supermarket systems, refrigerated transport or ice makers.

Job 1: same-owner rooftop swap. The owner is replacing the old rooftop unit with a new one built for the same refrigerant, and a second unit on the same roof is low on charge. The tech recovers the old unit's charge into a labeled cylinder. Because both units belong to the same owner, 82.156(h) allows the refrigerant to go into either of the owner's appliances without recycling or reclaiming. Our caution: check the cylinder first, follow the new unit's installation instructions, and record the pounds in and out. If the new unit used a different refrigerant, this gas is not an option for it.

Job 2: a customer's gas on another customer's system. Another customer's system is low, and the contractor, out of virgin refrigerant, offers to use the clean-looking gas recovered earlier from a different customer's unit. That is a change of ownership. Under 82.154(d) the contractor may not sell, distribute or offer that used refrigerant for use as a refrigerant unless a certified reclaimer has reclaimed it. Charging it into the second customer's system and billing for service would be offering it for use. What is allowed: send the cylinder to a certified reclaimer, then buy reclaimed or virgin refrigerant for the second job.

Job 3: a mixed cylinder. A recovery cylinder labeled with the same HFC reads about 30 psi above the pressure-temperature chart after sitting a full day, and an identifier shows another refrigerant mixed in. A mixed cylinder cannot be fixed on site, since the Appendix A limit on other volatile impurities is 0.5 percent by weight. It cannot be vented (82.154(a)(3)). It should not be put back even in the owner's own equipment, which is our judgment, because 82.156(h) says nothing about quality and the gas would degrade the system. What is allowed: keep it labeled and separate and ask a reclaimer whether they accept mixed material, or arrange destruction. Expect extra fees.

Field card: Reusing Recovered Refrigerant

Same owner may reuse. New owner means reclaim first. Mixed means reclaimer or destruction.

  • 82.156(h): recovered refrigerant may go back to its own appliance or another appliance of the same owner without recycling or reclaiming.
  • 82.154(d): selling, distributing or offering used refrigerant for use as refrigerant requires reclamation by a certified reclaimer, with a few narrow exceptions.
  • Appendix A to subpart F: lab-verified purity, including 0.5 percent or less of other volatile impurities by weight.
  • Reclaimers: test each batch, release 1.5 percent or less, keep records, report to EPA by February 1.
  • Part 84 (HFCs): 15 percent virgin limit for reclaimed material from January 1, 2026; reclaimed refrigerant for servicing three subsectors from January 1, 2029.
  • Never vent recovered refrigerant. One refrigerant per cylinder, labeled.
