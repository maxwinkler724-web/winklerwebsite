Tracking Your Own Job Times as a New HVAC Tech

Last updated: 2026-11-04

New techs are often told to "be faster" without being told what fast looks like. A simple log of your own start and finish times gives you something better than a guess: your own numbers, a plan for the day that holds up, and clear questions to bring to a senior tech.

Why bother timing yourself

The federal Occupational Outlook Handbook says HVAC technicians typically have a lengthy period of on-the-job training, usually working alongside experienced technicians, and that they take on harder tasks over time. It also says these workers keep records of their work. Timing your tasks is one more record, and it is the one that shows your own progress. It is not for proving anything to anyone. It is for you.

A log answers three practical questions. How long does a typical task really take me right now? How much does that vary? And is the task I dread actually slow, or does it only feel slow? Feelings about time are unreliable on a hot roof or in a crawl space. Written times are not.

What to write down

Break each call into the same five tasks so the numbers can be compared from one call to the next:

  • Drive: leaving the last stop to arriving at the door, including parking and carrying tools in.
  • Diagnosis: from first look at the equipment to knowing what is wrong and what it needs.
  • Repair: from starting the fix to the equipment running and checked.
  • Cleanup: tools and waste out, area left as found.
  • Paperwork: writing up the ticket and talking through the result with the customer.

For each task write a start time and a finish time, then subtract. Use the same clock every time, a watch or phone clock, and write the time when you switch tasks, not at the end of the day from memory. Add one short line of context, such as "attic access, second floor" or "waited for a part from the truck." Context is what turns a long number into an explanation. Keep the page to times and facts: no complaints and no opinions about the customer or coworkers.

Why new techs take longer, and how it changes

There are honest reasons a new tech is slower. You are still learning where tools and parts live on the truck, you check more things twice, and you are building the habit of a steady diagnostic order. Paperwork is slow when you are unsure what to write. Some of the extra time is also wasted trips to the truck, which a better first look can cut.

Over a season, expect times to drop at first and then flatten. Early drops come from habits: carrying the right tools in on the first trip, writing notes as you go. Later gains are smaller and depend on seeing more kinds of faults. The curve will not be smooth. A hard call in week four can be longer than an easy one in week one, which is why you look at averages and ranges rather than single calls. Never rush a safety step or skip a measurement to make a number look better. A shorter time that comes from skipping a check is a worse result.

Using averages to plan a day

An average is the sum of the times divided by how many you have. For planning, add a cushion on top, since half of your jobs will run longer than average. The cushion should be a stated percentage, chosen by you and adjusted as your range narrows. The method has three steps: average each task, add the averages for the work you expect, then add the cushion and the drive.

Our own worked example (assumptions are ours, not data from any company or study). Assume a new tech logs the diagnosis time, in minutes, on eight no-cooling calls of the same general kind over four weeks, two calls per week: 70, 62, 58, 55, 52, 50, 48, 45.
Step 1, add them: 70 + 62 = 132; 132 + 58 = 190; 190 + 55 = 245; 245 + 52 = 297; 297 + 50 = 347; 347 + 48 = 395; 395 + 45 = 440 minutes.
Step 2, divide by the number of calls: 440 / 8 = 55 minutes average.
Step 3, look at the spread. The first two weeks average (70 + 62 + 58 + 55) / 4 = 245 / 4 = 61.25 minutes. The last two weeks average (52 + 50 + 48 + 45) / 4 = 195 / 4 = 48.75 minutes. The range is 70 - 45 = 25 minutes.
Step 4, add an assumed cushion of 20 percent: 55 x 0.20 = 11 minutes, so the planning time is 55 + 11 = 66 minutes. Because the recent weeks are faster, the tech might also use the last-two-weeks average of 48.75 with the same cushion (about 58.5 minutes) once the trend holds for another week or two. Both numbers are stated assumptions, not rules.

Notice what the example does not do. It does not plan on the best call (45 minutes) or on the worst (70). It plans on the average plus a cushion you can name.

Setting honest arrival windows

Your arrival time at the next stop depends on how long the current one runs. If the planned time for the current task is 66 minutes and the logged slowest time is 70, the spread is only a few minutes for that one task. Spread adds up over a day of several tasks plus drives, so a window narrower than your own range will often be missed. A common-sense approach is to build the window from your log: earliest arrival if everything runs near your fastest, latest if it runs near your slowest. Tell the customer the window you can keep. If you are running late, say so early. An honest, slightly wider window is better than a tight one you miss. Your dispatcher or lead sets the actual windows; give them your logged numbers rather than hopes.

Spotting tasks that run long, and asking for tips

Once you have a few weeks, scan each task for entries that sit well above your average. Ask two questions: is it always this task, or was it one odd call? The context line usually answers. If one task stays long across many calls, such as the repair step on a certain kind of part swap or the paperwork step, that is a good question for a senior tech.

Bring facts, not apologies: "My cleanup averages 20 minutes and I think it is the tool packing. How do you stage yours?" Most experienced techs are happy to share a habit when the question is specific. Ask at a calm moment, not in front of a customer, and try one suggestion at a time so you can see in the log whether it helped.

Keep it private and factual

Your log is a personal learning tool. Write only times, task names and short technical context. Leave out customer names, addresses and anything that identifies a household, and do not photograph or copy company records into your own notes. Follow your employer's rules about what you may record and share. If you are asked to hand over your times, that is a decision about your notes, not a requirement of this lesson. For what to record about readings and findings, see the lessons linked below rather than repeating them in this log.

Field card: Tracking your own job times

Same five tasks, start and finish, every call.

  • Tasks: drive, diagnosis, repair, cleanup, paperwork.
  • Write the time when you switch tasks, plus one short context line.
  • Average = total minutes / number of calls. Plan on the average plus a stated cushion.
  • Our example: 440 / 8 = 55 min, plus 20 percent (11 min) = 66 min planning time.
  • Build arrival windows from your range, not your best call.
  • Long task? Bring the numbers to a senior tech and ask how they stage it.
  • Keep notes to times and facts. No customer names or addresses.
