Mildew on the Bathroom Ceiling: Why It Forms and How to Stop It

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Gray or black spots on the bathroom ceiling and the top of the walls are a moisture problem first and a cleaning problem second. This lesson explains why the ceiling is the first surface to get wet, what the exhaust fan has to do, and how to clean and repaint safely once the moisture is under control.

Why the ceiling sweats first

A hot shower puts a lot of water vapor into the room. Warm air holds more vapor than cool air, and warm air rises, so the moist air collects at the ceiling. In most houses the bathroom ceiling has an unheated attic above it, which makes it the coldest large surface in the room, especially in winter. When moist air touches a surface colder than its dew point, the vapor turns to liquid on that surface. That is the same thing that happens on a cold glass of water in summer. This explanation of rising air and a cold ceiling is our own; the sources below support the underlying points that condensation shows up on cold surfaces and that high indoor humidity is the cause.

A federal environmental agency guide on mold and moisture says condensation can be a sign of high humidity, suggests keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent (ideally 30 to 50 percent), and says to insulate cold surfaces. It also says the key to mold control is moisture control. Cleaning without fixing the moisture only buys a few weeks.

Run the fan during and after the shower

A university extension page on bathroom mold says to run the exhaust fan during the shower and for a few minutes afterward. A national-lab guide on bathroom exhaust fans recommends leaving the fan on for an hour after a shower for effective humidity control. Those two sources bracket the answer. As our own rule of thumb, about 20 minutes after the shower is a practical middle: long enough to clear most of the vapor, short enough that people actually do it. If the mirror is still fogged or the ceiling still feels damp, run it longer.

Use a timer or a humidity-sensing switch

Nobody remembers to go back and switch the fan off. A federal energy department training document says timer or fan delay switches are ideal for clearing bathroom moisture, and describes a delay switch that keeps the fan running for a set time after the light goes off. The national-lab guide likewise recommends a timer switch instead of a standard wall switch. A humidity-sensing switch is the other option. Our own description: it turns the fan on when the room humidity jumps and off when it drops, so it also catches the steam from a shower someone forgot to ventilate. If you are not sure the wiring or the fan itself is suitable, ask a licensed electrician or HVAC contractor to install it.

A simple tissue test for fan suction

The national-lab guide shows a fan that holds a tissue against the grille as a sign of enough suction. Our own version of the test: turn the fan on, hold a single square of toilet tissue up to the grille, and let go. If the fan holds it, it is pulling air. If the tissue falls or flutters away, the fan is weak, the duct is crushed or blocked, the damper is stuck, or the fan has no makeup air. A pass does not tell you the airflow in cfm, and the guide notes that installed airflow can fall below the rating on the box, so a fan that barely holds a tissue deserves a closer look.

Where the fan exhausts matters

The federal energy training document states that exhaust airflow must terminate outside and not in attics. The national-lab guide says the same and adds that crawlspaces and wall cavities are not acceptable either. A fan that dumps into the attic simply moves the wet air to a colder place, where it can condense on the roof sheathing and insulation. That can cause mold in the attic and may stain the ceiling you are trying to protect. Look in the attic with a flashlight: the flexible duct should run to a roof or wall cap, be attached at both ends, and not sag in a way that traps water. The related lesson on fans vented into the attic covers this in more depth. If the duct is loose or ends in the attic, that is a repair for a qualified contractor. Ducts through unheated space should also be insulated, which the energy training document ties to preventing condensation.

Crack the door for makeup air

A fan can only push out as much air as can come in. The extension page says you should feel air entering under the closed bathroom door while the fan runs. If you do not, the fan is starving. Our own advice: open the door about an inch while the fan runs, or trim or add an undercut gap if the door is tight, and have a contractor do that last part if you are unsure. The cold-bathroom lesson linked below shows how air balance and a stuck damper affect heat as well.

Worked example (our own)

This example is our own, with stated assumptions, to show why twenty minutes of fan time is reasonable.

  • Assumption 1: a 10-minute shower releases about 0.5 pound of water vapor into the room. This is an assumed round number, not a measured one.
  • Assumption 2: the fan really moves its rated 50 cfm. The national-lab guide lists 50 cfm as the minimum for intermittent exhaust, and the energy training document says spot fans commonly fall in the 50 to 70 cfm range.
  • Assumption 3: the bathroom is 5 ft by 8 ft with an 8 ft ceiling.

Step 1, room volume: 5 x 8 x 8 = 320 cubic feet.

Step 2, air the fan moves in 20 minutes: 50 cfm x 20 min = 1,000 cubic feet.

Step 3, air changes: 1,000 / 320 = 3.125, so about 3 full changes of the room air.

Step 4, water in plain units: water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon, so 0.5 / 8.34 = about 0.06 gallon, a little under 8 fluid ounces. That small amount, spread through 320 cubic feet of warm air, is enough to push the humidity high and wet a cold ceiling.

What it tells us: three air changes replace most of the moist air, but only if makeup air arrives and the fan exhausts outdoors. If the fan delivers only half its rating because of a long, crushed or attic-venting duct, 20 minutes gives about 1.5 changes, and the ceiling stays damp. This is a simplified mixing picture; real rooms do not mix perfectly.

Wipe down, then clean small areas safely

After a shower, a quick wipe of the walls and ceiling edges with a towel removes water before it sits. For small mildew spots, a university extension page on mold cleanup says to scrub hard surfaces with dish detergent and water and a stiff brush. The federal guide says a moldy area smaller than about 10 square feet (roughly a 3 foot by 3 foot patch) can usually be cleaned by the homeowner.

  • Never mix bleach with ammonia or with other cleaners. Both the federal guide and the extension page warn about this; the federal guide notes toxic fumes can result.
  • Ventilate. Open windows or doors and run the fan when using cleaning products, and exhaust the air outdoors.
  • Wear gloves, preferably long ones. The extension page also lists an N95 respirator and goggles; ceiling work drops spores and cleaner into your face.
  • Do the work from a stable step stool, not a chair, and take breaks.
  • Let the surface dry before you disinfect or paint. The federal guide says to dry wet areas within 24 to 48 hours.

When the area is too big for DIY

Both the federal guide and the extension page put the line at about 10 square feet. Call a professional if the mold covers more than that, if there has been a lot of water damage, if it is on the HVAC system, or if the water came from sewage. The federal guide also suggests getting experienced help for suspected hidden mold, and speaking with a health professional first if anyone in the home has health concerns. Soft or sagging drywall or a ceiling stain that keeps growing may be a roof or plumbing leak, which a fan will not fix. This page is general education, not medical or legal advice.

Repaint only after it is dry and clean

The federal guide says not to paint or caulk over moldy surfaces: clean and dry first, because paint over mold is likely to peel. Once the surface is clean and dry and the fan routine is working, repaint. Our own tip: give the room a few weeks of good fan use first. If new spots return in the same place, the moisture source is still there.

Quick checklist

  1. Fan on before the shower, off about 20 minutes after.
  2. Tissue test passes.
  3. Duct goes outdoors, not into the attic.
  4. Door cracked or undercut for makeup air.
  5. Wipe wet surfaces; clean small spots with detergent; never mix bleach and ammonia.
  6. Dry, then repaint.

Field card: Mildew on the Bathroom Ceiling: Why It Forms and How to Stop It

Stop the moisture first, then clean, then paint.

  • Warm moist air rises; the ceiling under a cold attic is the first place to reach the dew point.
  • Fan on during the shower; our rule of thumb is about 20 minutes after (sources say a few minutes to an hour).
  • Use a timer or humidity-sensing switch so the fan is not forgotten.
  • Tissue test: a single tissue should hold on the grille.
  • Exhaust must go outdoors, never into the attic.
  • Crack the door so air can come in.
  • Our example: 50 cfm x 20 min = 1,000 cu ft; room 5 x 8 x 8 = 320 cu ft; about 3 changes.
  • Under about 10 sq ft: detergent, gloves, ventilation, no bleach and ammonia together. Larger: call a pro.
