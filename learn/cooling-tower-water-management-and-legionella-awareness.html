Cooling Tower Water Management and Legionella Awareness

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

A cooling tower is an open system that breathes in dust and lives in warm, wet air. That is why public health agencies treat towers as a place where Legionella bacteria can grow, and why a written water management program matters.

Awareness, not a procedure

This lesson is general awareness only. It does not set limits, sampling schedules, disinfection steps or test methods. Your site’s written water management plan, your water treatment provider and the rules where you work decide those. If you do not know where the plan is or what it says about your tasks, ask before working on or near the tower.

What Legionella is and how people are exposed

OSHA explains that Legionella bacteria occur naturally in water and soil and are usually harmless outdoors, but can become a hazard when they grow in poorly maintained water systems, cooling towers and HVAC systems. Workers are typically exposed by breathing water droplets or fine mist. The illness is treatable but serious, it is not contagious, and older adults and people with weakened immune systems are at higher risk.

Why towers are singled out

CDC’s cooling tower toolkit explains that towers reject heat by evaporating water, which creates tiny droplets that can carry bacteria over long distances if Legionella is present. Both open and closed-circuit towers carry similar risks and need the same basic operation and maintenance.

What a water management program is

Both OSHA and CDC describe the same idea. The program describes the water system and its components, identifies where Legionella could grow, decides where control measures are needed and how to monitor them, plans responses for when a control measure fails, and documents everything. CDC calls an effective program the primary strategy to control Legionella growth and spread.

The four factors people use to think about it

CDC’s toolkit organizes control around four ideas, which you can remember as STAR:

  • Sediment and biofilm. Deposits give bacteria a place to live and shelter them from treatment.
  • Temperature. The toolkit gives a favorable growth range of about 77 to 113 F (25 to 45 C), and notes that keeping water outside that range where possible helps. A tower often sits in that range, so other controls matter.
  • Age of water. Stagnant water is a problem. The toolkit mentions flushing low-flow runs and dead legs at least weekly and balancing run time among multiple towers or cells.
  • Residual disinfectant. Treatment needs to be present and monitored, with pH, to work.

What the program generally includes

  • Automated treatment feed and automated blowdown to keep water quality in range. Blowdown sets the cycles of concentration; a U.S. DOE guide says many systems run at about two to four cycles and six or more may be possible, depending on make-up water quality and the treatment program.
  • Regular monitoring of disinfectant residual and pH, more often when results swing.
  • High-efficiency drift eliminators to limit droplets leaving the tower, and placing towers away from building air intakes (the toolkit mentions at least 25 feet).
  • Cleaning and disinfection at least annually, and following the manufacturer before startup, during idle periods and after shutdown.
  • Records kept on site: log sheets, service reports and test results.

What a technician does

  • Know who runs the program and where the logs live.
  • Report slime, cloudy water, odor, stagnant water, idle cells holding standing water, and missing or damaged drift eliminators.
  • Record treatment readings on rounds and report drift.
  • Treat anything that makes mist, such as hosing, pressure washing or fan work, as a respiratory-hazard task until your hazard assessment says otherwise, and use the protection your site requires.
  • Do not start a long-idle tower without the steps your plan requires.

A tower left idle and wet is not safe just because it is not running. Never skip, edit or backfill treatment records.

Printable checklist

  • I know where the water management plan and logs are kept
  • I know who the water treatment provider is
  • Treatment feed tanks, tubing and pumps look normal
  • Treatment and conductivity readings logged
  • No slime, cloudiness, odor or standing water in idle cells
  • Drift eliminators in place and undamaged
  • Mist-producing work has a hazard assessment and PPE
  • Long-idle tower not started without plan steps
  • Problems reported, not fixed quietly

New Jersey note

New Jersey now has a state law, P.L. 2024, c. 66, that requires a written water management program for buildings with cooling towers or evaporative condensers. As we read it, the program is to be developed by about September 2026, put into use by about December 2026, and announced with a posted notice. Penalties exist. Details can change, so confirm with the NJ Department of Health. See the plain-English guide to the NJ law.
