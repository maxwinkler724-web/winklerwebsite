Loading Dock Doors and Heat Loss in Plants and Warehouses: Seals, Shelters, High-Speed Doors, Air Curtains, Building Pressure and Truck Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dock door is the biggest hole in a plant or warehouse envelope, and it is open when the weather is worst. This lesson is for facility techs. It shows how to put a number on the heat that leaves through a dock, which fixes are cheap, how building pressure and truck exhaust change the picture, and what to check before you blame the heaters.

How an open door loses heat

Air moves through an open overhead door for two reasons: wind pushing on the face of the building, and the stack effect, where warm indoor air rises and leaves high while cold air comes in low. Our stack effect lesson covers the second one. Every cubic foot that comes in has to be heated from outdoor temperature to indoor temperature, and heaters can only do that by burning fuel or using power.

The PNNL warehouse design guide for small warehouses names dock doors as a source of infiltration. It models a closed dock door at 0.40 cfm per square foot of door, about 32 cfm for an 8 by 10 ft door, and an open door with a truck in place at 783 cfm. That is a modeling assumption for a truck backed against the door, not a measurement of your dock. An open door with no truck in it will move far more air. The same guide recommends weatherseals for dock levelers and trailer hinges.

Seals, shelters and the gap around the trailer

When a trailer is backed in, the door opening is no longer the problem. The gap around the trailer is. Two products close it. A dock seal is a compressible pad, usually foam, that the trailer squeezes as it backs in. A dock shelter is a frame with side and head curtains that the trailer backs into, leaving a little clearance. A utility-funded efficiency program says in its cold storage guide that shelters work better than foam seals, and that foam seals are a low-cost retrofit that may suit some facilities. We read this as a general trade-off: seals are tighter at the pad when trailer height and width are consistent, shelters handle mixed trailer sizes and are easier on a pad that gets hit.

The cheapest fixes are often on the existing seal. Look for torn fabric, flattened foam that no longer springs back, a head pad that sits above the trailer roof, and worn leveler and hinge weatherseals. If trailers do not line up with the pad, the seal cannot close the gap no matter how new it is.

High-speed doors and vestibules

The same efficiency program guide recommends high-speed doors on loading docks to limit outdoor air infiltration. A fast door is open only while a truck or lift moves through, so it spends far less time open than a slow door. The doors still need to be set to close promptly, and a door that is held open by a blocked sensor loses the advantage.

A vestibule is a second door set that makes an air lock. PNNL modeled the Standard 90.1 vestibule rule for entrance doors. For a warehouse prototype it estimated peak infiltration of 612 cfm without a vestibule and 374 cfm with one, and a national energy saving of about 0.36 percent of building energy. The report notes that the 2004 rule exempted doors used mainly for vehicle or material handling, so it was written for people doors, not dock doors. For docks, a vestibule is usually a closed dock bay or a fast door ahead of the main door.

Air curtains and heaters aimed at the door

An air curtain helps when the door is open to a mild draft and is set up right: the right mounting height, a sheet that reaches the floor, and no big pressure difference pushing against it. Our air curtain lesson covers sizing, and the maker guidance we cite there says that strong wind gusts, negative pressure inside the building and heavy door traffic can call for more airflow. At a dock the trailer fills much of the opening, so check that the curtain is not blowing into the trailer gap and that it is switched on by the door position, not left running all night. In our judgment an air curtain is a poor fix for a building that is badly out of pressure: fix the pressure first.

Unit heaters aimed at a dock door warm the workers and the floor and make the dock livable, and they are fine for that. They do not stop infiltration, and a heater throwing air across a door can disturb an air curtain. Our unit heater lesson covers throw, capacity and freeze risk. A cold dock with the heaters running flat out is often a leak problem, not a heater problem.

Building pressure: exhaust and makeup air

If exhaust fans pull more air out of the building than makeup air puts in, the building goes negative and the difference arrives through the leaks, mostly dock doors. The EPA indoor air building guide advises keeping occupied spaces under positive pressure relative to the outside to avoid infiltration of outdoor pollutants, and says to verify that outdoor air is not entering through openings near loading docks. The PNNL warehouse guide shows the other side of this: its bulky storage area has four 20,000 cfm exhaust fans that run at 85 F, with relief dampers of 10 square feet each that let makeup air in, and the dampers leak about 500 cfm each when the fans are off. Leaky relief dampers and idle exhaust fans are an infiltration path on their own.

A quick field check: with the dock doors closed, open a personnel door and feel which way the air goes. A strong inward pull, or a door that is hard to open, points to exhaust running ahead of makeup air. Check that makeup air units are enabled and running whenever the exhaust fans run, see our makeup air unit lesson, and check that relief dampers close tight.

Truck exhaust and outdoor air intakes

Trucks idling at a dock put exhaust where a door, a seal gap or an air intake can pull it in. EPA says an idling diesel engine burns about 0.8 gallons of fuel per hour and emits more than 18 pounds of CO2 per hour, and that for trucks with exhaust catalysts, long idling lets the catalyst cool and raises NOx. EPA's most effective idling fix is shutting the engine off, and it lists no-idling signs and driver comfort stations as steps a facility can take. Many places set their own idling limits, so ask your safety or environmental manager what applies at your site. NIOSH names forklifts among workplace carbon monoxide sources and cites a 200 ppm ceiling limit as an alarm level for personal monitors.

For intakes, the EPA and DOE Labs21 guide says to avoid placing intakes near vehicle loading zones, and warns that a canopy over a loading dock does not stop hot exhaust from rising to intakes above it. See outdoor air intake separation distances. A dock that smells of diesel inside usually has a seal gap, a negative building, or an intake in the wrong place.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption for illustration, not a measurement or a published figure. Replace them with your readings.

  • Open dock door, 9 ft wide by 10 ft tall: area = 9 x 10 = 90 sq ft.
  • Assumed average air velocity through the opening: 50 ft/min (about half a mile per hour, a light wind and stack effect combined).
  • Assumed indoor temperature 65 F, assumed outdoor average during open hours 25 F, so delta T = 40 F.
  • Assumed door open 6 hours per day for 150 heating days, which is 900 open hours.
  • Assumed fuel cost $1.20 per therm (100,000 Btu) and an assumed heating system efficiency of 80 percent.

Step 1, airflow. cfm = area x velocity = 90 x 50 = 4,500 cfm.

Step 2, heat loss. Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T = 1.08 x 4,500 x 40 = 194,400 Btu/h. The 1.08 is our own rounding of 60 minutes per hour x 0.075 lb per cu ft x 0.24 Btu per lb per F for standard air. A University of Georgia extension bulletin uses the same idea for infiltration, with a rounded factor of 0.02 per air change in its greenhouse equation.

Step 3, fuel. 194,400 / 0.80 = 243,000 Btu/h of fuel, which is 2.43 therms per hour. At $1.20 per therm that is about $2.92 per hour, or about $17.50 per 6-hour day.

Step 4, season. 2.43 therms/h x 900 h = 2,187 therms. 2,187 x $1.20 = about $2,624 per door per heating season.

Dock seal comparison. Assume a good seal around a backed-in trailer cuts the leakage by 70 percent, a made-up round number. Airflow drops to 4,500 x 0.30 = 1,350 cfm. Heat loss = 1.08 x 1,350 x 40 = 58,320 Btu/h. Fuel = 58,320 / 0.80 = 72,900 Btu/h = 0.729 therm/h. Season = 0.729 x 900 = 656 therms, or about $787. The saving is about $2,624 - $787 = $1,837 per door per season. If the seal only cuts leakage by 50 percent, the saving is about $1,312. A plant with ten active dock doors multiplies those numbers, which is why door timers, sensors and seal repair are worth a walk-through.

Because a real truck blocks much of the opening, a real dock with a trailer in place may leak less than this example, and an empty open door may leak more. Use the arithmetic to compare options, not to predict a bill.

Simple fixes, in order

  • Door timers and sensors. Set closing delays to the shortest the traffic allows, and clear blocked or dirty sensors so fast doors do not stay open.
  • Seal repair. Replace torn or flattened seals, and the leveler and hinge weatherseals.
  • Exhaust and makeup balance. Make sure makeup air runs with the exhaust fans, and that relief dampers close.
  • No-idling signs and a shutoff habit. Cheapest way to keep exhaust out of the dock.
  • Then consider fast doors, shelters or a vestibule, and only then more heater capacity.

Field card: Loading dock doors and heat loss

Close the gap, balance the building, then heat what is left.

  • Dock heat loss: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T, with cfm = door area x air velocity.
  • Our example: 9 x 10 ft door, 50 ft/min, 40 F delta T = 4,500 cfm, 194,400 Btu/h (assumed inputs).
  • Shelters beat foam seals in an efficiency program guide; foam is the low-cost retrofit.
  • Check seal condition, leveler and hinge weatherseals, and door sensors first.
  • Exhaust ahead of makeup air makes the building negative and pulls air through the docks.
  • EPA: idling diesel burns about 0.8 gal/h; shutting the engine off is the best fix.
  • Keep intakes away from loading zones; a canopy does not stop exhaust rising.
