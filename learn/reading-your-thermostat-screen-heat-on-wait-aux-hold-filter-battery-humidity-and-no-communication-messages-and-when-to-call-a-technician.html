Reading Your Thermostat Screen: What Each Message Means

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A modern thermostat screen is a small status board. It shows what the system is doing, what it is waiting for and sometimes what it wants from you. Most messages are routine. A few mean you should stop and call a technician. This lesson sorts them into those groups, in general terms, so you know which is which.

Your manual is the final word

Read the manual for your own model. Makers use different words and icons for the same function, and the same word can mean different things on different models. Everything below is a general guide, not a list for any one thermostat. If this page and your manual disagree, follow the manual.

The basics: mode and call icons

Most screens show two kinds of information. The mode is what you chose: heat, cool, auto or off. The call is what the system is doing right now: a flame, sun or snowflake icon, or words like "Heating" or "Cooling," usually with the target temperature. A message such as "Heat on" or "Heating to 70" means the thermostat is asking for heat. It does not prove warm air is coming out yet. Gas and oil furnaces and boilers take a short while to start, and a heat pump may take a few minutes to produce warm air.

If you ask for heat and the room stays cold for a long time with the call showing, that is a service question. Our blank or wrong temperature lesson covers the case where the screen itself looks wrong.

A flashing "wait" or delay message

Many cooling and heat pump systems hold the compressor off for a short time after it stops, so pressures can settle before it restarts. The thermostat or the equipment enforces that delay, and many thermostats show it as a flashing "Wait," a clock icon or a countdown. This is normal. Do not turn the system off and on to hurry it, because each restart can begin the delay again. If the message never clears, or the system never starts after the time your manual gives, treat it as a service problem. The wiring and testing side is in our compressor time delay lesson.

Auxiliary and emergency heat on a heat pump

A heat pump moves heat from outdoors. Federal guidance says standard air-source models lose heating output as the outdoor temperature falls, so they often have backup heat, usually electric resistance strips. The same guidance says that backup heat costs more to run and that a thermostat made for heat pumps raises the temperature gradually so the backup does not switch on unnecessarily.

On the screen, "Aux" or "Auxiliary" usually means the thermostat is allowing the backup to assist. It appears automatically, most often during a big temperature recovery or very cold weather. "Em" or "Emergency heat" usually means someone selected a mode that lets the backup heat work by itself. Treat the second as something you chose on purpose, not as a normal daily setting. The full difference, and why a lockout control may keep the backup off in mild weather, is in Emergency Heat vs Auxiliary Heat and Lockouts. We do not repeat it here. A state extension fact sheet says a strip heat lockout keeps the backup off when outdoor temperatures are mild, and it suggests calling a technician if the heat pump cannot keep up when it is above about 40°F outside.

Hold versus schedule

"Schedule" or "Program" means the thermostat is following the times you set. "Hold," "Override" or "Until 6:00 PM" means you changed the temperature by hand and the thermostat will keep that setting, either until a time shown or until you cancel it. A permanent hold is a common reason a house is warm at 3 a.m. Federal guidance says programmable models let you override the schedule without erasing the program. For how much a schedule can save, and why heat pumps need care with deep setbacks, see Thermostat Setback Schedules.

Fan on versus auto

"Fan Auto" runs the blower only during a heating or cooling call. "Fan On" runs it all the time. Federal guidance says to leave it on Auto unless an indoor air quality professional has set the system to run continuously. The trade-offs are in Thermostat Fan Auto vs On.

Filter and maintenance reminders

A "Change filter," wrench or calendar message is usually a simple timer. It counts days or fan run time and knows nothing about how dirty the filter really is. Federal guidance says to check or clean filters about monthly or as the manufacturer directs, and to check that registers and baseboards are not blocked. So do this: look at the filter, replace it if it is dirty or due, and then clear the reminder using the steps in your manual. The reset is often a menu option or a button held for a few seconds, but it varies by model. Filter size and airflow arrow details are in Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit. A reminder that says "service due" is a timer for a visit and does not mean something has failed.

Low battery warnings

Many thermostats use batteries as the main power or as backup when wired power is present. A low battery icon means replace them soon, because a dead battery can blank the screen and stop the heating or cooling call. Use the type your manual names, and replace all at once. If you have no battery compartment and the screen is blank, the cause is more likely power or the low-voltage supply, which is a different problem.

Humidity readouts

Many screens show indoor relative humidity. It is the reading at the thermostat's location only, and the sensor is a basic one. A university extension article recommends 30 to 40 percent indoor humidity in winter. It says above about 40 percent raises window condensation, below about 30 percent can dry skin and nasal passages, and that mechanical hygrometers are often off by more than 20 percent. Treat the thermostat number as a rough trend. A low winter reading is usually weather, not a failed system. For wet windows, see Condensation on Windows in Winter.

Remote sensor readings

Some thermostats accept small wireless room sensors. The screen may show a room name and its temperature, and the thermostat may average the rooms or favor one. If a room reads far from what you feel, check whether that sensor is included in the control and whether its battery is low. A sensor can show "offline" while the main unit works. Moving a sensor out of sunlight or away from a supply vent changes what it reads. Whether a given model averages or picks one room is a manual question.

Error and no-communication messages

Messages such as "No communication," "Check wiring," "Sensor error" or a code number mean the thermostat has lost contact with the equipment or a sensor, or the equipment has reported a fault. Some of these clear on their own, but they are not routine. A simple check is whether any switch near the indoor unit is off and whether the breaker is on. Do not open the equipment or probe wiring. If the message returns, or comes with no heat or cooling, call a technician and give them the exact wording or code.

When a message means call a technician

  • A message that returns after you cleared it, or an error code you cannot find in the manual.
  • No heat or cooling together with a communication, sensor or fault message.
  • Backup or emergency heat that stays on when the weather is mild, or a heat pump that cannot keep the house comfortable above about 40°F outside.
  • Any message that comes with a burning smell, smoke, water leaking, or a breaker that keeps tripping. Turn the equipment off and call; for gas smell, leave and call from outside.

Never open the cabinet or bypass a safety control. Messages that you can clear yourself are limited to the ones in your manual: batteries, filters, mode and hold settings.

Field card: Reading Your Thermostat Screen

Most messages are routine. Repeats and faults are not.

  • Heat on or Cool on means a call, not instant comfort.
  • Flashing Wait means a compressor delay. Leave it alone.
  • Aux is automatic backup; Em heat is a mode someone chose.
  • Hold overrides the schedule until a time or until canceled.
  • Filter reminders are timers. Inspect, replace, then reset by the manual.
  • Humidity on the screen is a rough, one-spot reading.
  • Errors that repeat, or no heat with a fault message: call a technician.
  • Your manual is the final word.
