What Happens in a Home Energy Audit: Blower Door and Infrared

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you search "what does a home energy audit include," the honest answer is: a set of measurements, not just a walk-through with a clipboard. A good auditor measures how leaky the house is, looks at heat loss with a camera, checks that fuel-burning appliances are safe, may test the ducts, and then writes up what to fix first. This lesson explains each step so you know what you are paying for.

Who does audits and what the names mean

The U.S. Department of Energy calls this a home energy assessment (or audit) and says it shows how much energy a house uses and recommends cost-effective improvements. Two national groups are tied to the work. RESNET raters, who produce a HERS Index score, use a blower door, a duct leakage tester and infrared cameras, according to the HERS Index site. BPI (the Building Performance Institute) writes standards and publishes a building analyst field guide that covers combustion safety. Neither is a government license, so ask any auditor which credential they hold and what their report will include.

Step 1: The blower door test

A blower door is a calibrated fan mounted in an exterior doorway. It pulls air out of the house, so outside air rushes in through every gap. Penn State's summary of the DOE material says it mimics roughly a 20 mph wind and that auditors often run it before and after air sealing to see how well the work performed. DOE's fact sheet adds one important detail: use a calibrated unit. An uncalibrated fan can help find leaks, but it cannot measure how much air is getting through.

Reading the result: CFM50 and ACH50

The tester brings the house to 50 pascals of pressure difference with the outside and records the fan's airflow. That number is CFM50 (cubic feet per minute at 50 pascals). To compare houses of different sizes, it is converted to air changes per hour at 50 pascals, or ACH50. An HVAC trade-site article describes the steps: convert CFM to cubic feet per hour by multiplying by 60, then divide by the building volume. A Washington State University Energy Program test form writes it as ACH50 = (CFM50 x 60) / volume.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

These numbers are invented for practice, not a real home. A 1,800 square foot house with 8 foot ceilings has a volume of 1,800 x 8 = 14,400 cubic feet. Say the blower door reads 2,400 CFM50.

  1. Convert to cubic feet per hour: 2,400 x 60 = 144,000.
  2. Divide by volume: 144,000 / 14,400 = 10.0 ACH50.
  3. Suppose air sealing brings the retest to 1,800 CFM50. Then 1,800 x 60 = 108,000, and 108,000 / 14,400 = 7.5 ACH50.

Lower is tighter. Two cautions from the sources: ACH50 only describes the house under test pressure, so it is not how many times the air really changes on a normal day. And volume rules vary. The Washington form defines volume as conditioned floor area times ceiling height, while the 2018 Washington energy code text it cites uses a fixed 8.5 feet for that state's compliance test. The same code sets a limit of 5 ACH50 or less, which is that state's number and not a New Jersey figure.

Step 2: The infrared camera

An infrared camera shows temperature differences on walls, ceilings and around windows and doors. Penn State's page stresses its limits: it does not see through walls, its colors are computer-generated, and it works best alongside a blower door and should not be the auditor's only tool. With the house depressurized, leaks show up as dark streaks in the viewfinder, according to DOE and Vermont's Energy Saver pages. Gaps in insulation show up as temperature differences too, which is why the camera helps find both.

Step 3: Combustion safety on gas appliances

Tightening a house can change how furnaces and water heaters vent. That is why Vermont's page says safety tests are done before and after air-sealing. Penn State's summary describes combustion appliance zone testing on every fuel-fired appliance, with checks for fuel leaks and draft problems, a combustion analyzer reading flue gases and carbon monoxide, and testing of smoke and CO detectors.

BPI's building analyst field guide, which refers to ANSI/BPI-1200, outlines the procedure in general terms. The tester identifies the combustion appliance zone and measures its baseline pressure. Next comes worst-case depressurization, meaning the exhaust fans and air handler combination that pulls the most air out of the space, followed by a check for spillage from an atmospherically vented appliance. Flue gas CO is measured and compared with the action level in the standard, and ambient CO in the zone is monitored the whole time. A gas leak check on fittings is part of the guide too. The guide does not print numeric limits, so this page does not either; the auditor applies the current standard. For what analyzer numbers mean, see Combustion Analyzer Readings.

Step 4: Duct leakage testing

For forced-air homes, DOE describes a variable-speed duct blower attached to the ductwork to measure how much air leaks from the ducts. Vermont's page mentions using a smoke-generating device with a manometer to pinpoint leaks. Leaky ducts in an attic or crawlspace can waste a lot of what the equipment makes. The problems themselves are covered in Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Step 5: The report and priorities

DOE says the assessor should give you a written report of findings and improvement ideas, from minor jobs such as sealing gaps and adding attic insulation to major ones such as replacing equipment. Vermont's page says the next step is deciding on priorities, budget and timeline, and mentions possible moisture and ventilation recommendations. DOE gives a typical cost of about $250 to $600 or more, and says some contractors reduce the rate if you hire them for the work. Penn State's page says acting on audit recommendations can save 5 to 30 percent on energy bills; treat that as a broad range, not a promise.

A DIY walkthrough

You can do a rough version yourself. DOE's list suggests checking outlets, window and door frames and attic hatches, and confirming caulk and weatherstripping are in good shape. At night, shine a flashlight at gaps while a helper watches from outside, or close a door on a sheet of paper and see if it slides out freely. DOE also describes a smoke test with exterior doors, windows and flues closed and exhaust fans running. That one deserves care: exhaust fans can change how a flue draws, so our own advice (not DOE's) is that if you have gas or oil appliances, you skip it and let a professional do the combustion-safe version. For why a house may cost a lot to run, start with Why Is My Heating or Cooling Bill So High?

New Jersey programs

The New Jersey Clean Energy Program page we opened lists Home Performance with ENERGY STAR among programs that transitioned to the utilities effective July 1, 2021, and says to contact your utility for eligibility and how to apply. We did not read incentive details, so check with your utility directly.

Technician's corner

When you service gas equipment in a tightened home, a clean combustion test in October is not a guarantee in January with the dryer and range hood running. Spillage and CO checks are done under worst-case exhaust conditions for that reason. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics and Carbon Monoxide Alarms. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the standard your employer or program requires.

Field card: What Happens in a Home Energy Audit

Measure first, then fix, then retest.

  • Blower door: a calibrated fan depressurizes the house; read CFM50.
  • ACH50 = (CFM50 x 60) / house volume in cubic feet.
  • Infrared camera: shows temperature differences; use with the blower door, not alone.
  • Gas appliances: draft, spillage, flue CO and fuel leak checks, before and after air sealing.
  • Duct blower measures duct leakage.
  • Report: findings, priorities, budget.
  • Skip DIY depressurization with gas appliances; ask a pro.
