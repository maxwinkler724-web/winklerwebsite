Heating and Cooling Tiny Houses and Very Small Homes: Tiny Heat Loads, Oversizing, Moisture and Ventilation, Ductless Heads, Direct-Vent Heaters and CO Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tiny house of 150 to 400 square feet needs very little heat, and that surprises people. The trouble is rarely "not enough heater." It is a heater that is too big for the room, and a small sealed box that fills with moisture from two people living in it. This lesson covers sizing, moisture, fresh air, heating options, and the carbon monoxide (CO) rules that matter most in a space this small.

Why even the smallest equipment can be oversized

A well-insulated small space has a low heating and cooling load. A U.S. Department of Energy Building America study of low-load homes says fixed-capacity equipment is often oversized for them, which raises first and operating costs. The same report explains the moisture side: an oversized unit reaches the thermostat setpoint very quickly without moving much air across the coil, so it removes little moisture. Field monitoring found that cycling on and off hurts dehumidification, especially on summer nights, while variable-capacity equipment ran far longer each day (about 65% to 70% of a typical day, versus 30% to 35% for the fixed-capacity unit).

A federal page on minisplit heat pumps says the same thing in plain words: proper sizing and location of each indoor unit are crucial, and oversized units can short-cycle, which wastes energy and weakens temperature and humidity control. For the causes of short cycling in general, see AC Short Cycling: Causes. For a related small-building case, see Heating and Cooling a Backyard Studio, Shed or Small ADU, which covers insulation first and the power feed, so we do not repeat it here.

Moisture in a small, tight volume

People, cooking and showers all add water vapor. A University of Missouri Extension fact sheet lists daily moisture for an average family of four: about 16.8 lb from breathing and perspiring, 2.0 lb from cooking on an electric range with no exhaust vent, 2.0 lb from showers or baths, and 55.9 lb (6.7 gallons) in total once laundry, dishwashing and plants are included. The Building America Solution Center adds that the ideal indoor humidity in winter is 35% to 50%, and that visible condensation or frost on windows and cold walls is a sign of too much humidity.

In a big house that water spreads through a lot of air and leaks out through the walls. In a tiny, tight house there is little air to hold it and little leakage to carry it away. That is why condensation on windows, damp bedding and mold in the loft are such common tiny-house complaints.

Our own worked example: two people in 2,400 cubic feet

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption, not a measurement of any real home.

  • Two occupants. We scale the Missouri household-of-four figures for breathing, cooking and showers to half (assumption: moisture scales with people). Laundry, plants and dishes are left out.
  • Floor area 300 sq ft, average ceiling height 8 ft, so volume = 300 × 8 = 2,400 cubic feet (assumed).
  • Indoor air 68°F; air density 0.075 lb per cubic foot; saturated air at 68°F holds about 0.0147 lb of water per lb of dry air (standard psychrometric value, our own recall, rounded).

Moisture added per day: (16.8 + 2.0 + 2.0) ÷ 2 = 10.4 lb per day, about 1.25 gallons (at 8.34 lb per gallon).

Water the air can hold: air mass = 2,400 × 0.075 = 180 lb. At saturation, 180 × 0.0147 = about 2.65 lb. At 50% relative humidity, about 1.3 lb.

Comparison: 10.4 ÷ 2.65 = about 3.9. In one day the people add nearly four times the water that the air could hold even at 100% humidity. With no way out, that water ends up on the coldest surfaces.

How much fresh air to take it away: assume cold outdoor air at about 0.002 lb water per lb of dry air and a 40% indoor target, 0.40 × 0.0147 = 0.0059. The difference is 0.0039 lb per lb. Per cubic foot: 0.0039 × 0.075 = 0.000293 lb. Air needed: 10.4 ÷ 0.000293 = about 35,500 cubic feet per day, or 35,500 ÷ 1,440 minutes = about 25 cfm. That is about 25 × 60 ÷ 2,400 = 0.6 air changes per hour, a modest flow, but it must be a planned path, not whatever leaks in. A real house differs with weather, showers taken and how tight the walls are.

Ventilation: exhaust fans and a small ERV or HRV

The Building America Solution Center describes the main choices. Exhaust-only systems use kitchen and bath fans to remove stale air and let replacement air leak in; they use little energy but can depressurize the house. Balanced systems (HRV or ERV) move air out and in at similar rates and recover heat; an HRV transfers heat only, while an ERV also transfers moisture. The same guide says the 2012, 2015 and 2018 IRC require whole-house mechanical ventilation for relatively airtight homes, and that Standard 62.2 gives minimum home ventilation requirements. We name the standard only; check the rate with whoever designs the system.

  • Run bath and cooking fans during and after showers and meals. The Solution Center suggests a timer or humidistat on the bath fan, a smooth, short, straight duct, and a discharge outdoors, never into an attic, basement or crawlspace. See Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting and Moisture Control.
  • A range hood that exhausts outdoors lowers cooking pollutants, according to EPA, but the Solution Center warns that range hoods can depressurize a home if makeup air is not provided. Depressurizing is a real risk in a tiny house with a combustion appliance.
  • A small ERV or HRV gives a controlled fresh-air path. Pick the type and read the maintenance notes in ERV vs HRV.

Heating options for a very small home

  • One small ductless heat pump. A federal minisplit page says installation generally needs only about a three-inch wall hole for the conduit, the outdoor unit can sit up to 50 feet from the indoor unit, and condensate needs a place to drain outdoors. It suggests minisplits where ductwork is impractical. Run time matters: size to the real load. See Mini-Split Basics. A heat pump does not bring in fresh air, so it does not replace ventilation.
  • Direct-vent propane heater. A direct-vent (sealed-combustion) appliance draws combustion air from outdoors through one sealed pipe and sends exhaust out through a second sealed pipe, so, per the Building America Solution Center, the risk of backdrafting is eliminated. It also warns that naturally drafted appliances are not recommended in airtight homes, where fans can pull combustion gases into the living space. See Gas Wall Furnaces and Direct-Vent Wall Heaters.
  • Electric heat. A small electric heater adds no combustion gases, but follow its instructions and see Electric Space Heaters: Safety. Check the circuit rating before adding one.

What not to use indoors

Unvented kerosene and propane heaters, and outdoor patio heaters, do not belong in a tiny house. The Building America Solution Center says to avoid unvented space heaters such as kerosene, diesel and propane models when ventilation is inadequate, and that all combustion appliances produce moisture along with other emissions. EPA says to take special precautions with unvented fuel-burning heaters and describes CO as a colorless, odorless gas. A CPSC kerosene heater guide says these heaters give off pollutants such as CO and nitrogen dioxide and tells users to open a window about an inch. CPSC also warns that using outdoor heaters, grills or generators indoors can cause deadly CO buildup in minutes.

Our own chemistry arithmetic shows the moisture problem: burning propane (C3H8 + 5 O2 gives 3 CO2 + 4 H2O) makes 72 parts water for every 44 parts fuel by weight, about 1.6 lb of water per lb of propane burned. In a room already holding 10 lb of water a day, that matters. Outdoor gear is covered in Outdoor Gas Appliances at Home.

CO and smoke alarms

CPSC says to put CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas, test them monthly, and notes that combination smoke and CO alarms exist. If a CO alarm sounds, CPSC says leave the building immediately and call 911 from outside. The U.S. Fire Administration says to place smoke alarms inside and outside each sleeping area, to test them monthly, and to replace the whole unit 10 years after its manufacture date. In a sleeping loft, ask how to cover the loft and the floor below; a closed door slows smoke, an open loft does not. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Propane cylinders and freeze protection

  • Cylinders stay outside. A state railroad commission safety reminder says portable cylinders should never be stored inside a residential building or garage, and should be kept away from dryers, furnace vents and children's play areas, and out of excess heat.
  • Wheels-based units. Our own reasoning: the plumbing sits close to an exposed underside, so it freezes sooner than in a house on a foundation. A university extension guide says to insulate pipes in cold spots, seal gaps where cold air enters, use heat tape on exposed pipes, keep the heat on, and open cabinet doors under sinks; it also says never thaw a pipe with an open flame. If the unit will sit unheated in a hard freeze, drain it instead.

Field card: Tiny houses and very small homes

Small load, small equipment, planned fresh air, no unvented fuel.

  • Fixed-capacity gear is often oversized for low-load homes; short cycles remove little moisture.
  • Winter indoor humidity target 35% to 50%.
  • Our example: two people add about 10.4 lb of water a day to 2,400 cubic feet; about 25 cfm of fresh air carries it out.
  • Run bath and cooking fans; use a small ERV or HRV for a steady fresh-air path.
  • Choose a ductless head, direct-vent heater or electric heat. No unvented propane or kerosene, no outdoor heaters.
  • CO alarms outside sleeping areas, smoke alarms inside and outside them, test monthly.
  • Propane cylinders outdoors only; protect pipes under wheels-based units.
