Adiabatic Humidifiers in Air Handlers: Wetted Media, Fogging, Ultrasonic and Legionella Controls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A steam humidifier boils water. An adiabatic humidifier does not: it lets water evaporate into the airstream, and the energy for that comes from the air itself. That one difference explains the low power draw, the cooling effect, the strict water rules and the Legionella paperwork. This lesson compares the four common types and works one example with real numbers.

Check the site documents

Check the site documents. General education from public pages read in 2026. Your equipment manual, the engineer and the building's written water plan decide the real job. Maker figures are the makers' own claims, and naming a maker is not a recommendation.

How it works, and why the air gets colder

Liquid water needs heat to become vapor. One engineering data table lists 1053.7 Btu per pound at 70 F. In a steam unit that heat comes from an element, electrodes or a burner. In an adiabatic unit it is taken from the air passing through, so the air leaves cooler and more humid. A U.S. Department of Energy and Oak Ridge National Laboratory technology alert on ultrasonic units says the same thing: the cool mist absorbs energy from the supply air as it evaporates, a secondary cooling effect.

Four ways to put water in the air

  • Wetted media. Water runs down a corrugated pad and air passes through. Munters describes a recirculating version that bleeds off part of the water to limit mineral buildup, and a direct version that sends mains water through constant-flow valves and lets the excess flush the cassettes. Condair's overview says media needs no evaporation distance but is consumable, adds pressure drop and controls RH less tightly (about plus or minus 10 percent).
  • High-pressure atomizing (fogging). A pump pushes water through fine nozzles. Condair's HP page gives about 1,000 psi, droplets of about 10 to 40 micrometers, air velocity typically 150 to 700 fpm and a mist eliminator downstream. It needs more duct length for the fog to evaporate but holds about plus or minus 2 to 4 percent RH.
  • Compressed-air atomizing. Armstrong describes injecting pressurized water into the discharge orifice of a compressed-air system. Condair lists compression cost and louder nozzles as drawbacks.
  • Ultrasonic. A piezoelectric disk vibrates under mineral-free water and throws off a fine mist; the water is never heated (DOE alert). DriSteem lists duct units from 7.9 to 55.3 lb/h for air velocities of 200 to 800 fpm.

Energy compared with steam

The DOE alert says ultrasonic units cut humidifier energy use by 90 to 93 percent compared with systems that boil water. Condair says high-pressure systems often use up to 90 percent less. The ENERGY STAR data center guidance gives a field example: one operator's ultrasonic humidifiers drew under 1 kW where the original steam units drew 30 kW. The same page notes that steam adds heat the cooling system must then remove.

Water quality and white dust

Whatever minerals are in the water either stay behind or leave as airborne dust. EPA's humidifier fact sheet says ultrasonic and impeller types appear to produce the greatest dispersion of microorganisms and minerals, that a white dust can appear, and that demineralized water reduces it. The DOE alert says ultrasonic units need mineral-free, deionized or reverse osmosis (RO) water, which cuts scale but raises other costs. Condair's HP manual asks for drinking-water-quality feed, sets a limit for its RO version's treated water (below 20 µS/cm), and caps total silicate at 12 mg/l. Wetted media is more forgiving: Armstrong offers one pad version for RO or DI water and another for tap or softened water.

Carryover, eliminators and the drain pan

If droplets do not evaporate, they land on coils, filters and the fan. Maker literature handles this with a mist or droplet eliminator. Munters' separator is designed to prevent carryover at face velocities up to 5.0 m/s (984 fpm) and is recommended above 4.0 m/s (787 fpm). DriSteem's design guide warns that steam and evaporative systems both need adequate absorption distance, or wetting and equipment problems follow. Condair's HP manual calls for a drain tub with separate drains before and after the separator, each with its own siphon sized for duct pressure. Check pan slope and trap every visit.

Drain-down and flush cycles

Stagnant water is the enemy. Condair's HP manual says to keep the unit powered so it does not sit, runs a hygiene pre-flush (default 10 minutes) after 48 hours or more offline, and calls for full disinfection and a risk assessment before restart after a shutdown over 48 hours. It also lists disinfection at least once a year and after maintenance or repair, and running the fan until the unit is dry before service. Munters describes a motorized drain valve and a tray designed to avoid stagnation. These are examples, not settings to copy.

Legionella and the water management plan

CDC's site names centrally installed misters, atomizers, air washers and humidifiers among the devices where Legionella can grow, and says owners should follow the maker's cleaning and maintenance instructions. Its toolkit says a building with such a device should have a water management program for that device, even if the building as a whole does not need one. CDC lays out seven steps: build a team, describe the system with flow diagrams, find where Legionella could grow, set and monitor control measures, plan responses when limits are missed, confirm it works, and document everything, with a review at least yearly. The toolkit also points to ASHRAE Standard 188 (risk management for building water systems) and ASHRAE Guideline 12; we name them here only, so get the current editions from the owner or engineer. EPA says Legionella grows best between 80 and 120 F.

As a technician, find out whether the humidifier is in the plan, who owns it, and what the logs show. Report standing water, slime or odor, and do not change disinfectant settings on your own. Our cooling tower water management lesson covers the same program idea for towers.

When adiabatic beats steam, and when it does not

DriSteem's design guide says evaporative humidification may fit better when energy efficiency is a priority, large humidity loads are required, free cooling provides value or lower operating costs are wanted. Data centers are the clear case. ENERGY STAR lists nozzle, ultrasonic and wetted media systems as the low-energy group and steam canisters, infrared, direct steam and gas-fired units as the energy-intensive group. Steam keeps its place where there is no room for the evaporation length a fog system needs (Condair lists a longer installation length for high-pressure units), or where the owner requires it: Condair's overview marks wetted media and ultrasonic as not suitable for healthcare. For the steam side, see our commercial steam humidifier lesson.

Our own caution: in winter the evaporative cooling is not free, because the heating coil must make up the temperature the evaporation takes out.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own calculation. Every input is an assumption chosen for teaching, not a design.

Assumptions. Airflow 10,000 cfm of standard-density air entering the humidifier at 70 F with a humidity ratio of 0.0040 lb water per lb dry air. Target 0.0070 lb/lb, so the rise (ΔW) is 0.0030. Water is supplied at room temperature, and we ignore the small sensible heat of the water itself. Latent heat of evaporation: 1053.7 Btu/lb at 70 F (Engineering Toolbox).

  1. Where 4.5 comes from. Air at 70 F weighs about 0.0749 lb/ft³ (Engineering Toolbox). Multiply by 60 minutes per hour: 0.0749 × 60 = 4.49, so we use 4.5 lb of air per hour per cfm. Engineering Toolbox's latent heat constant of 4840 is consistent: 4840 ÷ 4.5 is about 1,076 Btu/lb, close to the latent heat values used below.
  2. Water rate. 4.5 × 10,000 cfm × 0.0030 = 135 lb/h.
  3. Heat taken from the air. 135 lb/h × 1053.7 Btu/lb = about 142,250 Btu/h, or 11.9 tons of sensible cooling (12,000 Btu/h per ton, a standard unit conversion).
  4. Temperature drop. Engineering Toolbox gives sensible heat as 1.08 × cfm × ΔT. So ΔT = 142,250 ÷ (1.08 × 10,000) = 13.2 F, and the air leaves near 56.8 F.
  5. Sanity check. By our own calculation (Magnus vapor-pressure formula), air at 56.8 F saturates near 0.0098 lb/lb, so the leaving air is about 71 percent RH, below saturation.
  6. Steam comparison. Boiling the same 135 lb/h takes at least 135 × 970.4 Btu/lb (latent heat at the boiling point, Engineering Toolbox) = about 131,000 Btu/h, roughly 38 kW at 3,412 Btu/h per kW (standard unit conversion), before heating the water to boiling. That heat goes into the air.

The drop depends on ΔW only, not on airflow.

Field card: Adiabatic Humidifiers in Air Handlers

Water in, heat out of the air, and no standing water.

  • Adiabatic: water evaporates using heat from the air, so the air cools as it humidifies.
  • Water rate (lb/h) = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW (lb/lb). Cooling (Btu/h) = lb/h × about 1,050.
  • Types: wetted media, high-pressure fog, compressed-air atomizing, ultrasonic.
  • Ultrasonic and fog units need RO or DI water. Hard water becomes white dust.
  • Check eliminator, drain pan, trap and slope for standing water.
  • Follow the maker's flush, drain-down and disinfection steps after idle periods.
  • Humidifiers are devices CDC says may need their own water management program.
  • In cold weather, the evaporative cooling must be made up by the heating coil.
