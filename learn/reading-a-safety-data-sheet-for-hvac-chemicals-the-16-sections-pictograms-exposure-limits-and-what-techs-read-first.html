Reading a Safety Data Sheet for HVAC Chemicals: The 16 Sections, Pictograms, Exposure Limits and What Techs Read First

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every refrigerant cylinder, coil cleaner and water treatment jug on a truck has a safety data sheet (SDS) behind it. Most techs never open it. This lesson shows how the sheet is laid out, which five sections answer most jobsite questions, and what your employer owes you.

What an SDS is and why the layout is fixed

The federal Hazard Communication Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1200, requires each SDS to carry at least 16 numbered sections with headings, in a fixed order (paragraph (g)(2)). Appendix D of the standard lists what goes in each one. Because the order never changes, you can learn once where to look. A sheet for a gas cylinder and a sheet for a liquid cleaner have the same skeleton, even when the content differs.

Appendix D marks sections 1 through 11 and 16 as required content. Sections 12 through 15 (ecological, disposal, transport and regulatory information) must still appear as headings, but OSHA says it will not enforce their information requirements, because other agencies cover those topics. Appendix D also says that if nothing is known for a subheading, the sheet must say so rather than leave it blank. A blank is a flag to ask the supplier.

The 16 sections in plain words

  • 1. Identification. Product name, supplier contact, an emergency phone number, recommended uses.
  • 2. Hazard identification. The classification, signal word, hazard statements, pictograms and precautionary statements.
  • 3. Composition. Ingredient names and concentrations. A trade secret claim must be stated as such.
  • 4. First aid. What to do for each exposure route, plus key symptoms and when to get medical care.
  • 5. Fire-fighting. Suitable extinguishing media and hazards that arise in a fire, including hazardous combustion products.
  • 6. Accidental release. Spill precautions, protective equipment, containment and cleanup.
  • 7. Handling and storage. Safe handling and storage conditions, including incompatibilities.
  • 8. Exposure controls and personal protection. Exposure limits, engineering controls and PPE.
  • 9. Physical and chemical properties. Appearance, boiling point, flash point, pH, density and similar.
  • 10. Stability and reactivity. Conditions and materials to avoid, hazardous reactions and hazardous decomposition products.
  • 11. Toxicology. Routes of exposure, symptoms, acute and chronic effects.
  • 12 to 15. Ecological, disposal, transport and regulatory information.
  • 16. Other information. Includes the date the sheet was prepared or last revised.

The five a tech reads first

You do not need to read 16 sections before opening a jug. For field decisions, these five do most of the work. This ranking is our own teaching choice, not an OSHA rule.

  • Section 2, hazards. Gives the signal word, pictograms and hazard statements. Read it to learn what the product can do to you.
  • Section 4, first aid. Read it before you need it, so the eyewash and the phone number are not a surprise.
  • Section 7, handling and storage. Tells you what not to store or mix it with, and how to keep the container.
  • Section 8, exposure limits and PPE. Lists the limits (OSHA, ACGIH or others) and the protective gear the supplier recommends.
  • Section 10, stability and reactivity. Names conditions to avoid, incompatible materials and decomposition products.

Close behind are section 6 (what to do in a spill) and section 3 (what is in it), which matter when something goes wrong or when you are choosing between two products. Section 1 has the emergency phone number.

Signal words and pictograms

A label carries one signal word. The standard uses only two: Danger for the more severe hazards and Warning for the less severe. Appendix C adds that when Danger is on a label, Warning must not be. Examples from Appendix C: Danger goes with skin corrosion (Category 1A to 1C, "causes severe skin burns and eye damage") and serious eye damage. Warning goes with skin irritation, serious eye irritation, and every category of gas under pressure.

Two pictograms matter most to HVAC work. The gas cylinder pictogram goes on gases under pressure; the matching statement for compressed and liquefied gas is "contains gas under pressure; may explode if heated," while refrigerated liquefied gas says it "may cause cryogenic burns or injury." The corrosion pictogram goes with skin corrosion and serious eye damage. Appendix C also says the exclamation mark must not be printed for skin or eye irritation when the corrosion pictogram is used, so a corrosive cleaner will not show both for the same effect. The pictograms live on the shipped label and in section 2 of the SDS. A signal word does not tell you how a product compares with one from another supplier; read the hazard statements.

Refrigerant SDS points

Asphyxiation. Breathing refrigerant is a hazard even where it is not flammable. The NIOSH pocket guide entry for one older refrigerant gas lists dizziness, oxygen deprivation, loss of consciousness, irregular heartbeat and cardiac arrest as symptoms of breathing it, and gives an OSHA and NIOSH time-weighted limit of 1,000 ppm. The guide entry for another old gas lists a NIOSH recommended limit of 1,000 ppm but states that OSHA has no established limit. That is why section 8 of a sheet may show a supplier limit, a NIOSH or ACGIH figure, or none at all. Read which agency each number comes from.

Frostbite. The same NIOSH entries list frostbite from contact with the liquid and give the first aid as treating the skin or eyes for frostbite and providing respiratory support if breathing is troubled. Match that against section 4 of the sheet in your hand.

Decomposition when heated. A NIOSH health hazard evaluation of soldering on refrigeration systems reported that if a system is not flushed before soldering, fluorocarbon passing the leak reaches the heat source, and thermal decomposition may produce phosgene, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen fluoride gases. The recommendation was to ventilate the refrigerant away from the work area before working on a leak. The report is from 1981, but section 10 of a current sheet should list similar decomposition products. See our refrigerant safety lesson.

Safety class. Refrigerant sheets often cite the ASHRAE 34 safety class. In the scheme described in an EPA-hosted white paper on flammable refrigerants, the letter shows toxicity (A is lower, B is higher) and the number shows flammability (1 is no flame propagation, 2 is lower flammability, 3 is higher). A subclass L on class 2 means a burning velocity of 10 cm/s or less. So A1 is lower toxicity with no flame propagation, and A2L is lower toxicity with lower flammability and slow flame travel. An OSHA interpretation letter notes that several blends in ASHRAE 34 group A2L qualify under the Hazard Communication standard as Category 1 flammable gases, which means ignitable at 13% by volume in air or less, or a flammable range of at least 12 percentage points. "Lower flammability" still means flammable; the sheet governs your ignition-source and ventilation steps.

Worked example: an invented coil cleaner sheet (ours)

Invented for teaching. This excerpt is made up by us. It describes no real product and no real maker. Do not use it as guidance for any real chemical.

Excerpt, section by section:

  • Section 2. Signal word: Danger. Skin corrosion Category 1. Eye damage Category 1. "Causes severe skin burns and eye damage." Pictogram: corrosion.
  • Section 4. Eyes: rinse with water for several minutes, remove contact lenses if easy, get medical help right away. Skin: remove contaminated clothing, rinse skin with water, get medical help. Swallowed: do not induce vomiting, call the poison center or doctor.
  • Section 7. Keep container closed. Store in the original container, away from acids and from metals such as aluminum. Wash hands after handling. Do not mix with other cleaners.
  • Section 8. No established exposure limit listed. Use chemical splash goggles and a face shield when pouring concentrate, chemical-resistant gloves of the type named in the sheet, and protective clothing.
  • Section 10. Reacts with acids and with some metals. Avoid mixing with acid-based products.

Picking the steps out.

  1. PPE comes from section 8 (goggles, face shield for concentrate, gloves, clothing). Section 2 explains why: the Danger word and the corrosion pictogram point to damage to eyes and skin.
  2. First aid comes from section 4: rinse, remove contaminated clothing, call for medical help. Know where the eyewash is before you open the jug (see our eye and face protection lesson).
  3. Storage comes from section 7 (closed, original container, away from acids and aluminum), backed by section 10 (the reactions behind that rule).

Dilution arithmetic (assumed). Suppose the invented label says to mix 1 part concentrate with 4 parts water. Total parts = 1 + 4 = 5. Concentrate share = 1 / 5 = 0.20, which is 20% by volume. The hazards in section 2 describe the concentrate, so the full PPE applies when pouring and mixing, even if the mixed solution is milder. The mixed solution is the container you may carry up a ladder, so it needs a workplace label, covered below. The 1 to 4 ratio is an assumption for this example.

What your employer owes you

  • SDS access. Under paragraph (g)(8), the employer keeps an SDS for each hazardous chemical and keeps it readily accessible in the work area during every shift. Electronic access is allowed if it does not create barriers to immediate access.
  • Labels. A shipped container label shows the product identifier, signal word, hazard statements, pictograms, precautionary statements, and the supplier's name, address and phone. Workplace labels may use the product identifier plus words, pictures or symbols that give the hazard (paragraph (f)).
  • Written program. Each workplace needs a written hazard communication program with a chemical list tied to the SDS names (paragraph (e)).
  • Training. Employees are trained when first assigned and whenever a new hazard is introduced. Training covers how to read labels and SDSs, how to detect a release, the hazards in the work area including asphyxiation, and PPE and emergency procedures (paragraph (h)).

If a chemical shows up on your job and you cannot find its sheet, ask for it. Do not guess. Also see respirators, work gloves and first aid for the gear and steps a sheet points to.

Field card: Reading a Safety Data Sheet

Two, four, seven, eight, ten.

  • An SDS has 16 sections in a fixed order; sections 12 to 15 are not enforced by OSHA.
  • Read first: 2 hazards, 4 first aid, 7 handling and storage, 8 limits and PPE, 10 stability.
  • Danger is the more severe signal word; Warning is the less severe. Never both.
  • Check which agency each exposure limit comes from, or whether there is none.
  • Breathing refrigerant can cause oxygen deprivation; liquid can freeze skin and eyes; heat can break it down into toxic gases.
  • A1 means no flame propagation; A2L means lower flammability, still flammable.
  • Your employer must keep SDSs reachable every shift and train you to read them.
