Drain Water Heat Recovery Units for Showers: How the Copper Coil Works, Sizing by Length, Savings and Plumbing Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When you shower, most of the heat you paid for goes down the drain. A drain water heat recovery (DWHR) unit catches some of it and hands it to the cold water coming in. This lesson explains how the copper coil works, why length matters, what savings are realistic, and what a plumber needs to settle before installing one.

Check before you buy

Check the local rules. Plumbing codes, product listings and prices change. This page is general education built from public documents read in 2026, not an installation guide. Plumbing work belongs to a licensed plumber, and the local plumbing inspector decides what is acceptable where you live.

What a DWHR unit is

The U.S. Department of Energy describes the non-storage type as a copper heat exchanger that replaces a vertical section of the main waste drain. A coil of copper tube carrying incoming cold water is wrapped around that drain section. Heat passes through the copper wall from the warm drain water to the cold supply, and the two streams never mix. There are no moving parts and nothing to switch on.

Why showers are the best match

A simple unit only works while warm drain water and cold supply water move at the same moment. The Department of Energy says that without storage the system helps during the simultaneous flow of cold water and heated drain water, such as showering. A dishwasher or clothes washer dumps its warm water at a different time than you draw cold water, so those loads need a storage-type unit instead. A Natural Resources Canada page agrees: units work best when plenty of warm water drains while cold water is drawn, mostly showers.

Effectiveness and length

Effectiveness is the share of the possible temperature rise the cold water actually gets. With equal flow on both sides, it is (preheated temperature minus cold temperature) divided by (drain temperature minus cold temperature). A rating of 0.50 means the cold water rises halfway toward the drain water temperature. That formula is our own plain-language restatement of the standard definition.

Length matters because the cold water needs contact time. A 2017 conference presentation, which cited Natural Resources Canada test data, listed vertical units with a 2-inch coil at equal flow of 9.5 liters per minute:

Unit lengthTested efficiency
38 in.32.9%
48 in.39.2%
72 in.51.7%
120 in.60.5%

The gains shrink as the unit gets longer. The 2007 Natural Resources Canada characterization report said longer pipes add only marginal benefit because the two water temperatures converge. Going from 38 to 72 inches added about 19 points here, while going from 72 to 120 inches added about 9, and the longer unit needs more height and more copper. The presentation came from a maker, so check any model against independent test results.

A U.S. state energy-efficiency program says the best candidates have at least 4 feet of vertical drain line serving one main shower. A 2025 Oak Ridge National Laboratory paper found vertical units generally performed better than horizontal ones under similar conditions, and that at low flow a vertical unit's inner wall is only partly wet, which lowers effectiveness. The same 2017 presentation showed a 120-inch coil at 60.5% vertical but 30.3% horizontal. A RESNET document adds that no performance rating standard exists for horizontally installed units, so treat sloped or horizontal installs with caution.

Plumbing configurations

  • Preheat to the water heater only (unequal flow). Simple to pipe, but the shower itself still gets cold water at the old temperature. A state efficiency program says this cuts savings by about 20% compared with equal flow.
  • Preheat to the shower only. The City of Ottawa's advisory allows the outlet to go to a heater inlet or to the cold supply of a shower or bathroom group.
  • Preheat to both (equal flow). Recommended by the same program because it recovers the most heat. The Natural Resources Canada report calls the heater-plus-shower arrangement configuration B.

Preheated "cold" water can change how a shower valve behaves; see water heater temperature setting and mixing valves.

Vertical stack and pressure loss

The unit replaces a straight, plumb section of drain, so you need the vertical height in the right place, usually a stack below a shower on an upper floor or a basement ceiling run. The Ottawa advisory says a unit is not required where there is insufficient vertical fall. The RESNET document, quoting the 2015 IECC, limits the potable-side pressure loss to under 3 psi for a unit serving one or two showers and under 2 psi for three or more. A tight coil on a long unit can add restriction, so ask for the tested pressure-loss figure.

Double-wall and vented: the safety idea

The coil carries drinking water, and the drain carries waste. If a single copper wall corroded through, waste could reach the potable side. The presentations we read describe falling-film and multi-pipe units as "double-wall vented," and the 2017 presentation lists a double-wall leak test among the requirements of IAPMO PS-92-2012. In plain terms, we read this as two separate walls with a vented gap between them, so a leak in either wall shows up outside the unit instead of crossing over. That plain-language reading is ours; confirm with the product listing and local inspector. Because double-wall construction may or may not be required in a given area, ask your plumber to confirm the unit is listed for use where you live.

Cost, savings and payback

  • The Department of Energy lists purchase price at $300 to $500 and payback at 2.5 to 7 years, depending on how often you use it and the incoming water temperature. Installation is usually cheaper in new construction, and a qualified plumbing and heating contractor is needed.
  • A 2015 Ontario technical brief on a 36-inch unit priced at $605 (excluding installation) gave payback near 3 to 5 years with electric water heating and about 6 to 12 years with gas, across showerheads from 6.5 to 18 liters per minute.

The pattern holds in every source: more showers, higher flow and electric heat shorten payback. A single person on gas in a warm climate may never recover the cost.

Worked example

Inputs: incoming cold water 50 F (our own assumption for a cool-season supply); shower drain water 95 F (our own assumption); unit effectiveness 0.517 (cited above, the 72-inch tested value, equal flow). Assumptions for the household: 40 gallons of shower water per day (our own); 8.33 lb per gallon and 1 Btu per lb per degree F (standard water values); gas water heater at 80% efficiency (our own).

  1. Rise in the cold water: 0.517 x (95 - 50) = 0.517 x 45 = 23.3 F.
  2. Preheated water temperature: 50 + 23.3 = 73.3 F.
  3. Heat recovered per day: 40 gal x 8.33 lb/gal x 23.3 F = about 7,750 Btu.
  4. Fuel saved per day at 80% efficiency: 7,750 / 0.80 = about 9,690 Btu.
  5. Per year: 9,690 x 365 = about 3.54 million Btu, or about 35 therms (1 therm is 100,000 Btu).

On electric resistance heating at 100% efficiency, 7,750 Btu / 3,412 Btu per kWh = about 2.3 kWh a day, roughly 830 kWh a year. The same method with the 38-inch value of 0.329 gives only about 4,930 Btu a day, which shows how much length matters. This ignores pipe losses and partial flow, so real results will be lower.

What to ask a plumber

  • Is the unit tested and labeled to CSA B55.1, and what is its rated efficiency at my flow rate?
  • Is it a listed double-wall vented design acceptable to the local plumbing inspector?
  • Where is the plumb stack, and how long a unit fits?
  • Will you pipe equal flow to both the heater and the shower?
  • What is the pressure loss, and will my shower valve still control temperature?
  • What is the total installed cost and does a permit apply?

Plumbing connections to a drain and to potable water are plumber's work, not a do-it-yourself job and not part of an HVAC contractor's scope. The Department of Energy says to use a qualified plumbing and heating contractor.

Field card: Drain Water Heat Recovery for Showers

Warm drain water preheats cold supply water, but only while both flow at once.

  • Copper coil of cold supply wraps a vertical drain section; the waters never mix.
  • Best on showers; cost $300 to $500 per the Department of Energy; payback 2.5 to 7 years.
  • Longer helps with diminishing returns: 32.9% at 38 in. to 60.5% at 120 in. (vertical, tested).
  • Equal flow (heater and shower) beats heater-only by about 20% in savings.
  • Vertical beats horizontal; ask for a double-wall vented, listed design.
  • Effectiveness = (out - cold) / (drain - cold). Example: 50 F in, 95 F drain, 0.517 gives 73.3 F.
  • A licensed plumber installs it; the local inspector decides.
