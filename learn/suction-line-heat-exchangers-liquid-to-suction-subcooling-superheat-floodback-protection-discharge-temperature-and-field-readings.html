Suction-Line Heat Exchangers: Subcooling, Superheat and Field Readings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A liquid-to-suction heat exchanger is a short, simple part with a big effect on the readings you take. It lets the warm liquid leaving the condenser hand some heat to the cold gas heading back to the compressor. This lesson covers what it does, where you will meet it, and how it changes the numbers on your gauges and thermometers.

What it does

Two things happen at once, and they are the same heat. The liquid line is cooled, so the liquid is subcooled further before it reaches the metering device. The suction line is warmed, so the gas is superheated further before it reaches the compressor. Because the refrigerant is the same fluid on both sides, every Btu that leaves the liquid goes into the suction gas, apart from small losses to the surroundings. See Superheat and Subcooling Basics for the definitions we use here.

The extra subcooling matters because a colder liquid enters the evaporator with less flash gas, so each pound of refrigerant can absorb more heat there. A National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) study of this arrangement, by Domanski, Didion and Doyle, treats the exchanger as a way to trade that gain against the cost of a warmer, thinner suction gas.

Where you will see one

  • Commercial and low-temperature refrigeration. Refrigeration piping can include an exchanger, such as a pipe-in-pipe or clamped-on type, between the liquid line and the suction line. We did not find a government or university source on how often this is done, so we make no claim about how common it is.
  • Capillary-tube appliances. In household refrigerators and freezers the capillary tube is often joined to the suction line along part of its length (soldered or run inside it) so the two exchange heat. A Purdue University doctoral dissertation on capillary tube and suction line heat exchangers states that these are used in household refrigerators and freezers, so their design affects energy use. See Capillary Tube Systems; a heat-exchanging cap tube makes the charge even more sensitive.
  • Transcritical CO2. A University of Illinois paper on a CO2 refrigerated container design (given at a Purdue conference) reports a modeled cycle COP of 0.567 for the basic direct-expansion layout and 0.767 with an internal heat exchanger added, a gain it states as 35.3 percent. That is one design under its own conditions, not a rule for every system. For the basics of the cycle, see CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems.

Which refrigerants gain and which lose

The NIST paper is a theoretical study. It finds that whether the exchanger helps depends on the operating conditions and on three fluid properties: heat capacity, latent heat and the coefficient of thermal expansion, with heat capacity having the biggest influence. It sorts fluids by how they do in the plain cycle. Fluids that perform well in the plain cycle are only marginally affected, and the effect on COP and volumetric capacity may be positive or negative. Fluids that perform poorly in the plain cycle benefit from the exchanger. The abstract we read does not name individual refrigerants, so we do not either. Before you add or remove one on a real system, check the equipment maker's data for that refrigerant and that unit.

Compressor protection and its price

Floodback. Any liquid that leaves the evaporator unboiled meets the warm liquid line inside the exchanger and has a chance to boil off before it reaches the compressor. That is why designers like the arrangement. It is not a cure for a bad feed: a flooded evaporator or an overcharge can send more liquid than the exchanger can boil. This is our explanation of how it works, not a figure from a source. Compare CPR valves and crankcase heaters for other protection.

Hot discharge. Warmer suction gas enters the compressor, so the same compression ends in a hotter discharge. That is the price of the extra capacity per pound, and it is why the exchanger is not free. On a system already running a high discharge temperature, extra suction superheat adds to it. Our explanation again; for how to measure and judge it, read Compressor Discharge Line Temperature as a Diagnostic. Warmer, thinner gas also means the compressor moves fewer pounds per stroke, which is the volumetric capacity effect the NIST paper weighs against the gain in refrigerating effect.

What it does to your field readings

These points are our own reasoning from the heat balance above, not figures from a source.

  • Suction superheat. Superheat measured at the compressor includes the heat picked up in the exchanger. Superheat at the evaporator outlet, where the sensing bulb sits, is lower. Know which one you are quoting; see line set sizing and suction velocity for what long suction runs add by themselves.
  • Subcooling. Subcooling measured at the condenser outlet is lower than subcooling at the metering device inlet, because the exchanger takes more out of the liquid downstream. A reading taken after the exchanger can look generous on a unit that is actually undercharged.
  • Charge diagnosis. Charging targets on the nameplate or maker's chart assume the exchanger is in the loop. Do not compare a unit with one to a unit without one.
  • Liquid line sensors and sight glass. Bubbles seen upstream of the exchanger can clear downstream of it. See Filter Driers and Sight Glasses.
  • Frost and sweating. A cold-looking liquid line leaving the exchanger is normal; a frosted one before the metering device points to a restriction, a topic our capillary lesson covers.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, not from a source. The refrigerant is R-134a. Enthalpy values come from the NIST Chemistry WebBook fluid property tables (IIR reference state). The inputs below are our assumptions: liquid enters the exchanger at 100 F and leaves at 85 F; the evaporator runs at 20 F saturation (33.124 psia); the vapor leaves the evaporator at 30 F; we treat the liquid as saturated liquid at its temperature, and we ignore heat gain from the room and pressure drop.

  1. Liquid enthalpy at 100 F: 108.89 Btu/lb. At 85 F: 103.65 Btu/lb (NIST).
  2. Heat given up per pound: 108.89 minus 103.65 = 5.24 Btu/lb.
  3. Vapor enthalpy at 33.124 psia and 30 F: 171.86 Btu/lb (NIST).
  4. Refrigerating effect without the exchanger: 171.86 minus 108.89 = 62.97 Btu/lb.
  5. With the exchanger: 171.86 minus 103.65 = 68.21 Btu/lb. The gain is 5.24 Btu/lb, or 5.24 / 62.97 = about 8.3 percent per pound.
  6. The suction gas takes up the same 5.24 Btu/lb: 171.86 + 5.24 = 177.10 Btu/lb. NIST lists 175.98 at 50 F and 178.06 at 60 F, so the temperature is 50 + 10 × (177.10 minus 175.98) / (178.06 minus 175.98) = about 55 F. A rise of about 25 F.
  7. Check: NIST gives a vapor specific heat of about 0.206 Btu/lb-F here, and 5.24 / 0.206 = about 25 F.

This is gain per pound of refrigerant only. The 55 F gas is less dense than 30 F gas, so the compressor moves fewer pounds, and it works against a hotter discharge. Net capacity and efficiency depend on the fluid and conditions, as the NIST study explains.

Field card: Suction-Line Heat Exchangers: Subcooling, Superheat and Field Readings

Same heat, two effects: colder liquid, hotter suction.

  • The exchanger subcools liquid and superheats suction gas with the same heat.
  • Fluids that do poorly in the plain cycle tend to benefit; fluids that do well are marginally affected, positive or negative (NIST study).
  • Our example: 100 F to 85 F liquid is 5.24 Btu/lb, about 8.3 percent more effect, and suction gas about 25 F warmer.
  • Hotter suction gas means a hotter discharge. Check it.
  • Say where you measured superheat and subcooling: evaporator outlet, compressor inlet, condenser outlet or metering device.
