Dirt Separators and Magnetic Filters: Protecting ECM Pumps and Boilers from Magnetite

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a drain on an older hot water system and the first thing out is often black water. That black material is iron oxide sludge, and it is the main thing a dirt separator or magnetic filter is there to catch. This lesson covers where the sludge comes from, why newer circulators and boilers tolerate it poorly, where the catcher goes, how to blow it down, and what to do before a new boiler is connected.

Check the manual in front of you

Check the manual in front of you. Separators and filters differ in design, ratings and cleaning steps. This page was built from public guides, trade articles and a few maker documents read in 2026, and it is general education. The installation manual for the exact device and boiler is the rule for the job.

Where the black sludge comes from

A trade article in Plumbing and Mechanical describes magnetite as an iron oxide sludge that forms when air and water mix in an untreated system with iron pipework. PHCP Pros says the black water comes from iron pipe and cast iron boiler sections rusting, and that oxygen in fresh makeup water drives it, so every refill adds more. A boiler maker's technical article agrees that corroding iron and steel parts produce magnetite. Our own summary: iron plus oxygen makes sludge, and a "closed" loop that keeps leaking and refilling keeps feeding it. For the air side, see Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents.

The Plumbing and Mechanical article adds that the particles are tiny and travel everywhere, settling in low-flow spots such as dead legs and the insides of radiators.

Why ECM pumps and modern boilers are hurt

  • ECM circulators. A PHCP Pros author reports losing three ECM circulators; he found the rotor's permanent magnet exposed to the water, so it acted as an iron filter. On the third pump, iron oxide built up on the rotor and stopped the impeller even though power was present. A second PHCP Pros article reports an ECM pump blocked by only a small amount of magnetite and says the pump's magnets pull iron oxide out of the water. The Plumbing and Mechanical article says ECM pumps tolerate magnetite poorly and warns of callbacks if water quality is not maintained. Study the symptoms in Boiler Circulator Pump Not Working.
  • Narrow boiler passages. The same article says high-efficiency boilers have narrow waterways, so small amounts of debris clog them and hurt performance more. It also cites losses of up to 15 percent in heat transfer and up to 6 percent in boiler efficiency within about three weeks of corrosion; treat those as one publication's figures, not a rule. The PHCP Pros sludge article adds that boilers with stainless steel or aluminum heat exchangers tolerate poor water less, and that makers may require water testing and refuse warranty claims. See Water Quality for Modern Hot-Water Boilers.

Valves suffer too. An ACHR News story about a campus residence hall with two to three no-heat calls a day found black iron oxide powder in its control valves; after an air and dirt separator was fitted, valve clogging was virtually eliminated.

How the two devices work

Dirt separator. A PHCP Pros article by a hydronics educator says a separator is a wider section of pipe that slows the water, so heavier particles drop toward a large bottom chamber. Unlike a Y-strainer it has no fine screen to plug, so it does not choke flow as it fills. A maker's manual we read lists separation down to 5 microns, and the PHCP Pros article says reaching that takes several passes through the device.

Magnetic filter or magnetic separator. This adds removable magnets that capture iron oxide, including magnetite, instead of letting it circulate. The maker's manual warns the magnets can damage nearby electronic devices, so keep them away from phones and meters.

Two different jobs. A magnet catches only iron-bearing particles. Scale, solder flux and sand are not caught by it. A maker article we read suggests pairing a magnetic filter with a strainer or particle filter for that reason.

Where it goes

The maker's manual says to install the separator vertically, preferably on the return circuit upstream of the boiler, so dirt is caught before it reaches the heat source; flow can run either direction. The boiler maker's article agrees that a magnetic filter belongs on the return line ahead of the boiler. The Plumbing and Mechanical article takes the same view: take air out on the supply side, and take magnetite out on the return side. PHCP Pros adds that separators suit retrofits onto old iron pipe, which keeps shedding rust.

Blowing down and logging what comes out

A hydronics maker's FAQ gives a schedule and procedure we can repeat as general practice. On a retrofit with old piping and emitters, do the first blow down within a day or two, and repeat every few days if it looks very dirty. On a new, relatively clean system, wait a week or two for the first one. Once the water consistently runs cleaner, three or four a year is typical, with more if corrosion continues.

  1. Confirm the fill valve is not isolated so pressure lost during the purge is replaced.
  2. If the device has a magnet, remove it first so the captured magnetite can flush out, then refit it after closing the valve. Shutting pumps off is optional but helps the dirt purge instead of being carried through.
  3. Open the drain into a white bucket. The FAQ says purge about 1 to 2 quarts on a smaller system or a few gallons on a larger one, and close the valve when the discharge runs clear.
  4. Let it settle a few minutes and look. The FAQ says reddish-brown material suggests rust.

Our own log idea: write the date, the volume drained, the color and amount of solids, whether the magnet was loaded, and the pump and boiler condition. Falling solids over several visits tell you the system is cleaning up; steady or rising solids point to an air or leak problem. Pair this with the inhibitor checks in Closed-Loop Water Treatment.

Before a new boiler goes on

A separator does not replace cleaning. England's revised Approved Document L, as set out in a 2022 government circular letter, says all central heating and primary hot water circuits should be thoroughly cleaned and flushed before a new heating appliance is installed, and that a suitable chemical inhibitor should be added to the primary circuit. A university technical guideline in British Columbia asks for each loop to be flushed and cleaned with a cleaning chemical circulated for 24 hours, or as the cleaning company recommends, with a water treatment company confirming the chemicals are removed. A U.S. university standard requires new piping to be cleaned, flushed and treated before startup, with test results approved before connection. One PHCP Pros author runs cleaner about 4 to 6 hours at operating temperature, flushes until clear, then adds inhibitor. Our own lesson on this is Flushing and Cleaning an Old Heating System.

Worked example: why the wider body lets particles drop

Assumptions (our own, for teaching): 10 gpm of water; pipe of 1.049 in. inside diameter (a common 1 in. steel pipe); a separator body with a 4.0 in. inside diameter. Real bodies vary, so read the maker's data.

  1. Convert flow: 10 gpm x 0.13368 ft³/gal / 60 s = 0.02228 ft³/s.
  2. Pipe area: π x (1.049 / 2)² = 0.8643 in²; divided by 144 = 0.006002 ft².
  3. Pipe velocity: 0.02228 / 0.006002 = 3.71 ft/s.
  4. Body area: π x (4.0 / 2)² = 12.57 in²; divided by 144 = 0.08727 ft².
  5. Body velocity: 0.02228 / 0.08727 = 0.255 ft/s.

The water slows by a factor of (4.0 / 1.049)², about 14.5, so a particle in the body is carried along by a gentle current instead of a brisk one and has time to sink to the chamber. It is not a rating; size from the maker's flow tables.

Take care with magnets. Remove magnets only as the manual directs, hold them away from electronics, and open drains only on a system that is within the device's pressure and temperature rating, with a hose or bucket ready for hot water.

Field card: Dirt separators and magnetic filters

Air out on the supply side, iron out on the return side.

  • Black sludge is iron oxide from iron and steel parts plus oxygen.
  • ECM rotor magnets collect it; narrow boiler passages clog on it.
  • Install on the return, upstream of the boiler, per the manual.
  • Blow down early on a retrofit, then a few times a year.
  • Magnet out first, white bucket, log color, volume and solids.
  • Clean, flush and inhibit before a new boiler goes on.
