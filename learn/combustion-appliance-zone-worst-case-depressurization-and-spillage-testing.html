Combustion Appliance Zone: Worst-Case Depressurization and Spillage Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "worst case depressurization test CAZ," here is the plain version: a tester sets the house up so its fans pull as hard as they can on the room holding the gas appliances, measures how far that room's pressure drops, and then checks whether the flue gas still goes up the vent. This lesson explains the room (the CAZ), the steps, the published pressure limits, the spillage and CO checks, and what fixes look like.

Check the standard in your program

Check the standard in your program. Limits and timings differ between documents and change between editions. We read a 2012 BPI standards document, a DOE training deck, a DOE-linked weatherization field guide that reprints the DOE Standard Work Specifications (SWS) limits table, and an Energy Conservatory guide. We did not read the current ANSI/BPI-1200 text, and the SWS website itself would not load for us. Use this page to learn the idea, and use the edition your employer or program requires for real jobs. This is not a how-to for homeowners.

What a combustion appliance zone is

A DOE-linked weatherization field guide puts it simply: the combustion appliance zone, or CAZ, is "an area containing one or more combustion appliances." In a typical house that is the basement or utility room holding a furnace and water heater, or a closet with a louvered door. The key idea in the DOE training material is that natural-draft appliances draw their combustion air from the CAZ, and the CAZ pressure is measured against outdoors. If the CAZ pressure drops, the chimney has less pull to work with. See Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained for why Category I appliances are the ones at risk.

Why tight houses and big fans cause trouble

Exhaust fans, a clothes dryer and a range hood move air out of the house. If the house is tight, replacement air does not arrive fast enough and the whole building drops below outdoor pressure. Natural Resources Canada explains that this can reverse airflow in a chimney so that air flows down it instead of up, which is backdrafting. The EPA lists back-drafting from furnaces and gas water heaters as a source of carbon monoxide indoors. The Building America Solution Center says Category I appliances take air from the CAZ and are "susceptible to backdrafting," and that exhaust fans or a fireplace could depressurize a tight home. Many people forget that weatherization itself makes the house tighter, which is why the DOE weatherization material says to run the draft test before and after the work. For the spill-switch and draft-hood side, read Water Heater Backdrafting. For the fans, see Kitchen Range Hoods at Home and Clothes Dryer Vents.

The worst-case depressurization test, step by step

The procedure below follows the DOE-linked field guide's summary of the SWS test and the 2012 BPI standards document. Details such as which fans to include differ by edition.

  1. Set up. Close exterior doors, windows and fireplace dampers. Turn the combustion appliances off or to pilot, and clean the dryer lint filter first.
  2. Base pressure. Measure the CAZ pressure relative to outdoors with the appliances off. BPI calls this the base pressure. The Energy Conservatory notes its digital gauge has a baseline feature that zeroes this reading so the display shows only the change.
  3. Worst case. Turn on the dryer and all exhaust fans. Open and close interior doors, including the CAZ door, until the CAZ is as negative as it gets. A smoke puffer at doors helps find the worst positions. Then turn on the furnace air handler (without burner) and adjust doors again, because leaky return ducts in the CAZ can make things worse.
  4. Subtract. BPI states it directly: the worst-case depressurization is the difference between the worst-case pressure and the base pressure.
  5. Compare to the limit. Use the table for that venting arrangement. Windy days make readings unreliable; the Energy Conservatory suggests cycling the fans to get a good reading.

Published limits by appliance type

These are the limits for the CAZ depressurization (the difference from step 4). The 2012 BPI document, the Energy Conservatory guide and the field-guide copy of the SWS table agree on the rows below, with minor wording differences. Treat them as limits in pascals (Pa), as magnitudes.

Venting arrangementLimit (Pa)
Natural-draft water heater alone2
Natural-draft water heater common-vented with a natural-draft furnace or boiler3
Individual natural-draft furnace or boiler5
Mechanically assisted (induced draft) furnace or boiler alone15
Power vent or sealed combustion appliances25 or more (BPI 2012 lists 50)

Other documents disagree. A DOE training deck compared four sources and showed an orphan water heater at 2 Pa in a 1996 program but 5 Pa in a Canadian standard and RESNET, with a BPI footnote of 5 Pa for a properly sized chimney and 2 Pa if oversized. That is why you use the edition your program names, not a number from memory.

The spillage test

Pressure alone does not decide it. With the house in worst-case mode, you fire the appliances and look for spillage at the draft hood. The Energy Conservatory says to test the smallest-input appliance first when appliances share a vent, then run both together, and to check for flame rollout when the burner lights. Check with chemical smoke at the draft diverter at the end of the test period, not with a flame.

The clock varies. The Energy Conservatory lists 3.0 minutes for water heaters and gravity furnaces and boilers, and 2.0 minutes for space heaters. The DOE-linked field guide says spillage that continues for 2 minutes or more needs corrective action. A DOE training deck showed 1 minute for a furnace and 3 for a water heater, and the 2012 BPI document fails an appliance that spills more than 60 seconds after startup. Cold vents often spill for a short time at startup, which is why the time limit exists. If spillage continues, turn off the fans or open a window and recheck. If spillage stops, the cause is pressure. If it does not, the Energy Conservatory says to check the flue and chimney for obstructions and sizing. See Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat.

The CO checks

The field guide measures CO in the undiluted flue gas after 5 minutes of operation at worst-case depressurization, and stops testing if CO in the room exceeds 35 ppm. The 2012 BPI document uses the same 35 ppm ambient stop and gives action levels for flue CO. In its table, 0 to 25 ppm with a pass means proceed; 26 to 100 ppm with a pass means recommend the CO problem be fixed; and 26 to 100 ppm that fails only at worst case means recommend a service call or home repairs. Levels above 400 ppm mean stop work or, with spillage, shut off the fuel and have service called immediately. Other documents print different flue CO fail lines (the field guide uses 200 ppm as measured), so again follow your program. Everyone in the building should have working alarms: Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do, and Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Worked example (invented numbers)

A basement holds a natural-draft water heater and a natural-draft furnace, both common-vented into one masonry chimney. The tight house has a range hood, a dryer and two bath fans. Gauge: CAZ relative to outdoors.

Step 1, base pressure. Appliances off, doors and windows closed: -1.0 Pa.

Step 2, fans and doors. Dryer, hood and bath fans on: -4.0 Pa. Closing the basement door to the first floor: -5.5 Pa.

Step 3, air handler. Air handler on, doors readjusted: -6.0 Pa. This is the most negative reading, so it is the worst case.

Step 4, subtract. -6.0 - (-1.0) = -5.0 Pa. In inches of water column, using 1 Pa as about 0.004 in., that is 5.0 x 0.004 = 0.02 in. w.c.

Step 5, compare. The pair is a natural-draft water heater common-vented with a natural-draft furnace, so the limit is 3 Pa. 5.0 is greater than 3, so the house exceeds the limit by 2.0 Pa, or 5.0 / 3 = 1.67 times the limit.

Step 6, spillage. Water heater fired alone first (smallest input), with a 3.0 minute period: chemical smoke at the draft hood is pushed out at 3:00. That is a fail. Furnace then fired with the water heater: furnace hood draws smoke in, water heater hood still spills. Fans off and a window opened: spillage stops within a minute, so the cause is house pressure, not a blocked chimney.

Step 7, CO. Flue CO at the water heater after 5 minutes at worst case: 40 ppm undiluted. Ambient CAZ CO: 4 ppm, under the 35 ppm stop. By the 2012 BPI table, 26 to 100 ppm that fails only at worst case calls for a service call or repairs.

Step 8, fix and retest. The crew adds a make-up air path for the range hood and dryer and seals return leaks at the furnace. Retest: base -1.0 Pa, worst case -3.2 Pa. 3.2 - 1.0 = 2.2 Pa depressurization, which is under 3 Pa, and no spillage at 3.0 minutes. If the numbers had not come down enough, the next step is a sealed combustion replacement.

What fixes look like

  • Reduce the pull. The Energy Conservatory lists duct repair, undercutting doors, transfer grilles, reducing exhaust capacity and teaching occupants safe fan use. The field guide adds sealing return leaks and isolating the CAZ from returns and fans.
  • Make-up air. Provide make-up air for dryers and exhaust fans, or a combustion air inlet to the CAZ (field guide). Sizing combustion air openings is in Combustion Air in Closets and Basements.
  • Fix the vent. Remove obstructions, repair disconnects and leaks, check sizing, and add a liner or cap.
  • Sealed combustion. The Building America Solution Center says direct-vent equipment takes air from outdoors through a sealed pipe, so "the risk of backdrafting is eliminated."

Field card: Worst-Case Depressurization and Spillage

Measure the change, not the raw reading.

  • CAZ: the area holding the combustion appliances; measured against outdoors.
  • Worst-case depressurization = worst-case pressure minus base pressure.
  • Limits (BPI 2012, SWS table): water heater alone 2 Pa; water heater with natural-draft furnace 3 Pa; individual natural-draft furnace 5 Pa.
  • Spillage test period varies by source: about 1 to 3 minutes; use your program's number.
  • CO: stop if the room exceeds 35 ppm; flue CO action levels start at 26 ppm in BPI 2012.
  • Example: -6.0 - (-1.0) = -5.0 Pa against a 3 Pa limit fails.
  • Fixes: reduce fan pull, add make-up air, fix the vent, or go sealed combustion.
