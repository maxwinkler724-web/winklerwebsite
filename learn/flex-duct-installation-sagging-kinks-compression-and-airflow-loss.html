Flex Duct Installation: Sagging, Kinks, Compression and Airflow Loss

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Flexible duct is fast to install, which is also why it is so often installed badly. If you searched "flex duct installation sag," the short answer is: keep it pulled straight, support it often with wide straps, let it sag very little, and never bunch it up. The numbers behind each rule are below, plus one worked example.

Why flex is different from metal

Flex duct is a wire helix wrapped in a thin inner core, then insulation, then an outer jacket. That inner core is only smooth when the duct is stretched. Slack, sag and kinks turn it into a ribbed, narrowed tube. The basics of duct systems are in Ductwork Basics and Common Problems, and the way friction rate is used to size runs is in Duct Sizing Basics. This lesson is about what happens to that friction rate after the duct is hung.

The installation rules to know

The Air Diffusion Council (ADC) publishes the industry installation standard for flex duct. We read two summaries and one reproduced page. Always follow the maker's printed instructions for the duct in your hands, because they can be stricter.

  • Support spacing. The Building America Solution Center (BASC) guide quotes the ADC 5th edition as no more than 4 feet between supports, with the manufacturer's closer interval winning if there is one. A reproduced page from the older 4th edition said 5 feet. If your duct maker or inspector uses either number, use the tighter one.
  • Maximum sag. No more than 1/2 inch of sag per foot of span between supports. A 4-foot span may sag 2 inches at most.
  • Strap width. Hanger material touching the duct should be at least 1.5 inches wide. Narrow straps or wire can crush the core and the insulation, and BASC notes compressed insulation can sweat. See Sweating Ducts and Vents.
  • Vertical runs. Support straps at least every 6 feet.
  • Bend radius. The centerline radius should not be smaller than one duct diameter. The ACCA blog gives a field test: if a duct of the same size would fit inside the turn, the bend passes. Run straight out of a metal collar for a few inches before turning, and support before and after sharp bends.
  • Fully extended. The reproduced ADC page says to install the duct fully extended, not compressed and not with excess length, because that noticeably increases friction losses.
  • Connections. That same page shows the core slid over the fitting and past the bead, taped or sealed, and held with a clamp. Nonmetallic clamps must be marked 181B-C, tape 181B-FX and mastic 181B-M. The jacket and insulation are then pulled back over the joint and secured. For the sealing materials themselves, see Sealing Duct Leaks.

What compression does to airflow

Compression here means how far the duct falls short of its full stretched length. The ACCA blog says the ADC diagram shows friction rate roughly doubling at 15 percent compression and quadrupling at 30 percent.

Real measurements are harsher. Texas A&M researchers (Weaver and Culp), as summarized by Energy Vanguard, tested 6, 8 and 10 inch flex at 0, 4, 15, 30 and 45 percent compression. At 0 percent, properly stretched flex performed about like rigid metal. For a 6 inch duct moving about 70 CFM, the pressure needed was roughly 0.25 to 0.5 inch w.c. at 15 percent compression and 0.5 to 1.0 at 30 percent, depending on how the duct was supported. A separate Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study (Abushakra, Walker and Sherman, 2004) found moderate compression typical of field installs could raise pressure drop by a factor of four, and more compression pushed it close to ten, and that earlier references underestimated the effect. The Energy Vanguard author argues that for joist-supported flex at 30 percent the factor is closer to 10 than ADC's 4, and notes Manual D includes the Texas A&M data in Appendix 17.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

These numbers are invented for teaching. The multipliers are the ADC ones reported by the ACCA blog and a researcher's higher estimate from the Energy Vanguard article. The method is simplified.

  1. Sag check. A branch is hung with supports 4 feet apart. At the low point the duct hangs 3 inches below a straight line. Allowed sag: 0.5 in/ft × 4 ft = 2 inches. 3 inches is more than 2, so it fails. Sag per foot: 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75 in/ft, above 0.5. Fix: add a support in the middle. Two 2-foot spans may each sag 0.5 × 2 = 1 inch.
  2. Friction. Say the design friction rate is 0.10 in. w.c. per 100 feet and the branch is 20 feet long. Stretched: 0.10 × 20 ÷ 100 = 0.020 in. w.c.
  3. Compressed 15 percent, ADC doubling: 0.020 × 2 = 0.040 in.
  4. Compressed 30 percent, ADC quadrupling: 0.020 × 4 = 0.080 in. With the factor of 10 suggested for sagging, joist-supported flex: 0.020 × 10 = 0.200 in.

That one branch alone could eat 0.08 to 0.20 inch of a budget that, in the sizing lesson's example, was 0.50 inch for the whole system. Less pressure to spend means less air, and the farthest rooms lose first. Airflow and Static Pressure Basics explains why.

What a tech sees on a "no air to one room" call

From the rules above, these are the things worth looking for in the attic or crawl space on a single-room complaint. Verify each against the maker's instructions.

  • Duct bunched like an accordion, or a long run with extra length stuffed into a short span.
  • Sag well past the half inch per foot limit, or hanging on a thin wire or narrow strap.
  • A kink at a turn, usually right off a collar or boot, or a duct stepped on or buried under a stored item.
  • A joint where the core has pulled off the fitting. The ductwork lesson lists disconnected and collapsed runs as common, out-of-sight problems.
  • Torn jacket or crushed insulation; BASC notes narrow straps can lead to air leakage and energy loss.

Straightening, adding supports and re-making a joint are normal fixes. Check the result by measuring airflow or static pressure, not by feel alone.

Safety: attics are hot, have poor footing and may hide live wiring, so step on framing only and use proper protection.

Field card: Flex Duct Installation

Pull it tight, hang it wide, keep the sag small.

  • Supports: manufacturer interval, no more than 4 ft (ADC 5th ed. per BASC; older edition said 5 ft).
  • Sag: 1/2 inch per foot of span at most.
  • Straps 1.5 inches wide or more; vertical runs every 6 ft.
  • Bend radius at least one duct diameter; run straight off collars.
  • Install fully extended. ADC: 15 percent compression doubles friction, 30 percent quadruples it. Field data can be worse.
  • Clamps marked 181B-C, tape 181B-FX, mastic 181B-M.
  • Verify with measurements.
