Ductwork Basics and Common Problems

Ducts are the part of the system most people never see and most often blame last. Learn what they do and what typically goes wrong with them.

What ducts do

A ducted system moves air from the equipment to the rooms through supply ducts and back through return ducts. Supply ducts are usually metal or fiberglass-based, and the same ducts often carry both warm air for heating and cool air for cooling. The return side matters as much as the supply side, because a blower can only deliver the air that it can pull back in.

Common pieces

A trunk and branch layout has a main duct that feeds smaller branches. Pieces include plenums at the equipment, elbows, takeoffs, dampers, boots at the register, and grilles. Round, rectangular and flexible duct are all in common use. Each fitting adds some resistance to flow, which ties back to Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Why ducts waste energy

A U.S. DOE Building America guide says duct systems in many houses are only about 60 to 75 percent efficient because of leaks and poor insulation, which means a quarter or more of the energy can be lost. Figures like this vary widely from building to building, but the point is solid. Air that leaks into an attic or crawl space was heated or cooled for nothing, and ducts running through unconditioned space exchange heat with it. That is one reason ductless mini-splits are attractive for some projects. See Mini-Split Basics.

Common problems

  • Leaks. Gaps at joints, seams and the equipment connections lose air and can pull dusty, humid air into the system.
  • Poor insulation. Missing or damaged insulation in unconditioned space can cause comfort complaints and sweating on cold supply ducts.
  • Undersized ducts or too few returns. The result is noise, high resistance and rooms that never get comfortable.
  • Crushed or kinked flexible duct. A pinch can choke a whole branch.
  • Disconnected or collapsed runs. Often out of sight and found only by looking.
  • Balance problems. One room is too hot or cold because the air is not distributed as designed.
  • Dirty ducts and coils from a poor filter.

What a good fix looks like

Repairs focus on sealing the leaks, supporting runs so they do not sag, and insulating where needed. Joints are normally sealed with materials made for the job, such as mastic or listed tape, following the product's directions and local code. Duct repair that is not planned around airflow can make things worse, so check the result by measuring, not by feel alone.

Sweating ducts

A supply duct that carries cold air through warm, humid space can drip. The cause is that the duct surface is colder than the dew point of the surrounding air. Insulation and a vapor-tight outer layer help. Learn why in Psychrometrics for Technicians.

Safety

Attics and crawl spaces are hot, tight and often have poor footing, insulation, wiring and sharp sheet metal. Wear suitable protection, watch for live wires, and never cut into duct near a flue or combustion appliance without knowing what is there.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It gives no setpoints, pressures or procedures.

Field card: Ductwork Basics and Common Problems

Find the air that is lost or blocked.

  • Supply and return both matter. Check both.
  • Leaks waste conditioned air and pull in dirty, humid air.
  • Undersized, kinked or crushed duct raises resistance and noise.
  • Insulate ducts that run through unconditioned space.
  • Seal with products made for the job, then verify airflow by measuring.
