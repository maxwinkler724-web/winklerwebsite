Steam Radiator EDR: Measuring Radiators to Size a Replacement Boiler

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A steam boiler is not sized like a hot-water boiler or a furnace. You do not start with a heat loss. You start with the radiators already in the house, measure them, and add up their steam-condensing ability. That number is called EDR. This lesson shows how to measure a cast iron radiator, look up its EDR, total the house, and match the total to a boiler's steam rating.

What EDR means

EDR stands for equivalent direct radiation. It is a square-foot number that describes how much steam a radiator can condense. In trade writing, one square foot of EDR is worth 240 Btu/hr when the radiator holds 215 degree F steam in 70 degree F air. A trade article from HeatingHelp (Dan Holohan's site) puts it this way: a steam boiler's ability to make steam has to match the system's ability to condense steam. Boiler makers carry the same idea into their ratings. Dunkirk's manual says steam radiation is sized in square feet of EDR, based on steam at just under 2 psi in the radiator.

So a radiator rated 10 sq ft of EDR gives off about 10 × 240 = 2,400 Btu/hr on steam. The square-foot number is the one you carry through the job.

Why not just use a heat loss?

A heat loss tells you what the rooms need on the coldest day. The radiators tell you how much steam the system can actually take. A steam boiler that makes less steam than the radiators can condense will run long and may never fill them. One that makes far more tends to short cycle. U.S. Boiler Company's sizing page tells installers to measure the radiators, add them up, and match that total to the brochure rating, and warns that a boiler larger than the system needs can short cycle. Heat loss can still be a sanity check, but the connected radiation is the number that sizes the boiler.

Two more loads ride along with the radiators:

  • Piping. The steam mains and risers have to be heated every cold start. U.S. Boiler says an uninsulated steam main counts like a radiator and has to be added to the total.
  • Pickup. This is the extra steam needed to warm the iron and steel in the system on startup. Holohan's article assumes the piping adds about one-third of the radiation load, which is where the 1.33 factor comes from.

How to measure a cast iron radiator

Columbia Heating Products' sizing guide and U.S. Boiler's page agree on the basic method for floor-standing radiators:

  1. Name the type. A column radiator has wider, rounded columns, about 2-1/2 inches across per the Columbia guide. A tube radiator has narrower tubes, about 1-1/2 inches wide. Count them from the narrow end of a section.
  2. Measure the height from the floor to the top of the radiator, feet included. A forum thread among steam pros stresses measuring from the floor, and one poster had to redo his math after reading the wrong height column.
  3. Count the sections, the individual slices along its length.
  4. Look up the square feet per section in a table, using the height and the number of columns or tubes. Overall depth and length do not matter.
  5. Multiply the per-section value by the number of sections.

Reading an EDR table

Published tables list square feet per section by height. I cross-checked two tables for the values below: Columbia Heating Products' guide and a radiation table PDF hosted on InspectApedia. A 3-column radiator 22 inches tall is listed at 3.00 sq ft per section. U.S. Boiler uses that same 3 sq ft multiplier in its own example. Wall-hung radiators use a separate chart. Very old, unusual or thin-tube radiators may not appear in common tables at all. That is why Holohan sells a whole book of ratings, E.D.R., aimed at people who size a replacement boiler by measuring existing radiators.

How boiler makers list steam ratings

Steam boilers carry a rating in square feet of steam next to the Btu number. Weil-McLain's EG brochure lists the EG-30 at 47 MBH AHRI net steam, which it also states as 196 sq ft. Divide: 47,000 / 240 is about 196. The same table lists the gross figure (DOE steam heating capacity) at 62 MBH. Gross is bigger than net because the net rating already holds back the piping and pickup load. Weil-McLain, Peerless and Dunkirk each state that their steam ratings use a piping and pickup allowance of 1.333, so net × 1.333 lands close to gross (47 × 1.333 is about 62.7).

The practical rule from Dunkirk's manual: pick a boiler whose net rating is equal to or greater than the installed radiation, in square feet. Compare sq ft to sq ft. Do not match the boiler's input or gross Btu to the radiators.

Worked example: a five-radiator house

This house is invented. Assume the basement mains and risers are well insulated, so no piping is added to the total.

Step 1. Measure and look up each radiator.

RoomType and heightSq ft per sectionSectionsEDR (sq ft)
Living room4 tube, 26 in2.75162.75 × 16 = 44.0
Dining room3 column, 22 in3.00143.00 × 14 = 42.0
Kitchen3 tube, 23 in2.00122.00 × 12 = 24.0
Front bedroom4 tube, 23 in2.50142.50 × 14 = 35.0
Back bedroom2 column, 26 in2.67122.67 × 12 = 32.0

Step 2. Total the radiation. 44.0 + 42.0 + 24.0 + 35.0 + 32.0 = 177.0 sq ft EDR.

Step 3. Convert to Btu/hr as a cross-check. 177 × 240 = 42,480 Btu/hr of radiator output.

Step 4. Compare to a maker's net steam table. Weil-McLain's EG brochure lists net AHRI steam ratings of 196 sq ft (EG-30), 258 sq ft (EG-35), 321 sq ft (EG-40) and larger. The smallest model whose net rating is at least 177 sq ft is the EG-30 at 196 sq ft.

Step 5. Check the margin. 196 − 177 = 19 sq ft, about 11 percent above the connected radiation (19 / 177). In Btu terms, the EG-30's 47 MBH net is above the 42.5 MBH the radiators can shed. No extra piping allowance is needed here because the 1.333 factor is already inside the net number.

Step 6. Ask "what if the pipes were bare?" Suppose a survey found uninsulated main that counts as 25 sq ft of radiation (an invented figure for this example). The total becomes 177 + 25 = 202 sq ft, which is above the EG-30's 196. The next size up, the EG-35 at 258 sq ft, would then be the pick. Insulating the main first can keep you in the smaller boiler.

Verify the final pick against the maker's current installation manual, because model lines and ratings change.

Where this goes wrong

  • Counting a radiator that has been removed or capped, or missing one hidden in a closet or behind a cover.
  • Using the wrong height column. Measure from the floor.
  • Adding the pickup twice. A net rating already includes it. Only add more for unusual piping, as makers say.
  • Treating a bigger boiler as a fix for cold radiators. Slow radiators are often a venting problem, covered in Steam Radiator Air Vents.

The new boiler also needs the right near-boiler piping. That piping follows the boiler maker's drawing, as covered in Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping. For the big picture, see Steam System Fundamentals and One-Pipe vs. Two-Pipe Steam Systems.

Field card: Steam Radiator EDR

Size to the radiators, not the room guess.

  • 1 sq ft EDR = 240 Btu/hr on steam (215 F steam, 70 F air).
  • Measure height from the floor, tubes or columns, and sections.
  • Sq ft per section × sections = radiator EDR. Add them all.
  • Add uninsulated mains as radiation. Insulated piping rides on the 1.333 factor.
  • Match total to the boiler's net steam rating in sq ft, equal or higher.
  • Do not add pickup twice. Do not oversize.
