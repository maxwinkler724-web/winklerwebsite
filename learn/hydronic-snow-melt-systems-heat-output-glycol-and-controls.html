Hydronic Snow Melt Systems: Heat Output, Glycol and Controls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A snow melt driveway is a radiant floor turned inside out: instead of warming a room, the tubing warms pavement that is open to wind, cold air and a night sky. That changes the numbers a lot. This lesson covers how the system is built, how makers describe the heat it needs per square foot, and how to turn that into a boiler size.

How the system is built

A hydronic snow melt system runs warm fluid through PEX tubing set in concrete, asphalt or sand under pavers. Uponor describes the fluid as a warm water and glycol solution, and Watts Radiant describes glycol circulating in tubing embedded in concrete or set beneath brick pavers. The heat comes from a boiler or another low-temperature source. One Uponor publication notes that snow melting needs only low water temperatures, so waste heat such as condensate can feed it.

Most installs keep the glycol loop separate from the boiler water with a heat exchanger. ComfortPro's AquaHeat guide says brazed-plate exchangers are typical in residential work and shell-and-tube units in commercial work, and that either needs its own air separator and expansion device. Direct systems, where boiler water goes to the slab, are also built, but then the whole boiler loop carries the glycol. For that reason, a dedicated boiler is common: a trade magazine installation story notes snow melt loads are short bursts that suit a separate boiler.

Heat output per square foot and duty classes

Snow melt loads are far higher than floor heating loads. Uponor's design manual says so directly. Makers group the load into duty classes. ComfortPro's AquaHeat guide gives these ranges:

  • Class 1 (most residential walks and driveways): generally 80 to 125 Btu/hr per square foot.
  • Class 2 (retail and commercial paving): typically 125 to 250.
  • Class 3 (helipads, toll plazas, critical areas): 250 up to about 450.

Other sources use other words. A Plastics Pipe Institute (PPI) paper and a REHAU load table describe performance with a snow-free area ratio and a frequency percentage instead of classes. A ratio of 1.0 means the surface is kept clear while snow falls; lower ratios let snow pile up and melt later. The ratio and percentage are looked up in the ASHRAE Handbook, HVAC Applications volume. The PPI paper's Albany, NY table runs from 89 Btu/hr per square foot (ratio 1.0 at 75 percent) up to 321 at 100 percent, which shows how fast the load climbs when you demand a drier slab more of the time. The chapter number in the Handbook has changed between editions, so look it up in your copy.

Class 1 or 2 is a design decision by the owner and designer, not a matter of how big the boiler is. Local weather data change the result: AquaHeat's Class 1 examples range from 83 to 107 Btu/hr per square foot in the cities it lists.

Glycol, briefly

Because the loop sits outdoors, it needs antifreeze. AquaHeat calls for at least 30 to 35 percent glycol and no more than 50 percent. PPI recommends premixed propylene glycol at up to 50 percent. Glycol carries less heat per gallon and adds head loss, so the flow and pump have to be rechecked. The full treatment, including testing, is in the glycol lesson, and the math is in hot-water flow rate math.

Idling versus on-demand melting

A cold slab takes a long time to warm. To react quickly, a system can idle: hold the slab just above freezing before snow arrives. PPI gives typical settings of an idle near 28°F and a melt target near 38°F, and warns idling can raise annual energy use by about 4 to 8 times, though it gives the fastest response. Trade press describes idling as costly, mostly from night-sky losses. Uponor's specification lists three styles: constant idle, semi-automatic and fully automatic. Many installs skip idling and rely on a sensor to start melting when moisture shows up, then accept a slower start.

Sensors and controls

The tekmar 654 (sold by Watts) is a typical control. Its datasheet lists a snow/ice sensor for automatic start and stop, a snow sensor for automatic start with a timed stop, and a slab sensor when no snow/ice sensor is used. It also lists idling, slab protection, and warm weather shut down. It can run a boiler or a mixing device. The datasheet states the control is not a safety limit, so the boiler still needs its own limits. Sensor placement matters: PPI says to put moisture sensors where snow lands first and avoid tire tracks and sheltered spots.

Mixing to protect the slab and boiler

Two temperatures can go wrong. Fluid that is too hot stresses the slab and tubing; Uponor lists 200°F as the sustained limit for its PEX and lower for HDPE. Fluid that is too cold can send a non-condensing boiler into flue-gas condensation. AquaHeat says non-condensing boilers need mixing valves, injection piping or primary-secondary piping, with return temperature held above 140°F, while condensing boilers have no minimum return restriction. A snow slab starts very cold, so this protection is not optional. See mixing valves and injection mixing and boiler return temperature and condensing.

Worked example: a 600 sq ft driveway

This driveway is invented. The numbers come from the sources above; assumptions are labeled.

  1. Area: 600 sq ft (given).
  2. Duty class (assumption): Class 1, residential.
  3. Output per square foot: AquaHeat's Class 1 range is 80 to 125 Btu/hr per sq ft. Use the top, 125 (assumption: a conservative pick).
  4. Load at the slab: 600 × 125 = 75,000 Btu/hr.
  5. Edge and back loss: AquaHeat gives no numeric allowance and says its chart already assumes 1.5 inches of polystyrene under the tubing, so add 0 in the base case.
  6. Boiler efficiency (assumption): 95 percent, the same figure PPI's cost example uses.
  7. Boiler input: 75,000 ÷ 0.95 = 78,947, call it about 79,000 Btu/hr input.

Sensitivity: at the low end, 600 × 80 = 48,000 Btu/hr, or 50,526 input. If you apply the 20 percent downward-loss allowance in PPI's parking-ramp example, 75,000 × 1.20 = 90,000 Btu/hr, and 90,000 ÷ 0.95 = 94,737 input. Real jobs use a designer's tool and local weather data, and the boiler must also cover any other load on it.

Running cost (assumption): take PPI's example gas price of $0.75 per therm (100,000 Btu). Fuel burned is 78,947 Btu/hr, which is 0.789 therm/hr, or about $0.59 per hour of full-output melting. Idling for long hours costs far more than the melt hours.

Why insulation matters

Heat that goes down into the soil is wasted. Estimates vary: PPI says an uninsulated slab can lose over 50 percent, and an RPA article says about 25 percent is common. AquaHeat calls for at least 2 inches of closed-cell insulation under the slab and vertical insulation at exposed edges. Uponor's specification also keeps tubing at least 6 inches from the edge.

Field card: Hydronic Snow Melt Systems: Heat Output, Glycol and Controls

Slab load times area, divided by efficiency.

  • AquaHeat ranges: Class 1 80 to 125, Class 2 125 to 250, Class 3 250 to 450 Btu/hr per sq ft.
  • Boiler input = area × Btu/hr per sq ft ÷ efficiency.
  • Glycol: 30 to 35 percent minimum, 50 percent maximum (AquaHeat).
  • Non-condensing boiler: keep return above 140°F with mixing.
  • Idling holds the slab above freezing and can multiply energy use.
  • Insulate under and at the edges.
