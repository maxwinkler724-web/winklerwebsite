Drying Clothes Indoors in Winter: Water, Humidity and Mold

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rack of damp shirts in the living room looks harmless, but every pound of water in that laundry ends up in your air. In a small, tight winter home it can fog windows and feed mold. In very dry air it can also help a little. This lesson shows how much water we are talking about and how to handle it.

How much water is in a washed load?

Federal appliance rules describe the water left in laundry as "remaining moisture content": the weight of water divided by the dry weight of the clothes, shown as a percent (see source 5). A national laboratory report on washer design options found horizontal-axis machines left between 36 and 49 percent in a 50/50 cotton and polyester test load, and a vertical-axis machine left 52 percent. It also noted that some models already reached about 40 percent (source 6). So a load that comes out of a washer is not close to damp. It still carries roughly a third to a half of its own dry weight in water.

An extension article on dryers says a typical load can release about one to two gallons of water (source 4), and a state bulletin warns that a load air-dried indoors may hold several gallons of water (source 7). Those figures cover big or poorly spun loads. Use your own load size and spin result for a better estimate, as in the worked example below.

Where the water goes

Every drop evaporates into room air. One extension household moisture budget lists a dryer vented indoors at 26.4 pounds of moisture per day, out of 55.9 pounds per day, or 6.7 gallons, for a family of four (source 3). That budget also advises avoiding hanging wet clothes inside during cold weather. In a small home with little outside air, there is little room for that water to go, as our example shows.

Window condensation and mold

Cold glass is the first place you will see it. Another extension page lists condensation on windows in winter as a sign of excess moisture and says that when relative humidity is above 50 percent, moisture problems may occur (source 1). The state bulletin adds that moisture from indoor drying can condense on cool surfaces such as windows and foundation walls and encourage mold and damage to window frames, and that visible condensation or frost means indoor humidity is too high (source 7). The federal environmental agency suggests keeping humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and says water-damaged items should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth (source 2). For the window side of this story, see the related lesson on condensation below.

Racks near heat registers and radiators

The fire service guidance in source 8 says to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from all heat sources, naming radiators and space heaters. Fabric can burn, so do not drape laundry on a radiator, a baseboard heater or a space heater, and do not set a rack against one. Our own conservative caution: give hot registers and furnace areas the same clearance. A rack or wet towel sitting on a supply register also blocks airflow, which starves that room and can upset the whole duct system. Keep return grilles clear too. Put the rack where air can move around it, away from the heat sources and out of walkways.

Removing the water

  • Exhaust fans. The extension page in source 1 and the federal agency page in source 2 both recommend bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans to carry moisture outdoors. A fan only helps when it is ducted outside, and (our own note) it pulls replacement air in through leaks, so expect some extra heating load. See the bathroom fan lesson below.
  • Dehumidifier. Source 1 says to use a dehumidifier when you cannot remove moisture at its source. Run it in the same closed room as the rack, with its drain emptied or piped, and watch a humidity meter rather than guessing.
  • Faster spin. The laboratory report in source 6 lists a faster final spin as one way to leave less water in clothes, and it notes that different fabrics need different top spin speeds so clothes are not damaged. Follow the care label, then pick the highest spin the fabric allows. Less water at the start means less to remove later.
  • Spread and space. Our own tip: hang items so air can reach every side, and dry one load at a time so the fan or dehumidifier can keep up.

When indoor drying actually helps

Recommended winter humidity in a cold climate is low. One extension page suggests 25 percent in winter (source 1), and another gives 35 to 50 percent during the heating season in cold climates (source 3). If a reliable meter shows air below that range and the windows stay clear, a small rack can add useful moisture, with fewer static shocks. Add water in small amounts, check the meter and the glass, and stop when the windows start to bead. See the dry-air lesson below.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, with assumed numbers, not measured data. Assumptions: one load with 8 lb of dry clothes; 50 percent remaining moisture after spin (inside the range in source 6); a small apartment of 800 square feet with 8-foot ceilings; air at 70 F and 30 percent relative humidity; no ventilation or leakage; all the water evaporates into the air; dry air weighs 0.075 lb per cubic foot.

  1. Water in the load: 8 lb x 0.50 = 4 lb. At about 1.04 lb per pint (8.34 lb per gallon divided by 8), that is 4 / 1.04, about 3.8 pints, or roughly half a gallon.
  2. Room volume: 800 x 8 = 6,400 cubic feet. Air mass: 6,400 x 0.075 = 480 lb.
  3. Humidity ratio rise: 4 / 480 = 0.0083 lb of water per lb of dry air, which is 0.0083 x 7,000 = about 58 grains per lb.
  4. Starting point: 70 F at 30 percent has a humidity ratio of about 0.0046 (32 grains per lb) and a dew point near 37 F.
  5. New humidity ratio: 0.0046 + 0.0083 = 0.0130. At 70 F that is about 83 percent relative humidity, and the dew point rises to about 64 F.

The dew point was calculated with a standard approximation (the Magnus-type relation in source 9). So the dew point climbs from about 37 F to about 64 F, a rise of about 27 F. Window glass at 45 F (an assumed temperature) would stay clear before, but with a dew point of 64 F it would run with water. A second and third load would not just keep adding: saturation at 70 F is only about 0.0157, so three loads (12 lb) would push the air past saturation and roughly 6.7 lb of water would have to land on cold surfaces. A real home leaks, so these numbers are an upper bound, but they show why a small tight home fogs quickly. If a faster spin gets the same load down to 40 percent, the water is 3.2 lb, the humidity ratio rise is 47 grains per lb, and the dew point lands at about 61 F, still above that cold glass. Spin helps, but it does not remove the need for ventilation.

Field card: Drying clothes indoors in winter

Every pound of water in the load ends up in the air.

  • A spun load still holds roughly 35 to 50 percent of its dry weight in water.
  • Our example: 8 lb load, 50 percent, 4 lb of water (about 3.8 pints).
  • One such load in 6,400 cubic feet with no ventilation raises the dew point from about 37 F to about 64 F.
  • Keep racks at least 3 feet from radiators and space heaters, and off registers.
  • Use a ducted exhaust fan or a dehumidifier, and a humidity meter.
  • Spin as fast as the care label allows.
  • Indoor drying only helps when air is truly dry and the windows stay clear.
