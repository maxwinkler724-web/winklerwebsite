Gas Furnace Heat Exchanger Types: Clamshell, Tubular, Secondary, and Inspection Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace heat exchanger is the metal wall between fire and the air your family breathes. Three designs share that job, they wear out in different ways, and no single inspection method can clear all of them. This lesson sorts out the types, how airflow and temperature rise load the metal, what each check can and cannot show, and why a bad exchanger turns into a repair-or-replace decision that depends on the maker's paperwork.

Safety first. A cracked or suspected exchanger is a carbon monoxide (CO) risk. This page is for study. Testing, opening a furnace and deciding what to do belong to a trained technician working to the maker's instructions and local rules.

The three exchanger designs

Clamshell (older, non-condensing). The word describes cells that sit side by side in the burner flame path, with hot gas flowing through each cell and house air sweeping across the outside. A Purdue University dissertation modeled this style in residential gas furnaces. It predicted flue gas pressure drop, flue gas temperature along the exchanger and the metal surface temperature, and it found that the dimples pressed into the cells added a lot of pressure drop without helping heat transfer much.

Tubular or serpentine primary. Here the flame gas runs through tubes, often bent back and forth so the gas makes several passes. A national laboratory report on a high-efficiency furnace describes a high-temperature primary exchanger followed by a condensing one, and refers to the tubular exchanger of condensing furnaces.

Secondary condensing exchanger (90 percent and up). Furnaces at 90 percent AFUE or more are called condensing because they pull water out of the flue gas to recover heat. A national-lab venting report says non-condensing furnaces reach about 82 percent AFUE and that no gas furnaces fall between 82 and 89 percent because condensation problems occur in that range. A DOE-funded Oak Ridge report says typical condensing furnaces use corrosion-resistant super-ferritic stainless steel exchangers, which it prices at about ten times regular aluminized steel. It measured condensate from a standard high-efficiency furnace at pH 3.42 to 3.76, more acidic than acid rain (pH about 4.2 to 4.4),.

Condensate handling is part of the exchanger's health. A Minnesota state guide says a plugged condensate line on a 90 percent furnace can cause improper operation or shutdown and should be checked at each filter change. The federal measure guideline advises the house sanitary drain in cold climates, and notes that paint and solvent vapors in a garage can corrode an exchanger.

How airflow and temperature rise load the metal

Temperature rise is the supply air temperature minus the return air temperature. The maker prints an allowed range on the nameplate. The federal guideline says furnaces are typically sized for about a 50 F rise, and tells installers to check temperature rise and gas input against the maker's instructions. A Minnesota guide notes that a clogged filter reduces airflow.

Here is the logic, in our own words. Less air across the exchanger carries away less heat, so the metal runs hotter and the air leaving runs hotter, which is a high rise. Every start-up then heats and cools the metal through a bigger temperature swing. A Vermont weatherization manual, writing about oil furnaces, says short cycling means "the heat exchanger is seeing temperatures it wasn't designed for, and will crack," and it says to check the maker's specifications if they differ from its own range. We read that as support for the idea, not as a crack timetable. We have no source for how many cycles it takes. See high-limit trips and airflow for the switch that guards against this.

Worked example (our own): is the rise inside the nameplate range?

Everything below is assumed for teaching, not measured on a real furnace.

  • Assumed nameplate rise range: 35 to 65 F. Assumed return air 68 F and supply air 140 F, both measured.
  • Assumed heating output: 57,600 Btu/h. We got that by assuming a 60,000 Btu/h input and 96 percent efficiency (60,000 x 0.96 = 57,600). A real job should use the nameplate output or a clocked gas input.

Step 1, temperature rise. 140 - 68 = 72 F. That is above the 65 F top of the range.

Step 2, airflow estimate. The sensible heat relation is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT, so cfm = Btu/h divided by (1.08 x dT). The 1.08 comes from air density of about 0.075 lb per cubic foot, 60 minutes per hour and a specific heat of 0.24 Btu per lb per F (0.075 x 60 x 0.24 = 1.08). A federal furnace-fan test rule uses those inputs and notes the factor shifts with air density, so this is a field estimate.

cfm = 57,600 / (1.08 x 72) = 57,600 / 77.76 = about 741 cfm.

Step 3, compare. At the 65 F limit the same furnace would need 57,600 / (1.08 x 65) = 57,600 / 70.2 = about 821 cfm. So 741 cfm is roughly 10 percent below the airflow needed to stay inside the nameplate,.

What it means. A rise of 72 F says the exchanger is seeing less air than its maker allows, or that gas input is higher than the nameplate. Either one raises metal temperature. A technician checks the filter, blower speed, registers, duct static pressure and gas input against the nameplate. The estimate is only as good as the output number and the thermometer placement. A rise below the range (under 35 F in this example) points the other way, too much air or too little heat, and also needs a look at the maker's instructions.

Inspection methods and their limits

An industry guideline for induced draft furnaces, published by a trade association for trained technicians, lays out five steps in order. As we read it:

  1. Flame watch. Start the furnace and watch the flame when the blower starts. Floating, rolling or distorted flame can point to a split seam, crack or separated part.
  2. CO in the airstream. After at least 5 minutes of run time, compare CO in return air with CO in supply air. Higher CO in the supply than the return suggests the furnace may be making CO.
  3. Flue CO. Under 200 ppm needs no further action under the guideline. At 200 ppm or more, go to step 4.
  4. Installation check. Orifices, pressure switch settings, gas input, burner alignment, venting and combustion air are checked, then flue CO is rechecked.
  5. Visual inspection. Take the furnace apart enough to see every outside surface. A crack or hole big enough to affect combustion should be visible to the naked eye.

Limits matter here. The guideline's thresholds are its own, and it does not explain their basis. In our own view, a camera image is a lead, not a verdict. Smell tests do not work for CO, because CPSC and CDC both call CO odorless. We did not find the secondary condensing coil covered by the guideline, so a clean result there does not clear the coil. A crack in a tight run can pass every step.

Flame rollout and pressure switch behavior are clues, not proof. Flame that leaves the burner box, or a rollout switch that trips, points at blocked flue passages, weak draft or poor combustion air before it points at a crack. A pressure switch that will not close points at the inducer, hoses, vent or, in a condensing furnace, a blocked drain or trap. See our lessons on flame rollout switch trips, pressure switch and inducer diagnosis, how a cracked exchanger is inspected and delayed ignition. This paragraph is our own reasoning.

Carbon monoxide safety

CPSC says CO is a colorless, odorless, poisonous gas and that about 170 people in the United States die each year from CO made by non-automotive consumer products. CDC reports more than 400 deaths, more than 100,000 emergency department visits and more than 14,000 hospitalizations each year from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires. CDC lists furnaces among the sources. Both agencies advise a yearly professional check of heating systems and describe flu-like symptoms (CPSC adds "but without the fever"). CPSC advises alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. A CPSC staff review of 83 investigated incidents from 2002 to 2009 grouped breaches or disconnects of the vent, chimney or heat exchanger as 16 cases and blockages of those parts as 15, with an average unit age of 12 years. The staff said the data are not a statistical sample, and the groups mix vents with exchangers. If an alarm sounds, get outside and call for help, then have the system checked before it runs again.

Repair or replace: why maker rules and warranties decide

The industry guideline says that if a crack, split or hole is found, the heat exchanger or the furnace is replaced. It does not describe patching. Replacing the exchanger or the whole furnace then comes down to the model's paperwork. One residential limited warranty we read from a maker gives 10 years on a standard heat exchanger and longer terms for listed stainless models, sometimes only for the original owner who registered the unit on time. It covers the part only, with labor, removal and shipping excluded. If an exact replacement is not available it may offer a credit toward another unit, and it excludes damage from improper installation or maintenance, corrosive or contaminated air, and improper gas conversion. Terms differ by maker and model, so ask for the actual document for the furnace in front of you.

Field card: Gas Furnace Heat Exchanger Types

Different metal, different failure, no single test.

  • Clamshell: older cell style; tubular: serpentine tubes; secondary coil: condensing stage in 90 percent and up furnaces.
  • Condensate from one tested high-efficiency furnace was pH 3.42 to 3.76. Check the drain.
  • Temperature rise = supply minus return. Compare it with the nameplate range.
  • Airflow estimate: cfm = Btu/h output / (1.08 x dT).
  • Our example: 72 F rise on a 35 to 65 F range means about 741 cfm against 821 needed.
  • Guideline steps: flame watch, airstream CO, flue CO (200 ppm), installation check, visual.
  • CO has no smell. Alarms outside sleeping areas.
  • Repair or replace follows the maker's document for that model.
