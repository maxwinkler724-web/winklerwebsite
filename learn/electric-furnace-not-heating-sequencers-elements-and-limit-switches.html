Electric Furnace Not Heating: Sequencers, Elements and Limit Switches

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An electric furnace has no flame, no gas valve and no vent. Heat comes from resistance elements, a blower moves the air, and a few switches decide when each part gets power. When the house is cold, one of those switches or elements has stopped the chain. This lesson shows the chain, the usual break points, what a homeowner can safely look at, and what a technician measures.

How the heat is made and staged

The Department of Energy describes an electric furnace as a blower pushing air across a stack of three to seven resistance coils called elements, each typically rated around five kilowatts. The elements switch on in stages so the home's electrical system is not hit with the whole load at once, and a limit control shuts the furnace down if the blower fails or a filter is blocked.

Many furnaces and air handlers stage the elements with sequencers. The Nordyne E2 Series service manual explains it: the blower and element number 1 come on first and go off last, and a bimetal sequencer then closes the circuits to the other elements one after another until all are on. The same manual warns that sequencers should never be mixed. Newer air handlers can do the staging with relays and a circuit board instead. Goodman's AEPF air handler instructions, for example, handle two-stage electric heat through thermostat wiring and a cut jumper, and Carrier's FV4C fan coil instructions describe an "Intelligent Heat" staging option for listed relay heaters. Either way, the idea is the same: the thermostat asks for heat, the blower starts, and elements join in steps.

Heat kits are separate parts. Goodman's heat kit spec sheet (HKR, HKA, HKP and HKS kits) lists sequencers for temperature control, a fuse link as a secondary high-temperature limit, staging-ready control wiring, and factory breakers on some models. Goodman's AEPF instructions say only HKR-series kits may be used in that air handler, so a kit must match the air handler it goes in. Rheem's RBHP air handler brochure says its factory-installed heater kit includes circuit breakers that also serve as a service disconnect.

Why it blows cold: the usual suspects

  • No power to the heater. Carrier's FV4C instructions say the unit needs its own branch circuit, and that electric heat packages with circuit breakers can use the breaker as a disconnect. A tripped breaker, on the panel or on the heater package itself, means nothing heats.
  • Airflow trouble. The Nordyne E2 manual lists failed blowers, dirty filters, no return air and restricted ducts as causes of overheating. The DOE says clogged filters reduce airflow. Less air over the elements means the limit opens.
  • Open limit switch. The E2 manual says its limits are automatic-reset bimetal devices that close again after cool-down, and it says never to fit a limit of a different setting or type. If a limit stays open after cooling, the manual calls it defective.
  • Failed sequencer. The E2 manual says a sequencer whose contacts stay open after three minutes is defective.
  • Burned-out element. The E2 manual says to check continuity through each element, and that a coil grounded to the cabinet must be replaced. It also requires replacement elements of an identical kW.
  • Thermostat or heat-pump settings. A heat pump system may be waiting to bring the strips on. See Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockouts.

Safe owner checks

Stay outside the cabinet. Check the thermostat is set to heat and calling above room temperature. Check the breaker panel for a tripped breaker; the baseboard lesson explains what a tripped double-pole breaker looks like and why one that trips again means stop and call a pro: Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working. Check the filter: the DOE advises cleaning or replacing filters every month or two during the cooling season, and more often if the system runs constantly or the home is dusty. Then use No Heat: First Checks. Do not open the cabinet. Goodman's MBVC blower instructions warn that multiple power sources may be present, and both Goodman and Carrier tell the installer to turn off power before servicing.

For technicians: prove it dead, then measure

Switch off every supply, lock out and tag out, and prove dead with a meter you have checked on a known source before and after. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC and Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings. Check the nameplate: the unit may have more than one supply, and Goodman's MBVC wiring page says to check the wiring diagram on the unit because it can change.

  1. Dead checks, resistance: read each element across its leads with the wires off. The E2 manual says to check continuity and also to check for no continuity to ground. It gives 70 to 90 ohms as the expected reading for a sequencer heater coil; compare any other model with its own manual.
  2. Limits: let the unit cool, then read across each limit. The E2 manual allows five to ten minutes of cool-down and calls a limit with no continuity after that defective. If it opens again later, find the airflow problem first.
  3. Live checks, amps: with the blower running and the thermostat calling for full heat, clamp each stage's supply conductor as each sequencer closes. See Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter. A stage with zero amps and a good element points to its sequencer contact, limit or breaker.

Worked example: one 5 kW element

Power is volts times amps, and resistance follows from Ohm's law, so R = V² ÷ P and I = P ÷ V. A 5 kW element is a typical size in the DOE's description. Rated 5,000 W at 240 V:

  • R = 240 × 240 ÷ 5,000 = 57,600 ÷ 5,000 = 11.52 ohms
  • I = 5,000 ÷ 240 = 20.83 amps

Suppose the supply measures 230 V under load. Current falls with voltage: I = 230 ÷ 11.52 = 19.97 amps, and power is 230 × 19.97 = about 4,593 W. That is 92 percent of 5,000 W, which matches the 0.92 factor Goodman prints for 230 volts in its heat kit spec sheet.

Now compare a hypothetical meter. With power off and proven dead, the meter reads 11.6 ohms: within a fraction of an ohm of 11.52, so the element is good. If it read "OL" (open), the element is broken. If the clamp then shows 0 amps with the sequencer closed, look at limits and wiring. If it shows 19.8 amps on a 230 V supply, that stage is delivering what it should.

To convert to Btu/h, multiply kW by 3,412. Five kW: 5 × 3,412 = 17,060 Btu/h. Goodman rates its 10 kW HKR kit at 9.50 kW on 240 volts, so 9.5 × 3,412 = 32,414 Btu/h. At 208 V the same kit delivers 0.75 of its 240 V capacity, about 24,311 Btu/h. A stage that is not drawing its amps is also missing its Btu/h.

Field card: Electric Furnace Not Heating

Power, thermostat, airflow, limit, sequencer, element.

  • Owner: thermostat set to heat, breaker not tripped, filter clean. Do not open the cabinet.
  • Heat comes in stages: blower and element 1 first, then each sequencer adds one.
  • Cause of limit trips: failed blower, dirty filter, no return air, restricted ducts.
  • Tech: lockout/tagout, prove dead, ohm each element (R = V² ÷ W), then amp each stage (I = W ÷ V).
  • 5 kW at 240 V: 11.52 ohms, 20.83 A, 17,060 Btu/h.
  • Fit only the limit, sequencer and elements the manufacturer lists.
