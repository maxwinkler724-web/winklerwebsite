Reading a Defrost Cycle on the Gauges

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Defrost is a short, loud, steamy event and it is easy to misread. Gauges help, but only if you know what the machine is supposed to be doing.

What is supposed to happen

HVAC School's defrost write-ups describe the same core steps. The board energizes the reversing valve, so the unit runs the cooling-mode refrigerant path. Hot discharge gas goes through the outdoor coil to melt frost, the outdoor fan is off, and backup heat comes on indoors so the house does not cool off. Some boards add a short delay before the valve shifts to soften the noise.

When it starts and stops

  • Time and temperature boards start a timer when the coil sensor closes near freezing, with intervals of 30, 60 or 90 minutes in the examples. One supplier article adds a 120-minute option on some Amana models and says defrost ends near 60 F or on a maximum time, while a Carrier example ends near 65 F; these are examples only and vary by maker.
  • Demand boards compare coil and outdoor air sensors and may enforce a maximum time between defrosts.
  • Termination examples are a coil temperature in the 60s F or a maximum defrost time, whichever comes first. The exact numbers are board specific.

Learn the numbers for your board from its documentation. See Heat Pump Defrost Control Boards and Sensors for more.

Where to put the gauges

HVAC School's heat pump servicing article reminds you that in heating mode the larger vapor port carries discharge gas, because the flow is reversed. Find the reversing valve and identify which tube is always suction. Place a thermometer about six inches from the compressor outlet for discharge temperature, and place the suction clamp close to the compressor, not at the valve. Record readings at a first visit so you have a baseline. The sources we read give no pressure targets during defrost.

What you can expect, in principle

The sources do not give gauge numbers, so this part is general reasoning that you should confirm on your equipment. When the valve shifts, the high and low sides swap roles in the outdoor coil, so you should expect pressures to move noticeably at the moment of the shift and then settle as the coil warms. A reading that does not move at all at the moment you hear the valve is worth investigating. Do not use a reading taken in the middle of a defrost to judge charge. Wait for stable heating-mode operation.

Signs defrost is not working

  • Heavy frost or an ice block that stays after a long run. HVAC School says excessive frost means the defrost cycle is not working as it should.
  • Defrost starts but never ends, or ends instantly: check the termination sensor and the maximum-time setting.
  • No backup heat during defrost: check the wiring and the strip heat itself.
  • No valve shift when the board calls for defrost: check the board output, then the valve coil.
  • Fault codes on the board: read them first, as HVAC School advises.

Safety

Do not chip ice with tools that can puncture the coil. Outdoor fan blades and live voltage are still hazards. Remember backup heat strips may start suddenly.

Field card: Reading a Defrost Cycle on the Gauges

Know the sequence first. Then use gauges to confirm it.

  • Defrost: valve energized, outdoor fan off, backup heat on, hot gas through the outdoor coil.
  • Start and end by timer/temperature or demand logic. Examples: coil near freezing to start, 60s F or a time limit to end.
  • Heating-mode port labels can mislead: the larger vapor port is discharge.
  • Do not judge charge during defrost.
  • Heavy frost means find the cause; read board codes first.
