Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

A bang in the pipes is the system telling you that water is where it should not be, or moving when it should not be moving. Learn the sounds and the usual suspects before you start swapping parts.

Steam: condensate meets steam

In steam piping, condensate is supposed to drain away by gravity while steam moves on. When water collects instead, steam can push it into slugs that slam into elbows and valves. Engineering sources describe a second, often worse, mechanism: steam coming into contact with cooler condensate collapses quickly, leaving a void, and the surrounding water rushes in and collides. Notably, the hardest impacts can happen when the water is only moderately cooler than the steam rather than very cold.

To give a sense of scale, a plant-engineering article by a steam-equipment maker's engineer says condensate-induced hammer, where a steam pocket collapses in cooler condensate, can reach pressures above 1,000 psi, versus a few hundred psi for ordinary flow-driven hammer. Treat those figures as an illustration of why the second kind is worse, not as a number to design to.

Common steam causes

  • Pipe that sags or is pitched the wrong way so water pools.
  • Radiators not pitched so condensate can run out, or quick vents that hold condensate back.
  • A one-pipe radiator valve left partly closed.
  • Undripped mains or risers where water can collect.
  • Failed steam traps that let steam into return lines.
  • Clogged returns that drain slowly.
  • Dirty boiler water that foams or carries water over into the piping.
  • Boiler piping that does not match the manufacturer's drawing, or a boiler firing well above the connected load.
  • Uninsulated supply pipes that make extra condensate at startup.
  • Opening a steam valve fast, high steam velocity, or starting up cold with no gradual warm-up.
  • Isolation valves and bypasses set up with no way to warm the line up gradually.

Heating-boiler tips from the trade press

A well-known steam writer (a trade source) suggests these checks on low-pressure heating systems. Mains should be pitched toward the drip: about 1 inch per 20 feet when steam and condensate flow the same way, and about 1 inch per 10 feet when they flow in opposite directions. Compare the near-boiler piping with the boiler manual, and watch for a long nipple on the Hartford loop (see Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping). Keep low spots free of water. Insulate the supply piping. Avoid motorized valves on gravity-return systems. Dirty water or pH above about 9 causes priming, so check pH (7 to 9 is the range he cites, a residential cast-iron rule of thumb; commercial steel boilers run alkaline, to their water treatment program's band) and keep the water line at the boiler maker's normal level (about the middle of the gauge glass on most residential steam boilers), never high. A replacement boiler can also turn a wet return into a dry one and start the banging. These numbers are the writer's rules of thumb, so the boiler maker's piping drawing still governs.

Trap failures and what not to do

Failed or undersized traps cause hammer. A steam-equipment maker's article says that if a trap has failed closed, condensate can be bled off at the strainer blowdown, and that if it has failed open, you should not valve it shut, because that traps condensate behind it. Waterlogged coils can also point to a missing or failed vacuum breaker. Follow the plant's procedure, and let the line cool and isolate it before opening anything.

Hot water: a different set of causes

Hot-water systems are full of water, so banging is usually something else. Common stories include air trapped in piping, which can gurgle and knock; a fast-closing valve or zone valve that stops flowing water suddenly; vapor forming (flashing) in valves or pumps where the water is hot and system pressure is low; pipes expanding against hangers and ticking or creaking as they heat; and pumps or flow rates that push water faster than the pipes can handle. Ticking at heat-up is usually expansion noise, which is annoying but different from a hammer. Check expansion tank and fill pressure as well, since a flat system pressure can let steam pockets form.

A safe way to approach it

  • Note when it happens: at startup, at the end of a cycle, or when a certain zone calls.
  • Check the water level, water quality and pressure first on a steam boiler.
  • Look for sagging pipe and level the radiators and pipes that can be adjusted.
  • Confirm one-pipe valves are fully open and vents are the type the system wants.
  • Check traps and returns on two-pipe systems.
  • Leave pressure relief devices and limit controls alone.

Water hammer is not just noise. Repeated impacts can crack fittings and valves, and a steam leak is a burn hazard. Let the system cool and isolate it as the procedure requires before working on piping.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the exact equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints or wiring and piping steps.

Field card: Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water

Bangs mean misplaced water. Find where it is trapped.

  • A failed-open trap is never fixed by valving it shut.
  • Steam hammer: pooled condensate meets steam. Check pitch, drips, traps and valves.
  • Dirty boiler water can carry water into the piping. Check the boiler water and firing rate.
  • Hot-water banging: look for air, fast-closing valves and low pressure.
  • Ticking at heat-up is usually expansion, not hammer.
  • Never defeat a limit or relief device to quiet a system.
