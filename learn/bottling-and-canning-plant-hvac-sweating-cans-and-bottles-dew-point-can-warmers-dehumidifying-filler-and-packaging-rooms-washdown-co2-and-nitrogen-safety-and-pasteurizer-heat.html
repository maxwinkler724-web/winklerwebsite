Bottling and Canning Plant HVAC: Sweating Cans, Dew Point and Warmers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A filling line puts very cold drinks into containers in a room full of warm, wet air. The containers sweat, and the water ruins labels, soaks cartons and feeds mold. This lesson shows how to find the dew point, why the fix is usually to warm the containers, how to dry the packaging room, and what to watch for with washdown water, CO2 and nitrogen.

Why cold containers sweat

Air holds only so much water vapor at a given temperature. A university extension fact sheet on condensation in food facilities explains that when a surface is at or below the air's dew point, vapor turns to liquid on it, and it uses a cold container in warm, humid air as the classic case. A can filled with a cold product sits well below the dew point of most plant air, so it fogs up within seconds of leaving the filler.

That film matters downstream. Wet cans and bottles lift paper labels and glue, make shrink film and carton board go soft, let stacked trays slide, and leave damp cartons that stay wet inside a warehouse. The same extension sheet warns that condensation on food facility surfaces can support pathogen growth and biofilms and can carry soil and microbes to new places. We treat a dripping, sweating line as a sanitation problem as well as a packaging problem.

Finding the dew point

You need two readings: air temperature and relative humidity, taken near the filler discharge and the packaging area with a calibrated meter. Then you compute the dew point. A federal weather agency technical memo gives a saturation vapor pressure relation in millibars, Es = 6.1078 × e17.27T/(237.3+T), with T in degrees C, and states that vapor pressure equals relative humidity (as a fraction) times Es at the air temperature. The dew point is the temperature at which Es equals that vapor pressure. Solving for it gives a form that matches the 237.3 constant used in a university weather FAQ:

Dew point (C) = 237.3 × g / (17.27 − g), where g = ln(RH) + 17.27 × T / (237.3 + T)

This is a fit to a curve, so it is good for field work, not for lab-grade calibration. For the arithmetic, RH is a fraction such as 0.65.

Worked example (our own example, not from a source)

Our own assumptions: the packaging room is 75°F (23.9°C) at 65 percent relative humidity. The filled containers leave the filler with a surface temperature of 38°F (3.3°C). We want the surface to stay about 3°F above the dew point, which is also our own choice of margin.

  1. Compute g: 17.27 × 23.9 / (237.3 + 23.9) = 412.7 / 261.2 = 1.580. ln(0.65) = −0.431. So g = 1.580 − 0.431 = 1.149.
  2. Dew point = 237.3 × 1.149 / (17.27 − 1.149) = 272.7 / 16.12 = 16.9°C, which is 62.4°F.
  3. Compare: the container is at 38°F and the dew point is 62.4°F, so the surface is about 24°F below the dew point. It will sweat heavily.
  4. How dry would the room have to be to stop it? The dew point must fall to 38°F (3.3°C). Es at 3.3°C is 7.76 mb and Es at 23.9°C is 29.64 mb, so RH = 7.76 / 29.64 = 26 percent. With the 3°F margin, a little under 24 percent. Cutting the room from 65 percent to about 25 percent is not practical in a room with open doors, wet floors and people.
  5. So warm the container instead. Suppose the room is dried from 65 to 45 percent. The same arithmetic gives a dew point of 11.2°C, or 52.2°F. Adding our 3°F margin, the containers need to leave the warmer at about 55°F (12.8°C). That is a 17°F rise from 38°F, instead of a 40-point humidity drop.

Result: in this assumed case, dehumidifying alone would need about 25 percent RH, while dehumidifying to 45 percent plus a can warmer that lifts the containers to about 55°F stops the sweating. Real plants change by shift and season, so we log room dew point rather than relative humidity, since dew point does not move when the room temperature drifts.

Can and bottle warmers

A warmer is a tunnel or spray section after the filler that brings the container surface above the room dew point before it reaches labeling and packing. Its heat is a real load: the warm water or air has to come from somewhere, and the energy goes to the drain or into the room. Do not guess at warmer capacity or temperatures. Ask for the equipment data sheet and the line's rated speed. From the HVAC side, our jobs are the humid air the warmer puts into the room, the makeup air, and keeping its vent or exhaust from pulling wet air into the packaging area.

Dehumidifying the filler and packaging areas

The extension fact sheet separates two dehumidifier types. Coil-based units cool air below the dew point to wring out water, but the air leaving them can be very humid, and the sheet notes it typically exceeds 90 percent, so it can still condense on a slightly cooler surface. Desiccant units remove water directly and can serve rooms that need very low dew points, but are more complex to run and maintain. Our lesson on desiccant dehumidifiers covers the choice.

The same sheet lists moisture control steps that apply to a packaging room: seal ceiling penetrations, pressurize the space with dry air, mix stagnant air with a low-speed fan so there are no cold or still pockets, and put an anteroom with a dehumidifier between a cold room and a warmer one. Our additional advice: hold the packaging room pressure positive to the wet filler and washdown areas, so humid air flows away from cartons and not toward them.

Washdown humidity

Most lines are washed down, so the room gets steam, spray and wet floors on a schedule. The fact sheet's advice is to cut water spray from hoses, fix leaks, and keep floors and drains dry. For us, that means asking for a dry-down time after washdown before the line restarts, running dehumidification and exhaust through that period, and watching the evaporator and ductwork in a wet room for corrosion. Drain traps that dry out can also send odor and moisture back up. Our egg washing room lesson shows the same wet-room thinking.

Heat from conveyors and pasteurizers

Motors, drives, compressors and air blowers on conveyors and fillers all add sensible heat to a plant, and a tunnel pasteurizer adds heat and moisture. We do not give figures here because they depend on the equipment. Get the heat rejection and ventilation data from the equipment documents, then add them to the room load. A pasteurizer exhaust should be ducted outdoors, not left to drift into the packaging area, or your dehumidifier will fight the very moisture the line makes. CO2 and nitrogen safety near fillers

Many lines use CO2 for carbonation and for purging, and nitrogen for dosing or for headspace. Both are gases that can quietly displace oxygen. The federal workplace safety agency's chemical database lists carbon dioxide as a colorless, odorless gas with a vapor density of 1.53, so it is denser than air and can pool low. It gives an exposure limit of 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average, a short-term value of 30,000 ppm, and an immediately dangerous level of 40,000 ppm. It also lists dry ice as a form of carbon dioxide that sublimes at −109°F.

The same agency's bulletin on nitrogen says the gas is odorless, workers may not notice trouble, and oxygen below 19.5 percent is under its minimum for worker protection. At 8 to 10 percent oxygen, a person can pass out with little or no warning, and the bulletin lists fatal injury in under 6 minutes at 6 to 8 percent. It also warns that purge gas going into a process can make a hazardous atmosphere in a nearby enclosure.

  • Do not enter a pit, tank, sump or enclosed area near a gas line unless the site's confined space permit and gas testing say it is safe.
  • Low spots and pits near CO2 piping need ventilation and a gas monitor that the site's safety staff selected and placed.
  • If you are working on ventilation near fillers, ask which gas lines run there and who tests the air.
  • Do not disable or move a gas alarm to stop a nuisance trip.

Field card: Bottling and Canning Plant HVAC

A container sweats when its surface is below the room dew point.

  • Measure room temperature and RH, then compute the dew point and compare it with the container surface.
  • Our example: 75°F at 65% RH has a 62.4°F dew point; 38°F cans sweat. Stopping that by humidity alone needs about 26% RH.
  • Dry the room and warm the container; at 45% RH the dew point is 52.2°F.
  • Keep the packaging room at positive pressure to wet filler and washdown areas.
  • CO2 is denser than air; nitrogen is odorless. Both can displace oxygen. Follow site gas testing and confined space rules.
  • Get warmer and pasteurizer heat data from the equipment documents.
