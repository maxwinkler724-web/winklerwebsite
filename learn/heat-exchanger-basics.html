Heat Exchanger Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat exchanger moves heat from one fluid to another without letting them mix. This lesson covers the two most common designs, what goes wrong over time, and why trends tell the story.

What a heat exchanger does

Heat flows from warmer to cooler. A heat exchanger gives that flow a controlled place to happen. Hot fluid passes along one side of a wall, cooler fluid passes along the other, and heat moves through the wall. Examples are everywhere: a steam-to-water converter that makes hot water for a building, a water-cooled chiller condenser, a car radiator, or the coil in your home water heater.

Shell-and-tube

A shell-and-tube exchanger is a bundle of tubes inside a larger cylinder, the shell. One fluid runs through the tubes and the other flows around them in the shell.

  • Strengths: sturdy, handles high pressures and temperatures, and common in large plants.
  • Tradeoffs: takes more space, and cleaning may mean pulling the tube bundle or cleaning tubes individually.

Plate

A plate exchanger is a stack of thin, ridged metal plates with gaskets or brazing sealing the edges. The two fluids flow through alternate channels between plates.

  • Strengths: compact and efficient because the plates create a lot of surface area and turbulence.
  • Tradeoffs: narrow passages can clog or foul, and gasketed types have limits on pressure, temperature and gasket compatibility. Always check the manufacturer's ratings.

Fouling

Fouling is any buildup on the heat-transfer surfaces. It might be mineral scale, rust, sludge, biological film or oil. A thin layer acts like insulation and reduces how well heat moves through the wall. Fouling also restricts flow, which raises pressure drop and pumping energy. The cause is usually in the fluid, so water treatment and filtration are part of heat exchanger care.

Approach

Approach is a way to describe how close the exchanger gets to its theoretical best. In general it is the gap between the temperature of the leaving fluid and the temperature of the other fluid entering. A smaller gap generally means the exchanger is doing a more complete job. As fouling builds up, the gap tends to grow. The word is also used in cooling towers, where it describes how closely the water gets to the air's wet-bulb temperature. Specific targets belong to the equipment's design data.

Why trends matter

A single temperature reading seldom tells you much. What matters is how readings compare to the same unit when it was clean and at similar load. Plants that log inlet and outlet temperatures, flows and pressure drops can notice a slow decline, schedule cleaning at a sensible time, and avoid surprise failures. Trends also help separate a fouled exchanger from other problems, like a stuck valve or a pump that is not delivering flow.

Safety notes

  • Exchangers can hold hot, cold, pressurized or hazardous fluids. Isolate, depressurize, drain and lock out before opening anything, following site procedure.
  • A leak inside the exchanger can let one fluid enter the other. That matters a lot if one side is potable water or a refrigerant.
  • Cleaning chemicals and mechanical cleaning have their own hazards. Follow safety data sheets and manufacturer directions.
