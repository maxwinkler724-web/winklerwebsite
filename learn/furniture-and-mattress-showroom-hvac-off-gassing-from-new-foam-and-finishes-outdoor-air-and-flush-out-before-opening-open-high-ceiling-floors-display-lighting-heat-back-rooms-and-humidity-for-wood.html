Furniture and Mattress Showroom HVAC: Off-Gassing, Flush-Out, Open Floors, Lighting Heat and Wood Humidity

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A furniture or mattress showroom is a big, tall, mostly empty-looking box that is also full of new foam, glue, finishes and pressed wood. The air has to be diluted before the doors open, the tall space heats unevenly, and the wood stock wants moderate humidity. This lesson covers those jobs and ends with an outdoor air and winter heating example.

How to read this page

Every building has its own drawings and code edition, and the equipment maker's manual always wins. Where a number comes from a public source we say so. Where it is our own assumption or arithmetic we label it. For the ordinary storefront, fitting room and mall pressure story, see our retail store and mall tenant lesson. This page covers only what is different about furniture.

Off-gassing: where the smell comes from

New furnishings release organic gases for a while after they are made. A federal indoor air page lists building materials and furnishings among the sources of volatile organic compounds, says paints, varnishes and wax contain organic solvents, and says these products can release compounds both in use and, to some degree, in storage. The same page says studies found several organics average 2 to 5 times higher indoors than outdoors. Its advice is to increase ventilation when products that emit them are in use, and, for furniture and paneling, to put a sealant on exposed surfaces.

Formaldehyde is the specific worry with pressed wood. A federal page on formaldehyde says the most significant sources in buildings are likely pressed wood products made with urea-formaldehyde resin, such as particleboard, hardwood plywood paneling and medium density fiberboard, which it calls generally the highest-emitting pressed wood product. Products made with phenol-formaldehyde resin, such as exterior plywood and strandboard, emit at considerably lower rates. It states that in homes with significant amounts of new pressed wood products, levels can be above 0.3 ppm, and it advises increasing ventilation after bringing in new sources. We apply that idea to a showroom: a floor with hundreds of new pieces is a big new source, so treat opening day as the peak.

The same page also advises using air conditioning and dehumidifiers to keep temperature moderate and humidity down. A tech cannot remove the source, but can make sure the outdoor air actually arrives, which is the next section.

Outdoor air and flush-out before opening

Dilution only works if the outdoor air damper opens and the fan moves air. On a new showroom, confirm the economizer or outdoor air damper stroke, the minimum position and any outdoor air measurement before turn-over (our own checklist). Our indoor air quality and ventilation basics lesson covers the principles.

A flush-out is a planned period of heavy ventilation before people use a new space. A university green building manual page describes the idea as forcing large quantities of outdoor air through a new building to remove pollutants before occupancy, and says flush-out periods vary from 3 to 90 days. A university construction specification sets one example target: 14,000 cubic feet of outdoor air per square foot of floor area, with the inside temperature held at 60°F or higher and relative humidity at or below 60 percent, done after construction and before occupancy with interior finishes installed. That specification does not say whether furniture must be in place, and your job's specification may differ, so use it as an example only. That page also advises delaying absorbent materials such as carpet until construction emissions subside.

The temperature and humidity limits matter to a tech. Flushing a cold building with cold dry air removes little, and flushing a humid one can drive moisture into foam, cardboard and wood. Cold outdoor air also means a large heating load, which the worked example shows.

Large open showrooms and high ceilings

Showrooms avoid partitions so customers can walk between room settings. That leaves one tall air volume. Warm supply air rises, so in winter the ceiling zone gets warm while the sales floor, where customers stand and sit, stays cooler. Cold air also falls off glass storefronts and along walls. Fixes are a supply path that throws air down into the occupied zone, return placement low as well as high, and ceiling fans that mix the layers. Our HVLS fans and destratification lesson explains the mixing side. Whether a fan can run near stored goods or sprinkler heads is a code and manufacturer matter, so check before adding one.

Display lighting heat

Showrooms light furniture hard so fabric and finish look right. Nearly all electric power used by lights ends up as heat in the room (our own physics note), at 3.412 Btu/h per watt. Fewer connected watts means a smaller internal load, so a store that has changed its lighting has a different summer load and a different winter balance (our own note). Lighting heat only helps while the lights are on, so it never offsets the load at night or on a cold morning start-up.

Back-room storage and receiving docks

Showrooms receive cartons and mattresses in boxes. The back room and dock have their own jobs. The dock door is a large opening for truck deliveries, and the storage area collects the packaging that is itself a source of odor. Keeping the back room slightly negative to the showroom keeps odors from drifting onto the sales floor, and an exhaust fan or return path can do that (our own description). Our loading dock lesson covers seals, shelters and truck exhaust. Never put an outdoor air intake near the dock where trucks idle.

Humidity limits for wood furniture

A National Park Service museum handbook appendix on wooden objects gives limits that apply to wood furniture. It names 50 percent plus or minus 5 as the ideal in most regions, with 35 to 40 percent acceptable in dry climates and 55 to 60 percent acceptable on the coast, staying below 70 percent. Below 35 percent relative humidity, wood loses moisture quickly and can split; below 30 percent, glue can dry out, joints may loosen and finishes can turn brittle. Above 70 percent, mold and insects can become a problem, glue can weaken and finishes can bloom. It says raising humidity from 30 to 70 percent can expand wood up to 2 percent across the grain, enough to split a 2-foot panel by nearly half an inch.

For the tech, this means a showroom with a lot of solid wood should not sit at 20 percent in January because heated outdoor air is dry, and should not sit at 70 percent in July because the cooling coil cycles off. A humidity sensor on the floor, away from supply diffusers and doors, tells the truth. See our museum climate control lesson for how seasonal drift is handled in careful spaces. A showroom is not a museum, so a modest target and a log are often the realistic goal (our own view).

Worked example (our own): outdoor air and winter heating for the air

This is our own example with stated assumptions, not a design.

  • Showroom floor area: 20,000 ft² (assumption).
  • Occupancy: 60 people at a busy time, about 3 per 1,000 ft² (assumption).
  • Outdoor air rates: 0.12 cfm per ft² of floor and 7.5 cfm per person. A DOE building rating tool's assumptions document lists these retail values for its building type; it draws them from a model ventilation standard. We cite it as plain text and use these two numbers as given, so confirm with the code edition in force where you work.
  • Winter design: outdoor air 15°F, mixed supply must be heated to a 68°F room (assumptions).

Step 1, area part: 0.12 cfm/ft² × 20,000 ft² = 2,400 cfm.

Step 2, people part: 7.5 cfm × 60 people = 450 cfm.

Step 3, outdoor air: 2,400 + 450 = 2,850 cfm.

Step 4, temperature rise: 68 − 15 = 53°F.

Step 5, heating for that air: 1.08 × 2,850 × 53 = 163,134, so about 163,000 Btu/h. The 1.08 is the standard sensible-heat factor for air at normal conditions; see where the 1.08 comes from. Only sensible heat is counted, and the building's own wall, glass and door losses are extra.

Lighting check: if lighting is 1.0 W/ft² (assumption), that is 20,000 W × 3.412 = 68,240 Btu/h while the lights are on, so open-hours lighting offsets part of this air load, but overnight it offsets none.

Flush-out check: using the example flush-out volume of 14,000 ft³/ft², the building needs 14,000 × 20,000 = 280,000,000 ft³. If the unit moves 6,000 cfm of outdoor air (assumption), that is 280,000,000 / 6,000 = 46,667 minutes, about 32 days of continuous running. That is why flush-out is planned long before opening, and why heating the cold air during it costs so much.

Field card: Furniture and Mattress Showroom HVAC

New products off-gas: bring in outdoor air, then keep wood in a moderate humidity band.

  • Federal pages name furnishings, finishes and urea-formaldehyde pressed wood as indoor sources.
  • Confirm outdoor air damper and fan operation before opening; flush with outdoor air.
  • Example flush-out target: 14,000 ft³ per ft², at 60°F or higher and 60 percent RH or lower.
  • Tall rooms stratify: mix or throw supply air down, and place return low as well.
  • Lights add 3.412 Btu/h per watt only while on.
  • Back room slightly negative to the showroom; keep the intake away from the dock.
  • Wood: about 50 percent RH plus or minus 5; avoid below 30 and above 70.
  • Heating for outdoor air: 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise.
