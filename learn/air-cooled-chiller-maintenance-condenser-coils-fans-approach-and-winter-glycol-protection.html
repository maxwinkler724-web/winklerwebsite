Air-Cooled Chiller Maintenance: Coils, Fans, Approach and Winter Glycol Protection

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An air-cooled chiller is a big condensing unit with a water loop attached. Most of its upkeep comes down to three questions: can air get through the coil, are the fans and controls doing their job, and will the water in the evaporator survive winter? This lesson walks through what maker manuals and a federal fact sheet say about each, and shows how to put a number on a coil cleaning.

Check the unit's own manual

Check the unit's own manual. The manuals we read cover different models, refrigerants and regions, and they do not always agree with each other. Use this page to learn what to look for, then follow the instructions and tables for the machine in front of you. For the basics of the machine type, see Chiller Types: Air-Cooled vs. Water-Cooled.

Coil cleaning, and the microchannel caution

Many current air-cooled chillers use all-aluminum microchannel condenser coils. Daikin Applied's manual for its AGZ-E scroll chillers says standard coils are all-aluminum microchannel and rates standard coils as unsuitable for marine, industrial and combined corrosive environments, while epoxy-coated coils are rated for all three. A Daikin Applied Europe manual for its scroll chillers says microchannel coils need frequent cleaning because dirt can block the narrow spaces between fins. Its method is to remove larger debris first (it suggests a vacuum cleaner), then wash periodically under pressure. Before washing, it says to disconnect power, wait for the fans to stop and make sure the blades cannot turn for any reason, wind included.

Other makers add cautions. Trane's manual for its Aries N chillers says never to use sharp tools on the condensing coil and not to clean with detergent liquid above 50 °C (122 °F, our own conversion), because the higher temperature can raise circuit pressure enough to open the safety valve. An Aermec service manager, quoted in a 2019 Health Estate Journal article, warns that high-pressure washers can do more damage than good and that wrong fluids can cause corrosion. A Johnson Controls dealer page says York microchannel coils must be cleaned at least quarterly, with a coil cleaner and, for the premium e-coat, a salt reducer, and that quarterly cleaning is a warranty requirement. A different maker may set a different interval, so read your unit's schedule. For the same ideas on a small outdoor unit, see Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil: What to Use and What to Avoid.

What a dirty coil costs

A dirty coil raises the condensing temperature and the head pressure; Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure explains the chain. On efficiency, the figures we found are broad. The U.S. Department of Energy's Hospital Energy Alliance Chillers Fact Sheet says dirty evaporator and condenser tubes can raise compressor energy use by as much as 30 percent or more, and that when condensing temperatures rise from 95 °F to 105 °F, cooling capacity drops 7 percent. The Aermec article repeats the 30 percent figure and cites an ASHRAE study that put the possible gain from cleaning at 10 to 15 percent. These are upper-end claims, not a number your chiller will match. The sources we read give no kW per ton penalty per degree for air-cooled units, so the worked example below uses your own logged numbers.

Fans, pressures and approach

Fans. Isolate power before working on fans or compressors, as Daikin Applied Europe says. For EC fans with an integrated drive, Trane says to wait at least 5 minutes after disconnecting power before opening the electrical box. Trane also says the EC fan drive must stay energized to avoid condensation, so check the manual before you leave one de-energized for the season.

Pressures and approach. Log the discharge and suction pressures and convert each to a saturation temperature with the chart for that refrigerant. Daikin Applied Europe prints a saturation table for R32 in its manual; see Pressure-Temperature Basics for the method. On an air-cooled machine the condenser approach we use here is the saturated condensing temperature minus the ambient (outdoor air) temperature. Water-side approach is covered in Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend. The point is the trend: if the approach climbs at the same load and ambient, look at the coil first.

Compressors, flow and strainers

  • Oil and starts. Daikin Applied Europe says each start moves oil into the refrigerant circuit, so the control allows no more than 10 starts per hour. Trane says its units must be energized for 12 hours before the first start after several days stopped; the Daikin Applied AGZ-E checklist says crankcase heaters must run 24 or more hours before startup. Oil levels and changes follow the unit's own schedule; the pages we read do not give one.
  • Flow switch. Daikin Applied calls the flow switch an equipment protection control that should never be used to cycle the unit, and says to calibrate it to shut the unit off below the minimum flow. Daikin Applied Europe suggests an alarm at 50 percent of nominal flow. Trane says the pump must never run without water.
  • Delta T. The DOE fact sheet says chillers are typically designed for about a 10 to 12 °F water temperature difference, and a low difference points to a problem. Your design figure is on the submittal.
  • Strainers. Daikin Applied requires a cleanable strainer within 5 feet of the evaporator inlet, with perforations no larger than 0.063 inch. Trane specifies a 0.4 mm mesh filter at its evaporator inlet, and says clogged plates can seize and damage the exchanger even when flow looks normal. Daikin Applied Europe gives 1.0 mm for brazed-plate evaporators. Clean the strainer and compare pressure drop to the log.

Low ambient operation

Cold air drops the head pressure that the expansion device needs. Makers handle this with fan control. Daikin Applied says its standard AGZ-E minimum ambient is 32 °F, or down to -10 °F with the low-ambient fan VFD option, based on wind not above 5 mph; stronger wind lowers discharge pressure and raises the minimum, and it recommends field-installed louvers. Daikin Applied Europe says operating below 5 °C (41 °F, our own conversion) requires its fan speed modulation or Speedtrol option. Trane says to fit windbreak barriers beside the coils if the unit runs below 0 °C in winds over 2 m/s (about 4.5 mph, our own conversion), without blocking compressor compartment ventilation. We did not find a maker page here that describes fan cycling steps, so check your controls manual.

Winter freeze protection for the evaporator

Daikin Applied lists these choices: drain the evaporator and flush with glycol if the chiller will not run in winter, add glycol sized with burst protection about 10 °F below the minimum design ambient, insulate exposed piping, and add heat tape. Its evaporator heater protects down to -20 °F ambient, but only if the heater disconnect stays closed, unless the evaporator is drained or has enough antifreeze. The controller runs the pump whenever a circuit is enabled and also starts it if water temperature falls below the freeze setpoint. Daikin Applied Europe likewise supplies a thermostat-controlled electric heater and lists continuous water flow as a method.

Glycol percentage is set by the maker's table, and the tables differ. Trane lists 15 percent ethylene or 20 percent propylene for -5 °C ambient, and 40 percent at -20 °C. Daikin Applied Europe lists ethylene at 10 percent for -3 °C, 20 percent for -8 °C, 30 percent for -15 °C and 40 percent for -20 °C. Daikin Applied warns that glycol lowers suction pressure, cuts cooling performance and raises water-side pressure drop, and says to use industrial-grade glycol, not automotive antifreeze. Testing and top-ups are covered in Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops.

Keep the records

Daikin Applied Europe says the operator must keep a record of operating data for every unit and a separate log of maintenance and service. Trane likewise says to keep a written record of all maintenance and repair. A sheet with ambient, saturated condensing temperature, approach, pressures, leaving and entering water, amps and strainer condition gives you the baseline that makes the next reading meaningful.

Worked example: what an 8 °F approach drop means

Our own scenario: a 150-ton air-cooled chiller at 95 °F ambient reads a saturated condensing temperature (SCT) of 120 °F before cleaning and 112 °F after, at the same load.

  1. Our own, approach before: 120 − 95 = 25 °F.
  2. Our own, approach after: 112 − 95 = 17 °F.
  3. Our own, change: 25 − 17 = 8 °F lower, which is also how far the SCT fell.

To turn that into dollars you need the kW per ton from your own log before and after. We did not find a sourced kW per ton or percent-per-degree figure for air-cooled units, so we leave one out rather than guess. The DOE sheet's per-degree savings figures are for condenser-water reset, a different case. Here is the arithmetic with the rate stated:

  1. Our own, ton-hours: 150 tons × 1,500 full-load hours = 225,000 ton-hours.
  2. Our own, kWh per 0.01 kW/ton improvement: 225,000 × 0.01 = 2,250 kWh.
  3. Our own, dollars at an assumed $0.15 per kWh: 2,250 × 0.15 = $337.50 for each 0.01 kW/ton your log shows.

So if your before-and-after readings differ by, say, 0.05 kW/ton, multiply $337.50 by 5. Use your own power meter or controller readings at matching load and ambient.

Field card: Air-Cooled Chiller Maintenance

Clean coil, free fans, steady approach, protected water.

  • Follow the unit's manual for cleaning method and interval; makers warn against sharp tools, hot detergent and careless pressure washing.
  • Isolate power and stop the fans before washing.
  • Approach (air-cooled) = saturated condensing temperature minus ambient. Trend it at matching load.
  • Flow switch protects equipment; do not use it to cycle the unit. Clean the strainer.
  • Cold weather: check low-ambient fan control and wind barriers or louvers if the maker calls for them.
  • Freeze plan: drain, or glycol per the maker's table, plus heaters energized and pump control per the manual.
  • Log operating data and maintenance for every unit.
