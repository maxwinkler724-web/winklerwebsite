Ventilation for Electroplating and Metal Finishing Shops: Tank Exhaust, Makeup Air and a Worked Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A plating line is a row of open tanks full of hot acid, alkaline or metal-salt solution. The air above them is part of the process: it has to carry mist away from workers, and whatever leaves through the exhaust has to be replaced with outdoor air that is often freezing. This lesson walks through the hoods, the capture velocities, the duct and fan materials, the scrubber, and the winter heating load for the replacement air.

What comes off the tanks

A government plating-shop health guide says heated solutions, or solutions with current passing through them, can mist, and that the mist irritates the skin and the upper airway. Federal air-emission notes explain the mechanism: current splits water into hydrogen and oxygen, and when those bubbles break at the surface they throw off a fine spray carrying the bath liquid. Chromium baths are the worst case. The same notes say only about 10 to 20 percent of the applied current actually deposits chromium, so most of it makes gas bubbles, and a fine mist of chromic acid droplets forms when they burst. The health guide also warns that if acid carries over into a cyanide bath it can form hydrogen cyanide, so those tanks need exhaust or an added rinse step. Hazards differ tank by tank, which is why exhaust is sized tank by tank.

Lip exhaust and push-pull hoods

Lip (slot) exhaust. A narrow slot runs along the long edge of the tank, level with the rim. Air is pulled across the liquid surface and into the slot, so mist is drawn sideways instead of rising into the breathing zone. A state workplace safety rule on open-surface tanks calls this kind of hood a lateral hood: it does not project over the whole tank, and the air moves substantially horizontally. Lateral hoods keep the work area open for racks and hoists, which is why they are common. A baffle or side panels help. An energy guide for metal finishers says adding side panels to a baffled slot hood cut the required rate from about 250 to about 125 cfm per square foot in its example.

Push-pull. A row of small nozzles on the near side blows a thin jet of air across the bath toward the exhaust hood on the far side. The jet rides along the liquid surface and carries mist into the hood. Researchers at the federal occupational health institute reported in 1985 that in a preliminary test on a 4 ft wide tank, control was reached with less than 30 percent of the exhaust flow that exhaust alone needed. The energy guide says push-pull suits tanks wider than about 3 ft and can cut exhaust by about 50 percent, but works poorly where anodes, cables or bus bars sit between the push and pull sides, and compressed air used for the push can carry oil. The state rule limits push air to 15 percent of the exhaust rate and wants the receiving hood at least one-quarter of the tank width tall. Too much push can blow mist past the hood, so push flow is set once and then fixed.

Capture velocity and exhaust rate

Capture velocity is the air speed the hood must create at the farthest point where mist is released, strong enough to overcome room drafts and pull the mist in. For plating tanks, the energy guide explains that the tank's hazard class and its gassing rate set the control velocity: 150 ft/min for the most hazardous, heavily gassing baths (hexavalent chromium is its example), 100 ft/min for a middle group and 50 ft/min for the mildest tanks that still need exhaust. A low-hazard, low-gassing tank such as a nickel sulfate bath with no air agitation can rely on general room ventilation, and the guide notes that exhausting it wastes energy. The state rule publishes the same kind of tables, with lateral-exhaust control velocities of 50 to 150 ft/min. It also gives exhaust rates per square foot of tank surface that rise with the control velocity and with the tank's width-to-length ratio, from 50 up to 375 cfm per square foot. For chromic acid tanks the federal health guide recommends 150 to 300 cfm per square foot of tank surface.

Two cautions. Drafts matter: the energy guide says to keep cross-draft at an exhausted tank at or below 75 ft/min, so a door or a supply grille aimed at the tank can defeat a good hood. And more is not better: that guide says exhausting above the required rate wastes energy and can pull mist out of the solution. We looked for open-surface-tank design values in the federal general-industry ventilation rule text we read, which covers abrasive blasting, grinding and buffing, and spray finishing, and did not find them there, so the rates in this lesson come from the state rule and the federal health guide. Use the rule that applies to your shop and the design drawings.

Corrosion-resistant ducts and fans

A federal air-pollution cost manual says that for corrosive gases at high concentration, duct material should be stainless steel or plastics such as fiberglass-reinforced plastic. It also says plastic ductwork cannot tolerate surroundings above 150°F, while metal ductwork can take temperatures up to about 1,000°F. The same question applies to the fan wheel, housing and shaft seal; we did not find a figure to quote, so follow the equipment maker's material guidance. A technician's practical takeaway: do not splice ordinary galvanized duct or a standard fan into a plating exhaust run, and expect plastic duct to need extra support and fire thinking at higher temperatures.

Scrubbers, in general terms

Federal emission notes say mist eliminators and wet scrubbers are the add-on controls used most often on plating lines. A mist eliminator catches droplets by impaction on blades or a mesh pad. A scrubber wets the gas stream so droplets grow heavier and can be caught in a packed bed. Shops also add surface-active chemicals that lower surface tension, floating polypropylene balls, or foam blankets to reduce mist at the source, often together with a scrubber. The energy guide notes foam can trap hydrogen, which is a reason to ask before adding one. Scrubber sizing and discharge limits are a design and permit matter, not an HVAC guess.

Makeup air heating and keeping the room from going negative

Every cubic foot exhausted must come back in somewhere. The energy guide says the consensus ventilation standard it follows calls for makeup air at 90 to 110 percent of exhaust, but for toxic emissions makeup should not exceed 100 percent, so the building stays under slight negative pressure and the mist is pulled toward the tanks instead of out into the plant. It also says makeup air must be heated in winter, that holding it near the minimum saves a lot of energy, and that the balance should be checked yearly and after any system change. Our own reminder: slight negative is the goal, not strong negative. A room starved of makeup air makes doors hard to open, pulls unfiltered air through cracks, and can backdraft fuel-fired equipment in the building. Tank covers and floating media between work cycles let a shop cut exhaust; for the evaporation side of that story see the related lesson on heat loss from open hot-water tanks.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all ours and for practice only: one tank 4 ft wide by 10 ft long; lateral slot exhaust sized at 150 cfm per square foot of tank surface; makeup air set at 90 percent of exhaust; winter design outdoor temperature 15°F; makeup air delivered at 65°F; heating at standard air conditions.

  1. Tank surface = 4 ft x 10 ft = 40 sq ft.
  2. Exhaust = 40 sq ft x 150 cfm per sq ft = 6,000 cfm.
  3. Makeup air = 0.90 x 6,000 = 5,400 cfm. The other 600 cfm (10 percent) arrives as leakage, which is what keeps the room slightly negative.
  4. Temperature rise = 65 - 15 = 50°F.
  5. The 1.08 factor comes from 60 min per hour x 0.075 lb per cu ft (standard air) x 0.24 Btu per lb per °F = 1.08.
  6. Heating load = 1.08 x 5,400 cfm x 50°F = 1.08 x 270,000 = 291,600 Btu/h.
  7. If the heater is 80 percent efficient (assumed), fuel input = 291,600 / 0.80 = 364,500 Btu/h.

Check: 5,400 x 50 = 270,000, and 270,000 x 1.08 = 291,600. Sensible heat only; leakage air at 15°F is extra and not counted here.

What push-pull would change: if push-pull cut exhaust by the 50 percent the energy guide reports as possible, exhaust is 3,000 cfm, makeup is 0.90 x 3,000 = 2,700 cfm, and the load is 1.08 x 2,700 x 50 = 145,800 Btu/h, half the first answer. A real design depends on tank width, hazard class and tests, so treat this as an illustration of why exhaust is the lever on heating cost.

Field card: Ventilation for Electroplating and Metal Finishing Shops

Capture the mist, replace the air, keep the room slightly negative.

  • Heated, gassing baths throw off acid or alkaline mist; chromium baths are the worst.
  • Lip (slot) exhaust pulls air sideways across the bath; push-pull adds a thin jet from the far side.
  • Higher hazard and gassing means higher control velocity and more cfm per square foot.
  • Keep cross-drafts at the tank low; do not aim supply grilles at the bath.
  • Use corrosion-resistant duct and fan parts; plastic duct has a temperature limit.
  • Makeup air near 90 to 100 percent of exhaust, heated in winter.
  • Sensible heat: 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise in °F.
