Trampoline Parks and Indoor Play Centers HVAC: Jumper Heat, Big-Box Buildings, CO2 and Party Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An indoor play center is usually an old warehouse or big-box store filled with trampolines, a foam pit and party rooms. On a Saturday afternoon it holds a few hundred people, and many of them are children jumping flat out. On Tuesday morning it is nearly empty. The HVAC has to handle both, and this lesson shows how the loads and the ventilation behave.

Check the rules where you work

This is general education from public pages read in 2026. The mechanical code, the fire marshal and the engineer of record decide each job. We name ASHRAE Standard 62.1 in plain text only, because the standard is copyrighted and its editions differ.

Why this building is hard

  • High ceilings and a metal roof. Warm air collects under the deck. See HVLS fans and destratification.
  • People are the main load. Hard exercise puts out far more heat than office work, and a large share leaves the body as moisture.
  • Peaks and valleys. A weekend birthday rush can be many times the weekday crowd.

How much heat does a jumper give off?

NIOSH, the federal workplace safety institute, says the body makes about 1 kcal per kilogram of body weight per hour at rest, and up to about 5 kcal per kilogram per hour (7 W per kilogram) for moderately hard industrial work. Its example screening estimate for a 70 kg worker at a moderate workload is about 300 kcal/h, or 348.9 W. We treat 7 W per kilogram as an upper bound for a bouncing child. We did not find a free public table that gives a sensible and latent split for jumping, so the split in the worked example is our own assumption. At high activity more of the heat leaves as sweat evaporation than at rest, which is why these rooms have a low sensible heat ratio. Our sensible heat ratio lesson explains why that matters at the coil.

Outdoor air: what the table says

Washington State's adopted code table (carried over from the 2018 International Mechanical Code) lists "Gym, stadium, arena (play area)" with only an area rate of 0.30 cfm per square foot and no per-person rate. Spectator areas show 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot at 150 people per 1,000 square feet. Restaurant dining rooms show 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot at 70 people per 1,000 square feet. A Penn State student thesis appendix lists the same 0 cfm per person and 0.3 cfm per square foot for gym play areas under the 2007 edition of ASHRAE 62.1, at 30 people per 1,000 square feet. Because the play-area rate is per square foot, a real crowd can breathe the air faster than the table assumes, so the design team and the code official decide what is used.

CO2 and demand-controlled ventilation

CO2 stands in for how many people are breathing in the room. A NIST report by Persily and coauthors describes CO2-based demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) as supplying outdoor air based on actual occupancy rather than design occupancy. In a PNNL field test of retrofit rooftop-unit controls, the DCV logic used a return-air CO2 high set point of 1,000 ppm and held outdoor air at the minimum below it, allowing it to rise toward the maximum above it. Our DCV lesson covers the math and the failure modes. Cautions that matter here:

  • The CDC NIOSH ventilation FAQ says CO2 monitoring can inform ventilation, but it cannot show who is infected or whether the system is removing airborne particles. It adds that wall monitors can read artificially low if air currents pass over them and that rooms are rarely well mixed. A court full of moving air is exactly that case. Mount sensors at breathing height, away from diffusers and fans, and check them against a calibrated handheld meter.
  • The same page cites readings below 800 ppm as one possible target, and suggests a baseline per room and a look for readings above 110% of baseline when airflow and occupancy are unchanged.
  • Because the play-area rate is per square foot, CO2 usually works as a trim above a floor, not as a reason to shut outdoor air off. When the court is closed, an unoccupied mode makes sense.
  • DCV does nothing if the damper cannot move; see rooftop unit basics.

Zone the party rooms and the cafe separately

Party rooms are small, packed for an hour and then empty. The cafe has food odor and kitchen heat. Our own suggestions: give each its own thermostat or zone control and its own CO2 or occupancy signal, keep the cafe slightly negative to the court so food smell does not drift out, and keep party room air from flowing toward the front desk. A separate unit for the court means one party room does not drag the whole building to a cold setpoint. Fitness centers face the same sweat loads.

Foam pit smell

A foam pit holds a lot of foam blocks and fabric that get sweaty, dusty and sometimes damp. The smell is a mix of dust, moisture and chemical off-gassing. The EPA's page on volatile organic compounds says to increase ventilation when using products that emit them, and notes that high concentrations can linger long after the activity ends. Our own approach: keep the pit on a ventilated zone with a return or exhaust near it, not in a dead end, keep humidity down so blocks dry out, and do not mask odors with scented products.

Keep ducts and diffusers out of impact range

Children jump high and throw things. Our own guidance, not from a code table: put duct and diffusers above the jump zone, not on low walls; use round, protected supply that a person or a ball cannot dent or knock loose; keep condensate lines and sensors out of reach; and ask the designer where the netting and padding stop. Ductwork hung over the court needs structural and fire protection review, as fans do. For HVLS fans, follow the maker's clearance and sprinkler instructions, as the HVLS lesson shows.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Everything below is our own arithmetic on assumptions we call out. Do not size equipment from it.

Assumptions: 150 jumpers on a 12,000 ft² court; four party rooms of 400 ft² with 20 people each; a 1,500 ft² cafe at 70 people per 1,000 ft² (105 people); outdoor 90°F at 60% RH; indoor 76°F at 50% RH; sea level (14.696 psia). Other building gains (roof, walls, lights, fans) assumed at 60,000 Btu/h sensible. Outdoor air rates come from the Washington table, with the party rooms and cafe using the restaurant dining row.

  1. Heat per jumper. Assume a 35 kg child at the NIOSH 7 W/kg: 35 x 7 = 245 W. 245 x 3.412 = 836 Btu/h. Assume jumping 70% of the time: 836 x 0.70 = 585 Btu/h.
  2. Split (assumed 45% sensible, 55% latent). Sensible 585 x 0.45 = 263 Btu/h. Latent 585 x 0.55 = 322 Btu/h.
  3. 150 jumpers. Sensible 150 x 263 = 39,450. Latent 150 x 322 = 48,300. Total 87,750 Btu/h.
  4. Outdoor air. Court: 12,000 x 0.30 = 3,600 cfm. Party rooms: 4 x (20 x 7.5 + 400 x 0.18) = 4 x 222 = 888 cfm. Cafe: 105 x 7.5 + 1,500 x 0.18 = 788 + 270 = 1,058 cfm. Total about 5,546 cfm.
  5. Humidity ratios, W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw). Outdoor: pw is about 0.418 psia, so W = 0.622 x 0.418 / (14.696 - 0.418) = 0.0182. Indoor: pw is about 0.222 psia, so W = 0.0095. Difference: 0.0087 lb per lb.
  6. Moisture brought in. 4.5 x 5,546 x 0.0087 = about 217 lb/h.
  7. Outdoor air latent. 4,840 x 5,546 x 0.0087 = about 233,500 Btu/h.
  8. Outdoor air sensible. We use the common 1.08 constant: 1.08 x 5,546 x (90 - 76) = about 83,900 Btu/h.
  9. Total. Party room and cafe guests (185 people, our planning value of 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent each): 46,250 sensible and 37,000 latent. Sensible 39,450 + 46,250 + 60,000 + 83,900 = 229,600. Latent 48,300 + 37,000 + 233,500 = 318,800. Total 548,400 Btu/h, and 548,400 / 12,000 = about 46 tons. Sensible heat ratio: 229,600 / 548,400 = 0.42.

Two takeaways. First, outdoor air is more than half of this stacked load (about 317,000 of 548,000 Btu/h), which is why DCV and an unoccupied mode matter on empty weekdays. Second, a ratio near 0.4 is far below what a plain cooling coil delivers, so the units need a dehumidification plan; see hot gas reheat in rooftop units. Real peaks are lower than this stacked case.

Field card: Trampoline Parks and Indoor Play Centers HVAC

Count the jumpers, then count the sweat.

  • NIOSH cites about 7 W per kg for moderately hard work; jumpers are a high, intermittent load.
  • The play-area table row is per square foot (0.30 cfm/ft² in the code table we read), so CO2 trims above it.
  • Keep CO2 sensors out of fan and diffuser airflow; check against a handheld meter.
  • Split court, party rooms and cafe into separate zones or units.
  • Expect a low sensible heat ratio and plan for dehumidification.
  • Keep duct and diffusers out of impact range; follow fan sprinkler instructions.
