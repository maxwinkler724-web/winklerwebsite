Thermal Fluid (Hot Oil) Heating Systems in Plants

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some plant processes need oil at 500 degrees F or more, and a steam boiler would have to hold hundreds of psi to get there. A thermal fluid system gets the temperature with a pump and an oil loop that runs near atmospheric pressure. The trade-off is a different set of hazards: hot oil burns, and it degrades. This lesson covers the loop, the fire risk, fluid care and the interlocks.

Why plants use hot oil instead of steam

Water has to be held under pressure to stay liquid or to make steam at high temperature. One maker comparison we read lists saturated steam at about 235 psi at 400 degrees F and about 1,600 psi at 600 degrees F. A thermal fluid has a very low vapor pressure at the same temperatures, so the loop only needs enough pressure from the pump to push the oil around. The same comparison puts pump discharge at roughly 35 to 65 psi for most systems. The U.S. Department of Energy process heating sourcebook says thermal fluid systems can give high-temperature service, up to 750 degrees F, without the high pressures, and that steam systems generally top out around 400 degrees F unless pressure goes very high. That is the core reason: high temperature at low pressure. Heat goes to dryers, reactors, presses and tanks through coils or jackets.

The loop: heater, pump, expansion tank, users

The heater is a coil or tube bundle that is fired or electric. The circulating pump moves oil through the heater and out to the users, which are the process coils or jackets. The oil returns cooler. The expansion tank gives the oil room to grow as it heats, since oil expands noticeably between cold and hot. One maker paper advises keeping the tank cool enough to touch by hand once the system is at temperature (about 140 degrees F) because oil that sits hot and meets air oxidizes, and recommends a nitrogen blanket on the tank headspace. A trade article we read describes a continuous, low nitrogen purge through the vent. A vent line runs to a catch tank, so check that it is clear before any startup.

Flash point, fire point and autoignition

These three numbers describe how oil behaves around heat and air. In plain terms (this follows how fluid makers describe them):

  • Flash point: the lowest temperature at which the oil gives off enough vapor to flash when an ignition source is present. OSHA defines flash point in its flammable liquids rule, 29 CFR 1910.106, as the minimum temperature at which a liquid gives off vapor within a test vessel.
  • Fire point: the temperature at which the oil keeps burning after an ignition source lights it.
  • Autoignition: the temperature at which it ignites by itself with no spark or flame.

A trade article says most thermal fluids have a flash point between 300 and 450 degrees F. Systems often run above the flash point because the oil is sealed in. That works only as long as it stays sealed. A fluid maker paper says never to run a system above the fluid's autoignition temperature. Read the data sheet for the exact fluid in your plant; do not rely on these ranges.

Why leaks into insulation are a fire risk

Hot oil that weeps from a flange, valve stem or gasket can soak into porous insulation. The insulation spreads the oil into a large surface exposed to air and also holds in heat. A Department of Energy OSTI record we read describes tests on insulation samples soaked with combustible liquids, with spontaneous ignition temperatures between 55 and 184 degrees C (131 to 363 degrees F) depending on the liquid. That is well below the autoignition figure printed on a fluid data sheet. The same record suggests cellular glass insulation and metal jacketing as options. A fluid maker paper adds that soaked oil can oxidize and smolder, that cutting into smoldering insulation lets in air and can start a fire, and that closed-cell insulation is recommended. A trade article says most fires in these systems are insulation fires or come from loss of flow, cracked heater tubes or leaks.

In the field: a stain, a smell or smoke from lagging is a leak until proven otherwise. Do not strip hot insulation near a suspected leak without a plan and extinguishing media ready. See the pipe supports and pipe labeling lessons below for the insulation and marking practices that go with this.

Fluid degradation and annual sampling

Oil wears out in two main ways. Oxidation happens when hot oil meets air, forming acids and sludge, which is why the expansion tank is kept cool and blanketed. Thermal cracking happens when oil is overheated, past the maker's maximum film temperature, so molecules break into lighter pieces (low boilers) and carbon. Low boilers lower the flash point, and carbon fouls heater tubes. One fluid maker paper recommends an annual fluid analysis that includes an acid number, which tracks oxidation, and a distillation or boiling range test, which tracks low boilers from cracking. Take samples from a consistent point, send them to the lab and trend the results.

Minimum flow and film temperature in the heater

The oil in contact with the heater tube wall is hotter than the average (bulk) oil temperature. A fluid maker article puts the film temperature at about 50 degrees F above bulk, and says degradation roughly doubles for every 18 to 20 degrees F of added bulk temperature. If flow drops while the burner or elements keep putting in heat, the film temperature climbs, even if the outlet reading looks normal. Plugged strainers and mis-set bypass valves are named as causes of low flow. That is why heaters have a stated minimum flow and why low flow is the first interlock. Compare with the low-flow idea in the boiler kettling lesson linked below.

Startup: boil off the moisture first

Water in a hot oil loop is dangerous because it turns to steam. One fluid maker paper says water can expand about 1,100 times, and that a small amount of water in a full expansion tank can push hot oil out the vent. It warns that running at low heat does not purge water, it only moves it to the bottom of the tank. The recommended approach is to keep the expansion tank above 212 degrees F, keep it hot while steam vents, and continue until pump suction is above about 220 degrees F with no more water coming out. Do not hydro-test with water. Follow the fluid maker's startup procedure and your plant's lockout and permit rules.

Safety interlocks

  • Low flow: a trade article says low-flow shutdown should be part of the burner safety interlock, and that pressure-based sensing is more reliable long-term than a flow device sitting in the oil.
  • High temperature: a high-temperature cutoff guards the heater and fluid, but it can miss a sudden rise from fouling or a poor sensor location, so placement matters.
  • Low level: a low-level switch on the expansion tank warns before the pump loses suction and cavitates. A maker article mentions adding fluid when the low-level switch trips.

Never bypass an interlock to keep production running. Test them on a schedule your plant sets, and write down results.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example. All inputs are assumptions chosen for the arithmetic, not data for any real fluid or plant. Check the fluid maker's data sheet for real specific heat and density.

  • Assumed mass flow: 42,000 lb/h (about 108 gpm if the oil density is assumed at 6.5 lb/gal).
  • Assumed oil specific heat: 0.60 Btu/lb-F.
  • Assumed supply temperature: 550 F. Assumed return temperature: 480 F. Temperature drop: 550 - 480 = 70 F.

Heat delivered = mass flow x specific heat x temperature drop.

  1. 42,000 x 0.60 = 25,200 Btu/h per degree F.
  2. 25,200 x 70 = 1,764,000 Btu/h.
  3. Convert to kW: 1,764,000 / 3,412 = about 517 kW (1 kW is about 3,412 Btu/h).
  4. Check the gpm: 42,000 / 60 = 700 lb/min; 700 / 6.5 = about 108 gpm.

So under these assumptions the users take about 1.76 million Btu/h, or roughly 517 kW. The heater must supply that plus losses. The same arithmetic shows the low-flow danger. If the burner keeps releasing the same heat and flow falls, the temperature rise across the heater gets larger, because Q stays fixed while mass flow shrinks.

Field card: Thermal Fluid (Hot Oil) Heating Systems in Plants

Low pressure, high temperature, so watch leaks, flow and water.

  • Thermal fluid reaches high temperature without steam-level pressure.
  • Loop: heater, circulating pump, users, expansion tank with an inert gas blanket.
  • Leaks into insulation can burn well below the data sheet autoignition number.
  • Oxidation and thermal cracking age the oil; sample it yearly.
  • Low flow raises film temperature in the heater.
  • Boil water off at startup; never rush it.
  • Interlocks: low flow, high temperature, low level. Do not bypass.
