Pet Doors as Air Leaks: Flap Gaps, Wind and a Gap Area Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pet door is a hole in your exterior door or wall with a flap over it. The flap keeps most of the weather out, but it is never as tight as the wall around it. This lesson explains why pet doors leak, what makes the air move, and how to check yours with a strip of tissue.

Why a flap is a leak

A federal consumer guide to air sealing says leaks can occur anywhere gaps exist in a home. A pet door is a gap that was cut on purpose. The flap swings, so it cannot be glued shut, and it has to clear its own frame. That leaves a thin gap all the way around. In winter, cold air comes in through the low gaps and warm indoor air leaves through the high ones. In summer it runs the other way.

Because the opening is in a door or wall, it is also a place where the building's insulation stops. Our own field note: a thin plastic flap has very little insulating value compared with the door around it, so even when it is closed the area feels cold to your hand in winter.

What pushes air through the gaps

  • Stack effect. A state university extension page explains that warm air rises and escapes through gaps high in the house, while cold air is drawn in through gaps low in the house. A pet door sits near the floor, which is exactly where the incoming air enters. See how an unused fireplace loses heat for another example of the same pull.
  • Exhaust fans. The same extension page has you run the bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans during a leak check so that leaks show air being drawn into the house. Every fan that sends air outdoors pulls the same amount back in through leaks, and a flap is one of them. See bathroom exhaust fans.
  • Wind. The same page recommends a windy day for leak tests because moving outdoor air makes leaks easier to notice. Our own field note: a flap facing the wind flutters and lets in a puff, while a flap on the lee side tends to stay still.

For the bigger-building version of these forces, see building pressurization and stack effect.

Worn, curled and single flaps

Our own field notes, not taken from a cited source: a flap that has curled, cracked or stiffened in the cold no longer lies flat against its frame, so the gap grows. A flap with torn edges or a chewed corner leaks at that spot all the time. A single flap gives one barrier. A double flap puts two flaps in series with trapped air between them, so air has to pass two gaps instead of one. Neither one is airtight.

Weatherstripping around the frame, and on the flap edges where the product allows it, can close part of the gap. A state university extension bulletin says doors that open most often need the more permanent types of weatherstripping, and that floor-mounted gaskets wear out quickly in high-traffic spots. A flap is the highest-traffic door in many homes, so expect to replace worn strips and check them each season.

Magnets, panels and where it sits

Our own field notes: magnets in the flap edge pull it shut after the pet passes, which cuts the time it hangs open or flutters. A solid closing panel that slides or snaps over the opening blocks the flap completely and is the best choice when no pet needs to go out, such as at night or while you are away. Check that the panel seats against a gasket, not just against bare metal or plastic.

Placement matters too. A door facing the prevailing wind sees more pressure on it than one on a sheltered side. Putting the opening on a side of the house out of the wind helps, as does a spot away from the garage door or a window that you open. Never block a flap in a way that could trap an animal outdoors in bad weather, and never block a flue, vent or combustion air opening to seal a leak.

Wall tunnels and sliding-door inserts

Our own field notes: a wall-mounted tunnel has two flaps and a stretch of tunnel between them, which gives more air-trapping distance than a door insert, but it also adds a hole through the framing that must be sealed around its outer edge. A pet door panel that fits into the track of a sliding glass door is held in place by pressure, and it tends to leave gaps at the sides, top and where it meets the track. Look for daylight at those corners and seal them with a flexible strip rather than leaving them open.

A simple tissue check

Two extension pages describe holding a lit incense stick or smoke pen near suspect spots and watching where the smoke goes. We use a version that needs no flame or smoke, which is our own adaptation: hold a strip of tissue or light paper near the closed flap on a cold or windy day, with the bathroom and kitchen fans running. Hold it at the bottom edge, the sides and the top corners. If the tissue flutters, bends or is pulled toward the gap, air is moving there. If you do use a smoke stick as the sources describe, first turn off combustion appliances and close fireplace flues, since the page lists that as part of the test.

Another pair of tests from the bulletin: with the room dark, a helper on the inside looks for light around the closed door's edge, and a dollar bill that slides out easily from a closed door means the weatherstripping needs repair. A flap is different from a hinged door, so treat these as a rough guide.

Worked example: gap area of a flap (our own example)

These are our own assumptions, not a measurement of any product. We assume the flap opening is 10 inches wide by 14 inches tall, and that a uniform gap of 1/8 inch (0.125 inch) runs all the way around it.

  1. Perimeter = 2 x (10 + 14) = 2 x 24 = 48 inches.
  2. Gap area = perimeter x gap width = 48 x 0.125 = 6.0 square inches. (We ignore the tiny overlap at the corners.)
  3. Comparison hole: 2 inches x 2 inches = 4 square inches.
  4. Ratio = 6.0 / 4 = 1.5.

Check: 48 x 1/8 is the same as 48 divided by 8, which is 6. Yes. So under these assumptions, a closed flap with only a 1/8 inch gap leaks like a hole one and a half times the size of a 2 by 2 inch square. If the gap were 1/4 inch it would double to 12 square inches, because the area scales directly with the gap width. Real leakage also depends on the pressure and on how the air flows through such a long thin slot, so this tells you the size of the opening, not the airflow.

What to do

  • Do the tissue check in cold or windy weather and note which edges move.
  • Replace curled or cracked flaps and worn strips.
  • Use the closing panel at night and when you are away, if no pet needs access.
  • Seal gaps around frames and inserts. A federal guide lists weatherstripping and door sweeps among the do-it-yourself materials for doors.
  • For a whole-house look, the same guide describes a blower door test and infrared imaging as professional methods.

Pets also affect the equipment itself; see pets and your home heating and cooling.
