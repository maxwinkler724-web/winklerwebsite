Gas Quick-Disconnect Hoses and Convenience Outlets for Movable Equipment

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some gas equipment has to move: a cooking line rolled out for cleaning, a portable heater carried around a job, or a grill that plugs into a gas line at the house. A quick-disconnect hose lets that happen without a wrench, but only if the hose, the stop valve and the restraint are right. This lesson is the apprentice view of what to look for.

Check the instructions first

Read the instructions for the equipment. This page is general education from government and university sources plus our own reasoning. The maker's instructions and the fuel gas rules where you work decide what is allowed on a real job, and the local gas utility or inspector settles any doubt. We give no code section numbers on purpose.

Where movable gas connections show up

  • Commercial cooking equipment on casters. Ranges, fryers and ovens that roll so the floor and wall behind them can be cleaned.
  • Portable garage and construction heaters. Moved from bay to bay or from room to room as work progresses.
  • Outdoor grills and heaters plumbed to the house. A wall or post outlet takes a hose end so the grill can be pulled out and put away.

A fixed appliance is connected once with rigid pipe or a short appliance connector. Our own rule of thumb: if the machine is expected to move, it needs a connection designed to be disconnected, and a plan for what holds the machine back.

How a quick-disconnect closes when unplugged

A gas quick-disconnect is a two-part coupling. The plug on the hose end fits into a socket on the supply side. Inside the socket is a spring-loaded valve that is held open only while the plug is seated, so pulling the plug lets the supply side snap shut. This is our own plain-language description of a typical coupling, and makers differ, so confirm how yours works from its instructions. A federal worker safety agency letter on compressed-air tool couplings makes a related point: a sleeve that must be pulled against a spring counts as a deliberate action, which helps keep a pressurized hose from separating by accident, and a built-in check valve is described as an additional safety feature. Gas is not compressed air, but the same idea applies: a good coupling takes a deliberate motion to open, and closes the supply when it comes apart.

Do not rely on the coupling's internal valve as your only shutoff. Treat it as backup.

Shutoff valve ahead of the disconnect

Put a manual shutoff valve on the supply side, upstream of the quick-disconnect, where a person can reach it without stretching over a hot appliance. Our own habit for the sequence: close the valve first, then disconnect, then move the equipment. When the equipment returns, reconnect, open the valve slowly, and leak check before lighting. The lesson on sediment traps, drip legs, shutoff valves and appliance connectors covers fixed valves and connectors in more detail.

Restraining cables: the hose should never take the pull

A rolling appliance that is pushed too far away will pull on whatever is attached to it. If that is a gas hose, the first thing to fail is the weakest connection. A restraint is a cable or chain anchored to the structure and to the appliance, with enough slack that normal movement for cleaning does not tighten it, and short enough that the hose and coupling are never stretched. Your equipment instructions say where the anchor points are and how long the cable may be, so follow them. The federal worker safety agency rule for construction LP-gas use gives a related principle for heaters: hose is to be long enough to reach without kinking or straining, and short as practicable. It also says gas connectors, valves and piping must not be used as a support for a heater.

Why the hose must be rated and inspected

The federal construction LP-gas rule says hose is to be designed for a working pressure of at least 250 psig, with suitability determined by listing by a testing agency, and it requires heaters to have an automatic device that shuts off gas flow if the flame goes out. That is the construction-site LP-gas rule, not a rule for every natural gas hose, but it shows what "rated for the use" means: a purpose-made gas hose, matched to the fuel and the pressure, not a garden hose or a hose picked because it fit.

A consumer product safety agency fact sheet on gas grills tells owners to check hoses for cracking, brittleness, holes and leaks, to avoid sharp bends, and to keep hoses away from hot surfaces and grease drips. Replace what is damaged. A rolling appliance gets those hazards on top: scuffing from casters, kinking in a corner, and heat from the cabinet.

The same agency warned years ago about older uncoated brass flexible connectors. It said solder at the fittings can fail, recommended replacing any uncoated brass connector, and noted that moving an appliance, even slightly to clean behind it, could cause a weakened connector to fail. Equipment that is moved often is where a worn hose or connector shows up first.

Never run a hose through a wall or floor

A flexible hose or appliance connector is meant to be seen from end to end, in the open, in the same room as the equipment. If a hose goes through a wall, floor, ceiling or cabinet partition, nobody can inspect it, it can get pinched, and the hidden part can leak into a closed space. The wall or floor penetration is the job of fixed gas pipe, ending at a convenience outlet you can reach. This is our own teaching point and matches common maker and fuel gas code language, but confirm the exact wording for your site.

Leak checks with soap solution

The consumer product safety agency gives the basic method for LP grills: open the gas supply valve fully, brush soapy solution on the connection points (one part liquid detergent to one part water), and watch for bubbles. Bubbles mean a leak. If tightening the connection does not stop it, close the valve and have a qualified person repair it, and do not light the appliance until it is fixed. Do this every time the hose is reconnected, not only at the start of the season. Never use a flame to search for a leak.

If you smell gas, a university fire and life safety document gives the standard response: do not operate electrical switches, do not use a phone inside the building, avoid ignition sources, leave by the shortest safe route, and call emergency services from a safe place. That document also notes that natural gas is sensed at low concentrations, so a smell is a reason to act, not to investigate.

When the equipment is moved for cleaning

  1. Close the shutoff valve ahead of the quick-disconnect.
  2. Let hot equipment cool, then disconnect the hose at the coupling, not by pulling on the hose.
  3. Move the equipment only as far as the restraint allows, keeping the hose off the floor path and away from casters.
  4. Inspect the hose and ends in daylight or good light: cracks, kinks, cuts, scuffs, loose fittings.
  5. Roll back, reconnect, open the valve, soap test the connections, and check that the pilot or burner lights normally.
  6. Confirm the restraint is reattached before you leave.

Worked example (our own): can the hose carry the load?

Our own example with assumed numbers. These values are for practice only and are not from any maker.

  • Appliance input: 150,000 Btu/h (assumed).
  • Gas heating value: 1,037 Btu per cubic foot. We use the 2025 national average delivered natural gas heat content published by a federal energy statistics agency; your utility's value differs.
  • Hose: 3/4 inch, 5 feet long (assumed), with an assumed capacity of 200 cubic feet per hour at a 0.5 inch water column pressure drop (a made-up table value for this exercise; read the real one from the maker).
  • Supply pressure at the outlet: 7 inches water column (assumed).

Step 1, needed flow. 150,000 Btu/h divided by 1,037 Btu/ft³ = 144.65, so about 145 cubic feet per hour (cfh).

Step 2, compare with capacity. 145 cfh is below the assumed 200 cfh, so the hose can carry the load.

Step 3, estimate the drop at the lower flow. Pressure drop in a short hose rises faster than the flow, so for a quick estimate we scale by the square of the flow ratio (our own approximation, a little conservative). Ratio: 144.65 / 200 = 0.723. Squared: 0.523. Drop: 0.523 x 0.5 = 0.26 inch water column.

Step 4, check the margin. 0.26 / 7 = 0.037, about 3.7 percent of the assumed supply pressure. That is small, and it leaves most of the pressure for the appliance regulator and burners. If the hose were 150 cfh instead, the load would exceed capacity and we would need a larger hose or a shorter run. Always size to the maker's table, and measure inlet pressure with the burners on; see checking gas inlet and manifold pressure with a manometer.

The same check applies to the outlet itself and to the pipe behind it: the whole run has to carry the load of everything on it, which the flexible gas appliance connectors lesson also covers for coated corrugated connectors. For outdoor grills and heaters, also see outdoor gas appliances at home.

Field card: Gas Quick-Disconnect Hoses and Convenience Outlets

Valve first, restraint always, hose in plain view.

  • Shutoff valve upstream of the quick-disconnect, within reach.
  • Close the valve, then disconnect, then move the equipment.
  • A restraint cable takes the pull; the hose never does.
  • Use a hose rated for the gas, the pressure and the use. Replace cracked, kinked or scuffed hose.
  • Never run a hose through a wall, floor or cabinet partition.
  • Soap test every reconnection. Bubbles mean a leak. Never use a flame.
  • Smell gas: no switches, no phone inside, leave, then call.
  • Example: 150,000 / 1,037 = about 145 cfh (assumed inputs).
