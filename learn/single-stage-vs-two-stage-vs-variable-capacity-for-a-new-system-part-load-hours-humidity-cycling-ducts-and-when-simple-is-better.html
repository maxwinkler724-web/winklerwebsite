Single-Stage vs Two-Stage vs Variable-Capacity for a New System: Part-Load Hours, Humidity, Cycling, Ducts, and When Simple Is Better

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new outdoor unit comes in three flavors: one speed, two speeds, or a compressor that turns down smoothly. The choice is mostly about what the equipment does on the many mild days, not the few hot ones. This lesson walks through how each type behaves at part load, what that does to humidity, comfort and cycling, what the ducts and controls must be able to do, and when the plain single-stage unit is still a sound call.

Check the installation instructions

Check the installation instructions. Turn-down limits, airflow settings and thermostat requirements differ by maker and model. This page is general education built from public research reports; the equipment's own instructions and rating tables win. We do not discuss brands, prices or incentives.

Why part load is where the hours are

Equipment is sized for a design day that happens a few hours a year. Every other cooling hour asks for less, often much less. A single-stage unit has one output, so on those hours it can only turn itself on and off. A National Renewable Energy Laboratory simulation of an oversized-versus-right-sized house in a hot-humid climate noted that even right-sized units already cycle often through most cooling hours. Because the thermostat holds a minimum on-time of about five minutes in that model, the lowest hourly load it can follow is about 8 percent of capacity.

Three ways to meet a partial load:

  • Single-stage: full output or off. Part load is met by on/off time.
  • Two-stage: a high stage and a lower stage (the two-stage lessons below cover how the low stage is made and wired). Part load is met by running the low stage longer, with the high stage called in only when the house falls behind.
  • Variable-capacity (inverter): output follows the load over a range, down to some minimum fraction of capacity. Below that minimum, it cycles like the others.

How the stages are made is covered in Two-Stage Air Conditioner and Heat Pump Compressors, Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors and, for gas heat, Two-Stage and Modulating Gas Furnaces. We stay on the selection question.

Humidity: longer, colder runs remove more water

Moisture comes out when air passes a coil colder than its dew point. At the start of every cycle the coil is warming up to wet, and at the end the coil drips and sometimes re-evaporates into the airstream. Many short cycles therefore spend a larger share of their time in the poor-dehumidifying stretches. A national-lab test of residential dehumidifiers (not air conditioners, so treat it as an analogy) found that with 3 to 6 minute run times, 17 to 42 percent of the removed moisture went back into the air when the fan kept running after the compressor stopped.

A Florida Solar Energy Center paper reports that fixed-capacity systems run near 400 cfm per ton while variable-capacity systems range from about 180 to 650, and that lower airflow per ton gives a colder coil, a lower sensible heat ratio and more moisture removal. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load. The same paper warns the opposite can happen: a variable unit run in a high-airflow efficiency mode at low load can leave the house damp, in one test about 62 percent indoor humidity, and in tight low-load homes a separate dehumidifier may still be needed. A DOE meeting presentation on this topic reached a similar view: slow runs at low output keep the coil cold and reduce short cycling, and airflow as low as 180 cfm per ton lowers the sensible heat ratio.

A research-home study by Oak Ridge National Laboratory found average indoor humidity the same as the single-stage baseline (about 44 to 46 percent) in the variable units' comfort mode and about 49 to 50 percent in their efficiency mode. So humidity control depends on the airflow and mode settings, not just on the compressor type.

Comfort swings and cycling losses

A single-stage unit pushes the house down by its full capacity, shuts off, and waits. The air and the room surfaces drift apart, supply air is cold at first and the room can feel drafty and then warm. Lower-output runs smooth this out; the supply air is steady and the thermostat sees a smaller swing. Oak Ridge's report on two variable-capacity heat pumps in a research home recorded cooling-season energy use 41 to 44 percent lower than the home's earlier single-stage systems, though the equipment and sizing differed, so it is not a clean one-variable comparison.

Keep cycling losses in proportion. The NREL oversizing study estimated cycling losses of about 5 percent for right-sized single-stage units at two efficiency levels and about 6 percent for a right-sized two-speed unit. It concluded that the large penalties for oversizing came mainly from off-cycle parasitic power such as crankcase heat, not from cycling efficiency, and that it did not study dehumidification. Fan or controls that stay energized between cycles matter. For the field side of cycling see AC Short Cycling.

Ducts and airflow

  • Static pressure and cfm per ton. Each type has its own airflow window at each stage. A low stage moves less air, and the duct system must still deliver it evenly to every room without noise or condensation on registers. A leaky or undersized duct system limits any type; a variable unit does not rescue it.
  • Low-speed delivery. At low airflow, supply air is cold and moves slowly. Throw and mixing in far rooms is worth checking, and so are supply registers that can sweat.
  • Matching coil and air handler. The indoor blower and metering device must be approved for the outdoor unit and its low stage. Read the manufacturer's expanded performance data as in Reading AC Expanded Cooling Performance Tables.

Ducts also affect measured output. A Brookhaven National Laboratory field report on cold-climate heat pumps noted airflow measurement uncertainty and uneven air across supply registers, which is a reminder to verify airflow, not assume it.

Thermostat and control compatibility

A two-stage unit usually wants a thermostat with two cooling stages, or an adaptive staging algorithm. A variable-capacity unit may take a simple on/off call, a stage call, or require the maker's own communicating control. Using a thermostat that does not match can lock the unit in one stage, disable low-speed fan modes or hide dehumidify settings. The Brookhaven report mentions a non-original thermostat that lacked fan-only mode and offered only three fan speeds, which limited airflow testing. In the same study, measured heating cycling rates averaged 2.1 to 3.7 cycles per hour by system type, so cycle count is a useful check on how the control is behaving. Look at Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Furnaces and Air Handlers for blower setup and check the compatibility list before ordering. Research recommended adding humidity sensors so the control can respond to moisture, not just temperature.

When the simpler option is the right call

  • Dry, mild climate or few cooling hours. The part-load advantage is small if humidity is rarely a problem.
  • The load is right-sized and the ducts are good. Right-sized single-stage cycling losses were modest in the NREL work.
  • Controls and service access. More boards, sensors and settings mean more to diagnose. Pick what local techs can set up and service.
  • A small, well-matched change. Two-stage is a middle step with simpler controls than a full variable system.
  • Poor duct system. Fix airflow first.

The more complex type earns its place when the house has a low sensible load with real moisture, when long quiet runs matter to the owners, or when the load range is wide. Check the load calculation first; a right-sized load drives all of this (see Heat Load Calculation Intro).

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own made-up example, not measured data. A mild cooling day: the house load is 30 percent of a 3-ton unit's rated capacity for 10 hours.

Assumptions (ours): 3 tons = 36,000 Btu/h. Capacity does not change with outdoor temperature or stage. Load stays at 30 percent. The thermostat holds the room inside a fixed temperature band that stores 2,520 Btu of heat from one edge of the band to the other (we chose this so the single-stage unit comes out at a clean 3 starts per hour). The two-stage low stage is 65 percent. The variable unit turns down to 30 percent. No start-up losses, no fan delay.

  1. Load: 0.30 x 36,000 = 10,800 Btu/h. Over 10 hours that is 108,000 Btu.
  2. Single-stage: on-time = 2,520 / (36,000 - 10,800) = 0.10 h = 6 min. Off-time = 2,520 / 10,800 = 0.233 h = 14 min. Cycle = 20 min, so 3 starts per hour. Run fraction = 6 / 20 = 0.30, or 18 min per hour.
  3. Two-stage, low stage only: 0.65 x 36,000 = 23,400 Btu/h. On-time = 2,520 / (23,400 - 10,800) = 0.20 h = 12 min. Off-time = 14 min. Cycle = 26 min, so 60 / 26 = 2.3 starts per hour. Run fraction = 12 / 26 = 0.46, or about 27.7 min per hour.
  4. Variable-capacity: minimum output 0.30 x 36,000 = 10,800 Btu/h, equal to the load. It runs continuously: 0 starts per hour, run fraction 1.0, 60 min per hour.
  5. Over 10 hours: about 30 starts and 180 run-minutes (single-stage), about 23 starts and 277 run-minutes (two-stage), 0 starts and 600 run-minutes (variable). All three deliver the same 108,000 Btu.

The perfect match for the variable unit is an idealization: a real load drifts, and when it falls below the unit's minimum output, that unit starts cycling too. What the example shows is the direction: the lower the lowest output, the longer and fewer the runs, which is what helps coil temperature and humidity removal. The same pattern is why oversizing hurts; see the short-cycling lesson above.

Field card: Single-stage vs two-stage vs variable-capacity

Part-load hours decide it, and the ducts and control decide whether you get the benefit.

  • Single-stage: on/off only. Simple and easy to service; more starts at part load.
  • Two-stage: runs the low stage longer; needs a two-stage call or adaptive control.
  • Variable: follows the load down to a minimum, then cycles.
  • Longer runs at low airflow per ton give colder coils and more moisture removal.
  • Efficiency mode at low load can leave humidity higher; check settings.
  • Verify duct airflow and the thermostat's compatibility before the install.
  • Our example: 30 starts, 23 starts and 0 starts over 10 hours at 30 percent load.
