Florist Floral Coolers and Flower Walk-Ins: Cut Flower Temperatures, High Humidity, Low Coil TD, Ethylene and Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A flower cooler looks like any other walk-in, but the product is different: stems that lose water fast, bloom damage from ethylene, and a customer who wants a fresh bloom and a clear glass door. This lesson covers the holding temperature extension sources give, why florists need small coil TD and high humidity, how ethylene and produce cause problems, and what a service technician finds on the common calls.

What temperature do cut flowers want?

Extension sources agree on a narrow window for long holding. An NC State extension publication on storing cut flowers gives 40 to 60 F for short-term storage and 32 to 35 F for long-term storage for most crops. An Iowa State page says the same thing in other words: 40 to 60 F for short holding, with 33 to 35 F ideal for longer storage. A University of Illinois postharvest guide puts the cooler at the crop's optimum, about 32 to 36 F, and says that range also slows the ethylene that some flowers give off. A University of Kentucky guide for growers says to store most species below 42 F but above freezing. It lists zinnia and basil as needing care below 40 F, and hibiscus as staying above 38 F.

Not every bloom likes the cold. A University of California extension publication says most cut flowers tolerate temperatures as low as 32 F, but gladiolus, bird-of-paradise, protea, some orchids, anthurium and ginger should be kept no cooler than 50 F. A shop that holds roses and anthurium has to pick one setting or keep the tropicals in a separate warmer cabinet. That split is our own suggestion, not something these sources state. The controller setpoint is not the whole story either. In our experience, stems near the discharge face of the coil can see air colder than the thermostat reads, which is why we place them away from it (see the sketch).

High humidity and low coil TD

Stems and petals lose water to air that is drier than the tissue. A University of Florida postharvest chapter says the driving force is the gradient of water vapor pressure between the tissue and the air, that water loss is proportional to the vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and that fresh plant tissue sits very close to saturation, around 99.4 to 99.5 percent relative humidity. A University of California extension publication recommends 90 to 95 percent relative humidity for conditioning flowers, while noting that this is hard to reach at home.

In a refrigerated box, humidity comes down to the coil. A coil that boils far below the box air pulls water out as frost and condensate, drying the room. The coil TD is the gap between the box air and the refrigerant boiling temperature. A University of Wisconsin extension handout gives minimum relative humidity at a 35 F storeroom by the air temperature drop across the coil, and says the coil TD is about twice that air drop. For our full treatment of TD, see Evaporator Design TD in Walk-In Coolers.

Airflow

The same Wisconsin handout notes that evaporator fans promote air mixing, that fan motors can cost more to run than the compressor, and that stacked product should not rise above the bottom of the evaporator, with clearances kept so air circulates. For a flower box that means gentle movement, clear of the discharge, and buckets with space between them.

Worked example (our own, with cited numbers)

Part A: humidity from coil TD. The Wisconsin table at 35 F gives these minimum RH values: air drop of 2 F, 92.3 percent; air drop of 4 F, 84.7 percent. Doubling the air drop gives coil TD of 4 F and 8 F. So a coil built for a 4 F TD can hold the box near 92 percent, while an 8 F TD coil sets a floor near 85 percent. These are minimums, so door openings, wet buckets and defrost water usually push the real number up.

Part B: water loss at two box temperatures. The saturation vapor pressures come from a University of Colorado table: 6.9 mb at 35 F and 10.2 mb at 45 F. Assumptions (ours): stems are at box temperature, tissue is at 99.5 percent RH (from the Florida chapter), the box holds 90 percent RH at both temperatures, and the stems, surface area and airflow are the same.

  1. VPD = (tissue RH minus box RH) x saturation pressure at that temperature.
  2. At 35 F: (0.995 minus 0.90) x 6.9 = 0.095 x 6.9 = 0.66 mb.
  3. At 45 F: 0.095 x 10.2 = 0.97 mb.
  4. Ratio: 0.97 / 0.66 = about 1.48. Because water loss is proportional to VPD, a 45 F box dries stems about 48 percent faster than a 35 F box at the same humidity.

Now compare humidity at one temperature. At 35 F, an RH of 92.3 percent (4 F coil TD) gives VPD = (0.995 minus 0.923) x 6.9 = 0.50 mb. An RH of 84.7 percent (8 F coil TD) gives (0.995 minus 0.847) x 6.9 = 1.02 mb. The larger TD roughly doubles the drying rate (1.02 / 0.50 = about 2.0). So both temperature and TD matter, and TD can matter more.

Ethylene, fruit and decaying stems

Ethylene is a plant gas that makes some flowers age early. The University of Illinois guide says the major source is aging and decomposing flowers and foliage, and that fungi, fruits and vegetables also give it off; apples and oranges are named as major sources. It also lists leaking gas lines, gas or kerosene heaters and vehicle exhaust. It says one air exchange per hour is sufficient to keep ethylene down, and that coolers should not open onto spots where exhaust collects. A University of California extension publication warns that ethylene from produce such as apples, bananas or carrots may drastically shorten the life of many cut flowers, and the University of Kentucky guide says not to store cut flowers in the same cooler as fruits or vegetables.

For a service tech the lesson is practical. A shop that keeps its lunch, fruit baskets or arrangements with fruit in the flower box has an ethylene source, and a clogged drain with standing water is a place for decay. Pull the dead stems, clean the buckets, and keep produce out. If a customer asks for a fruit gift basket, build it in another cooler. See Produce Cold Storage, Ripening Rooms and Controlled Atmosphere for the ethylene side of fruit rooms.

Glass doors, fogging and anti-sweat heaters

Many shops use a glass-door display cooler out front. Condensation forms when warm humid air touches a surface below its dew point. A University of Vermont blog gives an example: a wall at 34 F and 95 percent RH has a dew point of 32.2 F, so even a small cold spot can sweat. On a glass door, the cold glass meets shop air. The door has a heater, often called an anti-sweat heater, to keep the glass and frame above dew point. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory report on display cases says these heaters are an internal load, adding to the refrigeration load as well as using electricity, and a major share of the load in frozen-food door cases. See Anti-Sweat Heaters on Glass Door Display Cases for wattage and controls.

Maintenance: drain, coil, door

The Vermont blog tells owners to rule out a blocked evaporator drain when moisture appears, and to check door seals by looking for daylight around a closed door. We add these shop habits as our own recommendations: clear the drain and pan each service, brush or rinse the evaporator and the condenser coil, and replace torn gaskets. A dirty condenser raises head pressure and shrinks capacity. Frost on the coil is normal between defrosts, but a heavy block is not; see Defrost Timers and Termination Thermostats.

Holiday load spikes

Holiday weeks bring more product and more door openings. Boxes sized for retail storage are often sized for 24 to 48 hours of stock, according to the NC State extension publication, which is a sign that a holiday surge can exceed design. Warm stems and wet buckets add heat and moisture and the door is open more. Ahead of the rush we suggest cleaning coils, checking the defrost schedule, and loading in stages instead of putting the whole delivery in at once. These steps are our own advice.

Common service calls

  • Box too warm. Check for a dirty condenser, a frosted coil, a stuck-open door or a failed fan. Count the product added in the past day. Check the thermometer location before you chase the setpoint.
  • Frozen petals near the coil. Stems were placed in the discharge stream. Move buckets away, and check that the controller is not reading warm because the sensor is out of the airflow.
  • Fogged doors. Look at the heater, the door gasket and shop humidity. The glass is likely below dew point because the heater is off or weak, or the door was propped open.
  • Water on the floor. Look at the drain and the pan before blaming the roof.

These checklists are our own field guidance, not sourced numbers. For walk-in basics and the federal rules for door heaters see Walk-In Coolers and Freezers.

Field card: Flower coolers

Cold, wet, gentle and clean.

  • Long holding: 32 to 36 F depending on source; tropicals no colder than 50 F (UC).
  • Aim for 90 to 95 percent RH, which needs a small coil TD.
  • Our example: 45 F dries about 48 percent faster than 35 F at 90 percent RH.
  • No fruit or vegetables in the flower box.
  • Stems out of the coil discharge.
  • Fogged glass: heater, gasket, shop humidity.
  • Drain and coils clean before the holidays.
