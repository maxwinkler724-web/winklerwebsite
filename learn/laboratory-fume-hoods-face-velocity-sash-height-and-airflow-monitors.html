Lab Fume Hoods: Face Velocity, Sash Height and Airflow Monitors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A lab fume hood is a small exhaust system with a window in front. If the air moving through that window slows down, vapors can drift out toward the person working. This lesson explains the numbers a lab tech or HVAC apprentice hears about hoods: face velocity, sash height, constant-volume versus VAV, monitors and alarms, and what the room and the roof have to do to support them.

Check the site's own rules

Safety first. Hood limits, alarm settings and test methods are set by the lab's safety office, the hood maker and the building design. This page is general education built from public sources. Follow your site's written procedures, and never bypass an alarm, a sash stop or a lockout.

How a hood captures vapors

A hood pulls room air in through the open front, sweeps it across the work area and out through the back and top into a duct. North Dakota State University's hood guidelines describe it that way. Air moving inward keeps vapors from escaping through the opening, much like a kitchen range hood but with a tested, enclosed work space. The sliding window is the sash. The opening under it is the face, and the speed of the air through that opening is the face velocity, usually in feet per minute (fpm).

Face velocity: the number to know

Face velocity is the main performance indicator for a hood, according to a Lab Manager article. Sources give different ranges, and the range depends on the hood, the hazard and the site, so treat each as that source's statement and not a universal law:

  • The Lab Manager article says ANSI/AIHA Z9.5 specifies an average of 80 to 120 fpm, with no single reading more than 20 percent from the average, and that OSHA's non-mandatory guidance gives 60 to 100 fpm as typical. We did not read those standards themselves.
  • Northwestern University's hood handbook gives 60 to 100 fpm, 50 fpm for minimally hazardous work and 80 to 120 fpm for hoods tested for hazardous chemicals.
  • The University of Oregon's training page calls 80 to 100 fpm optimal. Florida State's design guideline asks for an average of 90 to 100 fpm at an 18 inch sash, and the University of Virginia specification targets 80 fpm at 18 inches.

Faster is not better. Northwestern and Lab Manager both warn that above about 125 fpm the air becomes turbulent and can pull contaminants toward the user. OSHA's 1983 interpretation letter adds that the specific face velocities in OSHA's carcinogen standards do not apply to laboratories. The lab standard, 29 CFR 1910.1450, instead requires the written chemical hygiene plan to include a requirement that fume hoods work properly. Its Appendix A says hoods should be maintained, monitored and routinely tested, and that lab air should be exhausted outdoors, not recirculated.

Sash height changes the math

The face area is the width times the sash height, and cfm = area × velocity. A higher sash means a bigger opening, so more air is needed to hold the same velocity. Lab Manager notes many hoods fall below a safe velocity above their rated working height. Sash stops and arrow labels mark that height, commonly 18 inches at test time, and Northwestern says hoods often have a stop near 14 inches. Do not remove or defeat a stop. Keeping work at least six inches back inside the hood, and the sash closed when idle, appears in the NDSU and Northwestern guidance.

Three ways hoods handle the changing opening

  • Constant volume, no bypass: exhaust cfm never changes, so velocity rises as the sash closes and falls as it opens (NDSU, Northwestern).
  • Constant volume with bypass: a grille opening above the sash lets room air in as the sash closes, so total exhaust stays steady and the face velocity climbs less (Northwestern). Labconco's article, quoting Z9.5, says a bypass hood's face velocity should not exceed three times the full-open value and its exhaust should change by less than five percent.
  • Variable air volume (VAV): a sash sensor and a control valve or damper change exhaust cfm to hold face velocity about constant (NDSU, Oregon). Lab Manager says closing a VAV sash can cut airflow by 60 to 80 percent. That is why the lab's supply air must be controlled to follow it.

An auxiliary-air hood brings outside air to the sash, but Northwestern's handbook generally discourages them.

Monitors and alarms

A hood monitor reads airflow and warns when it is low. Oregon says VAV hoods display face velocity, while older constant-volume hoods often have a pressure gauge and an alarm. Northwestern says a low-velocity alarm can come from a sash held too high, a failed building exhaust or lost power; do hazardous work only after exhaust recovers, and never disable the alarm. A tinsel or ribbon indicator only shows that air is moving; it does not measure velocity. The University of Virginia specification, for new hoods, calls for an audible and visible local alarm at 10 percent or more variance from specification for over 10 seconds, and monitor calibration during balancing.

Exhaust fans, stacks, makeup air and room pressure

The hood is only as good as the system behind it. Florida State's guideline requires fans on hazardous exhaust to meet code (NFPA 45 is named), discharge at 2000 fpm minimum with the stack at least 10 feet above the roofline, and ductwork kept negative relative to occupied areas. The Labs21 guide on exhaust dispersion (a joint EPA and DOE program, run by NREL) warns that fume hood exhaust can reach air intakes, and recommends enough exit velocity to avoid stack-tip downwash and no rain caps that stop the plume rising.

Every cfm exhausted must be replaced. Stanford's design guidelines say makeup air should come in slowly through a large opening so it does not create jets, ideally at the far end of the room from the hood, and should keep each lab negative. Room air currents at the hood should stay below 20 percent of face velocity. Northwestern adds that lab doors and windows should stay closed to hold negative pressure. Supply valves that track sash position are part of the University of Virginia specification.

Annual face velocity checks

Oregon says its safety office tests each hood at least annually and after repairs, recording average face velocity and sash height. Northwestern's annual check includes average face velocity with the sash open, the sash height where velocity reaches 100 fpm, a smoke test and an alarm check. Lab Manager says annual checks use a calibrated anemometer and a grid of readings at several sash heights. NDSU averages three diagonal readings at an 18 inch sash. The University of Virginia spec allows readings no more than 20 percent from the mean. New hoods are typically type-tested to ASHRAE 110, a tracer gas method; that is a separate test from a field velocity check. For the instrument side, see Measuring Airflow (CFM).

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

Our own numbers for a hood with a 5 ft wide opening, 100 fpm target. Area = width × height, with inches converted to feet.

  1. Sash at 18 in. Height = 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5 ft. Area = 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 sq ft.
  2. Exhaust at 100 fpm. cfm = 7.5 × 100 = 750 cfm.
  3. Sash raised to 28 in. Height = 28 ÷ 12 = 2.333 ft. Area = 5 × 2.333 = 11.67 sq ft.
  4. VAV hood holding 100 fpm. cfm = 11.67 × 100 = about 1,167 cfm, up 417 cfm from step 2.
  5. Constant-volume hood keeping 750 cfm. Velocity = 750 ÷ 11.67 = about 64 fpm, well below the 100 fpm target. That is why sash stops and alarms exist.

The same math is behind the first rule: raising a constant-volume sash costs velocity, and raising a VAV sash costs air that the building must then supply. For fan-speed effects, see Fan Laws.

Field card: Lab Fume Hoods

cfm = open area × face velocity.

  • Face velocity is the speed of air through the sash opening, in fpm.
  • Ranges vary by source and site (60 to 100 and 80 to 120 fpm both appear); use the site's number.
  • Constant volume: velocity falls as the sash rises. VAV: cfm rises to hold velocity.
  • Do not defeat sash stops or silence alarms.
  • Our example: 5 ft wide, 18 in sash = 7.5 sq ft = 750 cfm at 100 fpm; at 28 in, VAV = about 1,167 cfm, constant volume = about 64 fpm.
  • Hoods are tested at least yearly with a calibrated anemometer grid.
