Nursing Home HVAC: Resident Room Temperature Rules, Emergency Power and PTACs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you look up nursing home temperature requirements, you find two numbers that look alike and are not the same rule: a federal 71 to 81 F range and a New Jersey 65 to 81 F range. This lesson is for the facility tech who has to keep a resident wing inside those limits, including the day the power goes out.

Check the rule that applies to your building

Check the rule that applies to your building. This page was built from federal and state pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal advice. Which rule applies depends on the facility type, when it was first certified, and the state. Ask the facility administrator or the state health department before you rely on any number here.

The federal rule: 71 to 81 F

The federal nursing facility rules are in 42 CFR Part 483. Section 483.10(i)(6) is titled "Comfortable and safe temperature levels." It says facilities initially certified after October 1, 1990 must maintain a temperature range of 71 to 81 F. The rule is about the facility's certification date, not the age of the HVAC equipment. A building that has had three new chillers can still be an older-certified facility, and the CMS State Operations Manual appendix tells facilities to list their certification dates at the front of their emergency plan. This federal section is for nursing facilities. We did not read assisted living rules for this lesson, and those are set state by state, so check with your state agency.

New Jersey adds its own limits

A New Jersey Department of Health memo dated April 14, 2022 describes P.L. 2021, c. 260, which took effect May 1, 2022. It applies to nursing homes, dementia care homes and residential health care facilities. The memo says the maximum in resident-use areas dropped from 82 F to 81 F and that resident-use areas may not fall below 65 F, so the state range is 65 to 81 F. Facilities without air conditioning must run adequate ventilation, such as ceiling, wall or portable fans, in resident-use areas, and may not bill residents for that equipment. The memo lists two exemptions: rooms for strenuous activity, and living units where the resident controls the temperature independently. It also says a nursing home may follow the CMS levels where CMS sets a different range. The Commissioner may waive the limits for an unusual event beyond the facility's control, such as a declared emergency. The memo describes future rulemaking and gives no inspection or penalty process, so ask the facility how it logs and proves compliance.

In plain terms: for a federally certified nursing home first certified after 1990, the federal text sets a 71 F floor, and 81 F is the ceiling in both the federal and New Jersey rules. Our own advice for a tech: treat 81 F as a hard alarm point, not a goal. Aim for a setpoint well below it so a single failed unit does not put a room over the line.

Emergency preparedness: what the rule says about temperature and power

42 CFR 483.73 is the emergency preparedness rule for long-term care. Paragraph (b)(1)(ii) requires policies on alternate sources of energy to maintain temperatures to protect resident health and safety and for the safe, sanitary storage of provisions, plus emergency lighting. Paragraph (e) requires emergency and standby power systems based on the emergency plan, and (e)(2) requires the inspection, testing and maintenance that the referenced codes call for. Under (e)(3), a facility with an onsite fuel source needs a plan for keeping emergency power running during the emergency, unless it evacuates.

The CMS State Operations Manual guidance adds two points a tech should know. First, a facility does not have to heat or cool the whole building evenly. It must keep the necessary areas at safe temperatures as identified in its own risk assessment, and if it cannot, it needs a relocation or evacuation plan that acts in time. Second, the guidance says a portable generator should connect through a power transfer system following the maker's instructions, not through extension cords, and should be sited with ventilation, usually outdoors. Our generator room and carbon monoxide lessons cover the equipment side.

PTACs and through-wall units in resident rooms

Many resident rooms are served by a PTAC or heat pump version, one per room. That gives you something a central system does not: a failure takes out one room, not a wing. It also means 20 rooms have 20 filters, 20 condensate paths and 20 outdoor grilles. Our own checks on a round: filter clean, outdoor coil clear, condensate not backing up into the room, and the room reading on a thermometer that is not the unit's own sensor. Our PTAC lesson covers the internals.

For emergency power, a PTAC fleet is a large block of motor load. The first design question is which rooms or which common area carry the cooled-space plan, and the second is whether the generator can start those compressors without tripping.

Worked example: how much cooling, and can the generator carry it?

Every number in this example is our own assumption, not a rule or a maker's rating. Replace them with the real room loads, the maker's data and the generator's rating before you use the method.

  1. Rooms: 20 resident rooms in one wing, assumed 5,000 Btu/h of heat gain each in a hot afternoon (envelope, lights, equipment). 20 x 5,000 = 100,000 Btu/h.
  2. People: assume 24 people (20 residents plus 4 staff and visitors) at 450 Btu/h each. 24 x 450 = 10,800 Btu/h.
  3. Corridor and common area: assume 8,000 Btu/h.
  4. Total: 100,000 + 10,800 + 8,000 = 118,800 Btu/h. Divide by 12,000 Btu/h per ton: 118,800 / 12,000 = 9.9 tons.
  5. Installed PTACs: assume 20 units rated 9,000 Btu/h. 20 x 9,000 = 180,000 Btu/h, or 15 tons, so the nameplate capacity is about 1.5 times the estimated load. That margin helps the wing pull down after an outage.
  6. Generator: assume a 60 kW emergency set whose other loads (lighting, life safety, the nurse call system and similar) draw 35 kW, leaving 60 - 35 = 25 kW. Assume each PTAC draws 900 W while cooling. 20 x 900 = 18,000 W = 18 kW.
  7. Result: 25 - 18 = 7 kW of margin on paper, but compressors draw a much larger current at start. With all 20 starting together, the set could sag. In this example the answer is to cool selected rooms, or stagger starts with timed restarts, rather than put every unit on one start.

The point of the example is the method: load in tons, then watts at the generator, then a starting plan. The facility electrician and the generator maker own the final loading limit.

Soiled utility rooms, odors and infection control

The CDC environmental infection control guideline page says an HVAC system's purposes include controlling odors and removing contaminated air, that air from toilet rooms and other soiled areas is usually exhausted directly outdoors through a separate duct system, and that exhaust outlets for contaminated areas should sit above roof level and be arranged to limit recirculation. The same page says to monitor ventilation in line with the engineers' and makers' recommendations, to keep filters installed without gaps, to bag dust-filled filters on removal, and to avoid shutting down HVAC in patient-care areas except for maintenance, repair, testing or construction. On a soiled utility room that means the exhaust fan is an infection-control item, not a comfort item: a dead fan or a closed damper changes which way air moves toward the corridor. Our own check: confirm the exhaust fan runs and the door, with a tissue or smoke pencil, pulls air into the room. For pressure monitors and air changes, see our pressure rooms lesson instead of repeating it here.

Keeping older residents safe in heat

CDC says older adults adapt less well to sudden temperature changes, are more likely to have chronic conditions, and more often take medicines that interfere with temperature control or sweating. It also says not to count on a fan as the main way to cool off in very high temperatures, and to spend as much time as possible in cooled indoor spaces. For a facility tech, that is why a cooled common room is the backup when a wing loses cooling. The longer version is in our older adults heat and cold lesson.

Heat emergency plan: what a tech can do before the heat arrives

  • Test PTACs in spring, not on the first hot day.
  • Keep a log: room readings taken with a thermometer, not the unit sensor.
  • Know which circuits are on emergency power and which rooms they cool.
  • Check generator fuel and exhaust per the facility's testing plan, as 483.73(e) requires.
  • Tell the clinical lead early if a wing is drifting toward 81 F. Relocation is a clinical decision.

Field card: Nursing Home HVAC and Temperature Rules

Know the range, know the power path.

  • Federal 42 CFR 483.10(i)(6): 71 to 81 F for facilities first certified after October 1, 1990.
  • NJ law (P.L. 2021, c. 260): resident-use areas 65 to 81 F, with exemptions and an emergency waiver.
  • 42 CFR 483.73: alternate energy to maintain safe temperatures; test and maintain emergency power.
  • Soiled utility exhaust goes outdoors, separate from supply; a dead fan is an infection-control problem.
  • Fans are not the main cooling method in extreme heat; keep a cooled space.
  • Our own example: 118,800 Btu/h = 9.9 tons; check watts and starts on the generator.
