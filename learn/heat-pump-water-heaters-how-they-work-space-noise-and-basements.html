Heat Pump Water Heaters: How They Work, Space, Noise and Basements

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump water heater does not burn fuel or glow an element red-hot to make most of its heat. It collects warmth from the air around it and moves that warmth into the tank. That one fact explains nearly every pro and con: why it uses so little electricity, why it needs room to breathe, why it makes noise, and why a damp basement may get a little drier and cooler.

Check the manual for your model

Check the manual for your model. Numbers below come from specific manufacturer sheets and federal pages read in 2026. Different brands and sizes have different limits. This is general education, not an installation guide. The model label and installation manual in your hands win over anything on this page.

How it works in plain language

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that a stand-alone heat pump water heater pulls heat from the surrounding air and transfers it to water in a storage tank. Because it moves heat instead of making it, DOE says it can be two to three times as energy efficient as a conventional electric resistance water heater. ENERGY STAR puts it another way: a certified unit delivers roughly 3 to 4 units of heat for every 1 unit of electricity it uses. If you have read Heat Pump Basics, this is the same idea as a heat pump for the house, shrunk down and pointed at a tank. The cycle itself is covered in Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

UEF: the rating on the label

UEF stands for Uniform Energy Factor. ENERGY STAR describes it as the Department of Energy's standard measure of water heater efficiency, replacing the older Energy Factor in 2017. A higher UEF means less energy per unit of hot water, but only compare models in the same usage bin. ENERGY STAR says traditional water heaters generally fall between 0.63 and 0.95 UEF (a standard electric tank around 0.93), while certified heat pump models typically land between 3.3 and 4.1. ENERGY STAR's key product criteria set the floor at 3.30 for integrated heat pump models and 2.20 for those built for a 120 V, 15 A circuit.

Space and air volume

The unit is chilling the air it uses, so it needs a lot of air. DOE's page says the location needs at least 1,000 cubic feet of surrounding air, and that the room should stay between 40 and 90 degrees F year round. Manufacturers are more specific, and they differ:

  • Rheem ProTerra: its spec sheet says an unducted unit in a room over 700 cubic feet needs no extra ventilation. In a smaller room it calls for a full louvered door or two louvers, top and bottom. It lists an ambient operating range of 37 to 145 degrees F.
  • A. O. Smith Voltex: the product pages we read gave no air volume or temperature range (they point to the installation manual). They do offer an air duct adapter kit, and the company says no minimum space is required if the inlet and outlet are ducted with it.
  • Bradford White AeroTherm: its spec sheet says the heat pump runs between 35 and 120 degrees F air temperature, and outside that range the unit runs in electric mode.

ENERGY STAR's installation guide adds that most 240 V units need at least 700 cubic feet (some as little as 450), and that 120 V units meant to replace gas heaters may need more than 700. A tight utility closet is the classic problem. Cold air blowing out of a closed closet starves the unit and drops its efficiency. Ask the installer which number applies to the exact model.

Operating modes

Names vary, but the idea is the same: the unit can use the heat pump alone, the electric elements alone, or a mix. Bradford White lists Heat Pump (only), Hybrid (factory default, with electric help for faster recovery), Electric (only), and Vacation. Rheem lists Energy Saver, Heat Pump, High Demand, Electric and Vacation/Away. A. O. Smith lists Efficiency, Hybrid, Electric and Vacation. When the air or water is outside the heat pump's comfortable range, or when a big draw empties the tank, the elements pick up the slack. Electric mode behaves like an ordinary electric tank, and is the fallback if the compressor side is down. The older checks in Water Heater No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks still apply to the elements and thermostats.

Condensate drain

As the evaporator cools the air, water vapor condenses on it. ENERGY STAR's installation guide says the unit makes a small amount of clean, non-acidic condensate that can run to a nearby drain or be lifted by a condensate pump, and that the unit must be level so the line flows freely. A. O. Smith says a condensate pump is required where there is no suitable drain. A basement floor drain is ideal. If a pump is used, it can fail like any other; Condensate Pump Not Working covers the common causes. A drip under the unit may be condensate, not a tank leak; compare with Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom.

Cooling and drying the basement

DOE notes that heat pump water heaters cool the space they are in. A. O. Smith states plainly that the compressor pulling heat from the surrounding air also dehumidifies and cools that air. In a warm, damp basement in summer that can be a welcome side effect. In a cold basement in winter it is a downside: it chills the space and can push the room toward the unit's lower limit, so the elements run more. A water heater is not a substitute for a dehumidifier. See Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage for sizing real humidity control.

Noise

Expect the sound of a fan and compressor, about like a small refrigerator or dehumidifier. Published figures: Rheem lists 49 dBA for the ProTerra sheet we read, A. O. Smith 51 dBA on its Voltex release and 45 dBA on its 120 V model, and Bradford White about 55 dBA. These are manufacturer numbers measured their own way, so a bedroom wall above the unit is worth thinking about. ENERGY STAR's guide suggests a rigid foam base and isolation pads to cut vibration. Bradford White's Electric (only) mode runs with no heat pump airflow or sound.

120 V plug-in models

Some units are made for a 120 V circuit, aimed at swapping out a gas tank without new 240 V wiring. A. O. Smith's 120 V Voltex page lists a 120 VAC, 15 amp shared circuit and two 900-watt elements. ENERGY STAR's guide says some 120 V units need a dedicated circuit and others a shared one, and notes that they are typically sized two steps up from the gas unit (for example 40 gallons to 65). Their efficiency is lower than the 240 V units. Read the manual for the circuit your model needs; do not assume.

Worked example: yearly energy

ENERGY STAR's listing for a Rheem ProTerra model (CPROPH50 T2 RH400-30, a Canadian-market listing, medium usage, UEF 3.88) shows 846 kWh per year. ENERGY STAR gives a standard electric tank example at UEF 0.93. In the same usage bin, energy used is in inverse proportion to UEF, so the same hot water load on a 0.93 tank is estimated like this:

  1. Hot water energy delivered: 846 kWh x 3.88 = 3,282 kWh (rounded).
  2. Electric tank: 3,282 kWh / 0.93 = 3,529 kWh per year, about 3,530.
  3. Difference: 3,530 - 846 = 2,684 kWh per year, a drop of about 76 percent (1 - 0.93 / 3.88 = 0.76).

The 846 figure is from the listing; the 3,530 is our estimate by that ratio, not a label value. Your real use depends on household size, inlet water temperature and room conditions. For comparison, Rheem's own EnergyGuide article shows a ProTerra Hybrid label at 1,224 kWh per year; labels differ by model size and usage bin.

Pros and cons at a glance

Pros: uses about a quarter of the electricity of a standard electric tank in the example above; can cool and dry a warm basement; electric fallback when demand is high. Cons: needs air volume and a mild room; makes noise; needs a condensate path; cools the space; the elements still run in cold rooms or heavy draws.

Tech corner

ENERGY STAR's listing shows the Rheem unit as a 240 V integrated model with a 4.5 kW element input and R-134a refrigerant, with the compressor cutting out below 37 degrees F air temperature. Recovery efficiency is listed at 430 percent, which is the heat-pump advantage in one number. For gas-to-electric swaps, check breaker size and wire against the nameplate, per Electrical Basics for HVAC. Refrigerant work needs EPA 608 certification.

Field card: Heat Pump Water Heaters

Moves heat from room air into the tank, so it needs air and drains water.

  • Efficient: certified models typically UEF 3.3 to 4.1; standard electric about 0.93.
  • Space: DOE says 1,000 cubic feet; Rheem sheet says 700 cubic feet unducted. Check the model.
  • Air range varies: Rheem 37 to 145 F, Bradford White 35 to 120 F.
  • Needs a level unit and a condensate drain or pump.
  • Cools and dries the room; cold basements reduce efficiency.
  • Modes: heat pump only, hybrid, electric, vacation.
  • Noise: about 45 to 55 dBA per manufacturers.
  • 120 V models exist; follow the circuit rules in the manual.
