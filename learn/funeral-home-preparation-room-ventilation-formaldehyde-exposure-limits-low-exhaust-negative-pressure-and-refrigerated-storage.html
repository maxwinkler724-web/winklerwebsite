Funeral Home Preparation Room Ventilation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Funeral homes keep a room where bodies are prepared for viewing. The workers there handle formalin, a solution of formaldehyde, and formaldehyde is a regulated irritant and cancer hazard. This lesson covers the exposure limits, why the exhaust goes where it does, and what the airflow does to a heating bill.

Check the issuing agency

Check the issuing agency. Rules and guidance change, and the building code, the local inspector and the state board decide what applies to a job. This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It is not legal advice or a design.

The exposure limits

The federal formaldehyde standard is 29 CFR 1910.1048 in the eCFR. It sets a permissible exposure limit of 0.75 parts per million as an 8-hour time-weighted average, a short-term exposure limit of 2 ppm over 15 minutes, and an action level of 0.5 ppm as an 8-hour average. Paragraph (f)(1) says the employer must use engineering and work practice controls to hold exposure at or below those limits. Exposure monitoring must be repeated at least every six months when results are at or above the action level, and at least yearly when they are at or above the STEL.

NIOSH, in its hazard control sheet Controlling Formaldehyde Exposures During Embalming, says average exposures during embalming can reach 9 ppm, and that eyes, nose and throat irritation begin near 5 ppm. It names local exhaust ventilation as the primary control, and says plain dilution ventilation would need 4 to 13 times more exhaust air for similar control. The same sheet adds that local exhaust has much lower makeup air heating and cooling costs.

Exhaust near the table, not just somewhere in the room

NIOSH describes a local exhaust design with a pair of 6-foot slot hoods, one on each side of the table, drawing about 700 cfm through a fan located outside the room. For a 1-inch slot it lists a slot velocity of 720 feet per minute. The sheet does not discuss a low or downdraft grille.

Why low and close? Two reasons, and the second is our own reasoning. First, the vapor is released at the table, so pulling it away at the table keeps it from drifting past the worker's face. An older NIOSH health hazard evaluation of one funeral home shows the other side of this: its two small floor-level registers sat on a wall away from the tables, and NIOSH said they only diluted the room air. Second, formaldehyde gas has a vapor density of 1.04 relative to air in the NOAA CAMEO Chemicals entry, which describes it as heavier than air. That is only slightly heavier. We would not count on gravity alone; the sweep from a high supply across the table to a low grille does the work.

One-way air, no recirculation

In that same NIOSH evaluation the room was served by a rooftop unit that mixed in about 20 percent fresh air and returned the rest through ceiling louvers. NIOSH reported detector-tube readings of about 4 ppm at the tables and about 2 ppm at the supply louvers, and noted the filters only caught dust and removed no vapor. Its recommendations included a once-through air system, even though that might mean resizing refrigeration and heating equipment, and a formaldehyde meter with an alarm set at 1.0 ppm. The lesson for us: the preparation room air should be exhausted outdoors and not returned to a shared air handler. The New Jersey Department of Health preparation room guide (below) makes the same point from the code side: the BOCA code it cites requires the air to be exhausted to the outdoors at an approved exterior location. For discharge placement, see Laboratory Exhaust Fans and Stacks.

Negative pressure and the door

The same New Jersey guide, listing ASHRAE criteria that it borrows from autopsy rooms, says the room should be negatively pressurized in relation to adjacent areas. It also says a source of makeup air should be provided so the room does not go excessively negative and so the air mixes better. Corridor air leaks in under the door (the undercut in the drawing) or through a transfer opening, and room air does not leak out. With zero makeup, the door would pull hard and exhaust flow at the table would fall. Pressure monitors and anterooms are covered in Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms, so we will not repeat it. The guide gives no pressure number, so we state none.

What New Jersey rules say

N.J.A.C. 13:36-5.5, the State Board of Mortuary Science rule on preparation rooms, allows outside ventilation by windows, transoms or air conditioning, and says every preparation room must comply with State and local ventilation laws. It also says the room must be ventilated so no deleterious odors enter any other part of the funeral establishment. The text we read gives no exhaust rate and no air change rate, and it has no refrigeration wording. The Department of Health guide, listed 2006, gives the industry practice of 10 to 15 air changes per hour from the New Jersey Funeral Directors Association, and cites 12 ACH minimums for autopsy rooms as a guide. Treat 10 to 15 ACH as a starting range to check against the local code official, not a mandate.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

All inputs below are assumptions we made up to show the arithmetic. They are not from a real building.

  • Room: 20 ft by 16 ft with a 9 ft ceiling.
  • Target: 15 air changes per hour, the top of the 10 to 15 range above.
  • Winter outdoor temperature: 15°F. Makeup air heated to 55°F.
  • Makeup air supplied: 620 cfm, so some inward leakage remains.

Step 1, volume. 20 x 16 x 9 = 2,880 cubic feet.

Step 2, exhaust. The guide's method: required cfh = volume x ACH, then cfm = cfh / 60. So 2,880 x 15 = 43,200 cfh, and 43,200 / 60 = 720 cfm. At 12 ACH the same room would need 576 cfm.

Step 3, inward balance. 720 cfm exhaust minus 620 cfm makeup leaves 100 cfm to come in under the door and through leaks. We picked 100 cfm for the example. Real offset is set by test and balance.

Step 4, heating load. We use the sensible heat formula, Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise, explained in HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas. The rise is 55 - 15 = 40°F. So Q = 1.08 x 620 x 40 = 26,784 Btu/h, about 27,000 Btu/h.

Step 5, check. If all 720 cfm had to be outdoor air heated the same way: 1.08 x 720 x 40 = 31,104 Btu/h.

That heat is paid for every hour the fan runs. Equipment that tempers this air is described in Make-Up Air Units.

Refrigerated storage as a load

Many preparation rooms have a refrigerated unit for holding remains before and after preparation. To a refrigeration tech it is a small walk-in or a reach-in cabinet with a box load, door openings, a condensing unit that rejects heat, and a defrost cycle. Its heat rejection goes to the room or to the outdoors, so where the condensing unit sits matters to the room's air balance. Do not place a condensing unit so that it draws room air that carries vapor. We found no public New Jersey rule giving a required holding temperature, so we state none. See Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics for load and defrost basics.

What a tech checks

  • Exhaust runs whenever the room is in use and the fan is outside the room, so the duct is under negative pressure inside the building.
  • The grille is near the table, not across the room, and is not blocked by cabinets.
  • No return air from this room goes to a shared air handler.
  • The door pulls inward and the undercut or transfer path is clear. A smoke pencil or a strip of tissue at the door shows direction.
  • Makeup air is actually running.

Field card: Funeral Home Preparation Room Ventilation

Capture at the table, exhaust outdoors, keep the room negative.

  • OSHA formaldehyde: PEL 0.75 ppm (8-hour), STEL 2 ppm (15 minutes), action level 0.5 ppm.
  • NIOSH names local exhaust near the table as the primary control, not room dilution alone.
  • No recirculation of the room air to a shared air handler.
  • Room negative to adjacent areas, with some makeup air so the exhaust is not starved.
  • Airflow starting range from NJ health guide: 10 to 15 ACH. Exhaust cfm = volume x ACH / 60.
  • Makeup heating: Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise.
  • Refrigerated storage is a normal refrigeration load; mind where it rejects heat.
