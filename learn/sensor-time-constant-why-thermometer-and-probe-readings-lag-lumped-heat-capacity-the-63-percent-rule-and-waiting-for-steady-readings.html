Sensor Time Constant: Why Probe Readings Lag and How Long to Wait

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermometer does not read the temperature of the thing you touch. It reads its own temperature, and that has to catch up. This lesson explains the lag, the simple math behind it, and how to tell when a supply-air, superheat or subcooling reading has stopped moving because the system settled, not because you got tired of waiting.

Why a probe lags

Every sensor has some mass, and mass has to be warmed or cooled before the sensor can show a new temperature. A university atmospheric sciences lab page says the lag exists because the instrument itself has a finite heat capacity. Heat reaches the sensor only through its surface, by convection from the air or fluid around it. So a big, heavy sensor in still air is slow, and a tiny sensor in fast air is quick.

The lumped heat capacity model

If the sensor is small enough that it is nearly one temperature all the way through, we can treat it as a single lump. Heat transfer lecture notes from Seoul National University give that test as a Biot number below about 0.1, and define the time constant as tau = rho V c / (h A). In sensor terms, rho V c is the sensor's heat capacity (what we write as m c), h is the convection coefficient at its surface, and A is the surface area touching the air or fluid. The same notes show the lump's temperature difference from its surroundings decaying as e^(-t/tau).

Read the formula as a ratio: heat capacity on top, heat-flow ability on the bottom. More mass or more specific heat makes tau longer. More surface area or a higher h makes tau shorter.

The 63.2 percent rule

A university temperature-measurement handout defines the time constant as the time for a sensor to complete 63.2 percent of the change from its starting temperature to the final temperature. The handout writes this as e^(-t/tau) = (Tfinal - T) / (Tfinal - T0). Rearranged (our own step), the reading after a sudden change is T = Ta + (T0 - Ta) e^(-t/tau), where T0 is the starting reading, Ta is the new surrounding temperature and t is the time since the change.

The other percentages follow from the exponential (our own arithmetic): after 1 tau the probe has completed 63.2 percent of the step, after 2 tau 86.5 percent, after 3 tau 95.0 percent, after 4 tau 98.2 percent and after 5 tau 99.3 percent. An international meteorological instrument presentation on air thermometers makes the same point: a 95 percent response takes about three time constants.

About five time constants to settle

A sensor maker's white paper on time constants says five time constants is roughly equivalent to a fully stabilized value. It adds that real sensors are more complicated than the simple model, and their time constant tends to grow as they near the final value. Treat five tau as a planning figure, not a guarantee. The practical point: the last degree or two of a reading takes a long time to arrive.

What makes tau shorter or longer

  • Thin beats thick. In that presentation's tests of platinum resistance thermometers, diameter was the key factor, and it recommended sensors of 3 mm diameter or less. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory report found the 63.2 percent response time of a sheathed thermocouple rose roughly as the square of the sheath diameter.
  • Moving air beats still air. Faster air raises h. In those tests, the average 63.2 percent time constant fell from 68.0 s at 0.5 m/s to 35.4 s at 3.0 m/s. The maker white paper also describes time constant as roughly inversely proportional to the convection coefficient.
  • Thermowells lag. In the Oak Ridge report, response in a thermowell was dominated by how well the sensor was thermally coupled to the well wall. In dry wells the poor contact was the major factor, and in a lab test, liquid metal filling the gap made the response about ten times faster. Our takeaway: a loose sensor in an empty well reads late. See our sensor mounting lesson for wells and mounting.
  • Pipe clamps lag (our reasoning). A clamp sensor has to wait for the pipe wall, the clamp and the contact film to change temperature, and it loses some heat to room air on its outside face. That puts extra mass and resistance in series, so tau is usually much larger than a bare probe in air. We have no published figure for it, so the 90 s used below is an assumption.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Example 1: probe moved into supply air. Assumptions: a probe with tau = 20 s, reading 75 F in room air, is plunged into a steady 55 F supply airstream that stays at 55 F. The step is 75 - 55 = 20 F, so T = 55 + 20 e^(-t/20).

  • 20 s: e^(-1) = 0.3679, so 20 x 0.3679 = 7.36, T = 62.4 F
  • 40 s: e^(-2) = 0.1353, so 20 x 0.1353 = 2.71, T = 57.7 F
  • 60 s: e^(-3) = 0.0498, so 20 x 0.0498 = 1.00, T = 56.0 F
  • 100 s: e^(-5) = 0.0067, so 20 x 0.0067 = 0.13, T = 55.1 F

The reading falls from 75 F toward 55 F and never overshoots. At 20 s it is still 7.4 F too warm. If you wrote down the number at 20 s, the supply air would look about 7 F warmer than it is, and the temperature split (return minus supply) would look about 7 F too small.

How long until it is within 0.5 F of 55 F? We need 20 e^(-t/20) ≤ 0.5, so e^(-t/20) ≤ 0.025, so t ≥ 20 x ln 40 = 20 x 3.69 = about 74 s.

Example 2: clamp sensor on a suction line. Assumptions: a clamp sensor with tau = 90 s sits on a suction line whose true temperature changes from 50 F to 45 F and then holds. The step is 5 F, so T = 45 + 5 e^(-t/90).

  • 90 s: 5 x 0.3679 = 1.84, T = 46.8 F
  • 180 s: 5 x 0.1353 = 0.68, T = 45.7 F
  • Within 0.5 F means 5 e^(-t/90) ≤ 0.5, so e^(-t/90) ≤ 0.1, so t ≥ 90 x ln 10 = 90 x 2.303 = about 207 s, roughly 3.5 minutes
  • Five time constants is 450 s, 7.5 minutes, when the clamp reads 45.03 F

The clamp took nearly three times as long as the air probe to get within 0.5 F (207 s against 74 s), even though its step was only 5 F. A slow sensor makes every wait longer, and a small step does not save you as much as you might hope.

Waiting for steady readings in the field

  • Two things are settling. The system and the sensor both take time. A reading that is still creeping may be the sensor catching up, or the system still changing. A sensor catching up slows down smoothly, the way the curve above does.
  • Supply air. After moving a probe from room air into the duct, give it several time constants and watch for the number to stop moving. Fast air helps the probe, so put it in the airstream, not against the duct wall.
  • Superheat and subcooling. Line sensors lag more than air probes, so wait until the line temperature and the pressure reading are both steady before you subtract. A reading taken during a slow drift can leave a superheat or subcooling number that is off by the lag. See superheat and subcooling basics.
  • Cut the lag you can. Press the clamp tight to clean pipe, insulate over it, and keep the sensor out of room air.
  • Do not take a wait time from this page. Our time constants are assumed. Follow the meter maker's instructions and the equipment instructions, and watch the live reading.

For the cooler and freezer version of this idea, see air versus product temperature. Humidity sensors lag too; see humidity meters and wireless probes. An infrared camera sees only surfaces, so it has its own limits: thermal imaging cameras.

Field card: Sensor Time Constant: Why Probe Readings Lag and How Long to Wait

A probe reads its own temperature. Wait for it to catch up.

  • Tau = heat capacity / (h x area). Less mass, more surface, faster air: shorter tau.
  • T = Ta + (T0 - Ta) e^(-t/tau). It moves toward the new value and does not overshoot.
  • 1 tau: 63.2 percent. 2 tau: 86.5. 3 tau: 95.0. 5 tau: 99.3.
  • Thin probe in moving air is fast. Clamp and thermowell sensors are slow.
  • Our example: tau 20 s, 75 F to 55 F air, reads 62.4 F at 20 s and 56.0 F at 60 s.
  • Our example: tau 90 s clamp, 50 F to 45 F, within 0.5 F after about 207 s.
  • Watch the live reading until it stops moving before you calculate.
