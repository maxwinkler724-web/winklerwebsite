Gas Fireplace Inserts in Existing Fireplaces: Liners, Chimney Checks, Heat Output and Service

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An open fireplace sends most of its heat up the chimney. A gas insert is a sealed, factory-made firebox that slides into the old opening and is vented through liners, so more of the heat stays in the room. This lesson explains what an insert is, why the old chimney needs attention first, and how to judge the heat it gives.

Insert, log set or stove?

These three get mixed up. A gas log set is a burner and decorative logs placed inside the existing firebox, and the old chimney carries the exhaust. We cover it in Vented Gas Log Sets in Wood-Burning Fireplaces. A gas insert is a complete appliance with its own firebox, glass front and controls, fitted inside an existing masonry or factory-built fireplace. A freestanding gas stove sits on the hearth or floor and has its own vent. A national lab survey of hearth products sorts units into these same types: fireplace or insert, log set, stove and outdoor unit (national laboratory survey). For fireplaces that were built as gas fireplaces from the start, see Gas Fireplaces: Direct Vent, B-Vent and Vent-Free.

How an insert is vented

Our own plain-language description: many inserts are direct vent, which means the burn chamber is sealed from the room. Outdoor air comes in and exhaust goes out through the vent system. In an old chimney that is usually done one of two ways: two separate flexible liners, one carrying combustion air down and one carrying exhaust up, or one coaxial liner, a pipe inside a pipe, that does both jobs. The exact kit, length limits and termination are set by the maker, and the model fuel gas code language one city reproduces says listed direct-vent appliances are installed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (a city fuel gas code chapter). The same text says the clearance table does not apply to the combustion air intake of a direct-vent appliance. The installer does not improvise the vent from parts on the truck.

Why the chimney comes first

The old chimney is now a pathway for liners, so its condition matters before any insert goes in.

  • Inspect and clean. A university extension fireplace safety publication says fireplaces and chimneys should be inspected and the flue kept free of soot, creosote and obstructions. Heating systems, vents and chimneys should be inspected and cleaned by a qualified professional (federal product safety agency; a university extension). The federal public health agency says to have the chimney checked or cleaned every year, and warns that a blocked chimney lets carbon monoxide build up indoors.
  • Look for blockages and damage. The inspector checks chimneys and flues for blockages, corrosion and disconnections (federal product safety agency). The model code text we read also expects a masonry flue to have a cleanout, a clear passageway and a continuous liner without cracks or gaps (a city fuel gas code chapter).
  • Check that the pair is allowed. The same code text says an appliance is not connected to a flue serving a factory-built fireplace unless the appliance is specifically listed for it. The federal environmental agency says a factory-built fireplace needs an insert specifically designed and tested for that unit. A city handout says never to put an insert into a metal fireplace unless both the fireplace and the insert are listed for that use (a city wood stove and insert brochure; that handout is about wood appliances, so we use it only for the matching rule). Check the labels on both.
  • Use listed liner material. A city handout says metal liners in masonry chimneys must be of approved, listed materials installed to the listing (a city building handout). Our related lesson Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat covers liner basics.

Sealing the damper area

A damper left open around the liners would let room air flow up the chimney and let cold air drop down. City guidance for a gas appliance in a masonry fireplace says the old damper must be removed or welded open (a city fireplace conversion handout), and that handout is for gas logs, not inserts. A wood-insert brochure says a connection works only if the damper is permanently sealed airtight and filled with fireproof material (the same city brochure). Those two rules point different ways because the appliances differ. For an insert, follow the maker's instructions for the block-off plate and insulation, and the local inspector's view. Heat output and efficiency

The federal energy department says traditional open masonry fireplaces, designed more for show, should not be considered heating devices. The federal environmental agency calls open fireplaces typically very inefficient heaters and says decorative gas logs are not designed to be a significant source of heat. Those pages are about wood and decorative logs. The energy department also says a well-fitted wood insert can work nearly as efficiently as a wood stove, which shows why a sealed, fitted firebox beats an open one. We found no federal rating for gas inserts, and the national lab survey says its authors are not aware of reliable efficiency estimates for fireplaces. So the efficiency below is our assumption, not a measured value. Use the maker's label for your unit.

The survey lists a typical main burner input of 35 kBtu/h for vented fireplaces, inserts and stoves, and 1.0 kBtu/h for a standing pilot. Our example uses a smaller insert so the numbers stay simple.

Worked example: insert versus vented log set

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume an insert with 30,000 Btu/h input at 75 percent delivered efficiency, and a vented log set with 35,000 Btu/h input (the survey typical) at 20 percent of heat reaching the room. Both efficiencies are assumptions. We assume 240 burn hours per year, close to the survey average of 234 hours for a main burner. One therm is 100,000 Btu.

  1. Insert gas per hour: 30,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.30 therm. Over 240 hours: 0.30 × 240 = 72 therms.
  2. Insert heat delivered: 30,000 × 0.75 = 22,500 Btu/h. Over 240 hours: 22,500 × 240 = 5,400,000 Btu, or 54 therms of heat.
  3. Log set gas per hour: 35,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.35 therm. Over 240 hours: 84 therms.
  4. Log set heat delivered: 35,000 × 0.20 = 7,000 Btu/h. Over 240 hours: 7,000 × 240 = 1,680,000 Btu, or 16.8 therms of heat.
  5. Heat delivered per therm burned: 0.75 for the insert and 0.20 for the log set, a ratio of 0.75 ÷ 0.20 = 3.75.
  6. To match the insert's 22,500 Btu/h, the log set would need 22,500 ÷ 0.20 = 112,500 Btu/h of input, which is 1.125 therms per hour.

With these assumptions the insert delivers about 3.2 times as much heat for 14 percent less gas (5,400,000 ÷ 1,680,000 = 3.21; 72 ÷ 84 = 0.857). A standing pilot adds gas on top: using the survey figures of 1.0 kBtu/h for 4,593 hours, 1.0 × 4,593 = 4,593 kBtu, or 45.9 therms a year. That is why the ignition type matters.

Pilot and ignition types

The survey describes a standing pilot as lit continuously unless the user turns it off, and an intermittent pilot as lit only on a call for heat and then put out automatically. Among gas units it found 67 percent standing pilot, 19 percent intermittent and 14 percent match lit. Intermittent systems need power to light, so ask what happens in an outage and what the manual says to do. The log set lesson explains pilot sensors in more detail.

Blowers, power, clearances and surround panels

Many inserts have a blower to move room air past the firebox, which needs an outlet or wired supply. Our own advice: read the maker's manual for the power requirement, the control location and any battery backup, and keep cords away from hot surfaces. For clearances, one city says to follow the listing instructions for a listed appliance (a city building handout). The surround panel or trim kit covers the gap between the insert and the old opening, and the manual says which kit and which mantel and wall clearances apply. Do not trim or substitute panels. A permit is required in that city for gas, oil and solid fuel appliances, and one installed without it may void fire insurance, so ask your local office.

Carbon monoxide alarms and yearly service

The federal product safety agency says to put CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, test monthly, and not to ignore an alarm that sounds. The federal public health agency says to replace a detector by the maker's instructions or every 5 years, and to have gas appliances serviced each year. Alarms back up maintenance and do not replace it. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

A yearly service visit can include: checking the liners and termination for blockage, corrosion and loose joints; checking seals and the glass gasket; cleaning the burner, pilot and blower; checking that the control and safety work; and testing for CO near the unit. Follow the maker's list.

Field card: Gas Fireplace Inserts

Fix and clean the chimney first, then fit a listed liner kit.

  • Insert: sealed firebox in an old fireplace. Log set: burner in an open firebox. Stove: freestanding.
  • Direct vent: two liners (air in, exhaust out) or one coaxial liner.
  • Inspect and clean the chimney before and each year after.
  • Seal the damper area as the maker directs.
  • Our assumed example: 75 percent versus 20 percent delivered, 3.75 times the heat per therm.
  • Alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas. Test monthly.
