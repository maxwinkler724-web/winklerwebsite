Wire Terminations in HVAC Controls: Connectors, Crimps and Ferrules

Last updated: 2026-11-01

Many HVAC electrical callbacks trace back to a wire end, not a failed part. A twist-on connector that was never snug, a crimp done with pliers, or a strand left out of a barrel can run fine for a season and then cook a contactor. This lesson covers how to make a wire end that stays put, and how to read one that has already gone bad.

Start with the rule you are working under

Federal workplace rules set the baseline. OSHA's electrical standard at 29 CFR 1910.303 says listed or labeled equipment must be installed and used according to the instructions in its listing. It also says a conductor connected to a terminal must make a good connection without being damaged, using pressure connectors, solder lugs or similar means, and that splices must be made with devices identified for the use. The NEC carries matching installation rules. In plain terms: the terminal block, the connector and the tool each come with a marking or instruction, and that marking governs that joint.

Always prove the circuit dead before you strip or touch a wire. See Loose Electrical Connections in HVAC Equipment for torque values, aluminum lugs and finding hot spots. This lesson stays on the wire end itself.

Strip length and nicked conductors

Strip to the length the connector or terminal asks for, no more. Too long leaves bare copper outside the connector or barrel. Too short leaves too little wire to grip. A California state transportation agency's equipment page says strip lengths must follow the terminal maker's recommendation and that wires must be stripped with the proper tool. Use a stripper sized to the gauge, not a knife or the notch of a pair of side cutters.

A nicked conductor is a weak point that breaks later and heats in the meantime. NASA's workmanship standard for crimping and cabling (written for spacecraft, so stricter than an air handler needs) rejects a conductor that is nicked, cut or scraped until base metal shows, and bars stripping tools that nick or gouge. If you see a ring cut into the copper, cut that end off and strip again.

Twist-on connectors

  • Read the marking. A university extension guide says connectors are color-coded for size and that you follow the label for the number and sizes of conductors. The wire count and gauge combinations are on the package or the connector, so do not guess from the color alone. A combination such as one 14 gauge with one 18 gauge is either listed or it is not.
  • Prepare the ends. The same guide says to twist the conductors clockwise and cut them even, then spin the connector on clockwise. It also says to twist stranded wire first and squeeze it with pliers. Some connector labels call for pre-twisting and some say to let the connector do the twisting, so follow the label on the one in your hand.
  • No bare copper showing. The point of the connector is to keep conductors from touching each other or exposed metal. Look at the skirt after seating it. A bare strand sticking out the bottom is a short waiting to happen, so strip a little less or step up a size.
  • Tug each wire. Pull each conductor gently after seating. If one slips, redo it. Splices also belong in a box or enclosure. OSHA once answered that a motor lead joined to facility wiring and covered only with electrical tape violated the standard.
  • Not for every job. The extension guide notes these connectors are not often used on wire thicker than 10 AWG. The consumer safety agency also says twist-on connectors are not an acceptable permanent repair on aluminum wiring, because many overheated severely in its testing.

Crimped spade, ring and fork terminals

Insulated terminals are color-coded by wire range, so read the range printed on the terminal or its box instead of trusting memory. The California agency page names red, blue or yellow nylon-insulated double-crimp terminals for 22 to 10 gauge but does not give a color-to-gauge chart. Use a ring where a screw could loosen, since a ring stays captive. A spade or fork can slide out if the screw backs off.

The tool matters as much as the terminal. The same agency page says to use the tool the terminal maker recommends, and for 22 to 10 gauge it names a ratcheting tool. NASA's standard requires a ratchet that does not release until the crimp cycle is complete, and says a damaged tool comes out of service. A ratcheting crimper gives a repeatable squeeze. Pliers crush the barrel unevenly and leave a joint that looks done but is not.

What a good crimp looks like

NASA's inspection guide for crimped terminals gives the picture. The wire ends must be visible in the barrel and the conductor should reach the crimp edge or slightly beyond. Rejects include protruding or birdcaged strands, damaged insulation, an incomplete crimp, indents that crowd the inspection hole, and a barrel with cracked plating. The standard sets the insulation gap at 0.25 mm (0.01 in.) minimum, and 1.25 mm (0.05 in.) maximum for 18 AWG and larger. In field terms, there should be a hair of space between insulation and barrel, not a bare inch.

Tug tests

The spacecraft standard tests crimps with a measured pull. For copper wire it lists minimum pull-out forces of 32 lb (142 N) for 18 AWG, 41 lb for 16 AWG, 65 lb for 14 AWG, 103 lb for 12 AWG and 159 lb for 10 AWG, and it halts production until three of three test samples pass. Those figures come from that standard, not from an HVAC rule, and you will not hang a scale on a thermostat wire. The idea carries over: a good crimp holds far more than the wire's own weight. Give every crimp a firm hand tug before you land it. If it moves, cut it off and do it again.

Ferrules, push-in connectors and screw terminals

Stranded wire under a screw clamp can spread out and squirm away from the pressure. A wire-end ferrule, a small crimped sleeve, bundles the strands into one tidy conductor. Whether a terminal accepts a ferrule, and what size, is set by the equipment maker's instructions, so check them. For soldering, NASA's standard prohibits crimping solder-tinned stranded wire. As our own practice, we do not solder stranded ends that will sit under a clamp unless the equipment instructions say to.

Push-in terminals follow the same logic. They list the wire gauge, strip length and whether solid or stranded wire is allowed, usually printed on the device. Treat that marking as the rule, and give the wire a tug after it seats.

Aluminum versus copper

OSHA 1910.303 says pressure connectors must be identified for the material, and that dissimilar metals may not be intermixed in a terminal or splicing connector where they touch. Terminals used for aluminum must be marked as such. The consumer safety agency adds that aluminum connection damage is cumulative and that failures have happened with no prior warning. If a terminal's marking does not say aluminum, do not put aluminum in it. For the grounding side, see Equipment Grounding and GFCI for HVAC Equipment.

Heat-damaged terminals on contactors and capacitors

Look for brown or blue discoloration on a terminal, melted or brittle insulation on the wire, a cracked contactor housing, or a capacitor terminal that has loosened or burned off its connector. Heat starts at the joint: resistance there turns current into heat, so damage tells you the joint ran hot for a long time.

  • A discolored wire end is no longer good wire. Cut back to clean bright copper, re-strip and fit a new terminal. Do not just re-crimp the old one.
  • If the terminal on the part itself is pitted, burned or loose, replace the contactor or capacitor, since a fresh connector will not stay tight on softened metal.
  • Check the other end of that same circuit. A hot contactor lug often has a partner with the same problem.

Worked example: heat at a bad connection (our own)

Our own example. Assumptions: a compressor circuit carrying 25 A; a poor crimp at 0.05 ohm; a good joint at 0.001 ohm. These resistances are assumed round numbers for the comparison, not measurements of a particular part.

  1. Heat in a resistance is P = I2R. With 25 A, I2 = 25 × 25 = 625.
  2. Bad joint: 625 × 0.05 = 31.25 W.
  3. Good joint: 625 × 0.001 = 0.625 W.
  4. Ratio: 31.25 / 0.625 = 50, so the bad joint makes 50 times the heat.
  5. Voltage drop across each, V = I × R: bad joint 25 × 0.05 = 1.25 V; good joint 25 × 0.001 = 0.025 V.

About 31 W is released inside a lug the size of a fingertip with almost no airflow, while the good joint adds less than a watt. The 1.25 V drop is also 1.25 V the compressor does not get; on a 240 V circuit that is about half a percent, small by itself, but the heat is the real problem. See Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits for the drop formula applied to whole runs.

Field card: Wire Terminations in HVAC Controls: Connectors, Crimps and Ferrules

Marking is the rule, tool is half the joint, tug proves it.

  • Dead first. Prove it with a meter.
  • Strip with a gauge-sized stripper to the marked length. Recut any nicked end.
  • Twist-on connector: follow the label for wire count and gauge, no bare copper showing, tug each wire.
  • Terminal color shows wire range; read the range on the box.
  • Use a ratcheting crimper sized for the terminal. Insulation to barrel, strands in, crimp on metal.
  • Ferrule stranded wire only where the equipment instructions allow it.
  • Aluminum only in terminals marked for it.
  • Burned terminal: cut back to clean copper, new terminal, replace a burned part.
  • Example: 25 A at 0.05 ohm = 31.25 W; at 0.001 ohm = 0.625 W.
