Opposed vs Parallel Blade Dampers: Curves, Authority, Leakage

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A damper is a variable restriction, and the blade layout decides how that restriction grows as the blades close. Two layouts cover almost every multi-blade damper you will meet in an air handler or duct: parallel blades and opposed blades. This lesson explains how each behaves, how to judge whether a damper can control at all, and how to check one in the field.

Two blade layouts

On a parallel-blade damper every blade rotates the same way at the same time, so the blades stay parallel to each other at every position. On an opposed-blade damper neighboring blades rotate in opposite directions, so as the damper closes the blades form a series of V shapes. Both can be built with the same frame, shafts and linkage, which is why you often need to look at the blades themselves to tell them apart.

How each layout shapes airflow versus position

With the same pressure across the damper, airflow does not fall in a straight line as the blades close. The shape of that line is the damper's characteristic. In field terms, the layouts differ in how much the first part of the stroke does. A university construction specification we read puts a number on this through its sizing rules: opposed-blade dampers sized for at least 10 percent of the duct system pressure drop, and parallel-blade dampers sized for at least 30 percent, and it adds a balancing damper where more drop is needed to get a linear response. In plain words, a parallel-blade damper needs more of the system's pressure drop assigned to it before it will respond smoothly, while an opposed-blade damper gets there with less.

A university computer study of dampers at the outdoor and return air inlets of an air handler found that opposed blades produced a higher pressure drop than parallel blades, which the authors attribute to the blades turning toward each other and resisting the flow. At the same blade angle, the opposed outdoor-air damper passed less air than the parallel one, while the opposed return-air damper passed more as the angle opened. That is one simulated unit, so treat it as direction, not a rule for every damper. A national laboratory report on self-correcting controls adds a field point: actual damper response is non-linear, so a linear signal-to-position assumption can misset a minimum position.

Which layout for which job

The same university specification calls for two-position (open or closed) dampers to be parallel-blade, and allows either type for modulating service. A common way to read that is: parallel for open/closed and mixing duty, opposed for modulating and balancing duty. Parallel blades have low resistance wide open and are fine when the damper is either open or closed. Opposed blades give a smoother, more usable response across the stroke when something must be trimmed to a flow, such as a balancing damper in a branch. The specification we read for ductwork accessories from another university requires opposed-blade construction for rectangular balancing dampers and limits each to 0.05 in. w.c. of drop at design airflow, which keeps a balancing damper from stealing fan power when it is wide open.

Damper authority

Damper authority is our name for a simple ratio: the pressure drop across the fully open damper divided by the total pressure drop of the branch or system it controls, with the damper included, at design flow.

Authority = damper drop (full open) / total drop

A high ratio means the damper is a big part of the circuit, so changing its position changes the flow. A low ratio means the rest of the duct dominates, and the damper does almost nothing until it is nearly closed. This is why the sizing minimums in the specification above are expressed as a share of system pressure drop.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions: a branch has a total drop of 0.75 in. w.c. at design flow. The damper is fully open at 0.15 in. w.c. at that flow. The fan keeps 0.75 in. w.c. available across the branch no matter what the damper does, and every drop varies with the square of flow. These are simplifications to show the idea.

  1. Authority = 0.15 / 0.75 = 0.20, or 20 percent.
  2. The rest of the branch is 0.75 - 0.15 = 0.60 in. w.c. at design flow.
  3. Close the damper until its resistance is 4 times the full-open value. Its drop at design flow would be 4 x 0.15 = 0.60. Flow fraction = square root of 0.75 / (0.60 + 0.60) = square root of 0.625 = 0.79.
  4. At 9 times the open resistance: 0.75 / (0.60 + 1.35) = 0.385, square root = 0.62.
  5. At 16 times: 0.75 / (0.60 + 2.40) = 0.25, square root = 0.50.

Result: the damper had to raise its own resistance sixteen-fold to halve the flow, and its first fourfold increase trimmed only about 21 percent. In a real damper that means most of the useful flow change is packed into the last part of the stroke, so the control loop sees low gain early and high gain late, which invites hunting. Against the sizing rules we read, 20 percent is above the 10 percent minimum for opposed blades and below the 30 percent minimum for parallel blades. So this damper is reasonable as an opposed-blade modulating damper and a poor fit as a parallel-blade modulating damper. For open/closed duty the ratio matters little, since the damper only needs to be open or closed. If a modulating damper must be parallel, one option is adding drop elsewhere in the branch or choosing a smaller damper, within the fan's capacity.

Blade seals, jamb seals and leakage classes

Blade seals sit along each blade edge and jamb (side) seals sit where the end blades meet the frame. The specification we read asks for dampers furnished with blade seals and stainless steel side seals, with flexible edge seals in harsher exhaust service, and ties allowed leakage to a damper leakage class from a test standard (named in plain text only: a damper-industry air leakage rating standard). The tightest classes are for dampers that must shut off airflow. Class numbers are set by a standardized test at stated pressures, so compare classes only under the same test.

Leakage matters in practice. In the self-correcting controls report, the authors note that when one damper in a mixing section is fully open, about 10 percent leakage occurs through the other. A university and national laboratory study of low-pressure air-handling leakage found minimum-position VAV box damper openings from 0 to 9.3 sq. in., averaging 2.7 sq. in., with flow through that opening as much as 23 percent of design in some cases.

Stratification after a parallel-blade damper

Because parallel blades steer the stream to one side, a mixing section behind them can deliver layered air: cold outdoor air on one side and warm return air on the other. A freeze or temperature sensor placed in one layer then reads wrong. A national laboratory evaluation of outdoor-air measurement found that the louver and damper produce highly non-uniform velocities in the plane just behind them, and that placing a damper close to a probe changed errors substantially. The university mixing study found opposed blades mixed better than parallel in most partly open cases, though not at every angle. Averaging sensors, distance, and mixing devices help.

Field checks

  • De-energize first. Lock out and tag the fan and any actuator power before reaching into a duct or linkage.
  • Stroke. Command the actuator open and closed and watch the shaft. Does it reach both ends? A guide for re-tuning building controls says a damper commanded fully open with near-zero airflow may be failed closed through linkage failure, and the reverse for failed open.
  • Linkage. Look for loose set screws, slipped actuators and bent rods. Actuator and linkage wear can change minimum positions after an air balance, according to a national laboratory report.
  • Blade closure. With the damper closed, look for light, gaps, bent blades and torn seals.
  • Leakage by feel or smoke. This is our own field practice, not from a source: with the damper commanded closed and the fan off, feel for air at the edges, or on a running system watch a small puff of test smoke made for airflow tracing. Check building rules and detector locations first.

The re-tuning guide above also notes that a damper is a good candidate for control tuning only if its actuator and linkage are not broken, its seals are good and leakage is minimal when closed. Fix the hardware before touching the settings.

Field card: Opposed vs Parallel Blade Dampers

Parallel to open and close, opposed to trim, and check authority first.

  • Parallel blades turn together; opposed neighbors turn opposite ways.
  • Two-position and mixing duty: parallel. Modulating and balancing duty: opposed.
  • Authority = full-open damper drop / total branch drop.
  • Our example: 0.15 / 0.75 = 0.20, so flow falls only 21 percent over the first fourfold resistance rise.
  • Spec sizing rules we read: opposed at least 10 percent, parallel at least 30 percent.
  • Parallel blades can leave layered air; place sensors with that in mind.
  • Check stroke, linkage, blade closure and seals before changing settings.
