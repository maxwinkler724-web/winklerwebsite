How Long Do HVAC Systems Last? Repair or Replace

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Nobody can tell you the exact day a furnace or air conditioner will quit. What you can do is understand typical ranges, weigh a repair against a replacement, and know which warning signs are really safety problems.

Typical lifespans are ranges, not deadlines

Published numbers vary, so treat them as labeled examples. One reference page that attributes its figures to the Department of Energy gives 15 to 20 years as a typical life for a central air conditioner. A consumer article says a typical air conditioner lasts ten to fifteen years and that failures tend to start after about ten. ENERGY STAR does not give a lifespan. It says to consider replacing a heat pump or air conditioner older than 10 years and a furnace or boiler older than 15. Those are prompts to start comparing options, not expiry dates. Climate, installation quality, usage and upkeep all move the real number.

What shortens or stretches life

  • Airflow and dirt. Clogged filters and dirty coils make the blower and compressor work harder and hotter, and heat ages motors and electronics. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter.
  • Oversizing. A unit that is too big short-cycles, and every start adds wear. See Furnace Short-Cycling.
  • Duct leaks make the system run longer than it should.
  • Tune-ups. The reference page notes ENERGY STAR recommends pre-season tune-ups. None of the sources used here put a number of years on what maintenance buys, so no one can promise you one.

Repair or replace: ways to think about it

A common contractor rule of thumb, described by one reference page, is to replace when a repair would cost more than about half the price of a new system. It is a rule of thumb, not a standard. Another article suggests repairing a roughly five-year-old unit in most cases and getting a careful estimate once a unit is past ten years. Questions that help beyond any rule:

  • How old is it, and has it had repeated repairs?
  • Is the repair on a major part such as a compressor or heat exchanger, or something small?
  • Are energy bills rising? ENERGY STAR notes frequent repairs plus rising bills can mean lost efficiency.
  • Are there comfort, humidity, dust or noise problems that point to ducts, sizing or coils?
  • What refrigerant does the system use, and will future service be harder? Ask your contractor.

Other costs to weigh

Efficiency matters as well as repair cost. ENERGY STAR says a properly installed ENERGY STAR heat pump or air conditioner can save up to 20 percent on heating and cooling costs, and describes an ENERGY STAR furnace as 15 percent more efficient than a conventional one. Those are the program's labeled examples, and your savings depend on what you have now. A newer system may also mean a different refrigerant or a different installation, so replacement is a project to plan, not just a part swap. If a system is older but runs well, keeping it and budgeting for its replacement is a reasonable plan. Keep the repair invoices, because a pattern of repeat visits is useful evidence when you decide.

Get a second view

Ask for a written estimate, the reasoning, and a load calculation for a new system rather than a swap by size alone. See What to Ask an HVAC Contractor Before Hiring and Heat Load Calculation Intro.

Safety box: do not wait on these

Some problems are not about age. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave the home and call for help (Carbon Monoxide Alarms). If you smell gas, leave and call from outside. Burning smells, scorched wiring, a tripping breaker that will not stay on, or water near electrical parts mean shut the equipment off at the thermostat and call a licensed pro. Do not bypass safety switches or limits to keep an old unit running.
