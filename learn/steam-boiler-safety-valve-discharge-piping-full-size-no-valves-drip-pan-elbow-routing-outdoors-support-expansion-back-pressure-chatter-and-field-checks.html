Steam Boiler Safety Valve Discharge Piping: Full Size, No Valves, Drip Pan Elbow

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A safety valve can be perfect and still fail its job if the pipe after it is wrong. The discharge line has to carry a full-capacity blast of steam to a safe place, drain itself, and leave the valve free of pipe weight and thrust. This lesson is about that pipe. It does not repeat valve basics or the causes of a dripping valve; those are in the two lessons linked below.

What the discharge line has to do

The valve outlet is the only exit for steam when the controls have failed. Three jobs follow from that. The line must pass the full flow without building pressure behind the valve. It must put the steam and hot condensate where nobody stands. And it must not push, pull or twist the valve body. The published facility and state rules we read all come back to these three jobs, even when they word them differently.

Full size, short, straight, and no valves

State boiler rules we read say the discharge pipe must be at least as large as the valve outlet, and that no stop valve of any kind may sit between the valve and the open air. A state heating-boiler checklist asks that the line be as short and straight as possible. A university facility specification says to run the vent full size and unobstructed through the roof and not to reduce it. Another university specification puts the same idea as discharge to the outside "without valves."

The reason is plain. A valve in the line, a cap, a reducer or a plugged elbow is a way to trap steam. A closed valve turns the safety device into a decoration. Even an open valve is a risk because someone can close it later. If a line was not built that way, it is not ours to fix by cutting corners; it is a finding to report.

The drip pan elbow, or a separate drip and vent

Steam that condenses in a vent line has to go somewhere. If the line holds water above the valve seat, the water sits on the disc and can slug out when the valve lifts. A state rule we read calls for an open drain so water cannot collect in the valve or pipe. A second state document asks for drainage that removes water collected above the seat.

The common answer is a drip pan elbow. The valve outlet discharges into an elbow with a pan, and the vent pipe stands above it with a small gap. A federal facility test manual describes the valve discharging into a drip pan ell, then through a slip joint into an oversized vent pipe, with about a one-inch gap, so there is no direct connection between the vent and the valve. That gap is what keeps pipe weight and expansion off the valve. A national health agency technical bulletin describes the same layout and says the condensate is piped to the nearest floor drain. A university specification says the drain should run to a floor drain without valves.

Where the arrangement is not an elbow, the second answer is a separate drip and vent: a drain connection at the low point and a vent that rises from there. The federal manual says each valve needs its own vent and its own drain. A university specification adds that relief vents should not tie into other equipment vents. A state checklist asks that there be no low points where water can collect, and that the open drain hole be large enough (it names a size for larger valves; we leave the number to the checklist).

Routing to a safe point

Outdoors or above the roof is the usual answer. The health agency bulletin says the vent is piped to the roof, and that the discharge must be well above it and away from outdoor air intakes. A state rule says the discharge must be placed or piped so it does not endanger people working in the area. Another state rule says that an open discharge must not strike other piping or equipment and should point away from platforms. A university specification limits the number of 45-degree elbows between the drip pan elbow and the end of the pipe, to keep the run simple.

Support and thermal expansion

An open steam discharge pushes back on its pipe, much like a fire hose pushes on the person holding it. The pipe, not the valve, has to take that push. The health agency bulletin tells designers to support the valve and its pipe against reaction forces, and warns that weak support can cause serious failures from bending. A state rule says the same: the piping is to be supported to withstand reaction forces and arranged to avoid undue stress on the valve. Another state rule says that if an elbow is used it must sit close to the valve outlet; otherwise the pipe must be securely anchored and supported. A state checklist asks that the outlet pipe not be longer than needed and be properly supported.

Expansion matters because the pipe goes from room temperature to steam temperature in seconds. A state rule says drip pan connections must allow for expansion so the piping does not bind. The slip joint and gap in the drip pan arrangement do that job. Our own plain reading: fix the vent at its supports, let the gap absorb growth, and never let the valve body be the anchor.

Why a long or undersized vent makes a valve chatter

When the valve lifts, steam rushes into the discharge pipe. If the pipe is too small or too long, pressure builds behind the valve outlet. A manufacturer's pressure relief technical seminar manual says built-up back pressure and inlet pressure loss can make a conventional valve close early and become unstable, which is chatter. It says the only fix is to reduce those two. The manual also gives a common guideline: built-up back pressure should not exceed 10 percent of the pressure at the valve inlet during relief, and inlet piping loss should not exceed 3 percent. These are guideline figures from that manual; your valve maker's instructions govern. Chatter pounds the seat and can destroy the valve in minutes, and a damaged seat then weeps.

Our own worked example: how much steam must the line carry?

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume a steam boiler with a maximum burner output of 1,000,000 Btu/h. We assume each pound of steam takes about 1,000 Btu to make from feedwater (a round figure for easy arithmetic, not a measured value for any boiler).

  1. Steam made per hour = 1,000,000 Btu/h divided by 1,000 Btu/lb = 1,000 lb/h.
  2. Per minute = 1,000 lb/h divided by 60 = about 16.7 lb/min.
  3. Per second = 1,000 lb/h divided by 3,600 = about 0.28 lb/s.

So the valve must be able to pass about 1,000 lb/h, and everything after it must let that flow out without much back pressure. Applying the manual's 10 percent guideline to an assumed 15 psig set pressure gives 0.10 x 15 = 1.5 psi of built-up back pressure as an illustration. That is our arithmetic on an assumed setting; the real limit comes from the valve maker and the rules where you work. The flow in this example is the reason a pipe cannot be neck-down or capped, and the reason its end must be where a person is not.

Hot-water relief valve discharge, for contrast

A hot-water boiler relief valve passes hot water and some flash steam, not a steady flow of dry steam, so the goal shifts to scald protection. A state heating-boiler checklist says the discharge must be arranged so there is no danger of scalding attendants, and that no shutoff of any kind may be placed between the valve and the atmosphere. A city water heater handout says the pipe must be the size of the valve outlet, have no valves, obstructions or means of isolation, and discharge by gravity through an air gap to a drain. The basic rules are the same as for steam: full size, no valves, drain it, put it somewhere safe. The difference is where it ends, which is typically a low, visible point such as a drain rather than a roof vent.

Field checks

  • Weeping. A wet or dripping outlet or drain means the seat is leaking. Note it and report it; the dripping-valve lesson covers causes.
  • Rust trails. Rust streaks on the vent, the elbow or the wall below point to repeated small releases or wet pipe.
  • Missing or blocked drains. No drain on the elbow, a drain plugged with scale, or a drain run into a pipe with a valve is a defect.
  • A capped or plugged vent. A cap, plug, bird nest or frozen condensate in the vent end is an emergency finding.
  • Pipe weight on the valve. A vent bolted rigidly to the valve, sagging, or hanging from it should be supported separately.
  • Valves, reducers, tees. Look for anything in the line that was not on the original arrangement.
  • Termination. Check the end is clear of intakes, walkways and other piping.

The federal facility test manual we read lifts valves with steam pressure at least semi-annually and does not accept hand lifting, bench testing or gagging in place of that. Valve testing is its own topic; follow the owner's procedures and do not work on a live boiler without authorization.

Field card: Steam Boiler Safety Valve Discharge Piping

Full size, no valves, drained, supported, safe end.

  • Discharge pipe at least as large as the valve outlet; no valve, cap or reducer.
  • Drip pan elbow (or separate drip and vent) with an open drain to a floor drain.
  • Vent supported on its own, with a gap or slip joint so the valve carries no pipe weight.
  • End outdoors or above the roof, away from intakes and people.
  • Short, straight run; few elbows; no low points that hold water.
  • Long or small vent: back pressure up, valve chatter.
  • Hot-water relief: full size, no valves, ends low and visible at a drain.
  • Our example: 1,000,000 Btu/h at 1,000 Btu/lb = 1,000 lb/h.
