Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water System

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The baseboard in one room is stone cold while the rest of the house is warm. On a hot-water (hydronic) system, the most common first suspect is air trapped where water should be. This lesson explains why air causes cold spots, the safe checks you can do, and what belongs to a heating professional.

First, is it hot water or steam?

Look for a pressure gauge on the boiler that reads in psi and slim finned baseboard or radiators with a small valve at one end. Hot-water systems are full of water. A steam system usually has a low-pressure gauge and radiators with a vent on the side. If you have steam, this page does not apply. Read Steam Radiator Air Vents instead.

The short answer

Air is lighter than water and collects at high points: the top of a radiator, a bend in a pipe, the end of a baseboard run. Caleffi's free idronics journal says "cold spots" on radiators are indicative of entrapped air, because the air blocks water from the heat-transfer surface. A big enough pocket in a tall enough system can also stop the circulator from pushing water over the top of the loop. Bell & Gossett's air management manual lists air-bound circuits, noise and poor efficiency among the problems of poor air control.

Safe checks before touching anything

  • Which rooms? Upstairs or the far end of a loop points toward air or low pressure. One whole zone cold points toward a zone valve, thermostat or circulator.
  • Is the boiler running? A cold boiler is a different problem. Do not reset or bypass safety controls.
  • Look at the gauge. A reading far below your boiler manual's cold number is a clue. Pressure is covered in Boiler Pressure Too High or Too Low.
  • Listen. Gurgling, trickling or hissing often means air moving through the pipes (Caleffi and B&G both describe noise from air in the water).
  • Check clearances. Furniture or curtains over a baseboard hurt output but are not air problems.

Bleeding air: what manufacturers describe

The U.S. Department of Energy's HVAC guide for homeowners lists "bleed air from each radiator" as start-of-season maintenance. Weil-McLain's boiler manual describes the same idea for a whole system. After the main purge, it says to remove leftover air with the system's manual air vents, opening them one at a time, starting with the lowest floor, and closing each when water comes out. Slant/Fin, a baseboard maker, says provision must be made to vent air from the system and recommends a vent at every drop. So bleeding means opening a manual vent briefly until air stops and only water appears, then closing it.

Points to remember:

  • Only if your unit has a vent. Not every baseboard or radiator has one. Do not drill, pry or remove parts to make one.
  • Hot water scalds. Bell & Gossett warns that venting a hot system is hazardous and says its automatic vent should be manually vented only when system temperature is below 100 F. Let the system cool and keep a cup and rag under the vent.
  • An automatic vent is not a bleeder. B&G says to loosen that cap one full turn for automatic operation. Leave adjusting it to a pro if unsure.
  • Never touch the relief valve. It is a safety device.
  • Check the gauge afterward. Each time air leaves, water has to replace it. Pressure that drops after bleeding may need topping up by someone who knows your fill valve. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.
  • Air that keeps coming back is a symptom, not a chore. Call a professional.

Why low pressure leaves the top radiator cold

Water needs enough pressure to reach the highest radiator. Bell & Gossett gives the conversion: about 2.31 feet of water height equals 1 psi. Its manual also says the top of a low-temperature system can need as little as 4 psi, and Caleffi recommends at least 5 psi of positive static pressure at the top so air vents work properly.

Worked example

A two-story house has the highest baseboard 20 feet above the boiler gauge. The gauge reads 8 psi cold.

  1. Water height that 8 psi supports: 8 psi × 2.31 ft/psi = 18.5 ft.
  2. The highest baseboard is at 20 ft, so the water cannot reach it. It stays full of air.
  3. Pressure needed just to lift water to 20 ft: 20 ÷ 2.31 = 8.66 psi.
  4. Add the 4 psi B&G allows at the top: 8.66 + 4 = about 12.7 psi. Using Caleffi's 5 psi gives about 13.7 psi.

This house needs roughly 13 psi cold, so 8 psi is too low and the top room stays cold. This is an illustration, not your setting. Your boiler manual and data plate govern, and a professional adjusts the fill valve.

Zone valves and circulators

If a whole zone is cold and the gauge looks normal, air is less likely than a zone valve that is not opening or a circulator that is not turning. Taco's zone valve instruction sheet describes a manual opening lever: push it all the way down for flow through the valve, and push back up to restore automatic operation. It also notes the lever moves easily when the valve is open and meets resistance when closed. The same sheet warns never to remove the power head while the thermostat is calling for heat, and to disconnect the No. 1 wire and wait four minutes before servicing. That is a technician's job. Taco's circulator sheet adds that the bearings are water-lubricated, the pump must not run dry, and the system must be filled before the circulator is run. Both are good reasons not to switch pumps on and off to see what happens.

Balancing and layout

Even without air, rooms can heat unevenly. On a series loop the water passes through each baseboard in turn, so later units get cooler water; Slant/Fin's instructions describe this layout and call for balancing valves in split circuits. See Hot-Water Balancing Basics and Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature before adjusting anything.

For the technician: tracing air

  • Purge by zone. Weil-McLain's manual: hose on the purge valve, isolate the boiler, close zone isolation valves, open the quick-fill and then the purge valve, and open one zone at a time so water pushes air out until none comes out. Then restore valves, confirm cold-fill pressure and clear remaining air at the manual vents.
  • Check static pressure at the top. Caleffi: feed valve set for 5 psi minimum at the top of the system.
  • Find where air enters. Caleffi and B&G describe an expansion tank on the circulator discharge letting pressure fall below atmospheric in places so vents and packings draw air in. Both favor the tank and feed valve at the pump suction. Also check a waterlogged tank (the DOE guide: confirm the tank is charged with air, not water), a leaking fitting, a failing vent, and constant make-up water.
  • Dissolved air. B&G says warm, low-pressure water holds less air. Air separators at the boiler outlet and automatic high-point vents handle what is left.
  • After bleeding recheck pressure and expansion tank precharge.

Field card: Cold radiators on hot water

Air rises, so the high point goes cold first.

  • Steam radiators with side vents go to the steam lesson, not this one.
  • Top-floor or end-of-loop cold usually means air or low pressure. A whole zone cold means check zone valve, thermostat, circulator.
  • About 2.31 ft of water height equals 1 psi. Top of the system needs roughly 4 to 5 psi on top of that.
  • Bleed only at vents the maker provides, system cool, one at a time, lowest floor first.
  • Never touch the relief valve. Check the gauge after bleeding.
  • Air that returns means find the cause: leak, tank, vent or fill.
