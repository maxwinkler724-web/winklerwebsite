High-Leg Delta Services and HVAC Equipment: 208 V to Neutral, Marking and Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most service panels give you one clean rule: every 120 V point reads 120 V. A four-wire delta service breaks that rule on purpose. One of its three hot conductors reads about 208 V to neutral, and if a 120 V control circuit is landed on it, the equipment can be damaged. This lesson explains what that conductor is, how it is marked, and how to prove which leg is which with a meter before you connect anything.

Safety first

Panel and service work needs a qualified electrician. This lesson is for understanding and for diagnosing what you are looking at. Opening a service panel, moving conductors or changing breakers is not apprentice work on your own. OSHA's electrical safety-related work practices rule says live parts are generally de-energized before work, and that work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Follow your employer's lockout procedures and the meter-rating guidance in Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs.

What a high leg is

A four-wire delta service starts with a three-phase transformer bank connected in a delta. The utility center-taps one of the three transformer windings and grounds that tap. The result is a service with three phase conductors, A, B and C, plus a grounded neutral. Between any two phase conductors you read about 240 V. From the neutral to the two phase conductors on the tapped winding you read about 120 V. The third phase conductor, the one at the opposite corner of the delta from the tap, is the high leg. A university tech note on three-phase systems describes it this way: it measures 208 V from the neutral, and it is called the high leg or wild leg and sits in the center (B) position. A national-lab electrical design standard calls the same conductor the high (stinger) leg.

The 240/120 V designation therefore describes the voltages most single-phase loads will see (240 V between any two phases, 120 V from neutral to A or C), but it quietly hides the odd one out.

Why it reads about 208 V

The tap sits at the middle of a 240 V winding, so neutral to A and neutral to C are each half of 240 V, which is 120 V. The high leg is the far corner of an equilateral triangle of 240 V sides. The neutral sits at the midpoint of one side, so the distance from neutral to the opposite corner is the height of that triangle, which is 240 V multiplied by 0.866, or about 208 V. Another way to say it: 120 V times the square root of 3 (1.732) is about 208 V. This is our own arithmetic; the three-phase relationship is explained in Three-Phase Power Math for HVAC Techs, so we do not repeat the full derivation here.

Do not confuse this with a 208Y/120 V wye service, where all three phases read 120 V to neutral and 208 V between phases. On the high-leg service the line-to-line values are 240 V, and only one conductor to neutral is 208 V.

How the high leg is marked

The National Electrical Code (named here in plain text) requires that the high-leg conductor be identified. The university tech note cites NEC Section 110.15 and says the conductor must be marked with orange tape or paint where it is visible. The national-lab standard likewise says to mark the B-phase conductor or bus bar orange. In practice you look for an orange conductor, orange paint or tape at the panel lugs, at the meter base, and at a disconnect. The code's wording allows an effective marking other than orange, so a missing or odd color is a reason to check with a meter, not a reason to assume.

Two cautions from our own experience of how this goes wrong. First, markings fade, get painted over or are never applied on old work. Second, the B position is the usual place for the high leg, but a service can be wired differently, so the marking is a prompt to measure, not a substitute for it.

Why 120 V loads must never be fed from it

A 120 V control transformer primary, a 120 V receptacle, a 120 V control board and a 120 V motor or fan are all designed for roughly 120 V. The high leg offers about 208 V to neutral. The university tech note warns against putting single-pole breakers on the high leg because they would supply 208 V to 120 V-rated loads, and says the high leg needs care to prevent misuse. For motors it points to a three-pole breaker, and single-phase 240 V loads can use a two-pole breaker rated for the job.

The rule of thumb for equipment: 120 V loads connect from neutral to phase A or phase C only. Three-phase 240 V equipment can connect to A, B and C. A 240 V single-phase load, including a control transformer with a 240 V primary, can connect between any two phase conductors because every pair reads 240 V.

What happens to a 120 V control board on the high leg

The ratio is 208 divided by 120, about 1.73. That is roughly 73 percent over the design voltage (our own arithmetic). A coil, relay, transformer primary or power supply on a control board is not built to take that. We have no manufacturer test data to quote, so we describe only what the voltage does in general: a transformer primary at that much overvoltage draws excess current and saturates, a switching supply on a board can fail, and a coil can overheat. Damage may be immediate or may show up as a weak, hot component that fails days later, and the 24 V secondary may read high on its way out. If a new board dies right after startup on a high-leg building, check which conductor it is landed on before you order a second one.

When 24 V control voltage reads well above 24 V on a freshly connected transformer, do not just keep going. Stop and check the primary voltage. Low-voltage problems and transformer sag are covered in Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits.

Meter checks before you connect equipment

  1. Confirm the nameplate voltage and phase of the equipment and whether it has a neutral connection requirement. Most rooftop and condensing units take line power only, and their 24 V control comes from a transformer inside.
  2. Use a meter with the correct rating and leads for the panel, and wear the PPE your procedure calls for. If the check requires an energized panel, a qualified person takes the readings.
  3. Measure line to line on all three pairs (A-B, B-C, A-C). A true 240 V delta reads about 240 V on each.
  4. Measure each phase to neutral. Two should read about 120 V and one about 208 V. The 208 V conductor is the high leg.
  5. Compare that result with the orange marking. If they disagree, stop and tell the electrician or the building owner. Do not rely on the color alone.
  6. Record which conductor is the high leg on your service ticket before you leave, so the next tech does not have to rediscover it.
  7. Connect 120 V items (control transformer with a 120 V primary, receptacle circuits) only to a phase that read about 120 V to neutral, and have the electrician make that connection.

An unusual reading is information. A phase-to-neutral value of 120 V on all three conductors would suggest a different service type; a very low or missing neutral reading suggests an open neutral. Both are cases for the electrician. For unbalance and loss of a phase on motors, see Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measured job. Assumptions: a four-wire service on a small commercial building; a rooftop unit nameplated 240 V three-phase and a separate 120 V thermostat-interface board that a previous owner wanted powered from "any hot and the neutral." A qualified electrician is taking readings with a rated meter at the main disconnect.

Assumed readings:

  • A to B: 241 V
  • B to C: 240 V
  • A to C: 240 V
  • A to neutral: 120 V
  • C to neutral: 121 V
  • B to neutral: 208 V

Step 1, line to line. All three pairs sit near 240 V, so this is a 240 V delta and not a 208Y/120 V wye. Step 2, line to neutral. A and C read about 120 V. B reads 208 V. Step 3, check the arithmetic. 120 V x 1.732 = 207.8 V, matching the 208 V reading, and 208 / 120 = 1.73, so B is about 73 percent above a 120 V rating. Step 4, identify. Conductor B is the high leg. The orange tape seen at the lug agrees, which closes the check. Step 5, decide. The rooftop unit can connect to A, B and C. The 120 V board must be fed from A or C and neutral, never B, and that connection is the electrician's job. Better, the board's power can come from the unit's own control transformer. The service ticket then records "B is the high leg."

Field card: High-Leg Delta Services

Two phases read 120 V to neutral. One reads about 208 V. Never put 120 V on that one.

  • Four-wire 240/120 V delta: 240 V between any two phases.
  • High leg to neutral is about 208 V (120 V x 1.732).
  • Marked orange or otherwise effectively marked under the National Electrical Code; verify with a meter.
  • 120 V controls, transformers with 120 V primaries and receptacles go on the two 120 V phases only.
  • Three-phase and 240 V single-phase loads can use the high leg between phases.
  • Read all three line-to-line pairs and all three phase-to-neutral values before connecting.
  • Panel work needs a qualified electrician.
