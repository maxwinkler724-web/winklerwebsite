HVAC for Leather Tanneries and Hide Plants: Fumes, Humidity and Drying Rooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A tannery turns raw hides into leather using water, alkali, salts, acids and coatings, and every one of those steps puts something into the air: moisture, gases, mist or solvent vapor. This lesson walks through where HVAC fits, from the wet beamhouse to the spray booth to the heated drying room, and finishes with an airflow calculation we worked ourselves.

Safety first: this is plant work with chemical hazards

Confirm the plant's own procedures before you open any duct or enter any room. Tannery air can contain gases that hurt you before you notice them. Gas testing, lockout, confined space entry and respiratory protection belong to the plant's safety program and the competent person on site. This page is general education, not a procedure.

What happens in a tannery, in air terms

The federal environmental agency's emission-factor chapter on leather tanning describes the sequence. The beamhouse comes first: hides are soaked to remove salt and restore moisture, fleshed, and unhaired, mainly by liming in pits or drums. Deliming and bating follow, usually in a water solution of ammonium salts with enzymes. Then comes the tanyard, where chrome tanning accounts for about 90 percent of domestic production according to that chapter. After tanning, hides are dried by methods such as air drying, toggling, pasting, vacuum drying or high-frequency drying, and then finished with buffing, waxing and spray coatings.

For an HVAC tech the useful way to read that list is by air problem: the beamhouse is warm, wet and chemical; the tanyard is wet and acidic; the drying area moves large amounts of water into the air; and finishing adds solvent or coating mist and dust.

Beamhouse: humidity, wet floors and corrosion

Drums, pits and washdown keep floors wet and the air humid all shift. Practical consequences we see in this kind of plant (our own field-practice notes, not a sourced rule): condensation forms on any surface colder than the air's dew point, so uninsulated supply ducts, cold-water piping and outside walls drip; fans and louvers in damp air corrode quickly; and slip hazards on wet floors affect where you can safely set ladders and access panels. Choose coils, casings, fasteners and duct material that tolerate constant wet and chemical exposure, and ask the maker what coatings or materials it offers for corrosive atmospheres. See our lesson on car wash HVAC for the same wet-and-corrosive thinking in another plant type.

Hydrogen sulfide and ammonia

The federal environmental agency chapter says sulfides from liming and unhairing, and from later steps, can release hydrogen sulfide, and that alkaline sulfides in wastewater convert to hydrogen sulfide at a pH below 8.0. In plain terms, adding acid to sulfide-bearing liquor or wastewater is a way to make the gas. The same chapter says ammonia is released during deliming and unhairing, and during drying if ammonia is used to help dye penetration.

The federal worker safety agency's hydrogen sulfide page lists tanning among the industries where the gas occurs. It calls the gas highly flammable and toxic even at low concentrations, says it smells like rotten eggs at low levels but that you can no longer smell it after a while at low levels, or sooner at high levels, and says it is heavier than air, so it can travel along the ground and collect in low, enclosed spaces. Two lessons for the HVAC tech: your nose is not a detector, and a low pit, trench or sump area is where it gathers, so exhaust pickup near the floor matters there.

The same agency's ammonia data page gives a permissible exposure limit of 50 ppm as an 8-hour average, describes a pungent, suffocating odor, and lists a vapor density of 0.6 compared with air, meaning the gas is lighter than air. Its refrigeration properties page says ammonia is extremely irritating to the eyes, throat and breathing passages even at small concentrations, with an odor threshold of 5 to 50 ppm. Ammonia from deliming therefore tends to rise, while hydrogen sulfide sinks. A single room exhaust location will not serve both gases equally well, which is why the plant's industrial hygienist, not the HVAC tech alone, decides capture points. For sulfide-gas background see our lesson on anaerobic digester heating, which covers sulfide gas in another setting.

Local exhaust at drums and spray finishing

General room dilution is a poor answer when a gas source is a known point, such as a drum door, a pit cover or a spray booth. Local exhaust captures the contaminant at the source before it enters the breathing zone, so it needs far less air than diluting the whole room. Our lesson on electroplating shop ventilation explains lip exhaust, capture velocity and corrosion-resistant duct runs, and the same ideas carry over to drum hoods. The lesson on candle and soap workshops compares local and room exhaust for a small shop.

For finishing, the federal environmental agency's page on leather finishing operations says facilities can be major sources of hazardous air pollutants such as glycol ethers, toluene and xylene, and points to the federal air toxics rule for leather finishing. The emission-factor chapter adds that many tanneries are moving to water-based coatings and that dust collectors or scrubbers control particulates from shaving, drying and buffing. For the HVAC tech this means a spray booth is regulated process equipment: do not change its airflow, filters or ducts without the plant environmental contact, and expect combustible mist or dust rules to apply to fan and motor choices.

Hide drying rooms: airflow, temperature, humidity

A drying room works like any evaporative drying space. Warm air with a low humidity ratio goes in, picks up water from the hides, and leaves with a higher humidity ratio. A university extension note on air and shed drying of lumber says final moisture is set by air temperature, relative humidity and drying time, and that fans speed drying but should be shut off in very humid air to avoid re-wetting. A second extension note on drying hardwood describes reversible fans and baffles that carry moist air away while delivering heat, a controlled kiln humidity, and the wet-bulb depression (the gap between dry-bulb and wet-bulb readings) as a measure of drying force. Lumber is not hide, and we use these only as the general principle: a drying room needs airflow, heat and humidity control working together. Keep the hide-specific temperature and humidity settings from the tannery's process specification; we do not give them here.

Two control habits follow. Control humidity by venting or by dehumidifying, not by simply adding heat, because hotter air that cannot take more water just stalls. If a plant dries with desiccant or heat pump dehumidification instead of venting, see our lesson on desiccant dehumidifiers. Second, watch the dew point of the exhaust air: duct walls colder than that point will drip, so insulate and slope exhaust runs.

Makeup air heating

Every cfm exhausted from a drum hood, spray booth or drying room has to be replaced. Without planned makeup air, the building goes negative, doors are hard to open, burners draft badly and gases can be pulled from one area into another. In cold weather the makeup air must be heated, and in a corrosive plant the heater and its casing face the same chemical atmosphere as everything else, so locate intakes away from exhaust discharge points. The worked example below shows how big that load can get.

Worked example (our own, assumed numbers)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for teaching, not a design value or a figure from any source.

  • Assumption: a drying room holds 200 hides. Each loses 6 lb of water over a 10 hour cycle.
  • Assumption: supply air is at 100°F with a humidity ratio Win = 0.010 lb water per lb dry air.
  • Assumption: exhaust air leaves with Wout = 0.020 lb/lb.
  • Assumption: standard air density of 0.075 lb per cubic foot, sea level.

Step 1, water removed per hour. 200 hides x 6 lb = 1,200 lb of water. 1,200 lb / 10 h = 120 lb of water per hour.

Step 2, humidity ratio change. ΔW = 0.020 - 0.010 = 0.010 lb water per lb dry air.

Step 3, dry air mass flow. 120 lb/h / 0.010 = 12,000 lb of dry air per hour.

Step 4, volume flow. 12,000 lb/h / 0.075 lb/ft³ = 160,000 ft³/h. Divide by 60 min/h: 160,000 / 60 = about 2,670 cfm.

Check. Using the shortcut water (lb/h) = 4.5 x cfm x ΔW, which comes from 60 min x 0.075 lb/ft³ = 4.5: 4.5 x 2,667 x 0.010 = 120 lb/h. The two routes agree.

Dew point check (our own, from standard saturation-pressure values). At sea level, W = 0.020 corresponds to a dew point near 77°F, and W = 0.010 to about 56°F. A duct, wall or coil surface below 77°F in the exhaust path will collect water, which is why the exhaust run needs insulation.

Makeup air heating load, assuming once-through air. If every cfm is replaced with outdoor air at 20°F and warmed to 100°F, the sensible heat is about 1.08 x 2,667 cfm x 80°F = about 230,000 Btu/h (1.08 is the usual sensible-heat factor for standard air, our assumed value). Evaporation itself needs heat too: assuming about 1,050 Btu per lb of water, 120 lb/h x 1,050 = 126,000 Btu/h. Real rooms recirculate part of the air, so a real design will differ. The takeaway is that heating makeup air is often the biggest energy line in the room, and it is why recirculating with dehumidification can pay off.

Field card: HVAC for Leather Tanneries and Hide Plants: Fumes, Humidity and Drying Rooms

Hydrogen sulfide sinks and hides from your nose; ammonia rises; drying rooms need dry air in and a path out.

  • Beamhouse: warm, wet, chemical. Expect condensation and corrosion.
  • Sulfide turns to hydrogen sulfide gas when acidified (pH below 8.0).
  • Hydrogen sulfide is heavier than air; ammonia is lighter. Do not rely on smell.
  • Capture at the source with local exhaust; do not alter spray booth airflow without the plant's say-so.
  • Drying room water removed (lb/h) = 4.5 x cfm x change in humidity ratio.
  • Replace every exhausted cfm with planned, heated makeup air.
  • Insulate exhaust runs so duct walls stay above the exhaust dew point.
