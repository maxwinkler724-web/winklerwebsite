Checking Compressor Oil Level in the Crankcase Sight Glass: When to Read It, Foaming, Adding Oil with a Hand Pump and Removing Excess

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a semi-hermetic or open compressor, the round sight glass on the crankcase is the quickest way to see whether the bearings have oil to work with. A reading is only worth something if you take it at the right moment, so this lesson covers where the level should sit, when to read it, what foaming means, and how to add or remove oil.

Check the maker's data first

The compressor maker's literature decides the right level and the right oil. Makers do not all agree on one range, and the figures below come from documents we read for different kinds of compressor. Treat them as examples and use the data for the machine in front of you.

Where the level should sit

There is no single number that fits every compressor. A trade-press article on oil charges says a level covering one-fourth to one-half of the glass is normally acceptable, and tells readers to follow the maker where the maker differs. A service procedure for a transport refrigeration unit with a scroll compressor asks for one-half to three-quarters full, read after 15 minutes of running. A maker's guide for a tandem semi-hermetic set wants at least one-half of the glass at standstill before commissioning, and accepts a minimum of one-quarter while running. An assembly manual for a semi-hermetic compressor does not give a fraction at all: the oil must be inside the visible part of the glass, between the printed minimum and maximum marks, and the manual warns of damage if the compressor is overfilled or underfilled.

So the useful habit is: find the marks or the fraction in the maker's data, and note which condition it applies to, running or stopped. In our experience the level at standstill reads higher than while running, because some oil is out in the pump and piping when the machine is on; that is our shop reasoning, not a sourced figure.

When to read it

  • Running, and settled. The transport guide reads the glass after 15 minutes at full capacity. The tandem guide has the unit run at least an hour at nominal conditions, then checks again at both light and heavy load, because oil return changes with refrigerant flow.
  • Stopped, after waiting. That same guide switches off, waits 5 minutes, and then reads the standstill level against its reference. Reading the instant the machine stops catches oil still draining back.
  • Not right after start-up. The assembly manual says to run at least 10 minutes before confirming oil is visible. A start often drops the crankcase pressure fast, which is where foaming comes from.
  • After a defrost. A trade-press article says trapped evaporator oil can be returned by warming the evaporator, and that more than one defrost may be needed. Our reading of that: oil is moving back to the crankcase after defrost, so a glass that looks low before a defrost may look different afterward. Wait for the system to settle again before you decide to add anything.

Foaming and what it tells you

Refrigerant dissolves in oil while the compressor sits. The assembly manual says that idle-time diffusion can cause foaming and migration of oil, and that an oil sump heater is available to reduce it. A foamy glass right after start-up is expected on a cold machine. Foam that stays after the machine has run and warmed is a different story: the transport guide, when draining excess, says heavy foaming as oil leaves may mean refrigerant is still in the oil, and to run the system 15 minutes to warm the sump before rechecking. Do not read a foam line as the true level and do not add oil on that reading. For the related trip and start-up story, see oil pressure safety controls and superheat checks and oil return.

Do not add oil just because the glass looks low

The trade-press article cautions against adding oil the moment the level is low. Oil may be trapped in the suction line or evaporator and come back later, and the compressor will then be overfilled. Decide first whether oil left the system (a leak) or is simply out in the piping. The article also suggests rechecking, even the next day. Too much oil is a risk too: the tandem guide warns that excess oil risks liquid slugging at start and says the level should be brought back to the recommended range, and the assembly manual warns of oil hammer if large amounts are topped up.

Adding oil with a hand pump

  1. Get the right oil. The tandem guide says to use only the oil the maker specifies and to keep it sealed and dry. The assembly manual names different factory oils for ester-type and mineral-type charges, with an X in the model designation marking an ester charge. The transport guide uses polyol ester oil for its refrigerant. Mineral and POE are not interchangeable, and see compressor oils for why.
  2. Connect. An oil charging pump instruction sheet says to connect the hose to the pump, loosen the end at the system valve just enough to purge air from the line, then tighten, and open the valve before pumping. It says the pump can be used with the unit running, with no need to shut it down.
  3. Pump slowly. The same sheet says to use slow, even strokes, and gives 2.6 fl oz per stroke against 200 psi for that pump. The transport guide, on a stopped compressor at about 3 psig, pumps slowly and allows 5 to 10 minutes for oil to drain to the sump, stopping at its target.
  4. Run and recheck. The tandem guide runs the compressors 20 minutes, shuts off and rechecks at standstill. If the level keeps falling after oil was added, it says oil return is poor and the cause must be found.

Removing excess oil

The transport guide drains oil through an access valve on the oil pressure fitting while the unit runs, watching the glass and stopping at the target range. The tandem guide says part of the oil charge may need draining when a compressor is exchanged. Use the maker's drain point. The assembly manual says to dispose of used oil under national regulations.

Reading oil color

An international refrigeration institute abstract on compressor troubleshooting says oil darkens mainly from oxidation, pointing to air in the system, and that moisture and brazing without inert gas can contribute. It says a color index runs from 0 to 10, with high numbers suggesting acid and probably moisture, and adds that a color change does not by itself mean lack of lubrication. So dark oil is a reason to test, not to condemn. See compressor burnout cleanup and oil analysis.

Worked example (ours): glass reads low after a long piping change

All numbers here are our own assumptions for teaching. A semi-hermetic compressor has a nameplate oil charge of 4.5 pints. After a 100 ft run of new line was added, the glass reads below the lower mark while running. The settled reading says oil is short, but the new line will hold some.

  1. Convert the charge: 4.5 pints x 16 fl oz per pint = 72 fl oz.
  2. Estimate oil held in the new line. Assumption, not a sourced figure: the oil film in the line holds about 0.05 fl oz per foot. 100 ft x 0.05 = 5 fl oz, about 7 percent of the charge (5 / 72).
  3. Add part of it, not all. First add 2 fl oz. At 2.6 fl oz per stroke for the pump in the sheet, that is less than one stroke, so use the pump's own marks or a graduated cup. 5 - 2 = 3 fl oz still possible.
  4. Run 20 minutes, let it settle, and read the glass. If it is still under the lower mark, add another 1 fl oz, run again, and read again.
  5. Stop when the level is inside the maker's range, even if less than 5 fl oz went in. If the level keeps dropping, look at oil return, not at the pump.

If the glass had risen past the upper mark after a few days, that would be oil returning from the new line, and some would need to come out.

Field card: Compressor Oil Level

Read it settled, add it slowly, add the right oil.

  • Find the maker's range; examples ran from 1/4 to 1/2 up to 1/2 to 3/4 of the glass.
  • Read after the machine has run and settled, not right after a start.
  • Foam after warm-up can mean refrigerant in the oil.
  • A low glass can be trapped oil; wait before adding.
  • Use only the specified oil type. Pump in small amounts, run, recheck.
  • Dark oil: test it, do not just replace it.
