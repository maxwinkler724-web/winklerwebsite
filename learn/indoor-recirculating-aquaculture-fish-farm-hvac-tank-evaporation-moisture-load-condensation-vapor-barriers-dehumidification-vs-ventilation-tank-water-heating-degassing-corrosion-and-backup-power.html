Indoor Fish Farm HVAC: Tank Evaporation, Condensation, Water Heating and Backup Power

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An indoor recirculating fish farm is a warm, wet building with a lot of open water in it. The tanks are the biggest moisture source, the roof and walls are the cold surfaces, and the fish depend on power and air for survival. This lesson walks through how the HVAC and plant side of that building works, with a worked evaporation example of our own.

Why this building is different

A regional extension fact sheet on aquaculture buildings says the inside of a recirculating system is typically held around 80°F in winter. Air that warm and that moist has a high dew point; the same sheet notes that for moist 85°F air the dew point can be anywhere from 65 to 80°F. Any surface colder than that collects water. The sheet reports that most farms its authors visited had winter condensation problems from poor ventilation, and that the farms without them either ventilated heavily or ran large dehumidifiers. If you have read our aquarium and fish room lesson, this is the same physics at warehouse scale, and the plant decisions are bigger.

Where the moisture comes from

Water leaves an open tank as vapor, and each pound that evaporates pulls heat out of the water and puts it into the room as latent load. That load rises with the tank surface area, the water temperature and the air movement over the water, and it falls as the room air gets more humid. Aeration and splashing increase it. A university energy-team page on pools puts the heat taken per pound of 80°F water at 1,048 Btu, and a university poultry page uses 1,044 Btu per pound for agricultural work. The familiar round figure of about 1,060 Btu per pound is within about 1 percent of those. We use the sourced 1,048 in the example below.

Keeping the building envelope dry

The extension fact sheet gives the envelope advice that matters most to a mechanical tech:

  • Insulate enough that inside surfaces stay warm. Condensation shows up first on windows, doors and bare concrete block, which have low R-values. One layer of concrete block is about R-2; adding 1 inch of rigid foam board brings the wall to about R-9.
  • Put the vapor barrier on the warm side. The sheet recommends a 4 to 6 mil vapor barrier under the inner sheathing, over the insulation. One farm without a ceiling barrier had moisture condense in the attic and drip onto the insulation, which cut its R-value.
  • Use closed-cell foam where it is exposed to the room. The sheet recommends it to hinder moisture migration, and says open-cell foam should not face the rearing space.
  • Finish with washable, waterproof sheathing, such as painted metal or smooth fiberglass board.
  • Insulate heated floors. The sheet calls floor insulation a must where tubes or coils are embedded in the slab.

Ventilation versus dehumidification

The sheet recommends 1 to 2 air changes per hour for these buildings, found by dividing the room volume in cubic feet by 60 to get cfm for one change. Of the five sites it surveyed, only two reached 1 air change per hour. It says the basic trade-off is that outdoor air removes moisture but adds heating load in winter, and it calls ventilation more economical than desiccant drying. Heat recovery on the exhaust helps with that trade-off. Where ventilation alone cannot hold the line, a large dehumidifier is the second tool; see desiccant dehumidifiers for when a wheel beats a cooling coil.

The same sheet says to watch for condensation and raise the minimum ventilation rate until it stops. It also says room humidity sensors in this setting are often unreliable and do not last long, so room temperature sensors should drive the automatic control.

Heating the air and the tank water

Water holds a great deal of heat, so tank water temperature changes slowly, which helps the fish. The sheet lists plastic tubing coils and plate heat exchangers as ways to move heat into the water, and a regional recirculating-systems fact sheet lists immersion heaters, heat exchangers, chillers and heat pumps. A heat pump or a boiler loop feeding a plate exchanger can both do the job. For another building with warm open water and the same moisture problem, see indoor pool dehumidification.

The heat to warm water is gallons times 8.34 pounds per gallon times the temperature rise in degrees F (our arithmetic). Fill water starts cold, and the sheet notes that warming groundwater to system temperature takes at least a day per foot of water depth, so plan for slow starts.

The species sets the target. An extension fact sheet on tank culture of tilapia gives 81 to 84°F as the optimum growth range, with acceptable growth from 77 to 90°F. Water at 50 to 60°F stresses them, cuts feeding and raises disease risk. The recirculating-systems sheet says fish generally tolerate a 5°F change, and that larger changes should be made at about 1°F every 20 to 30 minutes. For control, that means no fast water-temperature swings from an oversized heater or a valve that hunts.

Carbon dioxide and ammonia, in general terms

The treatment side makes gases the HVAC side has to respect. Fish begin to stress when dissolved carbon dioxide passes about 20 ppm, and tank plants remove it with packed-column aerators or air-lift devices that strip it out of the water. The overview sheet says this works best on the return flow just before the water goes back to the tank. The extension fact sheet notes that outdoor air holds about 400 ppm of carbon dioxide, that blower air drawn from a poorly ventilated room can exceed 1,000 ppm, and that it drew its air from outdoors where it could. So the blower intake location is a mechanical design choice, not an afterthought.

Ammonia comes from fish waste. The un-ionized form is the toxic one, and its share rises with pH and temperature. The recirculating-systems sheet recommends keeping it under 0.05 ppm and says a disrupted biofilter can push ammonia or nitrite to stressful levels within hours of feeding. That is a water problem, but a heat or power failure that upsets the biofilter becomes a fish problem quickly.

Corrosion in humid air

Fans, coils, fasteners and electrical gear sit in warm, wet air all year. A federal-lab study on steel with sea-salt deposits found the most corrosion loss at 64 percent relative humidity and above, and that a simple humidity cutoff did not reliably separate safe from corrosive air. Treat this as a hint for marine systems rather than a rule for freshwater rooms. In practice, specify corrosion-resistant coils and coatings, sealed or wet-rated enclosures and stainless fasteners, and put switchgear and control panels in a drier room where you can. See the grow room lesson for a similar moist-room layout.

Backup power and alarms

This is where the HVAC tech can save a crop. The overview sheet warns that failure of aeration or oxygenation can mean total loss of the fish in half an hour or less. The recirculating-systems sheet gives an example at 84°F: half-pound fish at one quarter pound per gallon fell to a stressful 3 ppm oxygen in about 16 minutes, and one-pound fish at one pound per gallon in under 6 minutes. It recommends a backup generator on an automatic transfer switch when staff are away, a phone alarm for power or water-level problems, and a pure-oxygen tank that opens through a solenoid valve when power fails. A tilapia fact sheet agrees that alarms, stored oxygen, generators and fast response can save a crop. Plan generator fuel and the generator room too; see emergency generator room ventilation. Test the alarms with the generator, not on paper.

Worked example (our own): evaporation from one tank room

This is our own example, with assumed inputs. It is a size check, not a design.

  • Assumed open tank water surface: 1,000 ft².
  • Assumed water at 80°F, room air at 82°F and 50 percent relative humidity.
  • Assumed evaporation rate: 0.048 lb per hour per square foot. We got this by dividing the 216 lb per hour reported for a 4,500 ft² indoor pool under those conditions in the university energy-team page (216 / 4,500 = 0.048). A pool in use has swimmer activity; a still fish tank may run lower, and an aerated one can run higher.
  • Latent heat: 1,048 Btu per pound (sourced, 80°F water).

Step 1: water evaporated = 1,000 ft² × 0.048 = 48 lb per hour.

Step 2: in gallons, 48 / 8.34 = about 5.8 gallons per hour, or roughly 138 gallons per day that makeup water must replace.

Step 3: latent load = 48 lb/h × 1,048 Btu/lb = about 50,300 Btu per hour. With the rounded 1,060 it would be 50,900 Btu per hour.

Step 4: 50,300 / 12,000 = about 4.2 tons of latent load that the dehumidifier or ventilation air has to remove, and about the same heat that the tank water, in turn, must be given back to hold its temperature.

Humidity matters a lot. The extension fact sheet uses 82°F air at 75 percent relative humidity and 4,000 ft² of tank, and counts 38,000 Btu per hour of evaporation heat. It does not show how it got that, but spread over the area it is far less per square foot than our 50 percent case. The takeaway: evaporation depends strongly on room humidity (and on the surface activity), so the room humidity setpoint is a load decision as much as a comfort one.

Field card: Indoor Fish Farm HVAC

Water in the air, cold surfaces, and power for the fish.

  • Open tanks are the main moisture source; evaporation is latent load, about 1,048 Btu per pound at 80°F.
  • Insulate, put a vapor barrier on the warm side, and keep inside surfaces above the dew point.
  • Ventilation is usually cheaper than drying, but adds winter heat load; recover exhaust heat.
  • Match water temperature to the species and change it slowly, about 1°F per 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Carbon dioxide is stripped from the water; take blower air from outdoors.
  • Specify corrosion-resistant equipment and keep electrical gear dry.
  • Aeration failure can kill a crop in under an hour: generator, transfer switch, alarms, stored oxygen, tested.
