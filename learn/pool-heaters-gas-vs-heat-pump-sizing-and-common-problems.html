Pool Heaters: Gas vs Heat Pump, Sizing and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pool heater does one job: move a lot of Btu into a lot of water. A gas heater makes the heat by burning fuel. A heat pump pool heater moves heat out of the air. This lesson compares the two, shows how to size one with a simple formula, and lists the problems that show up most.

How each one works

A gas pool heater burns natural gas or propane and passes the pool water through a heat exchanger. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) says gas heaters heat a pool quickly and hold a set temperature in any weather, which suits a pool used only now and then. They are rated by Btu output, roughly 75,000 to 450,000 Btu per hour.

A heat pump pool heater is a small air conditioner running in reverse. DOE describes a fan pulling outdoor air across an evaporator coil, refrigerant absorbing that heat, a compressor raising its temperature, and a condenser handing the heat to the pool water. If you want the refrigeration cycle itself, read Heat Pump Basics. Heat pumps cost more up front but, in DOE's words, generally cost less to run and last longer. DOE says a heat pump can last 10 years or more, and a gas heater typically five or more with proper care.

Efficiency terms

Gas heaters use thermal efficiency: usable heat out divided by fuel energy in. DOE says an 80 percent heater turns about 80 cents of each fuel dollar into pool heat, and newer models reach 89 to 95 percent. The federal minimum for gas-fired pool heaters made on or after April 16, 2013 is 82 percent thermal efficiency, according to the AHRI trade association. DOE's appliance standards office confirms pool heater standards date to 1990 and that an updated rule took effect on the April 2013 date.

Heat pumps use COP, coefficient of performance: heat delivered divided by electrical energy used. DOE lists typical pool heat pump COPs of 3.0 to 7.0, which is 300 to 700 percent "efficiency." That is not a violation of physics. The heat pump moves heat that already exists instead of making it. DOE's test point is 80°F air, 63 percent relative humidity and 80°F water. Those household ratings (SEER2, AFUE and so on) are a different family; see What Do SEER2, HSPF2, AFUE and EER2 Mean.

Why heat pumps slow down in cool air

A heat pump has less heat to collect when the air is cold. DOE says performance drops as outdoor air cools and that units work well above about 45 to 50°F. Pentair prints three test points for its UltraTemp line. For one model it lists 117,000 Btu/h at 80°F air with COP 5.3, and 82,000 Btu/h at 50°F air with COP 4.0.

Colder air also means frost. Hayward's heat pump manual says high humidity with low air temperature can frost the evaporator, a low-pressure switch then stops the unit (the display reads LP), and it restarts as the air warms. The same manual says low-ambient shutdown is usually in the 45 to 55°F range and is not a fixed number. A gas heater does not care about air temperature, which is why it wins for a quick warm-up in spring or fall.

Sizing: DOE's rule of thumb

DOE gives the same approximate outdoor formula for both types. Take the temperature rise: the pool temperature you want minus the average air temperature of the coldest month you will swim. Then:

Btu per hour = pool surface area (sq ft) × temperature rise (°F) × 12

DOE says this assumes about a 1 to 1.25°F rise per hour and a 3.5 mph wind. Multiply the answer by 1.5 for a 1.5°F-per-hour rise or by 2.0 for a 2°F rise. Wind, dry air and cool nights call for a bigger unit. DOE also says to have a trained pool professional run a proper sizing analysis.

Worked example: Btu and hours

Take a 15,000 gallon pool, 450 sq ft of surface, that you want to warm 10°F. Water weighs about 8.33 pounds per gallon and one Btu raises one pound of water 1°F.

  1. Btu needed = gallons × 8.33 × rise = 15,000 × 8.33 × 10 = 1,249,500 Btu.
  2. Gas heater, 100,000 Btu/h output: 1,249,500 ÷ 100,000 = about 12.5 hours.
  3. Heat pump at 85,000 Btu/h (Pentair's 90I model, 80°F air): 1,249,500 ÷ 85,000 = about 14.7 hours.
  4. Same pump at 56,000 Btu/h (50°F air): 1,249,500 ÷ 56,000 = about 22.3 hours.
  5. Fuel for an 82 percent gas heater: 1,249,500 ÷ 0.82 = about 1,523,800 Btu of gas, which is 15.2 therms (100,000 Btu each).
  6. Electricity at COP 5.7: 1,249,500 ÷ 5.7 = 219,200 Btu, and 219,200 ÷ 3,412 Btu per kWh = about 64 kWh. At COP 4.0 it is about 92 kWh.

This ignores heat lost to the air while you wait, which is why the real times are longer. Hayward also calls its heat pumps "not fast pool heaters" and suggests allowing at least 48 hours before swimming. Now multiply by your own gas and power prices to compare. The DOE's rule of thumb for sizing this pool is 450 × 20 × 12 = 108,000 Btu/h if the coldest-month air averages 60°F and you want 80°F.

The biggest saver is a cover

DOE says evaporation is by far the largest source of pool heat loss, and that covering a pool when it is not in use is the single most effective way to cut heating costs, with savings of 50 to 70 percent possible. A cover only saves if it actually gets used, so pick one you will not skip.

Common problems

  • Low flow or pressure switch trips. Gas heaters need water moving before they fire. Hayward's gas heater manual lists a 20 gpm minimum for its small unit, and Jandy's JXi manual lists 30 gpm minimum, with a bypass valve if flow is above 100 gpm. Dirty filters and closed valves are the usual causes. Jandy's switch is factory set to 7 psi; Hayward's is 3 psi. Never bypass a switch.
  • Scale. DOE says scale in the burner or heat exchanger cuts efficiency over time. Hayward's manual says high pH, alkalinity or calcium hardness cause scaling.
  • Corrosion from water chemistry. Hayward's gas heater manual says low pH, low alkalinity or low hardness are corrosive, and Jandy says damage to the heat exchanger from corrosive water is not covered by warranty. Test weekly. Chlorinators go downstream of the heater with a check valve.
  • No ignition. Hayward's electronic-ignition unit tries three times at 30-second intervals and then locks out for an hour. Jandy shows an ignition fault after three failed tries. Low gas pressure, blocked venting and a failed igniter are typical causes; a technician should check them.
  • Icing on a heat pump. See the section on cool air above. Check for airflow blockage, and let it restart on its own as the air warms.

Install requirements for gas units

A gas heater needs correctly sized gas piping, the right inlet pressure, a vent, and clearances. Makers differ. Hayward's small unit lists 3.0 to 10.5 inches water column for natural gas, 10 inches of clearance on the sides and top, and outdoor-only installation. Jandy's JXi lists 4 to 10.5 inches for natural gas, prohibits plastic venting, and asks for 36 inches on top. Follow the manual for the exact model, and see Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method. Heat pumps need airflow instead: Hayward asks for 2 feet on all sides and 6 feet above, and Pentair asks for 24 inches around the unit. See also Outdoor Unit Clearances. DOE recommends a qualified pool professional for installation.

Field card: Pool Heaters, Gas vs Heat Pump

Gas is fast and weather-proof. Heat pumps are cheap to run and slow in cold air.

  • Gas: rated in Btu output, thermal efficiency 82 percent minimum (2013 rule), newer units up to 89-95 percent.
  • Heat pump: COP 3.0 to 7.0 at 80°F air; lower at 50°F air.
  • DOE size estimate: area × rise × 12 = Btu/h.
  • Btu to heat water = gallons × 8.33 × rise.
  • Covers cut heating cost 50 to 70 percent.
  • Low flow, scale, bad chemistry and frost cause most problems.
  • Follow the maker's manual for gas pressure, venting and clearances.
