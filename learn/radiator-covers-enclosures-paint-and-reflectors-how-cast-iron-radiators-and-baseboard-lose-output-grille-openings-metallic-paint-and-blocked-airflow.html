Radiator Covers, Paint and Reflectors: What Cuts Output

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cast-iron radiator or a run of baseboard is only as good as the air and surfaces around it. A decorative cover, a coat of silver paint or a sofa pushed against the wall can take a real bite out of the heat reaching the room. This lesson uses old government and university test data to show how much, and when a cover turns a marginal room into a cold one.

Two ways the heat leaves

A radiator gives heat to a room in two ways. Its hot surface radiates heat to cooler walls, furniture and people, and it warms the air touching it, which rises and is replaced by cooler air from below. That second path is convection. The 1925 federal Bureau of Standards letter we use below notes that a cellular radiator heats the air mainly by conduction and convection, which is why changing its surface finish changes the output less than you might expect. We found no sourced radiation-versus-convection percentage for cast iron, so we do not quote one here. Fin-tube baseboard leans even harder on convection: air must flow in at the bottom, rise through the fins and leave at the top. See Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature for how water temperature changes output.

Paint: metallic versus non-metallic

The Bureau of Standards letter (LC 107, 1925) reports tests by other researchers on a two-column, thirteen-section radiator. Coated with aluminum paint, it gave off only about 80 percent as much heat to the room as the same radiator in ordinary non-metallic paints such as white, green or zinc-oxide enamels. The letter expects a gain of 15 to 30 percent from repainting a multi-section house radiator with non-metallic paint in place of aluminum. It also says the color does not matter much: it "need not be black." The key is whether the paint contains metal flakes. The letter defines non-metallic paint as paint without flakes of pure metal, aluminum or bronze, and says a non-metallic coat may go over aluminum paint. It reports no test numbers for bronze paint, so treat bronze as a metal-flake finish with the same warning and do not assume a specific percentage.

Covers and enclosures

The University of Illinois Engineering Experiment Station tested steam radiators in enclosures in the 1920s. In Bulletin No. 169 (1927) a 20-section, three-column cast-iron radiator was tested with 216°F steam, and a bare radiator counted as 100 percent. The authors concluded that enclosures close to commercial types cut the radiator's capacity by about 15 percent, a common commercial shield by about 8 percent and a cloth cover by about 12 percent. The openings mattered more than the box:

  • A top outlet beat a front outlet. A front outlet, even a large one, was less effective.
  • Taller enclosures did better. With a 9¼ by 50 inch open top, the relative capacity was 89.1 percent at 38 inches high and 99.3 percent at 72 inches.
  • A grille in the opening cost output. The same top outlet fell from 89.1 to 86.5 percent with a grille, and a narrow 4 by 50 inch front outlet fell from 77.0 to 69.2 percent.
  • Inlet area counted too. The bulletin recommends an inlet across the whole front and ends, and warns against long, narrow inlets.

A related University of Illinois test in Bulletin No. 349 (about 1942) rated free-standing radiators at 195°F mean water and 200 Btu/hr per square foot of EDR, and reduced the rating by 12.5 percent for radiators set in wall recesses. A laboratory study from a UK university measured a hot-water panel radiator in a timber casing with a fascia grille and reported reductions of up to 40 percent. That paper had internal inconsistencies, so we use only its "up to" figure as a warning, not as a design number. Panel radiators are not cast iron, and the other test radiators were steam units, so these figures are signs of size and direction, not guarantees for your radiator.

Our takeaway: a cover needs open space at the bottom and top, and a cover that is open at the front or has a small top slot behaves like the worst cases above. A shelf over the radiator is a mild version of the same problem because it turns the rising air sideways.

Reflectors behind the radiator

A reflective sheet on the wall behind a radiator on an outside wall is meant to send radiant heat back toward the room instead of warming the wall. A federal guide for contractors (PNNL and ORNL, 2011) lists installing reflectors behind wall radiators among its boiler tune-up items. In the Illinois research-house hot-water study (Bulletin No. 349), the extra heat loss of an uninsulated brick recess behind a radiator, compared with a fully insulated wall, was about 16 to 1 in zero-degree weather. One reflective surface, or 1 inch of rigid insulation, cut that ratio to about 9 to 1. Both together brought the loss close to the loss behind a free-standing radiator. Those are recess results in one test house. We could not source a percentage gain in room heat for a loose foil sheet behind a free-standing radiator, so we state none.

Furniture, drapes and baseboard

We found no government test giving a percentage for drapes or furniture. Our own reasoning follows from the cover data above: anything that blocks the air path in at the bottom or out at the top of baseboard behaves like a cover with a small opening, and a drape hanging in front of a radiator catches both the warm air and the radiant heat. Keep clearance in front and above, keep the bottom slot open and clean, and see Hot-Water Baseboard: Cleaning, Bent Fins, Dampers and Ticking. A radiator that is cold at one end is a different problem; start with Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water System, or for steam, Uneven Heat in One-Pipe Steam.

When a cover makes a cold room colder

A 1929 University of Illinois test (Bulletin No. 192) put a six-section steam radiator in a laboratory room with two exposed walls and a cold space held near zero. A bare radiator held the breathing level at 69.4°F. The best enclosure held 69.5°F and evened out the ceiling-to-floor difference. One poorly designed enclosure held only 66.2°F, and the report says the enclosed radiator "failed to heat the room." Two cloth covers gave 66.4°F and 61.2°F at breathing level. The report's lesson is plain: a well-designed enclosure can save steam with equal comfort, while a bad one needs extra radiation to avoid an underheated room. In our shop-talk version: if a room is already barely keeping up, adding a cover is how you lose it.

Worked example (our own example, assumed numbers)

Everything below is an assumption we chose to show the arithmetic, except the cited percentages named in each line. Assume a room with a design heat loss of 10,500 Btu/hr when it is 70°F inside and 0°F outside. Assume a cast-iron radiator of 60 square feet of EDR on hot water at 195°F average, with an output of 200 Btu/hr per square foot (the rating basis in Bulletin No. 349; modern ratings vary, so check the actual data).

  1. Bare radiator: 60 × 200 = 12,000 Btu/hr. That is 1,500 above the 10,500 loss. The room holds 70°F.
  2. Room conductance: 10,500 ÷ 70°F difference = 150 Btu/hr per °F.
  3. Cover with a 15 percent penalty (the Bulletin No. 169 figure for commercial-type steam enclosures, which we assume carries over): 12,000 × 0.85 = 10,200. That is 300 short. The room settles about 300 ÷ 150 = 2°F low, near 68°F. To cover it, the radiator would need 10,500 ÷ 0.85 = about 12,350 Btu/hr bare, or about 62 sq ft EDR.
  4. Poor cover with a 40 percent penalty (an assumption based on the "up to 40 percent" lab result): 12,000 × 0.60 = 7,200. That is 3,300 short, so the room settles where 7,200 ÷ 150 = 48°F of difference is held, about 48°F inside against 0°F outside on the design day. We ignore the small output rise as the room cools.
  5. Aluminum paint (about 80 percent of non-metallic, from LC 107, applied to our assumed radiator): 12,000 × 0.80 = 9,600. That is 900 short, about 6°F low, near 64°F.

On a mild day none of these show. On the design day, the margin is gone. Repainting or opening the cover restores it, which costs far less than a bigger radiator.

Field card: Radiator Covers, Paint and Reflectors

Open top, open bottom, non-metallic paint, clear front.

  • Metal-flake paint (aluminum, bronze) hurts radiant output; a 1925 federal letter reports about 80 percent of the heat for aluminum paint.
  • Old Illinois tests: enclosures near commercial types cut steam radiator capacity about 15 percent; shields about 8; cloth about 12.
  • Top outlet beats front outlet; taller enclosure beats short; grille costs output.
  • Keep bottom and top openings clear. No shelf or drape over the top.
  • Reflectors help most on outside-wall recesses; no sourced percentage for loose foil.
  • If a room barely heats, a cover can make it cold. Check the heat loss against bare output.
