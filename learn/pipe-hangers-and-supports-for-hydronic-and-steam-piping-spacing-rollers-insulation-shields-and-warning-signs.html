Pipe Hangers and Supports for Hydronic and Steam Piping: Spacing, Rollers, Insulation Shields and Warning Signs

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A pipe full of water is heavy, and every foot of it has to be held up by something. Hangers do that job, and they also decide whether a hot line can slide as it grows, whether insulation survives, and whether a pump or valve ends up carrying pipe it was never built to carry. This lesson covers spacing, hanger types, shields, risers, and the warning signs a tech can spot from a ladder or a lift.

What hangers do and what they do not

A hanger carries weight. It does not stop movement. That is the job of anchors and guides, which we cover in the thermal expansion lesson. The City of San Diego piping support specification we read also says hanger rods are to carry tension only. A rod bent sideways by a pipe that moves is a rod doing a job it was not sized for.

Hanger spacing for steel pipe

Three cautions go with any table like this:

  • Owner rules can be tighter. A university facilities specification from Columbia says hangers are at most 10 ft apart regardless of service, and 5 ft when single shields are used on pipe 2 in. and under on concrete. Read the project spec first.
  • Point loads break the table. A Michigan State utility specification says the spans of the support standard it cites do not apply where valves or heavy fittings add concentrated weight. It also requires a hanger at every change of direction and within 1 ft of fittings and valves.
  • The table lists size only. The San Diego table gives spans by pipe size and does not change them by service, so check the project spec for steam, hot water and chilled water. What changes with service is the hanger type, covered next.

The San Diego table is for steel pipe. Copper, plastic and other materials have their own, shorter spans, so do not borrow this table for them.

Clevis hangers versus roller hangers

An adjustable clevis hanger holds the pipe from above on a rod and lets you set the height. Columbia requires adjustable clevis hangers for horizontal piping in general. Northwestern University's guidelines describe the clevis type as the one for stationary pipes, NPS 1/2 to NPS 30, insulated or not.

A roller hanger lets the pipe slide lengthwise on a roll. Columbia requires roller hangers on hot piping 3 in. and larger, and on chilled-water piping with straight runs longer than 150 ft. Northwestern lists single-pipe rolls for NPS 1 to 30 hung from two rods where lengthwise movement may occur, and adjustable roller hangers on a single rod for NPS 2-1/2 to 24 where horizontal movement may occur. Michigan State uses adjustable roller hangers for steam piping inside buildings.

Two more rules from these documents matter on loops and offsets. Columbia says hangers at expansion loops, bends and offsets must allow horizontal adjustment and must get their final adjustment while the pipe is hot, so the hanger is plumb at operating temperature. Michigan State calls for adjustable clevis hangers when the distance from an anchor to the expansion joint midpoint is more than 5 ft.

Insulation shields and saddles

Insulation is soft compared to steel. If a bare clevis strap or roller sits on it, the weight of a full pipe squeezes the insulation flat, which wrecks the thermal value and, on cold lines, breaks the vapor seal. A shield spreads the load over a wide area.

  • Northwestern uses a protection saddle where the insulation has no vapor barrier and a protection shield, spanning 180 degrees, on cold piping with a vapor barrier. The shield length follows the maker's written recommendation so the insulation is not crushed.
  • Its minimum thermal-hanger shield for NPS 1/4 to 3-1/2 is 12 in. long and 0.048 in. thick, and the insert at a clevis or band hanger must cover the lower 180 degrees of the pipe. On rollers with shields, NPS 4 and larger also gets a steel weight-distribution plate.
  • On cold piping, Northwestern extends the insert 2 in. past the sheet metal shield, and Columbia says the vapor barrier jacket must cover the saddle with the gap between saddle and pipe filled with insulation to prevent sweating.
  • Michigan State sets the saddle height equal to the insulation thickness and puts one at every hanger or roller.

For the insulation side of the story, see the pipe insulation lesson.

Riser clamps

Vertical pipe hangs from a clamp that bears on the structure at a floor. Northwestern lists extension pipe or riser clamps and heavier steel riser clamps for NPS 3/4 to 24. Columbia requires heavy wrought iron or steel clamps, bolted or welded to the pipe, one clamp every two floors (one at each floor for copper tubing), with pre-insulated clamps that have their own vapor barrier on cold risers. Where a riser grows with heat, a clamp that bears on the floor acts like an anchor, so plan it with the expansion design.

Keep pipe weight off pumps and valves

Northwestern says hangers and supports must not pass the piping's live loads, dead loads or movement stresses to connected equipment, and they should not block access. It also calls for added attachments at concentrated loads such as valves, flanges and strainers. In practice that means a hanger on each side of a pump, a support near a heavy valve, and a support close to a coil or chiller connection, so a flange does not have to be pulled into place by bolts. For flange and coupling details see the grooved coupling lesson.

Signs of trouble

These are our own field checks, built on the rules above:

  • Sagging between hangers. A visible belly means spans are too long or a hanger is missing or has let go.
  • Broken, bent or loose hangers. A rod that is slanted instead of plumb usually means the pipe moved more than the hanger allows.
  • Pipe resting on ductwork or other trades' supports. It was never meant to be a support, and it can dent the duct. Compare with the duct hanger lesson.
  • Crushed insulation at a hanger. The shield is missing, too short, or has slipped.
  • Sweating on cold pipe at a hanger. A crushed or unsealed spot lets moist air reach the cold pipe.
  • Ticking, groaning or banging. Pipe that cannot slide on a clevis, or a roller that has seized, makes noise as it grows and shrinks.
  • Strain at a pump or valve flange. Gaps, leaks or a stuck valve can mean the equipment is carrying the pipe.

Before any hydrostatic test, remember the water adds weight; see the new piping testing lesson.

Worked example (our own example)

A 60 ft straight run of 3 in. steel pipe, full of water. Assumptions: Schedule 40 wall; spacing from the San Diego table; insulation, fittings and valves ignored; both ends of the run are supported.

  1. Pipe weight, 3 in. Schedule 40: 7.58 lb/ft. Water in it: 3.20 lb/ft. These come from a Nuclear Regulatory Commission pipe table.
  2. Weight per foot: 7.58 + 3.20 = 10.78 lb/ft.
  3. Total weight of the run: 60 × 10.78 = 646.8 lb.
  4. Spacing from the table for 3 in. pipe: 12 ft.
  5. Number of spans: 60 ÷ 12 = 5. With a hanger at each end of every span, that is 5 + 1 = 6 supports.
  6. Load per interior hanger (each carries half of the span on each side, so one full span): 12 × 10.78 = 129.4 lb.
  7. The two end supports each carry half a span: 6 × 10.78 = 64.7 lb. Check: 4 × 129.4 + 2 × 64.7 = 647 lb, matching step 3.

This is a simple tributary-length estimate, not a stress analysis. Real loads rise with insulation, jacketing, valves and fittings, and a hot line over 3 in. also needs roller hangers under the Columbia rule above. Check the hanger and rod rating from the maker's data and the project spec before you choose parts.

Field card: Pipe Hangers and Supports

Hangers carry weight, anchors and guides control movement.

  • 3 in. steel pipe: 12 ft maximum span in the San Diego table. Check the owner spec, which may be tighter.
  • 3 in. Schedule 40 with water: 10.78 lb/ft (7.58 pipe + 3.20 water).
  • Extra hanger within 1 ft of valves and fittings and at direction changes.
  • Clevis for stationary pipe; roller where pipe slides, such as hot lines 3 in. and up (Columbia rule).
  • A shield or saddle at every hanger on insulated pipe.
  • Pipe weight must not rest on pumps, coils or valves.
  • Warning signs: sag, slanted rods, crushed insulation, pipe on ducts, noise.
