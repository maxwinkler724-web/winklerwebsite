What to Ask an HVAC Contractor Before Hiring

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Replacing a furnace or air conditioner is a big decision. These questions help you compare contractors fairly and protect yourself.

The short answer

Before you hire anyone for heating or cooling work, confirm they are properly licensed and registered where you live, ask for proof of insurance, get a written quote that spells out the work, and ask how they decided on the size of equipment. A good contractor will answer these without fuss. Take your time. A big job should not be decided under pressure.

Questions worth asking

  • Are you registered with Consumer Affairs, and what is the number? The New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs advises checking a contractor's registration before you sign or pay. Registration requires a physical business location and proof of liability insurance, and the registration number belongs on the contract. You can check online at NJConsumerAffairs.gov or by phone.
  • Are you a licensed HVAC contractor, and what is the number? Heating and cooling work can have its own license, separate from home improvement registration. Ask for each number and look each one up with the issuing office.
  • Can I see proof of insurance? The state says registration requires proof of at least $500,000 in liability insurance. Ask for a copy of the policy, then contact the insurer to confirm it is valid, rather than taking a verbal answer.
  • Can you give me a written quote and contract? New Jersey guidance says home improvement contracts of $500 or more must be in writing. They should list the contractor's legal name, business address and registration number, start and completion dates, a description of the work, the total price, any warranties in writing, and the brands or grades of materials.
  • How did you size the system? A load calculation (often called Manual J) estimates how much heating and cooling a home needs. ENERGY STAR's bid sheet suggests asking each bidder whether they will measure your home and size the equipment with Manual J or an equivalent method, rather than just matching the old unit. Carrier notes an oversized furnace heats too fast and shuts off early, while an undersized one runs long.
  • What exactly is included? Equipment model numbers, ductwork changes, permits, and removal of old equipment. ENERGY STAR's bid sheet also suggests asking whether the contractor will inspect and seal ducts and test duct leakage, check refrigerant charge and airflow, install new refrigerant lines and a matched indoor coil and outdoor unit, show you how to change the filter, leave the manuals and paperwork (Manual J, AHRI certificate, test results), offer third-party verification, and tell you about local rebates.
  • What does the warranty cover, and who honors it?
  • Will you pull the permit? The state advises making sure the contractor obtains the required permits from your town. Ask your local building department too.

Paying and protecting yourself

The state suggests avoiding paying the full amount in advance. A common schedule is one-third at signing, one-third at the midpoint, and one-third when the work is finished. Verify registration before you hire anyone and before you pay. You can check at NJConsumerAffairs.gov or call the Division of Consumer Affairs at 1-800-242-5846 (toll free in New Jersey), and you can ask about complaint history. To file a complaint, use the same number or 973-504-6200. Start with recommendations from people you trust, ask for references, and call them. Getting two or three quotes lets you compare scope, not only the bottom line.

Things you can safely check

  • Look up the registration and license numbers before the first visit.
  • Read the contract slowly at home. Ask for changes in writing.
  • Ask for the equipment model numbers and look up their efficiency ratings (see our SEER2 lesson).
  • Take photos of your existing system and label so you can compare quotes.
  • Check that your property is ready: clear space around the equipment and a safe path to it.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. That includes tasks a salesperson might suggest you "just try."

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out.
  • Any contractor refuses to show a registration or license number or proof of insurance, or the paperwork is vague.
  • You are pressured to sign or pay in full on the spot.
  • Your current system shows a safety sign: smell of gas, soot, or an alarm. Get out, then call the gas utility or 911 first. Shop for a contractor later.

Field card: Hiring an HVAC Contractor

Verify first, sign second, pay in stages.

  • Registration and HVAC license numbers: look both up yourself.
  • Proof of liability insurance; confirm it with the insurer.
  • Written quote with scope, models, warranty, dates, and price.
  • Ask how the system was sized (load calculation).
  • Pay in stages, never in full up front, and keep every document.
