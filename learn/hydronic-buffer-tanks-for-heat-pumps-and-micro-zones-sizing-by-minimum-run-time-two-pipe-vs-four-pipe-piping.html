Hydronic Buffer Tanks for Heat Pumps and Micro-Zones

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A buffer tank is a big insulated water jug piped between a heat source and the rooms it serves. It gives a heat pump or a small boiler somewhere to put its heat so it can run long, steady cycles instead of blinking on and off. This lesson covers why the tank exists, how to size it from a minimum run time, and the piping and sensor choices that make it work.

Why a buffer tank exists

Three jobs show up again and again.

  • Minimum run time. A hydronic trade-magazine article on combining heat pumps with buffer tanks describes each small radiator or towel warmer as a "micro-zone" that may draw under 10 percent, even under 5 percent, of the heat source's capacity. Piped straight to a single-speed source, one such zone calling alone makes the source short cycle. The article says the same applies to air-to-water heat pumps, even ones that can modulate down to about 20 percent. A heat pump maker's web page adds that buffers typically aim for 10 to 20 minutes per run and that compressors commonly tolerate about 3 to 6 starts per hour. Treat those as one maker's figures, and check your own unit's manual.
  • Defrost. The U.S. Department of Energy says a heat pump's reversing valve can reverse refrigerant flow to melt frost off the outdoor coil in winter. In an air-to-water unit we expect that heat to come from somewhere on the water side during that time. A tank gives it a reserve to draw on, so the rooms do not feel it. How a particular unit handles defrost is the maker's call, so read its manual.
  • Hydraulic separation. A boiler maker's buffer tank instructions say a correctly piped tank acts as both a thermal buffer and a hydraulic separator. The heat source and the distribution loop can then run different flow rates.

The same logic applies to geothermal water-to-water units (see Geothermal Ground-Source Heat Pump Basics) and to a small modulating boiler whose lowest fire is bigger than the smallest zone. A trade-magazine article on microloads gives an example: a 65,000 Btu/h boiler with 5:1 turndown cannot fire below 13,000 Btu/h, so a 5,000 Btu/h zone makes it short cycle. For the boiler side of this problem, see Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling: Oversizing, Zoning and Fixes.

Sizing from minimum run time

The designer picks two numbers: the shortest acceptable heat-source on-cycle, and how far the tank water may rise during it. The tank must absorb whatever the source makes that the loads do not take. That is an energy balance, and it is our own arithmetic, not a quoted formula (the published formulas we found were images we could not read, but the 60 gallon result in the microload article matches this arithmetic when we run its inputs through it).

V = (Qsource − Qload) × t ÷ 60 ÷ (lb per gal × Btu per lb·°F × ΔT), where V is gallons, Q is Btu/h, t is minutes, and ΔT is the allowed tank temperature rise in °F. A Penn State University energy page says it takes 1 Btu to raise a pound of water one degree Fahrenheit and that a gallon weighs about 8.3 pounds, so that is what we use.

Worked example

Assumptions (all assumed, none measured): heat pump output 48,000 Btu/h; minimum run 10 minutes; one small load of 2,000 Btu/h still drawing during the run; tank cycles between 100°F and 120°F, so ΔT = 20°F. The 48,000/10-minute/2,000/100-to-120 inputs are the example in the heat pump article; the arithmetic is ours.

  1. Net heat going into the tank: 48,000 − 2,000 = 46,000 Btu/h.
  2. Over 10 minutes: 46,000 × 10 ÷ 60 = 7,667 Btu.
  3. Heat stored per gallon over 20°F: 8.3 × 1 × 20 = 166 Btu.
  4. Volume: 7,667 ÷ 166 = about 46 gallons. Round up to the next tank you can buy.

Our own sensitivity check: allow only a 10°F swing and the same arithmetic gives about 92 gallons. Ask for 20 minutes instead of 10 and the volume doubles. Tank size is a trade of swing against run time against space. The article also notes larger tanks usually cost more, need more room and lose more heat while idle.

Flow check, using the article's 3 gpm per ton rule of thumb: 48,000 Btu/h is 4 tons, so about 12 gpm. A 46 gallon tank turns over in roughly 4 minutes (46 ÷ 12, our own arithmetic). That is why mixing matters in the next sections.

Two-pipe, three-pipe, four-pipe and series

  • Four-pipe. Source and load sit on opposite sides of the tank. The heat pump article says this gives excellent hydraulic separation, but all heat must pass through the tank, which slows recovery when the tank has cooled. It also calls for a check valve on the heat source side to stop reverse thermosiphoning.
  • Two-pipe. The load sits between the buffer and the source, so heat can reach the load directly and setback recovery is faster. The article says this needs a valve giving roughly 1 to 1.5 psi of forward-opening resistance on the heat source piping, and should be used only when tank temperature controls the source.
  • Three-pipe. The author's preferred compromise for heat pumps: supply goes direct to the load while return flow is forced through the lower tank.
  • Series (in-line). The heat pump maker's page describes the tank sitting directly in the heating loop so all water passes through it. It calls this simpler and cheaper, but it does not fully decouple the circuits, so it suits small single-circuit systems. That page also calls the four-pipe type "parallel," so labels differ from source to source. Confirm the layout from the drawing, not the name. For the separator idea behind this, see Primary-Secondary Piping Basics.

Sensor location, mixing and stratification

The heat pump article mounts the control sensor near the middle of the tank, calls that a reasonably accurate average, and holds mid-tank water between 100 and 110°F in its example. A boiler maker's tank has a 3/8 inch sensor well for this purpose. The heat pump maker's page instead describes three sensors (top, middle, bottom) and says the primary return must connect to the bottom of the tank. Because heat pump flow is high, the article says not to expect much stratification, and vertical connections add mixing. Horizontal ones reduce it and let cooler water at the bottom reach the heat pump. Follow your unit's manual for the sensor type and wiring.

Standby loss and insulation

A buffer is not a water heater, and it sits warm all winter. The heat pump maker's page lists minimum rigid foam of 50 mm for tanks up to 500 liters and says a poorly insulated 300 liter tank can lose 2 to 4 kWh a day. Our own takeaway: keep the tank as small as the run-time math allows, insulate the tank and every fitting, and keep it inside the heated space where practical.

When you may not need one

  • The system has plenty of water content and no micro-zones, such as a few large radiant loops that rarely close together. See Radiant Floor Manifolds.
  • The heat pump maker's page says a hydraulic separator alone can work only with very high building thermal mass and a heat pump that modulates to a very low minimum output.
  • A modulating boiler whose lowest fire sits just above the smallest load. The microload article calls this the ideal, but warns that oversizing a modulating condensing boiler and running it at low fire for long periods can destroy the burner head. Check the manufacturer's data for your model.

Commercial plants add supply temperature limits and hybrid designs; see Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Commercial Hot-Water Heating.

Field card: Hydronic Buffer Tanks for Heat Pumps and Micro-Zones

The tank is a battery for run time.

  • Micro-zones under 10% of source output cause short cycling on a single-speed source.
  • Size: V = (Q source minus Q load) x minutes / 60 / (8.3 x swing in F).
  • Example: 48,000 Btu/h, 10 min, 2,000 load, 20 F swing = about 46 gal (assumed inputs).
  • Four-pipe: best separation, slower recovery. Two-pipe: faster recovery, needs a resistance valve and tank-temperature control.
  • Sensor near mid-tank; high flow means little stratification.
  • Insulate it; standby loss never stops.
  • Skip it only with big water content or a modulating source matched to the smallest load.
