Clean Openings in HVAC Disconnects and Control Cabinets: Knockouts, Punches, Step Bits and Sealing What You Do Not Use

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every conduit that reaches a disconnect box or control cabinet needs a hole in thin sheet metal. A clean, correctly sized hole takes a few minutes. A ragged, oversized or chip-filled one can cut a conductor, let water in, or leave a loose connector that is no longer bonded. This lesson covers how to make the opening, size it, finish it and close what you do not use.

Start with the safe condition of the job

Before you cut anything, de-energize and verify, and follow your site lockout rules. We cover the electrical side in Electrical Basics for HVAC and AC Condenser Disconnects, Fuses and Breakers. Making a hole is a tool job too. The federal workplace safety agency's general industry rule on hand and portable powered tools says each employer is responsible for the safe condition of the tools and equipment employees use. Its personal protective equipment rule says employees must use appropriate eye or face protection when exposed to flying particles, among other hazards. Drilling and punching sheet metal throws small, sharp chips, so wear safety glasses with side protection every time. The agency's hand and power tools booklet also says to keep tools in good condition, to inspect a tool for damage before use, and to tag and remove damaged portable electric tools. See also Hand and Power Tool Safety for HVAC Techs.

Where the opening goes, and where it must not

Look for an existing knockout first. A knockout is a partly cut disc in the enclosure wall that you remove with a hammer and punch or a screwdriver and pliers, leaving a round opening. Knockouts are placed by the maker where the enclosure can take conduit. If the enclosure has a knockout on the side or bottom, use it before you cut a new hole.

Our shop rules for new holes in the field:

  • Not through a factory-wired panel. If the panel face, back or side carries wiring, a board, a contactor or a transformer, a new hole can put a tool or a chip where it should never be. Use a provided entry, or ask whether the maker allows a field entry and where. Follow the equipment instructions.
  • Not into the top of an outdoor disconnect. Rain runs down onto the top and a hole there lets water straight into the switch. Enter from the bottom or side, and keep the fitting facing down or sideways as the instructions allow.
  • Not near the live parts. Pick a spot where a connector body and locknut clear the terminals inside, with room to tighten.

If the enclosure is a cabinet that has to stay closed to its original rating, a field-made hole may change it. Check the instructions before cutting. We do not decide that for you here.

Removing a knockout

A knockout is a thin web of metal. Set the knockout so the correct side faces out, tap one side of the disc with a punch, a hammer or a screwdriver handle, and bend it back and forth until it breaks free. Then grip the remaining ring with pliers and wiggle it off if a tab is left. Support the enclosure so you do not bend the wall, and keep your other hand out of line. If the knockout is not sized for the fitting, you can enlarge it by stepping up with a punch or step bit, but do not pry a larger fitting into a knockout that is too small.

Punch sets and step bits

For a new hole in thin sheet metal there are two common tools.

  • Knockout punch set (manual or hydraulic). You drill a small pilot hole, set the die on one side and the punch on the other, and tighten the draw stud with a wrench or a hydraulic driver. The die shears out a round slug and leaves a clean-edged hole with little distortion and little loose swarf. Manual sets suit light sheet and small sizes. A hydraulic driver saves effort on large sizes or thicker steel.
  • Step drill bit. A cone-shaped bit with a series of steps, each cutting a slightly larger diameter than the one before. It works well in thin sheet. Run it at a moderate speed with light pressure, clamp or support the work, and stop at the step that gives the diameter you want. A step bit tends to leave a burr on the exit side. It can also grab and spin the sheet when the last step breaks through, so hold the work firmly.

Whatever you use, mark the center, center-punch it, and plan the other side before you cut. The drill-and-core lesson, Drilling and Coring Through Walls, Floors and Roofs, covers larger building holes, and the sheet metal lesson, Hand Sheet Metal Work for HVAC Techs, covers duct work. We do not repeat them here.

Matching the hole to the conduit and connector

The hole is sized for the threaded body of the connector or fitting that passes through the wall, not for the conduit itself. A conduit trade size is a name, not a measurement of the hole. The connector's threaded nipple has its own outside diameter, and the hole must be just larger than that, so the locknut seats flat and the fitting does not rock. A hole that is too small will not take the fitting. A hole that is too large leaves a gap where the locknut cannot bite and where water, insects and dust can get in. For the conduit side of the job, including liquidtight fittings and bonding, see Condenser Whip Conduit: Liquidtight Flex, Fittings and Grounding.

Our own worked example: step-bit count for a trade-size 3/4 connector

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a table. Measure your own parts before you cut.

  • Assumption 1: the conduit is trade size 3/4 inch, and its connector's threaded body measures 1.06 inches across the threads with calipers.
  • Assumption 2: we allow 0.03 inch of clearance so the body slides through without wobble.
  • Assumption 3: the step bit has steps every 1/16 inch (0.0625 in), starting at 1/4 inch.

Step 1, minimum hole: 1.06 + 0.03 = 1.09 inches.

Step 2, round up to a step. In sixteenths, 1.09 inch is 1.09 × 16 = 17.44 sixteenths. The next whole step is 18 sixteenths, which is 18 ÷ 16 = 1.125 inches (1-1/8 inch). The hole then has 1.125 − 1.06 = 0.065 inch of total gap, about 0.03 inch per side.

Step 3, count steps. The first step is 1/4 inch, or 4 sixteenths. The last is 18 sixteenths. The bit has to pass 18 − 4 = 14 increases after the first step, so it cuts through 14 + 1 = 15 steps in total. Stop when the 15th step is through the wall.

If the 1-1/8 inch step were too loose for your connector, check whether a different connector or a reducing method fits the job. Do not cut past the step and then ream. Our numbers are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. Different connectors have different thread diameters, and a knockout punch for the same trade size may cut a different diameter than our step bit.

Deburr, then clean

Every cut edge in sheet metal is sharp and may carry a burr that can cut a conductor jacket or a hand. Remove the burr from both faces with a deburring tool, a file or a larger bit turned by hand, and ease the edge. Then clean the chips out of the enclosure before anything is energized:

  • Cut over a catch: tape, a magnet or a vacuum nozzle held at the hole.
  • Tilt the enclosure so chips fall out, then vacuum inside. Do not blow chips around with compressed air. They can lodge in a contactor, relay or board.
  • Check the coil and cabinet floor below. Metal chips on a condenser coil fin can rust and stain it and are hard to remove from fins.
  • Where it is practical, pull the control board or cover the electrical parts before you cut, and remove the cover only once the area is clean.

Protect the conductors

A state electrical inspection checklist based on the 2023 National Electrical Code, which we read, says cables and raceways must be protected from damage under rule 300.4, and it lists a bushing as required where a conductor of 4 AWG or larger enters an enclosure through a raceway. For smaller conductors, a bushing on the connector throat, a grommet in a hole used for cable, or a connector designed to protect the jacket all do the same job: they keep a sharp metal edge away from the insulation. Use a fitting made for the cable or raceway you are running, and never pull conductors through a bare hole.

Close what you do not use

The same state checklist item for the National Electrical Code section 110.12 says unused openings in boxes must be effectively closed. It also says a nonmetallic box must be replaced if a cable opening is punched out but not used. The federal electrical rule for general industry says almost the same thing: unused openings in boxes, raceways, cabinets, equipment cases or housings are to be effectively closed, protecting about as well as the equipment wall itself. In practice, that means a knockout you removed by mistake, or a leftover hole after changing a fitting, gets a plug made for that opening, tightened in place. Tape, foam and a sheet-metal screw are not a fix. Outdoors, a plug that keeps water out is part of the job.

Field card: Clean Openings in Disconnects and Cabinets

Pick the spot, size it, deburr it, clean it, close what you do not use.

  • Eye protection on. De-energize and verify first.
  • Use an existing knockout before cutting a new hole.
  • No new holes through factory-wired panels, and none into the top of an outdoor disconnect.
  • Size the hole to the connector's threaded body, not to the conduit name.
  • Our example: 1.06 in body + 0.03 in = 1.09 in, so the 1-1/8 in step, the 15th step on a 1/4 in start.
  • Deburr both faces; vacuum chips out of the box and coil.
  • Bushing or grommet where a sharp edge could touch a conductor.
  • Plug every unused opening.
