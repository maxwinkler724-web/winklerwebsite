Orphaned Water Heater Chimney Liner: Sizing Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a furnace or boiler is replaced with a sealed, plastic-vented unit, the old gas water heater is often left alone in a chimney that was built for two appliances. This lesson walks through the sizing math a tech or inspector uses to judge that chimney, and how a liner size is chosen from a vent table.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on any house. The numbers below come from a 1999 edition of the National Fuel Gas Code tables (reproduced on a water heater maker's literature site), a 2021 local code appendix, and maker documents. Current NFPA 54 or IFGC tables, your local amendments and the water heater and liner instructions decide a real job. Venting category basics are in Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained.

What "orphaned" means

An orphaned water heater is a draft-hood (natural draft) tank heater that used to share a flue with another appliance, which has since been replaced with a unit that has its own vent. InterNACHI's inspector article describes it that way, and a Manitoba government notice uses the same idea, calling it a stand-alone water heater left on a shared metal vent after a furnace or boiler change. One FVIR water heater manual (a distributor's copy, filed under A. O. Smith) names the condition "orphaning" and says the vent "must be properly resized for a single appliance," because poor sizing can cause spillage, poor combustion, excessive condensation, pilot outage or delayed ignition.

The same story applies when a heat pump replaces the furnace: anything that removes the big, hot flue gas stream leaves the water heater alone. The drafting basics are in Water Heater Backdrafting.

Why a big or exterior chimney fails the little heater

A natural draft vent works because hot gas is lighter than outdoor air. A 40,000 Btu/hr heater makes a small, fairly cool gas stream. In a wide flue that stream spreads out and cools, so the draft weakens and water vapor can turn to liquid on the walls. Contractor magazine writer Dave Yates says that liquid carries nitric and sulfuric acids and can spall (flake) masonry. An exterior chimney has walls open to the weather, which cools the gas faster still. The Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA) warns that modern furnaces can have many problems when vented through average flues made for older furnaces. Its FAQ adds that liners for gas and oil do not require insulation, but even gas appliances on an exterior chimney benefit from it.

For liner basics see Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat. This page adds the table side of the question.

What the sizing rules say

  • Maximum area (the "7 times" check). The 1999 code text says the flow area of the vertical vent must not exceed seven times the appliance's draft hood outlet area, unless designed by approved engineering methods. The 2021 Seattle fuel gas code appendix, section 504.3.17, states the same ratio. Some places are stricter: Minnesota's amendment changes it to four times.
  • Maximum chimney area by connector. The 1999 single-appliance masonry table lists a flue area range for each connector size: 12 to 49 sq in. for a 3 in. connector and 19 to 88 sq in. for 4 in. Those maximums are the 7x rule, rounded.
  • Capacity. The tables list the largest input a vent can carry for each diameter, height and lateral run. A water heater uses the "NAT" (natural draft) column. The Type B table has no NAT minimum.
  • Corrugated liners. A listed flexible liner is sized from the Type B table with its maximum capacity cut by 20 percent. Hart & Cooley's Flexi-Liner manual says the same.
  • Connector. A. O. Smith's FVIR guide says a change to a larger vent size must be made at the draft hood outlet. State Water Heaters' bulletin says a 4-inch draft hood must not go to 3-inch pipe. Rheem's furnace kit instructions say a connector may not be more than two sizes above the appliance outlet.

Worked example: 40,000 Btu/hr heater, 8 x 12 exterior chimney

Invented case: a 40,000 Btu/hr natural draft water heater with a 3 in. draft hood outlet (a 4 in. outlet is checked too). It vents into an 8 in. by 12 in. exterior masonry chimney, 15 ft tall, with a 5 ft lateral connector run. The old furnace used to share this flue.

Step 1: chimney area. Taking 8 in. by 12 in. as nominal: 8 × 12 = 96 sq in.

Step 2: outlet areas. Area = 3.14 × radius × radius. 3 in. outlet: 3.14 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 7.07 sq in. 4 in. outlet: 3.14 × 2 × 2 = 12.57 sq in.

Step 3: ratios. 96 ÷ 7.07 = 13.6 times. 96 ÷ 12.57 = 7.6 times.

Step 4: compare to the 7x check. Limits: 7 × 7.07 = 49.5 sq in. and 7 × 12.57 = 88.0 sq in. At 13.6 times, the 3 in. case is nearly double the limit. At 7.6 times, the 4 in. case is a little over.

Step 5: try the effective area. A tile flue's usable area is smaller than its nominal size. The Seattle appendix example treats an 8 x 12 liner as 63.6 sq in. Then 63.6 ÷ 7.07 = 9.0 times (still fails) and 63.6 ÷ 12.57 = 5.1 times (passes). So the verdict depends on the outlet size and on which area the code table uses. Where the limit is four times, as in Minnesota, even 63.6 sq in. fails for a 4 in. outlet (4 × 12.57 = 50.3).

Step 6: capacity into the chimney. The 1999 single-appliance masonry table (single-wall connector, 15 ft, 5 ft lateral) lists NAT maximums of 32 for 3 in. and 61 for 4 in., in thousands of Btu/hr. A 40,000 Btu/hr heater is over the 3 in. figure and under the 4 in. one.

Step 7: size a liner from the Type B table. Type B vent, 15 ft, 5 ft lateral, NAT maximum: 3 in. = 45 and 4 in. = 87 (thousands of Btu/hr). A rigid 3 in. liner carries 45, above 40, so it passes with little margin. A corrugated liner takes 80 percent: 3 in. = 0.8 × 45 = 36, below 40, so it fails; 4 in. = 0.8 × 87 = 69.6, above 40, so it passes. Check area: 12.57 ÷ 7.07 = 1.8, well inside the 7x check, with the step up made at the hood outlet.

Result: as a bare flue, this chimney fails the ratio check. In this invented case a 4 in. flexible liner or a 3 in. rigid liner passes the tables. A real job also needs the current code table, the actual height and offsets, and a chimney inspection.

Options

  • Liner sized to the heater. Done as above, following the liner maker's instructions. DuraVent's literature says the liner diameter should match the appliance outlet and refers to the appliance maker for limits, so a step up like the one above needs the heater maker's rules behind it.
  • Power vent water heater. Bradford White's power vent manual describes 3 in. or 4 in. plastic vent to the outdoors, with maximum lengths in its tables. It also warns that when a Category I appliance is replaced, the original vent may no longer be sized for the attached appliances. A separate sealed vent stays out of the old chimney.
  • Heat pump water heater. The U.S. Department of Energy says these units need a space between 40 and 90 degrees Fahrenheit with at least 1,000 cubic feet of air around them, and that they cool the room. Details in Heat Pump Water Heaters.

After any change, do the spillage check from the manual. New to the site? Start Here.

Field card: Orphaned Water Heater Chimney Liner: Sizing Basics

Ratio first, then the table, then the draft test.

  • Orphaned: a draft-hood water heater left alone on a flue sized for two appliances.
  • Area: 3.14 × radius × radius. 3 in. = 7.07 sq in.; 4 in. = 12.57 sq in.
  • The 7x check: flue area at most 7 times the draft hood outlet area (4x in Minnesota).
  • Use the NAT column of the vent table. Flexible liners: take 80 percent of the Type B number.
  • Step up in size at the draft hood outlet, never down.
  • Spillage check after the work. Alternatives: power vent or heat pump water heater.
