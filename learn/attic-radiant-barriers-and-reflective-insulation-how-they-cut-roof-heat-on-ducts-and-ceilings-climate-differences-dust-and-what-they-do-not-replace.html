Attic Radiant Barriers and Reflective Insulation: How They Cut Roof Heat on Ducts and Ceilings, Climate Differences, Dust and What They Do Not Replace

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a sunny afternoon the underside of a roof deck can be hotter than the air around it, and it sends heat across the attic as invisible radiation. A radiant barrier is a shiny, low-emittance sheet meant to turn some of that heat back. It helps in the right place, in the right climate, and it is easy to oversell. This lesson explains what it does, how it is installed, and why it never takes the place of air sealing and insulation.

Where this fits

We keep the basics of attic R-values in our attic insulation and air sealing lesson and the duct and air handler problems in air handlers and ducts in unconditioned attics. This page covers only the radiant piece. It is general education built from federal and university pages we read in 2026, not a design for your house. Your building department, the product instructions and a qualified installer decide the details.

How heat crosses an attic

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that heat moves three ways: conduction, convection and radiation. Most insulation slows conduction and, to a lesser degree, convection. A radiant barrier works on the third path. A university extension publication says radiant heat travels as electromagnetic waves between surfaces at different temperatures, so a hot, dark roof deck warms whatever cooler surface it can "see," such as the top of the attic floor insulation and any duct. Shiny foil reflects that energy, while dark, rough surfaces absorb it and warm up.

The property that matters is emittance, a number from 0 to 1 for how readily a surface gives off radiant heat. The extension publication says to look for high reflectivity and very low emittance. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) report measured emissivities of about 0.035 to 0.055 on clean foil barriers, and 0.125 to 0.185 after dust settled on them. Radiant barriers have no assigned R-value, since R-value rates resistance to conduction.

The air space rule

DOE says the reflective surface must face an air space to work, and recommends at least 1 inch between the foil and the underside of the roof. The extension publication adds that a one-sided product has its shiny side toward the air space and cannot be sandwiched tightly between two materials. Pressed against a solid surface, foil just conducts heat like any other metal sheet.

Three ways to install one

The ORNL page on radiant barriers lists three placements, and DOE and the extension publication describe them too.

  • Stapled under the rafters or trusses. The foil faces the attic space below, and the air gap is the open bay above it. DOE says to install it so dust does not build up on the reflective side, and foil facing down between rafters is its example. The extension publication describes draping the foil so it droops about 1.5 to 3 inches between rafters in new work.
  • Foil-faced roof sheathing. DOE lists foil-faced plywood or oriented strand board. The foil is on the underside of the roof deck and faces down into the attic. Because the deck itself carries the foil, there is nothing to staple and no sagging sheet. It is mostly a new-construction choice.
  • Laid over the attic floor insulation. This is the easiest retrofit, and ORNL found in one test house that horizontal barriers on the insulation performed better than a truss barrier. It is also the placement we caution about. DOE says not to place the barrier directly on top of attic floor insulation, because dust can collect on it and it may trap moisture in fiber insulation. ORNL says the attic floor is the placement most affected by dust, and its modeling suggests a dusty attic floor installation loses about half its effectiveness in roughly one to ten years. The extension publication says barriers laid on an attic floor must be perforated so water vapor can pass.

Reflective insulation is the related product. DOE's insulation table describes foil, film or paper facings fitted between framing members and says it is most effective against downward heat flow, with effectiveness depending on the spacing and number of foils. Same rule: the foil needs an air space next to it.

Climate: big in the sun, small in the Northeast

DOE says radiant barriers are more effective in hot climates than cool ones, especially when cooling ducts are in the attic, and that in cool climates more thermal insulation is usually more cost-effective. DOE cites studies showing cooling cost reductions of 5 to 10 percent in a warm, sunny climate. ORNL reports that with R-19 attic insulation, summer ceiling heat gain dropped about 16 to 42 percent in tests. Ceiling gains are only about 15 to 25 percent of the cooling load, so ORNL expects the cooling part of the bill to fall by roughly 2 to 10 percent, and possibly as much as 17 percent in some cases. One ORNL test in Tulsa found savings that could not be measured. ORNL also notes that little testing was done in climates colder than Knoxville.

That is the Northeast picture in short. Cooling season is shorter, so there are fewer hours to save, winter radiant heat gain from the roof matters more than in the South, and the extension publication notes a barrier can block that gain. ORNL says winter heat-loss reductions are smaller than summer heat-gain reductions. Savings are largest with low insulation levels and smaller with high levels; the extension publication says a barrier in an attic with R-30 or more saves much less than one in an attic with R-11 or less. We would not call it a priority for most Northeast homes, and the order of work in our attic insulation lesson still applies.

What it does for attic ducts and air handlers

A barrier under the roof deck lowers how much radiant heat lands on everything in the attic. DOE says the benefit is greater when cooling ducts are in the attic. ORNL says the barrier may lower attic air temperatures and so reduce heat gain to attic ducts, but adds that this effect was estimated with computer models and not field tested. So treat it as a modest helper. It does not fix a leaky or thinly insulated duct. For that, see duct insulation R-values and, if the unit itself sits up there, the air handler lesson linked above. A cooler attic can also mean less sweating on cold supply ducts only if insulation and vapor jackets are intact; our sweating ducts lesson covers that.

Worked example (ours): how much radiant heat does foil turn back?

This is our own example with assumed inputs. We assume a roof deck surface at 150 F, an attic floor surface at 95 F, parallel surfaces that see only each other, and no air movement or conduction in the picture. These are simplifications; real attics are messier. We use the Stefan-Boltzmann relation for net radiant heat flux, q = F x sigma x (T1^4 - T2^4), with sigma = 0.1714 x 10^-8 Btu/(h ft2 R^4) and absolute temperatures.

  1. Convert to Rankine (F + 459.67): T1 = 150 + 459.67 = 609.67 R (338.7 K). T2 = 95 + 459.67 = 554.67 R (308.2 K).
  2. Fourth powers: 609.67^4 = 1.3816 x 10^11 and 554.67^4 = 0.9465 x 10^11. The difference is 0.4351 x 10^11. Times sigma: 0.1714 x 10^-8 x 0.4351 x 10^11 = 74.6 Btu/(h ft2). That is the ideal black-body value.
  3. Effective emittance factor for two parallel surfaces: F = 1 / (1/e1 + 1/e2 - 1). Ordinary surfaces, assume e = 0.90 for both: F = 1 / (1.111 + 1.111 - 1) = 0.818. Flux = 0.818 x 74.6 = 61.0 Btu/(h ft2).
  4. With a foil surface, assume e = 0.05 on one side (ORNL measured 0.035 to 0.055 clean) and 0.90 on the other: F = 1 / (20 + 1.111 - 1) = 0.0497. Flux = 0.0497 x 74.6 = 3.7 Btu/(h ft2).
  5. Reduction: (61.0 - 3.7) / 61.0 = 0.94, about 94 percent less radiant exchange.

Now the reality check. Our 94 percent applies only to the radiant term in an idealized pair of surfaces. A real ceiling also gains heat by conduction and convection, which the foil barely touches, and attic air and framing change the picture. That is why ORNL measured ceiling heat gain reductions of 16 to 42 percent and bill reductions of a few percent. If the foil gets dusty and its emittance climbs to the 0.15 range ORNL measured, F becomes 1 / (6.67 + 1.111 - 1) = 0.147 and the flux is about 11 Btu/(h ft2), so the benefit shrinks from 94 percent to roughly 82 percent even before the other heat paths. That is why dust matters.

Not a substitute for air sealing and insulation

A radiant barrier does not stop air leaking through the ceiling plane, which moves heat, moisture and sometimes duct air. It does not add R-value. The extension publication says you still need insulation. Seal first, then insulate to the level your code and climate call for, then think about a barrier as an add-on in a hot, sunny location, particularly with ducts in the attic. Leaky ducts in particular waste far more than a barrier recovers; start with sealing duct leaks. Attic ventilation also interacts with savings, so do not block soffit or ridge vents with foil. ORNL listed ventilation as a factor but did not quantify it in the page we read.

Condensation, wiring and fire-code cautions

  • Moisture. A solid foil sheet on top of fiber insulation can hold moisture in it, per DOE. The extension publication says barriers placed in walls or on an attic floor must be perforated for vapor to pass, and an ORNL report observed condensation on the underside of perforated barriers in cold weather that dried during the day without causing problems in that test. Do not seal off bath fan or dryer exhaust ducts into the attic and call it a barrier problem; see the attic fan lesson for airflow basics.
  • Electricity. DOE warns that reflective foil conducts electricity, so avoid contact with bare wiring. Staple around cables and junction boxes, not through them.
  • Fire code. DOE tells do-it-yourselfers to follow the maker's instructions and check local building and fire codes. In general terms, codes may set limits on how readily a facing burns, and where it is placed near chimneys, flues, recessed lights and other heat sources. The pages we read did not give specific numbers, so ask your building department before buying.
  • Attic safety. Installing under rafters means working in a hot attic. Take the precautions in our attic heat stress lesson.

Field card: Attic Radiant Barriers

Foil, air gap, clean surface. Add it after sealing and insulation.

  • It reflects radiant heat only. No R-value, so it never replaces insulation.
  • The shiny side must face an air space, about 1 inch or more.
  • Under rafters or foil-faced sheathing keeps dust off. On the attic floor it collects dust and may trap moisture.
  • Bigger benefit in hot sunny climates and with ducts in the attic; small in cool ones.
  • Dust raises emittance and cuts performance.
  • Avoid bare wiring; check local building and fire codes first.
  • Our example: 150 F deck to 95 F floor, 61 to 3.7 Btu/(h ft2), about 94 percent less radiant exchange in the ideal case.
