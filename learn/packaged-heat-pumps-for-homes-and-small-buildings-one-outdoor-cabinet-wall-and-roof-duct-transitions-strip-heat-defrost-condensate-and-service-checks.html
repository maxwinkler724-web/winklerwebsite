Packaged Heat Pumps: One Outdoor Cabinet, Ducts and Service Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A split system has a cabinet outside and an air handler inside. A single-package heat pump puts the compressor, both coils, the blower and the backup heat in one outdoor cabinet. Only ducts and a few wires cross the wall. This lesson covers where that layout fits, what happens inside the cabinet, and what we check when we service one.

Where packaged units fit

Packaged heat pumps are common where there is no indoor room for an air handler: slab-on-grade homes with no basement or attic, crawl-space homes, small commercial buildings and manufactured homes. The cabinet sits on a slab or pad beside the building, or on a roof curb. A supply duct and a return duct run through the wall or roof, with a transition fitting where the round or rectangular duct meets the cabinet opening. For the larger commercial version of this idea, see Rooftop Unit Basics. For the vertical packaged units used in hotel rooms, see VTAC and Hotel Guestroom HVAC. We do not repeat those lessons here.

The national program requirements the federal energy department publishes for manufactured homes allow an outdoor package unit, but only if it is a heat pump. They also limit the outside supply and return duct runs to under 20 feet each and require exterior ducts to be wrapped with R-8 insulation. The same document says air-handling equipment and ductwork cannot be placed in a garage. Those are program rules for that home type, not a rule for every job, so always follow the local code and the maker's instructions.

What is inside the cabinet

Because the whole refrigerant circuit stays in one factory-built cabinet, there are no field-run line sets between an outdoor and an indoor unit. The indoor coil section sits behind a panel on the duct side of the cabinet, with the blower and an electric strip heat bank. The outdoor coil and fan sit on the other side. Use the new-install refrigerant listed on the unit data plate; this lesson does not depend on a specific refrigerant.

Defrost and strip heat inside the package

In heating, the outdoor coil is the cold coil, so frost builds on it. A state university research paper we read says most units start a defrost on a timer after the compressor has run 50 to 90 minutes, and that a defrost lasts up to 10 minutes. The same paper says nearly all heat pumps run the strip heat during defrost to prevent cold blow. That is why you may see the strip heat contactors pull in for a few minutes on a cold morning even when the thermostat is not calling for backup. See Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat for the reversing valve side.

Strip heat also backs up the compressor when the home needs more heat than the heat pump can deliver. In a packaged unit the strip elements sit just downstream of the indoor coil inside the cabinet, and they are usually staged in more than one step by the control board or by a staging thermostat. Kilowatt sizing, breakers and staging are in Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips.

Condensate from the indoor coil

In cooling, the indoor coil section makes water, and in a packaged unit that water leaves through a drain on the cabinet, outdoors. There is no pan under an attic air handler to overflow into a ceiling. The risks are different: a drain that ices in winter or one that dumps against the slab or foundation. Check that the drain outlet is clear, that it is not blocked by dirt or debris, and that the water leaves the pad area and runs away from the building. Do not assume the same drain is clear because it was clear last season.

Keep outdoor ducts tight and insulated

This is the weak point of the packaged layout. The supply duct carries conditioned air through weather, so heat leaks through the duct wall and air leaks through any open seam. A state energy planning guide we read says ducts work best in insulated spaces and asks installers to pay special attention to insulation and duct sealing. A state university extension page lists duct condition as something to assess, and says performance is best when the duct system is in good condition.

  • Seal every duct seam and the joint between the duct transition and the cabinet. Use materials made for duct sealing and follow the maker's instructions.
  • Wrap the whole outdoor run, with the wrap sealed against rain at every joint and at the wall or roof penetration.
  • Keep the outdoor run short. Shorter runs lose less heat and leak less.
  • Mount the unit above snow level and keep it out of the path of dripping water, as the state planning guide advises for outdoor units in general.

Worked example: heat lost from a short outdoor supply duct (our own example)

This is our own example, not from a maker or a code. It uses a simple heat flow relation, Q = A x dT / R, where Q is heat loss in Btu per hour, A is surface area in square feet, dT is the temperature difference in degrees F, and R is the insulation R-value.

Assumptions (ours): 6 ft of 14 inch round duct; an R-8 wrap; 100 F supply air (an assumed heating-mode value); 20 F outdoors. We use the wrap R-value only and ignore air films, the duct wall and the slightly larger outside diameter of the wrap. We also ignore leakage. Real loss would be a little lower than this from the films and wall, and higher with any leakage.

  1. Diameter in feet: 14 / 12 = 1.167 ft.
  2. Surface area = pi x diameter x length = 3.1416 x 1.167 x 6 = 22.0 square feet.
  3. Temperature difference: 100 - 20 = 80 F.
  4. Q = 22.0 x 80 / 8 = 220 Btu per hour.

That is about 220 Btu per hour for 6 ft. By the same arithmetic, a 20 ft run would lose about 733 Btu per hour (area 73.3 square feet x 80 / 8). A few hundred Btu per hour sounds small, but it comes straight off the delivered heat, and a damaged or wet wrap loses much more. Air leaking out through a bad seam is a separate loss and is usually worse than the wall conduction.

Service checks

  • Filter location. Find where the filter sits (a return grille, a slot in the cabinet or the return duct) and check it. A university extension home-maintenance page we read says to clean or replace filters following the owner's manual, and that some should be replaced as often as once a month. The same page says to clear leaves and debris from around the outdoor condenser.
  • Pad or curb level and support. A cabinet that has settled or tilted can strain the duct joint and send water to the wrong place. See Heat Pump Outdoor Unit Pads, Stands and Wall Brackets.
  • Panel seals. Check that every panel is on, the gaskets are in place and the screws are tight. A missing panel lets rain and outdoor air into the indoor coil section.
  • Crankcase heater. If the unit has one, check the heater as the maker describes. See Crankcase Heaters.
  • Strip heat sequencing. With power safely controlled and the maker's procedure, confirm the elements come on in steps, not all at once, and that the supply air gets warm. Compare current draw with the data plate. Lock out and tag out before any hands-on work.
  • Duct wrap and seams. Walk the outdoor run. Look for torn wrap, open seams and gaps at the cabinet and the wall.

Field card: Packaged Heat Pumps

One cabinet outside, two ducts and a drain to the house.

  • Fits slab, crawl-space and manufactured homes and small buildings with no indoor air handler space.
  • Defrost and strip heat both live in the cabinet; strip heat often runs briefly during defrost.
  • Indoor coil condensate drains outdoors: keep the outlet clear and pointed away from the building.
  • Seal and wrap the outdoor duct run; keep it short.
  • Our own example: 6 ft of 14 inch duct, R-8, 80 F difference loses about 220 Btu per hour.
  • Service: filter, pad level, panel seals, crankcase heater, strip heat steps.
