Geothermal Heat Pump Desuperheaters for Hot Water: Two-Tank Piping, Pump Control and How Much Water They Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A geothermal heat pump already makes hot refrigerant gas every time the compressor runs. A desuperheater catches a slice of that heat and sends it to the water heater. This lesson covers how the part works, how it is piped, what controls the little pump, and what goes wrong in the field.

What a desuperheater is

The Building America Solution Center guide from a national lab describes the desuperheater as a second coaxial (tube-within-a-tube) coil that takes heat from the compressor discharge gas, which is superheated refrigerant vapor. Refrigerant leaves the compressor, passes through the desuperheater, then goes on to the reversing valve. Domestic water flows through the other side of the coil. Only the superheat is taken, so the refrigerant stays a vapor and the heat pump keeps working as it normally does. For the bigger picture, see Geothermal Ground-Source Heat Pump Basics. The same idea on refrigeration racks is in Refrigeration Heat Reclaim.

The small pump and the tank

The coil is fed by a small circulating pump, not by house water pressure. In one maker's installation instructions for a desuperheater pump kit, the pump is not self-priming and runs when the thermostat calls for the compressor. The control board waits about two minutes, then compares the water inlet and outlet temperature sensors. If the difference is too small, it stops the pump and checks again every ten minutes. That wait matters: the discharge gas needs time to get hot, and pumping water through a cold coil only wastes power.

The pump moves water between the coil and a storage tank. Two arrangements are common:

  • Single tank. The kit heats the same tank that serves the house. The maker says the tank must then cover the whole daily need, or be the existing heater on a retrofit, with its thermostat kept below about 125°F.
  • Two tank. The kit heats a preheat tank, and that tank feeds the cold inlet of the regular water heater. The same maker calls this the arrangement with the most savings and says it is required when the regular heater burns fuel. The preheat tank should hold at least half of the household’s daily hot water use, using about 16 gallons per person per day.

A federal building-research case study of a home in a cold climate found the single-tank arrangement worked best there. Climate, tank size and the backup heater all matter, so follow the kit instructions for the job in front of you.

How much hot water it makes

The Department of Energy says that in cooling season the heat taken from the house heats water for free, and that in winter water heating costs fall by about half. The lab guide says a desuperheater can supply 25% to 50% of a home’s domestic hot water and does best in hot climates where the unit runs in cooling most of the time. The reason is simple: the desuperheater only works while the compressor runs. In a mild shoulder month the compressor may hardly run, and so there is little water heating.

A New York state energy agency study of geothermal sites reported desuperheater pumps running as much as 5,000 hours a year at some sites, with about 2 MBtu/h of capacity (that is, about 2,000 Btu/h). It gave an upper bound of about 10 MMBtu per year to the hot water tank, and said that was an estimate, not a measurement.

Worked example: share of annual hot water

Assumptions (ours, not measured): a family of 4 using 16 gallons per person per day (the per-person figure is from the maker’s instructions); 50°F incoming water heated to 120°F, a 70°F rise; water weighs 8.34 lb per gallon and 1 Btu raises 1 lb by 1°F; desuperheater output of 2,000 Btu/h (the figure from the state study); and an assumed 1,500 compressor hours per year.

  1. Daily use: 4 × 16 = 64 gallons.
  2. Daily heat needed: 64 × 8.34 × 70 = about 37,400 Btu.
  3. Yearly heat needed: 37,400 × 365 = about 13.6 MMBtu.
  4. Heat from the desuperheater: 2,000 Btu/h × 1,500 h = 3,000,000 Btu, or 3.0 MMBtu.
  5. Share: 3.0 ÷ 13.6 = about 22%.

That lands below the 25% to 50% range, which makes sense with a modest run time. At 5,000 hours the same math gives 10 MMBtu, or about 73%, but that needs the tank to accept the heat all the time, so treat it as a ceiling. Change the assumptions and the answer moves; this is a method, not a promise.

Temperature limits and the high-limit switch

The maker’s instructions describe an over-temperature switch in the line-voltage circuit that cuts power to the pump if the inlet water gets too hot, around 125°F, to prevent scalding. Keep that in mind with stored water: see Water Heater Temperature Setting for scald and Legionella tradeoffs, and never alter or plug the tank’s pressure relief valve.

When the pump should be off

  • No compressor run. The pump follows the compressor call in the maker’s kit. With no hot gas there is nothing to collect, and a running pump can pull heat out of the tank.
  • Tank already hot or discharge gas not hot enough. The lab guide says sensors on the water outlet and on the hot gas line shut the pump off in those cases, and some units use a diverter valve to bypass the coil.
  • While you check refrigerant pressures. The maker says to turn the kit off first, or the readings may look like a charge problem.

Scale and service problems

  • Scale. Hard water leaves deposits on the coil. The maker says a scaled coil can be cleaned with food-grade phosphoric acid, following the cleaner maker’s directions. A strainer in the line catches sediment.
  • Air-locked pump. The pump is not self-priming. The maker says to open a hot faucet to bleed air, then use the purge port once the system is full. A pump that hums but moves no water, with a small temperature difference, is the usual sign. This is also why the pump should never run dry.
  • Backwards piping (Our own reasoning). If inlet and outlet are swapped, the board compares the wrong sensors and the coil sees warm water on the wrong end. Check flow arrows, labels and temperatures after any repipe.
  • Pump running with the compressor off (Our own reasoning). Since the pump should follow the compressor call, look at wiring, the control relay and the thermostat terminal it was tied to.
  • Freezing. The maker says to install the kit indoors in conditioned space.

Field card: Geothermal Desuperheaters

No compressor, no hot gas, no pump.

  • Coil takes superheat from the discharge gas; a small pump moves tank water through it.
  • Two-tank: preheat tank feeds the regular heater; maker says most savings and required with fuel-fired heaters.
  • Source ranges: 25% to 50% of hot water (lab guide); free in summer, about half in winter (DOE).
  • High limit near 125°F cuts the pump; pump is not self-priming, so purge air.
  • Our own example: 3.0 of 13.6 MMBtu a year, about 22% with assumed run time.
  • Cleaning scale: follow the cleaner maker’s directions.
