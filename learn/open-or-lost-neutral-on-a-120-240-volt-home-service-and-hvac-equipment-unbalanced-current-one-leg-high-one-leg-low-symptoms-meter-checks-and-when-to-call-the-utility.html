Open or Lost Neutral on a Home Service and HVAC Equipment

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A furnace board that faults for no reason, lights that surge when the AC starts, a burned control board in a house that "has always had power." Sometimes the cause is not the equipment at all. It is a loose or broken neutral on the home's 120/240 V service. This lesson explains what the neutral does, what happens when it opens, how to measure it, and why the tech stops and calls the owner to call the utility.

Safety first

This page is for recognizing the problem, not for repairing the service. The service drop and the meter side belong to the utility. A tech who suspects a lost neutral measures what is safe to measure, stops, and tells the owner to call the utility. Treat every conductor as live, including the neutral and any bare metal that should be at ground potential. Federal workplace safety rules for electrical work require de-energizing and testing before hands go in, and allow only qualified people to work on energized parts (29 CFR 1910.333). Follow your employer's procedures and the National Electrical Code (NEC) and local rules where you work.

What the neutral does

A typical home service has three conductors from the utility: two hot legs and a neutral. A university extension bulletin describes the 120/240 V system this way: each hot wire is 120 V to the neutral, and 240 V between the two hot wires. Loads that need 120 V connect from a hot leg to the neutral; loads that need 240 V connect across both hot legs. A state university tech note adds that the neutral carries current just like the hot conductors and is grounded to the earth at the service panel.

The key idea is that the neutral carries only the difference between the two legs (our own explanation). If leg A feeds 20 A of 120 V loads and leg B feeds 15 A, the neutral carries about 5 A. If the legs are perfectly matched, the neutral carries nothing. A 240 V compressor, strip heater or condenser motor sits across both legs and puts no current on the neutral at all. The neutral is the reference that holds each leg at about 120 V no matter how the 120 V loads are split.

Why an open neutral makes one leg high and one low

When the neutral opens upstream of the panel, from a loose lug, a corroded splice or a broken conductor, the reference is gone. Every 120 V load on leg A is now in series with the 120 V loads on leg B, with the full 240 V across the pair. A federal measurement-laboratory paper on surge protection puts it this way: when the neutral connection is lost, the voltage at the midpoint is set by the ratio of the impedances on the two sides, and the lightly loaded side can see an overvoltage that approaches twice normal. The same paper notes that causes range from a transient loose connection to a permanent break or corrosion, and that the resulting overvoltage current will not be interrupted by overcurrent protection.

That last point matters. A breaker sees normal or even low current, so nothing trips while a lightly loaded leg climbs toward 240 V.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed values. Assume the neutral is fully open and the only loads running are a 60 ohm resistive load on leg A and a 20 ohm resistive load on leg B. Real loads are not pure resistances and they change as they heat, so this shows the idea, not an exact prediction.

  1. The two loads are now one series loop across 240 V. Total resistance: 60 + 20 = 80 ohms.
  2. Current: 240 V / 80 ohms = 3 A, the same through both loads.
  3. Voltage across the 60 ohm load: 3 A x 60 ohms = 180 V.
  4. Voltage across the 20 ohm load: 3 A x 20 ohms = 60 V.
  5. Check: 180 V + 60 V = 240 V.

With a healthy neutral each load would see 120 V. The load that draws less current (higher resistance) takes the larger voltage. The 60 ohm load, designed for 120 V, now sees 180 V, which is 50 percent too high (180 / 120 = 1.5). Its power would rise from 120 V squared over 60 ohms, which is 240 W, to 180 V squared over 60 ohms, which is 540 W. The 20 ohm load drops to 60 V, half of normal, and its power falls from 720 W to 180 W. Electronics on the high leg are stressed; motors and heaters on the low leg underperform.

Symptoms you may see

  • Lights that brighten and dim when the AC, blower or another 120 V load starts or stops. Starting a load changes the split, so the leg voltages swing. The federal consumer product safety agency lists dimming or flickering lights, buzzing or sizzling sounds, a smell of overheated plastic and hot switch plates or outlets among warning signs that call for a licensed electrician.
  • Odd furnace or air-handler board faults: random lockouts, relays that chatter, a board that resets, or a fault code that clears and returns. Low voltage on a leg can drop out relays; high voltage can damage the board.
  • Burned electronics: a failed control board, ECM motor control, thermostat power supply or surge device. High-leg overvoltage is a common suspect.

These symptoms also fit other faults, such as loose wiring inside the house or a failing component, so confirm with a meter before drawing a conclusion. The surge and brownout lessons linked below cover those causes.

Measuring it

Use a properly rated meter, correct leads and the safe test-before-touch habit. Measure only where you can do so safely, such as the equipment's line side or a receptacle; do not open the meter or touch service conductors.

  1. Leg A to neutral and leg B to neutral. A healthy service reads close to 120 V on each, and the two readings are close to each other. Our own rule of thumb: a large split, such as one leg well above 120 V and the other well below, is a red flag.
  2. Leg A to leg B. With an open neutral this usually still reads near 240 V. That is the clue: the sum of the two line-to-neutral readings stays near the leg-to-leg reading while their split changes.
  3. Repeat while a large load starts. A reading that moves a lot when the load changes points to a poor neutral connection.

Do not "fix" the odd readings by moving wires in the panel. The panel interior is a qualified-person job, and the lugs on the supply side of the main are not yours to touch. A neutral problem that shows on every circuit at once points toward the service. A problem on one circuit points to that branch circuit or its panel termination. Either way, report it to the owner, who should call a licensed electrician for the house side and the utility for the service drop and meter side.

Who owns what

The utility owns and maintains the service drop and the meter side. A bulletin from a state university extension notes that power suppliers usually have their own rules for the service installation. The homeowner's electrician handles the panel and everything after it. When the tech suspects a lost neutral at the service, the right move is to stop work on the equipment, explain the readings to the owner in plain words, and tell them to call the utility. If there are burning smells, sparks, or hot panel parts, the owner should leave the area and call emergency services as well.

Shared neutrals in branch circuits (general terms)

Inside the house, a multiwire branch circuit uses two hot conductors on opposite legs that share one neutral. That neutral also carries only the difference between the two legs, so it works the same way as the service neutral. If it opens inside the circuit, the loads on the two hot conductors end up in series across 240 V, with the same high-and-low split as in the example. For that reason, work on these circuits calls for a qualified person who follows the NEC and local rules. Do not splice, swap or "borrow" a neutral between circuits.

Field card: Open or lost neutral

The neutral holds each leg near 120 V. Lose it and the lightly loaded leg climbs.

  • Neutral carries only the difference between the legs.
  • Open neutral puts 120 V loads in series across 240 V.
  • Our example: 60 ohm and 20 ohm in series, 3 A, 180 V and 60 V.
  • Check leg to neutral on both legs, then leg to leg.
  • A big split with leg to leg still near 240 V is a red flag.
  • Breakers may not trip.
  • Stop. The owner calls the utility for the drop and meter side.
