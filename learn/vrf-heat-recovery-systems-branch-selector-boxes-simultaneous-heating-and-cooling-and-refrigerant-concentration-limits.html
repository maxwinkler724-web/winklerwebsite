VRF Heat Recovery: Branch Selector Boxes, Simultaneous Heating and Cooling, and Room Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a heat recovery VRF building, one office can be cooling while the room next door is heating, all on one outdoor unit. The part that makes that possible is the branch selector box. This lesson explains what the box does, how heat moves between zones, and why the smallest occupied room decides whether the refrigerant charge is acceptable.

Check the maker and the code

Check the maker and the code. Piping layouts, operating ranges, timers and safety features differ by maker and model, and the refrigerant safety rules differ by code edition and local adoption. This page was built from public maker pages and government reports read in 2026. It is general education. The installation manual and the code in force on the job decide.

Heat pump VRF versus heat recovery VRF

In a heat pump VRF system, every indoor unit runs in the same mode at a given time. ACHR News describes it this way: units that want the other mode wait in standby, and the mode is set by a manual switch, a master zone, an outside input or automatic changeover. A heat recovery system lets each indoor unit choose its own mode, so energy leaving a cooling zone can go to a heating zone. The same article says this suits buildings with mixed loads, such as sunny perimeter rooms and an interior core, and that heat recovery projects often cost 30 to 40 percent more than similar heat pump systems. It also warns that savings depend on climate and use, and that a space with cooling load all year, like a server room, gains little. Our VRF and VRV Basics lesson covers the common parts first.

Two-pipe and three-pipe designs

The difference is how many refrigerant lines run from the outdoor unit to the branch selector box. A three-pipe system carries liquid, high-pressure gas and low-pressure gas. Fujitsu General's training page on its VR-II heat recovery cycle describes three lines of this kind running from the branch unit to each indoor unit, with internal three-way valves sending each zone to the hot gas or the suction side. A two-pipe system, as ACHR News describes it, needs a controller box with liquid and gas separators, a tube-in-tube heat exchanger and several flow control valves. Two-pipe designs use less piping, but long runs to the box can eat that advantage, and ACHR News adds that using more than one box still brings in a third pipe. The NREL report on VRF modeling says the two layouts differ in pipes between the outdoor unit and the branch controller, and that three-pipe systems can give better low-temperature heating because of lower line losses. Your maker's manual tells you which layout you have.

What the branch selector box does

Think of the box as a traffic director. Each port to an indoor unit has valves. A zone that wants cooling is connected to the low-pressure side, so its coil works as an evaporator and absorbs heat from the room. A zone that wants heating is connected to the hot gas side, so its coil works as a condenser and gives heat to the room. Lennox's submittal sheet for a 2-port box lists solenoid valves that route refrigerant by mode, connections for liquid, gas and discharge gas on the outdoor side, and a pressure equalization valve to keep refrigerant noise down during mode changes. The same sheet lists a limit of 54,000 Btu/h per branch and 108,000 Btu/h in total for that model; other models differ. ACHR News notes that one controller can hold up to 16 circuits and should be placed where service access is good and valve noise will not bother people.

How rejected heat feeds the heating zones

Fujitsu General's page describes the effect plainly: indoor units in heating act as condensers, units in cooling act as evaporators, and the branch unit moves refrigerant to where it is needed, so heat taken from cooling zones is reused in heating zones. The NREL report says the same thing in system terms: the system recovers heat from the cooling zones for the heating zones, and the outdoor unit handles the rest of the load. Fujitsu's page lists three operating states. In simultaneous operation the zones trade heat. In a mainly cooling state, compressor speed follows the cooling demand. In a mainly heating state, compressor capacity follows the heating load. The box does not make heat. It chooses which loop each room joins.

Mode changeover limits, oil return and defrost

Simultaneous operation only works inside an outdoor temperature window. ACHR News (2012) says the outdoor temperature range for simultaneous operation varies by maker and can be narrow, so check the maker data; outside it the system runs fully heating or fully cooling. That article dates from 2012, so use your maker's current data. Two housekeeping routines also matter to a tech who sees odd behavior. Oil return: Fujitsu General's technical page describes timed routines that push oil back from the indoor units and piping to the outdoor unit, and the indoor LEDs flash slowly while it runs. In that model, cooling oil recovery follows more than 3 hours of accumulated compressor run time, and heating oil recovery follows more than 8 hours. Defrost: on the same page, indoor expansion valves open and indoor and outdoor fans stop during defrost while compressors keep running, and defrost ends at a set liquid temperature or after 10 minutes (15 minutes at lower connected capacity). Those timers belong to one maker's system; yours will differ. For line sizing and oil return in long runs, see Refrigerant Line Set Sizing: Suction Velocity, Oil Return and Long Runs.

Why the smallest occupied room matters

A VRF system puts a large amount of refrigerant into a building through many rooms, and a single piped system shares charge between them. If a line or coil leaks in one small room, the whole system's releasable charge can end up in that room. So the check is done room by room, not building by building. ASHRAE Standard 34 classifies refrigerants and sets their concentration limits, and ASHRAE Standard 15 uses those limits for the design and installation of systems. Standard 15 treats VRF as a high-probability system. We do not reproduce either standard; read them through your local code.

A Fairfax County, Virginia government guidance sheet on VRF gives a plain method based on the Virginia Mechanical Code: find the total system charge in pounds, find the volume in cubic feet of each enclosed occupied space served, divide, and compare each answer with the limit. The sheet gives 26 pounds per 1,000 cubic feet for R-410A, reduced by half for institutional occupancies. It says the volume counts only space the refrigerant can reach through permanent openings, so door undercuts, louvers and transfer grilles do not add volume, and it says ventilation cannot be used to raise the limit under the standard it cites. Lennox's engineering blog gives roughly 25 to 26 for R-410A and roughly 4 to 5 for A2L refrigerants such as R-32 and R-454B in the same units. A2L systems use a related calculation; see A2L Charge Limits and Room Size.

Worked example (Our own, assumed inputs)

Assumptions, all Our own: a system with 60 lb of total R-410A charge, serving a small storage-office that is 10 ft by 12 ft with a 9 ft ceiling, and an open-plan floor that is 20 ft by 30 ft with a 9 ft ceiling. The 26 lb per 1,000 cubic ft limit comes from the Fairfax County sheet above and is a stand-in: look up the limit in the code edition and refrigerant for your job.

  1. Small room volume: 10 x 12 x 9 = 1,080 cubic ft.
  2. Concentration: 60 lb / 1,080 cubic ft = 0.0556 lb per cubic ft, or 55.6 lb per 1,000 cubic ft.
  3. Compare: 55.6 is more than 26, so this room fails the check as drawn.
  4. Open floor volume: 20 x 30 x 9 = 5,400 cubic ft. 60 / 5,400 = 0.0111, or 11.1 lb per 1,000 cubic ft. That passes.
  5. Smallest room that would pass at 60 lb: 60 / 26 x 1,000 = about 2,308 cubic ft.

The tiny room is the problem. The designer must reduce the charge on that circuit, connect the room to a bigger space with a permanent opening, serve it by a different system, or add engineered protection that the code and the maker allow. This is also why some jobs use shutoff valves inside the branch box.

Leak detection in small rooms

Some makers build detection and shutoff into the system. The Lennox submittal sheet lists refrigerant detection sensors, a shutoff valve on each outlet port, an output for an exhaust fan and capacitor-backed power to close the valves in an outage. A Trane white paper on A2L systems describes detection in 30 seconds and shutoff valves closing within 15 seconds, citing the A2L equipment standards. The Fairfax sheet describes detection with a visible and audible alarm and backup power as an alternative engineered system needing a code modification request. Whatever your system uses, do not bypass or park a detector or a valve. See A2L Leak Detection Sensors.

Service checks for a tech

  • Confirm which mode each zone is calling for and which the box has set before you condemn a coil.
  • Check that the system is not outside its simultaneous operation temperature window.
  • Look for a routine (oil recovery or defrost) before you call a weak zone a fault.
  • Read the box as a unit with valves, strainers and sensors: leak-check the box connections, not only the lines.
  • Record added charge. If charge changes, the room-by-room check changes too.
  • If a room has been shrunk by a new partition, the check must be done again.

The NREL report adds that VRF charge faults are more common because of the long piping, and the makers it lists use pressure tests of 150 to 600 psi, so follow your own maker's test procedure.

Field card: VRF Heat Recovery

The box picks the mode; the smallest room sets the limit.

  • Heat pump VRF: one mode at a time. Heat recovery VRF: each zone picks its mode.
  • Two-pipe and three-pipe designs differ in lines to the branch box.
  • Cooling zones act as evaporators; heating zones act as condensers.
  • Oil recovery and defrost are normal routines, and timers differ by maker.
  • Concentration check: total charge (lb) divided by room volume (cubic ft) times 1,000.
  • Only permanent openings add room volume.
  • Never bypass a detector or shutoff valve. Look up the limit for your code and refrigerant.
