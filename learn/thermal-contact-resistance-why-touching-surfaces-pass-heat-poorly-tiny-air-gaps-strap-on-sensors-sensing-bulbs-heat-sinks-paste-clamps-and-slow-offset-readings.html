Thermal Contact Resistance: Why Sensors and Heat Sinks Read Wrong

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Press a sensor against a pipe and it feels like they touch. To heat, they mostly do not. Two real surfaces meet at a scatter of tiny high points with air in between, and that air is a poor conductor. The result is a temperature drop right at the joint, and it shows up in the field as a reading that is slow and sits off from the true value.

Why touching surfaces pass heat poorly

No surface is perfectly flat. Under magnification a pipe wall, a sensor housing or a heat sink base is a landscape of peaks. When two of them are pressed together, they touch only at the peaks. A federal space-agency tech brief on joint heat flow says the true contact area may be only about 0.1 percent of the apparent contact area, and that the air gaps add significant resistance to heat flow. A national laboratory paper on interface materials puts the real contact area at 2 to 6 orders of magnitude below the apparent area. Heat has to squeeze through those few contact spots (engineers call this constriction) or cross the air-filled gaps.

That extra opposition is called thermal contact resistance. It behaves like one more layer in the heat path, in series with the pipe wall, the sensor housing and everything else. Our related lesson on thermal conductivity of HVAC materials shows why this matters: copper and steel walls are such good conductors that the thin joint can end up being the biggest resistance in the whole path.

What changes the size of the resistance

  • Contact pressure. The national laboratory work models bare-contact resistance as falling with pressure, which makes sense: more force flattens more peaks and adds contact spots.
  • Gap filler. Both the national laboratory paper and a space-agency laboratory report describe thermal interface materials as working by filling the gaps between the contact points. Thermal grease is named as a common type in the national laboratory papers.
  • Roughening is not a fix. The space-agency brief reports that a deliberately roughened interface showed no significant improvement in heat conduction.
  • Dirt, oxide and paint. These are our field observations rather than numbers from the sources: a scale, corrosion or paint film is one more layer in the path, and it keeps the metals from touching at all.

Where it bites in the field

Strap-on pipe sensors and clamp-on thermometers

A strap-on sensor reads the pipe wall, not the fluid inside it, and only if heat moves freely between the wall and the sensor body. A loose strap, a sensor sitting on a corroded or painted spot, or a clamp that touches along only a thin line leaves a gap. The sensor then reads between the pipe and the room air around it. On a hot pipe it reads low; on a cold pipe it reads high. See temperature sensor mounting for the full set of mounting choices.

Expansion valve and thermostat sensing bulbs

A sensing bulb strapped to a suction line works the same way: the bulb has to follow the line temperature, and a bulb that touches along a short strip or sits loosely in its clamp follows it poorly. Our field practice is to clean the line to bare metal, seat the bulb fully along the tube, and clamp it firmly. Insulating over the bulb and line afterward keeps room air from pulling the bulb away from the pipe temperature. Follow the maker's instructions for bulb location. Our lesson on TXV bulb placement covers where on the line it goes.

Heat sinks on drives and solid-state relays

A power part dumps its losses into a sink only as well as the joint allows. A dry, uneven or dusty joint raises the part's temperature for the same load, which can end in an overtemperature trip. A thin, even layer of thermal compound fills the gaps, which is the same mechanism the national laboratory and space-agency papers describe. See heat sinks for variable frequency drives.

Compressor and pipe heaters

A heater band or strap-on heater passes its heat into the shell or pipe through the same kind of joint. A loose band keeps the heater element hotter than it should be while the shell warms slowly. Check that bands are snug and seated on clean metal; see crankcase heaters for testing.

How a poor contact shows up

Thermal contact resistance adds a steady offset and also slows the response. Part of the offset appears as soon as heat flows through the joint. The slowness is explained in sensor time constant: a bigger resistance between the pipe and the sensor means the sensor takes longer to catch up. The telltale signs are:

  • The reading creeps toward its final value long after the pipe has stopped changing.
  • It sits well away from a good reference reading taken at the same spot.
  • Pressing on the sensor or tightening the strap makes the reading jump toward the right value.
  • The reading follows the room more than the pipe, especially on uninsulated pipe.

The fixes

  1. Clean metal. Remove scale, paint, oxide and old compound at the contact spot.
  2. Tight clamps. Use the maker's clamp or strap, and seat the sensor along its full length. Higher pressure flattens more peaks.
  3. Thermal compound. A thin, even layer displaces air from the gaps. More is not better: a thick layer becomes a resistance of its own. Follow the maker's instructions for any sensor or part that calls for it.
  4. Insulate over the sensor. Wrap insulation over both sensor and pipe so room air does not pull the sensor reading away from the pipe.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all ours and chosen for round numbers: a contact area of 1 square inch; a heat flow of 10 watts through the joint; a bare dry joint with a contact resistance of 0.5 F per watt per square inch; the same joint with a thin layer of compound at 0.05 F per watt per square inch. These are not measured values.

The drop across a contact is the resistance per unit area times the heat flow, divided by the area:

  • Bare joint: 0.5 F·in²/W × 10 W ÷ 1 in² = 5 F drop.
  • With compound: 0.05 F·in²/W × 10 W ÷ 1 in² = 0.5 F drop.

Compound cuts the drop by a factor of 10 under these assumptions. For a heat sink, 5 F of joint drop means the part runs 5 F hotter than it would with a perfect joint. For a sensor, the joint drop is an offset error only if heat is actually flowing through it; the more heat leaks out from the sensor to the room, the bigger the error. A well-insulated sensor with a good contact has little leakage, so the sensor reads close to the pipe.

Field card: Thermal contact resistance

Surfaces touch at peaks, and air fills the rest.

  • Real contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent area.
  • A loose or dirty sensor reads between the pipe and the room.
  • Clean to bare metal, clamp firmly, add compound where the maker calls for it.
  • Insulate over the sensor and pipe.
  • Slow, offset readings that improve when you press the sensor point to a bad contact.
