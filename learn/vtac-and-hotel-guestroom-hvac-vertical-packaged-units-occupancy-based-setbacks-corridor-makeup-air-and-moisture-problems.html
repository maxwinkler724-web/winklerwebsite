VTAC and Hotel Guest Room HVAC: Closet Units, Setbacks, Makeup Air and Moisture

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many newer hotel rooms have no box under the window. The equipment is in a closet, with a duct to the ceiling and a louver in the outside wall. That is a VTAC, a vertical terminal air conditioner or heat pump. This lesson covers how it is built, how the room gets fresh air and stays out of negative pressure, how guest room energy controls save money, and why some rooms smell musty. For the under-the-window version, see PTAC Units: How They Work, Cleaning and Common Problems; we do not repeat that lesson here.

VTAC versus PTAC

A PTAC is a wall sleeve and chassis, about 42 inches wide, under a window. A trade article sponsored by an equipment maker describes a VTAC as the same idea stood on end: a packaged unit in an enclosure, which the article says can sit in a closet and serve one room or several adjoining rooms. It has top air discharge ducted to the room, and the article says the unit is also called a single packaged vertical unit (SPVU). Because nothing sits under the window, curtains can hang to the floor.

The refrigeration cycle is the same as in a PTAC. What changes is the air path and where you work: filter, coil and drain are in the closet.

What a maker manual says about the install

Friedrich's Vert-I-Pak installation and operation manual (a maker example, not a recommendation) shows how much of the job is building details:

  • Wall plenum and louver. A wall plenum goes through the exterior wall, sized by wall thickness (one for walls 4 to 8 inches, another for 8 to 14 inches). A louver is sold separately. The manual says to seal flanges and unused holes with silicone and to flash the opening, and to let the flashing cure before the chassis goes in. If you use a plenum or louver from another maker, the manual says Friedrich must evaluate it to confirm pressure drop stays within limits.
  • Ducted supply, free return. The manual says never to run the unit without a supply duct attached. It lists a maximum external static pressure of 0.3 inch w.c. for the smaller models and 0.4 for the larger, and says higher static causes poor airflow and early component failure. Return air is not ducted; a separate return grille needs at least 250 square inches of free area.
  • Drain. Pitch the drain line one-quarter inch per foot, plumb it to the building drain, and use a trap. The manual warns that local code may require a secondary pan or trap, and that a blocked drain can send water out through the plenum and louver.

Outdoor air: the louver is not the ventilation system

The wall louver mostly feeds the condenser side of the unit. In the Friedrich manual, ventilation air is an option: a FreshAire door passes up to 60 CFM of outdoor air, and the slide control on the chassis opens or closes it. A closed door on a closet unit does not guarantee a room has code ventilation. Rates are set by the ventilation standard, as covered in ventilation rates and balancing, and by the mechanical design of the building.

Many buildings handle that separately with a corridor makeup air unit. In the approach Building Science Corporation describes, conditioned makeup air is delivered to the corridor and reaches rooms through undercut doors, while bathroom exhaust fans pull the air out. Its article notes that undercut doors as a makeup path are "not legal everywhere," so confirm the local code. A central design that does the job well is a dedicated outdoor air system, which treats the humidity of the outdoor air before it reaches rooms.

Guest room energy management

Guest rooms are empty a lot. ENERGY STAR's lodging guidance says studies show rooms are unoccupied about 12 hours a day on average, yet many hotels condition them anyway. It describes linking the room controls to the reservation system so an unsold room stays ventilated with minimal heating or cooling, and preconditioning a sold room about an hour before arrival. It says an energy management system can cut energy costs 35 to 45 percent; that is a whole-system claim, not a number for HVAC alone.

A U.S. Department of Energy technology demonstration run with Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) is more specific. It tested occupancy-based guest room controls built from an occupancy sensor (passive infrared or ultrasonic) plus a wireless door switch, and found average HVAC savings of 18.4 percent across its sites, with a range of 10.8 to 26.5 percent. It estimated 167 to 589 kWh saved per room per year. It also listed what goes wrong: sensors that cannot see most of the room, broken or missing sensors and door switches, and gaps around through-wall sleeves that let outside air in. A PNNL-coauthored study of three hotels modeled setbacks of 64°F heating and 78°F cooling in unoccupied rooms and quoted earlier studies saying occupancy-based controls can save 10 to 30 percent of HVAC energy.

On codes, we name them and send you to the adopted edition. The DOE report says ASHRAE 90.1-2010 and the 2012 IECC both have off-hour control requirements, with exceptions for small zones (under 15,000 Btu/h for 90.1-2010, under 6,800 Btu/h for the IECC), and that California's 2013 Title 24 proposal asked for captive key card, occupancy or automatic controls with 5°F setbacks. Later editions changed, so check the adopted edition.

Worked example: what a setback might save (our own estimate)

These are assumptions, not measurements. Assume one room whose HVAC uses 2,400 kWh a year with no controls. Assume it is unoccupied 12 hours a day (the ENERGY STAR average), and that setting back to the 78°F cooling and 64°F heating limits cuts the unit's energy by 40 percent during those hours. The 40 percent is our guess.

  1. Share of hours affected: 12 ÷ 24 = 0.50.
  2. Savings share: 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.20, or 20 percent.
  3. Energy: 2,400 × 0.20 = 480 kWh per room per year.
  4. Cost, at an assumed $0.15 per kWh: 480 × 0.15 = $72 per room per year, or $7,200 for 100 rooms.

Cross-check: 20 percent sits inside the DOE demonstration's 10.8 to 26.5 percent range and close to its 18.4 percent average, and 480 kWh falls inside its 167 to 589 kWh range. At the DOE average, the same room would save 2,400 × 0.184 = 441.6 kWh. A real hotel with sensors that miss movement would land lower. Recovery time also matters: a deep setback in humid weather lets moisture build, which leads to the next section.

Why rooms get musty

Negative pressure. Building Science Corporation's hotel article says that when rooftop exhaust fans pull air from bathrooms without ducted makeup air, the building goes negative "pretty much all the time." It says to supply 10 to 15 percent more makeup air than exhaust in each room. Through-wall PTAC and PTHP units supply some air, but the article says they run rarely and do not reliably balance the exhaust fans. We found no source that says the same for VTACs, so do not assume a VTAC closes that gap.

Humid infiltration. A negative building pulls outdoor air through walls. The article says hot, humid air can enter a wall cooled on both sides by room air, and that mold shows up under vinyl wall coverings on interior demising walls. It lists marine climates and Climate Zone 4A as problem areas; New Jersey falls in 4A.

Oversized units. An oversized unit cools quickly and shuts off. ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner fact sheet says an oversized unit will cool the room but remove only some of the humidity, leaving a damp, clammy feeling. Read sensible heat ratio and latent load for the mechanics.

What EPA says to aim for. EPA's brief mold guide recommends indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and says to keep air conditioner drip pans clean and drain lines clear. Our own reading: a guest room with a closed door, a setback and a negative bathroom exhaust is exactly where a humidity reading should be checked, not guessed. More on the coil side is in mold on the AC coil or in ducts.

Filter, drain and coil service

The Friedrich manual gives no fixed intervals. It says to replace the filter when it gets dirty, never to run without one, to vacuum the indoor coil gently with a brush attachment without bending fins, to keep outdoor air intake and exhaust free of debris, snow and ice, and to check the condensate drain periodically. ENERGY STAR's lodging page suggests checking filters monthly in heavy-use seasons and replacing at least every three months. Since the unit lives in a closet, add a secondary pan and a float switch when code or the manual calls for it; see secondary drain pans and condensate float switches.

Field card: VTAC and Hotel Guest Room HVAC

Closet unit, ducted supply, free return, clear drain.

  • VTAC = vertical packaged unit in a closet, top discharge ducted to the room; PTAC is the under-window box.
  • The wall louver mainly serves the condenser. Ventilation air is an option or comes from a corridor or central system.
  • Never run a VTAC without its supply duct. Follow the maker's static pressure limit and free return area.
  • Drain: pitch, trap, keep clear; check code for a secondary pan.
  • DOE demo: occupancy controls averaged 18.4 percent HVAC savings (10.8 to 26.5 percent range).
  • Setback only saves if sensors and door switches work.
  • Musty rooms: negative pressure, humid infiltration, oversized unit, dirty drain pan. Aim for under 60 percent RH (EPA).
