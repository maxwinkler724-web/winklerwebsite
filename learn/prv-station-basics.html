PRV Station Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Boilers often make steam at a higher pressure than the equipment it serves can handle. A pressure reducing valve station steps the pressure down. This lesson explains the parts and why protection downstream matters.

Why reduce pressure

Higher-pressure steam is efficient to produce and distribute, but much of the equipment it serves, such as heating coils, kitchen equipment, or process vessels, is designed for lower pressure. A pressure reducing valve, usually shortened to PRV, sits between the two and delivers steam at a lower pressure to the downstream system.

What a typical station includes

A PRV station is more than one valve. Though layouts vary, you will often see:

  • Isolation valves before and after, so the station can be taken out of service.
  • A strainer ahead of the PRV to catch scale and debris that could damage the valve seat.
  • A steam trap or drain point ahead of the PRV to remove condensate, since wet steam is rough on valves.
  • The pressure reducing valve itself, which may be self-acting or pilot-operated.
  • Pressure gauges before and after, so staff can see what the station is doing.
  • A relief valve downstream, to protect the lower-pressure system if the PRV fails.
  • A bypass in some designs, with its own valve, which must be managed carefully.

Why relief protection is critical

A PRV is a mechanical device that can wear out, stick or leak. If the seat gets damaged or debris holds it open, high-pressure steam may pass through to piping and equipment that were never built for it. A properly sized and installed relief valve on the low-pressure side is the safety net. It opens to release steam before pressure rises to dangerous levels.

This is why safety valve and relief valve rules exist. Relief devices should be selected, installed, tested and maintained according to the applicable codes and the manufacturer's instructions. They are never to be plugged, gagged, adjusted or bypassed by anyone who is not authorized. The discharge must also be piped to a safe location, since a relieving valve releases very hot steam.

How a PRV behaves

A PRV does not hold downstream pressure perfectly under all conditions. When the demand changes a lot, the valve may struggle. If a valve is badly oversized for light loads, it can hunt, chatter and wear quickly. Some stations use two valves of different sizes to handle both small and large loads. Selection and sizing are engineering tasks that use manufacturer data. One manufacturer’s guide recommends two pressure-reducing stages when supply and downstream pressures differ by more than 10 to 1, or when the total drop exceeds 100 psig, keeping each valve’s drop to about 100 psig because larger drops shorten plug and seat life. It also asks that the load fall between 50 and 100 percent of the valve’s capacity, since an oversized valve hunts and wears.

Signs of trouble

  • Downstream pressure creeping above where it should be.
  • Relief valve discharging or weeping.
  • Noise, vibration or chatter at the valve.
  • Pressure that swings with each change in load.
  • Leaks around the stem, flanges or body.

Safety first

Steam stations are hot, loud and unforgiving. Do not adjust or service a PRV or relief valve unless you are trained, authorized and have followed lockout and isolation procedures for your site. Release of pressure and hot condensate can cause severe burns. The U.S. Department of Energy discusses steam pressure reduction in its steam system resources for those who want the energy side of the story. For what you actually do on the job, your employer's procedures and the manufacturer's literature come first.

Reducing pressure is only safe when relief protection is in place.

  • A PRV steps steam pressure down for lower-rated equipment.

  • A downstream relief valve protects against a failed PRV.
