Forced-Air Zoning: Dampers, Bypass and Zone Panels Explained

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A zoned duct system lets one furnace or air handler serve several thermostats. The hard part is not the wiring. It is what happens to airflow when only the smallest zone is calling. This lesson goes through the parts, the two main damper types, the small-zone problem, the temperature limits, and how to check a damper that will not move. For the short overview, see Zoning and Zone Dampers Basics.

What a zoned duct system is

Three pieces make a zoned system. Motorized dampers sit in the ducts. A zone control panel listens to the thermostats and decides which dampers open and what the equipment does. Each zone has its own thermostat. Aprilaire's 6504 installation instructions, for example, describe a panel that supports up to four zones, with one Aprilaire 8570 thermostat per zone. Zonefirst's checklist says to use at least one damper for each zone. Honeywell's TrueZONE HZ322 handles 2 or 3 zones, and EWC Controls' NCM-300 up to three, both with motorized dampers.

In short, when a thermostat calls, the panel positions the dampers and signals the furnace, air conditioner or heat pump.

Two damper types

The damper motor decides how the panel must be wired, so check the type first.

  • Power open, spring close. EWC's UZC4 instructions describe it this way: connect 24 volts AC to the two motor terminals and the damper opens. Remove the power and the spring closes it. This is a two-wire motor, so a lost signal leaves it closed.
  • Spring open, power close. The opposite arrangement, which Honeywell lists for its ARD and ZD dampers. Lose power and these dampers drift open.
  • Power open, power close. The motor drives both directions. Honeywell lists its RRD and MARD dampers this way and Aprilaire's instructions call for 3-wire cable for power open/power close dampers.

The load differs too: Honeywell lists 8 VA for each spring-open ARD or ZD damper and 2 VA for each power-open/power-closed RRD or MARD. Add up the dampers before you pick the transformer.

The small-zone airflow problem

Equipment is built to move a range of air. When zones close, the same blower pushes into fewer ducts. If the one zone left open is small, the airflow can fall below what the equipment needs. The Aprilaire instructions warn that insufficient airflow or excessive temperatures could damage equipment and point you to the equipment maker for minimums. See airflow and static pressure for why pressure rises as airflow falls.

Carrier's zoning design guide states the rule plainly: each zone built should support the minimum airflow of the equipment selected. Its example numbers for its Infinity equipment are at least 275 CFM per ton in high-stage cooling and 175 CFM per ton in low stage. Your equipment will have its own figures, so use its manual. The zonedampers.com design manual says the main goal is constant airflow through the unit.

Ways to deal with it

  • Bypass damper. A duct that carries extra supply air back to the return when zones close. Zonefirst's checklist sizes it as total CFM minus smallest zone CFM, says to install it as far from the air handler as possible but before any zone damper, and recommends a leaving-air sensor when bypassing to the return. A Honeywell bypass sheet uses the same rule and notes that weighted-arm dampers can bypass too much or too little for some zone combinations.
  • Dump zone. An outlet that sends extra air to a non-critical space such as a basement or hallway. The zonedampers.com manual says either a bypass or a dump zone is used but rarely both. Carrier says a dump zone must have a damper.
  • Variable-speed or multistage equipment. Carrier says modulating and multistage equipment give some leeway in minimum airflow. Honeywell's HZ322 panel has a DS/BK terminal that reduces fan speed when one zone calls for cooling, which it says requires less air to be bypassed.
  • Skip the bypass where the maker says so. Carrier says a bypass must not be used with its Infinity zoning. An Energy Vanguard article lists the cost of a bypass: cold air thrown into the return plenum lowers the temperature of the air entering the coil, makes the coil colder and raises freeze risk.

The drawback is the loop: Carrier says a bypass dumps too much conditioned air into the return and causes excessive leaving air temperature. That is why the next section matters.

Supply air limits

Most panels watch the air leaving the equipment with a discharge or supply air sensor, and shut the call off when the air gets too hot or too cold. The limit numbers come from each panel's manual, and they differ.

  • Honeywell HZ322: the high limit adjusts from 110 to 180 degrees F (default 160) and the low limit from 30 to 60 degrees F (default 40).
  • Aprilaire 6504: switches choose the plenum sensor high limit, 140 or 170 degrees, and the low limit, 40 or 45 degrees. The low limit guards against coil freezing and the high limit against overheating.
  • EWC NCM-300: knobs marked 110 to 170 for heating and 34 to 52 for cooling (check the sheet for units). The panel turns heat or cool outputs off for at least 3 minutes when a limit is crossed.

Set the limits from the equipment maker's manual, not just from the panel defaults. A failed sensor is a fault, not a reason to skip the sensor. Honeywell's PURGE light blinks when the sensor has failed.

Worked example: zone CFM against equipment minimum

Hypothetical numbers for practice, not from any real job. A 3-ton system has a design airflow of 1,200 CFM. The smallest zone, a bedroom wing, is designed for 300 CFM. Suppose the manual gives a minimum of 275 CFM per ton (Carrier's published high-stage figure, used here only as an example).

  1. Equipment minimum: 3 tons x 275 CFM per ton = 825 CFM.
  2. Smallest zone as a share of the minimum: 300 / 825 = 0.364, about 36 percent.
  3. Excess air when only that zone calls at the minimum: 825 - 300 = 525 CFM. That is 525 / 825, about 64 percent of the blower's air with nowhere to go.
  4. Bypass sizing by the Zonefirst and Honeywell rule: 1,200 - 300 = 900 CFM. That is 75 percent of design airflow, which says a bypass for this layout would be large.

The Honeywell sheet's sizing table has a model rated to 1,000 CFM, so 900 CFM fits that class. A small zone on a large unit is a design problem. Options from the sources include a bypass or dump zone, or equipment that can slow down, as long as the equipment maker allows them.

Troubleshooting a stuck damper

Start with the panel, then the thermostat, then the motor. The lights mean different things on each panel, so read the maker's manual.

  • Panel power. Zonefirst's MMP2 manual says the first check is for 24 volts AC power to the panel, and lights the green power LED when it is present. Input ranges differ by maker: Honeywell lists 18 to 30 VAC and Aprilaire 20 to 30 VAC for the panel. See Relays, Transformers and 24V Control for how the transformer fits in.
  • Call recognized. Zonefirst puts a green LED by each zone relay that lights on a call. If it stays off, measure 24 VAC between thermostat terminal C and the call terminal (Y, W or G). No voltage means the call is not reaching the panel.
  • Damper energized. EWC's UZC4 lights a ZONE OPEN LED when that zone's dampers are energized open. If the LED is on but the damper does not move, check the damper side.
  • Faults. EWC's panels have a ground fault or breaker indication for a short in the thermostat or damper wiring. Find the short before resetting.
  • The motor. On a power-open/spring-close motor you should see the damper open when 24 volts is applied to its two terminals, and close when removed. Zonefirst's MMP2 motors are a different style: a DC meter should read plus 12 volts at the plug with the damper open and minus 12 volts closed. Power at the plug but no movement points to a bad cord, connection or motor.

Turn the power off before you touch wiring. Do not jumper out a limit or hold a damper open to make a complaint go away.

Smart registers are a different thing

Smart vents are motorized covers on the room registers, not dampers in the duct. Keen Home describes phone-controlled vent covers paired with room sensors. Flair says its vents use pressure sensors and that it never closes more than one third of the vents in a system, which it attributes to a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study. These are the companies' own claims. Closing registers raises duct pressure the way closing dampers does. Read Does Closing Vents in Unused Rooms Save Energy? for the pressure side, and Upstairs Too Hot for a problem zoning is often sold to fix.

Field card: Forced-Air Zoning

Few open dampers, same blower: check airflow first.

  • Parts: motorized dampers, a zone panel, one thermostat per zone.
  • Power open/spring close: 24 V opens it, no power closes it. Power open/power close needs a third wire.
  • Each zone should be able to carry the equipment minimum airflow (Carrier guide).
  • Bypass size = total CFM minus smallest zone CFM; it sends air back through the equipment.
  • Dump zone needs a damper (Carrier). Bypass or dump zone, rarely both.
  • Discharge air sensor limits come from the panel manual: they differ by maker.
  • Stuck damper: panel power, call LED, 24 V at terminals, then the motor.
