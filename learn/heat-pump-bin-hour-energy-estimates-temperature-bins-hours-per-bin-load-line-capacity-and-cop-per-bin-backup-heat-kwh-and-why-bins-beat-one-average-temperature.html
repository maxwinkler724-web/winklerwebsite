Heat Pump Bin-Hour Energy Estimates: Bins, Load Line, COP and Backup kWh

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A heat pump does not run at one outdoor temperature. It runs at a spread of them, and its output and efficiency change with every one. A bin-hour estimate chops the heating season into temperature bins, works out the energy for each bin, and adds them up. This lesson shows the steps and finishes with a five-bin example of our own.

What a temperature bin is

A bin is a band of outdoor temperature, such as 30 to 34 F, treated as one condition. Each bin carries a count of hours, the number of hours per year that the outdoor air sits in that band. Bins are usually 5 F or 10 F wide. A national laboratory field report on cold climate heat pumps grouped its measured data into 10 F bins, and a national laboratory paper on the federal heating rating procedure describes 5 F bins. Narrower bins follow the curves more closely but need more arithmetic. The method is the same either way.

Step 1: hours per bin from climate data

Hours per bin come from a weather file for your location: a typical year of hourly outdoor temperatures that you sort into bins and count. The federal test procedure for central heat pumps does a version of this. Its heating rating uses a set of fractional bin hours for one defined climate region, taken from a rating standard that the federal rule incorporates by reference. That is a rating tool, not a forecast for your town, so for a real house use local data. The sum of all bin hours is the number of hours you chose to count, often the heating season only.

Step 2: house load per bin from a load line

A design load calculation gives one number, the heat loss at the outdoor design temperature. Draw a straight line from that point to the outdoor temperature where the house needs no heating, the no-load temperature. Read the load for any bin off the line. The federal rating procedure uses the same idea: the national laboratory paper describes a building load line that reaches zero at a no-load ambient of 65 F for the standard line, with a steeper alternative line that reaches zero at 55 F. For your own house the no-load temperature is lower than 65 F, because solar gain and internal heat do part of the work. See the heating degree days lesson for where that lower number comes from.

Step 3: heat pump capacity and COP at each bin

Makers publish extended performance data: capacity and input power at several outdoor temperatures, often with a few indoor temperatures and airflows. Pull the capacity and the COP (heat out divided by electrical energy in, both in the same units) for the bins you use. Expect both to fall as it gets colder. A national laboratory report on cold climate heat pump prototypes states that conventional heat pumps lose capacity below about 40 F and deliver only a fraction of rated capacity in the teens. In its field data the median heating COP below 30 F ran from 1.6 to 2.7, and the median in the 0 to 5 F bin was 1.9. Those are results from that project, so use your own unit's data, not those medians.

Step 4: heat pump share and backup heat below the balance point

In each bin compare capacity to load. Where capacity is at least the load, the heat pump covers the bin. We assume it matches the load by cycling or modulating, and the example below ignores cycling losses. The federal procedure does account for them and applies a default degradation coefficient of 0.25 when the cyclic test is not run, according to the national laboratory paper. Where load is above capacity, the heat pump runs flat out and the shortfall comes from backup heat. The outdoor temperature where capacity equals load is the balance point; our balance point lesson covers it. Electric resistance backup has a COP of 1, so each 3,412 Btu of shortfall costs 1 kWh. The same paper says that below the highest-speed balance point, resistance heat supplies the shortfall, which pulls the season's net efficiency down. For how strips are staged and sized, see the backup heat strips lesson.

Step 5: add up the kWh

For each bin:

  • Heat pump heat = capacity-or-load (whichever is smaller) x hours.
  • Heat pump kWh = that heat divided by 3,412 Btu per kWh, then divided by COP.
  • Backup kWh = (load minus capacity, if positive) x hours, divided by 3,412.

Sum each column over all bins. The seasonal COP for the bins you counted is total heat delivered divided by total kWh converted to the same units.

Sample arithmetic for the 32 F bin: the load of 16,800 Btu/h is under the 25,000 Btu/h capacity, so the heat pump covers it. 16,800 x 900 = 15,120,000 Btu. Divide by 3,412 to get 4,431.4 kWh of heat, then by the COP of 2.7 to get 1,641.3 kWh.

For the 22 F bin the load is 22,800 and capacity is 20,000, so the shortfall is 2,800 Btu/h. 2,800 x 500 = 1,400,000 Btu, divided by 3,412 = 410.3 kWh of backup. In the 12 F bin the shortfall is 12,800 Btu/h x 150 hours = 1,920,000 Btu, or 562.7 kWh.

Totals: 4,833.2 + 973.0 = 5,806.2 kWh. Total heat needed is 48,600,000 Btu, which is 14,243.9 kWh of heat, so the seasonal COP for these bins is 14,243.9 / 5,806.2 = 2.45. Backup supplies 973.0 / 5,806.2 = 16.8 percent of the electricity but only 3,320,000 Btu, about 6.8 percent of the heat, which shows how costly resistance heat is in energy terms.

Why bins beat one average temperature

Now do it the shortcut way. The hours-weighted average outdoor temperature of our five bins is 144,000 divided by 3,750, or 38.4 F. The load line gives 12,960 Btu/h there, and 12,960 x 3,750 = 48,600,000 Btu, the same total heat as before because the load line is straight. Interpolating our table at 38.4 F gives a capacity of about 28,200 Btu/h and a COP of about 3.15. That says the heat pump covers the load all season with no backup: 14,243.9 kWh of heat divided by 3.15 is about 4,525 kWh. The bin total was 5,806 kWh, so the single temperature falls short by about 1,281 kWh, roughly 22 percent of the bin answer.

The reason is that capacity and COP are curves, and the cold hours are the ones that need backup and run the lowest COP. An average hides those hours. The load line alone averages well, but the machine does not.

Limits of the method

  • Defrost takes energy. The cold climate field report estimated that defrost used roughly 5 to 8 percent of energy between 5 and 35 F in its data, so add a correction or use the maker's frost-adjusted values.
  • Real backup does not switch in at a clean temperature. In that report, switchover settings were around 30 to 35 F but varied by site, and in the coldest bins backup ran about 25 percent of the time. Thermostat settings change the result.
  • Your load line is only as good as your load calculation and your no-load temperature.
  • The weather file is a typical year. A cold winter has more cold-bin hours.
  • Fan, controls and standby power are not in these numbers.

Once you have kWh, reading your electric bill shows how to compare an estimate to metered use. The design temperature behind the load line is covered in outdoor design temperatures.

Field card: Heat Pump Bin-Hour Estimates

Hours x load, split into heat pump and backup, bin by bin.

  • Bin = a band of outdoor temperature with a count of hours.
  • Load per bin comes from a line between the design point and the no-load temperature.
  • Capacity and COP per bin come from the maker's extended data.
  • Heat pump kWh = Btu / 3,412 / COP. Backup kWh = shortfall Btu / 3,412.
  • Below the balance point, resistance backup has a COP of 1.
  • A single average temperature hides the cold hours and understates energy; in our example it was low by about 22 percent.
