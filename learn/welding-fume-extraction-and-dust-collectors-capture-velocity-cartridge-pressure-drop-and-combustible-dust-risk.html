Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors: Capture Velocity, Cartridge Pressure Drop and Combustible Dust Risk

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A shop or maintenance area that welds and grinds usually ends up asking the HVAC tech the same question: why does the fume arm barely pull anything, and why is the collector gauge reading high? This lesson explains how capture hoods work, how fast their pull fades with distance, what a cartridge collector gauge is telling you, and where combustible dust turns a collector into a fire problem.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. This page is general education built from federal agency pages and maker documents read in 2026. Confirm with the shop's safety lead, the equipment maker and the authority having jurisdiction before you rely on any number here.

What local exhaust is trying to do

Welding fume rises off the arc in a hot plume. The goal of local exhaust is to catch that plume at the source, before the welder breathes it, rather than dilute it in the whole building. OSHA's welding ventilation rule, 29 CFR 1910.252(c), says the planning depends on the size of the space and ceiling height, the number of welders, and what fumes the metals and coatings release. In small or low spaces (under 10,000 cubic feet per welder or ceilings under 16 feet) the rule calls for mechanical ventilation at a minimum of 2,000 cfm per welder unless local exhaust hoods, booths or approved airline respirators are used.

Two health points drive the design. NIOSH says most welding fume contains some manganese, that it can reach the nervous system when inhaled, and that confined space welding can raise exposure significantly. OSHA says a major source of hexavalent chromium exposure is hot work such as welding on stainless steel, and its chromium (VI) standard sets a permissible limit of 5 micrograms per cubic meter as an 8-hour average, with an action level of 2.5. That standard puts engineering controls ahead of respirators where feasible.

Capture velocity and the distance problem

Capture velocity is the air speed a hood creates at the point where the contaminant is released. A hood is a suction opening, and suction fades quickly as you move away from it, because air is pulled in from all directions in front of the opening. The OSHA Technical Manual makes the point with a plain or narrow flanged hood on a 3,000 fpm duct: capture velocity is about 300 fpm at one duct diameter away and about 30 fpm at two. It says the distance from the source should not exceed 1.5 duct diameters, and that a simple capture hood should not be below 50 fpm. One welding equipment maker likewise suggests about 1.5 duct diameters and a position above the arc at roughly 45 degrees. It also warns that strong cross-drafts can cut a hood's effectiveness by as much as 75 percent, so a shop fan across the bench can defeat a good arm.

For welding, 1910.252(c)(3) says a movable hood must be placed as close as practical and must draw 100 linear feet per minute in the welding zone at its farthest distance. It then gives minimum airflow for a 3-inch flanged opening: 150 cfm at 4 to 6 inches from the arc, 275 cfm at 6 to 8, 425 cfm at 8 to 10 and 600 cfm at 10 to 12. The air needed climbs steeply with distance, the same story the equation below tells.

For other operations, a NASA Glenn Research Center occupational health manual lists typical capture velocities: 100 to 200 fpm for welding and plating, and 500 to 2,000 fpm for grinding and abrasive blasting, because grinding throws particles hard.

Worked example: our own airflow at 6 inches versus 12 inches

Several sources print the hood equation Q = V(10X² + A), where Q is airflow in cfm, V is the capture velocity wanted at distance X in fpm, X is the distance from the hood face in feet and A is the hood opening area in square feet (the AIHA calculator page defines the terms this way). The NASA manual applies the same form with a 0.75 factor for a tapered snorkel-type hood. Assumptions, all ours:

  • Our own: a 6-inch round plain opening, so A = π × 0.25² = 0.196 ft².
  • V = 100 fpm, matching the OSHA welding-zone figure.
  • No 0.75 factor, so the answer is on the conservative (higher cfm) side. A flange or taper on the hood would let it do the job with less air.

At 6 inches (X = 0.5 ft): 10 × 0.5² = 2.5. Add A: 2.5 + 0.196 = 2.696. Q = 100 × 2.696 = about 270 cfm.

At 12 inches (X = 1.0 ft): 10 × 1.0² = 10. Add A: 10 + 0.196 = 10.196. Q = 100 × 10.196 = about 1,020 cfm.

Doubling the distance raised the airflow by 1,020 / 270 = about 3.8 times, nearly four. Now the reverse: if the fan only delivers 270 cfm and the welder works 12 inches away, the velocity there is 270 / 10.196 = about 26 fpm, roughly a quarter of the target and below the OSHA manual's 50 fpm floor. That is the usual story behind the arm that barely pulls: it was fine at 6 inches and useless at 12.

Behind the hood, a duct sized at the 4,000 fpm that OSHA's table uses needs 270 / 4,000 = 0.0675 ft², about a 3.5-inch round duct. For grinding at 500 fpm and 12 inches the same equation gives 500 × 10.196 = about 5,100 cfm, which is why grinding is better hooded or enclosed at the wheel. OSHA's table lists 275 cfm at 6 to 8 inches for a 3-inch flanged hood, near our 270 cfm, but the hoods differ, so treat it as a sanity check, not a match.

From hood to fan: transport and static pressure

Behind the hood, ducts must keep fine fume and dust moving so it does not settle. OSHA's table is built on about 4,000 fpm in the pipe; the ACGIH Industrial Ventilation manual is where designers get duct velocities for other materials. The fan has to overcome the hood entry loss, the arm and duct friction, and the collector. If you change fan speed, see Fan Laws: Airflow, Static Pressure and Horsepower When You Change Fan Speed, and to confirm real cfm in the duct use Duct Traverse with a Pitot Tube. A kinked or crushed arm adds resistance and cuts airflow.

Cartridge collectors, pulse cleaning and the gauge

Most shop collectors push the dirty air through pleated cartridges. Dust builds a layer on the media, and the pressure drop across the filters climbs. A pulse of compressed air blown backward through each cartridge knocks the cake off. The gauge on the collector, a differential pressure reading in inches of water column, is the best health check you have. Pulse air comes from the compressed air system; see Compressed Air Systems for Facility Techs.

Numbers belong to the model. One maker's manual for a cartridge collector says new filters read about 0.5 to 1.5 inches, normal operation falls between 1.5 and 6, and the filters need inspection or replacement at 8 or more. Other sections of that same manual give 4 to 6 and 4 to 7 inch targets, which shows why you read the manual for the exact unit. It caps the pulse regulator at 80 psi. In general terms: a reading that creeps up and stays up after cleaning means loaded or damaged filters, a weak pulse system or low compressed air. A sudden low reading can mean a cartridge is loose, torn or missing. Fume or dust visible at the fan discharge means stop and fix it; that manual says not to operate with dust visible there and never to open the filter access doors while running, and to lock out before entering the enclosure.

Return-to-room air

Some units discharge filtered air back into the shop instead of outdoors. That saves heated or cooled air, but the final filter becomes the last barrier for manganese and chromium fume, so a leak or worn seal sends fume back to the breathing zone. Air exhausted outdoors must be replaced; see Make-Up Air Units and Commercial Kitchen Ventilation Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics. For capture ideas in a lab, see Laboratory Fume Hoods, and for terms see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Combustible dust: when the collector becomes the hazard

OSHA says any combustible material can burn rapidly in finely divided form and that dust suspended in air can explode. Metal dusts such as aluminum and magnesium, and some iron dust, are listed as combustible in OSHA's combustible dust directive. That matters because grinding aluminum or other metals sends fine dust, and sparks, into the same collector that handles welding fume. OSHA's directive says hot work, including welding, cutting and grinding, must not be done where combustible dust is generated or handled, and it describes collectors inside buildings as needing explosion prevention or protection, with explosion vents ducted to a safe outdoor location. It also names collectors that return air to the building as a concern, and clean-air return systems as needing spark detection and extinguishing and fast abort gates. Dust piling up on equipment and ducts can be cited under 1910.22.

The standards behind this are NFPA 652 (fundamentals of combustible dust) and NFPA 660, a newer consolidated standard that a collector maker says folds in NFPA 652 and several others, along with NFPA 484 for combustible metals. A dust hazard analysis is how a facility decides what protection a process needs; that maker lists outdoor placement, spark mitigation and explosion isolation as options. Explosion venting and isolation are for the safety lead and fire authority to decide, not the HVAC tech.

Field card: Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors

Distance kills capture. Watch the gauge. Respect the dust.

  • OSHA 1910.252 asks for 100 fpm in the welding zone at the hood's farthest position; keep the hood close, about 1.5 duct diameters at most.
  • Our own example: a 6-inch plain hood at 100 fpm needs about 270 cfm at 6 inches and about 1,020 cfm at 12 inches (Q = V(10X² + A)).
  • Cross-drafts can cut hood effectiveness by up to 75 percent per OSHA.
  • Read the collector gauge against the maker's manual for that model; a rise that stays after cleaning means loaded filters or weak pulse; a sudden drop can mean a leak.
  • Stainless welding is a hexavalent chromium source; manganese is in most welding fume.
  • Grinding metals can make combustible dust and sparks; do not mix it into a collector that has not been evaluated.
  • Name NFPA 652 and 660 to the safety lead. Lock out before opening a collector.
