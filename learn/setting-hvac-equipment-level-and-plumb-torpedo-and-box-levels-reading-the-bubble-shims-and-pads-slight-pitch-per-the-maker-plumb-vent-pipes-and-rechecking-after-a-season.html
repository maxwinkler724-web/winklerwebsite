Setting HVAC Equipment Level and Plumb

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A new air handler, furnace, condensing unit or ductless head can be wired, piped and charged perfectly and still cause callbacks if it sits a little out of level. This lesson covers how to check level and plumb, what to put under equipment, and where the maker asks for a deliberate tilt instead of a dead-level set.

Why level matters

These are our own plain-language reasons, and the maker's installation instructions for the exact model always decide the limit. A condensate pan only drains if gravity moves water toward its outlet, so a unit that tilts away from the drain leaves a standing pool. That pool can overflow, grow slime or freeze. Equipment with rotating parts is built to run in a stated position; a compressor sump, a blower shaft or a fan bearing that is tipped can see oil or load where the designer did not expect it. A gas furnace is the same: the maker's instructions say how the unit must be positioned for the burners and for any drain in the inducer or vent connection, and the installer follows that page. Finally, a unit that is out of level and not fully supported can rattle against its pad or wall bracket, which is a noise complaint waiting to happen.

Public guidance agrees on the basic point. A federal job aid for installing a ductless system says to fasten the indoor mounting bracket to the wall, level, and to set the outdoor unit on a non-wicking pad or stand so it is level, plumb and stable. A national-lab standard work specification for split systems says the same for outdoor and indoor units, and adds that vibration pads or isolators go in according to the maker's specifications. A university facilities specification tells installers to set units level so they run without noticeable vibration.

Choosing and reading a level

A torpedo level is short, usually under a foot, and fits on a furnace casing, a bracket or a pipe. A box level is longer, which matters: a longer level spans more of the equipment, ignores small surface bumps and shows a given error with a better lever arm.

  • Bubble centered between the lines. That is level in the direction the level points. Turn the level 90 degrees on the same surface and check again, because a unit can be level front to back and still lean side to side.
  • Flip test. Set the level, note the bubble, then turn it end for end in the same spot. A good level reads the same both ways. If the bubble moves, the level is off and should not be trusted.
  • Read the unit, not the pad. Check on a flat machined surface of the cabinet or on the pan rim, not on a bent panel or a lip.
  • Plumb. Use the vertical vial against the side of the cabinet, a bracket or a vent pipe. For a vent pipe, check two faces 90 degrees apart so you know it is plumb in both directions.

Shims and pads

Shims fill the gap between the unit and a support that is not flat. Use material that will not rot, rust through or crush: composite or plastic shims, or manufactured leveling feet, where the maker allows them. Wood absorbs water outdoors and shrinks indoors. A thin stack of loose washers can squeeze out. Keep any shim fully under a structural rail or foot, not under a thin sheet metal panel, and secure it if the maker says to. A national-lab specification calls for a non-wicking equipment pad outdoors, which also reduces water travel up into the base.

Pads settle. Soil under a pad compresses, frost heaves it and rain washes under an edge. A pad that was level on install day can lean after a hard winter, which is why a recheck is worth the two minutes. Outdoor pads, stands and wall brackets are covered in their own lesson, so we do not repeat that here.

When the maker wants a slight pitch

Not everything should be dead level. The instructions for many ductless indoor heads call for the unit to be mounted with a slight tilt toward the drain side, and instructions for horizontal furnaces and coils often call for a slight pitch toward the condensate connection. We do not give a number here because the allowed tilt differs by model: read the installation instructions that came with the unit and follow them. The same goes for the direction of the pitch; confirm which side the drain leaves from before you shim. The attic and horizontal furnace case is in a separate lesson.

The pitch of the unit is not the pitch of its drain pipe. Two California city handouts that summarize the state mechanical and plumbing codes list a minimum drain and secondary drain slope of 1/8 inch per foot with supports every 4 feet. Drain pipe is covered in the lessons on PVC condensate drain piping and condensate traps. Always follow the code that your local inspector enforces.

Worked example (our own)

These numbers are our own assumptions for practice, not a maker requirement. Assume a 24 inch box level. Assume you need to lift one end of the level by 1/8 inch (0.125 in) to bring the bubble to center. Assume the pad or base under the unit is 30 inches wide.

  1. Slope. Rise divided by run: 0.125 in / 24 in = 0.005208 in per inch.
  2. Inches per foot. 0.005208 x 12 in = 0.0625 in per foot, or 1/16 inch per foot.
  3. Rise across the pad. 0.005208 x 30 in = 0.156 in, about 5/32 inch. So a 30 inch wide base would stand about 5/32 inch higher on one side.
  4. Degrees. Angle = arctangent of 0.005208 = about 0.30 degrees.

For comparison, a slope of 1/8 inch per foot (the drain pipe figure above) is 0.125 / 12 = 0.0104, or about 0.60 degrees. So the equipment error in this example is half of that pipe slope, and a unit with a built-in pitch would need a shim of that kind of small size. The takeaway: tiny angles are made of tiny shims, so use the level and thin, stable shim stock, not guesswork.

Plumb vent pipes and recheck after a season

Vertical vent pipes and the stacks above equipment should run plumb unless the maker's venting instructions give an angle, and horizontal runs follow the slope stated in those instructions. The city handouts above also list a level working platform 30 inches deep and 30 inches wide at the service side of a furnace installed in an attic, which is a useful reminder that a level, solid place to stand is part of a good install. For a vent angled between 46 and 60 degrees from vertical, those handouts limit horizontal sections to 75 percent of the overall vent height. Use the actual code and the vent maker's instructions for the real job.

After the first season, recheck. Put the level on the same surfaces, look at the drain pan outlet for staining, listen for rattles and look at shims for rot, crushing or slipping. Note what you find on the work order so the next tech starts with the answer.

Field card: Setting HVAC Equipment Level and Plumb

Level the unit the way the maker says, then recheck after a season.

  • Read the maker's instructions first: some units set level, some need a slight pitch toward the drain.
  • Center the bubble, turn the level 90 degrees and check again.
  • Flip the level end for end to prove it reads true.
  • Use shims that will not rot or crush, fully under a structural rail or foot.
  • Pads settle: recheck after the first season.
  • Our example: 1/8 in under a 24 in level is 1/16 in per foot, about 0.30 degrees.
