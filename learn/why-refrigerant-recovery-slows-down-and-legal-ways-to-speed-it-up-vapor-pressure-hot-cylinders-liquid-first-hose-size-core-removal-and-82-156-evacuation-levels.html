Why Refrigerant Recovery Slows Down, and Legal Ways to Speed It Up

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every tech has watched a recovery machine start fast and then crawl. Nothing is wrong with the machine. Physics is working against you as the system empties, and a few legal habits at the start of the job make a large difference. This lesson explains why recovery slows, what actually speeds it up, and the evacuation levels the federal rule requires before you open a system.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026, not legal advice. Evacuation levels and recovery duties can change. Confirm them in the current federal regulation and follow your equipment maker's instructions.

Why recovery slows near the end

A recovery machine works by pulling refrigerant out of the system and pushing it into a cylinder. What it can pull depends on the pressure in the system. While liquid sits in the system, that liquid is boiling off vapor at the pressure that matches its temperature, so the machine always has something to pull. Once the liquid is gone, only vapor is left. Every pound the machine removes lowers the vapor pressure, and the machine moves less mass with each stroke because the vapor is thinner. Cooling makes it worse: pulling vapor out makes the remaining refrigerant colder, and colder refrigerant has a lower pressure. Late in the job the system is cold, the pressure is low, and the flow is small. That is why the last few pounds can take longer than the first fifty.

The EPA's Section 608 test topics list the factors that affect recovery speed: ambient temperature, equipment size, and hose length and diameter. They also say that recovering liquid at the start of the process speeds things up. Those items map directly onto what you can control.

The cylinder heats up too

The other end of the hose has its own problem. The machine compresses the vapor and condenses it in the recovery cylinder, and that releases heat. As the cylinder warms, the pressure inside it rises, and the machine has to push against a higher pressure. A recovery machine's maker manual we read says that lowering the pressure in the storage tank speeds recovery. Hot weather, a cylinder sitting in sun, and a nearly full cylinder all work against you. A trade-press article on fast recovery also notes that unusually fast heat buildup can be a clue that non-condensable gases are in the refrigerant.

Legal ways to speed it up

  • Liquid first. Recover liquid before vapor. The maker manual says the key to a quick job is to get the liquid out first and then the remaining vapor. Liquid is dense, so a machine moves a lot of mass per minute. The EPA test topics for Type II appliances say the same: recovering liquid at the beginning speeds the process, and the Type III topics add that vapor must also be recovered after the liquid. Use both service ports where the system allows, and open the vapor side after most of the liquid is out. Our low-pressure lesson covers liquid-first on chillers.
  • Short, wide hoses. The trade article recommends the shortest hose possible and a larger diameter (3/8 inch) between the machine and the cylinder. The maker manual recommends the shortest length of 3/8 inch or larger hose, with a hose no longer than 3 feet suggested, and says a bigger hose means less friction and quicker recovery. A narrow or half-open valve raises back pressure and can stall the machine.
  • Remove valve cores. A valve core is a small spring-loaded pin in a service port. Both sources say cores and the depressors in hoses restrict flow, and the maker manual says restricting parts can cut flow by up to 90 percent and can turn a 20 minute job into one that runs for hours. Use a core removal tool made for the job and follow its instructions. Quick-disconnect fittings with built-in check valves can also add back pressure.
  • Keep the cylinder cool. Keep the machine and cylinder in the shade, give the cylinder airflow, and on a big job swap to a second cylinder when the first gets hot. The trade article says submerging the cylinder in ice water is the most effective option in extreme heat, and the maker manual describes a tank cooling method that lowers cylinder pressure (it calls for a minimum amount of liquid already in the tank first). Follow the machine's manual for the details.
  • Use a recovery cylinder that is not too full. The EPA test topics list the risks of filling a cylinder more than 80 percent. The maker manual says liquid expansion can burst a tank filled above 80 percent of its volume. A cylinder near its limit also has little room left to condense vapor. Reusable recovery cylinders must be an approved type; disposable cylinders are never refilled.
  • Do not mix refrigerants. The test topics also list the need to avoid mixing. See the mixed-refrigerant lesson below for what a mixed cylinder costs you.

What not to do: never vent to speed up a job. Under 40 CFR 82.154, no person maintaining, servicing, repairing or disposing of an appliance may knowingly vent refrigerant into the environment. Recovery with equipment that meets the federal equipment requirements is the lawful route, and the knowing release of recovered class I or class II refrigerant is a violation. If recovery will not finish, you have legal options such as a second cylinder, cooling, or a different machine, never a vent.

Required evacuation levels in 40 CFR 82.156

The recovery is not finished when the machine slows. Section 82.156(a) requires you to evacuate refrigerant, including all liquid, to set levels before opening or disposing of an appliance other than a small appliance or a motor vehicle air conditioner. You may evacuate the whole appliance, or only the part you are working on if it can be isolated to a system receiver. The levels in the table are in inches of mercury vacuum, measured against 29.9 inches of mercury standard atmospheric pressure, except for low-pressure appliances.

Other points in the same section: if you are not evacuating to the atmosphere after work that is not a major repair, medium-, high- and very high-pressure appliances go to no higher than 0 psig before opening. If a leak makes the table level unreachable, or would badly contaminate the recovered refrigerant, the non-leaking parts still go to the table level and the leaking parts go to the lowest level you can reach without substantial contamination, never above 0 psig. Small appliances have their own rule: recover 80 percent with older equipment, or 90 percent with a running compressor (80 percent if it does not run) on newer equipment, or evacuate to 4 inches of mercury vacuum. For records on 5 to 50 lb disposal jobs, see the regulation text. Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not measured data. A machine and a system we invented to show the idea:

  • Assumption: the system holds 12 lb of refrigerant, 10 lb as liquid and 2 lb as vapor at the start.
  • Assumption: the machine moves liquid at 1.5 lb per minute.
  • Assumption: with 3/8 inch hose and valve cores removed, the machine averages 0.15 lb per minute on vapor as pressure falls. With long thin hose and the cores left in, it averages 0.075 lb per minute (half).

Liquid first, good setup: liquid time = 10 lb ÷ 1.5 lb/min = 6.7 min. Vapor time = 2 lb ÷ 0.15 lb/min = 13.3 min. Total = 6.7 + 13.3 = 20 min.

Liquid first, restricted setup: vapor time = 2 lb ÷ 0.075 lb/min = 26.7 min. Total = 6.7 + 26.7 = 33.4 min.

No liquid-first step (vapor only, good setup): if the machine had to treat all 12 lb at the slow vapor rate, 12 lb ÷ 0.15 lb/min = 80 min.

The lesson in the numbers: nearly all the time goes to the last two pounds of vapor, so hose size and cores matter, and getting the liquid out first matters most. Real machines and systems differ, so use your own manual for rates.

Field card: Why Recovery Slows and How to Speed It Up

Liquid first, short wide hose, cores out, cool cylinder, never vent.

  • Vapor pressure falls as the system empties and chills, so flow drops.
  • The cylinder heats as vapor condenses in it; cool it or swap it.
  • Use 3/8 inch or larger hose, as short as possible.
  • Remove valve cores with a core removal tool.
  • Do not fill a cylinder past 80 percent.
  • Reach the 82.156 level before opening; venting is never an option.
