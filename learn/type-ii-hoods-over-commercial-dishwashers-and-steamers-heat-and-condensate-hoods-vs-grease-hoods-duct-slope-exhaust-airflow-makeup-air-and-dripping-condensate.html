Type II Hoods Over Dishwashers and Steamers: Heat and Condensate Hoods

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dishwasher does not make grease, but it makes a lot of hot, wet air. A Type II hood is the catch basin for that air: it pulls the steam out before it fogs the room, rots the ceiling and drips on clean dishes. This lesson covers how Type II hoods differ from grease hoods, where they go, how they are ducted, and what goes wrong with them.

Check the local code

Check the local code. Kitchen hood rules are adopted and amended state by state and city by city. We read government-hosted copies that may be older than the edition your jurisdiction uses. This page is general education, not a design or an inspection ruling. Confirm every requirement with your local building and health departments and with the equipment maker.

Type II versus Type I

A Type I hood goes over cooking that makes grease-laden vapor, such as fryers and griddles. It needs grease filters, a welded, fire-rated grease duct and a fire suppression system. We cover that side in Commercial Kitchen Ventilation Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics and Restaurant Kitchen Exhaust Fans, Upblast Fans, Grease Ducts, Hinge Kits and Cleaning.

A Type II hood is the heat and condensate hood. It serves equipment that makes heat, steam or combustion products but no grease. A Georgia state health document describes Type II systems as installed where cooking or dishwashing appliances produce heat and steam. There are no grease filters to clean and no grease fire to suppress, so the hood is simpler. The trouble moves from fire to water: condensation, corrosion and drips.

Where the International Mechanical Code calls for one

We read Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes pages that amend the International Mechanical Code, section 507. The page we opened is a historical version published in 2015, so the numbers may differ in your edition. It says: "Type II hoods shall be installed above dishwashers and appliances as required by Table 507.2.2." It also requires a Type II hood over appliances that produce products of combustion but not grease or smoke. Where a Type II hood is required, a Type I or Type II hood may be used.

In that table, conveyor-rack and door-type dishwashers (hot-water or chemical sanitizing), gas and floor-mounted electric steamers, and gas rethermalizers fall under a light-duty Type II hood. Larger steam-jacketed kettles and gas pasta cookers sit in the medium-duty class. Electric under-counter dishwashers and electric countertop steamers need no hood, though a footnote says their heat and moisture still count in the building heating and cooling loads. A hood is also required over an electric appliance that produces grease at or above the limit stated in the table footnote. The Georgia document also says a Type II hood is not required when the dishwasher maker supplies its own heat and vapor exhaust, so ask the inspector which path applies.

Duct material, slope and condensate

The Minnesota section on hood construction says Type II hood materials must be stainless steel not less than 0.024 inch thick, with copper of a stated weight or another approved material as alternatives. For the duct, the same state's section 506.4 says ducts serving Type II hoods must be "constructed of rigid metallic materials," and that ducts conveying moisture-laden air must be leakage tested. Flexible duct and thin galvanized pipe are not a fit for steam.

None of the government pages we read gives a duct slope number, so we do not give one. Here is our own field reasoning. Steam cools in the duct and turns to water. If that water has nowhere to go it pools at a low spot, drips from joints and rusts through the bottom. Good practice is to pitch horizontal runs so water runs back to the hood, where the gutter catches it, or to a drain point the maker specifies. Take the actual pitch and drain method from the hood maker and local code. The Georgia document adds that hood and duct design must prevent drippage onto utensils and equipment.

Dripping condensate and the food safety rule

This is a health code issue as well as a mechanical one. Washington State rule WAC 246-215-04226 repeats the federal food code idea (the 2009 FDA Food Code, 4-204.11): exhaust hood systems in food preparation and warewashing areas must be designed to prevent condensation from dripping onto food, equipment, utensils, linens and single-service articles. The Georgia document also says hoods must keep condensation from collecting on walls and ceilings and dripping into food. A hood that drips on a clean rack is a health violation, not only a nuisance.

Exhaust airflow and makeup air

Airflow comes from three places that can disagree. The dishwasher maker states its exhaust. The Georgia document sets a minimum net airflow for Type II hoods used for dishwashing of 100 cfm per linear foot of hood length, and lists minimums of 500 cfm for a single-rack machine and 600 cfm for a conveyor, with maker recommendations taking precedence. The Minnesota table for light-duty equipment lists 200 cfm per foot for a wall-mounted canopy. Because these differ, use the highest value your authority and the maker require. Overhang matters too: the Minnesota table calls for a wall-mounted canopy to extend 6 inches past each end and 12 inches in front of the equipment.

Georgia says makeup air should be approximately equal to the exhaust air, and that outside windows and doors are not to be used as makeup air. Short makeup air shows up as steam rolling out of the machine, doors that suck shut, and a hood that seems weak when the fan is fine. For unit choices see Make-Up Air Units: Direct-Fired vs Indirect-Fired.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a design. We assume a door-type dishwasher with a 5 foot long Type II hood. We use the Georgia minimum of 100 cfm per linear foot as the rate, and we assume the maker lists no higher figure.

  1. Exhaust: 5 ft x 100 cfm/ft = 500 cfm. That also meets the Georgia single-rack minimum of 500 cfm.
  2. Makeup air: approximately equal to exhaust, so about 500 cfm.
  3. Sensible heat removed: we assume the air leaves the hood at 110 F and that the room is supplying makeup air at 70 F. Delta T = 110 - 70 = 40 F. Using the standard sensible heat relation Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T, Q = 1.08 x 500 x 40 = 21,600 Btu/h. At 12,000 Btu/h per ton, that is about 1.8 tons of sensible heat carried outdoors, before counting the latent heat in the steam.
  4. Winter makeup air: if outdoor makeup air at 20 F is heated to 70 F, delta T = 50 F and Q = 1.08 x 500 x 50 = 27,000 Btu/h. A cold-weather kitchen pays that bill whenever the hood runs.

If the authority had required the Minnesota table value of 200 cfm/ft instead, the same hood would need 1,000 cfm, and every number above would double. That is why the rate must be settled first. For measuring the real airflow, see Measuring Airflow.

Corrosion and common service calls

  • Dripping at the duct. Check for a flat or reversed run, an open joint, or a stuck drain. Water in the bottom of the duct is the usual cause of rust-through.
  • Steam escapes into the room. Check for a slipping fan belt, a dirty or backward fan, closed dampers, a hood too small or too high, or not enough makeup air. Measure before you adjust.
  • Rust and white scale. The code calls for stainless steel hood material. Scale from hard water dries on the hood and fan. Our booster lesson, Commercial Kitchen Hot Water: Dishwasher Booster Heaters, covers the scale side.
  • Overflowing gutter or drain. A gutter that drains to a receptacle needs emptying or clearing. Georgia requires gutters to drain to an approved receptacle.
  • Safety. Lock out and tag the fan before opening it. Dishwasher water and steam can scald.

Field card: Type II Hoods Over Dishwashers and Steamers

Type II hoods handle heat and steam, not grease. Keep the water out of the dishes.

  • Type I is for grease. Type II is for heat, steam and combustion products.
  • The International Mechanical Code names door-type and conveyor dishwashers and steamers for Type II hoods (Minnesota copy, 2015).
  • Hood is stainless steel; duct is rigid metal and tested for moisture.
  • Pitch duct so condensate returns to the hood or a drain; get the pitch from the maker and local code.
  • Georgia minimum for dishwashers: 100 cfm per foot of hood; makeup air about equal to exhaust.
  • Sensible heat: Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T.
  • Drips onto clean utensils are a food code violation.
