Hearing Protection for HVAC Techs in Mechanical Rooms: OSHA and NIOSH Noise Limits, NRR Derating and Fit

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler room, a chiller plant and a generator enclosure can all be loud enough to hurt hearing over a career, and the damage is slow and painless until it is permanent. This lesson covers the two noise limits you will hear about, how a protector's rating gets adjusted, and how to get a real seal from earplugs and muffs.

Check your own site and employer rules

Check the rule that applies to you. This page is general education built from federal pages we read. Your employer's hearing program, your site's posted noise areas and any state rule control what you actually must do.

Two limits, two exchange rates

The federal rule is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95. Its Table G-16 lets a worker spend 8 hours a day at 90 dBA (slow response), and the allowed time halves for every 5 dB louder: 4 hours at 95, 2 hours at 100, 1 hour at 105, a half hour at 110. That 5 dB step is the exchange rate. OSHA also sets an action level of an 8-hour average of 85 dBA, where the employer must run a hearing conservation program, make protectors available at no cost, offer annual hearing tests and give annual training. OSHA's technical manual notes that an 8-hour average at 85 dBA is a 50 percent dose.

NIOSH is a research agency, so its numbers are recommendations, not law. Its recommended exposure limit (REL) is 85 dBA over an 8-hour shift, and for every 3 dBA increase NIOSH recommends cutting the exposure time in half. Because it halves time every 3 dB instead of every 5 dB, the NIOSH time limits fall much faster as noise rises. The figure shows the difference.

What the plant can reach

The OSHA Technical Manual lists a boiler room at about 90 dBA in its chart of typical sound levels, next to a construction site at 100 and heavy equipment operation at 120. We did not find a cited figure for chiller plants, cooling towers or generators, so measure your own. The same manual gives a quick field check: if you cannot be understood at about 3 feet without raising your voice, the area is generally too noisy. A sound level meter or dosimeter gives the real number.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

Assumptions: a tech works 6 hours in a plant room measured at 98 dBA, wearing earplugs labeled NRR 25. The 98 dBA and the NRR 25 are our assumptions, not measurements.

  1. OSHA allowed time. 98 is 8 dB above 90. Time halves every 5 dB, so the 8-hour limit is divided by 2 raised to 8/5 (1.6). That is 2 to the 1.6, about 3.03. 8 divided by 3.03 is about 2.6 hours. The 6-hour day is over the limit.
  2. OSHA dose. Appendix A gives D = 100 x C/T. D = 100 x 6 / 2.64, about 227 percent. The average is TWA = 16.61 x log10(D/100) + 90 = 16.61 x 0.357 + 90, about 95.9 dBA.
  3. NIOSH allowed time. 98 is 13 dB above 85. Time halves every 3 dB, so divide 8 hours by 2 to the 13/3 (4.33), about 20.2. That is about 0.40 hour, roughly 24 minutes.
  4. NIOSH average. A 6-hour day is three quarters of 8 hours, which lowers the average by 10 x log10(8/6), about 1.25 dB. 98 minus 1.25 is about 96.7 dBA.
  5. Derate the NRR the OSHA way. OSHA Appendix B says that with A-weighted measurements you subtract 7 dB from the NRR first. 25 minus 7 is 18. Protected average: 95.9 minus 18, about 78 dBA, under the 85 dBA action level and in NIOSH's suggested 75 to 85 dBA window.
  6. Reality check. OSHA's manual says fit-testing studies found roughly half of workers get less than 5 to 15 dB without training. If our tech only gets 10 dB, the average is 95.9 minus 10, about 86 dBA, over the action level. The label number is not what the worker gets.

The 7 dB step is OSHA's method for estimating adequacy. We did not confirm a NIOSH percentage method from a page we could open, so we apply only the OSHA step above.

Earplugs or muffs?

NIOSH says earplugs are usually more comfortable for long wear and hot conditions, while muffs are heavier, can be uncomfortable in warm places, and are often easier to fit correctly. Earplugs can take more effort to learn. Muffs and canal caps can interfere with hard hats, respirators and eye protection; OSHA's manual adds that glasses, facial hair and hard hats can break a muff seal. In a hot plant room, plugs often win. When you must wear a respirator or glasses all day, check the muff seal.

NIOSH also warns against overprotection: it suggests aiming for a protected level of about 75 to 85 dBA, since too much reduction isolates a worker from alarms and speech and tempts them to pull the protector out. That matters in a plant where a relief valve or alarm may be your warning.

Getting the fit right

  • OSHA requires proper initial fitting and supervision of correct use, and lets workers choose from a range of suitable protectors.
  • NIOSH describes a no-equipment check: count aloud while slowly cupping and uncupping your ears. With a good fit your voice should sound about the same.
  • NIOSH says individual fit testing is the best way to check what each worker gets; it yields a personal attenuation rating (PAR), and the protected exposure is the measured exposure minus the PAR. OSHA's manual says fit-testing cannot replace the NRR for judging adequacy, but it is a good supplement.
  • Do not wear corded plugs or a headband type under muffs; OSHA's manual says the cord or band can break the muff seal.

Double protection

If one protector is not enough, OSHA's manual says to wear earmuffs with earplugs. NIOSH specifically recommends muffs over plugs for exposures of 100 dBA or more, or for impulse sounds. Note the NRR is based on continuous noise and may not predict protection against impulse noise, according to OSHA's manual. And quieter equipment is better still: NIOSH's Buy Quiet idea is to choose quieter machinery, and it says building quiet in at design is cheaper than adding controls later. OSHA's rule also puts feasible engineering or administrative controls ahead of protectors.

Signs of hearing damage

NIOSH lists trouble hearing with background noise, feeling that people mumble and often asking others to repeat themselves. Ringing, buzzing or roaring in the ears (tinnitus) can be an early sign of overexposure and is a reason to get tested. On an audiogram, OSHA's manual describes a dip near 4,000 Hz as an early indicator. A standard threshold shift is an average loss of 10 dB or more at 2,000, 3,000 and 4,000 Hz compared with your baseline test. Don't wait for symptoms to show.

Field card: Hearing protection in plant rooms

Measure it, rate it honestly, seal it.

  • OSHA: 90 dBA for 8 h, time halves every 5 dB; 85 dBA average triggers a hearing program.
  • NIOSH: 85 dBA for 8 h, time halves every 3 dB.
  • OSHA A-weighted method: NRR minus 7, then subtract from the measured average.
  • Our example: 98 dBA, 6 h, NRR 25 gives about 96 dBA unprotected and about 78 dBA protected.
  • Cannot be understood at 3 ft without shouting: too loud.
  • Count aloud while cupping the ears to check the seal.
  • Muffs over plugs at 100 dBA or more; ringing in the ears means get tested.
