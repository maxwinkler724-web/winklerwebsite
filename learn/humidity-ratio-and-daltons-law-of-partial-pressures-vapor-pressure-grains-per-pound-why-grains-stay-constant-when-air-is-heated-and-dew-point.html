Humidity Ratio and Dalton's Law: Grains per Pound for HVAC Techs

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Relative humidity is the number most thermostats show, but it is a ratio of two pressures that both change with temperature. Humidity ratio, in grains of water per pound of dry air, counts the actual water. This lesson shows how the two connect through Dalton's law, and why heating air drops RH without drying it.

Air is a mixture, and each gas has a partial pressure

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, plus a little argon and a variable amount of water vapor. Dalton's law of partial pressures says the total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of what each gas would exert alone. A University of Hawai'i atmospheric science text writes the atmosphere this way: total pressure equals the nitrogen share plus oxygen plus argon and so on, plus the water vapor share. Meteorologists call the water vapor share the vapor pressure, written pv here. In our field terms, p is the total (barometric) pressure and pv is the slice of it that belongs to water.

Vapor pressure is small. Our worked example below ends up near 0.45 kPa out of about 101 kPa, under half of one percent of the total. That is why a few grains of moisture matter so much to a coil, a duct surface or a person's comfort.

Relative humidity is a pressure ratio

Air at a given temperature can hold vapor only up to a limit, the saturation vapor pressure pws, which climbs steeply with temperature. The New Mexico State University atmospheric science encyclopedia gives relative humidity as 100 times the actual vapor pressure divided by the saturation vapor pressure at the same temperature. The Hawai'i text adds a useful warning: RH shows how close air is to saturation, not how much vapor it contains.

So RH = 100 × pv / pws. Two samples at the same RH can hold very different amounts of water if their temperatures differ. A 70 F room at 50% RH holds far more water than a 30 F day at 50% RH, because the saturation pressure at 70 F is several times larger.

Humidity ratio: counting the water

Humidity ratio W is the mass of water vapor divided by the mass of the dry air that comes with it. Because the vapor behaves as a gas mixed with the dry air, the masses follow from the partial pressures and the ratio of gas constants. Both the Hawai'i text and the NMSU encyclopedia give the mixing ratio as about 0.622 times e over (P minus e), where 0.622 is the ratio of the gas constants of dry air and water vapor (287 over 461 in the Hawai'i text). In HVAC symbols:

W = 0.622 × pv / (p − pv) in pounds of water per pound of dry air.

The pressure units cancel, so use kPa or psia as long as p and pv match. Techs usually want grains, a small unit. The NIST conversion table lists the grain as exactly 6.479891 × 10-5 kg and the avoirdupois pound as 0.4535924 kg, and dividing those gives 7,000 grains per pound. So grains per pound = 7,000 × W. Note the denominator: it is dry air, not the moist mixture, which is why the (p − pv) term appears.

Why heating lowers RH but not grains

Heating air in a duct or furnace adds heat but no water, and takes none away. The pounds of vapor and pounds of dry air stay put, so W and grains per pound do not change. If total pressure also stays the same, rearranging the equation shows pv stays the same too. But the saturation pressure at the new, higher temperature is larger. With the same top number and a bigger bottom number in pv / pws, RH falls. This is why winter indoor air feels dry once it has been heated, and why a high RH reading in a cold space can describe less water than a modest RH reading in a warm one.

Cooling does the reverse until RH reaches 100%, then water starts to condense. That temperature is the dew point.

Dew point is vapor pressure in disguise

The Hawai'i text defines dew point as the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant moisture and pressure, to reach saturation. In pressure terms, the dew point is the temperature at which the saturation vapor pressure equals the air's actual vapor pressure. It follows that dew point, vapor pressure and humidity ratio all carry the same information: if you know one and the barometric pressure, you can find the others. Dew point also does not change when air is heated or cooled without condensation, matching the grains line. Below freezing the same idea is called the frost point, as that text notes.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Outdoor air at 30 F and 80% RH is heated to 70 F in a furnace and duct, with no humidifier. Find the vapor pressure, the grains per pound before and after, and the new RH.

Assumptions (ours): total pressure is 101.325 kPa (standard sea-level pressure) both outside and after heating; no moisture is added or removed; the saturation pressure at 30 F is taken over liquid water, as weather tables do.

  1. Convert 30 F to Celsius. (30 − 32) / 1.8 = −1.11 C.
  2. Saturation pressure at 30 F. The NMSU encyclopedia gives an approximation for saturation vapor pressure over water in millibars: 6.11 × 10[7.5 T / (237.3 + T)], with T in C. At T = −1.11: exponent = 7.5 × (−1.11) / (237.3 − 1.11) = −0.0353, and 10−0.0353 = 0.9220, so pws = 6.11 × 0.9220 = 5.633 mbar = 0.563 kPa. Cross-check: the NIST water table shows 0.61165 kPa at 273.16 K (the 0.01 C point), and 0.563 is a little lower at a colder temperature, as it should be. The Hawai'i text's exponential formula gives about 0.564 kPa at 30 F, also close.
  3. Vapor pressure. pv = 0.80 × 0.563 = 0.4504 kPa.
  4. Humidity ratio before. W = 0.622 × 0.4504 / (101.325 − 0.4504) = 0.2801 / 100.875 = 0.002777 lb/lb.
  5. Grains before. 0.002777 × 7,000 = 19.4 grains per pound of dry air.
  6. After heating to 70 F. No water was added, so W is still 0.002777 lb/lb, which is 19.4 grains per pound, and pv is still 0.4504 kPa.
  7. Saturation pressure at 70 F. 70 F is 21.11 C, or 294.26 K. The NIST water saturation table gives 2.5051 kPa at 294.26 K.
  8. New RH. 0.4504 / 2.5051 = 0.1798, about 18%. (The NMSU formula gives 2.505 kPa at 70 F, so it agrees.)
  9. Dew point, before and after. Solving the NMSU formula for the temperature where pws = 0.4504 kPa gives about −4.1 C, or 24.6 F. It is the same before and after heating. Using the NMSU constants for ice, the frost point is about 25.4 F.

Result: 19.4 grains per pound both times, vapor pressure 0.45 kPa both times, RH from 80% to about 18%. The air is no drier by weight, yet it reads like a desert. Adding humidity to reach, say, a comfortable indoor level means adding grains, not just heat.

Where to use this in the field

When a wet-bulb and dry-bulb pair gives you a humidity ratio, you are reading the same quantity this lesson derives. See Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field for getting a trustworthy reading, and Measuring Air-Side Cooling Capacity for what to do with it. Grains also explain latent load: a coil that removes grains is doing latent work, covered in Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load. When people ask why a duct or pipe sweats, compare its surface temperature to the dew point, as in Will It Sweat?

Field card: Humidity Ratio and Dalton's Law

Heat the air and RH falls; the grains do not move.

  • Total pressure = dry air share + water vapor share (Dalton's law).
  • RH = 100 × vapor pressure / saturation pressure at the air temperature.
  • W = 0.622 × pv / (p − pv) in lb/lb; grains per lb = 7,000 × W.
  • Heating or cooling without condensation: grains, vapor pressure and dew point stay the same.
  • Our own example: 30 F and 80% RH is 19.4 gr/lb; heated to 70 F it is about 18% RH, still 19.4 gr/lb.
  • Dew point is the temperature where saturation pressure equals the actual vapor pressure.
