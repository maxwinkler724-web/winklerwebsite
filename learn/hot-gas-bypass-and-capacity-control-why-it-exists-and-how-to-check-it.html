Hot Gas Bypass and Capacity Control: Why It Exists and How to Check It

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling system is sized for the hottest day, but most hours are not the hottest day. When the load drops, the compressor can pump faster than the coil can use, and suction pressure sinks. Hot gas bypass is one fix: send some of the compressor's own hot discharge gas back to the low side so the coil never gets too cold. It works, and it wastes energy, so it is worth knowing both sides.

The problem it solves

At low load, less heat enters the evaporator, so less liquid refrigerant boils off. The compressor keeps pulling, and suction pressure falls. Lower pressure means a lower boiling temperature (see Pressure-Temperature Basics). Once the coil or the water it cools gets near 32°F, you get ice, the same snowball described in AC Evaporator Coil Frozen. The other outcome is short cycling: the low-pressure control trips, the compressor stops, pressure recovers, and it starts again. Danfoss describes its capacity regulator as a way to ride out low-load periods, avoid low suction pressure and avoid compressor cycling. Trane's engineering newsletter says the same job in two words: modulate capacity and prevent frost on the coil.

How it works

A bypass valve sits in a line that runs from the high-pressure discharge side to the low side. It watches the pressure on the low side. Danfoss says its KVC regulator opens as the pressure downstream of it, meaning suction pressure, falls. When the valve opens, hot gas flows to the low side. That adds load to the compressor, so it can no longer pull the evaporator down, and pressure stops falling. The set point is a pressure, not a temperature, which is why you will convert it with a chart.

Three ways to pipe it

Sporlan's discharge bypass valve bulletin describes where the hot gas can go, and Trane's newsletter ranks them.

  • Hot gas to the evaporator inlet. Sporlan's bulletin sends it to a side port on the distributor, or to an auxiliary connection on its ASC connector. Trane calls this the preferred arrangement: the expansion valve feeds enough liquid to cool the bypassed gas, and the coil stays wet and evenly fed.
  • Hot gas to the suction line with a desuperheating expansion valve. The hot gas and the expansion valve's output meet in one tee before the compressor, and the valve's sensing bulb sits downstream of that tee, near the compressor. Trane describes a liquid-injection valve cooling the bypassed gas so the compressor motor does not overheat.
  • With other regulators. Sporlan notes a bypass valve can be combined with an evaporator pressure regulator or a crankcase pressure regulator. If a crankcase regulator is present, the bypass valve's setting must be lower than the regulator's.

The first two are the ones you will meet most. Both rely on the metering device doing extra work, so see Metering Devices: TXV and Piston and TXV Sensing Bulb Placement.

Valve setting and adjustment

Sporlan's bulletin says turning the adjusting screw clockwise raises the setting and counterclockwise lowers it, and lists the hex wrench sizes by model. Its procedure is to put the system at a load high enough that suction pressure sits above the target, then slowly lower the load. The valve is opening when you hear hissing and the pressure at a gauge on the valve outlet rises. Do not force the screw past its stops. Danfoss lists a factory setting of 2 bar for its KVC and about 0.45 bar per turn on the common sizes. The Danfoss guide states a turning direction only for other valves in the series, so read the direction on the specific valve's data sheet. Sporlan also says equipment makers sometimes change the factory setting to suit a particular unit, so check with the maker before you turn anything on a packaged machine.

Worked example: is the set point safe?

An invented example. A small R-134a chilled-water package has a hot gas bypass valve set to begin opening at 35 psig, measured at the evaporator outlet. The question is whether that setting keeps the refrigerant well above freezing. The chart is Arkema's Forane 134a table, which lists saturation pressure in psig.

  1. Look up 35 psig. The table shows 35.0 psig at 40°F. So the valve begins to open when the refrigerant is boiling at about 40°F.
  2. Find the freezing line. The table shows 27.8 psig at 32°F.
  3. Pressure margin: 35.0 − 27.8 = 7.2 psi above the 32°F pressure.
  4. Temperature margin: 40 − 32 = 8°F above freezing.
  5. Compare with a published guide. Trane's newsletter suggests a minimum design saturated suction temperature of 43 to 45°F for variable-volume comfort cooling and 40 to 43°F for constant volume. Our 40°F sits at the bottom of the constant-volume band and below the other, so this setting is on the low side, with no room to lower it.
  6. Allow for valve behavior. Trane says a proportional valve needs about 6 psig to open fully and holds suction within roughly ±4°F. Four degrees below 40°F is 36°F, still above 32°F. If the valve really did have to drop 6 psi before it was fully open, suction would sag to 35 − 6 = 29 psig. The table puts 28.6 psig at 33°F and 29.5 psig at 34°F, so 29 psig is about 33.4°F, only about 1.4°F above freezing. That is the number to worry about.

The takeaway: a set point that looks safe in the middle of its range can have almost no margin when the valve is wide open. Convert the setting, then also convert the lowest pressure you see on the gauge. Water-side numbers can differ from refrigerant numbers, because the refrigerant boils colder than the fluid it cools, so freeze limits for your equipment come from the maker.

The energy price

Trane is blunt: the bypassed gas does no useful cooling, so hot gas bypass greatly reduces operating efficiency, and the newsletter treats it as a last resort. It also adds a line, a valve and a place for leaks or oil to hide. For that reason energy codes limit it. Trane's article reports that ASHRAE Standard 90.1 restricts hot gas bypass on systems over 7.5 tons unless they have multiple unloading steps or continuous modulation, with caps of 50 percent of capacity for 7.5 to 20 tons and 25 percent above 20 tons. Washington's state energy code table limits bypass capacity to 50 percent of total capacity at or below 240,000 Btu/h and 25 percent above that. Which edition and which limits apply to a job is set by the local code, so confirm with the inspector. This lesson did not read the standard text itself.

Alternatives that waste less

  • Compressor capacity modulation. A Copeland bulletin for its Scroll Digital line describes cycling a solenoid so the scrolls separate and the compressor delivers no flow part of each 15-second cycle. Ten seconds loaded gives roughly 66 percent and five gives about 33 percent, and the line is described as modulating from 10 to 100 percent. The same bulletin names units that use hot gas bypass today as good retrofit candidates.
  • Variable speed. A drive changes compressor speed to match load. See Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.
  • Staging. More than one compressor or step of unloading, which is also the route 90.1 favors according to Trane.

Service checks

  • Start with the basics. Airflow or water flow, filters and coil cleanliness come before any valve work, as in the frozen-coil lesson linked above.
  • Is it supposed to be open? A bypass valve that is open at full load is stealing capacity. Sporlan lists dirt, a failed diaphragm, a plugged or disconnected equalizer line, or a lost air charge in a remote-bulb element as reasons for a valve that fails to open, and dirt, a plugged equalizer, a disconnected line or an overheated spring for one that will not close.
  • Watch the outlet pressure and the sound. That is how Sporlan's own setting procedure detects opening.
  • Protect the compressor. Copeland's bulletin says a low-pressure control set at 95 psig cut-out for R-410A (55 psig for R-407C and R-22) can help prevent evaporator icing. Never jumper a pressure switch to get past a trip; see RTU Refrigerant Circuit and Compressor Protection.
  • Brazing a valve. Sporlan says to wrap the body in a wet cloth to stay under 300°F, aim the flame away, and recover refrigerant before applying heat.

Field card: Hot Gas Bypass and Capacity Control

Hot gas bypass trades efficiency for a coil that stays above freezing.

  • It sends discharge gas to the low side so suction pressure stops falling at low load.
  • The set point is a pressure. Convert it with the right refrigerant's chart.
  • Evaporator inlet is the preferred entry. Suction-line entry needs a desuperheating valve.
  • Check margin at the lowest pressure you can read, not only at the set point.
  • Bypassed gas does no useful cooling. Energy codes limit it.
  • Do not jumper protection controls. Adjust only with the maker's procedure.
