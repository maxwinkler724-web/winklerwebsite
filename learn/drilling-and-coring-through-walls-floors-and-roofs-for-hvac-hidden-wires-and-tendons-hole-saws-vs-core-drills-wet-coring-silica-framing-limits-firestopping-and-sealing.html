Drilling and Coring Through Walls, Floors and Roofs for HVAC Lines and Ducts

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every new duct, line set or vent starts with a hole. A hole through a wall, floor or roof can hit a live wire, cut a structural cable, weaken a stud, break a fire rating or start a leak. This lesson walks through the order we work in: look first, pick the right tool, control the dust, then close the hole properly.

Step 1: find out what is inside before you drill

A federal energy department lessons-learned sheet on penetration work collects real incidents where a drill or core bit met something hidden. Its recurring themes are plain. Utilities were not marked on the surface. Drawings were incomplete or the drawings for embedded conduit were never reviewed. A scanner built to find rebar did not show conduit that had too little iron in it. One scan reached only about 4 inches, but the wiring sat about 6 inches inside the wall, and nobody scanned the other side. In another case a conduit was strapped right to a piece of rebar, so the rebar reading hid it.

The same sheet gives a good checklist of questions: have all the available drawings been reviewed for hidden hazards, has the other side of the wall or slab been checked, are identified electrical hazards de-energized and locked out, and are there depth limits for the hole. It also asks whether the crew should stop and investigate when the bit hits an obstruction. We answer that one the same way every time: stop, back out, and find out what it is.

For floors and ceilings in a building with a concrete structure, ask one more question: is this slab post-tensioned? A state university system's guidelines for post-tensioned concrete say drilling into such a slab is not allowed unless the tendon locations were marked in advance, the slab was x-rayed, or the tendons were recorded by photography. Those guidelines also tell designers to avoid penetrations near column supports and tendon anchorages. Our rule as apprentices is simple: if the slab might be post-tensioned, we do not core or anchor until the building owner or engineer confirms the tendon locations in writing.

Step 2: hole saw or core drill

Our own rule of thumb: a hole saw suits wood framing, drywall and thin sheet material. A diamond core bit on a core drill suits concrete and masonry, because a hole saw is not made to grind through them. For handheld hole saws, the bind-up and kickback habits are covered in Hand and Power Tool Safety for HVAC Techs, so we do not repeat them here. The extra point for large cores is torque. When a large bit jams on rebar or grit, the tool body wants to spin the other way, and a stand-mounted rig that is firmly anchored resists that far better than hands do. Our own advice: use the side handle, keep both hands on the tool, never core from a ladder you cannot brace, and follow the tool maker's manual for the anchoring method.

Step 3: wet or dry, and why silica matters

Concrete, brick, block and mortar contain crystalline silica. Drilling or coring them makes respirable dust, fine enough to reach deep into the lungs. The federal rule for construction silica, 29 CFR 1926.1153, sets an action level of 25 micrograms per cubic meter and a permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic meter, each as an 8-hour average. Its "Table 1" approach lets an employer skip measuring exposure if the crew uses the listed controls for each task.

  • Handheld and stand-mounted drills: Table 1 calls for a shroud or cowling connected to a dust collector that meets the tool maker's airflow specification, with a filter at least 99 percent efficient and a filter-cleaning mechanism. The listed work practices include using a HEPA-filtered vacuum to clean out the hole. That is dry-drilling with capture.
  • Rig-mounted core saws or drills: Table 1 calls for a tool with an integrated water delivery system that feeds water to the cutting surface. A federal fact sheet on these rigs says to keep a continuous water stream on the bit at a rate that minimizes visible dust, to check hoses and nozzles, and to use GFCI protection and watertight connectors because water and electric tools share the work area. It adds that if a crew uses a vacuum dust collector on such a rig instead of water, Table 1 no longer applies and the employer must assess exposure.
  • Cleanup: the rule restricts dry sweeping, dry brushing and compressed air where they could add to exposure. The fact sheet says to clean up slurry before it dries and turns back into airborne dust, using a shovel or a HEPA-filtered wet vacuum. It also notes that indoors, wet methods may need extra ventilation, so do not assume water alone is enough in a closed room.

This is a general summary. The employer sets the written plan, the respirator rules and the exact controls, and the full text of the rule governs. A related lesson on this site goes deeper into silica for HVAC work; ask your lead for it or find it in the lesson list.

Our own worked example: how much concrete comes out

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measured job. Assumptions: 4 cores, each 4 inches in diameter, through a slab that is 8 inches thick. Normal-weight concrete density of 145 pounds per cubic foot, which sits inside the 140 to 155 pounds per cubic foot range a federal highway agency primer gives for normal-weight concrete.

  1. Radius = 4 in ÷ 2 = 2 in.
  2. Volume of one core = π × (2 in)² × 8 in = 3.1416 × 4 × 8 = 100.5 cubic inches.
  3. Four cores = 4 × 100.5 = 402.1 cubic inches.
  4. Convert: 402.1 ÷ 1,728 cubic inches per cubic foot = 0.233 cubic feet.
  5. Weight = 0.233 cubic feet × 145 lb per cubic foot = about 33.7 pounds in total, or about 8.4 pounds per core.

What this tells us: each slug is heavy enough to hurt a foot or a person below, so plan where it falls or how it is caught. It also shows a limit of the math. The slugs are the solid cores. The dust or slurry comes from the thin ring the bit grinds away around each core, and we have no figure for that ring in this example, so we do not claim one. The respirable dust concern is not about the weight of the slab. It is that the ground-away ring is made of the same silica-bearing material and breaks into very fine particles. With water on the bit those particles are caught in slurry. Dry, they can hang in the air. That is why the rule asks for water or capture, and we do not invent an exposure number here. Measuring exposure is the employer's job.

Step 4: wood framing, at a general level

Holes in studs and joists take away wood that carries load. The model residential code, the International Residential Code, limits this in Section R602.6 for studs and Section R502.8 for floor joists. A city building department handout that summarizes them says a bored hole in a stud may be up to 60 percent of the stud width, that in exterior walls and bearing partitions a hole larger than 40 percent and up to 60 percent needs a doubled stud, and that a hole may not sit in the same section as a notch. For joists it says holes stay at least 2 inches from the top and bottom edges and no larger than one third of the joist depth. The handout is a city summary, so the code your local building department adopted is what counts. When a duct or line set needs more than these limits allow, we ask the framing lead or engineer for a different route. We do not guess.

Step 5: seal the hole, and firestop it if the wall is rated

Whatever goes through gets sealed. A federal energy department sheet on sealing penetrations says HVAC, plumbing and electrical holes are often oversized, that caulk is enough for small gaps, and that holes larger than 1/4 inch may need support for the sealant, such as backer rod. Its table pairs holes from 1/4 to 2 inches with one-component foam or mastic and larger holes with stiffer infill. That is for ordinary air sealing at the building envelope. For an exterior wall, also finish the outside with a weather-tight seal and a slope or drip that sheds water away, as shown in Mini-Split Wall Penetrations and Line-Set Covers. For roofs, see Roof Penetrations for Rooftop HVAC Equipment.

Fire-rated walls and floors are different. A university fire-safety guideline says a firestop system should be installed anywhere there is a penetration in a rated barrier, that it must be a tested, listed system whose rating meets or exceeds the barrier it sits in, that products from different makers should not be mixed, and that it should be installed by qualified personnel. General foam or caulk from the sealant shelf is not a listed firestop. If we make a new hole in a rated assembly, we tell the lead before drilling, and the opening is closed with the right listed system. The full story is in Firestopping HVAC Pipe and Duct Penetrations.

Field card: Drilling and Coring Through Walls, Floors and Roofs

Look, then drill; wet, then clean; seal, then label.

  • Review drawings, scan both sides, and lock out any identified live circuit. Stop at any obstruction.
  • Post-tensioned slab? No coring until tendon locations are confirmed in writing.
  • Hole saw for wood and thin material; core bit for concrete and masonry. Brace the rig; watch for bind-up.
  • Silica: use water on a core rig or a shroud with a dust collector on a drill. No dry sweeping or compressed air.
  • Studs and joists: stay inside the adopted residential code limits or ask.
  • Rated wall or floor: close with a listed firestop system, not general sealant.
  • Exterior holes: seal and shed water away.
