Gas and CO Detection in Boiler and Mechanical Rooms: Sensors, Placement, Alarms and Burner Shutdown

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler room holds a flame, a fuel line and a lot of metal that hides small leaks. A fixed detector watches the air all day so a person does not have to be standing there to smell it. This lesson covers what the sensors measure, where they go, what the alarm numbers mean, and how a detector can tie into a burner shutdown and an exhaust fan.

Check the code and the maker

Check the code and the maker. Whether a room needs fixed detectors, and what they must do, comes from the adopted code, the authority having jurisdiction and the equipment instructions. NFPA 715 (fuel gases detectors) and NFPA 720 (carbon monoxide detection and warning equipment) are the standards usually named for this work. We name them in plain text only; read the edition your jurisdiction adopted. This page is general education, not a design.

Why a room detector, and what it does not replace

The smell of natural gas is added on purpose. Connecticut's gas pipeline safety office notes that an odorant, mercaptan, is added so people notice leaks. A nose is still a poor alarm in a room nobody is in, and people tire of a smell after a while. A fixed detector is the always-on backup. It does not replace combustion analysis, leak checks with solution, or the flame safeguard on the burner. For what a household should do when someone smells gas, see Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do. For home CO alarms, see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. This lesson is about the plant room.

Light gas, heavy gas, and the LEL

The Connecticut page says natural gas is lighter than air and disperses quickly after a release, while propane is heavier than air and settles in low areas. NIOSH lists propane at a relative gas density of 1.55 (air is 1) and a lower explosive limit of 2.1 percent. The Connecticut page gives natural gas a burnable range of roughly 5 to 15 percent in air and propane roughly 2 to 10 percent. The U.S. Coast Guard data on NOAA's CAMEO Chemicals page for methane, the main part of natural gas, lists a lower explosive limit of 5.3 percent. Sources round this a little differently, so the example below states its own assumption.

The lower explosive limit, or LEL, is the thinnest mixture that can burn. Detectors report a "percent LEL" scale: 100 percent LEL means the gas is at the LEL, and 20 percent LEL means one fifth of the way there. That is why detectors alarm at small fractions of it. Gas at 20 percent LEL cannot burn yet; it is the warning that a leak is real.

Two sensor families for combustible gas

A magazine comparison of the two common technologies describes them this way. Catalytic bead sensors respond to nearly any combustible gas or vapor, but silicone and sulfur vapors can poison them, and low oxygen is a problem. Infrared sensors are not affected by those poisons and can work without oxygen, but they cannot detect hydrogen or acetylene. OSHA's bulletin on portable monitors adds that catalytic hot-bead LEL sensors can drift after exposure to volatile silicones, hydrides, halogenated hydrocarbons and sulfides. A boiler room has aerosol sprays, sealants and leak-test products around, so poisoning is a real maintenance concern. Check which technology a model uses and what its manual says about contaminants.

Carbon monoxide is measured by a separate CO sensor. Combustible and CO detectors are different devices with different jobs: one watches for unburned fuel, the other for incomplete combustion.

Where the sensors go

The mounting rules follow the gas density, as the figure shows.

  • Natural gas (methane): high. A fixed-detector maker's mounting article says to install within inches of the ceiling. A boiler-room guide from a detector maker says about 12 inches below the ceiling.
  • Propane: low, near the floor, per the same mounting article. The boiler-room guide we read gave two different propane heights in two places, so follow the instructions for the exact model.
  • Carbon monoxide: the boiler-room guide says 4 to 6 feet off the floor, at breathing level. CO is close to air in weight and mixes through a room.
  • Airflow: keep detectors away from fresh-air ducts, doors and windows, which can affect the sensor. Where a leak could start anywhere in a room, the mounting article suggests several detectors.
  • Coverage: the boiler-room guide gives one model's coverage as up to 1,257 square feet when mounted in the center, with a 15 to 20 foot radius. Other models differ.

Alarm points: percent LEL and ppm

Combustible detectors are set by percent LEL. One maker's data sheet for a room detector lists two levels, a pre-alarm at 13 percent LEL and a main alarm at 20 percent LEL. Another maker's page for a fan-control detector lists a 0 to 50 percent LEL range with adjustable setpoints and selectable alarm and fan time delays up to 10 minutes. These are one maker's settings each, not a code requirement, so set yours from the instructions and the design.

CO is set by ppm, and the exposure limits give the context. NIOSH lists a recommended limit of 35 ppm as a time-weighted average, a 200 ppm ceiling and an immediately dangerous level of 1,200 ppm. OSHA's permissible exposure limit is 50 ppm as an 8-hour average. A garage or boiler room alarm is usually set well below the dangerous level. For how a garage fan system stages on CO, see Enclosed Parking Garage Ventilation; this page does not repeat that.

Interlocks: burner off, fan on

A detector with a relay can be wired so that a gas alarm drops out the burner or safety circuit, starts an exhaust fan, and sounds a horn. The boiler-room guide describes a controller that works with its detectors to shut down boilers when gas is present, with up to 4 detectors and up to 8 boilers or appliances on separate relays, and horns and strobes placed outside the room near the entry. A separate maker lists a fan relay on its detector, with an adjustable minimum run time. The design, including which gas valve closes and who resets it, belongs to the engineer and the adopted code. A fan that moves air through a room that holds gas near an ignition source is a design choice, not a default, so never improvise it. The combustion air supply also matters for the room; see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters. A refrigerant machinery room uses the same ideas with different gases: see Refrigeration Machinery Rooms.

Bump tests, calibration and sensor life

OSHA's bulletin on portable gas monitors separates two things. A bump test passes challenge gas over the sensors, strong enough to set off all alarms, to confirm gas reaches the sensor and the alarms work; it does not measure accuracy. Calibration is the measuring accuracy against a known traceable test gas. A "calibration check" confirms the response is within the maker's limits (typically 10 to 20 percent of the gas concentration, but check the maker), and a full calibration adjusts the reading. The bulletin cites ISEA's position that a portable unit should be bump tested or checked before each day's use, says a failed bump test calls for full calibration, a failed calibration means removal from service, and tells the user never to use expired test gas. It also recommends keeping calibration records for the life of the instrument.

That daily rule is for portable meters a tech carries. For fixed units, the boiler-room guide says to test the alarm setpoints, test gases and sequence of operation at commissioning and to follow the maker for calibration and test intervals. It gives no sensor life. One room-detector listing states a life expectancy of 7 or more years with a 2-year warranty; that is one product. A maker's manual is the only place to get a replacement interval for your model.

Worked example: how long to reach the alarm

These are our own numbers with stated assumptions. We assume natural gas is methane with an LEL of 5 percent by volume (the CAMEO page lists 5.3, so this is a round, slightly conservative value), a mechanical room 30 ft by 20 ft with a 10 ft ceiling, a perfectly mixed room, no ventilation, and leak rates of 20 and 100 cubic feet per hour. A real room will not mix evenly, and fresh air will dilute the gas.

  1. Alarm level: 20 percent LEL = 0.20 × 5 percent = 1.0 percent gas by volume.
  2. In ppm: 1 percent = 10,000 ppm, so the alarm sits at 10,000 ppm of gas. (A 10 percent LEL setting would be 0.5 percent, or 5,000 ppm.)
  3. Room volume: 30 × 20 × 10 = 6,000 cubic feet.
  4. Gas needed: 0.01 × 6,000 = 60 cubic feet.
  5. At 20 cfh: 60 ÷ 20 = 3.0 hours. At 100 cfh: 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6 hour, about 36 minutes.

Result: with no ventilation and even mixing, a 20 cfh leak takes about 3 hours to reach 20 percent LEL in the whole room. Because natural gas rises, a ceiling sensor over the leak can see 20 percent LEL sooner than the room average. For propane, the same math with a 2.1 percent LEL gives 0.20 × 2.1 = 0.42 percent, or 4,200 ppm.

Portable detectors for techs

A tech who enters a boiler room, a basement or a mechanical room should carry a portable four-gas or combustible-plus-CO meter and bump test it before use, per the OSHA bulletin above. A portable meter reading a rising level is a reason to leave, call the gas utility or fire department, and not to hunt for the source with a lit tool. This is a general good practice that follows the daily check rule; follow your employer's procedures.

NJ note

New Jersey's fire code section N.J.A.C. 5:70-4.19 requires CO alarms in dwelling units of one- and two-family and attached single-family buildings, listed to UL 2034 and installed under that section and NFPA 720, near sleeping areas. It is a dwelling rule, so it does not tell you how to equip a commercial boiler room. For a plant room, ask the local code official which code editions and detector requirements apply.

Field card: Gas and CO Detection in Boiler and Mechanical Rooms

Light gas high, heavy gas low, CO at the nose.

  • Natural gas is lighter than air: sensor near the ceiling. Propane is heavier: near the floor. CO: breathing height.
  • LEL is the thinnest burnable mixture. 20 percent LEL of 5 percent gas is 1 percent, or 10,000 ppm.
  • Catalytic bead sensors can be poisoned by silicones and sulfur; infrared sensors are not, but miss hydrogen.
  • Alarm points and relay actions come from the maker, the design and the adopted code.
  • CO limits: NIOSH 35 ppm average, 200 ppm ceiling, 1,200 ppm IDLH; OSHA PEL 50 ppm.
  • Bump test: confirms response. Calibration: confirms accuracy. Check portable meters before each day's use.
