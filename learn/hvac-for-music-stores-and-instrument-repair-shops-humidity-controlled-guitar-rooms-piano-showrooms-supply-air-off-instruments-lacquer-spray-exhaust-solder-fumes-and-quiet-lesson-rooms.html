HVAC for Music Stores and Instrument Repair Shops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A music store sells wood that moves, and a repair shop works with flammable finishes and hot solder. Both need more than a thermostat. This lesson covers the humidity-controlled guitar room, the piano showroom, keeping supply air off instruments, sensors on humidifiers and dehumidifiers, spray finishing exhaust, solder fume capture and quiet lesson rooms.

Check the rules where you work

Why wood instruments need steady humidity

An acoustic guitar is thin wood glued under tension. The National Park Service guidance on wooden objects says wood loses moisture quickly below about 35 percent relative humidity (RH) and can split, and below about 30 percent glue can dry out, joints can loosen and finishes can turn brittle. It also says wood can swell up to about 2 percent across the grain between 30 and 70 percent RH, enough to grow a 2-foot panel by almost half an inch. Above 70 percent, mold and insects become problems, and for fungus control the guidance says RH must never exceed 65 percent. Its general figure for most areas is 50 percent RH, plus or minus 5, with 35 to 40 percent acceptable in dry climates.

The same appendix warns that rapid RH changes cause severe damage, advises against shutting heating or cooling off at night, and suggests a humidistat that overrides the thermostat. For a store, that is the design brief: a steady band, all day, all year, including nights and closed days. Our own working target for a guitar room is 40 to 55 percent RH at ordinary room temperature. That is our reasoning from the ranges above, not a quote.

Homeowners get the household version of this advice in Pianos, Guitars and Wood Instruments at Home. We do not repeat it here. This lesson is about the commercial side.

The humidity-controlled guitar room

Many stores keep their acoustic guitars in a separate room or glass-walled vault, because holding 45 percent RH in the whole sales floor, with doors opening all day, is hard. The room approach is our own design idea, and it works only if the room is reasonably tight. A few points from our reasoning:

  • Keep the room small and the envelope tight. Moisture you add leaves with every air change, so less leakage means less water and steadier RH.
  • Run the room's conditioning all the time. The sources above warn against turning systems off at night. Put the humidifier and any dehumidifier on their own controls so they do not depend on the store's occupied schedule.
  • Pick equipment for the climate. In heating season the job is adding water. In humid summer weather the job is removing it, and a cooling coil may not hold RH low enough in a lightly loaded room. For types of dehumidifiers, see Desiccant Dehumidifiers.
  • Match the humidifier to the load. A small steam or evaporative unit can serve a guitar room. See Commercial Steam Humidifiers for dispersion and water quality.

Sensors: where they go and what they do

A humidifier is only as good as its humidistat or RH sensor. Our own placement rules: mount the sensor in the room at instrument height, on an interior wall, away from supply air, sunlight, a humidifier discharge and the door. A sensor in the airstream reads the wrong air and makes the humidifier short-cycle or overshoot. Add a second, independent logging RH gauge that the store staff can read, so a failed sensor shows up as a disagreement instead of a cracked top. Use a high-limit humidistat on the humidifier so it cannot push the room toward condensation on windows or cold walls.

Piano showrooms and keeping supply air off instruments

A piano is the biggest piece of wood in the building, and its soundboard and action are glued and restrained. The National Park Service wood appendix describes a wooden tabletop beside a radiator drying faster on its upper surface and cupping, which is the same mechanism that works on a soundboard. Our reading for a showroom:

  • Do not aim a supply diffuser at any instrument. Use diffusers that throw along the ceiling, or move the instruments instead of the diffuser.
  • Keep instruments off exterior walls and out of direct sun, where surface temperature and RH swing the most.
  • Keep return and transfer air paths clear so the room does not see a draft path across the display.
  • Keep the showroom's schedule steady. Setting back overnight changes RH, because warm air holds more water than cold air.

For the museum version of setpoints, drift and dew point, see Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control.

Worked example (our own): moisture a guitar room humidifier must add

This example is our own. The inputs are assumptions chosen for teaching, not measurements from any store.

Assumptions. Room 12 ft by 15 ft by 8 ft. Air change rate 0.5 per hour (leakage plus any ventilation; assumed). Indoor target 70 F and 45 percent RH. Outdoor winter air 20 F and 70 percent RH. Sea-level pressure of 14.696 psi. Air density of 0.075 lb per cubic foot (a common round figure). We ignore the moisture stored in the wood and fixtures and any moisture from people.

  1. Room volume. 12 x 15 x 8 = 1,440 cubic feet.
  2. Air per day. 1,440 x 0.5 = 720 cubic feet per hour, or 12 cfm. Over 24 hours that is 720 x 24 = 17,280 cubic feet. Mass: 17,280 x 0.075 = 1,296 lb of air per day.
  3. Moisture in each air stream. Using a standard saturation-pressure relation (we used a common Magnus-type formula), saturation pressure is about 0.362 psi at 70 F and 0.054 psi at 20 F. Vapor pressure is 0.45 x 0.362 = 0.163 psi indoors and 0.70 x 0.054 = 0.038 psi outdoors. Humidity ratio = 0.622 x p / (14.696 - p), giving about 0.00698 lb of water per lb of dry air indoors (49 grains per lb) and about 0.00160 outdoors (11 grains per lb).
  4. Difference. 0.00698 - 0.00160 = 0.00538 lb of water per lb of air.
  5. Water to add per day. 1,296 x 0.00538 = about 7.0 lb, which is about 0.84 gallon (7.0 / 8.34), or roughly 107 fluid ounces.

So under these assumptions the humidifier must evaporate about 7 lb of water a day, around 0.3 lb per hour. That figure scales directly with air leakage: at 1 air change per hour it is double, and a leaky door or an exhaust fan that pulls room air makes it much worse. Our number is a floor, because wood and fixtures also absorb moisture when the room is first brought up. Real units are sized with a margin and a manufacturer's capacity data. For the household version of this method see how much water a humidifier uses per day, if you want a second set of numbers.

Finishing and lacquer spraying in repair shops

Refinishing a guitar or touching up a piano means spraying solvent-based finish. OSHA's spray finishing rule, 29 CFR 1910.94(c), says spraying must be enclosed or confined in a spray booth or spray room. It requires the booth to be built to the related design requirements in the OSHA flammable-finishes rule, with no combustible material in the booth or its ducts. Total exhaust must be enough to dilute solvent vapor to at most 25 percent of its lower explosive limit. Clean make-up air must equal what the booth exhausts, and air entering through self-closing doors, dampers or louvers must not exceed 200 feet per minute unless the fan characteristics deliver the required airflow. Where make-up air is heated, the rule calls for at least 65 F at the point of entry when outdoor temperature may drop below 55 F, and no make-up air heating equipment may sit inside the booth. The rule also keeps exhaust ducts separate from other processes and from combustion chimneys and flues.

For a small booth with air-operated guns and crossdrafts up to 50 feet per minute, OSHA's table lists a design velocity of 150 feet per minute into the booth (range 125 to 175). That is a federal minimum for the booth face, so a home furnace return or the store's rooftop unit is not a substitute. Two things follow for the HVAC tech. First, the exhaust fan and the make-up air must be treated as one system, so a booth that starves for air in winter is a symptom to chase. Second, the booth and its fan are not general-comfort equipment, so work on them follows the shop's own safety rules and the local fire official. For fan interlocks, filters and heater details, read Spray Paint Booth HVAC.

Soldering fumes in electronics repair

Amplifier and pedal repair means soldering at a bench. A university safety page and a national laboratory work-practices sheet both say the main fume concern in electronic soldering is the flux, and that heated rosin flux can irritate the eyes and airways and cause or worsen asthma. The laboratory sheet adds that tin and lead fumes are normally not a concern at electronic soldering temperatures, that the fume rises into the operator's breathing zone, and that frequent soldering may need local exhaust. The university page recommends a capture hood or a benchtop extractor with a HEPA filter, and says that if local exhaust is not available, soldering should be done in a fume hood. Neither source gives airflow numbers, so we give none.

Our own reading for the shop: a capture point at the work beats room air changes. A bench extractor is a small piece of equipment with a filter that needs changing. If the extractor returns air to the room, the filter has to do the job, so ask the maker which type it is. Do not rely on the guitar room's supply air to push fumes away, since that also disturbs the humidity.

Lesson rooms need quiet air

Teaching rooms are small, with hard surfaces and instruments that people play and listen to closely. A rumbling diffuser or a noisy mixing box is heard in every quiet passage. Our own advice, not from a source: use low-velocity supply, oversize diffusers, lined or flexible duct runs near the room and fan equipment that is not in the next wall. Keep the room's supply and return from sharing a short path through a transfer opening, since that carries sound from one lesson to the next. For the noise-control tools used in studios, see Recording Studio and Broadcast Booth HVAC.

Field card: HVAC for Music Stores and Instrument Repair Shops

Steady RH for the wood, enclosed exhaust for the finish, capture for the solder, quiet air for the lessons.

  • Wood guidance: about 50 percent RH plus or minus 5; below 35 percent splits, below 30 percent glue and finish suffer.
  • Avoid fast swings; do not shut the room's control off at night.
  • Sensor on an interior wall, away from supply air; add a separate logging gauge.
  • Do not aim supply air at instruments or place them on outside walls.
  • Our own example: a 1,440 cubic foot room at 0.5 changes per hour needs about 7 lb of water a day (20 F, 70 percent RH outside; 70 F, 45 percent inside).
  • Spray finishing belongs in a booth; make-up air equals exhaust; 25 percent of lower explosive limit.
  • Solder flux: local capture at the bench.
