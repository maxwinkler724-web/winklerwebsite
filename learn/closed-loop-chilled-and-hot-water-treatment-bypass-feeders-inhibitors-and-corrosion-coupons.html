Closed-Loop Water Treatment: Feeders, Inhibitors and Coupons

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A closed chilled or hot water loop looks like it should last forever: the same water goes round and round. In practice it slowly loses water, takes on fresh water, and the metal inside keeps rusting unless someone checks the chemistry. This lesson covers why, what the common inhibitors do, how a bypass feeder adds chemical, and how a makeup meter and a corrosion coupon turn a "feels fine" loop into numbers.

Check the program in front of you

Check the program in front of you. Target ranges depend on the inhibitor product, the metals in the loop and the water treater's written program. The numbers below come from the sources named in each sentence and are teaching examples, not setpoints for your job. Follow the treater's label, the chemical's SDS and your site procedure.

Why a "closed" loop still corrodes

The Nalco Water Handbook (Veolia Water Technologies) chapter on closed recirculating systems lists the usual ways oxygen gets in: the surge tank surface, pump packings and the makeup water itself. It also notes that oxygen returns during shutdowns, then attacks metal when the system heats up again. Makeup is needed mainly when water leaks out or the system has been drained, because little evaporates. Mixed metals add galvanic corrosion; the handbook names cast iron, steel, copper, copper alloys and aluminum as the usual mix. Bacteria matter less than in an open tower, but the handbook notes that microorganisms can consume nitrite, so a non-oxidizing antimicrobial may be needed. Chardon Laboratories adds that bacteria can cause microbiologically influenced corrosion, that scale and sludge hurt heat transfer and flow, and that a glycol can break down into acidic byproducts.

Getting the air out is a separate job; see Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents. Treatment handles the oxygen that is already dissolved.

Common inhibitor programs

  • Nitrite. Chardon says nitrite reacts with steel to form a protective iron oxide film. The Nalco handbook gives 600 to 1,200 ppm as nitrite for steel at a pH above 7.0, with far higher numbers when copper or aluminum is present.
  • Molybdate. The same handbook gives 200 to 300 ppm as molybdate at a pH of 7.0 to 9.0 and warns against it with brine antifreezes.
  • Blends. A Chem-Aqua article on FMLink says closed-loop inhibitors are typically blends: nitrite with borate or other buffers, a copper inhibitor, dispersants and organic scale inhibitors, and that some areas limit nitrite and molybdate. A water-treatment vendor page (Delta Water Group) lists 15 to 25 ppm of an azole for copper and admiralty brass, and a mixed-metals blend at 150 to 250 ppm nitrite, 200 to 300 ppm molybdate and 5 to 15 ppm azole. Treat vendor ranges as one maker's example.
  • Government-derived ranges differ. An engineering-continuing-education text adapted from the federal Unified Facilities Criteria lists a nitrite-azole program at 600 to 1,000 ppm nitrite with pH 8.5 to 9.5 and at least 10 ppm tolyltriazole when copper is present, and a nitrite-molybdate-azole blend at much lower numbers. The point for a learner: the "right" ppm belongs to the product, so read its label.
  • Glycol loops. The Nalco handbook says glycol antifreezes are not compatible with chromate programs, so nonchromate inhibitors are used. Chardon says glycol systems need an inhibitor package compatible with the antifreeze, and a campus standard we read calls for premixing with demineralized water, not untreated city water. See Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops for the antifreeze side.

Boilers have their own maker limits for pH, hardness and approved products, covered in Water Quality for Modern Hot-Water Boilers. Open tower loops are a different problem; see Condenser Water Treatment.

The bypass (pot) feeder

A bypass feeder is a pressure tank piped across a pressure difference in the loop, such as across a pump, so some circulating flow runs through it. Neptune's bulletin describes it as a convenient way to introduce treatment chemicals into closed circulating systems, and says adding a filter bag kit lets it double as a side-stream filter. A campus design standard we read (University of Colorado Boulder) calls for a 2-gallon bypass feeder on closed systems under 1,000 gallons and a 5-gallon unit on larger ones; Central Washington University requires a pot feeder with normally closed isolation valves on both sides, and a side-stream filter sized for 5 percent of peak system flow. Those are those campuses' own specifications, not universal rules.

Adding chemical safely

Neptune's bulletin says the quick-opening cap binds under pressure and tells you to bleed pressure from the tank before removing it. Its current feeders are rated to 300 psi and 200 degrees F maximum. That bulletin does not give a full step list, so use the maker's manual and your site's lockout and chemical-handling procedure. In general terms: read the SDS for the product, wear the protective gear it calls for, isolate and relieve the feeder pressure before opening, pour in the measured dose, close, and open the valves slowly. After any addition, test and log the result.

Testing and filtering

Chardon says monthly testing is adequate for most systems and quarterly may suffice, but new systems or problem histories may need weekly or biweekly checks. Its example targets are a pH of 8 to 10 and total iron below 1 ppm, with the provider setting the specific numbers. The NIH Office of Research Facilities bulletin lists what to test (pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, metal ions, chloride, turbidity and inhibitor level) and gives a pH of 9.0 to 10.5 for carbon steel. The Chem-Aqua article suggests filtering about 2 to 5 percent of pumping capacity and says anaerobic bacteria should be zero. It also says any pitting-type corrosion is unacceptable, even when the average looks fine.

The makeup meter is a leak alarm

The federal-criteria-derived text names a totalizing makeup meter, read and logged periodically, as the preferred way to spot loss. It treats a loss of inhibitor of more than about 2 percent in four months (roughly 0.5 percent a month) as excessive. Falling inhibitor and falling conductivity together point to a leak; falling nitrite with steady conductivity points to bacteria eating the nitrite. Central Washington University's standard wants the system volume measured at fill and tagged on the makeup line, and its non-glycol makeup meter reports to the building automation system. A meter that suddenly runs faster is a leak finding itself.

Corrosion coupons and the mpy number

A coupon is a small clean metal strip, weighed before and after it sits in a rack on a side stream. Chem-Aqua's article suggests 90-day exposures at a flow of roughly 3 to 5 feet per second past the coupon. The corrosion rate in mils per year (mpy, where 1 mil is 0.001 inch) comes from weight loss, metal density, area and time. An ALS corrosion-coupon introduction gives the formula, and with weight loss in milligrams and area in square inches it reduces to:

mpy = 534 × weight loss (mg) ÷ (density (g/cm³) × area (in²) × hours)

Acceptable rates vary by source. Delta Water Group lists carbon steel below 0.2 mpy and copper below 0.1 mpy for chilled water, and below 1 mpy and 0.2 mpy for industrial systems. The other government-derived and university sources we read gave no mpy limit, so use your treater's contract language.

Worked example (Our own, inputs made up)

Our own arithmetic. A 2,500 gallon closed loop; a makeup meter reads 120 gallons over 30 days.

  1. Percent replaced per month: 120 ÷ 2,500 = 0.048 = 4.8 percent.
  2. Per year: 12 × 4.8 = 57.6 percent of the volume (1,440 gallons).
  3. Simple dilution model (Our own, assumes the water is well mixed and each month 4.8 percent leaves with its inhibitor and is replaced with untreated makeup, no chemical added): each month leaves 1 − 0.048 = 0.952 of the inhibitor.
  4. After 12 months: 0.95212 = 0.554, so about 55 percent of the original level remains. Starting at a made-up 1,000 ppm nitrite, month 1 is 952 ppm and month 12 is about 554 ppm.
  5. Compare: the federal-criteria-derived text calls about 0.5 percent a month excessive. A 4.8 percent monthly loss is nearly ten times that, a sign to hunt the leak.

Our own coupon: a mild steel strip, 3.000 in × 0.500 in × 0.0625 in (made up), loses 40 mg over 90 days.

  1. Area: 2 × (3 × 0.5 + 3 × 0.0625 + 0.5 × 0.0625) = 2 × 1.71875 = 3.4375 in² (ignoring any mounting hole).
  2. Hours: 90 × 24 = 2,160 h.
  3. Numerator: 534 × 40 = 21,360.
  4. Denominator: 7.86 × 3.4375 × 2,160 = 58,360.5.
  5. mpy = 21,360 ÷ 58,360.5 = 0.37 mpy.

Against the vendor chilled-water figure of 0.2 mpy for carbon steel, 0.37 is high; against the industrial figure of 1 mpy it passes. Same coupon, different yardstick, which is why you read the contract. Inspect the coupon for pits too.

Field card: Closed-Loop Water Treatment

Closed is not sealed: log the makeup, test the inhibitor, read the coupon.

  • Oxygen, leaks, mixed metals and bacteria all attack closed loops.
  • Inhibitor ranges belong to the product and metals; follow the treater's label.
  • Bleed pressure from a bypass feeder before opening; read the SDS, wear the PPE.
  • Makeup meter: 120 gal in 2,500 gal = 4.8 percent per month.
  • mpy = 534 × mg ÷ (density × in² × hours).
  • Pitting counts even when the average mpy looks low.
  • Glycol loops need a compatible inhibitor package.
