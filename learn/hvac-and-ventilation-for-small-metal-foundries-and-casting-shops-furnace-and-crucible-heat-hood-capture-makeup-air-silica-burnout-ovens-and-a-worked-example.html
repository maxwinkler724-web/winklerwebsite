HVAC and Ventilation for Small Metal Foundries and Casting Shops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A small foundry is a hot room with a fume problem. A crucible furnace throws heat at the people around it, melting metal puts fume in the air, mold sand and powders make silica dust, and a gas-fired furnace both burns room air and can make carbon monoxide. The HVAC job is to capture the fume at the furnace, replace the air that leaves, and keep the shop livable in winter. This lesson covers art bronze, aluminum and jewelry casting rooms and small commercial shops.

Know the limits of this lesson

This is general education. Furnace makers, the authority having jurisdiction and your site safety plan decide what is required. We cite worker-safety and university sources below, and where those sources give no number, we say so and keep numbers labeled as our own.

Where the heat and the hazards come from

University art-safety guides we read describe the same set of problems. Melting metal releases metal fume, and one guide says the lead and zinc in bronze are highly toxic in fume form. Furnaces may produce carbon monoxide. Pouring burns the binders in sand molds, and breaking up sand molds can release a lot of silica dust. Furnaces also give off high heat and infrared radiation, which a guide ties to heat stress, skin burns and possible cataracts. Another guide says infrared is especially intense in a foundry. A federal worker-health research page on foundry controls lists respirable crystalline silica as the main concern, with carbon monoxide, metal fumes and mold-material decomposition products also in the mix.

Radiant heat: it heats people, not air

Radiant heat travels in straight lines and warms whatever it lands on, including a worker's skin, before it warms the room air. That is why a room thermostat can read fine while the person at the furnace is overheating. The federal worker-health research page on heat stress lists indoor radiant heat sources, lack of acclimatization and not drinking enough as risk factors, and says employers should use engineering controls and work-practice controls and train workers. It gives no temperatures or water amounts, so we do not either. For HVAC people, engineering controls mean things like shielding between the furnace and the work area, spot cooling or a cooler break area, and keeping fresh makeup air flowing past the pouring crew. Pouring in pairs, preheating tools and moving slowly with a crucible are work practices from the university guides.

Capture the fume where it starts

One university safety guide says plainly that the furnace should be locally exhausted to remove combustion gases and fume from molten metal, and that the pouring area needs local exhaust too. The same guide explains why: capture drops off dramatically as the hood gets farther from the source, so the hood belongs close, and you should confirm airflow before work starts and test capture with smoke or an air-current tube. A canopy hood over a furnace works because hot fume rises. It works poorly if a cross draft from a door or a burner-room fan blows the plume sideways. Another university guide says to confirm the exhaust is working before each use and not to look into the exhaust while it runs. Neither guide gives a required airflow, so we do not state one as a rule. Use the furnace maker's instructions and a designer's sizing, then verify with smoke.

The federal worker-health research page gives a useful caution for dust: in a sand casting-cleaning study, a downdraft booth reduced worker exposure 60 to 90 percent, depending on tool, and modeling of a shake-out area found floor exhaust captured the contaminant while overhead exhaust had no clear effect. Silica fume and dust are not the same as furnace fume, so pick the hood to match where the contaminant goes. For the related kiln-room side of this topic, see our pottery kiln room lesson. We do not repeat that material here.

Silica from sand and powders

The federal worker-safety agency's silica page lists industrial sand used in foundry work among the sources of respirable crystalline silica. The federal worker-health research page gives a recommended exposure limit of 0.05 mg/m³ as an 8-hour average, and says measured exposures in one casting-cleaning job were 124 to 160 micrograms per cubic meter, well above it. One university guide advises silica-free sand where possible and mixing under local exhaust or with respiratory protection. Treat any dry powder or sand the same way: check its safety data sheet, mix it in an exhausted spot, and keep dust out of the shop air handlers. Good filtration on a recirculating unit does not replace capture at the source. For burnout ovens and investment powder in small jewelry and dental work, read our dental laboratory lesson. The university guides we read did not cover burnout directly, so we treat it here only as a heat and exhaust load: give the oven the venting the maker requires, and do not let it release into the occupied room.

Makeup air: the part that makes everything else work

Every cubic foot per minute the hood exhausts has to come back in from somewhere. If it does not, the building runs negative. Doors become hard to open, the hood loses capture, and a gas furnace or water heater in the same space can be starved or have its flue gases pulled back into the room. See combustion air for furnaces, boilers and water heaters for the basics. Carbon monoxide is the reason to take this seriously: the university guides list furnaces in metal casting as a source, and a guide pairs furnaces with local exhaust. Fuel-burning gear still needs its own combustion air supply and venting as the maker specifies. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do for what to do when an alarm sounds.

In design terms, a common approach is to tie the makeup-air fan to the exhaust fan so one cannot run without the other, bring the air in low and slowly near the work so it does not blow the plume off the hood, and heat it in winter. A burner flame and a hood are both sensitive to drafts. This is our own reasoning, not a rule from the sources, so follow the equipment instructions and local codes.

Worked example (our own): heating the makeup air, and the furnace heat

This is our own example with assumed inputs. It is not from any source and not a design. The 1.08 factor is the usual rule for standard air: 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb-°F × 60 min/h = 1.08.

Assumptions: a hood exhausts 2,000 cfm. One gas-fired crucible furnace has 200,000 Btu/h input and uses about 12 ft³ of air per ft³ of gas (our estimate, including excess air). Gas has about 1,000 Btu/ft³. Outdoor air is 15°F in winter and we want 65°F supply, a 50°F rise.

  1. Combustion air: 200,000 ÷ 1,000 = 200 ft³/h of gas. 200 × 12 = 2,400 ft³/h of air, which is 40 cfm.
  2. Total makeup: 2,000 + 40 = 2,040 cfm.
  3. Heat to warm it: 1.08 × 2,040 × 50 = 110,160 Btu/h, about 110,000 Btu/h.

Radiant plus convective heat from the furnace shell. Assume a cylindrical shell 3 ft in diameter and 3 ft tall, 400°F surface, 70°F surroundings, emissivity 0.8 (assumed), and a still-air convection coefficient of 1.5 Btu/h-ft²-°F (assumed). We ignore the lid and base.

  1. Side area: π × 3 × 3 = 28.3 ft².
  2. Absolute temperatures: 400 + 460 = 860 R and 70 + 460 = 530 R.
  3. Radiant: 0.1714×10-8 × 0.8 × 28.3 × (8604 − 5304) = 0.1714×10-8 × 0.8 × 28.3 × 4.68×1011 = about 18,100 Btu/h.
  4. Convective: 1.5 × 28.3 × (400 − 70) = about 14,000 Btu/h.
  5. Total: about 32,100 Btu/h, of which radiant is about 56 percent.

Check: 32,100 ÷ 110,160 is about 0.29, so even if all the shell heat stayed in the shop, it covers under a third of the makeup-air heating need. A hood carries much of the convective part outdoors, so do not count on it. The 18,100 Btu/h of radiant heat is the part that lands on the crew. Real furnaces have hotter lids, open pour ports and different shell temperatures, so measure before sizing anything.

Winter makeup air heating

As the example shows, heating the makeup air is a big load. Common choices are a gas-fired or hydronic makeup-air unit that tempers the air, or an indirect heater that keeps the combustion products out of the airstream. Fuel-fired equipment in a shop with dust and fume needs the combustion-air and venting steps in its instructions. We do not recommend a product. Interlocking the makeup-air unit with the hood so the supply starts when the hood runs also avoids blowing cold air on an idle shop.

Field card: HVAC and Ventilation for Small Metal Foundries and Casting Shops

Capture at the source, replace what you exhaust, protect the crew from radiant heat.

  • Furnace and pouring area both need local exhaust, with the hood close to the source.
  • Test capture with smoke before work starts.
  • Silica sources include sand and powders; mix and handle them under exhaust.
  • Makeup air equals exhaust plus combustion air; heat it with 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise.
  • Our example: 2,040 cfm and a 50°F rise is about 110,000 Btu/h.
  • Radiant heat warms people first; shield, spot cool, rest and drink water.
  • Gas furnaces need their own combustion air and venting; use carbon monoxide alarms.
