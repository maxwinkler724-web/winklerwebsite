Nail Salon and Beauty Shop Ventilation: Source Capture, General Exhaust and Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A nail salon is a small room with a lot of solvent and plastic-monomer vapor and a lot of people breathing it all day. A ceiling fan and an open door are not a ventilation system. This lesson covers what is in the air, why the exhaust has to start at the table, how the outdoor air and makeup air work out, and how to keep the shop from sending odor next door.

Check the local code

Check the code that applies to the shop. Salon ventilation rules differ by state and city, and they change. This page is general education built from federal pages, a public code summary and published studies read in 2026. The rates below come from the sources named, and the worked example uses stated assumptions of our own. Confirm requirements with the local building and licensing authorities before you design or price anything.

What is in the air

OSHA's nail salon page says polishes, glues and other products may contain toluene, formaldehyde, dibutyl phthalate and methacrylate compounds. NIOSH's nail technician page adds acetone and ethyl acetate to its list and says workers can breathe these in, absorb them through skin or swallow them. OSHA's worker guide describes acetone as a cause of headaches, dizziness and eye, skin and throat irritation. It links toluene to headaches, dizziness and organ damage, says formaldehyde can cause cancer, and lists methyl methacrylate and ethyl methacrylate in artificial nail products with possible effects such as asthma and irritated eyes, skin and airways.

For the HVAC tech, the point is the mix. Some of these are solvent vapors that evaporate from an open bottle or a soaked cotton ball. Others are dust from filing. The same guide says ventilation is the best way to lower chemical levels in the salon and tells workers to keep containers closed, which cuts the load before the fan ever sees it.

Why ceiling exhaust alone fails

A single exhaust grille high in the room pulls air from everywhere at once, so the air at the table barely moves toward it. The vapor rises off the work, passes the technician's face, and mixes into the room before the fan can take it out. That is why published code language and studies keep returning to capture at the source. The New York rule, as summarized by the Environmental Law Institute, requires an exhaust system designed to capture vapors, fumes and dust at their source and to vent them outdoors. Each station needs an inlet no more than 12 inches horizontally or vertically from the point of chemical application.

A NIOSH study of salon air in New York City (Pavilonis and coauthors, 2018) checked ten salons against that kind of specification. The authors state that none of the salons in the sample met the source capture specifications, and none had local exhaust installed. Only seven of the ten were running their HVAC system during sampling. The others relied on open doors. The authors also found contaminant levels fairly even across each room, which they read as evidence that general mixing matters too. So the right design has both layers: capture where the vapor is made, and general exhaust and outdoor air for what escapes.

Source capture: tables and portable units

A ventilated table pulls air down or back through a grille beside the work and carries it away from the breathing zone. NIOSH describes downdraft tables as designed to vent dust or chemical exposures away from the breathing zone, and its nail technician page lists portable units as an option that can filter air or exhaust it outdoors. A 2012 NIOSH laboratory report tested downdraft tables and portable capture systems with tracer gas and found the potential to reduce exposure by at least 50 to 60 percent. It also noted that its test arrangement had not been proven acceptable to technicians in working salons, and it called for more study of flow rates and filtration. Treat those numbers as a lab result, not a promise for a given product.

Recirculating units that only filter the air are not the same as ducted exhaust. The International Code Council's code summary says the codes it covers expect exhausted air to go outdoors and not be recirculated to any space. If a portable unit filters and returns the air, what it does not catch, vapor-wise, comes back. We recommend asking the authority who enforces the code whether a recirculating unit is accepted before you rely on one.

Outdoor air and general exhaust rates

ASHRAE Standard 62.1 is the ventilation standard many codes build on; we name it here and do not copy it. The ICC CodeNotes page on nail salon exhaust, based on the 2021 International Mechanical Code, gives the table row for nail salons: 25 people per 1,000 sq ft, 20 cfm of outdoor air per person plus 0.12 cfm per sq ft, and an exhaust rate of 0.6 cfm per sq ft. A footnote requires source capture at each manicure and pedicure station, at least 50 cfm each. If that source capture runs continuously while the shop is occupied, its flow can count toward the room's exhaust rate. The same summary says return air from the salon may go back only to the same salon, and that existing buildings must follow the current code when ventilation is replaced.

New York's rule, per the ELI summary, sets exhaust and supply each at the greater of the IMC rate or 50 cfm per nail station, balances outdoor air to equal the exhaust rate, and requires the system to run any time someone is in the shop. New Jersey's Board of Cosmetology and Hairstyling rule at N.J.A.C. 13:28-3.1 says licensed shops must be properly lighted and ventilated, and sends shops licensed after May 18, 2009 to N.J.A.C. 5:23, the Uniform Construction Code, for ventilation. We did not find a number in the board rule itself. See Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics for how those outdoor air tables are read.

Keeping the salon negative to the next tenant

In a strip mall the shop shares walls, ceiling space and sometimes a roof unit with its neighbors. The code summary's rule on return air tells us half of the answer: the salon's air may not be returned to a system serving other tenants. Our own reasoning supplies the rest. If the shop is slightly negative, air leaks into it through gaps in the demising wall instead of out, so odor stays with the people who make it. That means the exhaust has to run a little above the outdoor air brought in, and the walls and ceiling plenum above the demising wall need to be sealed. The source summaries give no pressure target, so we do not state one; check the local requirements. Building Pressurization and Stack Effect explains why a front door fights you, and Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors covers capture velocity, which applies to table hoods the same way.

Makeup air and odor complaints

Every cfm exhausted must come back in somewhere. If we do not supply it on purpose, it arrives through the front door, gaps and the neighbors' walls. That is how a negative salon becomes a salon with a door that is hard to open, a whistling storefront, and odor complaints in both directions. New York requires supply balanced to the exhaust rate, which is the same idea. Supply it at the front, away from the tables, and heat or temper it in winter, because cold air dumped on a manicurist's hands is how supply gets switched off. Odor complaints usually trace back to four things: exhaust that is off or undersized, a supply that is blocked, a door propped open, or an outlet too near a neighbor's intake. The code summary sets exhaust outlet distances: 10 ft from property lines, 10 ft from operable openings and 10 ft above grade, and 3 ft from walls and roofs.

Filter and maintenance schedule

OSHA's worker guide says to change the charcoal filters in ventilated tables at least once a month, to clean the catch basin at least once a week, and to replace HVAC filters at least once a year. It also says that if there is no exhaust system, the HVAC fan switch should be on and not on auto. Our own addition: a charcoal filter that is saturated stops adsorbing and may just pass vapor, so a change date on a label at each table beats waiting for a smell. See Commercial Kitchen Ventilation Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics for how heated makeup air is handled on a hood system.

Worked example: our own salon

These are assumptions, not a design. The shop has 1,200 sq ft and 8 manicure stations. We use the ICC CodeNotes rates (2021 IMC) above: 25 people per 1,000 sq ft, 20 cfm per person, 0.12 cfm per sq ft, 0.6 cfm per sq ft exhaust, 50 cfm per station for source capture. Winter design outdoor temperature is 15°F and the shop is held at 68°F (both our own assumptions, so delta T is 68 minus 15 = 53°F). The 10 percent exhaust-over-supply offset is also our own assumption.

  1. Source capture: 8 stations x 50 cfm = 400 cfm.
  2. Room exhaust: 1,200 sq ft x 0.6 cfm/sq ft = 720 cfm. Because the source capture runs continuously, the code summary lets its 400 cfm count toward this, so the general exhaust grille at the back handles 720 − 400 = 320 cfm.
  3. Occupancy: 1,200 sq ft x 25 per 1,000 = 30 people.
  4. Outdoor air: 30 x 20 cfm = 600 cfm, plus 1,200 x 0.12 = 144 cfm, for 744 cfm total.
  5. Outdoor air (744 cfm) is higher than the table exhaust (720 cfm), and we want the shop negative. We set the total exhaust to 744 cfm x 1.10 = about 820 cfm, so the supply fan provides 744 cfm and about 76 cfm is drawn in through leaks, mostly from the outside.
  6. Winter heat to bring 820 cfm from 15°F to 68°F, using the sensible heat rule of thumb (our own): 1.08 x 820 cfm x 53°F = 46,937 Btu/h. If only the 744 cfm supply is heated: 1.08 x 744 x 53 = 42,587 Btu/h.

The point is the load: exhaust is not free, and heat recovery or a smaller exhaust, where the code allows it, changes the answer. Confirm all rates against the code that applies.

Field card: Nail Salon Ventilation

Capture at the table, exhaust outdoors, supply on purpose.

  • Vapors: acetone, toluene, formaldehyde, methacrylates (OSHA, NIOSH).
  • Source capture at each station, inlet within 12 inches of the work (ICC summary, New York rule).
  • ICC summary: 50 cfm per station, 20 cfm per person plus 0.12 cfm per sq ft outdoor air, 0.6 cfm per sq ft exhaust.
  • Return air from the salon stays in the salon; exhaust goes outdoors.
  • Keep it slightly negative to neighbors; supply what you exhaust.
  • OSHA guide: table charcoal filters monthly, catch basin weekly, HVAC filters yearly.
  • Our own: heat load = 1.08 x cfm x delta T.
