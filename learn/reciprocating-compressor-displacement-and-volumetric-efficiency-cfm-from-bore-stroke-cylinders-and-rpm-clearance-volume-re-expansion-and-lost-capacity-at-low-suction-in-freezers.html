Reciprocating Compressor Displacement and Volumetric Efficiency

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A reciprocating compressor is a set of piston pumps. How much gas it can move depends on its size and speed, and on how much of each stroke is lost to gas trapped in the cylinder. This lesson shows the cfm math, explains why capacity falls as suction pressure drops, and works one example of our own.

Displacement: the size of the pump

Displacement is the volume the pistons sweep in a given time. Each cylinder sweeps a circle the size of the bore times the stroke length, and the compressor has several cylinders turning at a set speed. In inches, the arithmetic is:

  • Swept volume per cylinder, in cubic inches = 0.7854 × bore × bore × stroke.
  • Per revolution = swept volume per cylinder × number of cylinders.
  • Per minute = per revolution × rpm. Divide by 1,728 to convert cubic inches to cubic feet, which gives cfm.

The university lecture notes we read describe displacement volume as the difference between the cylinder volume at the bottom and top of the stroke, and apply it to the number of intake strokes per unit time. The bore-and-stroke formula above is just that same idea written for a round cylinder. Displacement is a size rating. It is not the amount of refrigerant actually delivered.

Clearance volume and re-expansion

At the top of the stroke the piston cannot touch the valve plate. A small space is left over, called clearance volume. It includes the gap above the piston and the valve ports. When the piston reaches the top, that space is still full of discharge-pressure gas.

On the way back down, that trapped gas expands. Until its pressure falls to suction pressure, the suction valve cannot open, so no new gas enters. Only the rest of the stroke draws in fresh vapor. The lecture notes define a clearance fraction as clearance volume divided by displacement volume, and give a volumetric efficiency for the ideal, clearance-only case:

Efficiency = 1 + C − C × (discharge pressure / suction pressure) ^ (1/n)

Here C is the clearance fraction, pressures are absolute, and n is an exponent for the compression and expansion, near 1.1 to 1.3 for halocarbon refrigerants in the source, with 1.2 used in its example. Look at the last term. As the pressure ratio rises, it grows, so efficiency falls. The source states that efficiency falls to zero when the clearance gas expands to fill the whole cylinder, and that efficiency is highest when the two pressures are equal.

Why capacity falls with a high compression ratio

Compression ratio is absolute discharge pressure divided by absolute suction pressure. In a freezer the suction pressure is low, so the ratio is large even when the head pressure is ordinary. Two things then work against the compressor, as the source explains:

  1. Efficiency drops. More of the stroke goes to re-expanding trapped gas, so less new gas enters.
  2. Each cubic foot of gas is thinner. Low suction pressure means low suction density, so each cfm drawn in carries less refrigerant mass.

Mass flow equals efficiency times displacement rate, divided by the specific volume of the suction gas (specific volume is the inverse of density). Both factors shrink together as suction pressure falls, so refrigerating capacity can fall much faster than the pressure does. The same notes say the real efficiency is lower than the ideal clearance-only value because of heat transfer, valve pressure drops and leakage.

Superheat, heat pickup and suction density

Hotter suction gas is less dense. The source treats superheat only through its effect on the specific volume at the compressor inlet, and notes that heat picked up from the compressor shell and pressure drop across the inlet valve both raise specific volume above what it was at the evaporator. For the apprentice, the practical points are these:

  • Superheat measured at the compressor is higher than at the evaporator outlet because of line heat pickup, and that lowers the mass delivered for the same cfm.
  • A restriction in the suction line or strainer lowers pressure at the valves, which has the same thin-gas effect.
  • Whether more superheat is acceptable is limited by the maker. Follow the compressor maker's published limits and do not use this lesson to set them.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example with made-up but plausible numbers. It does not describe any particular compressor.

Assumptions: 4 cylinders, bore 3.0 in, stroke 2.5 in, 1,750 rpm. We also assume two volumetric efficiencies: 0.85 for a medium-temperature job (low compression ratio) and 0.55 for a freezer job (high compression ratio). These two efficiencies are assumed, not measured. For comparison, using the clearance-only formula with an assumed clearance fraction of 0.05 and n = 1.2, a pressure ratio of 4 gives about 0.89 and a ratio of 12 gives about 0.65, so our assumed real values sit below the ideal ones, as the source says they should.

  1. Swept volume per cylinder = 0.7854 × 3.0 × 3.0 × 2.5 = 17.67 in³.
  2. Per revolution = 17.67 × 4 = 70.69 in³.
  3. Per minute = 70.69 × 1,750 = 123,700 in³.
  4. Displacement = 123,700 / 1,728 = about 71.6 cfm.
  5. Delivered vapor at 0.85 = 71.6 × 0.85 = about 60.8 cfm.
  6. Delivered vapor at 0.55 = 71.6 × 0.55 = about 39.4 cfm.

The same compressor at the same speed moves about 35 percent less vapor in the second case (39.4 / 60.8 = 0.65). And that is cfm measured at suction conditions. At the low suction pressure the gas is also less dense, so the refrigerant mass flow drops by more than 35 percent. For instance, if the freezer's suction gas were half as dense as the medium-temperature job's (a number we picked only for illustration), mass flow would be about 0.65 × 0.5 = 0.33 of the first case, roughly one third.

What this means in a freezer

  • Capacity ratings are given for stated suction and discharge conditions. A compressor that does fine in a cooler may be short on capacity in a freezer.
  • Pull-down after defrost, or after loading warm product, is slow because suction pressure is high at first and then falls as the box cools, and capacity follows it down.
  • High head pressure from a dirty condenser raises the ratio and costs capacity at exactly the time the load is largest.
  • Low suction pressure plus high compression ratio also drives discharge temperature up. See the related lesson on discharge line temperature rather than repeating it here.

If you suspect the compressor itself is weak, a high ratio is one explanation, and leaking valves are another. Rule out the ratio first, then see the valve testing lesson linked below.

Field card: Reciprocating Compressor Displacement and Volumetric Efficiency

Displacement is the size. Volumetric efficiency is how much of it you get.

  • Displacement (cfm) = 0.7854 × bore² × stroke × cylinders × rpm / 1,728, with bore and stroke in inches.
  • Delivered cfm = displacement × volumetric efficiency.
  • Clearance gas must re-expand before new gas enters, so a higher ratio means lower efficiency.
  • Low suction means a higher ratio and thinner gas, so capacity drops twice.
  • Compression ratio uses absolute pressures.
  • Heat pickup and suction restrictions thin the gas further.
