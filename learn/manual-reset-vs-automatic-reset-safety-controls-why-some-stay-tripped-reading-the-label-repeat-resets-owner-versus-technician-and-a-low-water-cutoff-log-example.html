Manual Reset vs Automatic Reset Safety Controls: Why Some Stay Tripped

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some safety controls reset themselves when the problem goes away. Others stay open until a person pushes a button. That difference is deliberate, and it changes what you should do when a heating system quits. This lesson explains both types, how to tell them apart, and why pressing reset again and again is the wrong move.

Two ways a safety control can behave

A safety control is a switch wired into the control circuit that opens when something unsafe happens: too hot, too little water, flame in the wrong place, too much pressure. Once it opens, the burner or heating element stops. What happens next depends on the type.

  • Automatic reset. The switch closes again by itself when the condition clears, for example when a hot sensor cools. The equipment may restart with nobody looking at it.
  • Manual reset. The switch stays open, even after the condition clears, until someone presses a button or flips a lever. Nothing restarts until a person does that.

Why a manual reset exists

The point of a manual reset is to force a person to look. Some conditions are serious enough that the equipment should not restart on its own, because the cause may still be there or may come back. A low water level in a boiler is a typical case, which is why the low-water cutoff is often built to need a hand reset. Our related lesson on boiler flame safeguards, purge and lockout describes a flame safeguard lockout as a safety shutdown that needs a manual reset, and warns that repeated resetting hides the problem and can pile up unburned fuel. Our flame rollout switch lesson notes that many rollout switches are manual reset and some are automatic, so you check the manual for that model.

Which controls are often manual reset

Each model is different, so the label and the manufacturer's manual decide. As a study list, the controls that are commonly manual reset include flame rollout switches, flame safeguard lockouts on burner controls, and low-water cutoffs on some boilers. A university campus public works procedure for a steam boiler tells the operator to reset the low-water cutoff switch by hand after a test, and to reset the high limit and low-water cutoff before lighting the burner (see Sources). High limits can be either type, so do not assume. For why a limit trips at all, read high limit trips and airflow.

How to read the label or button

  • Look for the words. "Manual reset," "reset," or "push to reset" printed on the control or its bracket is the first clue. "Auto" or "automatic reset" means it resets itself.
  • Look for a button or lever. A small button, often red, that you can press or that pops out when tripped, usually means a manual reset. A bare switch body with no button may be automatic, but that is not proof.
  • Check the wiring diagram and manual. The diagram on the equipment or in the manual names each safety and often says how it resets. If the label is faded or missing, treat it as unknown and ask a technician.
  • Watch for lockout lights or codes. A burner control that shows a lockout light or code has stopped on purpose. Our lessons treat that as a manual reset condition, so the cause matters more than the button.

Why repeated resetting is dangerous

A reset button is not a repair. If a safety trips again right after you reset it, the cause is still there. Our rollout lesson summarizes one manufacturer's advice that a single trip deserves attention and that several trips in a season mean you need a technician, not a reset button. Each extra reset also asks the equipment to light again, and the boiler lesson above points out that unburned fuel can pile up. Using a jumper or tape to defeat the control is worse: it removes the protection and hides the warning.

The federal consumer product safety agency urges a yearly professional inspection of all fuel-burning home heating systems, including furnaces and boilers, and a state university extension guide lists warning signs such as odors, soot at the burner, black smoke from the chimney or surging water in a boiler gauge, and says to get professional help when you see them.

What an owner does and what a technician does

Owner

  • Check the simple things: thermostat setting, power switch, and whether a filter or vent is plainly blocked.
  • Read the equipment manual to see whether the owner is allowed to reset the control at all. If the manual allows one try, try it once.
  • If it trips again, stop. Leave it off and call a technician. If you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave the building first and call for help from outside.
  • Never bypass, tape or jump a safety, and never keep pressing reset.

Technician

  • Lock out and tag the power before opening panels. The federal workplace safety standard on hazardous energy (29 CFR 1910.147) is meant to protect workers from unexpected start up and stored energy during servicing, and its sample procedure ends with checking the area, confirming controls are in neutral, removing locks and notifying affected workers.
  • Find out why the control opened before resetting it. Measure, do not guess.
  • Test low-water protection the way the maker and your site procedure describe. The university procedure above tests the low-water cutoff by blowing down at the drain and watching the burner shut off, then resets it by hand and confirms the burner relights. A federal energy-management maintenance guide also lists checking the water level control by letting it stop fuel flow, and calls for staff trained on the safety devices.
  • Leave the safety working. Do not jumper it to finish a call.

Worked example (our own): low-water cutoff trips and makeup water

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not real data. A small hot-water boiler has a manual-reset low-water cutoff and a makeup water meter. The owner reset the cutoff three times in one week and wrote down how many gallons the technician or owner had to add to bring the water back to the normal level.

Assumptions: the closed heating loop holds about 30 gallons in total; each gallon added equals a gallon that was lost since the last fill; no one drained the system on purpose that week.

DayCutoff trip?Makeup added (gal)
MondayYes1
WednesdayYes2
FridayYes4

Step 1, total added: 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 gallons for the week.

Step 2, average per trip: 7 gallons / 3 trips = about 2.3 gallons per trip.

Step 3, share of the system: 7 / 30 = 0.23, so about 23 percent of the loop volume was replaced in one week. A tight closed loop should need very little makeup water, so that is a lot.

Step 4, the trend: the amounts went 1, then 2, then 4. Each is double the one before (1 x 2 = 2, 2 x 2 = 4), and the gaps between trips were two days each. If the pattern holds, the loss is speeding up.

What the pattern suggests: the cutoff is doing its job and water is really leaving the system, since water had to be added each time. A growing loss points to a leak that is getting worse, an open or stuck relief valve, or a makeup valve that is not feeding correctly. It does not prove which one. It also argues against the idea that the cutoff is simply faulty. The next step belongs to a technician: look for wet spots and relief valve discharge, and check the makeup valve and cutoff. Resetting a fourth time and adding more water would hide the answer.

Field card: Manual reset vs automatic reset safety controls

A manual reset exists so a person looks. Look.

  • Automatic reset closes itself when the condition clears; manual reset stays open until pressed.
  • Often manual: flame rollout switches, flame safeguard lockouts, low-water cutoffs on some boilers. Check each model.
  • Read the label: "manual reset," a red button or lever, the wiring diagram, the manual.
  • If it trips again after one reset, the cause is still there. Stop.
  • Never jump, tape or bypass a safety.
  • Owner: simple checks, one reset only if the manual allows, then call a technician.
  • Technician: lock out, find the cause, test the control, restore it to working order.
