Heating and Cooling Shipping Container Offices and Conversions

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steel shipping container is a strong box, but as a room it is a poor one. Steel moves heat easily, the thin shell holds little insulation, and the air inside sweats when it touches cold metal. This lesson walks through why container offices and job site boxes run hot, cold and wet, and how to size and plan the heating and cooling.

Why a steel box behaves this way

Steel is a good conductor, so an uninsulated container wall is mostly an air film on each side of a thin metal sheet. On a sunny day the roof and walls can get far hotter than the air, and that heat walks straight through. At night the same steel gets cold, and heat leaves just as fast. A bare container is also leaky at the doors and any cut openings, so air moves through it without help.

The federal worker-safety agency lists heat-absorbing roofs among the heat sources behind indoor heat illness, and it says hazardous heat exposure can occur indoors in any season. A closed steel box in summer sun fits that description. Its page on controls names air conditioning, general ventilation, fans, shielding and insulation as engineering controls, with shade or a cooled place for rest breaks. For anyone working inside a container during a hot spell, cooling is a safety item, not only a comfort one. We also cover heat on the crew in heat stress in attics and on roofs.

Insulation choices and thermal bridging

A federal insulation guide lists typical R-values per inch: roughly 3 to 4 for fiberglass batts, about 5 for extruded foam board, 5.6 to 8 for polyisocyanurate board, and 7 to 9 for closed-cell spray foam. It also says air sealing comes first, because air moving through or around insulation cuts its effect, and that compressed or poorly fitted batts lose value. In a container, that points toward materials that fill the corrugations and seal air paths. See home insulation types explained for how each type behaves.

Thermal bridging is heat taking the easy path around insulation. In a container, the steel corner posts, rails and any added steel studs run from outside to inside. Insulation between studs does not stop heat moving along the metal. The same federal guide notes that continuous rigid foam blocks thermal bridging. Our practical reading: a continuous layer over the frame, even if thin, usually does more than thick insulation between studs. Because bridges and gaps lower the real result, we use an effective U-factor lower than the foam alone would suggest in the example below.

Condensation, especially on the roof underside

A university extension article on metal-walled buildings explains the rule: condensation forms when the inside surface of the metal is colder than the dew point of the indoor air. Insulating the inside of the metal stops condensation on the surface of the insulation but can leave it forming between the insulation and the steel. Preventing that hidden condensation takes a properly installed vapor barrier or closed-cell insulation that seals against the steel. The article also says ventilation is the primary way to reduce condensation potential, and warns that a vapor barrier must be protected from punctures.

For a container, the roof underside is the worst spot. Overnight the roof can cool below room dew point and drip. Insulation that is not sealed to the steel can let moist air reach that cold surface. A person with air conditioning running at night also adds moisture by breathing, so we recommend sealing the insulation to the steel and keeping the unit in dehumidifying operation when the box is closed up. See also condensate drains in the related list below if the unit itself drips.

Choosing equipment: through-wall and ductless units

A federal field report on ductless heat pumps describes them as inverter-driven systems that run at varied output, reporting efficiency ratings in the range of 10 to 12 heating and 20 to 25 cooling, and notes that heat distribution can be uneven when doors are closed. A container office is one small room, so a single indoor head fits. The tradeoff we see is that the smallest ductless heads are often larger than a well-insulated container needs, which can lead to short cycling. Through-wall units are simpler and cut a smaller opening, with condensate and noise issues covered in modular and portable office buildings. For general ductless background, see mini-split basics and the very small home lesson, heating and cooling tiny houses.

For heating, a heat pump covers mild weather, and electric resistance heat is an option for very cold nights. Fuel-burning heaters inside a sealed steel box raise combustion air and carbon monoxide questions, so we leave them out of this lesson.

Cutting openings without weakening the box

The corrugated side panels, corner posts and rails work together as a structure, which is why a container can be stacked. Cutting a window, door or unit opening removes part of that shell. We did not find a federal or university source with cutting rules, so we only say this: ask the box owner or a structural engineer before cutting, keep cuts away from the corner posts and the top and bottom rails, frame every opening, and seal and paint raw edges against rust.

Ventilation for people inside

A tight, insulated box needs fresh air, because people add carbon dioxide and moisture. Many ductless heads and through-wall units recirculate room air and do not bring in outdoor air on their own. A small exhaust fan, a unit with an outdoor air damper, or a heat-recovery ventilator can supply it. The federal worker-safety page says that more air flow raises evaporative cooling, but it also depends on the air not being hot. Keep intake air away from exhaust, generators and vehicles.

Electrical feed limits

Job site containers often run from a small generator or a temporary power pedestal. A compressor starting draws more than it does when running, so a small feed can trip or the voltage can sag. Use the equipment nameplate for its minimum circuit ampacity and maximum overcurrent device, and have a licensed electrician confirm that the feed, wire and breaker match. Add lights, outlets and heaters to the total before choosing the unit. We do not give wire or breaker sizes here, and local rules decide them.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for teaching, not a measured or published value, except where a source is named.

  • Standard 20 ft container: outside 20 ft long, 8 ft wide, 8.5 ft high.
  • Roof area: 20 x 8 = 160 sq ft. Wall area (two sides plus two ends, doors and openings ignored): 2 x (20 x 8.5) + 2 x (8 x 8.5) = 340 + 136 = 476 sq ft. Floor ignored (shaded, on blocks).
  • Indoor 75 F. Sun-heated roof surface 150 F (assumed). Wall surface 105 F (assumed). Temperature difference: roof 150 - 75 = 75 F; walls 105 - 75 = 30 F.
  • Uninsulated steel: U = 1.0 Btu/hr per sq ft per F (assumed, mostly air films).
  • Insulated: about 2 inches of closed-cell foam at R-7 per inch from the federal guide range = R-14, then derated for bridging at the frame to an effective R-10, so U = 0.10 (assumed).
  • Other loads, same in both cases: ventilation 30 cfm x 1.08 x (95 - 75) = 648 Btu/hr; two people at 250 Btu/hr sensible = 500; 600 W of lights and computers x 3.412 = 2,047. Total 3,195 Btu/hr. Latent load is left out, so real cooling needs a margin.

Without insulation: roof 160 x 1.0 x 75 = 12,000. Walls 476 x 1.0 x 30 = 14,280. Shell heat gain = 26,280 Btu/hr. Add 3,195 for the other loads: 29,475 Btu/hr. Divide by 12,000 Btu/hr per ton: about 2.5 tons.

With insulation: roof 160 x 0.10 x 75 = 1,200. Walls 476 x 0.10 x 30 = 1,428. Shell = 2,628 Btu/hr. Add 3,195: 5,823 Btu/hr, about 0.5 ton.

What it means: insulation cut the shell load by 90 percent (26,280 down to 2,628), and the whole sizing from about 2.5 tons to about 0.5 ton. In the insulated box, the people, computers and fresh air now make up more than half of the load. This is why picking equipment from the shell alone is wrong, and why a small ductless head may still be larger than the box needs. A unit picked for the bare box would be far too big for the insulated one, so decide on insulation before the equipment. Check the arithmetic: 12,000 + 14,280 = 26,280; 1,200 + 1,428 = 2,628; 648 + 500 + 2,047 = 3,195.

Field card: Container offices

Insulate and seal first, then size the unit.

  • Bare steel conducts heat in summer and out in winter.
  • Continuous insulation beats insulation between steel studs.
  • Seal insulation to the steel so moist air cannot reach the cold roof.
  • Ask the owner or an engineer before cutting openings; frame and seal each one.
  • Bring in fresh air for people; most small units do not.
  • Match the feed to the nameplate; a licensed electrician confirms it.
