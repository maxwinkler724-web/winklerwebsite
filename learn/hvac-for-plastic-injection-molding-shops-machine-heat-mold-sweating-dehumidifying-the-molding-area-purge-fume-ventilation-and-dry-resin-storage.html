HVAC for Plastic Injection Molding Shops: Machine Heat, Mold Sweating and Dry Resin

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A plastic injection molding shop looks like a factory, but the HVAC problems are the ones you already know: a lot of heat in one room, cold surfaces that sweat, air that has to stay dry, and fumes that need a path out. This lesson sorts out which problem belongs to which piece of equipment, so you know what to ask before you touch the air side.

Where the heat comes from

An injection machine turns electricity into heat in several places. Barrel heater bands melt the resin. Hydraulic pumps and valves turn motor power into oil heat. Resin dryers heat process air to dry pellets before they reach the hopper. Mold temperature controllers and the machine's own panels add more. Some of that heat goes into cooling water and leaves through a chiller or cooling tower; the rest goes into the room. Our process chiller lesson covers the water side, so here we stay with the air in the room.

Two federal energy reports give a feel for scale. A federal energy agency plastics-plant report from 2005 says one plant's hydraulic motor drives used 66 percent of its electricity. Every kilowatt-hour that a hydraulic pump takes ends up as heat somewhere, in the oil, the water or the room. A 2010 federal energy assessment record for an injection molding plant lists adjustable frequency drives or multiple-speed motors on its existing system as a way to save 226,244 kWh per year. Those kilowatt-hours would otherwise have become heat, so efficiency work and cooling work are linked.

The same DOE report describes uninsulated heated surfaces on some molding machines, and says insulating them reduces how long the heaters run. It also describes one plant that saved about 37,065 kWh per year by lowering barrel heater temperatures when machines were idle. Our reading is that less heater runtime means less heat dumped into the room, so insulated barrels and idle setbacks help the cooling load as well as the power bill. If a shop is hot, ask first whether barrels are covered and idle machines are set back.

Why summer heat matters to the product

The federal report says one plant had uncontrolled summer temperatures that hurt productivity and increased defects, and the assessment team recommended air conditioning the molding area even though it would raise energy use. That is a useful reminder: in a molding shop, room conditions can be a quality issue and not only a comfort issue. When a customer says the room is too hot, ask what the molders are seeing in the parts.

Worked example: room heat gain from the machines (our own example)

These inputs are our own assumptions and not measured data. Use them to practice the arithmetic, not to size a real shop.

  • Assumption 1: 10 machines.
  • Assumption 2: 45 kW connected load per machine (heaters, pump motor and controls added together).
  • Assumption 3: on average, 30 percent of connected load ends up as heat in the room. The rest is assumed to leave in cooling water or not be drawn at all, since machines rarely run every heater and the pump at full power at once.
  1. Connected load: 10 × 45 kW = 450 kW.
  2. Heat to the room: 450 kW × 0.30 = 135 kW.
  3. Convert with 3,412 Btu/h per kW (a standard unit conversion): 135 × 3,412 = 460,620 Btu/h.
  4. Convert to tons with 12,000 Btu/h per ton: 460,620 ÷ 12,000 = 38.4 tons.

So under these assumptions the machines alone put about 38 tons of heat into the room, before people, lights, resin dryers and the building envelope. Change the 30 percent share and the answer moves in direct proportion: 20 percent gives about 25.6 tons, and 40 percent gives about 51.2 tons. That sensitivity is why a real study needs measured machine power and a clear count of how much heat goes to water.

Mold sweating: chilled molds below the room dew point

Mold cooling water is often cold. If the mold surface is colder than the dew point of the room air, water forms on the steel. A federal environmental agency's moisture guidance explains the idea in buildings: condensation starts when air at a surface reaches 100 percent relative humidity, and the highest relative humidity in a room is always next to the coldest surface. A chilled mold is that coldest surface.

Here is our own example, labeled as such. Suppose the room is 80°F at 60 percent relative humidity. Using the standard Magnus approximation, the dew point is about 65°F (our own calculation, not a measured value). A mold running at 50°F is 15 degrees below that, so it will sweat. Drying the same 80°F room to 40 percent relative humidity gives a dew point near 53.5°F (our own calculation), which is still above a 50°F mold. The point is that relative humidity alone does not tell you; compare the mold surface temperature to the room dew point. For the psychrometric background, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

Wet molds cause rust, can streak or spot parts, and can drip into the machine area. Fixes are about the mold or the air: raise the cooling water temperature if the process allows it, insulate lines and manifolds, or lower the dew point of the air around the mold. Whether the process allows warmer water is a molding decision, so the HVAC tech brings the dew point number and the molder decides.

Dehumidifying the area or the mold enclosure

A whole molding hall is a big, leaky space, and every open door adds moisture. Drying that much air with cooling coils means very cold coil temperatures and a lot of reheat. Some shops instead dry only a small enclosure around the mold, or use a desiccant unit where a low dew point is needed. See Desiccant Dehumidifiers for how those work and Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load for why a coil that handles heat well may handle moisture poorly. The federal environmental guidance linked below also lists dehumidification as one way to lower indoor moisture, and notes that bringing in outdoor air helps only in heating periods. In a humid summer, more outdoor air usually brings more moisture in, so check the makeup air path.

Fumes from purging

When a machine is purged to clear old resin, hot material can give off smoke and vapors. A federal workplace health agency report on a plant with extrusion and molding work says earlier purges took about 30 minutes at higher temperature and pressure and released substantial smoke into the plant air. The plant changed to a purge of about 10 to 15 minutes that produced much less smoke. The same report notes that the highest formaldehyde reading, 0.051 ppm, came from a short area sample taken during a purge, when local exhaust was available but not being used. Personal samples in the extrusion area, 0.0032 to 0.0058 ppm, were below the agency's recommended limit of 0.016 ppm. The report recommended repairing the local exhaust system and using it during purges longer than normal, and said protective equipment is the least effective way to control exposures.

For an HVAC tech the lessons are practical. Local exhaust at the source works better than diluting the whole room. Exhaust only works if ducts are sealed and dampers are open; the same report found holes, disconnected ducts and broken dampers. Exhaust also removes room air, so it needs makeup air. Our fume extraction lesson covers capture velocity for a similar source-capture job. We do not give exposure limits or rates for a particular resin here, because they depend on the material; ask the safety lead and the resin's safety data sheet.

Keeping resin storage dry

Many resins pick up moisture from room air, which is why dryers exist. The federal report describes a plant where summer pellet dehumidification took 1 to 3 hours, leaving the production machine idle and losing labor time, and the assessment recommended large storage tanks with dehumidification units. Our reading is that a humid, warm storage area makes the dryers work harder, and that opened bags and hopper lids let in more moisture. HVAC can help by keeping the storage area dry and out of sunlight and by avoiding supply air that blows warm, humid air onto cold pellets, which can cause condensation on the pellets themselves. Dryer settings and drying times belong to the resin supplier and the process engineer.
