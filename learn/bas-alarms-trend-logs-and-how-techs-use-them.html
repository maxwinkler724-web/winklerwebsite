BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them

An alarm says something may be wrong. A trend says what happened before, during and after. Together they let a tech arrive at a call already knowing where to look.

Alarms: few, meaningful and answered

An Automated Logic guide calls alarms among the most misused BAS features, because too many false alarms teach people to ignore them. Its advice: alarm only on conditions someone will act on. It separates life-safety alarms (smoke in a return duct, flame detection, freezestats), which should be reported without delay, from HVAC performance alarms, which are harder to define. Another operator guide suggests sorting by urgency: critical now, urgent during normal hours, and performance or maintenance items logged for scheduled review.

Cutting nuisance alarms

  • Avoid single-condition alarms such as "room over 80 F". Add enabling conditions: occupied, running long enough, far from setpoint, no recent setpoint change.
  • Require the condition to persist so a door opening does not trigger a call.
  • Suppress downstream alarms when upstream equipment has failed. One failed air handler coil, or a chiller plant fault, can otherwise produce a flood of hot-room alarms.
  • Consider requiring repeated failures before alarming on a performance problem.

A school-district commissioning article adds a basic point: an alarm is no use if it fails to alert someone, so alarms must be routed to a person who can respond.

Trend logs: two ways to record

A trend log records point values over time. A building services article on trend logging describes two methods. Change of value (COV) records a point only when it changes, which suits on/off or open/closed points like fan status and gives the exact moment of each change. Interval logging samples at a set rate, which suits varying values like temperatures or fan speed; the article calls five-minute intervals acceptable for fan speed, and an ACHR News piece uses ten minutes as an example for analog values. The risk of interval logging is aliasing: samples taken too far apart miss what happened in between. The school-district article reports that logging at 600-second intervals made it impossible to verify sequences, and 30-second sampling fixed this. Tighten the interval temporarily while troubleshooting.

How a tech uses them

  • Confirm the alarm: pull the trend of the alarming point and its setpoint. Is it a spike, a drift or a sustained miss?
  • Find the start: trends show when it began, which often lines up with a schedule change, an override or a weather change.
  • Compare related points: command vs feedback (damper, valve, VFD speed), supply vs setpoint, status vs command.
  • Check what nobody saw: overnight and weekend behavior, which complaint calls miss.
  • Verify the fix: trend again after the repair to prove it held.

Limits and cautions

Trending is often left out of specs and then treated as low priority. Retention limits can erase old data, and some systems need point groups set up in advance. Logging too many points too fast can load the network, so do not change the setup without permission. Alarm limits and logging settings belong to the building owner and controls contractor; follow site procedure and record any change you make.

Field card: BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them

Alarm tells you to look. Trend tells you what to look at.

  • Alarm only on conditions someone will act on.
  • Add enabling conditions and persistence to cut nuisance alarms.
  • COV for status points, interval logs for analog values.
  • Tighten the interval while troubleshooting; watch for aliasing.
  • Route alarms to a real person; verify fixes with a new trend.
