Heating and Cooling for a Short-Stay Rental Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A home you rent to short-stay guests has two problems an ordinary home does not: strangers who do not know your system, and long stretches when nobody is there to notice a failure. This lesson covers the settings, habits and alerts that keep guests comfortable and the house protected.

Check local rules first

Rules vary. Rental, safety-device and inspection rules differ by place, and we do not make legal claims here. Check with your local authority and your insurer before you rely on anything below. This page is general education and not legal advice.

Thermostat limits, locks and remote control

Guests will turn the thermostat to extremes if they can. Many thermostats let you set a heating ceiling and a cooling floor, or lock the keypad. Our own suggestion: allow a modest range, for example guests may choose between 66 F and 76 F, and keep the unoccupied setback at a protective value. A state extension winter-storm guide says that if you leave a home in cold weather you should not let the temperature drop below 55 F, and that pipes can freeze and burst when outdoor temperatures reach 20 F or below. A remote or smart thermostat lets you change the setpoint between stays and see whether the system is running. For how away modes behave with phones and guests, see the related lesson on smart thermostat home and away modes. Phone-location away modes are a poor fit for guests, so our own advice is to use a fixed schedule tied to the booking calendar instead.

A simple guest card

A one-page card on the fridge or beside the thermostat prevents most calls. Our own suggested contents:

  • How to change the temperature, and the allowed range.
  • Keep windows and exterior doors closed while heating or cooling.
  • Where the thermostat, breaker panel and main water shutoff are, and who to call after hours.
  • Do not cover or block supply registers and return grilles, and do not move furniture against them.
  • Where the smoke and CO alarms are, and what to do if one sounds: leave the home, then call for help.

Filters on a schedule tied to booked nights

The federal environmental protection agency says all filters need regular replacement, that a dirty and overloaded filter will not work well, and that makers typically suggest replacement every 60 to 90 days. A rental is not occupied evenly, so a calendar date is a poor trigger. Our own approach: count booked nights and change the filter at a fixed count, for example every 45 booked nights, and check it at every turnover. Do not wait if it looks dirty, because the agency says to change more often when filters look dirty at swap time. Write the date and the night count on the filter frame, and keep spares on a shelf. Our 45-night figure is an assumption to adjust for pets, guests per stay and filter type; see how often to change your air filter.

CO and smoke alarms near sleeping areas

The federal consumer product safety agency advises smoke alarms on every level and inside each bedroom, and carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. It recommends battery backup, interconnected alarms (when one sounds they all sound), monthly testing and yearly battery changes or sealed 10-year batteries. Add the monthly test to your turnover list and log it. The same agency says carbon monoxide can come from heating systems that are installed badly, poorly maintained or have blocked venting, and it advises a professional inspection of furnaces, boilers and vents every year. Details on alarms are in carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.

Window AC and space heater rules

The consumer product safety agency says to keep space heaters at least three feet from furniture, bedding and curtains, to plug them directly into a wall outlet and not an extension cord or power strip, and to turn portable heaters off before sleeping. For a rental, our own policy suggestion is simpler: do not supply portable heaters at all, fix the real cause of a cold room, and ask guests in the house rules not to bring their own fuel-burning heaters. For window air conditioners, our own suggestions are to mount them securely, check the cord and plug at every turnover, keep the drain path clear, and follow the maker's instructions; see window air conditioners.

Freeze protection between stays

Heat can fail on the one night nobody is home. A low-temperature sensor with a phone alert, plus a leak sensor near the water heater, washer and under sinks, turns a surprise into a call. The extension guide above also suggests opening cabinet doors where plumbing runs, closing indoor valves to outdoor hose connections when possible, and knowing where the main water valve is. If you have a heat pump, it notes you may need emergency heat in a severe cold snap. More on away setpoints is in HVAC settings when you are away.

Worked example (our own): heat fails in an empty rental

These are our own assumptions, not measured data. The house is at 55 F when the heat fails on a winter night. It loses 1.5 F per hour with no heat. The alert is set at 45 F and the danger line is 40 F.

  1. Drop to the danger line: 55 F minus 40 F is 15 F.
  2. Time to 40 F: 15 F divided by 1.5 F per hour is 10 hours.
  3. Drop to the alert: 55 F minus 45 F is 10 F.
  4. Time until the alert fires: 10 F divided by 1.5 F per hour is about 6.7 hours.
  5. Warning left after the alert: 10 hours minus 6.7 hours is about 3.3 hours, which is also 5 F divided by 1.5 F per hour.

So the alert does not arrive 6.7 hours before trouble. It arrives 6.7 hours after the failure and leaves about 3.3 hours to act. A 50 F alert would fire at about 3.3 hours and leave about 6.7 hours, at the cost of more false alarms on a cold but healthy night. Real cooling rates vary widely with outdoor temperature, wind, insulation, leakiness and stored heat in the building, so a rate like 1.5 F per hour must be treated as a guess until you log your own house. A sensor placed in a cold spot, such as a crawlspace or garage wall, will drop faster than one by the thermostat.

Humidity when the house sits closed in summer

An agency guide on mold says indoor relative humidity should be kept below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and that the air conditioning system removes moisture from the air it cools. Switching the system off and closing the house between stays removes that dehumidifying. Our own suggestion: leave cooling on at a mild unoccupied setpoint, such as 78 F, and put a humidity sensor with an alert in the house so you see problems early. The same guide says a small humidity gauge can track levels. Keep drain pans draining and keep condensate drains clear; see the lessons on condensate leaks and coil mold for follow-up.

Turnover checklist

  • Reset thermostat to the guest range, check the lock and the display.
  • Look at the filter, change it if it is at the night count or looks dirty, and log the date.
  • Test smoke and CO alarms (monthly at minimum) and check for visible dust or damage.
  • Check that registers and returns are not blocked, and window AC cords and drains are fine.
  • Remove any guest-supplied portable heaters or fuel-burning devices left behind.
  • Check the sensors report in and their batteries are good.
  • Between stays, set the protective setpoint for the season and confirm the app shows the system running.

Field card: Heating and Cooling for a Short-Stay Rental Home

Limit the range, log the filter, alert before 40 F.

  • Rules vary by place; check locally and with your insurer.
  • Limit or lock thermostat ranges; keep an unoccupied setpoint of at least 55 F in cold weather.
  • Change filters by booked-night count (our own: about 45) and check at turnover.
  • Smoke alarms in each bedroom and on every level; CO alarms outside sleeping areas and on every level.
  • Space heaters: three feet clear, plugged into the wall, off at sleep.
  • Example (our own): 15 F at 1.5 F per hour is 10 h; a 45 F alert fires at 6.7 h and leaves 3.3 h.
  • Keep humidity under 60 percent in a closed house.
