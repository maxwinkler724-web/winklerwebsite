Liquid Receivers in Commercial Refrigeration: Pumpdown Capacity, King Valve and Winter Charge

Last updated: 2026-10-28

A receiver on a commercial refrigeration system is a tank with a job: hold extra charge while the system runs, and hold all of it when you pump the system down to work on it. This lesson covers how full is too full, what the king valve does, how to read the level, what protects the tank from overpressure, and how a winter flooding charge can use up the room you counted on.

What the receiver has to do

A trade-press article on the liquid receiver says the tank sits between the condenser and the liquid line and is usually chosen to hold about 90 percent of the total system charge. That gives a reservoir for high load and lets you trap the charge in the condenser and receiver during repairs. The same article says a maker rates receiver storage at 80 percent of internal volume at a refrigerant temperature of 90 F, and ties that rating to a standard (ARI 495, named in plain text). A trade-training page says the receiver is designed to hold the entire system charge at no more than 80 percent full, so the tank never goes liquid full, because a liquid-full vessel can reach dangerous hydrostatic pressure as the liquid expands. For where the receiver sits in the circuit, read Suction Line Accumulators and Liquid Receivers.

The king valve and pumpdown

The outlet service valve is typically called the king valve. The trade-press article says it lets you read system pressure at the receiver and isolate the charge in the condenser and receiver. The training page says it is a multi-position service valve at the receiver outlet that gives access to the circuit and is used for pumpdown, and that it may be fully front seated (turned clockwise). In general terms, pumping down means closing off the liquid leaving the receiver while the compressor runs, so the charge ends up held in the condenser and receiver and the low side is pulled down. The step list lives in Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves. Valve seating, caps and gauge ports are covered in Refrigerant Service Valves and Schrader Cores. Follow the maker's instructions for the unit, because valve designs and gauge port positions differ.

Inlet valves, sight glasses and level checks

A compressor maker's receiver data sheet lists receivers with fully flanged or rotolock valves on the ports (the rotolock type with a quarter inch service port), sight glasses with a level indicator (two on the horizontal vessels listed, three on most vertical ones) and fittings for a low-level alarm probe. It also tabulates a pump down capacity at 80 percent fill and 25 C for several refrigerants, with different numbers for each, which shows that capacity depends on the refrigerant. An EPA guide for supermarkets says to log the receiver level for each rack, that a drop from the previous reading may indicate a leak, and that readings should be taken when the system is not in winter flooding mode. An EPA leak-repair guideline says, for charging, to close the ball valve immediately downstream of the receiver and to charge the receiver to 60 percent on the liquid gauge or to the point of pressure equalization. Those are examples from those documents, not a rule for every system.

Relief protection

The trade-press article says a receiver may carry a fusible plug, a rupture disc or a pressure relief valve to release abnormal high pressure in a controlled way. What is required on a stationary system comes from the safety standard for refrigeration systems (ASHRAE 15, named in plain text) and the local code, so read those rather than our summary. As a government example only, the federal rule for refrigeration machinery on vessels, 46 CFR Part 58, Subpart 58.20, says a relief valve must be fitted in the discharge connection from the receivers and condensers, may relieve to the condenser, and must be set to relieve at a pressure not greater than the maximum allowable working pressure. That is a marine rule and does not govern a store. Field detail is in Pressure Relief Valves, Fusible Plugs and Rupture Discs.

Winter flooding takes receiver room

A condenser flooding valve holds head pressure up in cold weather by backing liquid up in the condenser, which takes extra refrigerant. The EPA supermarket guide says more refrigerant is required during the winter to permit flooding of the condenser, and that oversized receivers can lead to overcharged systems. The trade-press article says systems with a flooding valve need a receiver to store the excess refrigerant. Our reasoning: that excess has to fit in the same tank at pumpdown time, so the winter charge is what to check the tank against. For the head pressure side, see Low Ambient Kit and Head Pressure Control.

Worked example (ours)

These numbers are our own sketch, not a maker's data. We use R-134a because a government table gives its liquid density; we did not find a sourced density for R-448A. Our assumptions: a 40 lb summer charge, a receiver with 0.80 ft³ of internal volume (about 6 gallons), and 8 lb of extra winter flooding charge.

  1. Density: the NIST Chemistry WebBook lists saturated liquid R134a at 305.37 K (about 90 F) at 1178.7 kg/m³. Multiply by 0.062428 to get 73.59 lb/ft³.
  2. Liquid-full weight: 0.80 × 73.59 = 58.87 lb. Never plan on that.
  3. 80 percent capacity: 0.80 × 58.87 = 47.09 lb.
  4. Summer: 40 lb is 40 ÷ 47.09 = 85 percent of that capacity (40 ÷ 58.87 = 68 percent of the volume). Pumpdown fits.
  5. Winter: 40 + 8 = 48 lb. That is 48 ÷ 47.09 = about 102 percent of capacity (48 ÷ 58.87 = 82 percent of the volume), over the 80 percent line. A full pumpdown does not fit that receiver by this method; the answer is to recover part of the charge or use a bigger receiver, which is a decision for the maker's data and your site procedures.

Our simplifications: liquid at a cooler temperature is denser, so the same pounds take a little less volume, but the 80 percent rating is a design basis. Refrigerant held in the condenser and liquid line counts toward the total too. For the real vessel, use the maker's rated pump down capacity, not our arithmetic.

Field checks

  • Find the nameplate capacity and the refrigerant it was rated for.
  • Find the winter flooding charge on the system label or records.
  • After service, return the king valve and its cap to the position the maker's instructions call for.
  • Confirm a relief device is present and its condition meets your procedures and code.
  • Log the receiver level the same way each time, outside winter flooding mode.

Field card: Liquid Receivers

Count the winter charge before you count on pumpdown.

  • Receiver rating is usually 80 percent of volume at 90 F; design is often about 90 percent of the charge.
  • The king valve is the receiver outlet service valve; front seating it holds charge in the condenser and receiver.
  • Capacity depends on the refrigerant's liquid density.
  • Winter flooding needs extra charge that must also fit.
  • Relief requirements come from ASHRAE 15 and local code.
  • Our example: 0.80 ft³, R134a, 47.09 lb at 80 percent; 40 lb fits, 48 lb does not.
