Dogs and Cats at Home: Heating, Cooling and Pet Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A dog or cat that is home alone cannot open a window, adjust the thermostat or leave when the house gets too hot or too cold. That makes the heating and cooling system part of pet care. This lesson covers what to set, what to watch for, and what pets do to the equipment.

Why a system failure is a pet emergency

People can notice a dead air conditioner and act. Pets cannot. A veterinary college page calls heatstroke in dogs a medical emergency that needs intensive care, and says it begins when a dog can no longer regulate its own temperature (source 1). Another university page lists confinement and poor ventilation as risk factors, and says dogs that are older, overweight, flat-faced or have heart or breathing problems need special care on hot days (source 2). Cold matters too: an extension page warns that severe hypothermia can kill pets (source 4). So a failed air conditioner on a hot day, or a failed furnace on a freezing one, deserves faster action when animals are home. We also have a lesson on carbon monoxide during winter outages, since a cold house is when people reach for backup heat.

Safe thermostat settings while pets are home alone

We do not give one magic number, because none of our sources states one for homes. What the sources support is a rule: on hot days keep dogs, especially at-risk ones, indoors with air conditioning or fans running (source 2), and keep cats indoors in air-conditioned areas when extreme heat is forecast (source 6). In winter, limit time outdoors when the temperature drops below 32 degrees F (source 4). Our own practical advice, not from a source: do not set the thermostat to a deep setback or to "off" for a long day when animals are home, ask your veterinarian what range suits your animal's age, coat and health, and make sure water is available in a cool spot. Fresh water at all times is advice from a university diagnostic lab (source 7).

Heat stress signs

Sources agree on a core list. Heavy panting, drooling, vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, confusion, seizures and collapse appear on one veterinary college dog page (source 1). A second university veterinary page adds seeking cool spots, fast heart and breathing rates, raspy breathing, wobbliness and excessive thirst (source 2). A university feline health page lists weakness or collapse, heavy panting, drooling, vomiting and diarrhea (source 6). Thresholds vary by source. One veterinary college dog page puts normal dog temperature at 100.5 to 102.5 F and heatstroke at 105 F or higher (source 1). A second university veterinary page gives heat stroke as above 105.8 F (source 2), and a university veterinary lab page says above 104 F (source 7). Treat the exact number as a vet's tool; if you see the signs, act.

Steps from the sources: move the animal to a cool, drafty place, wet the fur with cool (not very cold) water for a cat (source 6), and get to a veterinarian. The veterinary college dog page says to start cooling immediately and drive with the air conditioning on (source 1). The second veterinary page says to stop active cooling at 103 F to avoid overcooling (source 2). Never leave a pet in a parked car; the federal public health agency says a car can warm almost 20 degrees F in 10 minutes even with a window cracked (source 5).

Pet hair, dander and your filter

The federal environmental agency says filters need regular replacement because an overloaded filter will not work well, that typical manufacturer guidance is every 60 to 90 days, and that a heavily soiled filter should be changed more often (source 3). The federal energy guide says to clean or replace filters monthly in seasons of peak use (source 8). Neither says how much faster pet hair loads a filter, so the number in our example below is an assumption. See filter size, direction and fit for installing the right one.

Our own practical points, not from a source: hair collects first on return grilles, where a vacuum brush clears it in a minute, and on blower and indoor coil surfaces when a filter is loaded or leaks around the frame. Look at the grille and the filter together. Hair and dander on the outdoor condenser coil are less common, but cottony debris and fur can pack the fins; the federal energy guide says to keep the area around outdoor units clear so air can circulate (source 8), and our condenser coil lesson covers safe cleaning. Turn power off first, and leave refrigerant lines and electrical parts to a technician.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example with our own assumptions. A house has a filter that normally lasts 90 days (assumption, in line with the 60 to 90 day range in source 3). We assume pet hair loading cuts that life in half, to 45 days. This is our assumption; no source we read gives a number.

  1. Check every 30 days: 365 / 30 = 12.2, so about 12 checks per year.
  2. Change every 90 days (ignoring pets): 365 / 90 = 4.06, so about 4 filters per year.
  3. Change every 45 days (our assumed pet-hair life): 365 / 45 = 8.1, so about 8 filters per year.
  4. Difference: 8 minus 4 = 4 extra filters per year. The 30-day check is what tells you which case you are in: if the filter looks heavily loaded at day 30 or 45, change it.

The check itself costs a minute. Write the date on the frame so you know the age.

First response to heat illness, step by step

The veterinary pages describe a similar order of actions. First, recognize the signs listed above, because dogs and cats can go from heavy panting to collapse quickly (source 6). Second, start cooling at once. The veterinary college says to wet a dog with cool water and to put it in front of the car air conditioning on the way to a veterinary hospital (source 1). For a cat, move her to a cool spot with a draft and wet the fur with cool, not very cold, water (source 6). Third, call or go to a veterinarian right away, because treatment there can include active cooling, intravenous fluids and oxygen, and the sooner it starts the better (sources 1 and 7). The veterinary college also notes that prolonged high body temperature can damage every organ, which is why the page treats this as an emergency and not as something to wait out (source 1). The second veterinary page lists flat-faced breeds, thick coats, obesity and sudden early-summer heat as extra risks (source 2). If your home is hot because the air conditioner failed, treat the pet like a patient even before the signs appear, and move it to the coolest room, with water, while you arrange repair.

Cold-weather care inside the house

A university extension page lists shivering, weakness, pale gums and confusion as signs of hypothermia, notes that wind and water make cold worse, and says to contact a veterinary professional as soon as possible if you suspect it (source 4). It also recommends a quiet resting spot with a blanket, bed, cushion or rug and fresh water, and says dogs need seven days of food and water in an emergency kit, with cats needing several days (source 4). A university feline health page says outdoor cats seek warmth in car wheel wells and engine compartments, and advises honking the horn or banging on the hood before starting the car (source 6). It also says to feed cats somewhat more in cold weather because they burn more calories staying warm (source 6). Our own advice, not from a source: during a furnace failure, move the pet bed into the smallest interior room, add blankets, and keep the animal away from ovens, fireplaces and any improvised heater. Never use a gas range for heat; see the carbon monoxide lesson linked above.

Before you leave the house (our own checklist)

This checklist is our own general advice, built around the sourced points above.

  1. Look at the thermostat mode and setting. Is it set to heat or cool as expected, and does the display show the right room temperature?
  2. Check that the filter is not heavily loaded, using the 30-day habit from the worked example.
  3. Leave fresh water in more than one spot, away from the food and litter box, since a university lab notes cats often drink more that way (source 7).
  4. Make sure the pet can reach the coolest or warmest area of the house, and that interior doors are not closed on the only comfortable room.
  5. Unplug or turn off any space heater, as the safety release advises when you leave the area (source 9).
  6. Confirm your remote alert is working and a backup person has a key.
  7. On a forecast heat wave or deep freeze, ask yourself whether a long day away is wise, and consider asking someone to check in.

Keeping pets away from outdoor condensers and space heaters

An outdoor unit has a spinning fan and electrical parts; fence or plant barriers that still leave airflow (source 8) help, and our childproofing lesson uses the same ideas for children, which apply to pets too. For space heaters, a federal consumer product safety news release says to keep the heater at least 3 feet from bedding, drapes, furniture or other flammable materials, to keep children and pets away, and to turn it off when you leave the area (source 9). A fire safety flyer from the federal fire administration adds to plug heaters directly into a wall outlet and not an extension cord or power strip (source 10). So a space heater is a poor way to keep a pet warm while you are out; see electric space heaters.

Remote temperature alerts

Our own advice, not from a source: a wireless thermostat or a separate indoor temperature sensor can message your phone when the house passes a limit you choose. Choose a high and low limit with your vet's input, place the sensor where pets rest rather than in the attic, and name a neighbor or friend with a key who can respond. Test the alert once. An alert does not fix anything; it only shortens the time before someone acts.

Field card: Dogs and Cats at Home: Heating, Cooling and Pet Safety

Pets cannot fix a failed system. Notice early.

  • Heavy panting, drooling, vomiting, weakness or collapse: cool the animal and call a veterinarian.
  • Do not leave pets in a parked car.
  • Check filters every 30 days; change when loaded (typical life 60 to 90 days per source 3).
  • Keep heaters 3 feet from anything that burns and keep pets away.
  • Set a remote temperature alert and name a backup person.
