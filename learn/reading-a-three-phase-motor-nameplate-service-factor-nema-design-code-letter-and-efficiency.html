Reading a Three-Phase Motor Nameplate: Service Factor, Design Letter, Code Letter and Efficiency

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The nameplate on a fan or pump motor is the only document guaranteed to stay with the motor. This lesson walks through each common field on a three-phase induction motor, works one nameplate through the arithmetic, and explains why running a motor deep into its service factor all day is a bad habit.

Check the source

Check the source. Standards such as NEMA MG 1 are copyrighted and we do not reproduce them. This page is built from federal, university extension and manufacturer pages we read in 2026. For any real replacement, match your motor's own nameplate and follow the maker's literature. Always de-energize and lock out the circuit under your site procedure before opening a terminal box.

The electrical fields

  • hp, volts, FLA. Oklahoma State University Extension describes hp as the output at rated load, volts as the design voltage (the motor can run within about plus or minus 10 percent), and FLA as the current at 100 percent rated load. It says wiring, breakers and starters are sized from FLA, and that measured amps well above FLA suggest overload.
  • rpm and Hz. Rpm is shaft speed at rated load. The extension sheet notes a 4-pole, 60 Hz motor has a no-load speed of 1,800 rpm. The rated rpm sits a little below that, and the gap is slip.
  • Service factor (SF). The extension sheet says an SF of 1.15 allows 15 percent over rated load for a short time and that continuous operation above rated load reduces efficiency and life. A NEMA-versus-IEC comparison on NEMA's public site gives a similar picture: the motor runs hotter, which reduces insulation and bearing life.
  • Service factor amps. Some makers print a separate "current at the service factor" line. WEG's training guide lists it as a nameplate item. Where it is absent, the only estimate we can offer is our own: FLA times SF.
  • Power factor. The extension sheet defines it as real power over total power and says low values can draw utility penalties. See Power Factor on HVAC Motors.
  • Dual-voltage motors. The extension sheet says wiring information for dual-voltage motors (for example 230/460) is at the bottom of the nameplate. See the plain-words connection section below.

Heat fields: insulation class, ambient, duty

The extension sheet says insulation class tells you the maximum winding temperature: Class B is 130 °C, F is 155 °C and H is 180 °C, and operating temperature is ambient plus the motor's own temperature rise. WEG's specification guide lists the same class temperatures. A marking such as "40C AMB CONT" means continuous duty in up to 40 °C surrounding air, per the extension sheet. Intermittent duty motors need rest periods. One ABB (Baldor-Reliance) catalog we read gives a 1.15 service factor as standard for ambients up to 40 °C and says changing the ambient limit upward through a special order reduces that service factor to 1.0, so the ambient and the SF are linked on the plate.

Starting and speed fields: design letter and code letter

A lecture from Southern Illinois University summarizes NEMA design letters by rotor resistance. Design B is the common general-purpose design. Design A has similar torque with higher starting current. C gives high starting torque. D is a high-slip design, with slip of roughly 5 to 15 percent in that lecture. Pumps & Systems magazine adds that most centrifugal pump motors are Design B, that Design C suits positive displacement pumps that start under load, and that swapping a C or D for a B is a common misapplication because the B may not bring the load up to speed.

The code letter is a different thing. The same lecture says it gives the expected locked-rotor inrush at rated voltage and frequency as kVA per hp, from A (0 to 3.15) up to V (above 22.4). Code G, for example, is 5.6 to 6.3 kVA per hp. Pumps & Systems agrees that letters further from A mean more inrush per hp. We use only code G below. The Oklahoma extension sheet does not print the table and neither does the trade article, so for any other letter confirm the range against the motor maker or NEMA before using it.

Efficiency, enclosure, frame, inverter duty

  • Nominal efficiency. The DOE Premium Efficiency Motor Selection and Application Guide says NEMA expresses efficiency as the average full-load value of a population of the same design, and that nominal value is what appears on the nameplate. NEMA's public comparison page adds that an individual motor must meet at least the tied minimum.
  • NEMA Premium. DOE says the marking is licensed by NEMA and can only be used by licensees, and that the premium standards cover Design A and B motors. One ABB (Baldor-Reliance) catalog says licensed motors carry the NEMA Premium mark on the nameplate.
  • Enclosure. The extension sheet describes ODP (open, drip proof) as open and suited to indoor use, and TEFC (totally enclosed, fan cooled) as sealed with an external fan.
  • Frame. The extension sheet says the NEMA frame must match for a replacement to fit. Integral frames start at 140.
  • Inverter duty. DOE's glossary describes it as intended for power from an inverter and points to NEMA MG 1 Part 31.

Dual-voltage connections in plain words

WEG's specification guide describes two common ways to build a dual-voltage winding. In series-parallel, each phase is split into two halves with nine leads brought out. Joining the halves in series suits the higher voltage and joining them in parallel suits the lower, with 220/440 as the common pair. In wye-delta, each phase is brought out and six leads are used. Our own reading: at either voltage each half-winding sees the same voltage, which is why the motor can do the same work, but at the low voltage the line current is about double. The same ABB catalog says its standard 440-frame motors at 460 V use a 12-lead connection. Follow the connection diagram on the plate lead by lead, and never guess from lead colors.

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

Nameplate: 15 hp, 460 V, FLA 18.0 A, SF 1.15, code letter G, 1,765 rpm, 60 Hz, 4 pole. All figures below are our own.

  1. Service factor amps (approximation). The plate gives none, so 18.0 A × 1.15 = 20.7 A. This is an approximation; a maker's printed value governs.
  2. Synchronous speed. 120 × 60 / 4 = 1,800 rpm.
  3. Percent slip. (1,800 − 1,765) / 1,800 = 35 / 1,800 = 1.94 percent.
  4. Locked-rotor kVA, code G. 5.6 × 15 = 84.0 kVA and 6.3 × 15 = 94.5 kVA, so 84.0 to 94.5 kVA.
  5. Approximate locked-rotor current. I = kVA × 1,000 / (1.732 × 460). The denominator is 796.7. So 84,000 / 796.7 = 105.4 A and 94,500 / 796.7 = 118.6 A. That is roughly 5.9 to 6.6 times FLA.
  6. Measured current. 16.2 A / 18.0 A = 0.90, or 90 percent of FLA. Against the approximate SF amps it is 16.2 / 20.7 = 78 percent.

Reading the result: 90 percent of FLA is below the nameplate current, so the motor is not overloaded by this measure. Method details are in Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter. Check voltage balance on all three legs too; see Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance.

Why living in the service factor shortens life

DOE's extend-life fact sheet says that for every 10 °C rise in operating temperature, insulation life is reduced by half, and it lists operation even within the service factor as a condition that causes overheating. The NEMA comparison page says a motor run on its service factor runs hotter, which reduces insulation and bearing life. The extension sheet says the SF is for brief overload. Treat it as a margin for unexpected conditions such as unbalanced voltage, not as extra horsepower to plan around. If a pump or fan motor sits above FLA all the time, look at the load, voltage and airflow first, then consider a larger motor. If winding condition is in doubt, see Megohmmeter Testing on Motors.

Field card: Reading a Three-Phase Motor Nameplate

Match the plate, then check the amps.

  • FLA is the current at rated load; compare measured amps to it.
  • Service factor is a short-term overload margin, not extra horsepower to plan around.
  • Slip = (120 × Hz / poles − rated rpm) / synchronous rpm.
  • Code letter is locked-rotor kVA per hp; inrush A = kVA × 1,000 / (1.732 × V).
  • Insulation class B 130 °C, F 155 °C, H 180 °C; about 10 °C hotter halves insulation life.
  • Match frame, enclosure, speed and design letter on a replacement.
  • Dual-voltage: follow the plate's connection diagram.
