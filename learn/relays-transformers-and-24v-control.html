Relays, Transformers and 24V Control

The thermostat on the wall does not carry anything close to the power a compressor needs. It sends a small signal through a low-voltage circuit, and relays do the heavy switching. Once you see that chain, many controls problems become easier to follow.

The transformer: making 24 volts

A transformer has two coils wrapped around a shared core with no direct electrical connection between them. Power goes into the primary, and a lower voltage comes out of the secondary. The voltage ratio follows the turns ratio. As a labeled example from a general reference, a 20 to 1 turns ratio turns 240 volts into about 12 volts. Control transformers in furnaces and air handlers do the same job to make roughly 24 volts AC for the controls. The current ratio goes the other way, so the low-voltage side can carry more amps than you might expect. A transformer is also rated in VA, and overloading it with too many devices or a short can burn it out. Some have a fuse or breaker on the secondary, and others have built-in current limiting. If it keeps blowing, look for the short, not for a bigger fuse.

The relay: a small signal controls a big load

A relay is an electrically operated switch. A coil forms an electromagnet that moves an armature, which opens or closes contacts. The coil circuit and the contact circuit are separate, so a low-power signal can control a much higher-power circuit. Contacts that are open at rest and close when energized are normally open (NO). Those that are closed at rest and open when energized are normally closed (NC). A contactor is the same idea built larger for heavy loads such as compressors. See Capacitors and Contactors.

Following a thermostat call

Most residential thermostats use 24 volts AC. Terminal labels vary by system, but common ones are:

  • R, the 24 volt supply from the transformer.
  • C, the common, the return side of the transformer.
  • W, call for heat. Y, call for cooling. G, blower fan.
  • O or B, the reversing valve on a heat pump. E, emergency heat.

When the room needs cooling, the thermostat connects R to Y. Current flows from the transformer, through the thermostat, to the coil of a relay or contactor and back to the common. The coil energizes, the contacts close, and line voltage reaches the outdoor unit. Heat calls work the same way through W, then the equipment's own control board runs its start-up sequence. Newer systems may use communicating wiring instead, which is covered in Communicating Controls Basics.

Safety devices live in this circuit too

Limit switches, pressure switches, float switches and similar devices are commonly wired in series so that if one opens, the call cannot complete. They are there to protect equipment and people. If a unit is not responding, an open safety is a clue about what is wrong, not an obstacle. Never jumper one out to make the unit run.

Staying safe

Low voltage is far gentler than line voltage, but the same transformer is fed by line voltage on its primary side, and the equipment around the controls carries line voltage. Keep your lockout and test-before-touch habits. See Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Field card: Relays, Transformers and 24V Control

A transformer makes 24 V, a thermostat closes a switch, and a relay coil does the heavy lifting.

  • Transformer: primary in, lower voltage out, no direct connection between them.
  • Relay: small coil signal switches a separate, larger circuit.
  • R = 24 V supply, C = common, W = heat, Y = cool, G = fan.
  • Safeties in series are clues. Never jumper them.
  • Repeated blown control fuse means find the short, not a larger fuse.
  • Wiring labels vary, so check the manufacturer's diagram.

New Jersey note. NJ's HVACR rules (N.J.A.C. 13:32A-1.3, amended 2022) let a master HVACR contractor do electrical work up to 30 volts, so 24 volt control wiring is in scope for that license. The under-10 volt exemption belongs to electrical contractors. Whether a non-master employee may do the same wiring is not settled by the sources we read, so confirm with the Board.
