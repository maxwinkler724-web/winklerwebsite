Roof Runoff and Icicles Over an Outdoor Heat Pump or AC Unit

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An outdoor unit that sits under the edge of a roof gets a steady shower whenever snow melts or rain runs off. In winter that water freezes on the unit. This lesson explains what the ice does, how to tell if your unit is in the wrong spot, and which simple fixes help.

What goes wrong

The fan on a heat pump or AC condenser pulls air through the coil and pushes it out the top grille. Water that drips onto that grille lands right where the fan blades spin. When the air is near or below freezing, the drops turn to ice on the grille, on the blades and on the coil fins below.

A national-lab guide for homeowners and contractors says not to install outdoor units where water or melting snow or ice will fall on them. It shows a case where a unit under a deck was shielded from direct snow, but melted snow dripped through, refroze on the unit and, in the guide's words, dramatically reduced capacity and efficiency. A second national-lab guide notes that dripping roof water can freeze onto a unit and encase it in ice, and that snow sliding off a roof can damage equipment.

Why defrost cannot fix it

A heat pump is built to melt frost that forms on its own coil. Federal energy guidance describes a reversing valve that sends warm refrigerant through the outdoor coil for defrost. That system expects a thin layer of frost made from moisture in the air. It is not built for a stream of new water from above that keeps refreezing on the fins, so ice can bridge across the fins faster than defrost clears it. Once the gaps between fins fill, air cannot get through, the coil gets colder, and more ice forms. See Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter: Normal Defrost or a Problem? for normal frost versus a problem, and Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat for how the cycle works.

Ice on the top grille is also a mechanical risk. A fan blade that spins into ice can bend or crack. A state energy agency field checklist for heat pump installs says roof slides and slush can block airflow and damage fan blades.

Why a unit should not sit under a drip line

Guidance from a cold-climate heat pump installation guide hosted on a federal energy site says to avoid installing outdoor units directly under a roof drip line when possible. If that cannot be avoided and no working gutter is there, it calls for drip caps or shields approved by the maker. A state agency quality checklist treats a unit with extra water, ice or snow coming from above as a major item to fix. For a roof with a gutter, it says to make sure the roof edge covers the overhead of the unit while still meeting the maker's clearances. The same checklist advises not placing a unit under fire escapes, outdoor stairs or decks that have gaps and no baffle.

Simple fixes, in the order we would try them

  1. Put a gutter or diverter above the unit. The state energy agency field checklist treats a roof with a working gutter as adequate protection when the fans point straight up, but warns that slush and slides can still hit the unit, so it lists other options as well. Keep the gutter and downspout clear in fall. An overflowing gutter that was clogged with leaves sends water to the same spot as no gutter.
  2. Move the unit. The same guides suggest the gable end of the house, where roof water does not run off, or a spot completely under an eave overhang so the drip line misses the unit. Moving a unit means refrigerant lines and wiring, so it is a job for a licensed tech, not a weekend project.
  3. Add a small roof or hood. A national-lab do-it-yourself guide says an awning should extend beyond the unit on all sides and be open on the sides. It says not to add sides unless they are ventilated, and that one kit design is not recommended because it may restrict airflow too much. It gives roughly 6 feet as a commonly recommended height above the top of the heat pump, though it says to check the owner's manual and that it should be at least 2 feet. The point is that the unit must still blow its air up and out, so a roof that traps discharge air makes things worse. The state checklist says the same: if snow-cover attachments are not available, build a cover that allows proper airflow.
  4. Raise the unit on a stand. Raising helps with snow depth and meltwater under the unit. It does not stop water from above, but it keeps the base out of snow. See Heat Pump Pads, Stands and Wall Brackets: Snow Height and Drainage.

A tip from the same sources: do not put the unit next to a walkway, because refrozen meltwater makes a slip hazard.

What not to do

  • Do not chip or pry ice with tools. This is our own caution, not a quote from a source. Coil fins are thin and the copper tubing inside is easy to puncture, and a leak means a repair and a lost refrigerant charge. Fan blades are also easy to bend.
  • Do not cover a running heat pump with a tarp or plastic. A heat pump needs free airflow in winter, and the awning guide above warns against designs that restrict it. A cover that wraps the unit also traps moisture. If a cover is used at all, it should be a rigid roof-style shield that leaves the sides open.
  • Do not climb onto a snowy or icy roof to knock off icicles. A state university extension page on ice dams warns that anyone on the roof in winter, or working on the roof from below, risks injury, and says to contact professionals.
  • Do not hit, hose or pour hot water on a frozen unit. This is our own caution: hard impacts and sudden heat can crack plastic parts and bend fins. Leave the unit to a tech if it will not clear, and keep it off until then.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed inputs. We assume a 10 ft by 6 ft section of roof drains toward the eave directly above one unit, with no gutter. We assume 0.5 inch of water from melted snow (water depth, not snow depth). We also assume all of it runs off at that one edge. These are assumptions, not measurements.

  1. Roof area: 10 ft x 6 ft = 60 sq ft.
  2. Conversion: 1 inch of water over 1 sq ft is 12 in x 12 in x 1 in = 144 cubic inches. One US gallon is 231 cubic inches. 144 divided by 231 = 0.623 gallon, which matches the 0.623 figure.
  3. Volume for 0.5 inch: 60 sq ft x 0.5 in x 0.623 gal per sq ft per inch = 18.69 gallons.

Check: 60 x 0.623 = 37.38 gallons for a full inch, and half of that is 18.69 gallons. Round to about 19 gallons. Using 8.34 pounds per gallon, an assumed round number for water, that is about 156 pounds of water coming off that one section. Even if a good share runs elsewhere, it is a lot of water for a unit's top grille to take on a freezing day. A gutter sends that volume to a downspout instead.

When to call a tech

  • The unit has ice on the fan or coil that does not clear after a normal defrost, or the fan will not turn, or you hear scraping or banging.
  • You see bent fan blades, a bent grille or oily residue near the coil.
  • Ice returns every time it melts and the roof edge is above the unit. A tech can judge whether to move it, add a shield or install a gutter.
  • You want to relocate or raise the unit. Line sets, wiring and clearances must be done correctly.

For the meltwater that forms under a unit, and the base pan, see Heat Pump Base Pan Heaters and Defrost Meltwater.

Field card: Roof Runoff and Icicles Over an Outdoor Heat Pump or AC Unit

Keep roof water off the unit and keep air moving through it.

  • Roof drip freezes on the grille, fan and coil, and defrost cannot clear ice that new water keeps adding.
  • Avoid placing a unit under a roof drip line; use a working gutter, a diverter, a shield, or move the unit.
  • Any roof or hood must leave air free to flow out the top and in the sides.
  • 1 inch of water on 1 sq ft = 0.623 gallon. 60 sq ft x 0.5 in = about 18.7 gallons.
  • Do not chip ice, climb on icy roofs, or wrap a running unit.
  • Call a tech for ice that returns, bent blades or noise.
