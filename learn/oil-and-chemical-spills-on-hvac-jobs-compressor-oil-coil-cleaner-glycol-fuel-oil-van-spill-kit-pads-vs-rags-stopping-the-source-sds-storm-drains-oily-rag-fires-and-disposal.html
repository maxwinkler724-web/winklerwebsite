Oil and Chemical Spills on HVAC Jobs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A spill on a job is rarely big. It is a quart of compressor oil from a cut line, a splash of coil cleaner, a glycol drip at a fitting, a condensate pan treatment tipped over, or a little fuel oil at a burner. Small spills still stain floors, hurt skin and eyes, start fires and reach drains, so we handle them with a repeatable routine.

What can spill, and why it matters

On a typical week a tech may carry or open compressor and refrigerant oil, coil cleaner, glycol for hydronic loops, tablets or liquid for condensate pans, and fuel oil at an oil-fired boiler or furnace. They are different liquids with different hazards: some are flammable, some irritate skin, some are slippery, and some should never reach a sewer or storm drain. Do not guess which is which. The label and the safety data sheet say.

Step 1: stop the source first

Before you grab a pad, stop more liquid from coming out. Our own routine: shut the valve, upright the container, close the cap, pinch or cap the cut line, or turn off the pump. Keep ignition sources away if the liquid is flammable. The university spill procedure we read says to control all ignition sources first for flammable liquids. Wiping a puddle while the leak keeps running just makes a bigger puddle. The federal environmental agency's spill-control guidance we read also stresses acting quickly, because a fast response limits the harm and the cleanup effort.

Step 2: read the safety data sheet

Every chemical you use on the job should have a safety data sheet, and the sheet has 16 sections. The federal workplace safety agency's brief on the sheets says Section 6 gives recommendations for responding to spills, leaks and releases, Section 7 covers safe handling and storage, and Section 13 covers disposal. Section 8 is where protective equipment is listed. Keep the sheets reachable on your phone or in the van binder. For the full walk-through, see the related lesson on reading a safety data sheet.

Step 3: contain and keep it out of drains

Liquid runs downhill to the lowest place, and on many sites that is a floor drain, a sink or a storm drain. The federal environmental agency's spill-control guidance recommends drain covers during spill cleanup and spill controls such as berms where liquid could reach a drainage path. It also advises against using water on a spill, so contaminated runoff does not reach storm drains. Our own habit is to ring the spill with pads or socks from the kit first, then absorb from the outside in. Never wash a spill down a drain with the hose. For the plumbing side of floor drains in mechanical rooms, see the related lesson linked below.

Pads versus rags

Shop rags and paper towels are tempting, but they are a poor spill tool. Absorbent pads and socks made for the job are sized and labeled for how much liquid they hold, so you can count them, and they go into a bag as one tidy waste. A rag soaked with solvent or fuel becomes a flammable item you now have to store. Our own rule: use rags only for a drip, and use pads for anything that spreads. The federal environmental agency guidance lists absorbent pads, booms or socks, granular absorbent, protective gear, thick plastic bags and drain covers as spill kit contents, and says to restock the kit as soon as you use it.

A small van spill kit

  • Absorbent pads and a couple of socks for ringing a spill.
  • Granular absorbent for what a pad cannot reach, with a scoop.
  • Drain covers that fit your area, or a way to seal a drain.
  • Gloves and eye protection suited to the liquids you carry.
  • Thick plastic bags, ties and tape, plus a pen for labeling.
  • A closed metal can with a lid for oily rags.
  • Printed or phone-saved safety data sheets for what is on the truck.

Oily rags and fire risk

The federal workplace safety agency's flammable liquids rule, 29 CFR 1910.106, says combustible waste in a work area should be kept to a minimum, held in closed metal waste cans and removed daily. A university safety newsletter we read adds that rags with certain plant-based oils, such as linseed oil, can heat on their own as the oil dries and can ignite when piled. It says petroleum oils on rags are not the same self-heating concern but still must be kept from sparks. The practical result is the same: do not leave oily rags in a pile, a bucket or a pocket. Put them in a closed metal can and take them out of the building when the job ends.

Bagging and disposal

Used pads, granular absorbent, gloves and rags are waste, and the right route depends on what soaked into them. The university cleanup guidance we read places absorbent and debris in a bag, seals it and attaches a waste label. Section 13 of the safety data sheet speaks to disposal, and local rules decide where it can go, so ask your employer or the local waste authority before you toss a bag in the customer's trash. Never pour liquids on the ground or into a drain.

Telling the customer

Tell the customer right away, in plain words, what spilled, where, what you did and what is left. Show them the cleaned area and describe any stain you could not remove. Take photos before and after. Ask your employer how spills are reported and to whom, since the federal environmental agency's guidance says a spill plan should name who notifies authorities. Do not guess about rules you do not know. For floor protection and the walk-through, see the lesson on protecting a customer's home.

When to stop and call for help

The university procedure we read tells people to call for help when a spill is large, involves several chemicals or creates a breathing hazard. Its numbers are for a laboratory, so they are not ours, but the idea carries over. If you smell strong fumes, the liquid is spreading toward a drain you cannot cover, you do not know what the liquid is, or you feel unwell, leave the area, keep others out and call your supervisor or emergency services.

Worked example: how many pads?

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume a spill of 1 gallon of liquid. We assume each pad holds 12 fluid ounces when fully used; real pads vary, so read your pad's label. We want a 25 percent spare margin for uneven soaking and a leftover thin film.

  1. One gallon is 128 fluid ounces.
  2. Pads for the liquid alone: 128 ÷ 12 = 10.67, so 11 pads.
  3. Add the margin: 128 × 1.25 = 160 fluid ounces of capacity wanted.
  4. 160 ÷ 12 = 13.33, so 14 pads.

Fourteen pads is the number to have on hand. If your kit has only six, the right move is to stop the source, contain the spill with socks, and call for more supplies, not to smear it with rags.

Field card: Oil and Chemical Spills

Stop it, read it, ring it, soak it, bag it, tell them.

  • Stop the source and remove ignition sources first.
  • Read the safety data sheet: Section 6 spills, 7 storage, 8 protection, 13 disposal.
  • Cover or block drains; do not use water on the spill.
  • Use pads and socks, not a pile of rags; count pads from capacity plus a spare margin.
  • Oily rags go into a closed metal can, emptied daily.
  • Bag, label and dispose by local rules.
  • Tell the customer, take photos, report as your employer directs.
