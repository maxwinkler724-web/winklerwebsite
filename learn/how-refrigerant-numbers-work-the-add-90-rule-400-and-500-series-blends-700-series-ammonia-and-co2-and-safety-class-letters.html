How Refrigerant Numbers Work: Add 90, Blends, 700 Series, Safety Classes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The R-number on a cylinder is not a model number. It is a code that, for many refrigerants, tells you what atoms are in the molecule, and for blends and inorganic fluids tells you which family you are holding. Once you can read it, R-22, R-134a, R-32 and R-744 stop being random labels.

Check the standard

Check the current edition. The numbering and safety classes come from Standard 34. This page is general education built from public pages we read in 2026. We did not read the standard itself, and new refrigerants are added over time, so confirm a specific refrigerant on its cylinder label and in current documents.

The add-90 rule for single compounds

Single-compound refrigerants built on methane, ethane or propane carry a number whose digits encode the molecule. A university compliance document describes it this way: the first digit is one less than the number of carbons (left off when there is one carbon), the second digit is one more than the number of hydrogens, and the last digit is the number of fluorines. A university atmospheric chemistry page gives the shortcut form: add 90 to the number, and the three resulting digits are the counts of carbon, hydrogen and fluorine, in that order. Its examples are 11 + 90 = 101 (one carbon, no hydrogen, one fluorine) and 12 + 90 = 102.

The two forms are the same idea. Adding 90 is the same as adding 100 and taking 10 off: it raises the carbon digit by one and lowers the hydrogen digit by one, which undoes the offsets built into the code. A federal lab document adds that the digit rules cover compounds with one to three carbons.

Chlorine fills what is left

The code never counts chlorine directly. Every carbon in these molecules is saturated, so the open bonds are fixed: one carbon has four, two carbons have six, three carbons have eight. That is the pattern 2C + 2. Whatever is not taken by hydrogen or fluorine is chlorine (or bromine, which this lesson skips). This is our own working from basic bonding, and the worked example below checks it against published formulas.

Letters after the number

A lowercase letter after the digits marks an isomer, a different arrangement of the same atoms, according to a federal lab document. R-134a is the arrangement CF3-CH2F, and the letter does not change the atom counts, so you ignore it while decoding. An uppercase letter on a blend, such as R-401A and R-401B, marks the same components in different proportions, according to an international refrigerant fact sheet filed in an EPA docket.

Worked example: decoding three refrigerants (our own example)

Our own example. Assumption: each refrigerant is a single compound with one or two carbons, so the add-90 rule and the 2C + 2 count apply. Reference formulas are from the NIST Chemistry WebBook.

  • R-22. Step 1: 22 + 90 = 112. Step 2: read the digits, C = 1, H = 1, F = 2. Step 3: one carbon has 2(1) + 2 = 4 open bonds. Step 4: Cl = 4 - 1 - 2 = 1. Predicted formula CHClF2. NIST lists chlorodifluoromethane as CHClF2, molecular weight 86.468. Match.
  • R-134a. Step 1: drop the letter, 134 + 90 = 224. Step 2: C = 2, H = 2, F = 4. Step 3: two carbons have 2(2) + 2 = 6 open bonds. Step 4: Cl = 6 - 2 - 4 = 0. Predicted formula C2H2F4. NIST lists R-134a as C2H2F4, molecular weight 102.0309. Match.
  • R-32. Step 1: 32 + 90 = 122. Step 2: C = 1, H = 2, F = 2. Step 3: four open bonds. Step 4: Cl = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0. Predicted formula CH2F2. NIST lists difluoromethane as CH2F2, molecular weight 52.0234. Match.
  • Bonus, propane. R-290: 290 + 90 = 380, so C = 3, H = 8, F = 0, which is C3H8, propane. That is why propane sits in the 200s while isobutane does not.

Reading it the other way is useful too. R-22 has chlorine, so it is an HCFC, while R-134a and R-32 have none. The R-number alone does not name the class, but the decode shows it.

400 and 500 series: blends

Blends get their own numbers. The international fact sheet says R-4xx blends are zeotropic (liquid and vapor compositions differ) and R-5xx blends are azeotropes (same composition at a given pressure). Blend numbers are assigned in sequence after a satisfactory review of the producer's data, so the number tells you the order, not the chemistry. You cannot add 90 to a blend. See Refrigerant Temperature Glide for why zeotropic blends need bubble and dew points, and HFO and HFO Blend Refrigerants for the newer ones.

600 and 700 series

A federal lab document says the 600 series covers miscellaneous organic compounds and the 700 series covers inorganic fluids. An EPA-hosted position document on natural refrigerants lists butane as R-600 and isobutane as R-600a, so the isomer letter works here too.

For inorganic fluids, the same document lists ammonia as R-717, carbon dioxide as R-744 and water as R-718. The pattern we see is 700 plus the molecular weight. NIST gives carbon dioxide a molecular weight of 44.0095 and ammonia 17.0305, which fits 744 and 717. We could not open a free public page that states this rule outright, so treat it as our own observation from the published numbers.

Our own check, CO2: molecular weight 44.0095, rounded to 44, then 700 + 44 = 744. That is R-744. Ammonia: 17.0305 rounds to 17, 700 + 17 = 717, which is R-717.

Safety class letters and numbers

A university compliance document says Standard 34 combines toxicity and flammability. The letter is toxicity: A is lower toxicity, B is higher. The number is flammability: 1 shows no flame propagation, 2 is flammable, and 3 is higher flammability. A National Library of Medicine article adds that class 3 means a lower flammability limit below 0.10 kg/m³ or a heat of combustion above 19 MJ/kg, and that class 2L was added in 2019 as a separate, lower-flammability class. The fact sheet describes 2L as class 2 refrigerants that burn very slowly. Together these give classes such as A1, A2L, A2, A3, B1, B2L, B2 and B3.

The natural refrigerants document calls hydrocarbons highly flammable and describes ammonia as having lower flammability, so propane and isobutane sit in the flammable end of the table. For how techs handle the 2L and 3 groups, see A2L and A3 Refrigerants; for oils see Refrigerant Oils.

Common mistakes

  • Adding 90 to a blend number or a 700-series number. The rule is only for compounds with one to three carbons.
  • Treating a higher blend number as a newer or better refrigerant. It reflects approval order only.
  • Dropping the trailing letter when you order or label. R-134a and R-134 are different molecules.
  • Assuming the safety class is on the R-number. It is a separate label; read it on the cylinder. See Refrigerant Cylinders and Labels.

Field card: Reading refrigerant numbers

Add 90, read three digits, fill the rest with chlorine.

  • One to three carbons: number + 90 gives C, H, F in order.
  • Cl = (2C + 2) - H - F, our own bonding check.
  • Lowercase letter = isomer (R-134a). Uppercase on a blend = different proportions.
  • R-4xx zeotropic blends, R-5xx azeotropes, numbered in approval order.
  • R-600 series: miscellaneous organics (R-600a isobutane). R-290 decodes to propane.
  • R-700 series inorganics: R-717 ammonia, R-718 water, R-744 CO2.
  • Safety class: A or B for toxicity, 1, 2L, 2 or 3 for flammability.
