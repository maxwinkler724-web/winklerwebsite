Safe Disposal of Old Refrigerators, Freezers, Window ACs and Dehumidifiers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An old refrigerator at the curb looks like scrap metal. It is also a sealed pressure system that still holds refrigerant, with foam in its walls that can hold gases of its own. Federal rules say who has to get the refrigerant out before the box is crushed. This lesson covers those rules, the paperwork that follows the appliance, and what a homeowner and a technician each do.

What counts as a small appliance

The regulation defines a small appliance as a product that is fully manufactured, charged and hermetically sealed at the factory with five pounds or less of refrigerant (40 CFR 82.152). The examples it lists include home and commercial refrigerators and freezers, room air conditioners (window units, portable units and packaged terminal units), dehumidifiers, under-the-counter ice makers, vending machines and drinking water coolers. The disposal rules we cover here sit in 40 CFR 82.155, and the recovery levels sit in 82.156 and 82.158.

Who has to recover the refrigerant

The rule is a chain, and each link has a job.

  • Whoever recovers. Anyone who recovers refrigerant from a small appliance for disposal has to reach the evacuation levels in 82.156 and use recovery equipment that meets the standards in 82.158 (82.155(a)).
  • The final processor. A scrap recycler, landfill operator or similar business that receives the appliance has two choices. It can recover any remaining refrigerant itself, or it can verify, by signed statement or contract, that all refrigerant that had not already leaked was recovered (82.155(b)). EPA's disposal page puts it plainly: the final disposer is responsible for recovering any refrigerant in the appliance.
  • The person doing the recovery. EPA's page says people who recover refrigerant from small appliances in the waste stream do not need section 608 certification for that task. That is separate from the certification you need to buy refrigerant or service other equipment; see EPA 608 Certification Basics.

Verification, signed statements and records

When someone other than the final processor recovered the refrigerant, the processor needs proof. Under 82.155(b)(2) a signed statement must show the name and address of the person who recovered the refrigerant and the date of recovery. A signed contract works too, if it says the supplier will recover the remaining refrigerant before delivery or verifies that it was properly recovered before the supplier received the appliance. EPA's page adds that no sticker is required for disposal.

Three more points from the section:

  • The processor must notify its suppliers that refrigerant has to be recovered before delivery. Signs, letters or similar means are acceptable.
  • Accepting a statement or contract the processor knew or had reason to know was false is a violation.
  • If all the refrigerant has already leaked out and recovery is not possible, the processor needs a signed statement saying so before delivery. The rule defines "leaked out" as escaping through system failures, accidents or other unavoidable occurrences, not through negligence or deliberate acts such as cutting refrigerant lines.

The processor keeps copies of every signed statement or contract on site, on paper or electronically, for three years (82.155(c)). For the technician's own record habits, see 608 Records: What Technicians Keep.

How much has to come out: recovery levels and equipment

Section 82.156(b) sets the small-appliance targets. Before opening or disposing of a small appliance, the person must recover refrigerant with certified equipment and reach one of these:

  • 80 percent of the refrigerant, if the recovery equipment was made before November 15, 1993.
  • 90 percent if the equipment was made on or after that date and the appliance's compressor is functioning, or 80 percent if the compressor is not functioning.
  • Or evacuate the appliance to four inches of mercury vacuum.

Section 82.158(e) puts the same idea on the equipment side: small-appliance recovery equipment has to be certified by an approved testing organization to recover 90 percent of the refrigerant in the test stand when the test stand compressor is operational, and 80 percent when it is not. Equipment must be used according to the maker's directions (82.156(g)). For machine care and speed tips, see Refrigerant Recovery Machine Care and Faster Recovery.

What a technician does on a sealed appliance

Get access. Sealed appliances often have no service port. The regulation defines a process stub as tubing that gives access to the refrigerant and can be resealed (82.152). If there is none, a technician may clamp a piercing valve (line tap) to the tube. The rule text we read does not name piercing valves. Our own plain reading is that piercing the tube is opening the system, so the recovery equipment has to be connected and used correctly as part of that step. The tap is a service tool, and the full story of why it leaks and what to leave behind is in Line Tap Piercing Valves on Small Appliances.

Pick the equipment type. The regulation defines two kinds. Self-contained recovery equipment removes refrigerant from an appliance without help from components inside the appliance. System-dependent recovery equipment needs components inside the appliance to remove its refrigerant (82.152). Our own plain reading: a system-dependent setup leans on the appliance, so a dead or seized compressor is a reason to use a self-contained machine. The 80 percent versus 90 percent split in 82.156(b) is also tied to whether the appliance compressor works. Section 82.158 limits system-dependent equipment: it may not be used on appliances holding more than 15 pounds of refrigerant unless it is permanently attached as a pump-out unit. Small appliances are far under that line.

Finish the paperwork. If you are not the final disposer, write the signed statement with your name and address and the date of recovery, and let it travel with the appliance.

The foam is a separate problem

Refrigerant is not the only thing in the box. EPA says the insulating foam in refrigerators and freezers made before 2005 can contain ozone-depleting substances in its blowing agent, and that the treatment of the foam is not regulated. Without recovery the foam is typically shredded or landfilled and the gases are released.

EPA's Responsible Appliance Disposal (RAD) program was a voluntary partnership that began in 2006; EPA's page now lists it as running 2006 to 2025. Partners agreed to go beyond the legal minimum by recovering foam and handling oil, mercury and PCB-containing parts properly. EPA's anniversary report says recovering and destroying the foam from 1,000 refrigerators avoids the greenhouse gas emissions of 115 cars driven for a year, and that partners collected 222,010 refrigerators, 25,957 freezers, 8,839 AC units and 5,867 dehumidifiers in 2020. Those are EPA's figures, not ours.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all our own: a small refrigerator whose nameplate lists an R-134a charge of 6.0 oz, a working compressor, and certified recovery equipment made after November 15, 1993. We use EPA's 100-year GWP for HFC-134a of 1,430 (EPA Technology Transitions GWP reference table, which lists IPCC 2007 as its source). The nameplate value is invented to show the arithmetic.

  1. Convert to pounds. 6.0 oz ÷ 16 oz per lb = 0.375 lb.
  2. Size check. 0.375 lb is far under 5 lb, so it is a small appliance.
  3. CO2-equivalent if all of it were vented. 0.375 lb × 1,430 = 536.25, about 536 lb CO2e.
  4. Recovery target. With a working compressor, 90 percent: 0.90 × 0.375 = 0.3375 lb (5.4 oz) recovered, leaving 0.0375 lb (0.6 oz).
  5. CO2e kept out of the air at the minimum. 0.3375 × 1,430 = 482.6, about 483 lb CO2e. The 0.6 oz left behind is 0.0375 × 1,430 = 53.6, about 54 lb CO2e.
  6. Compare. If the compressor were dead, 80 percent: 0.80 × 0.375 = 0.30 lb, so 429 lb CO2e kept out and 107 lb CO2e left.

The point: a little charge still carries a lot of warming potential, and the percentage you recover is the part you stop from reaching the air.

What a homeowner should do

  • Ask how the refrigerant is handled. Ask the hauler, retailer or utility program whether refrigerant is recovered before the unit is crushed, and whether the foam is recovered. EPA's FAQ suggests asking the company removing your unit about its disposal practices.
  • Check your utility. EPA says to check with your electric utility for rebate or recycling programs and confirm your unit qualifies. EPA also says to contact your municipality about bulky item pickup, since some require a professional to remove the refrigerant first.
  • Do not leave it at the curb where it may be landfilled or dismantled unsafely. EPA notes retailer pick-up fees of roughly $10 to $50.
  • Never cut or puncture lines. Cutting a line vents refrigerant and the rule treats deliberate acts like that differently from unavoidable leaks. Leave the tubing intact and let a recovery technician open it.
  • Do not resell a unit you do not trust. EPA points out that old units are often inefficient.

Field card: Safe Disposal of Old Refrigerators, Freezers, Window ACs and Dehumidifiers

Recover first, sign it, keep the record, never cut the line.

  • Small appliance: sealed at the factory, 5 lb of refrigerant or less (82.152).
  • Final processor: recover it, or verify by signed statement or contract (82.155).
  • Signed statement: name, address and date of recovery; processor keeps it 3 years.
  • Levels: 90% (compressor working, newer equipment), 80% (compressor not working or older equipment), or 4 in. Hg vacuum.
  • Self-contained equipment works alone; system-dependent equipment needs parts of the appliance.
  • Foam is not regulated; ask about foam recovery.
  • Homeowner: ask the hauler or utility how refrigerant is handled.
