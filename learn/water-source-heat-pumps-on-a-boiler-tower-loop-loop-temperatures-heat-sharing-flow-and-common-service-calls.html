Water-Source Heat Pumps on a Boiler/Tower Loop: Loop Temperatures, Heat Sharing, Flow and Common Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A water-source heat pump building looks odd at first: dozens of small compressor units, one shared pipe loop, and a boiler and a cooler that often sit idle. The idea is simple. Each unit trades heat with the loop, and the loop lets zones that need cooling hand their heat to zones that need heating. This lesson explains how that works, what the loop temperatures mean, and what service calls look like.

One reversible unit per zone

Each water-source unit is a reversible refrigeration circuit with an air coil on the room side and a water-to-refrigerant heat exchanger on the loop side, usually a tube-in-tube (coaxial) design. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory report for a federal agency describes the arrangement as compressor units linked by a common water loop served by a cooling tower and a heating source, and notes that heat recovery between units happens when some are mostly cooling while others heat. A university-system decarbonization review puts it in one line: the cooling zones reject heat into the loop while the heating zones absorb it. That review also says the system runs best when heating and cooling happen at the same time. For the refrigeration basics, see Heat Pump Basics; for a ground-coupled loop instead of a boiler and cooler, see Geothermal Ground-Source Heat Pump Basics.

Loop temperature band

The same university-system review lists the usual design: keep the loop water between 60 and 90 F. If the loop rises above 90 F, the excess heat is rejected through a cooling tower. If it falls below 60 F, an additional heat source turns on to hold the temperature, such as a boiler. The review adds that extended-range units with loop water from 45 to 110 F are becoming more common. A state facilities specification we read lists a ground-source unit as rated for entering water from 25 to 115 F, but that is a ground-loop product and not the boiler/tower band. The band is a design choice, so always read the setpoints on the job and the entering-water limits on the unit nameplate. The federal report cited above points out that a boiler on the loop becomes the primary heat source and does not reduce heating energy use, which is why designers prefer to let heat sharing do the work first.

Boiler, cooler and loop pump

The boiler is the loop's heat adder and the cooler or tower is its heat rejecter. In mild weather with a balanced building, neither runs, and the compressors simply trade heat through the water. In a cold snap most units heat, the loop falls toward its low limit, and the boiler fires. On a hot afternoon most units cool, the loop climbs toward its high limit, and the cooler runs. Our cooling tower lesson covers open towers, and dry coolers and closed-circuit fluid coolers covers the closed-circuit option drawn below.

The loop pump keeps water moving through every unit whenever any compressor can run. Our own practical point: no flow means no heat transfer, so the pump and a proof of flow come first. One university design standard we read, for hot water pumps, uses a current sensor on each pump as flow proof and raises an alarm if a pump that was commanded on gives no positive indication of flow after 90 seconds. That is an example of one proving method, not a rule for every loop. Many controls use a differential pressure or flow switch instead (our own note; check the project sequence).

At the unit: hose kit, valves and strainer

A state design manual specification for water-source heat pumps says the hose kit and its shutoff and flow control devices are field installed by the HVAC contractor, and it calls for a coaxial tube-in-tube water-to-refrigerant exchanger, high and low pressure safety cutoffs, and a drain pan sensor that stops the compressor if water rises too high. A national laboratory best-practices page says the upkeep of a fluid loop consists mainly of cleaning strainers, and it mentions a water regulating valve that limits the loop water entering a unit where loop temperatures swing widely. That page is written for ground-source systems, but the same strainer habit applies to any loop that feeds a coaxial heat exchanger. Our own advice: close the isolation valves before pulling a strainer, and note which way flow runs before reassembling.

Water treatment

A university facilities guideline we read requires an inhibitor and a bypass feeder on closed loops, a thorough flush, and a long circulation with cleaning solution before new piping is put in service, then retesting. It does not address heat pump loops by name, so treat it as a model for closed hydronic loops, not a loop recipe. Dirty or untreated water is the root of many unit problems below: scale and sludge collect in the narrow coaxial passages first. Follow the water treatment plan for the building; if there is none, tell the owner.

Common service calls

These are our own explanations from general refrigeration principles. See AC high and low pressure switches for testing.

  • High pressure lockout in cooling. The water side is the condenser. A hot loop, low flow, a clogged strainer or a fouled coaxial exchanger all push condensing pressure up until the switch opens. Check loop temperature and flow before touching refrigerant.
  • Low pressure or freeze lockout in heating. The water side is now the evaporator. A cold loop or low flow lowers the evaporating temperature and can trip the low pressure or freeze protection. Check the boiler and the loop pump.
  • Many units lock out at once. That points at the loop (pump off, closed valve, boiler or cooler not working), not at the individual units.
  • Air-side causes. A NYSERDA report on vertical stack units found air bypass around the coil at all five sites studied, which can ice a coil in cooling and cause high pressure trips in heating. Do not assume every trip is water related.
  • Condensate. A plugged drain or a tripped drain pan sensor stops the compressor. Clear the drain and check the pan before resetting.

Worked example (our own, with assumed values)

A building has 30 tons of cooling units and 10 tons of heating units running at the same time. We assume a cooling efficiency of 14 EER and a heating COP of 4.5. The university-system review lists ranges of EER 13 to 17.4 and COP 4.7 to 5.7 at standard test water temperatures, so our COP is a little conservative. These are assumptions for practice, not site data. One ton is 12,000 Btu/h, and 1 watt is 3.412 Btu/h.

  1. Cooling load: 30 tons x 12,000 = 360,000 Btu/h.
  2. Cooling compressor input: 360,000 / 14 = 25,714 W (EER is Btu/h per watt). Heat from that power: 25,714 x 3.412 = 87,737 Btu/h.
  3. Heat the cooling units put into the loop: 360,000 + 87,737 = 447,737 Btu/h.
  4. Heating delivered: 10 tons x 12,000 = 120,000 Btu/h.
  5. Heating compressor input: 120,000 / 4.5 = 26,667 Btu/h (about 7.8 kW). Heat taken from the loop: 120,000 - 26,667 = 93,333 Btu/h.
  6. Net heat to reject: 447,737 - 93,333 = 354,404 Btu/h, about 29.5 tons.

Heat sharing took about 21 percent (93,333 / 447,737) off the cooler's job, and the boiler adds nothing in this moment. We ignored fan heat, pump heat and pipe losses, so the real cooler duty is somewhat higher.

Field card: water-source heat pumps on a loop

Cooling units give heat to the loop, heating units take it, and the boiler and cooler cover the difference.

  • Typical design band is 60 to 90 F; read the actual setpoints and the unit limits.
  • Below the low limit the boiler adds heat; above the high limit the cooler or tower rejects heat.
  • Check flow first: pump, valves, strainer, hose kit.
  • Many lockouts at once means look at the loop. One unit means look at that unit.
  • Heat rejected in cooling is load plus compressor heat; heat taken in heating is output minus compressor heat.
  • Follow the building water treatment plan.
