Planning HVAC for a Commercial Tenant Fit-Out

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A tenant fit-out turns an empty shell or an old store into an office, clinic, gym or shop. The walls, lights and people change, but the rooftop unit on the roof often stays. This lesson walks through the questions to ask before anyone orders ductwork: is the existing equipment old or tired, does it have enough cooling and outdoor air for the new use, and how will the air be zoned, exhausted, controlled and balanced before move-in.

Start with the unit on the roof

Packaged rooftop units are common in commercial buildings. A Federal energy department protocol chapter says packaged systems typically run from 3 to 20 tons, and a national end-use report from the same department says packaged air conditioning serves about 48 percent of cooled commercial floor space. So there is a good chance the shell you are standing in is served by one. For how these units are built, read Rooftop Unit Basics.

Walk the roof and write down what you find: the nameplate model and serial number, the tonnage, the heat type, the refrigerant, the manufacture date, whether it has an economizer, and the curb and duct connections. Then note condition: rust, a dented coil, belt wear, a noisy compressor, dirty filters, a stuck outdoor air damper. A unit that is near the end of its life changes the plan, because the owner may be better off budgeting a replacement now than after the walls are closed. See Replacing a Rooftop Unit for what that involves. The same federal energy department chapter notes that typical design practice tends to oversize units, and that more oversizing means fewer operating hours and less efficient operation, so do not assume bigger is safer.

Who owns the equipment? Ask early

In many leases the landlord owns the roof and the rooftop unit, and the tenant pays for use. In others the tenant takes over the unit and its upkeep. This is a question for the tenant and landlord to settle in writing, ideally with their own advisers, and this lesson is not legal advice. What a technician can do is give them facts: the unit age, a condition report and a rough capacity check, so the conversation about who pays for a repair or a new unit starts from real numbers.

Does the new use need more cooling?

A change of use changes heat. A clinic packs more people, more equipment and more outdoor air into the same floor area than a quiet shop. The main internal gains are people, lights, plug equipment and the outdoor air brought in for ventilation, on top of heat through the roof, walls and glass. A real project needs a full load calculation for the new layout. The worked example below shows why a quick check is worth doing first.

Does it need more outdoor air?

Ventilation needs follow the use of the space and the head count. The federal energy department's 2025 ventilation assessment guide says testing and balancing should include updates in response to changes in building occupancy or space use, and the federal occupational health pages say to make sure existing systems provide at least the minimum outdoor air required by the ventilation codes that apply to the building's occupancy. In practice that means checking the outdoor air damper and its actuator, the economizer, the minimum position and, if used, the carbon dioxide sensors. The same guide says to inspect dampers, actuators and linkages by cycling them fully open and fully closed. The end-use report above notes that rising outdoor air requirements have led to energy recovery ventilators and dedicated outdoor air units, which is one option when the old rooftop unit cannot handle the new outdoor air load. For control strategy, see Demand-Controlled Ventilation.

Zoning for new walls and rooms

One thermostat and one unit can serve an open store fine. Add private offices, exam rooms, a server closet and a conference room, and the loads no longer move together. Sunny rooms, crowded rooms and equipment rooms call for cooling at different times. Options include several smaller rooftop units, one unit with zone dampers and a zone controller, or terminal boxes on a larger system. Ask for each room: how many people, what equipment, which wall faces the sun, and does it need to run after hours. Keep the thermostat out of direct sun and away from supply air.

Duct and diffuser layout

Lay the supply and return paths on the new floor plan before walls go up. Keep supply diffusers where they will not dump cold air on a desk or a treatment chair, give every closed room a return path or a transfer opening, and leave access to dampers and the volume-control devices that will need to be set later. Check that the old duct is the right size for the new airflow. For fan and duct pressure basics, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Exhaust for restrooms and break rooms

Restrooms and break rooms need exhaust, and exhaust removes air the unit has to replace. The federal occupational health ventilation page says restroom exhaust fans should be functional and running at full capacity when the building is occupied, and that exhaust for kitchens and cooking areas should be inspected and maintained and run while those spaces are occupied. Add the new exhaust flows to the outdoor air budget, route the discharge away from rooftop unit intakes, and make sure the building does not end up under strong negative pressure, which makes doors hard to open and pulls in untreated air.

Thermostat and control setup

Decide who can change setpoints, what the occupied and unoccupied schedules are, and whether the fan runs continuously during occupied hours. The ventilation assessment guide warns that startup and shutdown schedules should match how people actually use the space, and the occupational health page says to turn off any demand-controlled ventilation settings that cut supply air while the building is occupied if outdoor air is the goal. Label every thermostat with the zone it serves.

Startup and balancing before move-in

Do the startup checks in RTU Startup and Checkout Basics, then balance the air. A state university facilities page describes a typical order: the installer confirms everything is installed and working, the balancing devices are set fully open, the balancer measures and adjusts, and a final report records the results and any items that miss the design. It also calls for locking volume dampers with final settings clearly marked. Measure the outdoor air and the supply to each room, fix what falls short, and keep the report with the owner's records.

Worked example: old use versus new use (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design. Assume a 4,000 square foot space served by one rooftop unit. Assume the unit is nominally 10 tons (120,000 Btu/h) and that its age and condition leave it about 10 percent short, so we use 108,000 Btu/h as the usable capacity. Assume the roof, wall and glass heat gain stays at 30,000 Btu/h in both cases. Use 3.412 Btu/h per watt, 450 Btu/h per person (our assumption for sensible plus latent), and 4.5 x cfm x enthalpy difference for outdoor air, with an assumed difference of 12 Btu per pound.

  • Old use, a shop: 20 people x 450 = 9,000. Lights 1.5 W/sq ft x 4,000 = 6,000 W x 3.412 = 20,472. Equipment 0.5 W/sq ft x 4,000 = 2,000 W x 3.412 = 6,824. Outdoor air 400 cfm x 4.5 x 12 = 21,600. Envelope 30,000. Total: 9,000 + 20,472 + 6,824 + 30,000 + 21,600 = 87,896 Btu/h, or 7.3 tons. That is 81 percent of the 108,000 Btu/h usable capacity.
  • New use, a clinic: 45 people x 450 = 20,250. Lights 1.0 W/sq ft x 4,000 = 4,000 W x 3.412 = 13,648. Equipment 3 W/sq ft x 4,000 = 12,000 W x 3.412 = 40,944. Outdoor air 900 cfm x 4.5 x 12 = 48,600. Envelope 30,000. Total: 20,250 + 13,648 + 40,944 + 30,000 + 48,600 = 153,442 Btu/h, or 12.8 tons.
  • Result: 153,442 - 108,000 = 45,442 Btu/h short, about 3.8 tons. Even against the full 120,000 Btu/h nameplate, the new load is 128 percent.

The old shop looked fine, and the clinic overloads the same unit by more than a third. The biggest jumps are plug equipment and outdoor air. A real answer needs a full load calculation, a measured airflow check and a latent load check, but this quick math tells the owner and tenant early that a second unit, a larger one or a dedicated outdoor air unit may be on the table.

Field card: Planning HVAC for a Commercial Tenant Fit-Out

Check the roof, then the room, then the lease.

  • Record unit model, tons, age, heat type, economizer and condition.
  • Compare the new use's people, equipment and outdoor air to the old use.
  • Check that the outdoor air damper moves and that minimum air meets the new head count.
  • Zone rooms with different loads; give closed rooms a return path.
  • Add restroom and break room exhaust to the outdoor air budget.
  • Ask tenant and landlord in writing who owns and maintains the unit.
  • Start up, balance, mark damper settings and keep the report.
