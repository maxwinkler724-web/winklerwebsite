Inspection Mirrors, Lights and Borescope Cameras for HVAC Work: What They Show and What They Cannot Prove

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A good light and a small mirror let you see places your head will never fit. A borescope or articulating inspection camera takes you further, down a duct run, behind a coil or into the passages of a furnace. This lesson covers what each tool does, how to read its specifications, where it helps, and why a camera picture alone cannot prove a heat exchanger is sound or cracked.

The three tool families

Telescoping inspection mirror. A small mirror on an extendable rod, often with a swivel head. It has no battery and nothing to fail, and it is the first thing to reach for behind a burner box, under a drain pan or along the back of a cabinet. Its limits are the angle of the swivel and the light you can get onto the mirror.

Flashlights and headlamps. A headlamp keeps both hands free and lights where you look. A pocket flashlight lets you rake light across a surface at a low angle, which makes cracks, warps and pitting throw shadows. Carry both. Use a light rated for the location when you work near fuel gas or oil vapors, and follow the tool maker's manual on that point.

Borescope and articulating inspection camera. A small camera head on a flexible or semi-rigid probe, with built-in LEDs and a handheld screen. A university lab page on industrial borescopes describes flexible probes from 1 m up to 120 m and telescopic rigid rods up to 3 m, with 2-way or 4-way tip angulation and heads that rotate around the probe axis. Many digital models store pictures on a memory card, usually as JPEG stills and MP4 or ASF video, with date and time stamps.

Reading the specifications

  • Probe diameter. The head must fit the opening, and the university page notes that head size also affects how tightly an angled camera can bend. Smaller is not always better: a tiny head often has a smaller sensor and dimmer image. Match the probe to the narrowest opening you expect.
  • Tip articulation. A tip that bends 2-way moves in one plane; 4-way moves up, down, left and right. Articulation lets you look around a bend or sweep along a wall, but the probe behind the tip must still be able to follow the path.
  • Focal distance. The page lists example working ranges such as 5 mm to 50 mm for a front camera and 3 mm to 50 mm for a side camera, and says sharpness is best at a certain distance. Anything closer or farther is soft. A view of a surface 6 in away may be blurry on a head built for close work. Check the range in the tool maker's manual.
  • Viewing angle. The same page says small viewing angles suit detail work but are less useful for an overview. You can see a spot clearly and miss what is next to it.
  • Recording. Stills and video go into the service notes. Check that the date and time are right on the tool before you rely on the stamp.
  • Waterproofing. A waterproof rating protects the probe from splashes and drips. It does not make the tool safe to push into an energized or hot appliance. Follow the manual.

Where these tools help

  • Furnace burner area and heat exchanger openings. With the unit off, locked out and cool, a mirror and light, or a camera through the burner opening, can show soot, scale, rust flakes, a displaced baffle or a burner that has shifted. A federal energy agency guide lists "check heat exchanger for cracks or leaks" as a step in its furnace tune-up checklist and the same step in its boiler tune-up checklist. It does not say how the check is done. Our heat exchanger types lesson explains why some shapes hide most of their surface.
  • Boiler fireboxes. Open and inspect only as the boiler maker's instructions allow, with the boiler off, cool and isolated, and follow the maker's steps for any cleanout or inspection ports.
  • Inside duct runs. A camera can find a crushed section, a disconnected joint, liner that has come loose or debris, without cutting new access. Our duct liner lesson covers what to do about the liner you find. The federal energy agency guide also says the contractor visually inspects ducts for insulation levels and signs of mold or moisture.
  • Wall and ceiling cavities before drilling. A small hole and a camera show framing, pipes, wires and insulation before a larger hole goes through. This does not replace the checks in our coring lesson. A drill-sized pilot hole can still hit a wire.
  • Behind coils and under drain pans. A mirror often does the job. A camera on a short probe helps with a blocked pan outlet or a coil edge that is buried in the cabinet.

What a camera cannot prove

A heat exchanger inspection is a set of checks, not one look. Our cracked heat exchanger lesson walks through flame behavior, airstream readings, flue readings and the physical inspection, and it reports that a published industry guideline advises against using cameras to check for leaks. Keep these limits in mind.

  • A clean picture is not a clean bill of health. The camera shows only the part that was in front of the lens, from one angle, at one temperature. Some cracks are tight when cold and open when hot, which is another reason the cracked heat exchanger lesson gives for repeating checks.
  • A dark line is not a crack. Shadows, weld seams, stamped ribs, soot streaks and scale can all look like cracks on a small screen. A suspect picture is a reason to look closer, not a verdict.
  • Some parts cannot be reached. Many passages are too tight, too bent or sealed. In the worked example below, only part of one cell can be viewed.
  • A camera does not measure leakage. It cannot tell you whether combustion gases are reaching the house air. Analyzer readings and the other steps do that.

Treat a camera as a way to collect evidence and records. Treat what you see as one data point next to the others.

Worked example (our own): how much of one cell does a camera see?

This is our own teaching example, not a measurement from any real furnace. The assumptions are invented to show the arithmetic.

Assumptions: one heat exchanger cell is 30 in long measured along its center. The only access is the burner-end opening. The first bend in the cell stops the probe after it has traveled 10 in. Once at the bend, the tip articulates and the camera can see 4 in farther along the cell, which is within its focal range. The lens views about half of the inside wall at each spot along the way, because a tight cell and a narrow viewing angle leave the rest in shadow or out of focus.

  1. Length reached by the probe: 10 in.
  2. Added length seen by looking around the bend: 4 in.
  3. Total length seen: 10 + 4 = 14 in.
  4. Share of the cell length: 14 ÷ 30 = 0.467, about 47 percent.
  5. Unseen length: 30 − 14 = 16 in, or 53 percent.
  6. Wall coverage: 47 percent of the length × 0.5 of the wall = about 23 percent of the inside surface.

Under these assumptions, even a careful pass leaves most of the surface unseen. If the unit has several cells, repeat the work for each one. A different geometry changes the numbers. The lesson is the method: write down the cell length, the distance the probe traveled and the view distance, then calculate how much you did not see.

Heat and electrical safety

  • Power off and locked out. The federal workplace safety agency's lockout standard applies to servicing and maintenance where unexpected startup or release of stored energy could injure someone. It requires the equipment to be isolated from the energy source and rendered inoperative, and stored or residual energy to be relieved. Before work, the authorized person confirms the equipment is isolated and de-energized. Control-circuit devices such as push buttons and selector switches are not energy isolating devices, so a thermostat set to off does not count.
  • Let it cool. A burner area, flue and exchanger stay hot long after the flame goes out. Wait until the metal is cool to the touch before any probe goes in.
  • No probe in live equipment or hot flues. A probe near bare terminals, a moving blower wheel or hot metal is a hazard to you and to the tool. Read the maker's manual for the tool's temperature limits and do not exceed them.
  • Do not force it. A probe that will not advance has hit something. Back out and try another angle. A stuck probe in a heat exchanger is a bad day.
  • Gas smell or CO alarm. Leave the area and call from outside. The camera is not a leak detector.

Cleaning and storing the probe

Wipe soot, condensate and oil off the probe and lens after each job with a clean soft cloth, using only the cleaner the maker names. Check that the lens is not scratched or fogged and that the LEDs light. Coil the probe loosely so it does not kink past its minimum bend radius, which the maker lists, and store the tool in its case with the batteries charged. Keep a separate mirror and light for dirty jobs so you do not grind soot into the camera lens.

Recording photos in the service notes

  • Note date, time, unit make and model if allowed in your records, and where the view was taken from, such as burner-end opening, cell 2.
  • Take a wide shot first, then a close shot of anything that looks wrong, then the same area from another angle.
  • Write what the picture does and does not show, such as "left wall of cell 2, 10 in in, no visible crack; rest not reached."
  • Record any other checks done, so the picture is not read as the only test.
  • Keep the stills with the job record. Do not edit or crop out context.

For hot spots and temperature patterns, a thermal camera is a different tool with its own limits. See Thermal Imaging Cameras for HVAC and Infrared Thermography for HVAC and Electrical Gear.

Field card: Inspection Mirrors, Lights and Cameras

One view is a partial view.

  • Mirror and light first; camera when the path allows.
  • Check probe diameter, tip articulation and focal range against the opening.
  • Unit off, locked out and cool before anything goes in.
  • No probe in live equipment or hot flues.
  • Clean and dry the probe, coil it loosely, store it in its case.
  • Our example: 14 of 30 in seen is about 47 percent of the length.
  • A clean view does not rule out a crack; a dark line does not prove one.
  • Record what was seen and what was not reached.
