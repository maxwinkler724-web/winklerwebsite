Cleanroom HVAC: ISO Classes, HEPA, Air Changes and Pressure Cascades

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cleanroom is an ordinary-looking room with a very controlled air system. The HVAC does the cleaning: it pushes large amounts of filtered air through the space and holds the room at a higher pressure than its neighbors, so dirty air has nowhere to go but out. This lesson explains the pieces and the numbers, then sizes a small room step by step.

Check your site rules

Check your site rules. Cleanrooms are run under written procedures and, in drug manufacturing, under FDA rules. This page is general education built from the sources listed below. Follow the facility procedure, and let the facility's quality group and testing contractor decide what a room must meet.

What a cleanroom is

A Jefferson Lab lecture on cleanroom design describes a cleanroom, using the ISO 14644-1 wording, as a room where the concentration of airborne particles is controlled and other conditions are managed as needed. In practice that means three controlled things: particles (filtration and airflow), pressure (which way air leaks), and often temperature and humidity.

ISO classes in plain terms

ISO 14644-1 sorts rooms into classes by how many particles are allowed in a cubic meter of air. A lower number means a cleaner room. The Budlong cleanroom HVAC article lists the limits at 0.5 micron and larger as 3,520 per cubic meter for ISO 5, 352,000 for ISO 7 and 3,520,000 for ISO 8. The FDA aseptic processing guidance uses the same ISO 5 figure for critical areas, and recommends that the area next to the aseptic line be ISO 7 while it is operating, with ISO 8 suitable for less critical work such as equipment cleaning. The class is always stated for a condition: as-built, at-rest or in-operation. People in the room add particles, so one room can meet a class empty and miss it during work.

HEPA and ULPA filters

The filter is what makes the supply air clean. The U.S. convention, which the maker Spycor attributes to the DOE HEPA standard (DOE-STD-3020), is a HEPA filter that captures at least 99.97% of particles at 0.3 micron. Spycor's FAQ puts that another way: up to 3 particles in 10,000 get through. The Budlong article describes ULPA as typically 99.999% or better at the most penetrating particle size, with a higher pressure drop and more fan energy, so it is chosen only where the room needs it.

Fan filter units or ducted HEPA

There are two common ways to deliver the air. A fan filter unit (FFU) is a HEPA filter with a fan attached, according to American Cleanrooms. It drops into the ceiling grid and is usually fed from a plenum above. The 2 ft by 4 ft size is the most common. The older approach was a central air handler with ducts to HEPA terminal housings in the ceiling, which American Cleanrooms describes as costlier and slower to deliver and needing frequent rebalancing. Budlong notes that ducted unidirectional rooms typically need a plenum 1.2 to 1.5 meters deep, and that most cleanrooms use either a large recirculation air handler or a bank of FFUs for the bulk airflow, with a smaller outdoor air unit for ventilation and humidity.

Unidirectional and non-unidirectional flow

Non-unidirectional (turbulent) rooms mix filtered supply air with the room air and dilute whatever particles are there. Budlong says they cover ISO 6 through ISO 9 and most ISO 5 rooms without a critical zone, with filters over only part of the ceiling. Unidirectional (laminar) flow sends air downward in a steady sheet, at about 0.45 meters per second, roughly 90 feet per minute, and sweeps particles to the returns. Budlong says that needs ceiling filter coverage of about 80% or more over the protected zone. FDA gives the same 90 fpm figure for aseptic critical areas, with a range of plus or minus 20 percent, measured 6 inches from the filter face.

Air changes per hour

Air changes per hour (ACH) is airflow in cfm times 60, divided by room volume in cubic feet. The ranges below are industry practice as Budlong states them, not code limits, and the article calls air change rate an outcome rather than an input. ISO 8: 20 to 40 ACH. ISO 7: 60 to 90. ISO 6: 150 to 240. ISO 5 achieved by dilution: 240 to 600. FDA's guidance says at least 20 ACH is typically acceptable for ISO 8 supporting rooms and that significantly higher rates are normally needed for ISO 7 and ISO 5 areas. The sizing basics are in Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics; cleanrooms are the same idea at a much higher rate.

Worked example: a 60 ACH room

Our own arithmetic, using a stated room and stated numbers. The room is 20 ft by 15 ft by 10 ft, the target is 60 ACH, and each FFU is 2 ft by 4 ft running at a face velocity of 90 fpm.

  1. Our own: volume = 20 x 15 x 10 = 3,000 cubic feet.
  2. Our own: cfm needed = ACH x volume / 60 = 60 x 3,000 / 60 = 3,000 cfm.
  3. Our own: one FFU is 2 x 4 = 8 square feet, so cfm per FFU = 8 x 90 = 720 cfm.
  4. Our own: number of FFUs = 3,000 / 720 = 4.17, so round up to 5. Four would give 2,880 cfm, which is only 57.6 ACH.
  5. Our own: with 5 FFUs, airflow = 5 x 720 = 3,600 cfm, and ACH = 3,600 x 60 / 3,000 = 72 ACH.
  6. Our own: ceiling area = 20 x 15 = 300 square feet. FFU area = 5 x 8 = 40 square feet. Coverage = 40 / 300 = 13.3%.

The 13% coverage is typical of a non-unidirectional room. For comparison, Our own: covering 80% of the same ceiling would be 0.80 x 300 = 240 square feet, or 30 FFUs, moving 30 x 720 = 21,600 cfm, which is 432 ACH. That is why unidirectional zones are usually small areas inside a larger room. Real FFUs are rated by the maker and vary with filter loading and speed setting, so a design uses the published airflow for the unit, not the round number used here.

Pressure cascades, gowning rooms and airlocks

People and materials are the biggest source of particles, so they enter through a gowning room or an airlock. Budlong lists personnel airlocks and gowning ante-rooms as ISO 8 spaces, typically at 20 to 40 ACH with terminal HEPA filters and positive pressure. The cascade steps up as you move toward the clean space. FDA's guidance recommends a positive differential of at least 10 to 15 pascals between adjacent rooms of different classification with doors closed, and monitoring it continuously with alarms that are documented and investigated. Budlong gives 10 to 15 Pa as roughly 0.04 to 0.06 inch w.c., and the Jefferson Lab slides use a 0.02 inch w.c. minimum between rooms. These are different programs with different limits, so the site's own setpoint is the one that counts.

Temperature and humidity

FDA lists temperature and humidity controls among the features of aseptic processing areas but gives no setpoints in the guidance text we read. Budlong says temperature is usually the easy part because the recirculation airflow is so large, while humidity is often the binding limit. Latent load is handled at the outdoor air unit, and tight bands can call for dew point control, reheat or desiccant drying.

What techs check

  • Filter pressure drop. Spycor says to change a HEPA filter when the pressure drop reaches the maker's final resistance, which is commonly about twice the initial value, and also when airflow falls short or the filter is damaged. Scientific Safety Alliance lists pressure drop across the filter as part of filter testing.
  • Room pressure monitors. Read the differential gauges and alarms, and report drift or alarms to the facility contact. Do not silence an alarm to finish a job.
  • FFU fan speed. American Cleanrooms notes FFUs can have a speed control, either a rheostat on the unit or a remote. A speed change changes airflow, ACH and pressure, so make changes only as the site procedure allows.
  • Leak and scan testing. The testing contractor challenges the filter upstream with an aerosol and scans the downstream face, seals and frame with a photometer (Scientific Safety Alliance, FDA). FDA says one reading of 0.01 percent of the upstream challenge indicates a significant leak, and suggests testing at installation and at intervals such as twice a year for an aseptic processing room. Scientific Safety Alliance says annual testing is common. Techs do not run this test, but they should know a disturbed gel seal or a bumped filter can force a retest.

Field card: Cleanroom HVAC

Filter, flow, pressure: clean air in, higher pressure inside.

  • Lower ISO number means a cleaner room; the class is stated for a condition (at-rest or operating).
  • HEPA: at least 99.97% at 0.3 micron (US convention).
  • ACH = cfm x 60 / room volume in cubic feet.
  • Unidirectional: about 90 fpm, large ceiling coverage. Non-unidirectional: dilution, partial coverage.
  • Cleaner room is at higher pressure; FDA guidance cites at least 10 to 15 Pa between classes.
  • Watch filter pressure drop, room pressure alarms and FFU speed. Leak scans belong to the testing contractor.
