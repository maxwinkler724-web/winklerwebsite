Portable Propane Heaters in Shanties, Blinds and Tents: Carbon Monoxide and Fresh Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An ice fishing shanty, a hunting blind and a tent have one thing in common: very little air. A small flame in a small box can use up the oxygen and fill the space with carbon monoxide (CO) faster than most people expect. This lesson explains why, what the safety devices on a portable propane heater really do, and the habits that keep a heated shelter survivable.

Why a small space is different

Every fuel-burning heater needs oxygen and makes exhaust. In a house, the volume of air is large and the building leaks. In a shelter that is a few hundred cubic feet, with tight walls to hold the heat, the same flame has far less air to work with. When the flame gets too little oxygen, combustion is incomplete and the heater makes CO, a gas you cannot see or smell. A federal consumer product safety page explains that CO reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen, and that low blood oxygen can lead to unconsciousness and death. A federal health page adds that people who are asleep or have been drinking can die before they notice any symptoms.

Two separate problems stack up here. The first is oxygen depletion: the flame slowly eats the air. The second is CO and other exhaust gases building up. A tight shelter can suffer from both at once, which is why the fixes are the same: fresh air in, exhaust out, and an alarm that does not depend on you feeling well.

"Indoor use" does not mean "no fresh air"

Some portable propane heaters are labeled for indoor use. A federal consumer product safety news release explains that these models carry an oxygen depletion sensor (often shortened to ODS) that closes the gas valve when oxygen in the space falls to about 18 percent, and that the heater still has to be used with the ventilation the maker's instructions call for. A university extension page agrees: even with an indoor-safe model, open a door or window often. Older heaters without the sensor are made for outdoor use only. Look for words on the label such as "oxygen depletion sensor" or "designed for indoor use," and read the manual for the required opening sizes. Those sizes differ from one heater to the next, so we do not give one number for all of them.

A federal land-management agency technology report tested portable heaters. It noted that normal air is about 20.9 percent oxygen, and that makers' manuals ask for a low and a high opening, sized by the heater's input. It also made a point that surprised us: the sensor is a last line of defense, not a promise. In a sealed test chamber the sensor shut one heater off after roughly 30 to 66 minutes, depending on the burn rate, and CO reached about 70 parts per million in two of the three settings. In another test, with air changing at a steady rate, a heater reached about 38 ppm of CO without the sensor ever tripping. A state natural resources department page on ice shelters says the same thing in plain words: do not rely on a ventilation system or a low-oxygen shutoff to protect you.

The two-opening idea

The state ice-shelter page describes the layout: one opening within about a foot of the floor to bring fresh air in, and one opening near the top so exhaust can leave. Warm exhaust rises, so the high opening lets it out while cold fresh air slides in low. Snow and ice can plug those openings overnight, so check them often and keep any ceiling vents on a wheelhouse open and clear. The same page says to keep larger propane cylinders outside the shelter, with a small one-pound cylinder on a portable heater as the stated exception, and to use only heaters rated for indoor use, not open "sunflower" style heaters.

Alarms, sleeping and symptoms

Put a battery-powered CO alarm in the shelter and follow the alarm maker's placement directions. The state ice-shelter page says to test it regularly and replace it when it expires, and a federal health page says to replace detectors per the instructions or about every five years and to use battery-powered or battery back-up units. An alarm is the one safeguard that does not depend on a sensor in the heater.

Do not sleep with a fuel-burning heater running. The federal consumer product safety page says never to use fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in enclosed areas such as tents, campers or vehicles, and notes the risk is higher at high altitude. The university extension page says never to leave a fuel-burning heater unattended or run it while sleeping, even an indoor-safe model. Our own rule of thumb follows from that: if you plan to sleep, shut the heater off and use insulation, sleeping bags and warm clothing, unless the setup is vented and the maker designed it for sleeping use.

Know the symptoms. A federal health page lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, and says they are often described as flu-like. The extension page adds shortness of breath and blurred vision. If you or anyone in the shelter feels this way, get everyone into fresh air at once, as the state ice-shelter page advises. A federal health page says to call 911 or your local poison control center at 1-800-222-1222, or consult a health care professional right away. The consumer product safety page adds a warning for campers: if cold or flu-like symptoms show up while camping, see a doctor, because CO poisoning is often mistaken for those illnesses.

Fire clearances

The same heat that warms you ignites tarps and canvas. A university extension page for hunters says to keep at least 3 feet between the heater and any object around it, to keep it at least 10 feet from combustible tarps, canvas or similar coverings, and not to set it directly on plywood: place it on a 4 foot by 4 foot square of fire-resistant drywall or cement board. Those are that page's numbers; your heater's manual may ask for more. Do not use a heater to cook or dry clothes. Many heaters also include a tip-over switch. Our own advice is to read the manual to see whether yours does, and to set the heater on a flat, stable surface either way. Note that a 10 foot clearance to canvas is more than a small blind or tent may offer, so in a tent the right answer is often a different heat plan, not a closer heater. The federal land-management report went further and said that unvented propane heaters should never be used inside a personal tent.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with stated assumptions. It is not a design rule and it ignores leaks, wind and heater adjustment.

Assumptions: a shanty 6 ft wide by 8 ft long by 7 ft high, treated as a sealed box with no air leaks; a heater burning 0.1 pound of propane per hour; about 21,500 Btu per pound of propane, which is our own planning value, not taken from a source on this page; oxygen at 20.9 percent to start, as the federal land-management report states for normal air; and the heater's sensor trips at 18 percent, as the federal consumer product safety news release states.

  1. Interior volume: 6 ft x 8 ft x 7 ft = 336 cubic feet.
  2. Heat input: 0.1 lb/h x 21,500 Btu/lb = 2,150 Btu per hour.
  3. Check: 2,150 Btu/h divided by 21,500 Btu/lb = 0.1 lb/h. It matches.
  4. Oxygen available to burn: 20.9% - 18% = 2.9 percentage points of the volume. 336 ft³ x 0.029 = about 9.7 ft³ of oxygen.
  5. Oxygen the flame uses (our own chemistry): propane burns with 5 molecules of oxygen per molecule of fuel. 0.1 lb/h divided by 44.1 lb per lb-mol is about 0.00227 lb-mol/h of fuel, times 5 is about 0.0113 lb-mol/h of oxygen. At roughly 380 ft³ per lb-mol (our own approximation for cool air), that is about 4.3 ft³ of oxygen per hour.
  6. Time to the sensor point in a perfectly sealed box: 9.7 ft³ divided by 4.3 ft³/h is about 2.3 hours.

What it tells us: even at a modest 2,150 Btu/h, a tight 336 cubic foot space reaches the 18 percent line in a couple of hours, and a heater turned up higher gets there sooner. Real shelters leak, which buys time, but we cannot count on that, and CO can rise before the oxygen line is reached. That is why the manual's fresh-air openings and a battery alarm matter. A shanty heated at a higher setting, or one with more people breathing in it, shortens the time further.

What apprentices should take from this

When a customer asks about a portable propane heater for a garage, shop or camp, the right answer is the same chain of habits: use a heater made for indoor use, follow the opening sizes in its manual, keep a battery CO alarm running, keep cylinders outside, keep clear of combustibles, and shut the heater off before sleeping. Our lessons on gas appliances at home and on CO safety go further on the house side.

Field card: Propane heaters in small shelters

Fresh air in low, exhaust out high, alarm on, heater off at bedtime.

  • Indoor-use label still means follow the maker's fresh-air opening sizes.
  • The oxygen depletion sensor is a backstop, not a ventilation plan.
  • One low opening and one high opening; check them for snow and ice.
  • Battery CO alarm inside the shelter; test it and replace it on schedule.
  • Do not sleep with a fuel-burning heater running.
  • Larger cylinders stay outside the shelter.
  • Keep 3 ft around the heater and 10 ft from tarps or canvas, per one extension page.
  • Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion. Fresh air, then 911 or poison control, 1-800-222-1222.
  • Worked example (our own): 336 ft³, 0.1 lb/h, 2,150 Btu/h.
