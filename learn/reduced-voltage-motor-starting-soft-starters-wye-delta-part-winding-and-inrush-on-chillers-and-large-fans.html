Reduced-Voltage Motor Starting: Soft Starters, Wye-Delta, Part-Winding and Inrush on Chillers and Large Fans

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Close the contactor on a big motor and, for a second or two, it pulls several times its running current. On a chiller or a large fan that can dim the lights, trip a drop-out relay or upset the utility. Reduced-voltage starters exist to tame that surge. This lesson explains where the surge comes from, how each starter type cuts it, and which one fits which job.

Why a motor draws locked-rotor current

At the instant of starting the rotor is standing still, so an induction motor acts like a transformer with a shorted secondary and takes a lot of current until it speeds up. The Department of Energy motor tip sheet says a typical NEMA Design B motor may draw four to eight times full-load current while starting, usually for under 2 seconds and rarely over 10 seconds. An Eaton soft starter note uses 600 percent, or six times full-load amps, as its example, and a Kohler paper on generator sizing says six times is typical and stays high until the motor reaches about 75 percent of rated speed.

The nameplate code letter tells you the size of the surge in kVA per horsepower at locked rotor. Kohler's table lists code F as 5.0 to 5.59, G as 5.6 to 6.29, H as 6.3 to 7.0, J as 7.1 to 7.99 and K as 8.0 to 8.99 kVA per hp. Locked-rotor kVA is hp times that value. To see where the code letter sits on a plate, read Reading a Three-Phase Motor Nameplate. NEMA MG 1 is the standard behind these letters; we name it here and do not link a copy.

What the dip does to everything else

The big starting current flows through the wiring and transformer impedance, so the voltage sags for the whole building. A power-quality paper on motor starting sags (a training-site copy, so we cite it by name only) says the sag can dim lights, drop out contactors and upset sensitive equipment, and that starting power factor is poor, typically 15 to 30 percent, which makes the sag worse. Kohler says starting kVA falls with the square of voltage, that typical motors can ride through a 30 to 35 percent instantaneous dip before contacts drop out, and that many specifiers prefer to hold the dip to 20 percent. A deeper dip also leaves less torque, so the motor accelerates slowly or not at all.

Utilities care about this too. Eaton's catalog says a soft starter limits peak inrush "as required by many local codes," so before you size a starter for a new chiller, ask what the serving utility allows.

Wye-delta (star-delta)

The motor needs six leads. It starts connected in wye, so each winding sees about 58 percent of line voltage (Rockwell), then a timer switches it to delta. Current and torque are about one third of the full-voltage values. Open transition disconnects the motor for a moment during the switch, and Benshaw warns that the resulting surge can briefly exceed locked-rotor current. Closed transition adds a contactor and resistors to carry the motor through the switch and reduce that spike (Rockwell). Trane's chiller literature lists wye-delta as an electromechanical starter for chillers under 600 volts.

Part-winding

A dual-voltage motor is wired so half the winding is energized first and the second half follows after a short delay. Bitzer describes it for its compressors with two contactors, a delay of about 0.5 to 1 second, and no break in power during the change, unlike wye-delta. Eaton's warning matters: the motor will not start if the load needs more torque than the first half can develop. Eaton lists reciprocating compressors, pumps, blowers and fans for part-winding starting; Bitzer pairs it with start unloading on its compressors.

Autotransformer

Taps reduce the voltage to 80, 65 or 50 percent. Current and torque follow the square of the voltage, which gives the most torque per amp of line current (Eaton). Eaton notes it is the most expensive choice at lower horsepower ratings.

Solid-state soft starter

Silicon-controlled rectifiers ramp the voltage or hold a current limit. Eaton's example cuts the start from 600 to 300 percent of full-load amps, which leaves about 25 percent of the across-the-line torque. After the motor is up to speed, a bypass contactor takes over and removes the electronic losses. Benshaw describes the same bypass behavior. Ramp and limit are adjustable, so one unit fits several loads. The Schneider FAQ says a soft starter cannot hold a reduced speed and can overheat if it cycles more than about 8 to 10 times per hour.

Variable frequency drive

A drive raises frequency and voltage together from near zero, so starting current stays near rated current. Trane says its adaptive-frequency-drive chillers need the least inrush amps at start-up, which helps when a generator is the power source. See Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans for the running-side benefits.

Matching the method to the load

  • Centrifugal fans. They often have high inertia, so the starter must cover acceleration as well as running torque (Eaton). A long ramp with a soft starter, or a drive, suits them. Our own reading: a high-inertia fan may still be slow when the wye-delta timer expires, so the switch to delta can then cause a second surge.
  • Centrifugal pumps. Low inertia, sized mostly by the load (Eaton). Soft stop helps limit water hammer (Eaton catalog).
  • Chiller compressors. Trane lists wye-delta and solid-state starters for chillers under 600 volts and across-the-line starting only from 600 to 6000 volts.
  • Frequent starts. The DOE sheet says to keep starts within the NEMA MG 10 limits on starts per hour and rest time, and to consider adjustable speed drives, soft starters, wye-delta or autotransformers to ease starting stress. Eaton says more starts than the catalog allows means an oversized starter. Check the motor maker's starts-per-hour rating as well; we did not find a figure we could cite, so look it up for your motor.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own. Assumptions: a 100 hp, 460 V, three-phase motor on a chiller; code letter G, using Kohler's 5.6 to 6.29 kVA per hp; assumed full-load current of 120 A, picked for easy arithmetic and not from a nameplate. Check your own plate.

  1. Locked-rotor kVA = 100 hp x 5.6 = 560 kVA, up to 100 x 6.29 = 629 kVA.
  2. Current = kVA x 1,000 / (1.732 x 460). The denominator is 796.7. So 560,000 / 796.7 = 702.9 A, and 629,000 / 796.7 = 789.5 A.
  3. Compared with 120 A running, that is 702.9 / 120 = 5.9 times to 789.5 / 120 = 6.6 times, in line with the six-times figure above.
  4. With wye-delta at about one third: 702.9 / 3 = 234.3 A and 789.5 / 3 = 263.2 A, or 2.0 to 2.2 times full load.
  5. A soft starter set to the 300 percent limit in Eaton's example would hold the start at 3 x 120 = 360 A.

That is a big drop in the starting amps. Remember that the open-transition switch can add a short spike, and the motor must still accelerate the load on about a third of its torque. Power factor is the other half of the story; see Power Factor on HVAC Motors.

Safe troubleshooting

  • Isolate with a real disconnect. OSHA's 1991 interpretation says motor-controlled stop buttons and starter circuits are not energy isolation for servicing. Open the disconnect, lock it out, release stored energy and verify isolation before work. OSHA's eTool lists those steps. See Motor Starters and Overload Relays.
  • Read the arc flash label. NFPA 70 (the NEC) section 110.16 requires warning labels on equipment such as motor control centers that may be examined or adjusted while energized. Under NFPA 70E the label shows nominal voltage, the arc-flash boundary and an incident-energy or PPE level (Schneider blog). Do not work energized unless your employer's program, training and PPE allow it.
  • Start with the symptom. A failed wye-delta timer, a sticking transition contactor or a bypass contactor that never closes can each cause a long, hot start or a trip. Check the sequence against the maker's diagram. See Chiller Startup, Shutdown Sequence and Safeties.
  • Soft starter and drive capacitors can hold charge, so follow the maker's wait time after lockout.

Field card: Reduced-Voltage Motor Starting

Less voltage means less current, but also less torque.

  • Full-voltage start is about 6x full-load amps; code letter gives kVA per hp.
  • Locked-rotor amps = hp x kVA/hp x 1,000 / (1.732 x volts).
  • Wye-delta: about 33% current and torque; open transition can spike at the switch.
  • Part-winding: roughly 50 to 75% current; weak torque on the first half.
  • Autotransformer taps 80/65/50%: 64/42/25% current and torque.
  • Soft starter: set a current limit, then bypass contactor takes over.
  • Lock out at a true disconnect and verify; read the arc flash label.
