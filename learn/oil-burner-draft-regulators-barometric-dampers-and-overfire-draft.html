Oil Burner Draft Regulators: Barometric Dampers and Overfire Draft

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An oil burner needs the chimney to pull, but not too hard and not too softly. A barometric draft regulator is the swinging door that keeps that pull steady. This lesson covers what it does, where it sits, the difference between breech draft and overfire draft, and how a tech reads a draft gauge. The target numbers always come from the burner and boiler makers, never from this page.

Safety first. Draft and venting work on oil equipment belongs to a qualified technician. Nothing here is a repair procedure. If you smell fumes or smoke, or a CO alarm sounds, get outside and call for help. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

What draft is

Draft is the small pressure difference that pushes combustion air in and flue gas out of the appliance. On a chimney-vented oil boiler or furnace it is measured in inches of water column (inches w.c.) and read as a negative number, meaning the pressure inside is a little below the room. A bigger negative number means a stronger pull. The chimney makes more pull on a cold, windy day and less on a mild one, so without help the draft would swing all season.

What the barometric regulator does

InspectApedia describes the barometric damper (draft regulator) as a device used on oil-fired equipment that has a hinged door. When draft runs high, the door swings open and lets room air into the vent, which keeps the draft at the chosen level as wind and weather change. It says gas appliances normally use a draft hood instead, and warns never to put an oil-type regulator on gas equipment. Field Controls, a regulator maker, says the weight on the control is moved to set the draft, and that the burner must be running while the adjustment is made.

Where it is installed

InspectApedia describes it as typically a round tee in the vent connector between the appliance and the chimney. Field Controls' instructions say to locate it as close to the furnace or boiler as possible, in the position shown in its figures, at least 18 inches from a combustible ceiling or wall, and not in a room separated from the appliance. The Carlin burner manual, supplied by Weil-McLain, adds that the damper must be in the same space as the appliance, and says not to install or use a manual damper in the vent connector or chimney of an oil burner. For horizontal flues, Field Controls says to move the weight from the right-hand slot to the left-hand slot. Always follow the instructions that came with the actual regulator.

Breech draft and overfire draft

Two places matter. Overfire draft is the pressure in the combustion chamber, read through a small test hole above the flame. Breech (stack) draft is read in the flue connection near the appliance outlet. Beckett's burner adjustment bulletin says the stack draft should usually be a little higher than the overfire reading, and the difference is the draft lost crossing the heat exchanger. It adds that an extremely high drop can mean scale or soot blocking the passages. Beckett gives no number for the breech reading, so that value comes from the appliance maker.

What the makers say to target

  • Beckett says overfire draft is typically 0.02 inch w.c. "or whatever the manufacturer specifies." Its AFG manual says natural-draft overfire draft is typically -0.01 or -0.02 inch w.c. and to set the level the appliance maker specifies.
  • The Carlin manual says natural-draft appliances usually run -0.01 to -0.02 inch w.c. at the combustion chamber test port, and that appliances designed for forced draft run positive and must be airtight.
  • Field Controls says a domestic oil burner should be at about 0.02 to 0.03 inch overfire, and that its control covers 0.02 to 0.08 inch.
  • Riello says to keep the pressure in the combustion area as close to zero as possible, and to follow the heating appliance instructions.
  • A Weil-McLain page for a Riello-fired boiler shows a different style: adjust to +0.10 inch w.c. at the flue collar, which gives positive firebox pressure. Positive-pressure boilers are not set like natural-draft ones.

The takeaway: the boiler's nameplate and manual win. A target from another make is a clue, not a setting.

How a tech measures it

Field Controls says a draft gauge is required to set overfire draft accurately, and that CO2 and smoke readings must also be taken. The burner has to be running, and Beckett says to bring it to steady state first. The tech zeroes the gauge, reads the overfire port, reads the breech, and compares both with the maker's numbers. Read the draft only after the unit is warm, because a cold chimney reads weak. Combustion numbers are covered in Combustion Analyzer Readings.

Worked example: breech minus overfire

This is hypothetical, for teaching. A tech reads overfire -0.02 inch w.c. and breech -0.06 inch w.c. at steady state. The maker target, per Beckett and Field Controls, is about 0.02 inch overfire.

  1. Overfire check: -0.02 matches the typical 0.02 target, so no change is needed there.
  2. Draft drop across the appliance: 0.06 - 0.02 = 0.04 inch w.c.
  3. Convert to pascals. One inch of water is about 249.08 Pa at 39.2 degrees F (NIST). So 0.04 x 249.08 = 9.96, or about 10 Pa. The overfire reading is 0.02 x 249.08 = 4.98, about 5 Pa.
  4. Compare to the maker. Beckett says breech should be a little higher than overfire. 0.06 is higher, and the difference is plausible. If the boiler manual lists a breech target, compare to it. If the drop were far larger than the manual or past service history allows, Beckett says to suspect scale or soot and clean the passages.

The order of work

Beckett's adjustment bulletin gives a sequence: confirm fresh combustion air (about one square inch of free area per 1,000 Btu/h of input), run the burner to steady state, read overfire draft, read stack draft, set a trace of smoke, read CO2 or O2 at that trace point, add air until CO2 reaches 12.0 percent or O2 4.5 percent, and then confirm smoke is zero. The Carlin manual describes the same idea: make a trace of smoke, note CO2 or O2, add air to drop CO2 by 1.5 to 2 points, then recheck smoke for zero. The Carlin manual also says if the draft level changes later, recheck smoke and CO2 and readjust. So draft comes before the air-band decision, because changing the draft changes the fire.

Signs of too much or too little draft

InspectApedia says too much draft wastes heat up the chimney and can overheat the chimney. Too little can mean incomplete combustion, soot clogging of the heating equipment, puffbacks and carbon monoxide. For the puffback story see Oil Burner Puffback. Do not fix sooting by jamming the regulator shut; InspectApedia shows a regulator wedged shut with a pipe and says to find the cause instead, such as a soot-blocked flue or a short chimney.

Spillage and safety

The Carlin manual says a burner may run through a brief downdraft, but sustained downdraft is unsafe, and failure to fix it can cause serious injury or death. The Beckett AFG manual says flue gases could vent out the barometric damper into the living space if the vent is wrong. Field Controls says there must always be enough draft that the burner does not puff back into the room. The chimney itself can be the cause; see Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat. Nozzle size and pump pressure set the firing rate the draft must serve, covered in Oil Burner Nozzles, Pump Pressure and Firing Rate, and the startup sequence is in Residential Oil Burner Sequence.

Field card: Oil Burner Draft Regulators: Barometric Dampers and Overfire Draft

The maker's number wins. Zero, read, compare.

  • The regulator door opens to hold draft steady; it belongs on oil, not gas.
  • Install it near the appliance, in the same space, with the weight set per its instructions.
  • Overfire draft is read in the firebox; breech draft in the flue. Breech usually reads a bit higher.
  • Typical natural-draft overfire from Beckett and Carlin: -0.01 to -0.02 inch w.c.; Field Controls: about 0.02 to 0.03.
  • 1 inch w.c. is about 249 Pa, so 0.02 inch is about 5 Pa.
  • Use a draft gauge with the burner at steady state, then smoke to zero.
  • Sustained downdraft is unsafe. Do not jam a regulator shut.
