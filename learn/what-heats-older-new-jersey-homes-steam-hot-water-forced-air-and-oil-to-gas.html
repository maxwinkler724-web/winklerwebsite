What Heats Older New Jersey Homes: Steam, Hot Water, Forced Air, and Oil to Gas

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You can usually tell what heats your house in a minute of looking: a sight glass, a pump, a vent or a duct. Here is how to read the clues and what each system means in plain terms.

Safety first. Heating appliances that burn fuel can make carbon monoxide. Do not open, adjust or repair a boiler, furnace, burner or gas line yourself unless you are trained and licensed to. If an alarm sounds or you smell gas or oil fumes, get everyone outside and call for help.

Heat source and delivery are two separate choices

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a central heating system as three parts: a heat source (usually a furnace or a boiler), a control (usually a thermostat), and a way to distribute the heat. A furnace heats air and pushes it through ducts with a blower. A boiler heats water, then sends hot water or steam through pipes. Older Northeast houses commonly have one of these two families, and the fuel behind them varies.

What the fuel data says

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reported from its 2009 survey that over 80 percent of homes relying on heating oil were in the Northeast, and that heating oil equipment tended to be older than average but more likely to be regularly maintained. DOE likewise says heating oil is more common in the Northeast, where natural gas access is limited. As a labeled example from the EIA 2020 survey, Northeast housing units heated with natural gas numbered about 12.4 million, versus about 4.3 million using fuel oil or kerosene. Your own street may differ, so check your own equipment.

The four things you might have

Steam radiators. DOE calls steam radiant heat one of the oldest systems. A one-pipe system uses a single pipe to bring steam up and return condensate down, with an air vent on each radiator. A two-pipe system uses a separate return pipe and its radiators have no air vent. Radiators are smaller than hot-water ones, according to a home-inspection guide, because steam gives up a lot of heat as it condenses. See One-Pipe vs Two-Pipe Steam Systems and Steam Radiator Air Vents.

Hot-water (hydronic) radiators or baseboard. The boiler heats water that circulates through radiators, baseboard units, or in-floor tubing. DOE notes that boilers make zone heating easier but can cost more to install. See Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs Water Temperature and Hot Water Balancing Basics.

Forced warm air. A furnace, ducts and registers. DOE calls forced air the most common distribution type. The blower can be loud, and filters need regular attention (see How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV).

Oil-fired or gas-fired versions of any of these. Fuel is separate from delivery. A boiler or a furnace can burn oil or natural gas, and DOE lists propane and electricity as other fuel options.

How to tell what you have

  • Look for a sight glass. A steam boiler has a narrow vertical water-level glass; hot-water systems do not.
  • Look for a circulator pump. A pump attached to the boiler points to hot water.
  • Check the radiator. A small bleed valve suggests hot water; a vent on the radiator suggests one-pipe steam. Pipe count alone is not conclusive, since some steam systems have two pipes.
  • Ducts and registers mean forced air.
  • Fuel clues (general practice, not from a cited source): a tank in the basement or yard usually means oil, a gas meter and gas line usually mean natural gas. Check the equipment label or ask a licensed tech to confirm.

Pros and cons in general terms

DOE lists furnaces as relatively inexpensive, with a loud blower as the main drawback, and boilers as easier to zone but more expensive to install. A home-inspection guide says a well-maintained steam system is reliable, but that hot-water systems are generally more efficient, and that turning a steam system into a hot-water system can go badly when old pipes are too small or poorly arranged. Steam also has its own care rules: that guide advises not bleeding steam radiators, because they purge air by themselves. DOE gives life expectancy as 15 to 30 years for both furnaces and boilers, with efficiency ranges depending on age.

Thinking about oil to gas

Switching fuel is a project, not a part swap. A consumer HVAC article says that for an oil furnace you either pay to convert it or buy a new one, that conversion may cost as much as or more than a new unit, and that a home without a gas hookup needs a line from the street plus possibly new interior piping. It also lists oil tank removal as a separate cost. In New Jersey, a state construction bulletin says removing or abandoning a residential heating oil tank requires a demolition permit, and that only NJDEP-certified individuals and firms may service underground tanks. Treat that as a pointer, and confirm requirements with your town and a licensed contractor.

Field card: What Heats Older New Jersey Homes: Steam, Hot Water, Forced Air, and Oil to Gas

Know the delivery type, then the fuel, before you call for service.

  • Furnace = heats air, ducts and registers. Boiler = heats water, makes hot water or steam for pipes.
  • Steam boiler: sight glass. Hot water: circulator pump and bleed valves on radiators.
  • One-pipe steam radiators have an air vent; two-pipe have a separate return and no vent.
  • Fuel (oil, gas) is a separate question from delivery (steam, water, air).
  • Do not bleed steam radiators; ask a steam tech before touching vents or valves.
  • Oil to gas means burner or appliance, gas line, tank and permit decisions. Use licensed people.
