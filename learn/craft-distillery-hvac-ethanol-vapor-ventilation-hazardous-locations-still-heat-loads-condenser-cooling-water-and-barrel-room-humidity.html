Craft Distillery HVAC: Ethanol Vapor, Classified Areas, Still Heat and Barrel Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A craft distillery looks like a brewery with a still in it, but the still room is different. The product is a flammable liquid, the vapor is invisible, and one spark in the wrong place is not a tripped breaker. This lesson covers how ethanol vapor drives ventilation, why equipment placement matters, and where the heat and water go.

Check with the fire marshal

Layouts are decided by the adopted fire and electrical codes, the building official and the fire marshal. This page is general education from public pages read in 2026. Codes such as NFPA 30 and NFPA 70 are named in plain text only.

What makes ethanol vapor dangerous

The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists ethanol with a flash point of 55°F, a lower explosive limit (LEL) of 3.3% and an upper explosive limit (UEL) of 19% by volume in air. A flash point below room temperature means a spill makes ignitable vapor at ordinary temperatures. NOAA CAMEO Chemicals adds that the vapor is heavier than air (vapor density 1.59, air = 1), that flashback along a vapor trail may occur, and that vapor may explode if ignited in an enclosed area. Heavier than air is why pits, trenches and low floor areas need attention. Our A2L refrigerants guide uses the same idea of a flammable range, a source and a space where vapor collects, but here the fuel is a process liquid.

The Oregon OSHA craft distillery fact sheet says vapors escape from tanks, casks, pumps, pipes and hoses, and lists open flames, welding, static and electrical sparks, hot surfaces and friction as ignition sources.

Ventilation: stay well below the LEL

OSHA's flammable liquids rule, 29 CFR 1910.106, calls ventilation adequate if it prevents significant vapor-air mixtures above one-fourth of the lower flammable limit. For ethanol that is 25% of 3.3%, or 0.825% by volume (our arithmetic), which is 8,250 ppm. The same section asks for at least 1 cubic foot per minute per square foot of floor in industrial plants using flammable liquids. Ask the fire marshal which rule governs your room. Oregon OSHA also says to route new tank breather and emergency vents outside, and to charge the still boiler with wash below 40 percent alcohol.

Our reading: because the vapor is heavier than air, designers often want exhaust pickup near the floor, plus high exhaust for heat and steam. Confirm that with the engineer.

Worked example: dilution cfm for an evaporating spill

OSHA's Technical Manual gives qd = (387 × lbs) / (MW × min × d) in acfm, and Qd = (qd × Km × 106) / Ca, where Km is a mixing factor of 2 to 5 and Ca is the allowed concentration in ppm. The manual uses a toxic limit for Ca. For a fire case we substitute 25% of the LEL, which is our own adaptation.

Assumptions (ours): 2 lb of ethanol evaporates per hour (about 0.30 gal, using NIOSH specific gravity 0.79 and 8.34 lb per gallon of water); d = 1; MW = 46.1 and LEL = 3.3% from NIOSH.

  1. qd = (387 × 2) / (46.1 × 60 × 1) = 774 / 2,766 = 0.28 acfm.
  2. Ca = 0.25 × 3.3% = 0.825% = 8,250 ppm.
  3. Km = 5: Qd = (0.28 × 5 × 1,000,000) / 8,250 = about 170 cfm.
  4. Km = 2: Qd = (0.28 × 2 × 1,000,000) / 8,250 = about 68 cfm.

Two checks. If people work there, the health limit is tighter: NIOSH and OSHA list 1,000 ppm as the 8-hour limit. Half of that, 500 ppm, with Km = 5 gives (0.28 × 5 × 1,000,000) / 500 = 2,800 cfm. And for an assumed 1,000 sq ft room, the 1 cfm per sq ft rule gives 1,000 cfm. So a small routine leak rarely sizes the fan; the code floor, health limit and failure case do.

Classified areas and HVAC placement

Under 29 CFR 1910.399, Class I locations have flammable gases or vapors present or possible. Division 1 means ignitable concentrations may exist in normal operation, or from frequent maintenance or leakage. Division 2 means flammable liquids normally stay in closed systems, or positive mechanical ventilation normally prevents ignitable concentrations but could fail. Oregon OSHA says rooms where flammable liquids are distilled or blended must meet Class I Division 1, Group D electrical requirements. Under 29 CFR 1910.307, equipment must be approved for the class and the specific substance, or intrinsically safe.

  • Fan motors, controls, lights and sensors in the area must be the approved type. A stock exhaust fan does not qualify.
  • Air handlers, rooftop units, gas heaters and anything with a spark or flame go outside the classified area or behind approved separation. Oregon OSHA says to keep heaters and pilot-light gas appliances at least 10 feet from distilling areas.
  • The Division 2 definition relies on ventilation not failing, so expect airflow proving and alarms. The design team specifies the scheme.
  • Our caution: locate makeup air intakes away from tank vents, and do not tie still-room exhaust into a general system.

Still heat: steam and direct fire

The Connecticut DEEP distillery energy guide says more than 90% of fuel goes to hot water and steam, about 70 to 75 kBtu per gallon of 200 proof spirit, and that batch pot stills typically use more energy per gallon than column stills. It lists heating the still with indirect steam as a measure. Steam keeps the flame out of the still room; a direct-fired still puts a burner in the room where open flames are an ignition source. Our reasoning: uninsulated stills and piping also heat the room, so it needs cooling or dilution air even in winter. Our process chillers lesson covers process loads.

Condenser cooling water

The condenser turns hot vapor back into liquid, and the heat has to go somewhere. The Connecticut guide lists using a cooling tower instead of a chiller, free cooling, maintaining adequate condenser water flow, and glycol only below -4°F. See cooling tower basics and the brewery and winery glycol lesson rather than repeating them here. Our own estimate: gpm = Btu/h / (500 × rise in °F). Assuming 100,000 Btu/h and a 20°F rise, 100,000 / (500 × 20) = 10 gpm.

Barrel room temperature and humidity

The Connecticut guide lists regulating rack house temperature with a high-volume, low-speed ceiling fan. For climate effects we found only trade press, so treat this as unverified: one distilling education site says humid air lets alcohol leave faster than water so proof falls, dry air does the reverse, heat speeds evaporation, and typical loss is 2 to 4 percent per year. Set humidity and temperature with the distiller; see our wine cellar cooling lesson for the cooling side. Evaporating ethanol is also a vapor source, so ask the fire marshal how the barrel room is classified before installing any unit.

Field card: Craft Distillery HVAC

Invisible, heavy, flammable. Keep sparks out and air moving.

  • Ethanol: LEL 3.3%, UEL 19%, flash point 55°F, vapor 1.59 times air.
  • OSHA goal: vapor under one-fourth of the LEL (8,250 ppm, our arithmetic).
  • Dilution: Qd = qd x Km x 10^6 / Ca. Our example: 2 lb/h gives about 170 cfm.
  • Health limit and code floors usually size the fan.
  • Classified area: approved equipment only.
  • Gas heaters and pilots 10 ft or more from distilling areas (Oregon OSHA).
  • Condenser water: gpm = Btu/h / (500 x rise).
