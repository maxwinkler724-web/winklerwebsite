Pressure-Temperature Basics

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Pressure and temperature are linked in any refrigerant that is part liquid and part vapor. This lesson explains that link and two ideas built on it, superheat and subcooling, without any formulas to memorize.

The idea behind it all

Think about a pot of water. At the normal air pressure at sea level, water boils at one temperature. Take it up a tall mountain, where the air pressure is lower, and it boils at a lower temperature. Pressure and boiling temperature go together. Refrigerants follow the same rule, except they are chosen so they boil at useful temperatures at pressures a machine can handle.

Saturation

When a liquid and its vapor sit together in balance, the substance is said to be at saturation. At saturation, each pressure matches exactly one temperature, and each temperature matches one pressure. Chart makers call this a pressure-temperature relationship, and every refrigerant has its own. Manufacturers and reference tools publish these for each refrigerant. This lesson gives no values, because the numbers depend on the refrigerant and you should read them from a current, trusted chart.

Why this matters in a cooling system

A cooling system moves heat by letting refrigerant boil in one place and condense in another.

  • In the evaporator, refrigerant boils at a low pressure, so it boils at a low temperature, which lets it absorb heat from the space.
  • In the condenser, refrigerant is under higher pressure, so it condenses at a higher temperature, which lets it give heat off to the outdoors or other water.

The compressor creates the pressure difference that makes both jobs possible. If you can read the pressure on a gauge, you can use the saturation relationship to find what temperature the refrigerant is boiling or condensing at.

Superheat

After the last drop of liquid has boiled off, the vapor can keep absorbing heat and get warmer than its saturation temperature. The extra temperature above saturation is called superheat. In everyday terms: it tells you how far past just-boiled the vapor has traveled. Some superheat is useful because it shows the evaporator is not sending liquid toward the compressor, which can damage it. Too much can signal that the evaporator is starved of refrigerant.

Subcooling

On the other side, after the vapor has fully condensed, the liquid can lose more heat and get cooler than its saturation temperature. The amount below saturation is called subcooling. It indicates the liquid has been cooled enough to flow to the metering device without flashing into vapor too early. Low subcooling can mean a shortage of refrigerant or poor heat rejection. The exact meaning depends on the system.

Why technicians check them

Superheat and subcooling help describe what is going on inside a closed system without opening it. Together with other readings, they help narrow down charge problems, airflow problems, and restrictions. No single reading tells the whole story. Think of them as clues that need to be combined with the manufacturer's data for that exact unit.

Cautions

  • Use the correct chart for the correct refrigerant. Blends can behave differently, and some have a temperature glide when they change phase (general practice, so check the refrigerant's own chart).
  • Gauges and thermometers need to be suited to the refrigerant and in good condition.
  • Targets for a given unit come from the manufacturer's literature. Do not rely on rules of thumb you read online.
  • Pressurized equipment is hazardous. Follow site safety procedures.

Field card: Pressure-Temperature Basics

Pressure sets the boiling and condensing temperature.

  • At saturation, one pressure matches one temperature for a given refrigerant.
  • The evaporator boils at low pressure; the condenser condenses at high pressure.
  • Superheat is vapor temperature above saturation.
  • Subcooling is liquid temperature below saturation.
  • Use manufacturer data for the exact unit and refrigerant.
