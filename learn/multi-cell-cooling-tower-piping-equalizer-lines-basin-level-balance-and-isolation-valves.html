Multi-Cell Cooling Tower Piping: Equalizers, Basin Level and Isolation Valves

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When two or more cooling tower cells share one set of pumps and one header, their basins are really one big tank split into pieces. If the pieces cannot trade water, one can overflow while another runs dry at the pump. This lesson covers the equalizer line that prevents that, and the valves and checks around it.

Why parallel cells need equalizers

Cells in parallel never get exactly the same flow. A dirty strainer, a partly closed valve or a few extra feet of pipe on one outlet changes how much water each cell sends to the common suction. Baltimore Aircoil (BAC) says equalizers serve to correct differences in water level that develop during operation, and lists dirty strainers and valve position changes as typical causes. EVAPCO states it more bluntly: equalizers must be used when two or more towers are piped with a common header. Solenis, a water treatment company, describes the failure: imbalanced basins can starve a pump and cause cavitation, while the excess water can run to the drain.

A bucket picture helps: two buckets joined by a pipe near the bottom settle to the same level. The equalizer is that pipe. For the basics of what a basin holds and why, see Cooling Tower Basics.

What the equalizer is and is not

An equalizer fixes level, not flow. BAC states that its purpose is not to correct unbalanced flows caused by piping design. That job belongs to balancing valves and symmetrical piping. BAC advises keeping supply and return piping as symmetrical as possible. If your cells are fighting each other, the equalizer is a bandage, not the cure.

Sizing: what the makers say

Both BAC and EVAPCO give the same rule: size the equalizer to pass 15 percent of the flow of the largest tower. BAC ties that to a 1 inch (0.083 ft) basin level difference, with total friction loss in the equalizer, including entrance and exit, at or below 0.083 ft of water (0.036 psi). BAC also publishes a table, built for two towers 10 to 20 ft apart and including a gate or butterfly valve and typical fittings, that picks a pipe size from the larger tower's design flow. For 631 to 1,170 gpm it lists 8 inch IPS pipe. BAC also lists maximum equalizer connection sizes by model and says they must be adhered to, and that on some models the end connection goes on the end opposite the suction connection. Always use the maker's current table for your model. EVAPCO says connections are limited to the deep side of the basin, that a side connection needs less piping and collects less debris, and that a bottom equalizer needs a cleanout at its lowest point.

Overflows matter too. BAC says towers should be installed with their overflow levels at the same elevation, and warns that otherwise one tower can overflow while air is drawn into the pump through the other. EVAPCO likewise says overflow connections of all towers should be at the same level.

Worked example: how much level difference could build up?

Take two identical cells with a combined 2,000 gpm. One cell's outlet piping has an extra 1.5 ft of friction loss at its share of flow.

  1. Flow per cell. Our own: 2,000 gpm / 2 = 1,000 gpm each.
  2. Level difference without an equalizer. Our own simplification: the pump pulls from a common point, so the extra loss on one outlet must be paid for by a water level difference. Level difference is about equal to head difference, so about 1.5 ft, or 1.5 x 12 = 18 inches. The basin behind the lossy outlet rises and the other falls.
  3. Compare to the maker's design basis. BAC sizes for 1 inch (0.083 ft). Our own: 1.5 / 0.083 = about 18, so this case is roughly 18 times the difference an equalizer is sized for.
  4. Pick the equalizer. Larger tower flow is 1,000 gpm. 15 percent: 0.15 x 1,000 = 150 gpm (Our own arithmetic on the cited rule). BAC's table row for 631 to 1,170 gpm lists 8 inch IPS pipe.
  5. What happens at 1.5 ft. Our own rough scaling, assuming turbulent flow where friction head rises with the square of flow: flow scales with the square root of head. sqrt(1.5 / 0.083) = sqrt(18.1) = about 4.25. 150 gpm x 4.25 = about 640 gpm, nearly two thirds of one cell's flow moving through the equalizer.

Our own reading: an 8 inch equalizer could narrow the gap, but pushing about 640 gpm through it is far beyond what it is sized for, so the right fix is to correct the extra 1.5 ft of loss with a balancing valve or piping change. The 640 gpm figure is a rough estimate, not a maker value.

Isolation valves for taking a cell offline

BAC says to install manual valves at the inlet and outlet of each tower for final flow adjustment and to isolate a tower for service. Its rule: whenever the inlet valve is closed, close the outlet valve. With automatic inlet valves, use automatic outlet valves and operate both together. Solenis notes equalizers may carry valves so one cell can be isolated while the rest keep running, such as for winter operation or maintenance, and EVAPCO says valved equalizers make isolating cells easier and give redundancy. A cell that is closed off on the inlet and outlet but still open at the equalizer can drain into or fill from its neighbor, so close that valve before opening the cell. For cold weather use see Cooling Tower Winter Operation.

Balancing inlet flow

The maker source we read puts the balancing job on the manual valves at each tower inlet and outlet, with symmetrical piping. BAC says to contact its representative if flow varies with multiple pump operation. We did not find a source we could cite on hot water basin weirs or nozzle distribution balance for this lesson, so we leave that out here.

Pump suction and vortexing

Low basin level lets pumps pull air; a trade article by consultant James Piper in FacilitiesNet says that if the water level is too low, the pumps suck in air. Deppmann, a pump supplier, cites Hydraulic Institute guidance to limit open-tank suction velocity to 8 ft/s, notes that required submergence grows with velocity, and recommends keeping velocity near 5 to 6 ft/s and asking the tower maker for an anti-vortex baffle (a "doghouse") because many basins are too shallow. See Pump NPSH Available vs Required for the suction margin math.

Makeup, overflow and drain

BAC's example has a makeup float arrangement in each tower's basin and says to adjust the floats so the valves operate evenly, because uneven settings can leave one valve working hard while another stays closed. A different approach appears in Washington University in St. Louis facilities design standards: multi-cell towers with an external equalizer line with valves instead of a basin weir plate, equalizer pressure drop of no more than 3 in. w.c. at worst case, a level sensor in the equalizer piping, and towers ordered without a float makeup valve because building controls modulate the makeup valve. DOE FEMP says properly operated towers should not have leaks or overflows and to check the float control equipment if a basin level is not being maintained. For makeup and blowdown math, see Cycles of Concentration, Blowdown and Makeup.

One water treatment caution from Solenis: equalizers carry little flow, so debris and stagnant water can collect there and water may not receive proper inhibitor or biocide, creating a dead leg with corrosion and Legionella concerns. Ask your water treatment provider how the equalizer is covered.

What to check

  • Basin levels in every cell while pumps run. FacilitiesNet recommends checking daily.
  • Valve positions: inlet and outlet pairs open or closed together, and the equalizer valve open for normal operation.
  • Float valves for sticking; Piper notes they usually fail stuck open.
  • Makeup meter readings against what you expect.
  • The equalizer line itself for debris, restriction or unusual flow noise (our own practical suggestion, not a maker limit).
  • Overflow elevations equal across cells, per BAC and EVAPCO.

Related checks are in Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks.

Field card: Multi-Cell Cooling Tower Piping

Equalizers fix level. Balancing valves fix flow.

  • Common header means an equalizer (EVAPCO).
  • Maker rule: size for 15 percent of the largest tower flow at a 1 inch (0.083 ft) level difference (BAC, EVAPCO).
  • Overflow elevations must match across cells.
  • Inlet closed means outlet closed; close the equalizer valve too when isolating a cell.
  • Keep suction velocity modest and ask about an anti-vortex baffle.
  • Check basin levels daily, floats, and the equalizer for debris.
