Log Mean Temperature Difference for HVAC Heat Exchangers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat exchanger does not have one temperature difference. It has a big one at one end and a different one at the other. This lesson explains the log mean temperature difference, why it beats a plain average, why counterflow beats parallel flow, and how a tech can turn four field temperatures into a quick health check on a plate exchanger, a chiller barrel or a hot water coil.

Why the average is not simple

Picture hot water on one side of a plate and cooler water on the other. Heat moves because of the temperature difference between them. But as the hot stream gives up heat it cools, and as the cold stream takes heat it warms, so the gap between them changes along the length of the exchanger. Where the gap is large, heat moves fast. Where it is small, heat moves slowly. The right "average" gap therefore leans toward the smaller end, and the log mean is the average that does that correctly. University lecture notes define it from the two end differences, written here as ΔT1 and ΔT2 (CSUN, Purdue ME 418 notes; the DOE handbook states it with the larger difference first).

LMTD = (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1 / ΔT2)

That is the whole formula. It only needs the two ends. The OU design notes add the conditions behind it: a roughly constant U across the exchanger and no heat loss to the room. For a clean plate exchanger or a coil in normal service, those are fair assumptions for field checks.

Counterflow versus parallel flow

Which temperatures make up each end depends on how the fluids flow. The CSUN heat exchanger notes give the pairing:

  • Parallel flow (both fluids enter at the same end): ΔT1 = hot in − cold in, and ΔT2 = hot out − cold out.
  • Counterflow (fluids enter at opposite ends): ΔT1 = hot in − cold out, and ΔT2 = hot out − cold in.

The DOE Fundamentals Handbook says that under comparable conditions more heat is transferred in a counterflow arrangement than in a parallel-flow one. It also notes that in parallel flow the cold outlet can never rise above the lowest hot temperature, while in counterflow the cold outlet can approach the hot inlet. The OU notes add a useful picture: in a counterflow unit the two temperature lines do not cross, and as they get closer the area needed grows toward infinity. That closest gap is the pinch, and field techs call the end gaps approach temperatures.

Real exchangers are often cross-flow or multi-pass. Purdue and OU both handle that with a correction factor F applied to the counterflow LMTD, written Q = U × A × LMTD × F, with F equal to 1 for pure counterflow. Plate exchangers in a building loop are close to counterflow, which is why they can hold small approaches. Air-to-air plates are a different case; see fixed-plate air-to-air heat exchangers.

Q = U x A x LMTD in plain words

The heat rate Q (Btu/h) equals U (how well heat gets through the surface, in Btu/h per square foot per degree F) times A (surface area in square feet) times the LMTD (degrees F). In the field you cannot change A, so U and the LMTD decide what you get. The product U x A is one number for a given exchanger, and we can compute it from measured temperatures and a known heat rate.

Where fouling comes in

The OU design notes build U by adding resistances in series: the film on each side, the wall, and fouling layers on the surfaces. Scale, biofilm or debris adds resistance, so U goes down. With the same inlet temperatures and flows, a lower U x A means less heat moves and the approach temperatures grow. That is why the approach trend in plate heat exchanger fouling and approach and chiller approach temperatures is such a good early warning.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

Our own example, not from a manufacturer or a field job. Assumptions: a clean counterflow plate exchanger, hot side 180 F in and 160 F out, cold side 140 F in and 155 F out, and an assumed duty of 300,000 Btu/h. All numbers below are our own arithmetic.

  1. Counterflow end differences. Hot in meets cold out: 180 − 155 = 25 F. Hot out meets cold in: 160 − 140 = 20 F.
  2. Counterflow LMTD. (25 − 20) / ln(25 / 20) = 5 / 0.2231 = about 22.4 F. A plain average gives 22.5 F, close because the two ends are alike.
  3. Parallel-flow end differences, same four temperatures. Hot in meets cold in: 180 − 140 = 40 F. Hot out meets cold out: 160 − 155 = 5 F.
  4. Parallel LMTD. (40 − 5) / ln(40 / 5) = 35 / 2.0794 = about 16.8 F. A plain average gives 22.5 F, which overstates it by about a third. That is why the plain average fails when the ends differ a lot.
  5. Required U x A for 300,000 Btu/h. Counterflow: 300,000 / 22.4 = about 13,400 Btu/h per F. Parallel: 300,000 / 16.8 = about 17,800 Btu/h per F, roughly 33 percent more surface for the same job.
  6. Fouling check. Suppose fouling cuts U by 20 percent (an assumption). The counterflow unit now has 0.8 x 13,400 = about 10,700 Btu/h per F. To still move 300,000 Btu/h it would need an LMTD of 300,000 / 10,700 = about 28 F, which these inlet temperatures cannot supply. The real result is less heat and wider approaches.

Cross-check: the hot side drops 20 F and the cold side gains 15 F, so the implied flow-times-specific-heat values are 300,000 / 20 = 15,000 and 300,000 / 15 = 20,000 Btu/h per F. They differ because the cold side has more flow in this made-up case, which is fine.

How techs use the idea in the field

  • Plate exchanger. Take the four temperatures with matched sensors and a flow reading. Compute Q from either side, the two end differences, the LMTD, then Q / LMTD. Compare that U x A to the same number from a clean baseline. A falling number means fouling. A rising approach on the leaving cold side points the same way. Use the same method each visit. Our own rule: water carries about 500 Btu/h per gpm per F (8.33 lb per gallon times 60 minutes), so Q is roughly 500 x gpm x temperature change.
  • Chiller barrel. The refrigerant boils at nearly one temperature, so one end of the plot is almost flat, and by the same formula the flow arrangement matters little. What matters is the approach between leaving chilled water and saturated refrigerant temperature, trended over time. See chiller approach temperatures.
  • Hot water coil. The air and water are cross-flow with piping set for counterflow, so the real LMTD is the counterflow value times F. Field checks usually skip F and compare the same coil against its own earlier readings. See hot water coils in an air handler.

One caution: temperature-cross or very small differences make this method unreliable, because a 1 F sensor error on a 5 F end gap is a 20 percent error. Use matched, calibrated sensors.

Field card: Log Mean Temperature Difference for HVAC Heat Exchangers

Two ends, one log mean.

  • LMTD = (ΔT1 − ΔT2) / ln(ΔT1 / ΔT2).
  • Counterflow ends: hot in minus cold out, and hot out minus cold in.
  • Parallel ends: hot in minus cold in, and hot out minus cold out.
  • Counterflow gives a larger LMTD for the same four temperatures.
  • Q = U x A x LMTD (x F for cross-flow or multi-pass).
  • Fouling adds resistance, so U x A drops and approaches widen.
  • Trend U x A against a clean baseline; do not rely on one reading.
