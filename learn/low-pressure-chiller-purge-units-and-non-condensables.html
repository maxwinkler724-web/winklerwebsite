Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most refrigeration systems push refrigerant out when they leak. Some large chillers do the opposite: they pull the room in. This lesson explains why, how a purge unit cleans up the mess, and why the number of minutes it runs each day is one of the best leak clues on the log sheet.

Check your own machine's manual

Check the manual. Purge designs, settings and alarm limits differ by maker and model year. This page explains the idea using public documents. Your chiller's own service literature and site procedures come first.

Why a chiller can run below atmospheric pressure

Every refrigerant has a boiling point at normal atmospheric pressure. For R-1233zd(E), Honeywell's product brochure gives 18.3 °C (about 65 °F) at 101.3 kPa. Honeywell's vapor pressure table lists 48 kPa at 0 °C. That is less than half of atmospheric pressure. A chiller evaporator runs colder than that, so at a coil temperature near 0 °C the shell pressure falls below atmospheric. That is a vacuum, measured against the room. The same brochure calls the refrigerant a good fit for centrifugal chillers and other low-pressure uses. R-123, the older refrigerant in this class, and R-514A, one of its replacements, are described the same way by Trane and in ACHR News, which says R-514A closely matches R-123 performance. Those machines are the low-pressure equipment that Type III covers.

Compare that with a high-pressure machine. A leak lets refrigerant out. On a low-pressure chiller, any leak path into the low-pressure side can let air in. Trane's purge manual says air and other gases may leak into the low-pressure areas and accumulate in the condenser. Trane's product announcement lists air, moisture and other non-condensables as what the purge removes.

Why that matters: higher head pressure

Air does not condense at condenser temperatures. It rides along to the condenser, gathers there, and takes up space and pressure. Trane's manual says the gases accumulate in the condenser, raise condenser pressure and lower efficiency. The compressor then works harder for the same cooling. For the log, it shows up as a condenser pressure higher than the saturation pressure of the refrigerant at the temperature you measure. When the pressure is converted to a saturation temperature, that temperature is too high, and the condenser approach grows. Read Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend for how that number is built, and Pressure-Temperature Basics for the conversion. A rising condenser approach can also be fouled tubes, so check water flow and treatment first, as that lesson says.

What a purge unit does

A purge unit is a small, separate system bolted to the chiller. It works on gas drawn from the condenser, where Trane says the non-condensables collect. Trane describes the usual high-efficiency design in four steps:

  1. Chill the sample. A small refrigeration circuit runs a cold coil. Refrigerant vapor in the sample condenses on it. Air stays a gas.
  2. Return the liquid. The condensed refrigerant drains back to the chiller condenser through a liquid return line.
  3. Pump out the air. When the unit senses accumulated non-condensables, a pump-out compressor runs and vents the gas.
  4. Catch the leftovers. The discharge passes through a carbon tank that holds refrigerant molecules. A heater later drives them off and back to the chiller, a step Trane calls regeneration.

Purges are judged by how little refrigerant escapes with the air. AHRI's certification program rates them as grams of refrigerant per gram of air. A 2004 ACHR News item on a Trane unit quoted 0.02 lb of refrigerant lost per lb of air, which was the maker's own figure. Trane's older carbon canister manual points the same direction, calling proper use a path to near-zero refrigerant emissions. Not every chiller needs one: Carrier's brochure for a 19XR chiller, which uses HFC-134a at positive pressure, says purge units are not required.

What the pump-out minutes tell you

The purge only runs when there is air to remove, so its run time is a leak meter. Trane's purge manual describes a log sheet entry for daily pump-out (the last 24 hours) and a 7-day average, and says they show how the chiller's sealing compares with its own past pump-out times. It gives about 7 minutes a day as a typical starting point, varying by site, and a 10-minute default for the daily alarm limit, adjustable from 1 to 50 minutes. It also suggests leak-checking when pump-out is excessive. A study Q&A page (secondary) says the same about a purge that runs for long periods. The trend matters more than any one number.

Worked example: reading five weeks of pump-out

This is made-up data for practice. Suppose the log shows the 7-day average of daily pump-out time at the end of each week:

  • Week 1: 7 min/day
  • Week 2: 7 min/day
  • Week 3: 8 min/day
  • Week 4: 10 min/day
  • Week 5: 13 min/day

Step 1, weekly totals. Multiply by 7 days: 49, 49, 56, 70 and 91 minutes.

Step 2, total change. 91 − 49 = 42 more minutes a week. 42 ÷ 49 = 0.857, so about an 86% increase.

Step 3, rate of change. Daily time rose from 7 to 13 over 4 weeks: (13 − 7) ÷ 4 = 1.5 minutes per day, per week. It is also speeding up: the first two weeks rose by 0, and the last two by 1.5 and 3 a week.

Step 4, compare to the limit. Week 4 sits at Trane's 10-minute default, and week 5 is 13 − 10 = 3 minutes over it. Your own alarm may be set differently.

Step 5, second check. Say the machine uses R-1233zd(E). Honeywell lists 216 kPa vapor pressure at 40 °C. If the condenser pressure reads 245 kPa (absolute) while the refrigerant is at 40 °C, the excess is 245 − 216 = 29 kPa, which is 29 ÷ 216 = 13% above saturation. That gap is consistent with gas that is not refrigerant. A real reading needs good, calibrated sensors, a steady load, and your maker's procedure, so treat this as a flag, not a diagnosis.

What you do next: report the trend to the lead tech or engineer. The usual response is a leak search on the low-pressure side under the maker's procedure, not a purge setting change. Raising the alarm limit would hide the problem.

EPA 608 and leak-rate tie-ins

Servicing these machines is Type III work; see Type I, II, III and Universal Explained and EPA 608 Certification Basics. Under 40 CFR 82.156 as shown on Cornell LII, the required evacuation level for a low-pressure appliance is 25 mm Hg absolute, and if you must open the machine without evacuating to the atmosphere it must first be pressurized to no higher than 0 psig. That rule exists because these machines are normally under vacuum and air gets in when they are opened.

On leak rates, 40 CFR 82.157(k), titled "Purged refrigerant," says purged refrigerant that is destroyed at a verifiable destruction efficiency of 98 percent or greater is not counted toward the annual leak rate. Records for that exclusion are in (l)(9), and a notice to EPA within 60 days of first using it is in (m)(5). The text I read speaks of purged refrigerant that is destroyed. It does not say a purge unit's returned refrigerant is excluded, and a purge that sends refrigerant back to the condenser is not destroying it. Paragraph (a) also says the section does not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants, so whether it reaches your machine depends on its refrigerant. Ask your compliance lead. For general leak repair, see Leaks and Leak Repair Basics and 608 Records: What Technicians Keep.

Where it fits in the plant

The purge belongs on your rounds. Write pump-out time on the log next to approach and pressures, as in Reading a Chiller Log and Chiller Plant Daily Rounds Walkthrough. For where this machine sits, see Chiller Compressors Compared, Chiller Types and Chiller Plant Fundamentals. Do not open, reset or bypass a purge, or vent a purge discharge, unless your procedures and certification allow it.

Field card: Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables

On a vacuum machine, more purge time means air is getting in.

  • Low-pressure refrigerants boil below atmospheric pressure in the evaporator, so leaks pull air and moisture in.
  • Air collects in the condenser, raises its pressure and lowers efficiency.
  • The purge chills a sample, returns liquid refrigerant, and pumps the air out through carbon.
  • Log daily and 7-day pump-out minutes; trend them against their own past.
  • A rising trend means leak search, not a higher alarm limit.
  • A rising condenser approach can be air or fouled tubes. Check flow and treatment too.
  • Evacuation of low-pressure appliances: 25 mm Hg absolute (40 CFR 82.156).
