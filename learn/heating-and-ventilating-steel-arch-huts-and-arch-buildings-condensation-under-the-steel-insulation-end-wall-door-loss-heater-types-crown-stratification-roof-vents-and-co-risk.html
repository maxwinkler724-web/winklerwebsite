Heating and Ventilating Steel Arch Huts and Arch Buildings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A curved steel arch building has no attic and no ceiling to hide behind. The shell is the wall and the roof in one piece, so every heating and moisture problem shows up on the underside of the steel. This lesson covers how those buildings behave as shops, storage barns and small churches, and how to pick and place heat and ventilation for them.

What the building is

The federal park service describes the historic arch hut as an arched steel frame with plywood ends and a wooden floor, built so it could be prefabricated, shipped and assembled almost anywhere. Modern steel arch buildings follow the same idea with a ribbed steel skin from footing to footing. Two things follow for the HVAC tech. The air space is one tall, curved volume, and the only flat surfaces are the end walls, where the doors, windows and equipment usually go.

Why the steel drips

Steel is a poor insulator and its inside face follows the outdoor temperature closely. A university extension article on metal-walled farm buildings explains the mechanism: when the inner metal surface is colder than the dew point of the room air, water condenses on it. Warm, moist air from people, wet vehicles, damp floors or unvented fuel burners meets cold steel, and drops fall like rain from the crown.

The same article gives the general cures. Ventilation is the first tool for moisture control. Insulation on the inside of the steel stops surface condensation, but moisture can still collect inside or behind the insulation unless there is a properly installed vapor barrier or a closed-cell material. Any interior vapor barrier needs a protective layer so it is not punctured. Walls also need a way to drain condensate away.

Insulation choices in general terms

  • Sprayed closed-cell foam sticks directly to the steel, follows the curve, and acts as its own vapor retarder. A state extension publication lists closed-cell foam as a vapor retarder at roughly 2 in. or more of thickness, while open-cell foam passes vapor.
  • Blanket insulation with a facing works if the facing is on the warm side, every seam and edge is sealed, and a protective liner keeps it from tearing. A gap lets warm air reach cold steel and drip behind the blanket.
  • Rigid board with an air gap is another option the extension article lists, and so are structural panels.

The same publication says to seal air leaks before adding insulation. On a curved building that means the base line, the rib ends and the door frames.

End walls and big doors

The two end walls are flat, easy to frame and insulate, and where most of the glass and doors sit. A large rolling or sliding door is usually the weak spot. It leaks at its edges and, while open, lets the whole indoor volume swap with outside air. Do not size heat for the shell alone. Add the door load, and ask whether a vestibule, an air curtain or a smaller personnel door can cut it. Our farm shop lesson goes deeper on big-door leaks.

Heater types

  • Unit heaters move air. They warm up the space quickly and fit on the end wall or on a stand. The hot air rises to the crown, so they work best with a destratification fan.
  • Radiant tube heaters hang near the ceiling. An extension poultry-house paper says tube heaters send more of their energy straight to the floor and occupants instead of warming air first, and that the warmest zone sits at floor level, with less stratification than forced air. It notes that the exhaust there is vented inside, which is why the same paper says ventilation must still prevent carbon monoxide buildup. In a church or shop you would normally want the exhaust piped outdoors per the maker's instructions. Reflectors and clearance to combustibles matter on a curved ceiling that slopes toward the tube.
  • Ductless heads suit a finished, insulated and sealed room inside the arch, such as an office or fellowship hall, not a bare shell with a cold crown. The outdoor unit goes outside, on a slab or a wall bracket on a flat end wall.

For wall equipment the rule is simple: mount on a flat framed surface, such as an end wall or a built-up backing plate, not on the curved ribs. Use the maker's mounting instructions and fasteners that suit thin steel, and seal every penetration so wet air cannot get into the insulation.

Stratification at the crown

Hot air collects at the highest point. A university paper on destratification says removing stratification in heating can cut fuel use, that ceiling fans are the main method, and that the fans' own energy eats into the savings. The same paper says little is known about how often they must run. In practice that means a slow, steady downward flow, not a cold draft on people, and a control that runs the fan only when the crown is clearly hotter than the floor.

Ventilation and roof vents

The extension article says ventilation is the primary tool against condensation. Because heated, moist air rises, an exhaust opening at the peak removes the wettest air first. Matching makeup air must come in low on the walls so the fans or vents do not pull exhaust back from a flue. A ridge vent works with the building's natural rise of hot air, and a power exhaust fan adds a defined flow. Without makeup air, an exhaust fan pulls a negative pressure that can backdraft atmospheric-vented heaters. Keep intake and exhaust openings clear of the vehicle door.

Carbon monoxide

The federal workplace safety agency's fact sheet lists engines, forklifts, heaters and other fuel-burning equipment as common CO sources, especially in enclosed spaces, and says large amounts can incapacitate someone within minutes without warning. Its listed controls include effective ventilation, maintained fuel-burning equipment, and no gasoline engines in poorly ventilated areas. A tight arch building with a car or truck running, or an unvented heater, is exactly that setting. Vent combustion products outdoors, keep vehicles outside while warming up, and add working CO alarms. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.

Corrosion

A building that drips is a building that rusts. Constant wetting attacks the underside of the sheets, fasteners and the base line where the steel meets the slab. The extension article notes that a liquid membrane does not prevent condensation but can slow corrosion. Treat a leak or drip as both a moisture problem and a structural maintenance problem, and keep heater flue gases and condensate away from the steel.

Worked example: heat loss of an assumed arch building

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume a 40 ft wide, 80 ft long building whose cross section is a half circle with a 20 ft radius, so the walls start at the floor and the crown is 20 ft above it. Assume an insulated shell with an overall U-value of 0.10 Btu/h per square foot per degree F for both the curved shell and the end walls (an assumed value, not a measurement). Assume an indoor design temperature of 70°F and an outdoor design of 10°F, so the temperature difference is 60°F. Assume air leakage of 0.5 air changes per hour with the doors closed. We ignore the floor, solar gain and any open-door time.

  1. Curved shell area is half the side of a cylinder: π × 20 ft × 80 ft = 5,027 sq ft.
  2. The two end walls are two half discs, which together make one full disc: π × 20² = 1,257 sq ft.
  3. Total envelope area = 5,027 + 1,257 = 6,283 sq ft.
  4. Conduction loss = area × U × temperature difference = 6,283 × 0.10 × 60 = 37,699, about 37,700 Btu/h.
  5. Volume is a half cylinder: 0.5 × π × 20² × 80 = 50,265 cubic ft.
  6. Air leakage = 50,265 × 0.5 / 60 = 419 cfm.
  7. Infiltration load = 1.08 × 419 × 60 = 27,143, about 27,100 Btu/h. The 1.08 comes from our own arithmetic with common air values: 0.075 lb per cubic ft × 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F × 60 minutes per hour.
  8. Total design heat loss = 37,699 + 27,143 = 64,842, about 64,800 Btu/h.

Check: the shell area also equals π × r × (L + r) = π × 20 × 100 = 6,283 sq ft, which agrees. If the leakage doubles to 1.0 air change per hour, the infiltration load doubles to 54,287 and the total becomes 91,986 Btu/h, showing how much a leaky base and door frame can matter. A real design would also add the floor and the door load. This is a teaching example, not a sizing for any building.

A heater should be chosen from the maker's rated output and the true local design temperature, with the installer following the maker's instructions and local code.

Field card: Heating and Ventilating Steel Arch Huts and Arch Buildings

Cold steel drips; warm air hides at the crown.

  • Steel below the room dew point condenses water; fix the moisture and the surface temperature.
  • Insulation needs a continuous vapor barrier or closed-cell material and sealed seams.
  • Add the big-door load to the shell and leakage load.
  • Radiant tubes warm the floor; unit heaters pair with destratification fans.
  • Exhaust at the peak, makeup air low, and no vehicles running inside.
  • Vent combustion products outdoors; add CO alarms.
  • Mount equipment on flat framed surfaces, not curved ribs.
