Liquid Injection and Discharge Temperature Control on Low-Temperature Compressors: Injection Valves, Sensors, Setpoints and Overfeed

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A compressor running at low suction pressure squeezes gas a long way, and the discharge gets hot. On low-temperature racks and condensing units, many makers add a way to spray a little liquid into the suction side to hold that heat down. This lesson covers why it is needed, how the valves and sensors work, example setpoints, and what overfeed and underfeed look like.

Check the unit's own literature

Check the unit's own literature. Temperatures, valve kits and port locations differ by compressor and refrigerant. The numbers below come from maker and patent documents we read and are examples, not settings for your machine. Use the data for the exact compressor you are working on.

Why low suction pressure drives discharge heat up

Compression ratio is absolute discharge pressure divided by absolute suction pressure. At a low evaporating temperature the suction pressure is small, so the same condensing pressure gives a much bigger ratio. A big ratio means more work per pound of gas, and that work leaves as heat in the discharge gas. A US patent on demand cooling says that refrigerants used as replacements in low-temperature work produce high discharge temperatures, particularly at high load and high compression ratio, and that excessive discharge temperature can break down the compressor lubricant. The related lesson on discharge line temperature covers where heat comes from in more detail, and compressor oils covers what the oil does when it overheats.

A maker's guide for scroll compressors on R-448A and similar blends makes the same point in practice. It says liquid injection is required whenever the saturated suction temperature is below -20 °C (-4 °F, our conversion) for those refrigerants, and that the injection valve normally works only at high condensing temperatures, as a backup to a well-designed system.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Take an R-448A low-temperature system at -25 °F evaporating and 110 °F condensing. Sea-level atmospheric pressure of 14.7 psi is our assumption. Pressures come from a refrigerant maker's pressure-temperature table, printed by pressure, so we read the matching temperatures and interpolated between rows. We use the dew point side for both, which is our simplification because the blend has glide.

  1. Suction: the table prints a dew point of -25.0 °F at 7.0 psig. Absolute = 7.0 + 14.7 = 21.7 psia.
  2. Discharge: the table prints dew points of 109.1 °F at 246 psig and 113.9 °F at 264 psig. At 110 °F we get 246 + 18 × (0.9 ÷ 4.8), about 249 psig. Absolute = 249 + 14.7 = 263.7 psia.
  3. Ratio = 263.7 ÷ 21.7 = about 12.2 to 1.

That is a very large ratio for one stage. Our -25 °F is -31.7 °C, well below the maker's -20 °C line for requiring injection, so this system is squarely in injection territory. If a frosted coil or a weak metering device pulled suction down to 3.0 psig (the table's dew point there is -32.9 °F), the ratio would be (249 + 14.7) ÷ (3.0 + 14.7) = 263.7 ÷ 17.7 = about 14.9 to 1, and the discharge would run hotter still. Note the table does not tell us the discharge temperature itself. Discharge gas is superheated, so you measure it.

Valve types and where the liquid goes

  • Thermostatic injection valve. A bulb on the compressor senses temperature and opens a valve directly. A maker's installation sheet for one such valve gives factory opening points of 45, 75 or 110 °C depending on range, a proportional band of 20 °C, a maximum bulb temperature of 150 °C, and about 2 °C of change per full turn of the adjusting spindle. Its inlet goes on the liquid line (subcooled, as short a run as practical) and its outlet to the suction side.
  • Thermostatic valve built into a compressor kit. A maker's scroll guide describes a discharge temperature control valve rated at 120 °C (248 °F, our conversion) with its bulb in the top well of the compressor, closest to the discharge port. It injects only when needed and only the amount needed.
  • Electronic control with a solenoid or modulating valve. A maker's technical sheet describes a sensor in the discharge head, a controller, and a valve that meters saturated liquid into the suction cavity. Modulation starts at 130 °C (266 °F) and the valve is fully open at 140 °C (284 °F). The liquid enters after suction gas has already cooled the motor, which keeps the amount needed small.
  • On/off solenoid with an orifice. The patent describes a head sensor opening a solenoid at about 290 °F and closing it at about 280 °F, with the orifice limiting the flow so the compressor is not flooded. Its example was run at -25 °F evaporating and 110 °F condensing.

Whatever the style, the liquid must reach the valve as liquid. The scroll maker's sheet warns that subcooling at the valve must be enough to prevent flashing, a filter drier goes ahead of the valve, and liquid should be at the valve inlet before the compressor starts or the valve can be damaged.

The discharge thermostat is the safety, not the control

Injection holds the temperature. A separate discharge line thermostat shuts the compressor down if injection cannot keep up. The scroll guide says its external discharge thermostat opens at 129 °C plus or minus 4 K (about 264 °F, our conversion), mounts about 120 mm (4.7 in, our conversion) from the discharge outlet, and is mandatory on those models. The electronic control sheet adds that if the discharge stays above its preset maximum for 2 seconds, the compressor stops for 2 minutes and then restarts by itself. A thermostat that trips is telling you injection is underfed or something upstream is wrong. Do not jumper it out.

Demand cooling and what it does not cover

Demand cooling is the generic name for injecting only when the sensor asks for it. The electronic sheet says it is not approved for capacity-modulated compressors and is not compatible with inverter speed control. It also suggests reducing the need for injection by insulating the suction line, keeping return gas superheat low, holding suction pressure as high as is safe, and keeping condensing temperature low. A dirty condenser is therefore a discharge temperature problem as well as an efficiency problem.

Service checks

  • Sensor location. A patent notes that sensors on the outside of the discharge line read skewed by ambient and gas flow, which is why head-mounted sensors are used. Confirm the sensor is in the right well or hole, torqued, and tight. A maker sheet gives a PT1000 sensor tightening torque of 15 N·m. An open or shorted sensor sets an alarm. With power off and the sensor unplugged, the sheet says readings from above 100,000 ohms (very cold) to below 1,600 ohms (very hot) usually indicate a good sensor.
  • Valve feeding. Check for liquid, not vapor, at the valve inlet: a clear sight glass if the kit has one, a good drier, and enough subcooling. Verify the coil gets its control signal and that a thermostatic bulb is clamped with good metal contact and its capillary is not kinked. The valve maker says not to bend the capillary within 25 mm of the bulb.
  • Clearances. The scroll guide keeps the injection valve capillary at least 13 mm from the scroll side and the insulated capillary at least 3 mm from the top.
  • Replacement. The scroll guide says to replace the injection valve and filter drier when a compressor is replaced.

Symptoms: overfeed and underfeed

These points are our own shop reasoning, built from the maker cautions above.

  • Overfeed (valve stuck open, set too low, or leaking). Too much liquid reaches the suction side. Expect low suction superheat at the compressor, a cold suction line or crankcase, oil dilution and foaming, and in bad cases floodback. The patent notes that older injection methods leaked when cooling was not needed, flooded the compressor, and let high-pressure liquid migrate to suction during shutdown and slug the compressor at startup. See floodback and slugging.
  • Underfeed (valve stuck closed, plugged drier, flashing liquid, dead coil or a bad sensor). Discharge temperature climbs and the thermostat trips the compressor, often only on hot afternoons when head pressure is highest. Left alone, this is the oil breakdown and valve deposit problem the first section described.

Compare the discharge reading to the maker's data, not to a number you remember. Lock out and tag power before touching sensor or valve wiring.

Field card: Liquid Injection

Low suction means high ratio, high ratio means heat.

  • Ratio = absolute discharge divided by absolute suction. Our R-448A example: about 12.2 to 1.
  • Injection valve opens on a head or bulb temperature, not on suction.
  • Needs liquid at the valve inlet: drier, subcooling, sight glass.
  • The discharge thermostat is the safety. Never bypass it.
  • Overfeed: low superheat, dilution, floodback. Underfeed: hot discharge and trips.
