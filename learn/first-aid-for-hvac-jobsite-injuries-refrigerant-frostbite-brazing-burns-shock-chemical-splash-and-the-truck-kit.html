First Aid for HVAC Jobsite Injuries: Refrigerant Frostbite, Brazing Burns, Shock, Chemical Splash and the Truck Kit

Last updated: 2026-10-16

HVAC work puts cold liquid, open flame, live power, chemicals and exhaust gas into one day. This lesson walks through the first moves for each, based on government first aid pages, and explains what OSHA expects employers to have on hand. It starts with a warning: it is not a substitute for a hands-on class.

General information, not medical training. Reading this page does not make you ready to treat anyone. Take a first aid and CPR class from a recognized provider, learn what your employer's emergency plan says, and call 911 for any serious injury. When in doubt about how bad something is, call.

The order that applies to every injury

Before any specific injury, run the same short check, shown in the chart below. First, is the scene safe for you? A second victim helps nobody. Second, if electricity may be involved, is the power off? Third, is this serious enough for 911? Only then do you start a first aid step. The electrical page from MedlinePlus puts the same order in plain words: cut the power if it is safe to do so, call 911, and do not touch a person who is still in contact with the source.

Refrigerant on skin or in the eyes

Liquid refrigerant boils off fast and chills whatever it touches. A NOAA chemical datasheet for a common blend lists frostbite for skin contact with the liquid and says only medical personnel should attempt thawing of frosted parts. A NIOSH pocket guide entry for an older refrigerant lists frostbite for both skin and eye contact. So treat any liquid splash as a cold injury and a chemical exposure at the same time.

For ordinary frostbite, MedlinePlus lists these steps: get the person somewhere warm, remove tight jewelry and wet clothing, and wrap the area in sterile dressings if medical help can be reached quickly. It says do not rub or massage the area, do not use direct dry heat such as a heating pad or hair dryer, and do not break blisters. The CDC and NIOSH pages on cold illness agree on no rubbing and no heat lamp, stove or radiator, because numb skin burns easily. The same MedlinePlus page describes warm-water soaking only for cases where medical help is not nearby. That conflicts with the datasheet's wording about thawing, so for refrigerant we follow the product's safety data sheet and the 911 dispatcher, not a rule we remember. For the eyes, we found no step on these pages beyond getting emergency medical care, so treat the eye as an emergency and use the SDS. Our Refrigerant Safety Basics and cold stress lesson cover prevention.

Brazing burns

For a small burn with the skin unbroken, MedlinePlus says to cool it under running water or soak it in cool, not ice, water for 5 to 30 minutes, then cover it loosely with a dry sterile bandage. Do not remove clothing stuck to the skin, and do not put oil, butter, ice or creams on a severe burn. It lists several reasons to call 911, including a burn the size of the palm or larger, a full-thickness burn, a chemical or electrical cause, or being unsure how serious it is. If clothing is on fire, the page says to stop, drop and roll, and to smother the flames with thick material. Brazing prep and fire safety are in Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines.

Electrical shock

MedlinePlus says to cut the power if it is safe, by unplugging, removing the fuse or switching off the breaker, and to call 911. It adds that turning off only the appliance may not stop the current. It says not to touch the person with bare hands while they are still in contact with the source, and not to approach someone shocked by high-voltage current closer than 20 feet (6 meters) until power is off. Once the person is clear, check airway, breathing and pulse, and the page says to begin CPR if they are unconscious with no pulse. See Arc Flash and Shock Safety for lockout and meter habits.

Chemical splash to the eyes or skin

MedlinePlus says to flush the eye right away with a gentle stream of cool tap water for 15 minutes, with the injured eye turned down and to the side, then get medical help immediately. It says not to rub or press the eye and not to delay care while treating it yourself. For skin it says to remove clothing and jewelry that has the chemical on it and flush with cool running water for 15 minutes or more, with exceptions for dry lime and some metals where poison control should be called. Poison control is 1-800-222-1222, as OSHA's first aid guide also lists. Read Section 4 of the safety data sheet: OSHA's rule for SDS content says it must describe the measures for inhalation, skin contact, eye contact and ingestion, the key symptoms, and when to get immediate medical attention. Keep the SDS binder or phone folder where you can reach it fast.

Carbon monoxide exposure

NIOSH lists headache, confusion, fatigue, seizures, dizziness or loss of consciousness, and nausea. CDC adds that symptoms are often flu-like, and that sleeping or intoxicated people can die before symptoms show. The NIOSH page says to turn off the equipment, get outdoors or to clean air, call 911 if symptoms occur, and not drive yourself. Prevention, alarms and testing are in Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Cuts

For a minor cut, MedlinePlus says to wash with mild soap and water, press directly to stop bleeding, and cover with a clean bandage. It says call 911 for severe bleeding or bleeding that does not stop after 10 minutes of pressure, or if the person cannot feel the area. Sheet metal edges and rusty objects are the usual HVAC causes; the page lists rusty-object injuries and wounds deeper than a quarter inch (0.64 centimeter) as reasons to contact a provider, and it recommends a tetanus shot if you have not had one within 10 years.

What OSHA 1910.151 requires

OSHA's rule has three parts that matter here. Paragraph (b) says that if no infirmary, clinic or hospital is in near proximity to the workplace, someone must be adequately trained to give first aid, and first aid supplies must be readily available. OSHA letters add that the employer may tailor supplies to the workplace. Paragraph (c) says that where eyes or body may be exposed to injurious corrosive materials, suitable facilities for quick drenching or flushing must be provided in the work area. A 1994 OSHA letter, which the page itself warns may no longer represent OSHA policy, ties eyewash equipment to ANSI Z358.1. Construction work has a parallel rule, 29 CFR 1926.50. Which rule covers your job and employer is a question for your employer and OSHA, not this page.

What a truck kit holds

OSHA's non-mandatory appendix to 1910.151 points to ANSI Z308.1 (Minimum Requirements for Workplace First-aid Kits, cited there as the 1998 edition; today's title is ANSI/ISEA Z308.1) as enough for small worksites, and does not list the items itself. We therefore do not list contents here. Check the label on a kit that says it meets that standard, then add to it. OSHA's guide says supplies should reflect the injuries that actually occur, and that the employer should periodically reassess and restock. For an HVAC truck, that means thinking about burns, cuts and eye splashes. A sealed eye flush bottle is a fair question to raise, but the appendix does not say whether it replaces plumbed eyewash where corrosives are used, so ask your employer.

Worked example (ours): liquid refrigerant on the back of a hand

This is our own example and is not a treatment protocol. Assumptions: a tech is disconnecting hoses, a puff of liquid hits the back of one gloved hand, the skin turns white and numb, a partner is on site, and nobody is in contact with power.

  1. Make the scene safe. Close the manifold valves if you can do it without more exposure, and move away from the released refrigerant. (Chart box 1; refrigerant vapor can cause dizziness or asphyxia in enclosed spaces, per the NOAA datasheet.)
  2. Power. Nothing electrical is involved, so the second box is a yes. If a hose release happened near live wiring, stop and treat power first.
  3. Call for help. We call 911 if the skin is white, hard, blistered or numb, or if any liquid reached the eyes. Calling a poison control line (1-800-222-1222) is a good second call. The dispatcher can advise on thawing.
  4. Remove what holds the cold. Take off the glove if it comes off easily, and remove ring and watch, per MedlinePlus (remove tight jewelry and wet clothing).
  5. Do not rub, and no direct heat. No hair dryer, heater or torch, per MedlinePlus and CDC.
  6. Warm gently only if advised. Hold the hand against the body, such as in the armpit (CDC), and follow the SDS Section 4 and the dispatcher. MedlinePlus describes warm-water soaking when help is not nearby, but the datasheet says only medical personnel should thaw.
  7. Cover loosely with a dry sterile dressing and do not break blisters.
  8. Get seen. Stay with the person until help comes or someone else drives them. Report the incident to the employer.

Which step you can do depends on training you may not have. That is why we keep saying to take a class.

Field card: HVAC Jobsite First Aid

Scene, power, 911, then the step.

  • Not safe or power on: do not touch the person. Call 911.
  • Refrigerant frostbite: no rubbing, no direct heat, cover loosely, read SDS Section 4.
  • Burn: cool running water, loose sterile cover, 911 for large, deep or chemical burns.
  • Eye chemical: flush 15 minutes, get medical help.
  • CO symptoms: shut off equipment, fresh air, 911.
  • Cut: direct pressure, 911 if bleeding does not stop after 10 minutes of pressure.
  • Take a first aid and CPR class.
