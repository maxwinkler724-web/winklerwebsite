Fermenting at Home and Your House: Steady Spots, Warm Spots and Carbon Dioxide

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Homebrew, wine, cider, kombucha and a crock of sauerkraut all want a steady temperature, and your heating and cooling system decides which spots in the house offer one. This lesson covers the warm spots to avoid, the steadier spots to pick, the heat a busy ferment makes on its own, and why a small closed closet needs an air path while gas is bubbling.

Why the spot matters

Extension pages give different temperature ranges for different ferments, and the ranges are narrow enough that a house can miss them. Sauerkraut guidance from one extension page says about 70 to 75 degrees F is ideal and that a room over 80 degrees F risks spoilage. Another extension page on sauerkraut says to hold a steady 68 to 72 degrees F for roughly 7 to 14 days and to keep the crock in a well-ventilated spot. A third says 68 to 72 degrees F is ideal, that above 78 degrees F fermentation can run too far, and that below 60 degrees F it is inhibited.

Kombucha pages vary a little: one extension page says 64 to 79 degrees F, another says ideally 68 to 77, and a fact sheet says 68 to 72 with 62 to 68 also working, and warns against fermenting in the sun or anywhere it can get too hot. For wine, a main extension page gives about 68 to 86 degrees F for red and at or below 59 degrees F for white. For beer and cider, use the range printed on the yeast pack or recipe; we are not quoting a number for those here.

Taken together, most home ferments sit in the range of a comfortable living room, around the high 60s to mid 70s degrees F, and the sources punish heat more than they punish a little cool. That is the target for your thermostat-controlled house, and the swings are what to watch.

Spots that run too warm

Our own rule of thumb: any place where the house adds heat on its own schedule is a poor home for a ferment.

  • Near a supply register or baseboard. A supply register blows air that is much warmer than the room whenever the furnace or heat pump runs, and in summer it blows cold air in bursts. The vessel sees swings, not an average.
  • On or beside a radiator. A hot-water or steam radiator can pass its heat to a crock set against it for hours.
  • In a sunny window. Direct sun heats a jar well past the room temperature, and the kombucha fact sheet above says not to ferment in the sun. One extension page also says fermenting vegetables do better in the dark.
  • Beside a water heater. A tank sheds standby heat, and a utility closet around it stays warmer than the rest of the house. It is also a place where you want clear space for the appliance (see the combustion air section below).
  • On top of the refrigerator or near the range and oven. Both put out heat; our pantry lesson covers that.

Steadier spots

Our own rule of thumb: a basement or an interior closet tends to change slowly, because it has no sun and few supply registers, and the ground and walls around it change temperature slowly. Check this yourself: put a thermometer in the spot for a week and read it morning and evening before you commit a batch. A basement that runs damp or cold in winter may need a heating mat or a move upstairs; a closet that holds house air may be warmer than you expect. See our basement dehumidifier lesson if the space runs damp.

A busy ferment makes its own heat

A vigorous ferment releases some heat as the yeast works, so the liquid can run a few degrees above the air around it, and a big batch in a tight spot runs warmer still. The wine extension page we read does not state a number for this, so we are not giving one. The practical step is simple and our own: tape a thermometer to the vessel itself rather than reading the room, and decide by that number. Commercial tanks handle the same effect with cooling jackets; see our brewery and winery glycol chiller lesson for that scale.

Carbon dioxide in a small closed closet

Yeast and the bacteria in a crock turn sugar into acids or alcohol and release carbon dioxide gas. The federal workplace-safety chemical data page for carbon dioxide lists an 8-hour limit of 5,000 ppm and a short-term limit of 30,000 ppm for workplaces. Those are workplace figures, and we are not claiming a home threshold from them. Two plain points follow from how the gas behaves:

  • Do not seal a fermenter tight. Extension pages for kombucha warn that sealed bottles can build carbon dioxide pressure and even burst, and that the brew itself should be covered but not sealed. Use an airlock, a loose cloth cover, or whatever your recipe calls for, so the vessel has a gas path out. One extension page for pickled vegetables does call for a fitting lid and briefly opening it to release gas, which is another way to give the gas a path.
  • Do not shut a fermenter in a small closed space. Our own note: carbon dioxide is denser than air, so in a closet it settles toward the floor. Use a closet with a gap or louver at the bottom and a vent or cracked door at the top, or put the ferment in a room with the door open.

Worked example (our own): one pound of sugar in a closet

All inputs here are our own assumptions, not a measured result. We assume 1 lb of sugar (a simple sugar, 180 g per mole) ferments out completely into alcohol and carbon dioxide, the room is 70 degrees F, and the closet is the 4 ft by 3 ft by 8 ft one in the sketch.

  1. Gas made. Fermentation turns each sugar molecule into two alcohol molecules and two carbon dioxide molecules. Two carbon dioxide molecules weigh 2 x 44 = 88 g for every 180 g of sugar, so the ratio is 88 / 180 = 0.489. That is about 0.49 lb of carbon dioxide per 1 lb of sugar.
  2. Moles. 1 lb is about 453.6 g, so the gas is 453.6 x 0.489 = about 222 g. At 44 g per mole that is 222 / 44 = about 5.04 mol.
  3. Volume at room temperature. At 70 degrees F (about 294 K) and normal pressure a mole of gas takes up about 24.1 L. So 5.04 x 24.1 = about 121 L. One cubic foot is 28.3 L, so 121 / 28.3 = about 4.3 cubic feet.
  4. Closet air volume. 4 x 3 x 8 = 96 cubic feet.
  5. Comparison. 4.3 / 96 = 0.045, or about 4.5 percent of the closet volume.

Read that plainly: it is not a danger number. It is a worst case that assumes the sugar ferments out all at once, the gas spreads evenly, and the closet leaks nothing. Real ferments release gas over days, and real closets leak. A larger batch scales straight up, since ten times the sugar makes ten times the gas, or about 43 cubic feet, roughly 45 percent of the closet. The point of the exercise is that a ventilated room is the answer, and a tightly closed closet is a poor choice for anything that makes gas.

Fruit flies and moisture

An extension page on fruit flies says the adults are drawn to overripe fruit, vegetables and fermenting foods, and that wine, beer, juice and vinegar attract them too. It also says damp mops or towels can supply enough food and moisture for larvae, and that sanitation is the primary control: remove the breeding sites, rinse and drain bottles and cans, and wring out mops. For ferments, the same page and the kombucha pages point to a clean cloth or coffee filter held with a rubber band, which lets gas out and keeps insects out. Put the crock on a tray, since a sauerkraut page says to set the container on a plate to catch leaks and to wipe up spills right away. Fermenting liquids also add a little moisture to the air, and a damp basement adds more; see our basement dehumidifier lesson if the space runs damp.

Keep crocks away from combustion air openings

Our own guidance: furnaces, boilers and gas water heaters pull air for burning from the room or from a duct or louver, and that opening needs to stay clear. Do not stack crocks, buckets or shelving in front of a combustion air opening or in the clearance around a flue. Keep sticky spills, sanitizer and cleaner containers away from it as well. Our combustion air lesson explains how those openings work, and our crawl space lesson shows why a crawl space that holds equipment is a poor place for a ferment, since a small closed space holds gas and moisture.

Field card: Fermenting at Home and Your House: Steady Spots, Warm Spots and Carbon Dioxide

Steady temperature, a gas path out, and clear air for the burners.

  • Sources we read give roughly 68 to 77 degrees F for sauerkraut and kombucha, and above 78 or 80 degrees F is the warning line for kraut.
  • Avoid spots near supply registers, radiators, sunny windows and water heaters.
  • Try a basement or interior closet for a week with a thermometer first.
  • Tape the thermometer to the vessel; a busy ferment may run warmer than the room.
  • Never seal a fermenter tight; give gas a path out.
  • Our example: 1 lb of sugar makes about 0.49 lb of carbon dioxide, about 4.3 cubic feet, against a 96 cubic foot closet. Ventilate.
  • Keep crocks and shelving off combustion air openings.
