Electric Duct Heaters: Airflow Switches, Limits, SCR Control and Minimum Velocity

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An electric duct heater is a bundle of resistance elements sitting in the airstream. Nothing carries the heat away except moving air, so every safety on the unit exists to answer one question: is enough air going past the elements right now? This lesson walks through those safeties, the control choices, and a worked example using maker literature.

Check the maker's paperwork

Check the maker's paperwork. Every number below is from the maker pages listed under Sources or is labeled "Our own." Different makers publish different minimums and clearances, so the nameplate, wiring diagram and installation manual on the job always win over this page. This is general education, not a design guide.

What the heater is

Makers describe two common element styles. An open coil heater strings bare resistance wire across the duct, so air flows around and through the coil. Tutco's overview describes it that way, notes that it can be built in separate stages, and says low airflow can overheat and break the coil. A finned tubular heater puts the wire inside a metal tube. Indeeco describes a nickel-chromium coil centered in a stainless steel sheath with magnesium oxide powder insulating the wire from the sheath. Indeeco's manual treats both types separately because their minimum velocity charts differ, and it gives different maximum inlet air temperatures: 100°F for open coil and 80°F for finned tubular.

The three safeties and why each exists

  1. Airflow proof. Indeeco's manual says the heater must be wired so it cannot run unless air is moving, using a built-in airflow switch, a fan relay or another method shown on the wiring diagram. Its built-in switch needs at least 0.07 in. w.c. of static pressure in the duct. The pickup tube has an arrow: on the positive-pressure side of the fan the arrow points with the airflow, and on the negative side it points against it. If it is backward, the manual says to rotate the tube 180 degrees. Another maker's manual says plainly, "Never energize a heater without proper airflow."
  2. Automatic reset high limit. This is the primary over-temperature protection. Greenheck lists an automatic reset thermal switch for primary protection. It opens when the elements run too hot and recloses by itself after they cool. That self-resetting behavior is why a starved heater can cycle on and off and look like a thermostat problem.
  3. Manual reset backup limit. Greenheck lists a manual reset switch for secondary protection. Indeeco says its units carry both reset types and calls them thermal cutouts, not heat limiters or fusible links. Titus's specification calls for a primary automatic cutout plus a secondary manual one, or replaceable limiters on certain models. A manual reset is meant to stay open until a person finds out why the first limit was not enough.

Minimum velocity and direction

Indeeco's manual says the minimum velocity must be met at all points across the heater face, read from charts in feet per minute, and that missing it can cause serious damage or nuisance thermal cutout trips. Greenheck's page says the minimum airflow depends on inlet air temperature and that if airflow is below the chart value, the velocity must go up, the kW must come down, or both. A Johnson Controls manual shows finned tubular and open coil charts plotted against kW per square foot. Tutco, writing about open coil heaters, suggests a minimum of 200 fpm and says more is better. Makers measure heater load as nameplate kW divided by duct area in square feet; Indeeco adds that if the element enclosure is more than 1 in. smaller than the duct in length or width, you use the enclosure area instead. Airflow direction matters too, because pickup tubes and some stacked heaters are built for one direction.

Clearances

Indeeco's manual wants the heater at least 4 ft from grills, registers, filters, abrupt duct size changes, humidifiers and air handlers. It also wants duct elbows or turns at least 4 ft from the heater inlet and 2 ft from the outlet. Another manual says a heater mounted less than 4 ft downstream of a heat pump or similar obstruction may need turning vanes or pressure plates to even out the air, and cites NEC Article 424-59 for that. Indeeco also asks for 5 in. of free air above and below the cooling fins on units with a built-in SCR controller, and says not to enclose the terminal box.

Control methods compared

  • Staged contactors. Indeeco's Option G switches stages with a pilot duty thermostat or step controller and de-energizing contactors. Tutco says each stage of an open coil heater is its own circuit and can be powered separately for different outputs.
  • Step controller. Indeeco's Option K uses one for "vernier control" alongside proportional power control.
  • SCR proportional control. Titus explains that SCR control varies how long the heater is on during a fixed time base, not its kW. A 40% demand on a 2.0 second base becomes a 0.8 second pulse at full output. Titus lists these advantages: it matches the load more closely than steps do, wastes less energy when the space overshoots, gives steadier discharge temperature, and is silent where magnetic contactors click. Indeeco calls its SCR option designed for the most precise temperature control. The same Titus document does not discuss heat-up time or harmonics, so those are open questions for the maker. Indeeco's SCR-equipped units also need free air around the cooling fins.

None of these replaces the safeties. Indeeco's Option K, for example, still adds safety contactors controlled by the automatic reset cutout.

Listing and code names

Greenheck lists its heater as UL 1996 certified. Indeeco says the NEC and UL require the maker to supply overcurrent protection when total heater current exceeds 48 amperes, and that fuses are standard on its heaters over 48 amps. For wire sizing, Indeeco says to size conductors at 125% of rated load for heaters under 50 kW. NEC Article 424 is the code article for fixed electric space heating equipment; we name it here and do not reproduce it.

Worked example: 15 kW, 480 V, three phase, 1,200 cfm, 2.0 sq ft duct

Our own arithmetic, using the three-phase formula Indeeco prints in its manual and the 1.08 and 3,412 constants covered in the heat transfer formulas lesson.

  1. Our own: line current = kW × 1,000 ÷ (V × 1.732) = 15,000 ÷ (480 × 1.732) = 15,000 ÷ 831.4 = 18.0 A.
  2. Our own: heat output = 15 kW × 3,412 = 51,180 Btu/h.
  3. Our own: temperature rise = 51,180 ÷ (1.08 × 1,200) = 51,180 ÷ 1,296 = 39.5°F.
  4. Our own: face velocity = 1,200 cfm ÷ 2.0 sq ft = 600 fpm.
  5. Our own: heater load = 15 kW ÷ 2.0 sq ft = 7.5 kW per sq ft, the figure the maker charts use.
  6. Our own: conductor sizing at 125% of 18.0 A = 22.6 A, and 18.0 A is below the 48 A overcurrent threshold Indeeco names.

Comparison: 600 fpm is well above the 200 fpm that Tutco suggests as a minimum for open coil elements. We did not find a numeric minimum for this heater in the pages we read, because the makers publish theirs as charts that depend on kW per square foot and inlet air temperature. So we leave the real answer qualitative: read the maker chart at 7.5 kW per sq ft and your entering air temperature, and confirm the velocity at every point across the face, not just the average. A 39.5°F rise also tells you the duct downstream will be warm, which is useful when you check that the heater is actually making its rated kW.

Common failures and safe field checks

  • Nuisance limit trips. Indeeco's troubleshooting points to insufficient airflow first, so check velocity against the charts and look for clogged filters.
  • Heater will not run. Indeeco lists: associated controls on, disconnect energized, thermostat set above ambient, then press the manual reset buttons.
  • Burned or broken elements. Tutco links coil failure to low airflow. Another manual warns not to handle the elements and to investigate a protection failure before returning the heater to service.
  • Backward airflow switch tube. Check the arrow against the fan side, as above.
  • Loose connections. Indeeco says to check field and factory connections at least once a year or season and again after a short run.

Lockout first. One maker manual warns that electric heaters may start automatically and that more than one disconnect may be needed to de-energize the equipment. OSHA's typical minimal lockout procedure (29 CFR 1910.147 Appendix A) shuts down the equipment, deactivates the isolating devices, applies individual locks, handles stored energy and verifies isolation before work. Read the label and meter ratings in the arc flash and shock lesson, then verify zero volts with your meter.

Field card: Electric Duct Heaters

No air, no heat.

  • Three safeties: airflow proof, automatic reset limit, manual reset backup.
  • Nuisance trips: check airflow and filters first (Indeeco).
  • Lock out and verify zero volts before opening the terminal box.
  • Line current, three phase: kW × 1,000 ÷ (V × 1.732).
  • Rise = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × cfm); velocity = cfm ÷ area.
  • Our example: 15 kW at 480 V is 18.0 A, 39.5°F rise, 600 fpm.
  • Clearances and minimum velocity come from the maker's chart and manual.
