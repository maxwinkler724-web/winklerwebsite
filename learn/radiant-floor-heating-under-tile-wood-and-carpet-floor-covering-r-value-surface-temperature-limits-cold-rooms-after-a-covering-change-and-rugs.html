Radiant Floor Heating Under Tile, Wood and Carpet

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A radiant floor hands its heat to the room through whatever sits on top of it. Tile lets heat through almost freely. A carpet and pad can block most of it. This lesson shows how much a covering changes the water temperature you need, why a flooring swap can leave a room cold, and what your options are.

Check the maker's limits

Check the maker's limits. This page is general education built from public university, federal and national sources, plus our own arithmetic. The tubing, slab and flooring makers' instructions for the job in front of you always win. For how a floor is built, start with Radiant Floor Heating Basics.

R-value of a covering, in plain terms

Heat moves upward from the water through the tube wall, the slab or plates, and then through the floor covering. Each layer resists that flow. The resistance is its R-value, in F·ft2·h/Btu. For a steady flow, heat flux q (Btu/h per ft2) equals the temperature difference across a layer divided by its R:

q = dT / R, or turned around, dT = q × R.

Resistances in the path add up. A bigger total R means more temperature drop is needed to push the same heat through. A covering sits at the very end of the path, so its R is the one the installer or homeowner can change after the system is built.

What each covering does

A Penn State webinar handout on radiant floors says carpet can be treated as an insulator over a radiant system, and describes tile and stone as highly conductive. It gives one worked pair for carpet: a 24 oz plush carpet at R-1.12 plus a bonded polyurethane pad at R-2.09, for a total of R-3.21, which it compares to over half an inch of extruded polystyrene. That is a heavy combination. A thin carpet on a thin pad will be lower, so the R of about 2 we use below is a plausible middle, but it is our own assumption, not a published figure for any particular carpet.

A Washington State University modeling paper found that higher-conductivity surfaces such as tile warmed fastest but had stronger hot spots over the pipes, while wood warmed more slowly, held its temperature longer and gave a more even surface. It lists conductivities of about 2.5 W/m-K for tile and 0.15 for wood, roughly 17 to 1.

For vinyl and wood we found no public R-values from a government or university page that we could read, so we do not print numbers for them. Both usually fall between tile and thick carpet. Get the R-value from the flooring maker's data and the radiant designer's tables, and add the underlayment or pad, because those layers count too.

How warm the floor may be

The Washington State paper uses an acceptable floor surface range of 24 to 32 C (about 75 to 90 F) with a design target of 28 C (about 82 F). Radiant guidelines usually cap occupied floors lower than the top of that range; our basics lesson summarizes one guideline with limits of about 85 F for areas with long foot contact and 90 F elsewhere. Wood is stricter. The Penn State handout says the surface temperature of a solid hardwood subfloor should not exceed 80, with the unit not printed on the slide; we read it as 80 F. Treat that as the working ceiling for wood and confirm the exact figure with the flooring maker.

The limit caps the surface, so it also caps how much heat the floor can give. If a room needs more than the floor can deliver at its allowed surface temperature, add another emitter.

Worked example (our own numbers)

These inputs are our assumptions, not measurements from a particular job.

  • The room needs 25 Btu/h per ft2 from the floor.
  • The floor surface is held at 80 F.
  • Everything below the covering (tube wall, slab, any plates) adds up to R 0.9.
  • Tile and thinset: R 0.1. Carpet and pad: R 2.0.

Tile. Total R = 0.9 + 0.1 = 1.0. Water-to-surface difference dT = q × R = 25 × 1.0 = 25 F. Average water temperature is about 80 + 25 = 105 F. The drop across the tile itself is only 25 × 0.1 = 2.5 F.

Carpet and pad. Total R = 0.9 + 2.0 = 2.9. dT = 25 × 2.9 = 72.5 F. Average water temperature is about 80 + 72.5 = 152.5 F. The drop across the carpet and pad alone is 25 × 2.0 = 50 F, so the top of the slab under the carpet sits near 130 F.

That is the story in two lines: the same room needs about 105 F water under tile and about 152 F under carpet and pad. Our basics lesson mentions a guideline limit of 140 F for water in tubing embedded in concrete, so the carpet case does not fit, and a 130 F slab surface is far above what a wood or adhesive layer can tolerate.

Now run it backward. Suppose the system was set up for tile with 105 F water and then carpet and pad are installed. Output becomes q = dT / R = (105 − 80) / 2.9 = 8.6 Btu/h per ft2. That is about one third of the 25 Btu/h per ft2 the room needs. (With the heavier R 3.21 combination from the Penn State handout, the drop across the covering alone would be 25 × 3.21 = 80 F.) The floor also settles at a lower surface temperature than 80 F, because less heat reaches it, so the room feels cold underfoot as well as in the air.

Why a covering change leaves a room cold

Common patterns a tech finds:

  • New carpet or a thick rug over a floor designed bare or under tile.
  • Tile replaced by wood, or wood with a thick underlayment. Output drops, though less than with carpet.
  • A remodel that kept the old water setting. See Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation.
  • Loops that look fine. Flow balance (see Radiant Floor Manifolds) cannot fix an R problem. A hot return with a cool surface points to high covering R.

Raise the water temperature, or add emitters?

Raising the water sounds easy, but it is limited in three ways. Tubing and slab have maximum water temperatures. Wood and adhesives have maximum temperatures below the covering. And a hotter return gives up the efficiency of a condensing boiler; see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing. In the example above, raising the water from 105 F to roughly 150 F would be needed for carpet and pad, which is past the 140 F guideline we cite. A modest raise can help a floor that is a little short, but it is the wrong fix when the covering R has tripled.

Adding emitters means giving the room another source so the floor does not have to carry everything: a panel radiator, baseboard sized for low water temperature (see Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature), or a fan convector. Because those emitters can run on the same lower-temperature loop, they fit well when the floor is no longer enough.

The third option is the cheapest. Pick the covering with the load in mind before the floor goes in, and do the heat-loss calculation with the actual covering R. If a covering must change later, redo the numbers first.

Wood flooring: moisture and temperature

The U.S. Forest Products Laboratory's Wood Handbook explains why heated floors are hard on wood. Wood in service sees seasonal and daily changes in humidity and temperature, and it swells and shrinks as its moisture content moves. The handbook notes that shrinking and swelling can open gaps in flooring, and that shrinkage across the growth rings (tangential) is about twice that along them (radial). Its equilibrium moisture content table shows that at a fixed 50 percent relative humidity, moisture content falls from 9.5 percent at 50 F to 7.9 percent at 130 F. Warming the wood dries it, and indoor air in the heating season is already dry. The handbook also says that unless wood is very dry, shrinkage from moisture loss on heating exceeds thermal expansion, so a heated board gets smaller, not larger.

Put that together with the Penn State advice: choose quartersawn, narrow boards, keep the subfloor surface to 80 F or less, and know that most makers will not warranty solid hardwood over radiant heat, while many engineered floating floors are warrantied with some exceptions. Follow the flooring maker for the target moisture content at installation and for the allowed surface temperature. We could not open the Wood Handbook table of recommended installation moisture content, so we give no numbers for that.

Rugs over radiant floors

A rug is a partial carpet. A thin loose rug adds little R; a thick rug with a pad acts like the carpet and pad case above for the area it covers. Heat that does not escape raises the temperature of the floor beneath, which matters for wood. The Penn State handout shows trapped heat under a heated mat on laminate and recommends at least 2 to 3 inches of clearance between the floor covering and the underside of furniture. This is our own reasoning, extended from that: keep furniture and rugs from sealing heat against the floor, and do not cover most of a room's floor if the floor is the main heat source.

Field card: Radiant Floor Heating Under Tile, Wood and Carpet

Heat flux q = dT / R. Covering R is the last, and most changeable, layer.

  • Tile: very low R. Carpet and pad: high R and an insulator over radiant heat.
  • Our example: 25 Btu/h per ft2, 80 F surface, R 0.9 below the covering.
  • Tile (R 0.1): dT 25 F, water about 105 F. Carpet and pad (R 2.0): dT 72.5 F, water about 152.5 F.
  • Same 105 F water under carpet and pad gives about 8.6 Btu/h per ft2, one third of the need.
  • Wood: surface about 80 F or less, check the maker; heating dries and shrinks boards.
  • Fixes: lower the covering R, add a low-temperature emitter, or raise water only within the limits.
