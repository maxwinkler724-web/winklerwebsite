Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Furnaces and Air Handlers: Dip Switches, CFM per Ton, Dehumidify Mode, Ramp Profiles and Checking the Result

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A constant-airflow blower is told a number: deliver this many cfm. The number comes from switches, jumpers or a menu on the control board, and a wrong choice does not make a noise or throw a code. The system just runs a little off. This lesson covers what those settings usually are, how to choose them, and how to prove the result instead of trusting the setting.

The settings, in plain terms

Names and layouts differ by maker, so the manual for the unit in front of you governs. The same handful of choices shows up again and again. For the motor itself, see ECM Blower Motors; for the old tap-and-capacitor way, see PSC Blower Motors.

  • Cooling airflow select. Switches or a menu pick the airflow that matches the outdoor unit's tonnage. One maker's variable-speed furnace guide prints a table on a 350 cfm per ton basis, listing 2 tons as 700 cfm, 3 tons as 1,050 and 4 tons as 1,400.
  • Adjust or trim. A plus or minus step that nudges the chosen airflow up or down by a percentage. Read the percentage in your unit's table; we did not find one that applies to every maker.
  • Dehumidify. A thermostat or humidistat input that lowers the airflow while cooling runs.
  • Delays and ramps. How long the blower runs after the compressor stops, and whether it eases up and down.
  • Continuous fan. A separate, usually lower airflow for fan-only operation.
  • Heating airflow. Often chosen so the temperature rise lands inside the range on the furnace rating plate.

Choosing cooling cfm per ton

An air handler installer's guide from one maker says each ton of cooling needs between 350 and 450 cfm, or 400 nominally, and shades table cells outside 300 to 450 cfm per ton as not recommended. That matches the usual 400 default. Lab work from the University of Central Florida's Florida Solar Energy Center (a 1997 paper by Parker and co-authors) tested a 2.44-ton unit at several airflows. Going from 425 to 300 cfm per ton cut sensible capacity about 15 percent and raised latent capacity about 7 percent. At about 350 cfm per ton the unit kept roughly 95 percent of its potential latent capacity for about a 10 percent loss of sensible capacity. Below 200 cfm per ton the losses became steep and the paper warned of coil icing. It recommended staying at or above 350 cfm per ton.

So the choice is a trade. Lower airflow moves capacity from sensible to latent, which suits humid climates and costs some efficiency; higher airflow does the reverse. Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load works that trade in detail. The settings are not a license to go lower than the maker allows: stay inside the airflow range printed for that coil and outdoor unit.

Dehumidify, delays and ramps

Makers handle dehumidify differently, and the percentages below are examples, not rules. One variable-speed furnace guide says that on a combined cooling and dehumidify call the blower runs at 85 percent of the desired speed, after you switch the enable setting on. A troubleshooting guide for another line says the blower drops to 86 percent of cooling airflow in dehumidify mode, and to 65 percent for up to 10 minutes per cooling cycle in a "super" mode. The same guide shortens the cooling off-delay from 90 seconds to 5 seconds when dehumidify is called, so the last of the moisture is not blown back into the house. Some of these functions also need a jumper, such as one tying the thermostat's cooling and reversing-valve terminals together on a cooling-only condenser, before the ramp or dehumidify feature works. A dehumidify setting that never takes effect is usually a missing enable switch or jumper, not a bad board.

Notice what dehumidify does to your math. It subtracts from whatever airflow you already chose, so a system set low for humid weather can fall under the maker's minimum once dehumidify kicks in. Add the two reductions together before you commit to them.

Heating airflow and the label

For heating, the target is a temperature rise inside the range on the furnace rating plate, not a cfm per ton figure. One variable-speed furnace guide says the temperature rise must stay within the nameplate range at all altitudes. A gas furnace guide for a blower-tap model says to adjust the tabs for the proper rise for each installation. Two furnace guides we read aim for the middle of the range or somewhat above it, one of them to help avoid condensation in the venting. A U.S. Department of Energy Building America study (the Partnership for Advanced Residential Retrofit) tested furnaces that had averaged 19 years old when removed, mostly in Chicago, and found efficiency rose 6.4 percent after they were brought to the maker's input rate, static pressure and temperature rise. Its recommendations include picking a fan speed that puts the rise in range.

Continuous fan is its own setting. On one line, tapping the thermostat fan switch off for one to three seconds steps the continuous-fan airflow up one increment, the setting is then stored, and board switches no longer change it. That is a good example of why you read the manual: a setting can live somewhere other than the switches.

Do not trust the setting: verify

Field studies suggest installers often miss. A Department of Energy Better Buildings residential presentation summarizes earlier work: average airflow about 14 percent below design in a 1997 study, measured airflow ranging from 130 to 510 cfm per ton in another 1997 study, and 70 percent of units under 350 cfm per ton in a 1999 study. A 2017 laboratory report for the California Energy Commission tested ten furnaces with brushless permanent magnet blower motors. Nine of ten met its airflow efficiency criteria at maximum speed, and the tenth became compliant after two sets of dip switches were changed. The point is not the pass rate: the switches decided the outcome.

A practical check has two legs.

  1. Total external static pressure. Measure return and supply static, add them, and compare with the maker's airflow table for the speed you set. The method is in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. One maker's table assumes a coil and no filter, so read the notes under your table. Another notes that without its filter you add 0.1 in. w.c. to external static pressure.
  2. Temperature rise. In heating, rise gives a cfm estimate you can compare with the setting. Measuring Airflow (CFM) compares this with a flow hood and other methods.

If the two disagree with the setting, look for restrictions, the wrong switch bank, a missed enable or a motor that has hit its limit, since a constant-airflow motor holds airflow only within what the motor and duct system allow.

Worked example (ours)

Assumptions, all ours: a 3-ton cooling system, a gas furnace with 64,000 Btu/h of output (an 80,000 Btu/h input at 80 percent, chosen only for easy arithmetic), and a rating plate range of 35 to 65 degrees F. The constant 1.08 is explained in the measuring-airflow lesson linked above.

  1. Cooling choices. 3 tons x 400 = 1,200 cfm. 3 x 350 = 1,050 cfm. 3 x 450 = 1,350 cfm.
  2. Dehumidify on the 400 setting. 1,200 x 0.86 = 1,032 cfm, or 1,032 / 3 = 344 cfm per ton. That is just under the 350 floor the Florida paper recommends, so on this system we would pick the 400 setting for humid weather, or check the maker's allowed range first.
  3. Heating check, setting at 1,200 cfm. Rise = 64,000 / (1.08 x 1,200) = 64,000 / 1,296 = 49.4 degrees F. That sits inside 35 to 65.
  4. Same furnace, 1,050 cfm. 64,000 / (1.08 x 1,050) = 64,000 / 1,134 = 56.4 degrees F. Still inside, higher in the range.
  5. Reverse the math. Suppose you measure a 62 degree F rise. cfm = 64,000 / (1.08 x 62) = 64,000 / 66.96 = about 956 cfm. On 3 tons that is 956 / 3 = about 319 cfm per ton, below 350. The label may be satisfied (62 is under 65) while cooling airflow is low. The setting, a restriction, or the wrong bank of switches are the first places to look.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving a switch bank at its factory default because it looks right.
  • Setting for tonnage, then forgetting trim, dehumidify and ramp stack on top of it.
  • Changing the setting after measuring the rise and not measuring again.
  • Assuming constant airflow means constant on any duct. It does not.

Field card: Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Blowers

Set it from the manual, then prove it with static and rise.

  • Tons x cfm per ton: 3 tons at 400 is 1,200 cfm; at 350 it is 1,050.
  • One maker guide: 350 to 450 per ton, 400 nominal, outside 300 to 450 not recommended.
  • Dehumidify subtracts from the chosen airflow (examples: 85 or 86 percent). Recheck the result.
  • Heating: land the rise inside the rating plate range.
  • Rise to cfm: cfm = Btu/h output / (1.08 x rise).
  • Verify with total external static pressure against the maker's table.
