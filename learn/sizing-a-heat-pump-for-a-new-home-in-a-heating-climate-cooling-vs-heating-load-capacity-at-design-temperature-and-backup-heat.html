Sizing a Heat Pump for a New Home in a Heating Climate

Last updated: 2026-11-02

In a cold-winter house the heating load is usually bigger than the cooling load, so the tonnage that fits the summer is often too small for January. This lesson walks through the choice a designer faces on a new home: size to cooling, size up for heating, or use a variable-capacity unit and plan backup heat for whatever is left over.

Start with two loads, not one

A new home gets two numbers from the national residential load method: a peak cooling load and a peak heating load. The heating figure is calculated at the local heating design temperature, a cold outdoor value chosen so the system is not sized for a once-in-a-lifetime night. If you need the background on picking that temperature, see Outdoor Design Temperatures for New HVAC Systems. The DOE sizing guide for cold-climate heat pumps says to use approved design temperatures and not to pile extra safety factors on the result.

The ratio between the two loads tells you which problem you have. In a mild climate the numbers are close. In a heating-dominated climate the heating load can be well above the cooling load. In our example below, 42,000 Btu/h of heating against 24,000 Btu/h of cooling is a ratio of 1.75 (42,000 / 24,000). That gap is the whole reason this lesson exists.

Option one: size to the cooling load

Picking the unit whose cooling capacity matches the cooling load keeps the unit small and the summer operation clean. The cost shows up in winter. A heat pump loses capacity as the outdoor air gets colder, while the house needs more heat. A unit sized to a small cooling load can end up delivering only a fraction of the design heating load at the design temperature, and the rest comes from backup heat. That works, but backup is usually electric resistance heat, and it uses far more electricity per Btu than the heat pump does. The more hours the backup runs, the less efficient the whole system becomes.

Option two: size up for heating

The other choice is a larger unit so the heat pump covers more of the winter load. The DOE sizing guide gives two targets for new or well-insulated homes: match the system capacity at the design temperature to 100 to 115 percent of the estimated heating load, or design for 75 to 85 percent of that load and add supplemental heat for the rest. The same guide says to read capacity from the maker's extended performance tables, not from the nameplate tonnage.

The DOE Building America sizing guideline is more specific about the trade-off. It suggests keeping cooling capacity within 15 percent of the total cooling load, but says a heat pump in a heating-dominated climate may exceed the heating load by as much as 25 percent. It also admits that when the next standard size is more than 15 percent over, the larger unit should be chosen so the peak load is met, and it points to multispeed equipment as an option. In other words, the national method itself accepts some summer oversizing in a cold climate. The question is how much, and what it does to humidity.

Reading capacity at the design temperature

Nominal tons describe the unit at mild test conditions. What you need is capacity at the heating design temperature, taken from the extended performance table. Tables often list 47 F, 17 F and sometimes 5 F, and your design temperature may fall between rows. The DOE document on calculating 5 F performance shows the standard approach for filling a gap: a straight-line interpolation or extrapolation between the 17 F and 47 F points, using the same formula for power input. Variable-speed units add a wrinkle, because the table is built from specific compressor-speed tests, so use the maker's own rows when they exist. Our lesson Reading Heat Pump Extended Performance Data covers the table in detail, and Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity explains why capacity falls with the outdoor temperature. For units built for cold weather, see Cold Climate Heat Pumps.

How variable capacity softens the trade-off

A single-speed unit has one output. If it is big enough for January, it is big for July, and it will cycle on and off in summer. A variable-capacity unit can run at a fraction of its maximum, so a larger heating-sized unit can still match a small cooling load by running slowly for long periods. The DOE guide says minimum capacity matters as much as maximum capacity. It also says that if the unit's minimum-speed cooling capacity exceeds 115 percent of the design cooling load, you should look for equipment with a higher heating-to-cooling capacity ratio, a wider turn-down range, or both.

Real data helps here, with a caution. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory field study of variable-capacity heat pumps in a research house in Knoxville, Tennessee, found that sizing for the heating load did not hurt summer dehumidification in the comfort mode: average indoor humidity was about 45 percent downstairs, against 46 percent for the baseline single-stage units. It also reported no measurable cooling efficiency penalty between a modestly oversized unit and an extremely oversized one. The same report says it was one unoccupied house in a mixed-humid climate, so treat it as encouraging evidence and not a rule for every home. For the broader comparison of staging options, see Single-Stage vs Two-Stage vs Variable Capacity.

Sizing backup heat for the gap

The backup gap at the design temperature is the heating load minus the heat pump's capacity at that same temperature. Convert Btu/h to kilowatts by dividing by 3,412, since one kilowatt of electric heat makes 3,412 Btu/h. The DOE Building America guideline says supplemental and emergency electric resistance heat cover the gap between heat pump capacity and the design heating load. For the strip-heater kit size, branch circuit and staging, see Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips, and for how the thermostat treats auxiliary and emergency heat, see Emergency Heat vs Auxiliary Heat and Lockouts. The gap is largest at the design temperature and shrinks to zero at the balance point, so the strips run for a small share of the season.

The humidity risk of oversizing for cooling

A cooling unit that is too big satisfies the thermostat quickly and shuts off before it has removed much moisture. The DOE cold-climate sizing guide lists "ineffective summer dehumidification" along with excessive cycling and low efficiency as results of oversizing, and tells designers to include latent loads from air leakage in the calculation. The Building America guideline adds that more airflow lowers latent capacity, so a lower airflow setting is preferred in humid climates. A short-cycling single-speed unit in a humid summer is the worst case; our lesson AC Short Cycling covers the symptoms. A variable unit that runs long, slow cycles reduces that risk, which is why it pairs well with a heating-sized selection.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example, not a published case. Assumptions: a new home with a 24,000 Btu/h cooling load and a 42,000 Btu/h heating load at a 10 F design temperature. We assume the heating load falls in a straight line to zero at 65 F outdoors (a simplification). We compare two selections: a 2-ton unit (24,000 Btu/h nominal cooling) that delivers 15,000 Btu/h at 10 F, and a 3-ton variable-capacity unit (36,000 Btu/h nominal cooling) that delivers 27,000 Btu/h at 10 F. For the chart, we assume both capacity lines rise by the same amount per degree, reaching 24,000 and 36,000 at 47 F. Real curves bend, so check the maker's table. We also assume the 3-ton unit's lowest cooling speed is 40 percent of nominal, or 14,400 Btu/h.

Step 1, share of the heating load met at 10 F. 2-ton: 15,000 / 42,000 = 35.7 percent. 3-ton: 27,000 / 42,000 = 64.3 percent.

Step 2, backup gap at 10 F. 2-ton: 42,000 - 15,000 = 27,000 Btu/h, and 27,000 / 3,412 = 7.91 kW. 3-ton: 42,000 - 27,000 = 15,000 Btu/h, and 15,000 / 3,412 = 4.40 kW. At an assumed 240 V, those are about 33 A and 18 A of resistance load (7,913 W / 240 V, and 4,396 W / 240 V). The 3-ton cuts the gap by 12,000 Btu/h, or 3.52 kW.

Step 3, check against the DOE targets. The 100 to 115 percent target would be 42,000 to 48,300 Btu/h, and the 75 to 85 percent target would be 31,500 to 35,700 Btu/h. Neither unit reaches either target at 10 F. The 3-ton comes closest, 4,500 Btu/h short of the low end of the second target, so it fits "undersized for heating with backup" better than the 2-ton does.

Step 4, balance points on our straight-line chart. Where the load line meets each capacity line: about 37 F for the 2-ton and about 25 F for the 3-ton. Below those temperatures the unit cannot carry the house alone. The 3-ton has the lower balance point, so backup heat runs in a narrower range of cold weather.

Step 5, what the 3-ton does to cooling. Nominal 36,000 against a 24,000 load is 150 percent (36,000 / 24,000), well past the 115 percent figure in the DOE guide, and the 15 percent figure in the Building America guideline. For a single-speed 3-ton that would be a humidity and cycling problem. For the variable unit, the assumed lowest speed of 14,400 Btu/h is 60 percent of the cooling load (14,400 / 24,000), so it can run steadily instead of cycling. Its heating-to-cooling ratio is 27,000 / 36,000 = 0.75, against 15,000 / 24,000 = 0.625 for the 2-ton, and the DOE guide names a higher heating-to-cooling ratio as one way to handle cooling oversizing. The result depends on the real minimum speed and on airflow settings, so confirm both with the maker's data and a commissioning check.

What to take from the example

The 2-ton matches the summer load but leaves a 7.91 kW backup gap at design. The 3-ton cuts that to 4.40 kW and lowers the balance point from about 37 F to about 25 F, in exchange for 50 percent of nominal oversizing in cooling that only a variable unit with a low minimum speed can handle well. The better choice depends on the real extended data, the minimum cooling speed, the duct system and the price of electricity. In every case the load figures have to come first, with the backup gap checked at the design temperature and not at the mild 47 F rating point.

Field card: Sizing a Heat Pump for a New Home in a Heating Climate

Heating load minus capacity at design temperature equals the backup gap.

  • Get both loads from the national residential load method before picking tons.
  • Read capacity at the heating design temperature from extended data, not nominal tons.
  • DOE guide targets: 100 to 115 percent of heating load, or 75 to 85 percent plus backup.
  • Backup kW = gap in Btu/h divided by 3,412.
  • Cooling oversizing is a humidity and cycling risk; a variable unit with a low minimum speed softens it.
  • Confirm the minimum cooling speed against the cooling load.
