Uneven Heat in a One-Pipe Steam Home: Radiator Vents, Balancing by Distance and Radiator Pitch

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In many one-pipe steam homes the radiator nearest the boiler is hot in minutes while the one at the far end of the house stays cold for half an hour. The cause is usually air, not a weak boiler. This lesson explains how a radiator vent works, why far radiators heat last, and what a homeowner can reasonably check before calling a pro.

How a radiator vent lets air out and closes on steam

Every pipe and radiator in a one-pipe system starts the cycle full of air. Steam cannot enter a space that is still full of air, so the air has to leave first. A radiator vent is a small valve that is normally open. A maker's steam heating manual says these vents are open until steam reaches them and then close off, and a maker's one-pipe training guide says they close when exposed to steam temperature. Once the vent shuts, steam stays in the radiator and gives up its heat. When the radiator cools, the vent opens again and lets air back in, as the Department of Energy's balancing guide describes.

Radiator vents are different from main vents. The main vent sits near the end of the steam main and moves a large volume of air quickly so steam reaches all the radiators at about the same time. The radiator vent only handles the air inside one radiator and its short pipe. See Steam Main Venting Basics for the main side, and One-Pipe vs. Two-Pipe Steam Systems if you are not sure which kind you have.

Why far radiators heat last

Steam follows the air out. The radiators nearest the boiler get steam first and start venting first. The far ones wait for steam to travel down the main and up the runout, and then they have their own air to push out. One maker's manual says that in larger one-pipe systems the radiators at the far end are the last to vent and heat. A one-pipe training guide adds that if the main vent fails, the closest radiators heat first and the farthest may not heat at all.

Balancing: mains fast, radiators slow to fast by distance

The balancing idea has two parts. First, vent the mains fast. A trade guide on one-pipe balancing says to vent the mains quickly so steam reaches every radiator at about the same time, and to use a vent with a three-quarter-inch tapping because tiny holes do not pass air quickly. Second, slow down the radiators that would otherwise win the race. A maker's manual says the venting rate of the first radiators can be set lower than that of the far-end radiators, and that adjustable vents suit systems where circuit lengths differ. A one-pipe guide from another maker says to use adjustable vents when you want proportional venting, with larger radiators getting larger openings.

Two cautions. Several sources say a vent should also suit the size of the radiator, because a big radiator holds more air; a trade guide notes that an oversized radiator may need a second vent a few inches lower. And going too fast has a price: the one-pipe guide lists radiator vents that are too fast among the causes of water hammer. In our own words, aim for even heating, not the fastest possible venting.

Adjustable vents let you change the opening with a setting instead of swapping parts. The Department of Energy guide notes they can cost more and can be tampered with in shared buildings.

Worked example: two radiators, one vent size, then two

This example uses published air volumes and published vent rates, but the house is made up. All the numbers below that describe the house are assumptions. The method is the one the trade guide uses: air volume divided by vent rate gives venting time.

Published inputs. A trade publisher's balancing guide lists cast-iron tube radiators of about 1930 at 0.013 cubic feet of air per square foot of radiator rating, and 1-1/2 inch pipe at 0.014 cubic feet per foot. The same guide reproduces a maker's vent chart at 2 ounces of pressure. We use three of its rates and call them small (0.130 cfm), medium (0.235 cfm) and large (0.540 cfm).

Assumptions (ours). Near radiator: 30 square feet of rating with 5 feet of 1-1/2 inch runout. Far radiator: 60 square feet with 25 feet of runout. Steam reaches the near radiator 0.5 minutes after the burner fires and the far one at 2.0 minutes. Vents see 2 ounces of pressure the whole time. Real pressure starts lower, so real times are longer.

Air volume.

  • Near: 30 x 0.013 = 0.39 cu ft in the radiator, plus 5 x 0.014 = 0.07 in the runout, so 0.46 cu ft.
  • Far: 60 x 0.013 = 0.78, plus 25 x 0.014 = 0.35, so 1.13 cu ft.

Case A, both radiators get the medium vent (0.235 cfm). Near: 0.46 / 0.235 = 1.96 minutes, finished at 0.5 + 1.96 = about 2.5 minutes. Far: 1.13 / 0.235 = 4.81 minutes, finished at 2.0 + 4.81 = about 6.8 minutes. The far radiator is hot 4.3 minutes after the near one.

Case B, near gets the small vent (0.130), far gets the large vent (0.540). Near: 0.46 / 0.130 = 3.54 minutes, finished at about 4.0 minutes. Far: 1.13 / 0.540 = 2.09 minutes, finished at about 4.1 minutes. The two finish within a few seconds of each other in this made-up house.

The lesson: matching finish times matters more than matching vent sizes.

Signs of a bad vent

  • Cold radiator, or only warm at the bottom. The Department of Energy guide says blocked or non-functioning vents can leave a space too cold, and that a painted-over vent is a common failure. A vent that is not passing air should be cleaned or replaced.
  • Spitting water. A balancing article from a national laboratory says vents can even squirt water if the boiler is making wet steam, and the same page ties squirting vents to wet steam. A single spitting vent may just be worn out, but if several spit, think about the boiler, not the vents.
  • Hissing the whole cycle. Our own reading: a vent should go quiet once steam arrives, because makers say it closes then. If a vent keeps hissing while the radiator is hot, it is probably not closing and is wasting steam.

Never plug or cap a vent to stop noise, and let the radiator cool before you touch a vent, since steam and the vent body are hot. A homeowner trade guide says to work on vents only when the steam is off.

Radiator pitch and the supply valve

In a one-pipe radiator the condensate leaves through the same valve the steam enters. A maker's manual says one-pipe radiators must be pitched to the riser so condensate can drain, and a homeowner trade guide says to tilt a radiator toward its supply valve by raising the end opposite the valve with shims under the feet. The sources we read gave no angle, so we do not give one; a small, steady tilt is the idea, and a level or backward radiator can trap water.

The supply valve has one rule on a one-pipe radiator. A maker's manual says it must be wide open or closed, and the Department of Energy guide says the same: partly closing it blocks condensate and keeps steam out. See Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.

When to call a pro

  • Pressure too high. Sources put one-pipe operation in a low range: the Department of Energy guide says typically one-half to one and a half psig, and a trade guide says residential pressure is often far too high. If your gauge reads well above that, or the safety valve ever opens, shut the system down and call a pro. See Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping and Steam Boiler Short-Cycling Causes.
  • Water hammer. A trade guide warns that changing pipe size or pitch changes how steam and condensate meet. Do not reroute or resize steam piping yourself.
  • Boiler water line. If the water in the gauge glass is missing, will not come back, or only trickles back after flushing, a trade guide says to call a pro. A maker's guide notes that the level may drop out of the glass briefly on shutdown, which is normal, so watch whether it returns.
  • Several cold radiators, or changing vents, valves or piping you are unsure about. The main vent, wet steam and piping pitch are professional diagnoses.

Field card: One-Pipe Radiator Vents and Uneven Heat

Air out first, then steam; far radiators wait longest.

  • A radiator vent is open until steam reaches it, then it closes.
  • Vent the mains fast; slow the near radiators, or speed the far ones, so all finish together.
  • Adjustable vents help when circuit lengths differ. Change one at a time.
  • Bad vent clues: cold radiator, spitting water, hissing all cycle.
  • Tilt the radiator toward its supply valve. Valve fully open or fully closed, never half.
  • Our example: 0.46 cu ft near, 1.13 cu ft far; air volume divided by vent rate is venting time (assumptions labeled).
  • Call a pro for high pressure, water hammer, or a boiler water line that will not hold.
