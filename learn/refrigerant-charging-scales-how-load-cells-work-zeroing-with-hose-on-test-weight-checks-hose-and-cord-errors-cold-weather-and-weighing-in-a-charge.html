Refrigerant Charging Scales: Load Cells, Zeroing With the Hose On, Test Weight Checks and Weighing In a Charge

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When you charge by weight, the scale is the gauge. If it reads wrong by a couple of ounces, the system is charged wrong by a couple of ounces, and nothing else in the job will tell you. This lesson covers how the scale senses weight, how to zero it, how to check it, what throws it off, and how to turn nameplate numbers into a weighed-in charge.

Read the maker's manual. Scales differ in buttons, units and battery notes. This page is general education. Your scale's manual and the equipment installation manual win.

How a digital scale senses weight

Most digital charging scales are built around load cells. A load cell is a small metal block or beam that bends a tiny amount when weight is on it. Thin strain gages are bonded to it. When the metal is stretched or squeezed, a gage's electrical resistance changes slightly. A university lecture handout on strain gages explains that the resistance change is very small, so the gages are wired in a bridge circuit that turns it into a voltage you can measure, and that the output follows the strain only while the metal stays in its elastic range. The scale's electronics read that voltage and show it as pounds and ounces or kilograms.

Two practical points follow. First, the load cell measures force pushing on it, and any force counts, whether it is cylinder weight or a hose pulling sideways. Second, the elastic limit is real. Do not drop the cylinder onto the platform or stack more than the scale's rated capacity, because an overloaded load cell can stay bent and read wrong afterward.

Zeroing and taring with the cylinder and hose on

For weighing in a charge you care about the change in weight, not the absolute weight. So the order is simple.

  1. Set the scale on a solid, level surface, away from drafts and vibration.
  2. Stand the cylinder on the platform with the charging hose connected and arranged the way it will stay during the job.
  3. Open nothing yet. Let the reading settle, then press the zero or tare key so the display reads zero with everything attached.
  4. Open the cylinder and charge. The display now counts down (or shows a negative number) by exactly the amount that left the cylinder.

Taring with the hose on removes the hose and fittings from the arithmetic. If you move the hose after taring, you have changed the zero, so tare again or move it back. NIST Handbook 44 uses the same idea in its tolerance tables for weighing systems: they assume the device is adjusted to zero at no load, and the tolerance is measured from that zero.

Checking accuracy with known weights

A scale is only as good as its last check. A simple check uses known test weights placed on the platform: tare, add a known weight, and compare the display. Do it at a light load and again near the amounts you actually charge. The reading should land within what the scale maker states.

Two federal weights-and-measures ideas help here. NIST Handbook 44 sets tolerances for commercial weighing devices, and the part we read treats acceptance and maintenance tolerances as separate columns, which in that table are the same value for the device class it covers. NIST Handbook 105-1 covers field standard weights, the test weights inspectors carry. It states a one-third rule: a standard's combined error and uncertainty must be less than one-third of the tolerance of the device being tested. For us the lesson is plain. Your test weight should be much better than the error you are trying to find. A weight of unknown accuracy, such as a bag of tools or a full water bottle, cannot prove a scale is within an ounce. Use a weight with a stated mass, store it clean, and keep it in its case.

Charging scales are not legal-for-trade devices, so these handbooks are not a rule for your scale. They are a good model for how to think about error and checking. If a check fails, stop using the scale for critical charges and follow the maker's instructions for adjustment or repair.

Why a swinging hose or a cord throws the reading

The platform feels everything touching the cylinder. A hose that hangs from the valve to a manifold sitting on the ground may carry part of its own weight on the cylinder, and part of that load shifts as you move. A hose pulled tight can lift or push sideways on the valve. A power cord or a bag resting on the platform edge adds its own force. The display will drift or jump by an amount that has nothing to do with refrigerant.

The fix is mechanical. Give the hose slack, and support its weight from a hook or the unit so the loop hangs free. Keep cords off the platform and leave them loose. Keep your hand, knee and tool bag off the cylinder and platform while you read. Do not read the scale while the hose is swinging in the wind or while you move the manifold. Wait for a steady number.

Cold weather, drift and batteries

Scales have a rated operating temperature range, and outdoor winter work can fall outside it. We found one number worth knowing: in the weighing-system code of NIST Handbook 44, if a device has no stated range, the default is minus 10 to 40 degrees C (14 to 104 degrees F). That is the code for a different class of device, not for charging scales, so read your own scale's manual for its range. Watch for slow drift after the scale comes out of a warm van into the cold, and for a display that fades or a low-battery warning. Let the scale sit at working temperature, tare after it settles, and keep fresh batteries on hand. If the reading creeps while nothing is moving, do not trust it. Re-tare and re-check with a test weight.

Programmable and solenoid-controlled scales

Some scales let you enter a target charge and then open and close a solenoid valve in the hose line by themselves. The idea is the same: the load cell measures what left the cylinder and the electronics stop the flow near the target. Treat the shutoff as a convenience. The same rules apply. Tare with the hose on, support the hose, keep the cord clear, and confirm the final amount on the display. Some units also store entries for later. Follow the maker's manual for how.

Weighing in a charge: factory charge plus line-set adder

The nameplate charge is for the outdoor unit plus a stated line length, often 15 feet on residential split systems. Longer line sets need extra refrigerant, and the installation manual gives the adder in ounces per foot. Our lesson Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments covers where to find it. The scale's job is to measure the added amount.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example, with assumed inputs. The nameplate says 6 lb 4 oz for 15 ft of line. The installed line set is 35 ft. We assume an adder of 0.6 oz per foot. Your manual's value will differ, so never use this one on a real job.

  1. Nameplate charge in ounces: 6 lb x 16 oz + 4 oz = 100 oz.
  2. Extra length: 35 ft - 15 ft = 20 ft.
  3. Added charge: 20 ft x 0.6 oz/ft = 12 oz.
  4. Total charge: 100 oz + 12 oz = 112 oz = 7 lb 0 oz.

On the scale: tare the cylinder with the hose on, open it, and stop when the display shows 12 oz gone, which is 0 lb 12 oz.

Now suppose the scale is off by 2 oz. The error is 2 oz out of 12 oz, which is 2 / 12 = 0.167, or about 17 percent of the added charge. Against the total, 2 / 112 = 0.018, about 1.8 percent. The total looks small, but a 17 percent error in the added charge is large when the adder is the part you are measuring, and the system has no way to tell you that the scale was wrong. This is why hose support and a test weight check matter more than they first seem to.

Recording amounts

EPA Section 608 requires records of the amount of refrigerant added for some equipment. Under 40 CFR 82.166 as we read it on the eCFR, when a servicer adds refrigerant to an appliance normally containing 50 or more pounds, the servicer gives the owner or operator an invoice or other documentation that shows the amount added. The owner or operator must keep servicing records with the date, type of service and quantity of refrigerant added, and records must be kept for a minimum of three years unless otherwise indicated. The EPA recordkeeping page we read says the same. Even where the rule does not require it, we recommend writing the date, the refrigerant, the starting and ending cylinder weights and the amount added on the work order. See 608 Records: What Technicians Keep. Recovery work and cylinder handling are in Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step. Thresholds and rules can change, so confirm the current regulation.

Field card: Refrigerant charging scales

Tare with the hose on, hold it slack, check with a known weight.

  • A load cell bends slightly under weight; strain gages in a bridge turn that into a reading.
  • Tare with the cylinder, hose and fittings in their working positions.
  • Support the hose from a hook; keep cords and tools off the platform.
  • Check with a test weight whose accuracy is well better than the error you care about.
  • Let the scale reach working temperature; carry fresh batteries.
  • Our own example: 100 oz nameplate + 20 ft x 0.6 oz/ft = 112 oz total; a 2 oz error is about 17 percent of the 12 oz added.
  • Write down the amount added; the owner of a 50 lb or larger appliance needs it on the invoice.
