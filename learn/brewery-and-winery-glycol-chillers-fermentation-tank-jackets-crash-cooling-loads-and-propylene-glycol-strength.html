Brewery and Winery Glycol Chillers: Tank Jackets, Crash Cooling Loads and Glycol Strength

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fermenting tank gives off heat, and a finished batch has to be chilled hard before it is packaged. Small breweries and wineries handle both jobs with a packaged glycol chiller, a pump, and a jacket on each tank. This lesson explains how that loop is built, how much cooling a batch needs, and why the glycol is propylene glycol at a chosen strength.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. The pages we read come from chiller makers, glycol makers and wine and brewing references, and they do not always agree. Use this page to learn the ideas, then follow the documents for the equipment and fluid you actually have. Local rules for food plants apply too.

Why a brewery uses a secondary loop

A technical chapter on brewery refrigeration describes two ways to cool a tank. In direct expansion, the beer or juice trades heat with evaporating refrigerant at the evaporator. In a secondary loop, a glycol solution trades heat with the refrigerant, and the pump then sends that glycol around the building to the tanks. The same chapter says smaller breweries typically use standardized packaged water and glycol chillers, while larger ones typically use customized direct expansion systems. It also says refrigeration can take as much as 50 to 70 percent of a brewery's electricity use.

The parts of the loop

One chiller maker's page describes its units as designed to provide 20 F to 30 F glycol for fermentation tanks, brite tanks and wort coolers, and rates them at 25 F leaving fluid. It says that without glycol the lowest recommended operating temperature is 48 F, because plain water would freeze. The same page says some of its controllers handle 2 to 8 tanks. Another glycol source puts a typical brewery reservoir setpoint at 26 F to 28 F.

  • Chiller. A packaged refrigeration circuit cools the glycol. Compare its upkeep to any air-cooled chiller in the maintenance lesson linked below.
  • Reservoir. The tank that holds the glycol mix. It is the loop's thermal buffer and where you take a sample for testing.
  • Pump. It moves glycol through the supply header.
  • Jackets. A wine-equipment supplier says tank makers recommend a jacket covering about 30 percent of the tank's surface, and that an internal plate or coil needs about 72 percent of the area an external jacket would. A chiller maker's article describes coolant channels wrapped on the vessel.
  • Valves and sensors. The same article says temperature can be held by manual shut-off valves or by automatic control. Our own description of the usual automatic arrangement: a sensor on each tank, a controller, and a solenoid valve on each tank's jacket, so one tank can call for cooling without chilling the others. Our own reasoning on faults: a valve stuck open overcools a tank, and a valve stuck shut or a sensor reading too low lets a tank warm up.

How much heat there is

A chiller maker's sizing card gives rule-of-thumb loads. During active fermentation it uses 60 Btu per hour per barrel. For a 10 barrel tank that is 600 Btu per hour (10 x 60, from the card). For crash cooling from 75 F to 35 F, the card lists 4,290 Btu per hour for 10 barrels over 24 hours and 5,720 over 18 hours. The card says its factors are an approximation and tells you to consult the factory. Fermentation is a small steady load. Crash cooling is the big, short one, so the chiller and pump are sized around it.

For wine, a winemaking reference says a must of about 21.8 percent sugar (Brix) could theoretically raise its temperature by 28.2 C, with up to 20 percent of that heat lost to CO2 and the tank walls. It estimates that 1 kW of refrigeration can control about 20,000 liters of white wine fermenting for 28 days, or about 7,000 liters of red wine fermenting for 10 days. An energy-efficiency checklist from New Zealand's EECA also suggests avoiding crash cooling in fermenters and using a steady rate such as 1 C per hour, so check a recipe or winemaker's plan before chilling a tank fast.

Worked example: crash cooling a 10 barrel batch

Assumptions (ours, not from a source): a 10 barrel batch, cooled from 68 F to 34 F in 24 hours, with the beer treated as water at 8.33 lb per gallon and a specific heat of 1 Btu per pound per degree F. Real beer has a slightly different density and specific heat, and the steel tank and room heat add load, so treat the answer as a minimum.

The federal definition is that a beer barrel is 31 U.S. gallons (27 CFR 25.11, on the Cornell law site).

  1. Volume: 10 barrels x 31 gal = 310 gal.
  2. Weight: 310 gal x 8.33 lb per gal = 2,582 lb.
  3. Temperature drop: 68 F minus 34 F = 34 F.
  4. Heat to remove: 2,582 lb x 1 Btu per lb per F x 34 F = about 87,800 Btu.
  5. Over 24 hours: 87,800 / 24 = about 3,660 Btu per hour.
  6. In tons, using 12,000 Btu per hour per ton as the usual definition: 3,660 / 12,000 = about 0.30 ton.
  7. Over 18 hours instead: 87,800 / 18 = about 4,880 Btu per hour, or 0.41 ton.

Sense check. The maker's card figure of 4,290 Btu per hour for 10 barrels over 24 hours is for a 40 F drop. Our same method with 40 F gives 310 x 8.33 x 40 / 24, about 4,300, so the card appears to use the same basic arithmetic. If two such tanks crash at once, the load doubles to about 7,300 Btu per hour (0.61 ton). A chiller's rated capacity applies at its rating conditions, such as 25 F leaving fluid, so check the maker's capacity table at your glycol temperature.

Why propylene glycol, and what strength

Dow, a glycol maker, says its propylene glycol fluids and their corrosion inhibitor are FDA GRAS as food additives, and that this makes them attractive if a leak or spill could occur. Dow's fermentation cooling note recommends a 30 to 45 percent solution. The supplier guide says ethylene glycol (automotive antifreeze) is toxic and should never be used in a brewery loop or used to top up a propylene loop.

Strength sets the freeze point. A glycol supplier's brewery guide lists approximate freeze points of 30 percent at 8 F, 35 percent at 2 F, 40 percent at minus 6 F and 50 percent at minus 27 F. It suggests keeping the freeze point 10 F to 15 F below the coldest setpoint. The chiller maker's card says to protect against the lowest ambient or 20 F below the setpoint, and says most breweries run 25 F to 30 F fluid with about 40 percent inhibited propylene glycol. Our own check for a 28 F setpoint: 20 F below is 8 F, which a 30 percent mix just meets, while 40 percent gives a wider margin. The supplier guide warns that mixes above about 40 percent carry less heat and pump harder, which can slow crash cooling. Dow's table for its inhibited fluid separates freeze protection from burst protection: at 0 F, 36 percent gives freeze protection while 24 percent gives burst protection, and Dow says to choose a temperature at least 5 F below the expected low. In a tank jacket you want freeze protection, because slush in a jacket still blocks flow. Our glycol lesson covers that difference in more detail.

Inhibited or plain, and what to test

The supplier guide says USP grade propylene glycol has no additives and inhibited grade has a corrosion package for steel, copper and brass, that the two should not be mixed, and that you should flush before changing. It advises plain USP where a leak could reach beer, and inhibited only in fully isolated loops. Dow's fermentation note, by contrast, promotes its inhibited fluid for fermentation cooling. Because the two differ, follow the fluid maker's written guidance and local food-safety rules. The supplier names deionized or distilled water for mixing.

The supplier guide suggests an annual check of about 15 minutes: read strength with a glycol refractometer or hydrometer, check that pH is neutral to slightly alkaline, and look for cloudiness, stringy growth or a sour smell, which means drain, flush and refill. The EECA checklist likewise says to check glycol condition by density and to check for glycol leaks. For test technique, see the glycol testing lesson linked below.

Common faults to look for

  • Tanks will not reach temperature. Our own checklist: glycol too weak or too strong, low reservoir level, a closed or stuck valve, a sensor out of place, a dirty condenser coil, or a chiller sized for fewer tanks than are running.
  • Glycol turning cloudy or sour. A sign the fluid is breaking down; see the test steps above.
  • Glycol showing up in beer or wine. A leaking jacket or plate means stop and call the maker. Our own note: this is a food safety event, not only a repair.

Field card: Brewery and Winery Glycol Chillers: Tank Jackets, Crash Cooling Loads and Glycol Strength

Small steady load while fermenting, big short load when crash cooling.

  • Loop: chiller, reservoir, pump, supply and return headers, jacket and solenoid valve and sensor per tank.
  • Supply glycol near 25 F to 30 F, per the makers we read.
  • Rule of thumb from one maker: 60 Btu per hour per barrel while fermenting.
  • Crash cooling heat = pounds x specific heat x temperature drop. A barrel is 31 gal.
  • 10 barrels, 68 F to 34 F in 24 h: about 87,800 Btu, 3,660 Btu per hour, 0.30 ton (assumptions ours).
  • Propylene glycol only. Keep freeze point 10 F to 20 F under the setpoint.
  • Test strength, pH and appearance about yearly; do not mix inhibited with plain.
