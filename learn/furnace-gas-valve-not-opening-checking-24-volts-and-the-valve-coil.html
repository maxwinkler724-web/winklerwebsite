Furnace Gas Valve Not Opening: Checking 24 Volts and the Valve Coil

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The inducer ran, the pressure switch proved draft, the igniter glowed, and then nothing lit. At this point the board has done its job up to the gas valve, and the question is simple: is the valve being told to open, and if it is, does it? A meter answers the first half in a minute. This lesson shows where to put the leads, what each result means, and why the answer to the second half is never a hammer.

Check the maker's sheet

Check the maker's sheet. Terminal names, knob positions and test steps differ by valve model. The figures here are examples from the documents named, not specifications for your furnace. This page does not teach valve replacement or gas piping work.

Start with the easy, no-meter checks

Before any meter, confirm the furnace is actually asking for gas. The sequence of operation lesson covers the order: the valve is the step after the igniter warms up. If the board is blinking a fault code, read it first; From LED Code to Part walks through that. If the igniter never glows, start with igniter testing instead, because most boards will not open the valve without a proven warm-up.

Then check the two manual gas controls. First, the manual shutoff valve on the pipe feeding the furnace. Second, the control on the gas valve itself. Honeywell's literature for its VR8205 valve says the knob has two positions, ON and OFF, and its "burner won't come on" checklist begins with confirming the knob is ON. Emerson's sheet for the White-Rodgers 36J valves describes a two-position ON/OFF switch instead of a knob. A training-shop article on stuck valves makes the same point: the shutoff on the valve body must be in the ON or open position for the valve to work. Operating either control is for hand use only. Both makers say to use only your hand and never tools, and Emerson adds that force or attempted repair may result in a fire or explosion. If a Honeywell knob will not turn by hand, the sheet says a qualified service technician should replace the control.

Measuring 24 volts at the valve

A gas valve of this type is normally closed. A trade training article (see Sources) describes it as a valve that opens when 24 volts is applied to its coil, so the first electrical question is whether 24 volts arrives. Honeywell's checklist for the VR8205 says to raise the thermostat above room temperature, then measure AC voltage across the MV terminals at the gas control. The White-Rodgers 36J sheet lists the coil at 24 VAC, and other labels such as MV, MV1, MV2 and C (common) appear on its wiring diagrams, so find the labels on your valve and its diagram.

Timing matters. The valve is energized after the inducer, the pressure switch and the igniter warm-up, so a reading taken at the call for heat will be zero on a healthy furnace. Set the meter to AC volts, place the leads across the valve's two control terminals, and watch through the whole sequence. Look for 24 volts to appear when the igniter finishes warming, and to drop away when the board shuts the valve. Use the right range and keep your hands off bare terminals; Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings covers meter habits, and Relays, Transformers and 24V Control explains where the 24 volts comes from.

What the result means

  • No voltage at the valve. The problem is upstream of the coil. Honeywell's checklist says to check the control circuit. That means the board output, a loose connector, a broken wire or a missing common. The valve is not at fault yet.
  • Correct voltage, burners still dark. Honeywell's sheet says that when proper control voltage is present at the MV terminals and the knob is ON, replace the gas control. Before you condemn it, check the gas side. The same article says to compare inlet pressure to the data tag, and that normal inlet pressure with no outlet pressure points to a stuck valve. Low or zero inlet pressure points at the supply, a closed shutoff or the piping, not the valve. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer.

Coil resistance: only as the maker publishes it

Some techs also unplug the valve and read resistance across the coil with the power off. The two maker sheets we read (Honeywell VR8205/VR4205 and the White-Rodgers 36J installation instructions) give no coil resistance number, and neither lists resistance as a test. So there is no value to quote here. If your valve's own service sheet publishes a range, compare to that. A training-shop article says only that an OL (open) reading on the coil means the valve is bad. Even so, the voltage test at the terminals is what the Honeywell checklist is built on. Also, Honeywell warns never to put a jumper across the valve coil terminals, even for a moment.

Worked example: three readings, three stories

The furnace has a 24 VAC valve. You clip the meter on and watch during the igniter warm-up, taking three readings: (1) the board's valve output to common, (2) the valve terminals, and (3) between the valve's common terminal and the transformer common (C), which should read near zero. Numbers are made up for teaching.

  1. Case A. Board 24.6 V, valve 24.4 V, common side 0.1 V. The voltage drop along the wire is 24.6 − 24.4 = 0.2 V, tiny, so the circuit is fine and the coil is energized. The common path is clean (0.1 V). Burners dark means checking inlet pressure next, then the valve.
  2. Case B. Board 24.6 V, valve 0 V. The board is sending voltage but it does not arrive: 24.6 − 0 = 24.6 V is lost between them. Suspect the wire, plug or connector. The valve is not to blame.
  3. Case C. Board 0 V, valve 0 V, taken after the igniter warm-up should have ended. The board never commands the valve. Go back to the fault code, flame, limit and pressure switch circuits. The valve is not the problem.
  4. Case D. Board 24.6 V, valve 24.4 V, but the common reading is 12 V instead of near zero. That is a broken or loose common path, so fix the wiring before judging the valve.

As a load check, White-Rodgers lists 0.28 amps for a single-stage 36J and 0.43 amps for the two-stage version. The coil's draw in volt-amps is 24 × 0.28 = 6.72 VA, and 24 × 0.43 = 10.32 VA. Honeywell's VR8205 lists 0.5 amps, so 24 × 0.5 = 12 VA. Add these to the other 24 volt loads when you look at transformer size.

Why techs do not tap or force a valve

Some articles describe tapping a stuck valve with a tool to free it. The same article says a valve that opens that way is still bad and must be replaced. A valve that sticks is a gas safety part failing, not a part to coax along. Emerson and Honeywell both say hands only, never tools.

Replacement belongs to a qualified tech

The Honeywell installation sheet says the installer must be a trained, experienced service technician, and the training-shop article says gas valve replacement may be illegal to do yourself in many areas. The Honeywell VR8205 sheet says the control has no replaceable parts and should not be taken apart. After any gas valve work, Emerson and Honeywell instruct a leak check with soap solution (bubbles mean a leak) on the connections, with the burner running, and Honeywell adds a safety shutdown test with the gas off. Check outlet pressure against the rating plate too. If you smell gas at any point, leave and follow what to do if you smell gas.

Field card: Furnace Gas Valve Not Opening

Knob, volts, then pressure.

  • Confirm the pipe shutoff and the valve's own ON control, by hand only, never with tools.
  • Measure AC volts across the valve terminals during the igniter warm-up, not before.
  • No voltage: the problem is upstream (board, wire, plug, common). Voltage present: check inlet pressure, then the valve.
  • Coil resistance: only against a number the maker publishes. An open reading is a bad coil.
  • Never jumper a coil, tap a valve or force a control.
  • Replacement and leak checks are for a qualified tech.
