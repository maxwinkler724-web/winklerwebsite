Warehouse Air Turnover Units: Air Rotation Heating and Cooling for Big Boxes, Floor-to-Ceiling Mixing, Throw and vs Rooftop Units

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a tall warehouse heat rises and the thermostat sits near the floor. An air turnover unit fights that with air movement plus heat: it sits on the floor, pulls in the coolest air, conditions it, and throws it back up so the whole volume rolls over. This lesson covers how it works, how it compares with other options, and what to check around sprinklers and racks.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. We name NFPA 13 (sprinklers) and ASHRAE 62.1 (ventilation) in plain text only, since the standards are copyrighted. The maker's manual, the fire protection designer and the local inspector decide each job.

How an air turnover unit works

An air turnover unit is a floor-mounted cabinet with a fan, a filter, a heat source and a tall discharge. A trade press listing describes the pattern: room air is drawn in at the bottom, heated, and sent out the top so it spreads across the ceiling and then drops into the occupied zone, a continuous loop. The same listing says supply air stays within 20 F of space temperature and gives fans up to 60,000 cfm per unit. A maker's product page says to turn the room air over 1 to 3 times an hour for heating and 3.5 to 5 times an hour for cooling the comfort zone. Those are maker claims; we found no independent lab or utility test data, so size from the engineer's drawings.

The key words are floor-level return (the coolest air goes in), vertical throw (the jet reaches the roof zone) and rotation (the air circles back down). That maker page says a taller discharge raises the top of the comfort zone, and the burner and controls sit at floor level where a tech can reach them.

Why a tall building needs it

Facilities Net says the temperature rise is typically about 0.5 F per foot of height, and estimates 15 to 20 percent annual energy savings from reducing stratification. A U.S. Department of Energy field demonstration in a warehouse with 24 foot ceilings reports roof air 10 to 20 F above the setpoint, so heaters run longer to warm the floor. Oregon State researchers add that warmer air at the top loses energy through the roof.

For a 32 foot building, 0.5 F per foot is 32 x 0.5 = 16 F. In summer cool air settles low and the top stays hot.

Direct-fired or indirect-fired heat

Direct-fired means the burner flame is in the air stream, so there is no heat exchanger or flue to the outside. The ASHRAE Journal article on direct-fired heating says such units are often rated at 92 percent on a fuel basis that excludes the water vapor in the flue gas and are used for space heating in warehouses. Indirect-fired units use a heat exchanger and a flue instead. Our make-up air lesson covers the difference and the controls.

Outdoor air and combustion byproducts

  • That article lists ANSI limits of 5 ppm carbon monoxide in the airstream and 25 ppm in the room, and 0.50 ppm and 3 ppm nitrogen dioxide.
  • It says 100 percent outdoor air units cycle on and off, while recirculating units bring in outdoor air continuously and modulate the burner.
  • For warehouses it gives a ventilation rate of 0.06 cfm per square foot from ASHRAE 62.1-2013.

Cooling

The maker page says versions exist for heating only, heating and cooling, and cooling only. We could not confirm from independent sources how the cooling is produced, so read the unit's tag and piping. For temporary options see portable spot coolers and evaporative coolers.

Compared with the other ways to heat a big box

  • Rooftop units. The PNNL support document for the small warehouse design guide models packaged rooftop units with direct expansion cooling and gas furnaces for the office and fine storage, with ductwork. See rooftop unit basics. A turnover unit skips the duct system but takes floor space.
  • Unit heaters. The same PNNL document heats a bulky storage area with unit heaters held at 45 F. DOE says unit heaters account for nearly 18 percent of commercial space heating energy, and a maker's blog says they can contribute to significant stratification. See the hot-water and steam unit heater lesson.
  • Infrared tubes. They warm objects, not air. See infrared tube heaters.
  • HVLS and axial fans. They only move air; they add no heat. Facilities Net lists axial fans for ceilings up to 60 feet and large slow fans for 15 to 30 feet. See HVLS fans and destratification.

Sprinklers and racks

PHCP Pros summarizes NFPA 13: sprinklers within 7 feet of a unit heater need a high-temperature element, and the deflector must sit at least 36 inches above the top of stored goods. For large fans it says to center them between four sprinklers and keep them at least 3 feet below the deflector. The National Fire Sprinkler Association adds that HVLS fans shut down on a waterflow alarm. Those rules cover heaters and fans. Whether a floor-mounted turnover unit's discharge and shutdown fall under them is a question for the sprinkler designer and the authority having jurisdiction; we found no source that settles it.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are our assumptions, not a real building: 200,000 sq ft, 32 ft clear height, 2 air changes per hour (inside the maker's 1 to 3 for heating), a 10 F rise from mixed room air to discharge, standard air, and a roof U-value of 0.05 Btu/h per sq ft per F.

  1. Volume: 200,000 x 32 = 6,400,000 cu ft.
  2. Airflow: cfm = ACH x volume / 60 = 2 x 6,400,000 / 60 = 12,800,000 / 60 = 213,333 cfm.
  3. Heat for a 10 F rise: Q = 1.08 x cfm x dT = 1.08 x 213,333 x 10 = 230,400 x 10 = 2,304,000 Btu/h. The 1.08 is 60 x 0.075 x 0.24 (minutes per hour, air density, specific heat) in the cooling load notes we read.
  4. Units: at the listing's 60,000 cfm fan limit, 213,333 / 60,000 = 3.6, so at least 4 units, each about 53,333 cfm and 576,000 Btu/h.
  5. Outdoor air: 0.06 x 200,000 = 12,000 cfm, or 12,000 / 213,333 = 5.6 percent of the circulated air.

What that number means. 2.3 million Btu/h is the capacity if every cfm gets the full 10 F rise. It is not the building's heat loss; the loss decides how long the burners fire.

The stratification point. Assume the roof air sits 10 F above the floor air with no mixing (below the 16 F above, inside the DOE report's 10 to 20 F), and assume the units remove all of it. Roof loss falls by U x A x dT = 0.05 x 200,000 x 10 = 100,000 Btu/h. At an assumed 80 percent heater efficiency the fuel saved is 100,000 / 0.80 = 125,000 Btu/h, or 1.25 therms per hour. Compared with the 2,304,000 Btu/h of air heating, the saving is 100,000 / 2,304,000 = 4.3 percent. Walls, doors and fan power are not counted, so this is a size check, not a payback.

Field card: Air turnover units

Pull low, throw high, roll the air, and check the sprinklers.

  • cfm = ACH x volume / 60. Heat: Q = 1.08 x cfm x dT.
  • Maker claim: 1 to 3 air changes per hour heating, 3.5 to 5 cooling.
  • Stratification: about 0.5 F per foot (Facilities Net).
  • Direct-fired: no heat exchanger; check CO, NO2 and outdoor air.
  • Sprinklers: 3 ft fan clearance, 36 in storage clearance, 7 ft heater rule.
