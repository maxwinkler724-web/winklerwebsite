Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance

The low-water cutoff is a small control with one job: stop the burner before the boiler runs dry. It is also one of the most neglected parts on the boiler, which is why it gets its own lesson.

Why this one control gets so much attention

A steam boiler holds a fairly small amount of water, and the burner has to be shut off if that water gets too low. The low-water cutoff (LWCO) is the device that does it. A trade magazine article on LWCO care calls it the leading mechanical cause of boiler accidents, and describes a small boiler whose control was packed with mud. The control kept reading “full” while the boiler was empty, the burner kept firing, and the cast-iron sections cracked. A boiler maker's manual puts the same idea plainly: do not fire the boiler without water, because the sections will overheat. An insurer's maintenance guide adds that low water is a frequent cause of boiler outages and damage.

Two common kinds

  • Float type. A float rides in a chamber on the boiler and moves with the water level. The gauge glass is often attached to it. Common on residential boilers.
  • Probe type. A probe inside the boiler uses the water's electrical conductivity to tell whether water is there.

Some controls also open a feed valve or start a feed pump as the level drops, and cut the burner if it keeps falling. The insurer guide notes that many places now require two cutoffs, a primary and a backup, and the magazine article says most commercial steam boilers have a redundant one.

What a good test looks like

The big idea is to copy real life. The magazine author found controls that shut the burner when drained fast but failed to when drained slowly, which is how water actually leaves a boiler. That is why technical sources talk about a slow drain test. In general terms, with the burner running and the water level in view, a trained person lets the level fall and watches that the burner shuts off before the water leaves the bottom of the gauge glass. If the burner keeps firing, the boiler stays off until the control is repaired or replaced. One control maker's instructions say the same: if the burner does not shut off, shut the boiler down immediately and correct the problem. An open-and-flush of the float chamber also clears sediment and shows the piping is not blocked.

How often? It depends who you ask

The sources do not agree on one number, and that is the point. One control maker calls for blowing down the control weekly while the boiler runs, and for taking it apart and cleaning it every year. A boiler maker's manual says to check a float type weekly and to remove, inspect and clean a probe type every year, each according to the control maker's instructions. An insurer guide asks the operator to flush it daily. A provincial regulator's model schedule for commercial low-pressure boilers has a daily blowdown and test, plus a twice-yearly open, clean and slow-drain test by a licensed contractor. The New Jersey Department of Labor logbook guideline gives a similar pattern: blow down and test steam-boiler cutoffs daily (once per shift on high pressure), test hot-water boiler cutoffs monthly, do a slow-drain test on steam cutoffs twice a year, and recondition or replace the cutoff annually. It is a guideline, and the control maker’s manual takes precedence. The National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors publishes a suggested maintenance log program with the same steam and hot-water split, and it also says to follow the equipment maker’s recommendations in every case. NJ’s low-pressure pre-inspection checklist adds that an automatically fired boiler needs a cutoff that shuts off fuel when the water drops to the lowest visible part of the gauge glass, with no shutoff valves between the boiler and its water column. The Canadian schedule is a model only. For a real boiler, check the manufacturer's manual for that control and the rules where you work.

One maker’s catalog, by control series

Even inside one brand the answer changes with the model. A McDonnell & Miller catalog lists these maintenance intervals by series (read it as a published example; your control’s own label and manual govern):

  • 150, 157, 158 and 159 series (and S versions): blow down and test daily; inspect yearly.
  • 42 series: blow down daily; inspect and test yearly.
  • 67, 767, 70, 70-B, 61, 63, 64 and 764 series: blow down weekly; inspect and test yearly.
  • 69, 169, 269, 369 and 469 series: inspect and test yearly. No blowdown line is listed.

The catalog lists no monthly interval. That is why the steam-versus-hot-water guideline above and the maker’s table can both be right: they describe different boilers and different controls.

Ways a cutoff fails

  • Sludge, rust or scale packs the float chamber or fouls the probe, so the control reads high.
  • Plugged sensing piping, or a valve installed in it. The article says to confirm those lines are open.
  • A drain that is plugged or leaks. A drain not opened in a long time can be blocked and spray when forced.
  • Skipped maintenance. The article describes a warranty dispute after the owner did none. Check the maker's warranty terms and keep a maintenance log.

Safety habits

  • Lock out power and let the boiler cool before opening a control. Make sure the water is below a probe before taking it out.
  • Blowdown water can be hot and black, and it stains. Gloves help, and the article warns against plastic buckets.
  • One boiler manual says not to blow down the cutoff unless the blowdown piping was installed per the manual, and to pipe it near a floor drain. A control maker likewise calls for a discharge line so steam and hot water cannot scald anyone.
  • Never jump out or bypass a cutoff or any safety control. A city boiler-operator guide lists this as a basic rule.

Opening, cleaning and replacing a cutoff is work for a trained, licensed person. This page explains the ideas, not a procedure. The installation and service instructions for the exact control and boiler in front of you always govern.

Check yourself

Four short questions in our own words. Think first, then open the answer.

Is a quick open-and-shut of the drain enough to prove a cutoff works?

No, not by itself. One author found controls that passed a fast drain but failed a slow one, and a slow drain looks more like real conditions. The manufacturer says what test counts.

The gauge glass looks full, yet the boiler is actually low. What could be fooling the control?

Sludge, rust or scale in the float chamber, or plugged sensing piping, can make a control read full when it is not.

Two sources give different test intervals. Which one do you follow?

The manual for that control and boiler, plus the rules where you work. Intervals differ by maker, control type and jurisdiction.

During a slow test the water drops out of the gauge glass and the burner is still firing. What now?

Treat it as a failed control. Shut the boiler down and keep it off until the control is repaired or replaced. Never jump it out to keep heat on.

Field card: Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance

Small control, big consequences.

  • The LWCO shuts the burner off when water is too low; low water cracks boilers.
  • Float and probe types need different upkeep. Read the control maker's manual.
  • A slow drain test copies real life better than a fast one.
  • Sludge and plugged sensing piping can make a control read full when it is empty.
  • If the burner will not shut off on low water, the boiler stays off until it is fixed.
  • Lock out, cool down, watch for hot black water. Never bypass a safety control.
