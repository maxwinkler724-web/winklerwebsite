EPA 608 Type III Leak Testing of Low-Pressure Chillers: Why Air and Water Leak In, Purge Run Time and Safe Pressure Raising

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Most refrigeration equipment leaks refrigerant out. A low-pressure chiller mostly does the opposite: it pulls room air and sometimes water in. The Type III exam asks about this, and so does the machine room, because the first clue is usually a purge unit that runs longer every week.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page is general study help built from EPA, eCFR, federal laboratory, national measurement and university pages read in 2026. It is not a procedure for your machine. The maker's literature and your site rules come first.

Why the leak goes inward

Federal regulation (40 CFR 82.152) defines a low-pressure appliance as one that uses a refrigerant whose liquid saturation pressure is below 45 psia at 104 °F. R-123 is the usual example. Saturation pressure is set by temperature, so we can look it up. The table uses values from the national measurement institute's online fluid data for R-123. We converted bar to psi with 1 bar = 14.504 psi, and compared with standard atmosphere, 14.696 psia.

Refrigerant temperatureSaturation pressure (NIST)Compared with outside air
4 °C (39.2 °F)0.39065 bar = 5.67 psiaabout 9.0 psi below (-9.0 psig)
26 °C (78.8 °F)0.94793 bar = 13.75 psiaabout 0.95 psi below
37 °C (98.6 °F)1.3973 bar = 20.27 psiaabout 5.6 psi above (+5.6 psig)

Read the rows this way. The cold evaporator side sits deep in vacuum, about 9 psi under the room, so any gasket, flange, valve stem or pinhole there lets air in. A machine sitting idle near room temperature is only slightly under atmospheric, which is why some machines add a small positive-pressure system for shutdown; one university facilities specification asks for a factory-installed automatic positive-pressure system on such chillers, holding not less than 0.5 psig while the machine is not running. On a hot day a warm condenser can run above atmospheric, so the inward leak is mostly a cold-side and idle-machine problem. Pressure is only set by temperature where liquid and vapor are both present. We did not compute R-1233zd(E).

What comes in does damage. A federal laboratory report on essential-service chillers says non-condensable gas such as air raises condenser pressure, and that moisture beyond the refrigerant's saturation level, which it puts at only a few ounces per 1,000 pounds of refrigerant, becomes free water that reacts to form acids. The purge unit is the machine's answer; see Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables and do not repeat that lesson's detail here.

Purge run time is the leak gauge

EPA's Type III topics list "signs of leakage into a low-pressure system (e.g., excessive purging)." A purge that works harder is removing more air, and air only gets there through a leak. So the log of purge pump-out minutes per day is a free leak gauge. One reading means little. A trend, taken at similar load, means a lot. Rule out the purge itself (a worn pump or a plugged line also raises run time) before you blame the shell. For what to write down each round, see Reading a Chiller Log.

Raising pressure for a test: controlled heat first

You cannot find an inward leak by looking for refrigerant escaping from a vacuum, so the shell is brought up toward atmospheric first. EPA's Type III topics give the order of preference: the hot water method or a built-in system heating and pressurization device, then nitrogen. The regulation (40 CFR 82.156(a)(1)(ii)) says a low-pressure appliance is pressurized to no higher than 0 psig before it is opened, and that persons pressurizing appliances with refrigerants boiling at or below 85 °F must not use methods such as nitrogen that require subsequent purging. Check your refrigerant's boiling point against that line.

The table shows why warm water is the gentle choice. Between 78.8 °F and 98.6 °F the R-123 pressure rises from 13.75 to 20.27 psia, about 6.5 psi (20.27 - 13.75 = 6.52). Where liquid refrigerant is present, pressure follows the temperature of the warmed charge, and it cannot climb past the saturation pressure of the water temperature you choose. Nitrogen has no such ceiling: a regulator set wrong or a cylinder connected without one can push the shell far past what it was built for. For general nitrogen technique see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods, and for the companion overview Type III Low-Pressure Chiller Leak Testing.

The rupture disc and the maximum test pressure. Low-pressure machines carry a rupture disc as a one-time safety part on the shell. EPA's Type III list includes "maximum leak test pressure for low-pressure centrifugal chillers" but gives no number, and we could not confirm a disc setting from an EPA or university source, so we do not state one. Read the disc rating and the maker's test limit off your own machine, and pick the lower of the two with room to spare. Do not use the disc as a pressure relief you test against.

The hydrostatic tube test and moisture

When the purge unit collects water, or free water shows up in the refrigerant or oil, suspect a tube leak. The water side runs at higher pressure than the refrigerant side, so a leaking tube pushes water in. A hydrostatic test checks the water side under water pressure. New-equipment specifications show the scale: one university specification has the maker test the fluid side of the heat exchangers and water boxes to 1.5 times the rated pressure, and another does the same for water-side working pressure. Those are factory tests. In the field the idea is the same, to isolate the water side, hold it under controlled pressure, and watch for water crossing into the refrigerant side, but the steps, pressures and refrigerant handling come from the maker and from the recovery rules, not from this page.

Moisture also decides how you work. EPA lists the need to circulate or remove water from the chiller during evacuation to prevent freezing, and the need to introduce vapor before liquid to prevent freezing of water in the tubes. The details are in the Type III recovery lesson. A university specification also tells the installer to run tests and startup so as not to introduce moisture into the machine.

Worked example (our own, with made-up numbers)

This is our own example, not data from a real machine. A chiller using R-123 is logged weekly. The purge pump-out time is the weekly average minutes per day at similar load.

Week123456
Minutes per day101318212729

Trend. Change from week 1 to week 6 is 29 - 10 = 19 minutes per day over 5 weekly steps, so 19 / 5 = 3.8 minutes per day added each week. A least-squares line through all six points gives 4.0, so the simple method agrees. Run time is 29 / 10 = 2.9 times the starting value.

Assumptions. The purge pump removes 0.2 ft³ of gas per minute while running, and we count all of it as air. Air weighs 0.075 lb/ft³ (a typical room-air value). Both are our assumptions, and counting everything as air overstates the air a little.

Air estimate. Week 1: 10 × 0.2 = 2.0 ft³ per day. Week 6: 29 × 0.2 = 5.8 ft³ per day. The increase is 5.8 - 2.0 = 3.8 ft³ per day, or 3.8 / 24 = 0.16 ft³ per hour. In weight that is 3.8 × 0.075 = 0.285 lb per day, about 2.0 lb per week of extra air. In a steady state the air pumped out matches the air leaking in, so this is a rough size of the new leak.

Refrigerant carried out with it. One university specification rates a purge at no more than 0.1 lb of refrigerant per pound of purged air. At that assumed rating, 0.285 × 0.1 = 0.0285 lb per day, about 0.2 lb per week.

Decision. Our own trigger: leak test when run time rises three weeks in a row at similar load, or reaches double the starting value (20 minutes per day here). By end of week 4 the log shows rises in weeks 2, 3 and 4 and 21 minutes, which is above 20. We leak test at week 4, not week 6. If we waited, 3.8 added minutes per week from 29 minutes reaches a made-up 60 minute alarm in (60 - 29) / 3.8, about 8 weeks. The test comes first, starting with the purge unit and the cold-side gaskets, then a controlled warm-water pressure raise within the maker's limit.

Field card: leak testing a low-pressure chiller

Air in, not refrigerant out.

  • Cold evaporator R-123 is about 9 psi below atmospheric (NIST values); leaks pull air and moisture in.
  • Rising purge minutes per day, at similar load, is the early warning. Rule out the purge unit first.
  • EPA test order for raising pressure: controlled hot water or a built-in heater, then nitrogen.
  • Open a low-pressure appliance only at 0 psig or lower.
  • No rupture disc number on EPA's list; read the machine's disc and the maker's test limit.
  • Water in the purge points to tube leaks; the water side is tested hydrostatically per the maker.
  • Circulate or remove water during evacuation; vapor before liquid when recharging.
