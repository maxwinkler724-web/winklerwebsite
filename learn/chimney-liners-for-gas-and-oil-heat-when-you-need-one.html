Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat: When You Need One

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "do I need a chimney liner for my furnace," here is the short version: sometimes yes, and the most common trigger is a furnace replacement that leaves the water heater alone in an old chimney. This page explains what a liner does, when the question comes up, and how to have the chimney checked.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on your house. Liner and venting decisions follow the appliance maker's installation instructions, the fuel gas code adopted where you live, and a qualified inspector. We did not read the full text of NFPA 54 or NFPA 211, so where those standards come up, we say which page described them.

What a chimney liner does

A liner is a smooth, sealed channel inside the chimney. It carries combustion gases up and out and keeps them away from the masonry and from the living space. Older homes often have a plain brick chimney built for coal or early, low-efficiency furnaces. Contractor magazine writer Dave Yates says such chimneys were big because the old furnaces were only about 30 to 50 percent efficient, and the hot gas pushed a strong draft through them.

Code language backs the idea. A 2006 edition of the National Fuel Gas Code (NFPA 54), section 12.6.1.3 as reproduced on a chimney supplier's site, says a masonry chimney serving gas appliances must be lined with approved clay flue lining, a listed chimney lining system or other approved material that resists corrosion, erosion, softening and cracking from vent gases. Minneapolis, in its public guidance on chimneys, goes further: an approved metal liner (aluminum, stainless steel or a listed vent system) is required in a masonry chimney used for gas appliances, with approved alternates allowed.

Why gas heat can still damage a chimney

Burning gas makes water vapor. In an older, hot-running furnace the vapor leaves as steam. In a modern high-efficiency appliance, or in any appliance connected to a chimney that is too big and cold, the gas cools and some of that vapor turns to liquid on the inside of the chimney. Yates reports that this liquid contains nitric and sulfuric acids, and he describes frost showing on an outside wall in the outline of the chimney, white salt deposits running down the wall, and masonry flaking in freeze and thaw cycles. That flaking is called spalling. Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA) makes a related warning: modern furnaces can have many problems when they are vented through average flues meant for older furnaces.

Eroded flue joints and cracked liners can leak exhaust gas, and carbon monoxide (CO) is one of those gases. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics for how that happens.

The orphaned water heater

This is the usual reason a homeowner is told "you need a liner." Many houses vent the furnace and the water heater into one chimney. A new 90-percent-plus furnace (see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained) is not allowed to share that chimney flue. Trane's installation manual for its condensing furnaces says the furnace must not be connected to any vent serving another appliance, and that it must not be run through a chimney serving a wood-burning or oil furnace, an incinerator or any other gas appliance. The new furnace gets its own plastic vent through a wall. The old water heater stays behind, "orphaned," alone in a chimney sized for two.

The same Trane manual states the consequence: when a furnace is removed from a venting system serving other gas appliances, the venting system is likely to be too large for the remaining ones. It tells the installer to size the remaining system with the tables in the latest National Fuel Gas Code. It also lists a test: after five minutes of burner operation, check for spillage at the draft hood relief opening of each draft hood appliance, and correct the venting if spillage shows.

The options Yates describes are the same ones you will hear from a good contractor. First, a correctly sized liner (typically stainless steel or aluminum, or a listed system) that fits the water heater. Second, a new direct-vent or power-vent water heater and then closing off the chimney. Which is right depends on the water heater's manual, the code tables and the condition of the chimney. If a contractor swaps the furnace and says nothing about the water heater, ask about it.

Worked example: how big is too big?

Yates gives a rough rule of thumb: the chimney's cross-section should not be more than about seven times the area of the connected flue. He states it as a guide, and he says the code tables, chimney height and exposure also matter, so treat it as a sanity check only. Take a water heater with a 3-inch draft hood outlet and an old chimney flue about 8 inches by 12 inches, one of the sizes Yates says tends to condense.

  1. Area of a 3-inch round outlet: 3.14 × 1.5 × 1.5 = about 7.1 square inches.
  2. Rule-of-thumb ceiling: 7 × 7.1 = about 49 square inches (Yates gives about 49).
  3. Area of the 8 by 12 flue: 8 × 12 = 96 square inches.
  4. Compare: 96 ÷ 49 is a little under 2, so the flue is nearly twice the ceiling.

Minneapolis's public chimney page gives a stricter local check for the same case: a 3-inch draft hood may not connect to a chimney venting system larger than 6-inch round, which is 28.3 square inches. By that check, 96 square inches is about 3.4 times too large (96 ÷ 28.3).

Oil heat

The same ideas apply to oil. CSIA notes that liners for gas and oil appliances do not require insulation, though insulating still helps performance in an exterior chimney. A new oil burner on an old chimney should still be checked by a qualified chimney or oil technician. For the tank side of oil heat, see Home Heating Oil Tanks in New Jersey.

Yearly inspection and the inspection levels

CSIA's FAQ quotes NFPA 211 as saying chimneys, fireplaces and vents shall be inspected at least once a year for soundness. CSIA and NFPA both recommend yearly inspections for fire and CO safety, according to one chimney-care company's summary of the levels. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) also urges a yearly professional inspection of fuel-burning heating equipment, chimneys, flues and vents.

  • Level 1. A look at the readily accessible parts of the chimney and connected appliance, with a check that the flue is unblocked and clean. CSIA's inspection procedure says Level 1 fits annual inspections, routine cleanings and replacing an appliance with a similar one, "when nothing is changing."
  • Level 2. Level 1 plus attics, crawl spaces and basements, and it may include a camera scan. The chimney-care summary says it is called for when a new heating appliance is added, the fuel changes, the property is sold, or after a malfunction or event that may have damaged the chimney.
  • Level 3. Reaching concealed areas when a hidden hazard is suspected, often after an incident.

Replacing a furnace and leaving the water heater on the chimney is a change in how the chimney is used, so ask the contractor which level applies and who will do it.

Carbon monoxide: what to do now

CPSC says birds, animals and insects can block vents, that vent pipes can crack or separate and leak CO, and that its inspection advice covers leaks, blockages and loose or disconnected vents. It recommends a CO alarm in the hallway near each sleeping area, tested monthly. An alarm does not fix a venting problem. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do and Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

For the tech: how sizing is actually done

Vent sizing for Category I appliances is done with the tables in NFPA 54 or the International Fuel Gas Code (the Minneapolis page points to IFGC section 504 or engineered methods), together with the appliance manual. This page gives no table values because they depend on the code edition, the appliance input in Btu/h, connector size, vertical rise and chimney height. A simple job flow:

  1. List every appliance still connected and its input rating from the nameplate.
  2. Identify the chimney size, height, material and liner condition.
  3. Size the vent or liner from the current code tables and the manuals.
  4. Install the liner to its maker's instructions and label it for its fuel.
  5. Run the spillage check from the furnace manual and confirm CO with a combustion analyzer (see Combustion Analyzer Readings).

Field card: Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat

Replace the furnace, then re-check everything left on the chimney.

  • A liner is a sealed, correctly sized channel for exhaust inside a chimney.
  • Cool exhaust in an oversized chimney condenses and can spall masonry.
  • A condensing furnace gets its own vent; it does not share a chimney flue.
  • The leftover water heater is the "orphan"; size its vent from the code tables and its manual.
  • Yearly inspection is advised by NFPA 211 (via CSIA) and CPSC; Level 2 when the appliance or fuel changes.
  • Test for spillage and CO after any change. A CO alarm is a backup, not a fix.
