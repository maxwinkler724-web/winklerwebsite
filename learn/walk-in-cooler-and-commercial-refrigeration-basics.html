Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A walk-in is a refrigerated room: an insulated box plus a refrigeration system. When one fails, food is at risk, so knowing how the pieces fit helps you find problems faster.

The box and the system

A walk-in has insulated wall, ceiling and floor panels, a door with gaskets and a safety release, and a refrigeration system. Typical system parts include a compressor and condenser, an evaporator (unit cooler) inside the box, a metering device, piping, and line components such as a solenoid valve, filter drier, sight glass and pressure switches. A controller handles temperature, fans, defrost and alarms. See Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

Common layouts

  • Self-contained: evaporator and condenser together, often for small or leased spaces.
  • Remote condensing unit: evaporator inside, condensing unit outdoors or on a roof, common in restaurants and stores.
  • Parallel rack: a central system with several compressors serving many cases and rooms, used in supermarkets.

Evaporator styles include side-discharge and pancake types, ducted types for wine rooms, and older gravity coils with no fans.

Cooler versus freezer

One source cites U.S. food-safety guidance of 40 degrees F or below for refrigerated food and 0 degrees F for frozen food. For commercial boxes the limit that inspectors apply is the Food Code cold-holding temperature of 41 degrees F or below for food that needs temperature control for safety (section 3-501.16, discussed on the cold-holding page); your local health department's adopted code decides what applies. A freezer works at much lower temperatures than a cooler, so ice control matters more. Equipment choice, refrigerant, controls and settings come from the manufacturer and the application.

Defrost and heaters

Frost builds on the evaporator and blocks airflow. A defrost control, and in many units defrost heaters, melts it, and a drain pan and drain line carry the water away. Technician sources stress that drain pan and drain line heaters matter a lot on freezers, and that freezers commonly have door heaters too. A frozen drain line is a classic source of ice and leaks. Demand defrost, which runs when needed, can use less energy than a plain timer. See Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat for the heat pump version of the idea.

Controls and pump-down

Many walk-ins use a liquid line solenoid valve operated by the room thermostat. When the room is satisfied, the valve closes and the compressor pumps the refrigerant to the condenser side and then stops on low pressure. This is one way to limit refrigerant moving into the compressor during the off cycle. Low-ambient control, such as fan cycling or a headmaster valve, helps the system keep working in cold weather. Details differ by manufacturer.

Everyday habits that help

  • Keep doors closed. Open them briefly and reduce traffic.
  • Keep gaskets clean and tight. A sheet of paper that slides out easily, or light visible around the seal, suggests a poor seal.
  • On busy doors, strip curtains and door closers reduce the heat load.
  • Keep the condenser coil clean and shaded from direct sun, and keep the condensate drain clear.
  • Do not block evaporator airflow with stock.

Service and safety

Walk-ins carry high voltage, moving fans, refrigerant and a risk of being locked in. Use lockout/tagout. Make sure the inside door release works and that someone knows you are in the box. Recover refrigerant, never vent it. Some refrigerants and rules have been changing, so check the nameplate and current regulations. For leak repair on larger commercial refrigeration, the rule depends on the refrigerant: 40 CFR 82.157 covers CFC and HCFC appliances of 50 pounds or more, while HFC systems of 15 pounds or more (with GWP above 53) follow the AIM Act Emissions Reduction and Reclamation rule, 40 CFR Part 84, from January 1, 2026. The trigger rates and repair deadlines are in Leaks and Leak Repair Basics. See Preventive Maintenance Checklist Habits. Protect stored product, and follow the company and health department rules for product that has been warm.

Field card: Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics

A walk-in is an insulated box plus a refrigeration system, and ice is its constant fight.

  • Know the layout: self-contained, remote condensing unit, or rack.
  • Evaporator inside, condenser outside or on top, controller managing defrost and fans.
  • Drain pan and drain line heaters (and door heaters on freezers) prevent ice problems.
  • Clean gaskets and condenser coils, close doors, and clear drains.
  • Lockout/tagout, check the inside door release, and recover refrigerant.
