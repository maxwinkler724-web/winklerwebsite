Hydronic Proportional Balancing: Index Circuit and Flow Ratios

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a hydronic system with several parallel circuits, the ones close to the pump tend to get too much water and the far ones too little. Proportional balancing is a step-by-step way to fix that with a flow meter and the balancing valves already in the piping. This lesson walks through the method, the arithmetic and the valve readings behind it.

Why systems need balancing

Water in parallel circuits takes the easy way. The CIBSE Journal article on proportional balancing starts from the pressure-drop relationship, which says the drop across a circuit grows with the square of its flow. Circuits that share the same supply and return pressure therefore split the flow by how hard each one is to push water through. Without balancing valves, the short, open circuits overflow and the long ones starve. Our Hot-Water Balancing Basics lesson covers that idea for small systems. Here we focus on the procedure a balancer follows on a multi-circuit system.

Two terms: flow ratio and index circuit

Flow ratio is how the CIBSE article compares each circuit with its design: the measured flow as a percentage of the design flow. In plain arithmetic that is measured ÷ design. A circuit at 100 percent has exactly its design flow. A circuit at 130 percent is overflowing, and one at 70 percent is short.

Index circuit is, in the CIBSE article, the circuit that is hydraulically furthest from the pump and shows the lowest percentage of design flow. It is not always the physically most distant one. Armstrong's paper does not use the words "index circuit" but describes the same idea as the most disadvantaged terminal unit, usually the one highest and furthest from the risers. Its valve stays wide open as the reference, because no valve in that circuit can be opened further to help it.

The proportional method, step by step

Different writers order the steps a little differently. Putting the CIBSE article, the Armstrong paper and a Deppmann article together, the common outline is:

  1. Prepare. Confirm the pump can deliver design flow. Open all balancing and control valves. CIBSE sets the pump to about 110 percent of design flow for the measurements, and Deppmann describes throttling the pump discharge valve to 110 percent of design.
  2. Measure every circuit and work out each flow ratio. Deppmann says the balancer measures the percentage of flow available against design.
  3. Find the index circuit, the one with the lowest ratio. Leave its valve open.
  4. Throttle the other circuits until each reads the same ratio as the index. CIBSE then locks each setting. Armstrong warns that as one valve is throttled, flow through the others rises, so the readings shift and the balancer re-reads.
  5. Work back toward the pump. Deppmann starts at the hydraulically farthest zone and moves back toward the pump. Armstrong balances terminals on a floor, then the floors against each other with riser valves. CIBSE adds sub-networks to the balanced group one at a time.
  6. Bring the total to design. CIBSE says that once the whole system is in proportional balance, total flow can be regulated to design by adjusting pump speed or the main regulating valve. Armstrong throttles its main balancing valve until the system reaches 100 percent of design flow. Deppmann throttles the pump discharge valve down to design, and says all the balance valves then move to design flow together.
  7. Trim, verify and record. Armstrong measures the pressure drop across the main valve to find the excess pump head, then has the impeller trimmed so the main valve can sit open. Deppmann remeasures all valves, sets the memory stops and records the results.

Why does step 6 work? CIBSE explains that once branches are set to a fixed ratio, that ratio holds as total flow changes, as long as no resistance changes. We also cover pump selection in Reading Pump Curves and System Curves.

Worked example: three branches

Our own example: three branches have design flows of 20, 15 and 10 gpm. We measure 26, 15 and 7 gpm.

Step 1, flow ratio = measured ÷ design (Our own):

  • Branch 1: 26 ÷ 20 = 1.30, or 130 percent.
  • Branch 2: 15 ÷ 15 = 1.00, or 100 percent.
  • Branch 3: 7 ÷ 10 = 0.70, or 70 percent.

Step 2, index circuit (Our own): Branch 3 has the lowest ratio, 70 percent, so it is the index. Its valve stays open.

Step 3, target flows for the others (Our own): each is set to match 70 percent of its own design.

  • Branch 1: 20 × 0.70 = 14 gpm.
  • Branch 2: 15 × 0.70 = 10.5 gpm.
  • Branch 3: stays at 7 gpm, its 70 percent.

Note that these targets use the index flow as first measured. In a real system, throttling branches 1 and 2 pushes more water through branch 3, so its ratio climbs a little and the balancer re-reads and repeats, as Armstrong describes. The method is to match the index's current ratio, not a fixed number.

Step 4, raise all circuits to 100 percent (Our own): the three flows now total 14 + 10.5 + 7 = 31.5 gpm, and design total is 20 + 15 + 10 = 45 gpm. Multiplying by the same factor keeps the ratio: 1 ÷ 0.70 = 1.43 (rounded).

  • Branch 1: 14 × 1.43 = about 20 gpm.
  • Branch 2: 10.5 × 1.43 = about 15 gpm.
  • Branch 3: 7 × 1.43 = about 10 gpm.

In the field the balancer does this by opening the main valve or raising the pump speed until the main reads 45 gpm, as CIBSE describes, and then re-measures. The branches move together because they were set in proportion.

Reading a circuit setter

A circuit setter has two test ports across a calibrated restriction. The Bell & Gossett instruction manual for its Circuit Sentry says you measure the differential pressure across the valve with a readout kit. If the reading is above the valve's minimum, the flow comes from the maker's graph for that size and setting. If it is below the minimum, the manual gives Q = Cv × √ΔP, with flow in gpm and pressure in psi. The flow graphs sit in a separate flow curve book or submittal. The manual also warns that hot water can leak from the ports when probes go in and out. Our Balancing Valves and PICVs lesson covers the Cv math. Use the chart for the exact valve and setting, not a generic one.

The compensated method

Caleffi's Idronics 8 describes a compensated approach. It picks the least favored crossover as a reference valve, sets it near fully open and locks it. It then sets each other crossover using a partner valve, restoring the reference pressure after each change. Branches and riser sets are balanced the same way, and Caleffi states the result is proportional balancing of all crossovers, branches and risers. We do not describe it further here because it needs the maker's procedure for the specific valves.

When pressure-independent valves remove the need

Caleffi says a pressure-independent balancing valve holds its calibrated flow, typically within plus or minus 5 percent, over a range of differential pressure. Active branches keep design flow no matter what other branches do, but only while the pressure across the valve stays between the valve's lower and upper limits. The valve must also be set to each branch's design flow. So these valves replace the manual hunt for ratios, but they do not remove checking pressure and flow. See the variable-flow lesson on differential pressure control.

Documenting the work

A Michigan State University spec for testing, adjusting and balancing (section 230593) asks for the hydronic test reports to be numbered from the pump to the end of the system, with single-line diagrams showing flow rates, pipe and valve sizes, balancing stations and balancing device positions. Pump data includes impeller size and flow. It also sets heating and cooling water flow at plus or minus 10 percent of design, and asks for branch flows to be corrected where they differ from the approved pump flow by more than 5 percent. The air-side version of reading such a report is in Reading a Test and Balance Report. Write down valve positions before you touch anything.

Field card: Proportional Balancing

Flow ratio = measured ÷ design. Lowest ratio is the index.

  • Open all valves, pump near 110 percent of design for readings.
  • Measure every circuit and find each ratio.
  • Leave the index circuit open; throttle others to its ratio.
  • Work from the far end back toward the pump; re-read as you go.
  • Raise all to 100 percent with the main valve or pump speed.
  • Our own example: 130, 100, 70 percent; targets 14 and 10.5 gpm.
  • Record settings and follow the maker's chart for each valve.
