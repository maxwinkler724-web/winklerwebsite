Converting a Gas Furnace or Boiler to Propane: LP Kits Explained

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace or boiler built for natural gas will not run safely on propane just because the pipe fits. The two fuels carry very different amounts of energy and are delivered at different pressures, so the burner parts have to change. This lesson explains what an LP (propane) conversion kit replaces, why, and why the job belongs to a qualified installer.

Safety first

This is understanding, not instructions. Do not convert an appliance from this page. Use the kit made for your exact model, follow its instructions, and have a qualified service agency do the work and test it. If you smell gas, leave the building before you do anything else (see the lesson linked below).

Why the two fuels differ

Gas appliances are rated in Btu per hour, so what matters is how many Btu pass through the burner. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) lists 1 cubic foot of natural gas at 1,036 Btu, a national average that it recalculates every year. In a 2014 article it gives pure propane vapor as 2,516 Btu per cubic foot, against about 1,030 for U.S. natural gas that summer. So a cubic foot of propane vapor carries roughly two and a half times the energy.

That has two effects at the burner. To release the same Btu per hour you need less than half the gas volume, which means a smaller orifice opening. And the manufacturers push propane through that smaller opening at a higher pressure. Weil-McLain says it directly: propane has higher energy content, so it needs less fuel flow at higher incoming pressure.

Worked example: same input, less gas

Take a furnace with an 80,000 Btu per hour input rating. Gas volume equals input divided by the heating value.

  1. Natural gas at 1,036 Btu per cubic foot: 80,000 ÷ 1,036 = about 77.2 cubic feet per hour.
  2. Propane vapor at 2,516 Btu per cubic foot: 80,000 ÷ 2,516 = about 31.8 cubic feet per hour.
  3. Ratio: 31.8 ÷ 77.2 = about 0.41. Propane needs about 41 percent of the volume.

Now the pressure side, using the running gas pressures Weil-McLain gives for its boilers: a minimum of 3.5 inches of water column (in. w.c.) on natural gas and 10.0 on propane. A pound per square inch is about 27.7 in. w.c. (from NIST conversion factors), so 3.5 ÷ 27.7 = about 0.13 psi, and 10.0 ÷ 27.7 = about 0.36 psi. Propane runs at roughly 2.9 times the pressure (10.0 ÷ 3.5), yet still well under half a psi. These are one maker's example numbers; your model's manual sets the real ones.

A natural gas meter cannot be used to clock a propane tank system, so the maker's orifice size and manifold pressure are what set the input. To see how natural gas input is clocked, read Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.

What an LP conversion kit changes

Kits differ, so read the one for your model. Across the maker instructions we read, the same parts keep showing up:

  • Burner orifices. Trane's kit for one- and two-stage furnaces replaces the natural gas orifices with #56 propane orifices, counted by furnace input, and adds new stainless burners. Lennox kits list orifice part numbers by heat level and burner count. Rheem's R4GM kit ties orifice size to model, burner count and elevation, and says not to re-drill orifices. Lennox and Rheem say to thread them in without pipe joint compound.
  • Regulator spring or valve. Trane swaps the natural gas regulator spring for a propane spring under the high-fire screw on certain White-Rodgers valves. Lennox swaps the stem and spring assembly on certain Honeywell valves. Rheem's kit replaces the whole gas valve.
  • Pilot orifice. None of the three kits we read addressed a pilot change; they cover electronic ignition. If an older standing-pilot appliance needs one, only its own manual can say.
  • Labels. Trane includes a propane label, a conversion label and a conversion responsibility label that is filled out and placed inside the furnace door. Lennox has the installer check the box matching the model number and complete the installer information on a converter sticker. Rheem has you place its conversion information label over the natural gas warning label and leave the rating plate alone.

Boilers follow the same pattern. Weil-McLain notes that most of its boilers ship set for natural gas and must be converted in the field with an approved kit, with every part on the packing sheet installed and every label applied. It also says cast iron boilers need a propane-compatible control and that values must be set with a combustion analyzer strictly to the manual.

Pressure targets, only as makers state

Targets vary by model, so treat these as examples. Trane lists final propane manifold pressure of 10.5 to 11.0 in. w.c. for its kit (with 9.5 for a few named models). Lennox's packaged-unit kit says manifold pressure is preset at 10.5 and to keep line pressure between 10.8 and 13.5 at maximum firing. Rheem's R4GM kit sets 9.5 manifold with 11.0 to 14.0 at the valve inlet. Weil-McLain gives a minimum of 3.5 for natural gas and 10.0 for propane with the boiler running, and warns not to exceed 14 in. w.c. static pressure because that can damage the gas valve. For how those readings are taken, see Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer.

Who does it and what proves it is done

All three furnace kits assign the work to a qualified service agency or technician. Lennox defines that as an experienced firm or person who meets the authority having jurisdiction's requirements, and says the installation "is not proper and complete" until the converted appliance's operation is checked. Trane's guide calls for a leak check of all fittings, a manifold pressure check and a full operating check. Weil-McLain calls for a combustion analyzer. The labels are part of the record: the next person to open the cabinet needs to see at once that it is now a propane appliance and who converted it. Check your local code, which can add permit or licensing rules (see the permit lesson in the related list). Altitude: only Rheem's kit speaks to it, with a separate orifice table by elevation, so use only your model's kit instructions.

Propane tank regulators, basics

Tank pressure changes with temperature and is far too high for a burner. A common setup uses two regulators. According to Propane101, the first stage sits at the tank and lowers pressure to 10 psi or less to feed the line. The second stage, at the building, brings it down to the appliance level, with 11 in. w.c. given as the household example. That matches the 11 in. w.c. inlet minimums on the furnace documents above. Regulators are set up for the system and are not a place for guesswork.

Propane safety

The Propane Education & Research Council (PERC) says propane vapor is heavier than air, so it collects in basements, crawl spaces and along floors, and moving air can carry it elsewhere. Trane's guide repeats the warning and recommends a gas detector for basement or confined-space installs. Propane has no odor of its own; makers add one. PERC describes "odor fade," a drop in odor strength that can come from air, water or rust in a container, leaks through soil, or odor sticking to piping, and notes that long exposure causes "odor fatigue." So not smelling it is not proof that it is absent. PERC's guidance for a suspected leak is to close valves promptly and evacuate the immediate and neighboring areas. Follow your propane supplier's and local emergency instructions too. The full steps are in Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do. For how a gas furnace runs once lit, see Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation.
