A2L Control Boards and Mitigation

The sensor only notices. The control board decides what to do about it. If you are used to boards that mostly pass 24V along, this one is different: its job is to be able to say no.

What the board is for

On equipment with leak detection, a control board takes in the sensor signal and runs the mitigation response. Depending on the manufacturer it may be a dedicated mitigation or dissipation board, or the function may live on the main air handler or furnace control. Some boards are field-installed with the equipment, others come factory-mounted in the control box. Where it lives and what it is called vary.

How the pieces relate

  • Sensor to board. A harness carries the sensor's signal and often its status. A damaged or unplugged harness is itself a reportable fault on many boards.
  • Board to indoor blower. On a detection, the board makes sure the blower runs, usually by driving the blower control directly rather than waiting for the thermostat. This is why airflow settings matter: the manufacturer may specify a minimum airflow for the charge involved.
  • Board to thermostat and heat. The board can override a cooling call and block a heating call so the compressor and heat sources are not energized during the event.
  • Board to other outputs. Depending on the system it can open zone dampers, de-energize other potential ignition sources it controls, signal a building controller or sound an alarm.
  • Board to the rest of the system. Communicating systems can pass the alarm and status over the data bus.

None of this is something you should memorize as a rule for all brands. It is a pattern. The wiring diagram and sequence of operation for the unit are the real answer.

Other mitigation options in codes and permits

Sensor-driven blower control is one tool. One city building bulletin lists a wider set of mitigation options for fitting an A2L system to a space: air circulation, combining connected spaces, rerouting piping, safety shut-off valves, mechanical exhaust, and choosing equipment that suits the room. Which ones apply, and what charge and room size they allow, is in the unit's installation manual and the code where you work.

Fault and lockout concepts

Boards generally track several different conditions, and it helps to keep them separate in your head:

  • Active leak alarm. The sensor is reporting refrigerant. The board is mitigating.
  • Sensor communication error. The board cannot hear the sensor. Usually a harness, connector or sensor problem.
  • Sensor fault. The sensor reports an internal problem.
  • History. Some boards remember a past event for a period and show it with a light or code, so you can learn that something happened after the unit has recovered.
  • Hold or lockout. Some products hold the response for a timed period after the sensor clears, or until power is cycled or the board is reset a specific way. Others require service action. This is manufacturer specific and is documented in the unit's manual.

Where a product does have a service-reset method, use only the one the manufacturer provides. Cycling power to clear a code you have not investigated is how a real leak gets re-energized.

Approaching a mitigation-related fault

A sound approach is the same disciplined one you would use anywhere, with the safety question placed first:

  • Treat it as possibly real. Make the space safe: ventilate, no ignition sources, no assumption that it is a false alarm.
  • Read the board's indicators and the manual's fault table. Write down what is active and what is history before you touch anything.
  • Separate sensor, harness, board and system. A communication error points toward harness and connections. An active alarm points toward refrigerant or contamination. A board that does not respond as the sequence says points toward the board or its outputs.
  • Do a leak search using the leak detector and methods the manufacturer allows for the refrigerant in the unit.
  • Use the manufacturer's test method to confirm every mitigation output works after the repair, and recheck that heating and cooling are properly locked out and released as described.
  • Replace parts only with specified parts, and complete any matching or configuration steps the maker requires.

What never to do

  • Never jumper, bridge or remove the sensor to make a unit run.
  • Never disconnect the board's blower or heat outputs so the unit "keeps working."
  • Never reset repeatedly until a fault stays clear.
  • Never assume a board from a different model or brand will do the same job.
  • Never leave the unit running on a defeated safety circuit, even for a hot day. A comfort call is not worth a fire risk, and defeating a listed safety circuit may violate the equipment listing and put you in a legal and insurance mess.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, thresholds, sensor positions or wiring steps, and those vary by manufacturer and by model.

A habit worth building

Take a photo of the board label, the indicator status and the wiring before you open anything. Some boards show a history that disappears with power loss, and a before picture is also your best friend if a customer later asks what you found. The mindset of starting from the manufacturer's procedure rather than from a guess is one that experienced techs write about repeatedly, and it pays off most on equipment with safety logic.

Field card: A2L Control Boards and Mitigation

The board runs the response. Your job is to find out why it did, not to stop it.

  • The board takes the sensor signal and runs the mitigation response.
  • Typical outputs: blower on, cooling and heat held off, optional dampers and alarms.
  • Keep sensor errors, active alarms and history separate when reading codes.
  • Use only the manufacturer's reset and test methods.
  • Never defeat a safety circuit, even temporarily.
