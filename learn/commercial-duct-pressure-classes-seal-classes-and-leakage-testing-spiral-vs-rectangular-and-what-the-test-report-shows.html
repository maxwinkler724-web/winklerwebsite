Commercial Duct Leakage Classes: Seal Class, CL and the Test Report

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "duct leakage class SMACNA test commercial," here is the short version. A commercial duct has a pressure class (how hard the system pushes), a seal class (which joints get sealant) and a leakage class, CL (how much air may escape). A test rig pressurizes a section, and the flow it needs to hold that pressure is compared with an allowed number. This lesson shows the arithmetic and how to read the paper. The home version of this test is a different animal; see Duct Leakage Testing: Total vs Leakage to Outside and CFM25.

Check your adopted code

Check your adopted code. Energy codes are adopted state by state with amendments, and editions differ. This page was built from public presentations, a state rule text and maker documents read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. The SMACNA HVAC Air Duct Leakage Test Manual, the IECC and ASHRAE Standard 90.1 are standards you should read in your own copy; we name them but do not reproduce them.

Three classes that sound alike

Pressure class is the static pressure, in inches of water column (in. w.c., also written w.g.), that the duct is built to handle. The test pressure should equal the duct construction class and must not exceed the rating of any part in the tested section, so a 3 in. trunk feeding 2 in. branches is tested at 2 in. or less, according to a 2024 SMACNA leakage presentation hosted by the Connecticut ASHRAE chapter.

Seal class tells you which joints must be sealed. Both the NEBB presentation and McGill AirFlow's bulletin, which reproduce the SMACNA table, list it this way:

Seal classWhat gets sealedUsual pressure classes
CTransverse joints only1/2, 1 and 2 in.
BTransverse joints and seams3 in.
AJoints, seams and all wall penetrations4, 6 and 10 in.

The Connecticut presentation adds detail. Transverse joints include girth joints, branch connections, collar tap-ins, terminal and louver connections, access door frames and abutments to the building. In Class B, spiral seams are exempt. In Class A, pipe, tubing, rod and wire passing through the wall count as penetrations. ASHRAE 90.1 Section 6.4.4.2.1, as printed in Addendum t to the 2019 edition, says ductwork and all plenums with pressure class ratings shall be constructed to Seal Class A.

Leakage class (CL) is the allowed leakage in cfm per 100 sq ft of duct surface at 1 in. w.c. NEBB gives the allowed leakage at any other pressure as F = CL x P^0.65, where F is in cfm per 100 sq ft and P is the test pressure in in. w.c. The SMACNA table values, as McGill and NEBB present them:

Seal classRectangular metal CLRound metal CL
C2412
B126
A63

What the energy codes require

Washington State's adopted commercial rule (an IECC-based text, archived 2019 version, section C403.2.8.3.3) requires ducts designed to operate above 3 in. w.g. (750 Pa) to be leak-tested under the SMACNA manual. The duct must show a leakage rate CL of 4.0 or less, using CL = F / P^0.65, and representative sections totaling at least 25 percent of the duct area must be tested. The designer must furnish documentation that the sections were tested and passed.

The Connecticut presentation reports the same CL 4.0 for both the 2021 IECC (Section C403.12.2.3, mandatory, 3 in. w.g. or higher) and ASHRAE 90.1-2019 (ductwork above 3 in. w.g. and all ductwork located outdoors), with at least 25 percent of representative sections tested. Notice that one source says "in excess of 3" and another says "3 or higher." Read the adopted text. The same presentation says the 2024 Uniform Mechanical Code goes further: all ductwork, CL 6.0, 10 percent tested, then 40 percent and then 100 percent if the earlier tests fail.

How the rig works

NEBB lists the parts: a speed-controlled blower, a flow grid or orifice plate to measure flow, manometers, and fittings to attach to the duct. The steps, from the Connecticut presentation:

  1. Pick the test pressure and a section small enough that its likely leakage is within the rig's capacity.
  2. Calculate the allowed leakage.
  3. Seal every opening but one, including ends, equipment and accessories, and connect the blower.
  4. Start with the inlet damper closed or the drive at its lowest speed, then raise flow slowly until the pressure is reached and steady. Do not overpressurize.
  5. Read the pressure drop across the orifice and convert it to cfm with the maker's table for that orifice size.
  6. If the reading is over the allowed number, hunt for leaks (smoke helps), repair, let the sealant cure, and retest, using a smaller section if needed.

Worked example: 500 ft of 24 in. round spiral

Our own numbers, with assumptions: we assume a 500 ft test section of 24 in. round spiral duct, a test pressure of 3 in. w.c., and a leakage class CL of 4 (the code value above). None of these come from a real job.

Step 1, surface area. Diameter is 2 ft. Surface per foot of length is pi x 2 = 6.283 sq ft. Over 500 ft: 6.283 x 500 = 3,142 sq ft.

Step 2, allowed cfm per 100 sq ft. P^0.65 = 3^0.65 = 2.042. F = 4 x 2.042 = 8.17 cfm per 100 sq ft.

Step 3, total allowed. 3,142 / 100 = 31.42 units of 100 sq ft. 31.42 x 8.17 = 257 cfm.

Step 4, compare. If the rig holds 3.0 in. and reads 240 cfm, the section passes with 17 cfm to spare (240 / 257 is about 93 percent of the limit). At 270 cfm it fails, and you hunt for leaks.

Step 5, a sanity check. At 2 in. instead, 2^0.65 = 1.569, so F = 6.28 and the allowance drops to about 197 cfm. The allowance grows with pressure, but not as fast as the pressure does.

Spiral vs rectangular

McGill AirFlow, a spiral duct maker, says that for the same seal class rectangular duct leaks at least twice as much as round and flat oval, which matches the table above. SEMCO, another spiral maker, says it estimates 85 percent of rectangular leakage occurs at transverse joints and 99 percent of spiral leakage does, and that spiral comes in longer sections (10 ft or more versus 4 or 5 ft), so there are fewer joints to seal. SEMCO's example system at 2 in. and Seal Class C shows 37.7 cfm per 100 sq ft for rectangular and 18.8 for round. Both are makers with a stake in the answer, so treat the magnitudes as claims, but the direction (fewer joints, simpler seams) is consistent with SMACNA's table. Spiral lock seams need no sealant in Class B, which is part of why round is easier to test well.

Our own reading of the tables: CL 4 sits between round Class A (3) and round Class B (6), and below the tabulated 6 for rectangular Class A. So a rectangular system has to be built better than its table value to pass the code limit.

Sealants and gaskets

The seal class tells you where sealant goes; it does not pick a product. Follow the duct maker's and the specifier's instructions, and remember the sealant must cure before a retest. SEMCO describes a gasketed round joint system that it says exceeds Seal Class A without added sealant; that is a maker claim, not a code rule. For how sealants behave in the field, see Sealing Duct Leaks: Mastic vs Foil Tape and Where to Look.

Reading a test report

NEBB says a suggested report shows the duct surface area, test pressure, leakage rate, maximum allowed leakage, pass or fail, the section tested and the required air tightness class. The Connecticut presentation's sample form adds project information, ductwork, accessories and equipment sections, and a witness signature if required; its example reads allowed 344 cfm, measured 337 cfm, PASS. Here is what ours would look like with the example above (assumed values):

Check the arithmetic yourself: area, pressure and class should reproduce the allowed cfm the way the steps above did. A passing section is only the sections tested, which may be 25 percent of the job.

Field card: Commercial Duct Leakage Classes

Area x F / 100 = allowed cfm.

  • Pressure class = what the duct is built for. Test at that pressure, never above any component rating.
  • Seal class C: transverse joints. B: joints and seams. A: joints, seams and penetrations.
  • F = CL x P^0.65 (cfm per 100 sq ft, P in in. w.c.).
  • Codes cited here: CL 4.0, ducts above 3 in. (check "above" vs "at"), at least 25 percent tested.
  • Example: 3,142 sq ft, 3 in., CL 4 gives 8.17 and 257 cfm (assumed values).
  • If it fails: find leaks, seal, let it cure, retest.
  • Report: area, pressure, class, allowed, measured, pass or fail, section.
