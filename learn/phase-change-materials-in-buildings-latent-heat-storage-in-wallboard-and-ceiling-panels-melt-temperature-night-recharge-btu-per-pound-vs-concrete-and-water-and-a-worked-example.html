Phase Change Materials in Buildings: Latent Heat Storage, Night Recharge and a Worked Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most materials store heat by getting warmer. A phase change material, or PCM, stores a large share of its heat while it melts, with the temperature barely moving. Pick one that melts near room temperature and a wall or ceiling can soak up a hot afternoon, then give the heat back at night. This lesson explains how that works, where you may meet it, and why it only pays off when it refreezes every day.

Latent heat versus sensible heat

Sensible heat is the heat you can feel as a temperature change. Latent heat is the heat a material absorbs or releases while it changes state at nearly constant temperature. Ice is the familiar case: a federal laboratory report from 1991 compares a pound of melting ice at 144 Btu with a generic PCM at about 100 Btu per pound of melting. A PCM works the same way, but the melt point is chosen near room temperature instead of 32 F. Our ice storage lesson covers the cold end of the same idea.

What the materials are, in general terms

Two families come up most. Paraffins are waxes. A 2021 national laboratory review says generic paraffin waxes melt between 45 and 70 C (113 to 158 F) and that pure single-chain waxes are generally needed for uses below 45 C. A 1991 report from the same lab lists single-chain paraffins that melt at 62 F, 69 F, 80 F, 87 F and 95 F, with heat of fusion roughly 77 to 109 Btu per pound. Salt hydrates are salts that hold water in their crystals. The 2021 review gives them a latent heat range of 100 to 290 kJ/kg (about 43 to 125 Btu per pound), with examples melting at 24 to 30 C and 31 to 32 C, which is 75 to 86 F and 88 to 90 F. A federal energy department project page calls salt hydrates nontoxic, nonflammable and less costly than most organic types, but notes they can undercool, melt unevenly after losing water and separate into layers.

Where PCM is used

Wallboard. The 1991 report tested paraffin soaked into gypsum wallboard, up to 35 percent paraffin by weight. At 30 percent, the board stored 25.2 Btu per pound of wallboard as latent heat. A later national laboratory paper modeled drywall with 20 percent PCM melting between 72.5 and 76.5 F, with a latent heat of 23 J/g (9.9 Btu per pound) of drywall, and found its effective specific heat jumps about 15 times during the phase change.

Ceiling panels and tiles. Another national laboratory paper measured full-size products with melt points near 69 F and 84 F, wrapped in polyethylene and thin aluminum foil, and described the PCM as form stable to limit leaks if a tile is damaged during installation.

Thermal storage tanks. The same latent idea is used in tanks and packs of sealed containers that charge at night and discharge by day. We describe that arrangement here in our own words, so the numbers in this lesson do not apply to any specific tank. Ice tanks are the common example.

Why it must melt and freeze every day

A PCM only helps while part of it is solid and part is liquid. Once it is fully melted, it behaves like any other material and stops storing extra heat. To work again it must freeze, which means the room has to fall below the freeze point and stay there long enough. A 1998 national laboratory conference paper record found that PCM wallboard plus mechanical night ventilation could allow cooling system downsizing in climates where night outdoor air drops below 18 C (64 F), and recommended other ways of discharging it where nights stay above 19 C (66 F). The same logic applies to a house: a hot, humid night that never gets below the freeze point leaves the PCM melted for the next afternoon. A 2023 national laboratory preprint adds that a PCM with a fixed melt temperature tuned for summer will not perform in winter, and that freezing a wall PCM took roughly 2 to 3 hours with strong heat transfer. Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example, not a published case. How much heat does a PCM load store over a 10 F swing, and how much concrete would it take to store the same heat?

Assumptions (ours). 100 lb of PCM. Latent heat 80 Btu/lb, in the range of the technical-grade paraffin in the 1991 report (about 82 Btu/lb). Melt range 72 to 78 F, inside a 70 to 80 F swing. Sensible heat of the PCM in the liquid or solid state 0.43 Btu/lb-F, which is the 1.8 J/g-K molten value from the national laboratory paper converted (1.8 / 4.184). Concrete 0.20 Btu/lb-F and 140 lb/ft3, from a table in a national laboratory conference paper. Water about 1 Btu/lb-F (a federal geological survey page gives 4,184 joules per kilogram per degree C) and 8.34 lb/gal (our standard value). We assume the PCM fully melts and fully refreezes each day.

  1. Latent heat: 100 lb x 80 Btu/lb = 8,000 Btu.
  2. Sensible heat over the 10 F swing, 4 F outside the melt range plus the melt range itself treated at the same rate (a simple approximation): 100 lb x 0.43 Btu/lb-F x 10 F = 430 Btu.
  3. Total PCM storage: 8,000 + 430 = 8,430 Btu, or 84.3 Btu per pound.
  4. Concrete over the same swing stores 0.20 x 10 = 2 Btu per pound. Mass needed: 8,430 / 2 = 4,215 lb.
  5. Ratio: 4,215 / 100 = about 42 times the mass. Volume: 4,215 / 140 = about 30 ft3, roughly 1.1 yd3.
  6. Water for comparison: 1 x 10 = 10 Btu per pound, so 8,430 / 10 = 843 lb, about 101 gallons.

Check: 4,215 lb x 2 Btu/lb = 8,430 Btu, and 843 lb x 10 Btu/lb = 8,430 Btu. The comparison depends on the swing. Over a wider swing the sensible part of concrete grows and the PCM advantage shrinks; over a narrower swing than the melt range, a PCM may never fully melt. For perspective, 8,430 Btu is about 0.7 ton-hours of cooling (12,000 Btu per ton-hour), storage that 100 lb of PCM provides in place of about 2 tons of concrete. Fire and leakage concerns

The federal energy department page lists flammability, low energy density, low thermal conductivity and high cost as hurdles for commercial paraffin and bio-based PCMs, and says payback on cost has often been long. The 2021 review lists flammability concerns for waxes and says all PCMs may degrade if exposed to the ambient air and need proper sealing. The 1991 report measured evaporation from a PCM board held at 100 F at about 0.17 lb per month for a 4 by 8 ft sheet, with oily droplets on cooler surfaces of the test chamber, and argued that painted or covered boards would not reach that temperature. It also found no paraffin migration after 200 melt and freeze cycles. The same report said flammability and fire retardants were still under study when it was written, so we do not teach a fire rating from it. Follow the building's fire rules and the maker's instructions.

What a tech might see

  • Ceiling tiles or wall panels that feel heavier than plain board. Confirm what they are before cutting or drilling; we do not know the contents of unmarked panels.
  • An oily stain or spot on a panel or ceiling. Our reading of the sources is that leakage is possible from damaged or overheated products, so report it instead of cleaning it up on your own.
  • A room that stays warm into the evening on a hot, humid night. The PCM may not be refreezing, as in the night-air limits above.
  • Cooling loads that look smaller than the glass and sun would suggest. Note it, but do not size equipment around PCM unless a designer's documents say how much credit to take.
  • Sealed containers or packs inside a storage tank. Treat them as the maker's documents describe and keep tank water temperatures within the design range.

Field card: Phase Change Materials in Buildings

PCM stores heat while it melts and gives it back while it freezes.

  • Melting holds temperature nearly steady while the material absorbs latent heat.
  • Paraffin and salt hydrate types melt at set temperatures; the melt point must match the room.
  • It only works if it fully melts by day and refreezes at night, so cool night air or night cooling is needed.
  • Our example: 100 lb of PCM at an assumed 80 Btu/lb stores about 8,430 Btu over 10 F, the same as about 4,200 lb of concrete.
  • Watch for flammability, leakage and subcooling; do not cut or drill unknown panels.
