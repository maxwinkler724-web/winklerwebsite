Surge Protection for AC, Heat Pumps and Furnaces: What It Does and Its Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A surge protector for an AC unit is a cheap-looking box with a long list of ratings on the label. This lesson explains what a surge does to modern HVAC electronics, how whole-home and at-the-unit devices differ, how to read the numbers, and the things no surge device can do.

What a surge does to HVAC electronics

Older equipment was mostly motors, a contactor and a capacitor. Today's equipment carries circuit boards: a furnace control board, an ECM blower motor with its own electronics, and in many heat pumps an inverter drive that runs the compressor. New York's Clean Heat program says a surge can leave a heat pump partly impaired or completely inoperable, and that a damaged circuit board cannot be repaired and must be replaced. Green Building Advisor makes the same point, adding that damage can come from one big event or from repeated small ones.

Where do surges come from? The Electrical Safety Foundation (ESFI) says external surges originate from electric utilities and lightning, and that most surges start inside the building, with an air conditioner switching on in summer as its example. That is why one device at the service panel is not the whole story.

Two places to protect: the panel and the unit

UL 1449 sorts surge protective devices (SPDs) into types. Summaries of the standard from Hubbell and from an industry overview describe them like this:

  • Type 1: connected on the utility side of the service disconnect, such as in or next to the meter socket.
  • Type 2: connected on the load side of the service disconnect, such as at the main panel or a branch panel.
  • Type 3: point-of-use devices, such as plug-in strips, installed at least 10 meters (30 feet) from the service panel.

A whole-home Type 1 or 2 device sits at the entrance and diverts surges before they spread through the house. ESFI says a point-of-use device protects only what is plugged into it. For a heat pump, the Clean Heat page describes a component device installed close to the unit, typically at the disconnect, and recommends using both types because whole-home protection may not catch surges created inside the house. See the condenser disconnect lesson for what lives in that box.

Is a whole-home device required?

A Leviton summary of NEC 230.67 (2020 edition) says every service supplying a dwelling unit must have an SPD, that it must be Type 1 or Type 2, and that it must be part of the service equipment or immediately next to it. It also says that when service equipment is replaced, the whole section applies. Green Building Advisor says the 2020 NEC added this for new homes and service upgrades, and the Clean Heat page says the 2023 NEC also has it. Which edition your town enforces is a local question. NFPA 70 is the copyrighted code book; we cite it by number and do not reproduce it.

Reading the label: VPR, In and SCCR

  • VPR (Voltage Protection Rating): the voltage the device lets through when tested with a 6 kV, 3 kA combination wave. Lower is better. The standard assigns it from a fixed list, 330 V up to 6 kV. The Hubbell summary warns not to compare a VPR with the older clamping or SVR numbers, because the test changed.
  • In (nominal discharge current): the surge current the device is tested with 15 times and must survive. Type 1 devices use 10 kA or 20 kA; Type 2 use 3, 5, 10 or 20 kA. A bigger In suggests more endurance, not a lower let-through voltage.
  • SCCR (short-circuit current rating): the fault current the device is tested to withstand without failing. It is about safety if the device fails, not about how well it clamps.

Worked example: comparing two published SPDs

Leviton's page for the R2120-B and a Square D data sheet for the SDSA1175 list these figures (both are 120/240 V split-phase Type 1 devices):

  • R2120-B: VPR 700 V line-to-neutral, 1200 V line-to-line; In 10 kA; SCCR 200 kA.
  • SDSA1175: VPR 700 V line-to-neutral, 1200 V line-to-line; In 10 kA; SCCR 25 kA.

Step 1, clamping: both show 700 V and 1200 V, so on these ratings they let through the same voltage. Step 2, endurance: both show In of 10 kA, a tie. Step 3, the difference: SCCR is 200 kA versus 25 kA, a ratio of 200 ÷ 25 = 8 times. The right choice depends on the fault current available at the service, which an electrician knows.

Now the voltage at the equipment. These numbers are illustrative, not a prediction. The test wave is 6 kV, so a 700 V VPR means the device held the test to about 700 V or less: 700 ÷ 6000 = 0.117, about 12 percent of the test voltage. A 240 V circuit peaks at 240 × 1.414 = 339 V, so 700 ÷ 339 = about 2.1 times the normal peak. For comparison, a Leviton spec sheet for a breaker-style SPD lists 600 V line-to-neutral. The difference from 700 V is 100 V, or 100 ÷ 700 = about 14 percent lower. Real installations add wire length and ground quality, so the number at the board can be higher than the label.

What an SPD cannot do

  • Direct lightning strikes. The Clean Heat page says no device available can protect against a direct strike.
  • Sustained high or low voltage. The Clean Heat page says surge protectors do not handle continuous overvoltage from a poorly regulated grid or aging wiring. A 208/230 V circuit above 253 V continuously can overheat and fail; call the utility. A Constellation article says standard surge protectors do not help while voltage sags in a brownout, though they do guard against the surge when it ends.

Indicator lights and replacement

Many units have status lights. A Leviton Type 1 manual says a green LED means surge protection is stable and a red LED means protection has failed, and that the unit sounds an alarm if a line loses protection. A Leviton spec sheet for a breaker-style unit says "Red LED = NOT Protected, Replace." Square D's sheet shows a green LED for normal operation. Check your own model's sheet, because lights differ. After a big storm or a nearby lightning event, look at the indicator. If it is red or dark, the unit is no longer protecting and needs replacement. A green light is good news but not a guarantee about the equipment.

Who installs it

ESFI recommends hiring a qualified, licensed electrician for this work, and the Leviton Type 1 manual states that installation of Type 1 devices must be performed by an electrician. Do not open the panel or meter socket yourself. For related restart questions, see Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start.

Field card: Surge Protection for HVAC

Lower VPR is better; no SPD stops a direct strike or a brownout.

  • Boards, ECM motors and inverter drives are the vulnerable parts.
  • Type 1/2 at the service; a second device at the disconnect catches surges made inside the house.
  • NEC 230.67 (2020) requires a Type 1 or 2 SPD on dwelling services.
  • VPR: let-through voltage (6 kV, 3 kA test). In: survives 15 surges. SCCR: fault current rating.
  • Example: 700 V VPR on a 6 kV test passes about 12 percent.
  • Red or dark indicator: replace.
  • Licensed electrician installs.
