Photo and Video Studio HVAC: Lighting Heat, Quiet Air, Haze and Pre-Cooling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A photo or video studio looks like an empty box, but it has two jobs that fight each other: pull out a lot of heat from the lights, and do it so quietly and gently that microphones, paper backdrops and hair never notice. This lesson explains how techs think about that, with a worked example of our own.

Why studios are different

An office is cooled for people and computers. A studio is cooled mostly for lights, and the lights are only on during shoots. Load swings from nearly nothing to a heavy peak in minutes. Add a tall ceiling, a smooth painted cyclorama wall that blends floor and background, and a crew that needs near silence during takes, and ordinary diffusers and a loud rooftop unit become the problem. For the sound side in a smaller room, see Recording Studio and Broadcast Booth HVAC. For rooms with an audience, see Theater and Auditorium HVAC and Movie Theater HVAC. We do not repeat those here.

Heat from the lights: hot lights versus LED

Nearly every watt a light draws ends up as heat in the room, because the light itself is absorbed by walls, floors and people and becomes heat too. That is why we count lighting by its electrical watts. The U.S. Department of Energy says incandescent bulbs release 90 percent of their energy as heat, and describes LEDs as using energy far more efficiently with little wasted heat. A federal lighting field brief reports that halogen theatrical fixtures draw 575 to 2,000 watts each, and that LED theatrical lighting with colored gels saved 50 to 90 percent compared with halogen. Older tungsten fixtures behave like that halogen group. Older daylight-balanced discharge fixtures also draw large power, and their ballasts and cables add to the room. A modern LED panel kit can light the same scene with far fewer watts, so the cooling load drops with it.

Two cautions. First, the LED saving is real, but a studio may keep a few hot lights for the look they give, so ask what will actually be on. Second, the panel's power supplies and dimmers are often in the room too, so count their watts. We never size from a fixture's lumen rating, only from the electrical load.

Turning watts into Btu per hour and tons

A Department of Energy notice states "1 kWh = 3,412 Btu." A kilowatt running for an hour is a kilowatt-hour, so each kilowatt of lighting adds 3,412 Btu/h of heat. A ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h. For the airflow, we use the standard sensible-heat relation Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference, where Q is in Btu/h, cfm is airflow, and the temperature difference is in degrees F. Where the 1.08 comes from is covered in our lesson on the 1.08 factor; it assumes ordinary air near sea level.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a real studio. Assumptions: a 30 ft by 40 ft studio with a 16 ft ceiling. Older hot-light setup: 12,000 W of tungsten fixtures on at once. LED setup for the same scenes: 3,000 W. This is a 75 percent cut, inside the 50 to 90 percent range the federal brief reports, but it is our assumption. All lighting watts become heat in the room. We hold the air to a 20 F rise between supply and room air. People, cameras and computers are left out so the lights stand alone.

  1. Hot lights: 12,000 W = 12 kW. 12 x 3,412 = 40,944 Btu/h. In tons: 40,944 / 12,000 = 3.41 tons.
  2. LED lights: 3,000 W = 3 kW. 3 x 3,412 = 10,236 Btu/h. In tons: 10,236 / 12,000 = 0.85 tons.
  3. Difference: 40,944 - 10,236 = 30,708 Btu/h. In tons: 30,708 / 12,000 = 2.56 tons. Switching to LED removes about two and a half tons of lighting load in this example.
  4. Airflow, hot lights: cfm = Q / (1.08 x temperature difference) = 40,944 / (1.08 x 20) = 40,944 / 21.6 = 1,896 cfm.
  5. Airflow, LED: 10,236 / 21.6 = 474 cfm.
  6. Difference: 1,896 - 474 = 1,422 cfm less air needed with LED.

Why the airflow matters for noise: more air through the same duct means higher velocity. A university facilities design standard asks that duct velocity stay at 1,500 feet per minute or below. Our arithmetic: 1,896 cfm / 1,500 fpm = 1.26 sq ft, about 182 square inches of duct for the hot-light case, against about 46 square inches of duct for the LED case (474 / 1,500 = 0.316 sq ft). That is only the lighting. A real design adds the other loads and goes through an acoustic review, since that standard also says the noise target for studios is set with an acoustical consultant, not by a rule of thumb.

Quiet, still air

Studios want little sound and little air movement on the set. Paper backdrops flutter, hair moves, and microphones pick up fan hiss. The same university standard says to place registers, grilles and diffusers 5 to 10 duct diameters away from dampers, elbows and branch takeoffs, since turbulence at those fittings makes noise. In practice we want large, low-velocity diffusers on the walls or high up, aimed away from the set, with the return high above the lights so the heat is picked up where it is made. Supply grilles aimed at the backdrop are a common cause of complaints. See Supply Registers and Return Grilles for throw and noise basics.

Tall stages and cyclorama walls

Tall open stages hold a lot of air above the crew, and the warm air from the lights rises into it. That is a reason for a high return, as in Figure 1. The curved, seamless cyclorama wall is finished to look smooth on camera, so grilles and wall penetrations on its face usually are not welcome; talk with the studio about where air may enter. These are layout habits we use, not code rules.

Haze, fog and smoke detectors

Haze and fog effects can set off smoke detectors. A state university fire marshal's page says smoke and haze can trip detectors and activate the building fire alarm, can hide exit routes, and must be reviewed and approved before the event, with notice given to building occupants. Staff may need to monitor the alarm system. As an HVAC tech, do not disable or cover a detector yourself. Ask the building's fire authority how the studio handles it. Because moving air carries haze, supply placement changes how it hangs in the room, which is a good thing to discuss with the crew. Escape rooms face the same issue, covered in our lesson on small closed rooms with fog.

Makeup, wardrobe, equipment and charging rooms

  • Makeup and wardrobe. Talent sits under bright mirror lights, so these small rooms get warm. Give them their own supply and a way to adjust, or a zone.
  • Equipment and charging rooms. Chargers, servers and storage drives give off heat all day, even on non-shoot days. Add up their watts and use the same 3,412 Btu/h per kW conversion. They may need their own cooling that keeps running.

Humidity for paper and props

Seamless paper and wood or fabric props are sensitive to damp and dry air. The Library of Congress recommends keeping paper in a cool, relatively dry (about 35 percent relative humidity), clean and stable place, away from locations with environmental extremes. A studio's shooting floor is a different setting, so the studio sets its own target. As a tech, the useful goal is stable humidity: avoid wild swings, and keep supply air from blowing across stored rolls.

Pre-cooling before a shoot

The lights add load the moment they go on, so a room that starts at setpoint stays steadier than one that starts warm. Our suggestion is to run the system ahead of call time and let it settle, so that it does not have to run flat out, and loud, during takes. A scheduled start tied to the booking calendar is the usual way. This is a practical suggestion of ours, not a published rule.

Field card: Photo and Video Studio HVAC: Lighting Heat, Quiet Air, Haze and Pre-Cooling

Count the watts, keep the air slow and far from the set.

  • Nearly all lighting watts become room heat; each kW is 3,412 Btu/h.
  • One ton is 12,000 Btu/h.
  • Airflow for a given rise: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature difference).
  • Keep duct velocity at 1,500 fpm or below where quiet matters.
  • Diffusers large, low velocity, aimed away from the set; return high.
  • Haze can trip detectors; leave detectors to the building's fire authority.
  • Do not forget makeup, wardrobe and charging rooms.
