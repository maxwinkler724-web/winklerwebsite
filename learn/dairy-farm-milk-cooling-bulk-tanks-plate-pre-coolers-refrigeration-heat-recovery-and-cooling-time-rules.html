Dairy Farm Milk Cooling: Bulk Tanks, Plate Pre-Coolers and Heat Recovery

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dairy farm is a refrigeration job with a deadline. Milk leaves the cow warm, and the tank has to pull it down fast. This lesson walks through the equipment between the milking pipeline and the tank, the cooling rules that set the clock, and a load calculation you can redo with your own numbers.

The cooling clock

Grade "A" milk is governed by the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance (PMO), a model rule published by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration that states adopt. An FDA/NCIMS document that quotes the 2017 PMO, Item 18r (Raw Milk Cooling), gives the raw milk rule as: cool to 50 F or less within four hours of the start of the first milking, and to 45 F or less within two hours after milking is complete, with the blend temperature after the first and later milkings not exceeding 50 F. FDA Form 2359 uses the same 45 F number for raw milk held until it is processed. The PMO is revised on a cycle, so a tech should ask the farm or the state dairy inspector which edition applies and read the current item. We could not open the current edition's Item 18r text, so treat the numbers here as the 2017 wording.

How the tank makes cold

Most bulk tanks use direct expansion (DX). The Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board (AHDB, the UK levy board) describes the evaporator as the outer jacket of the inner tank, so refrigerant cools the milk through the steel with few heat transfer steps. That makes the compressor a peak-load machine: it is sized for the load during and soon after milking, and the AHDB notes that electrical supply can become the limit when the compressor competes with vacuum pumps and other loads. DX also cannot run on cheap night electricity, because it has to run when the milk arrives.

An ice bank system builds ice in a separate jacket ahead of time, and the ice then chills the milk. AHDB says DX tanks often replace old ice bank tanks, since the ice jacket takes space and DX holds more milk in the same footprint. An ice builder can also be paired with a DX tank so ice made on cheaper power pre-cools milk through a plate heat exchanger. Irish research reported by the Farmers Journal found an ice bank used about 73 percent more energy than DX to cool milk. For the storage idea in a building, see Ice Thermal Energy Storage for Commercial Cooling.

The plate pre-cooler

A plate cooler is a plate heat exchanger with milk on one side and well water on the other, placed in the line between the receiver and the tank. University of Wisconsin Extension says milk arrives at roughly 95 to 98 F and well water runs about 50 to 55 F. A properly sized unit can lower the milk by up to 40 F, cutting refrigeration energy by about 60 percent, and can bring milk to within 3 to 4 F of the water temperature. Wisconsin recommends a 1:1 water-to-milk flow ratio; makers advertise 1:1 to 3:1, but it says half as much water as milk is common on real farms, which hurts results. Undersized water lines and water supply capacity are the two main reasons pre-coolers underperform. A NYSERDA audit report adds that plate coolers pay off when there are adequate volumes of milk and cooling water. Fouling and approach temperature are covered in Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach.

Refrigeration heat recovery

The compressor's discharge gas is hot. A refrigeration heat recovery (RHR) unit puts a heat exchanger in that line and preheats water. Wisconsin Extension says refrigerant can exceed 200 F entering the exchanger, that the unit can recover 20 to 60 percent of the heat removed from the milk, and that water reaches up to about 140 F depending on farm conditions. In its example, water heated from 55 F to 100 F by recovery and then to 165 F by the water heater saved an estimated 40 percent of water heating cost. Massachusetts Department of Agricultural Resources (MDAR) says recovery can cut water heating energy by more than 50 percent in its model. The same page prices a 50-gallon unit at $3,000 and a 120-gallon unit at $4,200. Pre-coolers and RHR compete for the same heat: Wisconsin says that if all recovered heat can be used, adding a precooler can raise overall energy cost, and that maximizing RHR is usually more cost-effective, so an audit comes first. The same logic on larger plants is in Heat Recovery Chillers.

Compressors and pumps

Farm Energy (extension.org), excerpting Wisconsin Extension, says scroll compressors are 15 to 20 percent more efficient than reciprocating compressors, have fewer moving parts, and recommends specifying them when buying a tank or replacing a failed compressor. MDAR gives the same 15 to 20 percent figure. See Chiller Compressors: Centrifugal, Screw and Scroll Compared. For variable speed drives, results are mixed. MDAR says variable speed vacuum pumps can save 30 to 65 percent of their energy and that variable speed milk pumps may raise plate cooler savings. The NYSERDA audit estimated a 33 percent vacuum pump cut, but it did not recommend milk pump drives on any farm and gave no savings for them. Check the measurements before you promise a number.

Worked example: tank load and pre-cooler credit

Assumptions (ours): 1,000 gal of milk per milking, arriving at 95 F, with the 45 F target from the PMO wording above. For properties we use classroom values from a Purdue engineering problem: specific heat 0.94 Btu/lb-F and density 64 lb/ft³. (These are not dairy-lab values, but they agree with Wisconsin's 56 Btu per pound for a 60 F drop, about 0.93.) Well water is assumed at 55 F, the pre-cooler is assumed to deliver milk at 65 F, and we ignore heat gains and agitator heat.

  1. Mass: 64 lb/ft³ ÷ 7.481 gal/ft³ = 8.555 lb/gal, so 1,000 gal is about 8,555 lb.
  2. Load with no pre-cooler: 8,555 × 0.94 × (95 − 45) = about 402,000 Btu. At 12,000 Btu per ton-hour that is 33.5 ton-hours. If it must happen in two hours, the average duty is about 201,000 Btu/h, or 16.8 tons.
  3. Pre-cooler removes: 8,555 × 0.94 × (95 − 65) = about 241,000 Btu, which is 60 percent of the load. That matches the "about 60 percent" Wisconsin reports.
  4. The tank still removes: 8,555 × 0.94 × (65 − 45) = about 161,000 Btu.
  5. Water side: at a 1:1 volume ratio, 1,000 gal of water weighs about 8,340 lb (8.34 lb/gal, our standard value). The rise is 241,000 ÷ 8,340 = about 29 F, so water leaves near 84 F.
  6. Electricity: 241,000 Btu ÷ 3,412 Btu/kWh = 70.7 kWh of heat. At an assumed tank coefficient of performance of 2.5, the compressor saves about 28 kWh per milking.

Real units rarely hit these numbers: MDAR says a single-pass plate cooler typically cools milk from 98 F to 71 F, so a 65 F result needs more plates or more water.

Maintenance checks for a tech

Farm Energy lists two basics: keep coils clean, since dirty coils and low refrigerant pressure raise operating cost, and watch the sight glass, because bubbles instead of clear liquid mean the charge needs service by a refrigeration tech. Add the checks that follow from the pieces above: confirm the water flow to the pre-cooler against the milk flow, look at supply pipe size, and compare milk temperature leaving the plate cooler with the well water temperature (the Wisconsin 3 to 4 F figure is a best case, not a promise). Compare the tank's pull-down against the 45 F target, using a calibrated thermometer, and note the milk volume when you log it. Before any work on a tank, follow lockout procedures and your refrigerant handling rules; see Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

Field card: Dairy Milk Cooling

Pre-cool first, finish in the tank, and use the heat.

  • PMO (2017 text as quoted by FDA/NCIMS): 45 F or less within 2 hours after milking; blend not above 50 F.
  • DX tank: evaporator forms the tank jacket; compressor sized for the peak.
  • Plate cooler: aim for 1:1 water to milk; weak water supply is the usual fault.
  • Our example: 95 F to 45 F for 1,000 gal is about 402,000 Btu; a 65 F pre-cooler takes about 60 percent.
  • RHR recovers 20 to 60 percent of the milk heat per Wisconsin Extension.
  • Check: clean coils, sight glass, water flow, milk temperature out of the pre-cooler.
