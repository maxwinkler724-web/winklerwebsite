Fluorescent UV Dye Leak Detection: Matching Dye to Oil, Dosing, Inspection and Cleanup

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Fluorescent leak dye is oil with a glow. It is added to a running system, travels with the oil, and shows up under an ultraviolet lamp wherever oil seeps out. It is a good way to find small, stubborn leaks, but only when the dye matches the oil, the dose is right and the system is allowed to carry it. This lesson covers all of that, and where dye fits next to bubbles, electronic detectors and a nitrogen standing test.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Dye products and equipment makers disagree on dose, oil match and whether dye is allowed at all. Our sources are vehicle-maker service bulletins hosted by a federal safety agency and EPA pages. They are not HVAC dye labels, so treat their numbers as examples, and always follow the dye label and the equipment manual.

How dye finds a leak

Dye is not a sealant. It does not plug anything; it only marks where oil escapes. A sealant is meant to change the circuit; see Refrigerant Leak Sealants in AC Systems: What Makers and EPA Say. Because dye is carried by the oil, a leak shows as a glowing film or drip at the spot, and the glow can be traced uphill to its source. If a joint is leaking only vapor with no oil, there may be little to see.

Match the dye to the oil

One vehicle-maker bulletin says to match the dye to the compressor oil and refrigerant listed on the system label, and not to mix two different dyes. Another says dyes it does not approve use solvents that can contaminate the system oil. A third calls for non-conductive POE oil in electric compressors and a dye made for that use. The lesson for HVAC is to read the dye label for the oils it lists (mineral, alkylbenzene, POE or PVE) and confirm what is in the system. See Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE for the oil families. Oil type on the nameplate or in the manual beats a guess.

Dosing

Dose is set by the dye maker or equipment maker, either by oil in the system or by refrigerant charge. Two examples from vehicle bulletins: one recommends 1/4 oz of dye for every 4 lb of refrigerant, and another uses a single 0.06 oz (1.7 g) capsule per injection. More is not better. One bulletin warns that adding excessive dye can lead to compressor damage. Check first whether dye is already in the system by looking at a service port with the lamp.

Injecting without adding air or moisture

Vehicle bulletins describe these habits, and they carry over:

  • Purge the injector or hose of air before it touches the system. One bulletin says to evacuate a new or open-stored connect set for about 3 minutes.
  • Connect to the low side, with the system holding enough refrigerant to run the compressor and carry the dye. Two bulletins say not to connect the injector to an evacuated system and not to run the system while it is connected. One adds that a charge of about 0.05 lb of refrigerant can be used to carry the dye.
  • Wipe the port and coupler clean after disconnecting, and store dye away from sunlight.

Our own note: keep dye in sealed containers, because oil-based products pick up moisture from the air, and keep dye off bare skin and paint.

Run time, lamp and glasses

One bulletin says to run the system 5 to 10 minutes, since large leaks show right away, and at least 90 minutes for smaller leaks. It also suggests a return visit 24 to 48 hours later. Another says a small leak may need 24 to 48 hours of running with periodic rechecks. Inspect in low light with the lamp the dye maker pairs with the dye (one bulletin says not every UV lamp works well with its dye) and wear the yellow glasses, which make the glow stand out and protect your eyes. Never look into the lamp. Leaks glow bright yellow-green.

False positives and cleanup

Old dye from a past job, a spill during injection, or residue wiped onto a fitting can all glow and look like a fresh leak. One bulletin says residual dye gives a false indication and advises re-running the system for 5 to 10 minutes and looking again to confirm nothing new appears. After repair, clean the area with the dye maker's cleaner and a cloth, then check with the lamp that no glow remains. Our own habit: wipe suspect spots clean at the start, so any glow later is new.

When dye is a poor fit

  • Very slow leaks. Our own note: they may not leave enough oil to see within one visit, so plan repeat checks; one bulletin already allows 24 to 48 hours of running for small leaks.
  • Buried or hidden parts. Coils behind panels, slabs or insulation hide the glow. Dye may drip into a condensate pan or drain instead.
  • Maker limits. One condensing unit manual we cited elsewhere says not to use dye for leak detection, and one vehicle bulletin lists models that do not allow dye. Using dye where the maker forbids it may put a warranty at risk, so check first.
  • Shared tools. Our own note: dye left in gauges and hoses can show up on the next job, so clean them.

How dye fits with other methods

Dye finds oil, not gas. A bubble solution shows gas escaping at an accessible spot. An electronic detector sniffs for refrigerant and works well for a quick sweep; see Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors. A nitrogen standing test confirms a repair holds pressure and is the usual check before charging; see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods. One bulletin says dye can be used for initial checks but should be confirmed with an electronic detector. A common order is electronic sweep, dye if allowed, bubbles at the suspect joint, then nitrogen after repair.

EPA rules, only what we read

EPA's page for appliances holding 50 or more pounds of refrigerant gives trigger leak rates per 12 months of 30 percent (industrial process refrigeration), 20 percent (commercial refrigeration) and 10 percent (comfort cooling and other appliances), a 30-day repair period (120 days if an industrial shutdown is needed), and initial and follow-up verification tests after repair. The page does not name dye. Dye is one way for a certified technician to locate a leak, not a required method. For the rate math, see Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances.

Worked example (our own)

A split-system heat pump has a 7 lb 8 oz refrigerant charge and a stated 20 oz of POE oil. These two figures are our assumptions for practice. We also assume the dye label allows the vehicle-bulletin ratio of 1/4 oz per 4 lb of refrigerant. That ratio is an assumption here; on a real job use the dye label.

  1. Convert the charge: 7 lb 8 oz = 7.5 lb.
  2. Divide by the ratio: 7.5 lb ÷ 4 lb = 1.875 units.
  3. Dose: 1.875 × 0.25 oz = 0.47 oz of dye.
  4. Check against oil: 0.47 oz ÷ 20 oz = 0.023, or about 2.3 percent of the oil volume. If the label sets a limit by oil, compare it here.

Inspection order: (1) lamp the service ports first for old dye; (2) inject, run the system; (3) check ports, flare nuts and braze joints at the outdoor unit; (4) the line set joints; (5) indoor coil return bends, drain pan and drain line; (6) re-lamp after the longer run or the return visit. Repair, clean, and finish with a nitrogen standing test.

Field card: Fluorescent UV Dye Leak Detection: Matching Dye to Oil, Dosing, Inspection and Cleanup

Dye marks oil, not gas; match it to the oil.

  • Dye is not a sealant; it only marks where oil escapes.
  • Dye label must list the system oil; do not mix dyes.
  • Dose per the label. Assumed example: 1/4 oz per 4 lb gives 0.47 oz for 7.5 lb.
  • Purge the injector, never inject into an evacuated system, keep air and moisture out.
  • Run 5 to 10 minutes for big leaks; small leaks need hours or a return visit.
  • Lamp in low light, yellow glasses, never look into the lamp.
  • Clear old dye first; clean up after repair; confirm with nitrogen.
