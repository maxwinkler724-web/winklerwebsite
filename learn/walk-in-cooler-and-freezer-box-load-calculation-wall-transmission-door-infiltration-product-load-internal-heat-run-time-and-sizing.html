Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Box Load Calculation: Transmission, Infiltration, Product, Internal Heat and Run Time

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Sizing a walk-in starts with one question: how much heat has to leave the box in a day? The answer is the sum of a few separate loads. This lesson walks through each one, shows why the daily total is divided by a run time, and works a small cooler from start to finish.

The parts of a box load

A refrigeration load calculation counts every source of heat that reaches the cold space over 24 hours, in Btu per day. A university thesis on walk-in test methods describes the same split used in federal test procedures: heat through the envelope, heat carried in by air through door openings, heat from product, and heat from lights, people and other equipment. A 1960s-era maker training manual on the refrigeration load (a compressor maker's elementary text) uses four headings that we will follow: transmission, infiltration, product and supplementary (internal) loads.

  • Transmission. Heat conducting through the walls, ceiling and floor.
  • Infiltration. Warm, humid air that replaces cold air each time a door opens.
  • Product. Heat removed to bring incoming product down to box temperature, plus respiration heat if the product is living produce.
  • Internal. Lights, people, evaporator fan motors and defrost heaters.

Transmission: U, area and temperature difference

The maker manual gives the basic form: heat gain equals U times area times temperature difference. U is the reciprocal of total R, so a panel rated R-28 has a U of 1/28, about 0.036 Btu/h per square foot per degree F. Panel makers print R-value as a total for the panel, so there is usually no need to add layers. Online university produce-room calculators work the same way, summing R-values for each surface and applying them to wall, ceiling and floor areas.

Federal rules set a floor on that R-value. A 2023 DOE notice summarizing the standards in 10 CFR 431.306 lists minimum R-values of 25 for medium-temperature (cooler) wall and ceiling panels, 32 for low-temperature (freezer) wall and ceiling panels, and 28 for freezer floors. That is why a freezer panel is thicker than a cooler panel at the same temperature difference, and why a freezer has a bigger temperature difference to carry.

The temperature on the outside of each surface matters. A wall in a hot room sees the room temperature; a floor on a slab sees a cooler ground temperature. Sun on an outdoor wall raises the effective temperature difference; the maker manual handles that by increasing the temperature difference from a table.

Infiltration: the door and the air

Each time a door opens, cold air spills out the bottom and warm air flows in at the top. That air has to be cooled and, because it carries moisture, dried. The thesis above lists the standard test-method forms: a sensible term of 1.1 times airflow in cfm times the temperature difference, and a latent term of 5500 times cfm times the humidity-ratio difference. The maker manual offers two simpler paths: an air-change estimate from a table based on box volume, or a velocity method based on door height and temperature difference. Either way, you end with cubic feet of air per day and a heat content per cubic foot taken from the entering and leaving conditions.

This is the load that moves the most with how the box is used. A box that is opened a few times a day and a box where staff walk in and out all shift can have very different infiltration. A university produce-room calculator suggests about 1 air change per hour for a well-sealed long-term store and closer to 3 for a cooler that is opened often. Strip curtains and tight door habits cut it; see the lesson linked below.

Product load and respiration

Product load above freezing is weight times specific heat times the drop in temperature. The same university calculator suggests 0.85 Btu per pound per degree F as a reasonable value for most produce. Below freezing the maker manual adds a latent term for freezing and a different specific heat for frozen product. Product also arrives all at once, not evenly, so the calculator asks for a pull-down time; its default is 24 hours, with faster times for fresh-market crops.

Fruits and vegetables are alive. The maker manual notes that they keep generating heat after harvest while meat and fish do not, and that the rate depends on storage temperature. The university calculator converts a carbon dioxide production rate into Btu per hour. Respiration heat is large for some crops and small for others, which is why a produce room and a beverage cooler of the same size can need very different equipment. See the produce cold storage lesson for more.

Internal loads

  • Lights. The maker manual gives 3.41 Btu per watt-hour. Count only the hours the lights are on.
  • People. The manual's sample uses 840 Btu per hour per person. Multiply by hours of occupancy.
  • Evaporator fan motors. The manual says all motor energy ends up as load in the cold space. Fan motors run most of the day, so they add up. The efficiency lesson linked below covers how motor type changes this.
  • Defrost heaters. Electric defrost heat also lands in the box or on the coil. A cooler running above freezing often defrosts with the compressor off and no heater, so the heater term can be zero. A freezer usually has one, and it counts.

Test-method thesis note: the federal box-load equations leave defrost and fan heat out of the box load and treat them as equipment-added heat. For a sizing calculation, we count them because the evaporator still has to remove them.

Why we divide by run time

A refrigeration system cannot run 24 hours a day. The coil frosts and must defrost, and a system that never cycled off could not recover. So the daily total is divided by the hours the compressor is expected to run, not 24. The maker manual gives these ranges: 18 to 20 hours when the evaporator is kept at 30 F or higher with no frost; 16 to 18 hours for storage at 35 F or higher that frosts and defrosts by stopping the compressor; and about 18 hours is usually acceptable for low-temperature rooms with positive defrost. A university calculator page also cites 16 hours of running in a 24-hour day, 67 percent, to allow for defrost.

Dividing by 16 raises the hourly load compared with dividing by 24. The result is the capacity the equipment must deliver at the design condition, not the average load.

The safety factor

The maker manual says 5 to 10 percent is often added for uncertain loads, and it also warns that too much margin can oversize equipment and cause trouble at light load. Oversized equipment short-cycles and can leave the box wetter or less steady. Use the factor for real uncertainty, not as a habit.

Worked example (our own)

This example is ours. It is not from any source and the inputs are assumptions. Real equipment selection must follow the equipment maker's rating tables and the designer's full calculation.

Box. 8 ft wide by 10 ft long by 8 ft high, cooler at 35 F, in a 90 F space. Assumed panels: 4 in thick, total R-28 (our assumption; it meets the federal R-25 floor for cooler panels). U = 1/28 = 0.0357. Assume the floor sits over a 70 F slab (our assumption).

Areas. Walls: 2 x (8 x 8) + 2 x (10 x 8) = 128 + 160 = 288 ft2. Ceiling: 8 x 10 = 80 ft2. Floor: 80 ft2.

Transmission (Btu/h = U x A x temperature difference).

  • Walls: 0.0357 x 288 x (90 - 35 = 55) = 565.7 Btu/h
  • Ceiling: 0.0357 x 80 x 55 = 157.1 Btu/h
  • Floor: 0.0357 x 80 x (70 - 35 = 35) = 100.0 Btu/h
  • Total: 822.8 Btu/h x 24 h = 19,747, rounded to 19,750 Btu/day

Infiltration. Volume 8 x 10 x 8 = 640 ft3. Assume 20 air changes in 24 hours (our assumption for a lightly used box): 640 x 20 = 12,800 ft3/day. Assume entering air at 90 F and 50 percent humidity has about 37 Btu per pound and cooler air leaving at 35 F and 90 percent has about 12 Btu per pound (our psychrometric estimates), a difference of about 25 Btu per pound. At about 0.075 lb per ft3, that is 1.9 Btu per ft3. 12,800 x 1.875 = 24,000 Btu/day.

Product. Assume 300 lb per day of packaged product arriving at 60 F with a specific heat of 0.85 (the university calculator's suggested value for most produce). 300 x 0.85 x (60 - 35 = 25) = 6,375 Btu/day. We assume no respiring produce is stored; a produce box would add a respiration term.

Internal.

  • Lights: assume 60 W for 4 hours = 240 Wh x 3.41 = 818 Btu/day
  • People: assume one person for 2 hours x 840 Btu/h = 1,680 Btu/day
  • Evaporator fans: assume two fans at 35 W each = 70 W x 24 h = 1,680 Wh x 3.41 = 5,729 Btu/day
  • Defrost heaters: none (off-cycle defrost assumed) = 0
  • Internal total: 8,227 Btu/day

24-hour total. 19,750 + 24,000 + 6,375 + 8,227 = 58,352 Btu/day.

Divide by run time. At 16 hours: 58,352 / 16 = 3,647 Btu/h. With an assumed 10 percent safety factor: 58,352 x 1.10 = 64,187 Btu/day, / 16 = 4,012 Btu/h. Note that infiltration is the largest single part in this example (41 percent), and that is entirely a product of our assumed air changes, so check that number first on a real job.

Freezers and other cautions

A freezer has a larger temperature difference, thicker panels, heated door frames and defrost heaters, and a longer run-time assumption (about 18 hours in the maker manual for positive defrost). The thesis also notes that published test-method inputs and field data can differ, so treat any single calculation as an estimate and cross-check against the equipment maker's calculation guide.

Field card: Walk-In Box Load

Add the four loads for 24 hours, then divide by run time.

  • Transmission = U x area x temperature difference, per surface.
  • Infiltration is door openings: air volume times heat per cubic foot.
  • Product = weight x specific heat x temperature drop, plus respiration for produce.
  • Internal = lights, people, fan motors, defrost heaters.
  • Run time: about 16 to 18 hours for coolers, about 18 for freezers (maker manual).
  • Safety factor 5 to 10 percent for uncertainty only.
