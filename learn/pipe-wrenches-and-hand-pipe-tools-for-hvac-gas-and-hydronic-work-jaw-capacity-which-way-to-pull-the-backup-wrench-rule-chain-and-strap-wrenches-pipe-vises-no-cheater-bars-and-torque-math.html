Pipe Wrenches and Hand Pipe Tools for HVAC, Gas and Hydronic Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pipe wrench looks simple, and that is why people misuse it. On a new gas or hydronic install it is the tool that tightens every threaded joint, and also the tool that cracks a valve body, twists a union or loads a bent handle into your face. This lesson covers how the wrench bites, which way to pull, why a second wrench belongs on the other side of the joint, and what to use when a pipe wrench is the wrong tool.

How a pipe wrench grips

A pipe wrench has two toothed jaws. The movable hook jaw sits at the end of the head. The fixed heel jaw is part of the frame. The hook jaw is loose in its slot on purpose. When you pull the handle, the load pushes the hook jaw into the pipe and the teeth dig in. When you release and swing back, the jaw lets go, which is what makes the tool ratchet.

A university engineering-lab page that reproduces a national occupational-safety fact sheet gives three habits we repeat on every job. Set the jaw so there is a small gap between the back of the hook jaw and the pipe, which puts the pressure on the teeth. Face the wrench forward so the pressure bears on the heel jaw. Keep the teeth clean and sharp.

Which way to pull. Pull the handle toward you, with your feet planted. The same page says to pull rather than push. Our own field reading of the jaw shape: the open mouth of the hook jaw faces the direction of turning, so the wrench tightens or loosens in only one direction per placement. If the wrench slips and spins on the pipe, flip it over. Do not keep forcing it. A pull keeps your hand and body moving away from the work if the wrench lets go, while a push drives your knuckles toward the pipe and anything sharp near it.

Choosing a size

Wrenches are sold by overall length, and each length has a maximum pipe size it can grip. The exact capacity for a given wrench is stamped on it or listed by its maker, so we do not guess a table here. Read the tool-maker's manual or catalog for your wrench. The practical rule from the same safety page: pick a wrench with enough capacity and leverage for the job, and if you need more leverage, get a larger wrench instead of adding a pipe extension to a small one.

A wrench that is far too big is also a problem. It makes it easy to apply more torque than a small fitting, valve or appliance connection can take. Match the wrench to the joint, not to the biggest tool in the truck.

Never use a cheater bar or pipe extension

Several university tool-safety documents say it plainly. One says never to use cheater bars to increase force on hand tools. Another says not to lengthen a wrench with a pipe or other cheater, because excessive force can make the wrench or the fastener slip or break. A broken wrench handle or a jaw that lets go suddenly sends your body into whatever is behind you. Federal construction rules also bar employers from issuing or permitting unsafe hand tools. A wrench with a pipe slipped over its handle is not the tool the maker built or rated.

The backup wrench rule

When you tighten a pipe into a valve, union, or appliance gas valve, the torque you apply to the pipe does not disappear. It travels through the threads into the body you are screwing into. If that body is only held by the piping on its other side, it twists the other joints, strains a union, or turns a valve body against its own seat. The fix is a second wrench on a flat of the fitting body, placed so its pull opposes the first.

The backup wrench provides the reaction. Whatever torque you put on the pipe, the backup wrench has to supply the same torque, in the opposite direction, or the body turns. This is our own mechanical reasoning, and the worked example below puts numbers on it. In practice:

  • Hold the valve or union body, not the stem, handle or any soft attachment.
  • On an appliance gas valve or a regulator, hold the body flat that the maker provides for this. If you cannot find a flat or a safe hold, stop and read the appliance instructions before you load the joint.
  • Do not clamp a pipe wrench across a thin-walled or cast body, where the teeth can crack it. Use a wrench with a smooth grip on the flats.
  • Keep the two wrenches working toward each other, so both are pulling and your hands stay clear of the gap between the handles.

Other tools for other surfaces

  • Adjustable wrench on flats. Use it on hex flats of unions, valves and fittings. Set the jaw snug so the movable jaw takes up all the slack, and position it so the pull loads the fixed jaw. A sprung or loose adjustable wrench rounds the flats. Federal construction rules say wrenches, including adjustable and pipe wrenches, must not be used when the jaws are sprung.
  • Chain and strap wrenches. Their teeth do not bite, so they suit finished, plated, thin-wall or plastic pipe, where pipe-wrench teeth would mark or crush it. Plastic pipe and fittings in particular are easy to over-tighten, so use only as much force as the maker's instructions allow.
  • Pipe vise and tripod stand. Cutting and threading is easier and safer with the pipe held in a vise on a stable stand than with the pipe in your hand or on a knee. Support long pieces at the far end so the weight does not pull the stand over.

The chain, strap and vise points are our own shop practice. Follow the tool-maker's manual for the tool in your hand.

Inspect before every use

University safety documents agree here. One says hand tools must be inspected before each use. Another says wrenches must not be used when jaws are sprung to the point that slippage occurs. A third says to discard wrenches with spread, nicked or battered jaws, to discard any with a loose, broken or bent handle, and not to try to straighten a bent wrench, which weakens it further. For pipe wrenches it adds that you should confirm the jaws are still sharp, because slippage causes injuries. The page about pipe wrenches also says to check for worn threads on the adjusting ring and the movable jaw. Tag or discard a bad wrench. Do not leave it in the box for the next person.

Hands, pinch points and valve bodies

  • Keep fingers on the handle, not between the handle and the pipe or wall. When the joint breaks loose, the handle swings toward the pipe.
  • If you must push, one university document says to use the heel of the hand with the fingers not wrapped around the tool, and to avoid cocking the wrench.
  • Check where your hand lands if the wrench slips. If the answer is "against a hanger, a wall or a sharp edge," change your stance or your tool.
  • Protect valve bodies. Put the backup wrench on a flat, avoid clamping across thin walls, and keep teeth off finished surfaces. A scored valve body or a distorted seat can leak later.

For grinders, saws and other powered tools, see hand and power tool safety for HVAC techs.

Worked example (our own): why a longer wrench is a bigger load

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a rating for any tool.

Assumptions. You pull with 60 pounds of force, square to the handle. The effective arm, measured from the center of the pipe to where your hand grips the handle, is 1.25 ft on a shorter wrench and 2.5 ft on a wrench twice as long. The backup wrench has a 1.0 ft effective arm.

Step 1, torque with the shorter wrench. Torque equals force times arm: 60 lbf × 1.25 ft = 75 ft-lb.

Step 2, torque with the longer wrench. The same pull: 60 lbf × 2.5 ft = 150 ft-lb. Doubling the handle doubles the torque without any extra effort, which is why a longer wrench, and even more a pipe slipped over the handle, can overload a joint before you feel it.

Step 3, what the backup wrench must do. It must supply the same torque the pipe delivers into the body. With the shorter wrench that is 75 ft-lb, so the force on a 1.0 ft backup arm is 75 ÷ 1.0 = 75 lbf. With the longer wrench it is 150 ft-lb, or 150 lbf on the same backup arm. That is more than twice your own pull of 60 lbf, which is a sign you are asking too much of the joint or the setup.

If the backup wrench is not there, the body takes that torque through the rest of the piping, and the weakest joint turns or breaks first. A longer backup arm lowers the force you need, but the torque it must carry is unchanged.

Field card: Pipe Wrenches and Hand Pipe Tools for HVAC, Gas and Hydronic Work

Pull, don't push. Back up the body. Never extend the handle.

  • Set the hook jaw with a small gap, face the wrench so the load is on the heel jaw, and pull.
  • Always use a second wrench on a flat of the valve, union or appliance valve body.
  • No cheater bars or pipe extensions. Get a bigger wrench instead.
  • Chain or strap wrench for finished or plastic pipe.
  • Discard wrenches with sprung or battered jaws, or loose, bent or cracked handles.
  • Same pull on a handle twice as long gives twice the torque (our example: 75 vs 150 ft-lb).
