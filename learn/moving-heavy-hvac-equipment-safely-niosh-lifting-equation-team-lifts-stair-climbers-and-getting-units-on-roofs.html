Moving Heavy HVAC Equipment Safely: NIOSH Lifting Equation, Team Lifts, Stair Climbers and Getting Units on Roofs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Water heaters, furnaces, coils and condensers are awkward as well as heavy. Most back injuries in the trade come from the lift itself, not from a fall. This lesson shows how the NIOSH lifting equation sizes up a lift, when to use a team or a machine, and where a rigger takes over.

Check the rules where you work

Check your own rules. This page is general education built from federal public sources read in 2026. Your employer, the equipment maker and local rules decide what is allowed on your job. Every weight in the worked example is our own assumption, not a limit for your lift.

Why lifting is a hazard worth measuring

OSHA lists lifting heavy items, bending, reaching overhead, pushing or pulling heavy loads and awkward postures as known risk factors for musculoskeletal disorders, and says these disorders are among the most common causes of lost or restricted work time. NIOSH uses a lifting equation to turn that idea into a number. The idea behind it: start from an ideal lift, then cut the allowed weight for every way the real lift is worse than ideal.

The NIOSH lifting equation in plain terms

The NIOSH applications manual gives the recommended weight limit as RWL = 51 lb × HM × VM × DM × AM × FM × CM. The 51 lb (23 kg) is the load constant: the most NIOSH would recommend for a two-handed lift under ideal conditions. Each multiplier is between 0 and 1 and shrinks the limit.

  • HM, horizontal: 10 divided by H, where H is how far your hands are, in inches, from a point on the floor midway between your ankles. Hold the load out at arm's length and HM drops fast. The manual sets HM to zero past 25 in.
  • VM, vertical: 1 minus 0.0075 times the distance your hands are from 30 in. height. Lifting from the floor costs you; so does reaching high.
  • DM, travel distance: 0.82 plus 1.8 divided by D, where D is how far the load moves up or down.
  • AM, asymmetry: 1 minus 0.0032 times the angle, in degrees, that the load sits off to one side of the body. The manual stresses that this angle is set by where the load is, not by how you stand. Twisting at the waist is the classic back-hurting move, and NIOSH's handling guide says to turn by stepping or pivoting your feet.
  • FM, frequency: looked up in a table by lifts per minute, how long the work lasts (1 hour or less, 1 to 2 hours, 2 to 8 hours) and hand height. For one lift every five minutes or less, the table gives 1.00 for up to an hour, 0.95 for 1 to 2 hours and 0.85 for 2 to 8 hours. For one lift per minute it gives 0.94, 0.88 and 0.75 for the same three durations.
  • CM, coupling: good, fair or poor handholds. Good is 1.00. Poor is 0.90. Fair is 0.95 when your hands start below 30 in. and 1.00 above that.

Divide the real load by the RWL to get the Lifting Index. The manual says a Lifting Index above 1.0 may raise the risk of low back pain for some workers, nearly all workers are at increased risk above 3.0, and the goal is to design lifting jobs at 1.0 or less.

The equation has limits. The manual says it does not cover one-handed lifts, lifting while seated or kneeling, lifting in a restricted space, unstable loads, lifting while carrying, pushing or pulling, fast motion, poor footing, or hot, cold or humid conditions outside its stated ranges. Attic hatches, basement stairs and tight mechanical rooms often break these assumptions, so a computed RWL there is a best case, not a green light.

Worked example (ours, with assumptions)

This is our own simplified example, using the NIOSH formulas above. Assumptions: a crew member lifts an 80 lb boxed part from a pallet to a truck bed, once, as a one-off task (frequency at or below 0.2 lifts per minute for under an hour). Hands start at V = 15 in. and end at 35 in., so D = 20 in. The box sits 14 in. in front of the ankles (H = 14 in.) and 45 degrees off to one side. Handholds are fair. We use the starting position because it is the worse one here.

  1. HM = 10 / 14 = 0.714
  2. VM = 1 − 0.0075 × |15 − 30| = 1 − 0.1125 = 0.888
  3. DM = 0.82 + 1.8 / 20 = 0.91
  4. AM = 1 − 0.0032 × 45 = 0.856
  5. FM = 1.00 (table, at or below 0.2 lifts per minute, up to 1 hour)
  6. CM = 0.95 (fair coupling, hands below 30 in.)

RWL = 51 × 0.714 × 0.888 × 0.91 × 0.856 × 1.00 × 0.95 = about 23.9 lb. The Lifting Index is 80 / 23.9 = about 3.3, over the 3.0 mark where the manual says nearly all workers are at increased risk. Hold the box 20 in. out instead of 14 and HM falls to 0.5, which drops the RWL to about 16.7 lb. Notice that no single multiplier is awful. It is the pile-up that does it. Fix the setup by raising the pallet, squaring up to the box so A is zero, and keeping it against your body. Better, use a cart. The equation does not model a two-person lift, so we do not claim a number for a team.

Team lifts: a stopgap, not a plan

NIOSH's manual material handling guide calls team lifting a temporary measure until a permanent fix is in place. It suggests a partner of similar height and agreeing on the plan before moving so nobody surprises the other. Sharing the lift can roughly halve the load per person, but it can raise slip, trip and fall risk. Use one person as the caller, and do not team lift on stairs when a machine can do it.

Hand trucks and stair climbers

The same guide says two-wheel carts suit loads up to about 300 lb, that wheels of at least 6 in. roll over bumps and holes more easily, and that hand trucks with brakes give more control on inclines. It also shows a powered two-wheel hand truck made for climbing stairs as an option, and advises avoiding stairs, slopes and obstacles when carrying. For a water heater or furnace on a basement stair, our practical advice is to use an appliance hand truck or stair climber rated for the weight, strap the unit to it with the strap it was built for, put the heaviest end downhill when lowering, and keep your body uphill of the load. Never stand below a load on a stair. Follow the maker's rating and instructions for the truck.

Floors, doors and condensers

Protect finished floors with hardboard or runners, and check door swings and handrails before the unit leaves the truck. For a condenser, lift by the base and handholds the maker intends, not by fan guards, coil fins or refrigerant piping. Pads, stands and brackets add their own weight and height; see the lesson linked below.

Getting units on roofs

A ladder carry is a poor way to move a condenser. Prefer a hoist, a ladder lift or a stair route sized for the weight, and keep everyone out from under the load. A crane lift is a different job. OSHA's crane rule for construction, 29 CFR 1926 subpart CC, defines a qualified rigger as one who meets its qualified-person criteria, and requires a qualified rigger for rigging used in crane assembly and disassembly. Treat any crane lift of big equipment as work for a qualified rigger and a qualified operator, with a lift plan agreed first. Our rule of thumb: if you are guessing the weight or the sling angle, stop. Read the rooftop unit lesson for the owner-side planning.

Back injury warning signs

NIOSH lists these as symptoms to watch for: aching or soreness, muscle or joint rigidity, swelling, loss of feeling or numbness, and pins-and-needles sensations. NIOSH also says to tell your employer right away rather than waiting for the discomfort to pass. NIOSH concluded there is not enough research to show that back belts prevent injury, and warned that a belt may tempt workers to lift heavier loads, so do not treat one as a license to lift more.

Field card: Moving Heavy HVAC Equipment Safely

Shrink the lift before you make the lift.

  • Ideal two-handed lift: 51 lb. Every awkward factor cuts it.
  • HM = 10/H: keep the load close. Square up so you do not twist.
  • Lifting Index over 1.0 is a risk; over 3.0 puts nearly all workers at risk (NIOSH).
  • Use a cart, appliance hand truck or stair climber before a team lift.
  • Team lift is a stopgap. Pick a partner of similar height and agree the plan.
  • Crane lifts of big units: qualified rigger and operator only.
  • Numbness, tingling or lasting aching: report early.
