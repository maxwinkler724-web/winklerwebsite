Vacuum Breakers on Steam Coils and Converters: Why a Throttled Valve Pulls a Vacuum

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steam coil that works fine at full load can flood, bang or freeze when the control valve throttles down. The cause is often a vacuum inside the coil or shell, and the cure is a small, cheap valve that lets air in. This lesson explains why the vacuum forms, where the vacuum breaker goes, which types you will meet, and how to check one.

Why a closing valve creates a vacuum

Steam heats by condensing. Each pound that condenses leaves a space that the incoming steam is supposed to refill. At full load the control valve is wide open, steam keeps coming, and the coil or shell sits at positive pressure. When the load drops, the valve closes down. The steam supply shrinks, but the cold air or water on the other side of the tubes keeps pulling heat out, so steam keeps condensing. If the valve passes less steam than is condensing, the pressure in the steam space falls. It can fall right through zero gauge and into a vacuum, because a condensing vapor takes up thousands of times less volume as liquid.

Our own plain-language summary: a modulating or fully closed steam valve plus a cold load equals a shell that behaves like a vacuum pump. Nothing else in the loop changed; only the balance between steam in and steam condensed.

How the vacuum holds condensate in the coil

A steam trap can only push condensate out if the pressure ahead of it is higher than the pressure behind it. Behind the trap is the condensate return, which is normally at atmospheric pressure or higher. When the steam space drops below that, the pressure difference reverses. The trap sees more pressure on its outlet than its inlet, a check valve in the line holds it shut, and condensate stays where it is. That is the stall described in the converter lesson and in the pump-trap lesson. A maker bulletin on heat exchanger drainage says a shell without a vacuum breaker can run at negative pressure and hold condensate in the shell.

As the water level rises it covers tube surface, which cuts capacity. The covered tubes then cool the condensate, which condenses even more steam and deepens the vacuum. When the load rises and the valve opens, fresh steam reaches the cold water and collapses violently. The same bulletin warns that steam entering a flooded shell can cause severe water hammer that can damage the trap, regulator, tubes and gaskets. A federal operating experience summary also notes that condensate-induced water hammer, caused by rapid condensation of steam by subcooled water, is the most common type reported at its facilities.

Freezing outdoor-air coils

This is our own explanation of how the stall becomes a freeze. On an air handler that takes in outdoor air, trapped condensate in the lower tubes does not stay hot. With no steam flowing through it, it cools toward the temperature of the air passing over it. If that air is below freezing, the water in the tubes can freeze and split them. One university design guide for air handlers is blunt about the exposure: it says steam coils are not to be used as preheats, and if a steam preheat cannot be avoided it must be a face-and-bypass coil whose valve goes fully open when the incoming air is 32 degrees F or colder. Even a coil that is not the preheat can see cold air during economizer operation or when dampers leak, so the breaker and the drainage matter on every unit.

Where the vacuum breaker goes

Put the breaker on the steam side, downstream of the control valve, so it senses the pressure inside the coil or shell rather than the supply pressure. Mount it at the coil or shell inlet piping, or at the tapping the maker provides, and above the highest condensate level so that it is in steam space and not under water. A university standard says every steam generating device is to have a vacuum breaker on the coil inlet piping. Another says to provide a pressure-rated breaker at the high point of steam coils served by float and thermostatic traps, and a third says to put them outside the air stream. The maker bulletin suggests a vertical pipe of about 6 to 10 inches ahead of the breaker so that it acts as a cooling leg that protects it from dirt and temperature extremes. A university specification also asks for isolation valves upstream of each breaker so it can be serviced without shutting the system down.

Types of vacuum breaker

  • Spring check style. A disc or poppet is held on its seat by a light spring. Pressure inside the coil keeps it shut. When the pressure falls below the spring setting, outside air pushes the disc open.
  • Ball style. A hardened ball sits on a seat and lifts off when the vacuum is enough to overcome its weight or a light spring. One university specification calls for a spring-loaded ball type with a brass body and stainless ball, and another allows either a hardened ball check or a spring-restrained style. The second also describes units factory set to open at 2 inches of water column of vacuum, which is only about 0.07 psi (2 times 0.0361).

Use a unit rated for the steam pressure and temperature at the inlet. A plain swing check valve is not a substitute; a design guide that we read says to use steam vacuum breakers, not check valves.

Pair it with the right trap and drainage

A breaker only helps if the condensate has somewhere to go. Several university standards call for float and thermostatic traps on coils: the float gives continuous drainage and the thermostatic vent clears air. The trap is placed below the coil outlet so gravity does the work. Requirements we read: at least 18 inches between the coil and the trap, 12 inches below the outlet of the device served in another standard, and no lifting of condensate after the trap on a modulating valve. Another spec asks that condensate drain by gravity to the trap and return lines slope downward in the direction of flow. If the return must rise, see the pump-trap lesson linked above. For the trap types themselves, see Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits.

Worked example (our own example)

We compare a converter shell at 2 psig with the same shell after the valve has throttled until the gauge reads a 5 inch mercury vacuum.

Assumptions (ours): sea-level atmosphere of 14.696 psia, which is 29.92 inHg; 1 inHg equals 0.4912 psi; the condensate return is vented to atmosphere (0 psig); condensate near 200 F weighs about 60.1 lb per cubic foot, so each foot of water column is 60.1 divided by 144, or 0.417 psi; the trap sits 18 inches (1.5 ft) below the shell outlet.

Saturation values come from a government saturated-steam table in SI units (pressure in MPa, temperature in degrees C), interpolated: 0.110 MPa is 102.292 C, 0.120 MPa is 104.784 C, 0.080 MPa is 93.486 C and 0.085 MPa is 95.125 C.

Case A, 2 psig. Absolute pressure is 2 + 14.696 = 16.696 psia. In MPa, 16.696 times 0.0068948 is 0.1151 MPa. That is 0.51 of the way from 0.110 to 0.120, so the temperature is 102.292 + 0.51 times (104.784 - 102.292) = 103.57 C. In F, 103.57 times 1.8 plus 32 is about 218 F. The shell is 2 psi above the return, so condensate flows out, and the 1.5 ft leg adds 0.63 psi (1.5 times 0.417).

Case B, 5 inHg vacuum. The pressure drop is 5 times 0.4912 = 2.456 psi, so absolute pressure is 14.696 - 2.456 = 12.240 psia, or 0.0844 MPa. That is 0.88 of the way from 0.080 to 0.085, so the temperature is 93.486 + 0.88 times (95.125 - 93.486) = 94.93 C, which is 202.9 F, about 203 F. Steam in a shell at that pressure is about 16 F cooler than in case A (218.4 - 202.9 = 15.5, or 15.6 unrounded) and about 9 F below the 212 F boiling point at sea level.

Why it cannot drain. Shell pressure is now 2.456 psi below the vented return. The 1.5 ft leg supplies only 0.63 psi, leaving 1.83 psi unmet. To drain by gravity alone the water column would need 2.456 divided by 0.417 = 5.9 ft of drop, far more than the 18 inches most units allow. So the condensate backs up in the shell. With any return back pressure above zero the shortfall is bigger still. A breaker that cracks at 2 inches of water holds the shell within about 0.07 psi of atmosphere, so the 1.5 ft leg (0.63 psi) would again win.

Field checks

  • Cracking pressure. With the unit isolated and safe, apply a small vacuum to the breaker and see that it opens near its marked setting, then seats again. Use the maker's setting and your site procedure; do not guess.
  • Stuck closed with dirt. Scale, pipe dope and rust sit on the seat, especially if the breaker is low or in a dirty line. A breaker that never moves protects nothing. Clean or replace it, and check that its cooling leg and strainer are not plugged.
  • Leaking steam outward. A hissing breaker or a wet, rusty cap while the coil is under pressure means the seat is cut or held open. Isolate it and replace the unit.
  • Symptoms of a missing or dead breaker. Hammering when the valve reopens, a cold lower coil, a trap that goes silent at part load, or burst tubes after a cold night.
  • Drainage. Check trap height, a clear strainer, and that the trap discharges to a return that is not under pressure.

Field card: Vacuum Breakers on Steam Coils and Converters

If the valve can close, the shell can pull a vacuum.

  • Throttled steam plus a cold load drops shell pressure below zero gauge.
  • A vacuum reverses the trap pressure difference, so condensate stalls in the coil.
  • Stalled water means poor heat, water hammer on reopening, and frozen tubes on outdoor-air units.
  • Breaker: steam side, after the control valve, at the inlet or tapping, above the condensate.
  • Pair with a float and thermostatic trap at least 18 in below the outlet and a gravity return.
  • Our example: about 218 F at 2 psig versus about 203 F at 5 inHg vacuum.
  • Check cracking pressure, dirt on the seat, and steam leaking outward.
