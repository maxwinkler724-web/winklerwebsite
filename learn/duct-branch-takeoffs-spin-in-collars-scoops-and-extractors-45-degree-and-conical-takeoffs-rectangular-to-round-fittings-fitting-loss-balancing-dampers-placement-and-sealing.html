Duct Branch Takeoffs: Fitting Shape, Dampers, Placement and Sealing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A branch takeoff is the fitting where a branch duct leaves the main trunk or plenum. It looks like a minor part, but it is the first thing the branch air has to get through, and a bad one can leave a room short of air even when the duct size and the register are right.

The takeoff styles you will see

Straight spin-in collar. A round collar with a flange that sits in a hole cut in the trunk. It is quick and cheap, and the air has to make a hard turn into it. Spin-in with a scoop or extractor. A blade or vane inside the trunk that catches some of the passing air and directs it into the collar. 45-degree (high-efficiency) takeoff. The branch leaves the trunk at an angle, so the turn is gentler. Conical takeoff. A fitting that tapers from the trunk opening down to the round branch size, so the air is guided in rather than snapped around a corner. Rectangular-to-round fitting. A transition that mounts on a rectangular opening and ends in a round connection for the branch.

The federal energy department and national laboratory documents we read were mainly about junction boxes and splitter boxes, not about each of these named styles, so we do not give published loss numbers for them. What follows about shape is our own explanation of how air behaves. Check a fitting maker's installation data for the fitting you are actually using.

Why shape changes loss and airflow

Duct designers describe a fitting's loss with a loss coefficient. The loss in inches of water column (in. w.c.) is the coefficient times the velocity pressure in the branch. A bigger coefficient means more of the fan's pressure is spent in the fitting and less is left to push air through the branch duct and register. In a laboratory study of residential air distribution components, a national laboratory found that a splitter box's branch coefficient was not one fixed number: it varied a lot with the geometry and with how much of the main flow went to the branch, with measured values in the tables we read ranging from about 0.6 to more than 6. That is a good reminder that a takeoff's loss depends on how it is built and how much air goes through it.

Our own worked example

This example uses our own assumed numbers, not measured data.

  1. Assumptions. Branch velocity pressure 0.04 in. w.c. (about 800 feet per minute by the usual velocity-pressure relation). A straight-tap style fitting is assumed to have a loss coefficient of 1.0. A smooth angled or conical style is assumed to have a loss coefficient of 0.4. The branch has 0.10 in. w.c. available for everything: fitting, duct, and register.
  2. Fitting loss, first case: 1.0 x 0.04 = 0.04 in. w.c.
  3. Fitting loss, second case: 0.4 x 0.04 = 0.016 in. w.c.
  4. Difference: 0.04 - 0.016 = 0.024 in. w.c.
  5. Share of the budget: 0.04 / 0.10 = 40 percent in the first case, 0.016 / 0.10 = 16 percent in the second.
  6. What is left for the duct and register: 0.10 - 0.04 = 0.06 versus 0.10 - 0.016 = 0.084 in. w.c. The second case leaves 0.084 / 0.06 = 1.4 times as much.
  7. Rough airflow effect: for a fixed duct and register, airflow rises and falls with the square root of the pressure pushing it. If the rest of the branch was sized to use 0.084, then with only 0.06 it would carry roughly the square root of 0.06 / 0.084, about 0.85, or 85 percent of design airflow. This is a simplification, because the fitting loss itself also falls as flow falls, so treat 85 percent as an upper-bound sketch of the trend.

The point is the scale: 0.024 in. w.c. sounds tiny, but when the whole branch only has 0.10 in. w.c., it is a large share.

Balancing dampers in the takeoff

A damper in the takeoff lets you throttle a branch that gets too much air so the extra pressure goes to weaker branches. The federal energy department case study on junction boxes says balancing dampers can raise pressure in runs with excess flow, pushing air to outlets that get less than their share, and that outlets directly opposite the inlet need much more balancing pressure. A damper is for trimming, not for fixing a bad takeoff. A damper that has to be nearly closed on one branch is a sign the layout is unbalanced. Set the damper handle so you can find it later, and see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics for what the pressure readings mean.

Where to place takeoffs

Not at the end cap. This part is our own inference from the junction-box research: in that research, outlets placed directly opposite the inlet needed significantly more balancing pressure. Air arriving at a dead end behaves similarly, so a branch right at the end cap is often the hardest to feed evenly. Leave some trunk past the last takeoff. Not crowded against the first fitting off the plenum. The same federal case study recommends spacing the first outlet away from the inlet, at least one outlet diameter, and the guidance it cites puts the first takeoff at least twice its diameter from the inlet. Air coming out of a transition or elbow is uneven, so a takeoff placed right there sees a skewed flow; the lesson on fan system effect explains the same idea at the fan. For two branches, the same fact sheet suggests a metal wye fitting, which has much lower pressure drops than a junction box.

Seal the collar

A national laboratory report on duct retrofits says takeoff-to-trunk connections should be sealed with mastic, takeoffs should be screwed to the metal trunk, and sealing a takeoff only to the outside of the duct wrap is a leaky practice. The same report says it is very typical to see tabbed takeoffs with no air sealing at all, and that flex branches are often not connected correctly to the supply trunk. A leaking collar loses air into the attic, crawl space or wall cavity before it reaches the room. Mastic is a sealant, not a fastener, so the collar still needs screws or tabs. See duct leakage testing for how leaks are measured.

How a poor takeoff starves a branch

  • A high-loss fitting uses up a big share of the branch pressure budget, as the worked example shows.
  • A branch at the end of the trunk gets whatever pressure is left and the least even flow.
  • A leaking collar sends part of the branch air somewhere else.
  • A damper left nearly closed after a past balancing job chokes the branch on its own.
  • A flex branch attached poorly or kinked at the takeoff adds its own losses; see flex duct installation.

When a room is short of air, check the takeoff before you blame the register: is the damper open, is the collar sealed and attached, and does the branch connect cleanly? The register opening and boot lesson covers the room end of the branch.

Field card: Duct branch takeoffs

Gentle turn in, sealed collar, clear of the end cap.

  • Loss = loss coefficient x branch velocity pressure.
  • Our example: 1.0 vs 0.4 at 0.04 in. w.c. gives 0.04 vs 0.016 in. w.c., out of a 0.10 budget.
  • Use the damper to trim, not to fix a bad fitting.
  • Keep takeoffs off the end cap and away from the first fitting off the plenum.
  • Screw the collar and seal it with mastic.
  • Short of air? Check damper, collar, flex connection, then register.
