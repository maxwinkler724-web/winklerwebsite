Hospital Laundry HVAC: Soiled Sort Rooms, Clean Side and Chute Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hospital laundry has a dirty end and a clean end, and the air is supposed to respect that. We walk through the soiled-to-clean barrier, what the public guidance says about pressure, the chute room, and how much heat the dryers dump on the clean side.

Check the design documents

Check the design documents. Pressure relationships and air change rates for a given building come from the design drawings, the adopted edition of ASHRAE/ASHE Standard 170 (named here in plain text only) and your infection prevention staff. This lesson is general education built from public pages read in 2026.

The idea: soiled side pulls, clean side stays clean

CDC's environmental infection control guidance says areas receiving contaminated textiles should be at negative air pressure relative to the clean areas, so aerosolized lint from soiled laundry does not settle on cleaned laundry. Its recommendation list rates this one Category IC, meaning it is strongly advised and supported by rules or standards, and it points to the construction standard in effect when the building was built. CDC's pages do not give an air change number, and we did not find a separate clean-side positive requirement there. For numbers we used two other public documents, below. For how a tech proves a pressure direction, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare.

The numbers we can cite

  • Federal model specification. The Indian Health Manual exhibit for laundry services calls for the receiving and sorting area to be negative to the rest of the facility with at least 10 air changes per hour. It also says doors should swing toward the negative side, soiled and clean areas should have separate ventilation, and air must never flow from the soiled area.
  • An older state table. One Illinois long-term-care code table lists soiled linen sorting and storage negative at 6 air changes per hour, exhausted outdoors; clean linen storage positive at 2, with outdoor exhaust optional; general laundry equal pressure at 6; and the soiled linen and trash chute room negative at 6, exhausted outdoors. It says its rates are minimums. We use it to show the row pattern, not as the hospital rule.

Hospital rows in ASHRAE/ASHE 170 are the ones that usually govern, and we did not read them. Take the pressure sign and air changes from the drawings for your building.

The barrier wall and pass-through washers

A barrier washer loads from the soiled side and unloads on the clean side, so the wall stays closed and the machine is the only opening. For the tech this means door closers, gaskets and sealed penetrations on the barrier are part of the pressure system. If the clean side loses its supply, or a sort-room exhaust fan fails, the pressure difference shrinks and the room can drift toward the wrong direction. Check the pressure monitor or a simple tissue or smoke test at the barrier door and at the pass-through during rounds.

Soiled sort room exhaust

CDC lists no air change number, so use what the design calls for. Exhaust is sized to the air change rate, and supply is set lower than exhaust by an offset so the room stays negative. A fan, filter or damper change that moves the balance has to be rebalanced and the monitor rechecked.

Linen chute rooms

CDC says laundry chutes should be kept under negative air pressure, and that chutes must be designed, maintained and used to limit aerosol spread. It also warns that a bag dropped down a chute can act like a piston and push air and contaminants through the building. That is why staff are told to close bags and not toss loose linen. On a service call we check that the chute room stays negative to the hallway, that the door closes and latches, and that the exhaust fan is running. Where the exhaust opening sits is a design detail, so read the drawings rather than assume.

PPE before the soiled side

OSHA's bloodborne pathogens rule (29 CFR 1910.1030) says contaminated laundry is handled as little as possible, with minimum agitation, and is bagged or contained where it was used and not sorted or rinsed there. Employees who have contact with contaminated laundry wear gloves and other appropriate PPE. CDC also expects laundry areas to have handwashing facilities. For a tech, that means asking the laundry supervisor what PPE and procedure apply before you open the sort room, go to the washer pits, or open a chute room hatch. Do not touch bagged linen to get at equipment.

Washers, dryers and the heat

Tunnel washers and washer-extractors add moisture, and hot-water wash cycles are common (CDC mentions at least 160°F for at least 25 minutes as a commonly recommended hot-water wash). Dryers add sensible heat and lint on the clean side. We cover dryer exhaust ducts, makeup air and lint fire risk in Commercial Laundry Rooms and Clothes Dryer Vents, so we do not repeat that here. CDC also says not to leave damp textiles in machines overnight, a cue to check drain and exhaust on any machine that holds wet loads.

Worked example

All inputs here are our own assumptions except the 10 air changes per hour minimum from the federal model specification.

Part 1: soiled sort room exhaust. Room 800 sq ft, ceiling 12 ft, 10 air changes per hour.

  1. Volume = 800 x 12 = 9,600 cu ft.
  2. Exhaust = 9,600 x 10 / 60 = 1,600 cfm.
  3. Assumed offset (our own): supply is 10 percent below exhaust, so 1,600 x 0.10 = 160 cfm.
  4. Supply = 1,600 - 160 = 1,440 cfm. The extra 160 cfm comes in through the barrier and doors from the clean side.

Part 2: dryer heat on the clean side. 4 dryers at an example 200,000 Btu/h input each, with an example 15 percent of that input lost to the room.

  1. Total input = 4 x 200,000 = 800,000 Btu/h.
  2. Room heat gain = 800,000 x 0.15 = 120,000 Btu/h.
  3. Tons = 120,000 / 12,000 = 10 tons.

The 15 percent is only an example. Real dryers differ, so use the maker data for the equipment you service.

Field card: Hospital Laundry HVAC: Soiled Sort Rooms, Clean Side and Chute Rooms

Soiled side negative, clean side stays clean.

  • CDC: areas receiving contaminated textiles negative to clean areas; chutes negative.
  • Federal model spec: sorting area negative, at least 10 air changes per hour, doors swing to the negative side.
  • Air changes and clean-side pressure: use the design documents and Standard 170.
  • Exhaust cfm = room cu ft x air changes / 60. Supply = exhaust minus offset.
  • Dryer heat in Btu/h divided by 12,000 gives tons.
  • Ask about PPE before entering the soiled side or opening a chute room.
