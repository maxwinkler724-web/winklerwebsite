A2L Mitigation Alarm: What to Do When It Trips

You arrive and the air handler is running its blower with no call, the compressor is off and a board is flashing. That is not a nuisance, it is the safety system doing its job. Here is a steady way to work it.

Step one: assume it could be real

The detection board is built to react before refrigerant reaches a level that matters. Published descriptions say the board starts the indoor blower to dilute refrigerant, overrides the cooling call, and blocks heat calls so that strip heat or a gas furnace cannot act as an ignition source. Some boards also alert a building system or sound an alarm. So the first assumption is that refrigerant may be present: ventilate, keep sparks and flame away, and do not rely on smell, since one manufacturer notes refrigerant may have no odor.

Step two: read before you touch

Most makers document board lights or codes. One publicly posted air handler manual shows a status light with different codes for an active alarm, a past refrigerant alarm, a past sensor communication error and a past sensor error, and says past codes are kept for a period afterward. That is a good model of how to think even if your brand differs: note what is active and what is history, and photograph the label, lights and wiring before changing anything.

Step three: sort the fault type

  • Active leak alarm: point toward real refrigerant, or something the sensor mistakes for it.
  • Sensor communication error: look at the harness and plugs first.
  • Sensor error: the sensor reports its own problem; replace only with the part the maker specifies.
  • Past event or timed hold: history and waiting periods are not the same as a live problem.

A well-known false-alarm source is vapor from sealants, adhesives and solvents used near the equipment. Ask what was built or painted nearby, and ventilate before blaming the part.

Step four: search properly

Use a leak detector that the equipment maker and the detector maker say suits the refrigerant, and never an open flame. The fixed sensor in the unit does not replace a leak search with a handheld tool.

Step five: reset and verify

One manual describes a short wait after checking harness connections, then a power cycle if refrigerant is still detected. Another maker says to confirm the detection system works after service but gives no test steps in the sensor kit sheet, pointing you to the air handler manual instead. A common thread: after any repair, use the manufacturer's method to confirm every mitigation response happens and then releases as described. The same manual also warns that the unit must stay powered except for service, so the safety function is available.

What never to do

  • Jumper, bridge, unplug or remove the sensor so the unit will run.
  • Reset again and again until a code stays clear.
  • Leave the unit running on a defeated safety circuit, even on a hot day.
  • Fit a sensor or board from a different model and hope it works.

The rule that overrides this page: the instructions for the exact unit in front of you, your employer's procedures and the codes where you work always govern. This lesson explains concepts only.

Field card: A2L Mitigation Alarm: What to Do When It Trips

A mitigation alarm is information, not an obstacle.

  • Treat the alarm as possibly real: ventilate, no sparks or flame.
  • Read the lights or codes and note active versus past before touching anything.
  • Sort the cause: real leak, vapors, harness, sensor, board.
  • Leak search with a suitable detector, never a flame.
  • After repair, verify every mitigation response by the maker's method. Never bypass.
