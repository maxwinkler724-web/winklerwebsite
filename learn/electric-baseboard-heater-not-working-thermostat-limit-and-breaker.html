Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working: Thermostat, Limit and Breaker

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An electric baseboard heater has almost nothing in it: a heating element, a safety limit, and a thermostat that switches full house voltage. That makes the checklist short. Most "dead heater" calls come down to a tripped breaker, the wrong kind of thermostat, or a heater that is shutting itself off because something is blocking it.

First, make sure it is electric baseboard

This lesson is about electric resistance baseboard, where the metal housing holds a heating element and two thick wires feed it. If water pipes and a boiler feed it, it is hydronic; see Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold and Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature. The U.S. Department of Energy describes it as heating elements in metal pipes with aluminum fins, warming air that rises into the room as cooler air enters at the bottom.

Homeowner checks

1. The breaker

Electric heaters are usually on their own circuit. Cadet's owner's guide says electric heaters need a dedicated circuit that is not shared with outlets, lights or other appliances, and that a 240-volt heater uses a double-pole breaker. Look for a double-width breaker tripped to the middle or off, then switch it fully off and back on. If it trips again right away, stop. A breaker that will not stay on means a fault; call an electrician. See AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker.

2. The thermostat is probably 240 volts

Most baseboard thermostats are line-voltage: they switch the same 120 or 240 volts the heater uses. They are not the 24-volt low-voltage thermostats used with furnaces and air conditioners, and the two are not interchangeable. The Department of Energy notes that baseboard heaters often use line-voltage thermostats. Mysa's compatibility guide separates them by wiring: line-voltage systems have thick wires joined with wire nuts, low-voltage systems have thin wires on terminals, and its baseboard model is line voltage only with a 16-amp limit (3,800 W at 240 V). Honeywell Home sells separate line-voltage models, such as a single-pole TL8130A and a double-pole TL8230A. For background on the 24-volt kind, see Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats and Thermostat Terminal Letters.

Some thermostats are single-pole (one leg switched, often no true OFF position) and some are double-pole (both legs switched, with a real OFF). Cadet and Marley both describe this difference. The CPSC found that people often believed a heater was off when it was not, because the lowest setting had no true OFF. Before any work, turn the breaker off; do not trust the dial.

3. Clearances and airflow

Block the air moving through the fins and the heater overheats. Manufacturers give different distances, so read your own label or manual:

  • Cadet: keep at least 12 inches clear above and in front of the heater, and keep furniture, bedding, papers, clothing and curtains away. Do not block the air intake or outlet.
  • Marley (D Series commercial manual): keep furniture at least 6 inches away. For full-length drapes, the bottom should stay at least 1 inch above the floor covering, with at least 2 inches between the heater front and the nearest drape fold. Short drapes should stay at least 6 inches above the top of the heater.

The CPSC's 2002 report on fixed-position electric heaters notes that some labels call for three feet from combustibles and that draped material ignited quickly on an open-coil heater in lab tests. When the numbers differ, use the larger one. Marley says to clean dust from the fins with a brush-attachment vacuum only when the heater is off and cool.

4. The built-in limit: why a heater may cycle off

Baseboard heaters such as Cadet's and Marley's have a high-temperature safety shutoff. Cadet says it cuts power when the unit gets too hot and resets itself after cooling. Marley's D Series manual says that if the heater is installed correctly and on the correct voltage, the over-temperature devices should never operate, and that if one keeps cycling the heater on and off you should turn off the power and call qualified service. Never remove or bypass the limit.

A heater that runs, goes cold, then returns is not "weak." Look for a couch, a long curtain, a rug edge, a toy box or packed-up dust blocking the airflow. Move it and let the heater cool. If it keeps happening with clear space, call a pro.

5. Smells and smoke on first use

New heaters may smell. Marley says a new smell is expected on new installations and should clear after about a half hour at full operation. Cadet says to run the heater at its highest setting for 30 minutes, that some smoke or smell is normal while manufacturing residue burns off, and to turn off the breaker if the element glows red or the unit repeatedly overheats and shuts off. A smell that is not new, or melting plastic, is different: shut off the breaker and have it checked. See Furnace and AC Smells.

6. Do not open the junction box

The wiring end holds line voltage whenever the breaker is on. Both manufacturers say to shut off power at the panel before service, and Cadet advises a licensed electrician if you are unsure. Marley's D Series manual leaves only cleaning to the owner.

Worked example: amps, watts and how many heaters fit a 20 A circuit

Power equals voltage times current, so current equals watts divided by volts. For a 1,500-watt heater on 240 volts:

  1. Current = 1,500 W ÷ 240 V = 6.25 A.
  2. Fixed electric space heating counts as a continuous load under the NEC (section 424.3(B) in the edition EC&M quoted; newer editions may renumber it, so check the edition your town enforces), and the branch circuit must be rated at least 125% of the heating load. For one heater: 6.25 A × 1.25 = 7.8 A.
  3. Two heaters: 12.5 A × 1.25 = 15.6 A. That is under 20 A, so two fit.
  4. Three heaters: 18.75 A × 1.25 = 23.4 A. That is over 20 A, so three do not fit.

The same result in the other direction: 80% of 20 A is 16 A, and 16 ÷ 6.25 = 2.56, so two heaters. Cadet's guide states the 80% idea and also caps the load at the thermostat's amp rating, so a thermostat serving two of these heaters must handle 12.5 A. Marley's manual pairs 12.1 to 16 A with #12 copper and a 20 A breaker; wire and breaker choices belong to the electrician.

For technicians: proving it dead and testing the parts

Follow your lockout/tagout procedure and prove dead with a meter before touching anything; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings and Electrical Basics for HVAC. A single-pole thermostat leaves one leg live when "off," so test both legs at the heater.

  • No power at the heater, breaker on: measure line to line at the thermostat terminals with the thermostat calling. Voltage in but none out points at the thermostat; no voltage in points back up the circuit.
  • Element: with power off and proven dead, disconnect the element leads and read resistance. Ohm's law gives the expected value: R = V² ÷ W, so a 1,500 W, 240 V element is 57,600 ÷ 1,500 = about 38.4 ohms. Compare with the nameplate. An open reading is a failed element.
  • Limit: the limit is a switch in series with the element. A cool limit should read closed; an open reading on a cool heater means a tripped or failed limit. Do not jumper or bypass it, since both manufacturers say not to remove it.
  • Voltage mismatch: Cadet says connecting a heater to a higher voltage than its label "will destroy the heater and could start a fire," and a lower voltage prevents proper heating. Confirm nameplate voltage against the measured supply.

Field card: Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working

Breaker, thermostat, airflow, limit, in that order.

  • Electric baseboard uses a line-voltage thermostat, not a 24 V one.
  • Check for a tripped double-pole breaker. If it trips again, call an electrician.
  • Clear furniture and curtains by your manufacturer's distance.
  • A heater that cycles off may be blocked and tripping its limit. Never bypass the limit.
  • New-heater smell: about 30 minutes at full output, then it should clear.
  • Homeowners leave the junction box closed.
  • Amps = watts ÷ volts. 1,500 W at 240 V is 6.25 A.
  • Fixed heat is a continuous load: size at 125%, so two 1,500 W heaters fit a 20 A circuit and three do not.
