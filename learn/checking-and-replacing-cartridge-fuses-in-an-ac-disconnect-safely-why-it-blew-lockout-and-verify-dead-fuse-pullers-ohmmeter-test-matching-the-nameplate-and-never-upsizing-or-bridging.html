Checking and Replacing Cartridge Fuses in an AC Disconnect Safely

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A blown fuse in the pull-out disconnect beside a condenser is one of the most common reasons an air conditioner is dead. Swapping it takes minutes. Doing it safely, and deciding whether to swap it at all, is the real skill. This lesson covers the sequence we follow at the disconnect.

Why it blew is the real question

A fuse is the weak link on purpose. It opens when current stays too high for too long. So a blown fuse is a symptom. Common causes include a shorted or grounded compressor or fan motor, a failing run capacitor that makes the compressor draw extra current, a locked rotor, a damaged wire, water in the disconnect, or a loose, hot connection. Old age and a power surge are possible too, but we only call it "just a bad fuse" after the circuit has been checked. Our sibling lessons cover the sizing math and the way fuses respond to overload; this lesson stays on the hands-on steps. See the related lessons below.

Step 1: Lock out and verify dead

The federal workplace standard for controlling hazardous energy says the shutdown has to follow a sequence: shut the equipment down in an orderly way, operate each energy isolating device, apply lockout or tagout devices, relieve stored energy, and then verify isolation before work starts. The federal electrical work-practices standard adds that a lock and a tag go on each disconnecting means, and that a qualified person must use test equipment to check that the circuit parts are de-energized, including a check for induced voltage or backfeed. A university electrical safety program we read puts it plainly: treat all equipment as energized until it is proven otherwise.

In practice at a condenser disconnect, that means:

  • Open the disconnect, lock it and tag it. Remember that the line side of a pull-out disconnect stays live from the service; opening the handle does not make the terminals dead.
  • Use a rated meter. Prove it works on a known live source, measure every pair of terminals you could touch (line side and load side, phase to phase and each phase to ground), then prove the meter again. Our lockout-tagout and verify-dead lesson walks through this live-dead-live habit.
  • Do not rely on a thermostat, a contactor or a pushbutton as the means of shutting off power. The federal standard says control devices cannot be the sole means.

Step 2: Use a fuse puller, never fingers or a screwdriver

Cartridge fuses sit tight in spring clips. People pry them out with a screwdriver, which can gouge the clip, slip, and short across live parts. A university electrical safety manual we read says to use only properly rated and sized fuse pullers to install and remove fuses, and to use insulated tools near live parts. A puller grips the fuse body, keeps your hand off the metal ends, and keeps the fuse square so you do not bend the clip. Grab the body, not the end caps, and pull straight out.

Step 3: Test the fuse out of the holder

A fuse that looks fine can be open, and one that looks scorched can be good. Some cartridge fuses have no window and no indicator. Test it with an ohmmeter while it is out of the holder and out of the circuit. If you measure a fuse that is still connected, current can flow through other paths and mislead you.

  1. Set the meter to ohms (or continuity) and touch one probe to each end cap.
  2. A good fuse reads very close to zero ohms. A blown fuse reads open, shown as OL or infinity.
  3. Test every fuse in the disconnect. One blown fuse on a three-wire single-phase condenser feed can leave a half-live circuit, and the other fuse may be damaged or weak.

Step 4: Match the nameplate

Look at the equipment nameplate, not the fuse that was in there; the last person may have guessed. The nameplate lists a maximum fuse or breaker size, and some nameplates also say the fuse must be a time-delay (dual-element) type. Match both: the amp rating and the type. Time-delay types are made to ride through the brief starting surge of a compressor or motor. A fast-acting fuse of the same rating can open on every start. Match the voltage rating and physical size of the clips too; the rating printed on the fuse must be at least the circuit voltage. Our nameplate lesson explains the maximum fuse value and the minimum circuit ampacity.

Never upsize, never bridge

A university safety program says to replace a fuse only with the proper type and never with a higher rating or a different type, and never to short out a blown fuse. We agree for every case. A larger fuse does not fix the fault. It lets the wire and motor run hotter than they were built for before anything opens. Pennies, foil and jumper wire remove protection entirely, and a fault then ends at the wire insulation, the motor or a fire. If a fuse keeps blowing, the answer is to find the fault, not to change the fuse.

Look at the clips and the box

Before the new fuse goes in, check the holder with the power still locked out:

  • Spring clips: They should grip firmly. A loose or sprung clip makes a poor contact that heats up and can cook the fuse and the base.
  • Heat marks: Brown or black discoloration, melted plastic or pitted metal at a clip means high resistance and heat. Replace the holder or the disconnect; do not just drop in a fuse.
  • Terminals: Look for discolored lugs and brittle insulation, and make sure the lug screws are snug at the torque the maker lists.
  • Moisture and pests: Water stains, rust, nests or insects mean the box needs more than a fuse.

When to stop and find the fault

A university program we read says a qualified person must investigate the cause of a tripped breaker before resetting it, to avoid equipment damage or fire. We treat a blown fuse the same way. Stop and troubleshoot if:

  • the new fuse blows when power comes back, or after only a short run;
  • you find heat damage at a clip, lug or wire;
  • you cannot verify that the circuit is dead, or you see unexpected voltage (stop work and ask for help);
  • the replacement does not match the nameplate and you have no right one on the truck.

One re-energize after a clean, matched replacement is reasonable. Repeated tries are not.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example. All the numbers are assumptions we chose for teaching. A condenser is dead. We have locked out the disconnect and verified it dead with a meter proven on a known source. We read the equipment nameplate: it says maximum fuse 30 A, time-delay type, and minimum circuit ampacity 24 A. The truck stock has these cartridge fuses, all with a voltage rating at or above the circuit voltage:

  • A: 20 A time-delay
  • B: 30 A fast-acting
  • C: 30 A time-delay
  • D: 35 A time-delay
  • E: 40 A time-delay

Pick the fuse. D and E are larger than the 30 A maximum, so they are out. A is 20 A, which is below the 24 A minimum circuit ampacity, so we expect it to open on a normal run. B has the right amps but the wrong type, which could open on a compressor start. C is 30 A and time-delay, so it equals the nameplate maximum and is the right type. Our pick is C.

Check the old fuse. We pull both fuses with a puller and set the meter to ohms. Assume fuse 1 reads 0.2 ohm end to end: continuous, so it is good. Assume fuse 2 reads OL, which means open: it is blown. Both are replaced with type C, not just the open one. Because the cause is unknown, we then check for a fault (the compressor winding and the capacitor, for example) before closing the disconnect, and we look for the reason this fuse opened.

Field card: Checking and Replacing Cartridge Fuses in an AC Disconnect Safely

Lock out, prove dead, pull, ohm test, match the nameplate.

  • A blown fuse is a symptom. Find the cause.
  • Lock and tag the disconnect, then prove it dead with a meter you have checked on a live source.
  • Use a fuse puller. No fingers, no screwdriver.
  • Ohm test each fuse out of the holder: near zero is good, open (OL) is blown.
  • Match the nameplate amp rating and the time-delay type.
  • Never upsize and never bridge a fuse.
  • Heat marks or loose clips mean repair the holder. A fuse that blows again means stop.
