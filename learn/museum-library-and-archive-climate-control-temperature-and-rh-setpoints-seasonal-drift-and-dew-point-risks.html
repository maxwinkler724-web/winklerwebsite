Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control: Setpoints, Drift and Dew Point

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Ask a conservator for the perfect temperature and humidity and the honest answer is "it depends, and stability matters more than the exact number." For a technician that changes the job: you are not chasing a single setpoint, you are keeping a slowly moving band, protecting cold walls from condensation, and keeping the equipment running when nobody is watching.

Why swings matter more than the number

Paper, wood, leather, ivory and many glues take on and give up moisture as the air's relative humidity (RH) changes, and they swell and shrink as they do. The Image Permanence Institute (IPI) guide says fluctuating RH shrinks and swells free organic materials and can crush or fracture ones that are held in place, and that repeated changes cause micro-cracks to progress slowly. The Canadian Conservation Institute (CCI) makes the same point: small reductions in the size of a fluctuation give large gains in safety, and a rare extreme, such as a humidifier failure in winter or a failed air conditioner in summer, can do more harm than modest routine variation.

IPI also notes that a short spike does not instantly change an object. A hardcover book stacked among others takes about a month to settle after a 20 percent RH change. That is why the slow drift of a season is treated so differently from a fast daily swing. A fast swing is the problem to avoid. A slow drift is something you can use.

What the published guidance says

The guidance does not agree on one number, and that is deliberate. It depends on the collection, the building and the climate. Here is what each source we read says.

The control classes come from the ASHRAE Handbook chapter on museums, galleries, archives and libraries, which CCI summarizes and has adopted for its advice. We name it here in plain text; read the current edition through ASHRAE if your project cites it. The NPS handbook also lists more sensitive materials separately: for example, teeth, bone and shell at 30 to 55 percent, most photographic materials at 30 to 40 percent, and unstable metal below 15 percent. So a mixed collection often needs separate rooms or sealed cases.

Seasonal drift as an energy saver

"Seasonal drift" means letting the target move slowly with the seasons instead of holding one flat value. NARA defines it as the slow, gradual change in the targeted indoor conditions of a storage room, and gives the example of heating and humidifying a room at the lower limits of its table during winter, noting that the extra cost of reaching a higher allowed temperature has no preservation benefit. NPS says the same thing for temperature, with set points higher in summer and lower in winter, changing over weeks and months. For RH, NPS allows a seasonal setpoint in climates with distinct seasons, keeping each seasonal setpoint within 5 points, for a total yearly range of 40 to 60 percent, with the data reviewed monthly. CCI defines seasonal adjustment as intentional change in settings to reduce energy use in winter and summer.

IPI says the stakes are real: some strategies could cut energy use by 10 to 30 percent without significant risk to the collection, and a 10,000 square foot storage area can cost $20,000 to $50,000 a year to condition. IPI's own breakdown for a typical system puts the humidifier at about 5 percent of use, so the savings come mostly from heating, cooling and reheating, not from skipping humidification. IPI also warns against cooling air below its dew point and then reheating it to remove moisture that is not there.

Dew point and cold exterior walls

RH depends on temperature, but dew point does not move when you simply heat or cool the air. IPI says that unless the mechanical system adds or removes water, indoor and outdoor dew points match, and that humidifying raises the indoor dew point while dehumidifying lowers it. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for the basics. The risk comes in winter: you hold a room at a comfortable temperature with a humidifier running, but the inside face of an exterior wall is much colder than the room air. The Conservation Center for Art and Historic Artifacts (CCAHA) explains that water condenses on any surface at or below the dew point of the air touching it. The NPS handbook warns that heating and humidifying in winter can cause condensation inside walls, especially in historic buildings, with mold, rot and flaking as the result. NARA requires clearance between records and an exterior wall to prevent condensation, and IPI's remedy for a cold wall is to keep collections away and add insulation.

Worked example: is the wall below the dew point?

Our own example, using a dew point method from an application note reprinted by an equipment-monitoring project. The note is a maker document (Sensirion, SHT application note, Rev 1.2, reprinted in the LSST ESS software documentation) and is cited as plain text only. It uses the Magnus approximation with the constants of Sonntag (1990) and says they apply from -45 to 60 °C.

Assumptions (ours, not from a source): a storage room at 68 °F (20 °C) and 45 percent RH, which is the low end of the NPS setpoint range and a plausible winter value. The inside surface of an exterior wall is 41 °F (5 °C), a number we picked; on a real job you would measure it with a surface thermometer or logger on the wall.

  1. Constants: β = 17.62, λ = 243.12 °C.
  2. H = (log10(45) − 2) / 0.4343 + (17.62 × 20) / (243.12 + 20) = (1.6532 − 2) / 0.4343 + 352.4 / 263.12 = −0.7985 + 1.3393 = 0.5408.
  3. Dew point = λ × H / (β − H) = 243.12 × 0.5408 / (17.62 − 0.5408) = 131.48 / 17.079 = 7.7 °C, or about 45.9 °F.
  4. Compare: the wall surface (5 °C) is 2.7 °C below the dew point (7.7 °C). Room air touching that wall is cooled past saturation (about 120 percent RH at the wall by the same formula), so water will condense there.

Run the formula backward and a 5 °C wall would stay dry only if the room were at roughly 37 percent RH or lower at 20 °C. That is below the NPS setpoint range, so lowering the RH is the wrong fix. Warm the wall, add insulation, move the collection away, or lower the humidity target only where the guidance for that collection allows it. This is a screening check; it does not model moisture inside the wall.

Humidifier and dehumidifier control

In winter the humidifier sets a floor under the indoor dew point. In IPI's Washington, DC example, humidification kept the dew point above 40 °F in winter, and in summer dehumidification held it under 45 °F. IPI adds that with cooling-based dehumidification, the indoor dew point lands within a few degrees of the cooling coil temperature, and that in spring and fall neither may be needed. NARA requires failsafe humidity controls, with a downstream humidistat that shuts the humidifier off when humidity is excessive. NPS cautions that humidistatically controlled systems can add humidity that is destructive to old building fabric. For equipment detail, see Commercial Steam Humidifiers, Desiccant Dehumidifiers and Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems.

Sensors, calibration and data loggers

NARA says sensors must be within 2 °F and 2 percent RH, must sit in the spaces and not only in return ducts, must record at least hourly, and that an independent system of data loggers is required. It also calls for maintenance and calibration schedules for control devices. IPI reports that standalone loggers have an accuracy of about 2 percent RH, or as much as 5 percent, so a logger is not a reference. NPS says to calibrate dataloggers, keep them away from windows and vents, and gather at least a year of data before settling on a setpoint. When the control sensor and the independent logger disagree by more than their stated accuracy, check both. See Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes for checking a probe.

Running 24/7 versus setbacks

Federal rule 36 CFR 1234.14 requires "24 hour/365 days per year" air conditioning for records storage of permanent paper records. IPI notes that storage areas typically receive conditioned air around the clock and that night and weekend setbacks are a common question, which it treats in separate publications we did not read. NPS warns that lowering the heat in the evenings may cause unacceptable daily swings in RH. So the usual answer is steady operation, with the savings taken from slow seasonal drift, less outdoor air, and no needless reheat, not from nightly shutdowns. Any setback should be tested against logger data first.

Field card: Collections Climate Control

Slow drift is fine. Fast swings and wet walls are not.

  • Ask the owner for the written target; published ranges differ by collection.
  • NPS: setpoint 45 to 55 percent RH, within 5 points; keep below 65 percent (mold).
  • Let setpoints move over weeks, not hours.
  • Dew point of room air versus wall surface: surface at or below dew point means condensation.
  • Humidifier needs a high-limit humidistat.
  • Check sensors against a reference; loggers can be off by 2 to 5 percent RH.
  • Do not set back or shut down without logger data and the owner's approval.
