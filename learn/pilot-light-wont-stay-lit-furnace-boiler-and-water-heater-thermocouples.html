Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit: Thermocouples and Thermopiles

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pilot that lights, then goes out the moment you let go of the button, is one of the most common gas appliance calls. Most of the time the cause is small. But this problem sits right next to gas safety, so this lesson starts with what a homeowner should and should not do, then moves to the millivolt checks a technician uses.

Gas smell comes first

If you smell gas, do not try to relight anything. Public Service Electric and Gas (PSE&G) says to get everyone out right away, move well away from the building, and call the utility or 911 from outside. It also says to avoid light switches, phones, matches and starting a car while you are still near the smell. Connecticut's utility regulator gives the same basic advice and adds that after any gas shutoff you should never turn the gas back on yourself; call the gas company. Smell and odor types are covered in Furnace and AC Smells: What Each Odor Means. Poor combustion can also make carbon monoxide, so read Combustion and CO Safety Basics and Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Which appliances still have a standing pilot

A standing pilot is a small flame that burns all the time. One HVAC trade article says standing pilot ignition is found in gas furnaces, boilers and water heaters. Other appliances light the burner electronically each cycle instead, so no flame burns between cycles (see Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics). If you can see a tiny steady flame through a small window or at the burner tray when the appliance is idle, you have a standing pilot. If you cannot find one, do not assume there is nothing to light. Read the label.

Relighting: the label is the procedure

Every gas appliance has its own lighting instructions, usually on a label on the unit. Steps differ by control, and this page does not give them. Bradford White's service manual says to light the pilot following the lighting instruction label or the installation and operation manual. Do that, and only that. If the label is gone or you cannot follow it with confidence, stop and call a qualified technician.

Why a pilot goes out or will not stay lit

  • Draft or wind. The trade article notes that even a breeze can blow out a pilot. Bradford White's troubleshooting chart for a pilot that goes out periodically lists incorrect or clogged venting and insufficient combustion air as probable causes, along with inconsistent gas supply or pressure. Blocked venting is also a carbon monoxide risk, which is why this is a job for a tech, not a repeat relight.
  • Dirty or weak pilot. Debris can block the pilot gas path, giving a small or lazy flame that does not heat the sensor. The same article lists a fouled pilot gas line as a cause, fixed by cleaning the pilot assembly.
  • A weak sensor. A tired thermocouple or thermopile is called the most common cause. The flame is fine, but the part cannot make enough voltage to hold the gas valve open.
  • A failed gas control. Rarer, and the same article ties it to water or corrosion.

A homeowner can safely check one thing: is the area around the appliance clear, with nothing stored against it and no open window blowing on it? Beyond that, repeated relight attempts are not a repair. If it keeps going out, call a pro. For water heaters, see Water Heater No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks.

Thermocouple vs thermopile

Both are heated by the pilot flame and make a tiny voltage, measured in millivolts (mV, thousandths of a volt). A thermocouple makes roughly 25 to 30 mV in Honeywell's data that holds the gas valve open once the pilot is lit. A thermopile is a generator of the same kind that Honeywell lists, for its Q313, at 600 to 750 mV. Same idea, much more output. If the flame goes out, the voltage falls and the gas valve closes. That is the safety job.

Technician section: millivolt checks

Use a meter that reads millivolts DC and follow Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings. Gas and open flame are present, so follow the manufacturer's procedure for the exact control. The figures below are brand-specific. Do not apply one maker's number to another maker's part.

  • Honeywell, open circuit (Form 60-2087, product data, Rev. 1-98). Q309, Q340 and Q390 thermocouples: normal range 26 to 32 mV, lowest acceptable 18 mV. Q335: normal 20 to 30 mV, lowest acceptable 15 mV. Q313 thermopile: normal 600 to 750 mV, lowest acceptable 540 mV. This document gives no closed-circuit value, and it is an older revision, so check the sheet that comes with the part.
  • Bradford White, White-Rodgers mechanical control (service manual RG150T6N). Open circuit: disconnect the thermocouple from the gas control, light the pilot per the label, hold the pilot button, wait three minutes, and read. 20 to 30 mV is good output. Closed circuit: leave the thermocouple connected, hold the button while it burns, clip one lead to the copper sheath and probe the top terminal at the back of the gas control. 10 mV or higher is OK; below 10 mV means replace the thermocouple.
  • BASO (Johnson Controls) application note Y99AB-4. For K14/K15 leads it lists normal 20 to 28 mV and not less than 15 mV; for K16/K19, normal 25 to 35 mV and not less than 17 mV. It also warns that fixed-load and closed-circuit tests can bypass the safety function, so gas must not reach the main burner.

Open circuit versus closed circuit. Open circuit means the meter is the only load, so you see the most the part can make. Closed circuit means it is still hooked to the valve magnet, so you see what the valve actually gets. Bradford White gives separate pass values for each test, so run the one your manual names. Honeywell also describes an effective ignition test that lowers the pilot until it starts to lift off the sensor to see whether the valve still holds, then replaces the part if the main burner fails to light within four seconds.

Worked example

A tech tests a Honeywell Q309 thermocouple on a boiler and reads 15 mV open circuit with a good-looking pilot flame.

  1. Lowest acceptable per Honeywell: 18 mV. The reading is 15 mV.
  2. Shortfall: 18 - 15 = 3 mV below the lowest acceptable value.
  3. Normal band is 26 to 32 mV. Midpoint: (26 + 32) / 2 = 29 mV. The part makes 29 - 15 = 14 mV less than a typical new one.
  4. Before replacing, the tech checks the flame is wrapping the tip, the connection to the valve is clean and snug, and the pilot assembly is clean. Weak flame can mimic a weak part.
  5. If the flame is good and the reading stays under 18 mV, replace the thermocouple, then re-read: a new part should land at or near the 26 to 32 mV band.

The same arithmetic on a Q313 thermopile reading 520 mV: 540 - 520 = 20 mV under the lowest acceptable value.

On boiler-side flame controls and lockout behavior, see Boiler Flame Safeguards, Purge and Lockout.

Field card: Pilot Won't Stay Lit

Gas smell means out, then call. Label means the label.

  • Gas smell: leave, call the utility or 911 from outside, never relight.
  • Relight only per the appliance's own lighting label.
  • Pilot out repeatedly: suspect draft, venting, combustion air, dirty pilot or weak sensor.
  • Thermocouple makes tens of mV; a thermopile makes hundreds.
  • Honeywell Q309: open circuit 26 to 32 mV, lowest 18 mV. Bradford White closed circuit: 10 mV or more.
  • Brand numbers stay with their brand.
