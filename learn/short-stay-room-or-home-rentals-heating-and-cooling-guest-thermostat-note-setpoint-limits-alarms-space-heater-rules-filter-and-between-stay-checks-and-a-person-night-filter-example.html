Short-Stay Rentals: Heating and Cooling for Guests

Last updated: 2026-11-04

If you rent out a room or your whole home for a few nights at a time, your guests are strangers to your heating and cooling system. They do not know where the filter is, what the remote does or which setting is safe. A little preparation keeps them comfortable, keeps the equipment healthy and keeps the fire and carbon monoxide risks low. This lesson walks through a guest note, setpoints, alarms, heater rules, filters, between-stay checks and when to call a technician.

Write a one-page guest note

Most comfort complaints from guests come from not knowing how to change the temperature, or changing it a lot. Put a short printed note near the thermostat or remote. This is our own suggestion, not a code requirement. It should say:

  • Which thermostat controls which area, and how to raise or lower the temperature one degree at a time.
  • For a ductless mini-split, which button picks heat or cool, which one sets the temperature and which one changes fan speed. Tape the remote holder to the wall so it does not wander. For a deeper look at those buttons, see the lesson on mini-split remotes linked below.
  • That the system takes time to respond, so a big change will not heat the room faster.
  • Not to cover or block registers, and not to close more than one or two doors for long periods.
  • Who to call if the system stops, and that a blinking alarm or a gas smell means leave and call for help.

A sensible setpoint range and lock features

Our own suggestion is a range of roughly 65 to 76 degrees Fahrenheit for occupied rooms. That keeps guests comfortable without letting someone run heat and air conditioning against each other or push a heat pump into expensive extremes. Many thermostats have a keypad lock or a limited-range setting that stops adjustments outside minimum and maximum values. Check your thermostat manual for the term it uses; names vary. Ductless remotes sometimes have a similar limit. If your equipment has no such feature, the guest note does the same job with words. Whichever way you go, test it yourself before a guest arrives so you know what a guest will actually see.

Alarms: every level, near sleeping areas, tested before each stay

A federal consumer product safety agency says smoke alarms belong on every level of a home and inside each bedroom, and that carbon monoxide alarms belong on every level and outside sleeping areas. It also recommends testing alarms every month and replacing batteries at least once a year, or using alarms with sealed ten-year batteries. A federal fire-safety agency's home checklist adds that smoke alarms should be tested each month, should be inside and outside sleeping areas, and should be less than 10 years old. Because a guest cannot judge an alarm they have never heard, we suggest pressing the test button before every stay. That frequency is our own, a tighter version of the monthly advice. Check that the alarm sounds, and look at the date on the back of each unit. Guests should also be told where the alarms are and that they must not be unplugged or have batteries taken out. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do for what to do when one sounds.

Space heaters and fireplaces: set the rules in writing

The consumer product safety agency says to keep flammable materials at least three feet from a space heater, to plug it straight into a wall outlet and never into a power strip, and not to leave one running unattended in a confined space or while you sleep. It reports that portable heaters are involved in about 1,700 fires a year, with roughly 80 deaths. For fireplaces, a federal fire-safety card says to keep a glass or metal screen in front of the opening, keep anything that can burn at least three feet away, store ashes in a covered metal container, have the chimney inspected and cleaned each year by a professional and put the fire out before going to sleep or leaving.

Those are good rules for a host to repeat to guests. Our own policy choice, which you may adjust, is to supply no portable heaters at all and to say in the note that guests should not bring their own. If a room is too cold, the better fix is a technician visit, not a heater on an extension cord. If you do allow a fireplace, put the screen rule and the "out before bed or leaving" rule in the note, and keep the chimney service current.

Filters: count occupied nights, not calendar months

A federal energy agency's air conditioner maintenance page says to clean or replace filters every month or two during the cooling season, and to check more often with constant use, dusty conditions or pets. The point for a host is that a filter loads with dust in proportion to how much air passes through it and how much dust is in that air. An empty house adds little dust, but people, pets, luggage, bedding and cleaning between guests add a lot. So tie your check to nights occupied.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a published rule. Person-nights (people times nights) is a rough load measure we made up for planning. It does not account for pets, smokers, dusty areas or fan-on settings.

Assumptions: a filter that the maker rates for about 90 days in a two-person home that is occupied every night. A short-stay home with 4 guests staying 15 nights a month.

  1. Baseline load the filter was rated for: 2 people x 90 nights = 180 person-nights.
  2. Guest load per month: 4 guests x 15 nights = 60 person-nights.
  3. If the guests are the only occupants: 180 / 60 = 3 months, about 90 days. The interval is the same as the baseline, because 60 person-nights a month matches 2 people x 30 nights.
  4. If you also live in the home all month (2 people x 30 nights = 60 person-nights) while guests add their 60: 60 + 60 = 120 person-nights a month. Then 180 / 120 = 1.5 months, about 45 days.

So the same guest schedule gives roughly 90 days in one case and 45 in the other, which shows how much the answer depends on who else lives there. Treat either number only as the time to take a first look. Pull the filter out and check it: if you cannot see the pleat color through the media, or it looks gray and furry, change it, even if the arithmetic says there is time left. If it is still fairly clean, leave it and look again after the next stay. A filter that is a bit loaded does little harm. One that is clogged can reduce airflow. See Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit for sizes and airflow arrows.

Between-stay checks

At each turnover, walk through this list. It is our own checklist built from the sources below.

  • Registers and returns open. Guests sometimes close vents to quiet a room or cool a space. Open them again so airflow stays balanced.
  • Condensate drain. The federal energy page notes that clogged drain channels keep a unit from reducing humidity and can cause moisture damage to walls or carpet. Look for water around the indoor unit, and see Mini-Split Cleaning and Maintenance Habits for ductless units.
  • Outdoor unit clear. That page says to trim foliage back at least 2 feet from the outdoor unit and to remove debris. Also clear fallen leaves and brush away snow so air can move.
  • No musty smell. A federal indoor-air page on mold says a moldy odor with no visible source is a warning sign, and that if you suspect mold in the HVAC system you should not run it until it has been checked. If you smell something, stop and call a technician.

Between bookings: setbacks, frozen pipes and humidity

Winter. A federal weather service page on severe cold says not to turn the heat down too much when you are away, to leave it set to at least 55 degrees and to open sink cabinets so warm air reaches plumbing. It also says to let faucets drip slowly when pipes are at risk. A setback to 55 degrees is therefore a floor, not a target; many hosts will pick a higher number, and that choice is our own. See Thermostat Setback Schedules for how much a setback really saves. If you use a smart thermostat, check how it handles guests; see Smart Thermostat Home and Away Modes.

Summer. The federal indoor-air mold guide recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and running air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. A closed, unconditioned house in humid weather can pass that line. Between bookings, leave the cooling on at a warmer setting rather than off, and consider a small humidity meter. Treat any specific warmer setting as our own judgment.

When to call a technician

  • A carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or anyone has headaches or nausea with a fuel-burning appliance running. Leave first, then call for help.
  • A gas smell, a scorched smell or smoke from any equipment.
  • Water at or under an indoor unit, or ice on the lines or coil.
  • A musty smell that returns after you changed the filter and cleared the drain.
  • Rooms that never reach temperature even with registers open and a clean filter.
  • Repeated breaker trips, error lights or a heater that shuts off in a few minutes.
  • Any frozen or burst pipe.

Field card: Short-Stay Rentals: Heating and Cooling for Guests

Note, alarms, filter, then a turnover walk-through.

  • Leave a one-page note on the thermostat and remote.
  • Limit the setpoint range if the thermostat allows it (our own range: about 65 to 76 F).
  • Smoke alarms on every level and in each bedroom; CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; test before each stay (our own frequency).
  • Heaters: 3 feet from anything that burns, plugged straight into the wall, never unattended. Fireplace: screen, ashes in a metal can, out before sleep.
  • Filter: estimate by person-nights (our own method), then look at it.
  • Turnover: registers open, drain, outdoor unit clear, no musty smell.
  • Winter away setting: never below 55 F. Summer: humidity under 60 percent.
