Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Noise: dBA Ratings, Distance and Placement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new outdoor unit can be fine for the owner and a problem for the neighbor. The number on the spec sheet does not tell you what the neighbor hears. This lesson explains what that number is, how distance and walls change it, and what a tech or homeowner can do about placement and vibration.

Sound power is not sound pressure

Two different quantities get called "decibels," and mixing them up causes most of the confusion. Sound power level describes the source itself: how much acoustic energy the unit gives off. It does not change with where you stand. Sound pressure level is what a person or meter actually picks up at one spot, and it depends on distance and surroundings. A university acoustics text states this split plainly and gives the formula that connects them (see Sources). A rating on the unit is a sound power number. Your neighbor's window sees a sound pressure number, and it will be lower than the rating.

What a dBA rating on an outdoor unit means

The "A" means the measurement is weighted to match how human hearing responds across pitch. The standard AHRI 270 sets how outdoor sections of factory-made air conditioners and heat pumps are tested and rated. The text we read says the rating is an overall A-weighted sound power level, rounded to the nearest decibel, measured with the unit running at standard rating conditions in a lab. So the label is a comparison number for shopping, taken under one set of conditions. It is not a reading at your fence line, and a unit working harder on a hot afternoon or in a defrost cycle can sound different.

The Department of Energy heat pump page says to choose a unit with an outdoor sound rating of 7.6 bels or lower. A bel is ten decibels, so that is 76 dB on the same scale (our conversion). It also says fans and compressors make noise, and suggests placing the unit away from windows and neighboring buildings and mounting it on a noise-absorbing base.

How sound drops with distance

For a small source in open air, the Federal Highway Administration states that levels fall about 6 dB(A) each time the distance doubles. The University of Alberta acoustics text gives the same result from the math: for a point source, doubling the distance lowers the level by about 6 dB. A condenser is not a perfect point, and the ground and house are not open air, so treat 6 dB as a good first estimate. For comparison, the FHWA page says a long line of traffic falls more slowly, about 3 dB(A) per doubling, which is why a highway sounds different from a single fan.

Walls, corners and the ground add sound

A unit on the ground radiates into half a sphere, because the ground reflects the sound. A unit beside one wall radiates into a quarter sphere, and one in a corner of two walls and the ground radiates into an eighth. Lecture notes from the University of Illinois physics department list a directivity factor Q of 2 for a source on a floor, 4 at the edge of two surfaces and 8 in a corner of three surfaces. Each doubling of Q adds about 3 dB at the same distance. So a corner is about 6 dB louder than open ground at any distance, using the standard definition 10 log Q (our arithmetic).

City of Seattle siting tips match that pattern. Their sound-rating table drops the allowed unit rating by about 3 dB when one reflective surface is within 10 feet and about 6 dB in a corner or between houses. They tell owners to avoid the side yard, spots within 10 feet of two adjacent walls, and the gap between two houses. The City of North Vancouver guide also warns about hard surfaces that bounce sound toward a neighbor and says to avoid the side yard in favor of the front or rear.

Worked example (ours)

Our own example, with assumed inputs. Assume a unit rated 72 dBA sound power, sitting on the ground in a yard, running at its rated condition. We use the point-source relation SPL = SWL + 10 log10 [Q / (4 π r²)], with r in meters, from the University of Alberta text. We ignore fences, shrubs, air absorption and the neighbor's wall.

  1. 10 ft, open ground (Q = 2). r = 10 × 0.3048 = 3.048 m. r² = 9.29. 4 π r² = 116.7. Q / 116.7 = 2 / 116.7 = 0.01714. 10 log10 (0.01714) = −17.7. SPL = 72 − 17.7 = 54.3 dBA.
  2. 40 ft, open ground (Q = 2). r = 12.19 m. r² = 148.6. 4 π r² = 1867. 2 / 1867 = 0.001071. 10 log10 (0.001071) = −29.7. SPL = 72 − 29.7 = 42.3 dBA. Check: 10 ft to 40 ft is two doublings, so 2 × 6 = 12 dB lower, and 54.3 − 12 = 42.3.
  3. Same unit in a corner (Q = 8). Q is four times larger, so add 10 log10 (4) = 6.0 dB. At 10 ft: 60.4 dBA. At 40 ft: 48.3 dBA.

The corner placement at 40 ft is as loud as open ground at 20 ft. Moving the unit out of the corner is worth as much as halving the distance to the neighbor.

Placement choices that help

  • Keep the unit off the neighbor's side of the lot and away from their windows and openings, as the City of North Vancouver guide advises.
  • Mount it on the ground rather than high on a wall. That guide notes that wall-mounted units are higher and let noise travel more freely.
  • Avoid corners, side yards and narrow gaps between buildings, per the Seattle tips.
  • A barrier must be solid and run to the ground, with no gaps, and sit close to the unit without blocking airflow, the Seattle tips say. A barrier needs clearance for air in and out, so follow the maker's clearance chart.
  • Seattle also notes that a wall that bounces sound back can reduce how well a barrier works.

Vibration pads and line set isolation

Some of what a neighbor or the homeowner hears is vibration carried through solid parts rather than sound through air. The City of North Vancouver guide recommends a firm base with rubber pads or dampeners to cut vibration. A maker's installation manual, as reposted by a parts distributor, also warns about vibration noise carried into the building by the refrigerant lines. It says lines fastened to joists or framing should use vibration-isolating hangers, and lines passing through a wall should be insulated and isolated from it. A tech who fastens a line set rigidly to a stud can turn a quiet unit into a humming wall. Check the manual for the unit in front of you. See also the fan blade lesson for vibration at the source.

Quiet modes on variable-speed units

The City of North Vancouver guide lists variable-speed fans and compressors, soft start and stop, and nighttime or low-sound modes as features to look for. The idea is simple: a unit that does not have to run flat out makes less noise. Whether a given model has a night mode, and how much it lowers the level, is on that maker's data sheet. We did not find a federal source that puts a number on the reduction, so we give none.

Defrost noise on heat pumps

In winter a heat pump reverses to melt frost off the outdoor coil, as the Department of Energy page describes. The reversal can produce a sudden whoosh or thump. A research group at Chalmers University of Technology found that noise at defrost start depends mainly on the pressure difference in the system when the reversing valve shifts, and that a lower difference gave a lower noise level. In plain terms, a short noise at defrost is a known behavior, and a loud bang or repeated noise is a reason to call for service. For other sounds, see furnace, AC and boiler noises and emergency and auxiliary heat.

Noise ordinances and complaints

Many towns set noise limits, often lower at night, and some measure them at the property line. Seattle is one example: its city page states an overnight limit of 45 dBA at the property line in a typical residential zone, from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. on weekdays. That is Seattle's rule, not ours or yours. Check your own town's rule and its measuring point before you place a unit. The Environmental Protection Agency's 1974 document identified 55 dB outdoors and 45 dB indoors as levels to prevent activity interference and annoyance, expressed as averages over long periods. Those are health-and-welfare guidance, not a legal limit, and not the same as our short example.

When a neighbor complains, talk first, then measure: a phone app is only rough, so use a real meter at the property line for any dispute. Moving a unit is hard, so the cheap time to fix noise is before the pad is poured.

Hearing protection for techs working beside running equipment is a separate subject; see hearing protection for HVAC techs. Noise from a heat pump water heater indoors is a related question, covered in heat pump water heaters.

Field card: Outdoor Unit Noise

The label is power. The neighbor hears pressure.

  • Rating on the unit = sound power (dBA, standard AHRI 270). Window level = sound pressure, lower.
  • About 6 dB drop per doubling of distance, open-air point source.
  • Corner about 6 dB more than open ground; one wall about 3 dB more.
  • Our example: 72 dBA unit, 54.3 dBA at 10 ft, 42.3 dBA at 40 ft; corner 60.4 and 48.3.
  • Ground mount, rubber pads, vibration-isolating line hangers, solid barrier with airflow clearance.
  • Check your local noise rule and where it is measured.
