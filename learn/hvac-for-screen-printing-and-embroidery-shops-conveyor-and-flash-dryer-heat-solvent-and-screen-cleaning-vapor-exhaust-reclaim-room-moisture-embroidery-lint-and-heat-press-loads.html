HVAC for Screen Printing and Embroidery Shops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A garment decorating shop looks like a small light-industrial space, but it mixes three different air problems: heat from dryers and presses, solvent and chemical vapor from ink and screen cleaning, and lint from embroidery. Each one needs a different answer, and the worst mistake is solving one in a way that makes another worse.

Where the heat comes from

A conveyor dryer carries printed shirts through a heated tunnel so the ink cures. A flash dryer is a smaller heater that sets one color between passes. Heat presses use a heated platen for transfers. Nearly all of the electric power these machines draw ends up as heat in the building, either inside the dryer's exhaust stream or in the shop. The cooling load question is how much reaches the room. A well-hooded dryer sends part of its heat outdoors with the exhaust air, while an uncovered tunnel exit, a flash unit and a press release theirs to the room air. We work that out below.

Dryer vapor needs local exhaust

A federal health hazard evaluation of small screen printing shops found that heating or mechanically drying inks releases more solvent vapor, and it described shops where the dryers vented into room air. Some shops had local exhaust hoods or ventilated drying booths, but coverage was inconsistent, and two shops used gas ovens with open pilot lights in areas that contained solvents. The report's advice was to consult fire protection experts about those pilots. Water-based and curable inks differ in what they release, so read the safety data sheet for the ink in use and let the safety officer or consultant set the exhaust requirement. Our job as HVAC techs is to make the airflow path work, not to decide the exposure limit.

The federal workplace safety technical manual explains the basic choice. Local exhaust captures a contaminant at or near its source and suits higher-hazard materials and point sources where people stand close. General dilution ventilation lets contaminants enter the room and dilutes them, which suits low-hazard vapors spread widely. A dryer exit and a wash-out booth are point sources, so they call for local capture.

Screen cleaning and reclaim rooms

Reclaiming a screen means removing ink, then stencil emulsion, then sometimes a haze. A federal environmental agency bulletin describes the traditional chemicals as a lacquer thinner for ink, a sodium periodate solution for emulsion, and a blend of xylene, acetone, mineral spirits and cyclohexanone for haze. It notes that scrubbing in a wash-out booth puts the worker's face close to the chemicals, and that enclosed automatic washers cut vapor exposure by as much as 70 percent. The health hazard evaluation found screen cleaning and blasting to be the highest-exposure tasks. A university lab guide that uses milder cleaners still says to use emulsion remover in a well-ventilated area and to spray away from the face.

For the HVAC side, a reclaim room has three needs. First, capture at the wash-out booth, with the hood close to the work, because the technical manual says hood effectiveness falls quickly when it sits far from the source and that strong cross-drafts can cut effectiveness by 75 percent. Second, moisture: pressure washing and drying screens add water vapor, so exhaust that runs while washing is the simplest control, and a coated-screen drying cabinet should not discharge into the dark room used for coating. Third, keep the room slightly negative to the print floor so vapor does not drift toward people, while remembering the next section.

Exhaust must not pull fumes across people

The technical manual says a hood should be placed so contaminants are not drawn through the worker's breathing zone. In a shop the same idea applies to the whole room. Put the supply or makeup air on the operator side and the exhaust on the source side, so air passes the person first and then the source. Exhausted air must also be replaced. The manual says that without makeup air the room goes slightly negative and exhaust flow drops, and that replacement through cracks and roof vents is not acceptable. Open doors that drag outside air across a dryer exit can also push vapor toward the print station. Check the direction with a smoke pencil, not by feel.

At the outdoor end, the manual says exhaust re-entry depends on volume, wind, temperature and where the intakes sit. It suggests stacks at least 10 ft above nearby rooflines or intakes and recommends keeping the stack as far from intakes as possible, with 50 ft recommended. Our own shop-sized reminder: do not put the dryer exhaust next to the makeup air louver. See the related lesson on outdoor air intake separation distances if you are not sure.

Embroidery lint and thread dust

Embroidery machines shed thread fibers and backing lint. This is a filter and coil housekeeping problem more than a vapor problem. Lint loads a return grille or a filter faster than the same air in an office, and a coil that has been fed lint loses airflow. Plan on checking filters more often than a typical schedule and keep the return away from the machines' dust, or add a simple prefilter. Our related lessons on commercial laundry lint and pet grooming hair cover the same filter and coil routine. Keep lint and thread out of the dryer exhaust path as well, since lint near a heated surface is a fire risk.

Worked example (our own): the room cooling load

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Do not use it as a design for a real shop.

  • Assumption 1: the electric conveyor dryer draws 18 kW while running.
  • Assumption 2: the dryer's hood and exhaust carry away 60 percent of that heat, so 40 percent escapes into the room.
  • Assumption 3: two heat presses at 1,800 W each, with the platen heater on half the time, so each averages 900 W.
  • Assumption 4: embroidery machines, lights, computers and people are left out here and would be added in a full load calculation.

For conversion we use two figures from a university energy glossary: 1 kW equals 3,412 Btu/h, and one cooling ton is 12,000 Btu/h.

  1. Dryer heat to the room: 18 kW x 0.40 = 7.2 kW. 7.2 x 3,412 = 24,566 Btu/h.
  2. Presses: 2 x 0.9 kW = 1.8 kW. 1.8 x 3,412 = 6,142 Btu/h.
  3. Total: 24,566 + 6,142 = 30,708 Btu/h.
  4. Tons: 30,708 / 12,000 = 2.56 tons.

So under these assumptions, about 2.6 tons of cooling is needed just for the dryer and presses. The dryer is the biggest term, and the 40 percent figure is the one that moves the answer. Tightening the hood or capturing the tunnel exits lowers the room load, but also remember the makeup air brought in for the hood has its own cooling load that this example leaves out.

Field card: HVAC for Screen Printing and Embroidery Shops

Capture the vapor at the source, replace the air, and count only the heat that stays in the room.

  • Dryer exits and wash-out booths are point sources: local exhaust, hood close to the work.
  • Place supply on the operator side so air passes the person before the source.
  • Exhausted air must be replaced on purpose, not through cracks.
  • Keep exhaust discharge well away from makeup air intakes.
  • Reclaim rooms add moisture and chemical vapor; do not recirculate that air.
  • Embroidery lint loads filters and coils fast: check them more often.
  • Our example: 18 kW dryer, 40 percent to room, two presses at half duty, about 30,700 Btu/h or 2.6 tons.
