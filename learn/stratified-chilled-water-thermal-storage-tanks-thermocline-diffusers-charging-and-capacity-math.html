Stratified Chilled Water Storage Tanks: Thermocline, Diffusers and Capacity Math

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some large plants keep a giant insulated tank of chilled water, fill it overnight, and drain it into the building the next afternoon. There is no ice and no divider inside. Cold water simply sits under warm water. This lesson explains why that works, what the diffusers do, how operators watch the tank, and how to size one with simple arithmetic.

How cold water and warm water share one tank

The U.S. Department of Energy describes the stratified tank as the most common chilled water storage design. It relies on the density difference between cooler supply water and warmer return water, with no physical barrier. Cold supply water sits at the bottom, warm return water floats on top, and the boundary between them is the thermocline, a sharp temperature gradient. DOE gives typical chilled water supply of 39 to 42 F and return of 55 to 60 F or higher.

Diffusers: slow, gentle inflow

Water has to enter and leave without churning the layers. The University of Wyoming describes its tank as having diffusers at the top and bottom that reduce turbulence so the water stratifies. A diffuser maker, Araner, says the design goal is calm, low-velocity flow, and that fast or turbulent inflow disrupts the thermocline, causing mixing and lost usable cooling. The same maker describes radial diffusers (plates, tolerant of flow surges) and slotted ring-pipe diffusers (sharper thermocline, but more sensitive to surges and with more pressure drop).

Research agrees in direction. An OSTI-listed scale-model study of single-pipe diffusers reported better performance with significantly lower inlet Reynolds numbers, and usually better performance with the diffuser lower above the floor. A Penn State full-scale test of a 1.4 million gallon tank with radial diffusers found a link between inlet flow rate and the starting thermocline thickness. For students, the takeaway is practical: the plant controls decide how hard water is pushed into the tank, so ramping flow gently protects stored cooling.

Charging and discharging

The University of Illinois describes its 6.5 million gallon stratified tank this way. At night, extra chilled water goes into the bottom of the tank while warm water is drawn from the top and returned to the chiller plants. During the afternoon peak, cold water is pumped from the bottom out to the buildings and the warmed return goes to the top. The University of Wyoming describes the same pattern: warm water is drawn from the top, cooled, and returned through the bottom diffusers. Notice that cold always moves through the bottom connection and warm through the top, in both directions. That is what keeps the layers in order.

A Texas A&M paper on a campus tank shows the simplest control idea. With no special control the tank acts as a passive reservoir on the plant's loop: it charges when chiller output exceeds campus demand and discharges when output falls short. See chiller plant staging for how chillers are added and dropped, and variable primary flow plants for chiller flow limits. Which valves and pumps connect the tank varies by plant, and the sources we read do not give a standard arrangement, so follow the site's drawings and sequence of operations.

Why plants bother: peak demand and load shifting

DOE says storage shifts cooling to lower-cost time-of-use periods and cuts peak electric load, with the greatest benefit where demand charges are high or on-peak and off-peak prices differ widely. Many office buildings have daytime cooling loads at twice their average or more, so chillers can be sized nearer the average. DOE also notes that many utilities offer $200 to $1,000 per kW of on-peak demand reduction. The University of Illinois reports an 8.5 MW peak demand reduction from its tank and says it lets the plant make extra chilled water at night under real-time pricing. Our ice storage lesson covers the electric bill side in more detail.

Water tank or ice

DOE explains that chilled water stores sensible heat, with a supply around 39 to 42 F. Ice stores latent heat at 32 F (144 Btu per pound for melting), but charging it needs fluid at least 15 F below the normal range of conventional cooling equipment, and may need special ice-making equipment. The water tank can use ordinary chillers at normal temperatures. The trade-off is tank size, because each pound of water holds only a small temperature swing of stored cooling.

Worked example: how big a tank for 4,000 ton-hours?

A ton-hour is 12,000 Btu. Suppose the tank is charged with 40 F water and the building returns it at 56 F. All arithmetic below is Our own.

  1. Temperature swing. Our own: 56 F minus 40 F = 16 F.
  2. Storage per gallon. Our own: 8.34 lb per gallon × 1 Btu/lb·F × 16 F = 133.44 Btu per gallon. (DOE gives the 1 Btu/lb·F heat capacity of water.)
  3. Energy needed. Our own: 4,000 ton-hours × 12,000 Btu per ton-hour = 48,000,000 Btu.
  4. Gallons. Our own: 48,000,000 ÷ 133.44 = about 359,700 gallons.
  5. Cubic feet. Our own: 359,700 ÷ 7.48 gallons per cubic foot = about 48,090 cubic feet.

Real tanks lose some capacity to the thermocline, because the band between warm and cold water holds water that is neither. So a real tank must be bigger than this ideal number. As a rough check, Our own arithmetic on two published tanks lands near 85 percent of the ideal: Illinois lists 50,000 ton-hours at a 13 F difference in a nominal 6.5 million gallon tank, and NC State lists 25,000 ton-hours at 12 F in a 3.5 million gallon tank. Nominal volume may not equal usable volume, so treat that as a sanity check, not a design rule.

Watching the tank: a string of sensors

A Texas A&M paper describes a 1.4 million gallon tank, 64 ft tall, with thirty temperature sensors spaced evenly along its height to monitor the layers, and with tank level reported in feet and percent. Operators there used the changing average tank temperature to estimate charge and discharge rates. The paper also shows a trap: if a lag chiller runs only part of the night, the tank can be overcharged and then run out earlier than planned, and the same paper notes a chiller that cannot hit design output reduces expected tank capacity.

Field issues

  • Thermocline growth. Araner says the thermocline expands as chilled water leaves and warmer water returns, and gets less efficient as it widens. It suggests pausing flow so the water can re-stratify, and notes that regular full discharge is recommended.
  • Mixing. Penn State found that at high flow, large-scale mixing from the inlet diffuser affected the thermocline even after it had moved away from the diffuser. Top-of-tank mixing differed between charge and discharge.
  • Water treatment and pressure. Wyoming's campus planners list chemical treatment (pH, corrosion protection, biological control) and system pressure control as concerns for a storage tank.
  • Low system delta-T. Wyoming lists system delta-T as a design concern. Less return-versus-supply difference means less stored cooling per gallon. See chilled water pumps and delta-T.
  • Heat gain. Texas A&M notes tank heat loss is small but real, so stored cooling is kept near minimum when it is not needed.

Field card: Stratified Chilled Water Storage Tanks

Cold at the bottom, warm on top, and keep the flow gentle.

  • Density keeps cold supply water under warm return water; the boundary is the thermocline.
  • Cold water uses the bottom connection and warm water the top, both when charging and when discharging.
  • Diffusers slow the inflow so layers are not stirred up.
  • Capacity per gallon (Our own): 8.34 × temperature swing in F, in Btu. A ton-hour is 12,000 Btu.
  • Example: 16 F swing gives 133.44 Btu per gallon, so 4,000 ton-hours needs about 359,700 gallons ideal, or 48,090 cubic feet.
  • Real tanks lose some capacity to the thermocline.
  • Watch the sensor string, tank level, water treatment and delta-T.
