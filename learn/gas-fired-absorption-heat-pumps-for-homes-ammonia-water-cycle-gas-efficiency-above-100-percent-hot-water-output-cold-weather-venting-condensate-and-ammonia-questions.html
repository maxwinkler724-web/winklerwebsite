Gas-Fired Absorption Heat Pumps for Homes and Small Buildings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas furnace or boiler can never deliver more than 100 percent of the gas energy it burns. A gas-fired absorption heat pump can, because the flame is only part of the heat it delivers: the rest is pulled from outdoor air. This lesson explains how that works, what the water side looks like, how cold weather changes the numbers, and how the machine compares with a condensing boiler and an electric heat pump.

What a gas-fired absorption heat pump is

An ordinary heat pump uses an electric compressor to move heat. An absorption heat pump replaces the compressor with a heated chemical loop. In the residential design described in a federal project report, the working pair is ammonia and water in a single-effect cycle, and the unit is gas fired. The same report says current gas furnaces are fundamentally limited to a gas-based coefficient of performance below 1.0, while the heat pump target was about 1.45 at 47°F outdoors (see Sources 1 and 2). Those are project figures from prototypes, not a guarantee for any product you will meet in the field.

If you have read our lesson on absorption chillers, the idea is the same, with a different pair of fluids and the output turned toward heating.

How the cycle moves heat

Follow the ammonia around the loop in the diagram below. This is our simplified description of a generic single-effect ammonia-water cycle, not a drawing of any one unit.

  1. Generator. The gas burner heats a solution rich in ammonia. Ammonia boils out of the water as a vapor. What is left is a weak solution, mostly water.
  2. Condenser. The hot ammonia vapor gives up its heat to the building's water loop and turns to liquid.
  3. Evaporator. The liquid ammonia passes through an expansion step that drops its pressure and temperature, so it boils at a temperature below the outdoor air. It absorbs heat from the outdoor air while it boils.
  4. Absorber. The cold ammonia vapor meets the weak solution returning from the generator and dissolves into it. This releases heat too, and that heat also goes to the water loop.
  5. Solution pump. A small pump sends the ammonia-rich solution back up to the generator. Pumping a liquid takes far less energy than compressing a vapor, which is why the burner supplies most of the drive.

Why the efficiency can top 100 percent

Count the heat leaving to the water loop at the condenser and absorber. It is the burner heat plus the outdoor heat picked up at the evaporator, with small pump and fan loads on top. So delivered heat divided by gas energy burned can be greater than 1. The federal project page quotes an annual fuel utilization efficiency of 140 percent against 100 percent for conventional gas heating (Source 1). A federal national-lab slide set used a similar target against a 92 percent condensing furnace (Source 3). Both are targets for developing equipment. In lab testing, the project report found about 1.45 at 47°F outdoors with 100°F return water, and one prototype reached 139 percent on an annual fuel utilization test point (Source 2).

Hot water for floors, coils and taps

These machines make hot water, not hot air. The project report tested prototypes with return water between 100°F and 120°F, and it describes a combination arrangement that also heats domestic water through an indirect storage tank (Source 2). From that, our own reasoning is that the best fit is a low-temperature emitter: radiant floor tubing, or a water coil in an air handler sized for lower water temperature. Domestic water is a separate priority, and the report notes the field controls it studied still lacked a top-off step at the end of a water-heating call. Our lesson on boiler return temperature and condensing explains the same low-return-temperature logic for condensing boilers.

Cold-weather performance

The report says performance is highest at warmer outdoor temperatures and lower return water temperatures. Prototypes were tested from 5°F to 47°F outdoors. Daily average gas-based COP in one field demonstration ranged from about 0.5 to 1.5, and the report blames a value below 1 at one site on poor expansion-valve control and short cycling (Source 2). That is the point for a student: the cycle is only as good as its controls and its operating conditions. A gas-based COP below 1 means the unit burned more gas than it delivered in heat, which a condensing boiler would not do. For electric heat pumps in cold weather, see cold climate heat pumps.

Venting, condensate and outdoor installation

  • Venting. The project unit used a forced-air premix burner. It first failed a blocked-vent test because carbon monoxide passed 400 ppm before the flame sensor shut it down, and the team added a second air-proving switch (Source 2). Contractors also reported difficulty finding exterior spots to end the intake and exhaust. Follow the maker's venting instructions and local code for clearances.
  • Condensate. A gas appliance this efficient condenses water from its exhaust. Contractors in the report worried most about pitching the condensate line so it does not trap air, and they said running it indoors is their usual practice. Heat trace for freeze protection was seen as an energy drain, and one contractor asked for an outlet on the unit for it (Source 2). See condensate neutralizers for the drain side.
  • Outdoor placement. The prototype outdoor unit was 34 by 47 by 46 inches, narrowed to fit a 36 inch gate. Contractors called it large and hard to set on sloped ground or in tight spaces (Source 2).

Ammonia safety questions

Apprentices hear "ammonia" and ask whether it is in the house. In the design the report studied, all of the refrigerant stays outside, and stakeholders did not treat it as a major concern, although contractors said homeowners need education about it (Source 2). A national-lab slide set describes a different, solid-salt ammonia design with the ammonia sealed in hermetic vessels in the outdoor unit (Source 3), and a national-lab review notes that ammonia chemisorption designs are aimed at cold-climate heating (Source 4). We cannot give a charge size or code limit for any particular unit here. Ask the maker for the nameplate charge and the installation manual, and ask the local authority about outdoor siting. For context, a federal occupational-health pocket guide lists ammonia as irritating to eyes, nose and throat, with a 300 ppm level considered immediately dangerous to life or health and flammability limits of 15 to 28 percent in air (Source 5). If a unit smells of ammonia, treat it as a leak: keep people away, ventilate from outside if safe, and call the maker and emergency services as the manual directs.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not measured data. We assume a small building needs 800 therms of delivered heat in a heating season (1 therm = 100,000 Btu). We assume a condensing boiler at 95 percent seasonal efficiency and a gas absorption heat pump at 130 percent seasonal efficiency on a gas basis. We pick 130 percent as a figure below the 139 to 145 percent lab results, to allow for cold days and cycling.

  1. Boiler gas input: 800 therms ÷ 0.95 = 842.1 therms.
  2. Heat pump gas input: 800 therms ÷ 1.30 = 615.4 therms.
  3. Gas saved: 842.1 − 615.4 = 226.7 therms.
  4. Share saved: 226.7 ÷ 842.1 = 0.269, about 27 percent.
  5. Outdoor heat in the heat pump case: 800 − 615.4 = 184.6 therms, which is 23 percent of the delivered heat (184.6 ÷ 800 = 0.231).

Break-even check: the heat pump only matches the 95 percent boiler if its seasonal gas-based efficiency is 95 percent. At 100 percent, gas input would be 800 therms, still 42.1 therms less than the boiler; below 95 percent it loses. The field days with a COP under 1 in the report show how a poorly controlled unit can give up its advantage. We leave out electricity for pumps and fans on both sides, and we state no prices.

Field card: Gas-Fired Absorption Heat Pumps

Burner heat plus outdoor heat equals water heat.

  • Single-effect ammonia-water cycle: generator, condenser, evaporator, absorber, small solution pump.
  • Gas-based efficiency can exceed 100 percent in lab conditions; field averages vary widely.
  • Output is hot water; lower return water temperature helps efficiency.
  • Colder air and higher water temperature lower capacity and efficiency.
  • Plan for vent termination, a pitched condensate line and a placement outdoors.
  • Ammonia: read the maker's manual, do not guess charge or siting rules.
  • Example: 800 therms delivered needs 842.1 therms at 95 percent and 615.4 therms at 130 percent.
