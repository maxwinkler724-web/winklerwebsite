Does Replacing a Furnace, Boiler or AC Need a Permit in New Jersey?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you are asking whether you need a permit to replace a furnace in NJ, the short, sourced answer is: usually yes, a permit is involved, but for a like-for-like swap it is a lighter kind called minor work. This lesson walks through how the state construction code sorts the job, and why your town's construction official has the final word.

Not legal advice. This page is general education built from state web pages and rules read in 2026. Rules and forms change. Your local construction office administers the code, so call your town's construction official before you start work or sign a contract.

Who decides: your local construction office

The Uniform Construction Code (UCC) is a statewide code in N.J.A.C. 5:23, published by the Department of Community Affairs (DCA). But permits are not issued by DCA in Trenton. The DCA permit forms page tells applicants to submit to their Local Construction Code Enforcement Office and lists a roster of local municipal contacts. So the person to call is the construction official in the town where the house stands.

Three buckets of work

DCA's permit forms page names three categories under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2: a full permit (5:23-2.14), minor work, sometimes called "permit lite" (5:23-2.17A), and ordinary maintenance, which needs no permit (5:23-2.7).

  • Ordinary maintenance. Under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.7(a), no permit application and no notice is needed. The list in the rule includes replacing motors, pumps and fans at the same capacity, repairing or replacing heating supply and return piping and radiation elements as long as the piping is not rearranged, duct work, air conditioning repair, and repair or replacement of control devices. The rule does not list replacing a furnace or boiler. It also excludes adding, altering, replacing or relocating gas piping, and most electrical wiring changes. DCA's 2018 summary adds that calling something ordinary maintenance does not waive code compliance.
  • Minor work. N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.17A lists replacement of existing low-pressure boilers, warm air furnaces and air conditioning units (including condensing units) with new equipment of like capacity. It also lists replacing a water heater with one of like capacity. DCA's 2018 summary says minor work does need a construction permit, but work may start once notice is given to the local enforcing agency.
  • Full permit. Work that does not fit the other two buckets goes through the regular permit process.

One wrinkle to know about: DCA Bulletin 09-1 on LP-gas systems (revised July 2026) describes replacing boilers or furnaces as needing a full permit or qualifying as emergency work, while describing water heater replacement as minor work. That bulletin is about propane systems, and the minor work rule describes like-capacity boiler and furnace swaps as minor work. We could not reconcile the two from the pages we read, which is one more reason to ask the construction official which category your job falls in.

The minor work notice process

Per N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.17A(b), notice goes to the local enforcing agency before work begins. It can be oral or written, in person or electronic, with a brief summary and the work location. Giving notice also consents to the agency entering to inspect. A permit application must then be delivered within five business days of the notice, and the fee is paid before the permit is issued. For inspection, the rule says the agency must inspect minor work within three business days of the request, or within another agreed period of up to 30 days. DCA's 2018 summary says the older 30-day final inspection window no longer applies. Fees are set locally, and we did not read a fee schedule, so no dollar amounts appear here.

Which subcodes touch the job

DCA's forms page lists separate technical section forms: Building (F110), Electrical (F120), Plumbing (F130), Fire Protection (F140), Mechanical Inspection (F145), and others. It says the type of project determines which apply. The F145 form lists a hot air furnace, hot water boiler, steam boiler, water heater and gas piping connections as equipment, with a use group of R-3 or R-5 and options for new, modification, conversion or replacement, plus inspection lines for appliance, chimney or vent, piping, cooling and final. Bulletin 09-1 says that for water heaters, piping falls under the plumbing subcode and clearances and venting under the fire protection subcode, and that for boilers and furnaces the inspection covers clearances, venting, and electrical and gas connections. DCA also lists form F370, Chimney Verification for Replacement of Fuel-Fired Equipment, among related forms. Which forms your town wants is a local question.

Who can pull the permit and do the work

Under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.15(d), the owner or the owner's agent may apply, as may a licensed contractor on the project. If someone other than the owner applies, an owner affidavit is needed. The same section says heating, ventilating, air conditioning and refrigeration work must be done by persons licensed under state law, with an exception for a single-family homeowner working on his or her own dwelling.

The state HVACR licensing law (P.L. 2007, c.211, section 9) has a similar exemption for a single-family owner who personally occupies the dwelling and does the work after getting all required permits. But work involving CFC or HCFC refrigerants must be done by a licensed Master HVACR contractor. A contractor on a job must hold the Master HVACR license from the Division of Consumer Affairs board; see NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers. The board's FAQ also says a municipality cannot require its own extra HVACR license, and that a bona fide representative can authorize another person by letter to obtain a permit, though the representative still signs it. The permit application form F145 has fields for a contractor license number and a home improvement contractor registration number or exemption reason.

Worked example: gas furnace and AC swap

Hypothetical job: a single-family house, owner-occupied, in a town we will not name. The old gas warm-air furnace and the outdoor AC condensing unit are being replaced with new units of the same capacity, in the same places. Walk it through the categories.

  1. Is it ordinary maintenance? No. Ordinary maintenance covers parts like motors and controls, and the rule does not list furnace replacement. Changing gas piping is excluded outright.
  2. Is it minor work? Likely, because 5:23-2.17A names warm air furnaces and air conditioning units replaced with like capacity. If the new units are larger or move, it may not fit, so ask.
  3. Give notice. Before work starts, tell the local enforcing agency by phone, in writing or by email: a short summary and the address.
  4. File the application. Within five business days of the notice. The mechanical technical section (F145) lists a hot air furnace and cooling inspection. Depending on the job, the town may also want electrical, plumbing or fire protection sections.
  5. Who does the work? For most homeowners, a licensed Master HVACR contractor does the job (our framing, not a rule quote). An owner-occupant homeowner exemption exists in the rule and the licensing law, but the AC swap involves refrigerant, and the licensing law says CFC or HCFC work must be done by a licensed master. Confirm with the town and the board before attempting any of it.
  6. Request the inspection. The agency has three business days from the request, or another agreed period of up to 30 days.

What we could not source

Field card: Furnace, Boiler or AC Permits in NJ

Call the town's construction official first.

  • The code is statewide (N.J.A.C. 5:23); permits come from the local construction office.
  • Ordinary maintenance: no permit, no notice. Furnace and boiler replacement is not on the list.
  • Minor work: like-capacity furnace, boiler, AC or water heater replacement. Notice first, application within 5 business days.
  • Inspection: agency has 3 business days from your request, or an agreed period up to 30 days.
  • Subcode forms depend on the job: mechanical (F145), plumbing, electrical, fire protection.
  • Owner-occupant exemptions exist, but CFC or HCFC refrigerant work needs a licensed master.
