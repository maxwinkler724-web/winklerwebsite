Dual Duct and Multizone Air Systems: Hot Decks, Cold Decks and Retrofits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some older buildings have two supply ducts running to every zone, one hot and one cold, or a single air handler with a row of zone dampers on its outlet. Both designs make the temperature each zone wants by blending hot and cold air. That is easy to control and hard on energy. This lesson explains how the blending works, where the waste comes from, and what published retrofits have done about it.

Dual duct: two ducts, one mixing box per zone

Trane's Trace 3D Plus help pages describe a dual-duct system this way: each room's mixing box gets air from a separate hot deck and cold deck. When the room is too warm, the box opens to the cold deck. When it is too cold, the box opens to the hot deck. Inside the deadband, the page says both sides close. Because the decks are separate, one room can heat while another cools at the same time. The page also says that blending hot and cold air happens only when a reheat minimum airflow is specified.

The LBNL dual-duct fault-detection data set documents a simulated example. The cold deck holds 55 F in summer, and the hot deck is controlled to 90 F in winter and transition seasons. Each terminal has a hot damper and a cold damper. The cold damper holds a minimum of 100 cfm during heating, and the hot damper holds a minimum of 100 cfm during cooling, so some of the other deck is always flowing. That is one reason these systems mix by design.

Constant volume or variable volume

Maker literature separates mixing from non-mixing boxes. Titus describes a mixing dual-duct terminal that blends hot (or tempered ventilation) and cold air in any proportion, and says a change in cold airflow follows a change in hot airflow to keep the total mixed volume constant. Titus also describes a non-mixing type that modulates hot and cold in separate streams and says it should not be used for simultaneous heating and cooling because of stratification. Price describes mixing units that give a uniform discharge temperature and non-mixing units that bring the two airstreams together further downstream. A Texas State University design standard says that for constant volume dual-duct systems a flow sensor goes in the cold duct and one at the box discharge, and that approved dual-duct installations need separate maximum cfm values for the hot and cold inlets. For single-duct variable volume boxes, see VAV Boxes Basics.

Multizone: the mixing happens at the unit

A multizone air handler puts the hot and cold decks side by side inside the unit. A pair of dampers for each zone blends the two streams, and a separate duct carries the blended air to that zone. An ASHRAE Journal article hosted by the Whole Building Design Guide (an Army Corps of Engineers project) says the supply fan runs at a single speed, so airflow stays constant, and the streams are mixed at the air handler outlet based on zone demands. The project's tech note describes a "Texas style" bypass deck: a two-deck unit whose bypass deck has no coil and uses mixed air for free heating, with heating coils typically in each zone duct. A neutral deck design adds a third, coil-free deck.

Why mixing wastes energy

The Army-hosted material states that the conventional multizone design runs the heating and cooling coils continuously and at the same time, so energy is spent whether or not any zone needs it. It adds that conditioned air leaks through closed zone dampers into the supply air, and that the constant-speed fan moves full airflow regardless of load. For dual duct, the Trane page notes that shutting off cooling does not shut off the air handler, because fans keep supplying untreated air to open boxes. In the two-fan version it says supply air reset saves cooling energy but raises room humidity and fan energy.

Worked example (Our own)

A zone mixing box delivers 1,000 cfm at 62 F. The cold deck is 55 F and the hot deck is 95 F. The arithmetic below is Our own, using the sensible heat formula covered in the heat transfer formulas lesson.

  1. Let x be the fraction of air from the hot deck. Mixing balance: x × 95 + (1 − x) × 55 = 62.
  2. Expand: 95x + 55 − 55x = 62, so 40x = 7.
  3. x = 7 / 40 = 0.175, so 17.5 percent of the air is from the hot deck and 82.5 percent from the cold deck.
  4. Hot deck flow = 0.175 × 1,000 = 175 cfm. Cold deck flow = 825 cfm.
  5. Heat carried by the hot deck air above the cold deck temperature: 1.08 × 175 cfm × (95 − 55) = 7,560 Btu/h.
  6. Check: 1.08 × 1,000 × (62 − 55) = 7,560 Btu/h. The two methods agree.
  7. Divide by 12,000 Btu/h per ton: about 0.63 ton of cooling-equivalent energy.

So in this example about 7,560 Btu/h of heat was added to air that had already been cooled to the cold deck temperature, and that is the part of the cooling that was reheated and mixed away. Our own simplification: this treats the hot deck air as starting at 55 F and ignores fan heat and duct gains. It shows the size of the mixing term, not a precise bill.

Deck reset and what fails in the field

The Army retrofit project used a binary hot deck reset: about 90 F when outside air is below 50 F, and about 80 F when above, so a hotter winter deck lets the fan run at lower airflow. It also closed coil valves when no zone called for them: a hot deck valve was enabled when a zone damper command fell between 0 and 25 percent, and the project noted that if all damper signals were under 5 percent, cooling was essentially not needed. In the LBNL data set, the simulated faults include stuck dampers and stuck coil valves, sensor bias on the hot and cold deck temperatures, and unstable control from a widened proportional band. Specifications also cap damper leakage on mixing boxes: a University of Michigan master specification limits terminal damper leakage to 5 cfm at 3.0 in. w.c., and Texas State's standard sets size-based limits at 6 in. w.c.

Retrofit options as cited

  • Replace with VAV. The Army project says contractor estimates for similar units were over $500,000, and its tech note lists $550K to $750K for a complete VAV replacement.
  • Add variable volume controls to a multizone unit. A fan drive, an outside airflow measurement array and new programming. The tech note reports air handler savings of 24 percent to 60 percent across five units and incremental retrofit costs of $7.2K to $13K, with typical payback of 3 to 5 years expected.
  • Bypass or neutral deck designs avoid heating and cooling the same air but have more coils or decks to maintain, per the same note.

Those savings are from one demonstration project, so treat them as an example, not a promise. See retro-commissioning for finding this kind of waste, VAV box calibration for box checks, and economizer settings, because the Trane page warns that a shared economizer deck can raise heating load.

Field card: Dual Duct and Multizone

Two decks, one blend: every degree of mixing is energy paid twice.

  • Dual duct: hot and cold ducts to a mixing box at each zone.
  • Multizone: hot and cold decks in the unit, zone dampers at the unit outlet, constant fan speed.
  • Mixing balance: x × hot + (1 − x) × cold = supply temperature.
  • 1.08 × cfm × dT gives sensible Btu/h.
  • Our example: 62 F from 55 F and 95 F is 17.5 percent hot deck, 7,560 Btu/h.
  • Cited retrofit ideas: VAV conversion, fan drive plus controls, hot deck reset, bypass deck.
