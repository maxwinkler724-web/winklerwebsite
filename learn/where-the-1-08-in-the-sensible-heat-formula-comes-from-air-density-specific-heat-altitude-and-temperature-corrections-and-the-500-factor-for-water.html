Where the 1.08 in the Sensible Heat Formula Comes From: Air Density, Specific Heat, Altitude and Temperature Corrections, and the 500 Factor for Water

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every tech learns Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT. Fewer know that the 1.08 is a product of three ordinary numbers, and that it is only right for one kind of air. This lesson builds the number from scratch, shows how to correct it for altitude, hot plenum air and humid air, and then does the same for the 500 used with water.

Why the factor is not a law of nature

Sensible heat moves when a mass of something changes temperature. The basic rule is heat = mass flow × specific heat × temperature change. A fan moves volume, not mass, so the 1.08 is a shortcut that turns cubic feet per minute into pounds per hour and folds in the specific heat. If the air is not the kind the shortcut assumed, the answer drifts. Our overview of the field formulas covers all four constants side by side. Here we stay with 1.08 and 500 and work the corrections.

Building 1.08 from three factors

Air density, 0.075 lb per cubic foot. A 1976 standard atmosphere table puts sea-level air at 1.225 kg/m³ at 15°C (59°F). NASA Glenn's equation of state page gives the ideal gas form, density = pressure ÷ (gas constant × absolute temperature), so at the same pressure, density falls in proportion as absolute temperature rises. Our own conversion: 1.225 kg/m³ is 0.0765 lb/ft³, and warming that air from 59°F to 70°F multiplies it by 518.67 ÷ 529.67, which gives 0.0749. So the familiar 0.075 is dry air near 70°F at sea-level pressure. The conversions use the NIST pound, gallon and foot factors.

Sixty minutes per hour. CFM counts cubic feet each minute and the answer is wanted per hour, so multiply by 60.

Specific heat, 0.24 Btu per pound per °F. A university psychrometrics unit gives 1.01 kJ/kg for dry air. Dividing by 4.1868 (the NIST factor between kJ/kg·K and Btu/lb·°F) gives 0.2412, which rounds to 0.24.

Multiply: 0.075 × 60 = 4.5, and 4.5 × 0.24 = 1.08. The 4.5 is the same number that appears in the enthalpy method; see measuring air-side capacity with enthalpy.

The matching water factor, 500

Water is easier because a gallon is a fixed volume of a nearly fixed-density liquid. A gallon weighs about 8.33 lb, a minute is 1/60 of an hour, and water's specific heat is about 1 Btu/lb·°F. So 8.33 × 60 × 1 gives 500 in Btu/h = 500 × GPM × ΔT.

Our own check against NIST's water property table at atmospheric pressure: at 20°C (68°F) density is 998.21 kg/m³ and Cp is 4.1841 kJ/kg·K. With 3.785412 liters per gallon and 0.4535924 kg per pound, that is 8.330 lb/gal and 0.9994 Btu/lb·°F, so the factor is 8.330 × 60 × 0.9994 = 499.5. The same arithmetic on other rows of that table gives these results.

  • 50°F water (10°C: 999.70 kg/m³, Cp 4.1952): 501.6.
  • 140°F water (60°C: 983.20, 4.1850): 492.1.
  • 176°F water (80°C: 971.79, 4.1968): 487.8.

Chilled water is almost exactly 500. Hot heating water at 176°F is about 2.4 percent lower, because hot water is lighter. Example (our own, with assumed numbers): a heating loop moving 20 GPM with a 20°F drop carries 500 × 20 × 20 = 200,000 Btu/h by the shortcut and 487.8 × 20 × 20 = 195,100 Btu/h by the corrected figure. For antifreeze mixtures use the maker's property data, not 500.

Correction 1: altitude

At altitude the fan still moves the same cubic feet, but each cubic foot holds fewer pounds of air. The factor falls in proportion to density. NASA Glenn's atmosphere model gives the standard troposphere equations: temperature falls 0.00649°C per meter, pressure follows a power law, and density = pressure ÷ (0.2869 × absolute temperature). Our own run of those equations at 1,524 m (5,000 ft) gives a density ratio of 0.863 to sea level.

A published table of the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere in English units lists, at 5,000 ft, 41.17°F, 12.228 psi and 20.48 × 10-4 slugs/ft³, against 59°F, 14.696 psi and 23.77 at sea level. Our own ratios: density 20.48 ÷ 23.77 = 0.862, pressure 12.228 ÷ 14.696 = 0.832.

Which ratio do you use? The table's density ratio assumes the air is at the table's cold 41°F. Return air inside a house is about the same 70°F as at sea level, so only the pressure drop thins it, and the right ratio is the pressure ratio, 0.832. Use 0.862 only for air that really is at standard-atmosphere temperature, such as outdoor air in a mild climate. The pressure units lesson shows where the altitude pressure drop comes from.

Correction 2: hot air in the plenum

The ideal gas relation also governs temperature. Air at 120°F is less dense than air at 70°F by the ratio of absolute temperatures (add 459.67 to each): 529.67 ÷ 579.67 = 0.914. The 1.08 becomes 1.08 × 0.914 = 0.987 if your CFM reading is taken at the hot supply. Rule of thumb: pick the density for the place you measure the airflow. A reading at the return grille is near 70°F, so 1.08 is fine there. A reading in the hot plenum needs the lower factor, or the reading should be converted back to return conditions. The airflow measurement lesson covers the temperature rise method and its measuring points.

Correction 3: humid air

The 0.24 is for dry air. Moist air also carries water vapor, which has a higher specific heat. The same university unit gives the humid specific heat as 1.01 + 1.89 × g kJ per kg of dry air, where g is the humidity ratio in kg of water per kg of dry air. Our own assumption: g = 0.010, about 70 grains per pound. The specific heat becomes 1.0289 kJ/kg, which is 1.9 percent higher than dry air. But the vapor also displaces dry air. Using 0.622 (see the humidity ratio lesson), vapor is 0.010 ÷ 0.632 = 1.58 percent of the mix by pressure, leaving 98.4 percent as much dry air per cubic foot. Net: 1.0187 × 0.9842 = 1.003. The two effects almost cancel at comfort conditions, which is why nobody puts a humidity term in the field formula.

Worked example (our own): the same furnace at sea level and at 5,000 ft

Assumptions: a gas furnace delivers 60,000 Btu/h to the air, a measured temperature rise of 50°F, and return air at about 70°F, so the standard 1.08 applies at sea level. We treat the 60,000 Btu/h as the heat actually delivered to the air at both sites; a real furnace is derated at altitude by the maker's rules, which we do not cover here.

  1. Sea level: CFM = 60,000 ÷ (1.08 × 50) = 60,000 ÷ 54 = 1,111 CFM.
  2. At 5,000 ft, return air at 70°F: factor = 1.08 × 0.832 = 0.899. CFM = 60,000 ÷ (0.899 × 50) = 60,000 ÷ 44.9 = about 1,335 CFM. That is 20 percent more actual cubic feet, which is just 1 ÷ 0.832.
  3. If you instead used the table's density ratio 0.862, because the air is at 41°F: factor = 1.08 × 0.862 = 0.931, and CFM = 60,000 ÷ 46.55 = about 1,289 CFM.
  4. Plenum check at sea level: if the 1,111 CFM at the return reads 1,216 CFM at the 120°F supply (1,111 × 579.67 ÷ 529.67), using plain 1.08 would give 1.08 × 1,216 × 50 = 65,664 Btu/h, 9.4 percent too high. The hot-air factor 0.987 gives 0.987 × 1,216 × 50 = 60,000.

The takeaway: at 5,000 ft the fan has to move about a fifth more cubic feet of thinner air to carry the same heat. A temperature rise reading does not care about altitude, so it stays a good test, but using 1.08 to turn a rise into CFM there would under-count the airflow by about 17 percent. See temperature split and furnace temperature rise for measuring the rise itself.

Field card: where the 1.08 and 500 come from

Know what is inside the constant, then correct it.

  • 1.08 = 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F (dry air near 70°F, sea level).
  • 500 = 8.33 lb/gal × 60 min/h × 1 Btu/lb·°F; about 500 at 68°F and about 488 at 176°F.
  • Altitude: multiply 1.08 by the pressure ratio for indoor air (about 0.83 at 5,000 ft).
  • Hot air: multiply by absolute temperature ratio (70°F over 120°F is 0.914).
  • Humid air: the vapor adds specific heat but displaces dry air, so it nets out near 1.00.
  • Our 5,000 ft example: 1,111 CFM at sea level becomes about 1,335 CFM.
