Ski Area Base Lodge HVAC: Boot Rooms, Entrance Crowds, Fireplaces and Freeze Protection

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A slope-side lodge is a building with a split personality. For a few hours at lunch it is packed with people in wet gear, the doors never stop swinging and the kitchen is at full fire. By night it is empty and, in the shoulder seasons, may sit closed with no one watching the pipes. This lesson walks through the loads and hazards that make these buildings different, with one worked example on boot room moisture.

Boot rooms and gear drying

Wet boots, liners, gloves and hats are a steady moisture source. The federal worker-safety research institute advises outdoor winter workers to wear waterproof, insulated boots and to carry extra socks, gloves and clothes. Guests do the same, so a lodge full of people changing into dry gear is expected, and the wet items end up somewhere. If that somewhere is a closed boot room, the water leaves the gear as vapor and the room turns into a damp, fogged space with sweating windows and musty walls.

There are two ways to handle it. One is to dilute the moisture with outdoor air, which in winter is very dry in terms of humidity ratio, the pounds of water per pound of dry air. The other is to dry the gear with warm, low-humidity air passing over it and send that air outside or through a dehumidifier. Our worked example below shows how much outdoor air the first approach needs, and why the answer is often larger than people expect. The practical lesson is that boot rooms want their own exhaust or drying equipment, a warm floor or heated racks, and a return path that does not push the damp air into the dining area.

Our own worked example: how much outdoor air does a boot room need?

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption for teaching, not a design value.

  • 60 pairs of wet boots, each holding 0.4 lb of water in shells and liners (assumed).
  • 60 pairs of wet gloves, each holding 0.2 lb of water (assumed).
  • All of that water evaporates over 6 hours of drying (assumed).
  • Indoor air held at a humidity ratio of 0.0065 lb water per lb dry air (assumed, roughly a comfortable room).
  • Outdoor air at 0.0010 lb water per lb dry air (assumed, cold winter air).
  • Indoor 70 F and outdoor 20 F (assumed) for the heating check at the end.

Step 1, moisture released. Boots: 60 x 0.4 = 24 lb. Gloves: 60 x 0.2 = 12 lb. Total 36 lb. Over 6 hours that is 36 / 6 = 6 lb of water per hour.

Step 2, humidity difference. 0.0065 - 0.0010 = 0.0055 lb water per lb dry air.

Step 3, mass balance. Standard air is about 0.075 lb per cubic foot, so 1 cfm is 0.075 x 60 = 4.5 lb of dry air per hour. Moisture removed = 4.5 x cfm x 0.0055 = 0.02475 x cfm lb/h. Set that equal to 6 lb/h: cfm = 6 / 0.02475 = about 242 cfm.

Step 4, check with the 4,840 latent factor. The factor 4,840 comes from 0.075 x 60 x about 1,076 Btu per lb of water. Latent load: 6 lb/h x 1,076 = about 6,456 Btu/h. Latent capacity of the air per cfm = 4,840 x 0.0055 = 26.6 Btu/h per cfm. So cfm = 6,456 / 26.6 = about 243 cfm. The two routes agree within rounding.

Step 5, what it costs in heat. The sensible factor is 4.5 x 0.24 = 1.08. Heating 242 cfm from 20 F to 70 F takes 1.08 x 242 x 50 = about 13,068 Btu/h. That is more than twice the latent figure, which is why a heat recovery unit or a dedicated drying system often beats simply opening up more outdoor air.

The answer is sensitive to the assumptions. Halve the gear count or stretch the drying time to 12 hours and the air drops to about half or a quarter. A mild, humid day with outdoor air at 0.0040 would shrink the humidity difference to 0.0025 and more than double the airflow. Always run the numbers on the actual gear count and the actual weather.

Big entrance doors and crowds in ski boots

Lodge entrances see waves of guests, and every opening lets cold air in. A federal national-lab report on building entrance infiltration found that door-opening frequency is the main driver of infiltration: a 30 percent swing in frequency moved peak infiltration by roughly 26 percent down to 25 percent up, while moving outdoor temperature from 60 F to minus 40 F changed it by only about 7 percent. The same report modeled vestibules cutting peak infiltration in every building type it listed, for example a quick-service restaurant from 1,913 cfm to 1,237 cfm. That is a different building, but it shows the idea: a vestibule works as an air lock when the two sets of doors are not open at the same time.

In a ski lodge the vestibule also has to hold a floor full of snowmelt, so floors need drainage or walk-off mats and the heating near the doors must survive soaked boots and slush. Cabinet or wall-mounted heaters near the doors can warm the zone, and our lesson on cabinet unit heaters in vestibules covers their low-limit freeze controls. Tall entry spaces with large glass also benefit from keeping outdoor air from falling down the glass and across the floor. Our own rule of thumb: ask where the cold air goes when the doors open, and put the heat there.

Lunch peaks, kitchen loads and intermittent occupancy

The dining hall is full for a short time. Bodies add heat and moisture, and the kitchen exhaust pulls a lot of air out of the building, which has to be replaced somewhere. If makeup air is not provided, the lodge goes negative and cold air whistles in through every door crack, and gas appliances may spill. See commercial kitchen hoods and make-up air basics and golf clubhouse HVAC for a similar swing pattern.

Because the crowd arrives and leaves on a schedule, controls matter. Demand-based ventilation, occupancy schedules and setback temperatures let the building coast overnight and recover before the doors open. Do not let a setback drop below the freeze point of anything with water in it. Pre-heating slowly costs less than catching up in a hurry, a point also made in our recreation center lesson.

Fireplaces and combustion air

A big lodge fireplace is also a big exhaust fan. It pulls air up the chimney, which has to come from somewhere, and in a tight, kitchen-exhausted building it may come back down the flue. The federal public health agency lists fireplaces, furnaces, gas water heaters and stoves among the sources of carbon monoxide and advises annual service of fuel-burning appliances and chimneys, proper venting of gas appliances, never using an unvented stove or fireplace for heating, and carbon monoxide detectors. For an apprentice that means checking that the room has a dedicated outdoor air path for the fire, that exhaust fans in the same space are not fighting it, and that detectors are present. See combustion air for the basics.

Rental shop and ski tuning areas

Waxing gives off fumes and grinding or edging makes fine dust. The federal worker-safety agency calls ventilation one of the most important engineering controls available for protecting workers, and the usual order of attack is to capture contaminants at the source (a downdraft bench or hood) before relying on general room dilution. Our own advice for the tune shop is a dedicated exhaust, makeup air that does not dump cold air on the workers, and a room kept at a slight negative pressure so fumes and dust do not drift into the rental floor. Specific limits and equipment belong to the designer and the safety agency, so confirm them rather than guessing.

First aid rooms and large glass

A first aid room usually wants its own thermostat, a door that closes and enough ventilation to feel fresh. Cold or overheated patients are common, so a small independent zone is a smart choice. Large glass facing the slopes brings strong sun and cold glass at the same time: a cold radiant draft on shaded mornings, high solar gain on bright afternoons. Perimeter heat at the glass and shading or zoning help. Zone by exposure rather than by room.

Freeze protection when closed, and snowmaking houses

A closed lodge in spring or fall may go weeks with no occupants. A state university extension page notes that insulation alone does not protect pipes through a long cold spell unless some heat or water flow is present, and that a running faucet is a last-resort stopgap. For a lodge that means a plan: drain what can be drained, keep a freeze-protected minimum temperature on and alarmed, check glycol in loops (see glycol freeze protection), and keep heat to coils and entry vestibules. Know where the main shutoff is.

Snowmaking pump and compressor houses are the opposite problem: motors and compressors make heat that has to be exhausted even in winter, with louvers and thermostatic dampers, while piping and instruments inside still need freeze protection when idle. Design each house for its running heat and its idle freeze risk.

Field card: ski area base lodge HVAC

Plan for the peak hour, the empty night and the closed season.

  • Boot room moisture: water per hour = 4.5 x cfm x humidity ratio difference.
  • Latent check: Btu/h = 4,840 x cfm x humidity ratio difference.
  • Our example: 6 lb/h of water and a 0.0055 difference needs about 242 cfm.
  • Door opening frequency drives entrance infiltration; vestibules help.
  • Kitchen exhaust needs makeup air, or the lodge goes negative.
  • Fireplaces: dedicated air path, detectors, annual service.
  • Tune shops: capture fumes and dust at the source.
  • Closed season: keep heat or drain; insulation alone does not stop freezing.
