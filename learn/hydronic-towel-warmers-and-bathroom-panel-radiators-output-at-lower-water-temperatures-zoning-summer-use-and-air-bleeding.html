Hydronic Towel Warmers and Bathroom Panel Radiators: Output at Lower Water Temperatures, Zoning, Summer Use and Air Bleeding

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot-water towel warmer looks like a small, simple radiator, and it is. That is why it is easy to under-size one. The number on the box assumes hot water, and many modern systems never send water that hot. This lesson covers how much output you lose at lower temperatures, how to pipe one, what to do in summer, how to clear air, and the burn cautions that matter in a bathroom.

Output falls when the water is cooler

A panel or towel warmer gives off heat in proportion to how far the average water temperature sits above the room air, and a little more than in proportion, because warmer surfaces also move air faster. Makers publish a rating at one set of conditions, so the first job is to read what those conditions are. Compare the rating to the water your system actually delivers, not to the highest number on the label.

Two common reasons the water is cooler than the rating: a condensing boiler and outdoor reset. A national lab guideline on condensing boilers explains that most condensing happens when the boiler return is 130 F or lower, and its example reset curve delivers about 145 F supply when it is 30 F outside and reaches 180 F at 5 F outside. The same guideline lists a baseboard that puts out 550 Btu/h per foot at 180 F average water and 310 Btu/h per foot at 140 F (its Table 4, at 1 gpm). That is about 56 percent of the output. Other lab data show the same direction: a measured set of baseboard ratings in another Building America report falls from 911 to 546 Btu/h per foot between 180 F and 140 F average water.

Because bathrooms are small, a towel warmer is often the only emitter in the room. When reset is running the supply cooler on a mild day, the bathroom is where people notice first. For the sizing side, see Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature.

Worked example (our own numbers)

All inputs below are our own assumptions for teaching, not data from a product. Always replace them with the maker's published table and a real room heat-loss calculation.

  • Assumed towel warmer rating: 1,200 Btu/h at 180 F average water temperature, in a 70 F room.
  • Assumed exponent: 1.3, a commonly used value for panel-style emitters. The maker's curve beats this number whenever you have it.

Step 1. Temperature difference at rating: 180 minus 70 = 110 F.

Step 2. Temperature difference at 140 F water: 140 minus 70 = 70 F.

Step 3. Ratio: 70 divided by 110 = 0.636. Raise it to the 1.3 power: 0.6361.3 = 0.556.

Step 4. Output at 140 F: 1,200 x 0.556 = about 667 Btu/h. At 120 F the same steps give (50 / 110)1.3 = 0.359, or about 431 Btu/h.

As a cross-check, the lab baseboard figures above give 310 / 550 = 0.56 between 180 F and 140 F, close to our 0.556. That is a baseboard, not a towel warmer, so we use it only to show the size of the drop is realistic.

Small bathroom heat loss (our own assumptions). A 5 ft by 8 ft room, 8 ft ceiling, one exterior wall of 64 sq ft with a 6 sq ft window, attic above, design outdoor 15 F and indoor 70 F, so a 55 F difference.

  • Wall: 58 sq ft x 0.07 x 55 = about 223 Btu/h (assumed U of 0.07)
  • Window: 6 sq ft x 0.35 x 55 = about 116 Btu/h (assumed U of 0.35)
  • Ceiling: 40 sq ft x 0.03 x 55 = 66 Btu/h (assumed U of 0.03)
  • Air leakage: 0.5 air change per hour x 320 cu ft x 0.018 x 55 = about 158 Btu/h (assumed 0.5 air change)
  • Total: about 563 Btu/h

Reading the result. At 180 F the warmer gives 1,200 Btu/h, more than double the load. At 140 F it gives about 667, only about 18 percent above the load. At 120 F it gives about 431, which is short by roughly 130 Btu/h. A bathroom kept at 75 F, a bigger window, or an exhaust fan pulling room air would erase the 140 F margin. The lesson: size the unit for the lowest supply temperature you expect on a design day, not the rating on the box.

Own zone or on a loop

A towel warmer can hang off an existing baseboard or radiator loop, or have its own zone. On a series loop it takes the water that the emitters before it left behind, so a warmer at the end of a series loop gets the coolest water of all (our own reasoning from how water cools as it gives up heat). A separate zone with its own thermostat or a zone valve gets full supply temperature and lets you call for heat in the bathroom without heating the whole loop. Zone-valve and circulator tradeoffs are in Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators. If the supply is too hot for a floor or fixture on the same system, see Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics. If you pipe it in tubing, see PEX Tubing in Hot-Water Heating.

In our experience a towel warmer on its own zone wants a call for heat that can be set separately from the room thermostat, since the room may be at temperature while the towels are still damp. That is a design choice, not a code point.

Summer use

If the boiler is off or the heating zones are locked out in summer, a hydronic warmer is cold. There are two common answers. One is a dedicated circulator and zone call, fed from a heat source that is still running in summer, such as a boiler that also makes domestic hot water. That is our own design idea, so confirm it with the equipment makers before you build it. The other is a warmer with an electric element, or a plug-in or hardwired electric model. We do not give wattage or wiring numbers here; follow the product instructions and the electrical rules where you work. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that electric resistance heat is 100 percent efficient at the point of use but is often more expensive to run than heat from combustion appliances. A small electric element used for an hour or two is a different cost story from heating a whole room.

Bleeding air

Air collects at the high point of a radiator or panel and blocks water from filling it. A lab report on a house with baseboard noted a section that may have been "air bound" because the upper tube saw little flow, a good reminder that cold spots are not always a temperature problem. See Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water Heating System for the full diagnostic list.

  • Use the bleed point at the top of the panel, with a cloth or container ready for water. Follow the unit maker's instructions on whether the circulator should be running.
  • Open it only a little and close it as soon as a steady stream of water appears.
  • Hot water can scald. Do not put your face or hands in the spray, and let the panel cool when you can.
  • Check the system pressure afterward, since bleeding drops it, and follow the boiler maker's instructions for refilling.

Thermostatic radiator valves

A TRV senses room air and throttles the water through that one unit. They help most where the room tends to overheat. Lab research on multifamily buildings is cautious: one report says they can potentially reduce overheating but depend on proper use by occupants, and an expert meeting report says TRVs are limited in effectiveness in some cases and can work when used correctly. A TRV on a towel warmer in a bathroom is common, but a TRV that shuts the warmer off because the room is warm defeats the point of a towel warmer. Choose the sensor setting and location with that in mind.

Scald and surface temperature cautions

A towel warmer is a surface people touch, often with wet hands, and sometimes children. A national health report states that hot water at 140 F can cause a serious burn within about 3 seconds, and that 120 F takes about 10 minutes, with the Consumer Product Safety Commission recommending water heaters be preset to 120 F. A panel running at supply temperature will have a surface temperature close to its water temperature, so reset-lowered supply is safer to touch than a high-temperature loop. Our own advice: where the supply is hot, use a guard, a lower-temperature design, or a mixing valve on that branch, and keep the product maker's clearance rules. The tempering valve on a hot-water heater is a related case; one lab report notes a house keeping its tank at or above 120 F needed a tempering valve to prevent scalding.

When electric fits better

Electric fits when there is no hot-water loop within reach, when the warmer is wanted all year, when the bathroom is a remodel with no easy pipe path, or when the demand is tiny and run-time is short. Hydronic fits when the warmer is a bonus on a system that already delivers hot water cheaply. The U.S. Department of Energy says electric resistance heat is often more expensive to run than combustion heat, which is the reason we would not use electric to heat a whole bathroom if a hot-water supply is nearby.

Field card: Hydronic Towel Warmers and Bathroom Panel Radiators

Size it for the coolest water you will send, not the label.

  • Ratings assume stated water and room temperatures. Read the conditions.
  • Our example (assumptions): 1,200 Btu/h at 180 F becomes about 667 at 140 F and 431 at 120 F with an exponent of 1.3.
  • Our example room loses about 563 Btu/h, so 120 F water falls short.
  • Condensing boilers aim for return water at 130 F or lower, so supply is often cooler than the rating.
  • A separate zone gets full supply temperature; a series loop end gets the coolest.
  • Bleed at the top, open a little, expect hot water, recheck pressure.
  • Water at 140 F can burn in about 3 seconds; 120 F takes about 10 minutes.
  • Electric elements or plug-in units cover summer, within the maker's rules.
