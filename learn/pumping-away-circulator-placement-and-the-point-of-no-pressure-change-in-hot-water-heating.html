Pumping Away: Circulator Placement and the Point of No Pressure Change in Hot-Water Heating

Last updated: 2026-10-28

Where the circulator sits relative to the expansion tank decides whether the pump raises pressure around the loop or lowers it. Put the pump so it pushes away from the tank and the pressure at the high points goes up when it runs. Put it the other way and those same high points can drop below atmospheric pressure and pull air in.

The point of no pressure change

A closed hot-water loop is full of water, and the expansion tank is the one place with a gas cushion. The pipe where the tank joins the system holds a steady pressure whether the circulator runs or not, because the pump cannot add or remove water there. An air management manual from a large pump maker calls that connection the point of no pressure change (PNPC). Everything else in the loop moves up or down from the PNPC pressure when the pump starts, depending on which side of the pump the PNPC sits.

Think of the pump as making a pressure step of its head. The PNPC is pinned. On the other side of the pump from the pin, pressure is the pinned value plus the step; on the pin side it is the pinned value minus the step, and then friction in the pipe bleeds the pressure back toward the pin as the water goes around the loop.

Pumping away versus pumping toward

The same maker manual says the pump should be placed so the PNPC sits on its suction (inlet) side. In that arrangement the pump pushes water away from the tank, and its pressure is added on top of the standing pressure everywhere in the loop. The manual says air stays dissolved until it reaches the air separator. If the pump instead discharges toward the PNPC, it lowers the pressure throughout the system, which the same manual links to flashing, cavitation and air release.

A hydronic-fittings maker's journal on air and dirt gives the same field advice from the symptom side. Its first listed cause of air trouble is an expansion tank improperly located on the discharge side of the circulator. It explains that pressure in some parts of the system may drop below atmospheric while the circulator is on, that air can be sucked in through loose valve packings when the pressure there goes below atmospheric, and that float-type vents can let air in for the same reason. Its cure is to move the tank to the circulator's inlet side.

Worked example (our own example, with assumptions)

This is our own illustration, not a design. Assumptions: the tank connection, circulator and boiler are all at the same height; the cold fill pressure at the tank connection is 12 psi; the circulator is making 10 ft of head; and half of the loop's friction loss has been used by the time water reaches the top radiator. We ignore water temperature and velocity effects.

  1. Convert head to psi. About 2.31 ft of water height equals 1 psi (pump maker manual). 10 ft ÷ 2.31 = 4.3 psi.
  2. Static pressure the top radiator loses to height. 25 ft ÷ 2.31 = 10.8 psi. With the pump off, the top sees 12 − 10.8 = 1.2 psi.
  3. Pumping away (tank at the pump inlet). Pump inlet is at the PNPC: 12.0 psi. Pump outlet: 12.0 + 4.3 = 16.3 psi. At the top radiator, the pump's 4.3 psi step is half used up by friction (4.3 ÷ 2 = 2.2 psi), so 1.2 + 4.3 − 2.2 = 3.4 psi.
  4. Pumping toward the tank (tank at the pump outlet). Pump outlet is now pinned at 12.0 psi. Pump inlet: 12.0 − 4.3 = 7.7 psi. Going downstream from the outlet, friction removes pressure, so at the top radiator we get 1.2 − 2.2 = −1.0 psi gauge, which is below atmospheric.

The lesson in the numbers: 12 psi on the gauge looks fine, but the top of this building has only 1.2 psi when the pump is off. Pumping away rescues it while the pump runs, yet the top still sits under the 4 psi that the pump maker manual says is typically recommended at the top of the system (the journal on air and dirt says at least 5 psi). Pumping toward the tank turns the same fill into a vacuum at the top. To meet 4 psi at the top you would want about 10.8 + 4 = 14.8 psi at the boiler gauge (15.8 psi for 5 psi). Whether that suits the boiler and its relief valve is a question for the boiler manual.

Where the air separator and the fill go

The pump maker manual says to put the air separator where the water is hottest and the pressure lowest, since that is where the least air stays dissolved, and the journal says central separators work best where gas solubility is lowest, near the heat source outlet. It also suggests making the fill connection on the pipe that joins the tank to the system, the PNPC, so pump pressure does not distort the gauge reading there and any air in fresh fill water goes straight to the tank or air elimination hardware. The journal recommends setting the automatic feed valve to keep a minimum static pressure of 5 psi at the top of the system. Our air separator lesson covers the hardware, and our fill valve lesson covers the feed valve.

What to check on an existing system

  • Trace the piping. Find the tank line and the circulator. Is the tank joined on the inlet side of the pump, close to it? Is the pump flange set so it pushes away from that connection?
  • Look for the symptom pattern. Air that returns at upstairs vents, noisy or cold top emitters, and vents or valve packings that spit or weep are the signs the journal ties to a discharge-side tank.
  • Read the gauge cold, then calculate the top. Subtract the height to the highest emitter divided by 2.31 from the gauge reading. If the answer is below the maker's minimum, the fill pressure is too low for the building. See cold radiators and air.
  • Check tank precharge with the tank isolated and the pressure relieved, as described in our expansion tank lesson.
  • Look for more than one connection to the system. Several loops with their own circulators share one pressure reference; primary-secondary piping shows how pumps in a secondary circuit relate to the main loop.
  • Check the pump head. More head means a bigger pressure step, so a high-head pump on the wrong side of the tank does more harm. See circulator head.

Moving a tank or circulator is piping work that must follow the boiler maker's drawing and local rules. Let the system cool and relieve pressure before opening anything; hot water under pressure scalds.

Field card: Pumping Away and the Point of No Pressure Change

Pin the tank at the pump inlet and push away from it.

  • The tank connection is the point of no pressure change; pressure there stays put.
  • Pumping away adds pump pressure everywhere; pumping toward subtracts it.
  • 2.31 ft of water = 1 psi. Head in feet ÷ 2.31 = psi.
  • Our example: 12 psi fill, 10 ft head, top at 25 ft gives 3.4 psi away, -1.0 psi toward.
  • Tank on the discharge side is a first suspect when air keeps returning.
  • Separator where water is hottest; fill at the tank line.
