Upholstery and Foam Shop HVAC: Spray Exhaust and Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An upholstery or foam fabrication shop looks like a quiet craft space, but the air inside carries three things an HVAC tech has to respect: flammable adhesive vapor, loose foam and fabric lint, and a lot of heat from steamers and heat guns. This lesson covers how to keep the air clean, the fire risk low and the shop comfortable.

What is in the air

Foam dust and lint. Cutting foam with saws or hot wires, and trimming fabric, throws fine particles and fibers into the air. They collect on filters, coil faces and the tops of lights and heaters. Our own field observation is that a shop like this loads a filter much faster than an office of the same size, so the filter schedule has to follow the shop, not the calendar.

Spray adhesive vapor. Contact adhesives are often sprayed from cans or guns, and many contain flammable solvents. The label and the safety data sheet from the adhesive maker tell you what is in it and how it must be ventilated. Read both before you plan any airflow. A federal health agency studied a foam cushion plant where workers sprayed adhesive on fabrication tables. It found vapor levels were high at open hoods whose capture velocity was only about 6 to 73 feet per minute, and it recommended roughly 100 to 350 feet per minute. Enclosing each table in a booth cut sprayer exposure by more than 60 percent, and the report also noted that foam stacked in front of a hood blocked airflow and that keeping outside doors closed reduced vapor drifting into other rooms (source 3).

Flammability and the fire picture

OSHA's spray finishing rule treats flammable spray areas as a place where ignition sources must be controlled. It bars open flames and spark-producing equipment from the spraying area and from within 20 feet unless a partition separates them, and it says space heaters, steam pipes and hot surfaces may not sit where combustible residue can build up (source 1). For the HVAC tech this means: no unit heater, gas-fired makeup air burner or radiant tube pointed at, or breathing air from, a spray station unless the designer and the local fire official have approved it. Our own advice is to ask who owns that decision before you wire or light anything.

Foam adds to the problem. An OSHA hazard bulletin says rigid polyurethane foams ignite easily and burn quickly, that foams with fire retardants should still be treated as combustible, and that burning foam makes intense heat and dense smoke with carbon monoxide the most significant gas. It says to keep open flames, torches and other high-heat sources away from the foam (source 2). A federal laboratory abstract adds that flexible foam can collapse into a burning liquid and make a pool fire (source 6). Lint is our own added concern: it is fuel, so keep it off heater cabinets, motors and fan housings.

Storage of foam and adhesive cans

Keep foam rolls, scrap bags and adhesive cans away from unit heaters, flues, steam pipes, hot lights and the heat guns' own work area. OSHA's spray finishing rule says to keep only what is needed near spraying, generally no more than one day's or one shift's supply, with bulk storage cut off in a separate place (source 1). It also says freshly sprayed pieces must dry where ventilation stops vapors from building up, and that rags soaked in finish go in approved metal waste cans emptied at least daily (source 1). Check your fire code and the maker's label for the exact limits that apply to your shop.

Local exhaust at the spray station

Capture the vapor at the source. A hood or booth at the spray table pulls the vapor away from the worker's face and out of the building before it mixes with the shop air. The same rule says each spray booth needs its own exhaust duct that discharges outdoors, that exhaust must not feed makeup air or other intakes, and that spray exhaust may not be recirculated (source 1). That also means return air from the shop must never carry spray vapor back through the furnace or rooftop unit. The rule also calls for the fan to run during spraying and long enough afterward to clear the vapor, so many designers tie the spray equipment or the heater to the fan with an interlock (source 1).

Makeup air

Every cubic foot of air the hood exhausts has to come back in from somewhere. If it does not, the shop goes negative, doors are hard to open, vapor leaks in from storage or other rooms, and combustion appliances can backdraft. Supply makeup air near the station, gently, so it does not disturb the hood's capture. In winter that air must be tempered, which is the biggest energy cost in the building. See the spray booth lesson linked below for interlocks and heaters, and the woodshop lesson for dust collector return air.

Steamers, heat guns and shop comfort

Fabric steamers and heat guns are electric resistance heaters, so every watt they use becomes heat in the room. One kilowatt-hour equals 3,412 Btu (source 4), so 1 kW is 3,412 Btu/h. They add to the cooling load in summer and offset a little heating in winter, but only while they run, so never size the heating plant around them. Also keep heat guns away from foam and lint, and put them in a cradle when you set them down.

For adhesive work, steady temperatures matter. Adhesive makers publish a working temperature range for the product and the surfaces. Use the range printed in the maker's written instructions as the shop's target, and keep spray stations out of drafts from heating and cooling supply grilles. We do not state a number here because it depends on the product.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design for any real shop. Assume sea level, standard air, and a sensible heating relation for outside air, Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference (source 5).

  • Assumption 1: the spray station hood exhausts 1,500 cfm, and all of it is replaced with outdoor makeup air.
  • Assumption 2: the shop is held at 68 F and the winter design outdoor temperature is 20 F, so the temperature difference is 68 - 20 = 48 F.
  • Assumption 3: two steamers at 1.5 kW each and two heat guns at 1.5 kW each, all running at once (a worst case). That is 4 x 1.5 = 6 kW.

Makeup air heating load: Q = 1.08 x 1,500 x 48 = 1,620 x 48 = 77,760 Btu/h.

Tool heat: 6 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 20,472 Btu/h.

What it means: the tools are about a quarter of the makeup air load (20,472 divided by 77,760 is about 0.26). Because they are not on all day, the heater still has to be sized for the full 77,760 Btu/h of tempered air, and a design must also cover the building's own heat loss, which we did not calculate here.

Field card: Upholstery and Foam Shop HVAC

Capture the vapor, replace the air, keep heat away from foam.

  • Read the adhesive label and safety data sheet before planning airflow.
  • Spray exhaust gets its own duct to outdoors and is never recirculated.
  • Keep flames, heaters and hot surfaces away from spray residue, foam and lint.
  • Keep only a shift's supply of adhesive near the spray station.
  • Makeup air: Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference (our example: 77,760 Btu/h).
  • Electric tools: 3,412 Btu/h per kW.
  • Check filters and coils often because lint loads them fast.
  • Use the adhesive maker's written temperature range.
