ERV vs. HRV: What Each Recovers and How to Maintain Them

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Both devices swap air between inside and outside while borrowing the temperature of the outgoing stream. The difference is whether moisture comes along. That one difference shapes the core, the frost risk and the maintenance.

What each one recovers

The Department of Energy describes both as ventilation devices with a heat exchanger, fans and controls. A heat recovery ventilator exchanges heat only. An energy recovery ventilator also transfers some water vapor, which helps stabilize indoor humidity. In winter, heat from outgoing air warms incoming air. In summer, cooler indoor air pre-cools incoming warm air. Neither device conditions the house or dehumidifies by itself, as one trade article puts it, so you still need cooling or a dehumidifier for moisture the house produces. Manufacturer marketing often claims big advantages for one type, so read comparisons with that in mind.

Cores in plain terms

A manufacturer comparison describes an HRV core as a plate-style exchanger and an ERV core as one with a membrane that lets moisture pass through. A design article adds that the core moves heat between two airstreams, and that moisture transfer in an ERV moves from the wetter stream toward the drier one. That is why an ERV can hold humidity in a dry winter and push it out in a humid summer. The same article says HRVs and ERVs may perform about equally well in many climates, so the choice depends on humidity goals and local conditions.

Frost and defrost

Frost is the cold-weather problem. A manufacturer white paper explains that when outgoing indoor air is humid and incoming outdoor air is cold, moisture can freeze in the core. It says HRV cores may ice up in the low 20s F while the membrane in an ERV can tolerate colder weather, and that any HRV drain pan or condensate line can ice too. That white paper is from an ERV maker, so treat the comparison as a viewpoint. It also lists general methods, which it attributes to ASHRAE:

  • Preheat coil. Warms incoming air so the core stays above its frost threshold and keeps ventilating, at the cost of some recovery.
  • Face-and-bypass damper. Reduces air through the core as it gets colder.
  • Exhaust-only defrost. Supply fan stops while the exhaust fan warms the core. It interrupts ventilation and can pull the house slightly negative.
  • Recirculation defrost. Indoor air is recirculated through the core to thaw it, also interrupting ventilation.

The same paper notes that lowering indoor humidity in very cold weather makes freezing less likely. A tech should know which scheme a given unit uses before diagnosing a short cycle or a reduced airflow.

Maintenance habits

  • Filters. A maintenance guide suggests checking every one to three months and cleaning or replacing at least every three to six months, sooner in dusty places. Washable filters must dry fully before going back in.
  • Core. Clean one to two times a year if the manufacturer allows it. HRV cores are often removable and washable, while many ERV cores allow only gentle vacuuming or rinsing. Reinstall in the same orientation.
  • Condensate. If the unit has a drain pan or trap, check it each season for standing water or mineral buildup and confirm the line slopes the right way.
  • Hoods and grilles. Look at outdoor hoods monthly and after storms for leaves, snow and nests.
  • Balancing. Have flows checked at install, after renovation and periodically. Do not move balancing dampers without a measurement tool.

Turn off power before opening a unit, and avoid bleach or harsh solvents unless the manual says otherwise.

When airflow is wrong

A design article on heat recovery ventilation stresses that every unit should be commissioned by measuring airflow against the design target. Low measured flow suggests high duct static pressure or a starved fan. Imbalanced flows can pressurize or depressurize a home. Listen for noise, look for window condensation and odors, and compare supply and exhaust before blaming the core.

Field card: ERV vs. HRV: What Each Recovers and How to Maintain Them

Same idea, different cores: heat only or heat plus moisture.

  • HRV moves heat only. ERV moves heat and some moisture.
  • Neither one dehumidifies or cools the house by itself.
  • Frost control uses preheat, bypass or defrost modes; each has a ventilation or energy cost.
  • Clean filters every few months and cores once or twice a year per the manual.
  • Balance and measure airflow at commissioning, and keep hoods clear.
