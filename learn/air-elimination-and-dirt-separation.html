Air Elimination and Dirt Separation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Air makes noise and blocks heat, and dirt wears out pumps. Separators are cheap protection, and where they go matters.

Where air comes from

An article on air removal lists these sources: dissolved oxygen and nitrogen in the cold fill water that come out of solution as it heats; top-up water; leaking valve stems, gaskets and seals; and pressure problems, such as an expansion tank on the discharge side of the pump so that the suction side sees lower pressure and can draw air in. Gas stays dissolved longer at higher pressure and cooler temperature and comes out when it is hot and pressure is low. Caleffi's example: at 30 psi absolute (about 15 psi on a gauge), heating water from 65 F to 170 F releases about 1.8 gallons of air per 100 gallons of water.

What air does

Reported effects include gurgling noise, blocked flow and poor heat transfer where air collects, damaged pump parts and oxygen-driven corrosion that makes iron oxides. A radiant loop with air in it can leave a whole loop cold. See Radiant Floor Heating Basics.

Types of separators

  • Air purger: uses buoyancy to send gassy water into a bulge at the top where a vent releases the air.
  • Vortex type: uses spinning flow so light gas collects in the center and rises.
  • Coalescing type: slows the water and uses a fine mesh so microbubbles join into larger bubbles that rise. Caleffi's description of its combined unit says the water swirls in an internal element, a float-type automatic vent at the top releases air, and dirt settles in a low-velocity zone into a chamber, which is settling rather than filtering so it does not clog like a mesh strainer.

Separators cannot pull out gas that is still dissolved. Over time the system keeps sending gas through the separator as it comes out of solution.

Placement

Heating systems: put the separator where the water is hottest and pressure is lowest, which is usually just after the boiler, and connect the expansion tank to the pump inlet side. Cooling systems: at the top of the system, or the chiller inlet. Caleffi's instructions for one product say install it vertically, after the boiler on the pump suction side. For the tank side of this, see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.

Dirt and magnetite

Ferrous debris from corroding iron and steel forms magnetite, nicknamed boiler ink, which is abrasive and very fine and can damage circulator impellers and bushings. Non-ferrous debris includes pipe tape, solder residue, copper shavings and scale. A magnet captures only iron-based particles, so a magnetic filter by itself misses the rest. Dirt separators are commonly put at the boiler or heat exchanger inlet, especially with compact heat exchangers or old cast-iron radiators.

Maintenance habits

  • Purge a separator's collected dirt through its drain, following the maker's steps. A magnetic one is typically opened by removing the magnet first before opening the drain.
  • Clean any startup strainer after the first day of operation if the product has one.
  • Purge loops one at a time after service, and fix leaks that let new water in. See Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics.

Always release pressure and cool the system before opening any vent or drain, and watch for hot water. Never open a boiler component that you have not isolated per the maker's instructions.

Field card: Air Elimination and Dirt Separation

Put the separator at the hot, low-pressure spot. Magnets miss most dirt.

  • Cold fill water releases gas as it heats, and leaks or low suction pressure add more.
  • Separators work best just after the boiler, where gas is least soluble.
  • Tank connects to the pump inlet side per the common guidance.
  • Separators remove free air and microbubbles, not dissolved gas.

More detail from the Caleffi guide

  • Automatic air vents work best when the top of the system holds a modest positive pressure. The Caleffi guide gives at least 5 psi at the top as its example, set with the fill valve.
  • When purging, keep going until the discharge has no visible bubbles for at least 30 seconds. Dissolved air can take much longer to come out when the separation hardware is poor.
  • Down-flowing pipe needs enough velocity (the guide gives at least 2 feet per second) to carry bubbles along, or gurgling can persist.
  • If water drains from upper piping every time the circulator stops, suspect air entering an open system.
  • Magnetite is the dark gray sludge of an oxygen-starved system. If oxygen keeps entering, it can turn into hematite, and pitting corrosion can follow.
  • Vertical upward-flow circulators with a spring check near the discharge are prone to large air pockets, which can leave the circulator running without lubrication.
  • The same guide suggests about 12 pipe diameters of straight pipe between a separator outlet and the circulator inlet.

Check the separator maker's manual for straight-pipe and placement rules.
