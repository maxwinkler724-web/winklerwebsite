Refrigerant Distributors and Evaporator Coil Circuits: Uneven Feed and Circuit Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-25

A larger evaporator coil is built from several parallel tubing paths, called circuits. Each circuit needs about the same share of refrigerant. The distributor makes that happen. This lesson covers how it works, what uneven feed looks like, and how to measure circuit outlets.

Why a coil needs a distributor

A thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) drops the pressure of the liquid and passes a cold mixture of liquid and vapor to the coil. A trade article puts that mixture at roughly 75 percent liquid and 25 percent vapor by weight, and the vapor takes up far more space. Without a distributor, some circuits get mostly liquid and others mostly vapor, with the lower circuits favored. The result is starved circuits, flooded circuits, valve hunting and possible floodback. A maker bulletin on distributors says the device exists to split the valve outflow evenly among the circuits.

See Metering Devices: TXV and Piston for the valve itself.

What is inside the distributor

  • Nozzle or orifice. The refrigerant passes through a nozzle that speeds it up. The speed mixes the liquid and vapor into a more uniform spray, which then hits a cone that divides it into equal passages. The maker bulletin says high velocity is the key to the distributor's success, and that nozzle sizing is generally more critical than tube sizing.
  • Equal-length tubes. The small tubes between the distributor body and the coil circuits should all be the same length and free of sharp bends, so each circuit sees the same resistance. Kinks reduce the tube area and add resistance to that one circuit.
  • A deliberate pressure drop. The nozzle and tubes are meant to drop pressure. The bulletin gives design totals of about 25 psi for R-134a, 35 psi for R-22 and R-404A type refrigerants, and 45 psi for R-410A. The drop comes out of what the valve has available, so the valve must be externally equalized.
  • Side ports. Some distributors have one or two side connections for hot gas bypass, defrost or reverse-cycle service. The bulletin notes that hot gas entering the side can deflect the liquid stream, so those designs use a different nozzle.

Installation details that affect feed

The bulletin says any position works, but vertical feed usually performs best where conditions vary. Mount it directly on the valve outlet, or use straight tubing under about two feet with no elbows. Each distributor needs its own TXV.

A research paper that tested a four-circuit distributor in horizontal, vertical-up and vertical-down positions found similar performance in all three at normal flow. Gravity only mattered at very low flow. The same paper found that swapping only the distributor changed which circuits got more flow, and its authors blamed manufacturing differences such as burrs on inner surfaces.

What uneven feed looks like

  • Frost or sweat pattern. A trade-press restriction article describes a freeze test with the blower disconnected: on a healthy coil, frost starts at the valve outlet, runs through the feeder tubes and spreads fairly evenly across the coil. Irregular patterns point to a restriction. That author warns it takes a trained eye, and that some extra frost after the distributor is normal. Frost starting ahead of the valve points to the inlet screen.
  • Cold and warm return bends. A starved circuit, one fed too little, leaves the coil warm because it picks up a lot of superheat. An overfed circuit leaves cold, near the saturation temperature, and may carry liquid.
  • Valve hunting. A trade article lists blocked, unequal or kinked feeder tubes among the causes of uneven circuit loading.
  • Weak capacity. In a NIST study (NISTIR 6946) with overall exit superheat held at 10 F, the coils lost a large share of their capacity when circuit superheat was uneven. An ORNL literature review names airflow maldistribution as the largest cause of capacity loss, so a dirty coil face can unbalance circuits even when the distributor is fine.

Checking each circuit

  1. Run steady, read suction pressure, convert to saturation temperature.
  2. Measure the temperature of each circuit's outlet tube, upstream of the common suction header. Use an insulated contact thermometer or a thermal imager. Bare shiny copper reads poorly with infrared, so tape a small spot first (our own tip). See Infrared Thermography for HVAC and Electrical Gear.
  3. Subtract saturation temperature from each reading to get circuit superheat, then compare circuits and the header near the bulb.

The hunting article suggests a quick screen: average the circuit outlet temperatures, and if the average runs more than 2 F above the header temperature near the bulb, a circuit imbalance is likely. Individual readings then show which circuit. Thermal imaging can pinpoint where the temperature change begins along a blocked tube.

Sum of outlet temperatures: 48 + 49 + 47 + 50 + 41 + 41 + 48 + 49 = 373 F. Average = 373 / 8 = 46.6 F.

Sum of superheats: 8 + 9 + 7 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 8 + 9 = 53 F. Average = 53 / 8 = 6.6 F. Check: 46.6 minus 40 equals 6.6 F.

Flooded circuits: circuits 5 and 6, at 1 F of superheat, are almost at saturation and likely carrying liquid. The other six run 7 to 10 F, so they are starved by comparison. The average alone hides this, so the 2 F screen would not flag it. Only the individual readings show it.

Mixed suction superheat if flows were equal: about 6.6 F, the straight average. Treat this as an estimate, since real flows are not equal.

Why the bulb is fooled: the TXV bulb sits on the common suction line after the circuits join. It sees the blended temperature, so it reports about 6.6 F of superheat. That looks healthy, so the valve holds its position. The hunting article describes the swing: a lightly loaded circuit sends cold refrigerant to the manifold, the bulb reads low, the valve closes, the warmer circuits heat the bulb, and the valve opens again. Kinks, debris and what to do about them

  • A kinked tube adds resistance to one circuit, so it tends to run starved with a warm outlet (our own reasoning from the maker and trade guidance).
  • Debris or solder in a feeder tube or nozzle does the same. One article cites a feeder tube filled with solder during a leak repair, so a recent repair is a clue.
  • A leak check will not find a plugged circuit. The maker bulletin says to test each circuit with a probe wire, an air jet or a flow meter, and that on removable-nozzle models you can pull the nozzle to look for solder.
  • Replacement. Our own judgment: a feeder tube or distributor can be replaced with a matched part, but a blockage inside the coil core you cannot reach, or tubing that keeps leaking, usually means a new coil. Recover refrigerant per EPA rules before opening the circuit.

Field card: Refrigerant Distributors and Coil Circuits

The bulb reads the blend, not the worst circuit.

  • Tubes: same length, no kinks. Nozzle: sets velocity and mixing.
  • Measure each circuit outlet, subtract saturation, compare.
  • Example: circuits 5 and 6 at 1 F superheat, average 6.6 F.
  • Leak checks will not find a plugged circuit. Use a probe wire, air jet or flow check.
