Hot-Water Flow Rate Math

Three numbers describe how much heat a water loop moves: gallons per minute, degrees of temperature drop, and Btu per hour. If you know two, you can find the third.

The idea in one sentence

Water carries heat. How much heat a loop moves depends on how much water flows and how many degrees the water cools on the way. Put those together and you can find any one of the three if you know the other two.

The formula

Caleffi's balancing issue writes the full formula with water's density and specific heat, then notes that for water at an average of about 140 °F the fluid part works out to 490. So:

flow (gpm) = heat (Btu/hr) ÷ (490 × temperature drop in °F)

Turned around: heat = 490 × gpm × drop, and drop = heat ÷ (490 × gpm).

Where 500 comes from

You will also hear the shop shortcut Btu/hr = 500 × gpm × drop. Caleffi and a plumbing magazine column explain that 500 is a rounded 8.33 pounds per gallon times 60 minutes, which fits cooler water. The magazine's author calls it a good ballpark number but not a precise one, and says exact work should use the fluid's properties at its average temperature. The two constants differ by only a couple of percent, so either is fine for a quick estimate. Put simply, the constant is about 500, and 490 for hot water. This lesson uses 490.

Worked examples

  • Find flow. A circuit carries 98,000 Btu/hr with a 20 °F drop. 98,000 ÷ (490 × 20) = 10 gpm.
  • Same system, other branches. At the same 20 °F drop, 49,000 Btu/hr needs 5 gpm and 29,400 Btu/hr needs 3 gpm. That adds up to the 18 gpm total the Caleffi example shows.
  • Find the drop. A primary loop flowing 10 gpm and handing 40,000 Btu/hr to a secondary circuit cools by 40,000 ÷ (490 × 10) = about 8.2 °F. This is the example from a Siegenthaler column on primary loops.
  • Rule of thumb. With the 500 shortcut and a 20 °F drop, each gpm carries about 10,000 Btu/hr. The column warns that this only holds at a 20 °F drop.

Antifreeze changes the constant

Siegenthaler gives 490 for water, 479 for 30 percent glycol and 450 for 50 percent glycol. A lower constant means the fluid carries less heat per gallon, so the same job needs more flow. Example: 60,000 Btu/hr at a 20 °F drop needs about 6.1 gpm of water (60,000 ÷ 9,800) but about 6.3 gpm of 30 percent glycol (60,000 ÷ 9,580).

Why more flow often does not fix a cold room

Caleffi works a radiant circuit supplied at a constant 105 °F. At 2.0 gpm it gives off 6,860 Btu/hr. Cut the flow in half to 1.0 gpm and output drops to 6,180, only about 10 percent less. Only when flow falls very low (0.2 gpm in their example, about 43 percent of maximum) does output fall hard. The curve is flat at the top, so doubling pump flow in a cold room may do little. Look for air, a closed valve, a dirty strainer, too-low supply temperature or an undersized emitter first. Caleffi adds that this flat curve is why balancing gets harder at low flow.

Using the numbers safely

These are estimating tools. Real design also needs pipe size, pump head, emitter ratings and the manufacturer's required flow range for the boiler. Check the manufacturer's manual for the minimum and maximum flow and temperature rise on the boiler in front of you, and follow it over any rule of thumb.

Check yourself

Four short questions in our own words. Think first, then open the answer.

A circuit needs to deliver 30,000 Btu/hr with a 20 °F drop in plain water. About how many gpm?

30,000 ÷ (490 × 20) = about 3.1 gpm.

Water flows at 4 gpm and cools 25 °F. How much heat does it carry?

490 × 4 × 25 = 49,000 Btu/hr.

The same heat load now uses 50 percent antifreeze. Does the loop need more flow, less, or the same?

More. The constant falls from 490 to 450, so each gallon carries less heat, and the flow must rise to move the same Btu/hr.

A room is cold and someone doubles the pump flow. Why might the room barely warm up?

Emitter output rises very little at higher flow. In Caleffi's radiant example, halving flow only cut output about 10 percent, so adding flow gives a small gain. Check air, valves, supply temperature and emitter size.

Field card: Hot-Water Flow Rate Math

Flow, drop and heat: know two, find the third.

  • Flow (gpm) = Btu/hr ÷ (490 × temperature drop °F), for water near 140 °F.
  • Shop shortcut: Btu/hr = 500 × gpm × drop. It is rounded, so use it for estimates.
  • Worked example: 98,000 Btu/hr at a 20 °F drop = 10 gpm.
  • Glycol lowers the constant (479 at 30 percent, 450 at 50 percent), so more flow is needed.
  • Output flattens at high flow. More gpm is rarely the cure for a cold room.
  • The boiler manual sets the allowed flow and temperature rise.
