Transporting Refrigerant Cylinders: Securing, Valves, Labels, the 80 Percent Fill Limit and Shipping to a Reclaimer

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The core refrigerant-handling exam asks about cylinders because a cylinder is where most of the real danger sits: a closed steel vessel full of liquid under pressure, riding in the back of a van. This lesson covers the habits and the federal ideas behind them, with a worked example of how much a recovery cylinder may safely hold.

Why the exam asks about transport

Recovery work ends with refrigerant in a cylinder, and that cylinder has to go somewhere: back to the shop, to a reuse point, or to a reclaimer. Exam writers like this topic because the right answers are short and checkable: secure the cylinder, keep the valve closed and capped, never overfill, never refill a disposable. A wrong habit here can burst a vessel or roll a heavy cylinder into a driver. We cover the rules in general terms only. This is education, not legal advice, and the current text of the rules and your employer's policy decide what applies to you.

Two kinds of cylinders in the van

A refillable cylinder, such as a recovery cylinder, has stamped markings, a test date and valves built for repeated use. A disposable cylinder is a one-trip container with a single valve, sold full of new refrigerant. A government analysis of cylinder use says the disposable type cannot be refilled, because its one-way valve leads the federal transportation safety rules to prohibit refilling it for safety reasons. Our reading of that for the exam is simple: a disposable cylinder is never a recovery cylinder, and you never carry refrigerant in one with the goal of refilling it. When the refrigerant is gone, the empty goes to recycling, not back into service. Our lesson on disposable cylinders and the no-refill rule covers what is left in the empty. To read the stamps on a refillable cylinder, see our lesson on refillable cylinder markings.

Secure them so nothing can roll, fall or leak

The federal small-load rule for tradespeople, called materials of trade, says packages must be securely closed, secured against shifting and protected from damage. Cylinders of nonflammable or flammable gas need no outer box under that rule. If cylinders are manifolded together, the rule allows it only when all the valves are tightly closed. Turn that into van habits:

  • Upright and restrained. Stand cylinders upright in a rack, strap or bin so they cannot roll, slide or fall during a hard stop. Upright is common practice and keeps the relief valve in the vapor space. Our own rule of thumb: if you can push it over by hand, it is not secured.
  • Valve closed, cap on. The federal cylinder rules treat the valve protection cap as part of the package and describe tests in which a cylinder with its valve installed is dropped from a height without leaking. A cap is the thing that makes that possible. Close the valve fully, then cap it. Do not carry a cylinder with a hose still attached.
  • Never in a sealed passenger space. Keep cylinders in the cargo area, away from the cab where possible, and keep the cargo area ventilated. This is our practical advice rather than a quoted rule.
  • Keep them away from heat. A closed van in the sun gets hot. See the next section.

Labels and hazard markings in general terms

The small-load rule requires cylinders made to a federal specification to be marked and labeled as the main hazardous-materials rules prescribe. A government brochure on the rule says such a cylinder carries the proper shipping name, an identification number and a hazard-class label. In practice that means the markings and diamond that came on the cylinder must stay readable. Do not paint over them or peel them off.

A recovery cylinder needs one more thing that is our practice, not a quoted rule: a clear tag or marker note of what refrigerant is inside and, if known, whether it is a single type or a mix. A cylinder of unknown or mixed refrigerant is treated differently later (see mixed and contaminated refrigerant), and the label is how the next person finds out. Our lesson on cylinder labels goes into color and label basics.

The fill limit and heat in a closed van

Liquid refrigerant expands as it warms. A cylinder filled with liquid to the top has nowhere for that expansion to go, and pressure can rise very fast. The federal rule for liquefied gas cylinders says the liquid may not completely fill the container at any temperature up to and including 131 degrees Fahrenheit, with a few listed exceptions that do not include ordinary refrigerants. The exam and technician training manuals express the safe-filling habit as a simple guideline: do not fill a cylinder to more than 80 percent of its capacity. A government analysis of cylinders cites a technician training manual for that guideline. We use the 80 percent figure because it leaves a vapor space as a cushion. For the burst physics and safe ways to warm a cylinder, see our lesson on overfilled cylinders.

In a closed van the cargo area can run much hotter than the outside air. A cylinder that was safe to fill on a cool morning can be near its limit by afternoon. Weigh the cylinder as you fill it, stop at the limit, and do not leave a recovery cylinder cooking in a parked van.

Worked example (our own): how much may this cylinder hold?

These are our own assumptions, chosen to make the arithmetic easy, not figures from any real cylinder. Always use the stamped water capacity and the refrigerant's real liquid density.

  • Assumed recovery cylinder water capacity: 58.0 lb of water (a stamped number on a real cylinder).
  • Assumed liquid refrigerant density: 1.20 times the density of water.
  • Fill limit used: 80 percent of the capacity by volume.

Step 1, mass at a completely full cylinder. Water capacity 58.0 lb of water means the same volume holds 58.0 × 1.20 = 69.6 lb of this liquid.

Step 2, apply the limit. 69.6 × 0.80 = 55.68 lb. That is the maximum net refrigerant mass for this assumed cylinder.

Step 3, check an assumed overfill. Suppose a tech lets 62.0 lb into the cylinder. The excess is 62.0 − 55.68 = 6.32 lb over the limit, which is 6.32 ÷ 55.68 = 11.4 percent over. Seen as volume, 62.0 ÷ 69.6 = 89.1 percent full instead of 80 percent, so the vapor space has shrunk from 20 percent to about 11 percent.

Step 4, transport weight. Assume an empty tare of 30.0 lb. At the limit the gross weight is 30.0 + 55.68 = 85.68 lb, under the 220 lb single-cylinder limit in the small-load rule. Four such cylinders weigh 4 × 85.68 = 342.72 lb, under the 440 lb total allowed for all such materials on one vehicle. Remember that figure counts everything covered by the rule, not only refrigerant. Our lesson on carrying cylinders in a service van covers those totals in more detail.

Shipping recovered refrigerant to a reclaimer

Under the federal refrigerant rules, a technician may sell used refrigerant to a reclaimer, and a reclaimer is a company approved to reprocess it to the purity specification for new product. Reclaimers must record the name and address of each sender and the quantity sent, by refrigerant type, and keep those records for three years. So when you send a cylinder, expect to be asked who you are, what refrigerant it is and how much. Mixed or unknown refrigerant is a conversation to have with the reclaimer before you ship, not after. For same-owner reuse versus reclamation, see our lesson on reusing recovered refrigerant.

Paperwork in general terms

A government brochure says that a load carried under the small-load rule does not need shipping papers, placards or formal hazardous-materials training records. It still requires the driver to be told hazardous material is aboard, plus the packaging, marking and labeling rules. Larger or non-qualifying shipments are a different situation with more paperwork, and a carrier may have its own rules. Keep your own record of what you recovered and where it went. We cannot tell you what your state or employer requires, so ask.

Exam wording to watch for

  • Cylinders should be secured upright, valves closed, caps on.
  • Never fill past the safe limit; leave vapor space for expansion.
  • Disposable cylinders are never refilled.
  • A recovery cylinder is refillable; a disposable cylinder is not.
  • Heat raises pressure; a hot closed van is a hazard.

Field card: Transporting Refrigerant Cylinders

Upright, closed, capped, under the limit.

  • Secure every cylinder so it cannot roll, slide or fall.
  • Valve fully closed and cap on; no hoses attached.
  • Keep markings and hazard labels readable; note the refrigerant inside.
  • Safe-fill guideline: no more than 80 percent of capacity. Weigh as you fill.
  • Our example: 58.0 lb water capacity x 1.20 x 0.80 = 55.68 lb.
  • Never refill a disposable cylinder, and never carry refrigerant in one to refill it.
  • Do not leave a cylinder in a hot, closed van.
  • Recovered refrigerant to a reclaimer: expect sender and quantity records.
