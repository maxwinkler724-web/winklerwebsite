HVAC for Dental Laboratories: Oven Heat, Dust, Acrylic Vapor and Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A dental laboratory makes crowns, bridges and dentures at benches, with small hot ovens, grinding motors, plaster and acrylic. The room is small, but it has heat, dust and vapor all at once. This lesson shows how to think about each one, and why a bench extractor changes the heating and makeup air numbers more than most people expect.

Three problems in one room

Treat the lab as three separate loads. First, heat from burnout ovens and porcelain furnaces. Second, dust from grinding, polishing and handling stone, plaster and investment powders. Third, vapor from denture acrylic. Each load has a different fix, and the fixes interact through one number: how much air leaves the room.

Heat from ovens and furnaces

A burnout oven drives wax or resin out of a mold. A porcelain furnace fires ceramic onto a metal or ceramic frame. Both are electric, both run hot, and both cycle on and off. Their nameplate watts are a ceiling, not an average. What matters for room heat is the average watts, which is nameplate watts times the fraction of time the element is on. The conversion is 1 kilowatt-hour = 3,412 Btu, so 1 kW of average input is 3,412 Btu/h of heat if all of it ends up in the room (federal energy statistics units page). Some of it leaves through the furnace vent, so the all-in-the-room assumption is on the high side for cooling and fine for a first estimate.

Fumes matter too. One occupational review lists gases and vapors from casting and porcelain baking among the hazards of the trade (national library archive review, in Sources). Follow the oven maker's instructions for venting. Do not assume the room exhaust covers a burnout oven.

Dust: silica, metals, plaster and stone

A federal health report on silicosis in dental laboratory technicians says mold and porcelain materials can be up to 70% crystalline silica by weight, and names the dusty steps: mixing powders, removing castings from molds, grinding and polishing castings and porcelain, and abrasive blasting with silica sand. OSHA's general industry silica standard, 29 CFR 1910.1053, sets an 8-hour permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic meter of respirable crystalline silica and an action level of 25. It requires engineering and work practice controls first, with respirators only for what controls cannot reach.

The review of dental technician hazards adds that alloy grinding dust can contain cobalt, chromium and nickel, and that plaster, ceramic and acrylic dusts are also present. It says overexposure is most likely where engineering controls are missing, and that water on the grinding surface almost completely trapped plaster dust in one demonstration. So wet methods and enclosed benches come before any HVAC fix.

Acrylic monomer vapor

Denture acrylic is mixed from a powder and a liquid monomer, methyl methacrylate. OSHA's chemical page lists an 8-hour limit of 100 ppm (410 mg/m3), a flash point of 50 °F (open cup) and a vapor density of 3.45, which means the vapor is heavier than air. The review says monomer vapor can irritate skin and eyes and cause allergic dermatitis and asthma, and that ordinary gloves, masks and glasses give poor protection against it. Because it is flammable, the mixing station needs capture at the source, a closed container habit and no ignition sources nearby. Heavier-than-air vapor does not mean you should exhaust only at the floor. Put the capture opening where the vapor is released, at the work.

Bench extractors versus room exhaust

A bench extractor captures dust or vapor within inches of where it is made, so it needs far less air than diluting the whole room. Room exhaust moves air through the space but does not stop a technician from breathing a plume at arm's length. Use local capture for grinding, polishing, mixing and pouring, and use a modest general exhaust or the building's normal ventilation for what is left. A collector that discharges back into the room only works if the maker's instructions allow that for the dust involved; for silica and metal dust, ask the collector maker and a safety professional before choosing it.

Makeup air and quiet comfort

Every cfm exhausted must be replaced. If the room has no planned makeup air, the lab goes negative, doors whistle, furnace vents can draft backward and the extractor loses airflow. Bring makeup air in at low speed, away from the bench hoods, so a draft does not blow dust past the capture opening. Tempering matters in winter; a cold stream across a bench is uncomfortable. For quiet comfort, use large, slow ducts and a collector placed outside the work room or in a sound-treated closet, since a bench worker beside a loud motor will turn it off.

Filter loading

A dust collector or filter gets heavier with every shift. As it loads, airflow at the bench falls even though the motor sounds the same. Use the collector maker's instructions for a pressure gauge or flow check, schedule changes by readings rather than by guess, and handle used filters as dust-bearing waste. Do not blow out or shake a loaded filter in the room. Because silica and metal dust make filter changes an exposure event, the person changing filters needs the same controls as the person grinding.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption, not a measurement.

  • Burnout oven: 3,000 W nameplate, on 40% of the time.
  • Porcelain furnace: 1,500 W, on 60% of the time.
  • Small second oven: 1,800 W, on 30% of the time.
  • Two benches, each with an extractor drawing 350 cfm, so 700 cfm total, all exhausted outside.
  • Indoor 70 °F, outdoor winter design 20 °F, so a temperature difference of 50 °F. All heat from the equipment is assumed to stay in the room.

Step 1, average watts. 3,000 x 0.40 = 1,200 W. 1,500 x 0.60 = 900 W. 1,800 x 0.30 = 540 W. Sum = 2,640 W = 2.64 kW.

Step 2, room heat. 2.64 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 9,008 Btu/h. In tons, 9,008 / 12,000 = 0.75 ton. That is the cooling load from the equipment alone, before people, lights and the building.

Step 3, makeup air heating load. Using the sensible heat relation Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference (a trade-math reference page lists it as 1.08 x CFM x delta T): Q = 1.08 x 700 x 50 = 37,800 Btu/h. That is 37,800 / 3,412 = 11.1 kW of heat, if the 700 cfm is heated all the way to room temperature.

What it means. The extractors, not the ovens, dominate the winter load: 37,800 Btu/h to temper the makeup air versus 9,008 Btu/h from the equipment. In winter the oven heat offsets about a quarter of the tempering load (9,008 / 37,800 = 0.24), and in summer it is a cooling load the system must also carry. The factor 1.08 assumes standard air, so it is a good approximation only at ordinary room conditions and near sea level. Sizing a real lab also needs the building load and the equipment maker's data.

Field card: Dental Laboratory HVAC

Capture at the bench, replace what you exhaust, watch the filter.

  • Heat: average watts = nameplate watts x duty fraction; 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h; 12,000 Btu/h = 1 ton.
  • Dust: molds and porcelain can be up to 70% crystalline silica; controls come before respirators.
  • Acrylic monomer: heavier than air, flammable; capture where it is released.
  • Makeup air heating: Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
  • Our own example: 2.64 kW of average oven load = 9,008 Btu/h; 700 cfm at 50 °F difference = 37,800 Btu/h.
  • Loaded filters cut airflow quietly. Use gauges and the maker's instructions.
