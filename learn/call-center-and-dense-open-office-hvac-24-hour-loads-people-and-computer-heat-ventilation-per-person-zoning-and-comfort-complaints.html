Call Center and Dense Open Office HVAC: 24-Hour Loads, People and Computer Heat, Ventilation per Person, Zoning and Comfort Complaints

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A call center packs many people and many screens into one floor, and the lights and computers may never shut off. The load is steady and heavy, the outdoor air requirement is set by headcount, and the zone with the most seats gets the complaints. This lesson shows how to size those pieces and how to keep the space comfortable when it never empties.

Why this space is different

An ordinary office has a few people per thousand square feet and a long empty night. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (named here in plain text) lists default occupant densities by space type. In the 2022 edition's Table 6-1, as posted in an ASHRAE addendum extract, "Office space" uses 5 people per 1,000 ft² and "Telephone/data entry" uses 60. That is twelve times the headcount in the same floor area. Each seat adds body heat, computer heat and breathing, so every per-person number in the system scales up. The 24-hour part matters too: with shifts overlapping, there may be no cool-down hour and no setback.

People heat

A seated person gives off both dry (sensible) heat and moisture (latent heat). Our own assumption for light desk work is 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent per person. We did not open an ASHRAE Fundamentals table for these, so treat them as planning values and check your design documents. The latent part ends up as water vapor the coil must remove. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.

Computer and monitor heat

Almost every watt a computer draws becomes heat in the room. Seat equipment is a computer, one or two monitors, a phone or headset and chargers. Older monitors drew much more than today's. ENERGY STAR's Version 4.0 monitor requirements (effective 2005 and 2006) capped on-mode power for the tighter Tier 2 level at 28 W per megapixel above one megapixel, so 56 W for a 2-megapixel screen, and sleep at 2 W. That spec is old and only gives a sense of scale; current equipment and your own seats may differ. ENERGY STAR's older guidance also notes that office equipment is often left on 24 hours a day, which in a call center is simply the schedule. If machines do not sleep, the heat does not drop at night.

Ventilation per person

In the 62.1 table, offices and telephone/data entry spaces both list 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per ft². The breathing zone outdoor air is people times 5, plus area times 0.06. With 60 per 1,000 ft², the people term dominates. That is why call centers are good candidates for demand control, and why the sensor is not a cure-all. Read the existing DCV and CO2 lesson for sensor placement and calibration, and ventilation rates and balancing for measuring airflow. We do not repeat them here.

What CO2 does and does not tell you. ASHRAE's 2025 position document on indoor carbon dioxide says 1,000 ppm is "at best an indicator" of outdoor air per person, tied to about 16 cfm per person, and that this does not apply the same way to every space type. It also calls the evidence on direct CO2 effects at typical indoor levels inconsistent. A 2012 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study (Satish and coauthors, Environmental Health Perspectives) had 22 people in an office-like chamber at 600, 1,000 and 2,500 ppm and saw declines on some decision-making scales at 1,000 ppm and larger ones at 2,500 ppm. The authors said confirmation was needed. We treat CO2 as a clue about airflow per person, not proof of a health effect.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions, not measurements: 120 seats, packed at the default 60 per 1,000 ft² (2,000 ft²); 150 W of plug load per seat; 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent per person; outdoor air at 95°F and 0.0160 lb water per lb dry air; space at 75°F and 0.0093; outdoor air at 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per ft². We leave out lights, envelope and fan heat to keep it simple.

  1. Plug heat: 120 × 150 W = 18,000 W. 18,000 × 3.412 = 61,416 Btu/h, all sensible.
  2. People: 120 × 250 = 30,000 Btu/h sensible. 120 × 200 = 24,000 Btu/h latent.
  3. Outdoor air: 120 × 5 = 600 cfm. 2,000 × 0.06 = 120 cfm. Total 720 cfm, or 6 cfm per person.
  4. Outdoor air sensible: 1.08 × 720 × (95 − 75) = 15,552 Btu/h.
  5. Outdoor air moisture: delta W is 0.0160 − 0.0093 = 0.0067. Moisture is 4.5 × 720 × 0.0067 = 21.7 lb/h. In Btu/h, 4,840 × 720 × 0.0067 = 23,348 Btu/h latent.
  6. Totals: sensible 61,416 + 30,000 + 15,552 = 106,968 Btu/h. Latent 24,000 + 23,348 = 47,348 Btu/h. Together 154,316 Btu/h, which is 12.9 tons at 12,000 Btu/h per ton, or about 1,290 Btu/h per seat.

Plug heat is about 40 percent of this load and outdoor air about a quarter. The load does not shrink at night unless computers sleep; 6 cfm per person is well below the roughly 16 cfm ASHRAE links to 1,000 ppm, so we would not be surprised to see readings above 1,000 ppm at full seats (that alone is not a code violation).

Zoning and comfort complaints

Many seats in one zone share one thermostat and one VAV box, so a hot corner can drive the whole zone. Our own rule of thumb: put zone boundaries where load changes, such as along the window wall, near a meeting room and around a section that runs longer shifts. See VAV Boxes Basics for minimums, reheat and why a box at minimum airflow can still leave a full room stuffy.

OSHA's indoor air quality page says OSHA has no IAQ standard, lists comfortable temperature and humidity and enough outdoor air as parts of good IAQ, and tells workers to ask employers to check HVAC. EPA's office occupant guide says heat-producing equipment near a thermostat may make the system deliver too much cool air, that furniture and partitions can block registers, and that "many perceived indoor air quality problems are often comfort problems." The guide also says system capacity is based partly on the projected number of people, so adding seats to a floor can call for HVAC changes. Columbia University's facilities page names ASHRAE Standard 55, Thermal Environmental Conditions for Human Occupancy, as the basis for its cooling-season range of 74 to 78°F with relative humidity under 60 percent, measured at the thermostat or desk level.

Binghamton University's page adds that clothing, activity and personal variation change what people feel. A practical order for a complaint call: log the time and seat; read the zone thermostat and a desk-level temperature; check the box airflow and damper; look for blocked diffusers and returns; check whether a heat source sits next to the sensor; then look at humidity. For known local equipment heat, see Cooling a Small Server Room.

Maintenance when the space never empties

This part is our own practical advice, not from a cited source. Schedule by zone on the lowest-headcount shift. Tell the floor supervisor before you shut a box or zone, and give an estimated time. Filters, sensor checks and diffuser cleaning can often be done in place with the system running. Work that stops airflow to a zone, such as coil cleaning or a box replacement, needs a plan for portable cooling or moving seats. Follow your site procedures and lockout rules.

Field card: Call Center and Dense Open Office HVAC

More seats means more heat, more outdoor air and more complaints.

  • 62.1 default density: 60 people per 1,000 ft² for telephone/data entry, versus 5 for ordinary office space.
  • Outdoor air: 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per ft².
  • Our example: 120 seats, 154,316 Btu/h, 12.9 tons, 720 cfm outdoor air.
  • Moisture: lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × delta W; Btu/h = 4,840 × cfm × delta W.
  • CO2 is a clue about airflow per person, not a precise health measure.
  • Check sensor placement and blocked grilles before touching setpoints.
  • Plan work by zone and shift.
