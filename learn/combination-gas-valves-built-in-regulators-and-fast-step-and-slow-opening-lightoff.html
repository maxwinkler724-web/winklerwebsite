Combination Gas Valves: Built-In Regulators and Fast, Step and Slow-Opening Lightoff

Last updated: 2026-10-28

A residential gas valve does more than open and close. One body holds a manual control, two shutoffs in series and a pressure regulator. Some versions also shape how fast the burner pressure rises at lightoff. This lesson explains what is inside, why a soft start matters, and how you can watch it on a manometer.

Check the model's literature

Check the model's literature. Pressures, step settings and reset times differ by valve family. The figures below come from the documents listed under Sources. They are examples, not settings to copy. Use the instructions for the valve and appliance in front of you.

What is inside the valve body

A combination gas control puts several jobs in one straight-through body. One maker data sheet lists a safety shutoff, a manual valve, two automatic operators and a pressure regulator. An open textbook chapter on automatic gas valves adds that on appliances under about 400 MBH, the safety shutoff, the operating valve and the regulator usually share one body.

  • Redundant shutoffs. Two automatic valves sit in series. On a call for heat both open. If one seat leaks while the burner is off, the other still holds. The textbook chapter says heating combination valves have been required to be dual seated since 1979.
  • Diaphragm operation. Inlet gas pressure works on a diaphragm to open or close the main disc. A small solenoid bleeds the pressure above the diaphragm to let it open. Because the port is tiny, the solenoid can be small and low powered.
  • Servo regulator. A second, smaller diaphragm positions the main seat so the valve can throttle, not just open fully. A spring pushes one way and outlet pressure pushes back. An adjustment screw sets the spring load, which sets the regulated outlet (manifold) pressure.
  • Taps. The maker sheet says inlet and outlet pressure taps are standard and reachable from the top of the control. The outlet tap is where you read manifold pressure. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer for the hookup and close-up.

The coil that moves the first valve is a separate topic, covered in Furnace Gas Valve Not Opening: Checking 24 Volts and the Valve Coil.

Three ways a valve can open

The same maker data sheet describes three opening characteristics for one valve family. The difference is what the outlet pressure does in the first seconds after the second valve is energized.

  • Fast (standard) opening. The servo regulator goes to its set pressure right away. The sheet gives a 3 to 6 second rise from zero to rated pressure for its standard-opening version in an 80,000 Btuh example at 7 in. w.c. inlet and 3.5 in. w.c. outlet.
  • Step opening. Outlet pressure holds at a low preset step for several seconds, then a timing diaphragm lets the valve go to full rate. The sheets list natural gas step settings of 0.7, 0.9, 1.2 or 1.7 in. w.c., chosen when the valve is ordered. The step is factory set and not field adjustable.
  • Slow opening. The second valve opens gradually through a flow restrictor, so outlet pressure climbs from zero to full over a few seconds, 3 to 6 seconds in the sheet's example.

Two-stage furnaces are a different idea. They use two regulators, one near 1 to 1.5 in. w.c. for low fire and one near 3 to 4 in. w.c. for high fire, according to the textbook chapter. That is a firing rate you choose and hold; a step or slow valve is only a softer way to reach one rate. See Two-Stage and Modulating Gas Furnaces: How Staging Works.

Why a soft lightoff matters

With an intermittent pilot or direct ignition system, the main burner gets gas only after the ignition source is working. If the valve then delivers full pressure at once, a lot of gas reaches the burners in a short time. If it does not light promptly, that gas collects before it burns.

A U.S. patent for a slow-opening gas valve describes the problem in plain terms. It says a main valve that opens rapidly and supplies gas at maximum pressure can cause a small explosion or rollout of flames from the combustion chamber, with a loud noise, in furnaces with small combustion boxes. It calls that undesirable from the customer's standpoint. The patent's answer is a valve that raises burner pressure gradually, reaching full pressure in about 8 seconds in its example.

Our reasoning from the patent: a soft lightoff gives the flame less gas to ignite at the first moment, so a late light costs less. This is also why a delayed-ignition complaint is a safety item. Check ignition timing, gas pressures and airflow as well. Do not treat a loud lightoff as only a noise problem. The ignition side is covered in Intermittent Pilot Ignition: Spark Pilot and Flame Proving Troubleshooting.

Seeing the step on a manometer

The outlet tap makes the opening character visible. One maker sheet says to turn the control knob to its pilot position before removing the outlet tap plug to connect a manometer. Follow the instructions for your valve, and reseal and leak-test the plug when you finish, as the manometer lesson explains.

  1. Connect the manometer to the outlet tap with the burner off, and zero it.
  2. Start a heat cycle and watch the reading from the moment the main valve is energized.
  3. A fast valve jumps to its set pressure. A slow valve climbs smoothly. A step valve stops at a plateau, holds, then rises to full.
  4. Note the plateau value, how long it lasts and the final value.

The maker sheet also says a step-opening control needs about 60 seconds to reset after the burner shuts off. If it is re-energized sooner, the step may be shortened or the burner may light at full flow. When you repeat a test, wait at least that long between cycles or the next step will look wrong. The same sheet says to check that the burner lights smoothly and without flashback at the step pressure.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a reading from a specific furnace. Assume a step-opening natural gas valve with a 1.7 in. w.c. step (one of the listed step settings) and a 3.5 in. w.c. full manifold pressure (the factory setting listed for natural gas). Assume the furnace is rated 80,000 Btuh at full pressure, and that the orifices and gas do not change.

Here is what a manometer on the outlet tap might show, with the times chosen by us for illustration:

Time after main valve energizedOutlet reading, in. w.c.What is happening
0 s0.0Valve energized, burner not yet lit
0.5 s1.7Step regulator in control
2 to 5 s1.7Burner lights and runs on the step
6.5 s2.6Timing diaphragm opening the valve
8 s onward3.5Full rate

Flow through a fixed orifice at low pressure rises with the square root of the pressure. The open textbook chapter on burner orifices states this as Q1/Q2 = √P1 / √P2. So input at the step compared with full input is:

  • Pressure ratio: 1.7 ÷ 3.5 = 0.486.
  • Square root: √0.486 = 0.697.
  • Input at the step: 80,000 × 0.697 = 55,755, about 55,800 Btuh.

The pressure is about half, but the input is about 70 percent. At the 2.6 reading, √(2.6 ÷ 3.5) = 0.862, so about 68,900 Btuh. The step ends up giving about 24,000 Btuh less gas than full rate, which is the whole point of the soft start. A 0.9 in. w.c. step would give √(0.9 ÷ 3.5) = 0.507, about 40,600 Btuh. Do not read a half-pressure step as half the gas; this is why pressure alone understates the input.

Regulator adjustment limits and replacement

The data sheets give a narrow window. Full-rate natural gas pressure on the standard and slow-opening models can be adjusted from 3.0 to 5.0 in. w.c. around a 3.5 factory setting, with nominal inlet pressure of 5.0 to 7.0 in. w.c. LP full-rate adjustment is 8.0 to 12.0 in. w.c. around 10.0. On step-opening models only the full-rate pressure is adjustable; the step is fixed. The appliance rating plate still sets the manifold pressure you aim for, and adjusting beyond it is not a repair.

The sheets say to replace the control, not repair it, when:

  • the pressure or flow you need cannot be reached by adjustment and inlet pressure is in the normal range (if inlet is out of range, correct the supply first);
  • there is no burner operation on a call for heat with proper voltage at the valve;
  • it fails checkout, or the knob is hard to turn or does not return;
  • it has likely passed 200,000 cycles, or it was flooded, damaged or heated.

The control has no serviceable parts and should not be disassembled. One sheet also says step-opening controls cannot be converted between gases, while standard and slow-opening ones can be converted with a kit. A replacement must match the original's opening type, because a standard valve fitted where a step valve belonged takes away the soft start.

Field card: Combination Gas Valves: Built-In Regulators and Fast, Step and Slow-Opening Lightoff

Read the plateau, then the final value.

  • One body: manual control, two shutoffs in series, servo pressure regulator.
  • Fast opening: set pressure right away. Slow opening: rises over a few seconds. Step opening: low plateau, then full.
  • Step valves need about 60 seconds to reset; test cycles too close together look wrong.
  • Input follows the square root of pressure: a 1.7 step on a 3.5 valve is about 70 percent of input (our example).
  • Natural gas full-rate adjustment window in the sheets: 3.0 to 5.0 in. w.c., factory 3.5. Step pressure is not adjustable.
  • Cannot reach pressure with normal inlet: replace the control. Do not disassemble it.
