Testing and Purging New Gas Piping Before Lighting Appliances

Last updated: 2026-11-04

New fuel gas piping has two jobs to prove before a burner ever lights: it must hold pressure without leaking, and the air inside it must be pushed out so the gas that reaches the burner is gas, not a slug of air. Both steps are routine, and both have hurt people when rushed. This lesson walks through the order of work and the reasons behind it.

Check the local rules first

The local fuel gas code and the authority having jurisdiction set the test pressure and the hold time. We do not give numbers for them here, because they change with the piping system, the operating pressure and the adopted code edition. Your inspector, the gas utility or the propane supplier can tell you what applies. This page is general education, not a procedure to follow in place of those rules or the appliance maker's instructions.

Why new piping gets tested before appliances are connected

A leak found before the appliances are hooked up is cheap to fix. A leak found after gas is flowing can fill a basement or a mechanical room. Testing the bare piping first also gives a clear result: if the gauge falls, the problem is in the pipe, not in a regulator, a gas valve or a flex connector you added later. The piping is tested with the system closed up and nothing downstream to blame. Inspectors usually want to see the test before the piping is covered or placed in service, so plan the test into the schedule instead of treating it as an afterthought.

Isolate what cannot take the test pressure

Test pressure can be higher than what an appliance regulator or combination gas valve is built to see. Appliance regulators, gas valves and controls are designed for a low operating inlet pressure, and a test at piping pressure can damage a diaphragm or crack a valve seat in a way you may not notice until the unit runs. So the rule of thumb is simple: the appliance stays disconnected or is closed off by its shutoff valve, and any line regulator that cannot take the test pressure is removed or isolated, with the open ends capped or plugged. Check the regulator's rating and the appliance instructions, and follow whichever is more limiting. For how the appliance side of the piping is built, see Gas Sediment Traps, Drip Legs, Shutoff Valves and Appliance Connectors, and for what the valve itself does, Combination Gas Valves.

The test medium

Use air or an inert gas such as nitrogen for the test. Never use oxygen. Oxygen makes oil, grease and many other materials burn fiercely, so a pipe with cutting oil or pipe dope residue becomes a fire hazard. Do not use the fuel gas itself as a test medium on new piping that has not yet passed. A federal worker safety agency letter on clearing new gas piping calls the practice of blowing flammable gas through new piping "inherently dangerous" and strongly recommends non-flammable, non-explosive alternatives. That letter was written about large industrial piping, but the reasoning carries down to every size: do not put a flammable gas where an ignition source might be.

Watching the gauge

Once the piping is closed and the test source is connected through a shutoff valve, bring the pressure up slowly. Use a gauge whose range suits the test, so the needle moves visibly if the pressure drops, because a gauge that is far over-ranged hides small losses. Close the test source and watch. Pressure will drift a little as the pipe warms or cools, for example when a line runs through a sunny wall or an unheated garage, so give it time to settle and note the air temperature. A steady reading for the hold period the local code requires is a pass. A falling reading means a leak or a temperature change, and you should find out which before signing off. Write down the start time, the pressure, the end time and the pressure, and keep the record with the job.

Finding leaks with bubble solution

A gauge tells you that there is a leak, not where. For that, brush or spray a bubble solution on every joint, fitting and connection while the pipe is under test. A university extension article on gas leaks describes the method: coat each threaded fitting, starting at the tank or meter and working through the trunk line and the lines inside the building. A small leak makes a few bubbles over a minute or two, and a large one makes many bubbles in under a minute. Do not look for leaks with a flame. Repair the joint, retest, and wipe off the solution when done. Electronic leak detectors are a useful second check, but they supplement the gauge and the bubbles and do not replace the hold test.

Purging air out of the new line

After the piping passes, it is full of air. If you connect the appliance and open the gas, that air reaches the burner first. The burner may refuse to light, lock out, or flicker as the slug of air passes through, and repeated lighting attempts keep sending gas toward the appliance while you wait. Purging means displacing the air with fuel gas while it is released at a controlled spot that is not an ignition hazard. A federal chemical accident investigation agency bulletin says to vent purged gas directly to a safe location outdoors, away from people and ignition sources, using a temporary hose or pipe where needed. Do not purge into a closed room, a boiler room or a garage.

Why indoor purging has caused explosions

That same bulletin was written after a 2009 explosion at a food plant, where workers purged a new line into an interior room while commissioning a water heater. Lighting the heater took a long time, so the purge went on intermittently, odor was noticed by several people, and the gas built up in the room until something ignited it. Other indoor-purge blasts are cited in the same bulletin. The lessons are plain: a long purge into a closed space adds up, a small ignition source is enough, and odor is not a reliable warning. The bulletin notes that noses tire out and that new piping can soak up odorant, so smell may fade even while gas is present. After the event, the national plumbing, mechanical and fuel gas codes were revised to restrict indoor purging for larger buildings and higher pressures, so the local code on your job may prohibit it outright.

Gas detectors during the purge

Use a combustible gas detector during any purge, at the vent and in nearby rooms, and keep it on until the work is done. The bulletin says to sample frequently or continuously, with the sample points chosen for where the gas will collect and how the room is ventilated. It does not set a single alarm number, so follow the detector maker's instructions and your site safety plan. Keep ignition sources away from the vent: no smoking, no grinding or torch work, and no switching of electrical devices in the area. Keep other people out of the area while venting. The federal worker safety agency letter makes the same points for large blows: vent upward and above structures, remove non-essential workers, eliminate ignition sources and monitor the atmosphere.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. Suppose a new house line is 60 ft of pipe with a 1.049 in inside diameter (a common 1 inch pipe size) plus 20 ft of pipe with a 0.824 in inside diameter (a 3/4 inch size). Assume a purge flow of 30 cubic feet per hour. Find the volume of the piping and how many pipe volumes pass through in 5 minutes. Assumptions: round pipe, no fittings counted, and the inside diameters above.

  1. Area of the 1.049 in pipe: 0.7854 × 1.049 × 1.049 = 0.8643 square inches.
  2. Length: 60 ft × 12 = 720 in. Volume: 0.8643 × 720 = 622.3 cubic inches.
  3. Area of the 0.824 in pipe: 0.7854 × 0.824 × 0.824 = 0.5333 square inches.
  4. Length: 20 ft × 12 = 240 in. Volume: 0.5333 × 240 = 128.0 cubic inches.
  5. Total: 622.3 + 128.0 = 750.3 cubic inches. Divide by 1,728 cubic inches per cubic foot: 750.3 / 1,728 = 0.434 cubic feet.
  6. Gas released in 5 minutes: 30 cubic feet per hour × (5 / 60) hour = 2.5 cubic feet.
  7. Pipe volumes passed: 2.5 / 0.434 = 5.76, about 5.8 pipe volumes.

The takeaway is that this small run holds under half a cubic foot, yet five minutes of purge sends out about 2.5 cubic feet of fuel gas, so where that gas goes matters more than how much air the pipe held. If the burner then fails to light and the purge drags on, the released gas grows every minute. Our own rule of thumb from this example: purge only as long as it takes to bring gas to the vent, confirm with the detector, then stop. Your local code or gas supplier may give a different method for how long to purge.

Field card: Testing and Purging New Gas Piping

Test with air or inert gas, never oxygen. Purge outdoors.

  • The local fuel gas code and the authority having jurisdiction set the test pressure and hold time.
  • Isolate or disconnect appliances and any regulator that cannot take the test pressure.
  • Test medium: air or inert gas. Never oxygen.
  • Watch the gauge for the full hold period and write down the readings.
  • Find leaks with bubble solution, never a flame.
  • Purge to a safe place outdoors, away from ignition sources.
  • Use a combustible gas detector. Do not trust your nose.
  • Pipe volume = 0.7854 × ID squared × length.
