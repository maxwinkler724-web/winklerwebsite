Garage and Workshop Heaters: Gas Unit Heaters and Electric Options

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you are asking what the best way to heat a garage safely is, the short answer has three parts: cut the heat loss first, pick a heater that sends its exhaust outdoors or makes none, and install it the way its manual says. This lesson compares the three common choices and shows how to estimate how much heat a garage needs.

Safety first. Do not run any fuel-burning heater in a space that holds gasoline, solvents, paint thinner or dust. Gas heater installation, gas piping and 240 volt wiring are work for licensed people where you live. This page is for understanding and for talking to the installer, not for doing the job yourself.

Start with the building, not the heater

A garage loses heat through its walls, ceiling, door and air leaks. The U.S. Department of Energy explains that heat flows from heated spaces toward unheated ones and outdoors, and that the heating system has to replace whatever leaks away. Its insulation guidance also stresses air sealing and moisture control next to adding insulation, and notes that insulation layers add their R-values together. So the cheapest "heater" is often weatherstripping on the door, sealing gaps and insulating the ceiling and walls. A smaller heater then does the job, and it runs less.

Choice 1: vented gas unit heater

A gas unit heater hangs from the ceiling or a wall and blows warm air across the space. Its combustion gases go out through a vent pipe. Modine's Hot Dawg manual says plainly that gas-fired heating equipment must be vented and must not be operated unvented. Some models are separated-combustion, meaning they bring in combustion air and send out exhaust through pipes to the outdoors; Reznor's SFT manual describes that arrangement. Mr. Heater's MHU series manual lists inputs of 50,000, 80,000 and 125,000 Btu/h at 80 percent efficiency. Modine's propeller models run from 30,000 to 125,000 Btu/h input. For vent categories in general see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV.

Mounting height and flammable vapors

Cars carry gasoline, and a workshop may hold solvents and sawdust. For that reason the unit heater manuals talk about both height and the air around the heater. Modine says to keep the bottom of the unit at least 7 feet above the floor unless it is guarded, and it points to the NFPA standards for repair garages and parking structures. Reznor says overhead heaters in garages need at least 8 feet, and Mr. Heater's MHU manual gives 8 feet for a U.S. residential garage and also says the heater must be located or protected so a vehicle cannot damage it. All three say not to put the heater where it is exposed to flammable vapors or an explosive atmosphere. They differ in the numbers, which is the point: read the manual for the exact model you own, because a different heater from the same maker can have a different rule. Gas supply

Unit heaters need a correctly sized gas line and the right inlet pressure. The Mr. Heater MHU manual gives 5 to 14 inches water column for natural gas and 11 to 14 for propane. How to size the pipe is in Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method.

Choice 2: electric (240 V) heaters

An electric garage heater makes no exhaust, so there is no vent and no combustion air. The DOE says electric resistance heating converts all incoming electric energy to heat at the point of use, but that electric heat often costs more to run than heat from a combustion appliance, and that heat pumps are usually the better choice if electricity is the only option. For a garage you only warm now and then, the simple installation can make electric a good fit; have an electrician size the circuit. Many garage models are 240 volt and hard-wired. A few ground rules from CPSC for any portable electric heater: plug it directly into a wall outlet, never an extension cord or power strip, and keep it at least three feet from drapes, furniture and bedding. Basics of the wiring side are in Electrical Basics for HVAC, and a related troubleshooting page is Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working.

Choice 3: unvented portable heaters

Unvented fuel-burning heaters release their combustion products into the room. CPSC says unvented heaters pose the greatest threat of carbon monoxide poisoning, need a steady supply of fresh air, and are banned in some areas. Mr. Heater's Big Buddy manual (model MH18B) says it needs ventilation openings of 18 square inches at both ceiling and floor level, and that without a window or roof vent you should not use it inside. It also warns about excess CO when air is short and says never to operate it while sleeping. That manual does not address garages, and it says a connected propane cylinder must not be stored in a garage. Another Mr. Heater manual, for a 30,000 Btu/h unvented propane model, does allow home garage use if the burner is at least 18 inches off the floor and a vehicle cannot hit it, and it still requires enough room volume or added fresh air. Manuals differ, so a heater being sold at a store does not mean it is right for your garage. Mr. Heater's manuals describe a pilot and oxygen depletion sensor that shuts the heater off when fresh air runs short; keep a CO alarm anyway. Read Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do and Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages, and see Combustion and CO Safety Basics for how CO forms.

Worked example: how much heat does a garage need?

Plain heat-loss arithmetic multiplies area by a U value by the temperature difference, where U is 1 divided by R (the method in the British Columbia open textbook on plumbing heating requirements). The numbers below for the garage are our assumptions for teaching, not measurements.

  • Garage: 20 ft by 20 ft, 8 ft walls, 16 ft by 7 ft overhead door. Heated to 50 F; design outdoor temperature 10 F. Temperature difference = 50 minus 10 = 40 F.
  • Walls: perimeter 80 ft times 8 ft = 640 sq ft. Subtract the door, 16 times 7 = 112 sq ft. Net wall = 528 sq ft. Assume R-13, so U = 1 / 13 = 0.077. Loss = 528 x 0.077 x 40 = 1,626 Btu/h.
  • Door: 112 sq ft, assume R-6, U = 1 / 6 = 0.167. Loss = 112 x 0.167 x 40 = 748 Btu/h.
  • Ceiling: 20 x 20 = 400 sq ft, assume R-19, U = 0.053. Loss = 400 x 0.053 x 40 = 848 Btu/h.
  • Total = 1,626 + 748 + 848 = 3,222 Btu/h. This ignores air leaks and the slab, so a leaky garage needs more.

Electric: EIA lists 1 kilowatt-hour as 3,412 Btu, so 1 watt = 3.412 Btu/h. 3,222 / 3.412 = 944 watts. A 5,000 W heater delivers 5,000 x 3.412 = 17,060 Btu/h, about five times this conduction-only loss. Because the example leaves out air leaks around the door and heat lost to the slab, treat 3,222 Btu/h as a lower bound, not a size to buy. Gas: at 80 percent efficiency, input = 3,222 / 0.80 = 4,028 Btu/h, while the smallest unit heaters in the manuals above start at 30,000 Btu/h input.

Check the room volume rule for unvented heaters

One Mr. Heater manual defines a confined space as less than 50 cubic feet per 1,000 Btu/h of total input. This garage is 20 x 20 x 8 = 3,200 cubic feet, so 3,200 / 50 = 64 thousand Btu/h. That is a combustion-air test in a manual, not a statement that an unvented heater is safe near cars.

A short tech section

  • Clearances. Modine's Hot Dawg manual allows 1 inch at top and sides for some models and 18 inches at the rear; Reznor's SFT lists 1 inch at the top for sizes 45 to 75, 18 inches at the rear and 20 inches at the access panel. Use the table in the manual for the exact model and the surface limit it gives (160 F in Modine's wording).
  • Combustion air. Modine says a confined space has 50 cubic feet or less per 1,000 Btu/h input and then wants two permanent openings sized at 1 square inch per 1,000 Btu/h, 100 square inches minimum each.
  • Venting. No plastic vent pipe on the Modine units. Slope and support rules are in the manual. Reznor's SFT manual approves only its listed horizontal and vertical vent options and says not to reuse an existing vent system.
  • Electrical. The Mr. Heater MHU unit still needs 120 V for its fan and controls, a separate disconnect, and 24 V thermostat wiring (Relays, Transformers and 24 V Control).

Field card: Garage and Workshop Heaters

Insulate and seal first, then pick a heater that vents or makes no exhaust.

  • Vented gas unit heater: exhaust goes outdoors; mount it high (7 to 8 ft to the bottom per the manual).
  • Electric 240 V: no exhaust; check nameplate watts and have the circuit sized.
  • Unvented portable: greatest CO risk per CPSC; many manuals demand fresh air openings.
  • 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h. Heat loss = area x (1 / R) x temperature difference.
  • Never use any heater near gasoline, solvents or dust.
  • Install a CO alarm and follow the manual for the model you own.
