Checking Heat Pump Charge in Heating Mode

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In summer, a technician reads superheat or subcooling and knows where the charge stands. In winter the same readings swing with outdoor temperature, frost and the control logic. This lesson explains why many installation instructions steer you toward weighing in the charge or following the equipment maker's heating chart, and what pressures and indoor temperature rise can still tell you.

Why superheat and subcooling mislead in heating mode

In heating mode the roles swap. The outdoor coil is the evaporator and the indoor coil is the condenser (see the diagram). The U.S. Department of Energy describes it the same way: liquid refrigerant in the outdoor coil absorbs heat from outside air and evaporates, then the indoor coil releases that heat as the refrigerant condenses. The reversing valve is what changes the direction of flow, and DOE says it also handles defrosting the outdoor coil in winter.

A trade-press article on winter charge checks notes that superheat and subcooling can be measured in heating mode, but that there are few set guidelines, so the readings do not clearly say whether the charge is right. It gives a typical heating-mode superheat of 8 to 14 degrees with an expansion valve, and says subcooling usually runs somewhat higher than normal, especially when measured at the outdoor unit. Those are one author's field rules of thumb, not a maker's specification. Other things shift the numbers too: a dirty filter or weak indoor airflow raises head pressure, a dirty or frosted outdoor coil pulls suction pressure down. These effects are why heating readings alone are weak evidence.

The California residential field-verification procedure shows how formal programs handle this. Its standard superheat and subcooling verification may be used only when outdoor air is 55 F or above, and it calls the weigh-in procedure acceptable at any outdoor temperature and required below 55 F when no applicable special protocol exists. That is a state energy code, not a rule where you work, but it shows the thinking: when the weather is cold, weigh in.

Common guidance, in order of preference

  1. Read the maker's instructions first. The winter-charge article stresses reading the maker's specifications before anything else. It describes some makers publishing heat-mode charging steps for ambient below a set temperature, some publishing a pressure curve based on indoor and outdoor dry-bulb temperatures, and one publishing a heat-mode chart as a guideline only, not a charging tool. In other words, the instructions in the box rule.
  2. Weigh in. After a repair, evacuate and weigh the full nameplate charge, plus or minus the line set adjustment. The same article calls weigh-in and weigh-out effective after a refrigerant circuit repair, though often impractical for a routine check. See Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments.
  3. Return in the cooling season. If the charge is only suspected and the system is heating acceptably, one approach is to verify with a cooling-mode method when outdoor conditions allow. See Charging Methods Overview for those methods.

What gauges and temperatures still tell you

Gauges are still useful for spotting a gross fault. The winter-charge article offers these rules of thumb for R-410A, to be used only after a complete defrost, a clean filter and clean coils, and with the maker's data taking priority:

  • Discharge line temperature roughly 100 to 110 F above outdoor temperature.
  • Suction saturation temperature roughly 20 to 25 F below outdoor temperature.
  • Condensing temperature roughly 30 to 40 F above indoor dry-bulb, varying with the unit and airflow.
  • Without gauges, with a frost-free coil and outdoor air between 15 and 65 F: suction line 5 to 15 F cooler than outdoor air, liquid line 3 to 15 F warmer than indoor air.

Treat these as a screen, not a target. A very low suction pressure with a high discharge temperature points toward undercharge, and the same article says an extreme case shows up as a discharge line near 210 F with 50 F outdoors. A clean filter and good airflow come first, because airflow faults copy charge faults.

Accumulator effects

An accumulator sits in the suction line to catch liquid before it reaches the compressor, so it can mask a suction superheat reading. Do not chase a superheat number by adding refrigerant. Read Suction Line Accumulators and Liquid Receivers for the mechanics.

Defrost interference

Frost on the outdoor coil lowers airflow and suction pressure, and a defrost cycle reverses the circuit entirely, so readings taken during or just after defrost are meaningless. The article says to defrost the outdoor unit completely before testing. DOE advises selecting demand-defrost control to limit unnecessary cycles. For the control logic, see Heat Pump Defrost Control Boards and Sensors.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions: the nameplate charge covers a 15 ft line set, the maker adds 0.6 oz per extra foot, the installed line set is 40 ft, airflow is 1,200 cfm, return air is 70 F and supply air is 95 F. These inputs are assumed for practice, not from any real model.

Step 1, line set adjustment. Extra length = 40 - 15 = 25 ft. Added charge = 25 ft x 0.6 oz/ft = 15 oz. Weigh the nameplate charge plus 15 oz.

Step 2, indoor temperature rise check. Temperature rise = 95 - 70 = 25 F. The sensible heat formula for standard air is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise. So 1.08 x 1,200 x 25 = 1,296 x 25 = 32,400 Btu/h. The 1.08 comes from 60 minutes x 0.075 lb/ft³ x 0.24 Btu/lb-F (our own derivation).

32,400 / 12,000 = 2.7 tons of delivered heat. Compare that with the maker's heating capacity at the same outdoor temperature. If the number comes in far below the published value with a clean filter, a clean coil and good airflow, a charge or other fault is likely. If it is close, the charge is probably not the problem. This is a screening check, not proof of charge, and the formula assumes sea-level air and no defrost in progress.

Gauges and the venting rule

Connecting gauges can release a little refrigerant, which brings up the federal venting rule. Under 40 CFR 82.154, people who service appliances may not knowingly vent refrigerant into the environment, with a limited exception for de minimis releases tied to good faith attempts to recycle or recover. EPA's venting page lists releases that occur when connecting or disconnecting hoses to charge or service appliances as de minimis when made in good faith. EPA 608 certification rules apply; check the rule and EPA's page for current wording. Follow your employer's and the maker's procedures.

Field card: Checking Heat Pump Charge in Heating Mode

In winter, weigh it or chart it, and use gauges to catch gross faults only.

  • Heating mode: outdoor coil is the evaporator, indoor coil is the condenser.
  • Read the maker's heating instructions first.
  • Cold weather: weigh in; one state code requires weigh-in below 55 F outdoors.
  • Defrost fully, clean the filter and coils, then read gauges.
  • Extra line set: (feet - basis) x oz/ft. Example: 25 x 0.6 = 15 oz.
  • Delivered heat: 1.08 x cfm x rise. Example: 1.08 x 1,200 x 25 = 32,400 Btu/h.
  • Do not vent refrigerant on purpose.
