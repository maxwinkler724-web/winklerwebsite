Recording Startup Readings on New HVAC Equipment: A Sample Sheet

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The install is not finished when the furnace lights and the outdoor unit hums. A startup sheet is a one-page record of what the equipment actually did on day one. It is the baseline the next technician compares against, the proof the install was checked, and the paper an owner can hand over when something goes wrong later.

Why a startup sheet matters

Baseline for later service. A reading means little alone. A static pressure of 0.7 inches of water column is a problem or normal depending on the unit and on what it read the day it was started. With a sheet, a technician years later can see what changed. Without one, they are guessing.

Proof of a correct install. The Building America Solution Center, a Department of Energy program run by a national lab, describes commissioning a ducted heat pump as a series of measured checks: a vacuum test, airflow and static pressure, and refrigerant charge. It says to compare each against the manufacturer's specifications and to keep detailed commissioning records. ENERGY STAR (the EPA and DOE program) tells homeowners to ask the contractor to confirm that airflow matches the maker's specifications and that the refrigerant charge was tested and adjusted. A filled-in sheet is how you show that you did.

Warranty records. Warranty terms differ from maker to maker, so read the terms for the equipment you install. Our own advice: keep the sheet with the registration paperwork, because it shows the date of startup and that the unit was running inside its limits.

Why the numbers matter in dollars. A NIST paper by Domanski, Henderson and Payne modeled installation faults on a split air conditioner. It found that a 30 percent refrigerant undercharge raised seasonal energy use by about 16 percent, and that a 36 percent drop in indoor airflow raised it by 8 to 12 percent. Those are simulated results, not field measurements, but they show why charge and airflow get their own lines on the sheet.

What to record, and how

The figure shows the test points on a furnace with a split system. The numbers match the list below, in the same order. Always take the target from the nameplate or the maker's instructions for that exact model. The limits quoted below are examples from public sources, and the maker's number wins.

  1. Line voltage and amps against the nameplate. Record voltage at the disconnect of the furnace and the outdoor unit, running amps on the compressor, outdoor fan and indoor blower, and the nameplate rated load or minimum circuit values beside them. Amps well above nameplate point to low voltage, a wiring fault or a mechanical problem.
  2. Total external static pressure (TESP) against the maker's limit. Per the Building America Solution Center, measure return static pressure after the filter and supply static pressure after the fan (in a furnace, between the heat exchanger and the indoor coil), then add the absolute values of the two readings. Compare the sum to the manufacturer's acceptable range. Record the blower speed tap too, because the number means nothing without it.
  3. Furnace temperature rise against the nameplate range. Subtract return air temperature from supply air temperature, measured after the furnace has run for several minutes. A Montana State University Extension weatherization worksheet gives a general desired rise of 30 to 50 degrees F with 80 degrees F as a maximum "or per nameplate" and measures after 5 minutes of operation. Use the nameplate range for the model in front of you.
  4. Gas inlet and manifold pressure. The same worksheet lists natural gas supply of 5 to 7 inches of water column with a manifold pressure of 3.5, and for LP gas 11 to 14 inches supply with a manifold of 10. Record fuel type, both readings, and the manifold target printed on the gas valve or in the maker's instructions. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer.
  5. Combustion analysis: CO and draft. The worksheet's limit is carbon monoxide in the appliance vent of less than 200 ppm. For atmospheric-draft appliances it lists a minimum draft that depends on outdoor temperature (for example, more than -0.010 inches of water column from 30 to 80 degrees F). Mark draft "not applicable" on sealed or power-vented furnaces, and note the analyzer's air-free or as-measured basis. See Combustion Analyzer Readings.
  6. Refrigerant charge method and result. Write down which method you used. The Solution Center guide says superheat is used for fixed-orifice metering devices and subcooling for TXV systems, and that targets depend on the system, so take them from the maker. Weigh-in is typical on a new install. Record line length, added charge by weight, suction and liquid pressures, line temperatures, and the computed superheat or subcooling. See Temperature Glide and Superheat/Subcooling.
  7. Evacuation micron reading and decay test. The Solution Center guide describes pulling a vacuum below 500 microns, valving off, and watching for at least 10 minutes; it passes if the reading stays under 500 and changes little (typically under 50 microns). Record the lowest reading, the start and end readings of the decay test, and the time. Also record the nitrogen pressure test if you did one. See Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.
  8. Condensate trap and safety switch test. Our own checklist items: trap primed, drain flowing, float or pan switch tested by tripping it and confirming the equipment shuts off, pressure switches and limit tested per the maker's instructions. Record pass or fail for each.
  9. Thermostat settings and filter. Record the thermostat model type, heating and cooling staging, any heat pump lockout or balance point settings, and the filter size and rating. The Solution Center guide also says to check that backup heat runs only below the balance point. A filter that is too small or too restrictive shows up in your static pressure line.

For measuring airflow and static pressure in more depth, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics and Measuring Airflow.

Worked example (our own, with assumed readings)

This is our own example, not a real job. We assume a gas furnace with a TXV split air conditioner, and we assume these nameplate and maker targets: temperature rise 35 to 65 degrees F, TESP limit 0.50 inches of water column, natural gas manifold 3.5 inches, subcooling target 10 degrees F, and a filter of 20 x 25 x 1 inch. The arithmetic. TESP is the sum of the absolute values: 0.38 + 0.47 = 0.85 inches. That is 0.35 over the assumed 0.50 limit. Temperature rise is 142 - 70 = 72 degrees F, which is 7 over the top of the assumed 35 to 65 range.

What the two bad readings point to. They agree with each other. High static pressure means the blower is pushing against too much resistance, and less air moving across the heat exchanger makes the air leaving it hotter. Both point to restricted airflow, not to a gas or burner problem, since the manifold pressure is on target. The likely first suspects are the small, restrictive filter (the return reading of -0.38 is taken after the filter, so the filter and return duct are suspect), the return grille and duct sizing, a dirty coil or a blower speed tap set too low. Isolate the cause by taking a static reading before and after the filter, then recheck with a larger filter. That is our own diagnostic approach, not a rule. Fix it before you leave and record both the first and final readings.

The 30 micron rise in the decay test is within the guide's "typically under 50" figure, but write the reading down anyway so the next visit has a comparison.

Where to keep the copy

Keep two copies: one with the owner, and one on the unit, in a sleeve or sticker on the furnace cabinet near the nameplate, where the next technician will look. Our own habit is to also keep a photo of the filled sheet in the company file.

Where EPA recordkeeping fits

Do not confuse the startup sheet with the EPA Section 608 records. EPA's page for technicians says technicians servicing appliances with 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant give the owner an invoice showing the refrigerant added, and that owners keep servicing records for those appliances. That page also says technicians who dispose of appliances holding between 5 and 50 pounds, including field-built residential split systems, keep disposal records, and EPA's technician Q and A says those records must be kept for three years. A typical new residential split system is below 50 pounds, so the startup sheet is mainly good practice here, not a federal form. Check the current rules for your refrigerant.

Field card: Recording Startup Readings

If it is not on the sheet, the next tech has to guess.

  • Take targets from the nameplate and maker's instructions, not from memory.
  • Volts and amps against nameplate for every motor and the compressor.
  • TESP is the sum of the absolute values of return and supply static.
  • Temperature rise is supply minus return, compared to the nameplate range.
  • Gas: inlet and manifold pressure, CO, and draft where it applies.
  • Charge: write down the method (superheat for fixed orifice, subcooling for TXV) and the result.
  • Vacuum: lowest micron reading and the decay over 10 minutes.
  • Test the condensate switch by tripping it; note the filter size.
  • Leave one copy with the owner and one on the unit.
