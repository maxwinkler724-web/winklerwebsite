Water Heater Expansion Tank Sizing, Air Charge and Closed Systems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Water takes up more room when it is hot. In an open plumbing system the extra water slips back toward the street. In a closed one it has nowhere to go, so pressure climbs until something gives. A small thermal expansion tank is the usual fix. This lesson covers how to size one, how to set its air charge, and how to tell when it has died.

Why a closed system traps the extra water

Several makers say the same thing in their instructions. A. O. Smith's tank manual says that when water is heated it expands, and that provision must be made for this in a closed hot water system. Resideo's (Honeywell) absorber sheet says expansion becomes a problem when the water system becomes closed, for example after a backflow preventer, check valve or pressure reducing valve is installed. Its words for the result: dangerously high pressures could result, or the heater's safety relief valve will frequently expel water. State's tank manual adds that many local codes require backflow preventers, which seal the system, and that without a tank the relief valve would discharge on every heating cycle.

So the trigger is not the water heater. It is a one-way device somewhere between the street and your house: a check valve at the meter, a backflow preventer, or a pressure reducing valve (PRV) with a built-in check. For how a PRV works on a boiler fill line, see Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer.

Plumbing codes address this directly. North Carolina's 2012 plumbing code, section 607.3.2, says that where a backflow prevention device, check valve or other device is installed on a supply system using storage water heating equipment such that thermal expansion causes an increase in pressure, a device for controlling pressure shall be installed. That is one state's wording; yours may differ.

Symptoms to look for

  • The temperature and pressure relief valve weeps or drips after the heater runs, then stops. A. O. Smith lists a valve that "weeps or discharges periodically" as the sign.
  • Pressure spikes: a gauge on a hose bib reads much higher right after a long heating cycle than before it.

A. O. Smith also lists other causes: a worn or lime-clogged relief valve, inlet pressure above 60 psi, or an air charge that does not match the incoming pressure. So a tank is not always the answer. Never plug or cap the relief valve or its pipe. Our leaking water heater lesson walks through finding the source of a leak.

How a potable tank works, and how it differs from a boiler tank

Inside is a flexible diaphragm. One side holds air, charged through a tire-type valve. The other side connects to the cold water pipe. As the heater warms, the extra water pushes in and squeezes the air. When hot water is drawn, the air pushes it back. Same idea as the tank in a hydronic loop (see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure), with differences that matter:

  • Water quality. Zurn Wilkins says its XT tanks are certified to NSF/ANSI 61 for domestic water. A boiler tank is not built for drinking water.
  • Charge target. A potable tank is charged to match the building supply pressure, commonly in the range of Amtrol's sizing tool, 40 to 80 psi. Zurn: adjust to equal line pressure, not to exceed 80 psi. A boiler tank is set to the much lower boiler fill pressure.
  • Sizing basis. Potable tanks are sized from the heater volume and temperature rise. Hydronic tanks are sized from total system water. Amtrol even has a separate hydronic sizing tool.
  • Location. Zurn says to install it on the cold supply between the heater and any check valve or backflow preventer. Amtrol says not to put it on the hot outlet, where heated water cooling in the tank delays hot water at the fixture.

Sizing: a worked example from Amtrol

Amtrol's Therm-X-Trol brochure (MC 10007, 2019) gives a method. Tank makers differ in their assumptions, so use the numbers from the maker of the tank you buy. Here is an invented case: a 50-gallon heater, 120°F setpoint, 60 psi supply, 150 psi relief valve.

  1. Expansion factor. Amtrol's Table 1, based on 40°F incoming water, lists 0.0100 (1.0 percent) at 120°F and 0.0150 at 140°F.
  2. Expanded water. 50 gallons × 0.0100 = 0.50 gallon. That is about two quarts of extra water, in the worst case from a cold fill to 120°F.
  3. Design pressure factor. This accounts for the tank working between supply pressure and the highest allowed pressure. Amtrol's Table 2 gives 1.8 for 60 psi line pressure and a 150 psi maximum. Its formula, (150 + 14.7) ÷ (150 − 60) = 164.7 ÷ 90 = 1.83, agrees.
  4. Total tank volume needed. 0.50 × 1.8 = 0.90 gallon (0.92 using 1.83).
  5. Pick a tank. Amtrol's quick sizing chart (40°F incoming, 150 psi relief) has rows for 140, 160 and 180°F maximum setting. For a 50-gallon heater at 60 psi it lists ST-5 at 140°F, ST-8 at 160°F and ST-8 at 180°F. Our own reading: the chart has no 120°F row, and a 120°F setpoint sits under the 140°F row, so ST-5. The listed acceptance volume of an ST-5 is 0.9 gallon, which covers the 0.50 gallon of expansion.

Compare Watts: its online calculator starts the cold water at 90°F by default and uses a final pressure rule of the initial pressure plus 50 percent, capped at 85 percent of the relief rating. Different inputs give different answers, which is why the maker's own chart or tool governs.

Setting the air charge

Tanks usually ship at 40 psi (Zurn, State and Resideo all say so). If your supply is 60 psi, the charge must be raised. Zurn's instruction is blunt: adjust the tank pre-charge to equal line pressure, not to exceed 80 psi. A. O. Smith's steps, in short:

  1. Shut the main cold supply valve and open a nearby faucet until the water stops, so the tank is not under pressure.
  2. Read the charge with a tire gauge at the air valve.
  3. Raise it only with a hand tire pump; to lower it, press the valve core.
  4. Know the true supply pressure. A. O. Smith suggests running a hot tap for 15 minutes, closing it, and reading a gauge right away, not a spiked reading from a heating cycle.
  5. Open the supply valve and restart the heater.

For why a mismatched charge costs you cushion, see the Boyle's law example in the leaking water heater lesson linked above. Raising the charge above supply pressure is no help either; Zurn caps it at 80 psi, and a PRV is needed if supply exceeds 80 psi.

Checking for a failed or waterlogged tank

Zurn says to check annually: shut off the supply, open a faucet to relieve pressure, and read the air valve with a tire gauge. If it does not match incoming pressure, add air. Replace the tank if the gauge reads zero or water comes out of the air valve. A. O. Smith reminds that this tank, as all tanks, will eventually leak. A tank that is empty of air or full of water no longer protects the heater, and the relief valve starts weeping again.

Who does the install

Local code governs. In New Jersey the Uniform Construction Code plumbing subcode, N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code (2021 edition) as its model code, and the plumbing subcode official is the authority having jurisdiction. A. O. Smith says to be sure installation meets all local plumbing codes and that a qualified installer needs ability equivalent to a licensed tradesman in plumbing. Adding a tank means working on the cold supply piping, so have a licensed plumber do it. Ask the local code office whether a permit applies, and see water heater temperature and scald risk before changing the setpoint.

Field card: Water Heater Expansion Tank

One-way valve on the supply means the system is closed. Closed means a tank.

  • Check valve, backflow preventer or PRV closes the system.
  • Weeping relief valve after heating is the classic sign.
  • Size from the maker's chart: heater gallons, temperature, supply and relief pressure.
  • Example: 50 gal × 0.0100 = 0.50 gal; × 1.8 = 0.90 gal; chart says ST-5.
  • Air charge equals supply pressure, set with the tank isolated and the water off.
  • Water from the air valve, or zero pressure, means replace the tank.
  • Never plug the relief valve. Local code and a licensed plumber govern the install.
