Choosing a Water Heater for a New Home

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A new house lets you pick the water heater before anything is closed in. The four common choices are a heat pump tank, an electric resistance tank, a gas tank and a gas tankless unit. They differ in what they need from the building: fuel, a vent, an electrical circuit, floor space, air and a drain. They also differ in where they can sit, and where they sit decides how long you wait for hot water at the far bathroom.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Codes, manufacturer instructions and the permit office decide what is allowed on your job. This page is general education built from federal sources read in 2026. Sizing the tank by first-hour rating is a separate topic, so we skip it here.

Fuel, venting and air

An electric resistance tank burns nothing, so it needs no flue. The Building America strategy guideline on water heater selection lists its typical rating at 0.90 to 0.95 energy factor, against 0.58 to 0.62 for a gas storage tank. A gas tank trades that for a gas line and a vent, plus air to burn. The same guideline warns that whole-house fans or other high-volume exhaust can cause back-drafting of a water heater, which matters in a tight new house. Our lessons on combustion air and power-vented and direct-vent heaters cover the venting detail.

A gas tankless unit asks for more gas. The Building America tankless measure guideline says a gas-fired unit adds 175,000 Btu or more to the annual gas load and that in most cases the in-house gas line serving it needs to be 3/4 in. It also says to use only venting products listed and tested for the particular unit, and that condensing tankless units need their combustion condensate removed. For trouble after install, see tankless problems.

Heat pump units need air and a drain

A heat pump tank takes heat out of the surrounding air. The Department of Energy says to install one where the room stays between 40 and 90 degrees F year-round, with at least 1,000 cubic feet of air space around it. The Building America heat pump measure guideline says units generally need a room of at least 750 cubic feet, and lists different minimums by model, so the installation manual of the actual unit decides. That guideline also says the room may cool by 2 to 6 degrees F while the unit runs, that air entry and discharge must be free of obstructions, and that the unit should not sit near bedrooms because of compressor and fan noise. For condensate it says to pitch a hose to a floor drain, or use a condensate pump if no drain is suitable. Our heat pump water heater lesson covers how they work.

The electrical circuit

Both electric types need a 240 V supply. The selection guideline says a heat pump unit needs a 240 V dedicated circuit. A typical resistance tank has two 4.5 kW elements. Our own arithmetic: 4,500 W divided by 240 V is 18.75 A per element. The installation instructions and the electrical code, not this page, set the breaker and wire size. An electrician has to know the type before the panel is laid out.

Location decides the wait

The Building America resource guide on core and manifold plumbing recommends hot water fixtures as close to the water heater as possible. It cites a 1,200 square foot home where time-to-tap can exceed 90 seconds. A tankless unit does not change this. The tankless guideline measured 28 seconds for hot water to arrive through 50 ft of 1/2 in PEX at 1 gpm, because the cold water sitting in the pipe has to be pushed out first. The wait is pipe volume divided by flow, whatever the heater type. The EPA guide for new-home hot water delivery prints ounces of water per foot of tubing. For 1/2 in pipe it lists 1.69 (copper M), 1.55 (copper L) and 1.18 (PEX, SDR 9). For 3/4 in pipe it lists 3.43, 3.22 and 2.35 for the same three. It also sets limits of 0.5 gallons stored in any pipe path and 0.6 gallons run at the fixture before hot water arrives. If a recirculation system is needed later, see recirculation pumps.

Worked example (our own, made-up house)

This is our own example, not a measured house. We assume a one-story home with a far bathroom sink, PEX (SDR 9) pipe with the printed volumes of 2.35 oz/ft for 3/4 in and 1.18 oz/ft for 1/2 in, and a flow of 1.5 gpm, the bathroom sink rate used in the EPA guide. The pipe lengths are assumptions. The wait here is only the time to push the cold water out of the pipe, so a real wait runs somewhat longer.

Heater A, in the garage. 35 ft of 3/4 in is 35 × 2.35 = 82.25 oz. 15 ft of 1/2 in is 15 × 1.18 = 17.70 oz. Total 99.95 oz. At 128 oz per gallon that is 0.78 gal. Wait: 0.78 gal ÷ 1.5 gpm = 0.52 min, or about 31 s.

Heater B, by the bath wall. 10 ft of 3/4 in is 23.50 oz, plus the same 17.70 oz is 41.20 oz, or 0.32 gal. Wait: 0.32 ÷ 1.5 = 0.21 min, or about 13 s. Moving the heater cuts the wait by about 18 s and the water run down the drain by about 0.46 gal each draw. Against the EPA limits, A (0.78 gal) is over 0.5 and 0.6 gal; B (0.32 gal) is under both. As a cross-check, 50 ft of 1/2 in PEX at 1 gpm is 59 oz, 0.46 gal, or about 28 s, matching the tankless guideline's measured 28 s.

Yearly energy, same hot water use. We assume 50 gal a day, water heated from 55 to 125 degrees F (a 70 degree rise), 8.34 lb per gallon, 1 Btu per lb per degree F, and 3,412 Btu per kWh. Heat delivered: 50 × 8.34 × 70 = 29,190 Btu a day, or 8.56 kWh. Over 365 days that is 3,123 kWh of heat in the water. For a resistance tank we assume an energy factor of 0.92, the 40-gallon electric minimum in the selection guideline: 3,123 ÷ 0.92 = 3,394 kWh a year. For a heat pump tank we assume 2.0, the low end of the guideline's "above 2.0" and the Measure Guideline's 2.00 to 2.51 listing: 3,123 ÷ 2.0 = 1,561 kWh a year. The difference is 1,833 kWh a year, and the ratio of 2.2 sits inside DOE's "two to three times more efficient." Rated factors are test numbers; a cold room lowers heat pump performance, and the selection guideline notes an indoor unit changes house heating and cooling loads. We left gas out because it needs a price per unit of gas, which we did not assume.

Planning it during rough-in

  • Pick the type and the spot together, near the bathroom and kitchen clusters, before framing.
  • Gas types: frame for the vent path and choose the gas line size for the largest unit you might fit. The selection guideline advises an adequate line for future high-capacity equipment.
  • Electric types: have the 240 V circuit pulled to the spot, and leave a spare for a later swap.
  • Heat pump types: give it open floor area and air volume, a pitched drain path and a spot away from bedrooms.
  • Run the short trunk first and the longer branches in the smallest pipe the code allows, as Building America suggests.
  • Coordinate chases and drain slope with the other trades; see HVAC rough-in for a new home.

Field card: Choosing a Water Heater for a New Home

Pick the type and the spot together, before framing.

  • Heat pump tank: 240 V dedicated circuit, 1,000 cu ft of air per DOE, drain or pump.
  • Resistance tank: 240 V, no flue, simplest to place.
  • Gas tank: gas line, vent and combustion air.
  • Gas tankless: bigger gas line (3/4 in typical), listed vent, condensate if condensing.
  • Wait = pipe volume / flow. Our example: 31 s versus 13 s.
  • Our assumed example: 3,394 kWh versus 1,561 kWh a year.
