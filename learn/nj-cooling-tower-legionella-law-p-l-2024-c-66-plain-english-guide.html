NJ Cooling Tower Legionella Law (P.L. 2024 c.66): Plain-English Guide

New Jersey now requires covered buildings, including those with cooling towers, to have a written Legionella water management program. Here is who is covered and what to confirm.

What the law is

New Jersey Public Law 2024, chapter 66 was approved on September 12, 2024 and took effect right away. Section 6 requires owners and operators of certain buildings to have a written water management program aimed at limiting Legionella, the bacteria behind Legionnaires' disease. The text says the program must be developed within 24 months and put into practice within 27 months. Counting from the approval date, that is about September 2026 and about December 2026. Those dates are a calculation, not wording in the statute, so confirm them with the state. As of this lesson, the first date has passed or is very close.

Which buildings are covered

The list is broad. It includes licensed hospitals with inpatient care, nursing homes and assisted living, correctional facilities with centralized hot water, residential buildings of six or more floors with central water heaters, hotels and similar buildings where one shared heater serves 25 or more units, subsidized or senior housing with a shared heater serving 25 or more units, and any building that health officials link to a Legionnaires' outbreak.

The line that matters most for HVAC people: any building with a cooling tower or evaporative condenser is on the list. So are buildings with spas, pools, decorative fountains, misters or humidifiers. For a tower or evaporative condenser, the text says the program may be limited to that device rather than the whole building. Residential properties with four or fewer dwelling units are exempt.

What a program usually contains

The statute says programs must be consistent with ASHRAE Standard 188 or a comparable national standard. A public summary of that standard lists these parts: a named team, a description of the water systems with flow diagrams and a hazard review, control measures with limits and where they apply, monitoring, corrective actions, verification, and documentation. Whether to test for Legionella, and how to use results, is up to the building manager under that standard. The law itself adds that testing is required for facilities linked to an outbreak or where CDC recommends it, and that records and test results be kept on site for at least five years and handed to the state health department on request. The standard's own text is paywalled and was not read, so treat this list as an outline, not a checklist for compliance.

What owners and techs can do now

Owners with a tower or evaporative condenser should find out whether a written program already exists, who is on the team, and where the records are kept. Techs can help by logging basin and treatment checks the same way every visit, noting dead legs, slime, odors or a long idle period, and reporting them instead of working around them. Those habits fit the cleaning, treatment and inspection topics in the linked lessons below.

The posted notice

After a covered owner complies, the statute calls for a written notice, posted somewhere on the premises that is easy to reach, saying the program is in place.

Penalties and who enforces

Civil penalties run up to $2,000 for a first violation and up to $5,000 for later ones, and up to $10,000 if a violation causes serious injury or death. Examples of violations include not carrying out a program, not reporting a positive test, or not giving the required notice. The Department of Health can assess penalties administratively (the violator has 35 days to ask for a hearing), and the state can also go to court.

What we did and did not check

We read the statute text itself (linked below). The detail about what a program contains comes from the statute's general wording plus a public summary of ASHRAE 188, not from the standard. We could not find the state environmental or health "best practices" guidance the law anticipated, and we found no state registry of cooling towers. We did not check local codes such as municipal property maintenance, fire or health rules. Some news and blog summaries give different dates; the statute text controls.

This is not legal advice. Owners should confirm their obligations with the New Jersey Department of Health and with a qualified water-treatment professional. Techs should treat it as background: your employer or customer owns the program, and you follow its procedures, the chemical maker's instructions and your site safety rules (respirator and chemical PPE when cleaning or handling treatment products).

Field card: NJ Cooling Tower Legionella Law

A cooling tower in New Jersey now means a written plan. Know the dates and confirm the details.

  • Law: P.L. 2024 c.66, approved Sept 12, 2024. Program developed within about 24 months (about Sept 2026), in use within about 27 months (about Dec 2026).
  • Any building with a cooling tower or evaporative condenser is covered; the program may be limited to that device.
  • Residential properties with four or fewer units are exempt.
  • Post the written notice where occupants can see it once the program is in place.
  • Penalties up to $2,000 first, $5,000 later, $10,000 if serious injury or death. NJ Department of Health enforces.
  • Not legal advice: owners confirm with NJ DOH and a water-treatment professional; local codes were not checked.
