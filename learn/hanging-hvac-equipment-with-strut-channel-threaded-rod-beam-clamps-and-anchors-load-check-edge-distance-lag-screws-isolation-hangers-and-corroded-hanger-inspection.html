Hanging HVAC Equipment from Strut, Threaded Rod and Anchors: Load Checks, Beam Clamps, Concrete and Wood

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A hung air handler, fan coil or pipe run is only as safe as its weakest link: the rod, the nut, the clamp, the anchor or the structure behind it. This lesson shows how to add up the load, split it across the rods, and compare it with a labeled allowable load to see how much margin is left.

Check the data sheet and the plans

Check the data sheet and the plans. This page is general education. Allowable loads for rods, clamps and anchors come from the maker of that exact part and from the engineer of record for the building. Where we say "check", we mean read the label, the data sheet or the drawings before you hang anything.

The load path, top to bottom

Think of the hanger as a chain. Weight goes from the unit into a strut or trapeze, up the threaded rods, through a beam clamp or an anchor, and into the building structure. Any link can be the one that fails, so the whole assembly is rated by its weakest part. A strong rod on a weak anchor is still a weak hanger.

Strut channel, nuts and clamps

Strut channel is a slotted steel channel that lets you slide a nut anywhere along its length. Shallow channel suits light trapeze work; deeper or doubled channel carries longer spans and heavier loads. The channel nut, the rod and the strut each have their own labeled limit, and the span between rods changes how much the strut can carry. Use the strut maker's load table for the span you actually have. Nuts and washers on a rod should be snug against the strut, with the rod end showing past the nut so you can see it did not pull through. Pipe and conduit clamps are for the line they are sized to, not for hanging a unit.

Threaded rod sizes and what the stress means

In our experience the common rod sizes for equipment and piping are 3/8, 1/2 and 5/8 inch, with larger sizes for heavy units. A rod carries weight as tension. Engineers call load divided by cross-sectional area "stress," and a materials-testing page from a state university defines engineering stress as load divided by the original area and tensile strength as the peak stress a material can support. A bigger diameter means more area, so more load capacity. Because the threads cut the rod, strength is figured on a smaller thread area, which is one reason you should not guess allowable loads from the nominal diameter. We do not give a rod load table here. Use the rod maker's data sheet, or get the number from the engineer, and treat any figure as "manufacturer data to check."

Keep rods plumb. A rod that is pulled sideways picks up bending the data sheet did not assume. Do not heat, bend or file rod to make it fit; use a longer rod or a different hanger layout instead.

Safety factor in plain terms

A set of class notes from a state university defines factor of safety as actual strength divided by required strength, and says a structure presumably fails if that ratio drops below 1. Allowable load is the strength divided by that factor. The margin you want is the room between the load on the part and its allowable load. Many labeled allowable loads already include a factor built in by the maker, so check the data sheet to see whether the number you read is an ultimate (breaking) load or an allowable load. Never compare a breaking load to your hanging load and call it safe.

Beam clamps

A beam clamp grips the flange of a steel beam so you do not drill the beam. It is rated for a beam flange width and thickness range and a load direction. Check that the flange is in the labeled range, that the clamp sits square and seats fully, and that the clamp's rating covers the rod size and the direction of pull. Many are rated for a straight downward pull only, so a sideways or swinging load needs a different detail from the engineer.

Concrete anchors, in general

A state transportation department field manual describes the common mechanical types. An expansion wedge anchor develops friction when tightening pulls a wedge through a sleeve and presses the sleeve against the concrete; it is tightened to the maker's torque with a calibrated wrench. A drop-in anchor has a tapered plug that is driven in with a setting tool to expand its base, and it works at shallower depths. Screw anchors thread into a drilled hole; follow the maker's hole size, embedment and bit requirements.

  • Clean the hole. The same manual says cleaning is the most common cause of failure, and that mechanical anchors are also sensitive to a poorly cleaned hole. Blow and brush as the maker instructs.
  • Edge distance and spacing. The manual explains that a nearby edge or another loaded anchor overlaps the cone of concrete that resists pull-out and reduces capacity. Keep anchors away from edges and from each other as far as the maker's table requires. Do not set an anchor in a spall or close to a slab edge because it is convenient.
  • Embedment. Reach the depth on the plans or the data sheet. A shorter hole is a weaker anchor.
  • Hit a rebar or a hollow? Stop and ask. Do not cut reinforcement, and do not move an anchor without the engineer.

Wood joists and lag screws

A federal wood research lab's fastening chapter says lag screws should be turned in with a wrench, never driven with a hammer, and that the lead hole for the threaded part is a percentage of the shank diameter: 60 to 75 percent for Douglas-fir and Southern Pine, and a smaller percentage for softer woods. It says threaded penetration of about 7 shank diameters in dense wood and 10 to 12 in less dense wood develops about the screw's full tensile strength. It also says not to use lag screws in end grain, where splitting can develop under side load. Hanging heavy equipment from a joist also adds load to a member that was designed for floor and ceiling loads, so ask whether the joist can carry it, and use blocking or a beam the engineer approves when it cannot.

Vibration isolation hangers

Spring or rubber isolation hangers sit in the rod or between the rod and the unit to keep fan vibration out of the structure. They have a labeled load range, and a hanger loaded outside that range will not isolate properly. Pipe and duct connections to an isolated unit need flexible connectors so the isolation is not bypassed. See Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors for how deflection is checked.

Seismic restraint

Isolation hangers allow movement, so units in seismic areas may need bracing and snubbers. A federal guide on nonstructural earthquake damage shows suspended fans swinging and hitting louver panels in a past earthquake, and says the design force is a percentage of the item's weight, calculated by an engineer. We do not size bracing here. See Seismic and Wind Restraints for HVAC Equipment.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

All of these numbers are our own assumptions for practice, not data for a real product.

  • Assumed dry weight of a suspended air handler: 480 lb.
  • Assumed water allowance for coil water and a full condensate pan: 40 lb.
  • Assumed allowance for strut, nuts, isolators and accessories: 20 lb.
  • Four hanger rods, load shared equally (assumption).
  • Assumed labeled allowable load for the chosen rod and its hardware, from a data sheet we would still need to check: 600 lb per rod.

Step 1, total load. 480 + 40 + 20 = 540 lb.

Step 2, load per rod. 540 / 4 = 135 lb per rod.

Step 3, compare. 600 / 135 = 4.4. The rod's labeled allowable load is about 4.4 times the load on it. The unused part is 600 - 135 = 465 lb, or 465 / 600 = 77 percent of the allowable load.

Step 4, what if the load is not equal? If one end of the unit is heavier and two rods end up carrying most of it, say the worst case is two rods sharing all 540 lb: 540 / 2 = 270 lb each, and 600 / 270 = 2.2. The margin is smaller but still above 1. A unit with an uneven center of gravity needs the maker's rod placement.

Step 5, check the other links. The same 135 lb goes through each beam clamp or anchor, so those also need labeled allowable loads at or above 135 lb in the direction of pull. If the anchor is near an edge, its allowable load may be lower than the table's headline value. The hanger is only as good as the smallest margin in the chain.

Inspecting old hangers for corrosion

Look at old hangers where moisture collects: near leaking coils, drain pans, roof penetrations, washdown areas and damp mechanical rooms. A federal wood research guide on metals notes that corrosion reduces a fastener's diameter and can sharply cut capacity, that moisture drives the rate, and that galvanizing does not guarantee protection in every setting. Signs to report: flaking rust, reduced rod diameter, rust staining at the nut or anchor, bent or stretched threads, loose nuts, cracked or crushed isolators, and a unit or strut that looks lower than it was. If you see section loss, do not paint over it. Support the load, mark the hanger, and tell the lead or the engineer.

Never stand or work under a load you are testing or lifting. Support the unit from below, or use a lift rated for it, while you hang and tighten it.

Duct and pipe runs have their own spacing rules and their own signs of sag; see Duct Hangers and Supports and Pipe Hangers and Supports for Hydronic and Steam Piping.

Field card: Hanging equipment from strut, rod and anchors

Add up the weight, split it, compare with the label, then check every link.

  • Load path: unit, strut, nuts, rods, clamp or anchor, structure. The weakest link rules.
  • Total load = unit + water and condensate + hardware. Divide by the number of rods.
  • Use labeled allowable loads, not breaking loads. Check the data sheet.
  • Concrete anchors: clean the hole, keep edge distance and spacing, reach the depth, torque to the maker's value.
  • Lag screws: lead hole, wrench not hammer, no end grain, and ask if the joist can carry the load.
  • Isolation hangers need the right load range; seismic bracing is an engineer's call.
  • Old hangers: look for rust, section loss and loose nuts, and report them.
