Ohm's Law and Power for Resistive HVAC Heaters: Why 240 Volt Strips Lose Output on 208, Ohm Checks, and Series vs Parallel Elements

Last updated: 2026-11-02

A heat strip, an electric furnace element and a water heater element are all just a length of resistance wire. Two short formulas explain how much heat each one makes, why the same strip makes less heat on a 208 volt building, and what an ohmmeter should read when the element is good or open.

The two formulas you need

Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance: V = I × R. NIST defines the ohm as one volt per ampere and the volt as one watt per ampere, which is the same relationship written as units. Electrical power in watts is voltage times current: P = V × I. Put the first formula into the second and you get two more forms: P = I² × R and P = V² / R.

For a resistive heater, the form that matters most is P = V² / R. The element is a fixed piece of wire, so its resistance does not change when you move it to a different building. Voltage is the thing that changes, and it is squared. Energy.gov notes that electric resistance heating turns all of the electricity it uses into heat at the point of use, so watts in is heat out. This lesson does not repeat the basics of meters and wiring; see Electrical Basics for HVAC for those.

Why a 240 volt heater makes about 75 percent on 208

Because power goes with the square of voltage, the output ratio is (208 / 240)². That is 0.8667 squared, which is 0.751. So a strip rated at 240 volts makes about three quarters of its rated kilowatts when it is fed 208 volts. This is our own arithmetic from the formula, and it assumes the resistance stays the same, which is close enough for field work. It is not a defect: the strip is doing what the formula says. It matters when you size backup heat on a 208 volt commercial or multifamily building. The kilowatt rating on the nameplate is only true at the voltage printed beside it. For sizing and staging, see Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips: kW Sizing, Breakers and Staging.

The current drops too, by the same ratio as the voltage (the element is a fixed resistor), so a 208 volt feed is easier on the breaker even though the building gets less heat. Do not confuse this with a sagging supply. If a nominal 240 volt supply measures low at the terminals while the strip runs, that is a separate problem; see Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits.

Worked example (our own example, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a manufacturer's data. Assumptions: a 5 kW, 240 volt heat strip made of two identical 2.5 kW elements wired in parallel across a single-phase supply; the elements are ideal fixed resistors; the supply is exactly 240 volts or exactly 208 volts at the terminals.

  1. Each element's resistance. R = V² / P = 240² / 2,500 = 57,600 / 2,500 = 23.04 Ω.
  2. Each element's current at 240 V. I = V / R = 240 / 23.04 = 10.42 A. Check: 2,500 W / 240 V = 10.42 A.
  3. Total current at 240 V. Parallel branches add current: 10.42 + 10.42 = 20.83 A. Check: 5,000 W / 240 V = 20.83 A.
  4. Total resistance. Two equal resistors in parallel give half of one: 23.04 / 2 = 11.52 Ω. Check: 240 V / 20.83 A = 11.52 Ω.
  5. Output at 208 V. Each element: 208² / 23.04 = 43,264 / 23.04 = 1,878 W. Both: 3,756 W, or 75.1 percent of 5,000 W.
  6. Current at 208 V. Each element: 208 / 23.04 = 9.03 A. Total: 208 / 11.52 = 18.06 A. Check: 3,756 W / 208 V = 18.06 A.
  7. Ohmmeter across the pair, power off. Both elements good: 11.52 Ω. One element open: the other is the only path, so the meter reads 23.04 Ω. Both open: the meter shows an open line (OL or infinity).

Checking an element with an ohmmeter

An ohmmeter sends a tiny test current of its own and reads the result, so the circuit must be completely dead before you connect it. A meter on a live element can be damaged and can hurt you. First lock out and verify dead (see the safety section below).

A resistance reading is only useful if you compare it to an expected value, and there are three ways to get one. You can calculate it from the nameplate as in the example (R = V² / P). You can compare it to a matching element in the same strip. Or you can use a published value if the maker gives one. A good reading is close to the expected value; an open element reads infinite; a reading much lower than expected can mean a short between turns or to a neighbor.

The cold versus hot caveat. Metals get more resistive as they warm. A university lab handout describes this as a small positive temperature coefficient, modeled as a straight line: R at temperature T equals R at a reference temperature times one plus the coefficient times the temperature change. A university lecture-demonstration page gives a nichrome coil measuring about 15 Ω cold and about 20 Ω at working temperature. So the number on the meter, taken with the strip cold, can be a little lower than the hot value that the nameplate watts imply. Treat the nameplate calculation as a ballpark, not a pass/fail limit, and lean on the open-versus-reading test and on the comparison with a matching element. We do not give a percentage tolerance here, because we did not find a source for one.

Also, an ohm reading does not replace a clamp-on amp reading with the strip running. It tells you the element is intact, not how much heat it makes. The water heater version of this check is in Electric Water Heater Elements and Thermostats.

Series versus parallel: what total resistance tells you

In series, resistances add and the same current flows through each. Two 23.04 Ω elements in series would read 46.08 Ω and, on 240 V, would draw only 5.21 A and make 1,250 W total (our own arithmetic). If one opens, nothing flows and the meter across the pair reads open.

In parallel, as in a heat strip, each element sees full supply voltage and the currents add. For two equal elements, total resistance is half of one. The useful diagnostic follows from that: if the meter reads about one element's value instead of half of it, one branch is open. The reading with one open (23.04 Ω in our example) is exactly double the good reading (11.52 Ω). With three or four elements the pattern is the same: the total is one element divided by the number of working elements.

Practical note: to say which element is open, you usually need to lift a lead on one element so you can read it alone. Take a note or photo of the wiring first, and make sure the element you are measuring is not also connected to a sequencer, a limit or another path that can fool the meter.

Safety: lockout and verify dead

OSHA's lockout/tagout rule (29 CFR 1910.147) sets an order: shut the equipment down, isolate it from every energy source, apply locks or tags to the isolating devices, release or make safe any stored energy, and verify that the equipment is isolated and de-energized before work starts. OSHA's electrical work practices rule (29 CFR 1910.333) adds that a qualified person must test the circuit parts that workers will touch with proper test equipment, and that the test must be able to detect induced voltage or backfeed. Controls such as push buttons, selector switches and interlocks cannot be the only means of isolation. Before power is restored, tools and jumpers must be removed and everyone must be clear. Full procedure for HVAC work is in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

For this job in practice: open and lock the disconnect, test your meter on a known live source, test the strip terminals dead, and only then switch to resistance. A strip fed by more than one circuit needs every disconnect locked.

Why this matters for study

Exam and field questions mix these ideas: they give you volts and kilowatts and ask for amps, or give a meter reading and ask what failed. Work through R = V² / P first, then use the parallel rule. The example above is a template: change the kilowatts, the voltage or the number of elements and rerun the steps.

Field card: Ohm's Law and Power for Resistive HVAC Heaters

Squared voltage, halved parallel resistance, dead before the meter.

  • V = I × R. P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R.
  • Element resistance from nameplate: R = V² / P.
  • Rated at 240 V, run at 208 V: output is (208/240)², about 75 percent.
  • Parallel elements: currents add; two equal elements read half of one.
  • One of two equal elements open: the meter reads double the good total.
  • Cold readings run a bit lower than hot resistance; compare to a matching element.
  • Lock out, verify dead with a tested meter, then switch to ohms.
