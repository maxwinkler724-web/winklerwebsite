Dry Coolers vs Closed-Circuit Fluid Coolers vs Open Cooling Towers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Three machines can throw a building's or a process's heat outdoors: the open cooling tower, the closed-circuit fluid cooler and the dry cooler. They look alike from the roof. They differ in what air temperature they chase, how much water they burn and whether the process water ever touches the weather.

Three machines, one job

Open cooling tower. The warm loop water itself is sprayed over fill and meets moving air. A little evaporates, and that cools the rest. See Cooling Tower Basics for the parts.

Closed-circuit fluid cooler. The process fluid stays inside a sealed coil. Spray water runs over the outside of the coil while a fan pulls air through. Baltimore Aircoil's application guide groups it with the evaporative products, so it gets the same wet-bulb advantage as an open tower.

Dry cooler. A finned coil with fans, and no spray at all. The same guide says heat moves "sensibly" from the fluid to the air, so it depends on dry bulb temperature, and it uses no water. The tradeoff is more coil area, more fan power and a bigger footprint.

Which air temperature does it chase?

An evaporative machine (tower or fluid cooler) can bring the fluid "within a few degrees of the wet bulb temperature," says Baltimore Aircoil. A dry cooler works against dry bulb, which must be well below the fluid temperature. On a humid summer afternoon wet bulb is far under dry bulb, so evaporation wins by a wide margin.

Both gaps are called approach. Baltimore Aircoil's formula sheet defines approach for a tower as leaving water temperature minus ambient wet bulb; ACHR News describes a dry or adiabatic cooler's approach against entering air dry bulb. Range is the other number: entering water minus leaving water. Range comes from the load and the flow. Approach comes from the weather and the machine size.

Why people pay for a closed circuit

An ACHR News comparison says anything the weather carries into an open tower, such as leaves, pollen and dust, can reach the loop. A closed-circuit cooler keeps the process fluid sealed in the coil, so it stays free of atmospheric contaminants. The same article says open towers usually need a separate heat exchanger to isolate a process loop, while a closed cooler usually does not. It also says closed coolers tend to cost more, weigh more and take more space than an open tower of the same capacity. In other words you pay once for a clean loop instead of cleaning heat exchangers later. Ask the maker which tradeoff fits your job.

Winter, glycol and drain-down

ACHR News says open towers often sustain damage from freezing, while a closed cooler can run dry in cold weather and glycol can be added for extra protection. The Guntner adiabatic brochure lists glycols among the fluids its coils handle. For how to choose and test a glycol mix, read Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops. Remember that glycol changes pump and heat-transfer performance, so a dry cooler's selection should be based on the actual fluid. Drain-down procedures and the lowest safe outdoor temperature come from the maker's manual for your unit.

Adiabatic pads: a dry cooler with a boost

Guntner describes pre-cooling pads that wet the air before it reaches a dry finned coil, bringing it toward wet bulb "without applying water to the finned surface." Wet mode is used for high ambient temperatures or higher loads; otherwise the unit runs dry like a normal dry cooler. The brochure claims water use up to 80 percent below evaporative equipment, says water is used only at peak conditions, and says the pads can be removed for dry operation or cleaning. Baltimore Aircoil adds that such systems can switch to dry-only near freezing. These are maker claims, so check them for your site.

Water use in one line

An evaporative machine loses water three ways: evaporation, blowdown and drift. The U.S. Department of Energy's FEMP page says make-up water replaces all three, and calls careful blowdown control the largest water-saving opportunity in tower operation. Cycles of concentration are roughly make-up volume divided by blowdown volume. Many systems run two to four cycles, and six or more may be possible. A dry cooler has none of this, which is why Baltimore Aircoil lists it as a fit where water is extremely limited. For the health side of wet equipment, see Cooling Tower Water Management and Legionella Awareness.

Worked example: a 500 gpm loop

We chose the numbers. The formulas are Baltimore Aircoil's. The loop is 500 gpm of water with a 10 F range. Every step below marked "Our own" is arithmetic we did, not a number a source printed.

  1. Heat rejected. Baltimore Aircoil gives BTUH = flow x range x 500 for water. Our own: 500 x 500 x 10 = 2,500,000 Btu/h.
  2. Tons. The formula sheet we read did not print a tower-ton value we could read cleanly, so we leave tons out and carry Btu/h. Use your maker's ton definition when you read a catalog.
  3. Evaporation. Baltimore Aircoil's rule is evaporation = flow x range x 0.001. Our own: 500 x 10 x 0.001 = 5 gpm. That is 5 / 500 = 1 percent of flow for a 10 F range. A second Baltimore Aircoil rule of 2 gpm per million Btu/h agrees: 2.5 x 2 = 5 gpm. In a day, 5 gpm x 60 x 24 = 7,200 gallons.
  4. Blowdown. Their rule is bleed = evaporation / (cycles - 1). Our own, at 4 cycles: 5 / 3 = 1.67 gpm. Ignoring drift, make-up = 5 + 1.67 = 6.67 gpm. At 3 cycles: bleed 2.5 gpm, make-up 7.5 gpm. At 6 cycles: bleed 1.0 gpm, make-up 6.0 gpm, which is 20 percent less than at 3 cycles, matching the DOE statement that going from three to six cycles cuts make-up by 20 percent.
  5. Approach on two days. The approaches here are assumptions for illustration, not catalog data. Assume a dry cooler selected for a 15 F approach to dry bulb and a fluid cooler selected for a 7 F approach to wet bulb. Our own: dry cooler on a 95 F dry bulb day: leaving fluid = 95 + 15 = 110 F, entering = 110 + 10 = 120 F. Fluid cooler on a 78 F wet bulb day: leaving fluid = 78 + 7 = 85 F, entering = 85 + 10 = 95 F.
  6. Reading it. The evaporative unit delivers fluid 25 F colder (110 - 85) for the same load, at the price of about 5 gpm of evaporation. The dry cooler uses no water but leaves 110 F fluid, which may be too warm for a chiller condenser. A pad system aims the entering air at wet bulb, so its result falls between the two.

Note that the evaporation rule was written for towers. Closed-circuit coolers also evaporate spray water, so treat the figure as a first estimate and use the maker's data.

Maintenance checks by type

  • Fans and drives. All three have them. Lock out before reaching in, and follow Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks for the walk-around.
  • Spray nozzles, fill and basin (open and closed-circuit). Uneven spray leaves dry spots and warm leaving fluid. The basin and treatment program matter on any wet unit.
  • Coils. Dirty fins or scale raise the approach. Clean to the maker's method and take care not to bend fins. Pads, where fitted, can be removed for cleaning according to Guntner.
  • Freeze protection. Follow the maker's drain-down and heater instructions. Never rely on a glycol mix you have not tested.

Field card: Dry Coolers vs Closed-Circuit Fluid Coolers vs Open Cooling Towers

Wet bulb for water, dry bulb for air, and a clean loop costs extra.

  • Open tower and fluid cooler approach wet bulb; a dry cooler approaches dry bulb.
  • Approach = leaving fluid minus the air temperature it chases; range = in minus out.
  • Heat (Btu/h) = gpm x range x 500 for water.
  • Evaporation is about gpm x range x 0.001, about 1 percent of flow per 10 F range.
  • Bleed = evaporation / (cycles - 1).
  • Closed-circuit keeps process fluid sealed; dry coolers use no water but run hotter.
  • Glycol and drain-down details come from the maker's manual.
