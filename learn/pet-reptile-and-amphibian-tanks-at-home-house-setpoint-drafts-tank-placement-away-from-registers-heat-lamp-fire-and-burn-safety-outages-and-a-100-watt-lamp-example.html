Pet Reptile and Amphibian Tanks at Home: Your Thermostat, Drafts, Heat Lamps and Outages

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A dog or a cat makes its own body heat. A lizard, snake, turtle or frog does not. Its body temperature follows its surroundings, so the house you heat and cool becomes part of the animal's heating system. This lesson covers how the house setpoint, drafts and register placement affect a tank, how to keep a heat lamp or heat mat from becoming a fire or burn risk, and what to do when the power or the air conditioning fails.

Why the house matters to these animals

A university ocean-science education page says all living amphibians and reptiles are ectothermic: they cannot hold a steady body temperature from the inside, so they take heat from their surroundings and move between warm and cool spots to manage it. A university museum activity page says the same about reptiles: their body temperature is set by the air, ground and water around them. For a pet, the tank is the whole world. The lamp or mat is the warm spot, and the room is the cool end, the nighttime, and the backup when a heater fails.

That is why a thermostat setback you would never notice can matter. If the house drops several degrees overnight, the tank drops with it, and the heater has to work harder to keep up. We do not give species temperature ranges here. They differ widely, and an animal care professional (an exotic-animal veterinarian or an experienced keeper's group) is the right person to give the numbers for your animal. Write them down, then use the house setpoint and the tank thermometers to stay inside them. This is our own advice, not a sourced rule.

Where the tank goes

Use the room, not just the tank, as your tool. These spots are the usual trouble, and the diagram shows them.

  • Supply registers. Warm air in winter and cold air in summer blow in bursts as the system cycles. A tank in that stream sees swings that the lamp then fights. Move the tank, or redirect or close the register only if a technician says that is safe for the system (see our bird lesson linked below, which makes the same point about cages).
  • Radiators and baseboard heaters. They are very hot at the surface. A federal fire-service page tells households to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from all heat sources, including radiators and space heaters. Plastic cords, curtains and bedding count.
  • Sunny windows. Direct sun through glass can overheat a closed tank quickly, because a tank has little air exchange, even in a cool room. This is our own observation.
  • Outside walls and doors. These are the coldest surfaces in the room in winter, and the draft path in a leaky house. A tank against one loses heat from the back and sides. Find and seal leaks with the steps in our drafty doors and windows lesson.

The best spot is usually a steady interior room, on a sturdy stand, away from all of the above and away from the door where traffic bumps it. Put a thermometer at the warm end and one at the cool end. A university veterinary teaching page says at least two thermometers must be in use to monitor temperatures, and that heating elements can be dangerous if used improperly, and it recommends a smoke alarm. Add an alarm-type thermometer with a probe that sounds a high and a low alert. That is our own suggestion, and it is the cheapest early warning you can have for a heater failure while you are asleep or away.

Heat lamps and heat mats: fire and burn risks

Federal consumer-safety guidance for space heaters is a good starting model for any heat source in a room: keep it clear of anything that can burn, plug it directly into a wall outlet rather than a power strip, and never leave it running unattended in a confined space. A federal fire-service page adds that portable heaters caused only 3 percent of home heating fires from 2017 to 2019 but 41 percent of the fatal ones. A tank lamp is a heater too, so the same habits apply.

  • Secure fixtures. A university extension page on farm heat lamps says to use a lamp with a guard, to hang it with a chain or clamp rather than twine, to keep it away from bedding, and not to use bulbs over 250 watts. It says to plug directly into an outlet and not through an extension cord, and to clean dust and cobwebs off before use. Those are farm rules, and a tank lamp is smaller, but the reasons carry over: loose fixtures fall, and dust and bedding burn. A tank lamp should also sit in a fixture rated for the bulb wattage you use, so check that rating.
  • Keep lamps off bedding and curtains. Our own rule of thumb: a lamp that can touch or tip onto substrate, a towel, a curtain or a cord is a hazard, so mount it so it cannot move and keep a clear gap around it.
  • Never cover a lamp. Heat has to escape. A blanket draped over a lamp or its fixture traps heat, and that is how a fixture overheats. This is our own safety rule.
  • Use a thermostat controller made for the heater. A university veterinary teaching page says under-tank heaters should be used with a thermostat or rheostat so they can follow changing room temperatures. Use a controller rated for the heater's wattage and for its type (a dimming or on/off type), and read its label. The probe goes where the animal's heat is measured, and a separate thermometer checks the controller.
  • Heat mats burn. Another university veterinary page warns that under-tank heat mats may cause thermal burns. Keep animals from lying directly on a hot surface and check surface temperatures with the controller's probe and a thermometer.
  • Smoke alarm. A university veterinary teaching page recommends a smoke alarm near a heated enclosure. Federal consumer-safety guidance says to put smoke alarms on every level and inside each bedroom, and to test them monthly.

Cords and outlets

A federal fire-service page says to check cords for damage and to avoid running heaters on extension cords. Keep cords where water, an animal and a person's foot cannot reach them. A tank with a mister, a water bowl or a leak puts water near plugs, so put plugs and controllers above the tank's water level with a drip loop in the cord. Our own advice: if a plug or cord is warm to the touch, turn the circuit off and have it checked by an electrician. Do not put several heat loads on one power strip.

Winter power outage

When the power drops, the lamp and mat stop, and the house starts cooling. A tank loses heat to the room on every side, and the room loses heat to the outside. Think in layers, from safest to riskiest.

  • Insulate the tank. Our own suggestion: cover the back and sides with a blanket or foam board to slow heat loss, but leave the front visible, leave required air openings clear, and keep covers away from any heater that is running. Never cover a lamp.
  • Warm the room, not just the tank. Close the door to a small interior room, which holds heat better than a big one with outside walls. See our winter outage and carbon monoxide lesson for safe options.
  • Safe warm-up options. Our own idea, to confirm with your animal care professional first: sealed bottles of warm (not hot) water placed outside the animal's reach and checked with a thermometer. Never put a hot object where the animal can touch it.
  • Do not use fuel-burning heaters indoors without proper venting. A federal consumer-safety page says never to burn charcoal inside a home, garage, vehicle or tent, never to use portable fuel-burning camping equipment indoors, never to use a gas range, oven or dryer to heat the home, and never to run a portable generator in or near an enclosed space; opening doors or windows does not prevent lethal carbon monoxide. That same release says to run a generator outdoors at least 20 feet from the house. That page also says to put carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, and that if one sounds you should get outside and call 911. Carbon monoxide can harm the animals too, so none of these shortcuts is worth the risk.

Summer air-conditioning failure

The same house that heats a tank in winter overheats it in summer when the cooling stops. Overheating can be faster than chilling because a lamp adds heat on top of a hot room. Our own plan: if your thermometer alarm trips high, turn off the basking lamp or heat source, move the tank to the coolest, shaded room (away from windows), and open the room to moving air without aiming a fan at the animal. Then call your animal care professional. Do not put the tank in a cold bath or a refrigerator; that is a shock, not a fix. Our drafty doors and windows lesson covers sealing leaks, which also keeps the room steadier.

Worked example (our own): how much heat a 100 watt lamp adds

This is our own example with our own assumptions, not a sourced figure. We want to know whether a basking lamp matters to the heating and cooling of its room.

Assumptions. (1) The lamp draws 100 W and all of that power ends up as heat in the room, because light is absorbed by surfaces and turns to heat. (2) It runs 12 hours a day. (3) One watt equals 3.412 Btu per hour, a standard unit conversion. (4) A small bedroom-sized room has a design heating load of 3,000 Btu per hour on a very cold day, an assumed round number for illustration. (5) A cooling ton is 12,000 Btu per hour.

  1. Heat per hour: 100 W × 3.412 Btu/h per W = 341.2 Btu/h.
  2. Heat per day: 341.2 Btu/h × 12 h = 4,094.4 Btu per day, about 4,100.
  3. Compare with the room's design load while the lamp is on: 341.2 ÷ 3,000 = 0.114, or about 11 percent.
  4. Compare with a full design-load day: 3,000 Btu/h × 24 h = 72,000 Btu. Then 4,094.4 ÷ 72,000 = 0.057, or about 6 percent.
  5. In summer, the extra load on the cooling system is 341.2 ÷ 12,000 = 0.028 ton, about 3 percent of one ton.

What it means. The lamp is a modest part of the room's heat on a cold day and a small extra load in summer. It does not replace house heat, and it does not keep the tank warm when the room is cold, because the lamp warms only a spot. The house setpoint and drafts still set the room temperature, and the lamp still has to work for the animal. Do not raise your lamp wattage to make up for a cold room; ask a professional.

Field card: Reptile and amphibian tanks at home

The house is part of the heater.

  • Reptiles and amphibians take heat from their surroundings, so the room matters.
  • Keep tanks away from supply registers, radiators, sunny windows and outside walls.
  • Keep burnable items at least 3 feet from heat sources; plug heaters directly into a wall outlet.
  • Secure lamps, keep them off bedding and curtains, and never cover a lamp.
  • Use a thermostat controller made for the heater; heat mats can burn.
  • Use two thermometers and a high/low alarm thermometer; add a smoke alarm.
  • Outage: insulate the tank, warm a small room safely, never use fuel-burning heaters or a generator indoors.
  • Ask an animal care professional for your species' temperature ranges.
  • Our example: 100 W × 3.412 = 341.2 Btu/h; 12 h = 4,094 Btu per day.
