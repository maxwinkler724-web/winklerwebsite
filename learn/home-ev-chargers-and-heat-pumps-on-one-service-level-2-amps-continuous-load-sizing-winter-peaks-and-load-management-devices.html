Home EV Chargers and Heat Pumps on One Service: Level 2 Amps, 125 Percent Sizing and Load Management

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump and an electric-vehicle charger are both big 240 volt loads, and the coldest winter evening is when they are most likely to run together. This lesson is about that overlap: how a home charger is sized, how it stacks with the compressor and strip heat, and how load management lets both live on one service. For the general question of whether a house can take a heat pump at all, read Will My Electrical Panel Handle a Heat Pump?; we do not repeat it here. Nothing below is licensing or legal advice. The electrician and the local inspector decide what is allowed at your address.

Level 1 and Level 2 in plain numbers

The U.S. DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center lists Level 1 charging as a standard 120 volt outlet at about 1.9 kW, adding roughly 5 miles of range per hour. It says Level 1 is typically used when only a 120 volt outlet is available, and that a home can cover daily driving this way only if a dedicated branch circuit is near the parking spot. The same center says Level 2 runs at 240 volts for homes, that most residential Level 2 chargers operate at up to 30 amps and 7.2 kW, adding about 25 miles per hour, and that equipment can be larger, up to 40 to 80 amps. It adds that a 30 amp unit needs a dedicated 40 amp circuit to meet Article 625 of the National Electrical Code. A separate DOE multifamily charging guide gives the Level 2 range as 20 to 30 miles per hour.

Notice the pattern: a 30 amp charger sits on a 40 amp circuit. That gap is the next idea.

Why a 48 amp charger needs a 60 amp circuit

A charger can run for hours, which the code treats as a continuous load. The AFDC home charging page says the National Electrical Code treats home charging as a continuous load, and the DOE multifamily guide says wiring size and overcurrent protection must be 125 percent of the charger's continuous rating. Our own arithmetic:

  • 30 A charger x 1.25 = 37.5 A, so the next standard breaker size is 40 A. That matches the AFDC statement.
  • 48 A charger x 1.25 = 60 A breaker and a conductor sized for it.
  • Going the other way, a 40 A circuit supports 40 / 1.25 = 32 A of continuous charging.

The 125 percent is for the branch circuit. The charger itself still draws 48 A while it charges, and that is the number that matters when we add it to everything else on the service. Electricians also use the code load-calculation methods (the slides cited below name sections 220.83 and 220.87) to decide whether a new circuit fits the existing service on paper. Which method applies, and how the charger is counted, is the electrician's call and the inspector's.

How the charger stacks with the heat pump

A heat pump has up to three electrical pieces: the outdoor unit (compressor and fan), the air handler blower, and electric resistance strip heat. DOE notes that electric furnace elements switch on in stages so the home's electrical system is not overloaded. Strips are the big one. Every kilowatt of strip heat at 240 volts is about 4.2 amps (1,000 / 240). A 15 kW bank is 62.5 amps, more than the outdoor unit and blower together. Strip heat comes on during very cold weather, during defrost, and during fast recovery after a setback; see Heat Pump Thermostat Setback, Recovery and Strip Heat.

Now add the winter evening. People arrive home, plug in, and start dinner and laundry. An LBNL speaker in an ENERGY STAR partner-meeting presentation put it bluntly: an EV could easily be the biggest home load. A DOE panel guide adds that breaker ratings usually add up to more than the panel, because loads rarely all run at once. The evening peak is the exception that makes load management worth discussing.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Every figure here is an assumption chosen for practice, not data from a real house. We simplify by treating all loads as 240 volt amps on the service; a real calculation looks at each leg of the service separately.

  • Service: 200 A main.
  • Our own planning limit: 160 A, which is 80 percent of 200 A. DOE's panel guide describes a similar habit, saying electricians recommend load never exceed 80 percent of service capacity.
  • Rest of house on a cold evening (cooking, water heater, lights, dryer): 55 A.
  • Heat pump outdoor unit running: 20 A. Air handler blower: 5 A.
  • Strip heat: 15 kW at 240 V = 15,000 / 240 = 62.5 A.
  • New charger: 48 A output on a 60 A circuit (48 x 1.25 = 60).

Step 1, simple peak sum with everything on: 55 + 20 + 5 + 62.5 + 48 = 190.5 A. That is under 200 A but 30.5 A over our 160 A limit, and it leaves almost nothing for a bad guess in the other loads.

Step 2, add a load-management limit. Assume a current sensor on the service conductors and a controller that lowers the charger's current limit through its control line whenever the total would pass 160 A.

  • Loads that are not the charger, strips on: 55 + 20 + 5 + 62.5 = 142.5 A.
  • Room for the charger: 160 - 142.5 = 17.5 A. Set the charger to 17 A, whole amps.
  • New peak: 142.5 + 17 = 159.5 A, under the 160 A limit.
  • Charger power while throttled: 17 A x 240 V = 4.08 kW, against 48 x 240 = 11.52 kW at full output.

Step 3, what the driver notices. Assume the car needs 40 kWh. At full output that is 40 / 11.52 = 3.5 hours. Throttled the whole time it would be 40 / 4.08 = 9.8 hours. Strips cycle, so in practice the charger returns to full output whenever they are off: with strips off, 55 + 20 + 5 + 48 = 128 A, under the limit. An overnight session still finishes, which is why the idea works. The assumed charger minimum is 8 A (the DOE multifamily guide says typical equipment needs about 8 amps at least); if the room ever fell below that, the controller would pause the charger instead.

Ways to share a service

The LBNL and EPA presentation sorts the options by complexity. A few of them in plain terms:

  • Timers and scheduling. The AFDC notes that smart Level 2 units come with charging timers, and that some utilities offer time-of-use rates. A DOE-hosted simulation study of homes with smart charging found peak-period EV charging was nearly eliminated and the daily peak from EVs dropped 23 percent. Scheduling pushes the charge off the dinner-hour peak, but it does not watch the strips, and the coldest hours are often before dawn.
  • Charger current limit. The LBNL speaker suggested limiting home charging to 7.2 kW, since an overnight session can fully recharge most EVs. At 240 V that is 30 A. That is a limit on charging power; ask the electrician whether the charger you pick can be set that way.
  • Circuit-sharing devices. The presentation describes a hard-wired device like a smart junction box as the least complicated option, for sharing one existing circuit with a new load.
  • Circuit pausing with a sensor. A relay with metering on the mains turns a load off at 80 percent of rated capacity, as the presentation describes it.
  • Energy management or load management systems. The DOE multifamily guide describes a hardware device that cuts off or reduces an EV circuit as the panel nears its limit, and says the code lets an installation oversubscribe capacity if the system includes automatic load management controls. A smart panel is the most flexible and most complicated version.

Whatever device is used, it should be installed by a qualified electrician, and the inspector may want to see how the limit is enforced. A load-management plan that protects the service on paper but is not wired and set correctly protects nothing.

What to tell the electrician before you call

You do not need to do any calculation. A good call covers these items:

  • Service size, from the meter label, the panel label or the main breaker (the DOE panel guide lists these places). Send a photo of the panel with the cover on and the label in view.
  • Heat pump and air handler data plates: model, the minimum circuit ampacity and breaker size, and the strip heat kilowatts.
  • Which vehicle, how many miles you drive on a typical day, and where you park, including the distance to the panel.
  • Whether you want a timer, utility time-of-use rates or load management. Ask which load-management option they would use and why.
  • Gas or electric cooking, dryer, electric water heater and any other big loads, since they are in the same winter-evening sum.
  • Ask who pulls the permit. The AFDC says permits may be required by local building authorities.

The HVAC tech's part is to know the heat pump side: how big the strips are, whether they can be locked out above a set outdoor temperature, and whether a control wire can tell the charger when strips are on. See Running a Heat Pump on a Generator for another case where strip heat has to be managed against a limited supply.

Field card: Home EV charger plus heat pump

Charger breaker is 125 percent of charger amps; the service sees the real amps.

  • Level 2 home chargers run at 240 V; many are 30 A on a 40 A circuit, larger units go to 48 A on 60 A.
  • Branch circuit = charger continuous amps x 1.25.
  • Strip heat is about 4.2 A per kW at 240 V.
  • Add the winter evening: house + compressor + blower + strips + charger.
  • A sensor on the service plus a charger control line can throttle the charger while strips run.
  • Scheduling alone does not watch the strips.
  • The electrician and local inspector decide what is allowed and how it is counted.
