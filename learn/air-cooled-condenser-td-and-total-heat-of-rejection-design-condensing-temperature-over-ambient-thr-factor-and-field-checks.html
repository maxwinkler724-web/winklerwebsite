Air-Cooled Condenser TD and Total Heat of Rejection

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condenser nameplate tells you how much heat it can throw away for each degree the refrigerant runs hotter than the air. That one number, the TD, ties together selection, head pressure and a quick field check. This lesson shows how to use it.

What TD means

Condenser makers define design TD as the condensing temperature minus the ambient (entering air) temperature. It is a temperature difference, so it is the same size in degrees whether you read it on a thermometer or work it out from pressure. The refrigerant has to run warmer than the air or heat will not flow into it, and the bigger the gap, the more heat moves. Because of that, a given coil rejects heat in rough proportion to its TD. Maker catalogs list capacity in MBH (thousands of Btu/h) for each 1 F of TD and in tables at several TDs, such as 10, 15, 20 and 30 F, and the 1 F column is just those tables divided down.

A bigger condenser costs more but runs a smaller TD, which means a lower condensing temperature, less compressor work and a longer-lived system. A smaller condenser runs a bigger TD. That is why makers publish guideline ranges by application. One maker's guide lists roughly 10 to 15 F for low-temperature, 15 to 20 F for medium-temperature, 20 to 25 F for high-temperature and 25 to 30 F for air conditioning. Treat these as starting points; the job's design condition, not a rule of thumb, sets the real value. For the energy side of the trade-off, see Floating Head Pressure Control.

Total heat of rejection

The condenser does not only handle the heat picked up in the evaporator. The compressor adds its own work, and that heat leaves through the condenser too. Maker literature defines total heat of rejection (THR) as the net refrigeration effect plus the heat of compression. Two ways to get it appear in maker catalogs:

  • Compressor maker data. The catalogs say to use the compressor maker's heat rejection value whenever you can get it.
  • A THR factor. THR equals compressor capacity times a factor from a table, indexed by evaporating and condensing temperature. For open-drive compressors the catalogs also give THR as capacity plus 2545 times brake horsepower, and for suction-cooled (hermetic-type) compressors as capacity plus 3413 times kW. Those two constants are just the Btu/h in one horsepower and one kilowatt.

The factors run above 1 and rise as evaporating temperature drops, because the compressor works harder. One maker catalog gives 1.24 for a suction-cooled compressor at 0 F evaporating and 90 F condensing. Another maker's selection example uses 1.48 for suction-cooled at minus 10 F evaporating and 105 F condensing, and a trade-press selection article uses 1.55 for a low-temperature R-404A system with a semi-hermetic compressor at minus 20 F evaporating. One maker note says suction-cooled hermetic compressors carry higher factors than open drives, because the motor heat ends up in the refrigerant.

Altitude

Thin air carries less heat per cubic foot, so a condenser rated at sea level loses capacity at elevation. Two maker catalogs handle this by raising the THR you select for: about 1.02 at 1,000 ft, 1.05 at 2,000 ft and 1.17 at 7,000 ft in one, with similar values in the other. A third maker note instead cuts condenser capacity, with factors of 0.94 at 2,000 ft, 0.88 at 4,000 ft and 0.81 at 6,000 ft. Both ideas say the same thing: at elevation the same coil runs a larger TD unless you pick a bigger one.

Worked example (ours)

These numbers are our own and the factor is an assumption.

  1. Evaporator load: 60,000 Btu/h.
  2. Assumed THR factor: 1.35.
  3. THR = 60,000 x 1.35 = 81,000 Btu/h. The extra 21,000 Btu/h is compressor heat.
  4. The condenser we selected is rated at 5,400 Btu/h per 1 F of TD.
  5. Design TD = 81,000 / 5,400 = 15 F. At 95 F air the design condensing temperature is 95 + 15 = 110 F.

Now the field reading: 95 F entering air and a head pressure that converts to 125 F saturated. Actual TD = 125 − 95 = 30 F, twice the design value. Since capacity is about proportional to TD, the coil is only moving 81,000 / 30 = 2,700 Btu/h per F, half of its 5,400 rating, or the system is making more heat than we assumed. Something is wrong, and 15 F of extra condensing temperature costs compressor power and capacity.

Field check, step by step

  1. Measure entering air. Put the thermometer in the air drawn into the coil, in shade, not touching metal and not in the fan's discharge. The reading that matters is what the coil actually breathes.
  2. Read head pressure at the compressor discharge service port or the receiver, with a calibrated gauge.
  3. Convert to saturation with the pressure-temperature chart for the refrigerant in the system. Note that blends have a glide, so use your chart's convention and stay consistent.
  4. Subtract entering air from condensing temperature. Compare with the design TD on the plan or nameplate.
  5. Judge the load. A TD below design at part load is normal; a TD above design at full load is the problem.

If TD is too high

  • Dirty or blocked coil. Less air gets through, so the refrigerant must run hotter to push the same heat. Clean it and check again. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Recirculation. If hot discharge air loops back to the intake, the coil sees warmer air than your thermometer found. A maker installation manual says to site units so air circulates freely and is not recirculated, away from walls and overhead obstructions, and with a gap of at least the width of the largest unit between condensers side by side. A building-engineering journal article on split-system units gives clearances of 36 in. from walls and an unrestricted 100 in. above on larger units, and notes that any restriction cuts fan airflow and capacity.
  • Hot roof air. Dark roofing and equipment exhaust warm the air over the coil. Measure at the coil intake, not at the roof hatch, and compare with the weather-station value.
  • Overcharge or non-condensables. Excess refrigerant backs up in the condenser and reduces the active surface, and air in the system adds pressure without adding heat transfer. Check subcooling and the standing pressure; see Superheat and Subcooling Basics and Air and Non-Condensables.
  • Failed fan or wrong fan rotation. Count the running fans and check direction.

If TD is too low in winter, the head-pressure controls may be holding the setpoint; see Low Ambient Cooling Head Pressure Control.

Field card: Air-Cooled Condenser TD

TD = condensing temperature minus entering air.

  • THR = evaporator load plus compressor heat (load times a THR factor, or compressor maker data).
  • Design TD = THR divided by condenser rating per 1 F.
  • Maker guideline ranges: about 10 to 15 F low temp, 15 to 20 F medium, 25 to 30 F air conditioning.
  • Measure entering air at the coil, read head pressure, convert to saturation, subtract.
  • TD well above design at full load: dirt, recirculation, hot air, overcharge, air in the system or a fan problem.
  • Elevation lowers capacity; a 2,000 ft site needs about 5 percent more condenser.
