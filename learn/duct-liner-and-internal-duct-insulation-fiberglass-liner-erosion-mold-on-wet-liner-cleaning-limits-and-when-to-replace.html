Duct Liner and Internal Duct Insulation: Erosion, Wet Liner and When to Replace

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a commercial air handler's supply trunk and you may find a fuzzy, fiberglass surface inside the sheet metal. That is duct liner. It quiets the system and insulates the duct, but it is porous, so it holds dirt and water in a way bare metal does not. This lesson covers what liner does, how fast air can move over it, and why wet or moldy liner is replaced, not cleaned.

Why ducts get lined

Liner does two jobs at once. It absorbs fan and air noise as sound travels down the duct, and it slows heat gain or loss through the duct wall. An ASHRAE Journal column on duct lining (May 2014) says catalog room sound levels for terminal units usually assume about 5 ft of lined duct downstream. It also describes one project where patient rooms measured 51 NC without insulated flex duct and 38 NC after adding it. For the NC rating idea, see HVAC noise in commercial buildings.

The tradeoff is moisture. The same ASHRAE column notes that mold needs dirt, darkness and dampness, and that dirt can collect in liner, especially when filters are changed rarely. It also says bare sheet metal can grow mold. EPA's duct page notes that experts disagree on whether fiberglass is more prone to growth than bare metal.

Air velocity and erosion

Liner has a speed limit. The trade association's liner design guide (a fibrous glass industry standard) says liner is designed for systems with air velocities not exceeding 6,000 feet per minute, or a lower limit if the maker rates it lower. It requires the airstream surface to pass the UL 181 erosion test, and it gives no separate numeric erosion limit. The ASHRAE column adds that ASHRAE 62.1-2013 requires airstream surfaces to resist erosion, and cites studies saying loose fibers typically release at initial startup, not over time.

The guide also changes the installation as velocity rises. Fasteners go closer together between 2,501 and 6,000 fpm (across the width, 6 in. on center instead of 12 in.; along the length, 16 in. instead of 18 in.). Upstream edges need a metal nosing above 4,000 fpm, and a nosing is also required where lined duct follows unlined duct. Adhesive must cover at least 90 percent of the liner contact area, and any transverse edge without a nosing gets coated.

Worked example: free area and velocity

The air does not travel through the sheet metal dimensions. It travels through the free area, which is the duct size minus the liner on every side. Liner takes thickness off both sides of each dimension.

Assumptions (our own, for practice): a 12 in. by 10 in. sheet metal branch carrying 1,500 cfm. We compare 1 in. liner with 2 in. liner against the 6,000 fpm limit and the 4,000 fpm nosing trigger from the design guide.

  1. Unlined, for reference. Area = 12 x 10 = 120 in.² = 120 ÷ 144 = 0.833 ft². Velocity = 1,500 ÷ 0.833 = 1,800 fpm.
  2. 1 in. liner. Free size = (12 - 2) by (10 - 2) = 10 by 8 in. = 80 in.² = 0.556 ft². Velocity = 1,500 ÷ 0.556 = 2,700 fpm. That is 45 percent of 6,000 and under 4,000, but above 2,500, so the tighter fastener row applies.
  3. 2 in. liner. Free size = (12 - 4) by (10 - 4) = 8 by 6 in. = 48 in.² = 0.333 ft². Velocity = 1,500 ÷ 0.333 = 4,500 fpm. Still under 6,000 (75 percent), but now over 4,000, so upstream edges need nosing.

Thicker liner for sound can push a modest duct into a much faster one without anyone changing the fan, so size from the free area and check the maker's own rated limit, which may be lower than 6,000 fpm.

Wet or moldy liner: replace it

EPA says insulation that gets wet or moldy cannot be effectively cleaned and should be removed and replaced. Its duct page adds that once fiberglass liner is contaminated with mold, cleaning is not sufficient to prevent regrowth, and that no products are registered as biocides for fiberglass duct board or lined ducts. The liner guide agrees: wet fibrous glass is very hard to dry under normal operating conditions, so affected liner is replaced, and only after the water source is found and eliminated. Otherwise the new liner gets wet too.

EPA's mold remediation guide for commercial buildings lists porous acoustic liners inside ductwork, and condensate drain pans in air handlers, as places where hidden mold can sit, and says not to run an HVAC system you know or suspect is contaminated. NIOSH notes that dampness from hidden water sources is hard to spot and that fixing the water source is more effective than air sampling. It also notes hypersensitivity pneumonitis in workers in buildings with contaminated ductwork and filters.

Cleaning limits for lined duct

When liner is dry and sound, cleaning is still restricted. The trade association's cleaning practice for fibrous glass systems allows contact vacuuming (with HEPA equipment if the vacuum exhausts into occupied space), air washing, and power brushing with bristles that are not too stiff, with the brush kept moving so it does not gouge the surface. Air washing is described as most effective in ducts no larger than 24 by 24 in. Steam and water-based cleaning are not to be used on insulated duct, because the insulation cannot be kept dry. EPA says the same, and says sealants should never go on wet liner to cover active growth. The practice also does not recommend sanitizing or sealing agents on the airstream surface, noting they can change acoustic performance and the listed surface burning rating.

For when cleaning is worth it at all, see commercial duct and air handler cleaning and should you get your ducts cleaned.

Sealing torn edges and access cuts

For rips and tears on the airstream surface, the cleaning practice says to coat the area with approved adhesive or coating, or replace the liner. Cut or flap edges get coated with adhesive that meets ASTM C 916. When a technician cuts an access opening, the practice closes it with a sheet metal patch overlapping the opening by about 1/2 in. on all sides, sealed and screwed about 8 in. on center, with screws that must not penetrate the liner. Patch sealants must be rated for the system's pressure and temperature.

External wrap, double wall and liner

Many owners now avoid liner. The University of Pennsylvania design guide says acoustical duct lining in any part of the duct system is prohibited and ducts needing insulation are insulated externally. Northwestern University's guideline lists lined ductwork as not allowed and permits only double-wall duct with fibrous glass between an inner and outer wall, with an antimicrobial coating and film over the insulation. The University of Florida standard does not allow internally lined duct for normal installation, allows an exception only with written approval and a metal liner over the insulation (which may be perforated for noise), and says existing lined duct is removed in renovations.

  • Liner: absorbs sound and insulates in one layer, but it is porous and hard to clean or dry.
  • External wrap: leaves the airstream surface bare metal. The University of Florida standard calls for a vapor barrier on exterior insulation. See duct insulation R-values; DOE's building science page cites a minimum of R-8 for ducts in unconditioned spaces such as attics.
  • Double wall: a perforated inner wall protects the insulation. Northwestern's guideline spells out the insulation conductivity, spacers that do not compress it, and the coating and film.

Inspecting near coils and humidifiers

The liner guide says to protect liner with a sheet metal sleeve and a drip pan next to evaporative coolers, humidifiers, cooling coils and outside air intakes, and that liner is not recommended where it may contact liquid water unless protected. It also says liner is not recommended immediately next to high-temperature heating coils without radiation protection. During a visit, check:

  • The first lined section after a cooling coil: stains, sag, a musty smell, or liner that feels damp.
  • Drain pans and traps. The guide says to inspect condensate drains.
  • Downstream of a humidifier: wet liner downstream suggests moisture is not being absorbed before it reaches the liner. See commercial steam humidifiers.
  • Loose or eroded edges, especially upstream edges and the first section after the fan.

Field card: Duct Liner and Internal Duct Insulation

Free area, not sheet metal size, sets the velocity.

  • Liner quiets the duct and insulates it, but it is porous.
  • Design limit: 6,000 fpm, or the maker's lower rating. Nosing on upstream edges above 4,000 fpm.
  • Free size = metal size minus 2 x liner thickness on each side. Velocity = cfm divided by free area in ft².
  • Wet, moldy or smelly liner: find the water, then replace the liner. Do not wet clean or seal over it.
  • Dry liner: HEPA contact vacuum, air wash or a moving soft brush only.
  • Rips and tears: coat with approved adhesive or replace. Access patches: screws must not enter the liner.
  • Check the first lined section after coils and humidifiers, and the drain pans.
