Water Heater Backdrafting: How to Check the Draft Hood and Spill Switch

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "water heater backdrafting," here is the plain version: an old-style gas water heater lets its exhaust gas climb out on its own, and anything that fights that climb can push the gas back into the room. This lesson covers what the draft hood does, how to check it, what a thermal switch can and cannot do, and why replacing a furnace can leave a water heater in trouble.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on your house. Combustion safety work follows the water heater maker's instructions, the fuel gas code where you live, and a trained tester. Our sources are manufacturer manuals, U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) training material, the EPA and trade articles. We did not read the current ANSI/BPI-1200 standard, so we do not print its limits as current rules.

The atmospheric water heater and its draft hood

An atmospheric (natural draft) tank heater has no fan on the vent. Hot flue gas rises through a draft hood on top of the tank, into a vent connector and up a vent or chimney. The hood has an open gap, called the relief opening, that lets room air mix in and keeps the burner from being yanked around by gusts. In the vent categories lesson, this is a Category I appliance: Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained. A structural-inspection writer notes that gravity venting "isn't a very reliable draft method," and that a cold vent may backdraft for a minute or two until it warms up.

Two manuals tell you not to mess with the hood. Rheem's Use and Care manual says the heater must be installed with the factory draft hood in place and that the vent connector must be the same size as the hood or larger. A. O. Smith's guide for its FVIR gas models says not to alter the draft hood in any way.

What backdrafting is and why it matters

Backdrafting, also called spillage, means flue gas comes out of the relief opening into the room instead of going up the vent. The EPA's carbon monoxide page lists back-drafting from furnaces and gas water heaters as a source of CO indoors and links vent problems to flues that are improperly sized, blocked, disconnected or leaking. A. O. Smith's guide warns that flow reversal of flue gases may raise carbon monoxide inside the dwelling. CO has no smell, so read Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do and keep working alarms near sleeping areas, as the EPA says the Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends.

Why the draft fails

Competing exhaust. Fans, a dryer and a range hood pull air out of a tight house. An ACHR News article explains that when the home's pressure drops enough, it can overcome the natural draft and flue gases spill into the living space. A weak or oversized vent. A cold, wide chimney does not pull well. Blocked or leaking vents, as the EPA notes.

A DOE-funded field study of 11 homes found only two with prolonged, excessive spillage, and those were the only two with venting out of compliance with the National Fuel Gas Code. Exhaust fan use, outdoor temperature and depressurization were statistically significant predictors of spillage. So fix the vent first. For hood sizes and make-up air, see Kitchen Range Hoods at Home.

The orphaned water heater

When a furnace or boiler is replaced by a condensing model with its own plastic vent, the water heater may be left alone in a chimney sized for two appliances. An inspector association article says the flue was sized for the larger appliance, the remaining exhaust does not carry enough heat to draft well, CO can spill into the home, and water vapor can condense and damage the flue. Its fixes are a properly sized flue, a metal liner sized for the water heater, or replacing the heater. The Trane manual quoted in our chimney liner lesson also calls for the spillage check after such a change. Details: Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat: When You Need One.

The simple spillage check

Rheem's manual says to test for spillage at the draft hood relief opening after 5 minutes of main burner operation, and to repeat the check yearly using a match, candle or smoke. A. O. Smith's guide uses a match at the relief opening after 15 minutes: a steady flame drawn in means proper draft, and if the flame flutters or blows out you should not run the heater until the vent or air supply is corrected. Both are manufacturer checks under the conditions at that moment. Neither passage describes running the exhaust fans during the check. If the flame or smoke shows gas leaving the hood, shut the heater off, open a window and call a qualified tech. Do not hold a flame near the heater if gasoline or other flammable vapors may be nearby.

Worst-case depressurization

A pro repeats the check with the house set up to pull hardest. DOE training material describes this as turning on all exhaust fans and appliances and adjusting doors, then watching the hood. It also measures pressure in the room with the water heater against outdoors. The code duration is 5 minutes; the DOE program in that material used 3 minutes for a water heater. The deck lists limits for an individual (orphan) atmospheric water heater of 2 Pa in a 1996 program and 5 Pa in a Canadian standard and RESNET, with BPI using 5 Pa for a properly sized chimney and 2 Pa if oversized. It also says depressurization above 5 Pa reverses most vents. That deck is dated, so the auditor applies the current standard. The audit lesson shows who does this: What Happens in a Home Energy Audit.

Worked example (invented numbers)

A tight house runs these fans at once: two bath fans (80 and 50 cfm), a 455 cfm range hood (the sized example in the range hood lesson) and a dryer we will assume at 150 cfm.

Add the fans: 80 + 50 + 455 + 150 = 735 cfm.

Compare with a trigger: the ACHR article reports a Canadian 1995 building code rule that make-up air is needed above 75 L/s, about 160 cfm, on chimney-vented homes. 735 / 160 = 4.6, so this fan set is more than four times that line.

Now the pressure test. Baseline in the water heater room: -0.5 Pa. Worst case: -4.0 Pa. Convert to inches of water column using the ACHR pairing 5 Pa = 0.02 in. (so 1 Pa = 0.004 in.): 4.0 x 0.004 = 0.016 in. w.c.

Judge it: 4.0 Pa is under the 5 Pa limit for a properly sized chimney (4.0 < 5), but over the 2 Pa limit for an oversized one (4.0 > 2). If this heater sits alone in an oversized chimney, it fails, and the DOE material lists a spill switch, a spill alarm with a CO alarm, or a power or direct vent upgrade as options at 2 to 5 Pa.

Thermal switches and "spill switches"

Many tank heaters have a resettable thermal switch. Bradford White's service manual says its switch shuts down the pilot and main burner and also deactivates them in the event of restricted airflow, and lists insufficient combustion air as a cause of tripping. A. O. Smith's guide says its switch trips for a blocked vent or insufficient combustion air, tells you not to disable it, and says to find and fix the cause before resetting. Rheem's manual describes a combustion shut-off device that reacts to flammable vapors or blocked combustion air openings.

What none of those pages says is that the switch is a guaranteed spillage detector for fan-driven backdrafting. Treat it as a safety net for blockage and heat, not a replacement for the test. Never bypass or jumper it. A tripped switch means something is wrong. The DOE material also lists a spill switch as an option for a water heater that fails at 2 to 5 Pa, but we could not confirm which products or models that means, so ask the maker.

What owners do and what pros do

  • Owners: keep working CO alarms, keep the hood and vent in place, leave air openings clear, do the manufacturer's yearly spillage check under normal conditions, and call for service if a switch keeps tripping or the heater sits in a chimney after a furnace swap. The audit lesson advises skipping home-made depressurization tests when gas appliances are present.
  • Pros: inspect the vent and chimney, do worst-case testing with a gauge, measure flue CO, size the vent or liner, and recommend a power vent or direct vent replacement if the vent cannot be fixed.

Field card: Water Heater Backdrafting

Gas should go up, not out at the hood.

  • Atmospheric heaters rely on a draft hood and a warm, correctly sized vent.
  • Spillage at the hood can bring CO into the home; have CO alarms near sleeping areas.
  • Manufacturer check: spillage test at the relief opening after 5 minutes (Rheem) or 15 minutes (A. O. Smith).
  • Fans, dryers and tight houses can pull the draft backward; pros test worst case.
  • Orphaned after a furnace swap? Ask about liner sizing or a new heater.
  • Thermal switches trip for blocked vents and air; never bypass one.
  • Example: 80 + 50 + 455 + 150 = 735 cfm; 4.0 Pa fails a 2 Pa limit.
