Internal Heat Gains in Cooling Loads: People, Lights, Plug Loads and a Meeting Room Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Walk into a full meeting room on a mild day and it feels warm, even though the weather outside is fine. The heat is coming from inside: bodies, lights and electronics. This lesson shows how techs size those internal gains in Btu/h, why they dominate offices and classrooms, and how to use the numbers when a room will not cool.

What counts as an internal gain

An internal heat gain is any heat released inside the conditioned space. The main ones are people, lights, plug loads (computers, displays, chargers, printers) and appliances. Walls, roofs and windows are covered elsewhere; see the lesson on window U-factor and solar heat gain and the one on infiltration and ventilation. Internal gains are separate from those, and they add on top. A university load-calculation handout says internal sources add to the cooling load and that leaving them out underestimates the capacity needed.

Each gain also has a type. Sensible heat raises air temperature. Latent heat is moisture that the coil has to condense. The lesson on sensible heat ratio explains why that split matters for dehumidification. Lights and equipment are almost entirely sensible. People are both.

People: the gain that changes with activity

A university load-calculation handout publishes a table of heat from adults for a 78 degree F room. We read three rows from it (the sensible and latent columns add up to the adjusted total in each row, which is how we checked we had the columns lined up):

ActivitySensible, Btu/hLatent, Btu/hAdjusted total, Btu/h
Seated at rest210140350
Seated, light work (typing)255255510
Heavy work, lifting, athletics6351,1651,800

Two points stand out. First, sensible heat rises slowly with effort while latent heat climbs fast, because harder work means more sweat and breath. The same handout says so directly. Second, it notes that at an 80 degree F room, total heat stays the same but sensible drops by about 8 percent and latent rises to match. A warmer room pushes more of each person into moisture.

Residential references use a single round figure for a seated, lightly active adult. A university energy-research report on residential air conditioning uses 230 Btu/h sensible and 190 Btu/h latent per person, and a national-lab home energy model document (plain text, not linked) lists the same 230 and 190. For a deeper look at the body itself, see how the body exchanges heat.

Lights: watts times 3.412

Every watt of electricity a light uses ends up as heat in the room, sooner or later. One watt is 3.412 Btu/h, and a kilowatt-hour is 3,412 Btu, the same constant the university residential report uses to convert kWh. The load-calculation handout writes the lighting gain as 3.41 times watts, then multiplies by use and special-allowance factors. For fluorescent fixtures that allowance covers ballast loss, and the handout suggests 1.20 for general rapid-start 40 W fixtures.

That is why an LED retrofit helps the cooling load. A federal energy agency page on LED lighting (plain text, not linked) says incandescent bulbs release 90 percent of their energy as heat and that residential LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy. The page says LEDs emit very little heat. Be careful with that phrase: the heat that remains is still your gain, equal to the new wattage times 3.412. Fewer watts means fewer Btu/h. Our own example: if a room had 1,200 W of old lighting and now has 600 W of LED, the lighting gain falls by 600 x 3.412 = 2,047 Btu/h. Heating-season benefit shrinks by the same amount, since that heat was helping in winter.

Plug loads, equipment and kitchen gains

Treat equipment the same way: measured or nameplate watts times 3.412, adjusted for how much of the rating it really draws. The handout's equipment method multiplies input heat by a usage factor, and its sample table lists items such as a coffee maker with both a sensible and a latent figure, since hot liquids give off moisture. A national-lab home model document (plain text, not linked) counts small appliances as 100 percent internal gain when they sit indoors. Kitchens are the extreme case. Cooking gear gives off heat and steam, and a hood removes only part of it, so a small cafe or break room kitchen line needs the appliance list, not a rule of thumb. We do not give appliance Btu/h figures here because published values vary by model and by how the table was built; use the nameplate and the hood's actual capture.

Diversity: not everything is on at once

If you add every rating at once you will oversize. Diversity is the fraction actually running at the peak hour. The same handout lists factors by building type. For offices it gives 0.70 to 0.85 for lights and 0.75 to 0.90 for people. For apartments and hotels it gives 0.30 to 0.50 for lights and 0.40 to 0.60 for people. Treat those as general ranges. A meeting room that is booked solid all day has high diversity; a cafeteria used for one hour has low diversity for most of the day but a sharp peak.

Why offices and classrooms are different from homes

The university residential report assumes about three people at peak and 2.4 on average in a house. Our arithmetic: 3 x 230 = 690 Btu/h sensible at peak, about a third of a ton from people. A 20-person room at the same per-person value is 4,600 Btu/h sensible from bodies alone, with the same area that might be only a few hundred square feet. Dense rooms are small in envelope but heavy in internal load, so they can need cooling when it is cool outside. Homes spread a few people over many square feet, and their cooling is mostly driven by walls, roofs, windows and air leakage. The handout puts it plainly: in internally loaded buildings the cooling load comes mainly from internal sources. See also the lesson on call centers and dense offices.

Worked example (our own): a 20-person meeting room

Our own example, with stated assumptions. The room holds 20 adults seated and doing light work. We use the handout's 255 Btu/h sensible and 255 Btu/h latent per person. Lighting is 600 W of LED. Equipment (displays, laptops, a projector) is 1,000 W. We assume everything runs at once and ignore envelope, solar and ventilation loads, which would be added separately.

  1. People sensible: 20 x 255 = 5,100 Btu/h.
  2. People latent: 20 x 255 = 5,100 Btu/h.
  3. Lights: 600 W x 3.412 = 2,047.2, about 2,047 Btu/h sensible.
  4. Equipment: 1,000 W x 3.412 = 3,412 Btu/h sensible.
  5. Sensible total: 5,100 + 2,047 + 3,412 = 10,559 Btu/h.
  6. Total: 10,559 + 5,100 = 15,659 Btu/h.
  7. Tons: 15,659 / 12,000 = 1.305, about 1.3 tons.

Check: the sensible share is 10,559 / 15,659 = 0.67, in line with the roughly 50 percent sensible share for people alone plus fully sensible lights and equipment. About 5,100 of the 15,659 Btu/h is latent, so about one third of the coil's work is moisture. At a 20 degree F supply-to-room temperature difference, our own arithmetic with the usual 1.08 factor for standard air gives 10,559 / (1.08 x 20) = about 489 cfm to carry the sensible part.

Sanity-checking a room that will not cool

  • Count heads and hours. Compare actual occupancy to what the system was sized for. Multiply by the sensible and latent figures above.
  • Walk the room for watts. Add up lights and plug loads, including space heaters, extra monitors and charging carts. Multiply by 3.412.
  • Compare to capacity. If the internal gain alone is near the unit's rated tons, there is nothing left for the walls and windows. The system is undersized for the use, not necessarily broken.
  • Check airflow and temperature drop. Use the 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference shortcut to see whether the air delivered can carry the load.
  • Check humidity. A room that is cold but clammy points to a latent problem. Crowds add moisture, and low airflow or short cycling hurts removal.
  • Look at the schedule. A room that handles a normal day but fails in a full meeting is a peak and diversity problem.

Follow manufacturer instructions and site procedures for any equipment work.

Field card: Internal heat gains

Watts times 3.412, heads times sensible plus latent.

  • Lights and equipment: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h, all sensible.
  • Seated, light work (university table, 78 F): 255 sensible, 255 latent per person.
  • Residential references: 230 sensible, 190 latent per person.
  • LED retrofit: lighting gain falls with the watts removed.
  • Diversity: not everything runs at once; do not stack every rating.
  • Tons = Btu/h / 12,000.
  • Dense rooms are internally loaded; homes mostly are not.
