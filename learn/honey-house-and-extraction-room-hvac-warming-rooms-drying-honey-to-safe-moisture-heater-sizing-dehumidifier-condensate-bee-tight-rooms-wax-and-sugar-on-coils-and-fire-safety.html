Honey House HVAC: Warming Rooms, Drying Honey and Safe Heaters

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A honey house is a small food plant with two opposite jobs. Before extracting, the supers need to be warm so the honey flows. After extracting, the honey sometimes needs drying so it does not ferment in the jar. Both jobs are HVAC problems: a heater, a dehumidifier, a tight room and a clean one.

Two rooms, two goals

Many small operations use one room for both jobs, but it helps to think of them separately. A university master-beekeeper handout says supers can be kept for 1 to 2 days in a warm room at 90°F, and a university extension guide says extracting at 80 to 90°F lets the honey spin out of the comb quickly. For the drying side, the same master-beekeeper handout says to keep humidity below 50 percent and use a dehumidifier. A state agriculture guide adds a ceiling on the other side: it says honey must not be exposed to temperatures above 104°F during extracting, processing, bottling or storage. So the warm room has a band, roughly 80 to 90°F, and an upper limit that a thermostat has to respect.

Why moisture matters: the fermentation limit

Bees cap the cells when the honey is ready. A university extension article says that is usually below 18.6 percent water, and a state agriculture guide uses 18.6 percent or less in capped frames as the check before extracting. A federal agriculture handbook (reposted by a college) gives a finer picture: honey under 17.1 percent water will not ferment in a year regardless of yeast count, honey between 17.1 and 18 percent is safe for a year if it carries about 1,000 yeast spores per gram or fewer, and above 19 percent it can be expected to ferment even with one spore per gram. The same handbook says honey starts absorbing moisture from the air when it is stored above 60 percent relative humidity, which is why an open pail in a damp room can drift the wrong way. For this lesson we use 18.6 percent as the upper limit and treat anything lower as extra margin.

Sizing the warming-room heater

The sources give temperatures, not heater sizes, so this part is our own method. A heater has to replace what leaks out through the walls and door. Take the room's heat loss per degree (we call it UA, in Btu/h per °F), multiply by the gap between the target and the outdoor air, then add margin for the cold supers and the door opening.

Our own example, with assumed inputs. Assume a small insulated warming room with a heat loss of 60 Btu/h per °F, a target of 90°F and a winter outdoor temperature of 40°F. The gap is 90 − 40 = 50°F. Loss = 60 × 50 = 3,000 Btu/h. At 3.412 Btu/h per watt, that is about 880 W. We would round up for the supers and door openings, but not by a lot, because an oversized heater cycles hard and overshoots toward the 104°F limit. A real room needs its own loss estimate, and the 60 is only an assumption.

Safe heater types

  • Thermostat-controlled electric heat is the simple choice: no combustion air, no flue, no flame. Add a separate high-limit control so a stuck thermostat cannot cook the honey.
  • Gas or oil-fired heaters need combustion air and venting to the outside. See Garage and Workshop Heaters. Beeswax residue and sugar dust are combustible, so keep a gas heater clear of the work area and never use an open flame near wax.
  • Unvented fuel-burning heaters add water vapor and combustion gases to the room. That works against drying, and a federal consumer product safety news release warns about carbon monoxide from fuel-burning portable heaters.

Fire safety around heaters

The same federal news release says to keep all sides of a portable heater at least 3 feet from items that can catch fire, to use a wall outlet and never a power strip, and not to run cords under rugs. Apply that to supers, cardboard, towels and wax. A university guide gives beeswax a melting point of 143 to 145°F, so warm-room air will not melt it, but a heater element or cooker can. Stack supers on a rack, away from the heater, with space for air to move. Keep an extinguisher near the door; see Electric Space Heaters for the homeowner view.

Dehumidifier sizing, with a worked example

Our own example, with assumed inputs. Assume 1,000 lb of honey measured at 21.0 percent water that we want at the 18.6 percent limit, over 3 days, and a dehumidifier that removes about 1.04 lb of water per pint.

  1. Solids stay put. Solids = 1,000 × (1 − 0.210) = 790 lb.
  2. Final honey weight = 790 ÷ (1 − 0.186) = 790 ÷ 0.814 = about 970.5 lb.
  3. Water removed = 1,000 − 970.5 = about 29.5 lb.
  4. Pints = 29.5 ÷ 1.04 = about 28.4 pints in total.
  5. Per day over 3 days = 28.4 ÷ 3 = about 9.5 pints per day.

That is less than 1.2 gallons per day at 8 pints per gallon. In practice the honey gives up water slowly, from the surface, so a unit rated well above 9.5 pints per day finishes faster. Ratings assume warm, humid air, so a cool room will deliver less. Drying honey that is far above the limit is a poor plan; the easier fix is to leave frames on the hive until they are capped. For other methods see Desiccant Dehumidifiers and Basement Dehumidifier Sizing.

Condensate

That 28 pints turns into liquid water that has to go somewhere. Pipe it to a floor drain or a condensate pump rather than a bucket that a worker has to remember to empty. Keep the hose and drain away from honey containers and food-contact surfaces, and slope the line so water cannot stand in it.

Keeping the room clean and bee-tight

A state agriculture guide says the honey house should be bee-proof so no bees or wasps can enter from outside, that door sweeps should go under all doors, and that screened windows with a small bee escape are recommended in the hot room and extraction room. That matters to HVAC work: every intake, exhaust and dehumidifier duct needs a screen or damper that bees cannot pass, and a hole cut for a drain or line set needs sealing. The same guide wants interior floors, walls, ceilings and fixtures of suitable construction, clean and well maintained, with the room cleaned and mopped at the start and end of each extraction day. Wherever we route ducts, they should not shed dust or drip onto open honey.

Wax and sugar on coils and filters

This part is our own field reasoning, not a sourced figure. Wax particles and sticky honey mist collect on filters and on the cold dehumidifier coil, which cuts airflow and drying. A dirty, iced coil removes less water. Wash or replace the filter on a set schedule, and clean the coil only when the unit is unplugged, using a method the maker allows. A coil that sweats in a room with open honey is a contamination risk, so keep its drip pan clean and draining.

Food-safety cleanliness at a general level

The same state guide says working surfaces should be cleaned with water, rinsed and sanitized, hands washed after contamination, and honey kept below 104°F. HVAC techs should treat the room like any food area: clean tools, no oil or refrigerant drips near containers, and no heater or dehumidifier parts that shed flakes.

Field card: Honey House HVAC: Warming Rooms, Drying Honey and Safe Heaters

Warm to flow, dry to keep, never above 104 F.

  • Supers: warm room about 90°F for 1 to 2 days; extract at 80 to 90°F.
  • Honey must stay at or below 104°F at every step.
  • Moisture: 18.6 percent or less; lower gives margin. Room humidity under 50 percent.
  • Pints needed = water removed (lb) ÷ 1.04, then divide by days.
  • Electric heat with a thermostat and a high limit; keep fuel-fired heaters vented and clear of wax.
  • Keep heaters 3 feet from anything that burns.
  • Screen every opening bees could use; drain condensate away from food.
