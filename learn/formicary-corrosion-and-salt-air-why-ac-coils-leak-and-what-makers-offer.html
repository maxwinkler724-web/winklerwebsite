Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air: Why AC Coils Leak and What Makers Offer

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling system that keeps losing refrigerant with no sign of damage often has a coil leaking through holes too small to see. Two kinds of corrosion cause a lot of these: a copper-only attack nicknamed "ant nest" corrosion on indoor coils, and salt-driven galvanic corrosion on outdoor coils near the coast. This lesson covers both, what the equipment makers offer against them, and what a slow leak does to a refrigerant budget.

What formicary corrosion is

Formicary means "ant nest." The Copper Development Association (CDA), speaking in an ACHR News report, describes it as pitting corrosion in copper tubing, bluish-black on the surface, with the damage hidden until the surface film is brushed away. A corrosion institute article (iCorr) adds that it runs in networks of microscopic tunnels under the surface and is seen almost only in copper. Those tunnels are what give the ant-nest look under a microscope, and they can get through small-bore tubing in weeks.

What causes it

The iCorr article says it takes three things at once: oxygen, moisture and a weak organic acid such as formic, acetic, propionic or butyric. The CDA report says the acids come from reactions between copper and organic residues left from manufacturing, installation or service, such as lubricants and fluxes that were not fully cleaned or neutralized, and it notes that moisture accelerates the change. The iCorr article lists more sources: residues in coil-forming oils, degreasers and detergent cleaners, chlorinated organic compounds, and products from the breakdown of esters, aldehydes and alcohols. It also says tighter, less ventilated buildings may carry more of these airborne chemicals from building materials, adhesives and cleaning products.

An ACHR News article on pinhole leaks says the indoor coil is a natural target because airborne contaminants collect in the condensate on it during cooling, and the wet contaminants turn acidic. It says corrosion starts on the outside of the tube and works inward, that perforation usually takes "weeks or months, not years," and that R-410A or its POE oil are not the cause, though higher pressure can make an already thinned coil leak sooner. The CDA's technical services manager calls affected systems a "relatively small" share and usually a one-off. So it is a real cause, not the usual one.

What it looks like and why it hides

The iCorr article says the holes are often tiny pinholes on the outside of the tube, ringed by discolored copper that can look gray, black, red-brown or purple. Leaks this small are hard to find. The ACHR article recommends isolating the coil and testing it with a very low vacuum and high nitrogen pressure, because a standard test may miss the leak. For search technique, see Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors and Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods.

What makers offer: aluminum and coatings

  • Different metal. The ACHR article says Trane was moving from copper to aluminum, which this corrosion does not attack. Lennox describes its Quantum Coil as an all-aluminum coil made from a proprietary alloy and names indoor volatile organic compounds, which can form organic acids with moisture, as one of the problems it is built to resist. Lennox publishes no test numbers on that page.
  • Other fixes. iCorr lists tin-plated copper hairpins, less susceptible tubing, moisture filters on ductless units, and screening of oils and tubing for acid-forming residue.
  • Why not just change the copper alloy? The CDA report says other copper alloys can cut thermal conductivity by as much as 70 percent, which would mean bigger equipment. Its recommended answer is thorough cleaning and rinsing at the factory and in the field.

Salt air and outdoor coils

Outdoor coils mostly face a different problem. Carrier's coastal page says salt can travel as far as two miles inland and that severity depends on distance from the ocean, wind, humidity, coil temperature and contaminants such as road salt and irrigation water. It says a standard copper tube and aluminum fin coil can suffer galvanic corrosion, which can lead to leaks. Carrier's coastal models use welded aluminum coils to remove the copper-aluminum pairing, add a thicker coating system and hardware plating, and carry a five-year parts warranty for seacoast corrosion failure. Carrier reports seven years of salt spray testing with no fin degradation, which is the maker's own claim. Trane's coastal literature describes its all-aluminum SPINE FIN coil and powder-coated or ceramic-coated hardware, and gives no coastal distance zones. Check your maker's current literature and warranty terms before you quote any of this to a customer.

Cleaning cautions

Cleaners can speed up the damage. Our outdoor coil lesson found a microchannel cleaner data sheet saying cleaners below pH 6 or above pH 8 can speed corrosion and cause leaks, and one maker's labels that say not for indoor coils. If you do not know the coil type, use water only. See Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil and Cleaning a Furnace Blower Wheel and Indoor Coil. Whatever you use on a copper indoor coil, the CDA message is the same: rinse it fully and let it dry, because leftover chemical film is what formicary corrosion feeds on.

Repair or replace?

Our own view: the sources do not give a rule, so this part is reasoning, not a quoted standard. Since the attack thins the tube across a wide area (ACHR), one found leak does not mean it is the only weak spot. A patched coil may be followed by a second leak, which is another trip and another refrigerant loss. Compare the cost of a replacement coil, what the warranty paperwork says, and the age of the rest of the system, and tell the customer the odds honestly.

Worked example: a slow coil leak against the EPA threshold

Take a home system with a stated factory charge of 9 lb of R-410A that loses 5 percent of its charge per year (both numbers are assumptions chosen for the example).

  1. Charge lost per year: 9 lb × 0.05 = 0.45 lb.
  2. In ounces: 0.45 lb × 16 oz/lb = 7.2 oz per year.
  3. EPA's Section 608 leak repair rules in 40 CFR 82.157 apply to appliances with a full charge of 50 lb or more. 9 lb is far below 50 lb, so they do not apply. The section also exempts appliances containing only substitute refrigerants.
  4. For comparison, the comfort cooling trigger rate in that section is 10 percent. Ten percent of 9 lb is 0.9 lb per year. A 25 percent leak would be 9 × 0.25 = 2.25 lb per year, or 36 oz.
  5. The newer AIM Act rule, 40 CFR 84.106, starts at 15 lb and excludes residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps. So this system is outside it also.

Being below the rule does not make a leak harmless: the customer still pays for every pound added and the system still runs low. The federal leak repair trigger is aimed at large appliances, so on a small home system the decision to fix a leak is a service and cost decision.

Field card: Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air

Wet copper plus organic acid plus air equals pinholes.

  • Formicary corrosion: tunnels in copper, needs oxygen, moisture and a weak organic acid.
  • Sources: forming oils, fluxes, degreasers, cleaners, airborne chemicals in tight buildings.
  • Leaks are pinholes, often only found by an isolated nitrogen test or a sensitive detector.
  • Makers offer all-aluminum coils; coastal units add aluminum coils and heavier coatings.
  • Salt can travel about two miles inland (Carrier).
  • Cleaning: right chemical or water only; rinse and dry copper.
  • Example: 9 lb × 5% = 0.45 lb = 7.2 oz per year; below the 50 lb rule.
