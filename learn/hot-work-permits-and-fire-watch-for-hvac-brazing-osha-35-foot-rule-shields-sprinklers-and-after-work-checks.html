Hot Work Permits and Fire Watch for HVAC Brazing: OSHA 35-Foot Rule, Shields, Sprinklers and After-Work Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A brazing torch is a small open flame, but the fire it can start is often not where you are working. Sparks, slag and heat travel through gaps, along pipes and into framing. This lesson covers what the federal rules actually say about moving hazards, the 35-foot distance, fire watch and permits, and what that means for a tech brazing in an occupied building.

Check the rules that apply to you

Check the rules that apply to you. This page is general education built from federal text read in 2026. Your employer, the building owner, the insurer and the local fire official may require more. Follow their permit process first.

Which OSHA rule applies

OSHA's general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.252, covers welding, cutting and brazing, and its section (a) is the fire prevention and protection part. In a commercial building where you service existing equipment, that is the rule most people mean. The construction rule, 29 CFR 1926.352, applies on construction work and has its own fire prevention paragraphs. Both lean on the same idea: first, move the work or the hazard; second, protect what cannot move. The general industry text also points to the hot work standard NFPA 51B for more detail. We name that standard here but do not reproduce it, so get the current edition through your employer or the building.

What 1910.252(a) says, in plain words

  • Move the work first. If the object can readily be moved, take it to a safe place. If it cannot be moved, move the fire hazards near it to a safe place.
  • Guard what stays. If the object cannot be moved and the hazards cannot all be removed, guards must confine the heat, sparks and slag and protect the hazards that cannot move.
  • Do not do the work if neither works. If those two steps cannot be met, the rule says welding and cutting must not be performed.
  • Close the openings. Floor openings, cracks that cannot be closed, wall holes, open doorways and broken windows must be covered so sparks cannot reach readily combustible material.
  • Extinguisher at hand. Suitable extinguishing equipment must be kept ready for instant use. The rule lists water pails, sand buckets, hoses and portable extinguishers as examples.
  • 35 feet. Combustibles within a 35 ft radius of the work must be swept clean, and where practicable combustibles are to be moved at least 35 ft away. If that is impracticable, they must be protected with flameproofed covers or metal guards or curtains. Combustible floors must be kept wet, covered with damp sand or shielded.
  • Fire watch. A fire watcher is required where more than a minor fire might develop, including where combustibles are closer than 35 ft to the point of operation. The watcher must have an extinguisher, be trained to use it and know how to sound an alarm. The watch must continue for at least a half hour after the work is done.
  • Authorization. The person responsible for authorizing the work must inspect the area first and set the precautions, preferably in a written permit.
  • Sprinklers. Cutting or welding is not permitted in sprinklered buildings while the sprinkler protection is impaired. It is also not permitted in areas management has not authorized, in explosive atmospheres, or near large stores of readily ignitible material.

Note what the rule does not say. "At least a half hour" is a floor, not a recommendation to leave at 31 minutes. Many building owners and insurers set longer holds after the work; that is their written permit, not an OSHA number, and we have not confirmed a specific figure for your building.

The construction rule, 1926.352

The construction paragraphs say much the same in shorter form: move the object when practical, otherwise remove or protect movable hazards, and use positive means to contain heat, sparks and slag around hazards that cannot move. Suitable extinguishing equipment must be immediately available in the work area. Where normal precautions are not enough, extra people must guard against fire during the work and "for a sufficient period of time" afterward, and they must be told the hazards and how to use the equipment. The text does not give a number of minutes or a 35 ft distance in those paragraphs. It also tells you to take the same precautions on the opposite side of a wall, floor or ceiling as on your side, because heat and sparks can pass through and ignite the other area. That matters a great deal in finished buildings.

Sprinklers and smoke detectors

The OSHA text treats impaired sprinklers as a reason not to start. It does not tell a tech how to shut a system off. Do not shut a valve, cap a head or cover a detector on your own. The building owner or facility manager has an impairment or shutdown process, and you should expect to follow it rather than make your own. Ask for it before the day of the job. A detector near your work spot could alarm from smoke, which in an occupied building is a real disruption, so the same conversation should cover whether and how that detector may be handled. If the owner's process cannot be arranged, the work does not happen at that spot. Move it, or reschedule.

Shields, wet rags and nearby material

The OSHA words for protection are guards, flameproofed covers and metal curtains, plus wet or covered floors. In HVAC practice that usually means a flame shield or heat blanket made for torch work placed behind the joint, and keeping wood, foam insulation and paper-faced material out of the flame path. A wet rag wrapped on the tube protects a valve body or a nearby fitting from heat. In our own view, a wet rag is not a substitute for a shield near wood, because it dries out during a long heat. Treat the shield as the guard and the rag as extra. Keep flammable pipe insulation pulled back or protected, and remember nitrogen flow inside the line (see the nitrogen purge lesson) does not protect anything outside the pipe.

Worked example (ours): line set joint in a finished basement ceiling

This is our own made-up job, not a real incident. Assumptions we are choosing: the joint is a copper line set coupling in a finished basement ceiling; wood joists run 8 in. from the joint on each side; there is drywall below and a bedroom above; the building owner has a hot work permit process; and the space has sprinklers or smoke detectors, which we treat as unknown until someone tells us.

  1. Can the work move? A joint already in the ceiling cannot. We could braze a new fitting at the bench or on the floor first, then make only the last joint in place. That is the OSHA first choice, so try it.
  2. Can the hazards move? Pull stored boxes, rugs, paper and furniture out of the 35 ft radius, or cover what stays with flameproofed covers. Our basement has framing we cannot move, so that goes to guards.
  3. Guard the framing. Place a flame shield between the flame and the 8 in. joist, shield the face of the drywall and ceiling above the joint, and keep foam or paper insulation back from the tube. Check the opposite side too: the floor above is the other side of this ceiling, and 1926.352 asks for the same care there.
  4. Permit. Ask the person who authorizes hot work for the building. They inspect the area and set the precautions, preferably in writing. Record the date, location, who is watching and the time you finish.
  5. Detectors and sprinklers. Ask whether a detector or head is near the joint and how the owner wants it handled. Do not disable anything yourself. If the sprinkler system is impaired, OSHA says the work does not proceed.
  6. Extinguisher and fire watch. A watcher with a trained extinguisher is needed because the joist is closer than 35 ft. The watcher must know the alarm. Check the ceiling cavity and the floor above while you work.
  7. After the last flame. The watch continues for at least a half hour after completion. Use that time to cool, tidy tools and look in the cavity again with a light. The permit and the building may require longer; follow whichever is longer.

If step 3 cannot be done, because the shield will not fit or the joist is scorching, stop and talk to the person who authorized the work.

What other agencies add

The U.S. Chemical Safety Board defines hot work as work such as burning, welding, cutting, brazing, grinding and soldering that can ignite a flammable atmosphere, and says it has identified over 60 deaths since 1990 from hot work explosions and fires on tanks. That is tank work, not basement brazing, but it shows why checking for flammables first matters. The NIOSH construction toolbox talk repeats the 35 ft clearance and says to assign a fire watcher when combustibles are closer than that, and to keep the employer-provided extinguisher ready; it gives no watch duration. For eye and burn protection see the related lessons below.

Field card: Hot Work Permits and Fire Watch

Move it, guard it, watch it, then check it.

  • Move the work first; then move or cover the hazards.
  • 35 ft radius: sweep it, clear it or cover it (OSHA 1910.252(a)).
  • Guard what cannot move: flame shield, flameproof cover, metal curtain.
  • Fire watcher with a trained extinguisher when combustibles are inside 35 ft.
  • Watch at least a half hour after completion (1910.252); the permit may say more.
  • Check the other side of the wall, floor or ceiling.
  • Sprinklers impaired means no hot work. Never shut off a system yourself.
  • Get the permit from the responsible person, in writing if you can.
