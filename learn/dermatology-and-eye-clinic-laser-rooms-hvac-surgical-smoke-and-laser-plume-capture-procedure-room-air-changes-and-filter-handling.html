Dermatology and Eye Clinic Laser Rooms: Plume Capture, Procedure Room Air Changes and Filter Handling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A laser treatment room looks like a quiet exam room, but the beam can make smoke at the treatment site, and the laser itself makes heat. This lesson covers what the HVAC tech should know: why room air alone does not clear plume, how a local smoke evacuator fits in, what air change numbers public sources list for procedure rooms, and how to handle the used filters.

The facility's rules come first

Check who decides. The practice's laser safety officer, infection control lead and engineer of record decide how each room runs. This page is general education from public pages read in 2026. Do not change airflow, pressure, filters or evacuator settings without their sign-off.

What laser plume is

NIOSH says the smoke from laser and electrosurgical work can contain toxic gases and vapors such as benzene, hydrogen cyanide and formaldehyde, plus bioaerosols that include blood fragments and viruses. OSHA describes plume as similar to other smoke, with carbon monoxide, polyaromatic hydrocarbons and trace toxic gases, and says it can irritate the upper airway. OSHA also says it has no specific standard for laser or electrosurgery plume, and that no disease transmission through surgical smoke has been documented. The HVAC point is simple: the smoke is made at the patient, inches from a face.

Local evacuation first, room ventilation second

NIOSH says general room ventilation by itself is not enough to capture contaminants where they are produced. It recommends general ventilation together with local exhaust, and prefers a portable smoke evacuator over room (wall) suction, which was designed mainly for liquids. NIOSH asks for the nozzle to stay within 2 inches of the surgical site and for a capture velocity of about 100 to 150 feet per minute at the inlet. Keep the evacuator running whenever the beam is making particles.

Distance matters because capture drops off fast. A 1989 evaluation in the CDC library of an evacuator used with a 30 watt carbon dioxide laser put the nozzle 2, 6 and 12 inches from the cut. Smoke was fully captured at 2 inches, and a significant amount escaped at 6 and 12 inches. A 2018 study in the same library used suction tips within 5 cm (about 2 inches) at about 35 liters per minute and found that two local exhaust setups cut particle counts to roughly a quarter to a third of the uncontrolled level. They reduced the smoke but did not eliminate it. So room air is the backup, not the plan.

Filters: what they catch and what they do not

NIOSH recommends a HEPA filter or equivalent for particulates, and says other filtering or cleaning processes can remove or inactivate gases and vapors. In that 2018 study, an in-line ULPA filter was rated 99.9999 percent at 0.1 micron, and a second evacuator used a four-stage stack with a pre-filter, charcoal, ULPA and post filter. That shows how stages are layered; it is not a requirement. Follow the evacuator maker's instructions.

Procedure room air changes and pressure

The CDC reprints a table from the 2001 AIA guidelines (Appendix B, Table B.2). Its procedure room row lists pressure out relative to adjacent areas (positive), at least 3 outdoor air changes per hour, at least 15 total, 30 to 60 percent relative humidity and 70 to 75 degrees F. The operating room row is similar, and its footnote asks for at least 0.01 inch water gauge (2.5 Pa) differential. The table dates from 2001, so your state's adopted edition of ASHRAE Standard 170 and the facility guidelines govern, not this page. Our cardiac cath lab lesson walks through a newer addendum row for the same kind of room, and our ambulatory surgery center lesson shows where this room sits on a pressure map.

A CDC-library article adds that AORN recommends at least 15 air exchanges per hour for the OR, the ASHRAE/ASHE standard requires at least 20 for new operating rooms, and wall suction needs adequate room ventilation. A laser room is not always an OR, so ask which class it is. For pressure basics see negative and positive pressure rooms.

Worked example: air changes and capture falloff

Assumptions (ours, not code values): a room 14 ft by 16 ft with a 9 ft ceiling; the 15 total and 3 outdoor air changes from the CDC row above; an evacuator pulling 35 cfm through a 1.5 inch round nozzle; and a simple model for a plain round opening, v = Q / (10 x squared + A), with x in feet and A the nozzle area.

Room air. Volume = 14 x 16 x 9 = 2,016 cubic feet. Total supply = ACH x volume / 60 = 15 x 2,016 / 60 = 504 cfm. Outdoor air at 3 ACH = 3 x 2,016 / 60 = 100.8 cfm, about 101 cfm. The evacuator pulls only 35 cfm, but at the source.

Capture. Nozzle area A = 3.1416 x (0.75 / 12) squared = 0.0123 sq ft. At 2 inches (x = 0.1667 ft): v = 35 / (10 x 0.0278 + 0.0123) = 35 / 0.290 = about 121 fpm, which lands in NIOSH's 100 to 150 fpm range. At 6 inches (x = 0.5 ft): v = 35 / (2.5 + 0.0123) = about 14 fpm. At 12 inches (x = 1 ft): v = 35 / 10.0123 = about 3.5 fpm. Moving the nozzle from 2 to 6 inches cuts capture velocity by roughly 9 times in our model, which matches the field result above: smoke got away at 6 inches. The model is a teaching sketch; a real nozzle differs.

Laser heat. Assume the laser and its power supply put 1,500 W into the room (our assumption; the maker's data sheet has the real value). Btu/h = W x 3.412 = 1,500 x 3.412 = 5,118 Btu/h. Using the sensible heat formula, rise = 5,118 / (1.08 x 504) = about 9.4 degrees F if all of it landed in the 504 cfm supply stream. If the unit rejects heat to its own chiller or cooling loop, less goes into the room air; read the maker's site-planning sheet for the split.

Eye clinics: steady and still

We found no public federal source with a number for temperature steadiness or draft limits in an eye laser room, so the maker's installation sheet and the surgeon decide. A tech can still check where diffusers throw and watch for swings when the laser cycles.

Filter changes are contaminated waste

NIOSH and OSHA say that at the end of a procedure, tubing, filters and absorbers are considered infectious waste and must be disposed of appropriately. OSHA says to use new tubing for each procedure and to change evacuator filters on the maker's schedule. The same article says staff who dispose of contaminated filters and plume supplies should wear PPE under the facility's sample procedure. As in our chemo infusion lesson applies: get the written procedure, wear the PPE it names, do not crush or tear the media, and bag the used filter in the facility's waste stream.

Field card: laser room plume and air

Capture at the source; the room is the backup.

  • Plume can carry toxic gases and bioaerosols; OSHA has no plume-specific standard.
  • Nozzle within 2 inches of the site; about 100 to 150 fpm at the inlet (NIOSH).
  • Our model: 35 cfm gives about 121 fpm at 2 in., about 14 at 6 in., about 3.5 at 12 in.
  • CDC table row for a procedure room: positive, 3 outdoor and 15 total ACH, 70 to 75 F.
  • cfm = ACH x volume / 60. Our example: 2,016 cu ft at 15 ACH is 504 cfm.
  • Laser heat: W x 3.412 = Btu/h. Our assumed 1,500 W is 5,118 Btu/h.
  • Used filters, tubing and absorbers are infectious waste; use the written procedure and PPE.
