Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start: Restart Checks After an Outage

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The lights are back on, but the house is still warm and the outdoor unit is silent. Before you decide something broke, know that many systems are built to wait a few minutes after power returns. This lesson walks through what to check, in a safe order, and when to stop and call a technician.

The short answer

After an outage, wait for the power to settle, then give the system a few minutes to start on its own. Check the thermostat and the breakers. If the system still does not start, or a breaker trips again, stop and call a licensed HVAC technician. Never run a generator indoors or plug one into a wall outlet.

Why it may not start right away: the restart delay

A compressor is the pump that moves refrigerant. If it is shut off and started again too fast, the pressure on its two sides has not evened out, and starting against that pressure is hard on the compressor. So thermostats and control boards add a short lockout after the compressor stops. Power loss counts as a stop.

Manufacturers publish the figure. Lennox's ComfortSense 5500 thermostat guide describes a 5-minute compressor short-cycle delay and says that after a power loss the system goes into compressor protection mode, with a "waiting" message on the screen when cooling is requested. The Honeywell T6 Pro guide says to wait 5 minutes for the system to restart safely, and says the display flashes Cool On (Heat On for a heat pump) during the wait. Honeywell Home's support page describes the same feature in softer terms: the thermostat keeps the compressor off for a few minutes and shows "Wait." Trane's installer guide for an add-on anti-short-cycle relay uses a 5-minute timer too. Some settings are longer: an ecobee4 installation guide sets the compressor minimum off time to 600 seconds, which is 10 minutes, meaning the unit will not come back on for 10 minutes after it cycles off.

Notice what is not promised. The ecobee and Lennox pages do not all say the timer restarts at power-up, and each model differs. Your own thermostat or control manual is the final word on your delay.

Worked example: how long to wait

Say the lights stop flickering at 3:00 PM. Trane's homeowner support article says that once power is steady you should wait 10 minutes for it to stabilize before turning HVAC breakers back on one at a time. Then the thermostat powers up and its delay starts.

  1. Power steady: 3:00 PM.
  2. Stabilize wait: 10 minutes, so breakers go on at 3:10 PM.
  3. If the thermostat uses a 5-minute delay: 3:10 + 5 minutes = 3:15 PM earliest start.
  4. If it uses a 600-second delay: 600 / 60 = 10 minutes, so 3:10 + 10 minutes = 3:20 PM earliest start.

So 15 to 20 minutes of silence from the outdoor unit can be normal, with nothing wrong. Add the time a house takes to feel cooler after the compressor starts. That is the reason to look at the thermostat screen before you start pressing things.

Thermostat blank or reset

If the screen is dark, it has lost power or is rebooting. Battery models need fresh batteries; wired and smart models depend on the system's power. The steps are in Thermostat Blank or Showing the Wrong Temperature. A smart thermostat can also take extra time to reconnect to Wi-Fi: Trane notes power and internet service may not return at the same time. A thermostat that comes back with its clock or schedule reset may hold the wrong mode, so confirm it is set to Cool or Heat and the target is a few degrees from the room temperature.

Breakers: reset once, not repeatedly

Look at the panel for a breaker that sits in the middle or off position and for the outdoor unit's disconnect. Trane suggests returning HVAC breakers to ON one at a time after the wait, rather than slamming everything on at once. An ESFI breaker guide says frequent breaker trips indicate a problem and should be inspected by a qualified electrician. So treat one trip during a power return as possible, and a second trip as a stop sign. Leave it off and call. The causes and the technician side are in AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker. If the thermostat is working and the indoor fan runs but the outdoor unit stays silent past the delay, see Outdoor AC Unit Not Running but the Indoor Fan Is.

Surges and what to protect

Power coming back can bring a surge. FEMA's Ready.gov power outage guidance says to unplug appliances, equipment and electronics to avoid damage from electrical surges, and Trane's article says to turn the HVAC breakers off during an outage. ESFI says surges can be damaging to nearly all equipment. A surge can hurt thermostats, control boards and fuses; the thermostat lesson above mentions fuses on the control board blowing after a surge. ESFI lists flickering or dimming lights, a burning smell, discolored switches and warm outlets as electrical warning signs. If you notice any of those near the HVAC equipment or panel, turn the system off at the thermostat and call a professional. Replacing boards, fuses or surge devices at the equipment is a technician job.

Cold-weather and long outages

Some outdoor units have a crankcase heater, a small heater that keeps oil in the compressor warm. Daikin's installation manual for a DP14HM unit says that if a crankcase heater is used, the unit should be energized 24 hours before the compressor first starts, and that compressor damage may occur if that step is not followed. That is an installation startup instruction. The manual does not give a separate wait after an outage, so we do not give one here. If your unit's manual gives a wait after long power loss, follow it; if not, ask your technician.

Generator safety

Many outages end with a generator story. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says never use a portable generator inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open, and to use it outside, far from windows, doors and vents. CPSC also says generators in confined spaces can produce high carbon monoxide levels within minutes, and that you should never power house wiring by plugging a generator into a wall outlet ("backfeeding"), which can electrocute utility workers and neighbors. The Missouri Public Service Commission, repeating OSHA guidance, says the safe way to connect a portable generator to house wiring is to have a licensed electrical contractor install a transfer switch. Do not connect a generator to an air conditioner or furnace yourself. See Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

For apprentices: what the tech checks

  • Is the call for cooling present at Y, and is the compressor timer holding the contactor open?
  • Line voltage at the disconnect and contactor, measured with a meter before anything is touched.
  • Fuse and board status, thermostat mode and settings, and the delay setting in the thermostat or control menu.
  • Crankcase heater and manufacturer instructions for your specific unit.

Never bypass a delay timer to make a call go faster. The timer is there to protect the compressor.

Field card: Restart Checks After an Outage

Wait, check, then call. Do not force it.

  • Wait for steady power, then about 10 minutes (Trane support article).
  • Turn HVAC breakers on one at a time.
  • Expect a 5-minute or longer compressor delay; read your thermostat screen.
  • Reset a breaker once. A second trip means stop and call.
  • Unplug sensitive electronics during surges; leave equipment repairs to a technician.
  • Generators go outside only; no wall-outlet backfeeding; transfer switch by a licensed electrician.
