Pump Power Math: Water Horsepower, Brake Horsepower and Wire-to-Water Efficiency

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pump nameplate says horsepower, but the electric meter bills kilowatts. This lesson walks the power from the wall to the water: water horsepower from flow and head, brake horsepower from the pump curve, input kW from motor efficiency, and the combined wire-to-water number that ties them together. We finish with a worked chilled water pump example.

Step 1: water horsepower

Water horsepower is the useful work the pump hands to the water. A U.S. Department of Energy sourcebook on pumping systems writes it as head times flow times specific gravity, divided by 3,960, with head in feet and flow in gallons per minute. The 3,960 is only a units constant that lands the answer in horsepower. A university extension fact sheet on pump efficiency gives the same form, using total head in feet and flow in gpm.

Water hp = gpm x head (ft) x SG / 3,960

Specific gravity (SG) is the fluid's weight compared with plain water. For ordinary chilled or condenser water we treat SG as 1.0. A glycol mix is heavier, so it needs an SG from the mix data, and the answer rises with it. Head is the total head the pump adds: the pressure the system needs from the pump, in feet of water. If you only have a gauge difference in psi, extension guidance uses 2.306 feet of water per psi.

Step 2: brake horsepower from the curve

The pump needs more shaft power than the water receives, because some energy is lost as friction and turbulence inside the pump. Pump efficiency is the ratio of power delivered to the water over power put into the pump shaft. The DOE sourcebook defines it that way and says that for a directly coupled pump and motor, the shaft power is the brake horsepower (bhp) the motor delivers. It also says pump efficiencies run from roughly 35 percent to above 90 percent depending on design, and that the best efficiency point (BEP) on the curve is where efficiency peaks.

Brake hp = water hp / pump efficiency

You get the pump efficiency by finding the operating point on the pump curve (flow and head from the system, see reading pump curves and system curves) and reading the efficiency line that passes through or near it. Many curves also print a brake horsepower line, which saves the division. If the operating point sits far from the BEP, the efficiency you read will be low and the shaft power high. DOE Pumping Systems Tip Sheet #4 flags pumps running 30 percent or more away from BEP flow, and notes that efficiencies of 50 to 60 percent or lower are quite common in the field.

Step 3: motor efficiency and input kW

The motor does not turn all the electricity it draws into shaft power. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory paper on industrial pumping defines motor efficiency as shaft power divided by electrical input power. Rearranged for our use:

Input kW = brake hp x 0.746 / motor efficiency

The 0.746 converts horsepower to kilowatts. The national standards institute's conversion table lists the electric horsepower as exactly 746 watts, and the mechanical horsepower at about 745.7 watts, so 0.746 is the usual shop number. Motor efficiency changes with load, so use the value for the load the motor actually carries, not just the nameplate full-load figure. For motors that drive pumps, the motor efficiency lesson goes deeper. To check the result against a clamp meter, the three-phase power math lesson shows kW from volts, amps and power factor.

Step 4: wire-to-water efficiency

Wire-to-water efficiency compares what the water gets with what the wire delivers. The Oak Ridge paper writes it as fluid power over electrical input power, which is the same as pump efficiency times motor efficiency. If a variable frequency drive sits between the wire and the motor, its losses join the chain (drive efficiency is another multiplier), and a drive-based example would use that third factor too. A DOE tip sheet on testing a pumping system uses the same idea in kilowatts: system efficiency equals flow x head x SG divided by 5,308 x electrical kW. That 5,308 is just 3,960 divided by 0.746, so it is the same equation with the units folded together.

Worked example: a chilled water pump (our own example)

These numbers are our own assumptions for teaching, not data from any real plant. Assume a constant-speed chilled water pump on a closed loop, water with SG 1.0, a design flow of 1,200 gpm, and a total head of 90 ft at that flow. Assume the operating point reads 78 percent pump efficiency on the curve, and that the motor (a 40 hp motor, assumed) runs at 94 percent efficiency at this load.

  1. Water hp = 1,200 x 90 x 1.0 / 3,960 = 108,000 / 3,960 = 27.27 hp.
  2. Brake hp = 27.27 / 0.78 = 34.97 hp. That is below the assumed 40 hp motor, so the motor is not overloaded.
  3. Shaft power in kW = 34.97 x 0.746 = 26.08 kW.
  4. Input kW = 26.08 / 0.94 = 27.75 kW.
  5. Wire-to-water = 0.78 x 0.94 = 0.733, or 73.3 percent. Check: 27.27 hp x 0.746 = 20.35 kW of water power, and 20.35 / 27.75 = 0.733.
  6. Cross-check with the kW form: 108,000 / (5,308 x 27.75) = 0.733.

Now suppose the pump drifts off its best point. If the efficiency read from the curve falls to 65 percent (assumed), brake hp is 27.27 / 0.65 = 41.96 hp, which is above the assumed 40 hp motor nameplate. Input kW becomes 41.96 x 0.746 / 0.94 = 33.30 kW, about 5.5 kW more for the same water delivered. If it ran 4,000 hours in a year (assumed), that is roughly 22,000 more kWh.

Condenser water pumps

The same four steps apply to a condenser water pump. The difference is the head. A closed chilled water loop only has to overcome friction in the pipes, coils, valves and the chiller barrel. An open cooling tower circuit also has to push water up to the tower distribution basin, so the static lift and the tower nozzle pressure are part of the head you plug into the formula. Static lift also changes how much a slower pump saves, so see the impeller trimming and VFD lesson.

Where the numbers go wrong

  • Using design head instead of actual head. If the balancing valve is throttled, the pump is making head that is burned off across the valve. The tip sheet counts only the required head, not the throttled head, so a throttled system shows a low system efficiency even when the pump itself is healthy.
  • Reading the wrong curve. Curves are for a stated impeller diameter and speed. Check them against the pump tag.
  • Mixing fluids. A glycol mix changes SG, so check the mix data before you use 1.0.
  • Treating motor nameplate efficiency as fixed. It changes with load.

For the chilled water side of the story, chilled water pumps and delta-T shows how flow ties to load.

Field card: Pump Power Math: Water Horsepower, Brake Horsepower and Wire-to-Water Efficiency

Water hp, then bhp, then kW, then wire-to-water.

  • Water hp = gpm x head (ft) x SG / 3,960.
  • Brake hp = water hp / pump efficiency (read at the operating point on the curve).
  • Input kW = brake hp x 0.746 / motor efficiency.
  • Wire-to-water = pump efficiency x motor efficiency (x drive efficiency if a drive is present).
  • Kilowatt form: system efficiency = gpm x head x SG / (5,308 x input kW).
  • Operating 30 percent or more away from the best efficiency flow is a warning sign.
