Ventilating a Home Film Darkroom: Fumes, Light-Tight Fans, Makeup Air and Heating the Replacement Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A film darkroom is a small closed room where people stand for an hour or more over open trays of chemistry, and the room also has to stay dark. That combination is a ventilation problem with an extra rule: every path for air must also be a path that blocks light. This lesson explains what the fumes are, how to move air without leaking light, and how to work out the fan size and the winter heating cost of the air you bring back in.

What is in the air

University art-safety and industrial-hygiene pages agree on the main vapors. Stop bath is diluted acetic acid, and one campus health sheet notes that repeated inhaling of acetic acid vapor, even from stop bath, may cause chronic bronchitis. Fixer is the second concern: a campus darkroom manual says the main fixing salt can give off sulfur dioxide when it is heated or mixed with an acid, and tells users never to mix fixer with stop bath. Another campus sheet says sulfur dioxide can form when fixing baths are heated or left standing a long time, and that many people with asthma are especially sensitive to it. Selenium toners are worse: one sheet says they give off large amounts of sulfur dioxide, and that treating selenium salts with acid may release a highly toxic gas. Several of the pages say toning solutions must be used with local exhaust, meaning a fan that pulls air right at the tray, not just a general room fan.

Developers are the quietest of the group, but powdered developers raise dust when mixed, and the campus pages say to mix powders in a hood, a glove box or with local exhaust. Two simple habits help every fan: cover baths when they are not in use, and take breaks for fresh air. The pages we read give no single air-flow rule for every case, and they tell readers to check the product safety data sheets and, in a workplace, the safety office. A home darkroom is a personal choice, so treat this lesson as general education and read the data sheet for every chemical you buy.

How much air

Two of the university pages give numbers for darkrooms. One industrial-hygiene fact sheet says there should be at least 10 air changes per hour, or 170 cfm, for a darkroom. An art-safety sheet from another university says 10 to 20 air changes per hour. One air change per hour means moving a volume of air equal to the room, once each hour. So a room with 640 cubic feet at 10 air changes per hour needs 640 × 10 ÷ 60 = about 107 cfm. The 170 cfm figure fits a room of roughly 1,000 cubic feet. Bigger or more toning means moving toward the upper end.

Light-tight exhaust and light-trap louvers

Light travels in straight lines and air can turn corners. A light trap is a baffled opening: the air path bends two or three times behind flat-black panels, so no straight line runs from outside to inside while the air still flows. These are our own plain-language ideas, not sourced figures. Build the exhaust the same way. Put a fan with a short duct to the outdoors on the wall, then add a baffled box or a bend on the room side and another where the duct leaves the house, painted flat black inside. Tape the seams, because a hairline gap lets in light just as it lets in air. Any trap adds resistance, so a fan that moves its rated flow in free air will move less once the trap is on. Pick the fan for the flow you need after the trap is added, and check the result by timing a smoke stick or a thin tissue at the opening.

Do not terminate the exhaust into an attic, a crawl space or another room. The vapors need to go outdoors, away from windows, doors and intakes, and a home's local building rules may say more about ducts and openings. Ask the local building department before cutting through walls.

Makeup air that does not leak light

Whatever a fan removes must come back in. If the room is tight and has no path for replacement air, the fan simply strains, the flow drops, and air sneaks in around the door in white-light leaks. Give the room one planned path instead: a light-trap louver in the door or wall, sized so the air moves slowly through it, drawing from a hallway or a conditioned part of the house. Use the same baffle idea, flat black and sealed. A door with a light-tight sweep at the bottom can serve as the path if it has a baffled undercut, but a plain gap under the door will let light in from the hallway.

Keep the room slightly negative

Because the fan removes a little more air than the planned path brings in, the darkroom sits at slightly lower pressure than the rest of the house. That is what you want: air then drifts from the house toward the darkroom, and fumes do not drift out. Slightly is the key word. If the room is too negative it pulls hard through every gap, whistles, and may pull air from places you do not want. If a gas furnace, boiler or water heater is in the same basement, a strong exhaust fan can compete with its flue, so have a technician check the combustion air; see the linked lesson below.

Do not use the central return

It is tempting to cut a return grille into the darkroom or to let it share a return path. Do not. In our view, a return duct would carry fumes to the furnace or air handler and out to every room, and a supply register would push air in and fight the exhaust. Also a duct connection is a light leak. The darkroom should have no supply, no return and no transfer grille tied into the house duct system. Its only air paths are the light-trap louver and the exhaust fan.

Small rooms, bathrooms and basements

A spare bathroom is a popular choice because it has water, a sink and an exhaust fan. The existing fan helps, but a standard bathroom fan is not light tight and is usually sized for moisture, not for 10 air changes of chemistry vapor; the bathroom lesson linked below covers sizing and ducting. A basement keeps a steady temperature, but it is often damp. A federal environmental agency guide on mold and moisture says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. Wet trays and wash water add moisture, so plan for the exhaust to carry it out and use the dehumidifier lessons below if the space stays damp. A sealed, windowless room has no way to clear the air when the fan is off, so run the fan whenever chemistry is open.

Keeping the room warm for the chemistry

Film developers are written for a stated temperature. A maker's black-and-white film processing guide, reposted on a university lab page, lists development times at 68°F (20°C) and at 75°F (24°C), and lists 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C) for the stop bath. Always follow the written range on the label or data sheet of the chemistry you use. Cold makeup air in winter pushes the room below that range, so you need to know how much heat the replacement air takes.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, with stated assumptions. The numbers are ours, not measured values from a real home.

  • Assumption: darkroom of 10 ft × 8 ft × 8 ft, so the volume is 10 × 8 × 8 = 640 cubic feet.
  • Assumption: air change target of 10 per hour. That is the low end of what the university pages above give for darkrooms; the choice of 10 for this room is our assumption.
  • Assumption: winter replacement air at 15°F and a room kept at 68°F, so the temperature difference is 68 − 15 = 53°F.

Step 1, exhaust flow. cfm = volume × air changes per hour ÷ 60 = 640 × 10 ÷ 60 = 106.7, which we round to 107 cfm.

Step 2, heat to warm the replacement air. Use the standard sensible-heat relation for air, Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference (the constant is explained in the linked lesson on heat-transfer formulas). Q = 1.08 × 107 × 53 = 115.56 × 53 = about 6,125 Btu per hour.

Step 3, upper end for comparison. At 20 air changes per hour the flow doubles to 640 × 20 ÷ 60 = 213 cfm, and the heat to warm it is 1.08 × 213 × 53 = about 12,190 Btu per hour.

What it means: in our example, the replacement air carries away about 6,100 Btu per hour of heat in cold weather while the fan runs. A small spare room may have little heat of its own, so the room will go cold unless that heat arrives from the house through the makeup path, or from a small heat source made for the room. Using a fan only while chemistry is open, and a timer or switch you can reach in the dark, keeps this load low. Do not use an open-flame or fuel-burning heater in a sealed darkroom.

Field card: Ventilating a Home Film Darkroom

Move air out, bring air in, keep light out, keep the house duct system out of it.

  • Fumes to control: acetic acid from stop bath, sulfur dioxide from fixer and toners, dust from powders.
  • Campus sheets give 10 air changes per hour or 170 cfm, and 10 to 20 air changes per hour.
  • cfm = room volume × air changes per hour ÷ 60.
  • Winter heat for replacement air: Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Exhaust and makeup air both go through baffled, flat-black light traps.
  • Keep the room slightly negative; no supply or return from the house ducts.
  • Follow the written temperature range of your chemistry; cover baths when idle.
