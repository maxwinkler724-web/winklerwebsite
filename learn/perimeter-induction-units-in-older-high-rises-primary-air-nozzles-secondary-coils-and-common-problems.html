Perimeter Induction Units in Older High-Rises: Primary Air Nozzles, Secondary Coils and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk the window wall of an office tower or hospital built in the 1950s to 1970s and you may find a low metal cabinet with no fan inside. It still conditions the room. A small stream of fast central air pulls room air through a water coil. This lesson explains that trick, what goes wrong with it, and what replaced it.

Check the building documents first

This page is general education built from patents, trade articles and maker literature read in 2026. Units differ by maker and year. Follow the maker's manual for the exact unit and the engineer's drawings for the building, and have only qualified people work on piping, controls and ductwork.

What an induction unit does

Trade articles describe the same chain. A central air handler sends primary air, which is mostly outside air, up to a terminal unit at the perimeter. A trade article on induction units says the air is pushed through nozzles, and the jet pulls room air in as secondary air. The secondary air crosses a heat exchanger fed with hot or chilled water, mixes with the primary air, and goes back to the room. Because both air and water do the work, these are called air-water systems. An engineering article on retrofits adds that the primary air handles ventilation and dehumidification while the coil handles most of the temperature control. A contractor's history credits Willis Carrier with the design, to shrink the vertical ductwork all-air systems needed, with units usually under perimeter windows.

Induction ratio

A Chalmers University research abstract defines the induction ratio as the induced room air flow divided by the primary air flow. A trade article on aging units says the room air pulled in is roughly four times the primary air volume. An article on upgrading old systems cites 1 part primary to 2 to 4 parts induced room air for chilled beams. So the figure depends on the product, and the unit's data sheet decides.

Pressure and the water side

A patent background describing these systems says the primary air comes through high-pressure ducting at on the order of 1.5 inches of water gauge, and says the units were widely used in non-residential construction from 1950 to 1975. A facilities article says inlet static pressure was often 1.5 to 3.0 inches w.c. or more, which imposed a significant fan energy penalty.

The same patent says most of these systems were two-pipe, with one supply line and one return line shared by chilled and heated water, so the loop cannot heat and cool at once. It notes that the problem shows up in early spring and late fall, when some rooms want heat and others want cooling. A product-category article says four-pipe systems use separate chilled and hot circuits so zones can heat or cool at the same time, at higher cost and with more piping. Our fan coil lesson covers the same two-pipe and four-pipe pattern and its seasonal changeover.

Controls

The same product-category article says each unit has a local water valve for zone temperature, so a sunny side and a shaded side can differ. Carrier's maintenance procedure for its water-control induction terminal refers to a water control valve and a field-supplied room thermostat, and sends the reader to the control maker's instructions. A trade article on a hospital replacement says newer units offer electronic damper controls and different operating modes. We did not find a source that says what controls a particular older unit has, so read the nameplate and drawings.

Why the coil is meant to stay dry

The patent background says these units are designed as a dry coil: the central system dries the primary air so the room coil only cools air sensibly. It warns that if people open valves wider or lower the water temperature, condensation can form, and with no drain system damage to the building rapidly follows. For the idea of dew point, see Psychrometrics for Technicians. For the same rule on modern beams, with a water temperature kept above the room dew point, see our chilled beams lesson.

Common field problems

  • Noise. A patent on induction nozzles says jet noise is approximately proportional to the eighth power of jet velocity, that raising supply pressure to fix cooling shortfalls makes this worse, and that the noise has frequently drawn tenant complaints. A history article lists high-velocity air noise and energy use as the main drawbacks.
  • Dirty lint screens and coils. Carrier's maintenance procedure says to clean the lint screen with a soft brush or vacuum at intervals set by job conditions, and to vacuum debris that falls through the discharge grille. The patent background says the coil sits right behind the filter element, so cleaning it is a bigger task than cleaning central filters.
  • Blocked grilles. The same patent says the flat top of the cabinet becomes a magnet for books and papers, which can block the outlet and lead to complaints of poor heating or cooling.
  • Water and drain pans. Carrier's procedure shows a condensate pan attached to the coil, so it is part of the unit to check during service.
  • Fading performance. A hospital case in a trade article found 45-year-old units operating at less than full capacity and air handlers running at 7 to 8 inches of pressure.

Worked example: how much room air, how much cooling

An article on aging units says induced air is roughly four times the primary air, so we use a 4:1 ratio. Everything below is our own arithmetic for one unit with 60 cfm of primary air.

  1. Our own: secondary (room) air = induction ratio x primary = 4 x 60 cfm = 240 cfm.
  2. Our own: total air delivered = primary + secondary = 60 + 240 = 300 cfm.
  3. Our own: secondary coil cooling the induced air from 75 F to 65 F, so dT = 75 - 65 = 10 F.
  4. Our own: sensible capacity = 1.08 x cfm x dT = 1.08 x 240 x 10 = 2,592 Btu/h, about 0.22 ton (2,592 / 12,000).

The coil only handles the 240 cfm of induced air, so the 60 cfm of primary air is not in that figure. The 1.08 factor assumes standard air; see the heat transfer formulas lesson for what it means and its limits.

Modern relatives and retrofit choices

A Price Industries article describes an active chilled beam as primary air entering a plenum and leaving through nozzles, creating low pressure that pulls room air through a water coil; the aim is to move the load from the air side to the water side. A facilities article says the same principle applied to old induction systems can reuse existing risers and piping, and advises keeping beam water above the local dew point, typically 58 to 60 F, with primary air dried to an indoor dew point typically below 55 F. An HVAC trade article contrasts beam water of no lower than 55 F with 40 to 45 F for fan coils, and says beams typically run under 20 NC.

For like-for-like work, a trade article on a hospital describes replacing induction units only, with better nozzles, giving 300 to 400 cfm per wall unit (about 35 percent more) and cutting air handler pressure by 1 to 2 inches after about a third of the units were done. The same article says fan coils or central overhead air were rejected because they needed new air handlers, ductwork and space. A maker page for a retrofit induction unit says it can run at lower static pressure or swap one for one into existing cabinets. We do not recommend a product; the engineer chooses by the building.

Field card: Perimeter Induction Units

Fast primary air in, room air pulled across the coil, mixed air out.

  • Induced air = ratio x primary cfm (about 4:1 in one source; read the data sheet).
  • Sensible coil capacity = 1.08 x induced cfm x dT. Example: 1.08 x 240 x 10 = 2,592 Btu/h.
  • Two-pipe systems cannot heat and cool at once; expect seasonal changeover.
  • Dry-coil design: do not drive the water cold or open valves to chase a hot room.
  • Clean the lint screen; keep the cabinet top and grille clear.
  • Noise rises steeply with nozzle velocity; do not raise supply pressure casually.
