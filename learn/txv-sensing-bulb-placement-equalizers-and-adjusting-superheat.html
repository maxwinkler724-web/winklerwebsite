TXV Bulb Placement, Equalizers and Adjusting Superheat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) can only react to what its bulb and equalizer tell it. Most "bad TXV" calls turn out to be a bulb in the wrong spot, a loose strap, a capped equalizer or a problem somewhere else in the system. This lesson covers where the bulb goes, how the equalizer is piped, and how to change the spring setting without making things worse.

Check the maker's sheet

Check the maker's sheet. Positions, turns and targets differ by manufacturer and valve model. This page summarizes published bulletins from Sporlan (Parker), Danfoss and Emerson, plus trade press, as general education. The sheet that came with the valve or the equipment always wins.

The three forces on the diaphragm

Sporlan's Bulletin 10-9 describes the valve as a balance of pressures. Bulb pressure pushes the diaphragm toward open. Evaporator (equalizer) pressure and spring pressure push toward closed. In steady operation the bulb pressure equals the other two added together, with the pressure drop across the port also helping to open a conventional valve. Because bulb pressure follows the temperature of the suction line at the bulb, the valve is really measuring superheat: bulb temperature compared with the boiling temperature at evaporator pressure. The bulletin calls the superheat needed to overcome the spring and equalizer pressure the static superheat. A little more superheat is then needed to lift the pin off its seat (opening superheat). Static plus opening superheat is the operating superheat, which Sporlan says usually ends up around 8 to 12°F on a properly applied valve.

Where the bulb goes

Sporlan's installation bulletin 10-11 gives this rule: on suction lines 7/8 inch OD and larger, mount the bulb at 4 or 8 o'clock on the side of a horizontal line, parallel to the flow. On smaller lines the bulb may go anywhere around the circumference, but the bottom is not recommended. Never put it in a trap or downstream of one, where liquid and oil collect. Emerson's operating instruction for its Thermo valves also says 4 or 8 o'clock on a horizontal run, as close to the evaporator outlet as practical. A Heatcraft engineer writing in ACHR News gives a size-based scheme instead (12 o'clock under 7/8 inch, 10 or 2 o'clock from 7/8 to 1-5/8 inch, 4 or 8 o'clock over 2 inches) and says never 6 o'clock. Makers differ on the small-line positions, which is a good reason to read the sheet for the valve in your hand.

Strap it tight, then insulate

Sporlan says to fasten the bulb with the two straps supplied with the valve, on a clean, straight section of suction line. A bad strap is not a small problem: a Danfoss troubleshooting article says up to half of the heat the bulb absorbs can come from the strap, and tells techs to use the maker's recommended strap and apply insulation tape or foam liberally. A Heatcraft article in ACHR News says to use metal clamps, not tape or wire ties. Sporlan's field notice also says mastic raises heat transfer to about four times that of mechanical mounting alone. On lines below freezing, use insulation that does not absorb water so ice cannot form around the bulb.

Internal or external equalizer

Bulletin 10-9 explains that an internal equalizer senses evaporator pressure at the valve outlet, which only works on single-circuit coils with small pressure drop, no more than the equivalent of a 2°F saturation change. An external equalizer is a small tube from the diaphragm to the suction line, so the valve sees pressure at the bulb instead. Sporlan requires one when the evaporator has a refrigerant distributor, and Bulletin 10-10 says externally equalized valves should always be used with air conditioning charges. An "E" in a Sporlan type name marks an external equalizer.

Where it connects matters. Bulletin 10-11 says to tap the line where it best reflects suction pressure at the bulb, generally just downstream of the bulb, and on the evaporator side of any evaporator pressure or temperature regulating valve. The line must be free of crimps and solder and must never be capped. Emerson adds that the tap should be placed so it cannot siphon oil out of the suction line.

Adjusting static superheat

Turning the adjusting stem changes spring pressure, and that changes static superheat. Sporlan, Danfoss and Emerson agree on direction: clockwise raises superheat, counter-clockwise lowers it. Remove the seal cap first, and replace it after. Sporlan Bulletin 10-11 says to make no more than one turn at a time and that up to 30 minutes may pass before the system settles. Emerson says to wait about 20 minutes between adjustments and to readjust at the lowest expected evaporating temperature.

How much change per turn? Sporlan's bulletins give no number. Danfoss's installation sheet for TUA and TUAE valves says ¼ turn = 1°F and to adjust a quarter turn at a time. A Danfoss article on its TR 6 valve says one turn clockwise is about 1°F, so the figure is valve-specific. Emerson's sheet for its Thermo valves lists a per-turn range that depends on the valve and evaporating temperature, for example 0.2 K at +10°C up to 0.8 K at -40°C for a TCLE on R-404A. Use the number for your valve, and recheck after each step.

Worked example (hypothetical)

This is a made-up system to show the arithmetic. Say a residential air conditioner with an R-410A system and a Danfoss TUA-type valve whose sheet says ¼ turn = 1°F. The equipment maker's target is 10°F superheat.

  1. Gauge reads 120 psig suction. A published R-410A chart lists 120 psig as 40.5°F saturation (BPA-hosted chart, source 8).
  2. A probe on the suction line a few inches from the bulb reads 52.5°F.
  3. Superheat = line temperature minus saturation = 52.5 − 40.5 = 12.0°F.
  4. Needed change = 12.0 − 10 = 2.0°F lower.
  5. Turns = 2.0°F ÷ 1°F per ¼ turn = 2 quarter turns, counter-clockwise (lowers superheat).
  6. Make it two separate ¼ turn steps, wait for the system to settle after each, and remeasure. Stop when the reading is at target.

This is only valid after the checks in the next section pass.

When not to adjust

Sporlan says the factory setting is correct for the majority of applications, that a valve should not be adjusted unnecessarily, and that the other possible causes of wrong superheat should be checked first. Many original-equipment valves, especially residential air conditioning and heat pump valves, cannot be adjusted in the field; Sporlan marks its non-adjustable valves with an N prefix. ClimateMaster's bulletin says most of its TXVs are non-adjustable and warns that removing the bottom cap does not give adjustment access and can cause damage or injury. Danfoss says to adjust only after other corrective measures are done. Bryan Orr writes in ACHR News that when superheat is far out of range, adjusting is very unlikely to fix it.

How often is the valve the culprit? An Emerson representative told ACHR News in 2006 that more than half of the valves returned from the field were not defective. That is one manufacturer's observation, not a survey. ClimateMaster's bulletin says to verify airflow and charge (through superheat and subcooling) before condemning a valve, and Orr adds a full, properly subcooled liquid supply and a correctly mounted bulb. Our symptom table and drier and sight glass lesson help sort those out.

Hunting

Hunting is the superheat swinging up and down as the valve opens and closes. Bulletin 10-11 lists causes: system traits like evaporator length and load, an oversized valve, bulb location including a bulb in a trap, maldistribution of refrigerant or load, moisture, and the superheat setting itself. Sporlan says moderate hunting without floodback is insignificant. If the setting is the cause, turn the stem clockwise one turn at a time; if hunting stops or eases, back off counter-clockwise one turn at a time to find the lowest superheat that stays stable.

Fixed orifice or TXV: what changes

Our metering lesson notes that pistons are usually charged by superheat and TXVs by subcooling, but the manufacturer's method governs. That is why the spring setting is a last step on a TXV system. See Metering Devices: TXV and Piston for the two types, Superheat and Subcooling Basics for the readings, and Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return for commercial coils. Danfoss says the superheat target comes from the system manufacturer.

Field card: TXV Bulb Placement, Equalizers and Adjusting Superheat

Bulb, strap, equalizer first. Stem last.

  • Forces: bulb pressure opens; equalizer plus spring close (Sporlan 10-9).
  • Bulb: 4 or 8 o'clock on lines 7/8 in. and up; never in or after a trap; not on the bottom.
  • Strap tight, clean pipe, insulate. Metal clamps, not tape.
  • External equalizer: just downstream of the bulb, never capped, no crimps.
  • Clockwise = more superheat. Counter-clockwise = less.
  • Small steps (one turn max for Sporlan; ¼ turn for Danfoss TUA). Wait 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Many OEM valves are non-adjustable. Check airflow, charge and restrictions first.
