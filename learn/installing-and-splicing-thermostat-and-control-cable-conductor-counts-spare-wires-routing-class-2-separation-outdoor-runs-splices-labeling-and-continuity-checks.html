Installing and Splicing Thermostat and Control Cable: Spares, Routing, Class 2 Separation, Splices and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most thermostat problems we chase are not bad thermostats. They are bad cable: a staple through the jacket, a twisted joint buried in an attic, a conductor chewed by a mouse, or a run with no spare wire when a new control needs one. This lesson covers how to choose and pull control cable, route it safely, splice it when you must, label it, and prove it works with a meter.

Pick the cable and pull the spares

Thermostat cable for residential work is usually 18 AWG. A residential building-code summary we read says thermostat cable must be at least 18 AWG and that thermostat wiring is a Class 2 circuit fed from a listed transformer limited to 40 VA (Source 1). Class 2 means the power source is limited in energy, which is why the cable can be thinner and lighter than line-voltage wiring. Our lessons on control transformers and Class 2 limits cover the VA side.

Cable comes in conductor counts, such as 18/5 (five conductors) and 18/8 (eight). A basic single-stage cooling and gas heat system needs only four or five: R, W, Y, G and C. See the terminal letters lesson for what each does. Heat pumps, two-stage equipment, humidifiers, zone dampers and outdoor sensors each want more. Our own rule of thumb is to buy cable with at least two more conductors than the job needs today. The cable cost difference is small, but a missing conductor later means a new pull through a finished wall. Spares also save you when one conductor is nicked: you swap to the spare and move on. A spare is also how a thermostat that needs a common wire gets one; see how smart thermostats get power. Land the spares on terminals or tape them back neatly at both ends so the next tech can find them.

Routing through walls and attics

The same code summary says thermostat cable can be stapled in framing bays with appropriate cable staples, and that conduit is not generally required in residential work but may be needed where physical protection is needed, such as exposed concrete walls or exterior runs (Source 1). Our practice on the staples:

  • Use staples made for thin cable, and set them so the cable sits snug but can still slide. A crushed jacket can look fine and fail months later.
  • Drive staples straight, not at an angle, and never staple two cables under one staple if you can avoid it.
  • Pull through bored holes with a smooth edge. Do not drag the cable across sharp drywall screw tips or metal edges.
  • In attics, keep the cable out of foot traffic and off the ductwork seams. Sagging cable on a flexible duct gets stepped on and pinched.
  • Leave a service loop at the air handler and behind the thermostat.

Keep Class 2 apart from line voltage

This is a code rule and a troubleshooting rule. The electrical code covers Class 2 circuits in Article 725. Section 725.136 says Class 2 and Class 3 circuits are not to share a cable or raceway with light and power conductors unless they are separated within it. A city building department bulletin we read repeats this and notes that ordinary metal-clad cable does not give the needed separation (Source 2). A trade article we read adds that inside a unit an internal barrier provides the separation, and that the thermostat cable should not ride in the same raceway as the unit branch-circuit conductors (Source 3). The residential summary says thermostat wire may share a framing bay with power wiring only if the two do not touch or bundle and exit through separate holes (Source 1). The exact exceptions change by code edition, so check the edition your inspector uses.

In practice: do not tie the control cable to a 240 V feed with a strap, do not share a knockout or box without a fixed barrier, and cross power cables at a right angle when you must. Inside the air handler and condenser, keep low-voltage conductors to their own side. Mixed bundles are also a safety risk: a fault in the power conductor can put line voltage on a thermostat.

Outdoor and sunlight runs to the condenser

The residential summary states that thermostat cable is not rated for exterior use or burial; outdoor or buried runs need cable rated for wet locations or direct burial, or conduit (Source 1). Check the jacket marking, because ordinary indoor jacket can turn brittle in sunlight. Run the last stretch to the condenser in conduit or use a cable marked for outdoor and sunlight use, add a drip loop, and seal where it enters the cabinet or wall. Do not leave a control cable lying on the ground where a mower or trimmer can reach it.

Splices: avoid them, and put them where you can reach them

Our first choice is no splice: pull one continuous cable. If you must join, the residential summary says splices in thermostat wire must be made in a listed enclosure or with a listed connector, and open splices in wall cavities are not permitted (Source 1). Our experience with the common methods:

  • Twist-on connectors. Fast and common. Strip evenly, twist the wires together a half turn first, then seat them fully in the connector. 18 AWG wire is thin, so use a small connector and tug-test each wire.
  • Crimp butt splices. Give a tidy, even joint with the right crimp tool. A cheap squeeze with pliers is not a crimp. Heat-shrink versions seal against moisture.
  • Soldered and heat-shrunk joints. Make a durable, low-resistance joint when done well, but a cold solder joint or a joint that stiffens the wire right at the edge of the solder can break under vibration. Use heat-shrink over the joint and leave slack on both sides.

Where splices should live: in an accessible junction box, in the equipment cabinet, or at a terminal strip, never inside a wall or buried in insulation. Label the box. A splice you cannot find is a fault you cannot fix.

Label both ends

Follow the standard color code where the cable allows it, but remember that colors are only convention and not every installer matches them. Our habit: wrap a label at both ends, write the terminal letter for each color (white = W, and so on), and note the date and any change from the usual colors. Photograph the terminal strip before disconnecting an old thermostat or board. If you land a spare for a future C wire, tag it "spare" or "C".

Damage to look for

  • Rodents. A builder trade article we read says rodents chewed ground-level low-voltage wiring and describes stainless braided sleeving on the first couple of feet above the ground as a fix (Source 4). See our pests lesson.
  • Staples. A staple that is too tight or driven through a conductor can cause an intermittent short or open.
  • Line-set abrasion. Cable taped to a refrigerant line set can rub through where it vibrates against a bracket or wall. Keep it off the copper, or protect it where it passes through.

Prove the run: ohms first, then volts under load

Before you energize, remove power and disconnect the cable at both ends. Twist one pair together at the far end and read the loop resistance at the near end. Each conductor should read low and the same as its neighbors, and no conductor should read open or touch another one or ground. Then reconnect, restore power, call for cooling and read AC volts at the condenser between Y and C with the contactor pulled in, and compare it with R to C at the transformer. A loaded drop of a volt or so is normal; a lot more means a thin or damaged conductor or a poor splice. This is where we use the numbers from the voltage-drop lesson.

Worked example (our own): a long 18 AWG run

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of a real job.

Assumptions:

  • Cable length, air handler to condenser: 200 ft one way (assumed).
  • Resistance of 18 AWG copper: 7.95 ohms per 1,000 ft at 75°C, from a university extension conductor table (Source 5).
  • Contactor coil current when pulled in: 0.25 A (our assumption; read the coil rating on your own equipment).
  • Source voltage at the air handler under load: 24.0 V (assumed).

Step 1, round-trip length. Current goes out on Y and back on C, so 200 x 2 = 400 ft.

Step 2, loop resistance. 400 / 1,000 x 7.95 = 3.18 ohms.

Step 3, voltage lost. V = I x R = 0.25 x 3.18 = 0.80 V (rounded).

Step 4, voltage at the coil. 24.0 - 0.80 = 23.2 V, a drop of about 3.3 percent. That is fine for most coils.

Step 5, add a bad splice. Suppose a corroded splice on the Y conductor adds an assumed 10 ohms. Total resistance becomes 3.18 + 10 = 13.18 ohms, so the drop is 0.25 x 13.18 = 3.3 V and the coil sees 24.0 - 3.3 = 20.7 V. Many contactors still pull in at that voltage, but the margin is gone and the coil may chatter on a weak day. The ohmmeter check would have flagged it: the loop would read about 13 ohms instead of about 3.

Field card: Installing and splicing control cable

Continuous cable, spares, separation, labels, then meter it.

  • 18 AWG is the usual size; buy two or more conductors than you need.
  • Staple snug, not tight; no crushed jackets, sharp edges or foot traffic.
  • Keep Class 2 apart from line voltage: separate holes, no shared bundle or box without a barrier (Article 725, section 725.136).
  • Outdoor runs need rated cable or conduit; indoor cable is not for sun or burial.
  • Splice only in a listed connector or enclosure, in a reachable place, never in a wall.
  • Label both ends; photograph the terminals first.
  • Check loop ohms with power off, then volts Y to C under load.
  • Example: 400 ft round trip = 3.18 ohms; at 0.25 A that is 0.80 V.
