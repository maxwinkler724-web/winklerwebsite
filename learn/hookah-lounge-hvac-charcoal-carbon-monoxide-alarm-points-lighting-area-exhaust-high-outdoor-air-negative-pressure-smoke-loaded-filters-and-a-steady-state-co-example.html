Hookah Lounge HVAC: Charcoal Carbon Monoxide, Exhaust and Outdoor Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A hookah is a water pipe, and the tobacco or herbal mix in it is heated by burning charcoal. That charcoal is a combustion source with no flue. In a lounge full of them, the air system is the only thing standing between the room and a carbon monoxide problem. This lesson covers what to look at and what to report.

Ventilation does not make smoke safe

Read this first. Moving air dilutes carbon monoxide (CO). It does not make tobacco smoke safe to breathe, and nothing here says otherwise. A university poison center news release on this topic advises people to avoid smoking and smoke exposure. We also give no legal advice about smoking or lounge rules; ask the local health department or the owner's attorney. Our job as techs is the air system and the safety of the people working in the building.

Why charcoal is the CO problem

A university news release from 2018 says hookahs are water pipes heated by burning charcoal, and that the charcoal releases CO that is inhaled along with the smoke. It says small spaces, many people smoking together and poor ventilation raise the risk. A 2024 university poison center release adds that smoke comes from both the flavored tobacco and the charcoal, which raises room CO. It also cites a 2021 study in which CO exposure rose nearly 12-fold after an hour-long session with 200 patrons. Herbal mixes are heated the same way, so a tobacco-free label does not remove the charcoal.

The same 2018 release says the medical literature then described about 100 hookah-related CO poisoning cases worldwide, and that the number was growing. A federal health agency report summarizing earlier studies of hookah workers describes a worker who lit 30 to 40 pipes and had a blood carboxyhemoglobin level of 33.8 percent, and a group of New York workers whose exhaled CO rose from 8.3 ppm before a shift to 49.4 ppm after it. The same report notes high fine-particle levels in the waterpipe cafes it reviewed.

The federal health agency describes CO poisoning symptoms as headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, often flu-like, and says sleeping or intoxicated people may die before they notice. In a lounge, a staff member with a headache every Friday night is a data point, not a coincidence. Passing that on is part of the report you write.

The limits we measure against

The federal workplace safety agency lists a permissible exposure limit for CO of 50 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average. The same chemical page lists a federal recommended limit of 35 ppm for up to a 10-hour workday, a recommended ceiling of 200 ppm, and an immediately dangerous level of 1,200 ppm. Those are workplace numbers for staff. They are not a promise that a lower level is comfortable or safe for customers, who also breathe the smoke.

CO alarms and fixed detectors

Home-style battery alarms are not the whole answer in a commercial room. Fixed CO detectors, wired to a panel or to the building controls, let the lounge raise an alarm, start extra exhaust and tell staff to act. Where the alarm points go is a decision for the owner, the installer, the maker's instructions and the authority having jurisdiction, so we do not invent setpoints here. What we can check:

  • Detectors exist in the main room, near the lighting area and in any back room where charcoal is stored or burned.
  • They sit at breathing height, away from supply diffusers that dilute the sample and away from doors that blow outdoor air onto them.
  • Each has a test record and a replacement date from the maker, since sensors wear out. The federal health agency says to replace home detectors per the maker's instructions or about every 5 years.
  • An alarm does something: a horn and light, a staff procedure, and ideally the exhaust going to high speed. An alarm that nobody hears is a decoration.

For sensor types and bump tests see Gas and CO Detection in Boiler and Mechanical Rooms, and for the home side see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. We do not repeat them here.

The charcoal lighting area

Most CO is released when charcoal is lit and the first burn is at its dirtiest. The worker report above is the evidence for why that area matters. Good practice is a defined lighting spot with its own exhaust, a hood or a capture inlet close to the coals, ducted outdoors and not recirculated. As a tech, confirm that the fan runs, that the duct is not crushed or disconnected, that the discharge is away from intakes and doors, and that there is a path for replacement air. A lighting area with a dead fan next to a CO detector that is also dead is the finding that goes at the top of your report.

Outdoor air, exhaust and pressure

The room needs outdoor air brought in on purpose, and exhaust taken out on purpose, with the exhaust somewhat larger so the lounge stays slightly negative to adjacent spaces. That way smoke leaks in from neighbors, rather than out into them, around shared walls, ceiling plenums and doors. Neighbor smoke complaints are a pressure and leakage problem you can often see with a smoke pencil at the door. The balance has to be done with the exhaust running, in the real operating mode, and checked again after any change.

Two cautions. First, do not close the outdoor air damper to save energy in winter; that is the single move that lets CO climb. Second, a strongly negative room pulls air through the nearest gap, which may be a flue, a back door or a neighbor's wall. See HVAC for Bars, Taverns and Nightclubs for the general crowd-air side, and Casino Floor HVAC for how a different smoky venue handles it. Intake louvers are covered in Outdoor Air Intake Louvers.

Heat and filters

Burning charcoal adds sensible heat right at the tables, and more exhaust means more outdoor air to condition, so a lounge often runs warm or has hot spots near the pipes even when the equipment is sized well. The next problem is residue. Smoke leaves a sticky film that loads filters and coils faster than a normal commercial room, so the pressure drop across filters climbs sooner and airflow, including the outdoor air share, drops with it. Check filter condition and the gauge at each visit, and do not assume a filter schedule written for an office fits. A wet or tarry coil is also a call-back waiting to happen.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own illustration using a simple mass balance. It is not a measurement, and the emission rate is our assumption, not a sourced figure. A real lounge needs a real test with a calibrated meter.

  • Lounge volume: 4,000 sq ft floor at a 10 ft ceiling gives 40,000 cu ft (our assumption).
  • Pipes burning at once: 20 (our assumption).
  • CO emission: 0.0005 cu ft per minute of pure CO per pipe (our assumption).
  • Outdoor air has no CO, and the room air is well mixed (our assumptions).

Step 1, total emission: 20 pipes x 0.0005 cfm = 0.010 cfm of CO.

Step 2, steady-state concentration = emission / airflow. At 2,000 cfm outdoor air: 0.010 / 2,000 = 0.000005, which is 5 ppm.

Step 3, at 400 cfm: 0.010 / 400 = 0.000025 = 25 ppm. At 200 cfm: 0.010 / 200 = 0.000050 = 50 ppm.

Step 4, compare. Under these assumptions, 200 cfm of outdoor air puts the room right at the 50 ppm 8-hour federal workplace limit, and anything less is over it. 2,000 cfm is a tenth of that. The room volume does not change the steady-state answer, it only sets how fast it gets there: the time constant is volume / airflow, 40,000 / 2,000 = 20 minutes at 2,000 cfm. If the lighting area makes short peaks, those peaks are higher than this average, and so is the exposure of the person standing at the coals. The lesson: a stuck-closed outdoor air damper or a dead exhaust fan can move a room from comfortable to over the limit with no change in how many pipes are burning, and this still says nothing about the smoke itself.

What an HVAC tech should check and report

  • Outdoor air damper position and actual airflow, in writing, at the operating setting.
  • Exhaust fans (room and lighting area) running, belts good, ducts connected, discharge clear of intakes.
  • Room pressure versus neighbors with the exhaust on; note any smoke path.
  • CO detector presence, location, test records and age; whether an alarm starts the exhaust.
  • Filter and coil condition, with residue noted.
  • Any combustion equipment in the building: flue draft and combustion air when the room is strongly negative.
  • Heat complaints near the pipes and unit capacity.

Report what you found to the owner in writing and keep a copy. If a detector alarms or anyone feels ill, get everyone outside and call 911, as the university poison center advises.

Field card: Hookah Lounge HVAC

Charcoal burns without a flue; dilute, capture, detect, and say so in writing.

  • Ventilation dilutes CO; it does not make smoke safe.
  • Check outdoor air, exhaust, lighting-area fan and room pressure with everything running.
  • Fixed CO detectors: location, test record, age, and what the alarm triggers.
  • Mass balance: concentration = emission / airflow.
  • Our example: 20 pipes, assumed 0.0005 cfm CO each, 200 cfm outdoor air = 50 ppm.
  • Smoke residue loads filters and coils fast; check the gauge every visit.
