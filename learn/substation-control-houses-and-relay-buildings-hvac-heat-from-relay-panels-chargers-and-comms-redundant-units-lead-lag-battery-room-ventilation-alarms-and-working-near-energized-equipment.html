Substation Control House HVAC: Relay Heat, Lead-Lag Units, Battery Rooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A small building at the edge of an electric substation holds the protective relays, communications gear and battery system that decide when a breaker opens. Nobody works there day to day, and the equipment must keep running when the weather does not cooperate. This lesson covers how that building is cooled and kept dry, why two air conditioners are the norm, how the battery room is ventilated, and how an HVAC tech works around energized equipment.

Check the owner's rules

Check the site owner's rules. Every utility and industrial owner writes its own access, escort and work rules, and the equipment makers' data sheets set the real temperature and humidity limits. This page is general education built from federal and university pages read in 2026. It is not a procedure for any particular site.

What the building does and what is inside

A federal grid report describes protective relays as devices that trip a circuit breaker when a grid problem appears, and says they can act within one to two electrical cycles. It also describes protection schemes that isolate a fault automatically so the damage and the outage stay small. The same report notes the shift from electromechanical hardware to digital devices with local intelligence, and from limited one-way analog links to two-way digital communications networks. The practical result for us is a room full of electronics that run all day: relay panels, communications racks, and a battery charger feeding the station battery.

A relay that cannot do its job because the room overheated or fogged up is not a comfort problem. It is a protection problem. A university-hosted conference paper on redundancy makes the same point from the protection side: duplicate the relays so one failure does not leave a fault uncleared, and monitor the trip circuit so a hidden failure becomes an alarm. The cooling system deserves the same thinking.

Where the heat comes from

  • Relay panels. Many panels, each a modest load, running constantly.
  • Communications gear. Radios, switches, multiplexers and similar equipment fed from the station dc supply.
  • Battery charger. It does not make heat from the battery. It wastes a small share of what it converts, so its loss is its input minus the dc power it delivers.
  • The building itself. Sun on walls and roof, and any leakage through doors and penetrations.
  • People and lights. Brief and occasional, so they rarely set the design size.

Nearly all the electrical power that enters electronics leaves as heat in the room. We convert watts to Btu per hour with 3.412 (our own unit conversion). The worked example below uses it.

Why steady and dry matters

A national laboratory guide on humidity in computing rooms explains the two failure directions. Air that is too dry raises the risk of electrostatic discharge, although the guide calls the mechanism not well understood. Air that is too humid lets moisture combine with gaseous or particle contamination, causing corrosion and failures from dust that holds water. The guide also says cooling coil surfaces should stay above the room dew point so no water condenses out, and that rapid swings in temperature and humidity can cause failures even when neither limit is crossed. The conference paper above lists water intrusion and condensation as causes of bus faults.

As a yardstick only: that guide gives a recommended range for computing equipment of 15.8 F to 59 F dew point with relative humidity no higher than 60 percent, and a second national laboratory guide gives a default recommended air-intake range of 65 F to 80 F. A relay maker may publish a different range for relays. The relay and communications data sheets, and the owner's design documents, are what you work to.

What that means for the HVAC design: hold a steady setpoint with a narrow control band, avoid short cycling that swings the room, keep supply air from blowing cold air onto cabinets, and do not let the coil run below the room dew point.

Two units, lead-lag, and the one-unit-down test

Control houses commonly use two wall-mount or packaged units, each sized to carry the whole load alone. A lead-lag controller picks which unit answers the room sensor and swaps the lead role on a timer, so run hours are shared. If the room keeps rising, the lag unit starts too. If one unit is down, the other still holds the room. For the controller settings, economizer choices and alarm contacts in an unmanned shelter, see the linked telecom shelter lesson instead of repeating it here.

Battery room ventilation

If the station battery is the vented lead-acid type, the room it sits in needs ventilation. The federal electrical safety rule for general industry requires enough diffusion and ventilation of gas from storage batteries to keep explosive mixtures from building up. Air rates, hydrogen, and lithium-ion thermal runaway are covered in the linked battery room lesson. For HVAC, the point is to keep battery room air away from the relay area, run the exhaust as the owner's design requires, and treat a failed exhaust fan as an alarm item, not a convenience item.

Alarms back to the operator

The building is unmanned, so alarms are how it speaks. Common points are high room temperature, low room temperature, one-unit fault, both-units fault, exhaust fan failure, and (where fitted) smoke or door contacts. The control house already has the communications path; the alarm contacts are wired to the owner's equipment, not ours. We wire and test only what the owner's drawings and escort allow. The redundancy paper's point about turning hidden failures into alarms is the reason a loss of one unit must be reported even though the room is still cool.

Dust and insect screening

These are our own practical suggestions, not figures from a cited source. Wall units breathe outdoor air through condenser coils, so screens and filters collect pollen, seeds, leaves and insects. Plan for coil cleaning visits, keep vegetation back from the units, and make sure any outdoor-air inlet, relief opening or conduit opening in the wall is screened or sealed. A plugged condenser looks like a failed unit to the controller, so dirty coils show up as high-temperature alarms.

Working near energized equipment

The federal rule for electric power work sets the frame. The host employer must tell contract employers about the installation characteristics that affect safety and the hazards it knows about, and contractors must train their people on what they learn and coordinate work rules with the host. Before a job the person in charge must brief the crew on hazards, procedures, precautions, energy-source controls and protective equipment. The rule also defines a qualified employee as someone trained to identify exposed live parts, determine nominal voltage, keep minimum approach distances, and use protective equipment. A person is not qualified until trained.

For an HVAC tech that means: go in only with the owner's permission and escort, follow the owner's rules for access and tools, do not open panels or touch anything outside the HVAC equipment you were sent for, and stop and ask when the work is near an energized part. Our arc flash and shock lesson is linked below, but the owner's rules govern. Never assume that a wall unit's disconnect can be worked on without the owner's say-so, because the unit may be fed from the station service panel.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Every input is an assumption for teaching, not from a cited source.

Assumptions: six relay panels at 250 W each; one communications rack at 600 W; a battery charger that is 90 percent efficient and carries only the 2,100 W of dc load already counted for the panels and rack; envelope and sun gain of 4,000 Btu/h at design; a 20 percent margin; two 1.5-ton (18,000 Btu/h nominal) wall units that give 80 percent of nominal at the hot design day.

  1. Relay panels: 6 x 250 W = 1,500 W.
  2. Communications: 600 W. Dc load = 1,500 + 600 = 2,100 W.
  3. Charger input = 2,100 / 0.90 = 2,333 W, so charger loss = 2,333 - 2,100 = 233 W.
  4. Total electrical heat = 1,500 + 600 + 233 = 2,333 W. Times 3.412 = 7,961 Btu/h.
  5. Add envelope and sun gain: 7,961 + 4,000 = 11,961 Btu/h (about 1.0 ton).
  6. Add 20 percent margin: 11,961 x 1.20 = 14,353 Btu/h.
  7. Each unit delivers 18,000 x 0.80 = 14,400 Btu/h, which is at least 14,353. Each unit alone carries the load, so one-unit backup is met. The spare is only 47 Btu/h, so a real design would check the actual unit data on the hot day.

Lead-lag hours over an assumed 30-day month (720 hours), with weekly rotation. Assume the lead unit runs 55 percent of the time it leads and the lag unit runs 8 percent of the time it lags. Unit A leads days 1 to 7, 15 to 21 and 29 to 30 (16 days, 384 hours). Unit B leads the other 14 days (336 hours).

  1. Unit A: lead 384 x 0.55 = 211.2 h; lag 336 x 0.08 = 26.9 h; total 238.1 h.
  2. Unit B: lead 336 x 0.55 = 184.8 h; lag 384 x 0.08 = 30.7 h; total 215.5 h.
  3. Check: 238.1 + 215.5 = 453.6 h, and 720 x 0.55 + 720 x 0.08 = 396 + 57.6 = 453.6 h.
  4. With no rotation, unit A would run 396 h and unit B 57.6 h. Weekly rotation brings them within about 22.6 h of each other.

If one unit fails, the other runs far more hours, so the failed unit should be repaired quickly. The rotation interval only shares hours while both are healthy.

Field card: Substation Control House HVAC

Two units, each able to carry the whole room, with alarms that report the first failure.

  • Nearly all electrical power in the room becomes heat: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Charger heat is its loss, not its output.
  • Keep the room steady and dry; condensation and corrosion hurt electronics.
  • Size each unit for the full load, then test the one-unit-down case.
  • Lead-lag rotation shares run hours only while both units are healthy.
  • Battery room: ventilate so gas cannot build up, and alarm the exhaust.
  • Screen and clean for dust and insects.
  • Enter only with the owner's permission and escort, and follow the owner's rules.
