Living Near a Busy Road: Keeping Traffic Pollution Out of Your Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

If your home sits close to a highway, a rail line or a truck route, the air outside carries fine particles and nitrogen dioxide from engines. Some of it gets in. This lesson covers what a homeowner and a tech can do with windows, filters, fan settings, intake placement, sealing and a bedroom air cleaner, and it ends with a worked example we made up to check the numbers.

What is in the air near a busy road

A federal research page says more than 45 million people nationwide live within 300 feet of a major road or other big transportation facility, and links traffic pollution to lung and heart problems. A federal booklet on cutting near-road exposure says the highest levels are generally within the first 500 feet of the road and fall to background levels by about 2,000 feet. Those figures come from the booklet, and they are a rule of thumb, not a guarantee for your lot. Wind direction, sound walls and trees all change it.

Two pollutants matter most here. Fine particles are tiny specks that pass deep into the lungs. Federal pages link particle pollution to aggravated asthma and reduced lung function. Nitrogen dioxide forms from burning fuel in cars, trucks and buses, and the federal page says short exposure can irritate airways and worsen asthma, especially in children and older adults. A particle filter does little about a gas like nitrogen dioxide. The federal air cleaner guide says most filters are built for either particles or gases, and that there is no widely used rating system for gas removal. So for the gas, the main tools are keeping the leaks closed and keeping windows shut when traffic is heavy.

How outdoor air gets in

The federal indoor air page lists three paths: infiltration through openings, joints and cracks, natural ventilation through open windows, and mechanical ventilation. Infiltration and window airflow both depend on wind and the indoor to outdoor temperature difference. On a calm mild day, little air moves. On a windy or very cold day, more leaks in. That is why sealing matters most on the side facing the road.

Windows: shut on bad days

The federal near-road booklet says to keep windows and doors closed during peak traffic, such as morning and evening rush hours, and notes that open windows can overwhelm the benefit of a filtered HVAC system. At home, that means opening windows when traffic is light, such as mid-morning or late at night, if you need fresh air, and keeping them shut when trucks idle or traffic is heavy. If you need cooling at night, near a road the road-side windows are the ones to leave closed.

Filters and fan settings

The federal air cleaner guide says furnace and HVAC filters only filter air while the system is running, and that the system usually runs less than 25% of the time in heating and cooling seasons. Running the fan longer cleans more air but, the guide notes, increases electricity use. It also says to use the highest-rated filter your fan and filter slot can handle. The booklet reports that medium-efficiency filters cut particles by about 20% to 65% and higher-performance filters by 74% to 97% compared with outdoor levels. These are results the booklet reports for its settings, not a promise for a house.

Two cautions from the trade side. First, a thicker, tighter filter adds resistance, so confirm with your tech that the blower and ductwork can take it;. Second, a filter that sits loose in its rack lets air go around it, so a snug fit matters as much as the rating. If the system has no way to run the fan without heating or cooling, ask your tech what fan setting your equipment allows.

Where the fresh-air intake should face

Many homes have no mechanical fresh-air intake, and that is fine. If yours does, or you are adding one, the federal booklet says to locate air intakes away from roadways, bus idling areas, drop-off zones and other sources. It gives no distance or height, so we do not either. In practice that means the side of the house away from the road, and not near a driveway where vehicles idle or a dryer or garage vent. Our whole-house ventilation lesson covers the types of ventilators and intake placement. A ventilator brings in untreated outdoor air, so on a bad traffic day, a tech can talk with you about running it less or adding filtration on the intake.

Sealing leaks

The federal booklet recommends sealing the building envelope against infiltration where mechanical ventilation provides the air, and keeping ductwork tested for leaks. Leaky return ducts in an attic, crawl space or garage can pull polluted air in, so duct sealing helps. Start with the road-side wall: window and door weatherstripping, gaps around pipes and wires, and the rim area in the basement. Our drafty doors and windows lesson and duct sealing lesson cover the methods. The booklet also notes that tightening a house changes pressure and moisture behavior, so do not seal and forget; if you tighten up a lot, ask about fresh air.

Noise and window units

Road and rail noise often pushes people to close windows, which also helps the air. A window air conditioner leaves a gap around the cabinet and is a hole through the wall. In our experience the gap seals and the unit's own vent damper matter, and a window unit does not bring in filtered air. Near a road, we would favor a ductless or central system that leaves the window shut. This is our own field opinion rather than a sourced finding, so treat it as a starting point for a talk with your tech.

Portable air cleaners for bedrooms

A portable cleaner filters one room. The federal guide says the clean air delivery rate, called CADR, tells you how many particles it can filter, that the rating covers particles only, and that you should size the unit to the room, using a larger unit for ceilings over 8 feet. The guide's chart lists a minimum of 65 cfm for 100 square feet and 130 cfm for 200 square feet, assuming an 8 foot ceiling. It also advises against devices that intentionally make ozone; the booklet says the same. A university study of a room beside a busy highway used portable filters, windows and doors to set low, medium and high exposures for 77 people over three two-hour sessions. Blood pressure rose with ultrafine particle exposure and the filters reduced that rise, by under 3 mm of mercury between high and low. That is a small study of short stays, so we use it only to show filters do change what people breathe.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any real home.

Assumptions: a bedroom 12 ft by 14 ft with an 8 ft ceiling; a target of 5 air changes per hour of cleaned air; a house of 2,000 square feet with 8 ft ceilings; a blower moving 1,200 cfm; a filter that removes 80% of fine particles in one pass; outdoor air leaking in at 0.3 air changes per hour; fan running 25% of the time in the normal case, or all the time in the other case.

Step 1, bedroom volume. 12 x 14 x 8 = 1,344 cubic feet.

Step 2, air to clean per hour. 5 x 1,344 = 6,720 cubic feet per hour.

Step 3, convert to cfm. 6,720 / 60 = 112 cfm. So the bedroom needs a cleaner with a particle CADR of about 112 cfm or more. Check against the federal chart: 168 square feet is between 100 sq ft (65 cfm) and 200 sq ft (130 cfm), so roughly 110 to 115 cfm is consistent. The chart's 65 cfm for 100 sq ft works out to 65 x 60 / 800 = 4.9 air changes per hour, which matches our 5.

Step 4, whole-house filter. House volume = 2,000 x 8 = 16,000 cubic feet. While the fan runs, clean air delivered = 1,200 x 0.80 = 960 cfm. With the fan on all the time that is 960 x 60 / 16,000 = 3.6 clean air changes per hour. At a 25% duty cycle the average is 960 x 0.25 = 240 cfm, or 240 x 60 / 16,000 = 0.9 per hour.

Step 5, compare indoor particle level to outdoors. In a simple model that ignores particles settling on surfaces and any indoor sources, the indoor-to-outdoor ratio is leakage rate divided by (leakage rate plus cleaning rate). Bedroom cleaner: 0.3 / (0.3 + 5) = 0.057, about 94% lower. Whole house, fan always on: 0.3 / (0.3 + 3.6) = 0.077, about 92% lower. Whole house at 25%: 0.3 / (0.3 + 0.9) = 0.25, about 75% lower.

What it means: in this made-up case, running the fan all the time gets the whole house close to what the bedroom cleaner does for one room, while a normal duty cycle cleans much less. Real homes leak more, particles settle, and filters do not hit 80% on every size, so treat the output as a way to compare options, not a forecast. The cleaner plus a closed bedroom door is a good fit for sleeping hours; the whole-house filter helps every room. Field card: Living Near a Busy Road: Keeping Traffic Pollution Out of Your Home

Seal the road side, filter what is left, and keep windows shut in rush hour.

  • Highest levels are nearest the road, generally within the first 500 feet.
  • Keep windows and doors closed at peak traffic; filters do not fix open windows.
  • Use the highest-rated filter the system can take, snug in its rack.
  • Furnace filters work only while the fan runs; check what fan settings your equipment allows.
  • Fresh-air intake goes away from the road and from idling spots.
  • Seal road-side leaks and return ducts.
  • Bedroom cleaner: volume x air changes / 60 = cfm of CADR needed.
  • CADR covers particles, not nitrogen dioxide.
