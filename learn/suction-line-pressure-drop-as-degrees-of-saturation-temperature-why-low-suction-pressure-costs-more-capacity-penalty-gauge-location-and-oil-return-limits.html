Suction Line Pressure Drop in Degrees: Why Low Pressure Costs More

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gauge reads pressure, but the evaporator cares about temperature. When refrigerant vapor loses pressure to friction in the suction line, the compressor sees a colder saturation temperature than the coil made. This lesson shows how to turn a psi drop into degrees, why the same psi costs more degrees on a low-temperature system, and how oil return limits how small the line can be.

Pressure drop in degrees

Inside the evaporator, liquid and vapor share one saturation pressure and temperature. As vapor leaves the coil and travels to the compressor, friction in the pipe, fittings and valves takes away some pressure. Less pressure means a lower saturation temperature for that vapor. So a drop of a few psi along the suction line is the same thing as a drop of a few degrees of saturation temperature between the evaporator outlet and the compressor inlet. Design work usually states the allowed loss in degrees for this reason: the degrees are what the refrigeration effect actually feels.

Why the same psi costs more degrees when suction is low

The saturation curve is steep at high pressure and flat at low pressure. At a warm evaporating temperature, it takes many psi to move the saturation temperature one degree. At a cold evaporating temperature, only a little pressure moves it a degree. Divide the psi lost by the psi per degree and the low-pressure system comes out worse every time.

A university conference paper on pressure loss in refrigerant heat exchangers makes the same point in its own words: the lower the saturation temperature, the greater the saturation temperature drop for a given pressure loss. For a 15 kPa loss (about 2.2 psi, our conversion) it reports 0.63 °C for condensing conditions and 3.48 °C for evaporating conditions. That paper analyzes heat exchangers, not suction lines, so we use it for the trend only.

The capacity and efficiency penalty

When the compressor inlet sits at a colder saturation temperature than the coil, the vapor arrives less dense. A compressor moves a fixed volume per revolution, so less dense vapor means fewer pounds of refrigerant per minute and less cooling. The compressor also has to climb a larger pressure ratio to reach the same condensing pressure, so it uses more power for each unit of cooling. A long or undersized suction line therefore costs capacity and efficiency at the same time. The coil is not at fault, and the symptom (low suction pressure at the compressor, normal-looking superheat at the coil) can look like a charge or metering problem if you do not check the line.

The university paper above says pressure loss raises power use and cuts heat transfer capacity, but it gives no efficiency figures, so the percentages in our example below are our own assumptions.

Measuring at the coil outlet versus at the compressor

Where you put the gauge changes the answer. A gauge at the compressor service port reads the lower pressure. A gauge or transducer at the evaporator outlet reads the higher pressure. The difference is the line drop.

  • For superheat at the coil, use the pressure at the coil outlet (or add the known line drop to the compressor reading) with the pipe temperature at the same spot. Pairing a compressor-side pressure with a coil-side temperature makes superheat look too high.
  • For compressor protection and for capacity, the compressor-side reading is the one that matters, because that is the state the compressor actually inhales.
  • Comparing the two readings on one system on one day is a quick field check on the line itself. A larger gap than the design intended points to a kinked, crushed, undersized or partly blocked line or filter.
  • With a refrigerant that has temperature glide, such as R-454B, use the dew point (vapor side) for suction-side saturation. See the glide lesson linked below.

Why you cannot just upsize the line

A bigger pipe lowers friction, but it also lowers vapor velocity, and oil needs that velocity. Oil leaves the compressor as a mist and has to come back. In horizontal runs, moving vapor drags oil along the bottom. In risers, the vapor has to carry oil up the wall against gravity.

A study of vertical and horizontal suction lines with POE oil by researchers at a university in Illinois found that oil retention rises sharply when vapor flow falls below a minimum flow needed for upward carry. Near that limit, the vertical line held about twice as much oil as the horizontal line. They recommend staying above that minimum at all times. The paper does not give one velocity for every pipe, so check the line-sizing data from the equipment maker for your refrigerant and tube size.

So sizing has two limits pulling in opposite directions: small enough for oil-carrying velocity at the lowest load, large enough to hold the loss to the allowed degrees at full load. Variable-capacity and multi-stage systems make the minimum-load end harder. Risers are where this matters most; see the riser and oil trap lesson linked below.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example. All inputs are assumptions, not chart values. Take an R-32 system with a long suction line. We use round illustrative numbers for how steeply saturation pressure changes with temperature. They are our own, not read from a pressure-temperature chart; use the chart for your refrigerant on a real job.

  • Medium-temperature case: evaporator outlet saturation 40 °F, assume 2.5 psi per °F near that point.
  • Low-temperature case: evaporator outlet saturation 0 °F, assume 1.25 psi per °F near that point.
  • Line drop in both cases: 3 psi from coil outlet to compressor inlet.
  • Assumed capacity loss: 1.5 percent per °F lost (our own figure for the illustration).
  • Assumed design limit: 2 °F of saturation loss (our own choice for the example).

Step 1, medium case: 3 psi ÷ 2.5 psi per °F = 1.2 °F lost. Capacity loss: 1.2 × 1.5 = 1.8 percent. Inside the 2 °F limit.

Step 2, low case: 3 psi ÷ 1.25 psi per °F = 2.4 °F lost. Capacity loss: 2.4 × 1.5 = 3.6 percent. Outside the 2 °F limit.

Step 3, the allowed drop that keeps each case at 2 °F: medium 2 × 2.5 = 5 psi; low 2 × 1.25 = 2.5 psi. The same pipe that is comfortable at 40 °F is too restrictive at 0 °F, and the low-temperature line would need about half the psi loss, which means a bigger line, subject to the oil velocity check above.

Step 4, gauge location, medium case: suppose the pipe at the coil outlet reads 50 °F. With the coil-outlet saturation of 40 °F, superheat is 50 − 40 = 10 °F. If you instead use the compressor-side pressure, saturation is 40 − 1.2 = 38.8 °F, and the calculated superheat is 50 − 38.8 = 11.2 °F, which is 1.2 °F too high.

Field card: Suction Line Pressure Drop in Degrees: Why Low Pressure Costs More

Count the loss in degrees, not psi.

  • Psi lost divided by psi per degree equals degrees of saturation lost.
  • Low suction pressure means fewer psi per degree, so the same psi drop costs more degrees.
  • Lost degrees mean less dense vapor at the compressor, less capacity and more power per unit of cooling.
  • Coil outlet pressure for coil superheat; compressor-side pressure for what the compressor sees.
  • Do not oversize to cut the drop: oil return needs vapor velocity, especially in risers at minimum load.
  • Use the maker's line-sizing data for your refrigerant. Our example numbers are illustrations only.
