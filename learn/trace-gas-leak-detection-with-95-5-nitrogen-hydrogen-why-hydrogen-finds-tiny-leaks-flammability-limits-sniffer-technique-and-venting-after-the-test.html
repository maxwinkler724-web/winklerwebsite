Trace Gas Leak Detection With 95/5 Nitrogen-Hydrogen: Why Hydrogen Finds Tiny Leaks

Last updated: 2026-10-31

A nitrogen standing test tells you a system leaks. It rarely tells you where, and it can miss a leak that is real but tiny. A premixed gas of 95 percent nitrogen and 5 percent hydrogen, often called forming gas, fills that gap: the nitrogen does the pressure work, and the small hydrogen share lets a sensor find the leak after the refrigerant is out of the system.

Why a little hydrogen finds tiny leaks

Hydrogen is the lightest gas there is. The hydrogen property table we read lists its density at about 0.07 times that of air. It slips through very small openings, and a sensor made for hydrogen can respond to small amounts of it. An EPA best-practices document on supermarket leak reduction says a trace of hydrogen or helium with the nitrogen lets leaks be found at a lower pressure, and that an ordinary refrigerant detector is not sensitive to hydrogen. Another EPA guide makes the same point: you need a detector suited to the tracer gas. It also says hydrogen leak detection is similar to electronic refrigerant leak detection in how you use it. So the hydrogen is not there to pressurize anything. It is a marker that rides along with the nitrogen.

The test is done on a system that has already been recovered. You are not looking for refrigerant at all; you are looking for the tracer. That keeps stray refrigerant vapor on the floor or in the coil fins from confusing the search.

Why 5 percent is treated differently from pure hydrogen

Pure hydrogen has a very wide flammable range in air. The hydrogen property table lists 4 to 75 percent by volume. A hydrogen safety information site for laboratories gives the lower limit as 4 percent and recommends keeping hydrogen below that after it mixes with air. The EPA document above states the contrast plainly for the premixed blend: pure hydrogen is explosive, while concentrations of 5 percent or less are not, and it notes that the blend meets the relevant international gas-classification standards. The hydrogen safety site says it is unlikely that a leak of a diluted hydrogen mix will form a flammable mixture as it dilutes into air.

Notice what that means in practice. The classification belongs to the premixed cylinder gas, not to any other hydrogen source, and it does not make the cylinder harmless. It is still a high-pressure gas and still needs cylinder discipline. Always read the label and the safety data sheet on the cylinder you actually have, and never connect a cylinder of higher hydrogen content to a test setup meant for the 5 percent mix. A cylinder that is mostly hydrogen is a different gas with different rules.

Regulator and cylinder handling

OSHA's compressed gas standard, 29 CFR 1910.101, tells employers to make sure the cylinders under their control are in a safe condition as far as visual inspection can show, and refers to industry pamphlets for in-plant handling, storage and use. It also requires cylinders to have pressure relief devices installed and maintained. For a tech, that turns into plain habits:

  • Secure the cylinder upright so it cannot fall, and keep the valve cap on when it is not in use.
  • Use a regulator made for that gas and pressure, with the outlet set below the test limit on the equipment nameplate before you open the cylinder valve slowly.
  • Fit a relief device in the test line if the regulator can deliver more than the weakest part of the circuit will take.
  • Check the cylinder for damage before you load it, and keep it away from heat and ignition sources.

The practices in that list come from general cylinder safety rules and the maker's regulator instructions, not from any single line of the OSHA text, so follow the instructions that ship with your regulator and your company's cylinder procedure.

Pressure limits come from the nameplate

The test pressure for the gas mix is no different from a nitrogen test: the limit is the lowest-rated part of the circuit, and the equipment nameplate or installation manual sets it. The EPA best-practices document describes sites using a low-pressure tracer test first, in a range of 50 to 100 psi, and then going on to higher pressure, but those are one program's figures, not a rule for your equipment. Use the number on the data plate in front of you.

Pair it with a standing nitrogen test

The two tests do different jobs. A standing pressure test proves the circuit holds pressure and has a pass-or-fail answer; the details are in Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods. The tracer search comes into play when that test fails and the leak will not show up with bubble solution, or when the system is large and has hundreds of joints. A common order is:

  1. Recover the refrigerant to the required level and isolate the circuit.
  2. Pressurize with the tracer mix to a pressure at or below the nameplate limit.
  3. Search with a hydrogen sensor, working slowly, using the same careful technique described in Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors: Heated Diode, Infrared and Search Technique.
  4. Repair, then retest, usually with dry nitrogen alone to the full test pressure and hold time the maker specifies.
  5. Release the test gas, then evacuate.

Hydrogen rises, so search high and from the top down

The hydrogen table lists hydrogen at about 7 percent of the density of air, so any hydrogen that escapes drifts upward. A hydrogen safety page for laboratories also advises putting hydrogen detectors at stagnation points near the ceiling where hydrogen can accumulate. For a search, that gives three habits that come from our own practice rather than from a cited rule: start at the highest point of the circuit, hold the probe slightly above each joint rather than below it, and move down in steps. If you start low, gas from a leak above you can fall across your probe and make clean joints look like leaks. Keep air movement low during the search, because a fan can blow the tracer away from the leak and spread it around the whole unit. Let the sensor settle between spots, as you would with any slow electronic detector.

Venting the test gas afterward

Once the repair is made and you are done, release the test gas slowly outdoors or into a well-ventilated area, away from ignition sources and people, through the regulator or a controlled hose. Do not dump it into a closed mechanical room or an attic. The worked example below shows why the amount is small, but small is not the same as zero.

Venting nitrogen is not refrigerant venting. The federal venting prohibition at 40 CFR 82.154 covers refrigerant, and the section lists nitrogen in any application among the substances exempt from that prohibition. The EPA page on the venting prohibition describes the rule as covering ozone-depleting substances and their substitutes. We did not find the text of those two EPA sources that addresses hydrogen in a leak-test mix specifically, and neither source discusses leak testing, so ask your compliance contact if you need a ruling on the mixed gas. Recover the refrigerant first, and leave the recovery log (see EPA 608 Certification Basics) as the proof that no refrigerant went out with the test gas.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example. All the inputs are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic, not data from any real system.

Assumptions: the refrigerant side of the system holds 2.0 cubic feet of internal volume. We test it at 150 psig with the 95/5 mix. The room is 12 ft by 15 ft with an 8 ft ceiling. We ignore temperature change and gas compressibility, which are small here.

  1. Convert the pressure to absolute: 150 + 14.7 = 164.7 psia.
  2. Find the pressure ratio to atmosphere: 164.7 / 14.7 = 11.2.
  3. Gas volume once released at atmospheric pressure: 2.0 × 11.2 = 22.4 cubic feet of mixed gas.
  4. Hydrogen at 5 percent by volume: 22.4 × 0.05 = 1.12 cubic feet of hydrogen.
  5. Room volume: 12 × 15 × 8 = 1,440 cubic feet.
  6. If every bit of it were released into the room and mixed evenly: 1.12 / 1,440 = 0.00078, or about 0.078 percent, which is roughly 780 parts per million.
  7. Compare with the 4 percent lower flammability limit: 4 / 0.078 is about 51, so the room average would sit near one fifty-first of the limit.

The even-mixing assumption is the weak point. Real gas leaves in a plume, and hydrogen rises, so local pockets near the ceiling or inside a closed cabinet can be much richer than the room average. The point of the example is the scale: the amount in a small circuit is tiny compared with a room, which is why venting outdoors or into open air is routine, and why a big system or a closed space needs more thought.

Field card: Trace gas leak detection with 95/5 nitrogen-hydrogen

Nitrogen holds the pressure, hydrogen marks the leak.

  • Recover the refrigerant first; you search for the tracer, not refrigerant.
  • Use a sensor made for hydrogen; an ordinary refrigerant detector may not respond to it.
  • Pure hydrogen burns from 4 to 75 percent in air; the premixed 5 percent blend is classed differently. Read the cylinder label.
  • Test pressure comes from the equipment nameplate, never from habit.
  • Start at the top of the circuit and work down; hold the probe above each joint.
  • Keep the air still, and let the sensor settle at each spot.
  • Vent slowly outdoors, away from ignition. Nitrogen is exempt from the venting rule; refrigerant is not.
