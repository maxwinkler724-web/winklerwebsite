Mixed and Contaminated Refrigerant: Identifiers, Cylinder P-T Checks, Separate Cylinders and Reuse Rules

Last updated: 2026-10-28

One cylinder with the wrong gas or a little air in it can turn a full cylinder of good refrigerant into a disposal problem. This lesson covers how to catch it: what a refrigerant identifier measures, a pressure-temperature check you can do on a settled cylinder, and the habits and rules that keep refrigerants apart and legal to reuse.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page was built from federal regulation text, EPA pages and equipment manuals read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. Your equipment manual, the cylinder markings and the current eCFR text come first.

What a refrigerant identifier measures

A refrigerant identifier pulls a small vapor sample and reports what is in it. Two equipment manuals we read, both for automotive-type units, say the sensor uses non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) sensing to find the weight percentages of a few refrigerants, hydrocarbons and air. Those manuals call a sample pure when it is at least 98 percent by weight of the expected refrigerant, and they fail anything below that. One adds that hydrocarbons at 2 percent by weight or more set off a hydrocarbon alarm with a warning that a flammable mixture may be present. The same manual lets the user set an air purge trigger between 2 and 9 percent, and sounds an alarm at 25 percent air. Those are the thresholds for those particular units, not a universal standard, and they cover the older automotive refrigerants. EPA says no identifier can reliably identify every blend, and that basic models only tell you whether a refrigerant is pure. EPA strongly recommends, but does not require, an identifier for automotive work. Check which refrigerants your unit is rated for before you trust its screen on a stationary system.

Sampling rules matter too. The manuals say to sample vapor only, never liquid, from the vapor port, and never to exceed the unit's pressure limit (300 psig in one manual). A liquid or oily sample can damage the sensor. Follow the manual for your model.

The pressure-temperature check on a settled cylinder

You do not need an identifier for a first look. A cylinder holding both liquid and vapor of a single pure refrigerant sits at the saturation pressure for its temperature. That is why a pressure-temperature chart works. Our lesson on pressure-temperature basics covers the idea, and the lesson on glide and dew points explains why blends need care. Two things can throw the check off. Air (a non-condensable gas) adds its own pressure on top of the refrigerant's, so the gauge reads high. A different refrigerant in the cylinder moves the pressure up or down depending on what it is. Either way, the gauge no longer matches the chart for the label on the cylinder. This reasoning is our own.

To make the check fair (our own practice, not a sourced procedure):

  • Let the cylinder sit long enough to be at the same temperature all through. A cylinder pulled off a hot roof or out of a cold van is not at room temperature, and the shell is warmer or cooler than the liquid inside.
  • Measure the cylinder temperature itself with a thermometer, not the air.
  • Use a gauge you trust, and make sure there is liquid in the cylinder. A cylinder with almost no liquid does not follow the chart the same way.
  • Compare against the chart for the refrigerant on the label. If they disagree by more than the spread between charts, stop and look closer.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions (ours, for practice only): a recovery cylinder labeled R-410A has sat in a shop for a full day. A thermometer on the cylinder reads 75 °F. The gauge on its vapor valve reads 245 psig.

  1. Look up the expected value. A government pressure-temperature chart for R-410A (a federal agency chart that names no publisher) lists about 218 psig at 74.9 °F and 220 psig at 75.5 °F, so we take about 218 psig at 75 °F. Different charts differ by a few psi, so treat this as a chart reading, not a lab value.
  2. Subtract: 245 − 218 = 27 psi too high.
  3. Interpret it. A pure R-410A cylinder at 75 °F should read near 218 psig. Reading 27 psi above that, with the temperature confirmed, points to a non-condensable gas, most likely air pulled in during recovery or a hose that was not purged. The excess is roughly the pressure the air itself adds.
  4. Rough size of the problem (our own estimate, ideal-gas thinking, ignores gas that dissolves in the liquid): the absolute pressure is 245 + 14.7 = 259.7 psia, so 27 / 259.7 is about 0.10, or on the order of 10 percent of the vapor by volume is air.

What to do next. Do not use that cylinder as is. Recheck the temperature and the gauge. If the numbers hold, the gas is suspect. Use an identifier rated for the refrigerant if you have one, or ask your supplier or a reclaimer how they want it handled. Do not just open the valve and blow off the top: once refrigerant has been recovered, knowingly releasing it is a violation of the venting prohibition in 40 CFR 82.154(a)(3). Any air purge should go through equipment designed for it, following that equipment's manual. If the pressure had been well below the chart instead, that would point to a different gas or a cylinder low on liquid, and the same caution applies.

Why mixed refrigerant is a problem

Reclamation means reprocessing recovered refrigerant to the specifications in Appendix A of 40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F, which is based on AHRI Standard 700-2016, and verifying it by the lab methods in that appendix. For example, the appendix limits other volatile impurities, including other refrigerants, to 0.5 percent by weight, and calls for a gas chromatograph test for non-condensables. A cylinder with a different refrigerant in it can fail those tests, and you cannot easily separate one gas from another. EPA's page on contaminated automotive refrigerants says some reclaimers will not accept every contaminated or mixed refrigerant, some set minimums of 500 or 1,000 pounds, and a waste hauler may require identification, charge for the service and often send the material to an incinerator. EPA advises confirming all costs, including identification, transport and destruction, before you commit. That page is written for automotive work, but the same economics apply to any mixed cylinder.

Keep one refrigerant per cylinder

  • Use a separate recovery cylinder for each refrigerant, and label it with the refrigerant name before you connect anything.
  • Do not reuse a cylinder for a different refrigerant without the cleanup your reclaimer or supplier specifies. Leftover heel can contaminate the next batch.
  • Record what went in. Our lesson on 608 records covers what to keep.
  • Purge hoses and manifolds before recovery so air does not ride in with the charge. Our push-pull lesson shows the hose setup.

Recovery cylinder rules

DOT-rated. 49 CFR 173.301 says cylinders must meet a DOT (or other listed) specification and be requalified and marked as Part 180 requires. A cylinder that is cracked, leaking, bulged or has a defective valve may not be filled and offered for transport, and a cylinder overdue for requalification may not be filled and offered for transport. A cylinder may be filled only by its owner or with the owner's consent.

Fill limit. 49 CFR 173.304(b) says the liquid may not completely fill the container at 131 °F (55 °C). The 80 percent figure is trade practice rather than a federal number, as our push-pull lesson explains. Weigh the cylinder on a scale as you fill it. For automotive recovery tanks without a float valve, EPA says never exceed 60 percent of gross weighted capacity, which is a lower limit for that setup.

Retest interval. 49 CFR 180.209 lists the requalification period for DOT 4B, 4BA and 4BW cylinders as 5, 7, 10 or 12 years, with the longer periods tied to specific paragraphs, such as non-corrosive gas service with an external coating. Five years is the base interval. When a cylinder passes, 49 CFR 180.213 says it is marked on the upper end with the month, the approver's identification number and the two-digit year. Read the stamp before you fill. If you cannot find a current date, do not fill it.

EPA rules on reusing recovered refrigerant

40 CFR 82.156(h) says refrigerant may go back into the appliance it came from, or into another appliance owned by the same person, without being recycled or reclaimed. EPA's reclamation page says used refrigerant cannot be resold to a new owner unless a certified reclaimer has reclaimed it. 40 CFR 82.154(d) bars selling or offering to sell used refrigerant for use as a refrigerant unless it has been reclaimed by a reclaimer certified under 82.164, with a few narrow exceptions such as transfers between a parent company and its subsidiaries. So: same owner, reuse is allowed; new owner, it must be reclaimed to the AHRI 700-based purity standard first. Mixed or contaminated refrigerant will not pass that test, so it goes to a reclaimer that will take it or to destruction. For the basics of the three terms, see Recover, Recycle and Reclaim Explained.

Field card: Mixed and Contaminated Refrigerant

Check the pressure, keep them apart, know who can reuse it.

  • Identifiers use infrared sensing; the units we read call 98 percent by weight pure and fail anything lower.
  • Settled cylinder: use the cylinder temperature and the chart for the label. Reading high points to air; low or odd points to another gas.
  • Example: 245 psig measured vs about 218 psig expected at 75 °F is 27 psi high, likely air.
  • Do not vent recovered refrigerant. Ask the supplier or a reclaimer.
  • One refrigerant per cylinder, labeled before use.
  • Cylinder must be DOT-rated, in requalification date, and not liquid full at 131 °F. Trade practice is 80 percent.
  • Same owner may reuse; sale to a new owner needs reclamation to the AHRI 700-based standard.
