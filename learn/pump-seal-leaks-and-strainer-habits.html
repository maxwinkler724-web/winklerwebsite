Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits

A dripping pump seal is rarely the root problem. It is a symptom of heat, misalignment, vibration or dirt, and the best fix starts with working out which.

Safety first

Pumps have rotating shafts and can start from a remote command or a drive. Before opening a coupling guard, strainer, seal or panel, follow lockout/tagout, and do not rely on a stop button. Heating water can scald instantly, so let the system cool and relieve pressure first. Never reach past a guard to feel a seal or bearing while the pump runs.

What a mechanical seal does

A mechanical seal keeps water from escaping where the shaft enters the pump. Two flat faces press together with a thin film of liquid between them, which lubricates and cools them. If that film is lost, the faces overheat and crack. A little weeping at startup can be normal for some seals, but a steady drip, spray or a growing puddle is not. Check the manufacturer’s description of what is acceptable.

A leak is information

A pump-service article advises diagnosing the leak before replacing parts: where it comes from (seal face, casing or connections), when it happens (startup, running or after shutdown), whether it is constant or intermittent, and what changed recently in maintenance or operating conditions. Intermittent leaks often follow pressure, temperature or vibration swings.

Common causes the article lists

  • Wear. Shaft nicks and worn O-rings or gaskets.
  • Misalignment or poor installation, which seats the faces unevenly.
  • Abrasive residue that wears seal faces.
  • Dry running. With no liquid film, heat builds fast.
  • Cavitation. Pressure fluctuations cause vibration that damages seals. See Pump Cavitation Basics.
  • Incompatible fluid or cleaning agents attacking seal materials.
  • Deadheading. Running against a closed valve overheats the pump and destroys the seal.
  • Reverse rotation after motor, starter or drive work. Check rotation with a brief bump test as the manufacturer allows.
  • Uneven casing bolt torque and vibration from water hammer.

Strainers: the quiet protector

Strainers keep scale, weld slag and debris away from pumps, valves and exchangers. A dirty strainer starves a pump, can cause cavitation, and can mimic a bad pump or a bad coil. Good habits:

  • Read pressure drop across each strainer on every round, and record the clean baseline when the system is new or freshly cleaned.
  • Clean the strainer when the drop climbs well above that baseline, using your site’s trigger first. As a published starting point, Caleffi says to clean a Y-strainer at about 5 to 6 psi of differential, and a Watts manual gives a 5 to 10 psi guideline; the strainer maker’s instructions and your site procedure decide.
  • Inspect the screen. A torn screen lets debris through.
  • Never remove a screen permanently to cure a pressure drop.
  • Open strainer covers only after isolating, relieving pressure and confirming it with a gauge, then open slowly and stand to the side.

What to write down

Note seal condition, drip rate and where the leak shows, bearing temperature by a proper thermometer, noise, and strainer pressure drop. Report a spraying seal, a hot or noisy bearing, smoke, or a burning smell right away. Replacing a seal means isolating, draining and often disturbing alignment, which is work for trained people following the manufacturer.

Printable checklist

  • Pump locked out before opening any guard, strainer or seal
  • Seal: drip rate, any spray, where leak shows
  • Bearing temperature, noise and vibration
  • Coupling guard in place
  • Rotation verified after any motor, starter or drive work
  • No running against a closed valve
  • Strainer pressure drop recorded and compared with baseline
  • Strainer screen undamaged
  • Suction pressure and cavitation noise checked
  • Report spray, heat, smoke or burning smell immediately

Field card: Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits

Read the leak before you replace the seal.

  • A leaking seal is a symptom; find the cause.
  • Dry running, heat, misalignment, cavitation and deadheading kill seals.
  • Check when and where it leaks before replacing parts.
  • Strainer pressure drop on every round; know the clean baseline.
  • Never remove a strainer screen to fix a pressure drop.
