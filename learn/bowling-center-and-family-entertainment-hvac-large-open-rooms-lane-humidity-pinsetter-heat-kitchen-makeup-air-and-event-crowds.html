Bowling Center and Family Entertainment HVAC: Lane Humidity, Pinsetter Heat and Event Crowds

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A bowling center is one big room with a low ceiling over the lanes, a taller lobby area, a machine area behind the pins, an arcade, a snack bar and a crowd that arrives all at once on league night. The lanes themselves care about humidity. This lesson walks through where the heat, moisture and carbon dioxide come from, and sizes a sample building with stated assumptions.

Check the manufacturer and the code. Lane type, equipment lists and local code decide the design. Confirm every number against the lane maker's manual, nameplates and adopted code.

One room, several zones

From the front door the building usually runs as an entry and arcade, a lobby with seating and a snack bar, the approach and lane area, and the pinsetter area behind the pins. Each zone has a different heat source and a different comfort need. Bowlers are active, lightly dressed and close together near the approaches. The machine area is warm and mostly unoccupied. The arcade is dense with electronics.

Lane humidity: what the lane maker says

A synthetic lane maker's care manual says indoor humidity is a large factor in lane conditions and approach sliding. It recommends a relative humidity of 45% plus or minus 5%, no higher than 50%, held at a constant temperature, and says to keep the lane surface and the air in the building as steady as possible. It warns that warm, moist air blown across a cold lane surface causes condensation, that prolonged high humidity can make synthetic lane panels expand beyond their design, and that very low humidity dries the approaches and makes them slippery. It also tells operators to make sure outdoor air dampers close automatically when outdoor humidity is high, and says airflow should circulate away from the foul line toward the bowlers' area, which may mean changing duct and vent layout. We found no lane humidity rule in the USBC equipment specifications manual itself; the humidity figure there is a 30% to 50% test condition for ball friction measurements, which is a test of balls, not a building requirement. We did not find a public source on wood lane humidity limits, so we do not state any.

Plain meaning for a tech: this building wants dehumidification and steady setpoints more than a deep temperature drop. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load to see why a unit that cycles off quickly in mild, wet weather leaves moisture behind.

Pinsetter and machine area heat

An electrical magazine article on pinsetter safety describes a common pinsetter as having a 1 horsepower motor for the basic machine, plus a half horsepower ball accelerator motor and a half horsepower power lift motor. Nameplate horsepower is not average heat, because the motors cycle. We did not find a published average watts per machine. In the example below we use an assumed average and label it as ours. Pinsetter heat lands behind the pins, so the usual goals are to keep that area from baking the lane ends and to keep its air from leaking forward onto the approaches.

Arcade, kitchen and crowds

Arcade. Arcade games are many small plug loads in a small room. Our own assumption in the example is an average per machine, since we did not find a public figure we could verify. The same idea is covered for gaming machines in Casino Floor HVAC.

Snack bar. A fryer or grill hood pulls hundreds or thousands of cfm out of the room, and every cfm out must come back in somewhere. If makeup air is missing, the building goes negative, doors are hard to open, and outdoor air sneaks in through the lane end where humidity is least welcome. Hood types, makeup air and balance are in Commercial Kitchen Ventilation. We do not repeat them here.

Crowds and CO2. One city administrative code table for minimum ventilation lists bowling alley seating areas at 10 cfm per person plus 0.12 cfm per square foot, and game arcades at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot. A NIST report explains that indoor CO2 sits above outdoor CO2 by the occupant generation rate divided by the ventilation rate, that most average generation rates fall from 0.004 to 0.005 L/s per person, and that the room only reaches steady state after about three time constants, so short or variable occupancy may never get there. ASHRAE's position document adds that CO2 is at best an indicator of outdoor air per person and that sensor accuracy and calibration are critical. Method for CO2-based control is in Demand Controlled Ventilation.

Worked example: sensible load and a moisture check

Assumptions (all our own unless noted): 24 lanes; 24 pinsetters at 400 W average each; 40 arcade machines at 250 W average each; 300 people at 250 Btu/h sensible each; 20,000 ft2 conditioned floor area; 14 ft average ceiling height; 3.412 Btu/h per watt. Envelope, lights, sun and outdoor air sensible load are left out on purpose, so this is only the internal part.

  1. Pinsetters: 24 x 400 W = 9,600 W. 9,600 x 3.412 = 32,755 Btu/h.
  2. Arcade: 40 x 250 W = 10,000 W. 10,000 x 3.412 = 34,120 Btu/h.
  3. People: 300 x 250 Btu/h = 75,000 Btu/h.
  4. Total internal sensible: 32,755 + 34,120 + 75,000 = 141,875 Btu/h. Divide by 12,000: about 11.8 tons.

Ventilation and moisture. Using the city table rates for bowling seating: 300 x 10 = 3,000 cfm, plus 20,000 x 0.12 = 2,400 cfm, total 5,400 cfm of outdoor air. Assume a humid day where outdoor air carries 0.008 lb of water per lb of dry air more than the room (our assumption; read a psychrometric chart for a real design day). Moisture: 4.5 x 5,400 x 0.008 = 194 lb/h. Latent load: 4,840 x 5,400 x 0.008 = 209,088 Btu/h, about 17.4 tons. That is larger than the internal sensible load. Formulas are explained in HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas.

CO2 check. 5,400 cfm x 0.4719 = 2,548 L/s, or 8.5 L/s per person with 300 people. Using 0.0045 L/s per person (inside the NIST range), steady state is 0.0045 / 8.5 = 0.00053, or about 530 ppm above outdoors. Room volume is 20,000 x 14 = 280,000 ft3. Air changes: 5,400 x 60 / 280,000 = 1.16 per hour, so one time constant is about 52 minutes and three are about 2.6 hours. A league crowd that arrives in half an hour and leaves in three hours never settles, which is why CO2 readings during events lag the crowd and why sensor placement matters.

What the numbers mean: the dehumidification side, not the internal sensible heat, may set the coil and reheat needs. A rooftop unit that meets 11.8 tons sensible but has no good dehumidification mode will let humidity climb in warm, wet weather. See Rooftop Unit Basics for the equipment.

Rooftop units and long duct runs

Long, narrow buildings often use several rooftop units with long trunk duct over the lobby and drops to the lane area. Long runs lose air to leakage and run quiet only at low velocity, so check balance in each zone, not just total cfm. Keep supply from dumping cold air straight down onto the lane surface or approach, which the maker manual flags for condensation and sticky approaches. Low lane ceilings limit duct height.

Field card: Bowling Center and Family Entertainment HVAC

Humidity first, then heat, then the crowd.

  • The lane maker's manual we read asks for 45% plus or minus 5% relative humidity, steady temperature.
  • Warm, moist air over a cold lane surface condenses. Keep outdoor air dampers closed when outdoor humidity is high.
  • Pinsetter and arcade watts are real heat. Use measured values, not nameplate horsepower.
  • Snack bar hood exhaust needs makeup air or the building goes negative.
  • Crowds change CO2 slowly. Expect a lag of an hour or more in large rooms.
  • Latent load from outdoor air can exceed internal sensible load on humid days.
  • Lane type decides humidity limits. Wood lane limits are not taught here.
