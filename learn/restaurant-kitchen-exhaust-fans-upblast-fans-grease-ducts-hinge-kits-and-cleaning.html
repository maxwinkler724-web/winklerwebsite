Restaurant Upblast Exhaust Fans: Grease Ducts, Hinge Kits and Cleaning

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The big round fan on a restaurant roof is easy to ignore until it drips grease on the membrane, throws a belt, or cannot be opened for the duct cleaner. This lesson covers the upblast fan, its hinge kit, the grease duct it serves, and how the numbers work.

Start with Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics for the whole-system picture. This page stays on the roof and in the duct.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. NFPA 96 (the standard for ventilation control and fire protection of commercial cooking operations) is adopted and amended locally. This page was built from fan maker, fire marshal and trade sources read in 2026. It is general education. Your fire official, the hood listing and the engineer's drawings decide what applies to a real job.

What an upblast fan is

A trade-magazine article on kitchen exhaust describes the usual roof fan as a backward-inclined centrifugal wheel that discharges upward, mounted on a roof curb, with "the motor out of the airstream." That last point matters. The air leaving a Type I hood carries grease vapor and heat, and a motor sitting in that stream would collect grease and cook. Instead the wheel sits in the airstream and the motor sits outside it, driving the wheel by belt. The same article says these fans typically work at about 2.0 to 2.5 in. wc static pressure, and that the discharge should be 42 inches above the roof surface.

Grease containment and roof damage

Grease that escapes the hood filters ends up in the fan and then on the roof. Two code-based sources we read address this. A Utah State Fire Marshal guideline says fan housings must have a grease drainage and containment system, and that the roof must be free of grease pooling or corrosive contaminants on the roof covering. A Snohomish County, Washington hood worksheet says the fan discharge must not impinge on the roof and that the fan needs a drain opening at its lowest point to carry grease to a collection device. A tech who sees a full or missing container, or dark staining around the curb, is looking at a fire load and a roofing problem at once. Report it to the owner and follow site rules for disposal.

Hinge kits: why the fan has to tip open

You cannot clean the duct below a fan that is bolted down. A hinge kit lets the fan swing up on its curb. Greenheck's hinge kit installation manual says the kit holds top and bottom hinges, a reinforcing plate, spacers and a restraining cable, and that it applies to its centrifugal upblast fans in sizes 220 to 480. The manual's main warnings:

  • Disconnect and lock out power first, following lockout/tagout as the manual cites OSHA. OSHA's 1910.147 requires that equipment be isolated from its energy source and made inoperative before servicing.
  • A rigid electrical connection may have to be changed to a flexible one by a qualified electrician so the fan can move. The Utah guideline likewise asks for hinged housings with flexible power cables.
  • The stop cable limits travel. It ties the top hinge to the curb.
  • The roof curb must be properly attached to the roof to carry the opened fan.
  • Wind gusts can lift the curb cap off the curb, so the cap needs its thumb screws or another suitable method.

Do not open a fan whose hinge hardware, cable or curb attachment you cannot confirm. Roof edges are a fall hazard in their own right; see Ladder and Roof Fall Safety for HVAC Work.

Belts and bearings

The Greenheck CUE/CUBE manual (an upblast centrifugal model) covers belt drive upkeep. It says to re-check and adjust belts after the first 24 to 48 hours, to tension them just enough to prevent slippage on the smaller sizes, and warns that over-tightening wears bearings and adds noise. It recommends checking belts periodically for wear and tightness and replacing with the same type. Pillow block bearings are factory lubricated, with annual lubrication recommended and the warning "do not over-grease." The manual lists lubrication intervals from every 1 to 3 months for dirty or hot duty to every 12 to 18 months for light duty. It also points commercial kitchen users to NFPA 96 for cleaning fans. See Belt Drives and Sheave Basics and Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings. Use the manual for the fan in front of you.

Grease duct basics

Sources we read agree on the main points, with details that vary by code edition:

  • Liquid-tight, welded. The ACHR News article and the Snohomish worksheet both call for continuous liquid-tight welds or brazing on the outside of seams and joints. The article lists a 1.0 in. wc air pressure test held 20 minutes among accepted tests.
  • Slope. Both say horizontal runs slope 1/4 in. per foot toward the hood or a grease reservoir, with a steeper 1 in. per foot for runs over 75 feet.
  • Access. The ACHR article says cleanout panels at least every 12 ft and at each change of direction. The Snohomish worksheet says no more than 20 ft apart and within 10 ft of turns over 45 degrees, generally 12 by 12 inches. The Utah guideline says 20 ft apart and 10 ft at changes of direction. These do not match, so the local code governs. Access panels should never be blocked or painted shut.

Cleaning frequency

We quote frequency only from a fire marshal page. The Utah State Fire Marshal guideline lists: monthly for solid fuel cooking; quarterly for high-volume cooking such as 24-hour operations, charbroiling or wok cooking; semiannually for moderate-volume cooking; and annually for low-volume cooking such as churches and day camps. It adds that systems must be cleaned when needed. The Utah text we read does not cite NFPA 96 by name, so confirm the schedule with your own fire official and the NFPA 96 edition your jurisdiction has adopted. Cleaning is a specialty job. For why it matters, a U.S. Fire Administration report says cooking caused about 64 percent of restaurant structure fires in 2002 and grease or cooking oil was involved in 68 percent of cooking fires. One example it gives was a restaurant three months overdue for cleaning its hood and chimney.

The hood and make-up air interlock

The Snohomish worksheet requires the make-up air system to be electrically interlocked with the exhaust system so that it runs whenever exhaust runs. The Utah guideline says exhaust fans must run whenever cooking equipment is on, and that make-up air openings must not be restricted, blocked or shut down during cooking. An interlock is a safety circuit. Do not jumper it to get a fan running; find out why it opened.

Worked example

Given: a 10 ft Type I hood over medium-duty equipment (fryers and griddles). The Snohomish worksheet table for unlisted hoods lists 300 cfm per linear foot for medium duty. Make-up air is set at 80 percent of exhaust. The grease duct is 16 in. by 16 in.

  1. Our own: exhaust cfm = 10 ft x 300 cfm/ft = 3,000 cfm.
  2. Our own: make-up air = 3,000 cfm x 0.80 = 2,400 cfm. The other 600 cfm comes from the room, which keeps the kitchen slightly negative.
  3. Our own: duct area = 16 in. x 16 in. = 256 sq in.; 256 / 144 = 1.778 sq ft.
  4. Our own: velocity = 3,000 cfm / 1.778 sq ft = about 1,688 fpm.
  5. Compare: the Snohomish worksheet and the ACHR article both give 500 fpm as the minimum duct velocity, and the article gives 2,500 fpm as the industry norm maximum. 1,688 fpm sits between them.

A real design uses the hood listing, not this table, and your adopted code decides which values apply.

Field card: Restaurant Upblast Exhaust Fans: Grease Ducts, Hinge Kits and Cleaning

Motor out of the grease stream, fan hinged, duct welded and sloped, interlock intact.

  • Upblast fan: wheel in the airstream, motor outside it, belt drive.
  • Lock out before opening a hinged fan; check the stop cable, curb and wind.
  • Grease drain and container: report full, missing or leaking.
  • Duct: liquid-tight welds, 1/4 in. per foot slope, access panels clear.
  • Cleaning frequency (Utah fire marshal summary): monthly solid fuel, quarterly high volume, semiannual moderate, annual low. Confirm locally.
  • Make-up air interlocked to exhaust. Never jumper it.
  • Example: 10 ft x 300 = 3,000 cfm; x 0.80 = 2,400 cfm; 3,000 / 1.778 = about 1,688 fpm.
