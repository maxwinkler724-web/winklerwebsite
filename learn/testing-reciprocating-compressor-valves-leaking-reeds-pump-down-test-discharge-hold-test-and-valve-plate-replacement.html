Testing Reciprocating Compressor Valves: Leaking Reeds, Pump-Down and Hold Tests, Valve Plates

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A reciprocating compressor that runs quietly, draws low amps and will not hold the box temperature is often not electrical at all. The thin steel reeds that act as check valves may be leaking. This lesson covers what that looks like, the two field tests used on compressors with service valves, why scrolls are different, and when a valve plate swap makes sense.

What the reeds do

Each cylinder has a suction reed and a discharge reed. On the intake stroke the suction reed opens and the discharge reed stays shut. On the compression stroke it flips. Both reeds only work as check valves if they seal flat against their seats. A university study of small reciprocating compressors measured leakage past the reed and seat edges and reported that leakage can significantly decrease compressor efficiency, with edge gaps of well under one micrometer in the samples tested (Purdue e-Pubs, International Compressor Engineering Conference, 2018). The point for a tech: you cannot see a gap that small, so you find the problem by what the gauges and the meter say.

Symptoms that point to leaking reeds

A refrigeration professor writing in a trade magazine lists the combination for bad valves: higher-than-normal discharge temperature, low head pressure, normal-to-high condenser subcooling, normal-to-high superheat, high suction pressure and low amp draw. His key clue is low head pressure together with high suction pressure at the same time. A trade-magazine trainers panel adds low capacity, a very quiet compressor, a space that will not reach setpoint and a compressor that runs longer than it should. A whoosh or whistle at shutdown can point to gas bypassing a valve.

Why these show up together (our explanation): gas that leaks back through a reed is compressed again and again, so the pump does little net work. That means low motor load, a smaller pressure difference between the gauges and extra heat in the head. Worn piston rings can look nearly identical. The same professor says rings give low head and high suction too, so the symptoms alone do not name the valve.

Rule out the easy stuff first: a feed-limited or iced evaporator, a stuck hot-gas valve or an unloader stuck open can also drop amps. Low charge makes suction low, not high, so high suction with low head argues against it. See measuring amp draw for how to read amps against nameplate.

Test 1: suction pump-down (vacuum) test

This applies only to a compressor with its own suction service valve and a gauge port, so a semi-hermetic or a serviceable open-style machine. The trade trainers panel describes it: gauge on the suction service valve, front-seat the valve, run the compressor, and the gauge should reach at least 20 in. Hg of vacuum within a minute or so. A Navy training manual for refrigeration plants gives the same 20 in. Hg figure, and says to close the suction stop valve gradually so the crankcase oil does not foam. If the suction side pulls down fast and holds, the suction reeds are sealing. If it stalls well short of that, suction gas is slipping past the reeds or the discharge reeds are letting hot gas back in.

Cautions (ours): run only as long as needed, watch the oil pressure and any low-pressure or oil safety, and never leave a compressor running in a deep vacuum. Check the maker's limits. Recover refrigerant, not vent it, if you must open anything.

Test 2: discharge hold test after shutdown

The Navy manual describes it this way: pump the compressor down to 2 psig, stop it, and quickly close the suction and discharge valves. If discharge pressure falls more than 3 pounds in a minute while crankcase pressure rises, the discharge valves are leaking. The same manual says a leaking discharge valve also shows up as suction pressure building quickly during the off cycle, so the compressor restarts after a very short rest. It also says to wait at least three days after new valves go in, because they may need to wear in.

Why scrolls do not follow the same rules

A scroll does not use suction and discharge reeds the way a piston machine does. Compression happens between two spiral wraps, with a check valve in the discharge. So a vacuum-pull test tells you little. A trade article on scroll service says not to pump a scroll down into a vacuum, and it quotes a maker bulletin that the compressor stops pumping when the pressure ratio exceeds about 10:1. A maker scroll service manual says a scroll that runs but does not pump draws very low current, far below nameplate load amps, and eventually trips internal protection. That is the same low-amp symptom, but the fix is a compressor change, not a valve plate. A whoosh at shutdown from a scroll check valve seating is normal and not a failed reed.

Worked example (ours): walk-in cooler

These inputs are assumptions we made up for teaching, not field data. A semi-hermetic compressor serves a walk-in cooler. The box will not drop below 45 F. Nameplate RLA is 12.0 A. We measure 6.5 A running. Suction gauge reads 58 psig. Discharge gauge reads 82 psig.

  1. Amps versus nameplate. 6.5 ÷ 12.0 = 0.54, so the compressor is at about 54 percent of RLA. That is low. Loaded compressors that are working hard run nearer nameplate, so a low reading suggests it is not doing much pumping.
  2. Pressure difference. 82 − 58 = 24 psi between discharge and suction. That is close together for a box compressor. Combined with step 1, this matches the symptom list above.
  3. Rule out the easy causes. Check the evaporator is not iced, fans run, the liquid line feeds, and no hot gas or unloader valve is stuck open. Assume all of these check out.
  4. Suction vacuum test. Gauges on, front-seat the suction service valve, start, and watch. Assume it stalls at 8 in. Hg and will not go further. That is far short of the 20 in. Hg the sources cite, so a suction-side leak is likely. If it had pulled 22 in. Hg and held, move to step 5.
  5. Discharge hold. With the suction down near 2 psig, stop, and close the suction and discharge service valves. Watch the discharge-side gauge for a minute. Assume it falls 12 psi while the crankcase gauge climbs. Over the 3 psi per minute mark, so discharge reeds leak too.
  6. Decision. Both fail, so the valve plate assembly is the repair. Before ordering, find out why it failed (see below).

Valve plate or condemn?

A semi-hermetic has a bolted housing, so a valve plate (reeds and seats as an assembly) can be replaced in the shop or on site. The Navy manual says defective discharge valves should be replaced as a whole assembly. A compressor maker's web service note shows a plate exchange on a semi-hermetic on a rack: power off, close the shut-off valves, turn on the oil heater, evacuate the compressor, charge it with nitrogen, open it and swap the plate, then check tightness with dry nitrogen and evacuate again. That note was written for a mildly flammable A2L refrigerant with ventilation and a gas detector, so follow that kind of precaution for any such refrigerant. Use the maker's torque and gasket values; we did not find them in public sources.

A hermetic has a welded shell. A reposted public training text says systems with hermetic compressors are not easily repaired, and that service usually means removal and replacement. So a leaking hermetic is condemned. Whether a plate swap or a new compressor is cheaper depends on local parts and labor. Also look at cause: liquid slugging or floodback destroys reeds (see floodback and slugging). A new plate in an unfixed system will fail again. If the motor is also suspect, check windings first: checking compressor windings.

Field card: Testing Reciprocating Compressor Valves: Leaking Reeds, Pump-Down and Hold Tests, Valve Plates

Low amps plus high suction plus low head: test the reeds.

  • Symptoms: low amps, high suction, low head, hot discharge, poor capacity.
  • Suction test: front-seat suction service valve, run, target about 20 in. Hg within a minute.
  • Discharge hold: pump to about 2 psig, stop, close both valves, a drop over 3 psi per minute means leaking discharge reeds.
  • Scrolls: do not vacuum-test or pump down into a vacuum.
  • Semi-hermetic: replace the valve plate and find the cause. Hermetic: replace the compressor.
