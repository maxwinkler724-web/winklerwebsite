Rotary Compressors in Mini-Splits, Window Units and PTACs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a small ductless outdoor unit, a window unit or a PTAC and the compressor is usually a squat black can with a tall canister bolted to its side. That is a rotary compressor and its suction accumulator. It fails and gets tested differently from the scroll or reciprocating compressors in larger equipment, so this lesson covers how it compresses, why the shell runs hot, and what a meter can and cannot tell you.

How the rolling piston compresses

Inside the shell is a short round cylinder. A roller (the rolling piston) rides on an off-center part of the crankshaft, so as the shaft turns the roller rolls around the inside of the cylinder wall and touches it at one moving point. A spring-loaded vane slides in a slot in the cylinder wall and is pressed against the roller, closing off the space between the roller and the wall. A US patent document on this design says the vane splits that space into a suction chamber and a compression chamber, and that gas enters through a suction port on the low-pressure side and leaves through a discharge port on the high-pressure side.

Think of it as one crescent-shaped gap that is growing behind the contact point (pulling in vapor) while the gap ahead of it shrinks (squeezing vapor toward the discharge port). The same patent notes that high pressure in the compression chamber helps push the vane against its slot wall, which is one reason vane and slot wear matters. A building-services engineering article on these machines says the vane and roller are sealed by a film of oil rather than metal contact, and that close machining tolerances are needed for that seal.

Twin rotary and why the shell is hot

Larger inverter compressors often use a twin rotary design: two rollers stacked on one shaft, set 180 degrees apart, which the same engineering article says balances forces and cuts vibration. Either style discharges its gas into the shell, not straight into a discharge pipe. A conference paper on a rolling piston compressor's shaft oil supply describes the shell interior as kept at high pressure, with the motor at the top, the compression mechanism below it, oil in the bottom of the shell, and compressed gas "discharged once into the shell" before leaving. That is a high-side shell.

Two practical results follow. First, the whole can sits at discharge pressure and carries hot discharge gas, so it is hot to the touch in normal operation. The engineering article says the rising gas helps cool the motor windings. Second, the oil sump is exposed to high-side pressure and temperature, so oil that leaves with the gas or gets diluted by liquid refrigerant is a real concern. We give no normal shell temperature here because we did not find a sourced figure; follow the maker's data. Caution: treat the shell and discharge line as burn hazards. One trade training article says an extremely hot shell may mean the internal overload tripped, and that the shell can cool while the heavy internals stay hot, so the overload may stay open longer than the surface suggests.

The accumulator and liquid sensitivity

The canister on the side is the suction accumulator. A university conference paper on accumulator design says its job is to prevent liquid refrigerant from being drawn into the cylinder. As the diagram shows, vapor leaves through a tube whose open end sits high in the canister, while liquid and oil collect in the bottom. The engineering article says the design is resilient to slugging and the accumulator traps returning liquid so it can be vaporized before compression. Resilient does not mean immune. A trade training article on compressor failure says liquid refrigerant destroys a compressor built for vapor, and that a flooded start occurs when liquid has settled in the crankcase during shutdown and boils violently at startup, washing oil off critical surfaces. It also warns that a clogged accumulator screen can starve a compressor of oil.

The engineering article says only a small amount of oil reaches the discharge pipe, so the sump holds most of the oil. Overcharge, a metering device or airflow fault, or refrigerant that settled in the crankcase during a long shutdown can all wash it out. See the floodback lesson for superheat checks.

Fixed speed versus inverter

A fixed-speed rotary in a window unit or PTAC is a single-phase PSC motor turning at roughly line-synchronous speed. For a two-pole motor on 60 Hz that is 3,600 rpm (our own arithmetic: 120 x 60 / 2), a little less under load. An inverter rotary, as in most mini-splits, is driven by the outdoor board with variable-frequency three-phase power. A maker's reference guide for an inverter twin rotary compressor family lists operating ranges in revolutions per second that run from as low as 10 to as high as 130 depending on model, which is 600 to 7,800 rpm (our own conversion). The engineering article notes that inverter motors let the compressor match load instead of cycling. Details of the drive are in Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.

Failure signs

  • Locked rotor. Hum, click, then trip. The trade training article lists an internally seized compressor next to a failed run capacitor and wiring faults as causes of a hum-and-click no-start, so test the capacitor first on fixed-speed units.
  • High amps. The same article says amps far above expected for the conditions suggest an internal electrical short. Amps well below expected suggest a capacity problem.
  • Low capacity. The article says a rotary that will not pump down toward 0 psi can point to too much refrigerant, a tripped low-pressure switch or internal damage.
  • Noise. A compressor that turns louder or rougher than before is a flag. The sources we read give no sound-by-sound table, so confirm with amps and pressures before blaming it.
  • Oil loss. The article says normal suction superheat with high discharge superheat may mean low oil.

Field checks: winding ohms

With power off, locked out and the capacitors discharged by your approved method, remove the wires from the terminals before measuring. Use a real ohmmeter on its lowest range; a trade association article warns that a clamp meter's ohms scale is not precise enough.

Single-phase fixed-speed rotary. Measure all three pairs. That article's sum rule for a good motor is Common-Start plus Common-Run equals Run-Start. The largest pair leaves out Common. It also says any continuity from a terminal to the shell is a short to ground, and that two open readings from Common can mean a hot internal overload, so let it cool and retest.

Three-terminal inverter rotary. The sum rule does not apply. The trade training article says the readings between all three terminals should be equal, and another training article gives U-V, V-W and W-U as the pairs and a general range of about 0.5 to 1.0 ohm, with each terminal to the shell reading open. That article says the ground insulation reading expected is generally above 500 megohms but varies by maker. Always check the maker's range. Wait several minutes after removing power before touching the drive or terminals.

Worked example (our own numbers, assumed for teaching).
A. Window unit rotary, single phase, wires off: C-S reads 2.4 ohms, C-R reads 1.2 ohms, R-S reads 3.6 ohms. Check: 2.4 + 1.2 = 3.6, which matches R-S, so the windings are consistent. The largest pair (R-S) leaves out Common; the higher reading from Common (2.4) is Start and the lower (1.2) is Run. If R-S had read 4.4, the sum would not add up and the motor is suspect.
B. Mini-split inverter rotary, wires off: U-V 0.8, V-W 0.8, W-U 0.8 ohm. All three equal, so the windings are balanced; the sum rule is not used. If W-U read 1.4 while the others read 0.8, one winding is off and that points to the compressor, once you also confirm each terminal reads open to the shell. If all three read open, check the maker's data and let the compressor cool before retesting, since an internal protector may be open. Low-ohm readings are small, so poor probe contact can mimic this; clean and re-measure first.

Field card: Rotary compressors

Hot can, side canister, three terminals: know which test applies.

  • Roller rolls in the cylinder; the spring-loaded vane splits suction from compression.
  • The shell holds discharge gas and oil, so it is hot. Treat it as a burn hazard.
  • The accumulator traps liquid and oil; it helps but does not make slugging harmless.
  • Single-phase: C-S plus C-R equals R-S.
  • Three-terminal inverter: all three phase-to-phase readings equal; no sum rule.
  • Each terminal to the shell should read open.
  • Check the maker's resistance range for your model.
