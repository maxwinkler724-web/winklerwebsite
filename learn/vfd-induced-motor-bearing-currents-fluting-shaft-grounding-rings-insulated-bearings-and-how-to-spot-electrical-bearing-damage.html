VFD Bearing Currents: Fluting, Grounding Rings, Insulated Bearings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A motor on a variable frequency drive can wear out its bearings with electricity instead of load. Tiny sparks pass through the bearing grease, and over months they leave pits and ridges that sound like a failing bearing. This lesson explains why that happens, how to recognize it, and what the usual fixes do.

Check the facts for your job

Scope. This is general education from public sources read in 2026. Choosing a fix is an engineering decision for your lead and the motor and drive makers. Measuring shaft voltage means working near a rotating shaft with a live drive, so that is for qualified people only, following your site electrical and rotating-equipment procedures.

Why a drive puts voltage on a shaft

A drive builds its output by switching its output devices on and off thousands of times a second. This pulse width modulation (PWM) is what lets it control motor speed. The three output phases do not switch at exactly the same instant, so the average of the three phase voltages measured to ground is not zero. That leftover is called common-mode voltage. A 2022 open-access review in Machines describes it as the average of the three phase-to-ground voltages, which would be zero only if the switches changed together.

The motor has small built-in capacitances between the stator winding and frame, between the stator and rotor, and between the rotor and frame. A 2021 university paper models the bearing voltage as a capacitive divider of the common-mode voltage. Fast edges push small currents through those capacitances, and the rotor, floating on a thin grease film, picks up a voltage. The DOE motor tip sheet says fast rise-time pulses can drive harmful bearing current even when overvoltage is not a problem, and that driven-load bearings can be affected too if the coupling is not insulated.

How the bearing gets hurt

The grease film is an insulator until the voltage across it gets high enough. The 2022 review says that once the voltage across the oil film exceeds its breakdown level, current jumps between the rolling element and the race, and the spark melts a tiny crater. This is the same process as electrical discharge machining (EDM), a shop method that removes metal with sparks. Each spark takes a microscopic bite out of the race.

Early damage is pitting. A trade-press article from the engineering press describes the small fusion craters as frosting, a dull grey look across the race. Later the damage can organize into evenly spaced ridges across the race, called fluting, which looks like a washboard. The 2022 review says fluting has more than one cause: craters raise contact pressure and ordinary wear adds to it.

A trade article by an industry author reports electrically damaged bearings often fail within three to 12 months of startup on drive-fed motors, versus a design life of roughly 100,000 hours. That is one source's anecdote, not a rate. The EC&M article also notes that inverter-induced bearing currents occur in a relatively small share of installations, so most drive-fed motors never show this.

What it looks and sounds like

  • Noise. A new growl, whine or rumble from a motor on a drive that was quiet at startup. Ducts can carry the noise through a building.
  • Vibration. Rising vibration at bearing fault frequencies. See the vibration lesson.
  • On the bench. Frosted or fluted races and dark, burnt-looking grease point to electrical damage. A lead decides the cause; keep the failed bearing.
  • Rule out the common causes first. Wrong grease amount, wrong interval and misalignment kill far more bearings. See the greasing lesson.

The usual fixes and what each one does

No source we read claims one fix handles everything. An EC&M article by two drive-industry engineers says no single method addresses every current component and some methods shift current to other paths, so more than one may be needed. The review likewise says traditional measures cannot fully eliminate bearing current.

  • Shaft grounding ring or brush. A conductive path that touches the shaft and bonds to the frame, so the voltage drains there instead of through the bearing. Brushes can wear and need upkeep, as a trade article notes. The engineering-press article above favors rings over brushes, but its author's employer sells rings, so read that as one viewpoint.
  • Insulated or ceramic bearings. A coating or ceramic parts block current. A trade article says they can still pass current through the insulation and can push it onto other equipment, such as a pump, encoder or the driven load. The EC&M authors suggest an insulated coupling in that case.
  • Cable and grounding. The EC&M article lists better high-frequency grounding between motor, drive and driven equipment. The DOE sheet says keep the drive close to the motor, since cables of 50 ft or more almost always need mitigation for overshoot. That sheet recommends isolating both bearings or a shaft grounding brush.
  • Carrier frequency. Carrier waves typically run from 2 kHz to 20 kHz. The DOE sheet says carrier frequencies above 5 kHz are likely to cause bearing damage unless protective measures are taken, and suggests staying at or below 5 kHz. In one dataset the 2022 review cites, a higher carrier frequency gave more discharges and larger current. Do not change drive parameters on your own. See VFDs on pumps and fans.

The standard that applies to motors built for drives is NEMA MG 1, Part 31, for definite-purpose inverter-fed polyphase motors. An industry article argues that the edition it read does not cover common-mode voltage for larger motors or high-frequency circulating current for smaller ones, and cites a NEMA recommendation to insulate bearings on large motors (typically frame 500 and up) when peak shaft voltage exceeds 300 mV. We have not read the standard itself, so check the current edition and the motor maker's data before relying on any of that.

Worked example: why a higher carrier frequency means more chances to spark

This is an illustration with assumed numbers, not a damage rate. Assumptions: one carrier cycle is one switching event per phase, and the drive runs 6,000 hours a year at a constant carrier. Many events cause no discharge at all, and the share that does depends on bearing temperature, load, speed and grease. We are only counting opportunities.

  1. 8 kHz is 8,000 events per second per phase.
  2. Seconds in 6,000 hours: 6,000 × 3,600 = 21,600,000 s.
  3. 8,000 × 3,600 × 6,000 = 8,000 × 21,600,000 = 172,800,000,000 events per year (about 1.7 × 1011).
  4. 2 kHz: 2,000 × 3,600 × 6,000 = 2,000 × 21,600,000 = 43,200,000,000 events per year (about 4.3 × 1010).
  5. Ratio: 172,800,000,000 ÷ 43,200,000,000 = 4. Four times as many opportunities at 8 kHz.

How a technician should act

  • If a replacement bearing dies early on a drive-fed motor, tell your lead and suggest an electrical cause. Save the old bearing.
  • Do not bypass or remove a grounding ring, brush or insulated coupling that came with the motor.
  • A megohmmeter checks winding insulation, not bearing currents. See the megohmmeter lesson.

Field card: VFD bearing currents

Fast drive pulses can spark across bearing grease.

  • PWM drives leave common-mode voltage; motor capacitances put some of it on the rotor.
  • Sparks through the grease film pit the race (frosting) and can leave ridges (fluting).
  • Signs: new growl or whine, rising vibration, burnt-looking grease, early repeat failures.
  • Fixes: shaft grounding ring or brush, insulated or ceramic bearing, short cable and good high-frequency grounding, lower carrier frequency. No single one covers all cases.
  • Do not remove a ring or change drive settings on your own.
  • Shaft measurements are for qualified people only.
