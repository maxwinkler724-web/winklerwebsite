Electric Blankets and Heated Mattress Pads: Staying Warm at Night on a Lower Thermostat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Turning the thermostat down at night saves heat, but a cold bedroom is hard to sleep in. A heated blanket or mattress pad warms the person instead of the whole house. This lesson shows how little power one uses, how to pair it with a night setback, and the safety rules that matter more than the savings.

Heat the person, not the house

A furnace, boiler or heat pump has to warm every cubic foot of air and every wall in the house. A heated blanket warms a thin layer next to your body. If you lower the thermostat at night, the house loses less heat to the outdoors, and the blanket makes up the comfort difference where you actually feel it. Our own plain-language rule: the bigger the part of the house you stop heating, the more the blanket is doing the work. How cold is too cold is a personal and health question, so do not set the house lower than is safe for everyone living there, including older adults, infants and pets.

Pair it with a night setback

A setback schedule drops the thermostat setting during sleeping hours and brings it back up before you wake. See Thermostat Setback Schedules: How Much You Really Save for how the schedule works and how much it can save. A practical pattern is to set the setback a few degrees lower than you normally hold, turn the blanket on a short time before bed, and use the blanket's own controller to bring the bed to a comfortable level. Cold walls and windows can still make a room feel chilly even at a normal air temperature; Why a Room Feels Cold at 70 Degrees explains why, and a blanket helps most when the cold comes from the room surfaces.

Worked example (our own): blanket vs space heater

This is our own example. The numbers are assumptions for illustration, not measurements, and not figures from any source. Real blankets and pads vary, so read the wattage on the label of the one you own.

  • Assumption 1: the electric blanket draws 100 watts while it is heating.
  • Assumption 2: a plug-in space heater draws 1,500 watts on its high setting.
  • Assumption 3: each runs the whole night, 8 hours, with no cycling off. (Real devices cycle, so these are upper limits for each.)

Blanket: 100 W × 8 h = 800 watt-hours. Divide by 1,000 to get kilowatt-hours: 800 ÷ 1,000 = 0.8 kWh.

Space heater: 1,500 W × 8 h = 12,000 watt-hours. 12,000 ÷ 1,000 = 12 kWh.

Ratio: 12 ÷ 0.8 = 15. Under these assumptions the heater uses 15 times the electricity of the blanket for the same night. Check: 15 × 0.8 = 12, and 100 W × 15 = 1,500 W, so the two ratios agree. We leave dollar costs out because rates differ by place and change. Multiply the kWh by your own rate if you want a cost. The comparison is also not exact: a space heater warms the air in a room and a blanket warms one person, so they do not do the same job. For why a heater is a poor choice to run while you sleep, read Electric Space Heaters: Safety, Real Cost and When They Make Sense.

Safety rules that come first

Heated bedding is a fire and burn product. The federal consumer product safety agency's older-consumer home checklist says electrically heated blankets should not be folded, covered by other objects or tucked in while in use, and that the power cord should not be pinched or crushed by the bed. It also says anything on top of the blanket, including other blankets, comforters and sleeping pets, can cause overheating of the heating elements and controls. The edges should hang freely over the sides and the foot of the bed. A second federal checklist adds that you should look for cracks or breaks in wiring, plugs and connectors and for dark, charred or frayed spots, and discard a blanket that shows them.

  • Follow the label. The maker's instructions decide whether a given product is meant to stay on all night, how to use the controller and how to wash it.
  • Do not fold or bunch it while on, and do not tuck it in. Folds trap heat.
  • Do not put heavy bedding on top. Weight and layers hold heat in the wires.
  • Not for everyone. A university health service notes that infants, older adults and anyone who has difficulty feeling heat face a higher burn risk and may not be able to move away. A university medical center says heating pads should never be used with infants and should not be used with people who cannot feel the heat or cannot move away. If someone cannot feel heat or turn the blanket off, a heated blanket is the wrong tool; see also Heating and Cooling a Baby's Nursery.
  • Replace old or damaged ones. A university extension service advises discarding a blanket with damage or more than 10 years old, and not donating it. A university health service gives a similar ten-year replacement guide.
  • Look for a safety lab listing mark. When you buy, choose a product that carries the listing mark of a recognized independent safety testing laboratory, and check recall notices. The federal fire administration says the same for portable heaters.
  • Waterbeds and adjustable beds. A university extension service says never to use an electric blanket on a waterbed or an adjustable hospital-style bed. If a product is specifically made for a waterbed, its label will say so; otherwise leave it off the waterbed.
  • Keep cords clear of the bed frame. Do not let the cord get pinched between the mattress and frame, headboard or wall, and do not run it under the mattress. Plug it straight into a wall outlet, not an extension cord or power strip.

The federal fire administration's heating handout also reminds readers to keep anything that can burn, such as bedding, at least 3 feet from a space heater and to turn heaters off when going to bed. That is one more reason a low-wattage blanket, used correctly, is the safer way to add warmth in bed than a heater near the bed.

Ask your installer or technician about the house

A blanket does not fix a cold room caused by leaks, a weak heat run or an undersized system. If one room stays cold, a technician can look at airflow and insulation. The blanket is a comfort tool for a lower thermostat setting, not a repair.

Field card: Electric Blankets and a Night Setback

Warm the person, keep the label in charge.

  • Our example: 100 W × 8 h = 0.8 kWh; 1,500 W × 8 h = 12 kWh; ratio 15 (assumed watts).
  • Use a night setback and the blanket together; do not drop the house colder than is safe.
  • No folding, bunching, tucking in or heavy bedding on top.
  • Not for infants or anyone who cannot feel heat or turn it off.
  • Discard if cracked, charred or frayed, or older than ten years.
  • Look for a recognized safety lab listing mark; plug straight into the wall; keep cords off the bed frame.
