Exercising Isolation Valves: Why They Seize and What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An isolation valve earns its keep on the day you need it to close. A valve that has sat open for years may not turn, or may turn and still pass water. Utilities that depend on valves for emergencies run exercising programs, and the same habits fit boiler rooms, chiller plants and risers. This lesson covers why valves seize, how often to cycle them, how to count turns, and what to do when one will not hold.

Why valves seize

A valve that never moves gives deposits time to build. A maker's troubleshooting sheet for buried water gate valves lists buildup in the seat area, debris wedged under the disc, and corrosion or debris between the stem and stuffing box "due to lack of operation." Mineral scale and rust in hydronic piping act the same way. Gate valves are the most exposed because sediment collects in the wedge pocket and on the seat faces. Butterfly and ball valves have fewer pockets, but scale and dried packing still lock them up. Over-tightening is a second cause: the same sheet warns that excessive torque on the operating nut may have damaged the stem or stem nut.

How often utilities exercise valves

Public guidance gives a range, not one number. An EPA preventive maintenance guidebook for small water systems says to inspect plant and pump house valves at least twice a year and to exercise all mainline valves at least once a year, splitting the job in halves six months apart. A National Academies review of distribution systems calls it desirable to turn each valve at least once per year. A state water department's standard procedure (SOP #331) takes a more spread-out view: a 3 to 5 year rotation for gate valves, with annual exercising for critical valves and mains 16 inches or larger.

These documents are written for water mains, not boiler rooms. For a building, our own suggestion is once a year for valves that isolate critical equipment, then adjust from your records. Your owner's O&M plan may set its own interval.

Counting turns to close

The state SOP gives a rule for gate valves with a turning nut: expected turns equal 3 times the nominal valve size in inches, plus 2 or 3. Its example: a 6-inch valve is 6 x 3 = 18, plus 2 or 3, so 20 or 21 turns. Its table lists about 8 turns for 2 inches, 14 for 4 inches, 20 for 6 inches, 26 for 8 inches and 38 for 12 inches. If you reach the expected count and the nut keeps turning, the valve may be a size larger than you think. If it stops short, something is blocking the wedge.

Two cautions. First, this formula is for water-main gate valves with a nut, and many are geared (the SOP says to multiply by the 4 or 4.5 to 1 gear ratio). Pipe-room valves with a handwheel and a rising stem can differ, so treat the formula as a rough check and let the first careful count become the number for that valve. Second, the EPA guidebook says to record the number and direction of turns to closure.

Exercising method

  1. Confirm the effect first. Cycling a valve in a live loop can starve a coil or isolate a pump.
  2. Start gently. The SOP says to begin with 5 to 10 rotations, then reverse two or three, and to close slowly.
  3. Close fully, reopen, and reclose until it seats, as the EPA guidebook describes, counting turns each way.
  4. Do not overtighten. The SOP warns that closing too tightly damages the valve and causes leaks.
  5. Record it (see below), and schedule repair for any valve that leaks or will not move.

Do not force it

The SOP says to avoid using a T-handle or extension to force a valve. We extend that to cheater bars on handwheels and pipe wrenches on stems. A seized stem can twist off, and then the valve cannot be operated at all. The SOP sets crew torque limits and sends any step beyond them to the superintendent first. A pipe-room valve rarely shows a torque rating, so stop at the first sign of binding and report it.

Packing adjustment

If the stem weeps, the SOP says most packing leaks can be stopped by snugging the gland. Tighten the gland nuts evenly, a small amount at a time, and note it on the work order, because a snugged valve may take more effort to turn later. The maker sheet likewise advises loosening and retightening stuffing box bolts evenly. Hard or used-up packing needs replacement on an isolated line, not under pressure.

Valve charts and records

Both public guides lean on paperwork. The EPA guidebook says to use a valve log sheet and to describe the physical condition and operating condition of each valve. It also says to keep a map of valve locations in a secure place because you must be able to isolate sections. The state SOP lists date, valve identification, condition, test results and personnel. We would add: tag number, what it isolates, position found, turns each way, and whether the stem was snugged. A valve chart matched to tagged valves lets a helper close the right one.

Planning before a shutdown

  • Exercise the valves you plan to rely on well before the outage, not on the morning of the work.
  • Find the valve that holds, and the next one upstream, so you have a second choice.
  • Exercise bypass valves first; the SOP does this and notes any bypass that will not move.
  • Order packing and gaskets if a valve is known to be bad.
  • Write the isolation steps on the work plan and check them against the valve chart.

When a valve does not hold

A valve can turn freely and still pass water, because scale holds the wedge off its seat. Do not trust one closed valve. OSHA defines double block and bleed as closing and locking or tagging two in-line valves and opening and locking or tagging a drain or vent between them, and an OSHA interpretation letter encourages that method for lockout, noting that energy is more likely to build up again when a single valve is used. If the line drips from the open drain, the valves are not holding. Stop, keep the lock and tag in place, and call for help: a supervisor or plant engineer. The fix may be closing a valve further upstream and draining a larger section. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC for the verification steps.

Worked example: turns and time

A chilled water room has 12 gate valves, all 6 inches, each with a rising stem we will assume behaves like the SOP rule. These are assumptions, not site data.

  1. Turns to close. 3 x 6 = 18, plus 2 or 3, so 20 to 21 turns (SOP rule). Use 21 to be safe.
  2. Turns per valve cycle. Close and reopen is 21 + 21 = 42 turns. Add 10 turns for the gentle start and reversals (assumption), so 52 turns.
  3. Time per valve turning. Assume 2 seconds per turn: 52 x 2 = 104 seconds.
  4. Time per valve handling. Assume 3 minutes (180 seconds) for walking, reading the tag, logging and wiping the stem.
  5. Per valve total. 104 + 180 = 284 seconds.
  6. Twelve valves. 12 x 284 = 3,408 seconds, or about 57 minutes.

Our own estimate: about an hour for one person if every valve moves. Each stuck valve will add time. The count against 21 turns is also a check, and a valve that stops at 14 is a repair ticket.

Field card: Exercising Isolation Valves: Why They Seize and What to Do

Cycle it, count it, log it, never force it.

  • Seizing comes from scale, rust at the stem and dried packing.
  • Public guides: plant valves at least twice a year, mains at least once a year.
  • Gate valve turns to close (water-main rule): 3 x size in inches + 2 or 3.
  • Record turns each way; a count that comes up short means an obstruction.
  • No cheater bars; stop at binding and report.
  • Snug packing evenly and note it.
  • Double block and bleed; open drain must stay dry.
