Hot Surface Igniter Care and Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The hot surface igniter is a small ceramic part that gets glowing hot to light the gas. It is also one of the most common reasons a furnace starts, waits, and then quits. Knowing how to check it quickly saves a lot of guesswork.

What it does

On a hot surface ignition furnace, the control board sends power to the igniter after the inducer has proven draft. The element heats for a warm-up period, one source says roughly 30 to 60 seconds, another cites 45 seconds on a specific control, and then the board opens the gas valve so the gas lights from the hot element. A separate flame rod then proves the flame. If the igniter never heats, the board has no reason to open gas, which is a safe failure. The big picture is in Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics.

Two materials, two personalities

HVAC School describes two common ceramic types. Silicon carbide igniters are older, rough looking, slower to heat and very brittle. Silicon nitride igniters are newer, smoother, faster, use less energy and last longer, but have less surface area. That matters when you replace one: the element has to sit in the gas flow from the burner so the gas actually reaches the hot surface. Photograph the bracket and position before you take the old one out.

Handle with care

  • Do not squeeze or poke the element. It is ceramic and can crack from a light bump.
  • Do not touch the hot area with bare fingers on a new part. One source warns that skin oil can create hot spots that shorten life.
  • Match the voltage on the replacement. One manufacturer-style summary says too little voltage will not heat the element enough and too much shortens its life. Most furnace igniters are 120 volts, but some are not.
  • Match the part to the furnace position, or use a universal that fits the mounting.

A simple test sequence

With the thermostat calling for heat, watch the start-up. First confirm the inducer runs and the pressure switch closes, because on most furnaces the igniter is not powered until then. Then:

  1. Look. A healthy igniter glows bright orange. No glow, or a dim glow, is the clue.
  2. Check voltage at the plug. Power off, then carefully meter at the harness during the warm-up window with power on. About line voltage should arrive if the board is calling for the igniter. No voltage means the problem is upstream, so look at the board, limits and pressure switch.
  3. Check resistance at the plug. With power off and the igniter unplugged, measure across the two terminals. Example ranges from sources: about 40 to 100 ohms in one article, 40 to 70 ohms on one maker's 80% model, and silicon carbide units often rated around 90 ohms. An open reading (OL) means a crack. A reading far above the rating means it is near the end of life. Your furnace's number is the one in its manual.

HVAC School suggests testing at the plug rather than pulling a suspect silicon carbide igniter out for a close look, since removing it can break it.

When the igniter is not the problem

Igniters get blamed for faults they did not cause. If the igniter never gets power, look at the pressure switch and inducer first (see Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis). If the igniter glows and gas lights but the furnace shuts down in seconds, suspect the flame sensor (see Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps). Always power down and lock out before replacing it, and run a full cycle afterward to confirm it lights.

Field card: Hot Surface Igniter Care and Testing

Glow, voltage, resistance: in that order.

  • A good igniter glows bright orange during warm-up.
  • No glow: check for voltage at the plug before condemning the part.
  • Resistance is an open (OL) or high reading when failing; use your model's rating.
  • Do not touch the hot area of a new igniter or knock the ceramic.
  • Match voltage and mounting position on replacement; power down first.
