Recessed Can Lights Below an Attic: Air Leaks and Safe Fixes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

If your top floor has older recessed can lights and an attic above, each can may be a small open hole between your living space and the attic. This lesson explains why that matters in winter and summer, what the labels on cans mean, why insulation must never be piled onto the wrong kind of can, and which fixes are safe.

Why a can light is a hole

An older recessed can is a metal housing set into the ceiling. To let heat out and to make the lamp easy to service, most older housings have vent slots, gaps around the trim ring, and open wiring knockouts. A university extension page on sealing air leaks calls recessed lights that reach into an unheated attic "extremely leaky." A federal energy agency installer job aid describes these fixtures as commonly installed in the ceiling between the upper story and the attic, which is why their gaps are a major leakage point.

Stack effect in plain words

Warm air is lighter than cold air, so in winter the warm air inside a house tends to rise and push out through any opening high in the house, such as the ceiling. That pushing is called stack effect. The air that leaves the top must be replaced, so cold outdoor air is pulled in through gaps low in the house. In our explanation, the top-floor ceiling is where the house pushes hardest, which makes recessed cans, attic hatches and wire holes the main escape routes. The same extension page notes that leakage rises in cold or windy weather.

What it costs you in winter

  • Heat loss. The same extension page says air infiltration can account for 30% or more of a home's heating and cooling costs. Can lights are only part of that, but they are an easy part to find.
  • Moisture in the attic. The federal energy agency guide to durable attics warns that warm, humid air from kitchens, bathrooms and dryers threatens attic durability, and it tells homeowners to look for gaps around lighting fixtures and seal them. In our explanation, that moist air can meet cold roof boards and collect as frost or damp spots on the attic side, so it is worth a look in the attic on a cold morning.
  • Ice dams. Another university extension page says warm air leaking through ceiling penetrations is, in many homes, the major mode of heat transfer behind ice dams, and it names recessed lights as a risk. Its first advice is to make the ceiling airtight so no warm, moist air flows from the house into the attic. See Ice Dams and Attic HVAC.

What it does in summer

A hole works both ways. A third university extension page says holes in cans plus missing insulation let heat move between the attic and the rest of the house, which raises energy use. In summer the attic can be much hotter than the rooms below, so heat can drop through the same gaps and the cooling system works harder.

Reading the label: rated, airtight, or neither

Look inside the can, near the socket, for a printed label. Two ideas matter.

  • Insulation contact rating. A can marked as rated for insulation contact has been built and checked so it can sit touching attic insulation without overheating. A can called "non-IC" in the federal job aids is not rated for that and needs clear space around it.
  • Airtight marking. Some modern cans are also marked airtight, meaning the housing was tested to leak very little air. That extension page recommends replacing leaky cans with airtight, insulation-contact-rated fixtures.

Older cans often have neither mark. If you cannot find a label, treat the can as non-rated and leaky, and ask an electrician to identify it.

Do not bury a non-rated can

A non-rated can is allowed to get hot and relies on air around it to shed that heat. The federal job aid for non-rated lights asks for 3 inches of clearance between the lamp and the insulation on all sides, and says the goal is fire safety as well as less air leakage. The same extension page warns that you cannot safely air seal recessed fixtures unless they are rated for insulation contact. So do not blow, pile or push insulation over an old can, and do not stuff the gaps with foam, rags or tape yourself. Heat trapped around a housing is a fire risk.

Safe fixes

  1. Replace the fixture. The best long-term answer is a new airtight, insulation-contact-rated housing, installed by an electrician. Many homeowners then caulk the gap between trim and ceiling, which the federal job aid describes.
  2. Ask about retrofit trims and LED retrofit kits. Some trims and kits are made to fit existing housings and cut air leakage. Whether one is allowed in your housing depends on its own labeling and your electrical code, so have an electrician confirm before you buy anything. This step is our own advice, not from the sources above.
  3. Use a fire-rated cover built for the purpose. If a non-rated can stays, the federal job aid describes a sealed enclosure over it built from fire-rated material such as 5/8-inch gypsum wallboard, with 3 inches of clearance from lamp to insulation, a top of R-1 or less kept free of insulation, and all edges sealed to the attic floor. Pre-made covers exist for this. Never use a plain foam or cardboard box.
  4. Remove the can. Patch the ceiling and use a surface-mounted light. Work with an electrician for the wiring.
  5. Hire help. Attics are dusty, cramped and very hot in summer, and wiring may be old. A licensed electrician or an experienced air-sealing contractor can do this safely.

Air seal first, insulate second: the federal job aid for attic floors says to finish air sealing before adding insulation. For R-values and the whole-attic picture, see Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for New Jersey Homes.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. We assume each old can leaks 2 cfm (cubic feet per minute) at normal house pressure, and that the top floor has 12 such cans. These inputs are assumptions for teaching, not measurements or a typical value from any source. Real cans vary.

  1. Total leakage: 12 cans x 2 cfm = 24 cfm.
  2. Per hour: 24 cfm x 60 minutes = 1,440 cubic feet per hour.
  3. Per day: 1,440 cu ft/h x 24 hours = 34,560 cubic feet per day.
  4. Check by a second route: 2 cfm x 60 x 24 = 2,880 cu ft/day per can, and 2,880 x 12 = 34,560. The two routes agree.
  5. Scale, with one more assumption: a house of 2,000 sq ft with 8 ft ceilings holds 16,000 cubic feet. 1,440 / 16,000 = 0.09 air changes per hour from these cans alone, or 34,560 / 16,000 = 2.16 house volumes of air per day.

That is air you paid to heat or cool, now moving through the ceiling. It will not match your house, but it shows why twelve small holes are worth fixing.

Bills and comfort

Sealing leaky ceiling holes tends to lower heating and cooling use and to even out room temperatures, fewer cold drafts and less attic heat dropping into rooms. The size of the saving depends on your house. The same extension page says that plugging even only the larger holes will still save money. Also check bath fans and dryer vents, which must exhaust outdoors; see Bathroom Exhaust Fans and Attic Ventilation Basics.

Field card: Recessed Can Lights Below an Attic

An old can is a hole with a light in it.

  • Warm house air rises through ceiling gaps (stack effect) and carries moisture into the attic.
  • Check the label: insulation contact rated, and airtight, are different marks.
  • Non-rated cans need 3 inches of clearance from insulation.
  • Never bury a non-rated can in insulation: fire risk.
  • Safe fixes: replace the housing, fire-rated cover made for the purpose, or an electrician-approved retrofit.
  • Example: 12 cans x 2 cfm = 24 cfm = 1,440 cu ft/h = 34,560 cu ft/day (assumed 2 cfm).
