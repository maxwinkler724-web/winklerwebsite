Measuring Airflow (CFM): Flow Hoods, Anemometers and the Temperature Rise Method

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The question is simple: how many CFM is this system really moving? You can measure at a register, in the duct, at the filter slot, or work backward from the furnace. Each way has strengths and traps. This lesson compares them and works one example two ways.

Why there is no single best way

Airflow in cubic feet per minute (CFM) is invisible, and every way of measuring it disturbs the air a little or leans on an assumption. This lesson walks through five common methods. The basics of static pressure and taking a rise are in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics and Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise.

A DOE Better Buildings page on installation quality describes how the ENERGY STAR rating procedure (Standard 310) handles this. It lets a rater measure total blower airflow with one of four methods: a flow hood, a flow grid, pressure matching, or the manufacturer's static pressure table. It also says most systems need only one or two measurements, not a sum of every register.

Method 1: the flow hood (capture hood)

A flow hood is a fabric hood on a frame that sits over a supply diffuser or return grille and funnels all the air through a sensor. TSI describes its Alnor EBT731 as made for taking direct air volume readings at diffusers and grilles, with a meter that senses supply or return flow. Contracting Business calls a balancing hood the ideal tool for register airflow.

It is not magic. Because the hood sits over the register, it adds resistance and can change the flow it is trying to measure. TSI lists back pressure compensation as a feature for that reason, and says it is used to keep readings accurate. A research report on commercial hoods found they are often inadequate for residential systems, and that results varied widely between models. It blamed poor calibration, uneven air across the grille, and the extra resistance the hood adds. In practice that means three habits: use a hood that is in calibration, cover the whole register so no air escapes around the edge, and be careful on high-velocity or very uneven diffusers.

Method 2: anemometer traverse (velocity times free area)

When a hood will not fit, you can measure how fast the air moves and multiply by the open area it moves through: CFM = average velocity (feet per minute) × area (square feet). Contracting Business describes two tools. A rotating vane anemometer is swept across the face for 30 to 60 seconds and shows an average. A hot-wire anemometer takes single-point readings in a grid, and the meter can average them. Fluke's guide for its 975 meter suggests grid points about three to five inches apart (no more than six), and holding the probe flush with a supply grille or about an inch from a return.

The tricky part is the area. The louvers and bars in a register squeeze the air, so the speed right at the face is higher than the speed in the duct behind it (the effect is called vena contracta). Fluke says the open area cannot be measured reliably in the field, so use the effective area, Ak, from the register maker's lab data. Using the full opening instead makes the answer too big. Contracting Business adds a caution that a published factor may not match your instrument, distance and sweep. Its fix is to measure a similar register with a hood, traverse the same register with your anemometer, and use the ratio as a correction factor for registers a hood cannot reach. Technique matters: keep the probe square to the flow, at a steady distance, and inside the airstream for the whole sweep.

Method 3: furnace temperature rise

On a gas or electric furnace you can back into airflow from the heat it puts in the air. The only new piece here is what the 1.08 means. It comes from air density (about 0.075 pounds per cubic foot), 60 minutes per hour, and the specific heat of air (about 0.24 Btu per pound per degree F). Multiply those and watch the units:

0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/(lb·°F) = 1.08 Btu·min/(h·ft³·°F).

So Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × rise, and CFM = Btu/h output ÷ (1.08 × rise). The constant assumes ordinary air near sea level. The method also needs the furnace's true output, which depends on gas input being right. Energy Conservatory's TrueFlow manual lists the temperature rise method, along with static pressure and fan curve methods, among approaches it calls problematic or time-consuming. Treat a rise-based CFM as a good sanity check, not a measurement.

Method 4: static pressure plus the blower table

Every blower has a performance table. You measure total external static pressure (the entering and leaving readings added together), confirm the speed tap in use, and read airflow where they meet. ACHR News explains that the table's footnotes show what was assumed, such as a wet or dry coil, a filter, or included coil and heat strip pressure drops, and your reading has to match. Contracting Business says the answer is only as good as the blower's condition and the exact model table; a table from a similar model may be off by 5 to 15 percent. A dirty wheel or wrong tap makes the table unreliable. ACHR News adds that a wrong pressure reading makes the airflow look wrong too.

Method 5: flow grid (TrueFlow style)

A flow grid is a metering plate that replaces the filter so all return air passes through it. Energy Conservatory's manual says the plate adds resistance different from the filter, so you take static pressure with the old filter and again with the grid, and the app corrects the flow. It states accuracy of ±5 percent in most setups and ±7 percent with the probe in a supply register, and a range of 300 to 2,500 CFM. Duct leakage between the grid and the blower makes the reading low by about the leakage. Obstructions within 6 inches upstream or 2 inches downstream can reduce accuracy. A Bonneville Power Administration test guide treats the calibrated plate as the preferred method and uses plenum pressure matching with a duct leakage fan when a plate cannot be installed.

Worked example: two methods, same house

This is a made-up system with round numbers, so the arithmetic is easy to follow. Say a furnace output of 60,000 Btu/h is taken from the nameplate, and the house has five supply registers.

A. Anemometer traverse at one register. Six hot-wire readings across the face: 620, 580, 650, 700, 560 and 610 fpm.

  1. Add them: 620 + 580 + 650 + 700 + 560 + 610 = 3,720.
  2. Average: 3,720 ÷ 6 = 620 fpm.
  3. The maker's data gives an effective free area (Ak) of 0.45 square feet. (Not the 0.50 sq ft you would get from the full opening.)
  4. CFM = 620 × 0.45 = 279 CFM.

Suppose the other four registers measure 300, 255, 290 and 196 CFM. The total is 279 + 300 + 255 + 290 + 196 = 1,320 CFM.

B. Temperature rise. Return air 68°F, supply 108°F, probes out of sight of the heat exchanger, readings steady.

  1. Rise = 108 − 68 = 40°F.
  2. 1.08 × 40 = 43.2.
  3. CFM = 60,000 ÷ 43.2 = 1,389 CFM.

Compare. 1,389 − 1,320 = 69 CFM, which is 69 ÷ 1,389, about 5 percent. That is a good agreement. The register total is expected to run a little low, because Contracting Business notes that nearly all ducts leak, so air escapes before it reaches the registers, and because the supply-air traverse has its own error. If the two answers differed by 20 or 30 percent, you would stop and look for a cause: a dead or leaking duct run, a wrong free area, bad probe placement, or a furnace not putting out its rated heat.

Picking a method on the job

  • One register, hood fits: use the hood.
  • Whole-system number: grid or blower table, with temperature rise as a cross-check.
  • Hard-to-reach register: anemometer, ideally calibrated against a hood on a similar one.
  • Numbers disagree by a lot: suspect the measurement first, then the duct system. See Not Enough Return Air and Duct Sizing Basics.

Field card: Measuring Airflow (CFM)

Two different methods that agree beat one that looks precise.

  • Flow hood: cover the whole register, use a calibrated hood, watch for back pressure.
  • Anemometer: CFM = average fpm × effective free area (Ak), not the full opening.
  • Temperature rise: CFM = Btu/h output ÷ (1.08 × rise). 1.08 is Btu·min/(h·ft³·°F).
  • Blower table: needs total external static, the right model table and the right speed tap.
  • Flow grid: needs the pressure correction; duct leaks make it read low.
  • Register totals run low because ducts leak.
