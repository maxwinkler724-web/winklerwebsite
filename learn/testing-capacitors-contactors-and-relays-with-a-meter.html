Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

These three parts fail often and look alike when they do. A meter turns a hunch into a reading, but only after the power is off and the stored energy is dealt with.

Before the meter touches anything

  • Lock out and tag the equipment. Prove it dead with a meter you have checked on a known source (Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC).
  • Treat every capacitor as charged. Discharge only by the method your employer and the manufacturer approve, such as a suitable resistor or tool, and never by shorting the terminals with a screwdriver. Voltage can rebound after a discharge, so recheck.
  • Do not put a meter in capacitance, resistance or continuity mode on a live circuit.
  • Swollen, cracked, leaking or scorched parts are replaced, not tested.

Capacitor: compare the reading to the rating

The can is marked with microfarads, a voltage rating and usually a tolerance. With the part discharged and its wires removed, a meter in capacitance mode gives a reading to compare against the label. Tolerance is the allowed deviation, shown as a plus-or-minus percent. Parts sellers list +/-6 percent for many run capacitors, some brands list 5 to 10 percent, and the code letters J, K and M mean 5, 10 and 20 percent. A 15 microfarad part at 5 percent has a band of 14.25 to 15.75. The Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures lesson mentions a 10 percent guide from another source. Read the tolerance on the label and follow the manufacturer's service guidance. We found no maker document giving a hard replacement cutoff, so this page gives none.

Reading outside the band means weak or failed. A zero may mean a short, and an over-range reading may mean an open part or a poor setup. A reading in tolerance does not prove every other property is fine. Some techs also check capacitance under load by measuring amps on the start winding wire; that is live work for trained people.

Contactor: coil and contacts

  • Coil, de-energized: resistance across the coil terminals. An open reading suggests a burnt coil. A finite reading only shows the winding is continuous, so compare to manufacturer data.
  • Coil voltage, energized (live work): if the thermostat calls and the contactor does not pull in, measure control voltage across the coil terminals. No voltage points upstream, correct voltage with no pull-in points at the coil or mechanism. As one maker's datasheet example (ABB definite-purpose contactors), a 24 volt AC coil is listed to pick up at 18 volts or less, about 75 percent of rated, and to drop out somewhere between 6 and 15 volts. The percentages are our arithmetic. Other makers differ, so a coil that only pulls in near full voltage or buzzes at low voltage is a clue to check against the datasheet, not a verdict.
  • Contacts, power off and wires off: a pole that should be open reading near zero ohms may be welded shut. A continuity beep is only a screening test, since it can miss resistance that shows up under load.
  • Look for pitting, heat damage and loose terminals. Do not file contacts unless the maker allows it, and never jumper one closed.

Relay: coil versus contacts

The coil and the contacts are separate circuits, so test them separately. Check the coil resistance with power off. Then, with the coil energized by its proper control signal, the contacts should show continuity, and with it off they should be open, according to a troubleshooting guide. Do this before blaming a control board, since a relay that never pulls in can look like a dead board. How a relay works is in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control, and the background is in Capacitors and Contactors.

Habits that save you

Photograph the wiring first, label the leads, put the meter in the right mode before touching anything, and write down each reading. A single reading is a clue, not a verdict. Cross-check with the symptom and the wiring diagram, as in Reading a Ladder Diagram.

Field card: Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter

Power off, discharge, then measure. Read it against the label.

  • Lockout, prove dead, then discharge the capacitor by an approved method. Never short it with a screwdriver.
  • Capacitance reading vs the label, within the printed tolerance; read the tolerance, do not assume.
  • Contactor coil: ohms with power off; control voltage across the coil when called (qualified live work).
  • Contacts: open when they should be, closed when they should be; welded poles read near zero when off.
  • Relay: test coil and contacts separately before blaming the board.
  • Ohms, continuity and capacitance modes: never on a live circuit.
