Portable Data Loggers for HVAC Diagnostics: Temperature, Humidity and Current Loggers, Placement, Sample Intervals and Reading the Trend

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A comfort complaint that happens "sometimes" will not show up while you stand there with a gauge. A small battery logger left behind can record every start and stop for days. This lesson covers which loggers to use, where to put them, how often to sample, how to install a current sensor safely, and how to turn the file into two simple numbers: duty cycle and cycles per hour.

What each logger tells you

  • Temperature and humidity loggers show what the room or duct air did over time. One in the living space and one in the supply or return air can show whether a complaint lines up with the equipment running.
  • Current loggers use a current transformer (CT) clamped on one conductor. Current above a small threshold means the motor or compressor is on. A DOE metering protocol chapter says current-only metering fits when the goal is only to determine operating hours.
  • Motor on/off loggers sense the electromagnetic field near a motor and record a time stamp each time it changes state. The chapter treats them as event loggers.

A logger records what happened. It does not say why. Pair it with AC Short Cycling: Causes, Oversizing, Charge, Thermostat and Safeties and Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter.

Placement

The DOE Building America indoor temperature and humidity protocol is written for homes, and its rules are a good habit for any room logger. It says to mount loggers on an interior wall, 4 to 5 feet above the floor, out of the direct path of supply register airflow, out of direct sunlight, at least 4 feet horizontally from any heating or cooling appliance, and not in a kitchen or bathroom. For an outdoor logger it calls for full shade in all seasons or a solar radiation shield, and not near the outdoor unit of an air conditioner.

For duct air, the same program put temperature and humidity probes in the supply airstream of an air handler to follow supply temperature. A return-side logger beside it lets you see the temperature drop across the coil without a gauge on the system. Check humidity loggers first; see Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes. The protocol also says to confirm the battery is at 100% before deployment.

Sample interval, battery and memory

The interval must be shorter than the thing you are trying to see. The DOE metering chapter gives the rule: for cycling equipment the interval should capture the cycle, and its example is an air conditioner that cycles every 25 minutes, which needs an interval of 5 minutes or less. For loggers that record snapshots, it asks for at least five recordings per cycle. It also says short-term monitoring should capture at least two cycles, and that most loads are fine at 15-minute data. The Building America protocol uses a one-hour step for room conditions, too slow for a cycling complaint.

Memory is a counting problem. Readings available divided by readings per day gives days of record. The DOE chapter wants a logger able to store at least a month of data and battery life that lasts to the next visit. Event loggers (on/off time stamps) fill quickly if the trigger is frequent, such as a short-cycling unit, so check capacity before leaving.

Time sync

If you compare a current file with a room temperature file, both clocks must agree. The DOE chapter says to confirm meter clocks are synchronized before installation, to check drift when you retrieve them, and states a time accuracy goal of one minute per month or better. Start every logger from the same laptop in one session.

Installing a current sensor safely

This is the part that can hurt you. The DOE chapter says only qualified personnel should work in live panels, and that split-core CTs can be fitted without shutting down the load. It also warns that current transformers can produce dangerously high voltages if the wires are not shunted, so use the CT type the logger maker specifies and never leave a CT secondary open. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.333(a)(1) requires live parts an employee may be exposed to to be de-energized before work on or near them, with limited exceptions, and (c)(2) says only qualified persons may work on circuit parts that have not been de-energized. In practice:

  • Choose the CT range from the expected peak load, as the DOE chapter advises, and stay within its voltage rating.
  • If you cannot clamp around a single conductor in a closed, labeled space without exposing live parts, de-energize and lock out first, or leave the job to a qualified person.
  • Confirm your meter and your own PPE for the available energy; see Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs.

Reading the trend

From a current file, count the starts and add up the on-time. Two formulas do most of the work:

  • Duty cycle = total on-time / total elapsed time.
  • Cycles per hour = starts / hours of record.
  • Average on-cycle = on-time / starts; average off-cycle = off-time / starts.

Many short on-cycles with short off-times point toward a control, charge, airflow or sizing problem (see the short-cycling lesson). Long on-time near 100% on a hot afternoon with the room not reaching setpoint points toward capacity or load. Now line up the room temperature: does the room drift up only when the compressor is off for long stretches, or does it keep climbing while the unit runs? The figure shows an assumed steady pattern.

Worked example (our own, assumptions labeled)

Assumptions: a current logger on a condenser at 1-minute intervals; 6 hours of record; 18 starts; total on-time 3.6 hours; a logger memory of 64,000 readings. These are our own numbers for practice, not field data.

  1. Duty cycle = 3.6 h / 6 h = 0.60, or 60%.
  2. Cycles per hour = 18 starts / 6 h = 3.
  3. Average on-cycle = 3.6 h x 60 min/h / 18 = 216 / 18 = 12 min.
  4. Off-time = 6 h - 3.6 h = 2.4 h. Average off-cycle = 2.4 x 60 / 18 = 144 / 18 = 8 min.
  5. Check: 12 + 8 = 20 min per cycle, and 60 / 20 = 3 cycles per hour. It matches.
  6. Memory at 1-minute readings: 24 h x 60 = 1,440 readings per day. 64,000 / 1,440 = 44.4 days.
  7. At 5-minute readings (assumed): 288 per day, 64,000 / 288 = 222 days. Slower sampling stretches the record, but a 12-minute on-cycle would then get only about 2 readings, below the five per cycle the DOE chapter asks for.

Whether three cycles an hour is a problem depends on the equipment and the load. Use the logger to ask a better question, then check the system.

Field card: Portable Data Loggers

Ask the question first, then log it.

  • Room logger: interior wall, 4 to 5 ft up, out of sun and supply air.
  • Interval short enough for the cycle: five or fewer minutes for a 25-minute cycle.
  • Days of record = memory readings / readings per day.
  • Sync clocks and check drift at pickup.
  • CT work: qualified person, de-energize where required, never an open CT secondary.
  • Duty cycle = on-time / elapsed; cycles per hour = starts / hours.
