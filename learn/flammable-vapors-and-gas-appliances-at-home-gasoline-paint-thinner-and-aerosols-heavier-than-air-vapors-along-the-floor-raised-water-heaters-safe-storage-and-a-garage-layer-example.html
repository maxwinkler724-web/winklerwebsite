Flammable Vapors and Gas Appliances at Home: Keeping Fumes Away from the Flame

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas water heater or furnace is a small open flame that may sit in a basement, garage or utility room, the same places many of us keep gasoline, thinner and spray cans. Vapors from those products can drift to the flame and ignite. This lesson explains how that happens and the habits that prevent it.

Why this matters

The federal consumer product safety agency says that gas-fired appliances such as dryers, furnaces and older water heaters can ignite vapors in the area, and it advises against keeping flammable liquids or gasoline-powered equipment inside a home, utility room or garage (safety alert on flammable liquids). A federal news release from 2003 put the toll from water heaters igniting flammable vapors at nearly 800 residential fires, with an average of about 5 deaths and 130 injuries per year (federal consumer product safety news release, 2003, cited as plain text in the Sources list).

What the vapors do

Gasoline, paint thinner, spray adhesives, solvent-based stains and aerosol propellants all give off flammable vapor. The federal workplace safety agency notes that flammable liquid vapors are often heavier than air and can build up in enclosed spaces (federal occupational safety page on flammable liquids). A university safety office adds that most such vapors are heavier than air, can travel along floors, and can flash back to a source of ignition (university environmental health and safety guidance (plain text, not linked)). In a house, that means a spill or a leaking can does not stay put. The vapor slides along the floor, around boxes and through doorways, until it meets something hot or sparking.

The consumer product safety agency also warns that vapors can be set off by household appliances, static electricity and pilot lights, and that a very small amount of liquid gasoline, less than half a pint once vaporized, can fill a home garage with explosive vapor (consumer product safety page on storing gasoline). Treat that as a warning about how little it takes, not as something to measure at home.

Where the flame is

  • Water heater burner and pilot. The burner sits close to the floor in many tank heaters, which is where heavy vapor collects.
  • Furnace pilot or igniter. Older pilots burn all the time. Newer igniters glow or spark only at the start of a heating cycle, but either can ignite vapor that has drifted in.
  • Other sources. Dryers, stove pilots, light switches, tool motors and static sparks.

Why heaters are raised and why newer ones differ

Minutes from a federal working group record that a national fuel gas model code asks for burners and burner ignition devices to be at least 18 inches off the floor, that participants noted flammable vapors can be present 12 inches off the floor, and that drafts or people moving can change where vapor goes, so a raised heater may not prevent ignition in every situation (consumer product safety working-group minutes). That is why many gas water heaters in garages sit on a stand. Whether yours must be raised, and by how much, depends on the local code and the heater's own instructions, so check local code and the label on the unit.

Newer residential water heaters add their own protection. The 2003 federal news release says tank-type gas water heaters made after July 1, 2003 include a flame arrestor, a device that traps and burns dangerous vapors inside the heater so they cannot ignite in the room. The same release warns that millions of older heaters are still in use, so the habits below still apply. If you are not sure which type you have, read the label and the manual, or ask a qualified gas technician.

Our own example: how much vapor is a 1 foot layer?

This is a simple illustration, not a vapor model. Real vapor moves with drafts, temperature, door openings and how much liquid spilled. Our own assumptions: a garage floor of 20 feet by 22 feet, with no cars or shelves taking up space, and vapor from a spill spreading evenly in a layer 1 foot deep.

  1. Floor area: 20 ft × 22 ft = 440 square feet.
  2. Layer volume: 440 ft² × 1 ft = 440 cubic feet.
  3. For a sense of size, 440 ft³ × 7.48 gallons per cubic foot is about 3,290 gallons of air-and-vapor mixture lying across the floor.
  4. Burner height: 18 in ÷ 12 = 1.5 ft. The burner is 1.5 ft − 1 ft = 0.5 ft, or 6 inches, above the top of our assumed layer.
  5. If the layer were 1.5 ft deep instead, it would reach the burner and hold 440 ft² × 1.5 ft = 660 cubic feet, half again as much.

Check: 20 × 22 = 440, and 440 × 1.5 = 660, which is 440 plus half of 440 (220). The 6 inch gap is small. Because vapor can be drawn upward by the heater's draft, as the working-group minutes note, raising the burner buys margin but does not make a spill safe.

Habits that keep vapors away from flame

  • Store fuel outside the living space. The consumer product safety agency says to keep gasoline outdoors, away from living areas and ignition sources, such as in a well-ventilated shed, in sturdy, sealed containers. It names glass, food containers and rusted cans as especially hazardous, and says private storage over a limited amount may be restricted locally. Check local rules.
  • Use approved containers. The 2003 release asks for tightly closed, clearly labeled, non-glass safety containers kept away from ignition sources and out of children's reach.
  • Keep solvents closed when not in use. The occupational safety page says paints, thinners and solvents belong in covered fire-resistant containers, solvent-soaked rags in covered metal containers, and spills should be cleaned up right away.
  • Ventilate and remove ignition sources when using solvents. Follow the product label. Open doors and windows, and keep flames, pilots, sparks and smoking away. A pilot you cannot move is a reason to do the job outdoors.
  • Do not refuel with the engine running. The safety alert says to shut the engine off and wait at least two minutes before refueling.
  • Change clothes after a spill. The consumer product safety agency says to change right away if gasoline gets on clothing, and not to machine wash or dry contaminated clothes.
  • Keep the burner area clean. A state university extension page advises keeping the area around the furnace housing free of dust, lint and litter. Do not stack boxes, rags or cans against the heater or furnace.

If you smell gas or strong vapors

Leave first. The federal pipeline safety agency advises turning off gas appliances if you can do so easily, leaving on foot right away, and calling 911 or the gas company from a safe distance. It says not to create any ignition source, including light switches, matches, starting a vehicle or using a phone near the leak, and not to open an automatic garage door with a vehicle inside (federal pipeline safety page). That guidance is written for natural gas, and the same rule of thumb fits a strong solvent or fuel smell: go out, then call from outside. Do not go back in to look for the source. Our related lesson on gas smells near the furnace covers the steps in more detail.

Field card: Flammable Vapors and Gas Appliances

Vapors are heavy, flames are low, and the two meet on the floor.

  • Gasoline, thinner, adhesives, stains and aerosols all give off flammable vapor.
  • Vapor is usually heavier than air and travels along the floor.
  • Pilots, burners and igniters can set it off, as can sparks and static.
  • Raised heaters and flame arrestors help; check local code and the unit label.
  • Our example: 20 x 22 x 1 ft layer = 440 ft³; burner at 18 in is 6 in above it.
  • Store fuel outdoors in approved containers; keep solvents closed; keep the burner area clean.
  • Smell gas or strong vapor: leave, then call from outside.
