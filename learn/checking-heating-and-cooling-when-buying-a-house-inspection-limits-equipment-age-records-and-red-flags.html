Checking Heating and Cooling When Buying a House

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A general home inspection looks at a lot of house in a few hours, so the furnace and air conditioner get a quick, mostly visual look. This lesson shows what that look usually includes, what it leaves out, how to read the age of the equipment, which signs deserve a closer look, and how to turn what you learn into a yearly set-aside.

Check your own rules first

This page is general education, not legal or financial advice. Inspection rules differ from state to state, and a contract can add or remove items. Read your inspection agreement and ask your agent or attorney about anything that touches the purchase contract.

What a general inspection usually covers

We use one state's published rule as a concrete example. Wisconsin's administrative code for home inspectors (SPS 131.32) says the inspector must observe and describe the heating equipment and distribution, the normal operating controls and energy source, the automatic safety controls, and the outside of chimneys, flues and vents. The inspector must run the system with its normal controls and open the panels a homeowner is meant to open for routine care. For central air conditioning, the inspector observes and describes the cooling and air handling equipment and its normal controls, and runs it with normal controls.

The same rule lists what is not required. For heating, that includes operating the safety controls, lighting or putting out fuel fires, looking inside flues, judging whether heat reaches every room evenly, and viewing a heat exchanger unless it is readily visible to an occupant. For cooling, it includes judging whether cool air is supplied evenly, checking refrigerant pressure or finding leaks, and measuring how much current the unit draws. It also says the inspector need not run cooling in weather that could damage the equipment, which matters if you are buying in winter.

Your state may word these differently, so read your own state's standards of practice or the inspector's sample report. The pattern is common, though: a general inspection checks that the equipment turns on, runs, and looks reasonable. It does not test how well it performs, how long it will last, or what is inside sealed parts.

Reading equipment age from the nameplate

Age is the single most useful fact you can collect, and you can get it in minutes. Find the nameplate on the furnace, the air handler, and the outdoor condenser. It lists the model and serial number, and the manufacturing date is usually encoded in the serial number. Our lesson How Old Is My Furnace or AC walks through it. Two cautions: the manufacturing date is not the installation date, and an indoor unit and outdoor unit may have been installed in different years. Photograph every nameplate, and if you cannot decode one, ask the maker with the model and serial number.

Typical service life, and what the numbers do and do not say

Published figures come from different methods, so we show two kinds and keep them separate.

  • A federal homeowner guide's replacement prompts. The U.S. Department of Energy's energy saver guide says to consider replacing a heat pump or air conditioner that is more than 10 years old, and a furnace or boiler that is more than 15 years old. These are prompts to start planning, not predictions of failure.
  • A national laboratory's survey estimate. A paper from a U.S. national laboratory, posted by the Department of Energy's scientific information service, fit lifetime curves to national shipment and housing-survey data. It estimated a median life of about 22.6 years for gas furnaces and about 18.0 years for central air conditioners (means of about 23.6 and 19.0). For heat pumps it found a median near 14.6 years. These are medians for a whole national stock, so half the units in the data lasted longer and half shorter.

In this lesson we use the planning prompt as the low end and the survey median as the high end of a "typical range." That gives about 15 to 23 years for a gas furnace and 10 to 18 years for a central air conditioner. This is our own way of combining the two sources, not a figure either source states. Climate, maintenance, installation quality and how hard the system works all move the real number.

Ask for records, then ask what they mean

  • Service records. Ask the seller for invoices or maintenance notes. Look for yearly tune-ups, repeated repairs of the same part, and any note about a cracked part or a refrigerant leak.
  • Permits. If equipment was replaced, ask whether a permit was pulled and closed. Whether and how a town requires it varies, so ask your agent or attorney how to confirm.
  • Warranty papers and manuals. Warranty terms are often tied to the original owner or to registration, so ask what, if anything, passes to you.
  • Fuel records. For oil heat, ask about delivery history. A sudden jump in use can point to a problem, as the state environmental guidance below describes.

When you later hire someone to look further, What to Ask an HVAC Contractor Before Hiring covers the questions.

Red flags worth writing down

  • Rust, flaking or scorching at the heat exchanger area. A general inspection often cannot see the heat exchanger. If you can see rust scale at the burner compartment or soot around it, note it and ask for a separate evaluation.
  • Water stains or rust under the air handler. This can mean a clogged or leaking condensate drain or a leaking coil. The DOE maintenance guidance for air conditioners mentions passing a stiff wire through the drain channels and checking the evaporator coil each year.
  • Old refrigerant on an old AC. Read the refrigerant type on the outdoor nameplate. The EPA says production and import of R-22 ended in 2020, with servicing of existing equipment as a continuing use. If the nameplate says R-22, ask your contractor how repairs would work and what they would cost.
  • Oil tank signs. Rust, wet spots or weeping on an aboveground tank, rusty or unstable legs, drips at the filter, spills near the fill pipe, and a stuck or stained gauge are on the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection checklist. That agency also says a buyer generally takes on responsibility for the tank, so ask your agent or attorney about it before you commit. Buried tank questions go to a qualified environmental professional.
  • Missing carbon monoxide alarms. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, and says furnaces and other fuel-burning appliances can produce CO when they malfunction. Plan to install working alarms on move-in day, and read our carbon monoxide lesson.
  • Old or damaged wiring at the equipment. Brittle, cracked or scorched insulation, loose splices or a burned smell at the disconnect or control box should go in your notes for an electrician or contractor to review.
  • Odd sounds on the inspection run. Our noises lesson helps you describe what you heard.

When to ask for a separate HVAC evaluation

Consider one when the equipment is at or past the low end of its range, when the inspection report notes a safety item or an unsafe or unusual condition, when records are missing, when cooling could not be tested because of cold weather, or when you see any red flag above. A heating and cooling contractor can open the equipment, test combustion and refrigerant circuits, and give a written finding. Expect to pay for it, and schedule it inside your inspection window; ask your agent or attorney how long that window is. If a replacement quote results, this lesson on reading a replacement estimate shows what a written contract should contain.

Worked example (our own example)

Suppose a house has a gas furnace made 18 years ago and a central air conditioner made 15 years ago, and today is 2026. Both have working nameplates. We assume the nameplate dates match the installs, so the furnace was installed in 2008 and the AC in 2011.

Step 1: compare age with the ranges.

  • Furnace: 18 years old against 15 to 23. It is 3 years past the low end and about 5 years short of 23 (23 minus 18 = 5).
  • Air conditioner: 15 years old against 10 to 18. It is 5 years past the low end and about 3 years short of 18 (18 minus 15 = 3).

Step 2: assume replacement costs. These are our assumptions, not quotes or averages from a source: $6,000 for a furnace and $7,500 for an air conditioner, installed. Your local prices will differ; get written estimates.

Step 3: pick assumed replacement dates. We plan for the air conditioner to be replaced in 3 years (it reaches 18) and the furnace in 5 years (it reaches 23). Either may last longer or fail sooner. That is why we plan on the cautious end of the dates.

Step 4: divide into a yearly reserve.

  • Air conditioner: $7,500 / 3 years = $2,500 per year.
  • Furnace: $6,000 / 5 years = $1,200 per year.
  • Years 1 to 3: $2,500 + $1,200 = $3,700 per year, about $308 per month (3,700 / 12).
  • Years 4 to 5: $1,200 per year, since the AC is funded.
  • Check: 3 x 3,700 + 2 x 1,200 = 11,100 + 2,400 = $13,500, which equals $7,500 + $6,000.

If you cannot carry $3,700 a year, spreading the AC over more years raises the chance of an unplanned failure, and you can decide that trade-off with your own finances. If the equipment lasts beyond the planned dates, the reserve simply covers the purchase later. How inspection findings affect the sale itself is a question for your agent or attorney.

Field card: Checking Heating and Cooling When Buying a House

Photograph nameplates, ask for records, plan a reserve.

  • A general inspection mostly confirms the equipment runs; it often skips the heat exchanger, refrigerant pressure and room-by-room performance.
  • Get model, serial and refrigerant type from every nameplate.
  • DOE prompt: consider replacing a furnace or boiler over 15 years, an AC or heat pump over 10 years.
  • Lab survey medians: about 22.6 years (gas furnace), 18.0 years (central AC).
  • Red flags: rust at the heat exchanger area, water under the air handler, R-22, oil tank rust, no CO alarms, worn wiring.
  • Ask for a separate HVAC evaluation when age, safety items or missing records call for it.
  • Budget example: $13,500 assumed over 5 years is $3,700 a year for 3 years, then $1,200 a year.
