Critical Radius of Insulation on Small Pipes and Wires: When More Insulation Can Increase Heat Loss, and Why HVAC Pipes Are Safe

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Insulation almost always cuts heat loss, so it is surprising that wrapping a very thin tube or wire can make it lose more heat at first. The reason is geometry: a thicker wrap adds resistance, but it also adds outside surface area for air to touch. This lesson shows where the turning point sits, why ordinary HVAC pipe and line-set insulation is far past it, and what it means for electrical cable heating.

Two things change when you add a wrap

Picture a small round tube at a fixed temperature in still air. Heat leaves in two steps, one after the other: it conducts outward through the insulation, then it moves from the insulation's outer surface into the air by convection and radiation. Our lesson on the three ways heat moves covers those modes. Adding insulation changes each step in opposite directions:

  • The conduction step gets worse for the heat (more resistance), because the heat now has a longer path through the material.
  • The air-film step gets better for the heat (less resistance), because the outer surface is bigger and the air film resistance, per foot of length, is inversely proportional to that surface.

On a big pipe the first effect wins from the first layer. On a very small tube the second effect can win for a while. Lecture notes from a university engineering college put it this way: adding insulation to a pipe can initially increase heat loss, and the loss peaks at the critical radius of insulation, after which it falls as more is added.

The formula, step by step

For a round tube with inner radius r1 (the bare tube's outside) and insulation out to radius r2, the two steps are resistances in series, per foot of length. This is the same series idea we used in the will-it-sweat lesson.

R per foot = ln(r2/r1) / (2πk) + 1 / (2π r2 h)

Here k is the insulation's thermal conductivity in Btu/h-ft-F, h is the outside film coefficient in Btu/h-ft²-F, and the radii are in feet. R comes out in h-ft-F/Btu. Heat loss per foot is the temperature difference divided by R. The first term grows with r2; the second shrinks. To find where their sum is smallest, take the derivative with respect to r2: it is 1/(2πk r2) − 1/(2π h r2²), which is zero when

rcrit = k / h

The same result appears in the university lecture notes and in a University of Waterloo heat transfer problem solution (see Sources). Read the derivative as a direction rule:

  • If r2 is less than k/h, the derivative is negative: R falls as you add insulation, so heat loss rises.
  • If r2 is more than k/h, R rises with every added layer, so heat loss falls.
  • So when the bare tube's own radius r1 is already larger than k/h, every layer helps. When r1 is smaller than k/h, loss rises until r2 reaches k/h, peaks there, then falls.

Two more directions worth memorizing: a better insulator (smaller k) shrinks rcrit, and a breezier or wetter surface (bigger h) shrinks it too. A bare, still-air surface, with small h, makes the effect strongest.

Worked example (our own): 1/8 in. tube and 1 in. tube

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume insulation k = 0.025 Btu/h-ft-F, a combined outside film h = 1.5 Btu/h-ft²-F (still air, convection plus radiation lumped together), and a 100 F difference between the tube wall and the air. These inputs are our picks for illustration, not measured values for any product. We also treat the tube wall as at one temperature, ignoring the fluid-side film and the metal.

Step 1: critical radius. rcrit = k/h = 0.025 / 1.5 = 0.01667 ft = 0.01667 × 12 = 0.200 in. (about 1/5 in., or a 0.4 in. outside diameter).

Step 2: the 1/8 in. OD tube. r1 = 1/16 in. = 0.0625 in. = 0.005208 ft, which is below rcrit.

  • Bare: there is no conduction term, so R = 1/(2π × 0.005208 × 1.5) = 1/0.04909 = 20.37. Loss = 100 / 20.37 = 4.9 Btu/h per foot.
  • At r2 = 0.2 in. (0.01667 ft): conduction term = ln(0.2/0.0625) / (2π × 0.025) = 1.1632 / 0.15708 = 7.40. Film term = 1/(2π × 0.01667 × 1.5) = 1/0.15708 = 6.37. R = 13.77, loss = 100 / 13.77 = 7.3 Btu/h per foot, which is 48 percent more than bare.
  • At r2 = 0.4 in.: conduction 11.82 + film 3.18 = R 15.00; loss = 6.7.
  • At r2 = 1.0 in.: conduction = ln(16)/0.15708 = 17.65; film = 1.27; R = 18.92; loss = 5.3 (still 8 percent above bare).
  • At r2 = 1.5 in.: conduction = ln(24)/0.15708 = 20.23; film = 0.85; R = 21.08; loss = 4.7, finally about 3 percent below bare.

The loss returns to the bare value near r2 = 1.32 in., which is about 1.25 in. of insulation on that 1/8 in. tube. Only past that is the wrap a net win.

Step 3: a 1 in. OD tube. Now r1 = 0.5 in. = 0.04167 ft, well above the 0.2 in. rcrit. Bare: R = 1/(2π × 0.04167 × 1.5) = 1/0.3927 = 2.55, loss = 39.3 Btu/h per foot. With insulation out to r2 = 0.75 in.: conduction = ln(1.5)/0.15708 = 2.58, film = 1.70, R = 4.28, loss = 23.4 (60 percent of bare). At 1.0 in.: R = 5.69, loss = 17.6 (45 percent). At 1.5 in.: R = 7.84, loss = 12.7 (32 percent). At 2.0 in.: R = 9.46, loss = 10.6 (27 percent). Every step goes down, with no hump. A 1 in. nominal steel pipe has a larger outside diameter than 1 in., which only moves it further past rcrit.

Why HVAC pipe and line sets are safe

With our assumed inputs rcrit is only about 0.2 in. Take a 3/8 in. OD refrigerant tube as a small case (radius 0.1875 in.); hydronic and larger refrigerant lines are bigger still. Those bare surfaces sit at or beyond the turning point, so insulation reduces loss. For the 3/8 in. OD tube, our numbers give 14.7 Btu/h per foot bare, 14.8 at r2 = 0.2 in. (a rounding-level 0.2 percent bump), and 10.8 with 3/8 in. of insulation (r2 = 0.5625 in.). In practice the hump is gone before the first layer ends. Other things matter far more on a real job: gaps, compressed wrap, unsealed seams and missing vapor retarders on cold lines, which the lessons on mechanical pipe insulation, line-set insulation thickness and bare pipe heat loss in mechanical rooms take up. Do not shorten or skip insulation on a cold line because of this lesson; on a cold line a gap or a thin spot invites condensation.

The effect can matter for objects whose radius is below rcrit: very thin wires, small-diameter instrument or sensor leads, and thin capillary-type tubes, all of which are smaller than our 1/8 in. example. Treat the 0.2 in. figure as a rough scale only. If your insulation is a poorer conductor (bigger k, such as a plastic jacket) or the air is still (smaller h), rcrit is larger and reaches bigger objects.

What it means for electrical cable heating

A current-carrying wire makes heat inside the conductor, so the question flips: for a fixed amount of heat, what keeps the conductor coolest? At steady state, conductor temperature equals air temperature plus the heat times the total resistance. Anything that lowers R (as thickening below rcrit does) lowers the conductor temperature, and anything that raises R raises it. The University of Waterloo problem solution works exactly this case, an electric wire with a plastic cover, using k = 0.15 W/m-C and h = 24 W/m²-C. That gives rcrit = 0.15/24 = 6.25 mm. Its conclusion is that doubling the plastic thickness, with the outer radius still below 6.25 mm, increases heat loss and lowers the wire's temperature.

Three practical cautions follow. First, a cable's jacket is chosen for voltage and temperature rating and protection, not as a cooling device; do not add wrap hoping to cool a wire. Second, the benefit only runs up to rcrit, and past that a thicker cover runs the conductor hotter. Third, and most important for a technician, the bigger heating risk is insulation around the cable. A National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) report on thermally insulated electric cables found that thermal insulation around cables carrying typical rated current can push the jacket past the common 60 °C limit, and that jacket temperature is quite sensitive to the insulation's thermal conductivity. Keep to the wire's rated ampacity and the National Electrical Code's rules on cables in insulation, and ask a licensed electrician when in doubt.

Field card: Critical Radius of Insulation

Small cylinders can lose more heat with a thin wrap. Big pipes cannot.

  • R per foot = ln(r2/r1)/(2 pi k) + 1/(2 pi r2 h).
  • Critical radius of a cylinder: r_crit = k/h. Loss peaks there.
  • If r1 is bigger than k/h, every layer of insulation cuts loss.
  • If r1 is smaller, loss rises up to k/h, then falls; it needs more insulation to beat bare.
  • Smaller k or bigger h gives a smaller r_crit.
  • Our example (k = 0.025, h = 1.5): r_crit = 0.2 in.; 1/8 in. tube loses 48 percent more at the peak.
  • HVAC pipe and line sets are above r_crit. Insulate them fully.
  • For cables, surrounding insulation is the heating risk. Follow ampacity rules.
