Ventilation and Cooling for Potato and Onion Storage Buildings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A potato or onion storage building is a big, slow air handler wrapped around a living pile. The crop gives off heat, moisture and carbon dioxide all winter, and the equipment's job is to move the right air through the pile at the right temperature and humidity. This lesson covers the layout, the controls and a worked heat and airflow example. It is a companion to the produce cold storage and ripening room lesson, which we do not repeat here.

How the air gets through the pile

In a bulk storage, fans push air into a plenum or a set of ducts under or beside the pile. The air then rises up through the gaps between the tubers or bulbs and leaves at the top. A state extension report on potato storage says ventilation removes field heat, extra moisture, and the carbon dioxide and heat the crop makes, supplies oxygen, and helps keep temperature and humidity even. It also says uniform airflow through the whole pile is important. Booster fans placed in a plenum to fix a weak spot can make distribution worse, according to a university-hosted fan-control report, so good duct layout matters more than extra fans.

Design airflow depends on the crop and the market. A university extension presentation gave 0.8 cfm per hundredweight (cwt) for seed, 1.0 for French fry stock and 1.5 for chip stock, and about 1.25 inches of water column of static pressure for a pile. Another extension page gives about 1 cubic meter per minute per cubic meter of onions as adequate airflow. See our airflow and static pressure basics if static pressure is new to you.

Outdoor air first, refrigeration when needed

When the outdoor air is colder than the pile and not too dry or too wet, the cheapest cooling is to bring it in. The fan-control report found that sites with variable-speed fans ventilated whenever outside air was available in winter. When outdoor air is too warm, as during harvest or a warm spell, some buildings add a refrigeration coil. A university extension talk noted that some growers turned refrigeration on for cool-down in some years. The same fan report says that during refrigerated operation most sites raised fan speed as a precaution against coil icing, and that a fouled cooling coil can keep a building from reaching its design airflow. Our economizers basics lesson covers the same free-cooling idea in ordinary air handlers.

Pull the pile down slowly. The extension talk gave target cooling rates of 3 to 4°F per week for seed, 1 to 2°F for processing stock and 2 to 3°F for fresh market. It also says pulling air too cold too fast causes condensation, and that pile-to-air differences should stay at 5°F or less to limit it.

Temperature and humidity targets

Potatoes and onions want opposite humidity. A state extension chart lists late-crop potatoes at 38 to 40°F and 90 to 95 percent relative humidity, and dry onions at 32°F and 65 to 70 percent. Another extension page puts onion curing at about 80 percent humidity with good air circulation. For potatoes, a university paper recommends a plenum humidity of at least 90 percent and preferably above 95 percent, and says to measure it in the plenum, downstream of the fans and humidifiers, not in the return air. Potato holding temperature depends on the market: a state pest-management guide lists 38 to 45°F for fresh market and 45 to 55°F for processing, and says curing is done at 50 to 55°F with humidity above 95 percent. For humidity terms, see humidity ratio and dew point.

Heat of respiration and carbon dioxide

Stored crops are alive. They take in oxygen and give off carbon dioxide, moisture and heat. A university potato bulletin reports respiration heat for stored potatoes of 16.6 Btu per cwt per day at 42°F, 17.6 at 45°F and 18.7 at 48°F, and says temperature has the greatest effect on respiration. If the heat is not removed, tuber temperature rises and respiration rises with it. A food-science fact sheet for dry onions gives a conversion: multiply milliliters of CO2 per kilogram per hour by 440 to get Btu per ton per day. At its stated onion rate of 3 to 4 mL per kg per hour near freezing, our arithmetic gives 1,320 to 1,760 Btu per ton per day, or 66 to 88 Btu per cwt per day.

Carbon dioxide builds up if the air stops moving. The same potato bulletin says maximum recommended CO2 levels range from 1,200 to 5,000 ppm depending on market requirements, and links high CO2 to darker fry color and off-flavors. Keeping air moving even at low flow, and having ventilation set from the plenum's measured conditions, is how a building avoids this.

Condensation on a cold roof

Warm, moist air from the pile that touches a cold surface drips back onto the crop, and wet tubers favor decay. A regional extension design publication from 1983 recommended an insulation value of R-30 in the ceiling, a vapor barrier facing the building interior, sealed seams, and ventilation of the space above the insulation. It is an older publication, so treat it as a design idea, not a current code requirement. Also avoid bringing warm air over cold tubers, which the state pest-management guide says causes condensation. A fan-control report noted that some buildings cycled fans in six-hour on and off blocks to limit condensation.

Sprout inhibitors

Treatments applied to stored crops to stop sprouting are regulated products. We do not give application rates or methods. Label and state rules apply, so the applicator must follow the product label and the rules in the state where the building is. Ask the building owner who is licensed and responsible, and keep fans, vents and any heater interlocks as the product label says during application.

Sensors and controls

Controllers in these buildings usually work off a temperature difference. A fan-control report describes measuring the difference between return air and plenum air, and between pulp temperature at the top and bottom of the pile. Controllers adjust fan speed to hold a set difference and include a minimum speed. The extension talk said to set sensors about 5°F below pulp temperature at loading. Humidity sensors should be tested every year, says the university humidity paper, and either calibrated or replaced. See temperature sensor mounting. For livestock buildings with the same air-moving problem, see barn ventilation.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume a pile of 20,000 cwt of potatoes held at 45°F. Assume the respiration heat is 17.6 Btu per cwt per day, taken from the potato bulletin table above. The pile size and the 3°F figure below are our assumptions, not measurements.

  1. Heat per day: 20,000 cwt x 17.6 Btu per cwt per day = 352,000 Btu per day.
  2. Heat per hour: 352,000 / 24 = 14,667 Btu/h.
  3. Assume the air may warm 3°F as it passes through the pile, which is our assumed temperature difference.
  4. Use Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference: 14,667 = 1.08 x cfm x 3, so cfm = 14,667 / 3.24 = 4,527 cfm.
  5. That is about 0.23 cfm per cwt.

Compare with design airflow. At 0.8 cfm per cwt, the pile gets 16,000 cfm, and the same heat warms the air only 14,667 / (1.08 x 16,000) = 0.85°F. So airflow is chosen for even temperature and for cooldown, not just to carry away respiration heat. Learn the formula in the heat transfer formulas lesson.

Field card: Potato and Onion Storage Ventilation

Move air evenly, keep it a little cooler than the pile, and keep the roof dry.

  • Potatoes: high humidity. Onions: low humidity (65 to 70 percent).
  • Use outdoor air when it is colder than the pile; use a coil when it is not.
  • Cool slowly and keep pile-to-air differences small to avoid condensation.
  • Respiration heat in our example: 17.6 Btu per cwt per day, about 14,667 Btu/h for 20,000 cwt.
  • Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
  • Measure humidity in the plenum. Test sensors every year.
  • For sprout inhibitors, follow the product label and state rules.
