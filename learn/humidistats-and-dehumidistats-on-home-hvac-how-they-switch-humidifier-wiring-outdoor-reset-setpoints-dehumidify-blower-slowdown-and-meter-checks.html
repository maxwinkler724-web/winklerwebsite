Humidistats and Dehumidistats on Home HVAC: How They Switch Humidifiers, Reset With Outdoor Temperature and Slow the Blower

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A humidistat is the simplest control in the house: a switch that opens or closes as the humidity changes. A dehumidistat is the same idea wired the other way around. This lesson covers what each one switches, how it ties into the blower and the 24 V circuit, why the winter setpoint has to drop when it gets cold, and how to check one with a meter.

A humidistat is a switch

Inside a humidistat is a sensing element that changes with moisture and moves a set of contacts. On a humidifier, the contacts close when the air is too dry and open when the room reaches the setpoint. A dehumidistat does the opposite job: it signals when the air is too humid. Many modern wall controls and thermostats use an electronic sensor instead of a mechanical element, but they still end up as a switch or a 24 V output that tells other equipment what to do. A spec sheet for one combination humidistat and dehumidistat lists setpoint ranges of 20 to 60 percent, with separate humidify and dehumidify limits you can program, and 24 VAC power on its C and R terminals.

How it runs a humidifier

On a typical whole-house humidifier the humidistat sits in series with a 24 V control circuit. When the contacts close, the circuit energizes a water solenoid valve (or a steam unit's contactor), and water flows onto the evaporator pad. The humidifier is also tied to the air handler so it does not spray water into still air. One maker owner's manual describes two modes: with blower activation on, the control can start the fan to humidify; with it off, the humidifier runs only when it needs humidity and either the fan is running or the system is making heat. A consumer owner's guide from another maker states it plainly: the furnace blower must be running to activate the humidifier. If a solenoid does not click on a test, the first check in that manual is whether the blower is actually running.

For wiring details on the 24 V side, including where R and C land, see Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats, and for humidifier hardware see Whole-House Humidifiers: Bypass, Fan-Powered and Steam. If you add a humidifier or other accessory to the same transformer, check its load with Control Transformers: Primary Taps, VA Sizing and Secondary Checks.

Where it mounts

The spec sheet we read says the control belongs on the return duct for proper sensing, or on a living-area wall away from drafts, doors, windows, bathrooms and kitchens when it is a remote sensor. The consumer owner's guide agrees: a humidistat is installed on an interior wall in the living area or on the cold air return. A return-duct control reads blended house air, and the humidifier runs when the blower runs anyway. A wall control reads the room where people live but can be fooled by a nearby shower or a sunny wall. Either way, read the maker's location rules before drilling.

Outdoor temperature and the winter setpoint

Some controls use an outdoor sensor to lower the target as it gets colder. Others rely on a printed table and a person who turns the dial down in November. The reason is condensation. Warm, moist indoor air meets cold glass, drops to the glass temperature and drops its water. The University of Missouri Extension explains that condensation forms when indoor air touches a cold surface such as a single-pane window, and recommends roughly 35 to 50 percent indoor humidity in cold-climate heating season. A North Dakota State University Extension slide set is more specific for double glazing: at 0 degrees F outside the inside glass surface is about 43 degrees F, and 70 degree air at 40 percent humidity cooled to that surface reaches nearly 100 percent, so it condenses. That source puts the winter recommendation at about 30 to 40 percent. The EPA's mold guide gives a wider range, below 60 percent and ideally 30 to 50 percent, and says condensation on windows can be a sign of high humidity.

The maker owner's manual we read gives a lookup table as a compromise between comfort and condensation: 45 percent at 40 degrees F outdoors, 40 at 30, 35 at 20, 30 at 10, 25 at 0, 20 at minus 10, and 15 at minus 20. It advises lowering the setting before temperatures drop. The consumer guide says the same thing in words: as the outside temperature drops, lower the setting to allow for the effects of dew point.

Worked example: why the setpoint has to drop

Our own example. These are assumptions, not measurements. Indoor air is 70 degrees F. The inside glass surface of a double-pane window on a cold day is 43 degrees F (the figure the extension slides give for 0 degrees F outside). We compare the indoor dew point with the glass temperature using a standard dew point approximation with constants a = 17.625 and b = 243.04 degrees C.

  1. Convert 70 degrees F to Celsius: (70 − 32) / 1.8 = 21.1 degrees C.
  2. For 40 percent humidity, compute γ = ln(0.40) + a × T / (b + T) = −0.916 + 17.625 × 21.1 / 264.1 = −0.916 + 1.408 = 0.492.
  3. Dew point = b × γ / (a − γ) = 243.04 × 0.492 / 17.133 = 6.98 degrees C, or about 44.6 degrees F.
  4. Compare: the dew point (44.6 degrees F) is above the glass (43 degrees F), so the glass is colder than the dew point and water condenses on it.
  5. Repeat at 35 percent: dew point is about 41.1 degrees F, which is below 43, so the glass stays dry with about 2 degrees F of margin. At 30 percent it is about 37.1 degrees F.
  6. The break-even humidity, where the dew point equals 43 degrees F, works out to about 37.7 percent.

So with these assumptions a 40 percent setting fogs the glass, and about 35 percent keeps it clear. A colder window, such as single-pane or one with a failed seal, has a lower glass temperature, which pushes the safe humidity lower still, which is why the table values keep falling with the outdoor temperature. Our numbers are a sketch for one assumed window; real glass temperature depends on the window and on air movement.

Dehumidify terminals and the blower

In cooling, the coil removes moisture only if it is cold enough. A lab report from a university solar-energy center found that lower airflow gives more latent (moisture) capacity but less sensible capacity: in one test, sensible capacity fell about 15 percent when flow dropped from 425 to 300 cfm per ton while latent rose about 7 percent, and warmer coil surfaces mean lower moisture removal. It also warned that airflow below about 200 cfm per ton can lead to coil icing. That is the logic behind a dehumidify terminal on a variable-speed furnace or air handler. When the humidistat or thermostat decides humidity is high during a cooling call, it signals the blower to run slower, the coil gets colder, and more water comes out of the air. The setup details are in Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Furnaces and Air Handlers.

The signal polarity differs between makers. A thermostat installation manual we read has a jumper that sets the dehumidify output either normally closed (de-energized to run the fan low) or normally open (energized to run low), and it releases when second-stage cooling is called or the call is satisfied. A humidistat spec sheet labels its dehumidifier terminals Float, DHUM, R, Fan and C. Always read the air handler's board to see whether it wants 24 V removed or applied on the dehumidify input, because the wrong guess does the opposite of what you want.

Whole-house dehumidifier controls

A standalone whole-house dehumidifier has its own control, often a built-in or remote humidity sensor. One installer manual we read describes a controller with a 24 V terminal and a separate dehumidify terminal, where 24 V on the dehumidify terminal runs the fan and compressor and 24 V on the fan terminal runs the fan only. That manual says the fan always runs during dehumidification, and shuts off for ten minutes after a cycle so water can drain. Check that your unit has a float or drain safety, and see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage for setpoint and drain basics. For a related blower question, Thermostat Fan Auto vs On covers how fan mode ties into humidity and comfort.

Checking a humidistat with a meter

This is our own procedure, a general method rather than a maker instruction, so follow the maker's test steps when you have them.

  1. Shut off power and lock out the equipment. Confirm with a voltage check that the 24 V circuit is dead.
  2. Disconnect one wire from the humidistat so you are measuring the switch alone, not the circuit around it.
  3. Set the meter to continuity or ohms and touch the two switch terminals.
  4. Turn the setpoint dial from its lowest setting to its highest. On a humidify control, the contacts should be open when the setpoint is below the room humidity, and close when you turn the setpoint above it. A dehumidify control behaves in reverse.
  5. Compare the switching point to a separate humidity meter. A control that never changes, or changes far from the room reading, is bad or needs recalibrating.
  6. For an electronic control with 24 V power and an output, measure for 24 V at the output terminal instead of continuity, with power on and your hands clear of live parts.

If the humidistat checks good, move on to the interlock, then the solenoid or coil and the transformer.

Field card: Humidistats and Dehumidistats

Switch on humidity, interlocked with air movement.

  • Humidify control closes on dry air; dehumidify control signals on humid air.
  • The humidifier needs air moving: check the blower or heat call interlock first.
  • Return duct or interior living-area wall; away from drafts, windows, baths and kitchens.
  • Lower the winter setpoint as outdoor temperature falls; glass sets the limit.
  • Dew point above glass temperature means water on the glass.
  • Dehumidify input slows the blower in cooling; check whether the board wants 24 V on or off.
  • Meter check: power off, lift one lead, continuity changes as the dial crosses room humidity.
