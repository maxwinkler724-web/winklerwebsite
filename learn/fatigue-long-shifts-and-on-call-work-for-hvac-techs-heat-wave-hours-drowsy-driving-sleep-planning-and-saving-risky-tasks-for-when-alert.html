Fatigue, Long Shifts and On-Call Work for HVAC Techs: Heat Wave Hours, Drowsy Driving, Sleep Planning and Saving Risky Tasks for When Alert

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The week the temperature peaks is the week the phone never stops, and the tech who answers it is often awake at hours when the body wants to be asleep. This lesson covers what long days and night calls do to your judgment, why the drive home can be the most dangerous part of the shift, and a few plain rules for planning sleep, sorting your tasks and speaking up before you are too tired to.

Why the hours stretch

This part is our own explanation, not a statistic. Cooling and heating calls do not arrive evenly. A heat wave or a cold snap makes many systems fail or run flat out at the same time, so the call list grows faster than a crew can work it down. Customers with no cooling or no heat are uncomfortable or at risk, so calls feel urgent. Parts run short, drive times between stops grow, and a job that should take an hour turns into three because the first fix did not hold. A 12 to 16 hour day is the result of many small delays, and each one feels reasonable while you are in it. That is the trap: you rarely decide to work a sixteen hour day. You just keep saying yes to the next call.

What the research says about long hours

Long hours show up in injury data. A study published with CDC records analyzed a large national survey of U.S. workers covering 1987 to 2000. It found that working at least 12 hours a day was linked to a 37% higher injury hazard rate, working at least 60 hours a week to a 23% higher rate, and jobs with any overtime to a 61% higher rate. The authors adjusted for age, sex, occupation, industry and region, and concluded the extra risk was not just a result of long-hour jobs being in dangerous industries. These are population figures from many kinds of work, not a measurement of HVAC specifically, but the direction is clear and the rates rose as hours rose.

NIOSH also describes what being awake for a long time does to performance. It reports that about 17 hours awake is roughly equal to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%, and about 24 hours awake to 0.10%, which is above the U.S. drunk-driving limit of 0.08%. It adds that anyone short on sleep should not drink any alcohol before driving or doing other critical tasks. A tech who got up at 6 AM and is still running calls at 11 PM is in that 17 hour zone.

The NIOSH sleep-and-work bulletin makes a point worth remembering: risk depends on more than hours alone. It names how much control the worker has over the schedule, non-work responsibilities and the details of the schedule itself. Control matters, which is why the later sections focus on things you can plan.

Drowsy driving after long calls

Your van is the place where fatigue can hurt people who never hired you. NHTSA says drowsy driving can cause a crash at any time of day or night but peaks from midnight to 6 AM and again in the late afternoon, when the body clock dips. NHTSA reports 644 deaths in drowsy-driving-related crashes in 2024, and its earlier estimate for 2017 was about 91,000 police-reported crashes, about 50,000 injured and nearly 800 killed. NHTSA cautions that these counts are likely an underestimate. It describes the typical crash as one driver alone, leaving the road at high speed with no sign of braking. It lists a late or long shift at work as one cause of fatigue.

The same page warns that severe sleep loss can cause micro-sleeps of four to five seconds. At 55 mph, NHTSA notes, that is more than 100 yards traveled while asleep. Its warning sign for a solo driver is drifting over lane lines or hitting a rumble strip. NHTSA says coffee or energy drinks alone are not always enough, and that one to two cups of coffee plus a 20-minute nap in a safe place, such as a lighted rest stop, helps only for a short time. Sleep is the real fix. For the vehicle side of the job (loading, distraction and weight), see Driving and Loading an HVAC Service Van Safely. This lesson adds the missing piece: who is behind the wheel and how long they have been awake.

Sleep basics for shift and on-call workers

CDC reported that adults aged 18 to 60 should get at least 7 hours of sleep a night, a recommendation from two sleep-medicine professional societies, and that sleeping less than 7 hours is linked to higher rates of obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke and frequent mental distress. NIOSH points to 7 to 8 hours a night as linked with better health and safety outcomes. The NIOSH bulletin also gives these worker tips:

  • Leave enough time to sleep after a shift, and protect that time.
  • Sleep in a dark, quiet, cool, comfortable room.
  • Avoid heavy meals and alcohol before bed, and limit caffeine and other stimulants for several hours beforehand.
  • See a health care provider if sleep problems persist.

For employers the same bulletin recommends at least 10 consecutive hours off duty per day, short breaks about every 1 to 2 hours during demanding work, and one or two full rest days after a run of long shifts. You may not control your employer's schedule, but these numbers give you something concrete to point to when you ask for relief.

Worked example: a heat wave week (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not data from a real crew. We assume one tech on five straight heat wave days, with 1 hour of total commute per day (30 minutes each way) and 3 hours per day for meals, a shower, family and chores. Drive time between calls is counted inside the shift. Sleep opportunity is the time left in a 24 hour day after those three items.

Compare that with CDC's at least 7 hours a night. Five nights at 7 hours is 35 hours, so the tech has 35 - 31 = 4 hours less opportunity than the minimum, and the average opportunity is 31 / 5 = 6.2 hours. Two more things make it worse. Sleep opportunity is not sleep: lying down, falling asleep and waking up take time, so real sleep is lower than the number in the table. And the shortfall is not spread evenly. The shortest night, 5 hours, follows the longest day. The 69 hour week also sits above the 60 hour mark where the study above found higher injury rates. Any night call that pulls you out of bed makes every number worse.

Plan the task list around your alertness

When you cannot fix the hours, you can still choose the order of the work. Think of tasks in two groups. This sorting is our own practice, not a rule from a standard.

  • Save for when you are alert: roof work and ladder climbs (ladder and roof fall safety), brazing and other hot work (hot work permits and fire watch), live electrical measurements, and lockout and verification steps (lockout/tagout and verify dead). These punish a single slip.
  • Fine for a tired day: thermostat checks, filter changes at ground level, paperwork, parts counts, cleaning and loading the van for tomorrow, and giving a customer a clear temporary plan.

If a call needs a risky task and you are past your limit, the safe answer is to make the equipment safe, tell the customer plainly what is needed, and schedule the work for a rested hour. Our own practice is to ask yourself before any climb or flame: "Would I do this if I were fresh?" If the honest answer is no, stop.

Breaks, water and food

The NIOSH bulletin recommends short breaks about every 1 to 2 hours during demanding work. In a heat wave the break also serves a second job; our heat stress lesson covers water, rest and warning signs, and the cold stress lesson covers winter. Our own practice is to stop for real food at a set time instead of grabbing something on the move, drink water between calls rather than waiting until you are thirsty, and keep the last meal of the night light, since NIOSH advises avoiding heavy meals and alcohol close to bedtime.

Speaking up to dispatch or a supervisor

Telling someone you are too tired is a safety report, not a complaint. Use plain facts: when you got up, how long you have been working, how far the next drive is, and what you cannot do safely right now. For example: "I have been up since 6 and it is 10 PM. I can do the no-cooling check at ground level, but I am not going on that roof tonight. Can we book it for the morning?" Offering what you can do keeps the conversation useful. If you lead a crew, make it normal for others to say the same. This lesson does not cover pay, hours rules or legal rights; ask your employer or the proper agency about those.

Simple personal rules

  • No roof work alone after midnight. See Working Alone as an HVAC Tech for check-in plans.
  • If you are drifting in your lane, yawning repeatedly or cannot remember the last few miles, stop driving. Call a ride, or nap in a safe, lit place before you go on.
  • After a very long day, go home and sleep before you start the next job.
  • Do not drink alcohol when you are sleep-deprived and need to drive.
  • Set a personal cutoff hour for risky tasks and tell your dispatcher what it is.

Field card: fatigue and long shifts

Tired is a hazard, not a badge. Save the risky work for when you are alert.

  • Long days raise injury risk: a CDC-hosted study linked 12+ hour days to a 37% higher injury hazard rate.
  • About 17 hours awake is roughly like 0.05% blood alcohol (NIOSH).
  • Drowsy driving peaks midnight to 6 AM and late afternoon (NHTSA).
  • Adults need at least 7 hours a night (CDC); count real sleep opportunity, not shift length.
  • Save roofs, ladders, brazing, live electrical and lockout for when alert.
  • Take a short break every 1 to 2 hours of demanding work; eat and drink on a schedule.
  • Say it plainly to dispatch: hours awake, what you can and cannot do safely.
  • Sleepy behind the wheel: stop. Call a ride or rest first.
