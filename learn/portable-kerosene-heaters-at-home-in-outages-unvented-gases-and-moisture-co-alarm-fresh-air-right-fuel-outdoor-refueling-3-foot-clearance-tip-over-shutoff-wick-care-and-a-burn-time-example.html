Portable Kerosene Heaters at Home: Safe Use During Outages

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When the power goes out in winter, many households reach for a portable kerosene heater. These heaters can keep a room warm without electricity, but they burn fuel inside the living space and they have no chimney. This lesson explains what that means, how to cut the risks, and how to estimate how long a tank of fuel will last.

Why an unvented heater is different

A furnace or boiler sends its combustion gases out through a flue or vent pipe. A portable kerosene heater does not. A university extension fact sheet describes these units as unvented: everything the flame produces goes straight into the room. That includes carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen dioxide, a gas that irritates the eyes, nose and throat. The same fact sheet says the burning also uses up oxygen from the room air, so fresh air has to come in to replace it.

Water is another product of burning fuel. A university extension fact sheet on unvented heaters says water vapor is a byproduct that stays in the space instead of leaving through a flue. In a closed house that extra moisture can show up as fogged windows and damp corners. Our lesson on window condensation in winter explains why that matters.

A federal product-safety guide to indoor air lists unvented kerosene heaters among common sources of both CO and nitrogen dioxide. It also says a yellow-tipped flame that will not clear up usually points to a heater that is out of adjustment and putting out more pollutants.

Carbon monoxide and the CO alarm

CO is a colorless, odorless gas that reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. A federal product-safety page lists headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, sleepiness and confusion as symptoms, and notes that CO poisoning is easily mistaken for a cold or flu. Because you cannot smell it, you need an alarm.

  • A university extension fact sheet says the room where a kerosene heater runs should have a CO alarm, a smoke alarm with fresh batteries and a fire extinguisher.
  • A federal CO fact sheet says to put CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, with battery power or battery backup. Its test button checks the circuitry only, so follow the alarm maker's instructions for testing and replacement.
  • If the alarm sounds, the same fact sheet says to get outside to fresh air and call emergency services, not to hunt for the source, and to stay out until responders say it is safe.

For more on alarms see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do, and for the bigger outage picture see Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages: Generators, Space Heaters and Venting.

Fresh air: crack a window or door

Because the heater has no vent, the room needs a path for fresh air. A federal kerosene-heater safety alert says to run the heater with the door to the rest of the house open. If the door must stay shut, or the house is tight or winterized, it says to open an outside window about one inch. A university extension fact sheet gives the same idea: crack a window or door and keep interior doors open so air can move between rooms. The heater's owner's manual sets the exact amount, so follow it. Do not use a kerosene heater in an airtight space, and do not run an unvented fuel-burning heater in a room where people are sleeping.

Use only the fuel the label names

Read the label on the heater. A federal safety alert says to use only water-clear 1-K grade kerosene and never gasoline, since even a small amount of gasoline raises the fire and explosion risk. A university extension fact sheet adds that you should use only the fuel the maker specifies. If the label and your fuel supplier do not agree, stop and ask before you fill the tank.

Refuel outdoors, off and cool

  • Never refuel inside the home. Turn the heater off, let it cool, and carry it outdoors, away from anything that can burn (federal safety alert).
  • Do not fill past the full mark. Fuel expands as it warms, and the extra space keeps it from spilling (federal safety alert and university extension fact sheet).
  • Never remove the tank or refuel while the heater is running or hot (university extension fact sheet).

Store fuel outside the living space

A university extension fact sheet says to store kerosene outdoors in a metal container with a tight lid, clearly labeled "kerosene." Another university extension fact sheet recommends a sealed blue container marked kerosene and warns against any container that once held gasoline. The federal alert adds that kerosene should never go in a red can and should be kept away from other fuel containers. Do not store fuel in the living space.

Keep 3 feet clear, and watch the placement

The federal kerosene alert says to keep the heater at least three feet from furniture and clothing. A federal fire-safety page says to keep anything that can burn at least three feet from all heat sources, and a federal consumer-safety news release says the same for drapes, furniture and bedding. A university extension fact sheet says to set the heater on a level, hard, nonflammable surface, not on rugs or carpet, and not to move it while it is operating. Keep children and pets away from the hot surfaces, and do not run the heater without its protective grille.

Tip-over shutoff, and never sleeping or away

A university extension fact sheet says a kerosene heater should have an automatic switch that shuts it off if it tips over. That is a backup, not a reason to relax. A federal fire-safety page says to turn space heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed. The federal CO fact sheet says never to operate unvented fuel-burning appliances in a room where people are sleeping. Treat that as a firm rule: when you leave the house or lie down to sleep, the heater is off, and you warm the sleeper with blankets and layers or move to a safer shelter.

That fire-safety page also reports that portable heaters were about 3 percent of home heating fires but 41 percent of the fatal ones in 2017 to 2019. A federal consumer-safety news release for 2020 to 2022 reports portable heaters in an average of 1,600 fires, 70 deaths and 150 injuries per year. Those figures cover portable heaters of all kinds, not only kerosene.

Wick care

The wick sets the flame, so treat it carefully. The federal kerosene alert says to set the wick to the maker's recommended level, not to run below the minimum wick-stop setting, to keep the wick and heater clean as the manual says, and to check that the burner is seated properly after lighting. A university extension fact sheet warns that a short wick can make CO levels rise. Keep the heater clean and properly adjusted, never run a damaged unit, and have repairs done by a qualified technician.

Check local fire rules first

A university extension fact sheet advises checking that local building and fire codes allow residential use before you buy or run one, since some places restrict indoor use. Call your local fire department or building office. We do not give legal advice here, and rules differ from place to place.

Worked example: how long does a gallon last?

Our own example, with stated assumptions. Two university extension fact sheets give kerosene a heat value of about 135,000 BTU per gallon. We use that figure as an approximation. We also assume a heater rated at 10,000 BTU per hour that burns fuel at its full rated input the whole time. Real heaters vary, so check the rating plate and manual.

  1. Fuel burned per hour = 10,000 BTU/h divided by 135,000 BTU/gal = about 0.074 gallons per hour.
  2. Run time per gallon = 1 gallon divided by 0.074 gallons per hour = about 13.5 hours at full output.
  3. Check: 135,000 divided by 10,000 = 13.5 hours.
  4. For an 8 hour daytime run at full output: 8 hours times 0.074 gallons per hour = about 0.59 gallon, so a little over half a gallon.

The takeaway is that a few gallons would cover a short outage if the heater ran steadily, but you should still refuel only outdoors, off and cool, and never run the heater unattended or while sleeping. Our own caution: a heater that sets the flame lower than full will burn less fuel but also give less heat, so this example is a planning estimate, not a measurement.

Field card: Portable Kerosene Heaters at Home

Unvented means everything stays in the room.

  • No flue: CO, nitrogen dioxide and water vapor go into the room.
  • Working CO alarm on every level and outside sleeping areas.
  • Fresh air: crack a window or door as the manual directs; keep interior doors open.
  • Fuel: only the grade the label names (1-K kerosene); never gasoline.
  • Refuel outdoors, heater off and cool; do not overfill.
  • Store fuel outdoors in a labeled kerosene container.
  • Keep 3 feet clear of anything that can burn; level, hard surface.
  • Tip-over shutoff is a backup. Off when you leave or sleep.
  • Wick at the maker's level; keep it clean.
  • Check local fire rules before using one indoors.
  • Example (ours): 10,000 BTU/h / 135,000 BTU/gal = about 0.074 gal/h, about 13.5 h per gallon.
