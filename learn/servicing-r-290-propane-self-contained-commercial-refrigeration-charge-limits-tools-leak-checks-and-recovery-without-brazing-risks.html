Servicing R-290 Propane Self-Contained Commercial Refrigeration: Charge Limits, Tools, Leak Checks and Recovery

Last updated: 2026-10-16

More self-contained coolers and ice machines now ship with R-290, which is propane. The refrigeration cycle is the same one you already know. What changes is that the charge is small, the gas burns, and some habits that are fine on R-134a or R-404A, such as a torch near the compressor, become dangerous. This lesson covers the service side. For the refrigerant classes themselves, see A2L and A3 Refrigerants, and for defrost and routine care see Reach-In and Display Case Defrost and Care.

Why propane, and why only in sealed boxes

EPA's Significant New Alternatives Policy (SNAP) program lists propane as acceptable in certain new equipment under use conditions. Propane has a low global warming potential, and in the 2024 SNAP rule EPA notes that smaller self-contained ice machines are restricted to refrigerants under a GWP of 150 by the Technology Transitions rule, and that R-290 performs adequately for them. The catch is flammability. ASHRAE puts R-290 in the A3 safety group. The Federal Register rule gives its lower flammability limit (LFL) as 21,000 ppm, or 2.1 percent by volume. Because of that, charges are kept small and the equipment is built to keep sparks away from any leak.

Charge limits: the old number and the newer ones

Under the older UL 471 standard, EPA's rule says the R-290 charge limit for stand-alone units is 150 g. Makers' service documents from the same period repeat that figure. The 2024 rule revised the conditions to point at UL 60335-2-89, 2nd edition, which allows larger charges. EPA describes roughly 500 g for open stand-alone units (UL 60335-2-89 sets 13 times the LFL, which the rule gives as 494 g) and roughly 300 g (304 g) for units with doors and drawers. One compressor maker summarizes it as a rise from 150 g to 300 to 500 g. For self-contained commercial ice machines, the rule says they fall under the scope of UL 60335-2-89, but the pages we read do not give a separate gram figure. Read the number on the data plate. The plate, not this page, is the charge limit for the box in front of you.

Units made before the transition period ended may still follow UL 471 or UL 563 if they are unchanged except for cosmetic changes, so you will see both generations in the field.

What the equipment should show

  • Red marking. EPA's rule requires red markings on service ports, pipes, hoses and other devices through which the refrigerant is serviced. One maker's service manual calls for red sleeves on process tubes and says to put back any sleeve you remove.
  • Labels. Warning labels must be permanent, with letters at least 1/4 inch (6.4 mm) high, and must be similar to or match the wording in UL 60335-2-89. A flammability symbol is required.
  • Sealed or spark-safe electrical parts. One maker's bulletin says electrical components near the refrigerant must be spark-resistant or sealed, and that only like-for-like replacement parts may be used.

Control ignition and air

Every maker document we read says the same few things. Remove ignition sources from the work area. Work where there is good ventilation and keep it going the whole time. Keep a CO2 or dry-powder extinguisher close. Do not work in a confined space. One maker adds a no-smoking placard near the work and says to keep vacuum pump outlets away from ignition sources; another routes pump discharge to open air.

Tools rated for propane

  • Leak detector. A combustible gas detector made for hydrocarbons, turned on before you start and kept near the work. A halide torch or any open-flame detector is not allowed. One maker says to set electronic detectors to 25 percent of the LFL maximum and to calibrate them in clean air.
  • Vacuum pump. A hydrocarbon guide warns that a pump switch can ignite gas, so use a pump suited to the zone. Another maker says to keep manifold hoses short.
  • Recovery machine and cylinder. A hydrocarbon guide says standard HFC recovery machines must not be used with hydrocarbons. Use equipment rated for it and a cylinder made for it.
  • Scale. Weigh the charge. A compressor maker recommends an electronic scale to control the charge with the accuracy required.

Leak checks

Sweep low, because propane is heavier than air. A guide from a compressor maker says the sensor should alarm at about 2 percent hydrocarbon by volume, well before a flammable mix forms, and that if an electronic detector cannot find a leak you can purge, fill with nitrogen and use a leak-detection spray. Maker manuals also allow bubble solution; one warns against chlorine-based detergents in leak fluid. After a repair, pressure test with nitrogen. One manual caps added nitrogen at 200 psi (13.8 bar) and tells you to evacuate to 500 microns and confirm the vacuum holds. See also Nitrogen Purge Flow While Brazing.

Venting versus recovery

Federal law first. 40 CFR 82.154(a)(1) bans knowingly venting refrigerant from an appliance during service, but exempts certain substitutes in listed end-uses. The eCFR text exempts propane (R-290) in retail food refrigerators and freezers (stand-alone units), vending machines, and self-contained commercial ice machines, among others, plus refrigerated food processing and dispensing equipment since July 15, 2024. The exemption does not apply to hydrocarbon blends. It is an exemption from the federal venting rule, not an order to vent. Check that your unit fits a listed end-use; merchandisers and bottle coolers may or may not, so confirm the category with the maker.

Makers differ on practice. One service manual says R-290 can be vented in a well-ventilated area with no ignition source and tells you to follow local rules. Another bulletin says to recover through piercing valves on the process tubes into an approved container before cutting into the system. A compressor maker says to direct the gas outdoors through a tube. EPA also states that recovered or disposed flammable refrigerant from commercial or industrial equipment is likely to be hazardous waste under RCRA. Check your local fire and waste rules. Our recovery basics are in Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step, but those steps assume non-flammable gas.

Opening the circuit: mechanical joints versus brazing

Do not heat solder joints to remove a compressor or filter-drier while the circuit holds propane. A hydrocarbon bulletin says to cut parts out with a tubing cutter, not a flame. One compressor maker says brazing is allowed only under nitrogen and that brazing near flammable refrigerant is otherwise strictly forbidden. The same maker describes opening systems with a pipe cutter and making connections with mechanical (flameless) fittings, after cleaning the tube ends and applying the fitting maker's sealant. Where you must braze, purge first. A hydrocarbon guide describes three vacuum-and-nitrogen washes and unsoldering immediately after the last one; a service manual says to purge at 3 to 5 psi for two minutes before brazing and keep purging until done. Follow the unit maker's procedure. Mechanical fittings are only acceptable where the equipment maker and the fitting maker allow them.

Charging by weight

Propane is charged by weight only. A hydrocarbon guide notes that the same cylinder volume can hold only about 40 percent as much hydrocarbon as HFC, so volume is useless as a measure. Use the charge on the serial label, evacuate, weigh in, and leak check before and after. Do not overfill.

Worked example: what a full release would reach

This is our own check. The inputs below are assumptions, not data from any product.

  1. Assumed charge: 300 g (about the closed-unit figure above). In ounces: 300 / 28.35 = 10.6 oz.
  2. Moles of propane (molar mass 44.1 g/mol, our own value): 300 / 44.1 = 6.80 mol.
  3. Gas volume at 68 F (20 C), ideal gas, 24.06 L per mol (our assumption): 6.80 x 24.06 = 164 L = 0.164 m³.
  4. Assumed small room: 12 m³ (about 424 ft³), sealed and fully mixed, no ventilation.
  5. Concentration: 0.164 / 12 = 0.0136, or 1.36 percent (13,600 ppm).
  6. Against the LFL of 2.1 percent (21,000 ppm, from the Federal Register rule): 1.36 / 2.1 = 65 percent.

A full release in a small room would reach about two-thirds of the LFL even if it mixed perfectly. Real leaks do not mix perfectly. Propane pools low, so local concentrations near the floor can be much higher. That is why ventilation and ignition control come first.

Field card: Servicing R-290 Self-Contained Equipment

Read the plate, kill ignition, weigh the charge.

  • R-290 is propane, A3, LFL 2.1 percent by volume (21,000 ppm).
  • Older limit 150 g (UL 471). Newer UL 60335-2-89 allows roughly 500 g open and 300 g with doors. The data plate rules.
  • Red marking and permanent flammable labels stay on.
  • Use a hydrocarbon-rated leak detector, pump and recovery gear. No flame detectors.
  • Ventilate, remove ignition sources, extinguisher at hand.
  • Cut, do not heat, joints. Braze only under nitrogen per the maker.
  • Charge by weight on a scale. Check venting and waste rules before releasing any gas.
