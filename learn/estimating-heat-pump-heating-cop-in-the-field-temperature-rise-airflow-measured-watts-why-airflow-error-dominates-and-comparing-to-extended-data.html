Estimating Heat Pump Heating COP in the Field: Temperature Rise, Airflow, Measured Watts, Why Airflow Error Dominates and Comparing to Extended Data

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A heat pump's heating COP is heat delivered divided by electricity used. In the field you can estimate both with a thermometer, an airflow reading and a meter. The method is simple. The catch is that the answer is only as good as the airflow number, and that is where most of the error hides.

The idea in one line

COP is a ratio of two powers, both in the same units. Heat delivered to the air is found from the air side: Btu/h = 1.08 x CFM x temperature rise. Electric input is found from the meter: watts x 3.412 converts watts to Btu/h (one watt-hour is about 3.412 Btu, per the NIST conversion tables). So:

COP = (1.08 x CFM x rise in F) / (watts x 3.412)

We do not re-derive the 1.08 here; where the 1.08 comes from covers its limits at altitude and at high plenum temperature. Heating air with a heat pump adds no moisture to the air stream, so the sensible formula is all you need.

Set up the test so the number means something

  • Strips off. Resistance heat adds Btu with a COP of 1 and wrecks the comparison. Lock out the backup heat the way the maker's procedure describes, and confirm the strips are not running before you read the rise.
  • Not in defrost, and not just after it. During defrost the unit pulls heat out of the house or runs strips, so the rise means nothing. Wait until the unit has run steadily, usually 10 to 15 minutes past the end of a defrost, before logging anything. See the lukewarm air lesson for what normal winter supply air looks like.
  • Steady outdoor and indoor conditions. Write down the outdoor dry-bulb temperature at the unit and the indoor return temperature. You need them to compare with the maker's data.
  • All the fan and compressor power. The blower heat is part of the air temperature rise, so the blower watts belong in the input.

Measuring the temperature rise correctly

The rise is the supply temperature minus the return temperature. A 1 F error on a 28 F rise is a 3.6 percent error, so the probe placement matters. Use the same meter, or two probes checked against each other in the same air first, so a bias cancels out.

  • Supply: after the coil. Put the probe in the supply plenum or the first part of the trunk, past the coil and past the strip section, where the air has mixed. Read several points and average them. Air right off one side of the coil can differ by several degrees from the other side. Keep the probe out of direct line of sight of strip elements, which radiate onto the tip.
  • Return: near the unit. Measure close to the air handler, ahead of the filter or just after it. A reading at the grille can be off if return ducts pass through hot or cold spaces. If there is outdoor air, a bypass, or several returns that join, measure the mixed air entering the coil, not any one branch.
  • Leakage between the probes and the unit. Return leaks pull attic or basement air into the stream after your return reading, and the air then reaching the coil is not the air you measured. Fix or account for leaks first.

The general method for splits and rises is in the temperature split lesson.

Measuring the electric input

Best is a true power meter that reads watts directly on the heat pump circuits. Next best is a clamp meter for amps plus a voltage reading, with a power factor correction. Volts x amps gives volt-amperes, not watts. Motors and compressors draw current that is partly out of step with the voltage, so volt-amperes run higher than the real watts, by 10 to 20 percent or more on a single motor. The power factor lesson explains why. If you use volts x amps and call it watts, your COP reads low, and you will wrongly suspect a fault.

Read the outdoor unit (compressor and outdoor fan) and the air handler (indoor blower, control board) and add them. For clamp technique and motor current, see the clamp meter lesson. Follow your site procedures for working near live parts.

Airflow: why it dominates the error

Temperature and watts can be read to within a few percent. Airflow usually cannot. Flow hoods on a ducted system, a traverse in an irregular duct, and blower tables read from a guessed static pressure can each be off by 10 percent or more; the airflow lesson compares the methods. And airflow enters the COP formula straight, one for one: a 10 percent error in CFM is a 10 percent error in COP.

NIST publishes the rule for combining errors in a product or quotient: you combine the relative uncertainties of each input, squared and added, then take the square root. Several small errors add little. One large one controls the result. That large one is airflow.

A field study of ductless heat pumps for DOE (NREL, report SR-5500-63913) found measured airflow was only 50 to 80 percent of the listed flow, a likely reason for the wide spread in measured performance. It estimated airflow from fan current, calibrated at each site with a fan-assisted flow hood. The lesson for us: never use the nameplate or catalog CFM in this formula. Measure it.

Worked example (our own, with assumed readings)

Assumptions: outdoor 35 F, 70 F return, 98 F supply (so 28 F rise), 900 CFM measured, steady run, strips off, no defrost, and 2.6 kW measured input (a true power meter, outdoor unit plus air handler).

  1. Heat delivered = 1.08 x 900 x 28 = 27,216 Btu/h.
  2. Input in Btu/h = 2,600 W x 3.412 = 8,871 Btu/h.
  3. COP = 27,216 / 8,871 = 3.07.

What airflow error does. If the true flow was 10 percent higher, 990 CFM, the heat is 1.08 x 990 x 28 = 29,938 Btu/h and the COP is 3.37. If it was 10 percent lower, 810 CFM, the heat is 24,494 Btu/h and the COP is 2.76. The same unit, the same day, and the answer swings from 2.76 to 3.37.

What a rise error does. A rise that is 1 F off (27 or 29 instead of 28) gives COP 2.96 or 3.18, about 0.11 either way.

Volts x amps trap. Suppose a clamp and voltmeter gave 12.5 A at 240 V, which is 3,000 VA, while the meter shows 2,600 W (power factor about 0.87, assumed). Using 3,000 as watts gives COP = 27,216 / (3,000 x 3.412) = 2.66, which is 13 percent low.

Combined estimate. With assumed errors of 10 percent for airflow, 3.6 percent for the rise and 2 percent for watts, the root of the sum of squares is the square root of (100 + 12.8 + 4) = about 10.8 percent. So the field COP is about 3.07 plus or minus 0.33, and nearly all of that comes from airflow.

Comparing to the maker's data

Look up the maker's extended performance table at the same outdoor temperature, the same indoor return temperature and the same blower setting. Interpolate between 47 F and 17 F rows if you must, but only between rows that are actually listed. The extended data lesson shows how to read them.

Suppose, as an assumption for illustration, the table shows COP 3.2 at 35 F. Then 3.07 is about 96 percent of table, which is well inside the error band. It is a pass, not a fault. A result under about 80 percent, with careful measurements, is worth chasing: charge, airflow, a dirty coil, or a failing compressor. Catalog COP typically excludes some real-world losses, such as defrost and duct effects, so a field value a little under the table is expected. Charge checks in heating are covered in the heating charge lesson.

Common mistakes

  • Using catalog airflow instead of measured airflow.
  • Reading in defrost, or just after one.
  • One supply reading at one side of the coil, or too close to the strips.
  • Calling volts x amps watts.
  • Forgetting the blower power or the outdoor fan.
  • Comparing to the 47 F rating when it was 35 F outdoors.

Field card: Field heating COP estimate

Rise, CFM, watts. Airflow is the weak link.

  • COP = (1.08 x CFM x rise) / (watts x 3.412).
  • Strips off, steady run, 10 to 15 minutes past defrost.
  • Supply after the coil, averaged; return or mixed air near the unit.
  • Use real watts. Volts x amps overstates watts on motors.
  • Add outdoor unit and indoor blower power.
  • 10 percent airflow error is 10 percent COP error. Our example: 3.07, or 2.76 to 3.37.
  • Compare to the maker's table at the same outdoor and return temperature.
