Mudroom and Entryway in Winter: Door Drafts, Wet Boots and Your Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The back door is the busiest hole in a winter house. Every trip in or out swaps warm indoor air for cold outdoor air, boots carry in snow that has to melt and dry, and the pile of shoes and coats often lands right on top of a heat outlet. This lesson walks through what that costs your heating system, with a small worked example, and what to change.

What happens each time the door opens

When a door opens in winter, cold air spills in low and warm air leaks out high, and a bit of the room's air is exchanged. The heating system then has to warm the new air back up to the thermostat setting. Sizing handbooks treat this as an infiltration load: the volume of air exchanged, times the heat capacity of air (0.018 Btu per cubic foot per degree F), times the temperature difference. A solar heating handbook from a federal lab library gives that 0.018 figure and the same multiply-it-out method. We use it below.

The door is not the only path. Gaps around the frame and under the threshold leak all day, even when no one is using the door. Those leaks are covered in Drafty Doors and Windows, so here we focus on the open-and-close part and the wet-boot part.

Our own worked example

This is our own example, not a measurement. The assumptions are ours, so change them to match your house.

  • The door opens 20 times a day (assumption).
  • Each opening swaps about 50 cubic feet of room air for outdoor air (assumption; a real swap depends on how long the door stays open, wind and the stack effect).
  • Outdoor air is 20 F and indoor air is 68 F, so the difference is 68 - 20 = 48 F.
  • Air heat capacity is 0.018 Btu per cubic foot per degree F (from the handbook above).
  • Snow melt carried in on boots and coats is about 0.5 pound of water per day (assumption), and it evaporates indoors at about 1,000 Btu per pound (rounded; the table in a university psychrometrics chapter lists about 970 Btu per pound at boiling, and the value is a bit higher at room temperature, so 1,000 is a fair round number).

Step 1, heat per opening: 50 cubic feet x 0.018 Btu per cubic foot per F x 48 F = 43.2 Btu.

Step 2, daily air swap: 43.2 Btu x 20 openings = 864 Btu per day.

Step 3, snow melt: 0.5 pound x 1,000 Btu per pound = 500 Btu per day. This heat comes out of the room air as the water evaporates, so the heating system has to put it back.

Step 4, total: 864 + 500 = 1,364 Btu per day. Over a 120-day heating season that is about 163,700 Btu.

That is a modest number, which suggests that brief door openings are not the biggest loss in this example. A door propped open, or a gap that leaks around the clock, would add far more, because the same multiplication keeps running. Our example also shows the moisture side: 0.5 pound of water a day is about a cup. Over a 30-day month that is about 15 pounds, close to 2 gallons (our arithmetic, at about 8.3 pounds per gallon) that has to go somewhere. If it is not dried quickly, it ends up in carpet, boot trays, closet walls and the air.

Wet boots, boot trays and moisture

Snow on boots is water waiting to evaporate. A boot tray with a lip catches the melt where it lands, so it does not soak into carpet or the wall base. Empty and wipe the tray often. A federal moisture guide for homes says mold needs moisture to grow, advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours, and suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. Wet boots left in a closed closet are exactly the kind of damp spot that guide warns about. See Condensation on Windows in Winter for how to check the humidity side, and Drying Clothes Indoors on Racks in Winter for a similar water load.

Boot dryers and plug-in safety

A plug-in boot dryer is a small electric heater, so the same habits apply. A federal product-safety agency's portable heater guidance says the plug should fit tightly in the wall outlet, that heaters should never be powered through an extension cord or power strip, that you should never leave a heater running while unattended or while sleeping, and that you should inspect the cord and plug for damage before use. That guidance is written for portable heaters, so we are applying it by analogy to dryers, and the maker's instructions come first. Set the dryer on a hard floor or shelf, not on a rug or a pile of coats, and keep gloves and coats off it. Unplug it when the boots are dry.

Blocked registers and baseboard

Shoe piles and long coats on hooks are the usual culprits. A floor register or a baseboard unit that is covered cannot push heat into the room, and the cold entry stays cold. Leave clear space around floor registers, return grilles, radiators and baseboard fins. Put the shoe rack on a different wall from the heat source. Furniture, Rugs and Curtains Blocking Vents and Return Grilles covers clearances and simple airflow checks. Do not add a plug-in heater to warm the area if the real problem is a blocked register.

Air locks, sweeps and weatherstripping

A small vestibule, or even a second interior door, makes an air lock. If the outer door is shut before the inner one opens, the exchange per trip is much smaller. Large buildings use the same idea; Building Entrances and Infiltration goes into it. For a house, an air lock can be as simple as closing the mudroom door behind you. If the mudroom is a cold space, the wall between it and the house is now the thermal boundary, so it needs sealing and a tight door too.

Weatherstripping and a door sweep close the all-day leaks that cost more than the door openings in our example. A quick check: close the door on a strip of paper. If it pulls out easily, the seal is loose. On a cold day, feel for moving air along the edges and under the door. If the sweep is worn or the gap is uneven, replace or adjust it.

Mold, smell and damp closets

A closet full of wet coats and boots, with no air movement, can hold moisture for days. Mold needs moisture to grow, so the fix is to dry things out: hang coats where air can reach them, set boots on a tray with space around them, and leave closet doors open or louvered when they are damp. A musty smell is a sign the area is staying wet. If a closet wall on the outside of the house also runs cold, moisture can condense there. That problem is explained in Cold Closets and Musty Spots Behind Furniture. If you find mold growth on a wall or ceiling, or the smell persists after drying, get qualified help rather than just heating the room.

Slip safety

Melted snow makes a wet, slick floor right where people step in and carry things. A federal workplace safety fact sheet on slips, trips and falls advises cleaning up spills right away, using non-slip matting and keeping mats clean and in place, and wearing sturdy shoes with slip-resistant soles. The same habits work at home: put a mat or a boot tray inside the door, wipe up puddles, keep the walkway clear of bags and shoes, and keep cords for any plug-in dryer out of the walking path. Tile and smooth vinyl are especially slick when wet.

Field card: Mudroom and Entryway in Winter

Shut one door before opening the next, and keep the heat outlets clear.

  • Our own example: 20 openings a day x 50 cubic feet x 0.018 x 48 F = 864 Btu per day.
  • Snow melt: 0.5 lb a day x 1,000 Btu per lb = 500 Btu per day, plus the moisture.
  • All-day leaks around the frame and sweep usually cost more than the openings.
  • Boot dryer: plug into a wall outlet, no extension cord or power strip, unplug when dry, follow the maker's instructions.
  • Keep floor registers, return grilles and baseboard clear of shoes and coats.
  • Dry wet items within 24 to 48 hours. Indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.
  • Use a mat or tray, wipe puddles, wear slip-resistant footwear.
