Fins and Extended Surfaces on HVAC Coils: Fin Efficiency, Material, Thickness and Fins Per Inch

Last updated: 2026-11-02

Open any coil and most of what you see is thin metal fins, not tube. Those fins are there for one reason: air is a poor carrier of heat, so the coil needs a lot of surface to hand heat to it. This lesson explains why fins pay off, why the far end of a fin does less work than the root, and what changes when fins are thin, corroded, packed tight, matted with dirt or loose on the tube.

Check the equipment and the job

Check the equipment and the job. Fin spacing, materials and cleaning methods differ by coil and maker. This page is general education about heat transfer. Follow the equipment instructions, shut off and lock out power before you open a unit, and wear gloves, because fin edges cut. The three basic ways heat moves are covered in Conduction, Convection and Radiation; this lesson builds on that.

Why fins: the air-side film is the bottleneck

Heat leaving a hot tube or entering a cold one has to pass through several resistances in series: the fluid inside the tube, any scale or fouling, the tube wall, and the film of air at the outside surface. A Purdue University heat exchanger text on heating coils states that air-side heat transfer coefficients are low, so the air side dominates the thermal resistance, and fins are added to cut that resistance by adding area. The convection rule is simple: heat moved equals the film coefficient, times the area, times the temperature difference. When the coefficient is small, the easiest lever left is area. An FSU engineering lecture lists the same three options (raise the temperature difference, raise the coefficient with a fan, or raise the area) and says fins serve the third.

The payoff is large. In the Purdue coil example, with about 9 fins per inch on a tube, fins supply about 84 percent of the total outside surface, with only about 16 percent from the bare tube. That is why a coil is mostly fin. Baseboard and fan convectors use the same trick on a hot-water tube; see Radiator Covers, Enclosures and Paint for what blocks that airflow.

Fin efficiency: not all the area works equally

Heat enters (or leaves) a fin at its root, where it touches the tube, and then spreads out through the metal while the air takes it off the faces. Metal is not a perfect conductor, so some temperature drop is needed to push heat out along the fin. On a heating coil the tip ends up cooler than the root. On a cooling coil the tip ends up warmer than the root. Either way, the tip metal sits closer to the air temperature, so each square inch out there moves less heat than a square inch at the root.

Engineers capture this with fin efficiency. A CSUN heat transfer note defines it as the actual heat moved by the fin divided by the most it could move if the whole fin were at the root temperature. For a straight fin with a tip that moves no heat, the Purdue ME 418 lecture gives the efficiency as tanh(mL) divided by (mL), where the fin parameter is m = square root of (2h / (k t)). Here h is the air-side film coefficient, k the metal's thermal conductivity, t the fin thickness and L the length from root to tip. The FSU note gives the matching temperature profile along the fin, and the CSUN note says a tip that does lose heat can be handled with a slightly longer effective length.

Read the formula as a story. A larger mL means a longer, thinner, less conductive fin working under a harder air film, and the efficiency falls. The Purdue text says fins are generally designed for efficiencies of about 0.6 to 0.9, and that shorter fins are more efficient but add less area, so there is a cost and performance trade. The same FSU lecture shows the conductivity effect with a handle example: a metal rod of the same size with a higher conductivity keeps its far end much hotter.

Worked example (our own example): same fin, three metals

This is our own teaching example. Every input is an assumption unless noted, and none of it describes a specific product. The fin is a straight rectangular fin with an insulated tip, a simplification the CSUN and Purdue notes both use.

Inputs.

  • Fin thickness t = 0.006 in (assumed). Convert: 0.006 x 0.0254 = 1.524 x 10-4 m.
  • Fin length root to tip L = 0.5 in (assumed). Convert: 0.5 x 0.0254 = 0.0127 m.
  • Air-side film coefficient: we borrow the 14.4 Btu/hr-ft2-F value from the Purdue coil example. Convert with the standard factor 1 Btu/hr-ft2-F = 5.678 W/m2-K: 14.4 x 5.678 = 81.8 W/m2-K.
  • Conductivity k, from a Georgia State University table, treated as assumed values: aluminum 205, copper 385, steel 50.2 W/m-K. Real fin alloys differ somewhat; the ranking is what matters here.

Step 1, the fin parameter squared: 2h / (k t). The numerator is 2 x 81.8 = 163.5 for all three.

  • Aluminum: k t = 205 x 1.524 x 10-4 = 0.03124, so 163.5 / 0.03124 = 5,234 per m2. Then m = 72.3 per m.
  • Copper: k t = 385 x 1.524 x 10-4 = 0.05867, so 163.5 / 0.05867 = 2,787. Then m = 52.8 per m.
  • Steel: k t = 50.2 x 1.524 x 10-4 = 0.007651, so 163.5 / 0.007651 = 21,376. Then m = 146.2 per m.

Step 2, mL with L = 0.0127 m: aluminum 72.3 x 0.0127 = 0.919; copper 52.8 x 0.0127 = 0.670; steel 146.2 x 0.0127 = 1.857.

Step 3, efficiency = tanh(mL) / (mL):

  • Aluminum: tanh(0.919) = 0.725, so 0.725 / 0.919 = 0.79.
  • Copper: tanh(0.670) = 0.585, so 0.585 / 0.670 = 0.87.
  • Steel: tanh(1.857) = 0.952, so 0.952 / 1.857 = 0.51.

Step 4, what it means. If all three fins have the same surface area and the same air, heat moved is proportional to efficiency. Copper moves 0.873 / 0.789 = 1.11 times the aluminum fin, about 11 percent more. The steel fin moves 0.513 / 0.789 = 0.65 times the aluminum, about 35 percent less, even though it is the same size. The temperature at the tip, as a share of the root-to-air gap, is 1 / cosh(mL): aluminum 0.69, copper 0.81, steel 0.30. That is the aluminum curve in the diagram.

What thickness and height do. Making the steel fin 0.012 in thick doubles k t, which gives m = 103.4, mL = 1.313 and efficiency 0.66, up from 0.51. Going the other way, making the aluminum fin 1.0 in long drops it to mL = 1.84 and efficiency 0.52. Taller fins add area but waste more of it. Our numbers use one assumed film coefficient; a faster airflow raises h and lowers efficiency a bit, because the metal has to feed a hungrier surface.

Real coils are more complicated: plate fins between many tubes, collars, wavy or louvered surfaces that raise h, and condensation on a cooling coil. This calculation shows the trend, not a design.

Why not just add more fins per inch?

More fins per inch means more area in the same coil face, but three things push back. First, air has to squeeze through a narrower gap, so the pressure drop rises and the fan moves less air or works harder. Second, in our reasoning, the air films on neighboring fins start to crowd each other as the gap narrows. Third, tight fins catch lint and dust faster. A national-laboratory report on coil fouling tested about 6, 12 and 18 fins per inch. It found that the sparse coil took about 1.8 times as long as its base case to foul and the dense coil only about 0.7 times as long. The Purdue heating-coil example gives a sense of scale for a clean coil: 4 rows at about 1,000 feet per minute face velocity had a core pressure drop around 0.055 inch of water, before entrance and exit losses. For how fin spacing and rows tie into leaving-air temperature and dehumidifying, see Cooling Coil Bypass Factor, Contact Factor and Apparatus Dew Point.

How bent, corroded, dirty or loose fins lose capacity

  • Dirt-matted fins. The same national-laboratory report says dust on evaporator coils raises pressure drop and cuts airflow. Typical coils in its model took about 7.5 years to double their pressure drop, airflow fell roughly 5 to 7 percent in the base case, and capacity and efficiency fell roughly 2 to 4 percent, with 10 to 20 percent or more for marginal systems. Its biggest lever was filter quality. Cleaning methods are in Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil.
  • Bent or flattened fins. This is our reasoning, from the formulas above: a crushed fin closes the air gap, so air bypasses that part of the coil and the film coefficient drops. Repair is covered in Bent and Flattened Coil Fins.
  • Corroded fins. Also our reasoning: corrosion eats thin metal, which lowers the thickness t, and in the formula a thinner fin has a larger m and lower efficiency. Corrosion deposits also add resistance. See Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air.
  • Poor fin-to-tube bond. The textbook relations we read assume the fin root is at the tube surface temperature, which implies a good bond. Our reasoning: a loose collar or a gap adds a resistance in series right at the root, so the whole fin works with a smaller temperature difference, however good the metal is.

Narrow fin-tube coils are not the only design. Microchannel Condenser Coils use a different geometry with its own cleaning cautions.

Field card: Fins and Extended Surfaces

Fins add area because the air film is the weak link; the tip works less than the root.

  • Air-side film coefficient is low, so area on the air side buys the most.
  • Fin efficiency (straight fin, insulated tip) = tanh(mL) / (mL), m = square root of 2h / (k t).
  • Longer, thinner, lower-conductivity fins have lower efficiency. Designs often fall around 0.6 to 0.9.
  • Our example (0.006 in, 0.5 in long): aluminum 0.79, copper 0.87, steel 0.51.
  • More fins per inch: more area, more pressure drop, faster fouling.
  • Dirt, bent fins, corrosion and a loose tube bond each cut capacity in a different way.
