Sump and Sewage Ejector Pits in Mechanical Rooms: Duplex Pumps, High-Water Alarms and Hot Discharge Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The pit in the corner of the boiler room is easy to ignore until it overflows. For a facility tech it is part of the plant: boiler drains, coil condensate and relief discharge may all end up there, and when its pumps fail the water lands on your equipment. This lesson covers what the pit does, what you should check on it, and where your job stops and the plumbing trade begins.

Scope of this page

This is a tech's-eye view, not an installation guide. Pit sizing, venting and pump selection belong to the plumbing designer and the local plumbing authority. We name codes in plain text only, and local rules decide what applies. For the floor drain side of the same room, see Mechanical Room Floor Drains and Trap Primers.

What drains to the pit

  • Condensate. Cooling coil drain pans and condensing boiler or furnace condensate often run, through an air gap or indirect connection, to a floor drain or pit. See Condensate Pump Not Working for the smaller local pumps.
  • Boiler blowdown and relief discharge. These are hot. A relief valve that drips or lets go sends boiler-temperature water to the nearest drain; see Boiler Pressure Relief Valve Dripping or Discharging.
  • Equipment drains and washdown water from the room floor.

Know which pit you have. A clear-water sump takes drainage only. A sewage ejector pit takes waste from fixtures. One university facility standard we read says submersible sump pumps are for clear liquids only and are not to be used as solid sewage ejectors. Never assume a pump is rated for solids.

Hot discharge limits and blowdown tempering

Hot water cannot simply go to the sewer. The federal pretreatment rule at 40 CFR 403.5(b)(5) bars heat in amounts that would raise the temperature at the sewage treatment plant above 40 °C (104 °F), and lets the approval authority set other limits if the plant asks. That is a limit at the plant, not at your drain, so cities set their own pipe-side limits in local sewer-use rules. We could not confirm a single national drain-side number, so find your local figure from the sewer authority and the plumbing code, and do not quote one from memory.

The EPA WaterSense guide for boilers says that where boiler condensate goes to the sanitary sewer, most codes require it to be cooled first, usually by tempering with cool water, and suggests an expansion tank as an alternative to adding water. In practice, blowdown is routed through a cooling or tempering arrangement installed by the designer, and the tech's job is to notice when it stops working: a blowdown line that is hot to the touch downstream of the tempering point, a closed make-up valve, or a pit that is warm and steaming. Report a tempering failure as a maintenance finding as well as a code one.

Pit hardware, piece by piece

Simplex and duplex

A simplex pit has one pump; a duplex pit has two. One building-division handout, quoting a California plumbing code section, says commercial ejector installations need dual pumps that work independently if one overloads or fails, with only one running at a time. An alternator swaps the lead pump each cycle so wear is shared, and brings the second pump on if the first cannot keep up. A university construction standard describes the same alternator idea. Our own point: if one pump of a duplex pair is dead, the pit is effectively simplex, and nobody will know unless someone tests it.

Floats and the high-water alarm

Floats turn pumps on and off at set levels. A county septic guide warns that cords, ropes or screens can tangle floats, and debris can hold a float or make a pump shut off, so floats should move freely and be cleaned. The same guide says the high-water alarm should be on a circuit separate from the pump, so a tripped breaker does not silence the warning. The university standard calls for a cover-mounted alarm with a bell and contacts for a remote alarm and building automation. If your pit alarm is not wired to anything anyone watches, treat that as a finding.

Check valves and isolation

The city handout cited above says the discharge line needs an accessible swing or backwater check valve and a full-way gate or ball valve on the discharge side of it. The check keeps the discharge pipe from draining back into the pit each cycle. A stuck or leaking check shows as short-cycling: the pump stops, water falls back, and it restarts. The isolation valve lets you service the check without draining the line.

Covers and gas

The same handout says commercial sumps or receiving tanks must be water and gas tight with gasketed covers, and vented separately through the roof. For you, that means a missing cover bolt, a cracked gasket or a dry vent seal lets sewer gas into the room. Put the cover back right every time.

Worked example: pump-down and run time

Every input here is an assumption of ours, chosen for easy arithmetic. Real pits and pumps differ.

Assumptions (ours): round pit, 30 in (2.5 ft) inside diameter; 18 in (1.5 ft) between the pump-off and pump-on floats; steady inflow 5 gal/min; pump delivers 40 gal/min at the actual system head. One cubic foot is 7.48 gal.

  1. Pit area = 0.785 × 2.5 × 2.5 = 4.91 sq ft.
  2. Volume per pumping cycle = 4.91 × 1.5 = 7.36 cu ft, and 7.36 × 7.48 = 55 gal.
  3. Pump-down while inflow continues: net rate = 40 − 5 = 35 gal/min, so 55 ÷ 35 = 1.6 min, about 94 seconds.
  4. Fill time with the pump off: 55 ÷ 5 = 11 min.
  5. One full cycle = 11 + 1.6 = 12.6 min, about 4.8 starts per hour. The pump runs 1.6 ÷ 12.6 = 12.5 percent of the time.
  6. With an alternator, each pump gets about half the starts, roughly 2.4 per hour.
  7. Alarm margin: with the lead pump failed and the lag pump dead too, assume 12 in (1 ft) between the alarm float and the point of overflow. That is 4.91 × 7.48 = 36.7 gal, so at 5 gal/min you have about 7 minutes. A blowdown event or a heavy drain-down will shorten that sharply.

What to take from it: a 12.5 percent duty is what this assumed pit should show. If you see the pump running far more than that with no new inflow, suspect a leaking check valve, a tangled float or a drain that is running when it should not. If starts climb well above the expected rate, find out why before the pump burns out.

Monthly test routine (our suggested practice)

  1. Look before you open: wet floor, warm pit, alarm lamp, any odor in the room.
  2. Check the alarm panel and its separate power; use the test button if one exists.
  3. With the pit lid secured and no one inside, raise each float by hand through its reach, or run the control test, and confirm each pump starts and stops. Do this for both pumps.
  4. Confirm the alternator swaps the lead pump, and note run amps against the last reading.
  5. Listen for short-cycling, which points to a bad check valve.
  6. Confirm the high-water alarm reaches wherever it is supposed to go.
  7. Log the date, readings and anything you could not reach.

Lock out power before you handle floats or pumps. The county septic guide says to turn off the breaker and unplug cords before handling them, and warns that chamber gases are poisonous and a lack of air can be fatal, so the chamber is not to be entered.

Confined space caution

A pit can meet OSHA's definition of a confined space: a person can enter it to work, it has limited entry or exit, and it is not made for continuous occupancy. It becomes permit-required if it has, or could have, a hazardous atmosphere, material that could engulf an entrant, a shape that can trap, or another serious hazard. A hazardous atmosphere includes oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent, flammable gas above 10 percent of its lower flammable limit, or a substance above its exposure limit. OSHA's sewer-entry appendix warns that a sewer atmosphere can become lethal suddenly and without warning, and lists hydrogen sulfide at 10 ppm as an alarm point. Do not climb into a pit to free a float or recover a dropped tool. If a pit may need entry, follow your site's permit program; start with Confined Spaces in HVAC Work and, for the gas side, Sewage Lift Station and Wet Well Ventilation.

Field card: Mechanical Room Pits

Hot water in, tempered water out, two pumps that both work, an alarm someone hears.

  • Know whether the pit is clear-water or sewage; solids need a pump rated for them.
  • Federal pretreatment rule: no heat that pushes the treatment plant above 40 °C (104 °F). Local drain limits vary, so check them.
  • Test both pumps and the alternator, not just one.
  • Alarm on its own circuit and wired to someone who will respond.
  • Short-cycling points to a leaking check valve or tangled float.
  • Gasketed cover back on, vent intact.
  • Never enter a pit without a permit program and atmosphere testing.
