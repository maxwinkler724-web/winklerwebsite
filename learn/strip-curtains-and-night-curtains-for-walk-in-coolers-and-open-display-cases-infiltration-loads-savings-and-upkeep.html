Strip Curtains and Night Curtains for Walk-In Coolers and Open Display Cases

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every time a walk-in door swings open, warm wet room air slides in along the floor level of the doorway's top half while cold air spills out of the bottom. That exchange is a refrigeration load you never see on a nameplate. This lesson covers the cheap barriers that cut it: door closers, strip curtains, and night curtains on open cases.

Check the rule and the equipment

Check the equipment and local rules. This page is general education built from public documents read in 2026. Confirm the current federal text, your local code and the equipment maker's instructions before you rely on any number here.

What happens when the door opens

The cold box is dense, heavy air. The room is warmer, lighter and carries more water vapor. With the door open, the two swap places through the doorway: warm air enters in the upper part and cold air leaves in the lower part, as the drawing shows. The incoming air has to be cooled to box temperature, which is sensible load, and its moisture has to be pulled out on the evaporator coil, which is latent load. That moisture lands on the coil as frost. More frost means the coil needs more defrost time and the fans may run into a blocked fin pack. The defrost heat itself then becomes an added load. For how defrost cycles and fan delay work, see Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Defrost, Evaporator Fan Delay and Icing. A freezer shows the effect most: air at freezer temperature holds almost no water, so nearly all the moisture that gets in ends up as ice on the coil, door frame and floor. Large buildings have the same issue at dock doors; see Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings.

What the federal rule asks for

The U.S. Department of Energy rule at 10 CFR 431.306 sets prescriptive requirements for walk-in coolers and freezers made from January 1, 2009. Two matter here. Paragraph (a)(1) calls for automatic door closers that firmly close all walk-in doors that have been closed to within 1 inch of full closure; it does not apply to doors wider than 3 feet 9 inches or taller than 7 feet. Paragraph (a)(2) calls for strip doors, spring-hinged doors or another method of minimizing infiltration when doors are open. A closer that has been disabled, or a hook holding the door open, defeats the design. For door heaters, ports and the rest of the efficiency rules, read Walk-In Coolers and Freezers: Evaporator, Defrost, Door Heaters, Pressure Relief Ports and DOE Efficiency Rules.

Strip curtains: what makes them work

  • Overlap. Strips hang in side-by-side panels. If neighbors overlap, they close the gap between them; if they do not, air streams through the slits. Our own rule of thumb: you should not see light through the curtain.
  • Length. Strips should reach near the floor. Strips that are cut back or torn off leave a gap, and cold air spills out through the gap at floor level.
  • Condition. Strips that are stiff, cracked or yellowed do not spring back after someone walks through. In a freezer, stiff strips also freeze to each other.
  • Cleanliness. The strips are touched by hands, carts and boxes and sit in a warm, wet doorway. The FDA Food Code, in the 2022 edition, includes sections on nonfood-contact surfaces (4-602.13) and on cleaning physical facilities (6-501.12), and a state inspection guide based on it says inspectors look at whether the cleaning frequency is enough to prevent soil buildup. Strips with grime or mold are a sanitation problem, not just an energy one. Wash them on the customer's schedule and replace damaged ones.

Night curtains on open cases

Open multideck cases have no door. At night the store is closed, nobody is shopping, and the case still pulls warm room air into its cold air curtain. A roll-down night curtain closes that opening after hours. A University of Illinois program, SEDAC, states that display case shields used overnight can reduce the refrigeration load by 8.5 percent and compressor power by 9 percent during nighttime hours. A U.S. Department of Energy sector overview lists covering open cases when the store is closed as a recommended measure, and says doors on medium-temperature cases are acceptable to customers and can reduce energy use; it gives no percentage. An EPA working paper reports that grocers said night blinds can be misused, which is why a tech should check that covers are actually pulled down, latched and not left rolled up. See Reach-In and Display Case Defrost and Care for case basics.

An air curtain is the other barrier. It is a fan-driven jet of air across an opening, and it works on open cases, on dock doors and on some walk-in doors. We cover sizing in Air Curtains Over Commercial Doors.

Worked example: one open walk-in door

The formula is Btu/h = 4.5 × cfm × Δh, where 4.5 comes from 60 minutes per hour times an air density of about 0.075 lb/ft³ (our own explanation), and Δh is the enthalpy difference in Btu per pound of dry air.

Assumptions (our own, labeled): a 3 ft by 7 ft doorway; 1,000 cfm of warm air entering while the door is fully open; door open 10 minutes of every hour; room air 75°F and 50% relative humidity; cooler air 35°F and 85% relative humidity; sea-level pressure of 1013 mb.

Step 1, vapor pressure. A published saturation vapor pressure table from the University of Colorado Boulder lists 29.6 mb at 75°F and 6.9 mb at 35°F. Partial pressures: room 0.50 × 29.6 = 14.8 mb; cooler 0.85 × 6.9 = 5.9 mb.

Step 2, humidity ratio with W = 0.622 × pw / (p − pw): room W = 0.622 × 14.8 / (1013 − 14.8) = 0.00922 lb/lb; cooler W = 0.622 × 5.9 / (1013 − 5.9) = 0.00362 lb/lb.

Step 3, enthalpy with the common approximation h = 0.240t + W(1061 + 0.444t) (our own calculation): room h = 0.240 × 75 + 0.00922 × (1061 + 33.3) = 28.1 Btu/lb; cooler h = 0.240 × 35 + 0.00362 × (1061 + 15.5) = 12.3 Btu/lb. Δh = 15.8 Btu/lb.

Step 4, load while open. 4.5 × 1,000 × 15.8 = 71,100 Btu/h, about 5.9 tons. Averaged over the hour at 10 of 60 minutes open: 71,100 × 10/60 = 11,850 Btu/h.

Step 5, curtain. A California Energy Commission analysis of walk-in standards assumed that strip curtains give a 75 percent reduction in infiltration. Remaining load: 11,850 × 0.25 = about 2,960 Btu/h. Savings: about 8,890 Btu/h, roughly 0.74 ton of refrigeration capacity you no longer spend on warm air. That 75 percent is a planning assumption, not a measurement of your doorway, and a cut or tied-back curtain will do far worse.

Step 6, moisture. Using 4.5 × cfm × ΔW: 4.5 × 1,000 × (0.00922 − 0.00362) = 25.2 lb/h while open, about 4.2 lb/h averaged, or roughly 100 lb (about 12 gallons) per day at that door pattern if it all freezes out (our own arithmetic, with the 24-hour pattern assumed). That frost is why the coil needs defrost.

Tech checks at the door

  1. Closers: does the door pull itself shut from about an inch open, and is the closer arm intact?
  2. Door tied, wedged or hooked open? Ask why; usually carts and deliveries. Suggest a curtain or a hold-open that still leaves the strips in place.
  3. Strips: missing, cut back, torn, stiff or frozen together? Check overlap and length to the floor.
  4. Gasket and sweep: look for light, frost lines at the latch edge, or a sagging door that no longer seals. See the basics lesson, Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics.
  5. Frost pattern: heavy frost near the door side of the coil points to infiltration, not a refrigerant problem.
  6. Open display cases: night covers present, working, and in use after hours.
  7. Product temperature: if the box is struggling, check the holding temperatures in Commercial Refrigeration Cold Holding and Food Safety Checks.

Field card: Strip Curtains and Night Curtains

Keep the barrier intact and the door shut.

  • Open door: warm humid air in at the top, cold air out at the bottom.
  • Moisture becomes coil frost and more defrost.
  • Federal rule: automatic closers on doors up to 3 ft 9 in wide and 7 ft tall; strip doors or equal method.
  • Strips must overlap, reach the floor and stay clean and flexible.
  • Btu/h = 4.5 x cfm x delta h; the 75 percent curtain figure is an assumption.
  • Night covers: check they are used after hours.
