Intermittent Pilot Ignition: Spark, Pilot and Flame Proving

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Older furnaces, boilers and unit heaters often use an intermittent pilot (IP) system: the pilot is lit by a spark only when heat is called for, and the main burner stays shut until the control proves the pilot flame. This lesson walks the sequence, then the checks that find most "will not light" and "lights, then quits" calls.

Check the label first

Check the label first. This page is general education built from Honeywell instruction sheets read in 2026. Your appliance and its control have their own wiring diagram, lighting label and numbers. Where they differ from this page, the appliance documents win. Do not bypass a safety control to make a unit run.

The sequence, step by step

Honeywell's S8600, S8610 and S8670 instruction sheet describes it this way. On a call for heat, the spark and the pilot gas turn on together for a window called the trial for ignition. When the pilot flame is proved, the spark stops and the main valve opens. The S8670 differs only at the start: it runs a 30-second prepurge before the trial. If the flame is lost while running, the control restarts the ignition trial. Honeywell's universal S8610U sheet adds detail: the pilot must reach a higher flame current to open the main valve than it needed to stop the spark, and there is about a 2-second stabilization step before the main valve opens.

Lockout versus retry

Honeywell's sheet says every model in the family shuts off spark and pilot gas if no pilot is proven within the trial time (100% shutoff). What happens next depends on the model letter. B, H, D and J types lock out, and the call for heat must be cycled to restart; the troubleshooting notes say to set the thermostat down for at least 30 seconds. C, M, E and K types wait a 5-minute retry delay, then try again, repeating until the pilot lights or the call for heat ends. The trial time is 15 or 90 seconds, set by how the control is ordered. On the universal S8610U, a switch (SW2) selects it: off is 90 seconds, on is 15, and it ships at 90.

Other makers follow the same idea. A Robertshaw 780 series listing describes the 780-715 as a non-lockout control and the 780-845 as a lockout one that sparks for 90 seconds, purges six minutes, tries three times, then locks out for an hour. That is a retailer listing, so confirm against the Robertshaw sheet for your part. For a boiler's wider safeguard sequence, see Boiler Flame Safeguards, Purge and Lockout.

What the control needs to prove flame

The control proves flame by rectification: it sends a small signal to the sensor, and the flame carries a direct current back through the pilot burner to the control's ground. Honeywell's sheet says to measure it with a microammeter, with the pilot lit, no main gas flowing and the main valve lead disconnected. It must read a steady 1 microamp DC minimum, and 2 or more is recommended for reliable operation. Cleaning and metering habits are in Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps; the general idea is in Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics.

Pilot flame size and position

Honeywell's sheet describes a good pilot as blue and steady, enveloping 3/8 to 1/2 inch (10 to 13 mm) of the flame rod. A small blue flame points toward too little gas or air. The Q3451/Q3481 pilot burner sheet gives the same 3/8 to 1/2 inch for most models, and 1/4 to 1/2 inch (6 to 13 mm) for the Q3452 and Q3481. A flame that misses the rod or only licks its tip gives a weak signal even when it looks fine.

Spark gap and the ground wire

The Q3451/Q3481 sheet lists a spark gap of 1/8 inch (3 mm) for most pilot burners, and narrower bands for a few: 0.080 to 0.140 inch on the Q3451 and Q3481, 0.070 to 0.130 inch on the Q3452. To check the control's output, Honeywell's S8600 family sheet has you pull the jumper from the spark terminal and see how long an arc it makes: 1/8 inch or longer means the output is fine, and shorter means checking the fuse and input power before replacing the control. The circuit makes over 10,000 volts, so keep hands off, and the ignition cable should be no longer than 36 inches.

Ground matters twice, for spark and for flame sensing. Honeywell says a common ground is required between the pilot burner bracket and the control's burner ground terminal, and warns that without it you can get intermittent loss of spark or loss of flame current sensitivity. Ground wire should be thermoplastic rated at least 221°F (105°C), 14 to 18 gauge, moisture resistant. The pilot burner sheet adds that the ground can connect to a mounting screw of the igniter-sensor and may need a shield from burner radiant heat.

Common failures and where to look

  • Cracked ceramic. Both sheets say to look for a cracked or tracked ceramic insulator, which shorts the signal to ground. The Honeywell flash code for a flame rod shorted to ground points to lead damage, rod position or a cracked ceramic. The fix for a cracked igniter-sensor is replacement.
  • Weak or no spark. Check the spark gap, the cable condition and length, the ground, and the arc test above.
  • Poor ground. Shows up as sparking that comes and goes or a flame signal that wanders. Check the bracket-to-control ground wire.
  • Dirty pilot orifice or closed valves. Honeywell's checks include open manual gas valves and a pilot orifice that is not blocked, and 24 volts across the pilot valve terminals when the control calls for it.
  • Gas supply. The flash code for a failed trial lists gas supply among the causes, along with the pilot burner, wiring, a contaminated or misplaced flame rod and burner ground. This sheet gives no pressure number, so use the appliance rating plate and manual.
  • Flame lost in run. A separate flash code, with a similar list: gas supply, pilot burner, sense wiring, a dirty rod and burner ground.

Honeywell also lists a low secondary voltage code, so check the transformer under load before condemning the control. See Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit for standing pilots, which use a different sensing method. Unit heater owners can start with Garage and Workshop Heaters.

Worked example: is the signal good enough?

These numbers are our assumptions for teaching. A boiler has a retry-type control with a 90-second trial. You light the pilot, meter flame current as described above, and read a steady 0.8 microamp.

  1. Honeywell's minimum is 1.0 microamp. 1.0 minus 0.8 = 0.2 microamp short, or 0.8 / 1.0 = 80 percent of the minimum.
  2. Honeywell recommends 2 or more. 2.0 minus 0.8 = 1.2 microamps below that, so the reading is 40 percent of the recommended level (0.8 / 2.0 = 0.4).
  3. Conclusion: below the minimum, so expect failed trials. Check rod position, the 3/8 to 1/2 inch coverage, a cracked ceramic and the burner ground, then meter again.

Now the time cost. A failed trial runs the full 90 seconds, then a 5-minute (300 second) retry delay: 90 + 300 = 390 seconds per attempt. One hour is 3,600 seconds, and 3,600 / 390 = 9.2, so about 9 full attempts per hour. A 15-second version of the same control would cycle every 15 + 300 = 315 seconds, about 11 attempts (3,600 / 315 = 11.4). Also note that if the pilot lit at, say, 12 seconds into a 15-second trial, the margin is only 15 minus 12 = 3 seconds, so a slightly weak spark or gas supply could turn a working system into a failing one.

Field card: Intermittent Pilot Ignition

Spark and pilot gas, prove the pilot, then open the main.

  • Sequence: call for heat, spark plus pilot valve, flame proven, spark off, main valve opens.
  • Honeywell S8600 family: 15 or 90 second trial; failed trial shuts spark and pilot off.
  • Lockout types need the call for heat cycled; retry types wait 5 minutes and try again.
  • Flame current: steady 1 microamp DC minimum, 2 or more recommended (Honeywell).
  • Flame should be blue, steady, covering 3/8 to 1/2 inch of the rod on most Honeywell pilot burners.
  • Spark: arc of 1/8 inch or longer from the control; check gap, cable, ceramic and burner ground.
  • Do not bypass the safeguard. Over 10,000 volts at the spark circuit.
