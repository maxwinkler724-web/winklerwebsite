Print Shops and Paper Storage: Humidity Control, Static, Curl, Press Heat Loads and Solvent and Ozone Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Paper is a sponge made of wood fiber. A print shop, copy center or paper storeroom has to hold its air steady so the paper stays flat and feeds cleanly, while the same room also carries press heat, solvent vapor and copier ozone. This lesson ties those jobs together and ends with a humidification load worked out by hand.

Check the rules that apply to the site

Check the rules that apply to the site. Air permits, fire codes and the paper maker's own instructions vary by location and product. This page is general education built from agency and maker pages read in 2026. Confirm the details with the press maker, the paper supplier and your local authority before you design or change anything.

Why paper cares about humidity

Paper takes on and gives off moisture until it matches the air around it. When the edges of a stack match the air but the middle does not, the sheets distort. One paper maker's acclimation guide says extreme humidity can make paper curl or develop tight or wavy edges. A copier maker's paper guide is more specific: damp paper gives wavy edges, extra curl, wrinkling in the paper path and sheets that stick together, while dry paper gives tight edges, misfeeds, jams and registration problems. A paper maker's handling tips add that paper that is too dry can build static, so sheets cling and feed several at once, and that in digital printing moisture changes the electrical charge that controls toner placement.

So the three complaints a technician hears, curl, static and misfeeds, usually trace back to the same cause: the room's relative humidity (RH) moved, or the paper never matched the room. Temperature matters too, because cold paper carried into a warm room does not match the air until it warms up.

The RH ranges the sources give

No single number appears everywhere, so read the source and its purpose.

  • A paper maker's handling page says to keep 35 to 55 percent RH in all paper handling and storage areas, with paper at roughly 68 to 76°F.
  • A copier maker's paper guide gives the same 68 to 76°F and 35 to 55 percent for the storage location.
  • One paper maker's condition guide says its sheets are designed to lie flat at 70°F and 50 percent RH, and that it will not accept responsibility for humidity problems if the pressroom is below 40 percent or above 60 percent. A second guide from the same maker gives 35 to 60 percent.
  • For archives, the National Archives lists 50 to 65°F and 30 to 50 percent RH for long-term storage of paper records. That is a preservation goal, not a production goal, and it is colder than a press can comfortably run.

Our own reading: the overlap of the working ranges is about 40 to 55 percent RH at about 70°F, so that is a sensible target band for a pressroom or copy room, with the paper maker's number winning if the paper in your stock states one. For the archive side and seasonal drift, see Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control.

Conditioning paper before use

The makers agree on the habit: leave paper in its original wrap and let it come to room temperature before you open it. The wrap is moisture resistant, and it keeps the paper at its as-made moisture level. One maker's chart gives hours by temperature difference and stack volume: for a 10°F difference it lists 5 hours for 6 cubic feet and 11 hours for 24 cubic feet, and for a 30°F difference it lists 18 hours for 6 cubic feet and 35 hours for 24 cubic feet. Another maker's rule of thumb is at least 24 hours before a job. Winter deliveries from a cold truck are the usual trouble. Keep storage conditions steady over weekends too, because a shut-down building can drift and show up as Monday jams.

Choosing the humidification

In winter the air is the problem: heated outdoor air is very dry, and paper dries to match it. A shop that exhausts air over a press must bring in make-up air, and that raises the load. The two common ways to add moisture are steam, which is covered in Commercial Steam Humidifiers (dispersion tubes, absorption distance, water quality), and evaporative or fogging units, covered in Adiabatic Humidifiers in Air Handlers. Evaporation cools the air, which matters in a cold season, and any wetted system needs a water treatment plan. Our own practice note: whichever you pick, mount the sensor where it reads the room air the paper sees, away from the supply outlet, and follow the humidifier maker on airflow interlocks. To read the humidity ratios used below, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

Worked example: our own humidification load

Every input below is an assumption of ours, not data from a real shop.

  • Room: 60 ft by 40 ft by 12 ft, which is 28,800 cubic feet.
  • Outdoor air that must be humidified: 2,000 cfm (the make-up for the press exhaust plus ventilation). That is about 4.2 air changes per hour: 2,000 × 60 ÷ 28,800.
  • Winter outdoor air: 20°F, 50 percent RH.
  • Indoor target: 72°F, 45 percent RH.
  • Sea-level pressure, 14.696 psia.
  • Humidity ratios (our own calculation): we computed them from the standard saturation-pressure equations used in ASHRAE psychrometric work (we did not read them from a published chart). Outdoor 7.5 grains of moisture per pound of dry air; indoor 52.5 grains per pound. Treat them as assumptions and confirm them on a psychrometric chart you trust.

Step 1, the difference: 52.5 − 7.5 = 45.0 grains per pound of dry air.

Step 2, the water: lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW ÷ 7,000, where 4.5 is 60 minutes times 0.075 lb/ft³ for standard air and 7,000 grains make a pound. So 4.5 × 2,000 × 45.0 ÷ 7,000 = 405,000 ÷ 7,000 = 57.9 lb/h, or about 6.9 gallons an hour at 8.34 lb per gallon.

Step 3, the heat to make steam: at roughly 1,000 Btu per pound (our own round number), 57.9 lb/h is about 58,000 Btu/h, or about 17 kW. That is a large electric steam load for one room, which is why the cure is to cut outside air, not just add a bigger unit.

What this leaves out: the load to bring the paper itself up to equilibrium, leaks through the building, any recirculated air (which lowers the load) and the sensible heat to warm the 20°F air. It is a sizing first pass, not a design.

Press and dryer heat loads

Almost every watt that goes into a press, a drive, lights and a copier ends up as heat in the room unless it is exhausted. Our own conversion: 1 kW is about 3,412 Btu/h. If we assume, for illustration only, 15 kW of running equipment in the room, that is about 51,000 Btu/h, and it is a real cooling load in summer and free heat in winter. Heatset presses add a dryer; the EPA's page on Texas lithographic printing rules describes heatset dryers as hot-air dryers that drive off ink oils and release VOCs through their exhaust, so that heat leaves with the exhaust and the make-up air has to be conditioned. Ask the press maker for the actual heat release.

Solvent and VOC exhaust

The same EPA page lists the VOC sources in offset work: alcohol or substitute additives in fountain solution, solvent-containing inks, press cleaning solutions and heatset dryers. A federal rule, 40 CFR Part 63 Subpart KK, covers publication and packaging rotogravure and wide-web flexographic presses at major sources of hazardous air pollutants, and the eCFR text says lithographic, letterpress and screen presses are generally outside its affected sources. That does not mean a small shop is unregulated: state and local rules exist, as the Texas example shows, so ask your air agency.

For people, the concern is the operator breathing cleaning vapor. A NIOSH researcher reporting in 1998 found press operators in small lithographic shops were often overexposed or potentially overexposed when hand-scrubbing presses with solvent wipers, and that small shops often lack enough fresh air to dilute vapor while keeping comfort. In three shops that added fresh-air systems with air-to-air heat exchangers, airborne solvent fell by about 30 to 80 percent. The report is an abstract of a conference paper, not a design standard, but it makes the point: exhaust and make-up air are a pair. Put a local exhaust where the vapor is made, keep solvent cloths in closed containers, and size the make-up air so the room does not run negative and pull dry, dirty air in around the doors. Then the humidifier sees the make-up air flow, which is the 2,000 cfm in the example.

Ozone and particles from copiers and laser printers

An OSHA letter of 1988 says ozone forms around electrical sources such as copying machines, irritates the eyes and respiratory tract, and gives the OSHA limit as 0.1 ppm averaged over eight hours. Confirm the current limit in 29 CFR 1910.1000 Table Z-1, since the letter is old. NIOSH has no recommended exposure limit for ozone, per its 1988 PEL project page for ozone, which also notes the 1989 OSHA limit rule was remanded by a court. In a NIOSH health hazard evaluation of an office, breathing-zone ozone ran from below detection to 0.05 ppm, but 0.56 ppm was measured at the laser printer exhaust, and NIOSH recommended relocating the printer, supplying more outside air and checking the printer's ozone filter. Respirable dust in that building reached 90 micrograms per cubic meter. A 2008 review by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory scientists in Atmospheric Environment compiled emission data for ozone, particles and VOCs from computers, printers and copiers and noted that data are sparse. Our practical reading: put high-volume copiers and laser printers in a ventilated spot, change their filters on the maker's schedule, and do not buy an ozone-making air cleaner to solve a smell; see Bipolar Ionization and Other Electronic Air Cleaners.

Field card: Print Shops and Paper Storage

Steady RH, wrapped paper, and make-up air for every cfm exhausted.

  • Working band from the sources: about 40 to 55 percent RH at about 70°F; follow the paper maker if it states a number.
  • Keep paper wrapped and let it warm up; a maker rule of thumb is at least 24 hours.
  • Dry paper: static, tight edges, multi-feeds. Damp paper: wavy edges, curl, jams.
  • Humidification water: lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW grains ÷ 7,000. Our example: 57.9 lb/h.
  • Cut outside air before you oversize the humidifier.
  • Local exhaust at the press, closed solvent containers, make-up air to match.
  • Put copiers and laser printers where ozone and particles can be exhausted or diluted.
