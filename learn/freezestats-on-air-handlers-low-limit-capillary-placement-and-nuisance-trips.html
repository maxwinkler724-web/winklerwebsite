Freezestats on Air Handlers: Low Limit, Capillary Placement and Nuisance Trips

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot water or chilled water coil can freeze and split in a few minutes when a cold slug of outdoor air gets past it. The freezestat, also called a low limit, is the hardwired last line of defense. This lesson covers what it senses, where the capillary goes, how it is wired, why it trips when nothing is wrong, and how to test it.

Check the maker and the drawings

Check the maker and the drawings. Setpoints, element rules and wiring differ between brands and jobs. The numbers below are examples from the sources listed at the bottom. Your unit's installation manual and the control drawings win.

What a freezestat is

A freezestat is a capillary-tube temperature switch. A long, thin sealed tube is filled with a fluid that reacts to cold. When part of the tube gets cold enough, the pressure change flips a snap switch. Johnson Controls lists its A70 low limit with a 20 ft length of 1/8 inch tubing, and says it responds to the lowest temperature along any 1 ft section of the element. Siemens says the same of its 134 series: any one foot of the capillary operates the switch. Because the coldest foot decides, one short cold spot on a big coil is enough. Honeywell words it differently: its L482A sheet says the controller operates from the lowest temperature along the entire 20 feet. Trade articles quote shorter sensing lengths, 8 to 18 inches. The wording differs by maker, so read the sheet for the part on your unit.

Setpoint and reset

Typical setpoints sit just above freezing. The A70 sheet says its low cutout stop is sealed at 35°F. Honeywell's L482A has an internal stop limiting adjustment to 35 to 55°F, ships at 35°F, and has a fixed differential of about 5°F. Siemens lists 35 to 45°F on its 134-1510 and 134-1511. Trade articles call 35°F and 37°F typical. Many controllers come in either reset style: Johnson Controls lists the A70GA-1 as automatic reset and the A70HA-1 as manual reset. A manual reset forces someone to look at the unit and find out why it tripped before it restarts. Never reset and walk away; find the cause first.

What the trip does

The freezestat contacts are usually wired into the fan starter or safety circuit, so a trip stops the supply fan without help from the building automation system. One engineering article wires the normally closed contacts in series with the drive's safety interlock. The same article says the controls should also close the outdoor air damper and drive the hot water and chilled water valves open to a minimum (it uses 20 percent, adjustable) so water keeps moving through the coils. Another says a two-contact freezestat uses the line-voltage contact to shut the unit down and the low-voltage contact to send an alarm to the BAS. Some two-fan jobs keep the return fan running; follow your job's sequence.

Where the capillary goes

Put the element on the leaving-air side of the coil it protects, across the whole face. One HVAC trade article gives the reason: minimum outdoor air stratifies, so a short piece of tube on the entering side could see below-freezing air while the coil is fine. Dynacon's instructions show the element downstream of the water coil, run in a downward serpentine across the face with vertical lengths kept to a minimum, and the case at the top. ACI says to use horizontal serpentine runs only, and warns that too much vertical element will not operate properly. Honeywell wants the element horizontal, bends no tighter than a 1 inch radius, and no pulling on the tube. Penn State's facility standard adds a layout rule: at least 1 ft of element per 1 sq ft of coil, the bottom row within 6 inches of the bottom, rows no more than 12 inches apart, and the top row within 6 inches of the top. That is one owner's standard, not every maker's. The bottom of a coil is the part most likely to freeze, so a missing bottom row matters most.

Worked example: a 10 ft by 6 ft coil face

This is an invented unit. Use the 1 ft per sq ft layout rule above and 20 ft freezestat elements.

  1. Face area. 10 ft × 6 ft = 60 sq ft.
  2. Element needed. 60 sq ft × 1 ft per sq ft = 60 ft of element in the coil face.
  3. Count on paper. 60 ft ÷ 20 ft per freezestat = 3 freezestats.
  4. Check the turnarounds. A U-turn between two rows takes element that does not cross the coil. At 12 inch row spacing the turn is a half circle of 6 inch radius: 3.14 × 0.5 ft = about 1.6 ft. One freezestat then has 20 − 1.6 = 18.4 ft left for two passes, or 9.2 ft each. Three of them give 6 passes × 9.2 ft = 55.2 ft, which is less than 60. Three is not enough once the bends are counted.
  5. Use four. Four elements give 80 ft. Eight rows over a 72 inch face fit at 9 inch spacing, with the bottom row 5 inches up and the top row 4 inches from the top, inside the 6 and 12 inch limits. A 9 inch spacing makes the U-turn a 4.5 inch radius half circle: 3.14 × 0.375 ft = about 1.2 ft.
  6. Pass length. Each freezestat covers two rows. Keep a little spare for the lead from the case: 2 passes × 9.0 ft + 1.2 ft = 19.2 ft, about 0.8 ft spare. Eight passes × 9.0 ft = 72 ft of element across the coil, a ratio of 72 ÷ 60 = 1.2 ft per sq ft.
  7. Wire them. The four normally closed contacts go in series in the safety circuit, so any one opening stops the fan.

So the answer is four freezestats, not three, with 9 ft passes at 9 inch spacing. Each pass is about 1 ft short of the 10 ft coil width, so 6 inches are left at each side. If the designer wants the full width covered, use longer elements or a fifth control. The maker's sheet and the drawings decide.

Nuisance trips

A freezestat that trips when the coil is warm is usually telling you about its installation, not its age. Common causes in the sources:

  • The element is on the entering-air side, where stratified outdoor air can touch it. Move it to the leaving side.
  • Part of the element lies in a cold stream from a leaking damper or a coil bypass path. This is a real cold spot, so fix the airflow and do not just raise the setpoint.
  • The setpoint is too aggressive, or the case is in cold air. The Penn State standard says the case and bellows must sit where ambient is always above the setpoint.
  • A kinked or crushed tube. ACI warns against sharp bends and kinks in the element.

Testing

Honeywell's L482A sheet gives a checkout. If the surrounding air is above 50°F, immerse at least one foot of the element in ice water or wrap it in ice, and watch the contacts change as it cools through the setpoint. Press the manual reset button afterward and the contacts should return. If the air is below 50°F, turn the setpoint dial up through the ambient temperature instead. A commissioning article also says to confirm the BAS logs the trip with a timestamp and that the alarm arrives at the operator's screen. Other brands have their own test steps, so follow your maker's sheet.

The BAS side

The freezestat acts through its own wiring, so it does not depend on software. The BAS only reports. For comparison, the public Modelica library's implementation of ASHRAE Guideline 36 freeze protection has three stages, and its third stage (a freezestat signal, or supply air below set limits for a set time) shuts down the fans, closes the outdoor air damper, opens the cooling coil valve and sets a Level 2 alarm. Its reset is by a software switch for sensor trips, while a freezestat with a physical reset has no software reset. Field card: Freezestats on Air Handlers

Leaving side, whole face, horizontal passes, manual reset, find the cause.

  • Capillary switch: the coldest section (often one foot) trips it; check your maker's wording.
  • Typical setpoint: about 35 to 38°F, per the maker.
  • Trip: fan stops, outdoor damper closes, water valves open, BAS alarm.
  • Layout example: 1 ft of element per sq ft, rows 12 in apart or less, bottom row within 6 in.
  • Example: 10 ft × 6 ft face = 60 sq ft; four 20 ft elements once bends are counted.
  • Test: ice water on one foot of element above 50°F ambient, then reset.
