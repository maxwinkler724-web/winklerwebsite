Extension Cords and GFCI for HVAC Power Tools: OSHA Jobsite Rules, Cord Gauge, Voltage Drop and Daily Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A core drill, a hammer drill and a wet-dry vacuum all arrive at the job on the end of an extension cord. The cord is the part of the tool that gets stepped on, run over, soaked and forgotten. This lesson covers what the federal rules say about cords and ground-fault protection, how to check a cord before you use it, and how to pick a cord gauge with real arithmetic.

Check your own rules first

Check the rules that apply to you. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026. Your employer, the general contractor and your state may require more. Follow your site procedures.

Rule 1: GFCI or an assured grounding program

For construction work, 29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1) says the employer must protect workers with one of two methods. As we read the eCFR text, method one is ground-fault circuit interrupters on 120-volt, single-phase, 15- and 20-ampere receptacle outlets that are not part of the building's permanent wiring and are in use by employees. Method two is an assured equipment grounding conductor program covering cord sets, temporary receptacles and cord-and-plug equipment. A small two-wire portable generator (5 kW or less) with insulated circuit conductors has its own exemption.

A GFCI compares the current going out with the current coming back. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says it trips when the difference reaches about 6 milliamps, quickly enough to act before a shock affects the heart. That is why a portable inline GFCI or a GFCI-equipped cord at the tool end is the simplest way to cover an extension cord that runs from a building outlet.

An OSHA letter of interpretation (1992) explains the either-or choice. If every cord and every GFCI-equipped tool is protected for its whole length, no testing program is needed. If only some are, the employer must run the grounding program for all cord sets and cord-connected tools, including those with built-in GFCIs.

The assured program has five parts we can state from the rule: a written description kept on site, one or more competent persons, a visual check before each day's use, continuity and receptacle tests, and records. The rule sets test timing at before first use, after repair, after any event that could have damaged the item, and at intervals not over 3 months (6 months for fixed cord sets and receptacles not exposed to damage). Damaged equipment stays out of service until repaired.

Rule 2: the right kind of cord

Under 29 CFR 1926.405, extension cord sets used with portable tools must be three-wire (grounded) and designed for hard or extra-hard usage. OSHA's flexible-cords page says those ratings are marked on the cord roughly every foot, with examples such as S, SO, ST and STO for hard service and SJ, SJO, SJT and SJTO for junior hard service. A thin household lamp cord is not a tool cord.

The same section, 1926.405(g)(2), says flexible cords are used in continuous lengths without a splice or tap. The one stated exception is a hard-service cord No. 12 or larger, which may be repaired if the splice keeps the original insulation, outer sheath and usage characteristics. Cords must also have strain relief at plugs and connectors so a pull does not reach the terminals. The practical rule for an apprentice: if the jacket is cut or a plug is cracked, tag it out and get a new cord; do not tape it.

Rule 3: inspect before use

29 CFR 1910.334(a)(2) covers portable cord- and plug-connected equipment and extension cords: they are visually checked for external defects and signs of possible internal damage before use, and a defective item is not used until repaired and tested. (The construction rule above asks for the same check before each day's use.) A 30-second routine works:

  • Plugs and ends: blades straight, ground pin present, no cracks, no heat discoloration.
  • Jacket: no cuts, flat spots, bare conductors or crushed sections. Run the whole length through your hands.
  • Strain relief: the cord is not pulling out of the plug or connector.
  • Markings: the type and gauge are legible.
  • GFCI test: press Test, the power must drop; press Reset, power returns. The CPSC says to test a GFCI at least monthly and after a power failure; on a jobsite we also test it at the start of each shift, which is our own habit and a good one.

Keep cords out of water and doorways

OSHA explains that a wet connector can leak current to the grounding conductor and to anyone touching it. Its remedy is to limit moisture, for example with watertight or sealable connectors. 29 CFR 1910.334(a)(4) says cords used where workers may contact water or conductive liquids must be approved for those locations, and (a)(5)(i) says do not plug or unplug an energized cord with wet hands. In practice: keep connections off the floor and out of puddles, hang them or lift them on a block, and do not trail a cord through a drain pan area while a condensate line is being flushed.

Doors and windows are named by OSHA as sources of cord damage, and 1926.405(g)(1) bars cords from running through doorways as a replacement for fixed wiring. CPSC tells consumers not to run cords through doorways or under rugs. If a cord has to cross a door opening, route it so the door cannot close on it and protect it from damage; better, move the work plug.

Cord gauge, length and voltage drop

A long thin cord wastes some of the voltage as heat in the copper, so the tool sees less than 120 volts. The drop is current times the cord resistance, and the current travels out on one conductor and back on another, so use twice the cord length. For copper, the Georgia State University HyperPhysics wire table lists these resistances at 20 degrees C: 16 AWG, 4.016 ohms per 1000 ft; 14 AWG, 2.525; 12 AWG, 1.588. Resistance rises as copper warms, so real drops run a little higher.

Worked example (ours): a 15 A core drill on a 100 ft cord

These are our own calculations. Assumptions: the drill draws its full 15 A rating, the supply is 120 V at the outlet, the cord is 100 ft long (200 ft of conductor path), and we use the table resistances above.

  1. 16 AWG: 4.016 × 200 / 1000 = 0.803 ohm. Drop = 15 × 0.803 = 12.05 V, which is 10.0 percent of 120 V. Heat in the cord = 15² × 0.803 = about 181 W.
  2. 14 AWG: 2.525 × 200 / 1000 = 0.505 ohm. Drop = 15 × 0.505 = 7.58 V, or 6.3 percent. Heat = about 114 W.
  3. 12 AWG: 1.588 × 200 / 1000 = 0.318 ohm. Drop = 15 × 0.318 = 4.76 V, or 4.0 percent. Heat = about 71 W.

Picking a cord. We would choose the 12 AWG hard-service cord. The 16 AWG cord loses 10 percent of the voltage and about 181 W as heat in the cord, which is how motors run hot and cords get warm. Fourteen gauge is better but still loses over 6 percent for a full-load motor. The National Electrical Code carries a commonly quoted 3 percent and 5 percent guidance for conductor drops, but we did not read that text for this lesson, so we are not relying on it here; we simply pick the lowest drop that is practical. Also check the tool maker's manual, which may name a minimum cord size. Shorter is always better: a 50 ft cord halves each number above.

Field card: Extension Cords and GFCI for HVAC Power Tools: OSHA Jobsite Rules, Cord Gauge, Voltage Drop and Daily Checks

GFCI at the tool, hard-service cord, look before you plug.

  • Construction sites: GFCI on temporary 120 V, 15 and 20 A outlets, or an assured grounding program (29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1)).
  • Three-wire, hard or extra-hard usage cord. No taped repairs.
  • Inspect the whole cord and both ends before use; test the GFCI.
  • Keep plugs out of water. Do not handle energized plugs with wet hands.
  • Do not pinch cords in doors; avoid doorways.
  • Our example: 15 A, 100 ft: 12.05 V drop (16 AWG), 7.58 V (14 AWG), 4.76 V (12 AWG).
