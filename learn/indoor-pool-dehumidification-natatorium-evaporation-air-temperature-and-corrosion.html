Indoor Pool Dehumidification: Evaporation, Air Temperature and Corrosion

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A swimming pool is a big, warm water surface that never stops giving off vapor. Put it inside a building and that vapor has nowhere to go. This lesson explains why a pool room (a natatorium) needs its own dehumidification, what drives the moisture load, and what a tech should look at when the room turns damp or corrosive.

Why a pool room is not an ordinary room

The U.S. Department of Energy says evaporation is by far the largest source of energy loss from a pool, and that indoor pools "still can lose a lot of energy from evaporation." DOE adds that these buildings need ventilation to control the humidity that evaporation creates, and that the ventilated air must be conditioned, which adds cost. A normal air conditioner is built to handle people and sunlight, not a pool surface. That is why makers sell dedicated pool dehumidifiers. For general moisture-in-air ideas, see Psychrometrics for Technicians; for the small residential version of the same problem, see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage.

What drives evaporation

DOE says the higher the pool temperature and air speed, and the lower the humidity, the more water evaporates. The makers' design guides turn that into a formula taken from ASHRAE (we mention ASHRAE only as the guides do; we did not read the standard). The Seresco and PoolPak guides both print it as ER = 0.1 x A x AF x (Pw minus Pdp). ER is evaporation in pounds per hour, A is the water surface in square feet, AF is an activity factor, Pw is the vapor pressure at the water surface and Pdp is the vapor pressure at the room dew point, both in inches of mercury. The guides say it applies to air moving over the water at 10 to 30 feet per minute.

The activity factor reflects how much the water is stirred. PoolPak's table lists 0.5 for a residential pool, 0.65 for aquafit or physical therapy, 0.8 for a hotel, 1.0 for a public pool or swim meet, and 1.5 to 2.0 for a wave pool. More splashing means more moisture. Desert Aire's design guide also warns that raising air speed across the water from 30 to 125 feet per minute raises the load by 40 percent, so supply air should not blast the surface.

Air temperature and humidity: what the guides say

The Seresco, PoolPak and Desert Aire guides all repeat the same ASHRAE-based advice. Keep the room air 2 to 4 degrees F above the water, but not above about 86 F for most pools. Seresco's guide shows why: at the same water temperature, a pool kept at 78 F air and 50 percent RH evaporates almost 35 percent more than one at 85 F air. Cooler air pulls more water off the surface. Keep humidity between 50 and 60 percent RH. Desert Aire says above 60 percent is uncomfortable and invites mold and bacteria, and below 50 percent chills swimmers as they leave the water. The makers also say most pools do not run below 50 percent because the energy cost climbs. These are manufacturer design guidance, so follow the design documents for the actual building.

Chloramines and exhaust

The strong "chlorine" smell is not extra chlorine. Seresco and PoolPak say it comes from chloramines, compounds that form when chlorine reacts with swimmers' nitrogen compounds, off-gassing from the water. Trichloramine is the most volatile. The guides recommend keeping the room slightly negative, at 0.05 to 0.15 inch of water column, so air does not push into the building, and exhausting about 10 percent more air than the outdoor air brought in. Desert Aire's guide notes that trichloramine is about four times heavier than air and settles near the water, which is why it describes source capture, with exhaust taken from low points near the surface. Water chemistry stays the operator's job.

Dew point, condensation and corrosion

Seresco's guide gives an example: a pool room at 82 F and 50 percent RH has a dew point of about 62 F, and design dew points typically run 62 to 69 F. Any surface colder than that collects water. The guides name windows, exterior doors and skylights as the usual trouble spots and suggest washing them with 3 to 5 cfm of supply air per square foot. Desert Aire's guide says exterior walls, windows and doors must be kept above the dew point. A vapor retarder belongs on the warm side of the wall. The CDC Model Aquatic Health Code, for its part, asks that design documents for an indoor facility record the design space temperature, relative humidity and dew point, and state that a vapor barrier is in the walls and ceiling.

The corrosion part is what techs notice first. The makers say chloramines combine with moisture, so any condensation becomes corrosive. They suggest avoiding stainless steel duct and hardware, because chlorine attacks it, and using the best corrosion-resistant coatings on equipment in the pool air. Desert Aire's guide mentions epoxy paint on galvanized steel and aluminum and 316 stainless in some places. Condensation can turn acidic and harm doors, fixtures and structure. For the coil side of the story, see Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air.

Heat recovery to the pool water

A pool dehumidifier is a refrigeration circuit. It cools the air to wring out moisture, and then has to put that heat somewhere. The Seresco and PoolPak guides say the heat can go to pool water, and that in the cooling season it can supply all the pool water heating. They quote a COP "close to 8" and a payback of under a year. Those are maker claims for their own options. The guides say the water-side exchanger should be rated for pool water (cupronickel or titanium) and fed by a separate pump. Desert Aire says a diverting valve sends refrigerant to either the air reheat coil or the water coil. For comparison with a stand-alone pool heater, see Pool Heaters: Gas vs Heat Pump.

Worked example: a 2,000 sq ft pool

The guides print the formula but not ready-made vapor-pressure values, so we scale a maker's published result instead of guessing them. Desert Aire's design guide says a 3,500 sq ft pool with 82 F water, 84 F air and 55 percent RH evaporates about 159 lb/h. Assuming the load scales with area (our own assumption), here is a 2,000 sq ft pool at those conditions.

  1. Per square foot: 159 lb/h divided by 3,500 sq ft = 0.0454 lb/h per sq ft. Our own.
  2. For 2,000 sq ft: 0.0454 x 2,000 = about 91 lb/h. Our own.
  3. Per day: 91 lb/h x 24 h = 2,184 lb/day. Our own.
  4. Pints: water is about 8.34 lb per gallon and a gallon is 8 pints, so 1 pint is about 1.04 lb. 2,184 divided by 1.04 = about 2,100 pints per day. Our own conversion.
  5. Latent heat: Seresco's guide says each pound evaporated represents about 1,000 Btu. 91 lb/h x 1,000 Btu/lb = about 91,000 Btu/h, or about 7.6 tons of latent load. Our own arithmetic.

That is a rough, same-conditions estimate for study only. A real design uses the activity factor, the formula above and the actual room design conditions. Compare it with the roughly 40 to 45 pints per day of a modern basement unit, covered in the basement lesson linked above, to see why pools need dedicated equipment.

Service checks for a tech

The design guides give a few service hints, and we mark where they stop. We found no maker page in our reading with a filter or refrigerant schedule, so follow the unit's own service manual for those.

  • Coil and cabinet corrosion. Look for coating failure and pitting on coils, cabinets and nearby hardware, since the air is corrosive by nature. Seresco and PoolPak say to keep electrical parts out of the pool air stream.
  • Airflow. Desert Aire says supply air should run at a constant rate so it keeps washing the walls and windows, even when the pool is closed.
  • Controls. Desert Aire describes a VOC sensor that can raise outdoor and exhaust air, and drives that hold the negative pressure. Check that the room stays slightly negative.
  • Access. The PoolPak guide says "No Access = No Service or Maintenance" and calls for a factory-trained startup and a follow-up visit once the pool reaches design conditions.
  • Filters and refrigerant. Use the manufacturer's service manual and its schedule.

Field card: Indoor Pool Dehumidification

Warm water, so the vapor is constant; keep it out of cold surfaces.

  • DOE: evaporation is the largest pool energy loss, indoors too.
  • Makers' guidance: air 2 to 4 F above water, not over about 86 F; 50 to 60 percent RH.
  • Evaporation formula in guides: ER = 0.1 x A x AF x (Pw minus Pdp), lb/h.
  • Rule: 1 pint is about 1.04 lb; about 1,000 Btu per lb evaporated (maker figure).
  • Keep windows and walls above dew point; condensation turns corrosive.
  • Room slightly negative, 0.05 to 0.15 in. WC.
  • Heat recovery can warm pool water; check the unit manual.
