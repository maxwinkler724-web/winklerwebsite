Four-Way Mixing Valves on Older Boilers: Raising Return, Lowering Supply

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many older hot-water systems with cast-iron or steel boilers have a valve with four pipe connections near the boiler. It looks like a three-way valve with an extra port, and it does two jobs at once: it keeps cold return water from shocking the boiler, and it sends cooler water to a radiant or low-temperature loop. This lesson explains how, and how to check one in the field.

Why an older boiler needs a warm return

A non-condensing boiler is built so its flue gas stays hot enough not to turn to liquid in the boiler or vent. A national laboratory guide page on gas-fired boilers says that when outdoor reset is used on a non-condensing boiler, the return water should stay at or above 140 F to prevent condensing. The same laboratory's guide for condensing boilers says the reset on those should aim for return water below 130 F. Those two numbers show why the goals differ: an older boiler wants a hot return, a condensing boiler wants a cool one. Cold return water on an older boiler can wet the heat exchanger with acidic condensate. Many manufacturers also warn about sudden cold return on cast iron; confirm the limit in the boiler's own manual. For the chemistry, see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing.

The problem is that radiant floors and some baseboard loops want water far below 180 F. If you feed them straight from the boiler they overheat, and if you throttle the boiler down you invite flue gas condensation. The mixing valve lets the boiler stay hot while the loop runs cool.

How the four ports work

The four ports are boiler supply in, boiler return out, system supply out and system return in. Think of the valve body as two mixing points sharing one moving part. At the supply side, boiler water and system return water blend to make system supply. At the return side, system return water and boiler water blend to make boiler return. When the valve moves toward "more boiler water to the loop," the loop gets hotter and the boiler return gets colder. When it moves the other way, a share of hot boiler supply is short-circuited straight into the boiler return, so the boiler return rises and the loop supply falls. That second effect is the whole reason to use a four-way valve on an older boiler. A plain three-way mixing valve only blends on the supply side and does not lift the return this way.

For the plain mixing math and injection mixing, we do not repeat it here; see Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics and the mass-balance lesson on mixed water temperature for radiant floors and low-temperature loops.

One pump or two

Two pumps. The usual arrangement is a boiler pump that pushes water through the boiler side of the valve and a system pump that pushes water through the loop side. Each pump moves its own flow, and the valve decides how the two streams share. This keeps the loop flowing even when the boiler side is throttled, and it keeps boiler flow steady. In our experience this is the easier layout to troubleshoot, because you can feel each pump and pipe separately.

One pump. Some older jobs use a single circulator that serves both sides. The valve position then changes how much flow each side sees, so a stuck valve can starve either the boiler or the loop. Check what the original piping does before you change anything, and confirm the pump position against the drawing or the valve maker's piping diagram.

Manual or motorized, and outdoor reset

A manual valve has a handle or knob set once. It cannot respond to weather, so it is a compromise: set warm enough for the coldest day, it overheats the loop in mild weather. A motorized valve has an actuator driven by a controller. With outdoor reset, the controller lowers the target supply temperature as outdoor air warms and raises it as the air cools, and the actuator moves the valve to hold that target using a supply sensor. See Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation. Reset should lower the loop supply only. The boiler itself still has to stay hot, so its own limit control keeps the boiler water up; see Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not manufacturer data. Assumptions: boiler water at 180 F, system return water at the valve at 100 F, water treated as 500 Btu/h per gpm per degree F (8.33 lb/gal times 60 min/h, rounded), loop flow 10 gpm, boiler flow 3 gpm, no heat lost in the piping.

  1. Supply blend. If 3 gpm of 180 F boiler water joins 7 gpm of 100 F return: (3 x 180 + 7 x 100) / 10 = (540 + 700) / 10 = 124 F.
  2. Boiler side of the same case. If the 3 gpm of boiler water gives up 40 F to the loop, it returns to the boiler at 180 - 40 = 140 F, meaning 3 x 500 x 40 = 60,000 Btu/h left the boiler.
  3. Check the two cases agree. 60,000 Btu/h into 10 gpm is a loop rise of 60,000 / (500 x 10) = 12 F, so the loop return is not 100 F. For these flows the steady state has return 140 F and supply (540 + 7 x 140) / 10 = 152 F. The 124 F in step 1 is the instant a cold 100 F loop first meets the valve. As the loop warms, the actuator must throttle hot water or the supply climbs past 124 F.
  4. The return-lifting move. With the 100 F return, suppose the valve sends only 1.5 gpm of boiler water to the supply side and short-circuits 1.5 gpm into the boiler return. Supply = (1.5 x 180 + 8.5 x 100) / 10 = (270 + 850) / 10 = 112 F. Boiler return = (1.5 x 180 + 1.5 x 100) / 3 = (270 + 150) / 3 = 140 F, which meets the 140 F figure from the laboratory page.

Notice what step 4 shows: one valve position gave a cool 112 F loop supply and a 140 F boiler return together. A three-way valve cannot do both in the same move.

Why four-way valves faded

A condensing boiler wants return water below 130 F, according to the laboratory page above. A four-way valve that lifts the return works against that goal, so it does not belong on a condensing boiler. Condensing boilers have their own controls and are normally fed through simpler piping. Four-way valves remain on older installed systems, so techs still meet them. If you plan to replace the boiler, review the whole piping design first rather than reusing the valve by habit.

Field checks

  • Stuck spindle. If the loop is always hot or always cold, remove the actuator or handle and move the stem by hand with the system off and cooled. A stem that will not move, or a motor that hums without turning the stem, points to a seized valve or a bad actuator.
  • Wrong port orientation. A four-way valve is marked for its ports. If it was piped with supply and return swapped, the valve will move the wrong way: opening for more heat makes the loop colder. Compare the marked ports and arrows to your pipes and your reading of the sensors. Confirm the supply sensor position as well.
  • Both pumps running when they should not. If the system pump runs with no call, or the boiler pump runs without a boiler call, check the relay and the call wiring, and check for gravity or ghost flow through a leaking check valve.
  • Return pipe temperature. Use a surface thermometer on the boiler return pipe after warm-up and compare it with the manufacturer's stated limit. A return below the limit during normal running says the valve is not lifting it.
  • Confirm the 24 V or line-voltage control with the manufacturer's wiring diagram before replacing a motorized actuator, and follow lockout and tagout steps before opening any wiring.

If the loop has air or no heat at all, start with Hot Water, No Heat: Circulator, Zone Valve and Air Diagnosis.

Field card: Four-Way Mixing Valves

Hot boiler, cool loop, warm return.

  • Four ports: boiler supply, boiler return, system supply, system return.
  • Blends on the supply side and on the return side at once.
  • Older non-condensing boiler: keep return at 140 F or higher (laboratory guide page).
  • Condensing boiler: return below 130 F is the goal, so the valve works against it.
  • Our example: 3 gpm at 180 F plus 7 gpm at 100 F gives 124 F.
  • Check: stuck stem, swapped ports, pumps running with no call.
