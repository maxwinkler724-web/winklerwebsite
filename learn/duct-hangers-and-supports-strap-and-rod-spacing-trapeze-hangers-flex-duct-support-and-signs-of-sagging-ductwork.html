Duct Hangers and Supports: Strap and Rod Spacing, Trapeze Hangers, Flex Duct Support and Signs of Sagging Ductwork

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Ductwork looks like a fixed part of the building, but it is only as steady as what holds it up. A duct that sags pulls on its joints, leaks, rattles and can eventually come down. This lesson covers how ducts are hung, how far apart the hangers go, and how to spot trouble.

Check the rules where you work

Check the adopted code. Spacing numbers differ by code edition, state amendments, duct type and the duct maker. This page is general education. The adopted code, the engineer's drawings and the maker's instructions win.

Why support matters

  • Sag and airflow. A sagging flex duct narrows and bends, which raises friction. See Flex Duct Installation.
  • Joint leakage. Weight and movement open seams and tape. Leakage testing is covered in Duct Leakage Testing.
  • Noise. Thin sheet metal that is loose or unsupported can drum and rattle when air pressure changes.
  • Failure. A hanger pulled out of a structure drops the duct, and the weight transfers to the next hanger, which may not hold it.

Strap, rod and trapeze hangers

Strap hangers are bands of sheet metal wrapped under the duct and fastened at the sides. They suit small and medium ducts. Threaded rod hangers drop from the structure to an angle, channel or cradle under the duct; the rod is adjustable, so a run can be leveled. A trapeze is a horizontal cross member (angle or strut channel) carried by two rods, with the duct sitting on it. Trapezes suit wide or heavy ducts, and they let two or more parallel ducts share one support. The diagram below shows hanger spacing on a run and a trapeze in cross section.

Spacing for metal duct

We could not open the paid text of the International Mechanical Code (Chapter 6, Duct Systems) or the International Residential Code (Chapter 16, Duct Systems) on the code publisher's site, so we do not quote those sections. A state building code we did read, Massachusetts 780 CMR 6601.3.2, says metal ducts are supported by 0.5 inch wide, 18 gage metal straps or 12 gage galvanized wire at intervals not exceeding 10 feet, or by other approved means. It says nonmetallic ducts follow the maker's installation instructions. Larger and heavier rectangular ducts often need closer spacing or trapezes under the sheet metal industry duct construction standard, which gives hanger tables by duct size and pressure class (named here in plain text, not linked). Use the project specification for those. Pressure classes are explained in Commercial Duct Pressure Classes.

Flex duct support and sag

The Building America Solution Center, a DOE-funded guide, quotes the Air Diffusion Council flexible duct installation standard (5th edition): supports no more than 4 feet apart, or the maker's closer interval; sag no more than 1/2 inch per foot between supports; straps at least 1.5 inches wide, preferably wider; vertical runs supported at least every 6 feet. It also calls for support at each flex-to-rigid connection, before and after sharp bends, and at boxes and plenums. The page's own figure caption says 5 feet, which conflicts with its 4 foot text. The North Carolina Office of State Fire Marshal mechanical page says about 5 feet for horizontal runs, 1/2 inch per foot sag and 1.5 inch straps. If sources differ, use the tighter number.

Worked example

Assumptions (our own): a straight horizontal metal duct 60 feet long, hangers at both ends of each span, and the 10 foot maximum from the state code above.

  1. Spans: 60 ft / 10 ft = 6 spans.
  2. Hangers: spans + 1 = 7 (one at each end of the run and one between spans).

Now a flex run. Assumptions (our own): 20 feet of straight horizontal flex, hangers at both ends, 4 foot maximum spacing.

  1. Spans: 20 / 4 = 5 spans. Hangers: 5 + 1 = 6.
  2. Allowed sag per span: 4 ft x 1/2 in per ft = 2 in.
  3. At the 5 foot spacing in the NC page: 20 / 5 = 4 spans, 5 hangers, 5 x 1/2 = 2.5 in allowed sag.

The 4 foot case needs one more hanger and holds sag to 2 inches. Add hangers at bends and fittings; those are extra to this count.

Attach to structure, not to other trades

Hang from the building structure: beams, joists, slab inserts or other approved anchors. Do not hang ducts from other pipes, conduit, the ceiling grid or its wires. Those are not sized for the load, and another trade may move them. The Building America page lists support points as part of good practice; the load path is our own reasoning, and the engineer's drawings decide anchor type. See Supporting Refrigerant and Copper Piping for how pipe hangers are handled.

Insulated duct and saddles

Hanger material that digs into insulation compresses it. The Solution Center says straps narrower than 1.5 inches should be upgraded with sheet metal saddles, and that saddle supports should exceed 5.5 inches along the duct. On externally wrapped metal duct, a saddle or insulation shield under the strap keeps the wrap from being crushed. Seismic bracing is a separate design task; see Seismic and Wind Restraints.

Signs of sagging ductwork

  • Bellied bottom or a visible dip between hangers.
  • Slipped, cracked or peeling tape and mastic at joints.
  • Drumming or oil-canning panels.
  • Kinked flex, crushed insulation or wet spots under straps.
  • Hangers bent, rods leaning, or anchors pulling from the slab.

Field card: Duct Hangers and Supports

Hang from structure, keep spans short, protect insulation.

  • Metal duct: state code example allows up to 10 ft between supports; check the adopted code and project specification.
  • Flex: 4 ft maximum (ADC via BASC), sag no more than 1/2 in per ft, straps 1.5 in or wider.
  • Hangers = spans + 1 for a straight run.
  • Never hang from pipes, conduit or ceiling grid.
