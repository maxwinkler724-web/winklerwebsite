House Feels Humid With the AC Running: Causes and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The thermostat says 74 and the air still feels sticky. That usually means the air conditioner is cooling the air but not pulling enough water out of it. This lesson explains how an AC removes moisture, the common reasons it falls short, what you can try, and what a technician measures.

How an AC removes moisture

An air conditioner does two jobs at once. It lowers the air temperature, which is the sensible part, and it removes water vapor, which is the latent part. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America program points out that the cooling system is usually the only dehumidifier in a home. Our psychrometrics lesson explains the physics: air that touches a surface colder than its dew point leaves water on it. The indoor coil is that surface. The water runs into the drain pan and out the condensate line, covered in our condensate drain lesson.

Two details matter for everything below. First, moisture only comes out while the coil is cold and air is moving over it. Green Building Advisor notes that once the thermostat is satisfied, the system shuts off and stops removing moisture, and air temperature can drop faster than humidity does. Second, the coil's share of the work is fixed by the equipment. The sensible heat ratio (SHR) is the portion of the cooling that goes to temperature rather than moisture. Green Building Advisor calls 0.75 typical, and Building Science Corporation gives 0.7 as 70 percent sensible and 30 percent latent.

Why the house stays humid

The AC is too big and short-cycles

The Building America "Why to Rightsize" guide says an oversized system reaches the temperature target quickly and shuts off without removing much moisture, and that short runs leave no time for condensate to drain off the coil, so it can evaporate back into the air. It also calls latent capacity the key to right-sizing. The house feels cool and clammy at once. The cure is a proper load calculation, not a thermostat change; see Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J.

The fan is set to ON instead of AUTO

With AUTO, the blower stops with the compressor. With ON, it keeps moving house air across a wet coil after cooling stops. A 1987 Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC) paper measured the effect: in a 20-minute cycle with 10 minutes of compressor run time, about 19 percent of the moisture condensed was re-evaporated into the home, and in a 10-minute cycle with 5 minutes of run time, moisture removal fell by 44 percent compared with AUTO. A later FSEC report says this part-load loss is worst when the fan runs continuously while the coil cycles. If you set the fan to ON for comfort and the air feels clammy, try AUTO first.

Airflow is too high

Moving air faster across the coil keeps the coil warmer, so less water condenses. Green Building Advisor says slowing the fan makes the coil colder and helps remove more moisture, while warning that going too far can cause condensation in the ductwork or a frozen coil. Building Science Corporation shows a default near 530 cfm per ton and says about 350 improves moisture removal. This is a setting for a technician, not a homeowner.

Leaky ducts and attic air

ENERGY STAR says 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through a duct system is lost to leaks, holes and poor connections, and lists ducts in an attic, crawlspace or garage as a sign of poor performance. For moisture, the key is where a return leak draws air from. An FSEC study of small commercial buildings found that leaks from a hot, humid attic had large effects on indoor humidity while leaks from conditioned space had negligible effect. That study was not on houses, but the mechanism is the same. Our ductwork lesson covers leak points, and the two-story house lesson covers the attic side.

Outdoor air and the outdoor dew point

Building Science Corporation calls summer outdoor air a moisture load. Its figure: 300 cfm of outdoor air at 80 F and 60 percent RH adds about 132 pints of water a day, while a typical residential dehumidifier removes 30 to 50 pints a day. Mechanical ventilation is still needed for air quality; see Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics and ERV vs. HRV, which notes that neither device dehumidifies the house by itself.

What a homeowner can try

  • Buy a cheap hygrometer and read the room, not the thermostat. The EPA says indoor relative humidity should stay below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent.
  • Set the fan to AUTO and see if the air feels drier after a few days.
  • Use bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans, which remove moisture at the source (see the indoor air quality lesson).
  • Keep the filter clean and the return and supply grilles clear. A clogged filter hurts performance and can freeze the coil; see frozen evaporator coil.
  • Do not drop the thermostat far below your usual setting to chase dryness.
  • Ask a contractor for a load calculation and duct leakage test if the problem started with a new, larger system.

When a dehumidifier helps

If humidity stays high even when the AC is sized and set correctly, or in spring and fall when cooling rarely runs, a dehumidifier does the job the AC is not doing. Green Building Advisor says a whole-house unit can lower humidity when the AC is not running and costs much less to operate than central AC. ENERGY STAR says certified dehumidifiers remove the same amount of moisture as comparable models while using 20 percent less energy, suggests whole-home models for humid climates with central air and relatively well-sealed homes whose humidity stays above 55 percent, and gives 30 to 50 percent as the target. In winter the opposite problem appears; see whole-house humidifiers.

Worked example: why 80 F air at 50 percent RH sweats a coil

Numbers are from the sources named. Step 1: the National Weather Service gives a temperature of 80 F and a dew point of 60 F as 50 percent relative humidity. So moisture starts to condense on any surface below 60 F. Step 2: Building Science Corporation says coil discharge air is near 95 percent RH and that to get air with a 50 F dew point, the coil needs to be about 50 F or colder. A coil near 45 F to 50 F is well below 60 F, so it condenses water as air passes.

Step 3: the oversizing penalty. Building Science Corporation describes 1.5-ton (18,000 Btu/hr) units serving peak loads of about 7,000 to 9,000 Btu/hr. Take 8,000. Run fraction at peak is 8,000 / 18,000 = 0.44, about 44 percent of the time. At SHR 0.70, latent is 18,000 x 0.30 = 5,400 Btu/hr and sensible is 18,000 x 0.70 = 12,600 Btu/hr while it runs. The unit meets the sensible load by running only part of the time, and each short cycle starts with a warm, dry coil and ends with water left on it. That is why a correctly sized unit that runs longer does a better job on moisture. The 44 percent figure is this lesson's arithmetic from the cited numbers, not a measured value.

For the tech: what to check

  • Sensible heat ratio. Compare the equipment's SHR to the building. Building Science Corporation says envelope improvements cut sensible loads while latent load from ventilation and people stays the same, which creates a sensible-latent mismatch. A lower-SHR or variable-capacity system, or added dehumidification, may be needed.
  • Airflow per ton. Read the maker's manual. Trane's air handler installation instructions for its Integrated Dehumidification Solution recommend a cooling airflow setting of 400 cfm per ton or lower for humid climates, call 350 to 370 cfm per ton optimal, and say the thermostat can vary the blower from 35 to 100 percent airflow through a PWM signal. Measure airflow and static pressure first; see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Dehumidify terminal and wiring. A trade blog explains that many fan coils and furnaces have a dehumidification terminal (D, DH or similar) and that when it is de-energized the blower typically drops to 80 percent of full speed.
  • Coil temperature vs. dew point. Building Science Corporation says an 18 to 20 F split between return and supply indicates good dehumidification. Check the refrigerant side; see Superheat and Subcooling Basics.
  • Limits. Cutting airflow too far can freeze the coil or sweat the ducts. Stay within the manufacturer's allowed range and recheck.
  • Run time and return leaks. Look at cycle length, the condensate drain, and any return duct passing through an attic or other humid space.

Field card: House Feels Humid With the AC Running: Causes and Fixes

Cool air is not dry air.

  • The coil removes water only while it is cold and air is moving over it.
  • Oversized systems short-cycle: temperature is met, moisture is not.
  • Fan ON can re-evaporate water off the coil; try AUTO.
  • High airflow keeps the coil warmer and removes less moisture.
  • Leaks from a humid attic and outdoor air add moisture load.
  • Target indoor RH: below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent (EPA).
  • Tech: check SHR, cfm per ton against the manual, split, and coil temperature vs. dew point.
  • A dehumidifier covers what the AC cannot.
