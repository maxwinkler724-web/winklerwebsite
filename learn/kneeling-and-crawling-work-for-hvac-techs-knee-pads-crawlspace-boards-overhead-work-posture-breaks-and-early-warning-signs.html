Kneeling and Crawling Work for HVAC Techs: Knee Pads, Crawlspace Boards, Overhead Work Posture, Breaks and Early Warning Signs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A lot of service work happens at floor level or over your head: a furnace in a crawlspace, a coil in an attic, a drain pan under a unit. The strain builds slowly, one kneeling stretch at a time. This lesson covers what government and university research says about kneeling and the knee, how pads, boards and creepers help, how to break up the work, how to handle overhead work, and which early signs to report.

What the research says about kneeling

The evidence is real but not simple. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis prepared for the World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization pooled studies of ergonomic exposures such as kneeling, squatting, lifting and climbing. It found higher odds of knee or hip osteoarthritis in exposed workers (an odds ratio of 2.20 from three studies and 1,354 people), but it rated the quality of the evidence as low, partly because the studies relied on self-reported exposure. The studies also used different cutoffs, from about one hour to two hours a day, and the knee results mix kneeling with other exposures. We read this as a reason to take knee strain seriously, not as a number that tells you how many minutes is safe.

Older NIOSH work shows a different kind of damage. A NIOSH survey of floor layers and tile setters found that past skin infections around the knees were about four times as common as in a comparison group of bricklayers and millers, and kneecap skin was more often thickened and red. The authors asked for good knee pads that protect well and stay light and comfortable. A NIOSH-supported roofing study with nine volunteers on an adjustable slope found that dynamic kneeling while installing shingles likely raises the load on the knees, and that roof slope changed which joint motions were highest. Small lab samples like these show direction, not exact risk.

OSHA's electrical contractor tool lists kneeling and crouching among common construction ergonomic hazards: it can reduce circulation and injure the knees through contact with the floor. A university fact sheet adapted from a NIOSH publication adds that kneeling on hard surfaces can cause knee discomfort and osteoarthritis, and that sustained stooping puts high compression forces on the spine.

Knee pads and kneeling mats

NIOSH-authored research tested five pad types and a no-pad condition with eleven healthy men doing tile work. Pad design significantly changed how force was spread across the knee, and one gel design was preferred. The paper's conclusion is the useful part: pick a pad that suits the task, and combine it with a more neutral posture. The extreme reaches produced the highest knee forces. For us, that means moving closer to the work, not stretching from a fixed kneeling spot.

  • Pads you wear go with you into tight spots and stay put as you shuffle. Check that straps do not cut off circulation behind the knee.
  • A kneeling mat is a pad you carry and set down. It helps on concrete and framing but is easy to leave behind in a tight space.
  • Debris still matters. Pads do not make a nail or a screw point safe. Sweep or look first.

Crawlspace boards and creepers

The university fact sheet describes a creeper as a padded platform on low casters. You sit or kneel on it, so you do not have to squat or stoop for detail work. The sheet says the padding lowers the force on the knees compared with a hard surface, casters let you move while staying near the work, and a chest support on some models reduces the load on the spine during long stretches. It also admits creepers are sometimes impractical and says knee pads are the alternative. Crawlspaces fit that: a lot of them are only a couple of feet high.

  • A plain sheet of plywood or a plastic board gives a clean, firm surface over rough soil or a vapor barrier. Use it to slide, not to leave sharp edges poking out.
  • Keep wheels away from wires, hoses and open drains, and do not roll over damp, loose vapor barrier.
  • Test any board or creeper on level ground first, because casters on a slope move on their own.

Before you go in, read Confined Spaces in HVAC Work for entry and air checks, and Pests in Attics, Crawlspaces and Rooftop Units for what you may find on the floor.

Rotating tasks and taking breaks

NIOSH's ergonomics program pages group job rotation, scheduling more breaks and varying tasks under administrative controls. NIOSH also says engineering controls, such as changing tools and materials, are among the most effective ways to reduce these hazards, while administrative controls depend on people following them and can be less effective. So the order is: first change the work (boards, better tools, bringing parts to the bench), then use rotation and breaks to cover what is left.

On a service call this can be plain: do the crawling or kneeling step, stand or sit up to read the meter or write the notes, then go back. Plan the sequence so the most awkward step is not the last one before a long drive. A NIOSH page on overhead work also lists scheduled breaks in its work rules.

Our own worked example: a day of three calls

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not data from a study. Assume three calls and these minutes spent kneeling or crawling on each: an attic call, 50 minutes; a crawlspace call, 40 minutes; an under-unit call, 30 minutes. Assume the tech now stays down for the whole job each time. Then add a rule we chose for the arithmetic: stand, sit up or change position for 2 minutes after at most 15 minutes of kneeling. The 15 and the 2 are our assumptions, not safe limits from any source.

  1. Total kneeling: 50 + 40 + 30 = 120 minutes. The splitting rule does not change this.
  2. Longest unbroken stretch before: the attic call, 50 minutes.
  3. Attic call split: 50 = 15 + 15 + 15 + 5, so 4 stretches and 3 breaks.
  4. Crawlspace call split: 40 = 15 + 15 + 10, so 3 stretches and 2 breaks.
  5. Under-unit call split: 30 = 15 + 15, so 2 stretches and 1 break.
  6. Longest unbroken stretch after: 15 minutes, down from 50. That is 50 ÷ 15 = 3.3 times shorter.
  7. Added time if breaks are pure rest: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 breaks × 2 minutes = 12 minutes, which is 12 ÷ 120 = 10 percent of the kneeling time. If you use the pauses for standing tasks such as writing readings, the added time is smaller.

Pads and boards change the load on the knee, not the length of a stretch, so they stack on top of the split. The point of the example is the structure: the same 120 minutes can be done as nine shorter stretches instead of three long ones.

Overhead work posture

NIOSH's page on overhead work says arms raised above the shoulders strain the shoulders and neck, especially when work is long, repeated, forceful, or done with a twisted body. It names the combination of frequent work with the shoulders raised 60 degrees or more, repetition and force as increasing shoulder risk, and frequent neck flexion of 15 degrees or more with repetition and force as increasing neck risk. It also lists bursitis, tendinitis, rotator cuff tears and neck problems among the injuries.

Its controls fit our trade: reduce the time spent overhead, use a lift or platform to get close to the work instead of reaching, use bit extensions so a drill is held at waist or shoulder height, and set breaks. In an attic, that can mean a stable work platform across joists so the work sits at chest height, a light that lets you see without craning, and parts staged close by. For ladders and platforms see Ladder and Roof Fall Safety. Attics are also hot, which can make strain worse; see Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs. Cold stiffens muscles too: Cold Stress for HVAC Techs. NIOSH names cold temperatures as one of its ergonomic risk factors. Heavy parts add load to the back and knees; the team-lift and carry advice is in Moving Heavy HVAC Equipment Safely.

Early warning signs to report

NIOSH says that if employees report symptoms early, employers can take corrective measures to delay the development of work-related musculoskeletal disorders, and that jobs causing undue fatigue, stress or discomfort deserve follow-up. OSHA likewise recommends that employers encourage early reporting, since early reports can speed up job fixes and may keep symptoms from getting worse.

  • Knee, back, neck or shoulder discomfort that shows up regularly at the same point in a job, or lingers after you stop.
  • Swelling, redness or a thickened, sore spot over a kneecap.
  • Stiffness that makes it harder to get up out of a crawlspace than it was before.
  • Pain, numbness or tingling that returns, or that wakes you at night.

We are not a clinician and this is not medical advice. Report symptoms to your supervisor or safety lead and see a clinician. Do not wait until you cannot work.

Field card: Kneeling and Crawling Work

Change the work first. Pads and breaks cover the rest.

  • Pads change how force spreads on the knee. Pick one that suits the task.
  • Use a board or creeper where height allows. Keep the work near and the back fairly upright.
  • Split long kneeling stretches. In our example, 50 minutes became 15-minute pieces.
  • Overhead: get close with a platform or lift, light the work, take breaks.
  • Report symptoms early and see a clinician.
