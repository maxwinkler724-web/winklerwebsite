Furniture, Rugs and Curtains Blocking Vents and Returns: Clearances and Airflow Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A sofa pushed against a wall, a rug laid over a floor register, a curtain hanging in front of a radiator: none of these looks like an HVAC problem. But a duct system only works when air can leave the supply registers and return through the return grilles. This lesson explains what happens when furnishings get in the way, and gives simple clearance habits and checks you can do without tools.

Why blocked returns starve the system

The blower can only push out as much air as it can pull in. A Southern state university extension service explains that the return side withdraws room air and delivers it to the air handler, and that the supply side delivers conditioned air through the room registers. When something covers a return grille, the blower has less opening to pull through, so the airflow across the heating or cooling coil drops. We covered the details in Not Enough Return Air: Signs, Causes and Fixes and the sizing side in Sizing Return Air and Filter Grilles. Here we only look at what a household can do.

The same extension publication says that anything that interferes with air circulation makes the system less efficient and can lead to problems. The federal energy department contractor-to-homeowner HVAC guide makes the same point for heat pump owners: make sure vents and registers are not blocked by furniture, carpets or other items that can stop airflow.

How blocked supplies make rooms uneven

A supply register is the end of one branch of the duct system. Cover it and that room gets less air than the designer intended, while the other branches get slightly more. The covered room drifts hot in summer and cold in winter, and thermostat, which sits in a different room, does not notice. This is the same pattern we describe in Upstairs Too Hot, and it is why closing registers on purpose is not a free fix (see Does Closing Vents in Unused Rooms Save Energy?). A blocked register acts like a half-closed one, and the federal energy department guide warns that closing vents in unused rooms can reduce airflow through the air handler and cause pressure imbalances.

Throw matters too. A supply register is meant to push air out into the room. A couch face two inches from a wall register sends the air straight into upholstery, so the room air barely mixes and one corner stays different from the rest.

Clearance rules of thumb

The sources we read say to keep supplies and returns unblocked but do not give distances. The numbers below are our own rules of thumb, chosen to be easy to remember. They are not a code requirement and not from a maker or an agency.

  • Our own rule, returns: leave at least 12 inches of open floor or wall space in front of a return grille, and never lean boxes, stacked laundry or storage bins against it.
  • Our own rule, wall and ceiling supplies: keep at least 6 inches of clear space in front, so the air can leave the register freely. High wall supplies are rarely blocked by furniture but often by tall shelves or a bed headboard.
  • Our own rule, floor supplies: keep furniture legs and skirts clear of the opening, or move the piece. If a bed or couch must sit over a floor register, use a vented cover rather than blocking it, and ask a technician whether that room needs a different layout.
  • Our own rule, hand test: if you can feel strong air on your palm at the register but weak air a foot out, something nearby is blocking or redirecting the flow.

Rugs over floor registers and deflectors

A rug laid over a floor register stops the air at the floor. The register may look fine but delivers almost nothing, and a heavy rug can also trap warm air and heat the rug and floor. Cut the rug back or pull it clear so the whole opening is exposed. Do not cut into a register boot yourself. Moving the rug is enough.

Register deflectors, the clear plastic shields that snap on to a register, can redirect air away from a couch or under a bed. Use them with care. They add resistance, so one deflector on a weak branch may make that room get even less air. Use them to point air toward the middle of the room, not to cover half the opening, and do not stack them. A single room where a deflector helps and the rest still feels uneven is a duct question for a technician, as we explain in Register Booster Fans and Smart Vents.

Curtains over baseboard heat and radiators

Hot-water baseboard and radiators depend on air flowing up through the unit and out the top. A curtain that hangs to the floor in front of them traps the heat behind the fabric and against the window. A Midwestern state university extension heating maintenance page tells homeowners not to block air inlets and outlets, including radiators, with furniture, drapes or clothing, and says metallic paints and casings built around radiators can reduce heat transfer as much as 25 percent. We discuss covers further in Radiator Covers and Enclosures.

Our own habit: hang curtains so the hem ends above the top of the unit, with a gap of a few inches at the bottom, or tuck them behind a hook so they cannot swing in front. For electric baseboard heaters, check the maker's clearance label for how far fabric must stay from the unit, and follow it over any rule of ours.

Keep returns clear of stored items

Return grilles on hallway walls and in closets often turn into storage spots. Coat piles, boxes and laundry baskets can cover most of the grille without anyone noticing. If a return sits inside a closet, keep the louvers or door gap open and keep stored items well away from it. The federal energy department guide also says some contractors use door undercuts to give a room a return path, and that undercuts may not provide adequate airflow. That is another reason to keep a room's return path open.

Check airflow by hand or with a ribbon

These checks are our own practice and need no tools. They show where air is moving, not how many cubic feet per minute.

  1. Run the fan on a typical day. Hold your palm a few inches from each supply register. Strong air means flow is good; weak or no air in a room with a register that is open is worth noting.
  2. At a return grille, hold a strip of tissue or a light ribbon near the face. It should be pulled firmly against the grille. If it flutters weakly, check the filter and anything stacked in front.
  3. Move one piece of furniture away and test again. If the ribbon pulls harder, you have found the cause.
  4. Note which rooms stay uneven after clearing everything. That list helps a technician, who can measure static pressure and airflow at each register, as in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Worked example (our own): sofa against a return grille

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement.

Assumptions: a wall return grille 20 inches wide by 20 inches tall. Its bottom edge is 10 inches above the floor. A sofa with a 22-inch-tall back sits tight to the wall and is wider than the grille. We ignore the thickness of the grille bars.

  1. Grille face area: 20 in x 20 in = 400 square inches.
  2. Sofa back top is 22 in above the floor. The grille bottom is 10 in above the floor. Covered height: 22 - 10 = 12 in.
  3. Covered area: 20 in x 12 in = 240 square inches.
  4. Open area: 400 - 240 = 160 square inches.
  5. Share open: 160 / 400 = 0.40, or 40 percent. Share blocked: 60 percent.

What it means: only the top 8 inches of the grille can pull air, and it must draw that air over the sofa back. That is a rough guide to how much of the opening is lost. Real airflow does not drop in exact proportion, because air speeds up through what is left, which raises noise and resistance, and air also leaks in from the sides and gap behind the sofa. If we pull the sofa 12 inches off the wall in this example, the whole 400 square inches is exposed again.

Field card: Furniture, Rugs and Curtains Blocking Vents and Returns

Air has to get out of the supplies and back to the returns.

  • A blocked return starves the blower; a blocked supply leaves that room off balance.
  • Our own rule: 12 inches clear in front of returns, 6 inches in front of supplies.
  • Do not lay rugs over floor registers; a deflector adds resistance, so use only one.
  • Curtains should stop above radiators and baseboard; follow the maker's label for electric units.
  • Check by hand at supplies and with a tissue or ribbon at returns.
  • Rooms still uneven after clearing everything need a technician.
