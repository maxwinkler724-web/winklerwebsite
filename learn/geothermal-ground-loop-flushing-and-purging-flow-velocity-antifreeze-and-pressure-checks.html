Geothermal Ground Loop Flushing and Purging: Velocity, Antifreeze and Pressure Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A buried ground loop is a sealed pipe circuit, and anything left inside it, air or dirt, works against the heat pump for years. Before a closed loop goes into service it is flushed, purged, filled with the right fluid and pressurized. This lesson covers how that is done and how the same numbers help you spot a loop problem later.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. Numbers below come from specific installation manuals read in 2026. Different makers set different values, so the manual for the unit in front of you wins. This is general education, not an installation procedure.

For the loop types themselves, see Geothermal (Ground-Source) Heat Pump Basics. The U.S. Department of Energy says most closed-loop systems circulate water or a water and glycol blend through high-density plastic tubing. That fluid has to be clean, full of liquid and at the right strength.

Why flush and purge

Air and debris get in during pipe fusing, header work and the trench. A Bosch ground-source manual says all air and foreign material must be expelled from the earth coupled piping before operation, and it warns that air left in the loop can corrode the unit's coaxial heat exchanger. A ClimateMaster residential manual says all air and debris must be removed from the earth loop. For general air removal ideas in a boiler loop, see Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents.

The flush cart method

A flush cart is a portable pump, reservoir and filter set that is hooked to the loop's supply and return. The ClimateMaster manual says a flush cart with at least a 1.5 hp pump is needed to reach adequate velocity, and that the cart uses a filter in the return line to catch debris. Its general steps, in our words:

  1. Connect the cart to the loop and fill the reservoir with water.
  2. Run the pump so the water moves through every part of the piping at the velocity the maker names.
  3. Flush in both directions. The Bosch manual calls for flushing the loop in both directions at a high volume and high velocity, repeating until no air bubbles show in the return line.
  4. Close the valve into the reservoir to dead-head the pump; a ClimateMaster fluid cooler manual describes the resulting pressure surge forcing out trapped air. We read that manual for a fluid cooler loop, so check your own unit's steps.
  5. Add antifreeze if the design calls for it, then pressurize.

Minimum flushing velocity

Two makers state the same figure. The ClimateMaster residential manual says to flush with a high volume of water at a high velocity, 2 fps in all piping. The Bosch manual says 2 ft/sec. The ClimateMaster manual turns that into flow by pipe size: 4 gpm for 3/4 in, 6 gpm for 1 in, 10 gpm for 1-1/4 in, 13 gpm for 1-1/2 in and 21 gpm for 2 in. It also says that a fluid level drop of 3/8 in or less in the reservoir is the only sign that flushing is complete. Neither manual gives a fixed flushing time.

Antifreeze and checking the freeze point

Where loop water can get cold, the fluid gets antifreeze. The ClimateMaster residential manual lists methanol, propylene glycol and ethanol, and says freeze protection should be kept 15 °F below the lowest expected entering loop temperature. It also says not to antifreeze beyond a +10 °F freeze point. A WaterFurnace manual lists ethylene glycol, propylene glycol, ethanol and methanol, warns against concentrations above 35 percent by weight, and notes a pre-mixed antifreeze could improve reliability. It also gives a pressure drop multiplier for antifreeze (1.270 for 20 percent propylene glycol at 30 °F), so glycol makes the pump work harder. Read the glycol discussion in Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops as well.

Checking strength matters. The ClimateMaster manual calls for a well-mixed sample and a hydrometer. A refractometer is the other common tool. Nu-Calgon's Glycometer instructions say to put a few drops on the prism, wait 20 to 30 seconds, and read the freeze point at the bottom edge of the blue shade. They also say to know which glycol is in the loop, because propylene and ethylene use different scales, and to calibrate on water first (it should read 32 °F).

Pressure after filling

The ClimateMaster residential manual says to pressurize the loop to at least 50 psi and not more than 75 psi, and that 50 to 75 psi at the end of flushing should be sufficient for all seasons. The Bosch manual gives a different view for operation: 5 psig minimum at the pump suction and typical earth loop pressures of 5 to 40 psig. Because makers differ, use the number in your manual.

Pressure drop to check flow

Both the Bosch and ClimateMaster manuals tell you to check flow by the pressure drop across the unit's water heat exchanger and compare it with the maker's data sheet. ClimateMaster says to read the pressure at the water pressure ports and compare to its pressure drop table, and recommends a digital gauge. Bosch says its P/T plugs are for measuring flow, pressure drop and temperature. A reading lower than the table points to low flow. For maker flow guidance, ClimateMaster recommends 2.25 to 3 gpm per ton of cooling capacity.

Worked example: velocity, flow and unit gpm

Part 1. How many gpm does 2 ft/s take in 1-1/4 in IPS SDR 11 polyethylene? The Bosch manual's Table 1 lists this pipe at 7.8 gal per 100 ft. It does not print an inside diameter, so we derive the flow area from that volume. All arithmetic below is Our own.

  1. Volume of 100 ft: 7.8 gal × 231 in³/gal = 1,801.8 in³.
  2. Length: 100 ft = 1,200 in.
  3. Flow area: 1,801.8 ÷ 1,200 = 1.5015 in². Divided by 144 in²/ft² this is 0.010427 sq ft. (Equivalent inside diameter is about 1.38 in.)
  4. gpm = ft/s × area × 448.8 = 2 × 0.010427 × 448.8 = 9.36 gpm.

That is a little under ClimateMaster's table value of 10 gpm for this size. Our own reading: the two agree closely, and the maker's table is the number to use on a job.

Part 2. A heat pump needs a maker's flow of 3 gpm per ton (the top of ClimateMaster's range) for a 4 ton unit: 4 × 3 = 12 gpm. In the same pipe, 12 gpm is 12 ÷ (0.010427 × 448.8) = 2.56 ft/s. So the running flow in that pipe is above the 2 ft/s flushing velocity.

Signs of a loop problem

  • Entering water temperature that is lower than design or near the freeze limit. WaterFurnace lists a closed loop low water coil limit of 15 °F for freeze detection; ClimateMaster lists loop temperatures from 25 to 110 °F.
  • Pressure drop across the heat pump that is below the data sheet value, which suggests low flow, air or a restriction.
  • Loop pressure that keeps falling, which should be traced before the loop is called done.
  • Freeze lockouts. The Bosch manual troubleshooting for loop fluid freezing says to add antifreeze and measure freeze protection.

Multi-unit loops in buildings are covered in Water-Source Heat Pump Loops.

Field card: Geothermal Loop Flush and Purge

Clean, full, strong, pressurized.

  • Flush both directions at the maker's velocity (2 fps is stated by ClimateMaster and Bosch).
  • Use a flush cart pump sized by the maker, with a filter in the return line.
  • Test antifreeze strength with the right tool for the glycol in the loop.
  • Pressurize to the maker's range (ClimateMaster: 50 to 75 psi).
  • Compare pressure drop across the unit to the data sheet for flow.
  • gpm = ft/s × sq ft × 448.8.
