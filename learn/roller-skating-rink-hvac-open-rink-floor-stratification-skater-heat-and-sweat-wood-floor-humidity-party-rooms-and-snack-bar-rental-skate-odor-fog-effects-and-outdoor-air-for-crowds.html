Roller Skating Rink HVAC: Open Floors, Skater Heat, Floor Humidity, Rental Odor, Fog and Crowd Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A roller rink is a big, tall, open room with a polished floor, a crowd that is moving hard, a snack bar, party rooms and a counter full of used skates, often with fog and lights on top. The people load is large and wet, the floor does not like humidity swings, and the smells come from three different places. This lesson walks through each one and ends with our own worked example of a Saturday session.

Check the rules where you work

This is general education from public pages we read in 2026. The mechanical code, the fire authority and the engineer of record decide each job. Model codes and standards are mentioned in plain text only, and the numbers below are examples, not a design.

Why the open floor is hard

The skating floor is one big space under a high roof. Warm, moist air rises from the crowd and collects under the roof deck, so the lights and the roof sit in a warm layer while skaters stand in the cooler air below. For cooling that layering helps a little, because the warm air stays out of reach. For heating it hurts, because heat piles up where nobody is. A Purdue conference paper abstract on warehouse destratification says removing stratification can cut heating fuel cost, that a stratified room is preferred for cooling, and that the fans needed partly cancel the heating savings. A national laboratory guide for small warehouses also recommends destratification fans for high-ceiling spaces. Our lesson on HVLS fans and destratification covers fan clearance and sprinkler interlocks, so we do not repeat them here. Our own caution for a rink: slow air near skaters, and supply that does not blow across the rink at speed, because skaters feel drafts as they move.

How much heat does a skater give off?

The CDC workplace safety institute says the body makes about 1 kilocalorie per kilogram of body weight per hour at rest (1.16 watts), in its review draft of the heat criteria document. In the final 2016 document its example screening estimate for a 70 kg worker doing a moderate workload for a full hour is about 300 kcal/h, or 348.9 W, and 1 kcal/h equals 1.16 W. Skating sits in the same range. Two university activity tables place leisurely roller skating in the moderate band of 3.0 to 6.0 METs and brisk or fast roller skating above 6.0 METs. CDC explains that a MET estimates the oxygen used during activity, with moderate at 3 to 5.9 METs and vigorous at 6.0 or more. Our own bridge between these sources is to treat 1 MET as about 1 kcal per kilogram per hour, the resting figure above, so a 4 MET skater gives off about four times the resting heat. That mapping is our assumption.

The split matters as much as the total. Part of the heat is dry (sensible) and part leaves as evaporating sweat and breath (latent). We did not find a free government or university table that splits skating heat, so the split in our example is our own assumption: more latent than sensible for a working skater. See our lessons on how the body exchanges heat and on trampoline parks, which share this high-activity, sweaty crowd.

Wood and coated floor humidity

Wood swells when it takes on moisture and shrinks when it dries. A University of Massachusetts Amherst building-science article describes new wood flooring delivered at 6 to 9 percent moisture content that swelled and then shrank, leaving gaps when indoor humidity fell. Its advice is to install flooring only after it has equalized to its in-use moisture content and to control indoor humidity. The author recommends indoor relative humidity of 40 to 60 percent and notes that indoor humidity can fall to about 20 percent in cold periods and rise above 75 percent in humid summers. We did not find a free public limit written specifically for rink floors, so follow the floor maker's written humidity range. Our own practical point: a crowd adds a lot of moisture (see the example), a winter heating season dries the room, and a floor sees both. Control the room, not just the floor: keep dehumidification working in summer, use a controlled humidifier only if the floor maker asks for one, and log relative humidity at floor level so a trend shows up before gaps do.

Snack bar and party rooms

These are the small, packed, short-lived spaces. Give the snack bar and party rooms their own control zone or unit, as we describe for bowling centers, so a birthday party does not drag the whole hall to a cold setpoint. The snack bar has cooking heat and odor, so keep it slightly negative to the rink floor, with exhaust and makeup air that match; see our lesson on commercial kitchen hoods and makeup air for the details. The sketch above shows that pressure direction with an arrow into the side room.

Skate rental odor and exhaust

Rental skates are worn by many people, stored warm and often sprayed with disinfectant or deodorizer. The EPA page on volatile organic compounds lists cleansers, disinfectants, air fresheners and aerosol sprays among the sources and advises increasing ventilation when using products that emit them. It also says many such compounds are consistently higher indoors, up to ten times higher, than outdoors. Our own approach: keep rental storage and the counter in a space with dedicated exhaust and a path toward that exhaust, store skates dry, and do not use scented products to mask odor. If staff spray a product, ask what the label says about ventilation.

Fog, haze and lighting effects

Rinks run lights, mirror balls and fog machines. Our own list: lighting adds heat that sits near the roof, fog and haze can trip smoke detectors. Ask the fire alarm provider and the fire authority how effects and detectors must be coordinated. Our lessons on escape rooms and haunted attractions and on bars and nightclubs cover fog and dance floors in more detail.

Outdoor air for a crowd

Washington State's adopted code table for required outdoor ventilation air, as posted on the state legislature website when we read it, lists a gym, stadium or arena play area at 20 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 7 people per 1,000 square feet. It lists spectator areas at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, disco and dance floors at 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, bowling alley seating at 10 cfm per person plus 0.12 cfm per square foot, and dining rooms and bars at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot. A roller rink is not listed by name, so the designer and the code official choose the closest row. Note the default density: a busy rink holds far more skaters per 1,000 square feet than 7, so the table's default would under-count the crowd.

The CDC workplace safety institute's ventilation FAQ says carbon dioxide monitoring can give information on ventilation and can show a change in occupancy, but that a wall monitor may not represent the occupied space, readings can be artificially low when air moves over the sensor, and rooms are rarely well mixed. It offers readings below 800 ppm as one possible target, not for every space, and suggests recording a baseline for each room and looking into readings above 110 percent of baseline when airflow and occupancy are unchanged. In a rink, mount sensors at breathing height away from diffusers and fans, compare them with a calibrated handheld meter, and let CO2 trim outdoor air between a minimum and the crowd peak.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Everything below is our own arithmetic on assumptions we call out. Do not size equipment from it, and it covers the people load only, not the roof, lights, fans or outdoor air coil load.

Assumptions. A Saturday session holds 220 people: 150 skating on an 8,000 ft² floor and 70 off the floor (benches, snack bar, party rooms, rental counter) in 2,500 ft². Skaters average 4 METs, which we map to 4 kcal per kg per hour. Average skater mass is 55 kg, a mix of children and adults. Heat split for skaters: 40 percent sensible, 60 percent latent. People off the floor average 1.5 METs at 60 kg, split 60 percent sensible and 40 percent latent. Conversions: 1 kcal/h = 1.16 W (CDC), 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h, 1 ton = 12,000 Btu/h, latent heat of water about 1,050 Btu/lb, 8.34 lb per gallon.

  • Skater: 4 x 55 = 220 kcal/h. 220 x 1.16 = 255 W. 255 x 3.412 = about 871 Btu/h. Sensible 0.40 x 871 = 348. Latent 0.60 x 871 = 523 Btu/h.
  • 150 skaters: sensible 150 x 348 = 52,200. Latent 150 x 523 = 78,450. Total 130,650 Btu/h.
  • Off-floor person: 1.5 x 60 = 90 kcal/h. 90 x 1.16 x 3.412 = about 356 Btu/h. Sensible 0.60 x 356 = 214. Latent 0.40 x 356 = 142 Btu/h.
  • 70 off-floor people: sensible 70 x 214 = 14,980. Latent 70 x 142 = 9,940. Total 24,920 Btu/h.
  • Crowd total: sensible 52,200 + 14,980 = 67,180 Btu/h. Latent 78,450 + 9,940 = 88,390 Btu/h. Total 155,570 Btu/h, which is 155,570 / 12,000 = about 13.0 tons.
  • Sensible heat ratio: 67,180 / 155,570 = 0.43. A plain cooling coil often leaves latent heat behind at a ratio this low, so a dehumidification plan matters; see our lesson on sensible heat ratio.
  • Moisture: 88,390 / 1,050 = about 84 lb/h of water, or 84 / 8.34 = about 10 gallons per hour.
  • Outdoor air with the table above, using the actual crowd and treating the floor as a play area: 150 x 20 + 8,000 x 0.18 = 3,000 + 1,440 = 4,440 cfm. Off the floor at the dining and bar row: 70 x 7.5 + 2,500 x 0.18 = 525 + 450 = 975 cfm. Total about 5,415 cfm.
  • Variation: if every skater skates briskly at 6 METs, one skater is 6 x 55 x 1.16 x 3.412 = about 1,306 Btu/h, so 150 skaters give 195,900 Btu/h. With the same off-floor people the total is 220,820 Btu/h, about 18.4 tons, and the latent part is 0.60 x 195,900 + 9,940 = 127,480 Btu/h, about 121 lb/h of water. A faster crowd adds about 42 percent to the load and about 44 percent to the moisture.

Two takeaways. The crowd alone is a large cooling and dehumidification load that disappears when the session ends, so the controls need to follow the crowd, with CO2 trim and an unoccupied mode. And the water the crowd adds, about 10 gallons an hour in our example, is exactly what a wood floor sees if the dehumidification does not keep up.

Field card: Roller Skating Rink HVAC

Count the skaters, then count the sweat.

  • Warm air collects under the roof; mix it gently and keep drafts off skaters.
  • Roller skating is moderate to vigorous activity (3 to 6 METs or more), so a skater gives off several times resting heat, much of it as moisture.
  • Wood floors move with humidity; follow the floor maker's range and log floor-level relative humidity.
  • Snack bar, party rooms and rental counter get their own control and exhaust, kept slightly negative to the floor.
  • Do not mask rental odor with scented products; ventilate.
  • Fog and haze can trip smoke detectors; coordinate with the fire authority.
  • Use the code table row the code official accepts, count real skaters, and trim with CO2 sensors placed away from air streams.
