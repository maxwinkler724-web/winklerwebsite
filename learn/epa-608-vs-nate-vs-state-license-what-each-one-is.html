EPA 608 vs NATE vs a State License: What Each One Is

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Job ads and shop talk toss around "608," "NATE" and "licensed" as if they meant the same thing. They do not. One is federal law, one is a voluntary industry credential, and one comes from your state. This lesson sorts them out so you know which is which when you read an ad or plan your first years.

Check the issuing organization

Check the issuer. Rules, exam lengths and fees change. This page was built from official pages read on 2026-10-16 and is general education. Confirm details with the organization that issues each credential. Trade Study Bench does not offer certification or licensing, and nothing here counts toward any of them.

Three different kinds of credential

EPA Section 608: the federal one

EPA says technicians who maintain, service, repair or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerant into the atmosphere must be certified. It lists four types (I, II, III and Universal), says tests must be given by an EPA-approved certifying organization, and states that Section 608 credentials do not expire. Apprentices are exempt only while a certified technician closely and continually supervises them. The exam sections and study tips are in EPA 608 Certification Basics, so they are not repeated here. The key point for this lesson: 608 is about one subject, handling refrigerant, and the law requires it.

NATE: the voluntary industry one

NATE stands for North American Technician Excellence. Its own pages call it a nonprofit certification organization for HVACR technicians, founded in 1997. Nothing on the NATE pages we read says it is required by law, and it is not a government agency. Its Core and specialty exams cover broader technical topics than refrigerant rules alone.

Entry level. NATE lists a Ready-to-Work credential for technicians with under two years of experience. Per NATE, that exam is 50 questions, is not proctored, and is taken through the SkillCat app.

Core plus specialty. For the traditional pathway, NATE says you take two exams: a 50-question Core exam (general knowledge, safety, tools, basic electrical) and a 100-question specialty exam. The specialty decides whether your credential is in Installation, Service or Senior. Listed specialties include air conditioning, air distribution, air-to-air heat pump, gas heating, oil heating, hydronics gas, hydronics oil and commercial refrigeration. NATE recommends about two years of experience first. A score of 70 percent or higher passes.

Renewal. NATE credentials must be renewed every two years. NATE's recertification FAQ offers two routes: complete 16 continuing education hours (CEHs) in the two-year period and pay a renewal fee, or retake the specialty exam. NATE sets a grace period after the expiration date; once it is over, you must start over. Different NATE pages quote different fees and grace lengths, so check the current numbers on NATE's site before you plan.

Other voluntary credentials, briefly

NATE is not the only one. HVAC Excellence, run through ESCO Group, offers Employment Ready exams aimed at students and entry-level technicians (70 percent to pass) and Professional Level exams for people with two years of verified field experience who pass its Core exam first. These are also voluntary. The pages we read did not state renewal rules for them, so check with the issuer.

State and local licenses

A license is permission from a government to do certain work or run a certain business. It is the only kind of credential here that can be required just to work legally in a place, and rules differ by state. In New Jersey there is a master HVACR contractor license, boiler and refrigeration plant operator licenses such as the Black Seal, plus electrical, plumbing and home improvement rules. We keep those details in one place instead of repeating them: see NJ Trade Licenses: Who Needs Which, NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers and NJ Black Seal Basics. If you are outside New Jersey, ask your state licensing board.

Worked example: decoding a job ad

A sample ad (made up for this lesson) says: "Service tech wanted. 608 Universal. NATE preferred. NJ Black Seal a plus." Read it one line at a time.

  • "608 Universal." This is the gate. Universal means Core plus Types I, II and III, so you may work on any equipment type. It is federal, it does not expire, so treat it as required.
  • "NATE preferred." Voluntary. The employer is saying it would like to see it, not that it is a must. For a service tech, a Service specialty such as air conditioning or gas heating is the likely fit.
  • "NJ Black Seal a plus." A state operator license from the Department of Labor bureau. It points to boiler work at the shop. It is not the HVACR contractor license and it is not a substitute for 608.

Now the arithmetic on NATE, using the numbers above. The two exams total 50 + 100 = 150 questions. At 70 percent to pass, that is 0.70 × 50 = 35 correct on Core and 0.70 × 100 = 70 correct on the specialty. Keeping it alive: 16 CEHs over 2 years is 24 months, and 16 ÷ 24 ≈ 0.67 hours a month, or about 40 minutes. A little each month beats a scramble in month 23.

Which one first?

Get 608 first. It is the legal requirement and it does not expire. Learn the trade on the job, then consider NATE once you have the experience it recommends. Look into state licensing when your role needs it. For the bigger career picture, see How to Become an HVAC Technician.

Field card: EPA 608 vs NATE vs a State License

Law, industry, state: three different questions.

  • EPA 608: federal, required to handle refrigerant, does not expire.
  • NATE: voluntary nonprofit credential; Core (50 questions) plus specialty (100); renew every 2 years with 16 CEHs or a retest.
  • HVAC Excellence: another voluntary option; check the issuer for renewal.
  • State license: permission to do regulated work; rules vary by state.
  • In a job ad, sort each line: is it the law, a voluntary credential, or a state license?
  • Confirm every detail with the issuer.
