Reading Your Electric Bill for Heat Pump and AC Use: kWh, Baselines and Strip Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An electric bill is a monthly total of kilowatt-hours for the whole house. Your heat pump or air conditioner is only part of that total, but with a simple baseline you can pull out a rough number for it, and even guess how many hours the backup strip heat ran. This lesson shows the arithmetic with made-up data and says plainly where the method stops working. We use kWh only and quote no prices.

Two units that get mixed up: kW and kWh

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) explains that electricity is measured in watts, and that one kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts. A kilowatt is a rate, how fast something uses energy right now. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the amount of energy used when one kilowatt runs for one hour, and it is the unit utilities typically bill in. The relation is simple:

kWh = kW × hours

EIA's own example is a 40 W (0.04 kW) bulb on for 5 hours: 40 W × 5 h = 200 Wh = 0.2 kWh. Heating gear is the same math with bigger numbers. EIA also lists 1 kWh as 3,412 Btu, which is handy because a resistance heater turns each kWh it uses into about that much heat.

Think of kW as the speedometer and kWh as the odometer. Your bill shows the odometer. Your nameplate, or a clamp meter, shows the speedometer.

Start with the billing period

Look at the dates before you look at the total. A bill covers a billing period, which is rarely a calendar month, and the period can be 28 days one time and 33 days the next. Some bills are also estimated when a meter is not read, and the next bill corrects the estimate. Two habits help:

  • Divide by days. Our own example: a 33-day bill of 1,500 kWh is 1,500 ÷ 33 = 45.5 kWh per day, while a 28-day bill of 1,320 kWh is 1,320 ÷ 28 = 47.1 kWh per day. The longer bill is bigger, but the shorter one was the heavier-use period.
  • Check for the word estimated. If a bill is marked as an estimate, do not compare it to its neighbors until a real read follows.

Some bills also list a peak kW figure alongside the kWh. Whether yours does depends on the utility, so read the bill's own explanation of its lines. This lesson only uses the kWh line.

Find a baseline from the mildest months

In spring and fall, many homes need little heating or cooling. Those bills are mostly lights, refrigerator, electronics, cooking, laundry and hot water, which we call the baseline. A practical way to find it:

  1. Pick the two or three lowest-use months of the last twelve.
  2. Average them. That average is your monthly baseline.
  3. For any other month, subtract the baseline from the bill. What is left is the extra, which is mostly heating or cooling.

This is an estimate, not a measurement. It treats the baseline as flat all year, and it is not (see the limits below), but it is good enough to compare winter against summer and to tell whether a heat pump season looks normal for the house.

Turn extra kWh into run hours

Once you have extra kWh for a month, you can flip the equation: hours = kWh ÷ kW. You need a kW figure for the equipment, and there are two ways to get one.

  • Nameplate. The outdoor unit's data plate gives voltage and amps, and heaters often show a kW rating. The amps printed are limits and rated values, not what the unit pulls every hour, so a nameplate-based kW is a ceiling-type guess. A strip heater's kW rating is a good number to use, because resistance heat draws close to its rating when it is on.
  • Measured. A clamp meter reading of running amps, or a power meter that reads watts, gives a better figure. For single-phase equipment, volts × amps gives volt-amps, and real watts are somewhat lower, by the power factor. Our own assumption for a quick estimate: 11 A × 240 V = 2,640 VA, and with an assumed power factor of 0.9 that is about 2,376 W, or 2.4 kW.

Variable-speed equipment changes its draw by the hour, so one reading gives only one point. Treat the answer as a ballpark, and take readings at a couple of outdoor temperatures if you can. For safe electrical measurements, see Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Backup strip heat shows up as the big jump

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) says a properly installed air-source heat pump can deliver up to three times as much heat as the electricity it uses, because it moves heat rather than making it. Electric resistance strips do not: each kWh gives about 3,412 Btu, no more. That is why a cold snap during which the strips run a lot can raise a bill sharply even when the heat pump itself is working fine.

Signs of strip heat on a bill: the coldest month is far above the one before it, and the increase is bigger than the compressor alone would explain. To size what the strips could have drawn, use the strip's kW rating from the equipment; see Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips. If bills spike after thermostat setbacks, see Heat Pump Thermostat Setback and Strip Heat.

Smart meter hourly data, if your utility offers it

EIA reports that advanced (smart) meters record usage at least hourly and share it with the utility and the customer at least once a day, and that about 72% of U.S. electric meters were this type in 2022. If your utility gives you access to the hourly numbers, they can show what monthly totals hide. An hourly reading in kWh equals the average kW over that hour.

Our own example, with made-up numbers: a cold morning shows 3.0 kWh in one hour with the compressor running alone, and 11.6 kWh in the next hour. The difference, 11.6 − 3.0 = 8.6 kWh in one hour, means about 8.6 kW more load at once, which is the kind of step a resistance heat stage makes. How and whether you can see this data varies by utility, so check what yours offers.

Step 1, the baseline. The three lowest months are April (690), May (640) and October (650). Their sum is 690 + 640 + 650 = 1,980 kWh, and 1,980 ÷ 3 = 660 kWh per month.

Step 2, the heating-season extra. Take November through March: 840 + 1,300 + 1,480 + 1,320 + 1,000 = 5,940 kWh. Baseline for five months is 5 × 660 = 3,300 kWh. Extra = 5,940 − 3,300 = 2,640 kWh. The same extra from the table: 180 + 640 + 820 + 660 + 340 = 2,640.

Step 3, the cooling-season extra. June through September: 760 + 1,010 + 1,090 + 780 = 3,640 kWh, minus 4 × 660 = 2,640 kWh, leaves 1,000 kWh.

Step 4, check the total. Twelve months add up to 11,560 kWh. Baseline 12 × 660 = 7,920, plus 2,640 heating, plus 1,000 cooling = 11,560. It reconciles.

Step 5, cooling hours. August is 1,090 − 660 = 430 kWh above baseline. Assume a measured 2.4 kW for the whole cooling system. Hours = 430 ÷ 2.4 = about 179 hours. August has 31 × 24 = 744 hours, so that is 179 ÷ 744, about 24 percent of the month.

Step 6, strip heat hours. January is 1,480 − 660 = 820 kWh above baseline. Assume the heat pump alone, without strips, would have used about 1,100 kWh in total that month (our assumption, in practice you would estimate it from a milder month at a similar temperature). Then the part left for the strips is 1,480 − 1,100 = 380 kWh. With a stated 10 kW strip, running with all of it on, hours = 380 ÷ 10 = 38 hours, about 5 percent of January's 744 hours. If the strip only ran at half power, 5 kW, the same 380 kWh means 380 ÷ 5 = 76 hours. The heat pump's own extra is 1,100 − 660 = 440 kWh, and 440 + 380 = 820, which matches. Those 380 kWh also equal about 380 × 3,412 = 1.3 million Btu of resistance heat.

What this method cannot tell you

  • Baseline is not flat. Heat pump water heaters, dryers, pool pumps, electric vehicle charging, holiday lights and houseguests all move the baseline. Cold inlet water makes water heating use more in winter.
  • Weather differs. A January that was 5 degrees colder than last January will use more with no fault. Compare like weather, or look at per-day numbers.
  • Idle draws count. Crankcase and base-pan heaters, control boards and blowers draw power when no one calls for heat or cooling.
  • Net bills can mislead. If the home has solar, the bill may show only the net of what was used and what was sent out.
  • The strip estimate is only as good as the guess for the heat pump alone. A different assumption changes the hours a lot. Treat it as a flag for a conversation with a technician, not a diagnosis.
  • A bill cannot find a fault. A leaking refrigerant charge, dirty coil or bad sensor adds kWh, but a bill cannot tell you which.

For gas-heated homes, the same idea works with a different unit; see Reading Your Gas Bill. If your totals look high and you want a checklist, go to Why Is My Heating or Cooling Bill So High?.

Field card: Reading Your Electric Bill for Heat Pump and AC Use

kWh is the odometer, kW is the speedometer.

  • kWh = kW × hours, so hours = kWh ÷ kW.
  • Check the billing period dates first and divide by days to compare bills.
  • Baseline = average of the 2 or 3 lowest months; extra = bill minus baseline.
  • Nameplate kW is a ceiling; a measured reading is better; strip kW is close to its rating when on.
  • Strip heat shows up as the big jump in the coldest month.
  • Hourly smart meter kWh equals average kW for that hour, if your utility shares it.
  • Limits: other loads, weather, idle draws and solar all blur the estimate.
