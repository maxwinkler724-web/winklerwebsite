Storing Firewood Indoors and Near the House: Moisture, Insects, Clearances

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pile of logs by the wood stove looks cozy, but a big indoor stack can fog windows, carry insects and mold into the house, and sit too close to heat. This lesson covers where firewood belongs, how much to bring inside, and why dry wood burns cleaner.

Wet wood puts water in your air

Freshly cut wood is full of water. A university extension fact sheet on heating with firewood says green wood can hold as much as 50 percent moisture by weight, and that seasoning takes six to nine months. A federal wood handbook notes that green wood can be anywhere from about 30 to over 200 percent moisture content, because the figure compares water to the oven-dry wood. As wood warms indoors, that water leaves as vapor. In a tight winter house the vapor has nowhere to go, so it raises indoor humidity and can show up as fog or water on cold window glass. See Condensation on Windows in Winter and the similar case of drying clothes indoors. A university extension article on heating with wood adds that poorly stored wood holds moisture and invites fungi and insects, and that rotting logs bring fungus into a pile.

Our own worked example: how much water is that?

This is our own example, with assumed inputs. We assume 100 pounds of split wood at 30 percent moisture content on a dry basis, drying indoors to 20 percent. Dry basis means water mass divided by oven-dry wood mass, the definition in the federal wood handbook. The water does not change the dry wood mass.

  1. Wet mass = dry mass x (1 + 0.30), so dry mass = 100 / 1.30 = 76.9 pounds.
  2. Starting water = 100 - 76.9 = 23.1 pounds (check: 76.9 x 0.30 = 23.1).
  3. At 20 percent, water = 76.9 x 0.20 = 15.4 pounds, so the wood weighs 76.9 + 15.4 = 92.3 pounds.
  4. Water lost = 100 - 92.3 = 7.7 pounds.
  5. In gallons: 7.7 / 8.34 pounds per gallon = 0.92 gallon.

So roughly a gallon of water goes into the room air from one 100-pound load. That is why a stack that dries indoors for weeks matters to humidity. The assumptions are ours: real wood varies by species, and the drying may take days or months.

Insects and mold come in on logs

A university extension entomology publication says to bring in only what you need, at most a couple of days of supply. Long indoor storage lets insects develop in the wood until they emerge in the house, and long-horned beetles can emerge even in mid-winter because rooms are warm. It adds that most firewood pests leave the wood within a few days and that these pests do not attack finished, dried wood inside the home. Wood on the ground for a long time is open to carpenter ants and termites. The same source says to stack off the ground, which increases drying and reduces pest problems.

Mold spores ride on damp and rotting wood. The heating-with-wood article ties damp storage to fungi. Keep logs that look punky, white-fuzzed or rotten out of the house.

A small rack indoors, a big stack outdoors

  • Indoors: keep a small rack with only a day or two of wood, and make it dry, seasoned wood. This follows the entomology advice above.
  • Outdoors, off the ground: the heating-with-wood article suggests pallets or metal tubes under the stack, and another university extension fact sheet also says to elevate the pile and keep air moving through it.
  • Away from the house wall: The entomology publication says not to stack against the house or garage because of moisture or insect problems, and to keep at least 3 feet between wood and the structure. The second fact sheet says to keep several feet away from buildings. The heating-with-wood article says stacking against a building slows drying.
  • Cover the top only: The heating-with-wood article says never fully cover the stack in tarp or plastic. A federal environmental agency page on wood-burning practices likewise says to cover only the top, because covered sides trap moisture.

Clearance from a stove or fireplace

The firewood heating fact sheet advises keeping combustible materials away from the stove or fireplace, using a grate to hold wood, and keeping furniture at least 36 inches from the hearth. It does not give a distance for logs. Required distances depend on the appliance and how it was installed, so use the appliance manual and your local inspector. We do not give a number for log clearance here. A stack against the stove is still a fire risk and also keeps wet wood in the warmest spot. For an unused fireplace, see how an unused fireplace loses heat.

Keep wood away from equipment (our own guidance)

We found no extension source on this, so treat it as our own practical advice and defer to each equipment manual. Do not use a furnace room or water heater closet as a wood closet. Combustion equipment needs open space for air and for service, and a log pile is fuel that can sit close to a flame or hot flue. Wood stacked against an outdoor air conditioner or heat pump unit blocks the airflow it needs and gives pests a sheltered nest. Leave open space around the unit and never lean wood on it. See combustion air in closets and basements. Any wood or gas burning also calls for a working alarm: carbon monoxide alarms.

Why seasoned wood matters for smoke

Several extension sources agree on a target of about 20 percent moisture content or less. A university burning safety page says wetter wood makes more smoke and less heat, and that green wood smoke adds to creosote buildup in the chimney, which is a fire hazard. The firewood heating fact sheet says burning wet wood gives a smoky fire, creosote, low efficiency and a shorter burn. The heating-with-wood article says wet or rotting wood makes extra ash and smoke. The burning safety page says seasoning usually takes at least six to nine months. To check, look for end cracks, listen for a sharp crack when two pieces knock together (a dull thud means wet), or use a wood moisture meter as the federal environmental agency page suggests. Buy or cut wood well ahead, and keep unseasoned and seasoned wood in separate piles.

If you have a basement that stays damp, a dehumidifier may help, but it does not make a wet log safe to store inside.

Field card: Storing Firewood Indoors and Near the House

Season outside, bring in a day or two.

  • Wet wood sheds water: our example, 100 lb at 30% to 20% loses about 7.7 lb (0.92 gal).
  • Moisture content (dry basis) = water mass / oven-dry mass.
  • Indoors: a small rack, a day or two of seasoned wood.
  • Outdoors: off the ground, top covered, sides open, 3 ft or more from the house.
  • Log clearance to a stove: follow the appliance manual.
  • Keep wood out of furnace and water heater spaces and clear of outdoor units (our own advice).
  • Target about 20% or less moisture for less smoke and creosote.
