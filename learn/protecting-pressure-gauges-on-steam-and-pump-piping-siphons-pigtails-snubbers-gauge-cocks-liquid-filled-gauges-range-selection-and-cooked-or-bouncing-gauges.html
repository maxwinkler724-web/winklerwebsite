Protecting Pressure Gauges on Steam and Pump Piping

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pressure gauge is only useful if it survives the pipe it is screwed into. Live steam cooks a Bourdon tube, and a pulsing pump discharge shakes it to pieces. This lesson covers the small parts that protect a gauge: siphons, snubbers, isolation valves, liquid fill and a sensible dial range.

What is inside the gauge

A common dial gauge has a curved, flattened metal tube (a Bourdon tube) that tries to straighten as pressure rises. A linkage turns that small movement into needle travel. Two things hurt it: heat, which weakens the tube and the soft joints, and rapid pressure swings, which wear the linkage. Every protective part below deals with one of those two problems. For reading the dial and checking it against a test gauge, see the boiler gauge lesson; we do not repeat that here.

Siphons (pigtails) on steam

A siphon is a loop of small pipe, coiled like a pigtail or bent into a U, placed between the steam line and the gauge. Steam entering the loop cools and condenses, and the water collects in the bend. That water leg is the barrier: the gauge senses pressure through the water, and steam never reaches the tube. A federal regulation for locomotive boilers states the rule plainly: the steam gauge supply pipe shall have a siphon of ample capacity to prevent steam from entering the gauge, and the supply pipe must connect directly to the boiler and stay steam tight (49 CFR 230.43). A state university's mechanical specification calls for a pigtail siphon with a ball-type isolation valve at steam gauges, and another university spec asks for a siphon fitting and valve at each steam gauge.

Our own plain-language reasoning follows. A siphon is not a fix for a gauge that sits too close to a hot surface, and it only works if the water leg exists. A dry siphon passes steam straight through.

Fill a new siphon before startup

Our practice, not a quoted rule: when a new or dried-out siphon goes on, fill the loop with water before the gauge goes live, so the first rush of steam meets a water leg instead of the Bourdon tube. Fill it with the gauge off the line, then mount the gauge and open the isolation valve slowly. On a system already under pressure, open the valve in small steps and let the condensate form. If the line is hot enough that you cannot touch the loop, wait. A loop that has been warm for a while is full of hot water and can scald.

Snubbers and restrictor screws on pump discharge

A pump discharge pulses, especially a reciprocating pump or a centrifugal pump that is cavitating or running against a partly closed valve. The needle then flutters, and the linkage wears out. A snubber is a small fitting at the gauge inlet that narrows the passage so pressure changes arrive slowly. One university specification describes a brass snubber with a piston-type surge-dampening device. Another describes a brass bushing with a porous metal disc. Some gauges instead use a small adjustable restrictor screw in the inlet; the idea is the same, a tight opening in front of the tube. These are our description of the common arrangement; follow the gauge maker's instructions for adjustment.

The one university spec describes the steam and non-steam rule neatly: for fluids other than steam, install a valve and a snubber for each gauge; for steam, install a valve and a siphon fitting. A snubber is not needed on steam if a siphon is present, and a snubber that clogs can make a gauge read slowly or stick at an old value, so a sluggish needle is a reason to blow out or replace it.

Gauge cocks and needle valves

Every gauge connection deserves its own shutoff. A small valve lets a tech replace or test the gauge without draining the system. One university spec calls for a pressure gauge in a piping tee with a pressure gauge cock, and steam gauges with a pigtail and a shutoff valve rated for the temperature and pressure. Another asks for a throttle valve for each gauge so the same valve does both the snubbing and the isolation job. Practical habits:

  • Close the valve before removing a gauge, then bleed the trapped pressure slowly through the loose fitting. Wear eye protection.
  • Open the valve gradually after reinstalling, so the tube does not take a shock.
  • Do not leave a gauge valve cracked just a little for years; a throttled gauge hides real pressure changes.

Liquid-filled gauges for vibration

A gauge case filled with a damping liquid steadies the needle and lubricates the movement, which suits pumps, compressors and fans. One university spec calls for liquid-filled, direct-mounted, metal-case gauges at pump suction and discharge, and another asks for a water-filled gauge across pump supply and return lines to compare pressure. Liquid fill smooths vibration; it does not protect against steam heat. Steam still needs the siphon.

Choosing a range

Pick a dial so the normal reading lands in the middle of the scale. A university spec states gauge accuracy as a percentage of the middle half of the scale range, which is one reason to aim there: accuracy is specified in that band, and the needle has room to travel both ways. A federal regulation also requires a steam gauge to be accurate across the range up to 25 percent above the allowed boiler pressure (49 CFR 230.45), so a dial that ends close to the setpoint leaves little margin.

Our own worked example

This is our own example with stated assumptions. Assume a low-pressure steam header that normally runs at 12 psig, with a relief setting of 15 psig, and a pump discharge that normally runs at 45 psig with a shutoff pressure of about 60 psig. Assume stock gauge ranges of 0-15, 0-30, 0-60, 0-100 and 0-160 psi. The middle half of a dial is from 25 percent to 75 percent of full scale.

Steam header, 12 psig:

  • 0-15: 12 / 15 = 0.80, which is 80 percent, outside the middle half and too close to the end.
  • 0-30: 12 / 30 = 0.40, which is 40 percent, inside the middle half. The 15 psig relief point reads 15 / 30 = 50 percent, still on the dial with room to spare. Choose 0-30.
  • 0-60: 12 / 60 = 0.20, which is 20 percent, too low to read well.

Pump discharge, 45 psig:

  • 0-60: 45 / 60 = 0.75, which sits on the edge, and the 60 psig shutoff pressure would peg the needle.
  • 0-100: 45 / 100 = 0.45, which is 45 percent, inside the middle half. Shutoff at 60 / 100 = 60 percent, still on the dial. Choose 0-100.
  • 0-160: 45 / 160 = 0.28, in range but crowded toward the low end, so small changes are hard to see.

Result: a 0-30 psi gauge with a siphon for the steam header, and a 0-100 psi liquid-filled gauge with a snubber for the pump. The 25 to 75 percent rule is our own working guideline, not a code requirement.

Signs of a cooked or bouncing gauge

These are our field observations, so confirm against a test gauge before you decide:

  • Cooked: the needle rests off zero with the gauge isolated and vented, a discolored or cracked dial or lens, a soft or sticky needle, or a gauge that reads far from a known-good test gauge. Steam probably reached the tube through a dry siphon or a missing one.
  • Bouncing: a needle that flutters several psi, a loose or wobbling pointer, a dial that wanders when the pump starts. Check the snubber, the liquid fill, cavitation and the pump discharge valve.
  • Dead or slow: the needle does not move when the load changes, which points to a plugged siphon, snubber or valve. Close the valve and clear it.

For testing a gauge against a reference and checking zero, see checking HVAC meters and gauges in the field. A federal workplace rule for air receivers also asks that the indicating gauge be readily visible (29 CFR 1910.169), a reminder to mount gauges where people can read them.

Field card: Protecting Pressure Gauges on Steam and Pump Piping

Steam gets a siphon, pumps get a snubber, every gauge gets a valve.

  • Siphon: a loop or coil whose water leg keeps steam off the gauge tube.
  • Fill a new siphon with water before the gauge goes live (our practice).
  • Pump discharge: snubber or restrictor, and a liquid-filled case for vibration.
  • Shutoff valve at each gauge; open slowly after a swap.
  • Range: aim for normal pressure in the middle half of the dial (our guideline).
  • Examples: 12 psig steam on 0-30 psi (40 percent); 45 psig pump on 0-100 psi (45 percent).
  • Cooked: off zero, discolored, far from test gauge. Bouncing: fluttering needle, check snubber and cavitation.
