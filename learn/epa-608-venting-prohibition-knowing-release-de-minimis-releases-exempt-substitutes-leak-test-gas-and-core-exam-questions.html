EPA Section 608 Venting Prohibition: Knowing Release, De Minimis Releases, Exempt Substitutes and Core Exam Questions

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The venting prohibition is the rule most people mean when they say "you can't just let it out." This lesson reads the actual text of 40 CFR 82.154, explains what "knowingly" is doing in that sentence, lists the exempt substitutes exactly as the rule lists them, sorts small releases from violations, and shows how the topic appears on the Core exam. It is study material, not legal advice.

Certification basics, record keeping and leak-rate math are covered elsewhere, so we link them instead of repeating them: EPA 608 Basics, 608 Records: What Technicians Keep, and Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances.

What the rule says

The prohibition sits in 40 CFR 82.154(a), in the eCFR. Paragraph (a)(1) is addressed to anyone "maintaining, servicing, repairing, or disposing of" an appliance or industrial process refrigeration. It bars that person from knowingly venting or otherwise releasing refrigerant into the environment, with a list of exempt substitutes carved out. EPA's venting page (last updated December 8, 2025) states the idea in plain terms: the ban covers ozone-depleting substances and their substitutes while equipment is serviced, repaired or disposed of.

Two features matter for the exam and for the field.

  • It is about service and disposal work. EPA's page separates releases during service from emissions during normal equipment operation, such as leaks. Leaks are handled by the leak repair rules, not the venting ban. See the leak-rate lesson linked above.
  • It covers substitutes too. Paragraph (a)(3) says the knowing release of a Class I or Class II refrigerant "or a non-exempt substitute refrigerant" after it has been recovered from an appliance is a violation. So HFC refrigerants are in, unless the rule lists them as exempt.

What "knowingly" means for a technician

The paragraph (a)(1) wording is "knowingly vent or otherwise release into the environment." The rule text we read does not define the word, so we do not offer a legal definition. For study purposes, think of it as a choice: opening a valve to atmosphere, cutting into a charged line, or discharging a cylinder to get rid of it are deliberate releases. Paragraph (a)(3) leaves no gray area on one point: once refrigerant has been recovered, letting it go afterward is the violation it names. We give no penalty amounts here. EPA's Core test topics page says penalty questions are on the exam but does not state a figure on that page, so check EPA directly for the current numbers.

De minimis releases

Paragraph (a)(2) says "de minimis releases associated with good faith attempts to recycle or recover refrigerants" are not subject to the prohibition. The key is the condition attached to it, which the rule gives two ways:

  • (a)(2)(i): for anything other than the exempt substitutes, the release must happen while the required practices are followed: 82.155 and 82.156, and 82.157 for Class I or Class II appliances. Recovery or recycling machines meeting 82.158 are used when refrigerant is removed, and the technician certification (82.161) and reclamation (82.164) requirements are observed.
  • (a)(2)(ii): alternatively, the requirements of subpart B of part 82 are observed.

EPA's venting page gives one example: small amounts released when connecting or disconnecting service hoses. The page does not mention hose purging or leak-test mixtures, and neither do 82.154 or 82.156 in the text we read. 82.156(a) is the practice behind the exemption: it requires you to "evacuate the refrigerant, including all the liquid refrigerant" to the Table 1 levels with a certified machine and to verify that level before the appliance or part is opened, with limited exceptions. Think of de minimis as the unavoidable crumbs left after doing that correctly, not as a quantity you may release.

The exempt substitutes, as 82.154(a)(1) lists them

These are exempt from the venting prohibition, only as described. The list below paraphrases the rule's paragraphs (i) through (x).

  1. (i) Carbon dioxide in any application.
  2. (ii) Nitrogen in any application.
  3. (iii) Water in any application.
  4. (iv) Ammonia in commercial or industrial process refrigeration or in absorption units.
  5. (v) Chlorine in industrial process refrigeration (processing of chlorine and chlorine compounds).
  6. (vi) Hydrocarbons in industrial process refrigeration (processing of hydrocarbons).
  7. (vii) Ethane (R-170) in very low temperature refrigeration equipment and equipment for non-mechanical heat transfer.
  8. (viii) Propane (R-290) in listed uses: stand-alone retail food refrigerators and freezers, household refrigerators and freezers, residential and light commercial room air conditioners and heat pumps, vending machines, self-contained commercial ice machines, very low temperature equipment and water coolers; the entry also adds retail food refrigeration for refrigerated food processing and dispensing equipment, effective July 15, 2024.
  9. (ix) Isobutane (R-600a) in stand-alone retail food refrigerators and freezers, household refrigerators and freezers, and vending machines.
  10. (x) R-441A in stand-alone retail food refrigerators and freezers, household refrigerators and freezers, self-contained residential and light commercial room air conditioners, heat pumps and vending machines.

The pattern to learn: three are exempt anywhere (CO2, nitrogen, water), and the rest are tied to a use. A hydrocarbon that is exempt in a household refrigerator is not automatically exempt in another kind of equipment. Because the list changes by rulemaking, confirm it against the current eCFR text before you rely on it; this page was built from the section as it stood in 2026 (its citation history ends with 89 FR 50463, June 13, 2024).

Worked example: three field scenarios (our own)

Our own example, not from EPA. Assumptions: the technician is certified for the appliance type, uses a certified recovery machine, and the appliance contains a non-exempt HFC refrigerant. This is our reading of the rule text to practice reasoning, not legal advice or an EPA ruling.

  1. Purging hoses with low-loss fittings. After recovery to the required level, the tech closes the fitting valves and disconnects. A puff of vapor escapes from the hose. Reasoning: this is a small release tied to a good-faith recovery done with the 82.156 practices followed, which fits (a)(2)(i). EPA's page names hose connection and disconnection as the de minimis example. The rule text we read does not mention low-loss fittings by name, so the fittings are good practice, not the legal test. Result: de minimis, allowed. Pulling a hose and purposely discharging a full hose to atmosphere is a different act.
  2. Nitrogen and trace refrigerant leak test. A tech adds a small amount of refrigerant to nitrogen to find a leak, then vents the whole mix to atmosphere. Reasoning: nitrogen is exempt under (a)(1)(ii). The refrigerant in the mix is still a refrigerant. The 82.154 text we read has no carve-out for leak-test mixtures, and (a)(2) is limited to releases connected with good-faith recycling or recovery. Our conservative reading: treat it as not allowed. Recover the mix or use nitrogen with no refrigerant in it. EPA's venting page does not address this, so ask EPA or your instructor if a job depends on it. For other test gases, see trace gas leak detection.
  3. Cutting a line with charge still in it. To save time, the tech cuts the suction line on an operating HFC system without recovering first. Reasoning: 82.156(a) requires evacuating to the Table 1 levels and verifying before the appliance is opened. Skipping it means the 82.154(a)(2)(i) practices were not followed, so the release is not de minimis, and it is a deliberate release under (a)(1). Result: violation.

The decision in one picture

How it shows up on the Core exam

EPA's Core test topics page (last updated December 5, 2025) lists "the Clean Air Act prohibition on venting" under Section 608 regulations, and under the Clean Air Act and Montreal Protocol heading it lists venting bans that apply during servicing, at disposal and to substitute refrigerants. It does not mention de minimis releases. Expect questions like these: venting is prohibited during service and disposal; the ban covers substitutes as well as CFCs, HCFCs; recovered refrigerant may not be released afterward; and recovery comes before opening the system. Take the Core practice test after this lesson.

Five practice-style questions

  1. Which three substances does 82.154(a)(1) exempt in any application?
  2. A tech recovers R-410A, then opens the cylinder valve to let the rest of the refrigerant go. Which paragraph names that act, and is it allowed?
  3. True or false: small releases during good-faith recovery are always de minimis, regardless of the practices followed.
  4. Propane (R-290) is an exempt substitute. Does that mean venting it from any equipment is allowed?
  5. What does 82.156(a) require before an appliance or part is opened?

Answers

  1. Carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water.
  2. Paragraph (a)(3): knowingly releasing a Class I or Class II refrigerant or a non-exempt substitute after it has been recovered is a violation. Not allowed.
  3. False. For non-exempt refrigerants, (a)(2)(i) ties the exemption to following the required practices and equipment rules (or, under (a)(2)(ii), the subpart B requirements).
  4. No. Propane is exempt only in the uses the rule lists, such as stand-alone retail food refrigerators and freezers or household refrigerators and freezers.
  5. Evacuate the refrigerant, including all liquid, to the Table 1 levels using a certified machine, and verify the level, with the limited exceptions in the rule.
