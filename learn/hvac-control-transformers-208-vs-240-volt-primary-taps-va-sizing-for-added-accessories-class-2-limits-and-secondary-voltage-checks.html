HVAC Control Transformers: 208 vs 240 Volt Taps, VA Sizing and Class 2 Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A 24 volt control transformer looks simple, yet it causes some of the strangest calls: a contactor that chatters, a valve that hums, a fuse that blows after an accessory is added. Most of those trace back to two things, the primary tap you picked and the VA you asked of the secondary.

Safety first

The primary side carries line voltage, and the secondary is live whenever the primary is. Follow your site's lockout and verify-dead procedure before you move any lead, use a properly rated meter, and make live readings only if you are trained to. Never bridge a fuse to keep a system running.

What the primary tap does

A transformer's voltage ratio follows its turns ratio. A university set of lecture notes on transformer ratings defines the rated voltage ratio as rated primary voltage over rated secondary voltage, and says it ideally equals the turns ratio. The winding fixes the ratio, so if you feed a primary less than its rated voltage, the secondary comes out lower in the same proportion. A multi-tap control transformer has a common primary lead and several other leads, commonly marked for 120, 208 and 240 volts. Each tap is a different number of turns, so each one is a different ratio. You connect the line to the common lead and to the one tap that matches the supply, and you cap each unused lead separately. Always follow the nameplate and the wiring diagram on the unit in front of you.

Why the wrong tap gives low 24 V

Say a transformer is wired to the 240 volt tap but the building supply is 208 volts, common on three-phase commercial service. The secondary no longer makes 24 volts. As our own arithmetic, an ideal transformer gives 24 x 208 / 240 = 20.8 volts, about 87 percent of 24. A loaded real transformer reads lower still. A contactor coil's pull falls faster than the voltage does. As a simple rule of our own, treat the force as roughly following the square of the voltage: 0.867 squared is about 0.75, so about three quarters of the pull. That is the setup for a coil that hums, chatters and pits its contacts, or one that will not pull in at all when the line also dips. The fix is to move the line lead to the 208 volt tap, then measure again.

Measuring secondary voltage, unloaded and loaded

Measure at the transformer secondary terminals with the control wires disconnected, then again with the load calling. A reading that is high unloaded and sags under load points at an overload or a weak supply. A wrong tap makes both readings low by the same proportion. If the voltage is fine at the transformer but low at the contactor, the problem is wire length or a bad connection; see the voltage-drop lesson. Also measure the primary: a low supply is a utility or feeder issue, and a tap change will not cure it.

VA ratings and adding up loads

The same lecture notes give ratings in volt-amperes, equal to rated voltage times rated current, and warn not to exceed the rated voltage or the rated steady current. For a 24 volt secondary, our own relation is amps = VA / 24. To size one, add the VA of every device that can be on at the same time: contactor coil, gas valve, zone valves, humidifier solenoid, relays, the thermostat and any board. A device label gives its rating; coil data is often printed in amps, so multiply by 24.

Inrush versus sealed VA

A magnetic coil draws much more current while the armature is open than after it closes, because the air gap is large. The closed value is the sealed (holding) VA, and the larger one is the inrush. Add the sealed values for the steady total, then check the moment of pull-in, when inrush stacks on everything already running. A transformer that carries the sealed total but sags at pull-in gives a chattering coil, which looks just like a low-voltage fault.

Worked example (our own numbers)

All VA values below are assumptions of our own for teaching, not data for any product. Read your devices' labels for real numbers. A 40 VA transformer feeds:

  • Contactor coil: 6 VA sealed, assume 18 VA inrush
  • Gas valve: 10 VA
  • Three zone valves: 3 x 7 VA = 21 VA
  • Humidifier solenoid: 5 VA
  • Thermostat and board: 1 VA

Sealed total: 6 + 10 + 21 + 5 + 1 = 43 VA, already over the 40 VA rating. Current at 24 volts: 43 / 24 = 1.8 amps, against 40 / 24 = 1.67 amps for the transformer. At pull-in the peak is 18 + 10 + 21 + 5 + 1 = 55 VA, 38 percent over the rating. A 50 VA unit covers 43 VA but leaves only 14 percent spare. We assume a margin of our own, keeping the sealed load at 80 percent or less of the rating: 43 / 0.8 = 53.8 VA, so we pick the next size up, 75 VA (75 / 24 = 3.1 amps). Then we check the new unit is marked Class 2.

Second part of the example: the supply is 208 volts, and the transformer is left on its 240 volt tap. The secondary is 24 x 208 / 240 = 20.8 volts, so the pull-in problem above gets worse, because the coil is starved just when the inrush pulls the secondary down further. Moving the lead to the 208 volt tap restores about 24 volts unloaded.

Class 2 limits

OSHA's construction electrical rule (29 CFR 1926.408) describes a Class 2 circuit as one where power is limited either inherently, or by a power source combined with overcurrent protection, and caps the voltage at 30 volts AC for the overcurrent-protected type. It also requires a Class 2 power supply to be permanently marked with its class and electrical rating, and Washington's state rule says the same. The VA limits for Class 2 sources come from the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70, Article 725) and its tables, and they depend on the edition your jurisdiction has adopted, so read the current edition instead of trusting a number you remember. In practice a bigger transformer is not always a better answer. A larger unit may no longer be a Class 2 source, which changes the wiring and protection rules for everything it feeds. Check the nameplate for the Class 2 marking before you move up in size, and if one transformer would be too large to stay Class 2, split the loads between two.

Fuses, breakers and phasing two transformers

A secondary fuse or breaker protects the transformer and thin control wire. Replace it only with the rating on the equipment label. A bigger fuse hides a short or an overload and can overheat the winding; see the low-voltage short lesson. When two transformers feed one system, as with a zone panel or a field accessory, do not tie their secondaries together blindly. Our own check: with both energized and the loads disconnected, measure AC volts between one secondary lead of each. About 0 volts means they are in phase and may be joined. About double (near 48 volts) means they are out of phase, so swap the leads of one before joining. When in doubt, keep the secondaries separate, each with its own load and return.

Why adding accessories blows fuses

A humidifier, an extra zone valve, a smart thermostat or a relay all add VA. The old transformer was sized for the original loads, so the new total can exceed it, and the fuse trips at pull-in or after the winding heats. Another cause is an accessory wired to the wrong lead or in the wrong phase. Do the VA sum first, then wire. For damper actuators, see the actuator lesson, and for the contactor itself, Capacitors and Contactors.

Field card: Control Transformer Taps and VA

Match the tap to the supply, then add up the VA.

  • Secondary scales with primary voltage on a fixed tap.
  • 208 V on the 240 V tap: 24 x 208 / 240 = 20.8 V (our own arithmetic).
  • Amps = VA / 24 on a 24 V secondary.
  • Add sealed VA, then check inrush at pull-in.
  • Keep a margin; our assumption is 80 percent of rating.
  • Check the Class 2 marking before changing size.
  • Fuse to the label; never upsize.
