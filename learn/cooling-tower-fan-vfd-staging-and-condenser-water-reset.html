Cooling Tower Fan VFDs, Staging and Condenser Water Reset

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

A chiller uses less energy when its condenser water is cooler, but making water cooler costs tower fan energy. Reset and fan control are about finding the sweet spot in that trade, and doing it without freezing, surging or hunting.

The trade-off in one picture

The chiller has to lift heat from the chilled water up to the condenser water. The colder the condenser water, the smaller the lift and the less compressor work. The tower makes the water cold by moving air, so a faster fan costs more fan power. Total plant energy is the chiller plus the tower, not either alone. An engineering article on plant control warns that poorly done variable-speed condenser-water control can raise total energy by increasing chiller lift.

Condenser water reset

Reset means the condenser-water supply setpoint is not fixed. It slides with a variable such as outdoor wet-bulb temperature or building load. A federal laboratory checklist suggests resetting the supply temperature to within a few degrees of the outdoor wet-bulb, and notes this reduces lift and chilled-water system energy. That is its recommended target in a design-oriented checklist, not a number for your plant. Every plant has limits from the chiller maker (minimum entering condenser-water temperature) and from the tower.

Fan staging and VFDs

Tower cells can have single-speed, two-speed or variable-speed fans. Fan power falls steeply with speed. A facilities article states that a fan at half speed uses about one-eighth the power of one at full speed, which follows the fan affinity laws, and that variable speed also gives steadier temperature control.

The same laboratory checklist describes combining both ideas: variable-speed fans ramp up to about half speed before a second fan starts. A plant-performance article adds a related idea, running as many tower cells as the minimum-flow limits allow, because more wetted surface gives better heat transfer and lets each fan run slower. Manufacturers set the minimum flow per cell, and low-flow accessories can lower it.

Why the sequence still needs limits

A reset sequence must respect:

  • Chiller minimum entering temperature or minimum lift. Too cold can cause problems on some machines. The maker's data gives the limit.
  • Freeze risk. In cold weather, a low setpoint with little load can chill the basin. Basin heaters and bypass valves are protection, not substitutes for sound setpoints.
  • Tower flow limits and fouling. Condenser-side limits can come from the chiller or from tower fouling and freezing concerns.
  • Surge risk. The acceptance-test page below tells testers to watch for chiller surge or rising load in hot weather.

What an acceptance test looks at

California's energy-code acceptance procedure for condenser water reset is a plain example of commissioning thinking. It does not judge whether the sequence is best. It checks that the system follows its own sequence. A construction inspection confirms the documents, the documented sequence, and that outdoor air and water sensors are installed and checked against a reliable reference. The functional test then overrides the reset variable through the building automation system so the setpoint drops, then rises, then returns to automatic. At each step the tester watches whether the water temperature follows within a small tolerance, whether tower fans stage or change speed, and whether the chiller load moves the expected way.

How operators use this

  • Compare the setpoint to the actual condenser-water supply on a trend. A gap that does not close suggests a limit, a bad sensor or a tower that cannot keep up.
  • Watch for fans hunting between speeds. That often points to a tuning or sensor problem rather than a hardware fault.
  • A real problem can hide behind reset: a fouled fill or a stuck bypass valve will show as the supply temperature missing the setpoint. See Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks.

Sensors drive all of it, and a reset sequence is only as good as the readings it gets. A drifting wet-bulb sensor quietly sets the wrong target. See BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits.

Field card: Cooling Tower Fan VFDs, Staging and Condenser Water Reset

Cooler condenser water saves compressor work and costs fan work.

  • Reset slides the condenser-water setpoint with wet-bulb or load.
  • Fan power drops steeply as speed falls, so slower fans on more cells can save energy.
  • Respect the chiller minimums, tower flow limits and freeze protection.
  • An acceptance test checks that the plant follows its own documented sequence.
  • A setpoint the water never reaches is a clue, not a nuisance.
