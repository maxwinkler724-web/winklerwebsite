Vent-Free Gas Room Heaters: Oxygen Depletion Sensors, Room Limits and Moisture

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A vent-free gas room heater has no flue. Everything the flame makes, including a lot of water vapor, goes into the room, and the room is also where the flame gets its air. This lesson covers the one safety device built for that situation, the oxygen depletion sensor, then the room limits, the moisture, the alarms and the yearly checks. For the broader comparison with direct vent and B-vent units, see Gas Fireplaces: Direct Vent, B-Vent and Vent-Free; this page stays on the room heater itself.

Check your own manual and local code first

Your manual and your local code rule. Some areas ban vent-free heaters or restrict them far more than the model code does. This page is general education, built from government and university pages and state code text read in 2026. It is not an inspection and not legal advice.

How an oxygen depletion sensor pilot works

A U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission technical report describes the sensor as a pilot burner and a thermocouple wired to an electromagnetic gas valve. With normal air, about 20.9 percent oxygen, the pilot flame is blue and touches the tip of the thermocouple, and the heat it makes holds the valve open. As oxygen drops, the flame starts to lift off the pilot burner. Below roughly 18 percent oxygen it lifts clear of the thermocouple, the thermocouple cools, and the valve closes.

A second agency report that tested heaters with this device found shutoff at oxygen levels between 18.8 and 19.6 percent in those tests. The design target is about 18 percent, so the sensor is a backstop for a room that has run short of air. It does not tell you the heater is the right size for the room, and it is not a carbon monoxide alarm. The same agency said when it first proposed a sensor requirement that the device would shut off the gas before carbon monoxide could build to a life-threatening level; that was a 1980 statement about a proposal, so treat it as history and not as a guarantee. If a unit keeps shutting off, read it as a warning about the room or about a dirty pilot, and do not bypass or swap the pilot assembly.

Room size and input limits

The model fuel gas code (the IFGC) has a section on unvented room heaters, Section 621; a state rules table of contents that adopts the IFGC lists it by that title. We could not open the body of the model code text, because it is not freely posted. So the figures below come from the residential fuel gas rules a state has adopted, which use the same kind of limits. Check the edition your area enforces.

  • Bedrooms. A single wall-mounted unvented heater with an oxygen depletion sensor and an input of no more than 10,000 Btu/h, in a bedroom that also meets the room volume rule below.
  • Bathrooms. A single wall-mounted unvented heater with the sensor and an input of no more than 6,000 Btu/h, in a bathroom that meets the volume rule.
  • Room volume. The indoor combustion air rule in the same state text asks for 50 cubic feet of room volume for every 1,000 Btu/h of appliance input.

Many states and cities do not allow these exceptions at all, so a manual that says "may be installed" is not permission. The answer for your address comes from your local code office.

What the heater adds to the room

Complete combustion of the methane in natural gas follows CH4 + 2 O2 gives CO2 + 2 H2O. University engineering notes work this out as 2.25 pounds of water per pound of methane, and a university greenhouse fact sheet states it in volume terms: one cubic foot of natural gas makes about two cubic feet of water vapor. The same fact sheet says unvented heater flue gases usually also contain nitrogen dioxide, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds and particles. Water vapor is the largest and most predictable of these. Carbon monoxide rises when something is wrong: a dirty burner, a shortage of air, a bad pilot or a failed sensor. A government agency report put early symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning as flu-like, with headache, dizziness or nausea.

Worked example (our own): 20,000 Btu/h in one room

Our own example with assumed inputs. Assumptions: a 20,000 Btu/h heater input, natural gas at 1,036 Btu per cubic foot (a federal energy agency sample conversion factor), a natural gas density of 0.042 pounds per cubic foot and 2.25 pounds of water per pound of methane (both from the university notes, and approximate for real gas), and a closed room 14 ft by 15 ft with an 8 ft ceiling.

  1. Room volume: 14 x 15 x 8 = 1,680 cubic feet.
  2. Volume rule: 20,000 / 1,000 x 50 = 1,000 cubic feet needed. The room has 1,680, so it passes this test. The most input it supports is 1,680 / 50 x 1,000 = 33,600 Btu/h.
  3. Gas burned: 20,000 / 1,036 = 19.3 cubic feet per hour.
  4. Water vapor by volume: 19.3 x 2 = 38.6 cubic feet per hour.
  5. Water vapor by weight: 19.3 x 0.042 = 0.81 pounds of gas per hour; 0.81 x 2.25 = 1.8 pounds of water per hour. At 8.34 pounds per gallon that is about 0.22 gallon, roughly 28 fluid ounces, every hour. Cross-check: 38.6 cubic feet of vapor at about 0.0475 pounds per cubic foot (our own estimate from the molar weight of water) is also about 1.8 pounds.
  6. Compare with the room: 38.6 / 1,680 = 2.3 percent of the room volume per hour. Air at 68 F can hold about 2.3 percent water vapor by volume when saturated (our own estimate from a vapor pressure of 0.339 psia at 68 F and 14.696 psia total), and about 0.9 percent at 40 percent relative humidity (our own).

That means a sealed 1,680 cubic foot room at 40 percent humidity would be pushed past saturation in well under an hour if nothing left it. Real rooms leak, and walls, drapes and furniture absorb some water, so the real rise is slower, but the numbers show why a heater that passes the volume rule can still fog glass. Over 8 hours the same heater adds about 14 pounds, close to 2 gallons, of water to the house.

Window condensation and humidity

Condensation forms when humid indoor air touches a surface colder than its dew point, and cold window glass is usually first. Federal moisture guidance says condensation can be a sign of high humidity and says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. A vent-free heater running in a closed room can push that up fast. Wet glass, a musty smell or peeling paint nearby means the moisture is not leaving. Cracking a window, running a bath or kitchen exhaust fan elsewhere in the house, or running the heater less are fixes, and a unit that makes the glass run with water is worth a visit from a qualified gas technician. See Condensation on Windows in Winter for the wider picture.

CO alarm advice

The federal product safety agency says CO alarms should be on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas, tested monthly, with batteries replaced yearly in alarms that use them. The agency's 1980 release said most CO deaths tied to these heaters happened while the victim slept. A vent-free heater is one more reason to keep working alarms near the bedrooms. An alarm does not replace the sensor, and the sensor does not replace an alarm. If an alarm sounds or anyone has symptoms, turn the heater off, get everyone into fresh air and call for help. More on that is in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Yearly checks

  • Have the burner, pilot assembly and control area cleaned each year, and more often in a dusty home or with pets. Dust and lint on the pilot can change how the flame behaves. The federal agency's heater advice says to service these heaters regularly to keep the burner and control compartment clean.
  • Look at the flame pattern against your manual's picture. Yellow tips, a lifting pilot or soot are reasons to stop and call a technician.
  • Check for new moisture signs: glass condensation, stains, musty smells.
  • Confirm the room has not changed: a closed door, a new wall or a sealed-up window changes the volume and air supply.
  • Test every CO alarm and replace batteries on schedule.
  • Confirm the heater still has its data plate and an intact sensor assembly. Never replace the pilot with a part that is not made for that model.

Field card: Vent-Free Gas Room Heaters

No flue means the room is the flue.

  • Oxygen depletion sensor: pilot flame lifts off below about 18 percent oxygen, the thermocouple cools and the valve closes.
  • It is a backstop. It is not a CO alarm and not a sizing check.
  • Volume rule in the state text we read: 50 cubic feet of room per 1,000 Btu/h.
  • That text limits bedrooms to 10,000 Btu/h and bathrooms to 6,000 Btu/h, wall-mounted and sensor-equipped. Local code may ban them.
  • About 1 cubic foot of gas makes about 2 cubic feet of water vapor. Our own 20,000 Btu/h example adds about 1.8 pounds of water an hour.
  • Fogged windows mean moisture is staying in.
  • CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas; test monthly.
  • Clean burner and pilot yearly.
