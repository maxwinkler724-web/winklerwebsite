Heat Went Out in Winter: How to Keep Pipes From Freezing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The furnace or boiler quit, it is below freezing outside, and you are wondering about the pipes. Most of the protection is simple and cheap: keep warm air moving past the plumbing, keep water moving through it, and do not trade a frozen-pipe problem for a carbon monoxide problem.

Safety first. Never heat a home with a gas oven or stove, and never run a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal device indoors, in a garage or in a carport. If a CO alarm sounds or you feel sick, dizzy or weak, get outside to fresh air and call 911. Details are in Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

First, get the heat question handled

Pipes freeze because the space around them gets cold, so the best protection is getting the heat back. Run the quick checks in No Heat First Checks (thermostat mode, power, switch, breaker) and call a licensed tech if those do not fix it. While you wait, the steps below buy time. This page does not give a number of hours a house takes to cool, because it depends on the house, the wind and the outdoor temperature, and we did not find a source we trust for a general figure.

Where pipes freeze first

Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, adapting guidance from the American Red Cross and the Institute for Business and Home Safety (IBHS), says pipes are at risk when outdoor temperatures reach 20°F or below. The most exposed ones are outdoor hose bibs, sprinkler and pool lines, and pipes in attics, crawl spaces, basements, garages and exterior walls. The Red Cross adds that supply lines under kitchen and bathroom cabinets are easy to forget. IBHS points to pipes in hard-to-reach places such as attics, crawl spaces and along exterior-facing walls. Those are your first places to think about.

What to do while the heat is out

  • Open cabinet doors. The Red Cross says to open kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors so warm air reaches the plumbing. If you have children or pets, move cleaners and chemicals out of reach first.
  • Let faucets drip. The Red Cross says that in very cold weather you can let cold water drip from faucets served by exposed pipes, and that even a trickle helps.
  • Keep garage doors closed if water lines run through the garage (Red Cross).
  • Thermostat. The Red Cross says to hold the same setting day and night. AgriLife says that if you are away, leave the heat on and set no lower than 55°F. IBHS gives the same 55°F floor, even when no one is home. If you have a heat pump and the weather is extreme, AgriLife notes you may need emergency heat; see Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat.
  • Know your main. AgriLife says to find the main water valve before a freeze. IBHS says that for an absence longer than a week, with subfreezing weather ongoing or forecast, consider shutting off the water by closing the main valve slowly and letting a couple of faucets drain on the lowest floor.

Heating safely when the furnace is down

The CDC says never to heat your house with a gas oven, and never to run a generator inside your home or garage, even with doors and windows open. CPSC says to run portable generators outside only, at least 20 feet from the house, and not on a porch or in a carport. The Red Cross says the same about cooking ovens and stoves. For an electric portable heater, CPSC and the U.S. Fire Administration agree on the basics: keep all sides at least three feet from beds, clothes, curtains and anything that burns, set it on a solid, flat surface, plug it straight into a wall outlet and not a power strip or extension cord, and do not leave it running unattended in a confined space. Check the cord and plug for damage. Keep working CO alarms with battery backup, as covered in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

If your furnace is fuel-burning, CPSC says to clear snow away from the outside vents so exhaust cannot back up into the house.

If a pipe does freeze

A faucet that only trickles is the usual sign. The Red Cross says to keep that faucet open so flowing water helps melt the ice, then apply heat to the pipe: an electric heating pad wrapped around it, a hair dryer, a portable space heater kept away from flammable things, or towels soaked in hot water. Keep going until full pressure returns, and check the other faucets, since one frozen spot often means more.

Never use an open flame. The Red Cross and AgriLife both say not to thaw pipes with a blowtorch, a kerosene or propane heater, a charcoal stove or any open-flame device. AgriLife notes the fire hazard and the lethal carbon monoxide fumes.

Call a licensed plumber if you cannot find the frozen section, cannot reach it, or cannot get it to thaw. AgriLife also says that if a thawed pipe leaks, shut off the main valve until it is repaired. This is the moment to know where it is.

Worked example: a 10-day trip in January

You are leaving for 10 days and the forecast is below freezing all week. Use the sourced numbers above.

  1. IBHS says to consider shutting off the water for an absence longer than a week. 10 days − 7 days = 3 days past that line, so shutting the main (slowly) and draining a couple of faucets on the lowest floor is a good option.
  2. If you instead leave the water on, the heat must stay on. Set 55°F. 55 − 32 = 23°F of cushion above freezing in the rooms you can measure.
  3. AgriLife flags pipes as at risk at 20°F outdoors or below. 32 − 20 = 12°F below freezing outside is the point where exterior-wall and unheated-space pipes need the most help.

The 23°F cushion applies to the room your thermostat sits in, not to the cold wall behind the cabinet. That is why a setpoint alone is not a guarantee.

For techs: what to tell the customer

If you get the no-heat call, you are the one who can stop a second loss. Before you leave, confirm where the main shutoff is, tell the customer to open sink cabinets, and warn them off ovens and indoor generators. On a hot-water system, a frozen heating loop is a different problem from a frozen domestic line: see Hot-Water No-Heat Diagnosis, and for freeze protection in loops, Glycol in Hydronic Loops. Plumbing is a separate trade, so refer thawing and repair to a plumber when it is beyond a simple thaw. Next fall, the pre-cold-snap checklist keeps this call from happening.

Field card: Heat Out, Pipes at Risk

Warm air in, water moving, no flame, no fumes.

  • Open sink cabinet doors; let a trickle run from faucets on exposed pipes.
  • Same thermostat setting day and night; no lower than 55°F when away.
  • Never use a gas oven, stove or indoor generator for heat. Generators 20 feet away, outside.
  • Space heater: 3 feet clear, wall outlet only, never unattended.
  • Thaw with a heating pad, hair dryer or hot towels, faucet open. No open flame.
  • Know your main shutoff. Shut it if a thawed pipe leaks.
  • Cannot find or thaw it: call a licensed plumber.
