Adult Day Center HVAC: Warm-Up, Drafts, Toilet Exhaust and Heat Plans

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An adult day center is a small building with a hard schedule: empty all night, then 40 people who sit for hours arrive at 8 am and expect to be comfortable. This lesson covers what the building has to do before they walk in, and what to do on the day the cooling quits. The home side is in our older adults heat and cold lesson and the 24-hour side in our nursing home lesson.

Check the rule that applies to your building

Adult day licensing is set state by state, and some centers fall under other programs. This page uses New Jersey as the example, was built from public pages read in 2026, and is general education. Confirm the current text with your state health agency and the local code official.

What the state rule says about temperature and air

New Jersey licenses these centers under N.J.A.C. 8:43F, "Standards for Licensure of Adult Day Health Services Facilities." The state Department of Health lists the chapter on its rules page, and a 2021 readoption notice shows it running to November 18, 2028. In the text we read, the participant environment section says the facility temperature must be kept at a minimum of 70 F and a maximum of 85 F when participants are in the facility. It also says all rooms are ventilated to help prevent condensation, mold and noxious odors, that participant areas are free of noxious odors, and that hot water used for bathing and handwashing must not exceed 120 F. We found no cooling-specific or air-change rules in that section. The chapter requires written emergency plans, but the sections we opened do not mention heat or power loss.

That 70 to 85 F band is wide. It is a license limit, not a comfort target.

Seated people, drafts and warmer setpoints

Most of the day is spent in chairs, which means low activity. CDC says older adults adjust less well to sudden temperature changes and are more likely to take medicines that affect temperature control or sweating. A research paper on older adults' comfort notes that sweat output declines with age. ASHRAE's public page for Standard 55 says the standard works from air temperature, radiant temperature, humidity, air speed, activity and clothing, and aims at satisfying a majority of occupants. The page does not single out older occupants, so any adjustment is a judgment call. Our own practical approach:

  • Aim for a setpoint at the warm end of the normal range in cooling season and a bit above the usual office number in heating season, then ask staff how the room feels. Our own judgment, not a code number.
  • Draft is the usual complaint, not the temperature. Check supply diffusers over chairs and tables; redirect or swap to a wider throw pattern. Watch the front door at drop-off time.
  • Keep the quiet room on its own control if you can. It is often the room where someone is resting under a blanket, and the room staff hear complaints from first.
  • Put the sensor on an interior wall at seated height, away from the kitchen pass-through and the door.

Daytime-only schedules: warm-up and cool-down

With no overnight residents, the building can be set back hard at night and on weekends. The catch is recovery. A cold building has to give up its stored cold before the air feels right. Our setpoint scheduling lesson explains optimal start in general, and the halls lesson covers buildings used a few days a week. Here is a worked example for one center.

Worked example: how early must the heat start?

Every input below is an assumption of ours, not a measured value.

  1. Center: 3,000 sq ft, setback 60 F, needed 72 F at 8:00 am. Rise: 72 - 60 = 12 F.
  2. Assumed building thermal mass: 25,000 Btu per F (walls, floor, furnishings, air). Stored heat to add: 12 x 25,000 = 300,000 Btu.
  3. Heating output left over after losses (net, assumed, labeled): 100,000 Btu/h. Time = 300,000 / 100,000 = 3.0 hours.
  4. Start time: 8:00 - 3.0 h = 5:00 am. A fixed 7:00 am clock start gives only 1 hour: 100,000 x 1 = 100,000 Btu, which is 100,000 / 25,000 = 4 F. The room would be about 64 F at 8:00, well below the 72 F we want.
  5. Ventilation: our model has the 100,000 Btu/h net heat covering the building only, so outdoor air is a separate load. Using an assumed 30 F day, 1.08 x 480 cfm (found below) x (72 - 30) = about 21,800 Btu/h would be added if outdoor air ran during warm-up. That is one more reason to keep outdoor air closed until people arrive. See our formulas lesson for the 1.08 factor.

On a mild morning the setback gap is smaller and the time shrinks, which is why an optimal start routine learns the building rather than using one fixed clock time. Cool-down works the same way in reverse.

Outdoor air for 40 people

ASHRAE 62.1 sets outdoor air by adding a people rate and an area rate: Vbz = Rp x people + Ra x area. We read Table 6-1 rows in an ASHRAE addendum to the 2022 edition; the edition your jurisdiction adopted may differ, so confirm the rows with your engineer. The table has a day care row, but that row is "Daycare (through age 4)," a children's space: 10 cfm per person and 0.18 cfm per sq ft. We assumed the "Multiuse assembly (tables and chairs)" row instead, as a stand-in for tables and chairs: 7.5 cfm per person and 0.06 cfm per sq ft. The right row is a decision for the design engineer and code official.

  1. People: 40 x 7.5 = 300 cfm.
  2. Area: 3,000 x 0.06 = 180 cfm.
  3. Total: 300 + 180 = 480 cfm, assuming an air distribution effectiveness of 1.0 so no correction.
  4. Check on density: 40 people in 3,000 sq ft is 13.3 per 1,000 sq ft, far below the table's default 100 per 1,000 for that row, so we used the real count. With the children's row the same math gives 400 + 540 = 940 cfm, nearly double.

Toilet rooms, odors, kitchens and activity rooms

CDC's environmental infection control guidance says air from toilet rooms and other soiled areas is typically exhausted directly outdoors through its own duct system, not recirculated. Even if your center is not a health care building, that is the right habit. The state rule requires ventilation against noxious odors, and toilet rooms are where that rule is tested. Checks on a service call:

  • Confirm the exhaust fan actually runs, the damper opens and the duct ends outdoors. A fan that discharges into a ceiling space is a common find.
  • Check that makeup air has a path. A closed toilet door with no transfer opening starves the fan.
  • Our own rule: the fan runs whenever the center is occupied, not only when someone flips the switch. See our bathroom exhaust lesson for sizing.

The kitchen is usually a warming or serving kitchen; if it has cooking equipment, a hood and makeup air are a separate design and permit question. Activity rooms with exercise add heat from moving people.

When the cooling fails on a hot day

The state limit we read is 85 F while participants are present. Our own plan, written before the heat arrives:

  1. Pick an action point well below the limit. We suggest 80 F. At that reading, staff start the plan, not at 85 F.
  2. Read room temperatures with a thermometer, not only the thermostat.
  3. Close blinds and turn off non-essential lights, equipment and cooking.
  4. Move participants to the coolest room. CDC says not to count on a fan as the main way to cool off when it is very hot, and to spend time in cooled indoor spaces.
  5. Offer water, and watch for the warning signs CDC lists, such as muscle cramps, headache, nausea or vomiting. Staff call for medical care when those appear.
  6. Call the center's service contact early. Contact families and arrange pickup or an alternate location if the temperature cannot be held; that is a staff and clinical decision, not an HVAC one.

For the equipment side, keep a standing list of backup items: a portable cooling option sized to one room, a place to plug it that the circuit can carry, and written shutoff steps. Our cooling center lesson covers temporary equipment and generators.

Field card: Adult Day Center HVAC

Warm the room before they arrive, keep drafts off the chairs, keep the toilets exhausting.

  • NJ rule text we read: 70 F minimum, 85 F maximum when participants are present; confirm your state.
  • Our own example: 12 F rise x 25,000 Btu/F = 300,000 Btu; at 100,000 Btu/h net, 3.0 h, so a 5:00 start for 8:00.
  • A 7:00 clock start gives about 4 F in that example. Assumptions, not measurements.
  • 62.1 example (assumed row): 7.5 x 40 + 0.06 x 3,000 = 480 cfm.
  • Toilet exhaust goes outdoors and needs makeup air.
  • Set an action point (our suggestion: 80 F) before the 85 F limit.
