How Much Water a Home AC Removes Per Day: Pints, Gallons and Drain Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a sticky afternoon a drip may run steadily from the end of your drain line, and that can look alarming. It is usually normal. Your air conditioner is pulling water out of the air, and on a humid day that can add up to several gallons. This lesson turns the humidity part of the cooling load into pints and gallons, explains where the water goes, and lists checks you can do yourself.

Why an air conditioner makes water

The cooling coil inside the air handler or furnace cabinet is much colder than the room air. A U.S. Department of Energy home cooling guide says the cooling coils remove heat and humidity from the air. When air touches a surface colder than its dew point, some of its water vapor turns to liquid and runs off the coil. A state university extension page on home air conditioning describes the same thing: the coil gets coldest, the condensate forms and flows out of the system, and that keeps humidity lower. It also notes that the air conditioner controls temperature directly and humidity only as a side effect.

Two kinds of cooling work

Cooling a house takes two jobs. Lowering the air temperature is the sensible part. Removing water vapor is the latent part, the energy it takes to change vapor back to liquid. The extension page calls the latent load the humidity portion of the cooling load and says a contractor should estimate it accurately. The latent share is not wasted. It is real work the equipment does, and you can count it in water you can see.

The conversion: Btu to pounds to gallons

Two rounded numbers do most of the work.

  • About 1,060 Btu per pound. This is the energy to condense one pound of water vapor, the same energy it takes to evaporate it. Steam tables from the national standards lab give about 2,441 kJ/kg at 25 °C (the difference between vapor enthalpy 2,546.5 and liquid enthalpy 104.83 kJ/kg), which our own conversion puts near 1,050 Btu/lb. The value is a little higher for colder water, so 1,060 is a reasonable round figure for coil conditions.
  • 8.34 pounds per gallon. The same lab table lists liquid water at 997 kg/m³ at 25 °C, which converts to about 8.32 lb per gallon. The common rounded figure is 8.34, and the difference is far smaller than the uncertainty in any home estimate.

So: latent Btu/h ÷ 1,060 = pounds of water per hour. Pounds ÷ 8.34 = gallons. Gallons × 8 = pints, since there are 8 pints in a gallon.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. None of the inputs come from a particular house, so treat the answer as a size of the thing, not a prediction for yours.

Assumptions (ours): a 3-ton split system moving 1,200 cfm; dry air weighing 0.075 lb per cubic foot; coil removing 0.0020 pound of water per pound of dry air on a humid day, and 0.0010 on a mild day; a humid-day runtime of 8 hours and a mild-day runtime of 4 hours.

  1. Air mass: 1,200 cfm × 60 min × 0.075 lb/ft³ = 5,400 lb of dry air per hour.
  2. Humid-day water: 5,400 × 0.0020 = 10.8 lb of water per hour.
  3. Latent load: 10.8 lb/h × 1,060 Btu/lb = 11,448 Btu/h, about 11,400 Btu/h.
  4. Gallons per hour: 10.8 ÷ 8.34 = 1.29 gal/h, which is about 10.4 pints per hour.
  5. Humid day, 8 hours: 1.29 × 8 = about 10.4 gallons, or 83 pints.
  6. Mild day: 5,400 × 0.0010 = 5.4 lb/h. Over 4 hours that is 21.6 lb, and 21.6 ÷ 8.34 = about 2.6 gallons.

The humid day produces about four times the water of the mild day, because the air carries more moisture and the system runs longer. A U.S. Environmental Protection Agency research record reports that an air conditioner may produce three to ten gallons of water per day per 1,000 square feet of conditioned space in hot, humid weather. Our example, scaled to a house of about 1,500 square feet, sits inside that range. That is a check on size only, not proof that your system will match.

Why humid days fill the drain line

The water you collect depends on how much moisture is in the air passing the coil and how long the system runs. On a dry day the coil may barely sweat. On a muggy day, each pound of air gives up more water, and the system runs more hours. Both effects stack. The extension page also warns that an oversized unit cools the house too quickly to remove moisture well. If the system is cycling off before the coil has worked long, you may see less drain water and a clammy house. See House Feels Humid With the AC Running.

Where the water goes

  • Drain pan. The coil drips into a pan under it. Water leaves the pan through a drain connection.
  • Trap. Many installations include a trap, a low U-shaped bend that holds a plug of water so air cannot be pulled through the drain line. The depth and the need depend on the air handler layout. See AC Coil Condensate Traps.
  • Gravity run or pump. If the drain can slope down to a floor drain or the outdoors, gravity does the job. If not, a small condensate pump lifts the water. See Condensate Pump Not Working.
  • Float switch. A small float switch in the pan or the line senses a rising water level and cuts off cooling before the pan overflows. It is a safety device, and if it trips it is telling you the water is not leaving.

Ductless units follow the same path; see Mini Split Leaking Water.

Simple homeowner checks

  • Look for the outlet. On a warm day with the system running, find where the drain line ends outside or at a drain. Some drip or a thin stream is normal on humid days. No water at all on a muggy day with the system running for hours can mean a blockage, or just that the coil is not working as it should; compare with other days.
  • Check for standing water. Water in a secondary pan, a wet floor or ceiling stain below the air handler, or a musty smell near the unit all point to a drain problem. Turn the system off and call a technician.
  • Keep the filter and coil clean. The U.S. Department of Energy guide says to replace or clean filters regularly and to check the evaporator coil yearly. A dirty coil can freeze up and then melt into the pan faster than it can drain. See AC Evaporator Coil Frozen.
  • Clear the drain channel. The same guide suggests occasionally running a stiff wire through the drain channels to prevent clogs. Do this only where the drain is easy and safe to reach, with the power off.
  • Do not defeat the float switch. If it keeps shutting the system down, the fix is the drain, not bypassing the switch.

Leave electrical panels, switch wiring and refrigerant parts to a licensed professional. A homeowner check is looking, listening and keeping things clean.

Field card: AC water per day

Latent Btu/h ÷ 1,060 = lb/h; lb ÷ 8.34 = gal; gal × 8 = pints.

  • The coil removes water as a side effect of cooling; that is the latent load.
  • Our example: 3 tons, 1,200 cfm, about 10 gallons on a humid 8-hour day, about 2.6 on a mild 4-hour day.
  • A government research record cites three to ten gallons per day per 1,000 sq ft in hot, humid weather.
  • Water path: coil, pan, trap, gravity run or pump, outlet. A float switch shuts cooling off if water backs up.
  • Checks: look at the outlet, look for water near the unit, keep filters and the coil clean, never bypass the float switch.
