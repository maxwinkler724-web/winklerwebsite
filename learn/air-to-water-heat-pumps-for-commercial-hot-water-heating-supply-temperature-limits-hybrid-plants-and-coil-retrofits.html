Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Commercial Heating Hot Water: Supply Limits, Hybrid Plants and Coil Retrofits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An air-to-water heat pump takes heat from outdoor air and puts it into a hot water loop, so it can sit where a boiler sits. The catch is temperature. Many buildings were designed around 180 F water, and most of these machines are happiest well below that. This lesson explains why, how to estimate what an old coil will do on cooler water, and why many plants keep a boiler for the coldest hours.

How it makes hot water

The unit is a refrigeration circuit. An outdoor coil picks up heat from the air, the compressor lifts it, and a water-cooled condenser hands it to the heating loop. The DOE large-building guidance describes these units as using building infrastructure similar to a fuel-fired boiler, with electricity as the energy source. The same guidance says current units produce hot water in roughly the 120 to 130 F range, compared with about 180 F from a boiler. A DOE retrofit presentation gives 110 to 140 F as typical for air-to-water and water-to-water heat pumps and notes that capacity is controlled by the leaving condenser water temperature.

Why capacity and COP fall

Think of "lift," the gap between the outdoor air and the water you want to make. A maker white paper gives an example: 130 F water at 30 F outdoor air is 100 F of lift, and it says typical air-to-water units handle about 100 F of lift without vapor injection. Its stated limits are about 131 F leaving water without vapor injection and up to 149 F with it. The paper also says efficiency drops as leaving water temperature rises and as outdoor temperature falls. Cold weather therefore hurts twice: the load goes up while the machine's output goes down. The DOE guidance says cold climates see decreased performance and efficiency in colder weather, and that performance improves at lower supply temperatures.

We did not find a freely hosted curve with percentages we could cite, so we are not quoting one. Read the maker's performance table at your design outdoor temperature and the water temperature you plan to run.

Why a 180 F design is a problem

Caleffi's guidance on evaluating existing systems says most current air-to-water and water-to-water heat pumps cannot reach the 147 F that its example needs at design load, and that systems needing 160 F or more are unlikely to be fully served by current units. A trade-press article by John Siegenthaler adds that a distribution system designed for about 180 F average water can trip a heat pump's high-temperature limit. Hotter machines exist: Caleffi says units using R-290 and CO2 can run hotter but have limited North American availability.

The reason is the emitter. A coil, fan coil or radiator puts out heat in proportion to how much hotter the water is than the room or air. Cool the water and the output falls. A CSE magazine article gives one example: lowering the mean water temperature of a finned-tube radiator from 170 F to 110 F lowers output by approximately 65 percent. The fixes are more surface (more rows, a larger face area, or more finned length), a lower load from envelope work, or accepting that the heat pump covers only part of the design day. The DOE guidance says lower temperatures may call for changes to fan coil units or VAV boxes, and suggests lowering the water temperature step by step while checking comfort.

Worked example (our own)

Caleffi's Formula 4-1 relates required supply temperature to load: T_new = T_in + (Q_new / Q_existing) x (T_De - T_in). Rearranged, a coil's output on cooler water is roughly Q_new / Q_rated = (T_new - T_in) / (T_De - T_in). Our own arithmetic, using Caleffi's 70 F room temperature, for a coil rated 100,000 Btu/h at 180 F supply:

  1. Rated temperature difference: 180 - 70 = 110 F.
  2. At 140 F supply: (140 - 70) / 110 = 0.636, so about 63,600 Btu/h.
  3. At 130 F supply: 60 / 110 = 0.545, so about 54,500 Btu/h.
  4. At 120 F supply: 50 / 110 = 0.455, so about 45,500 Btu/h.

Check it against the cited examples. For the CSE radiator, the same linear method gives (110 - 70) / (170 - 70) = 0.40, a 60 percent drop against the article's roughly 65 percent. For the Siegenthaler baseboard (about 600 Btu/h per foot at 200 F water, 146 at 115 F average), our method gives 45 / 130 = 0.35 against the published 146 / 600 = 0.24. So the linear estimate runs optimistic. Treat the numbers above as a ceiling, and confirm with the coil maker's selection data at the actual flow and air conditions.

What it means for the setpoint: if the building needs 70 percent of rated coil output at design, Caleffi's formula says 147 F, above what most units make. A 130 F setpoint gives at best about 55 percent of rated. Either the coil gets bigger, the load gets smaller, or a second source covers the gap. Caleffi's simulation adds a useful point: with outdoor reset, the example needs only about 104 F at 40 F outdoor air, which Caleffi calls easily within the range of current hydronic heat pumps. So set the heat pump on a reset curve and reserve the highest setpoint for the coldest hours. The CSE article makes the same point: raise supply in extreme cold, with modest impact on annual efficiency.

Hybrid plants

The DOE guidance says buildings using air-to-water units may need auxiliary heat for extreme cold, and may keep a fossil or electric resistance system for peak days. Caleffi's simulation found a cold-climate air-to-water unit could supply upwards of 90 percent of seasonal space heating energy even with 180 F design water, assuming outdoor reset. A NYSERDA case study of an office tower shows the idea in practice: five roof-mounted air-to-water heat pumps were added while the steam boilers stayed in service at reduced load.

The boiler needs its own protection. Caleffi gives a guideline of keeping natural gas boiler inlet water at or above 130 F to avoid sustained flue gas condensation, so the piping must not feed the boiler a cold return. Follow the boiler maker's minimum return temperature.

Buffer tanks and defrost

When frost builds on the outdoor coil the unit defrosts, and that takes heat from the water loop. A maker white paper says defrost is most frequent between 25 and 40 F outdoor air, reduces capacity and efficiency, and lasts roughly 1.5 to 6 minutes, with no more than one defrost every 30 minutes. A rep-hosted Bluebox sizing page calls adequate water volume "mandatory, not optional" and gives rules of thumb: about 7 to 8 gallons per ton for roughly a 15 F maximum drop, and 10 to 12 gallons per ton for roughly a 10 F drop. It adds that in primary-secondary systems only the primary-side water reliably counts, so the buffer must be larger. Caleffi says hydronic heat pumps typically need 2 to 3 gpm per ton, that a fixed-speed unit on an extensively zoned system typically needs a buffer, and that an inverter unit may use a hydraulic separator instead. The maker white paper also says to count only "effective" volume, meaning water that stays open to the unit.

Refrigerant notes

The DOE guidance says some air-to-water products use low-GWP refrigerants such as R-513A and R-744 (CO2). A DOE-hosted research presentation lists an R-32 unit with water up to 149 F and a CO2 unit up to 150 F. The white paper's R-410A units condense at about 150 F, which is part of why the water limit sits where it does. Each refrigerant has its own handling and charge rules, so use the maker's manual and see CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems.

Field card: Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Commercial Heating

Lower water, bigger coils, boiler for the coldest hours.

  • Typical heat pump water: about 110 to 140 F; boilers often 180 F (DOE).
  • Lift = water temperature minus outdoor air temperature; more lift, less COP.
  • Output on cooler water is roughly (T_new - room) / (180 - room) of rating. Our own, and optimistic.
  • Use outdoor reset: coolest water that holds comfort, hotter only in extreme cold.
  • Defrost pulls heat from the loop; size water volume and count only effective volume.
  • Keep boiler return water above the boiler maker's minimum.
  • Check the maker's data for your design outdoor temperature.
