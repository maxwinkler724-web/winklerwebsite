Smart Thermostat Home and Away Modes: Phone Location, Sensors and Real Savings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many smart thermostats can switch to an away setting on their own, using the location of your phone, a motion or occupancy sensor at home, or both. It sounds like free savings. This lesson explains how each method decides nobody is home, where it goes wrong, how much a short setback really saves, and why some heating systems barely benefit.

How the phone location radius works

Our own plain-language description: the thermostat app keeps a circle around your home, called a radius or geofence. The phone reports when it crosses that line. When every phone on the account is outside, the thermostat is told the house is empty; when any phone comes back inside, it switches to home. Phone location is an estimate, and it is reported in bursts to save battery, so the thermostat may hear about a crossing minutes late. Our own rule of thumb: a very small radius (a few hundred feet) can trigger from location error while you are in the yard, and a very large one (miles) means the house goes to home mode late and recovers slowly. Check what choices your app offers.

Several phones, guests and pets

  • Several phones. The system only works if every adult who lives there is added. One person who forgets to add a phone can come home to a house that was set back, or the house can sit in home mode all day because a spare phone never leaves.
  • Guests. A guest has no phone on your account, so the house can go to away mode while the guest is inside. Use a manual hold or an indoor sensor for visitors.
  • Pets. A pet has no phone either. A university extension fact sheet on dog temperature needs says breed, age and health change the comfortable range, that flat-faced dogs shed heat poorly, and that panting works worse in humid, poorly ventilated spaces. Set an away limit you would accept for the animal, not only for your bill.

False away events and how sensors help

A false away event is when the system thinks you left but you did not: a phone in airplane mode or switched off, a dead battery, location permission turned down to save power, or a phone left at home while a person stays. The opposite also happens: a phone left in the car keeps the house in home mode. A motion or occupancy sensor adds a second opinion. A university research-center paper on occupancy-responsive thermostats notes that these sensors track whether a space is vacant, but that short vacancies, or rooms that never cool far enough, give little measurable savings. It also warns that shorter equipment run time is not the same as lower energy use. Treat the sensor as a way to prevent wrong calls, not as a savings engine.

How much a temporary setback saves

A federal energy-efficiency guide on thermostats says you can save as much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling by turning the thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees F for 8 hours a day, and that the percentage is greater in milder climates. Read it as a ceiling for a long, deep, daily setback. A university extension checklist gives a similar rule of about 1 percent per degree for an 8-hour setback. A 4-hour away period at 6 F is shorter and shallower than either, so expect well under the ceiling. The same research paper found that short vacancies do not give the building time to drift to the setback temperature, so little is saved.

Worked example (our own): degree-hours saved

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Savings track the area between normal indoor temperature and the actual indoor temperature, counted in degree-hours (F-h), because heat loss scales with the indoor-outdoor difference.

Assumptions (all our own): comfort setpoint 70 F; setback 6 F, to 64 F; the house drifts down in a straight line over 1.5 hours; it climbs back in a straight line over 1 hour after the setback ends; house heat loss 500 BTU per hour per degree F; 3,412 BTU per kWh.

  1. Geofence away, 4 hours on a weekday. Drift down: 0.5 x 1.5 h x 6 F = 4.5 F-h. Held at 64 F for the other 2.5 h: 2.5 x 6 = 15 F-h. Climb back: 0.5 x 1 h x 6 F = 3 F-h. Total = 4.5 + 15 + 3 = 22.5 F-h. The naive figure, 6 F x 4 h = 24 F-h, overstates it slightly.
  2. Schedule setback, 8 hours overnight at 6 F. Drift: 4.5 F-h. Hold for 6.5 h: 6.5 x 6 = 39 F-h. Climb back: 3 F-h. Total = 46.5 F-h.
  3. Heat saved. Away mode: 22.5 x 500 = 11,250 BTU, or 11,250 / 3,412 = 3.30 kWh of heat. Overnight: 46.5 x 500 = 23,250 BTU, or 6.81 kWh of heat.
  4. Compare. 22.5 / 46.5 = 0.48. The 4-hour away setback gives about half of what the overnight schedule gives, and only on days when someone actually leaves for the full period.

The two are not rivals: the schedule runs every night, and away mode adds on top when the house is empty. If your phone settings fire away mode on a day with a shorter outing, the saving shrinks toward zero.

Heat pumps and backup strip heat

A federal energy-efficiency guide on heat pumps says not to set back a heat pump thermostat if doing so makes the backup heating come on, and that backup systems such as electric strip heaters are usually more expensive to operate. The thermostat guide adds that programmable thermostats are generally not recommended for heat pumps, because a setback can make the unit run inefficiently, and says that specially designed models use algorithms to limit backup resistance heat. For away mode this means a deep, short setback can cost more than it saves. Keep the away offset small, use a model with a heat pump setting, and see the lesson on heat pump setback recovery and strip heat.

Hot-water and radiant floors

A system with a lot of water and mass reacts slowly. The thermostat guide says some manufacturers offer thermostats that track how the heating system performs so they can time heating for the scheduled comfort time on steam and radiant floor systems. Our own advice: a floor slab or cast-iron radiator can take hours to catch up, so away mode with a phone trigger may leave you cold when you arrive. For these systems prefer small offsets, a longer radius, or a schedule. The math of warm-up time is in the hot-water warm-up lesson.

Comfort, humidity, pets and plants

A federal environmental-agency guide on moisture says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. Our own note: a house set back in summer (a higher cooling setpoint) can drift humid, and a long unconditioned stretch invites that. If your thermostat offers a humidity limit, set it so away mode cannot let moisture climb. Our own note on plants: leaves and soil do best away from extremes, so keep away settings moderate for houseplants too, and avoid dropping the house near cold-damage temperatures. For longer trips see the lesson on settings when away from home.

Privacy settings on location sharing

Location history is personal data. A federal consumer-protection guide on securing connected devices tells owners to keep the control app updated, turn off features they will not use, and review privacy settings on connected devices. In practice: give the app location access only while it is needed if your phone allows it, share a single household account instead of passing location to many people, and check whether the app stores location history. If you do not want to share location at all, use sensors and the schedule only.

Simple fixes

  • Radius size. Pick a radius that clears your yard and driveway but is not so large that you are miles away before the house reacts. Test it by walking or driving out and back.
  • Add a delay. Where the app allows, require several minutes outside the radius before away mode starts.
  • Sensor placement. Put the sensor in the room people use most, away from supply registers and direct sun, since moving air and heat can trigger or blind it. Our own advice.
  • Schedule as backup. Keep a normal schedule so the house is comfortable even if the phone data is wrong.
  • Guests and pets. Use a hold, add the guest, or choose a modest away offset.

Field card: smart thermostat home and away modes

Away mode is a guess. Back it up.

  • All phones must be on the account; guests and pets are invisible.
  • Short outings save little; the ceiling is for long, deep setbacks.
  • Our own example: 4 h at 6 F is about 22.5 F-h, roughly half of an 8 h overnight setback (46.5 F-h).
  • Heat pump: avoid setbacks that turn on strip heat.
  • Radiant and hot-water floors recover slowly; use small offsets.
  • Keep humidity below 60 percent; limit location sharing.
