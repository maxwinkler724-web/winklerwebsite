Mini-Split Flare Fittings: Making the Flare and Torque Values

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A mini-split flare is a metal-to-metal seal made by a nut, a flared copper end and a brass seat. There is no gasket to hide a poor flare. This lesson covers how the flare is made, how to check it, and where the tightening numbers come from. Every number here is an example from one manual, not a setting to copy.

Anatomy of a 45 degree flare

The copper end is spread into a cone that sits against a matching cone on the unit's service valve or fitting. The flare nut slides over the tube before you flare, then pulls the cone onto the seat as it is tightened. Mitsubishi's accessory manual prints a flare angle of 90 degrees with 45 degrees (plus or minus 2) for the clutch-type tool, and Daikin's CTXF-F manual prints the shape as 45 and 90 degrees (each plus or minus 2). LG's manual lists the pipe as annealed temper. The nut that ships with the unit is the one to use. Daikin's manual warns that other nuts may cause leaks, and Fujitsu's says the same.

Cut, ream, flare

Cut the tube square with a tube cutter. LG's manual says to remove burrs with a reamer while holding the pipe end pointing down so metal powder stays out of the line, and Fujitsu's manuals say the same about holding the pipe down. A MrCool manual states that burrs can affect the airtight seal. A trade training site, hvacrschool.com, adds a limit on the other side: do not over-ream and thin the copper edge. hvacrschool.com adds that factory flares on line sets are often poor and are better cut off and remade. Slide the nut on before flaring.

Flare tools and setting the height

Manuals name more than one tool style. Mitsubishi's table lists a clutch type and a wing nut type, and Daikin's installer guide shows protrusion ranges for clutch, wing nut and imperial tools. The height the tube sticks out above the flaring block (called dimension A in these manuals) depends on the tool style and the tube size, so use the manual's table for your tool. Mitsubishi's table prints 0 to 0.02 inch (0 to 0.5 mm) for 1/4 inch tube on a clutch-type R410A tool. Fujitsu says a conventional tool needs about 0.020 inch (0.5 mm) more protrusion than the clutch-type value. hvacrschool.com notes that modern flaring blocks usually have built-in depth gauges.

Checking the flare

Look at it before you use it. Mitsubishi's manual describes a good flare as smooth all around, even in length all around, and shiny inside with no scratches, and tells you to cut off and remake a flare that is uneven, burred or tilted. LG's manual asks you to redo a flare that shows tilt, surface damage, cracks or uneven thickness. Some makers print a finished-flare size (dimension B). Mitsubishi's accessory manual gives 12.8 to 13.2 mm for 3/8 inch tube with an R410A flare tool and says a copper pipe gauge can help confirm it. Daikin's CTXF-F manual prints 12.8 to 13.2 mm for 3/8 inch tube as well. Do not reuse flares: Daikin's manuals say to use new ones, and a Daikin indoor-unit manual says a reused flare joint must be re-fabricated.

Oil on the flare: read your maker

Makers do not all agree. Daikin's R-32 manuals (CTXF-F and the installer guide) say to apply refrigeration oil only to the inside of the flare and not to use mineral oil on the flared part. Fujitsu's manuals say to apply refrigerant lubricant to the mating surfaces of the flare and the union, and its R-32 manual repeats the mineral oil warning. Mitsubishi's accessory manual says to apply a thin coat to the flare seat all the way around, while its wall-unit manual says not to put refrigeration oil on screw threads. hvacrschool.com says some manufacturers disagree with oiling at all and to follow their guidance. For the oil types themselves, see Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE.

Torque values come from the manual

The same size nut gets a different range in different manuals. The table lists what each one prints, in newton-meters (N·m). Not every size appears in every manual, and the ranges also change between models from the same maker.

Fujitsu's R-32 duct unit manual (ARUH12KUASi) prints the same N·m ranges as the table's Fujitsu column. A Daikin indoor-unit manual (FDMAQ50CV16 family) prints 14.2 to 17.2 N·m for 6.4 mm and 49.5 to 60.3 N·m for 12.7 mm. A Hessaire manual for R32 and R454B models prints 11 to 15, 22 to 26, 37 to 41 and 44 to 48 ft-lb for 1/4, 3/8, 1/2 and 5/8 inch.

Use two wrenches: hold the unit-side fitting with a backup wrench and turn the nut with the torque wrench. Fujitsu says to hand-tighten first, hold the body-side coupling with a wrench, and finish with a torque wrench at a right angle to the pipe. Daikin says to start the nut by hand for 3 or 4 turns. hvacrschool.com says to use a flare wrench rather than an adjustable one and to stay on the low side of the range if you lubricated the threads.

Worked example: N·m to ft-lb

Most torque wrenches in a service van read in ft-lb, while many manuals print N·m. Take a Daikin CTXF-F 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) line. The manual's range is 33 to 39 N·m. The conversion factor, my arithmetic and not from a manual, is 1 ft-lb = about 1.3558 N·m.

  1. Low end: 33 / 1.3558 = 24.3 ft-lb.
  2. High end: 39 / 1.3558 = 28.8 ft-lb.
  3. Set the wrench in the 24.3 to 28.8 ft-lb window.

Check against another maker: Fujitsu prints both units for the same nut size, 32 to 42 N·m and 24 to 31 ft-lb. My arithmetic: 32 / 1.3558 = 23.6 and 42 / 1.3558 = 31.0, which matches. Mitsubishi prints 25 to 30 ft-lb beside 34.3 to 41.2 N·m. Multiplying 25 by 1.3558 gives 33.9, not 34.3, so the printed N·m there comes from its kgf·cm figures (350 to 420), not a straight conversion. The lesson: round the way the manual rounds, and check the wrench scale and units before you pull.

Why over-tight and under-tight both cause leaks

Over-tightening is the one the manuals warn about. Mitsubishi says a nut fastened too tight may eventually break and cause a leak. Fujitsu says the flare nuts could break after a prolonged period, causing a refrigerant leak. Daikin says not to tighten nuts too tight. hvacrschool.com adds not to tighten harder to stop a leak: if a properly torqued flare still leaks, it was probably made wrong, so cut it off and remake it.

Leak check after the flare

Fujitsu says to check the connections for leaks before opening the refrigerant valves, using a detector made for the refrigerant, and, after the nitrogen pressure test, to complete evacuation per the outdoor unit manual. Daikin's CTXF-F manual describes pressurizing with nitrogen and applying bubble solution to every connection, and says not to use soapy water. The hvacrschool.com order is pressure test, bubble test, then evacuate and run a decay test. For method, gauges and limits, see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods, Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics and Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.

A2L notes

R-32 and R-454B are the refrigerants in many current mini-splits. Daikin's CTXF-F manual calls R-32 mildly flammable. Fujitsu's R-32 manual calls it flammable, says to use a cutter and flare tool made for R32 and not to use mineral oil on the flare, and says not to reuse R22-era pipe or flare nuts. Daikin's R32 installer guide says R32 has no odor, so you cannot smell a leak, and the CTXF-F manual restricts non-permanent joints in occupied spaces, except site-made joints that connect the indoor unit directly to the pipe. See A2L Installation and Brazing Practices and Mini-Split Installation Basics.

Field card: Mini-Split Flares

Cut square, ream, flare, inspect, torque to the printed range.

  • Pipe end down when cutting and reaming. Nut on before flaring.
  • Set the tube height for your tool style from the manual.
  • Inspect: smooth, even, no cracks. If not, cut and remake.
  • Oil only as that maker says. Backup wrench plus torque wrench.
  • Leak check before opening the valves.
