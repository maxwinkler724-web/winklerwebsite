Ammonia Refrigeration Basics for HVAC Techs: Properties, Hazards and Rules

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most HVAC techs work with man-made refrigerants. Ammonia is the old exception, and it is still the working fluid in many cold storage warehouses, food plants and ice rinks. This lesson covers what makes it different, the numbers on its exposure limits, and the federal rules that decide who may work on it. It is awareness only, not a way to start working on these systems.

Check the rules that apply to you

Check the rules that apply to you. Federal rules, state rules and your employer's procedures differ, and they change. This page was built from agency web pages read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice and not a work procedure.

Where ammonia is used and why

EPA's page on refrigeration substitutes says the majority of U.S. cold storage warehouses use ammonia in a vapor compression cycle. An ASHRAE position document, posted on EPA's site, adds that ammonia is a closed-loop refrigerant in many industrial refrigeration systems and describes it as highly energy efficient. It lists an ozone depletion potential of 0.00 and says ammonia does not directly contribute to global warming, summarizing it as zero ODP, zero GWP. That document dates from 2010, so treat the details as background. One manufacturer's technical page gives the same zero values.

Efficiency and a clean environmental record are why plants keep using it. The trade-off is toxicity, which the rest of this lesson is about.

Safety class: B2L

The ASHRAE document says Standard 34 classifies ammonia as Group B2 because of toxicity and flammability concerns. A manufacturer page lists R717 as B2L under the European standard EN 378-1. Our own reading: the B means higher toxicity and the 2L means mildly flammable, as explained in A2L and A3 Refrigerants. The class tells you ammonia is a toxic refrigerant first and a flammable one second. NIOSH gives flammable limits of 15% to 28% in air and says that although ammonia does not meet the DOT definition of a flammable gas, it should be treated as one.

Odor is a warning, not a safeguard

The sources do not agree on the odor threshold. The ASHRAE document gives an average of 5 ppm, OSHA's overview gives 20 ppm, and an OSHA fact sheet says about 2 ppm with a normal range of 2 to 55 ppm. The same fact sheet warns that regular exposure can dull a worker's sense of smell. So the nose is a useful early warning, but nobody should rely on it to decide whether a space is safe. Use a calibrated instrument and the site's procedures.

The exposure limits

The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists these for ammonia:

  • NIOSH REL: 25 ppm as a time-weighted average, written TWA, and 35 ppm short-term, written ST or STEL.
  • OSHA PEL: 50 ppm TWA.
  • IDLH (immediately dangerous to life or health): 300 ppm.

OSHA's overview also says 300 ppm is immediately dangerous, and its fact sheet recommends respiratory protection where exposure could exceed 50 ppm. Ammonia is described as corrosive to skin, eyes and lungs. For emergencies, OSHA's eTool says to use a positive-pressure self-contained breathing apparatus with a full facepiece where exposure above 300 ppm is possible.

Machinery (engine) rooms

Ammonia plants keep compressors and vessels in a dedicated room. Our lesson on machine rooms, detectors, alarms and ventilation covers the general idea. For ammonia specifically, the ASHRAE document says Standard 15 sets the design procedures, including ventilation levels. An OSHA interpretation letter on an ammonia control room points to that standard, says the room needs ventilation meeting it, and notes that a vapor detector system may address the explosion hazard without addressing the inhalation hazard. Industry standards from IIAR also cover ammonia systems; we name them here only so you know what to ask about, and we do not reproduce them.

Why steel and not copper

NIOSH lists ammonia as corrosive to copper and galvanized surfaces. A manufacturer page says R717 attacks copper and non-ferrous metals, so lines, heat exchangers and fittings should be steel or stainless steel. This is the opposite of the copper tubing you use on most comfort systems, so never reuse copper parts or assume a fitting is compatible. See Refrigerant Safety Basics for general habits.

The rules: PSM and RMP

OSHA's Process Safety Management standard, 29 CFR 1910.119, applies to a process involving a listed chemical at or above its threshold quantity. Appendix A lists anhydrous ammonia at 10,000 pounds. OSHA's eTool says systems with 10,000 pounds or more are covered, and its fact sheet says the standard has 14 elements, including employee participation, process hazard analysis, operating procedures, training, mechanical integrity and emergency planning. The standard exempts retail facilities, oil and gas well drilling or servicing, and normally unoccupied remote facilities. OSHA also says its ammonia practices are useful whether or not a system is a covered process.

EPA's Risk Management Program is the parallel rule. EPA says covered facilities must submit a risk management plan with a hazard assessment, a prevention program and an emergency response program, renewed every five years. The regulation 40 CFR 68.130 lists anhydrous ammonia at 10,000 pounds and ammonia at 20% concentration or more at 20,000 pounds.

Why EPA 608 is not enough

EPA 608 certification is built around ozone-depleting and substitute refrigerants. In 40 CFR 82.154, ammonia in commercial or industrial process refrigeration, or in absorption units, appears on the list of exempt substitutes. Our own plain reading: the federal refrigerant rules you study for 608 do not treat ammonia like R-410A, so 608 study is not a substitute for ammonia training (see EPA 608 Certification Basics). Ammonia work needs separate training. OSHA's PSM standard has its own training and mechanical integrity elements, and your employer must provide training on the hazards of the specific system.

Worked example: charge and detector reading

Both numbers below are made up for practice. Our own arithmetic.

Part 1: PSM threshold. A system has a stated charge of 12,000 lb of anhydrous ammonia in one process. The threshold is 10,000 lb.

  1. 12,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1.20, so the charge is 120% of the threshold.
  2. 12,000 − 10,000 = 2,000 lb over the threshold.
  3. At or above the threshold means the system is a PSM-covered process, and 40 CFR 68.130 uses the same 10,000 lb figure for RMP. Whether the facility is exempt is a question for its safety manager.

Part 2: detector reading. A handheld detector shows a steady 35 ppm. Each limit is compared as reading ÷ limit × 100:

  1. NIOSH REL TWA, 25 ppm: 35 ÷ 25 = 1.40, so 140%.
  2. NIOSH REL STEL, 35 ppm: 35 ÷ 35 = 1.00, so 100%.
  3. OSHA PEL, 50 ppm: 35 ÷ 50 = 0.70, so 70%.
  4. IDLH, 300 ppm: 35 ÷ 300 = 0.1167, so about 11.7%.

What it means: 35 ppm is above the REL average and right at the REL short-term limit, yet well under the PEL and far below IDLH. A spot reading is not a time-weighted average, so this comparison shows scale only and does not decide compliance. A reading like this still means stop, tell the people responsible, and follow the site's alarm procedure.

Why people get hurt

The U.S. Chemical Safety Board investigates ammonia releases. In 2010 an uncontrolled release at a refrigerated warehouse in Theodore, Alabama sent more than 130 members of the public for medical attention. In 2024 a relief valve discharge at a food plant in Sterling, Virginia released about 170 lb, according to company officials, and injured dozens of employees, some seriously. The CSB stressed effective emergency response, evacuation procedures and regular equipment maintenance. Treat each as a reason to learn the system before touching it.

Field card: Ammonia Refrigeration Basics for HVAC Techs: Properties, Hazards and Rules

Ammonia is toxic first, flammable second, and not covered by what you learned for 608.

  • NIOSH: REL 25 ppm TWA, 35 ppm ST; OSHA PEL 50 ppm; IDLH 300 ppm.
  • PSM and RMP thresholds for anhydrous ammonia: 10,000 lb.
  • Steel or stainless piping, never copper; ammonia attacks copper.
  • Smell is a warning, not a test. Odor figures in sources range from 2 to 20 ppm.
  • Do not work on an ammonia system without system-specific training and permission.
  • Our own arithmetic: 35 ppm is 140% of REL TWA, 100% of STEL, 70% of PEL, about 11.7% of IDLH.
