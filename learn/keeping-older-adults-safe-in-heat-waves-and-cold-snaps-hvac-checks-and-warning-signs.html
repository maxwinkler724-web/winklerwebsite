Keeping Older Adults Safe in Heat Waves and Cold Snaps

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you are looking up how to keep an elderly parent cool in a heat wave without AC, start with this: the federal sources say fans have limits, and a cooled place is the safer plan. This lesson is safety information, not medical advice. It covers who is at higher risk, the warning signs the agencies list, a few equipment checks a family member can do, and what to do if the power or the AC goes out.

Check the sources

This is general information. Health guidance changes and depends on the person. Call a doctor for personal questions and call 911 for an emergency. Where a number appears below, it is the number the named agency published, not our own advice.

Why older adults face more risk

The CDC page on heat and adults 65 and older gives three reasons: older adults do not adjust as well to sudden temperature changes, they are more likely to have a medical condition that changes how the body responds to heat, and they are more likely to take prescriptions that affect temperature control or sweating. CDC also says people who live alone or have chronic conditions may need extra checks from family, friends and neighbors.

For cold, a National Institute on Aging guide (the 2018 edition we could read) says older adults lose body heat faster than younger people, and that for them a body temperature of 95°F or lower can be dangerous. It also lists risk factors such as thyroid problems, diabetes, memory loss, some medicines, and living in a cold home. CDC names older adults with inadequate food, clothing or heating as at risk of hypothermia.

Warning signs the agencies list

Heat. The National Weather Service lists these signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, pale cool damp skin, a rapid weak pulse, cramps, dizziness, nausea or vomiting, headache and fainting. Its heat stroke list adds confusion, slurred speech, a body temperature above 103°F, and hot red skin, and it calls heat stroke a severe medical emergency: call 911. The NWS says not to give fluids to a person with heat stroke. CDC lists cramping, unusually heavy sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, headache, weakness and nausea as heat illness symptoms.

Cold. CDC lists trembling, unusual exhaustion, confusion, clumsy hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness, and says to treat hypothermia as a medical emergency (get medical help right away if the temperature is below 95°F). The NIA guide describes early signs as cold hands and feet, a puffy face, pale skin, slower speech, sleepiness and irritability, notes that some people do not shiver, and says later signs such as slow movement, a slow heartbeat, shallow breathing or fainting mean call 911.

Indoor temperature targets

For winter, the NIA guide suggests keeping indoor heat at about 68 to 70°F. The sources we read give no matching indoor number for summer, so this page does not invent one. What CDC says instead is to spend as much time as possible in air-conditioned spaces and, without AC at home, to contact the local health department or find a cooled shelter. A cheap room thermometer helps a family member see what the room is really doing; a thermostat setting is not the same as the room reading.

Fans: useful, with limits

CDC says a fan should not be the main cooling method in extreme heat. Its heat-and-health page says fans can be used when indoor temperatures are below 90°F and are not recommended above 90°F because they can raise body temperature. The NWS one-page heat safety sheet gives a similar caution about aiming a fan at yourself above 90°F. A fan moves air across skin; it does not lower the room temperature. For ceiling fan basics, see Ceiling Fans: Summer and Winter Direction and Real Savings.

Worked example: how hot is 95°F at 50 percent humidity?

The NWS heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity into how hot it feels. Its heat safety sheet gives this case: 95°F at 50 percent relative humidity is a heat index of 105°F. We checked it with the NWS Weather Prediction Center's published regression equation:

  1. Start with T = 95 and RH = 50.
  2. Put both into the equation HI = -42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127RH - 0.22475541T×RH - 0.00683783T² - 0.05481717RH² + 0.00122874T²RH + 0.00085282T×RH² - 0.00000199T²RH².
  3. The sum comes to about 105.2°F, which rounds to the NWS chart value of 105°F.
  4. NWS categories: Caution is 80 to 90, Extreme Caution is 90 to 103, Danger is 103 to 124, Extreme Danger is 125 or higher. So 105°F falls in Danger, where the NWS says heat cramps or heat exhaustion are likely and heat stroke is possible with prolonged exposure or activity.

Two cautions from the NWS: the values assume shade and light wind, and full sunshine can add up to 15°F. An upstairs room with sun on the windows can be worse than the weather report.

Equipment checks a family member can do

These are the homeowner-level items from our other lessons. Do them before the season, not during the first heat wave or cold snap.

  • Filter. Check and replace a dirty one. Get Your AC Ready for Summer shows how.
  • Outdoor unit. Clear plants and debris from around it, as that checklist describes.
  • Thermostat. Replace batteries if the model uses them, and confirm it calls for heat and for cooling. The heating-side steps are in Heating System Checklist Before the First Cold Snap.
  • Carbon monoxide alarm. Press the test button on each one and read Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. This matters most in cold weather, when heating equipment and portable heaters run more.
  • Space heaters. If one is in use as backup, read Electric Space Heaters: Safety, Real Cost and When They Make Sense first.

Anything inside the equipment, such as refrigerant, gas or wiring, is a job for a licensed professional, not a family visit.

If the power or the AC goes out

  • Heat. CDC says to contact the local health department or find a cooled shelter if there is no AC at home. NJ 211 shares cooling center listings from state agencies, county emergency management offices and local governments, organized by county; hours and rules are set by each site, so confirm before you go, and some counties may have no listings yet. You can reach NJ 211 by dialing 211. New Jersey also runs a Code Red program with cooling centers in some counties (Camden, Essex, Hudson, Mercer and Monmouth were listed).
  • Heat, while waiting. CDC says to avoid the stove and oven, wear loose light clothing, take cool showers or baths, drink water without waiting for thirst (unless a doctor has limited fluids), and rest.
  • Cold. CDC says to move a person who may be hypothermic to a warm room or shelter and remove wet clothing. The NIA guide suggests closing off unused rooms and blocking drafts to hold heat.
  • When power returns. Equipment may wait a few minutes before it restarts. See Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start.

Phone check-ins

CDC tells caretakers to visit at least twice a day during extreme heat and ask four things: Are they drinking enough water? Do they have access to air conditioning? Do they know how to keep cool? Do they show signs of heat stress? CDC also says to look in on friends and neighbors and ask someone to check on you. A phone call can cover the questions; if the person does not answer, that is a reason to send someone, not to wait. Agree on a check-in time and a backup person in advance.

Field card: Keeping Older Adults Safe in Heat and Cold

Check early, know the signs, have a cooler or warmer place to go.

  • CDC: older adults adjust less well to temperature swings, and conditions and medicines add risk.
  • Heat stroke signs include confusion, slurred speech, body temp above 103°F: call 911.
  • Hypothermia signs: trembling, exhaustion, confusion, slurred speech. Below 95°F is an emergency.
  • NIA winter indoor suggestion: about 68 to 70°F.
  • CDC: fans are fine below 90°F indoors, not recommended above; a fan is not the main plan.
  • 95°F at 50% RH is heat index 105°F, NWS Danger.
  • NJ 211: dial 211 for cooling center listings; confirm hours.
  • Before the season: filter, thermostat batteries, CO alarm test, clear outdoor unit.
