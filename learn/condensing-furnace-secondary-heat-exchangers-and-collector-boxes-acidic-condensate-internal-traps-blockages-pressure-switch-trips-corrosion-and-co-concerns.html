Condensing Furnace Secondary Heat Exchangers and Collector Boxes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a 90 percent furnace, the fire is only half the story. The flue gas is cooled a second time in a different piece of metal, and the water that drops out of it has to go somewhere. This lesson follows that water from the secondary exchanger into the collector box, through the trap and down the drain, and shows what goes wrong when it cannot.

Safety first. This page is for study. A leaking or corroded exchanger, a blocked vent or a tripping pressure switch is a carbon monoxide (CO) risk. Opening a furnace, bypassing a switch or deciding that an exchanger is sound belongs to a trained technician working to the maker's instructions and local rules.

How the two exchangers split the work

An Oak Ridge National Laboratory report on a condensing furnace describes the flue gas heating the house "through a high-temperature heat exchanger and a condensing heat exchanger in sequence." In plain terms, the primary exchanger sits right after the burners and takes the hot gas. The secondary exchanger comes next and pulls the flue gas below its dew point. A university combustion chapter from Purdue puts the dew point of natural gas flue gas between 120 and 140 F, depending on excess air, and says cooling below it condenses water vapor and raises furnace efficiency. A national-lab venting report adds that furnaces at 90 percent AFUE or higher are called condensing because they condense water out of the flue gas, and that no gas furnaces are sold between about 82 and 89 percent because condensation problems occur in that range.

So the primary exchanger works with sensible heat (a temperature drop) and the secondary adds latent heat (water changing from vapor to liquid). For clamshell and tubular shapes, stress cracks and temperature rise, see Gas Furnace Heat Exchanger Types. We do not repeat that here.

Where the water forms and why it is acidic

Water forms in two places. Burning the fuel makes it (see the worked example below), and the secondary exchanger is where the cooled gas drops it as liquid. The Oak Ridge acidic-gas study says the flue gas contains sulfur, nitrogen and carbon based acidic gases that combine with water vapor once the gas cools below its dew points. It measured untreated condensate from a standard high-efficiency furnace at pH 3.42 to 3.76, and compared it with acid rain at about pH 4.2 to 4.4. It also says acidic condensate "can cause serious corrosion and fouling problems," which is why condensing furnaces use corrosion-resistant stainless steel in the condensing exchanger.

What to do with the acid at the drain is covered in Condensate Neutralizers for High-Efficiency Furnaces and Boilers.

The collector box, the internal trap and the pressure switch

A collector box gathers the condensate from the secondary exchanger, and a trap sits in the drain path after it. Some designs build the trap into the furnace and others fit it outside (the training guide we read shows an external trap), and part names differ between makers, so read your own manual. A maker's installation and service manual for a condensing furnace, which we read, describes a collector box with several ports. At first start-up the technician pours water through the ports so the trap is full. Servicing includes flushing each port until clean water runs from the trap discharge, then clearing the box and the trap relief ports with a small wire. The same manual says to clean the trap by removing it, rinsing it and fitting a new gasket on reassembly. A separate maker training guide says the trap must be primed with water before start-up so it drains properly and so flue gas cannot escape through the drain.

The U.S. Department of Energy Building America measure guideline adds three useful points. Condensate traps are gravity fed. The drain should sit below the trap. And to check flow, remove the drain tube from the drain and watch that condensate runs normally. It also says to drain to the house sanitary drain in cold climates, never outdoors. For the drain side in an attic or horizontal install, see Gas Furnaces in Attics and Horizontal Installs, and for a pump after the trap see Condensate Pump Not Working.

The pressure switch hose matters too. In the manuals we read, the tube that feeds the pressure switch is connected to a collector box port. When the manual has you flush that tube, it says to hold the free end higher than the box opening so water does not run out, and not to blow into the tube while it is connected to the switch.

How a blockage shows up

  • Pressure switch trips and lockouts. The troubleshooting chart in the maker manual lists a plugged condensate drain, water in the vent pipe or a sagging pipe, obstructed pressure tubing and a restricted vent among the causes of the pressure switch not closing (a diagnostic code 3 in that manual). The manual does not explain the mechanism, so treat it as a checklist rather than a cause-and-effect rule. For the diagnosis steps, see Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis.
  • Water in the inducer. A maker technical bulletin on condensate drains says that if a drain line is blocked and nothing gives the water an overflow point, condensate can back up through the trap and collector box into the inducer, which opens the pressure switch, and that this damage is not covered by the warranty. It also says not to seal an open standpipe in the drain line.
  • Rust streaks. We found no government or university source that gives a rust-streak pattern for the secondary. The maker manual does say to check the burner compartment before each heating season for rust, corrosion, soot or excess dust and to have a qualified agency handle what it finds, and the Building America guideline links a possible exchanger failure to accelerated corrosion from paint and solvent vapors in a garage. Our reading: rust or white crust near the collector box or inducer points to water that has been where it should not be, and it deserves a trained look before the next call for heat.

Inspecting with a camera: what is realistic

Here we stay with what the maker says. The manual we read says the condensing side of the exchangers cannot be inspected or serviced without fully removing the exchanger assembly, and it gives no separate leak test. It also says heavy soot or carbon calls for replacing both exchangers and fixing the cause. We did not find a government or university procedure for scoping a secondary, so we do not describe one. What can be inspected without taking the furnace apart: the burner compartment, the open collector box ports, the trap and its gasket, the drain path and the vent pipe for sag or water. Anything found must be judged against the maker's paperwork, as covered in the heat exchanger types lesson.

Why corrosion or leaks here are a CO concern

The vent on a condensing furnace is a positive-pressure, airtight, corrosion-resistant system with condensate disposal, as the Oak Ridge venting report describes (called Category IV). The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says malfunctioning fuel-burning appliances such as furnaces can cause CO poisoning, asks that a heating system be professionally inspected every year, including chimneys and flues for blockages, corrosion and disconnections, and says an alarm is no substitute for upkeep. A CPSC staff review of 83 incidents with modern furnaces and boilers from 2002 to 2009 counted 16 vent, chimney or heat exchanger breaches or disconnects (8 deaths) and 15 blockages of the same parts (10 deaths). That memo does not separate condensing furnaces or the secondary exchanger, so we cite it for the general risk only. The CPSC memo defines a breach as a hole or opening in the wall of the component. Our reasoning: a hole in an exchanger wall puts flue gas and house air on the two sides of a thin piece of metal, and acidic condensate that corrodes through that wall is one way such a hole could start. We have no source that says how often that happens in a secondary. The DOE guideline says that on a CO alarm or vent failure you should shut the equipment down, ventilate the house and call a technician at once. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What To Do.

Blocked intake and exhaust pipes also matter for the vent side; see Sidewall Vent Terminations for High-Efficiency Furnaces and Water Heaters.

Worked example (our own): how much water does the fire make?

Everything here is our own arithmetic. Assumptions are marked as assumptions. Nothing is measured on a real furnace.

Cited relation. Burning methane follows CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O (Purdue combustion chapter, Section SM 9.3). One mole of methane makes two moles of water. Natural gas is mostly methane (U.S. Energy Information Administration), so we treat it as pure methane. A real gas has other parts, so this is an estimate.

Assumed input (assumption): 60,000 Btu/h of gas, a common furnace input size. The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 cubic foot of natural gas as 1,036 Btu, so 60,000 / 1,036 = about 58 cubic feet of gas per hour.

Step 1, energy per pound of methane. The National Institute of Standards and Technology lists the enthalpy of combustion of methane gas as about -890.7 kJ/mol, and its molecular weight as 16.0425. That is 890.7 / 16.0425 = 55.52 kJ/g. Using 1 Btu = 1.055056 kJ and 1 lb = 453.59 g, this is 55.52 x 453.59 / 1.055056 = about 23,870 Btu per lb (the higher heating value, which is how furnace input ratings are normally stated).

Step 2, methane burned per hour. 60,000 / 23,870 = 2.51 lb/h.

Step 3, water made per hour. Two moles of water (2 x 18.0153 = 36.03 g, NIST molecular weight) per 16.0425 g of methane is 2.246 lb of water per lb of methane. So 2.51 x 2.246 = about 5.6 lb of water vapor per hour.

Step 4, gallons. The U.S. Geological Survey lists 62.424 lb per cubic foot for water. A U.S. gallon is 231 cubic inches, or 231 / 1,728 = 0.1337 cubic foot, so a gallon weighs about 62.424 x 0.1337 = 8.34 lb. Then 5.6 / 8.34 = about 0.68 gallon per hour.

Step 5, the part that condenses (assumption). How much of that is caught depends on return temperature, air flow and excess air, and we have no source for a typical value. For teaching only, we assume 50, 70 and 90 percent.

What it tells us. Even with the lowest assumed share, a furnace running a full hour sends roughly a third of a gallon of acidic water through the collector box and trap. At the lowest assumed share (0.34 gallon per hour), 10 hours of run time is about 3.4 gallons. That is a lot of water to leave to a small trap, a thin port and a hose, and it is why one clogged passage can show up as a pressure switch call.

Field card: Secondary exchanger and collector box

Water in, water out, no blocked path.

  • Primary exchanger takes the hot gas. Secondary cools it below the dew point and makes liquid.
  • Untreated condensate measured at pH 3.42 to 3.76. Treat it as acid.
  • Collector box gathers condensate. The trap must be primed and the drain must run free.
  • Pressure switch not closing: check drain, trap, vent, tubing and inducer water, per the maker.
  • Do not seal an open standpipe. Do not blow into a pressure tube connected to the switch.
  • The condensing side cannot be fully inspected without removing the assembly, per one maker manual.
  • Suspected leak or CO: shut down, ventilate, call a technician.
