Fan Center Relay Boards and Isolation Relays in HVAC Controls: Two Transformers, Back-Feed, Switching Line Voltage, VA Ratings and Meter Tests

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A 24 volt transformer is small, and every coil you hang on it takes a share of its capacity. This lesson covers the fan center (a transformer and blower relay in one package), the isolation relay that lets one system signal another without sharing a transformer, and the meter checks that tell you whether a relay is the problem.

Check the equipment drawing

Check the equipment drawing. Terminal names, coil ratings and wiring rules differ by equipment. This page is general education. Follow the wiring diagram on the unit and the maker's instructions before you change anything. Basic transformer and relay ideas are in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control; this page builds on them.

What a fan center is

A fan center is a small package, usually a transformer plus a relay (often with a few screw terminals), mounted in or near an air handler or fan coil. The transformer steps line voltage down to about 24 volts AC. The relay's coil is fed from that 24 volt side, and its contacts switch the line-voltage blower motor. The thermostat's fan call (G) closes a circuit through the coil, the contacts close, and the blower runs. Add-on cooling coils on a furnace or air handler often use the same idea: one 24 volt signal, one relay, one line-voltage load. For the older furnace style of fan control, see Combination Fan and Limit Controls on Older Furnaces. This section is our own plain-language description; the exact terminals vary by board.

Normally open and normally closed

A relay is a coil that pulls a set of contacts. A university electronics handout describes a normally open contact as not connected when the coil is unpowered and connected when it is energized, and a normally closed contact as the reverse. Many relays have both on one common terminal. The coil circuit and the contact circuit are separate, and the same handout notes that a small coil current can switch a much larger load. That separation is the whole reason relays exist in HVAC controls.

Switching a line-voltage load from a 24 volt signal

The thermostat only carries the coil current. The contacts carry the load. Pick a relay whose coil matches your control voltage (24 volts AC here) and whose contacts are rated for the load's voltage and current. The UNLV handout also warns that motors and similar loads draw much more current at startup than while running, so the contact rating needs margin. In practice, wire the coil into the 24 volt circuit and the contacts into the line-voltage path. Never connect the two circuits to each other.

Why use an isolation (interposing) relay

Suppose one thermostat or one zone panel must start a second system, such as a hydro-air air handler with its own transformer. If you run the first system's 24 volts straight into the second system's board, you have joined two power sources. An isolation relay avoids that. The first system's transformer powers only the relay coil. The relay contacts are a dry switch, with no voltage of their own, so you wire them as the "call" into the second system's circuit. The magnetic coupling carries the signal and nothing else crosses. See Zone Valve End Switches and Zone Control Relay Panels for the boiler side and Hydro-Air Systems for the air handler side.

Why two transformers must never be tied together

This is our own explanation of the hazard. Every transformer has a polarity, and a university lecture on nameplate data and connections explains that matching polarity gives no phase shift while reversed polarity gives 180 degrees. Two small control transformers on different circuits, breakers or phases may not agree on which secondary lead is positive at a given instant. Join their secondaries and the two sources fight each other through very little resistance. The result can be a blown secondary fuse, an overheated transformer or a burned board. There is a second hazard: a transformer works in both directions, so a powered 24 volt winding can push a voltage back toward a de-energized primary. Treat that as live. The safe rule is simple: one transformer per powered control circuit, and an isolation relay wherever two circuits need to talk. If a manual says to tie commons, follow the manual for that equipment only.

Coil VA and contact ratings

A University of Utah transformer note defines the VA rating as rated voltage times rated current, and says you rearrange it to find rated current: current equals VA divided by voltage. It also says you should not sustain current above the rating. Add up the VA of every coil, valve and board on the secondary; the total must stay under the transformer's rating. A relay's coil VA is its voltage times its coil current, shown on the label. Contact ratings are separate: they list the volts and amps the contacts can switch, and the motor starting surge is the number that surprises people.

How to test with a meter

  • Coil voltage (power on). Set the meter to AC volts. Put the leads across the coil terminals while the call is present. You expect roughly the transformer's output. No voltage means the problem is upstream (thermostat, limit, wiring or transformer). Voltage present and no click means a bad coil.
  • Coil resistance (power off). Lock out power and remove one coil lead. Read ohms across the coil. An open coil reads infinite, and a shorted one reads very low. Compare against a known good relay of the same part, since AC coil readings on a meter vary by design.
  • Contact voltage drop (power on, load running). Read volts across the closed contacts. Near zero is good. A reading close to the full supply voltage means the contacts are not passing power. Because this is a line-voltage test, use a properly rated meter and leads. For capacitor and contactor tests too, see Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter.

Worked example (our own example): hydro-air, two transformers, one isolation relay

A boiler zone panel has its own 40 VA transformer (circuit A). The air handler has its own 40 VA transformer (circuit B). A zone call on the panel must start the blower. We use an isolation relay K1: the coil sits on circuit A, and its normally open contacts sit in circuit B in series with the fan center's blower relay coil. The diagram shows the idea.

Assumed loads on transformer A (our own assumptions, not catalog values): three zone valves at 6 VA each, a panel board at 4 VA, and the K1 coil at 2.5 VA.

  1. Valves: 3 x 6 VA = 18 VA.
  2. Add the board: 18 + 4 = 22 VA.
  3. Add K1: 22 + 2.5 = 24.5 VA.
  4. Headroom on a 40 VA transformer: 40 - 24.5 = 15.5 VA.
  5. Share of rating used: 24.5 / 40 = 0.6125, about 61 percent.
  6. Current at 24 volts, using current = VA / volts: 24.5 / 24 = about 1.02 amps. The transformer's rated current is 40 / 24 = about 1.67 amps.
  7. K1 coil alone: 2.5 / 24 = about 0.10 amp. The contact in circuit B adds no VA to A, because A's transformer never feeds it.

Circuit B (assumed): K2 coil 3 VA plus an assumed cooling contactor coil of 6 VA plus an assumed board of 3 VA is 3 + 6 + 3 = 12 VA, or 12 / 40 = 30 percent of its transformer. Had we run A's 24 volts straight into B's board instead of using K1, the two transformers would be tied together, which is the fault described earlier.

Field card: Fan Center Relay Boards and Isolation Relays in HVAC Controls: Two Transformers, Back-Feed, Switching Line Voltage, VA Ratings and Meter Tests

One transformer per powered circuit; relays pass the signal.

  • Fan center: transformer plus blower relay; coil on 24 V, contacts on line voltage.
  • NO closes when the coil is energized; NC opens.
  • Isolation relay: coil on circuit A, dry contact into circuit B.
  • Never tie two transformer secondaries together.
  • VA = volts x amps; amps = VA / volts. Total all coils under the rating.
  • Test: coil volts on, coil ohms off, contact drop near zero when closed.
