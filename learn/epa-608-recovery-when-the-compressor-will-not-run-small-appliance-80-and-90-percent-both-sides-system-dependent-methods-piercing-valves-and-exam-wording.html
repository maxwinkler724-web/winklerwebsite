EPA 608 Recovery With a Dead Compressor: 80 Percent, Both Sides, Exam Wording

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A recovery machine can only pull refrigerant out of a system it can reach. When the compressor will not run, part of the system holds liquid and vapor with no pump to move it. This lesson covers what the federal rule expects in that case for small appliances, how larger systems differ, and how the exam words it.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. Federal text changes. We read the online federal regulations in 2026 and this is general education, not legal advice. Follow the current rule and the machine maker's directions on the job.

What the small appliance rule says

The refrigerant management subpart defines a small appliance as one that is fully manufactured, charged and hermetically sealed in a factory with five pounds of refrigerant or less. Household refrigerators, freezers, room air conditioners, dehumidifiers and vending machines fit. Before opening one, or when disposing of it, the person must recover refrigerant in one of two ways under the federal recovery rule.

  • Recover a set percentage. The text we read sets 90 percent when the compressor is functioning and 80 percent when it is not, for recovery equipment made on or after November 15, 1993. For equipment made before that date it sets 80 percent.
  • Or evacuate to four inches of mercury vacuum. The rule offers this as an alternative way to meet the requirement.

So a dead compressor lowers the percentage target from 90 to 80. Our explanation of why: a non-running system leaves more refrigerant dissolved in oil and trapped in places the machine reaches slowly.

System-dependent versus self-contained

The definitions section separates two kinds of recovery equipment. System-dependent equipment needs help from components in the appliance, typically the appliance's own compressor, to move refrigerant out. Self-contained equipment can remove refrigerant without that help. The rule says system-dependent equipment may not be used on an appliance with a full charge of more than 15 pounds, unless it is permanently attached as a pump-out unit. It also says recovery equipment must be used in line with the maker's directions unless they conflict with the subpart.

Our reading for the exam: a method that relies on the appliance's own parts fits poorly when the compressor is dead. That is our inference from the definition, not a quoted sentence, so check how your study material words it.

Why open both sides

The regulation text we read does not spell out a high-side and low-side access step for small appliances, so treat this section as our explanation of good practice. In a running system the compressor moves vapor from the low side to the high side. When it will not run, it and the metering device act as a barrier between the two halves. If you connect only one side, the other side keeps much of its charge and your recovered amount falls short. Connecting the machine to both the high and low sides gives refrigerant a path out from each half.

  • Use the service ports or process stub if the appliance has them. Many sealed units do not, which is where piercing valves come in.
  • Hook the machine to each access point with its own hose, as in the sketch, and open both before starting.
  • Watch the machine's gauge, not just the clock, so you know when each side stops giving up refrigerant.

Warming the system

Cold liquid refrigerant and oil hold on to refrigerant, so recovery from a cold appliance is slow. The rule requires heat in one place: for low-pressure appliances using a refrigerant that boils above 85 degrees Fahrenheit at atmospheric pressure, the person must use heat to raise internal pressure as much as possible. For a small appliance, the regulation text we read gave no warming step, so we offer it only as practice: let the unit come up to room temperature, use only gentle heat if the maker allows it, never an open flame, and follow the maker's directions.

Piercing valves

A piercing valve clamps on a tube and punches a small hole so the machine can connect. It gives access when a sealed unit has no port, but it can leak later. See Line Tap Piercing Valves on Small Appliances for how they work and what replaces them on a unit that stays in service. On a unit with no ports, one on each side is a common way to reach both halves.

Type II and Type III systems

Larger appliances are handled by pressure class instead of a percentage. Before opening a medium-pressure or high-pressure appliance, the rule requires evacuating, including all liquid, to the levels in its Table 1. A low-pressure appliance must instead be pressurized to no more than 0 psig before it is opened. The levels depend on pressure class, charge size and the date of the recovery machine, so use EPA 608 Type II Required Evacuation Levels for the table. For low-pressure chillers, see EPA 608 Type III Recovery From Low-Pressure Chillers. Where liquid is most of the charge on a big system, push-pull recovery can move it faster.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions (ours): a sealed small appliance with a full charge of 4.0 pounds, which is under the five-pound limit; recovery equipment made after November 15, 1993; and the two percentages from the federal recovery rule.

  1. Compressor running, 90 percent: 4.0 × 0.90 = 3.6 pounds must be recovered. Left in the system: 4.0 − 3.6 = 0.4 pound.
  2. Compressor not running, 80 percent: 4.0 × 0.80 = 3.2 pounds must be recovered. Left in the system: 4.0 − 3.2 = 0.8 pound.
  3. Check: 3.6 + 0.4 = 4.0 and 3.2 + 0.8 = 4.0. The dead compressor case allows 0.4 pound more to remain (0.8 − 0.4), which is 10 percent of 4.0.

These are minimums. A good tech keeps pulling until the gauge shows the system is done.

How the exam asks about it

  • Percentages: 90 when the compressor works, 80 when it does not, with a post-1993 machine. Know which condition goes with which number.
  • The alternative to a percentage is four inches of mercury vacuum.
  • Small appliance means five pounds or less, factory sealed.
  • System-dependent equipment is not allowed above a 15 pound full charge unless it is a permanent pump-out unit.
  • A low-pressure appliance is pressurized to 0 psig before opening, not evacuated.

Field card: Recovery with a dead compressor

Dead compressor, lower target, both sides open.

  • Small appliance: 5 pounds or less, factory sealed.
  • Compressor running: recover 90 percent. Not running: 80 percent.
  • Or evacuate to four inches of mercury vacuum.
  • System-dependent equipment needs the appliance's own parts; not above a 15 pound charge.
  • Connect both sides so neither half stays charged.
  • Low-pressure appliance: pressurize to 0 psig before opening.
