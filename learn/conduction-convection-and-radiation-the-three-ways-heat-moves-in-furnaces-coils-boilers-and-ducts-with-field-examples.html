Conduction, Convection and Radiation: The Three Ways Heat Moves in Furnaces, Coils, Boilers and Ducts

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every temperature problem you meet in the field is heat moving by one of three paths, or by all three at once. Learn to spot which path is the bottleneck and a dirty coil, a sooted heat exchanger or a sweating duct stops being a mystery.

The three paths in one sentence each

Conduction is heat moving through a solid (or a still fluid) from hot to cold with no bulk motion, like heat travelling through a steel heat-exchanger wall. Convection is heat carried by a moving fluid, like flue gas sweeping over the inside of that wall or house air blowing across its outside. Radiation is heat sent as electromagnetic waves between surfaces that can see each other, with no fluid needed, like a burner flame warming the tube wall or a radiant panel warming a room.

This lesson gives the idea behind each rate and shows where it appears in equipment. For the shop formulas with the 1.08, 0.68, 4.5 and 500 constants, see HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas Explained. For stacked wall layers see Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings, and for whole-exchanger sizing see Log Mean Temperature Difference.

Conduction: wall thickness, area, material and temperature difference

Fourier's law says the heat flow per unit area is proportional to the temperature gradient, and the constant of proportionality is the material's thermal conductivity, k (Colorado State engineering notes). For a flat wall, heat flow is Q = k × A × ΔT ÷ L. More area or a bigger temperature difference moves more heat. A thicker layer (larger L) or a lower k moves less. A thick layer of a poor conductor is the worst case, and that is what soot, scale, rust and insulation are.

Typical values show the gap. A common physics reference table lists steel near 50 W/m·K and ordinary glass near 0.8, and still air near 0.024 (HyperPhysics, Georgia State University, which warns its values are not authoritative). A national-lab measurement of a diesel-exhaust soot deposit found 0.041 W/m·K, only about 1.5 times that of air, because the cake is roughly 98 percent void space. Soot is basically a thin blanket of trapped gas.

Convection: why air speed matters

Newton's law of cooling says heat flow per unit area is proportional to the difference between the surface and fluid temperatures, and the constant is the heat transfer coefficient, h (Colorado State). Colorado State gives rough gas values of about 10 W/m²K for natural convection and about 100 W/m²K for forced convection. That order-of-magnitude jump is the reason a furnace blower, a coil fan and a hydronic pump exist: moving the fluid thins the stagnant film hugging the surface, so h goes up and the same surface passes far more heat. Slow the air (dirty filter, closed registers, loaded coil fins) and h drops with it, so the coil or exchanger moves less heat at the same temperatures.

Radiation: emissivity and the fourth power

Radiated heat grows with the fourth power of absolute temperature, scaled by the surface's emissivity (a number from 0 to 1) and the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10-8 W/m²K4 (Colorado State). Use kelvin or Rankine, never Fahrenheit. Our own arithmetic: a perfect emitter at 300 K gives 5.67e-8 × 3004 = about 459 W/m², and at 600 K it gives about 7,350 W/m², sixteen times more for double the absolute temperature. That is why radiation is minor in a duct at 120 F yet matters inside a burner compartment, and why a radiant panel or infrared tube heater works by warming surfaces rather than air. Emissivity also explains why a shiny, uninsulated metal surface and a dull painted one read differently on an infrared camera; see Thermal Imaging Cameras for HVAC.

Worked example (our own): clean steel wall versus a sooted one

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measured case. Take 1 sq ft (0.0929 m²) of heat-exchanger wall, 0.035 in thick (0.000889 m). Assumptions: steel k = 50.2 W/m·K (HyperPhysics table); soot k = 0.041 W/m·K (national-lab measurement above, taken for a furnace soot layer, which may differ); soot layer 1/16 in thick (0.0015875 m); temperature difference of 100 F, which is 55.6 K (our assumption).

  1. Thermal resistance of the steel, R = L ÷ (k × A) = 0.000889 ÷ (50.2 × 0.0929) = 0.000191 K/W.
  2. Conduction through clean steel only: Q = 55.6 ÷ 0.000191 = about 291,000 W, or roughly 994,000 Btu/h. This absurd number says that steel is not the limit. The film on each side is.
  3. Soot resistance, R = 0.0015875 ÷ (0.041 × 0.0929) = 0.417 K/W. That is about 2,200 times the steel's resistance.
  4. Steel plus soot, conduction only: Q = 55.6 ÷ (0.000191 + 0.417) = about 133 W, or 455 Btu/h. The soot alone cut conduction by more than 99.9 percent.
  5. Add one gas-side convection film, assuming h = 30 W/m²K (our assumption, between Colorado State's natural and forced values): R = 1 ÷ (30 × 0.0929) = 0.359 K/W. Clean wall: Q = 55.6 ÷ (0.000191 + 0.359) = about 155 W (528 Btu/h). Sooted wall: Q = 55.6 ÷ (0.000191 + 0.417 + 0.359) = about 71.6 W (244 Btu/h).

With a realistic film in series, the sooted wall passes about 46 percent of the clean-wall heat, a loss of more than half, from a layer you could scrape off with a brush. The numbers are illustrative only; use them to see the pattern, not to size anything. A federal boiler tip sheet (U.S. Department of Energy, cited as plain text) reports the same kind of trend for waterside scale: its table puts 1/16 in of normal scale at about 3.9 percent extra fuel use, and says scale conductivity is an order of magnitude below bare steel.

Where each path shows up in equipment

  • Furnace heat exchanger. Flame radiates to the tube wall, hot gas convects to the inside surface, heat conducts through the steel, and the blower makes air convect it away. Dirt, soot or low airflow on either side raises the metal temperature and cuts output. See Gas Furnace Heat Exchanger Types.
  • Coil fins. Heat conducts along the fin and convects into the air. Fin dirt and matted lint add a conducting layer and choke airflow, so h falls. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Boiler. Combustion side gets radiation and convection; the water side gets scale. Both foul layers act like the soot above.
  • Radiant panel or floor. A warm surface sends heat to cooler surfaces by radiation, plus some convection to room air.
  • Duct wall. Conduction through the duct and its insulation sets how much heat a supply duct loses or gains. If the outside surface drops below the dew point of the surrounding air, it sweats; see Sweating Ducts and Vents.

Field thinking: which path is the bottleneck?

Ask where the biggest temperature drop is. A big drop across a thin layer means high resistance there. A tight approach between coil and air means the surface is working well. A high leaving-flue temperature with low supply-air rise points to a heat exchanger that is not passing heat across, from fouling on either side or too little airflow. These are checks to prompt a closer look, not a diagnosis; follow the equipment maker's instructions and your site procedures.

Field card: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Fouling is a blanket; air speed thins the film; radiation needs kelvin.

  • Conduction: Q = k × A × ΔT ÷ L. Thick, low-k layers (soot, scale, insulation) block heat.
  • Convection: Q = h × A × ΔT. Forced air gives roughly ten times the h of natural convection (Colorado State, rough gas values).
  • Radiation: proportional to emissivity times absolute temperature to the fourth power.
  • Our own example: 1/16 in of soot on 0.035 in steel cut the heat flow about 54 percent once a gas film was included.
  • Clean both sides and keep airflow up before blaming the wall.
