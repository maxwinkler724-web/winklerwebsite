Heat Pump Water Heater: How Much Heat It Pulls From the Room

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A heat pump water heater does not make heat from nothing and it does not make it from electricity alone. Most of the heat that ends up in the tank came out of the air around the unit. That is why the room gets cooler and drier, and why the same machine that helps a basement in July can add to the heating bill in January.

The energy balance in one line

Energy in equals energy out. The heat delivered to the water has two sources: the electricity that runs the compressor and fan, and the heat picked up from the air. So:

Heat removed from the room = heat delivered to the water minus compressor electrical input.

Coefficient of performance (COP) is the heat delivered divided by the electrical energy in. One national-lab report defines it as a ratio of the energy delivered as hot water to the electrical energy supplied. If heat delivered is COP times input, then heat removed is (COP minus 1) times input. At COP 1 (plain resistance heating) nothing is taken from the room. At COP 2, the room supplies as much heat as the electricity does. At COP 3, the room supplies twice as much as the electricity does.

Keep the units matching. One kilowatt-hour is 3,412 Btu, and a gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds, so heating one gallon by one degree Fahrenheit takes about 8.34 Btu. Those are standard unit conversions, not data from a study.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a measurement. The assumptions are ours:

  • The household uses 60 gallons of hot water a day.
  • Incoming water is 55°F and the tank is held at 125°F, a 70°F rise.
  • The unit averages COP 2.5 over the day. We picked that because field monitoring in the Northeast found COPs of 1.5 to 2.6 (see Sources).
  • We ignore tank standby loss, to keep the arithmetic clean.
  1. Heat delivered: 60 gal × 8.34 lb/gal × 70°F = 35,028 Btu per day. Call it 35,000 Btu.
  2. Electrical input: 35,028 Btu ÷ 2.5 = 14,011 Btu, which is 14,011 ÷ 3,412 = 4.1 kWh.
  3. Heat removed from the room: 35,028 − 14,011 = 21,017 Btu per day. Check with the other form: (2.5 − 1) × 14,011 = 21,017 Btu. Same answer.
  4. Spread over 24 hours, that is about 876 Btu per hour on average, a small fraction of one ton of cooling (12,000 Btu per hour). The compressor runs only part of the day, so the real cooling comes in bursts well above that average.

Now watch what a colder room does. At COP 2.0 the same day gives 35,028 ÷ 2.0 = 17,514 Btu of input and 35,028 − 17,514 = 17,514 Btu removed. At COP 1.8, input is 19,460 Btu and removed is 15,568 Btu. A poorer COP means more electricity and less free heat from the air. The air does less of the work exactly when it is coldest.

Cooling and drying a basement

The coil that absorbs heat is cold. When the surrounding air is humid, water vapor condenses on it and drains away, so the unit also dehumidifies. In a field study of heat pump water heaters in northern homes with unconditioned basements, 70 percent of the homeowners surveyed noticed cooling, dehumidification or both. The same report found that these units are most useful in summer, since they cut cooling loads, and costly in winter, since they raise heating loads.

How much water? Our own illustration, with an assumed split: suppose 20 percent of the 21,017 Btu is latent heat, meaning the energy released as vapor turns to liquid. At an assumed 1,050 Btu per pound of water, 4,203 Btu is about 4 pounds, or about half a gallon of condensate a day. The 20 percent split is our assumption and varies with air moisture and coil temperature. Treat it as an order of magnitude only.

Do not oversell it. A national-lab report on units installed in sealed attics and closets judged them not suitable as a replacement for a dehumidifier, because the moisture drop followed the hours the unit happened to run. The cooling effect also stays local. The report found it mainly affected the mechanical closet and attic, and temperatures bounced back within minutes after the compressor stopped. A damp basement with a real moisture source still needs the fixes in the basement dehumidifier lesson.

The winter penalty inside the heated space

Everything the unit removes from a heated space is heat your furnace, boiler or heat pump must put back. The Department of Energy's consumer guidance says these units tend to cool the space they are in and will not work efficiently in a cold space. It gives 40 to 90°F as the ambient range to keep year-round and at least 1,000 cubic feet of air space around the unit.

What the studies measured:

  • A field study of basement installations estimated a maximum seasonal cooling reduction of about 1.5 million Btu against a heating increase of about 3 million Btu, so the winter effect was nearly twice the summer one.
  • A national-lab test house with a heat pump water heater in the conditioned space found it supplied 8 percent of the total cooling benefit in summer and created 17 percent of the space heating load during an unusually mild winter, so that figure is likely low for a normal winter.
  • Energy models of three small homes with an encapsulated attic or closet showed cooling energy down 9 to 11 percent and heating energy up about 11 percent.
  • A lab-home study at a national laboratory found the heating system made up roughly 43 percent of the theoretical space conditioning load, so the full penalty does not always show up in the bill. Interior temperature changes of 1 to 2°F were judged unlikely to affect comfort.

The takeaway: in a mild or cooling-dominated climate, putting the unit in conditioned space can be a small net gain. In a cold climate, the winter cost counts against it. An unheated, but not freezing, basement or garage avoids the penalty but starts colder, which lowers COP, as the example showed. For the choice between locations see choosing a water heater for a new home.

Ducting options

Ducting changes where the cold air goes. The choices are no ducting, exhaust-only, intake-only, or both:

  • Unducted. Simplest. It cools the room it sits in, which is the basement benefit in summer and the penalty in winter.
  • Exhaust-only. The cold air leaves the building. In the national-lab test house this increased heating-season space conditioning energy by about 4 percent, which the authors tied to depressurization pulling in cold outdoor air.
  • Fully ducted (intake and exhaust from outside). The same test house saw heating-season HVAC energy fall about 7.8 percent versus an unducted unit, but it also gave up the summer cooling benefit, and the long duct run needed extra fan energy.
  • Ducted to a larger volume such as a sealed attic. A 2016 national-lab report found ducting did not change the measured COP in its tests, but cautioned that intake air temperature through the year must be considered.

Field details from that report: keep ducts at least 8 inches in diameter to avoid restricting airflow, space intake and exhaust terminals at least 5 feet apart and pointed in different directions so cold exhaust does not feed the intake, and insulate exhaust ducts against condensation on the outside. That report also noted that one maker allowed ducting up to 10 feet when the free air volume of the occupied zone is below 750 cubic feet, and that running a unit unducted in a small closet went against the maker's instructions and warranty. Always follow the installation manual for the unit in front of you.

Field card: Heat Pump Water Heater Heat Removed From the Room

Heat removed = heat delivered minus electric input = (COP minus 1) times input.

  • One kWh is 3,412 Btu; one gallon rises 1°F for about 8.34 Btu.
  • Our own example: 35,000 Btu delivered at COP 2.5 means 14,000 Btu electric in and 21,000 Btu out of the room per day.
  • Lower COP means less heat taken from the air for the same hot water.
  • Summer: the room gets cooler and somewhat drier. Not a dehumidifier replacement.
  • Winter in heated space: the heating system makes the heat back up.
  • Federal consumer guidance: 40 to 90°F ambient and at least 1,000 cubic feet of air space.
  • Ducting: follow the manual; 8 inch ducts, terminals 5 feet apart, insulate the exhaust.
