Rooftop Unit PM: Quarterly and Seasonal Tasks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A packaged rooftop unit sits in sun, rain and dust and gets ignored until it fails. A written plan fixes that: short quarterly visits, a spring cooling startup, a fall gas-heat startup, and the same numbers recorded every time so drift shows up before a breakdown does.

Check the unit's own paperwork first

The data label and the maker's literature win. The federal operations and maintenance guide from DOE's Federal Energy Management Program says it defers to the manufacturer on operation and maintenance, and a federal RTU maintenance fact sheet says the same. The plan below is a general frame, not a replacement. Use trained, qualified people, and lock out and tag the disconnect before you open a panel.

Do the air side first

An ASHRAE Journal article on rooftop air conditioners orders the work by the two loops in the unit, air and refrigerant, and says to fix the air side first because charge readings are unreliable when airflow is restricted. That is why filters, belt and coil come before gauges in every visit.

The seven check points: (1) economizer dampers and sensors, (2) filters, (3) evaporator and condenser coils, (4) fan, belt and bearings, (5) gas heat section, (6) compressor and electrical, (7) condensate drain and trap.

A schedule you can adopt

Sources agree on the rhythm, not one fixed list. PNNL's RTU field-test report says industry standards call for filter changes every 3 to 6 months and general servicing once a year. A 2006 ACHR News article says quarterly or semiannual service is common, with work at the start of both the cooling and heating seasons, and filters monthly in some applications. A belt-tension article suggests checking tension every three to six months. From those, our own plan:

WhenTasks (our own plan)
Every quarterFilters, belt look and tension, drain and trap, quick look at coils, damper movement, amps
Spring startupQuarterly list plus coil cleaning, refrigerant readings, economizer function test, contactors, bearings
Fall startupQuarterly list plus gas-heat startup, heat exchanger look, flame, temperature rise, combustion and CO check

The spring homeowner version is in Get Your AC Ready for Summer. The light-commercial version below adds the parts a rooftop has.

Filters and pressure drop

A filter's resistance to air is measured in inches of water gauge. An ACHR News filter-selection article defines final resistance as the point at which a filter should be replaced, and warns that pressure drop affects energy cost. In the field, read the drop across the filter bank with a manometer when the filters are clean, write it on the unit, and compare each visit. Change at the final value the filter or unit literature gives, or sooner if filters are visibly loaded. The same maintenance article says dirt can cut evaporator airflow by as much as 35 percent.

Belt, sheaves and bearings

An HPAC article gives a rule: deflection of 1/64 inch for every inch of belt span, so a 32 inch span gives 1/2 inch. It also says to retension a new V-belt after one to two days and to use a straight edge across the sheave rims for alignment. Replace belts that are cracked or frayed. PNNL lists bearing lubrication and pulley and belt checks as part of annual servicing. Basics are in Belt Drives and Sheave Basics.

Coils, drain and trap

The ACHR News 2006 article says to clean evaporator and condenser coils with a nonacidic cleaner and water, check the condensate drain, and look at the P-trap for cracks, because a faulty trap prevents proper drainage and a clogged drain can leak through the base pan. Trap depth for draw-through units is covered in AC Coil Condensate Traps.

Economizer

The same article says to test operation, linkage and full-stroke actuator movement, verify the limiting sensors and minimum outside-air setting, and clean and check damper blades, bushings, linkage and gaskets for a seal. Settings themselves are covered in Economizer Settings. Fault detection software finds some of these problems; see AFDD for RTUs.

Refrigerant circuit and electrical

Check charge against the maker's guidelines only after the air side is right. PNNL lists refrigerant charge checks, condenser coil temperature drop and amperage draw in annual servicing. ACHR News says to record amperages and compare them with the data label, look for overheated wires and worn contactors, and that loose connections can seriously damage a compressor. Sequence background is in RTU Cooling Sequence.

Fall gas-heat startup

ACHR News recommends a combustion analysis at every inspection, with CO and efficiency as key readings, and says to check the heat exchanger for blockage after limit cycling. For flame rectification it says the signal should usually not fall below 1.5 microamps and normally runs 3 to 7. Set the blower so temperature rise lands near the middle of the nameplate range. Another ACHR News article gives the output formula: cfm times temperature rise times 1.08. See also the safety chain for how limits and switches act.

Roof safety

OSHA's general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.28, requires protection for an employee on a surface with an unprotected side or edge 4 feet or more above a lower level, using guardrails, safety nets or personal fall protection. The construction rule, 29 CFR 1926.501, uses 6 feet and, for low-slope roofing work, adds warning-line combinations. Which rule applies depends on the work, so ask your safety lead. Plan the access route, the edge distance and the anchor before you carry tools up.

Worked example

Belt. Rule from the HPAC article: 1/64 inch per inch of span. Span is 32 inches (assumption for this example). 32 x 1/64 = 32/64 = 0.5 inch deflection target.

Gas heat. Assumptions (ours): 3,000 cfm, return 65 F, supply 110 F, nameplate input 180,000 Btu/h, 81 percent efficiency.

  1. Temperature rise = 110 - 65 = 45 F.
  2. Delivered heat = 1.08 x 3,000 x 45 = 3,240 x 45 = 145,800 Btu/h.
  3. Expected output = 180,000 x 0.81 = 145,800 Btu/h.

The two match exactly, which is a tidy teaching number; real readings scatter. The ACHR News example counted 62,485 against a 64,000 nameplate, about 2.4 percent low, as within 10 percent. Also compare the 45 F rise to the nameplate range: ours is assumed to be 35 to 65 F, so 45 is below the 50 F midpoint. Per ACHR News aim for the middle, so the fan could slow slightly. Slower airflow raises rise, so recheck.

Field card: Rooftop Unit PM

Air side first, record every number, lock out before panels.

  • Quarterly: filters, belt tension, drain and trap, damper motion, amps.
  • Spring: coils, refrigerant readings, economizer test, contactors.
  • Fall: heat exchanger, flame signal, temperature rise, combustion and CO.
  • Belt deflection 1/64 in per inch of span; 32 in span is 0.5 in.
  • Heat output = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise.
  • Roof edge: OSHA 4 ft (general industry), 6 ft (construction).
