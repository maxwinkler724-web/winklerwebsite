Firestopping HVAC Pipe and Duct Penetrations: Listed Systems and What Not to Disturb

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every pipe, conduit and duct that crosses a fire-rated wall or floor is a hole in a barrier. A listed firestop system closes that hole. When a tech reroutes a line or pulls old pipe, the system has to be put back the way it was tested, or the barrier no longer does its job.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Codes, listings and owner procedures vary. This lesson is general education built from public pages read in 2026. The listing for the actual system on the job, and the authority having jurisdiction, decide what is acceptable.

Rated assemblies and firestop systems

A fire-rated assembly is a wall, floor or ceiling built and tested to hold back fire for a stated time, such as one or two hours. A through-penetration firestop system is the combination of the opening, the penetrating item, the fill material and any sleeve or collar that was tested together. An ICC firestopping primer says the International Building Code requires testing under ASTM E814 or UL 1479 (test standards, named here in plain text) with a minimum pressure difference of 0.01 inch of water. Ratings belong to the whole system. A listing agency's guide information, as reposted by a contractors' association, states that individual components are not rated and cannot be swapped between systems.

F and T ratings in plain terms

  • F rating: how long the system stops flame passing through and survives a hose-stream test.
  • T rating: the same, plus a limit on heat. The temperature rise on the unexposed side is held to 325°F above starting temperature, per that a listing agency's guide information.
  • L and W ratings: optional air-leakage and water-resistance ratings that some listings add.

The ICC primer says the F and T ratings must be at least one hour and no less than the rating of the floor being penetrated. A federal design specification we read waives the T rating for small openings, up to 4 inch nominal pipe or 16 square inches of area, but treat that as that document's rule, not a universal one. The authority having jurisdiction decides.

Match the listing, not the look

A listing is a drawing plus notes. Its system number tells you the assembly and the penetrant. In the numbering described in the association copy, the first letter is F (floor), W (wall) or C (either), and the four-digit range tells the penetrant: 1000s are metallic pipe, 2000s nonmetallic pipe, 5000s insulated pipe and 7000s miscellaneous mechanical items such as air ducts. Change the wall type, pipe material, pipe size, insulation, fill product or depth and you are outside the listing. A firestop trade association inspection guide says a modification to a tested system voids its rating.

Metal pipe, plastic pipe and insulated pipe

  • Metal pipe (steel, copper) does not burn away, so the gap stays the same size. The ICC primer says non-intumescent materials suit metal pipe.
  • Plastic pipe (condensate drains, some vent and chilled water piping) melts or burns out. Listed systems use intumescent collars or wrap strips that swell when heated and close the void. The same primer says some intumescents swell up to 35 times their original size.
  • Insulated pipe has its own listings. The federal specification says to strip pipe insulation for 6 inches each side of the barrier unless the product is approved for insulated pipe. Follow the listing for how insulation or jacket passes through. Insulation basics are in our pipe insulation lesson.

Ducts

A duct through a rated barrier usually has a fire damper, covered in our fire damper lesson, so we do not repeat that here. The firestop piece is the gap between the duct or damper sleeve and the wall opening, sealed with the angles, fill and sealant that the damper maker and the listing call for. A city membrane-penetration guideline likewise requires a listed firestop assembly where a fire-wrapped duct passes through a rated assembly. Never fill these gaps with spray foam or leftover caulk.

Sleeves and annular space

A sleeve is a pipe or form set in the wall or floor so lines can pass later. The annular space is the gap between the penetrant and the edge of the opening. The association copy says each system lists a minimum and/or maximum annular space that must be kept to get the rating. For a round pipe in a round sleeve, measure from pipe surface to sleeve wall. With several penetrants in one opening, measure from the closest point of one to the next. An empty sleeve is not exempt: an unused hole needs its own listed closure.

Worked example: annular space check

Assumptions (ours, for practice only): a listing allows a minimum annular space of 1/2 inch and a maximum of 1-1/2 inches for copper pipe; the pipe is 2-1/8 inch outside diameter (2 inch nominal copper); the sleeve inside diameter is 4 inches. Your real listing will differ.

  1. Total gap across the sleeve: 4.000 − 2.125 = 1.875 inches.
  2. If the pipe is centered, each side is 1.875 ÷ 2 = 0.9375 inch. That is above 0.5 and below 1.5, so it passes.
  3. If the pipe sags until it rests on the sleeve bottom, the bottom gap is 0 inch (below the minimum) and the top gap is 1.875 inches (above the maximum). It fails at both ends.

The fix is not more sealant. Support the pipe so it stays centered (see our pipe support lesson) or use a listing that covers the real gap.

Labels and inspectors

Many sites tag each installed system with its listing number, rating and installer. According to the inspection guide, inspectors check that products carry testing-agency marks, that each installed system matches an approved listing, that annular space and fill depth match the details, and that nothing was concealed before approval. The ICC primer says buildings over 75 feet get a special inspector for firestop since the 2012 code. Typical misses: wrong product for the pipe type, sealant missing on one face, packing pushed out, and unlabeled repairs.

When service work disturbs a firestop

  • Do not cut or core a rated wall or floor without the owner's permission and a plan to firestop it. Find out which walls are rated before you drill.
  • Adding a line: the new line needs a listed system for its pipe type and size. The existing system may not allow another penetrant, since a listing covers specific penetrants.
  • Removing a line or old insulation: the hole is still open. Close it with a listed system for what remains, which may be a blank-opening listing, not just the old fill.
  • Found missing or damaged firestop: do not patch it with unrated material. Photograph it, note the location, and tell the building owner or manager in writing.
  • Above-ceiling work: many owners require a permit or an infection-control risk assessment for ceiling access. See our dust containment lesson. Ask before you lift tiles.

The federal specification asks that firestop work not be hidden until it is inspected, so wait for sign-off before you insulate or close a ceiling. Fire and smoke control systems work together; see our smoke control lesson too.

Field card: Firestopping HVAC penetrations

Same listing, same parts, or it is not firestop.

  • F rating: stops flame. T rating: also limits heat on the far side (325°F rise per a listing agency's guide information).
  • Ratings belong to the whole tested system, not to a product alone.
  • Plastic pipe needs intumescent devices. Metal pipe uses non-intumescent fill.
  • Check the listing's minimum and maximum annular space; center the pipe.
  • Never foam or caulk a rated hole. Never conceal before inspection.
  • Disturbed a firestop? Restore with a listed system and tell the owner.
