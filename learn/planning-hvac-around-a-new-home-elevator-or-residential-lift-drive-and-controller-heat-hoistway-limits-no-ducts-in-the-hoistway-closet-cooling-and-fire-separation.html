Planning HVAC Around a New Home Elevator or Residential Lift

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A home elevator adds a tall shaft, a motor, a drive and a controller to a house, and each of those touches the heating, cooling and ventilation plan. This lesson covers what to settle early: where the heat comes from, whether the hoistway sits inside the conditioned space, what to ask the elevator installer and the local inspector, and how to keep ducts out of places they do not belong.

Check the maker and the inspector first

Check first. Elevator rules differ by state and town, and each maker publishes its own limits. This page is general education built from government pages read in 2026. It is not design advice, and it does not replace the installation instructions or the local inspector.

Where the heat comes from

A residential elevator has a motor, a drive or pump unit, and a controller. Every one of them wastes some power as heat. The waste is small when the car is parked and large for the short moments the car moves. A state elevator program guide points out that machine-room-less units often need no dedicated system because moving hoistway air and door cycling keep temperature and humidity acceptable, but that added ventilation, exhaust, heating or cooling may be needed if the hoistway is unheated or unconditioned, the elevator gets heavy use, or the suspension needs a minimum hoistway temperature. That guide is written for larger elevators and does not mention residential ones, so we use it only as a picture of the questions to ask.

Is the hoistway inside the conditioned space?

A federal energy-code technical page separates conditioned space (heated or cooled, or indirectly conditioned through connection to space that is) from unconditioned space (neither). A hoistway that runs through a heated hallway behaves like part of the house. A hoistway on an exterior wall, or one that passes through an unheated attic or crawl space, may see winter cold and summer heat that the maker never intended. Ask three plain questions: what temperature and humidity range does the maker list, does the shaft stay in that range in January and August, and who is responsible if it does not? Get the maker range in writing. We do not state a number here because limits vary by model.

Keep supply registers and ducts out of the hoistway

Do not run a supply register, a return, a duct or a refrigerant line through or into the hoistway as a convenience. Three reasons stand out. First, a state elevator-program guide says air-conditioning evaporators and condensate drains may not be located over elevator equipment. Second, a hoistway is a fire-resistance-rated enclosure in the building code, and an interpretation from one state elevator program says openings and penetrations in a rated enclosure must be handled under the rules for fire-stopping. A duct through that wall is a penetration. Third, a register blowing into a shaft pulls conditioned air out of the house and can move smoke or odors between floors. If the elevator maker wants the shaft warmed or cooled, that is the installer's approved method and the inspector's call, not a duct stub added by the HVAC crew.

Machine room or controller closet

The elevator safety code does not require a machine room for a private residence elevator, according to a state technical clarification. Where one is provided, it says the electrical-code working clearances apply, including at least 36 inches of depth in front of equipment for a typical single-family voltage, and that the reduced clearances for trained-person-only rooms do not apply to a single-family home. The same clarification says nothing about ventilation, temperature or fire rating, and a state residential field checklist we read does not mention them either. That gap is exactly why the question belongs on your list: if the controller sits in a closed closet, someone must decide how its heat leaves. Also check that clearances in front of the panel are not lost when a transfer grille or fan is added.

Worked example (our own): can a closed controller closet stay cool?

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a maker rating. Assumptions: drive and controller lose 250 W while the car moves; a pump or traction motor in the same closet loses 400 W while moving; the equipment loses 25 W while parked; 20 trips a day at 45 seconds each; the house is at 78°F; we want the closet no hotter than 100°F, a rise of 22°F. These limits are our assumptions for practice.

  1. Heat while moving: 250 W + 400 W = 650 W. In Btu/h: 650 × 3.412 = 2,218 Btu/h.
  2. Moving time per day: 20 × 45 s = 900 s = 0.25 h.
  3. Energy per day: moving 650 W × 0.25 h = 162.5 Wh; parked 25 W × 23.75 h = 593.75 Wh; total about 756 Wh.
  4. Average heat: 756 Wh ÷ 24 h = 31.5 W, or 31.5 × 3.412 = 107.5 Btu/h.
  5. Airflow for the average: the usual sensible-heat relation for air is Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise (1.08 comes from 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb°F × 60 min). So cfm = 107.5 ÷ (1.08 × 22) = 107.5 ÷ 23.76 = 4.5 cfm.
  6. Sizing to the moving peak instead: 2,218 ÷ 23.76 = 93 cfm. That would be oversized for a closet that runs hot for under a minute at a time.
  7. Check the air alone: 650 W for 45 s is 29,250 J, or about 27.7 Btu (29,250 × 0.000948). A 30 ft³ closet holds 2.25 lb of air, which stores 0.54 Btu per °F, so the air by itself would climb about 51°F in one trip. In real life the equipment, walls and door absorb much of that, so we do not trust the air number or the average alone.

Reading the result. The average load is tiny (4.5 cfm), but a short trip can still spike a sealed closet, and back-to-back trips stack up. A sensible plan is a passive transfer opening or two sized to give a small multiple of the average airflow, plus a thermostat-controlled fan if the installer or maker asks for one. Treat 20 to 25 cfm as a first check on our assumptions, then replace every input with the maker's published heat loss and duty. If the closet is not open to cooler house air, the true answer is the maker's number, not ours.

Smoke and fire separation

An interpretation from one state elevator program says the building code sets the hoistway enclosure rating (typically one or two hours in the version it cites), that machine rooms need a rated enclosure when the building code requires fire-resistive construction, and that a door in a rated wall must carry a matching rating unless listed exceptions apply. A state residential checklist adds that hoistway doors must have a fire rating at least equal to the hoistway rating. Any grille, fan opening or transfer duct added to the closet wall can break that rating, so ask the inspector before cutting. The state guide also notes that fire dampers at openings in rated walls or ceilings go to the plan reviewer, and that smoke-control pressurization of a hoistway is allowed only under the building code and must be tested. Those are commercial examples; apply the idea, not the details, to a home.

Questions to bring to the installer and the inspector

  • What temperature and humidity range does the maker require, and where is it printed?
  • What is the heat loss of the drive, controller and motor, and how many trips per day does that assume?
  • Is the hoistway inside the conditioned space, and what happens at the top if the attic is cold or hot?
  • May we add a transfer grille or fan to the closet wall, and what fire rating must stay intact?
  • Is the closet allowed to be a storage closet, or must the working clearance stay empty?
  • Who runs power for any closet fan, and does it need a separate circuit or standby power?

A federal accessibility guide notes that private residence elevators are permitted only within a dwelling unit (or serving one private residence in a multi-unit building) and must have automatic operation, power-operated doors, automatic leveling and a two-way emergency communication device. That guide does not address machine rooms or ventilation, so it will not answer the HVAC questions above.

Field card: Planning HVAC Around a New Home Elevator

Get the maker limits in writing, then keep ducts out of the shaft.

  • Drive, controller and motor lose power as heat, mostly while the car moves.
  • Ask whether the hoistway is inside conditioned space and what range the maker allows.
  • No supply, return or refrigerant line into the hoistway; no coil or drain over elevator equipment.
  • Our example: 650 W moving, 20 trips of 45 s a day, 31.5 W average, about 4.5 cfm average at a 22°F rise.
  • Any new opening in a rated wall needs the inspector's answer first.
  • Keep working clearance in front of the panel clear.
