Finding a Liquid Line Restriction by Temperature Drop Across the Filter-Drier

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Liquid refrigerant leaving a condenser should reach the metering device as solid liquid. A plugged drier, a pinched line or a dirty valve inside the liquid line can wreck that, and the pipe temperature will tell on it. This lesson shows how to read the temperature change across a liquid line part, what it means, and how to replace a drier without breaking the rules.

Why a restriction makes liquid flash

Refrigerant boils at a temperature that depends on its pressure. Lower the pressure and the boiling (saturation) temperature falls with it. Leaving the condenser, liquid is a few degrees below its saturation temperature. That gap is subcooling, and it is a safety margin. When a part in the liquid line restricts flow, pressure drops across it. If the pressure falls far enough that the saturation temperature drops below the liquid's actual temperature, some liquid boils on the spot. That is flash gas. Boiling absorbs heat, so the refrigerant leaving the restriction is colder than the refrigerant entering it, and the pipe after the restriction cools down to the new, lower saturation temperature.

That cooling is the clue. A healthy liquid line is nearly the same temperature end to end. A trade article on liquid line diagnosis notes that across a typical liquid line you generally see only a few degrees of temperature drop, depending on ambient, condensing temperature and line length. A part that cools the line noticeably is doing something it should not. For the cause of pressure loss from line length and lift, see Liquid Line Pressure Drop, Vertical Lift and Flash Gas. This lesson is about finding a restriction that should not be there.

Measuring the drop with matched clamps

  1. Use two clamps that agree. Before the job, hold both probes together on the same pipe (or in the same ice bath) and compare. If they differ, note the difference and correct for it. A trade article on restrictions recommends your most accurate line clamps for this check.
  2. Clean the pipe contact. Wipe off dirt and oxide, press the clamp flat against bare copper and wrap it with insulation so room air does not skew the reading.
  3. Read the inlet, then the outlet. Place one clamp on the pipe just before the drier and the other just after it. Let both settle for a few minutes while the system is running steadily.
  4. Compare to ambient. A liquid line should not be colder than the air around it. One trade article suggests checking the line about 12 inches ahead of the TXV: if it is colder than ambient, a restriction upstream is the likely cause.

The same trade source says that if you find any confirmable temperature drop across a drier, you can treat it as restricted, then double-check. That fits the physics: a clear drier has almost no pressure drop and so almost no temperature change. Keep in mind that your hand cannot do this job. One article states that people can distinguish a temperature difference only above about 10°F across something, and that on an R-134a system at a 110°F condensing temperature a drier would need about 20 psi of pressure drop to show 10°F. A clamp reads small changes your fingers miss.

How big a drop means trouble

We found no published single number that applies to every drier and refrigerant, so treat the clamp reading as a pointer, not a verdict. A drop of a degree or two may be clamp error or heat loss to the room. A clear drop that you can repeat, with the outlet colder than the inlet and sometimes colder than ambient, points to a restriction. The firmer test is pressure. Many driers have a service port on each side, and the same trade article says a pressure drop of more than 2 psi measured with the same gauge means the drier has started to restrict. Use the temperature drop to find the suspect part and the pressure reading to back it up.

Other clues on the outside of the line

  • Frost or cold spot. A severe restriction can flash liquid at the drier and leave a local cold spot, or frost, right after it. Sweat (condensation) on just the outlet end of a drier, when the rest of the line is dry, tells the same story, though we did not find a published reading for sweat, so use it only as a prompt to measure.
  • Sight glass after the drier. A sight glass downstream can show a drier starting to plug. Bubbles after a restriction are expected, but the same article warns bubbles alone can also mean undercharge, a low receiver level or lost subcooling. Do not add refrigerant just because you see bubbles. For how to read the glass itself, see Filter Driers and Sight Glasses: What They Tell You.
  • Freeze pattern at the coil. On a healthy system, frost starts at the metering device outlet. Frost that begins before the metering device suggests a restricted inlet screen.

The symptom pattern

For a liquid line restriction on a system without a receiver, one trade source lists low suction pressure, high superheat, and normal to high subcooling. Normal or high subcooling argues against a low charge, and high superheat argues against an airflow problem. Another article on a partially plugged drier adds high discharge temperature, low condensing pressure, a low condenser split, low amp draw and short-cycling on the low-pressure control. Both say head pressure is not typically high with a restriction, which surprises many people. The evaporator is being starved, so the compressor moves less refrigerant. Compare this to a plain undercharge in Undercharge vs Overcharge Symptom Table, and review the terms in Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a field measurement. Assumptions: an R-134a medium-temperature system with a TXV and no receiver, running steadily; ambient air at 85°F; condensing temperature of 110°F; and, from the trade article above, about 20 psi of pressure drop lowers the saturation temperature by about 10°F near 110°F. We assume that ratio holds across the drop in this example.

  1. Clamp A (drier inlet) reads 100°F. Condensing temperature is 110°F, so subcooling is 110 - 100 = 10°F.
  2. Clamp B (drier outlet) reads 86°F. The drop is 100 - 86 = 14°F, far more than the few degrees of a normal liquid line.
  3. Outlet is 86°F against 85°F ambient, so the line after the drier is no longer warmer than the room.
  4. Check against our assumption: a 14°F fall in saturation temperature at 20 psi per 10°F is about 14 x 2 = 28 psi of pressure drop, well past the 2 psi the article gives as the start of restriction.
  5. The inlet liquid had 10°F of subcooling. The restriction removed more than that, so liquid flashed. That is why you would also see bubbles in a sight glass placed after the drier.

With the gauges on, we would expect low suction pressure, high superheat and normal or high subcooling at the condenser, matching the symptom list above. This points to a plugged drier. Conclusion: confirm with a pressure reading across the drier, then replace it. The cause matters too. If you are looking at burnout debris or acid, work through Compressor Burnout Cleanup before putting new parts in.

Replacing the drier safely

  1. Lock out power to the equipment and confirm it is dead before you touch refrigerant lines. This is our own safety step.
  2. Recover first, never vent. Under the federal rule for stationary appliances (40 CFR 82.156), refrigerant, including liquid, must be removed down to the required vacuum level before opening the appliance, using recovery equipment certified under the rule. You may recover from the whole appliance or from just the part being serviced if that part can be isolated into a system receiver, and you must confirm the required level before opening.
  3. Keep the new drier sealed until the moment of installation. One maker guide says not to unpack driers or cores until immediately before installation.
  4. Match the arrow. The same guide says the drier must be installed with flow in the direction of the arrow on its label. Use the same type and size, unless the maker's data for your system says otherwise.
  5. Evacuate before restart. The guide says moisture must be removed by evacuation before startup. Pull a deep vacuum and confirm it with a gauge, then recheck the temperature drop after the system runs. A repaired line should be within a few degrees end to end.

On heat pumps with a drier that works in both directions, see Heat Pump Metering in Both Directions, since the checks are different there.

Field card: Liquid Line Restriction by Temperature Drop

Liquid line should be about one temperature end to end. Colder after a part means a restriction.

  • Pressure drop lowers saturation temperature, so liquid flashes and the pipe cools.
  • Check two clamps against each other first, then read just before and just after the drier.
  • A normal liquid line shows only a few degrees of change; a repeatable drop at a drier points to restriction.
  • Back it up with pressure: more than 2 psi across a drier is the start of restriction.
  • Symptoms: low suction, high superheat, normal or high subcooling, head pressure not high.
  • Bubbles after the drier alone do not mean add refrigerant.
  • Recover to the required level before opening; fit the drier with the flow arrow; evacuate before restart.
