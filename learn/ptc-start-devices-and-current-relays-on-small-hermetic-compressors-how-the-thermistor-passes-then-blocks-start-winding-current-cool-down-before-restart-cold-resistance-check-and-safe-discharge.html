PTC Start Devices and Current Relays on Small Compressors

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Open the electrical cover on the back of a small refrigerator, freezer or dehumidifier compressor and you often find a small plastic box plugged straight onto the pins, with no moving contacts inside. That box is a PTC start device. This lesson explains what it does, how it differs from a start relay, and how to check it safely.

Why a small compressor needs a start device

Most small hermetic compressors use a single-phase motor with a run winding and a second, start winding. A state university engineering tech note says the start winding has higher resistance than the run winding and that it is not rated for continuous operation. A switching device must therefore take it out of the circuit once the motor is up to speed. In the older centrifugal-switch motors the note puts that at about 80 percent of full speed; a university lecture note gives a typical 75 percent. A sealed compressor has no room for a centrifugal switch, so the start device sits outside the shell (or, in some designs, inside it).

How a PTC passes current, then blocks it

PTC means positive temperature coefficient: the part's resistance goes up, sharply, as it gets hot. A patent describing a refrigerator compressor start relay shows the PTC element as a small disc with conducting plates on both faces, wired in series with the start winding. Here is the sequence, using figures from a patent for one PTC start design (a 100 V design, so treat the numbers as one example, not a rule):

  1. Cold. The disc has low resistance. The patent gives about 3.3 to 47 ohms for the starting PTC. Current flows through the start winding, up to about 10 A for roughly half a second to several seconds in that design.
  2. Self-heating. That current heats the disc itself. No outside heater is involved.
  3. Blocking. As the disc warms, its resistance climbs. The patent says the start-winding current falls to about 20 to 30 mA once the motor is running.
  4. Holding. A trickle of current keeps the disc hot for as long as the compressor runs. The patent says a conventional PTC drew about 2 to 4 W while holding.

That small holding current is why a PTC-equipped compressor draws a little extra even in steady running. Another patent notes the PTC disc may be placed near the shell wall or in the path of cold refrigerant gas in one design, so where the part sits changes how fast it cools.

Why it needs minutes to cool before a restart

The same patent says the PTC disc has a large heat capacity and can take about 20 to 30 seconds to several minutes to cool. Until it cools, its resistance stays high and it will not pass start current, so a compressor that tries to restart right after shutting off may only hum or not start. The tech note supports the idea that start windings are fragile: it says insufficient cooling time between starts can overheat the start winding. In practice this is why a freezer that was just unplugged should be left alone for several minutes before it is plugged back in.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This is our own example with assumed values. Assume a PTC reads 10 ohms cold, the supply is 120 V, and, for simplicity, we ignore the start winding's own resistance. Assume the hot value is 10,000 ohms.

  1. Cold current: I = V / R = 120 / 10 = 12 A.
  2. Cold power in the disc: P = V x I = 120 x 12 = 1,440 W (the same answer from V squared / R = 14,400 / 10).
  3. That heat goes into a small disc, so it warms in a moment and its resistance climbs.
  4. Hot current: I = 120 / 10,000 = 0.012 A, or 12 mA.
  5. Hot power: P = 120 x 0.012 = 1.44 W, a thousandth of the starting figure.

Real start windings add resistance, so real start current is lower than 12 A; the point is the ratio. Raising the resistance 1,000 times cuts the current 1,000 times. Our 12 mA lands in the same range as the 20 to 30 mA the patent gives for its own design, which makes the assumed numbers reasonable for teaching.

Current relays and potential relays in general terms

Some small compressors use a relay instead of a PTC. Our own plain-language summary: a current-type relay has a coil in series with the run winding; the large current drawn at start pulls the contacts closed, connecting the start winding, and as the motor speeds up and current drops, the contacts open. A potential relay works from voltage across the start winding rather than current, and is used on larger or harder starting loads. We cover that type in the related lesson on hard start kits. Both do the same job as the centrifugal switch: take the start winding out once the motor is up to speed.

Start capacitors and overload protectors in brief

Where a capacitor is used, it is in series with the start winding. A university lecture note shows a capacitor chosen so the start-winding current leads the run-winding current by 90 degrees, which gives the best starting torque. The tech note says these are usually dry electrolytic capacitors not rated for continuous duty, so frequent starts can overheat and fail them. An overload protector, a heat- and current-sensitive switch in the supply leg, opens when the compressor draws too much for too long and closes again after it cools. The related lessons below cover both in depth.

Measuring resistance cold

  1. Unplug the appliance. Do not rely on a switch or thermostat being off.
  2. Wait for the PTC to cool fully, several minutes at least; a warm disc reads high and looks falsely bad.
  3. Discharge any capacitor before touching terminals.
  4. Pull the start device off the compressor pins and read it on an ohms range. A cold, healthy PTC should read low, in the range of the patent's 3.3 to 47 ohms, and the maker's data gives the right figure for the part.
  5. An open or very high cold reading means a failed disc. A reading near zero can also be a fault; compare with the maker's value.

Winding checks are in the related lesson on checking compressor windings.

Safety: unplug and discharge

  • Unplug the appliance and keep the plug in sight.
  • Treat a capacitor as live until you have discharged it with a proper resistor-type discharge tool or procedure, then confirm with a meter.
  • A federal consumer product safety page about microwave ovens warns that the shock hazard can remain after the unit is unplugged and recommends professional repair. Our own reading: the same caution applies to any appliance with a stored-charge part.
  • Know your limits. Sealed-system work on a refrigerator needs training and a refrigerant certification.

Field card: PTC start devices

Low cold, high hot; wait before restart.

  • The PTC is in series with the start winding.
  • Cold it passes start current; self-heating then raises its resistance and it blocks current.
  • Allow several minutes to cool before a restart or a cold ohm reading.
  • Our own example: 10 ohms gives 12 A and 1,440 W; 10,000 ohms gives 12 mA.
  • Unplug and discharge capacitors before touching anything.
