Supply Air Temperature Reset on VAV Air Handlers: Ranges, Humidity, Reheat and Fan Energy

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most VAV air handlers are commissioned with a fixed 55 F supply air setpoint and never touched again. That works on a design day. On a mild day it makes the boxes reheat air the cooling coil just chilled. Supply air temperature (SAT) reset lets the setpoint float with outdoor conditions or zone demand. This lesson covers how, how far, and what it costs in fan energy.

Why a fixed 55 F wastes energy

A VAV box can only cool by delivering at least its minimum airflow of supply air, and it can only heat by adding reheat to that same air. When the outdoor air is mild and loads are light, many boxes sit at minimum airflow. If the air handler still makes 55 F air, the reheat coils warm it back up. The PNNL re-tuning guide describes the goal: in cold weather raise the supply temperature as far as possible without pushing many boxes into cooling, and in warm weather lower it as far as possible without pushing boxes into heating. A PNNL modeling report for large offices uses a constant 55 F setpoint as its baseline and says the savings from reset come mainly from reduced reheat in the shoulder and heating seasons.

Two common reset strategies

Outdoor air based. One sensor, easy to program. In the PNNL large-office report the setpoint is 55 F when outdoor air is above 75 F, 60 F when it is below 45 F, and varies in a straight line between. The figure below draws that schedule. The same report gives whole-building HVAC savings from 2 percent (Miami) to 19 percent (Duluth) for this strategy. The re-tuning guide cautions that outdoor-air reset may capture only about half the savings of a zone-feedback approach.

Zone demand (trim and respond). The air handler collects requests from the zones. ASHRAE Guideline 36 describes this approach, and the public Modelica Buildings Library documentation of its sequence shows default parameters: a 600 second delay, a 120 second sample period, 2 ignored requests, a trim of 0.1 K and a response of minus 0.2 K, capped at minus 0.6 K per interval. In plain words, the setpoint creeps warmer while few zones complain, and drops faster when several zones ask for more cooling. The PNNL re-tuning guide calls zone-demand reset preferred and prefers combining cooling and heating demand so you can tell when the setpoint has gone too far in either direction. Another PNNL setup (same large-office report) steps the setpoint up 0.2 F with no cooling requests and down 0.3 F when more than two zones request cooling, within limits of 55 and 65 F for outdoor air below 65 F.

The related lesson on duct static pressure reset uses the same trim and respond idea on the fan.

Typical ranges

The PNNL re-tuning guide says that for air handlers with zone-level reheat, 55 F minimum to 65 F maximum is usually a good range. It allows a 70 F maximum if zones stay mostly in heating at 65 F, and a 53 F minimum in humid climates for extra dehumidification. The Modelica documentation describes the cooling-mode maximum as typically 18 C in mild, dry climates and 16 C or lower in humid climates; by our own conversion that is about 64 F and 61 F. The Washington State energy code (Section C403.6.4, shown on the state legislature site) requires multiple-zone systems to reset supply air temperature by at least 25 percent of the difference between design supply air and design room air temperature, with exceptions that include zones with peak supply airflow of 300 cfm or less. Check the code your jurisdiction has adopted.

Humidity and dew point limits

None of the public sources we read give a dew point number for reset. Our own explanation: the cooling coil is also your dehumidifier. If you raise the supply temperature in humid weather, the coil runs warmer, removes less moisture, and the space humidity rises. That is why the sources hold the minimum lower (53 F) or the maximum lower (16 C or less) in humid climates. Treat the reset as something that must lose to a humidity override. Watch space or return air humidity on the trend, and if it climbs, lock the reset back toward the design supply setpoint. See also the economizer lesson.

The fan energy tradeoff

Warmer supply air carries less cooling per cfm, so the boxes open up and total airflow rises. The PNNL guide warns that cooling with warmer air requires more airflow, which raises fan power. The U.S. Department of Energy states that fan input power varies as the cube of the speed ratio, so flow changes are expensive at the fan. The fan laws lesson explains where that comes from. In the PNNL report, constant-setpoint changes raised fan energy slightly as supply temperature went up, while cooling and reheat energy fell.

Worked example

All inputs below are our own assumptions, chosen to make the arithmetic clear, not field data. We use the common sensible heat formula for standard air, Q = 1.08 x cfm x dT. (Our own: 1.08 = 0.075 lb/ft3 x 60 min/h x 0.24 Btu/lb-F.)

Assumptions: a zone with a sensible cooling load of 10,800 Btu/h and a 75 F room setpoint.

  1. At 55 F supply: dT = 75 - 55 = 20 F. cfm = 10,800 / (1.08 x 20) = 10,800 / 21.6 = 500 cfm.
  2. At 60 F supply: dT = 15 F. cfm = 10,800 / (1.08 x 15) = 10,800 / 16.2 = 667 cfm (rounded).
  3. Flow ratio = 667 / 500 = 1.33.
  4. Fan power ratio by the cube law = 1.33 cubed = 2.37. If every zone behaved like this one and the fan followed the cube law with no static pressure change, a fan drawing 10.0 kW would draw about 23.7 kW. That is our own arithmetic, and a worst case.

Real systems do better because duct static pressure reset trims the fan, and not every zone is at full load. Now the reheat side, again with our own assumptions: a box at its minimum of 300 cfm that needs 70 F discharge air. At 55 F supply: 1.08 x 300 x (70 - 55) = 4,860 Btu/h. At 62 F supply: 1.08 x 300 x (70 - 62) = 2,592 Btu/h. The saving is 2,268 Btu/h for that one box. In mild weather, when many boxes sit at minimum and few are at full cooling, the reheat saving wins. In hot weather the fan cost wins, which is why the schedules return to 55 F.

What techs check on trends

  • Does the discharge air temperature actually follow its setpoint? The PNNL guide says persistent deviations suggest leaking coil valves or PID problems. See the PID tuning lesson.
  • Does the setpoint reach 60 F or higher in cold weather? If not, find out whether something is justifying the cooler air, such as a zone with a bad sensor or a stuck request.
  • Does occupied-hours electricity rise after you enable reset? The guide says that may mean the setpoint is too high and fan power is climbing.
  • How many boxes are at minimum airflow with reheat on? A VAV box in reheat during cooling season is the symptom reset is meant to cure.
  • Does space or return humidity climb on warm, damp days?
  • Is one rogue zone pinning the setpoint low with constant cooling requests? Check that zone first.

Field card: Supply Air Temperature Reset

Warmer air saves reheat but costs fan power.

  • Fixed 55 F air makes boxes reheat in mild weather.
  • Outdoor air reset: simple; zone demand reset: preferred by PNNL.
  • Typical range from PNNL guide: 55 F to 65 F with zone reheat.
  • Humid climate: hold the minimum near 53 F or keep the maximum lower.
  • Flow rises as supply warms: cfm = load / (1.08 x dT).
  • Fan power follows the cube of speed.
  • Trend checks: setpoint tracking, humidity, kW after enabling, boxes in reheat.
