Indoor Climbing Gym HVAC: Chalk Dust Filtration, Tall Wall Stratification, Humidity for Grip and Bouldering Area Airflow

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A climbing gym asks the HVAC system to do four hard things at once: handle a cloud of white chalk dust, keep a 40 foot wall from turning into a hot attic at the top, hold humidity where hands grip well, and move air through a crowded bouldering room. We take them in turn, then work a filter-loading example.

Chalk is a particle problem first

Climbing chalk is magnesium carbonate. A 2012 measurement study in the Journal of Environmental Monitoring (Weinbruch and coauthors) tested climbers under controlled conditions and found the dust was hydrated magnesium carbonate hydroxide. They reported PM10 up to about 4,000 µg/m³ and PM2.5 up to about 500 µg/m³. They also found chalk use mainly released particles larger than 1 micron. EPA defines PM10 and PM2.5 as inhalable particles about 10 and 2.5 microns or smaller, and says particles under 10 microns can reach deep into the lungs.

That same study tested ways to cut the dust. A liquid chalk (powder in ethanol) gave concentrations similar to banning chalk. Pressed blocks, loose powder, coarse pieces and chalk balls did not significantly reduce dust. A 2026 review in Quality in Sport says climbing facilities often exceed WHO and local guidelines at peak hours, without giving new measurements. The takeaway: the chalk policy changes your filter load, so ask what it is.

Exposure limits in plain terms

NIOSH lists no recommended limit for particulates not otherwise regulated. OSHA lists 15 mg/m³ total and 5 mg/m³ respirable dust (8 hour averages) for that category and for magnesite (magnesium carbonate). Our own unit conversion: 4,000 µg/m³ is 4 mg/m³. That is not a compliance finding; the owner and a safety professional decide that. For HVAC the point is different: a space can sit under those limits and still bury a filter.

Filter choice for fine white dust

EPA says a MERV rating reports capture of particles from 0.3 to 10 microns, and advises using as high a rating as the system fan and filter slot can handle. Our own advice, not a rule: chalk is mostly above 1 micron, so put a cheap, thin pre-filter ahead of the main bank, take the chalk there and change it often, rather than choosing a very tight main filter that taxes the fan. Pressure drop and airflow: 1 Inch vs 4 Inch Furnace Filter: Pressure Drop and Change Intervals.

Check pressure drop, not the calendar. Fit a differential gauge across each filter bank, write the clean reading on the frame, and change at the filter maker's final resistance. ASHRAE's test standard reports a MERV only after loading the filter until its resistance is at least double its starting resistance, which is a good reminder that real filters are expected to climb in pressure as they load.

Keeping chalk out of the coils

A filter that is bypassed is a filter that is not there. Chalk that gets past the bank sticks to a wet cooling coil as paste, raises coil pressure drop and cuts airflow. Check the frame, door gaskets and side tracks for gaps. If a coil is already coated, see Commercial Duct and Air Handler Cleaning. Our own point: dry sweeping stirs chalk into the return, so ask whether staff clean wet or with a HEPA vacuum.

Tall walls and the hot layer at the top

Warm air rises. Over a 40 foot wall, the lead climbers at the top sit in the warmest layer while the floor reads the thermostat. We found no measurement of a climbing wall, so this comes from other tall spaces. An ASHRAE Journal article on an aircraft hangar with a 40 foot peak put temperature loggers at 5, 15 and 35 feet and ran a 20 foot diameter high-volume low-speed fan at low speed downward in winter to mix warm ceiling air with cooler floor air. A gas utility's public pilot report measured a garden center with unit heaters and found 12 to 14 F between 5 and 25 feet during sustained heating without fans. Four fans cut its gas use 21.4 percent, adjusted for heating degree days.

The same report also measured a university gymnasium and found no significant savings. Changing setbacks and occupancy made the comparison unreliable. Without fans, morning overshoot near the ceiling after setback was about 5 F greater. So fans help most where there is a clear temperature layer and a heating system running against it. For fan sizing, sprinkler interlocks and the full destratification method, see HVLS Fans and Destratification in Warehouses and Gyms. The layering of a tall open room is also covered in Atrium and Glass Lobby HVAC: Stratification.

Our own cautions: keep fan air speed gentle at the floor so chalk does not lift, and check temperature with a logger at the top of the wall, not just the wall thermostat.

Humidity for grip and comfort

Skin friction depends on moisture and temperature. A 2016 Biotribology study from the University of Twente (Klaassen, Schipper and Masen) tested in-vivo skin friction from 25 C and 40 percent relative humidity up to 37 C and 80 percent. The friction coefficient roughly doubled between cold and dry and warm and moist. The product of temperature and humidity best explained the change. For a climbing gym that means a hot, humid top-of-wall zone gives sweatier hands and more chalk use, which feeds the filter. We give no humidity target, because this is general skin friction, not a climbing standard. Set one with the owner and track it with a probe you trust (see Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes).

People add moisture. Pounds of water per hour is 4.5 × cfm × change in humidity ratio, and latent Btu/h is 4,840 × cfm × change in humidity ratio, with W = 0.622 pw / (p − pw).

Bouldering areas, yoga and fitness rooms

A public ASHRAE 62.1 addendum from 2010 added a gym and sports arena play area row of 20 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot at a default density of 7 people per 1,000 sq ft (the combined rate is 45 cfm per person). Current editions may differ, so use the edition your local code adopts. Bouldering packs people closer than that, so count real heads.

For yoga and fitness side rooms, see Locker Rooms and Fitness Centers HVAC and, for heated studios, Hot Yoga Studios, Saunas and Steam Rooms. Our own preference: keep the chalk area at neutral or slightly negative pressure to those rooms so dust is not pushed in.

Worked example: how fast does a chalky filter bank load?

All inputs below are our own assumptions, picked to show the method, not measured values.

  • Airflow 20,000 cfm through the filter bank, 14 open hours a day.
  • Average chalk dust in the air 500 µg/m³ (the 2012 study saw up to 4,000 in a controlled test).
  • Filter bank captures 80 percent of that mass.
  • Clean pressure drop 0.35 in. w.g.; our change limit 0.90 in. w.g.
  • Pressure rises 0.04 in. w.g. per week on a normal schedule, 0.08 on heavy chalk weeks.

Step 1, convert airflow: 20,000 cfm × 0.0283168 m³/ft³ × 60 min/h = 33,980 m³/h.
Step 2, dust through the bank: 33,980 × 500 µg = 16,990,000 µg/h = 17.0 g/h.
Step 3, captured: 17.0 × 0.80 = 13.6 g/h. For 14 hours: 13.6 × 14 = 190 g per day, or about 1.3 kg per week.
Step 4, time to the limit: (0.90 − 0.35) = 0.55 in. w.g. of headroom. At 0.04 per week, 0.55 ÷ 0.04 = 13.75 weeks. At 0.08 per week, 0.55 ÷ 0.08 = 6.9 weeks.
Step 5, heat check: sensible Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × change in temperature, so 1 F at 20,000 cfm is 1.08 × 20,000 × 1 = 21,600 Btu/h. Lost airflow costs real capacity.

Result: the heavy weeks could cut the change interval roughly in half. The real rise is not a straight line, so read the gauge weekly and set the interval from your own readings.

Field card: Climbing gym HVAC

Gauge the filters, watch the top of the wall, and track humidity.

  • Ask the chalk policy.
  • Differential gauge on every bank.
  • Thin pre-filter ahead of the main bank, changed often.
  • Seal bypass gaps.
  • Logger at the top of the wall, not just the floor thermostat.
  • Sensible heat: 1.08 × cfm × change in temperature.
