NICU HVAC: Temperature, Humidity, Quiet Air, Drafts and Filtration

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) looks like a ward, but for the person who services the air it is closer to a quiet, gentle, clean room that happens to hold twelve very small patients. Small babies lose heat fast, sleep badly in noise, and are among the people most at risk from mold spores. This lesson covers what the published design guidance asks of the air, and what that means for your service calls.

Check the project documents first

Check the project documents first. Every hospital works from its own design criteria, state rules and the standard edition that applied when the unit was built. This page is general education built from public sources we read in 2026. The clinical engineering and infection prevention staff decide what is right for a given room. You follow their written limits.

The ranges the guidance gives

The public text of the Recommended Standards for Newborn ICU Design (a consensus document written by NICU designers and clinicians; we cite it as plain text because the copy we read is a university repost) gives these ambient values: air temperature 72 to 78°F, relative humidity 30 to 60 percent with no condensation on walls or windows, and at least 6 air changes per hour of which at least 2 are outdoor air. The ASHRAE 170 public addendum we read (the 2008 addendum) lists the newborn intensive care row with the same 6 total and 2 outdoor air changes per hour, 30 to 60 percent RH and a positive pressure relationship. The temperature text in that addendum shows both 70 to 75°F and 72 to 78°F, and the formatting does not show which one was adopted, so use the number in your hospital's design criteria. Our own note: a setpoint inside a range is not a tolerance. Log the room when you arrive and when you leave.

Why drafts matter more here

The same design text says the airflow pattern should be low velocity and that supply and exhaust openings should be placed to limit drafts on or near infant beds. It also says registers should be placed to cut drafts, noise and airborne particles, and that heat sources near exterior walls can offset the cold wall effect, which drives convection drafts and condensation. A research paper on incubator airflow (Ginalski, Nowak and Wrobel, Brunel University) modeled air speeds inside one incubator of about 0.09 meters per second near the baby (about 18 fpm), and described a zone of nearly still air around the infant. That paper is about the inside of the incubator and gives no room-air limit. We found no published numeric room draft limit for isolettes or warmers, so the check below uses an assumed target, labeled as ours.

Noise is an air-system problem

The American Academy of Pediatrics 1997 statement on noise (Committee on Environmental Health, Pediatrics) is reported in a 2020 Translational Pediatrics paper (Hu and colleagues) as saying NICU sound should not exceed 45 dB. The Recommended Standards text we read gives hourly Leq of 45 dBA, hourly L10 of 50 dBA and a peak (Lmax) of 65 dBA in infant rooms, and sets noise criteria for mechanical equipment of NC-25 in infant rooms, NC-30 in spaces open to them and NC-35 elsewhere. For you that means: no new rattles after a repair, no loose panels, fans on the speed the balancer set, and no noisy VAV box left in a failed state. Our own habit: ask the charge nurse before you start a noisy task near beds. See HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings for how NC ratings work.

Filtration

The CDC Guidelines for Environmental Infection Control in Health-Care Facilities (2003) describe outdoor air passing through two filter banks, the first rated 20 to 40 percent and the second 90 percent or more (dust spot), and reserve HEPA (at least 99.97 percent at 0.3 micron) for special areas. The Recommended Standards text says ventilation air must meet the efficiency set in the FGI Guidelines, keep filters outside the infant care area so they can be changed safely, and notes that HEPA may help for immunocompromised newborns. We did not read the FGI text, and the ASHRAE 170 filter rows were not in the part we read, so the filter grade for your unit comes from your drawings. Our own rule: the filter bank is not the place to improvise. Same size, same grade, seated and sealed, and the differential pressure logged.

Open bay or single-family rooms

An open bay shares one air volume, so one noisy or drafty diffuser reaches many beds, and a contamination event reaches them all. A single-family room gives each baby a separate space and its own controls, which also means more terminal units to keep running and more zones to balance. We present this as our own plain reading, not a rule. Whichever layout you service, the diffuser rule is the same: put supply away from beds and keep the throw gentle.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions: a 12-bed open bay of 2,400 sq ft with a 10 ft ceiling, run at the 6 total air changes per hour from the guidance above.

  1. Volume = 2,400 × 10 = 24,000 ft³.
  2. cfm = 24,000 × 6 ÷ 60 = 2,400 cfm total supply.
  3. Outdoor air at 2 air changes = 24,000 × 2 ÷ 60 = 800 cfm minimum.
  4. Per bed = 2,400 ÷ 12 = 200 cfm.

Terminal velocity check. Assume six ceiling diffusers sharing the load (an assumption), so 2,400 ÷ 6 = 400 cfm each. Assume the catalog data for the one chosen diffuser shows a terminal velocity of 50 fpm at the end of its throw (assumed example). 50 fpm is 0.25 m/s, about 2.8 times the 0.09 m/s reported inside the incubator model above (0.25 ÷ 0.09). The Recommended Standards ask for low velocity near beds but give no number, so assume our own working target of 25 fpm or less at an isolette (an assumption, not a published limit; use your design criteria). The diffuser then fails the check if the 50 fpm edge of its throw reaches a bed. Fix by moving the diffuser, adding diffusers at lower cfm each, or choosing a pattern that spreads the air more, not by throttling blindly: a closed damper adds noise and cuts the air changes. Measure, do not guess. Read the velocity with a calibrated anemometer at bed height with the incubator and warmer running, as your hospital procedure allows.

Heat sources and fast service

Lights, monitors, warmers and phototherapy lamps add heat to the bay, and the room still has to stay in range and quiet. Our own advice: after any lamp or equipment change, ask whether the room is holding its range. A NICU call is urgent. Bring your tools, your filter, your badge and your gloves in one trip, check in with the charge nurse and keep the first visit quick.

Infection control during work

The CDC guideline strongly supports an infection control risk assessment before dust or water-aerosol work, with dust control barriers and daily checking of airflow in the work zone. It lists preterm newborns among the patients at greatest risk from aspergillosis, a mold infection linked to disturbed dust during construction and renovation. It also says water-damaged material should be dried within 72 hours or removed. For you: no ceiling tile lifted, no coil or filter section opened and no duct cut in or near a NICU until infection prevention has signed off, and report any leak or stain at once. For the pressure side of this work, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare and Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms. We did not find a separate lesson on infection control risk assessment on this site.

Field card: NICU HVAC

Gentle, quiet, clean, in range.

  • Design text: 72 to 78°F, 30 to 60% RH, no condensation.
  • At least 6 air changes, at least 2 outdoor. Our example: 2,400 cfm in a 24,000 ft³ bay.
  • Low velocity, diffusers away from beds, check throw against the bed.
  • Noise: AAP 45 dB statement; design text 45 dBA Leq and NC-25 in infant rooms.
  • Filters: same grade, sealed, outside the care area; follow the drawings.
  • Ask infection prevention before opening anything overhead.
