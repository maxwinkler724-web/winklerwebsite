Protecting Your HVAC During a Home Renovation: Dust, Fumes, Sealing Returns, Filters and Backdraft Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A renovation makes dust, fumes and debris, and the heating and cooling system is the one machine in the house that can carry all of it into every room. The fix is mostly simple and cheap: stop the air from getting into the ducts, keep the system off during the dirtiest work, and check the filter before you turn anything back on.

What can go wrong

A return duct pulls room air to the blower. If you run the system while someone sands drywall, that dust goes down the return, through whatever filter is in the slot, and onto the blower wheel and the coil. A building science guide from a national laboratory says plainly: do not run the HVAC system during construction, and avoid operating it while dry-walling, sanding or sawing, because a fiberglass filter will not stop fine dust. A CDC/NIOSH database page adds that drywall joint compounds can contain talc, calcite, mica, gypsum and a small amount of silica, and that sanding exposed workers to as much as 10 times the 15 mg/m3 limit for total dust in one NIOSH evaluation. That is the dust that would otherwise be heading for your equipment.

Dust on the blower wheel and coil cuts airflow, and on a cooling coil it can hold moisture. See our lesson on mold on the AC coil or in ducts if the coil is already damp and dirty.

Off, or on with a filter?

For dusty work, the answer from EPA is off. EPA's duct cleaning page says that during work that creates dust you should seal off the supply and return registers and not operate the heating and cooling system until after the dust is cleaned up. EPA's lead-safe guidance for do-it-yourselfers also says to turn off forced-air heating and air conditioning and cover the vents with taped plastic. The Renovate Right pamphlet suggests the same and says it keeps dust from moving through the ducts into the rest of the home.

Off has a cost: no heat in winter and no cooling in summer, so plan the dusty days for mild weather where you can. Do not use a system as a dust collector. A filter slot is not a work-site air cleaner, and a fine-dust job can load a standard filter very fast (see the worked example). If you must run the system for a short time, use a clean filter, keep it off during sanding and for a while after, and check the filter before each restart.

Sealing returns and supply registers

  • Return grille. Tape plastic sheeting over the grille face, taped on all four sides. EPA's remodeling page says to seal registers in the work area by taping plastic over them.
  • Supply registers. Cover them the same way. Supply openings can back-flow dust into the duct as well, and covering both keeps the duct from becoming a dust path in either direction.
  • Open duct ends. If a register is removed, cover the open boot. The laboratory guide says to cover register boots and duct openings with plastic or carpet protector taped in place, or with plywood if registers are not installed yet, and to leave the covers on until flooring, carpet, cabinetry and finish carpentry are done.
  • Air handler. If the equipment is in the work area, cover it too.
  • The only return. EPA's remodeling page warns not to block a cold air return with the furnace or air conditioner running if it is the only return in the house. Blocking it while the system runs starves the blower. Our rule: cover it only if the system is off.

Use tape that comes off cleanly, and use real plastic sheeting, not paper. Cloth duct tape is a poor duct sealant, according to EPA, so do not rely on it for repairs afterward either.

Floor refinishing, paint and solvent fumes

Floor sanding makes fine wood dust, and finishes release volatile organic compounds (VOCs). EPA's remodeling page says most paints release VOCs, that oil-based (alkyd) paints can expose people to much more solvent than latex paints, and recommends running exhaust fans in the painted area and supply fans in adjacent areas. It suggests continuing ventilation around the clock from the start of painting until two to three days after it ends. It also says to block return openings in rooms being painted or turn the system off, to store paint away from heating and cooling equipment rooms, and never to clean brushes with gasoline. A Colorado State University extension page adds that strippers containing methylene chloride should be used outdoors.

The HVAC point is that fumes follow the same paths as dust. A running system can spread them from the room being finished to the whole house, and some of the finish can settle in the ducts and filter.

Combustion appliances and backdrafting

This is the hazard people forget. EPA lists backdrafting from furnaces, gas water heaters, wood stoves and fireplaces as a source of carbon monoxide (CO) in homes. EPA's remodeling page says leaky return ducts can create negative pressure that contributes to combustion equipment backdrafting. Our own note: a renovation can add the same effect, since a temporary exhaust fan in a window, a sanding dust extractor that vents outside, or a closed door that cuts off makeup air can push the house toward negative pressure, and then burner exhaust may spill into the room instead of rising up the flue.

  • Tell the crew where the furnace, water heater and any fireplace are, and ask them not to block combustion air openings, flues or vents with plastic.
  • If an exhaust fan or extractor will run, keep a window or door open at a safe distance from the dust and have the appliance room tested afterward. EPA suggests asking an installer for a worst-case depressurization test.
  • Install working CO alarms. EPA says to put them outside each sleeping area (the CPSC advice it quotes is on each level outside each sleeping area) and says alarms are a backup, not a substitute for maintenance.
  • If anyone has headaches, dizziness or nausea that clears up outside, leave and get help. EPA lists those as signs of CO exposure.

Our lessons on water heater backdrafting and radon systems and backdrafting go into the checks.

Lead-safe work in pre-1978 homes

EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rule covers homes and child-occupied facilities such as childcare centers built before 1978. It requires that people paid to disturb painted surfaces in those buildings be certified and use lead-safe work practices. The rule generally does not cover homeowners working in their own homes, but it does apply if you rent out any part of the home, run a childcare center there or flip houses. EPA still recommends hiring a lead-safe certified contractor.

The federal regulation (40 CFR 745.85, on the eCFR) has details that matter for HVAC: ducts opening in the work area must be closed and covered with taped-down plastic sheeting or other impermeable material, and windows and doors in the work area closed. It prohibits open-flame burning or torching of painted surfaces, allows heat guns only below 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit, and prohibits high-speed sanding and grinding on painted surfaces unless the tool has a shroud and a HEPA vacuum attachment. Cleanup is checked with a visual inspection and wipe tests against a cleaning verification card. For DIY work, EPA says to mist surfaces before sanding, use HEPA-vacuum power tools, seal the doors and vents, and clean daily with a HEPA vacuum. If you cannot tell the home's age, EPA says it is safest to assume lead paint.

Do not try to vacuum lead dust out of your ducts with a household vacuum. If lead dust got into the system, stop and get a lead-safe contractor and an HVAC pro. Our lesson on asbestos and lead paint around old heating systems covers older equipment.

Worked example (our own): how fast one sanding job loads a filter

This is our own illustration, not a measurement. Every input is an assumption, chosen to show the arithmetic.

  • Assumed: sanding one 12 by 12 foot room with 8 foot ceilings makes 1.0 pound (454 g) of fine dust.
  • Assumed: the system runs one hour while sanding is going on and the return is not sealed, and 5 percent of that dust is pulled into the filter.
  • Assumed: a clean 1 inch filter starts at 0.10 inch of water pressure drop, and each 10 g of fine dust it holds adds 0.04 inch.
  1. Dust reaching the filter = 0.05 × 454 g = 22.7 g.
  2. Pressure drop rise = 22.7 ÷ 10 × 0.04 = 0.09 inch of water.
  3. New filter drop = 0.10 + 0.09 = 0.19 inch of water, almost double the clean value.
  4. If the same hour repeated on each of three days of sanding and taping with no filter change, the drop would reach 0.10 + 3 × 0.09 = 0.37 inch, and the blower would face that extra resistance on every cycle.

What it means. A filter that looks fine after weeks of normal use can be heavily loaded after one dusty day. For scale, a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory field study of home filters found loading raised pressure drop by less than 5 pascals in some homes, about 30 pascals in others, and 40 pascals (about 0.16 inch of water) in one home with a MERV 8 filter that fouled quickly. Since the study says loading depends on the home, our assumed numbers are only a way to show why you should look at the filter at the end of each work day, not at the end of the project. Changing it is cheap. A starved blower is not.

After the job: filter, coil and blower wheel

  1. Finish the cleanup first. Vacuum the work area (HEPA vacuum in pre-1978 homes) before uncovering anything.
  2. Uncover carefully. Fold plastic inward and bag it. Wipe or vacuum register faces. EPA says to clean the registers once the dust is up.
  3. Replace the filter. The laboratory guide says to replace all air handler filters after cleaning and to bag soiled filters before carrying them out. Make sure the new filter fits with no gaps, as EPA advises. See filter size, direction and fit.
  4. Look at the blower wheel and coil. With power off and the panel out, look for gray or white dust on the blower blades and the coil face. A thick coat is a service call. Cleaning a wheel or coil is a pro job, since it can damage fins or wiring.
  5. Consider duct cleaning only if needed. The laboratory guide recommends cleaning ducts before occupancy on new construction using vacuum and brush methods, with no liquids in the ducts. For an existing home, EPA suggests cleaning only when there is visible mold, pests or heavy clogging, or dust is blowing out of the registers.
  6. Change the filter again. After a week of normal running, check it again, and change it if it looks gray. See how often to change your filter and one-inch vs four-inch filters and pressure drop.
  7. Test the combustion appliances. If the work involved fans, new windows or sealing, have a pro check draft and CO.

If dust keeps turning up afterward, read our lesson on a dusty house.

Field card: Protecting your HVAC during a renovation

Seal it, shut it off, check the filter every day.

  • Dusty work: system off, supply and return registers sealed with taped plastic (EPA).
  • Never block the only return while the system runs.
  • Pre-1978 home: assume lead paint, use a lead-safe certified contractor, close and cover ducts in the work area.
  • Fans and sanding extractors can pull the house negative. Keep combustion air open and CO alarms working.
  • Our own example: 22.7 g of dust on the filter in one hour doubled its pressure drop (assumed numbers).
  • After the job: HEPA clean, new filter, look at blower wheel and coil.
