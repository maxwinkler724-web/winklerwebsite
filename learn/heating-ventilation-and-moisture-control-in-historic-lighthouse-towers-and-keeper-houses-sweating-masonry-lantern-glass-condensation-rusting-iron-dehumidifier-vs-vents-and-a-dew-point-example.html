Heating and Moisture Control in Historic Lighthouse Towers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A historic lighthouse tower is a tall, thick, cold stack of masonry or cast iron, often open to the sea air, with a glass room on top. Many are now museums, and the attached keeper houses are often rentals. Staff there fight the same enemy all year: water that forms on surfaces nobody meant to wet. This lesson explains why towers sweat, why adding heat can make things worse, and how vents, dehumidifiers and small ductless heads fit into a building you are not allowed to damage.

Why a tower sweats

Air holds water vapor. When air touches a surface colder than its dew point, some vapor turns to liquid on that surface. A thick masonry or iron tower has a lot of thermal mass, so on a mild, damp spring day the walls are still at winter temperature while the air is warm and wet. The wall is the coldest thing in the room, so it collects the water. The same thing happens on lantern room glass and on iron stairs and lens pedestals, which cool fast and stay cold.

A federal park service preservation brief on moisture says repeated condensation on interior surfaces is a sign that moisture is moving into exterior walls, where it can do lasting damage. It also says single-glazed windows can fog in winter with indoor humidity as low as 35%. A federal park service lighthouse handbook chapter on iron adds that condensation inside a tower can corrode interior ironwork, especially in brick-lined towers, and that salt, bird droppings and pollutants on iron speed the damage. Chloride left under paint can make coatings fail, so cleaning before painting matters.

Passive ventilation: useful, but not free

The iron handbook chapter says unoccupied towers need adequate ventilation, partly to limit heat buildup that can hurt sensitive electronics and partly to limit condensation that corrodes iron. Many towers were designed with low openings and lantern vents so air rises through the tower by itself, since warm air is lighter and leaves through the top. That is a sound idea, and the first job on a service call is to find out whether those openings are still open, screened and working. Blocked vents, painted-shut dampers and sealed doors are common.

The catch is that the incoming air carries its own moisture. If the outdoor air is damp and the walls are colder than its dew point, ventilating just feeds water to the walls. Ventilation works best when outdoor air is drier than the tower air or when the walls are warmer than the incoming dew point. Our own advice is to log outdoor and indoor humidity and temperature with inexpensive data loggers for a few weeks before changing anything, so you can see which hours help and which hours hurt.

Why heating a tower can make moisture worse

Heating the air does not remove water from it. A federal park service brief on heating and cooling historic buildings explains that moisture moves from warm areas toward colder ones and can condense inside walls or on windows. It also warns that heating or cooling one zone while leaving the next zone unconditioned can cause condensation at the boundary. In a tower, a heater at the base warms the air quickly, and warm air can pick up more water from damp plaster, wood and floors. That moisture then rises and finds the coldest surfaces: the lantern glass and the iron. The walls themselves warm very slowly because of their mass.

The same brief says the building should not be damaged to meet climate goals for collections, and that full humidity control can roughly double the size of basic equipment. For a tower the practical lesson is to treat heat as a small, steady background, and to deal with moisture directly. The iron chapter says minimal heating may be needed in extreme cases, which fits that picture.

Dehumidifier or ventilation?

A university extension page on household moisture says to find and remove the source first, then use ventilation or a dehumidifier if the source cannot be removed. It notes that moisture problems may start when indoor relative humidity goes above about 50%. The park service moisture brief says dehumidifiers can help in confined, unventilated spaces, but they should not be asked to pull dampness out of walls, and it says not to run a dehumidifier and a ventilation fan at the same time, since they work against each other.

Our own rule of thumb for towers: if the tower is closed up and the outdoor air is damp, a dehumidifier is the right tool and the vents should be closed or throttled while it runs. If the outdoor air is dry and cool, ventilate and leave the dehumidifier off. Controls that read both indoor and outdoor humidity can make that choice automatically. A federal energy department expert-meeting report on humidity control notes that dehumidifiers tend to cycle across a wide band, and the experts there generally favored a setpoint near 55% relative humidity to keep indoor humidity under 60%. That report was about modern homes in humid climates, so treat it as a starting point for a keeper house, not a tower rule. See Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage for the sizing basics.

Worked example (our own): how much water must a dehumidifier remove?

Our own example, with assumed numbers. This is not a real tower. Assume a round masonry tower 12 ft inside diameter and 60 ft tall.

  • Volume: pi x 6 ft x 6 ft x 60 ft = about 6,780 ft³. We use 6,800 ft³ (about 192.6 m³).
  • Tower air on a damp spring day: 55 F (12.8 C) and 85% relative humidity. Assumed.
  • Masonry surface temperature: 46 F (7.8 C). Assumed.
  • Outdoor air entering through vents: 55 F and 90% relative humidity. Assumed.
  • Air change rate through the vents and leaks: 0.5 per hour. Assumed.

Step 1, dew point of the tower air. A standard saturation-pressure relation gives about 1.46 kPa at 55 F. Vapor pressure is 0.85 x 1.46 = about 1.24 kPa. The temperature where 1.24 kPa is saturated is about 10.3 C, or 50.6 F. The dew point is about 51 F.

Step 2, compare. 51 F is higher than the 46 F wall, so the wall is wet. Put another way, air at the wall surface would be at about 119% relative humidity, which is impossible, so the extra vapor condenses.

Step 3, pick a target. Aim for a dew point of 42 F (5.6 C), 4 F below the wall, as our own margin. That is about 61% relative humidity at 55 F.

Step 4, humidity ratios. At sea-level pressure: tower air now is 7.78 g of water per kg of dry air; the 42 F target is 5.61 g/kg; the outdoor air (55 F, 90%) is 8.25 g/kg.

Step 5, air mass. Air density at 55 F is about 1.235 kg/m³, so 192.6 m³ holds about 238 kg of dry air.

Step 6, one-time pull-down. 238 kg x (7.78 - 5.61) g/kg = about 517 g, a little over half a liter (about 1.1 pints). That is small, which is why a dehumidifier seems to work quickly at first.

Step 7, steady load from vented air. At 0.5 air changes per hour, 238 kg x 0.5 = 119 kg of air per hour comes in. Each kg brings 8.25 - 5.61 = 2.64 g more water than the target air holds. 119 x 2.64 = about 314 g per hour, which is about 7.5 liters per day (about 16 pints per day).

What the numbers say. The pull-down is minor; the ongoing leakage of damp air is the real load. Cutting the air change rate in half cuts the steady load in half. The reverse is also true: wide-open vents on a damp day can overwhelm any dehumidifier. Real units lose capacity in cool air, so check the maker's data for your temperature, and confirm any real tower with measurements.

Keeper houses: warm the rooms without hurting plaster

A keeper house is closer to an old home than to a tower. The park service heating brief warns that insulation and vapor barriers added to historic walls can trap moisture and are hard to install without destroying historic material, and gives an example where plaster was lost after a barrier went in. It says controlling interior temperature and humidity can be more effective and less destructive than retrofitting walls. For rental houses, steady low heat, avoiding big temperature swings, and keeping humidity in a modest range protect plaster better than turning heat on and off. Keep unvented fuel-burning heaters out; the moisture brief lists unvented sources such as gas stoves and kerosene heaters as adding large amounts of moisture.

Small ductless heads, hidden equipment and running lines

The heating brief says fan coil units and smaller multi-zone units fit historic spaces with less disruption than large ducted systems, that equipment belongs in secondary spaces such as closets, attics, basements and existing chases, and that it should not sit on visible roofs or prominent site locations. It also says new ducts, pipes and equipment should be installed so they can be removed later without more damage, since mechanical systems typically last only 15 to 30 years. For ductless heads that means: choose rooms with less valuable finishes, reuse existing chases for line sets, route condensate by a path that cannot drip on plaster, and avoid cutting through original masonry or ironwork where a gap or a closet floor will do. Our own advice is to photograph every opening before and after, and to talk to the site's preservation staff before anything is drilled. Houses with old paint may also need lead-safe work; see Heat Pumps in Historic and Very Old Houses.

Salt air and outdoor units

Salt on metal speeds corrosion, as the iron chapter notes for ironwork. Outdoor units near the coast see the same attack on coils, fasteners and cabinets. Place the unit where it is shielded from spray if the site allows, rinse the coil on a routine, and check the maker's coastal options. See Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air: Why AC Coils Leak and What Makers Offer.

Electrical limits on remote sites

Remote light stations may have a small service, a long feeder, a generator or a solar and battery system. Our own advice: before choosing equipment, find out what the site can actually supply (voltage, phase, breaker size, generator capacity) and size heaters, dehumidifiers and heads to fit it. Inverter-driven ductless heads draw less starting current than many fixed-speed units, but confirm with the nameplate data, and have the circuit work done by a qualified electrician under local rules. Plan for power loss: a rental that loses heat in a storm can sweat badly when power returns.

Field card: Heating and Moisture Control in Historic Lighthouse Towers

Find the dew point first, then pick the tool.

  • Towers sweat when wall or iron temperature is below the air's dew point.
  • Heat alone does not dry the air and can push moisture to the coldest surfaces.
  • Check that the original low and lantern vents are open before adding equipment.
  • Ventilate when outdoor air is drier; dehumidify with vents throttled when it is damp.
  • Our example: 55 F, 85% air has a 51 F dew point, above a 46 F wall.
  • Our example: about 0.5 L pull-down, but about 7.5 L per day at 0.5 air changes per hour.
  • Keep fittings reversible, hidden and out of original fabric; ask preservation staff first.
  • Check the site's real electrical supply before choosing equipment.
