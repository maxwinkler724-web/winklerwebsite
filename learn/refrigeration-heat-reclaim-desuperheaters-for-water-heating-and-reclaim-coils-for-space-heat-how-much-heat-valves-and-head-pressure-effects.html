Refrigeration Heat Reclaim: Desuperheaters, Reclaim Coils and Head Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A supermarket rack throws away a lot of heat through its condenser fans, while the same building buys gas to heat water and air. Heat reclaim puts some of it to work. We cover how much heat there is, what each device can take, and what it does to head pressure.

Check your local rules first

Check the local rules. Adopted codes and the equipment maker's instructions decide what is allowed on a real job. This page is general education.

Where the heat goes and how much

The condenser must reject the heat the evaporators picked up plus the heat the compressors added. In plain terms: heat of rejection equals refrigeration load plus compressor input. Convert compressor kilowatts with 3,412 Btu/h per kW (our own standard conversion) and a ton of refrigeration with 12,000 Btu/h (our own standard conversion).

A U.S. Department of Energy lab report on supermarket heat recapture gives a store example: about 1,118,330 Btu/h of evaporator load, and about 1,667,000 Btu/h rejected with 161 kW of compressor power. That is roughly 49% more heat rejected than refrigeration load (ORNL report).

Desuperheater share

Refrigerant leaves the compressor as hot superheated gas. A desuperheater cools that gas down to saturation, and the condenser does the rest. The NREL Refrigeration Playbook says superheat is generally 15% to 30% of the total heat of rejection. The ORNL report found the desuperheating heat was only 26% (low-temperature rack) and 15% (medium-temperature rack) of the heat available if the refrigerant were fully condensed. A valve maker's reclaim bulletin puts a desuperheat-only coil at roughly 30% to 50% of heat of rejection. The sources differ, so treat the share as a range.

Desuperheated heat is the high-grade part. ORNL noted that when water must be circulated at up to 140°F, only desuperheating serves it effectively, sometimes with electric heat to finish. That is why water heating is usually a desuperheater job, and why the hot gas, not the condensing heat, is what you capture.

Reclaim coils for space heat

A reclaim coil sits in an air handler, often in the supply or outdoor air stream. NREL says the coil is usually sized for desuperheating only, so refrigerant leaves it still a gas. NREL's example (0°F outdoor air, 65°F return air, 20% outdoor air) gives about 52°F mixed air, and the mixed airflow is about five times the outdoor airflow, which gives the coil more air to heat. In a Canadian store study, refrigerant entering the coil ran from 140°F to 176°F through the year, and the recovered heat was not enough for full space heating (ORNL).

Full-condensing reclaim, which takes all the rejected heat, runs cooler. An EPA conference presentation says it is too cool for water heating and needs more coil area and airflow for space heat.

Three-way valves and split condensing

NREL describes a three-way valve in the discharge line that either feeds the reclaim path or the normal condenser. The EPA presentation describes a series layout with on/off logic, where the condenser still finishes the job, and a full-condensing layout with either/or logic. In a parallel or split layout the reclaim condenser must fully condense whatever gas it receives (NREL), and capacity control is needed to make sure it does.

Head pressure: reclaim versus floating head

Floating head pressure lowers the condensing temperature in cool weather and saves compressor energy. But a lower head means less superheat to capture. NREL says condensing temperatures generally run 70°F to 120°F, 70°F is a typical minimum, and the available superheat shrinks as head pressure drops. The EPA presentation notes that series reclaim can interfere with floating head, and asks whether the heat is truly free. ORNL concluded that raising condensing pressure just to get hotter reclaim heat can use more energy than it saves, and found the combined strategies saved about 7% to 8% in energy cost compared with either one alone.

The practical rule is to reclaim only what the building needs, and not hold the head up for a heat load that is not calling.

Worked example (assumptions are our own)

Assumed inputs (our own): a medium-temperature rack with 20 tons of refrigeration load and 25 kW of compressor input; desuperheater share 20% of rejected heat, chosen inside NREL's 15% to 30% range; average load 60% of full over 24 hours; water raised 60°F; water weighs 8.33 lb per gallon and takes 1 Btu per lb per °F (standard figures).

  1. Refrigeration load: 20 x 12,000 = 240,000 Btu/h.
  2. Compressor input: 25 x 3,412 = 85,300 Btu/h.
  3. Heat of rejection: 240,000 + 85,300 = 325,300 Btu/h, about 1.36 times the load (ORNL's store was about 1.49 times).
  4. Desuperheater share: 325,300 x 0.20 = 65,060 Btu/h.
  5. Daily heat: 65,060 x 24 x 0.60 = 936,864 Btu/day.
  6. Gallons: 936,864 / (8.33 x 60) = 936,864 / 499.8 = about 1,875 gallons per day heated 60°F.

That is a ceiling, not a promise, because a store may use less hot water than that. The valve maker bulletin's reclaim-tank figure of about 65 gallons per hour per 10 tons at an 80°F rise is a useful cross-check on scale (about 130 gallons per hour for 20 tons).

Potable water and double-wall exchangers

When refrigerant heat goes to drinking water, one failed wall must not put refrigerant or oil in the tap. Plumbing codes based on the Uniform Plumbing Code say heat exchangers used for heat recovery must protect the potable water system. A double-wall exchanger has a vented space between the two walls so a leak shows outside. Single-wall is allowed only with a heat-transfer fluid rated nontoxic, Class 1 (Minnesota Rules part 4714.0603, section 603.5.4). Editions differ, so confirm the local code.

Check valves, migration and oil logging

When the three-way valve sends gas to the condenser, the idle reclaim coil is often the colder spot in the system, and refrigerant migrates toward the colder of two points. The valve maker bulletin says to add a tight-seating check valve in the pump-out line to reduce migration, and notes that no check valve seals perfectly: a metal-seat check may leak about 750 cc/min, while a synthetic-seat one may leak as little as 10 cc/min. The same bulletin says the coil inlet is normally at the top and outlet at the bottom so refrigerant drains freely and oil is less likely to log. NREL adds that coils should keep refrigerant moving fast enough to avoid condensing and its refrigerant and oil management problems. For the oil side of the rack, see the oil separator lesson below.

Field checks

  • Confirm the three-way valve position matches the call for heat, and the pilot or actuator is not stuck.
  • Compare gas temperature through the desuperheater with tank water temperature; hot gas and a cold tank point to fouling or flow trouble.
  • Look for frost or cold spots on the idle coil when reclaim is off.
  • Check head pressure and the holdback or differential valves after switching modes. The valve maker example sets a holdback valve at 185 psi, an example only.
  • Watch rack oil and receiver level when reclaim season starts.
  • Verify the vent space of a double-wall exchanger is open and dry.

Field card: Refrigeration Heat Reclaim

Rejection = load + compressor heat. Take only the hot-gas part.

  • Heat of rejection: tons x 12,000 plus kW x 3,412 (Btu/h).
  • Desuperheat share is a range, about 15% to 30% (NREL).
  • Three-way valve picks reclaim path or condenser; check valve stops migration.
  • Do not raise head pressure for heat nobody needs.
  • Potable water: double-wall with vented space, per the adopted code.
  • Gallons per day = Btu per day / (8.33 x rise).
