Hospital Morgue and Autopsy Room HVAC

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hospital autopsy room and its body cooler are quiet places that need steady airflow and steady cold. This lesson covers what they are meant to do, so you can service them safely and with respect for the work done there.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. Codes, standards and guidance change, and the authority having jurisdiction and the facility's infection control and safety staff decide what applies. This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It is not a design.

The airflow targets

A public ASHRAE Standard 170 addendum shows an autopsy room row in its design table: pressure negative to adjacent areas, a minimum of 2 outdoor air changes per hour, 12 total air changes per hour, all room air exhausted directly outdoors, and no recirculation by room units. We read it in an excerpt with a footnote we could not see, so confirm it against your facility's edition. The same 12 figure shows up elsewhere. A NIOSH health hazard report on a county coroner's office calls 12 air changes per hour the recommended rate for autopsy rooms. CDC's page for autopsies of a patient with an infectious disease says autopsy suites should have at least 12 air exchanges per hour, stay negative to passageways and offices, and not return air to the building interior.

CDC's 2005 tuberculosis guideline says autopsy settings should meet or exceed the requirements for an airborne infection isolation room if possible, exhaust air outdoors and not recirculate it, and consider local exhaust at the saw. It also says to allow time for room air to clear before another procedure, or keep respirators on until it does. Pressure monitors, anterooms and the isolation room basics are in Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms and Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare, so we do not repeat them.

Downdraft and backdraft tables

A downdraft table draws air through its surface or along its back edge, so vapor and aerosols are pulled away from the face of the person working. The NIOSH report above recommends downdraft tables exhausted outdoors, notes they give exhaust at table level, and cites earlier NIOSH embalming research estimating that room dilution would need 4 to 13 times more exhaust to match local capture. In the same report, vents were partly blocked by boxes, bins and equipment, and the measured rate came out at 5.4 air changes per hour. Keep grilles clear. High and low exhaust points split the work, with the table drawing at the source and the room exhaust handling the rest. In our reasoning, the table's duct counts as exhaust in the room balance. The discharge point follows the guidance in Laboratory Exhaust Fans and Stacks. For the low exhaust and negative pressure ideas in a related room, see Funeral Home Preparation Room Ventilation.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Every input below is an assumption we picked to show the arithmetic. They are not from a real building.

Room. 400 sq ft with a 10 ft ceiling, at the cited 12 total air changes per hour.

  • Step 1, volume: 400 x 10 = 4,000 cubic feet.
  • Step 2, exhaust: 4,000 x 12 = 48,000 cubic feet per hour, and 48,000 / 60 = 800 cfm.
  • Step 3, supply with a 10% offset (our assumption; real offset comes from test and balance): 800 x 0.90 = 720 cfm. The 80 cfm difference is what corridor air must supply through the door gaps.
  • Step 4, outdoor air check: 2 outdoor air changes per hour is 4,000 x 2 / 60 = about 133 cfm of the 720 cfm supply.

Body cooler. An example box 4 ft x 8 ft x 8 ft. Emerson's refrigeration load guide gives a quick method for wall heat gain: a factor of 1.9 Btu per degree F of temperature difference per square foot per 24 hours for 4 inch glass fiber insulation. We borrow that factor for the arithmetic, and real panels may differ, so use the maker's data on a real box.

  • Step 1, surface area: 2 x (4x8 + 4x8 + 8x8) = 2 x 128 = 256 sq ft.
  • Step 2, temperature difference (assumption): 75 F room outside, 38 F inside, so 37 F. We use 37 F on every surface, which is a simplification, since the floor sits on a different temperature.
  • Step 3, wall gain: 1.9 x 256 x 37 = about 17,997 Btu per 24 hours.
  • Step 4, run time: the guide uses 16 to 18 hours of run time per day for boxes at 35 F and above that frost. At 16 hours, 17,997 / 16 = about 1,125 Btu/h. A 10% safety factor (the guide suggests 5 to 10%) gives about 1,240 Btu/h.

That is only wall heat. Door openings, fans, defrost, lights and the load of what is placed inside add to it. Our number is a floor, not a selection.

Body cooler temperature and alarms

We found no U.S. federal temperature rule for body storage. The UK Human Tissue Authority, in a public answer about mortuary conditions, says refrigeration of bodies should be at approximately 4 degrees C (about 39 F). Follow the facility's written setpoint; this is a foreign regulator's reference point. It says nothing on alarms. In a walk-in, the controller normally handles temperature, fans, defrost and alarms (see Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics for load and defrost). Our own advice: test the high temperature alarm with the facility's staff watching, confirm who receives it at night, and never silence it without telling them. The NIOSH report found autopsy room air leaking into the cooler room around a closed door, so check that door seal too.

Formaldehyde in specimen storage

Tissue specimens are kept in formalin, a formaldehyde solution. OSHA's formaldehyde standard (29 CFR 1910.1048 in the eCFR) sets a permissible exposure limit of 0.75 ppm as an 8-hour average, a short-term limit of 2 ppm over 15 minutes, and an action level of 0.5 ppm. The NIOSH report measured short-term personal samples of 0.10 to 0.46 ppm during autopsies, and it recommends putting lids back on containers promptly and repeating sampling after controls change. Our own advice: any vented specimen cabinet should exhaust outdoors, not to a shared return. Opening a cabinet or container is a work task for the facility's staff, not for the HVAC tech.

PPE and decontamination before service

  • Ask the facility for lockout and entry rules. The room may be out of service, or it may need clean-down first.
  • CDC's autopsy page lists a scrub suit, cap, impervious full-sleeve gown, eye and face protection, and at least an N95 respirator. It says to spray or wipe obviously contaminated PPE with an EPA-registered disinfectant before removal.
  • The same page says to clean and disinfect surfaces after an autopsy, using 0.5% sodium hypochlorite or another EPA-registered disinfectant. Do not open exhaust ductwork or filters in the suite until the facility says the area is released.
  • Under OSHA's formaldehyde standard, respirators are required when controls are not feasible or not yet enough, and medical surveillance applies at or above the action level.

Field card: Hospital Morgue and Autopsy Room HVAC

Negative, exhausted outdoors, never recirculated.

  • Autopsy room (ASHRAE 170 addendum excerpt): negative, 12 total ACH, 2 outdoor ACH, all exhaust outdoors, no recirculation. Confirm your edition.
  • Exhaust cfm = volume x ACH / 60. Our example: 400 sq ft x 10 ft x 12 / 60 = 800 cfm; supply 720 cfm at a 10% offset.
  • Downdraft tables capture at the source; keep grilles unblocked.
  • Cooler: follow the facility setpoint (UK regulator reference is about 4 C or 39 F); test the alarm with staff.
  • Formaldehyde: PEL 0.75 ppm, STEL 2 ppm, action level 0.5 ppm.
  • Before service: get release from the facility, wear the PPE they require, and do not open ducts until released.
