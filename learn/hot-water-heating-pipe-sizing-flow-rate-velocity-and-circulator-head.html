Hot Water Heating Pipe Sizing: Flow, Velocity and Circulator Head

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Pipe that is too small is noisy and makes the circulator work hard. Pipe that is too big costs more and can move air poorly. Sizing a zone takes three steps: find the flow the load needs, pick a pipe size that keeps the water at a sensible speed, then check that the circulator can push that flow through the circuit.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. This page uses numbers from Caleffi and REHAU literature for water near 140°F in copper tube. Your pipe, fluid, fittings and circulator will differ. Use the tables and pump curves for the exact products in front of you, and the boiler manual for its flow limits.

Step 1: flow from the load

Heat moved by water depends on how much flows and how many degrees it cools. The shop formula is GPM = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). Caleffi's idronics issue on hydronic fundamentals explains the 500 as a rounded 8.33 pounds per gallon times 60 minutes per hour, and says it is technically valid only for cold water, so a more exact constant is used for final work. Caleffi's later issue on distribution systems writes the same relationship with 500, and lists lower constants for glycol mixes. For sizing pipe, the rounded 500 is close enough. Our flow rate math lesson uses 490 for hot water and shows why the two differ by only a couple of percent.

Step 2: velocity limits and tube size

Velocity is how fast the water moves in the pipe, in feet per second (ft/s). Makers give limits for two reasons: noise and wear.

  • Caleffi's fundamentals issue says to keep average velocity between 2 and 4 ft/s. The low end helps carry air along to the air separator. Above 4 ft/s it warns of noticeable flow noise.
  • Caleffi's distribution issue gives a suggested limit of 4 ft/s for a single series loop, and suggests 2 to 4 ft/s for headers feeding several circulators.
  • REHAU's PEX pressure loss tables warn that velocity above 8 ft/s may cause excessive pressure loss, noise or erosion of components. That is an outer limit for the plastic tube, not a target. Most heating designers stay near the Caleffi range.

Caleffi's distribution issue includes a flow table for type M copper. At 4 ft/s, 1 inch carries 10.9 gpm, 1-1/4 inch carries 16.3 gpm and 1-1/2 inch carries 22.9 gpm. For smaller tube, Caleffi's own blog post lists 4.0 gpm for 1/2 inch, 8.1 gpm for 3/4 inch and 13.6 gpm for 1 inch at 5 ft/s. That 1 inch figure matches the 4 ft/s table (13.6 × 4 ÷ 5 = 10.9), so scaling is fair: velocity rises in step with flow. At 4 ft/s, 3/4 inch carries about 6.5 gpm and 1/2 inch about 3.2 gpm.

Step 3: head loss and equivalent length

Friction turns pump energy into heat, and the loss is measured in feet of head. Tube friction grows quickly with flow: Caleffi's formula uses the flow raised to the 1.75 power, so double the gpm and the loss goes up about 3.4 times. The formula is HL = a × c × L × gpm1.75, where a is a fluid factor (0.0475 for water at 140°F), c is a tube-size factor (0.061957 for 3/4 inch copper) and L is length.

Fittings and valves add resistance too. Designers convert each one into the length of straight tube that would cause the same loss, called equivalent length, and add it to the real tube length. Caleffi's sample 3/4 inch circuit uses: 90° elbow 2 ft, tee run 0.4 ft, tee side port 3 ft, ball valve 2.2 ft, swing check 3 ft and globe valve 20 ft. Note how one globe valve costs as much as ten elbows.

Step 4: the circulator and its curve

A pump curve plots the head a circulator can make at each flow: high at no flow, falling as flow rises. The circuit's system curve rises with flow. Where the two cross is the operating point, which Caleffi names exactly that. Caleffi also says to size a parallel system's circulator from the branch with the highest head loss, at the total system flow. Pick the pump whose curve passes through, or just above, your needed flow and head, using the maker's chart for that model. A very steep or very flat curve changes how much flow moves when zones open and close; see zone valves vs. zone circulators.

Worked example: a 60,000 Btu/h zone

  1. Flow. 60,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 60,000 ÷ 10,000 = 6.0 gpm.
  2. Pick a size. Check 3/4 inch type M copper. At 5 ft/s Caleffi lists 8.1 gpm, so 6.0 gpm is 6.0 ÷ 8.1 × 5 = 3.7 ft/s, under the 4 ft/s limit. For comparison, 1 inch at 6.0 gpm runs 6.0 ÷ 10.9 × 4 = 2.2 ft/s. We keep 3/4 inch, because the maker's head-loss data below is for that size.
  3. Equivalent length. Use Caleffi's sample circuit: 58 ft of tube, 4 elbows (4 × 2 = 8 ft), 2 straight tees (2 × 0.4 = 0.8 ft), 2 side-port tees (2 × 3 = 6 ft), 2 ball valves (2 × 2.2 = 4.4 ft), 1 globe valve (20 ft) and 1 swing check (3 ft). Total: 58 + 8 + 0.8 + 6 + 4.4 + 20 + 3 = 100.2 ft.
  4. Head loss. Caleffi's table for that circuit gives 6.79 ft at 6 gpm. That is 6.79 ÷ 100.2 = about 0.068 ft of head per foot of equivalent length. Check with the formula: 0.0475 × 0.061957 × 100.2 × 61.75 = 0.2949 × 23.0 = 6.8 ft.
  5. Operating point. Caleffi plots this circuit against a Grundfos UP15-42F circulator curve and reports the pump settling at about 7.5 gpm. Using the same formula at 7.5 gpm: 0.2949 × 7.51.75 = 0.2949 × 34.0 = about 10.0 ft. That 10 ft is our arithmetic on Caleffi's formula, not a value read from the curve.
  6. What it tells us. The pump moves 25 percent more than the 6.0 gpm we need. Velocity rises to 7.5 ÷ 8.1 × 5 = 4.6 ft/s, above the noise limit, and the water drop shrinks to 60,000 ÷ (500 × 7.5) = 16°F. Fixes: a smaller circulator, a lower speed setting if the pump has one, or larger tube (1 inch at 7.5 gpm is 2.8 ft/s).

The lesson in the numbers: the pump does not deliver "the design flow." It delivers whatever the two curves say, so check velocity at the operating point, not just at the design point.

Where this stops

Main loops that feed several zones are usually split by primary-secondary piping, and layout choices change head loss a lot; see series vs. parallel piping. After the pipe is in, balancing sets the flow to each branch. If a new circulator will not run, start with the circulator checks.

Field card: Hot Water Heating Pipe Sizing

Flow from the load, speed from the pipe, head from the circuit.

  • GPM = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). Example: 60,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 6.0 gpm.
  • Keep velocity about 2 to 4 ft/s (Caleffi). Above 4 ft/s expect noise.
  • Type M copper at 4 ft/s: 3/4 in about 6.5 gpm, 1 in 10.9 gpm, 1-1/4 in 16.3 gpm.
  • Head loss grows with flow to the 1.75 power.
  • Add equivalent length for fittings; a globe valve is about 20 ft of 3/4 in tube.
  • The operating point is where the pump curve crosses the system curve.
  • Check velocity at the operating point, not only at the design flow.
