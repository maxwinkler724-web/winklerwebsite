Outdoor Air Intake Louvers: Rain, Snow, Free Area and Wet Filters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An intake louver has two jobs that fight each other: let a lot of outdoor air in, and keep rain, snow and birds out. When the balance fails, the first sign is often a soaked filter, water in the plenum or a musty smell, not a louver complaint.

Check the job documents

Check the job documents. Louver ratings, limits and drain details come from the louver schedule, the maker's data and the engineer. This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. Follow the project documents and local code.

What an intake louver does

A louver is a fixed frame of angled blades in an outside wall or penthouse. It passes air to an outdoor air plenum or duct and blocks weather and pests. Behind it sit a damper, the filters and then the coil.

Free area versus gross area

The opening you can measure with a tape is the face (gross) area. The air only passes through the gaps between blades, frame and screen. A louver paper from a trade association defines free area as the minimum area through which air can pass, usually shown as a percentage, and warns not to confuse it with face area. Airflow, pressure drop and rain ratings are all tied to free area, so a schedule that lists only the wall opening hides the real number.

Free-area velocity and why water gets pulled in

Free-area velocity (check face versus free basis in any number you read) is airflow divided by free area, in fpm. The faster it is, the more it drags water off the blade surfaces and carries drops through the gaps. Each blade has a drainage path, but at high velocity air pulls water along instead of letting gravity win. An engineering article on snow intake says louvers are typically sized at no more than 500 fpm through the free area, that water penetration usually begins above 900 fpm, and that the ASHRAE Fundamentals handbook suggests roughly 400 to 500 fpm. A university design standard we read limits rain hoods to 1,000 fpm face velocity.

Drainable and storm-resistant blades

A plain angled blade sheds little. A drainable blade adds hooks or channels that catch water and run it down to the sill and out. The trade association paper says more steps, rain hooks and drainage features in a blade generally raise pressure drop. It also reports that a dual drainable design gave minimal protection in wind-driven rain, while storm-resistant (wind-driven rain) designs reject far more.

How water penetration is rated

The industry water penetration test is ANSI/AMCA 500-L (name only; we do not link standards). In the still-air test, water is applied as drops from above and as spray at the face while air is pulled through from behind. The result is the free-area velocity at which 3 mL of water per square foot of free area gets through, with a top rating of 1,250 fpm. The paper also describes a wind-driven rain test at 29 mph wind and 3 in. per hour rain, with an optional 50 mph and 8 in. per hour case. Class A means 99 percent or better rain rejection.

Snow and blowing snow

Fine snow behaves like air, not rain, and it slips past drainable blades. The university standard warns its rain limits may not be adequate for snow. The engineering article notes that melting snow can soak filters, feed microbial growth, corrode parts and leak into spaces below, and that heavy snow can pull filters through their racks. Its guidance: keep upward air speed in the vertical plenum under 200 fpm so snow falls out, add a snow baffle where needed, and watch for systems that keep full airflow in winter. A 100 percent outdoor air system does not turn down like a mixed-air unit, so snow risk is higher. An EPA design page (archived snapshot) says the intake bottom should be at least 8 inches above horizontal surfaces and higher in snowy regions.

Intake plenums, drains and bird screens

Expect some water to pass the louver. The engineering article advises sloping the plenum floor toward the outside or providing a drain, and sloping the first 10 ft of duct back toward the plenum, sealed watertight. The university standard calls for trapped, sloped intake plenum drains that are tested, and for a trap whose water depth covers the fan design static pressure. It requires galvanized or stainless bird screens of 1/4 in. mesh, accessible for cleaning. EPA's page says clogged, inaccessible intake screens are a common reason older buildings get too little ventilation air, and notes vertical louvers reduce roosting.

Wet filters and mold

EPA states that rain through inlet louvers is a common source of moisture in ducts, and that surfaces that stay wet plus dust can grow mold. A damp filter loads faster, can pull toward the coil and holds dust where it is wet. See the mold lesson for what to do when you find growth. Duct liner downstream of the louver is a special risk; the university standard keeps exposed insulation at least 1.5 ft downstream of the louver.

Worked example: sizing a bigger louver

Assumptions (ours, for practice): 12,000 cfm design outdoor airflow; existing louver 8 ft by 6 ft; free area 40 percent (assumed; real data comes from the maker); guideline 500 fpm free-area velocity from the engineering article above.

  1. Gross area: 8 x 6 = 48 sq ft.
  2. Free area: 48 x 0.40 = 19.2 sq ft.
  3. Velocity: 12,000 / 19.2 = 625 fpm.
  4. That is 125 fpm over the 500 fpm guideline, though below the 900 fpm where the article says penetration usually starts. There is little margin if wind and heavy rain arrive together.
  5. Needed free area at 500 fpm: 12,000 / 500 = 24 sq ft.
  6. Needed gross area at 40 percent: 24 / 0.40 = 60 sq ft, such as 10 ft by 6 ft.
  7. Check: 60 x 0.40 = 24 sq ft; 12,000 / 24 = 500 fpm.

Winter check on the bigger louver: at 30 percent outdoor air in a mixed-air unit, 3,600 cfm / 24 sq ft = 150 fpm, matching the article's low-flow example. If a 100 percent outdoor air unit holds 12,000 cfm all winter, it stays at 500 fpm, so snow needs a plan.

What techs check

  • Screens: clogged with lint, leaves or debris, and reachable to clean.
  • Louver blades: bent, painted over or blocked by added panels that cut free area.
  • Plenum floor: stains, standing water, rust, and a drain that is open and trapped.
  • Filters: stained, damp, sagging or pulled in the rack, and filter gauge reading.
  • Damper and coil: ice on the outdoor air damper or on a preheat coil in cold weather, and water at the damper seals.
  • After storms: look for water at the first filter bank. See economizers for damper behavior.

Field card: Outdoor Air Intake Louvers

Free area, not face area, sets velocity.

  • Velocity (fpm) = cfm / free area (sq ft).
  • Guideline in our sources: about 500 fpm at the free area; penetration often starts above 900 fpm.
  • Snow: keep plenum upward speed under 200 fpm; add a baffle.
  • Plenum floor sloped or drained; drain trapped.
  • Check screens, drains, stained filters, water in plenum, damper icing.
