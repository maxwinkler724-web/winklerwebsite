Window U-Factor and Solar Heat Gain Coefficient for HVAC Loads: Low-E Coatings, Gas Fills, Orientation and Why Sunny Windows Drive Cooling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a load calculation a window is two different heat paths in one hole in the wall. One path is ordinary conduction driven by the temperature difference. The other is sunlight coming straight through the glass. The national window rating label gives a number for each, and knowing which number matters in which room is most of what a tech needs to say about windows.

The numbers on the label

U-factor is the rate of heat flow through the window in Btu/h per square foot per degree F of difference. Lower is better. The Building America measure guideline from the national labs says the label method covers the whole window, including glazing, frame and spacers, and that typical values run from about 0.15 to 1.1. Divide 1 by U to get an R-value, so U-0.25 is about R-4.

Solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) is the fraction of incoming solar energy that ends up inside as heat, from 0 to 1. The same guideline says uncoated clear glass is above 0.80 and a typical double-pane unit is about 0.70, while highly reflective coatings on tinted glass can drop below 0.20. Visible transmittance (VT) is the share of visible light let through. Most double- and triple-pane units fall between about 0.30 and 0.70, and tints or extra glass layers lower it.

The label also lists air leakage and an optional condensation resistance score. Air leakage is a separate load; see Drafty Doors and Windows.

Whole window versus center of glass

A catalog may quote a center-of-glass value, which describes only the middle of the pane. The edge of the glass (near the spacer) and the frame usually perform worse. The Department of Energy advises looking for whole-unit U-factors and SHGCs rather than center-of-glass values, and a national-lab paper on frames says frame performance is usually the limiting factor in lowering total window U-factor. Whole-window SHGC and VT are also lower than the center-of-glass values, because the frame blocks sunlight. For load work, use the whole-window numbers from the label or the manufacturer's data for the actual unit. A center-of-glass number will understate conduction and overstate solar gain.

What low-e coatings and gas fills do

A low-emittance (low-e) coating is a nearly invisible metallic layer on a glass surface. Clear glass has an emittance near 0.84, and low-solar-gain coatings can reach about 0.04, which the Building America guideline says cuts radiant exchange between the panes by a factor of 5 to 10. The guideline groups coatings by SHGC: high-solar-gain above 0.40, moderate 0.25 to 0.40, and low-solar-gain below 0.25. Where the coating sits changes SHGC but not U-factor; surface 3 is suggested for heating climates and surface 2 for cooling climates. Spectrally selective coatings are described by the Department of Energy as filtering out 40 to 70 percent of the heat normally transmitted through insulated glass while passing the full amount of light.

Argon or krypton between the panes insulates better than air. The same guideline says argon works best at about a half-inch gap and that a well-built unit leaks under 0.5 percent of its fill per year. The fill lowers U-factor but does little to SHGC, which is why a window can have an excellent U and still let in a lot of sun. The coating decides the sun; the fill and frame decide conduction.

Orientation and shading

Federal guidance says to limit west-facing glass in hot climates unless it is properly shaded, to put south glass under a sized overhang, and to give east and west windows a low SHGC or shading. West glass is hit by direct sun in the afternoon, when outdoor air is usually also at its warmest. Shading choices (awnings, films, cellular shades) get their own treatment in Window Shades, Blinds and Awnings. A heating-dominated climate flips the preference: high SHGC on the south side collects free heat. The guideline notes the best SHGC depends on climate, orientation and shading, and that in hot climates glazing performance matters more than frame insulation because solar gain often dominates.

Worked example: which part dominates (our own example)

This is our own example, not a measured case. Assumptions: one 40 sq ft west window; outdoor air 20 F warmer than indoors; incident solar on the glass of 200 Btu/h per sq ft at the afternoon peak (an assumed round number, not a design value); U-0.30 for a good window and U-1.0 for an old single-pane unit; SHGC 0.25 for a low-solar-gain unit and 0.70 for a typical clear double-pane. We treat SHGC as a flat multiplier and ignore interior shading and angle effects.

Conduction gain = U x A x dT

  • U-0.30: 0.30 x 40 x 20 = 240 Btu/h
  • U-1.0: 1.0 x 40 x 20 = 800 Btu/h

Solar gain = SHGC x A x incident solar. Incident energy on the glass is 40 x 200 = 8,000 Btu/h.

  • SHGC 0.25: 0.25 x 8,000 = 2,000 Btu/h
  • SHGC 0.70: 0.70 x 8,000 = 5,600 Btu/h

Even with the leaky old U of 1.0, solar is 5,600 against 800, seven times larger. With the better window it is about 23 times larger. Dropping U from 1.0 to 0.30 saves 560 Btu/h, while dropping SHGC from 0.70 to 0.25 saves 3,600 Btu/h. The worst case, 6,400 Btu/h, is about half a ton for one window, and the sun-blocking glass alone removes about 0.3 ton of it. The picture reverses at night in winter, when there is no sun and conduction at a large temperature difference is all that is left.

Why sunny rooms are cooling-load problems

In our example the glass passed more than 100 Btu/h per square foot of sunlight (5,600 / 40 = 140), while conduction was only 6 to 20 Btu/h per square foot (240 / 40 = 6 and 800 / 40 = 20). Sunlight lands inside as soon as it passes through the glass. That is why a room with a lot of sunny glass can be uncomfortable while the system is sized correctly for the rest of the house, and why a load calculation, covered in Heat Load Calculation Intro, lists glass by orientation. For the wall and ceiling side of the same math, see Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings; the three heat-flow modes behind the figure are in Conduction, Convection and Radiation.

Condensation resistance in winter

Condensation resistance is an optional label score from 1 to 100; higher means the inside surface stays warmer and wetter air is less likely to condense. The labs' guideline says an aluminum spacer is a thermal shortcut at the glass edge, and a better spacer can raise the sightline surface temperature by 6 to 8 F at 0 F outdoors. Aluminum frames can get cold enough to collect moisture or frost, and risk is highest at the bottom edge of the glass. A lower U-factor helps because it keeps the inside surface warmer, but humidity still has to be managed: see Condensation on Windows in Winter.

Field card: Window U-factor and SHGC

U controls conduction. SHGC controls the sun.

  • U-factor: Btu/h per sq ft per degree F, lower is better; conduction = U x A x dT.
  • SHGC: 0 to 1, fraction of sunlight that becomes heat inside; solar = SHGC x A x incident solar.
  • Use whole-window numbers, not center-of-glass.
  • Low-e coatings and tints cut SHGC; gas fills and warm-edge spacers cut U.
  • West and east glass need low SHGC or shading in cooling climates.
  • In our example the sun was 7 to 23 times the conduction.
  • Winter condensation: check condensation resistance, spacer, frame and indoor humidity.
