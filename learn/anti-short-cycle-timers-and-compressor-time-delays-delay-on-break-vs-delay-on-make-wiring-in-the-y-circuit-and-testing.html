Anti-Short-Cycle Timers and Compressor Time Delays: Delay-on-Break, Wiring, Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A customer says the thermostat is calling for cooling, the indoor fan is blowing, and the outdoor unit just sits there. Many times nothing is broken. A timer is holding the compressor off on purpose. This lesson explains what that timer does, where it lives in the 24 V circuit, and how to prove it is the reason.

Why a compressor should not restart right away

When a compressor stops, the high-side and low-side pressures do not equal out instantly. If the motor is told to start against a big pressure difference, it has to work hard to turn. A motor that is stuck or barely turning draws locked-rotor current, a figure printed on the nameplate as LRA. A compressor maker's application manual defines LRA as the average current measured on a mechanically blocked compressor at nominal voltage, and says it is only a rough estimate of real starting current, which is often lower. Even so, starting current is several times running current, and every start stresses the windings and, on a single-phase unit, the start components. See Compressor Won't Start: Hard-Start Kits and Potential Relays for that side.

A patent on a compressor minimum off-time switch says the same thing in plain terms: restarting soon after a stop, for example when someone quickly turns the setpoint down right after the unit shut off, can damage the compressor. Two more limits show up in maker documents. One scroll compressor guideline sets a maximum of 10 starts per hour; another maker's manual says no more than 12 starts per hour and recommends a three-minute (180-second) time-out. Honest caveat: the 10-starts guideline also says there is no minimum off time for its scroll compressors because they start unloaded even with unbalanced pressures, and it says the critical rule is a minimum run time to return oil. So the delay is partly pressure and electrical stress, partly oil return, and partly the fact that the installed base includes many compressor designs. A short delay is cheap insurance either way.

Two places a delay can live

In the thermostat. Many thermostats have a built-in minimum off time. One installation manual says the compressor will not restart for at least five minutes after it turns off, including after a power interruption and restoration. Another describes a 5-minute short cycle protection timer that begins when the compressor output de-energizes, defaults to on, and can be turned off for one cycle; its test mode also skips the timer. A trade-press article adds a warning: delays built into some electronic controls only act when the control closes, so they may not protect the compressor if a safety opens and recloses. That gap is one reason a separate timer is added.

In an add-on timer. A small relay or solid-state timer wired in the Y circuit. A maker of a field-installed timer calls its part a five-minute delay-on-break relay. Another maker's timer sheet describes an adjustable delay-on-break from 0.1 to 5 minutes.

Delay-on-break, delay-on-make and interval

  • Delay-on-break. Timing starts when the call ends (the circuit opens). If the compressor has already been off longer than the delay, the next call starts it right away. The patent notes this does not add lag to every cycle. A timer sheet adds that repeated interruptions do not extend the delay. This is the usual anti-short-cycle choice.
  • Delay-on-make. Timing starts when the call arrives. The patent says this adds one to five minutes of startup lag every cycle, even after a long rest. It is better suited to staggering loads, and the patent prefers it after a power failure so many units do not start at once.
  • Interval. An interval timer turns its output on for a set time after a trigger, then off. We did not find a maker source giving it an anti-short-cycle role, so treat it as a different tool and read the sheet if you meet one.

Where it goes in the 24 V circuit

A basic cooling circuit is a loop: transformer R, thermostat Y contact, contactor coil, then back to common (C). An anti-short-cycle timer is placed in series between thermostat Y and the coil. One maker has you take the yellow wire off the contactor coil, land it on the timer input, and run a second yellow from the timer output to the coil terminal the first wire left. Another maker's sheet connects the thermostat Y wire to a pigtail on the timer, the common wire to the other pigtail, and the timer leads to the two sides of the coil. A third timer is a plain two-terminal part placed in series with the control voltage, and its sheet lists a 19 to 288 volt operating range and a 24 V rating of 250 VA inrush and 25 VA run. Read the sheet for your timer, because the terminal names differ. Background on the transformer and relay behavior is in Relays, Transformers and 24 V Control.

Worked example (ours): 2.5-ton system, 5-minute delay-on-break

Assumptions, ours and not from any one product: a 2.5-ton split system, an add-on delay-on-break timer set to 5 minutes in the Y line, and a thermostat that will call again 3 minutes after the compressor stops. The indoor blower runs from the thermostat G call, not through the timer.

MinuteWhat the circuit does
0:00Room reaches setpoint. Y opens, the timer output opens, the contactor drops, the compressor and outdoor fan stop. The 5-minute count begins.
3:00The room warms and the thermostat calls. Y closes and 24 V arrives at the timer input. The timer output stays open, so the coil is dead. The indoor fan runs, but only room-temperature air moves. 5 − 3 = 2 minutes of waiting remain.
3:00 to 5:00A meter across the timer input and C reads about 24 V. Across the output and C it reads near 0 V. This is the "no cooling for five minutes" call.
5:00The timer closes. The coil gets 24 V and the compressor starts. Total wait from stop to start: 5 min. Delay felt by the customer: 5 − 3 = 2 min.
LaterIf the next off period lasts 5 minutes or more, the next call starts the compressor at once.

Check yourself against the hour limits: at the fastest possible rhythm of a 5 minute off period plus, say, 5 minutes of running (our assumption), one cycle is 10 minutes, so 60 ÷ 10 = 6 starts per hour, under both 10 and 12.

Outage restart. Power returns at minute 0. Suppose the thermostat has a random start delay. One thermostat manual gives a random delay of 2 to 30 seconds after a power loss; assume it picks 17 seconds. The thermostat closes Y at about 0:17, but the timer, which we assume counts 5 minutes from power-up as one maker's power-cycle test implies, keeps the coil open until 5:00. The compressor starts at 5:00, not 5:17. The random part exists so many units on one supply do not all start in the same instant after power returns. Timers differ here, so read yours.

Why it looks like a fault

The blower runs, the thermostat shows cooling, and the outdoor unit is silent. A technician who arrives at minute 3 may see 24 V arriving at the timer input and no pull-in at the contactor, then suspect a bad contactor or timer. Test before you condemn. Some thermostats also show a waiting message during the delay. See AC Short Cycling: Causes, Oversizing, Charge, Thermostat and Safeties for why systems cycle in the first place.

How to test a timer

  1. Meter on the output. With the thermostat calling and the unit off, measure input to C and output to C. Input 24 V and output 0 V while the delay runs is normal. Use a meter you have checked, and stay clear of the line-voltage terminals inside the condensing unit. Meter basics: Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter.
  2. Time it. Start a stopwatch when the call ends or when you remove and restore power. One timer sheet's own test is to restore power and confirm the compressor does not start until the delay passes. Expect the output to close at the set time. If it never closes, or closes at once, the timer is suspect.
  3. Confirm the 24 V supply. A dead transformer or blown fuse looks the same. See Low-Voltage Short, Blown Fuse and Transformer Troubleshooting.
  4. Bypass for diagnosis only. Our practice: de-energize, jumper the timer input to output, restore power briefly to see whether the contactor pulls in, then remove the jumper. Do this only after the unit has sat off long enough for pressures to settle. A bypassed timer is a disabled safeguard. Never leave it bypassed, and never leave a jumper in a customer's unit.

Compare what you find with the installation sheet for that equipment, and tell the customer what the delay is for.

Field card: Anti-Short-Cycle Timers

No cooling for five minutes can be the timer, not a fault.

  • Delay-on-break counts from the end of the call; delay-on-make counts from the start of the call.
  • The timer sits in series in the Y line between the thermostat and the contactor coil.
  • Input 24 V with output 0 V during the delay is normal.
  • Time it with a stopwatch; compare to the set delay.
  • Bypass only to diagnose, only after the unit has rested, and always remove it.
  • Check the thermostat's own minimum off time and random start before you chase wiring.
