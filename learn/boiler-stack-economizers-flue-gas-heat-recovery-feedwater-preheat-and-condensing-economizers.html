Boiler Stack Economizers: Flue Gas Heat Recovery, Feedwater Preheat and Condensing Economizers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler sends a lot of heat up the stack. A stack economizer is a coil of finned tubes in the flue that grabs part of that heat and puts it into water headed for the boiler. This lesson covers what it does, how much it can save, why condensing units are a different animal, and what an operator checks to keep one safe and clean.

What an economizer does

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) steam tip sheet on feedwater economizers describes the idea plainly: take heat from the flue gas and use it to warm the water feeding the boiler. Less fuel is then needed to bring that water up to steam temperature. A maker page for a Cleaver-Brooks stack economizer says the same equipment can heat boiler feedwater, makeup water or hot water boiler return water. The ORNL steam training slides add that economizers are commonly used to preheat boiler feedwater and can recover roughly 40% to 60% of the flue gas heat entering the economizer, for an overall reduction of about 3% to 10% of the heat the boiler uses.

The DOE rule of thumb

DOE Steam Tip Sheet #3 states that boiler efficiency can generally be increased by 1% for every 40°F reduction in flue gas temperature. The same sheet gives a table of recoverable heat for natural gas at 15% excess air and a final stack temperature of 250°F, and it shows a payback of under two years in its example. Separately, the DOE Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) O&M guide gives a related rule that every 40°F cut in net stack temperature saves about 1% to 2% of fuel. Both are rules of thumb, so treat results as estimates and check them with a combustion analyzer (see Combustion Analyzer Readings).

The FEMP guide also gives a caution worth remembering: high stack temperature can mean poor heat transfer, so clean and tune the boiler before adding heat recovery. An economizer on a fouled, out-of-tune boiler just hides the problem.

Worked example

Our own arithmetic, using the DOE rule. Setup: a 10 MMBtu/h input natural gas boiler, stack at 450°F, economizer brings it to 300°F, 6,000 operating hours per year. Assumed numbers, labeled: boiler efficiency 80% (used the way the tip sheet's own example divides by 0.80), gas at $8.00/MMBtu (the price the tip sheet's example uses), feedwater flow 8,000 lb/h, feedwater entering the economizer at 200°F.

  1. Stack temperature drop: 450 - 300 = 150°F.
  2. Efficiency gain by the rule: 150 / 40 = 3.75, so about 3.75 percentage points.
  3. Heat recovered: 10 MMBtu/h x 0.0375 = 0.375 MMBtu/h, or 375,000 Btu/h.
  4. Annual heat recovered: 0.375 x 6,000 = 2,250 MMBtu/yr.
  5. Annual fuel saved, dividing by the 0.80 boiler efficiency as the tip sheet example does: 2,250 / 0.80 = 2,812 MMBtu/yr (about 28,100 therms).
  6. Annual dollars: 2,812 x $8.00 = about $22,500 per year.
  7. Feedwater temperature rise: heat = flow x 1 Btu/lb·°F x dT, so dT = 375,000 / (8,000 x 1) = about 47°F. Feedwater goes from 200°F to about 247°F.

This is an estimate. Real savings depend on load, how much of the year the boiler runs at high fire, and the economizer's actual performance, so treat our number as a screening figure, not a guarantee. Check one thing in step 7: water at 247°F, with gas leaving at 300°F, is physically reasonable because the gas stays hotter than the water.

Non-condensing versus condensing

A conventional (non-condensing) economizer keeps flue gas above its dew point so no liquid forms in the flue. The ORNL slides say it typically raises overall efficiency by 2% to 4%. A condensing economizer deliberately cools the gas below its dew point to capture the latent heat in the water vapor from burning gas. DOE Steam Tip Sheet #26B says such a unit can improve overall heat recovery and steam system efficiency by up to 10%, and the ORNL slides cite 10% to 15% overall boiler efficiency gains. For the same reason, the same tip sheet says boilers with condensing economizers can exceed 90% overall efficiency.

The price is chemistry. Tip Sheet #26B says the unit must be designed to withstand corrosion from condensed water vapor, and that heat exchange surfaces may need special corrosion-resistant materials or coatings. The condensate is acidic and must be neutralized before it goes to a sewer or is reused as process water, so a drain and a plan for treating it are part of the design. A maker page for a two-stage Cleaver-Brooks unit shows the idea: a conventional stack section for feedwater, a condensing section for other cool water streams, 316 stainless steel on gas-side surfaces, a stainless condensate drain, and condensing on natural gas only. Tip Sheet #26B also notes that condensing needs a cool place to put the heat, such as makeup water or low-temperature process loads; it cannot work if the water entering is already hot. The same physics shows up in Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers.

The dew point limit on stack temperature

For a non-condensing unit, the question is how low the gas may be cooled. DOE Tip Sheet #3 lists minimum exhaust gas temperatures of 250°F for natural gas, 300°F for coal and low sulfur fuel oils, and 350°F for high sulfur fuel oils, set to prevent condensation and possible corrosion of the stack. The ORNL slides give a matching natural gas tip: keep feedwater above 220°F so the flue gas does not fall below about 250°F, and they warn that with sulfur in the fuel, condensate forms sulfuric acid. The ORNL steam survey guide adds that the practical limit on heat extraction is corrosion; without sulfur, the water vapor dew point sets the limit. A Cleaver-Brooks product guide shows a chart that treats natural gas water vapor condensing around 140°F and #2 oil around 180°F, and lists target economizer exit temperatures of about 290°F to 300°F on gas. The limits differ by source because they answer different questions. Use the number in your own economizer's manual, not a number from this page.

Bypass and controls

Many economizers include a gas bypass so the economizer can be taken out of service or tempered. The Cleaver-Brooks stack economizer page describes an internal exhaust gas bypass that can temper the exiting gas for stack corrosion control, and the maker guide says an open bypass damper reduces heat recovery by up to 50%. One model offers a control that modulates the bypass to hold the cooled gas temperature. A bypass is also the answer to the other failure case: the same guide calls for a bypass diverter to deal with excessive flue gas back pressure from fouling. Know where your bypass is, whether it is manual or automatic, and what position it should be in during warm up and shutdown.

Water-side flow and steaming

The coil is only safe with water in it while hot gas is flowing. The Cleaver-Brooks guide says there must always be continuous water flow through the economizer whenever flue gas is moving through it, and that minimum flow should equal the boiler's maximum evaporation rate. It warns that even with those guidelines flow may be interrupted, which can cause steaming in the economizer. The guide's fix for that is a tank and pump upstream of the feedwater tank so flow is not interrupted; follow the piping in your own unit's manual. Feedwater temperature matters too: see Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics and Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

Inspection and the stack temperature trend

The best fouling alarm is a number you already log. The FEMP guide says to log stack temperature daily, because an increasing stack temperature can indicate reduced heat transfer. The ORNL steam survey guide agrees: record flue gas temperature at least daily with load, and measure as close as possible to the last point of heat exchange. For an economizer, that means the exit of the economizer, compared at the same firing rate. If the exit gas temperature creeps up at steady load, the finned tubes are probably sooted (check combustion first, since the FEMP guide says soot is mainly caused by incomplete combustion), or water-side scale is hurting heat transfer.

  • Soot and deposits. Open the inspection door when the unit is cool and locked out, and look at the fins. The Cleaver-Brooks guide specifies a gas-tight hinged inspection door for access to the heating surface.
  • Tube leaks. Look for water on the fins, the casing or in the drain, and report any leak to your supervisor. Our general practice list, not a maker rule; follow the maker manual for repair.
  • Differential pressure. Gas-side pressure drop rises as fins foul. The maker guide lists 0.25 inches w.c. as a typical maximum for most of its boilers, so compare the reading to your nameplate and baseline.
  • Bypass and sootblower. Cycle the bypass damper and check position. Where a sootblower is installed, the ORNL survey guide suggests comparing flue gas temperature before and after cleaning to judge it.

Fit these checks into the routine in Preventive Maintenance Schedule for a Commercial Gas Boiler.

Field card: Boiler Stack Economizers

Warm the water with the gas, but keep water moving and gas above its limit.

  • DOE rule: about 1% efficiency per 40°F lower flue gas temperature.
  • Our example: 450 to 300°F is about 3.75 points, about $22,500 per year at assumed numbers.
  • Feedwater rise = heat recovered / (lb/h x 1 Btu/lb·°F).
  • Non-condensing: stay above the minimum stack temperature in the maker manual (DOE lists 250°F for natural gas).
  • Condensing: corrosion-resistant surfaces and a neutralized drain are required.
  • Never run hot gas over a coil with no water flow.
  • Log stack temperature and gas-side pressure drop; rising numbers mean fouling.
