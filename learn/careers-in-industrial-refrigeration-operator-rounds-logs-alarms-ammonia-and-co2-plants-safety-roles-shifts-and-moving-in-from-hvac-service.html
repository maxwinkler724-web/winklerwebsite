Careers in Industrial Refrigeration: Rounds, Logs and Ammonia Plants

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Cold storage warehouses, food plants and ice rinks run on big refrigeration plants that someone has to watch every shift. This lesson describes that work for an HVAC tech who is curious: what a round looks like, what goes in the log, who responds when an alarm sounds, and how service skills carry over. It does not cover pay, and it is a career overview, not training.

Where the work is

The federal worker safety agency lists ammonia as a common refrigerant in meat, poultry and fish processing, dairy and ice cream plants, wineries and breweries, juice and soft drink plants, cold storage warehouses and other food processing. Ice rinks use similar plants at a smaller scale; we cover them in Ice Rink Refrigeration. Carbon dioxide systems are newer. A national lab describes a booster arrangement in which low-temperature compressors support medium-temperature ones, running transcritical; see CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems for the pressures.

The job titles vary by employer, so read each posting for what it really asks. The federal labor statistics handbook has no page for industrial refrigeration operators as such. Its closest page, stationary engineers and boiler operators, describes people who run engines, boilers and auxiliary equipment, work in mechanical rooms, and mostly work in manufacturing, government, educational services and hospitals. Treat it as a neighbor, not an exact match.

What a round looks like

The handbook says these operators read gauges, meters and charts, watch computerized controls, and record data and keep logs of operation, maintenance and safety activity. A federal environmental agency manual for ammonia refrigeration gives a sense of how often. It says the engine room log should capture temperatures, volumes, vibrations, lubrication levels and pressures at least once per operating shift, and that the system should be walked at least daily by sight, sound and smell. Its sample analysis logs compressor and vessel readings every 4 hours. It also tells operators to watch for rising temperatures and pressures, falling oil pressure, and ammonia coming out of relief valves.

That is the heart of the job: notice drift before it becomes an alarm. A reading that is fine alone can be wrong in a trend.

Alarms and detectors

The same manual lists example detector settings from a sample analysis, not targets for your plant: 25 ppm to alarm a monitored location and start normal ventilation in a compressor room, 35 ppm to close liquid solenoid valves in cooler and freezer areas, and 150 ppm to start emergency ventilation. Your plant sets its own values. For alarm trend ideas on the controls side, see Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules, which covers a similar habit.

Safety training and emergency response roles

For the hazards and the rules, read Ammonia Refrigeration Basics for HVAC Techs; we do not repeat them here. Three points matter for a career choice:

  • Who may operate. The environmental agency manual says only fully trained and qualified operators may operate ammonia systems, initial training comes before assignment to a new process, and refresher training is required at least every 3 years. Other people working in ammonia areas need awareness training.
  • Evacuate or respond. The manual describes two kinds of facility. A non-responding facility shuts the process down safely and evacuates, and workers do not put on response gear. A responding facility needs an emergency response program and plan. The worker safety agency says the federal emergency response rules generally apply to employers who use ammonia refrigeration.
  • Response roles. The worker safety agency's emergency page says an emergency action plan covers people who stay to run critical operations before leaving, evacuation routes, and accounting for everyone afterward. For responders, a senior official coordinates the response, a safety officer is designated, personnel wear positive-pressure breathing apparatus, and backup personnel stand by. It notes that local responders usually arrive first.

Ask in an interview which kind of facility it is. That decides whether you would ever be a responder or an evacuee.

Shifts and on-call work

The handbook says that where a facility runs around the clock, stationary engineers may work one of three 8-hour shifts or one of two 12-hour shifts on a rotating basis, and many work weekends and holidays. It does not mention on-call duty for them. For HVAC and refrigeration mechanics, the handbook says schedules may include evening or weekend shifts and that they may be on call for emergencies. For industrial plants, ask each employer how callouts and rotation work. We do not have a source that gives a typical pattern.

Skills that carry over from HVAC service

The handbook lists detail orientation, math, mechanical skills, problem solving, and comfort with computerized and networked controls for HVAC mechanics, and it notes they follow rules on refrigerant recovery. Reading pressure and temperature, checking oil, understanding the refrigeration cycle and electrical troubleshooting all transfer. New to you will be the scale, the written procedures, and the log discipline. Our overview of tracks is in HVAC Career Tracks Compared.

How people move in

The handbook says most stationary engineers learn through long-term on-the-job training under an experienced operator, moving from basic tasks to harder repairs. Some do apprenticeships, usually about 4 years with 8,000 hours of on-the-job training and 600 hours of technical instruction. Some state and local governments require licenses in several classes by equipment type and size, a license may not transfer between places, and some employers require their own exams before you work unsupervised. We make no claim here about any one state. Advancement comes from higher-class licenses or employer exams, plus ongoing training as equipment and regulations change.

Worked example: a sample round log (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not data from a real plant. A lead operator logs one compressor set on a freezer-side ammonia system. Gauge readings are in psig and the logged saturation temperature comes from converting them.

Our own stated conversion values (rounded from standard ammonia saturation data; we could not open a linkable table, so verify against your plant's chart): 16 psig is about 0 F, 33 psig is about 20 F, 150 psig is about 85 F, 180 psig is about 95 F.

Compression ratio, assuming sea-level 14.7 psia atmosphere. Add atmospheric pressure to each gauge reading first.

  1. Compressor 1: suction 16 + 14.7 = 30.7 psia; discharge 180 + 14.7 = 194.7 psia; ratio 194.7 / 30.7 = 6.3.
  2. Compressor 2: suction 33 + 14.7 = 47.7 psia; discharge 150 + 14.7 = 164.7 psia; ratio 164.7 / 47.7 = 3.5.

Compressor 1 works through the bigger ratio because it serves the colder suction, which is why operators trend it closely. A discharge that creeps up for the same load is a flag to check the condenser.

Run-hour tally, with assumed hour-meter readings over 24 hours.

  1. Compressor 1: 12,430.0 minus 12,406.0 = 24.0 h (ran all day).
  2. Compressor 2: 8,963.5 minus 8,951.5 = 12.0 h.
  3. Compressor 3 (standby): 0 h.
  4. Total: 24.0 + 12.0 + 0 = 36.0 compressor-hours out of 3 x 24 = 72 available, or 50 percent.

A tally like this is how an operator decides when to rotate the lead compressor so wear is shared. The numbers are assumed; real plants set their own rotation rules.

Field card: Careers in industrial refrigeration

Notice drift early, know your role in an emergency.

  • Rounds: gauges, logs at least once per shift, a daily walk by sight, sound and smell (agency manual).
  • Only fully trained, qualified operators run ammonia systems; refresher training at least every 3 years.
  • Know whether your site evacuates or responds.
  • Gauge to absolute: add atmospheric pressure (about 14.7 psia at sea level).
  • Compression ratio = discharge psia divided by suction psia.
  • Entry is mostly on-the-job training; some places license operators by class.
