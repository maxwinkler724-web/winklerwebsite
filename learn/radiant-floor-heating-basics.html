Radiant Floor Heating Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A radiant floor heats the room by warming the floor itself. Because the whole floor is the heat emitter, it needs far gentler water than the baseboard most people picture.

The idea

In a hydronic radiant floor, tubing is placed in or under the floor and warm water runs through it. The floor surface becomes a large, low-temperature heater. Tubing can be embedded in a slab or thin topping, or sit under a wood subfloor, sometimes with metal or graphite plates that spread the heat sideways. The same boiler or heat source can serve other emitters too, but the floor usually needs its own, cooler water temperature.

Why the water is cooler

One radiant manufacturer says its floor systems typically run at 80 to 110 F, and calls 170 F boiler water far too hot for that floor. Compare that with traditional fin-tube baseboard, which was designed for water around 180 to 220 F. That gap is the heart of most radiant design questions: if a high-temperature boiler feeds a radiant floor, something has to lower the temperature on the way in. See Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics.

General limits you will see in guidelines

An industry guideline from the Radiant Professionals Alliance lists the kinds of limits designers work inside. Treat these as examples of how the limits look, not as numbers to design from, because the tubing and floor maker's instructions can differ.

  • Water in tubing embedded in gypsum or concrete: no more than 140 F unless the manufacturer approves otherwise.
  • Floor surface: about 85 F in areas with long foot contact, 90 F elsewhere, and 85 F on hardwood.
  • Floor coverings: the covering's resistance to heat flow must not exceed what the design assumed. Thick carpet and pad can hold heat in the floor.
  • Insulation under the tubing matters. Without it, heat goes down as well as up.

Oxygen and tubing

Plastic tube can let oxygen diffuse into the water. The same guideline names three answers: oxygen-barrier tubing, a heat exchanger that isolates non-barrier tubing from the iron parts, or water treatment that inhibits corrosion. That is why you may see a plate heat exchanger in a radiant system. It also ties to Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.

Circuits and manifolds

Radiant tubing is usually installed as several loops fed from a manifold, the way a parallel system works. The guideline gives maximum loop lengths by tube size (for example, 300 ft recommended and 350 ft maximum for 1/2 inch inside diameter tube). Long loops lose too much pressure and temperature. Short, equal loops are easier to balance. A related lesson on series vs. parallel piping covers why manifold circuits get nearly equal water temperature.

Pressure test and handling

For embedded tubing, the guideline describes pressure testing at 100 psi for at least 30 minutes before the slab is poured, then dropping to 30 psi to watch the system during the pour. Follow the tubing maker's actual test procedure and your inspector.

What a tech sees in the field

  • Slow response. A slab holds a lot of heat and reacts slowly, so do not expect an instant change after a thermostat bump.
  • Cold floor, warm boiler: check the mixing device, the loop circulator and air in the loops.
  • A floor that is too warm or a mixing setpoint set by guesswork: check the setpoint against the floor maker's limits.

The boiler and mixing equipment you pair with a floor have their own limits, including return temperature. See Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing.

Field card: Radiant Floor Heating Basics

Radiant works because the whole floor is the emitter, so the water can be gentle.

  • Radiant floors typically run far cooler than fin-tube baseboard (one maker cites 80 to 110 F).
  • Water and floor surface temperature limits come from the tubing and floor maker. One industry guideline lists 140 F max in a slab and about 85 to 90 F at the floor surface.
  • Floor coverings and missing insulation change how much heat reaches the room.
  • Oxygen-permeable tubing needs a barrier, a heat exchanger or treatment.
  • Tubing loops are usually parallel from a manifold and kept to the maker's maximum length.
  • Feeding a floor from a hot boiler needs a mixing device.
