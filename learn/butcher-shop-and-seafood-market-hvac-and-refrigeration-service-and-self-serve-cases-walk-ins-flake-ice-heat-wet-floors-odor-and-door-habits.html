Butcher Shop and Seafood Market HVAC and Refrigeration: Cases, Walk-Ins, Ice and Wet Floors

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A butcher shop or seafood market is a cold, wet, smelly place with a warm sales floor right next to it. The refrigeration gear dumps heat into the same rooms the crew works in, the floors get hosed, and customers want to stand comfortably at the counter. This lesson walks through the equipment and the habits that decide whether the shop stays steady.

What the food safety sources actually state

Start with the numbers the inspector cares about. Those are product temperatures, and they differ a little between sources. A state agriculture department guidance sheet for retail meat says coolers where meat is stored and displayed must hold 40°F or less. A county meat-establishment rule says refrigerated meat, fish, poultry and shellfish must not exceed 41°F at any time, that frozen product must be at 0°F or lower, and that thermometers in walk-ins, showcases and freezers must be accurate within 2°F. A state meat-handling rule chapter defines safe refrigerated temperature as 40°F or below and safe frozen temperature as 0°F or below. An older state rule for food stores, which we read as plain text, allowed 45°F for display. Which number applies to your shop is your local health authority's call, so ask before you set a control.

What we did not find is a stated air temperature for a cutting room. The sources we read set the temperature of the food, not of the room, and the federal sanitation guide we read gives no cutting room air figure either. Many shops keep the room cool to protect product while it is out of the case, but that is a shop choice, so we do not put a number on it here. Ask the local health department what it expects.

Service cases and self-serve cases

A service case sits behind the counter and is loaded and sold from by staff. A self-serve case is open or glass-door and sits on the sales floor. Both pull heat out of the product and push it somewhere. A national laboratory report on supermarket refrigeration says the main load on open vertical cases is warm, moist store air entering the case, attributed at more than 80 percent of their cooling load. It also says the moisture freezes on the coil, which forces frequent defrost. That is why the sales floor humidity matters to your case: wet store air means more frost, more defrosts and more meltwater to drain. For how the invisible air curtain works and fails, see Air Curtains on Open Multi-Deck Display Cases.

The same report notes that air-cooled cases reject their waste heat into the sales area, which adds to the air conditioning load. A self-contained case therefore heats the room it stands in. A case on a remote rack sends that heat outdoors, but still chills the floor around it, which brings us to comfort.

Walk-in coolers and freezers

The walk-in is where most of the product waits. Keep three habits in mind. First, the door: every long open door lets warm, wet air in, and that becomes frost on the coil. Second, the coil: a heavy frost layer or a stuck defrost makes the box drift warm even when the compressor runs. Third, the thermometer: a reading that is off by a few degrees means the product is warmer or colder than you think. Read Evaporator Design TD in Walk-In Coolers for how coil size sets box humidity. Meat that sits in a dry, fast airstream loses surface moisture and color, so airflow aimed straight at open product is worth a look. Freezers must be held at 0°F or lower by the rules above, so confirm the defrost heater, door gasket and door heater before blaming the compressor.

Flake ice machines and their heat

Seafood counters and meat cases often sit on beds of flake ice. A flake machine makes ice by freezing a thin film on a cold surface and scraping it off, and every pound of ice it makes carries heat out of the water and into the condenser. An air-cooled machine blows that heat into its room. The ice machine has to be sited so its condenser has clear air, and a hot, cramped corner shortens its life and cuts its output. See Flake and Nugget Ice Machines for the machine itself.

A state food store rule we read also says ice sold or used for food must be made from potable water and handled in a sanitary way, that ice used only to chill food in storage may not be used or sold for people to eat, and that ice makers and ice storage may not sit under exposed sewer lines or leaking water lines. Keep that in mind when a new ice machine is being placed.

Wet floors, humidity and condensation

Cleaning with water is part of the job. The federal sanitation guide we read calls for adequate floor drainage where floors are flooded for cleaning, ventilation that controls odors, vapors and condensation, and notes that heavily beaded condensation on a ceiling that is not cleaned regularly is a problem. The state food store rule requires trapped drains on water-flushed floors and coved, sealed floor and wall joints, and says there should be no direct connection between the sewer and drains from equipment. The practical result for the HVAC tech is a room that is humid in the morning and dry by afternoon, and a coil, duct or ceiling that can sweat if the air is not moved and dried. Do not aim supply air so it blows over wet floors toward the product, and use corrosion-resistant coils and fittings where salt and cleaners are in the air.

Odor and drain trap issues

Most odor calls come from three places. A dry floor drain trap lets sewer gas rise, and a trap that is rarely used dries out; the fix is to refill it or use a trap primer, covered in Mechanical Room Floor Drains and Trap Primers. A case or walk-in drain line that is plugged with scraps or ice leaves a pool in the pan that smells. And a drain that connects straight to the sewer without an air break can pull gas back into the box, which is why the state rule above bans a direct connection. Walk-in evaporator drains in freezers also need heat to stay open, so a cold floor drain line is a place to look when a freezer floods.

Customer comfort next to cold cases

Open cases spill cold air at floor level, so shoppers standing at the case feel it on their ankles while the rest of the store is warm. Keep supply diffusers off the case air curtain, keep doors and entrance drafts away from the case, and keep warm sales floor air from blowing into the case. The ice cream shop lesson covers the same trade-off for a small store, see Ice Cream Shop HVAC and Refrigeration. The meat plant lesson, Meat and Food Processing Plant Washdown Room HVAC, covers the bigger version of the wet cold room.

Door and curtain habits

  • Close the walk-in door every time. Do not prop it for a delivery that is not being carried in.
  • Use strip curtains in busy doorways, and replace them when they are cut, since a torn curtain does little.
  • Pull case night covers if the case has them, and do not stack product above the load line.
  • Keep back doors and loading doors shut while the room is being cooled.
  • Wipe down gaskets and check that doors latch on their own.

Worked example (our own): cooling for a cutting room

This is our own example with our own assumptions, not a measurement. We picked every number below so you can follow the arithmetic.

Assumptions: a cutting room of 20 ft by 15 ft, so 300 sq ft. A self-contained service case in the room rejects 3,000 Btu/h to the room. An air-cooled flake ice machine in the room rejects 6,000 Btu/h to the room. Lights total 600 W. Two workers add 500 Btu/h each. Walls, ceiling, door leakage and everything else we are not counting separately is 10 Btu/h per sq ft. The walk-in condensing unit is outdoors, so it adds nothing. We ignore wet cleaning moisture and a margin.

  1. Ice machine: 6,000 Btu/h.
  2. Service case: 3,000 Btu/h.
  3. Lights: 600 W × 3.412 Btu/h per watt = 2,047 Btu/h.
  4. People: 2 × 500 = 1,000 Btu/h.
  5. Envelope and other: 300 sq ft × 10 = 3,000 Btu/h.
  6. Total: 6,000 + 3,000 + 2,047 + 1,000 + 3,000 = 15,047 Btu/h.
  7. Tons: 15,047 / 12,000 = about 1.25 tons of cooling.

The ice machine alone is 6,000 of 15,047, about 40 percent. If we moved its condenser outdoors, the room load would be 15,047 − 6,000 = 9,047 Btu/h, about 0.75 tons. That is a design choice to talk about, not a rule. A real sizing would also count wet cleaning and a margin.

Field card: Butcher Shop and Seafood Market HVAC and Refrigeration

Cold product, warm room, wet floor.

  • Product limits in the sources we read: 40 or 41°F cold, 0°F frozen. Ask the local health authority which applies.
  • Open cases take most of their load from room air, so a humid sales floor means more frost.
  • An air-cooled ice machine or case heats its own room.
  • Dry traps cause odor. Refill them or prime them.
  • Do not blow supply air at wet floors or open product.
  • Close doors, repair curtains, and check gaskets.
  • Our own example: 15,047 Btu/h is about 1.25 tons, 40 percent from the ice machine.
