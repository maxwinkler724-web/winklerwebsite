Measuring a Wall's Real U-Value With a Heat Flux Plate

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A wall's label says one thing and the wall may do another. Settled insulation, gaps, moisture and old repairs all change how much heat actually crosses it. A heat flux plate lets you measure the wall as it really is, in place, without cutting it open. This lesson covers how the plate works, where to mount it, how many days of data you need, and how to turn the numbers into a U-value and an R-value.

Why measure instead of calculate

Our lesson on heat loss through walls and ceilings shows how to add layer resistances in series and correct for framing. That gives a calculated value, and it depends on knowing what is inside the wall. In an older house nobody may have drawings. A university thesis on this topic points out that existing buildings often lack plans, and argues for measured values over design values. It describes the heat flow meter method as the commonly known in-situ technique, while noting that its accuracy under changing natural weather is not fully understood. So treat a field result as a good estimate with an honest error band, not a lab number.

How a heat flux plate works

The plate is a thin disc or square of material with a known thermal resistance. A thermopile, which is many small thermocouple junctions wired in series, measures the small temperature difference between its two faces. Heat flux is proportional to that difference, so the output voltage is proportional to the flux. You divide the voltage by the plate's calibrated sensitivity, which is printed on its calibration sheet, to get watts per square meter. One maker's user manual gives a typical sensitivity of about 60 microvolts per W/m², so building-wall signals are only a few hundred microvolts. Use a logger with good low-voltage resolution, and follow the maker's wiring limits.

The plate must touch the wall across its whole face. The same manual warns that even a 0.1 mm air gap can raise the plate's effective resistance by about 60 percent, and it recommends tape or adhesive, not paste that dries out. It also suggests at least two plates per location.

Where to mount it

  • Warm side, indoors. A published review of the method recommends measuring from inside, so sun and rain never hit the plate. Place air temperature sensors about 30 to 40 cm from the wall at the same height, because room air is stratified.
  • Away from studs, corners and heat sources. Pick a clear, uniform area of cavity wall, away from drafts, cracks, heaters, fans and sunlit glass. Corners and framing carry extra heat and would not represent the field of the wall.
  • Look first with a thermal camera. The same review lists a thermographic check beforehand for thermal bridges. Our lesson on thermal imaging cameras explains what the camera can and cannot see. Use it to find stud lines, and to spot cold or odd patches that suggest missing or wet insulation. Do not put the plate on a wet patch and call the result the wall's R-value. Fix the moisture problem first.

Why you need a big temperature difference and several days

The plate reads the true flux only if the heat flowing is large compared with sensor noise and with the day-to-day wobble. The published review requires indoor and outdoor air to differ by more than 10 °C (18 °F), a minimum of 72 hours of data, wind below about 3 m/s, and preferably cloudy weather. It says heavy structures can need 7 to 14 days. The plate maker's manual asks for at least 96 hours and for the indoor temperature to stay steady within about 3 °C, and suggests heating or cooling the room to create a steady, high flux. The sources differ a little, so we plan for four or more days of data and a winter night-and-day cycle.

The averaging method

True steady state never happens outdoors. The averaging method gets around that with long sums:

U = (sum of heat flux readings) / (sum of indoor-minus-outdoor air temperature differences)

Each reading pair is taken at the same moment, and the sums run over the whole test. Because every hour is weighted by how much temperature difference it had, the quiet, warm hours count for little and the cold, strong hours count for a lot. The result is in W/m²·K. To convert, divide by 5.678 for Btu/h·ft²·°F. R is the reciprocal of U. If you use air temperatures, the R you get includes both air films, just like the whole-wall figure in the series lesson.

Why single readings lie

  • Sun and wind. Sun on the outside raises the outer surface temperature and cuts or even reverses the flux for a while. Wind strips the outside air film and raises it. Neither is a property of the wall.
  • Thermal mass lag. Masonry and plaster store heat, so the flux you read now reflects the temperature difference of hours earlier. Heavy walls need longer runs, and it helps to start and stop the sums at the same time of day.
  • Room swings. Furnace cycling moves the indoor air temperature. Averaging over days smooths it.

Worked example (our own, with assumed readings)

All the readings below are our own invented numbers for teaching, not measured data. Assume a 2x4 wood-frame wall, plate mounted indoors in a cavity, air sensors indoors and outdoors, cloudy cold days, 24 hourly-average pairs logged per day for four days (96 hours). Step 1: sums. Every day has 24 readings, so the sums are 24 times the day means added up. Sum of q = 24 x (9.8 + 11.9 + 8.4 + 10.7) = 24 x 40.8 = 979.2 W/m². Sum of ΔT = 24 x (21.0 + 25.5 + 18.0 + 23.0) = 24 x 87.5 = 2100 K. The factor 24 cancels, so you can also divide 40.8 by 87.5. All four days have ΔT well above 10 K.

Step 2: U. U = 979.2 / 2100 = 0.466 W/m²·K.

Step 3: R. R(SI) = 1 / 0.466 = 2.14 m²·K/W. In US units, R = 2.14 x 5.678 = 12.2 (ft²·°F·h/Btu), equal to U = 0.082 Btu/h·ft²·°F. Check: 1 / 0.0821 = 12.2.

Step 4: calculated comparison. Assume these layer values (our own, for the example): inside air film 0.68, half-inch drywall 0.45, cavity insulation 11 (assumed to have settled below its nameplate), half-inch sheathing 0.62, wood siding 0.81, outside air film 0.17. Cavity path R = 0.68 + 0.45 + 11 + 0.62 + 0.81 + 0.17 = 13.73. For the stud path, replace the 11 with a 3.5 inch stud at an assumed 1.25 per inch = 4.375: R = 7.105. Assume 25 percent of the area is framing: U = 0.75/13.73 + 0.25/7.105 = 0.0546 + 0.0352 = 0.0898, so whole-wall R = 11.1.

Step 5: compare like with like. The plate sat in a cavity away from studs, so it should be compared with the cavity path, 13.73. Measured 12.2 is 12.2 / 13.73 = 89 percent of that, about 11 percent lower. That points to cavity insulation worse than assumed, perhaps more settling or gaps, and is worth a thermal camera look. The measured 12.2 is not the whole-wall value of 11.1. To estimate the whole wall from the measurement, you would still apply the framing correction.

Why not one reading? Suppose a sunny hour showed q = 4.0 W/m² at ΔT = 12 K. That single pair gives U = 0.33 and R = 17.0, far from 12.2. The sums over four days do not swing like that.

Judging the result

  • Run at least two locations when you can, and compare. Large disagreement means find out why before trusting either.
  • Report the dates, temperatures and conditions along with the number.
  • A measured value lower than calculated is a prompt to inspect, not a verdict. Good causes to check: wet insulation, voids, air movement in the cavity, and the sensor contact.
  • Heat loss from the air path (infiltration) is separate. See the air leak lesson.

Field card: Measuring a Wall's Real U-Value With a Heat Flux Plate

Plate on the warm side, in the cavity, for days, in the cold.

  • The plate has a known resistance and a thermopile; flux = voltage / sensitivity.
  • Mount indoors with no air gap, away from studs, corners and heat sources.
  • Scan with a thermal camera first for studs, voids and wet spots.
  • Want a difference above 10 °C and at least 72 to 96 hours; heavy walls take longer.
  • U = sum of flux / sum of temperature difference. R = 1 / U.
  • Divide W/m²·K by 5.678 for Btu/h·ft²·°F.
  • Compare cavity reading with cavity calculation, not with the whole wall.
