Attached Garages and Your Home Heating and Cooling: Keeping Fumes Out

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An attached garage shares a wall, and often a ceiling, with the rooms you live in. Car exhaust, gasoline vapor and solvent fumes do not stay on their side of that wall unless the wall is tight. This lesson explains how garage air gets into a house, what your heating and cooling equipment can do to help or hurt, and which simple habits and repairs keep the air inside clean.

How garage air gets into living space

A federal indoor air guide lists exhaust from attached garages as a source of carbon monoxide in homes, and it names attached-garage vehicle emissions as a source of benzene. It also lists stored fuels, paints, varnishes and many cleaning and hobby products as sources of organic fumes. Carbon monoxide is a gas you cannot see or smell, so the first sign of a problem can be a headache, not an odor. A federal health agency says the common signs are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, that they are often mistaken for the flu, and that people who are asleep may die before they notice anything.

Air moves through any gap when there is a pressure difference across it. Three paths matter most in a typical house:

  • The shared wall and door. Gaps at the framing, around pipes and wires, at the top and bottom of the wall, and around a worn door let air pass. A national-lab guide on sealing an attached garage points to the rim joist, the drywall edges at the top and bottom plates, the joist bays, and every penetration through the shared wall or ceiling.
  • Ducts or an air handler in the garage. Duct seams, a leaky cabinet, or a loose filter slot are holes in the pipe that carries your air. If the system sits in the garage, those holes open to garage air.
  • Return leaks. A return side runs below room pressure while the blower is on. If a return duct, a platform or a joist-bay return passes through the garage and has gaps, the blower pulls garage air in and delivers it to every room.

The same national-lab guide notes that when a house is depressurized, garage air can be pulled in, and that a house can end up short of makeup air. A federal test report on one two-story house (it says its results may not match other houses) measured the garage-to-house leakage and found that running house exhaust fans raised the share of house air that came from the garage, from about 1 percent to roughly 3 to 5 percent. Supply ventilation that pressurized the house suppressed nearly all of that transfer. One house is not a rule, but it shows the direction: pulling the house negative pulls the garage in.

Never take return air from a garage

As general practice, return air should never come from a garage. The national-lab guide says no return registers should be installed in the garage, and it advises avoiding supply or exhaust registers there as well. The reason is plain: whatever is in the garage, such as exhaust left from a cold start, gasoline from a mower or a can, paint thinner or pesticide, would be delivered by the blower to the whole house. If a duct run passes through the garage for any reason, it needs sealed seams and sealed joints, and it should be tested instead of assumed tight.

The same guide says the best practice is to keep heating and cooling equipment out of the garage. If an air handler must be there, it recommends an air-sealed closet, mastic-sealed and tested ducts, and for a fuel-burning furnace a sealed-combustion, direct-vent model. Our bonus room lesson covers the ducts that serve a room over the garage, and our parking garage lesson shows how large commercial garages handle the same gases; both are linked below.

Seal the shared wall and the door

  • The wall and ceiling. A federal energy page lists foam backer rod with caulk, or low-expansion spray foam, for gaps at shared surfaces between living space and an unfinished garage. Sealing also helps comfort, since the garage is usually not conditioned.
  • The door. As general practice the house-to-garage door should be solid, weatherstripped, have a tight sweep, and close itself. The national-lab guide recommends an insulated, fire-rated door with a good seal for new work, and for existing homes a weatherstripped door with a sweep and an automatic closing hinge. It says to test how tight the door seals. A door that someone props open for convenience defeats all of it.
  • Penetrations. Seal around pipes, wires and any hole a trade made years ago. A fire-rated wall needs a fire-rated fix, so ask a pro if you are unsure of the product.

A caution from a federal indoor air guide applies here: tightening a house reduces outdoor air coming in, which can raise indoor pollutant levels if the source is inside. Seal the garage first and keep sources out; do not seal the house and leave the sources in.

Habits that matter as much as sealing

A federal health agency says never to run a car or truck in a garage attached to a house, even with the garage door open. The federal product safety agency says the same, and adds that a vehicle should not run, or have run, in an attached garage or next to the home. Back the car out first, then let it warm up on the driveway. The indoor air guide also says not to idle the car inside the garage, and to buy only the fuel you will use right away and discard unused paint and fuel supplies. Store solvents and fuels in the garage in tightly closed containers, not in the house, and use them in a well-ventilated area.

Carbon monoxide alarms

The federal product safety agency advises alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, away from heating vents and not above fuel-burning appliances. The health agency says to place battery-powered or battery-backup alarms near each sleeping area. Our suggestion, based on those two statements: if anyone sleeps in a room over the garage, treat it as a sleeping area, and put an alarm in or right outside it. Alarms have a recommended replacement age, so check the maker's literature. An alarm is a backup; it does not replace the habits above. See our carbon monoxide alarm lesson for what to do when one sounds.

Worked example (our own): how much garage air can a return leak pull in?

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any house.

Assumptions: a two-car garage 22 ft by 22 ft with a 9 ft ceiling, and a return-duct leak that pulls 50 cubic feet per minute (cfm) of air out of the garage while the blower runs. All of the leak air is assumed to come from the garage, and the blower runs the whole hour.

  1. Garage volume = 22 x 22 x 9 = 484 x 9 = 4,356 cubic feet.
  2. Air pulled in per hour = 50 cfm x 60 minutes = 3,000 cubic feet.
  3. Ratio = 3,000 / 4,356 = 0.69, about 69 percent of the garage's volume each hour.
  4. Time to move one garage volume = 4,356 / 3,000 = 1.45 hours, roughly 87 minutes.

Meaning: a leak that sounds small, 50 cfm, moves most of a garage's air into the ductwork within an hour. In real life fresh outdoor air also leaks into the garage, and the leak air mixes, so the garage air would not be pure exhaust. The point is the scale: a modest duct leak is a large flow compared with the garage itself, so a tight, tested return matters more than people expect.

When to have a tech check duct leakage

  • Any duct, air handler or filter box is in the garage, or a duct passes through the shared wall or ceiling.
  • You smell fuel, exhaust or solvents in the house when the system runs, or after the car has been in the garage.
  • A room over the garage is hard to heat or cool, or the return seems weak.
  • An alarm sounded or someone has unexplained headaches that improve away from home. Leave and call for help first if an alarm sounds; do not wait for a service visit.

A tech can test duct leakage with a pressure test and seal the seams with mastic. Our lessons on duct leakage testing and sealing duct leaks explain what the test tells you.

Field card: Attached Garages and Your Home Heating and Cooling

Keep the garage's air in the garage.

  • Never take return air from a garage; keep equipment out of it where you can.
  • Seal the shared wall and penetrations; weatherstrip the door and make it self-closing.
  • Never run or idle a vehicle in an attached garage, even with the door open.
  • Alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, including a room over the garage.
  • Our example: 50 cfm x 60 = 3,000 ft³ per hour vs a 4,356 ft³ garage, about 69 percent.
  • Have a tech check ducts in or through the garage.
