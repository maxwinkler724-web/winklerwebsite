Ceiling Water Stain Below an Attic AC: Pan, Sweat, Fan or Roof?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A brown ring or a drip on the ceiling under an attic air conditioner is a message, and the first job is to read it. Four different problems leave almost the same mark: a full condensate pan, a register that sweats, a bath fan duct that drips, and a roof leak. The weather, the time of year and whether the cooling is running tell you which one you probably have.

Start with safety

Water and electricity do not mix. A federal consumer-product safety checklist warns that mixing electricity and water can cause a serious shock or fire hazard. A ceiling light fixture is wired into the same ceiling that is dripping. If water is falling near a light fixture, stay out from under it and switch the air conditioner off at the thermostat. If the fixture or its switch is wet, do not touch it; a federal emergency-management guide says to turn power off at the breaker, but never to do so if you would have to stand in water to reach the panel. In that case call an electrician.

Do not go into a hot attic alone. Attic air in summer can be far hotter than the outdoors (we have not measured yours, so we give no number). A federal worker-safety agency lists heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea and a fast pulse as heat exhaustion, and confusion, slurred speech or a hot dry skin as signs of heat stroke, which calls for 911 and rapid cooling. Tell someone you are going up, go in the cooler part of the day, and stop if you feel off. Most of the diagnosis below can be done from the room below.

1. Condensate pan overflow

A cooling coil pulls water out of the air. That water drops into a pan and leaves through a drain line. If the line clogs, the pan fills and spills over, and the water finds the ceiling below. A federal environmental agency tells homeowners to keep air conditioning drip pans clean and the drain lines unobstructed and flowing. We cover the hardware in secondary drain pans and float switches and condensate traps.

  • Season: only when the cooling runs, so late spring through early fall. A stain that shows up in January is not this one, unless the unit was run in cooling mode.
  • Pattern: the stain sits right under the air handler or its drain route, and it grows when the system has been running for hours, even on a dry day.
  • Clue: a float switch that has shut the system off, or a unit that quit cooling, points the same way.

2. Sweating ceiling registers and ducts

A cold surface in humid air collects water. The metal face of a register, or a duct with thin or damaged insulation, can sit below the dew point of the room or attic air. Water beads, runs, and stains the ceiling around the register. See sweating ducts and vents for fixes.

  • Season: humid cooling weather, often worst on muggy days and after the system has run a long stretch.
  • Pattern: a ring around one register, or a line along a duct, while the pan area is dry. The water is clean and appears whether or not it rained.
  • Clue: drops hanging off the register face when you look at it, and a duct that feels cold and damp.

3. Bath fan duct condensation

Shower steam is warm and wet. A state extension article says a shower or bath adds about 2.0 pounds of moisture per day to a home, and that condensation forms when warm, moist air meets a cold surface, leaving water, frost or ice. A bath fan duct running through a cold attic is exactly that surface. The same article says to vent the exhaust outdoors and not into the attic. A long, uninsulated or sagging duct can collect water that runs back toward the fan or leaks at a joint. Read more in bathroom exhaust fans: sizing, ducting and moisture control.

  • Season: mostly cold weather, when the attic is cold. Frost can build up on a cold night and melt on a warm day, so drips can follow a thaw.
  • Pattern: the stain sits under or near the bath fan or its duct, and it shows up within hours of showers.
  • Clue: it can happen with the air conditioner off for months.

4. Roof leak

A roof leak depends on rain, wind direction and snowmelt, not on whether the air handler runs. A state extension guide for checking a house suggests looking at the underside of the roof where it is reachable for water stains or dampness, and for damaged or loose shingles. A federal environmental agency notes that roof leaks are one way hidden moisture reaches materials above the ceiling.

  • Season: any season, but it tracks storms. The stain often appears one to several days after rain, when the insulation has soaked and spread the water.
  • Pattern: the stain is not under the air handler, a register or a bath fan. It may sit by a chimney, vent pipe or a wall line.
  • Clue: dry weather, dry ceiling, even with the air conditioner running all day.

Safe checks you can do from below

  1. Write down the date, the weather for the past two days, and whether the cooling or a shower was running. Take a photo of the stain with a coin beside it.
  2. Look at the room, not the attic: is the stain under the register, the bath fan, the equipment, or none of them?
  3. If the stain is wet and dripping, set a bucket, move furniture, and turn the air conditioner off at the thermostat. A pan overflow stops making new water once the cooling stops.
  4. Look at the indoor humidity if you have a meter. A federal environmental agency recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and says condensation can be a sign of high humidity.
  5. Check the outdoor drain outlet, if you can see it from the ground, for a trickle while the cooling runs.
  6. Dry wet materials fast. The same agency says drying within 24 to 48 hours generally prevents mold growth.

Worked example (our own): can a register sweat?

Our own example, with assumed inputs. Assume a house at 75 F and 55 percent relative humidity, and a register face that settles near 57.5 F because 55 F supply air is flowing behind it. These are assumptions for the example, not measurements of your house.

  1. Convert 75 F to Celsius: (75 - 32) x 5/9 = 23.9 C.
  2. Use a standard dew point approximation (the Magnus form with constants 17.62 and 243.12 C). First, g = ln(0.55) + 17.62 x 23.9 / (243.12 + 23.9) = -0.598 + 1.577 = 0.979.
  3. Dew point = 243.12 x 0.979 / (17.62 - 0.979) = 14.3 C, which is about 57.7 F.
  4. The register face at 57.5 F is about 0.2 F below the dew point, so it is right at the edge and can start to sweat.

Now change one input. At 60 percent the same math gives a dew point near 60.2 F, so a 57.5 F face is about 2.7 F below it and sweats more. At 50 percent the dew point is near 55.1 F, so the same face stays dry. That is why the same register drips on a muggy week and stays quiet in a dry one. A federal-agency humidity range and a dehumidifier or a longer air conditioner run time are the homeowner levers; see air handlers and ducts in unconditioned attics for the equipment side.

When to call a pro

  • Water near a light fixture, a wet fixture, or a ceiling that is bulging or sagging. Call an electrician and keep people out from under it.
  • The pan overflowed once. Clearing the clog is a service task, and the cause (a poor trap, a sagging line, no float switch) should be fixed. The pan can overflow again.
  • You see a stain but cannot tell which of the four it is, or the stain keeps returning.
  • You think the cause is the roof. That is a roofer, not an HVAC visit.
  • Insulation is wet, there is a musty smell, or you see mold. Dry quickly and ask a pro about the source.

Field card: Ceiling Water Stain Below an Attic AC

Match the stain to the weather and the equipment.

  • Pan overflow: under the equipment, cooling season, grows while the unit runs.
  • Sweating register or duct: ring around one register, humid days, dry pan.
  • Bath fan duct: near the fan, cold weather, after showers, even with the AC off.
  • Roof leak: follows rain or snowmelt, not tied to any equipment.
  • Water near a light: stay clear, switch the cooling off, call an electrician.
  • Do not go into a hot attic alone.
  • Our own example: 75 F and 55% RH gives about a 58 F dew point.
