Chiller Plant Staging and Sequencing: When to Add or Drop a Chiller

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A plant with two or more chillers has to answer one question all day long: how many machines should be running right now? Staging is the set of rules that answers it. This lesson covers why plants stage, the signals they use, why two chillers at part load can use less power than one near full load, and what to trend so you can tell whether the rules are working.

Why a plant stages chillers at all

A building's cooling load swings from a few percent of design on a mild day to near full load on a hot afternoon. One big chiller cannot follow that range well, and a plant with several machines can match the load more closely by turning machines on and off. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) building re-tuning simulator describes the basic setup: chillers are defined in priority order, identical machines often rotate priority, and a smaller "pony" chiller can be given top priority so it handles low-load hours. It also lists a staging scheme built on the part-load ratio at which to stage up and stage down, plus efficiency entered as kW/ton or COP.

For the plant basics, see Chiller Plant Fundamentals. For how the compressor type changes part-load behavior, see Chiller Compressors: Centrifugal, Screw and Scroll Compared.

Common staging signals

Sources describe several ways to decide that another chiller is needed. None is universal, and the right one depends on the plant's piping and controls.

  • Flow. An ACHR News article on primary-secondary control (Petrus, Dabhi and Fahd, 2022) stages chillers on secondary chilled water flow. Its example holds the flow threshold for 15 minutes before a stage change and labels the values adjustable examples. For the piping behind this, see Primary-Secondary Piping Basics.
  • Supply temperature and calculated load. Gil Avery's ASHRAE Journal article from 2001 says that staging on flow assumes load tracks flow and that the temperature difference stays at design, and he calls those assumptions "generally wrong" for many real plants. He recommends staging a chiller on with a low-set secondary supply temperature sensor, and staging off using a secondary return sensor plus a load calculation (flow times temperature difference).
  • Part-load ratio. The PNNL simulator treats the part-load ratio for stage-up and stage-down as an input to the staging scheme.
  • Load and lift together. A Consulting-Specifying Engineer article (2021) says to stage on both cooling load and lift, not load alone, because staging on load only "could cause multiple centrifugal chillers to operate in surge." Lift here means the gap between leaving chilled water and leaving condenser water temperature.
  • Efficiency. A 2016 Applied Energy paper by Huang, Zuo and Sohn studied cooling-load-based sequencing, where the load points that trigger staging are tuned to cut energy, including tower and pump energy.

Time delays and lockouts

A stage change that happens on every brief spike wears equipment and upsets the loop. The ACHR example above uses a 15 minute hold on the flow threshold and a 20 minute lockout after any stage change, which the authors say ensures a minimum on and off time before the next change. Their sequence also limits demand on the running chillers and waits up to five minutes before opening valves and starting the standby chiller. The step-by-step startup logic is covered in Chiller Startup, Shutdown Sequence and Safeties. Every number in that article is an example setting from one design, not a rule. Your plant's sequence of operations and the chiller maker's required minimum delays govern.

Why two chillers can beat one

Chiller efficiency changes with load, and for many machines it is not best at 100 percent. The Trane Engineers Newsletter Live program on variable-speed compressors shows the reason for centrifugal machines: at design conditions a fixed-speed 900 ton example is better (0.513 kW/ton versus 0.585 for the variable-speed unit), but the variable-speed advantage shows up at part load, and it needs reduced lift. A Trane program on all-variable-speed plants says "Multiple chillers improve system efficiency" in its annualized comparisons of one, two and three chillers. The Consulting-Specifying Engineer article says variable-speed chillers run most efficiently when the maximum number of chillers operate at low load.

Avery makes a related point for plants that stage too eagerly on flow: keeping extra chillers online loads each one lightly and runs more pumps and auxiliaries, and minimizing chillers online lowers plant kW/ton. So the answer depends on the machines and the conditions. Constant-speed machines, high lift, and a second chiller's extra pump and tower power can tip the result back toward one machine. The Trane booklet "State of the Art Chilled Water System Design" lists tower plus condenser pump energy by load (for its past-practice case, 0.11 kW/ton at 100 percent load rising to 0.44 at 25 percent), which shows why auxiliaries cannot be ignored.

Worked example: one chiller at 90 percent or two at 45 percent

Our own example. The part-load table below is made up for teaching. It is not data for any real machine. Two 500 ton chillers serve a 450 ton building load.

Load on one chillerkW/ton (made up)
25 percent0.70
50 percent0.50
75 percent0.52
100 percent0.58

Option A, one chiller (Our own arithmetic).

  1. Percent load: 450 tons / 500 tons = 0.90, so 90 percent.
  2. 90 percent lies between 75 percent (0.52) and 100 percent (0.58). Fraction of the way: (90 - 75) / (100 - 75) = 15 / 25 = 0.60.
  3. Interpolate: 0.52 + 0.60 x (0.58 - 0.52) = 0.52 + 0.036 = 0.556 kW/ton.
  4. Plant kW: 450 tons x 0.556 = 250.2 kW.

Option B, two chillers sharing equally (Our own arithmetic).

  1. Each carries 450 / 2 = 225 tons, which is 225 / 500 = 45 percent.
  2. 45 percent lies between 25 percent (0.70) and 50 percent (0.50). Fraction: (45 - 25) / (50 - 25) = 20 / 25 = 0.80.
  3. Interpolate: 0.70 - 0.80 x (0.70 - 0.50) = 0.70 - 0.16 = 0.54 kW/ton.
  4. Each chiller: 225 x 0.54 = 121.5 kW. Two chillers: 243.0 kW.

Compare (Our own arithmetic). 250.2 - 243.0 = 7.2 kW less for two chillers, about 7.2 / 250.2 = 2.9 percent. That is the chiller-only result. The second chiller also brings its own chilled water pump and condenser water pump and, usually, more tower fan work. If those added auxiliaries draw more than 7.2 kW in this example, one chiller wins. With a flatter table, such as a constant-speed machine whose best point is near full load, one chiller could win outright. That is why the sources keep returning to whole-plant kW/ton rather than chiller kW alone.

Stage-up rule example

Now a stage-up rule. This is an example setting, not a rule: stage the second chiller on when the first exceeds 90 percent load for 15 minutes.

  1. 90 percent of 500 tons is 0.90 x 500 = 450 tons (Our own). The first chiller must carry more than 450 tons.
  2. Suppose the load climbs to 470 tons. That is 470 / 500 = 94 percent, so the timer starts. Fifteen minutes is 15 x 60 = 900 seconds (Our own).
  3. If the load stays above 450 tons for the whole 900 seconds, the second chiller is called. If it dips to 440 tons at minute 10, the timer resets and nothing starts.
  4. Once both run and share 470 tons, each carries 235 tons, which is 47 percent (Our own).

Stage-down needs a different point than stage-up. Our own reasoning: if one chiller would land at 90 percent right after dropping the second, the stage-up rule would fire again at once. A stage-down point set well below the stage-up point, plus a lockout like the 20 minute one in the ACHR example, avoids that cycling.

Rotation, pumps and towers

Lead-lag rotation spreads run hours. PNNL notes that identical chillers often rotate priority, and the ACHR sequence alternates pumps between lead and lag on a run-time schedule. One maker's chiller plant control page also lists rotation among the functions its controls handle. Check your own sequence for who rotates and when.

Staging touches the whole plant. In the ACHR example, tower bypass valves and fan speed hold condenser water temperature, and the BAS proves pump flow before starting a chiller. The Consulting-Specifying Engineer article says to run as many towers as possible as long as none drops below its minimum allowable flow, typically 40 to 50 percent, and to stage chilled water pumps on flow ratio. The Trane all-variable-speed program says tower fan control is critical with variable-speed chillers and to prioritize stability. The Huang study found a sequence change that cut chiller energy 4.9 to 11.8 percent but raised cooling tower energy 5.8 to 43.8 percent, leaving total plant savings of 0.5 to 5.6 percent depending on the approach. Optimum Energy, a controls maker, reports one eight-chiller university plant improving from 0.897 to 0.681 kW/ton in the first year with its optimization system. That is a vendor-reported result for one site, not something to expect elsewhere.

What to trend

Our own suggestion: log total plant kW/ton, load in tons, the number of chillers running, and approach for each machine, and compare days with different staging. A rising approach on one machine, for example, points to a heat transfer problem rather than a staging problem. See Reading a Chiller Log: What to Record and How to Trend Approach. Also watch for short cycling between stages, which shows up as repeated start counts in the trend.

Field card: Chiller Plant Staging and Sequencing

Count the whole plant's kW/ton, not one machine's.

  • Staging decides how many chillers run; signals include flow, supply temperature with calculated load, part-load ratio, and lift.
  • Time delays and lockouts prevent cycling; the 15 and 20 minute values here are examples.
  • Two chillers at part load can use less power than one near full load, especially with variable-speed machines and low lift.
  • Add the second chiller's pump and tower power before calling it a win.
  • Lead-lag rotation spreads run hours across machines.
  • Trend plant kW/ton, tons, chillers on and approach.
  • Follow the site's written sequence of operations and the chiller maker's minimum delays.
