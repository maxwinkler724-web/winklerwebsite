NJ Trade Licenses: Who Needs Which, a Pathway Overview

Starting out, the hardest part of licensing is figuring out which door to knock on. In New Jersey the doors belong to different agencies. Here is a map, with the standing advice that the issuing board has the last word.

Check the issuing board

Check the issuing board. Laws, fees and rules change. This page was built from state web pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal advice. Confirm every requirement with the agency that issues the license before you rely on it.

1. Federal refrigerant certification (EPA Section 608)

EPA says technicians who maintain, service, repair or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerant must be certified. It lists four types: I for small appliances, II for high- and very-high-pressure equipment, III for low-pressure equipment, and Universal for all of them. The agency states that certification requires passing an approved test from an approved certifying organization and that credentials do not expire. Apprentices are exempt only while a certified technician closely and continually supervises them. This credential is federal and has nothing to do with the state boards below. See EPA 608 Basics.

2. State HVACR contractor license

The State Board of Examiners of HVACR Contractors licenses master contractors who plan, install, service or repair HVACR systems for pay. The Board's rule N.J.A.C. 13:32A-2.2 sets three experience routes: five years built on an approved apprenticeship or training program (trade-school credit counts as two of its four years) plus a year as a journeyperson, or a bachelor's degree plus one or three years of practical experience. You must be at least 21. The exam has two written portions, you need 70 or higher on each, and you wait six months before a retake. Continuing education applies too. The law also requires $500,000 general liability insurance and a $3,000 bond. It also says municipalities cannot require a separate local HVACR license. Details are in NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers.

3. Boiler and refrigeration plant operator licenses (Department of Labor)

A different agency, separate from the HVACR board, the Mechanical Inspection Bureau of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Compliance, handles Black Seal and Blue Seal operator licensing under N.J.S.A. 34:7. A state summary says an unlicensed person may not operate steam generators or heating plants above stated size limits, or a refrigerating plant over 24 tons that uses flammable or toxic refrigerants, with listed exemptions. The administrative code lists license classes by color of seal, including the black seal boiler operator and low pressure boiler operator licenses. For the black seal, the code describes helper or apprentice experience with a licensed operator, or a structured thirty-working-day training program approved by the bureau, as ways to qualify to sit the exam. These details are not a substitute for the bureau's current instructions. See NJ Black Seal Basics. Note that a contractor license and an operator license are not interchangeable.

4. Electrical

NJ's HVACR rules (N.J.A.C. 13:32A-1.3, amended in 2022) let a master HVACR contractor do electrical work up to 30 volts, so 24 volt thermostat and control wiring is in scope. Replacement hookups from an existing service disconnect are a separate case in the rule. Electrical contractors are the ones with an exemption for work under 10 volts, which is a different line from the HVACR one. New circuits and work above 30 volts generally belong to a licensed electrical contractor. Whether someone who is not a master may do the same control wiring is not settled by the sources we read, so confirm with the Board for your situation.

5. Plumbing

The State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers handles plumbing licenses. Its FAQ describes master plumber and plumbing contractor licenses and a bona fide representative, and says plumbing work is limited to licensed people and W-2 employees of licensed contractors. The page does not say how plumbing scope meets HVAC scope, and the HVACR law carves out certain master plumber work, so where a job crosses water piping, check both boards.

6. Home improvement contractor registration

This is a registration, not a trade license. The Division of Consumer Affairs FAQ says a business that sells or performs home improvements on residential property in New Jersey must register annually, including HVAC work and including subcontractors, unless an exemption applies. Licensed professionals working within their scope are listed among the exemptions, so the answer for a licensed HVACR contractor depends on the facts. The FAQ lists insurance, additional security, a written contract for jobs above a stated amount, and displaying the registration number. Confirm with the division.

A tidy way to think about it

  • Federal: are you handling refrigerant? Think 608.
  • State board: are you contracting HVACR work for pay? Think HVACR contractor license.
  • Department of Labor: are you in charge of a regulated plant? Think operator license.
  • Other boards: electrical beyond equipment, and plumbing, have their own licenses.
  • Division of Consumer Affairs: are you doing residential improvements? Think registration.

Employees often work under an employer's license, so ask what your own role requires before you assume. Employer, school and apprenticeship sponsors can tell you which credential matches your job.

Field card: NJ Trade Licenses: Who Needs Which, a Pathway Overview

One trade, many doors. Ask the issuing board.

  • Federal 608 covers refrigerant handling, and it does not expire.
  • State HVACR board licenses master contractors; separate from operator licenses.
  • Department of Labor bureau licenses boiler and refrigeration plant operators (Black Seal and others).
  • Electrical work above 30 V and plumbing have their own boards and scope limits.
  • Home improvement registration is separate and has exemptions. Always check the issuing board.
