Split-Level and Raised-Ranch Comfort: Cold Lower Level, Hot Upper Bedrooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Split-levels and raised ranches share one habit: the lower half-level feels cool in winter while the upper bedrooms bake in summer, even with a single thermostat and a system that "works." The causes are mostly geometry, ground contact and air paths, not a bad furnace.

Why the levels disagree

In our experience, three things stack up. First, warm air rises, so the upper level collects heat from the lower level, from the stairwell and from the roof above it. Second, the lower level touches the ground. Third, the single thermostat usually sits on one level, so the system shuts off when that spot is satisfied, whatever the other level is doing. In summer the roof and sun-facing walls load the upper bedrooms while the lower level, partly buried, stays cooler and can even feel clammy. In winter the order flips: heat leaks out of the upper level too, but the lower level has slab and wall surfaces that feel cold to the skin even when the air is the same temperature.

Supply and return placement on each level

A federal home-improvement guide says registers should give adequate throw, drop and spread, and that ducts should be laid out compactly with straight runs and few sharp turns. It also notes that central returns are often undersized in existing homes, and that rooms with closed doors may need their own return, a transfer grille or a jump duct. It cautions that door undercuts may not provide enough airflow. In a split-level this matters because bedrooms are often on the upper level behind closed doors, with the only return down a half-flight of stairs. The result is a pressurized bedroom that pushes air out under the door slowly, so supply drops off.

  • Upper level in summer: cool air should reach the ceiling-height zone and mix. Supply high or at the floor is fine if the return is not so close that the air short-circuits.
  • Lower level in winter: supply near the cold outside wall or window helps wash the cold surface. Floor registers on a slab are common and workable.
  • Returns: give each level a path back. A single return on one level starves the other. See blocked returns and uneven rooms.

Stairwell air movement

The open stair is a chimney. In winter, warm air leaves the lower level up the stairs and is replaced by cooler air, so the lower level never catches up. In summer the cool, heavy air tends to stay low, which is why the upper bedrooms feel hot while the lower level overcools. A return high on the upper level helps pull hot air off the ceiling in summer, and a return low on the lower level helps pull cold air in winter. This is our own explanation of the physics, not a measured figure for any one house.

Balancing dampers and fan settings

Most branch ducts have a hand damper. A common field approach is to partly close dampers on the over-served level and open those on the under-served level, one small change at a time, and recheck rooms after a day. The same federal guide advises against closing vents to cut heat in unused rooms because that can reduce airflow through the air handler and cause pressure imbalances, so do the trimming at the dampers, not by shutting registers tight. Fan speed is a second lever. Cooling usually wants more airflow than heating, which is why many blowers have separate cooling and heating speeds. A constant-fan setting can also help move air between levels when a single thermostat is the limit. For the pressure side, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

When zoning or a ductless head makes sense

The federal guide says that with a central forced-air system, zoning uses damper controls installed in the ducts, that the dampers affect efficiency and balance, and that for large homes one system per floor is more commonly and more effectively used. It also says ductless heat pumps provide zoned heating and cooling, can serve up to eight indoor units from one outdoor unit, and avoid the losses of leaky ducts in unheated spaces. For a split-level, this suggests two reasonable moves when balancing is not enough: dampers with a zone panel (see Forced-Air Zoning Explained), or a ductless head serving the worst level, often the upper bedrooms in summer or a finished lower level that never warms up. Which fits depends on duct layout and the load on each level, so have the loads checked first.

Slab and below-grade walls on the lower level

A federal foundation-insulation article says foundation heat loss can be significant in existing buildings, that below-grade temperature differences are smaller but the wall area can be large, and that open block cores can let air circulate and lower the inside wall surface temperature. A state university extension note gives code minimum insulation values for basement walls and slab edges and says outside insulation keeps the concrete warmer, while the rim joist is an often-overlooked spot for air leaks. For comfort, an uninsulated slab or wall is a cold surface that makes the lower level feel cooler than the thermostat says. Insulating the walls, sealing the rim joist and putting a rug or carpet pad on a bare slab can help, while the duct fixes above improve supply and return paths.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measured house. Assumptions: design heating loss is 9,600 Btu/h for the lower level and 14,400 Btu/h for the upper level. Design sensible cooling gain is 5,000 Btu/h lower and 13,000 Btu/h upper. We assume 400 cfm per ton of cooling, and we match furnace output to the heating load for simplicity. The factor 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb·F, which is a standard sensible-heat constant for standard air.

  1. Heating totals: 9,600 + 14,400 = 24,000 Btu/h. Shares: lower 9,600 ÷ 24,000 = 0.40 (40%), upper 14,400 ÷ 24,000 = 0.60 (60%).
  2. Cooling totals: 5,000 + 13,000 = 18,000 Btu/h = 1.5 tons. Shares: lower 5,000 ÷ 18,000 = 0.278 (27.8%), upper 13,000 ÷ 18,000 = 0.722 (72.2%).
  3. Blower airflow: 1.5 tons × 400 = 600 cfm.
  4. Cooling split: lower 0.278 × 600 = 167 cfm, upper 0.722 × 600 = 433 cfm. Check: 167 + 433 = 600.
  5. Heating split at the same 600 cfm: lower 0.40 × 600 = 240 cfm, upper 0.60 × 600 = 360 cfm. Check: 240 + 360 = 600.
  6. Temperature-rise check: lower 9,600 ÷ (1.08 × 240) = 37.0 F, upper 14,400 ÷ (1.08 × 360) = 37.0 F. Equal, so the shares are consistent. For cooling, 5,000 ÷ (1.08 × 167) = 27.7 F and 13,000 ÷ (1.08 × 433) = 27.8 F.

What it shows: the lower level needs about 240 cfm in winter but only about 167 cfm in summer. One damper setting cannot be right for both seasons, which is why seasonal damper trims, zoning or a separate ductless head for one level can pay off in houses like this. If one damper setting must serve both seasons, a compromise near 200 cfm on the lower level leaves it a little short in winter and a little over in summer, which matches the symptoms in the lede.

Field card: Split-Level and Raised-Ranch Comfort: Cold Lower Level, Hot Upper Bedrooms

Fix the air paths first, then the surfaces, then consider zoning.

  • Give every level a supply and a return path; check closed bedroom doors.
  • Trim balancing dampers in small steps; do not shut registers tight.
  • Expect different airflow shares in winter and summer.
  • Cold slabs and walls make the lower level feel cooler than the thermostat reads.
  • If balancing fails, compare zone dampers with a ductless head for one level.
  • Our own example: lower level 40% of heat, 27.8% of cooling airflow.
