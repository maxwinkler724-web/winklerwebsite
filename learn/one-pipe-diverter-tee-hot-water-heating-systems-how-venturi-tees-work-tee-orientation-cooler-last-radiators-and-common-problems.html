One-Pipe Diverter Tee Hot-Water Heating: How Venturi Tees Work, Tee Orientation, Cooler Last Radiators and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-28

Many older homes have one pipe that leaves the boiler, passes every radiator or baseboard, and comes back to the boiler. The radiators do not sit in that pipe. They hang off it, and a special fitting called a diverter tee (also called a venturi tee) pushes part of the water through each one. This lesson explains how that works, why the last radiators run a little cooler, and what to check when one stays cold.

Check the maker's instructions

Read the tee's own instructions. Tee marking and spacing rules differ by maker and by model. This page is general education built from the sources listed below. Follow the instructions that came with the fitting or the system, and use the checks here as a way to organize your thinking, not as a replacement.

One pipe, many branches

A university trades-training text describes two one-pipe layouts. In a plain series loop, the radiators are part of the main, so every unit gets whatever water the one before it left. In a diverter tee system, tees on the main send part of the water into parallel branch circuits, and a branch can be shut off without disturbing the rest of the loop. That text notes that the tee works on a venturi principle: where velocity is greatest, pressure is least. A trade-press article on these fittings describes the same thing as a push/pull pair: at the supply riser the tee pushes water into the branch, and at the return riser it creates a lower-pressure spot that pulls water out of the branch and back into the main.

Put simply, the tee is a small, deliberate restriction in the main. The restriction makes the pressure just before and just after the branch slightly different, and water takes the easier path through the radiator when the pressure on its two ends differs. No separate pump is needed for each radiator. For more on how one loop is divided among emitters, see Series vs Parallel Piping and Zoning.

How many tees, and which way they face

  • Radiator above the main. The trade sources we read give a classic rule of one tee, on the return riser. The same university text says an upfed unit needs one tee on the return connection.
  • Radiator below the main. Both the university text and a maker's installation guide for hydronic systems say to use two tees, one on the supply and one on the return, when the radiators sit below the main loop. A trade article explains why: hot water in a downward riser and a cold radiator can set up a buoyancy effect that fights the flow.
  • Spacing. A maker's installation guide says to keep the tees to each radiator at least 12 inches apart on the main. A trade article recommends at least 1 foot of straight pipe between the tees so turbulence can settle, and says greater spacing increases the pressure difference.
  • The marked ring. The trade article says its tee has a red ring or groove that must face the piping between the two tees. A valve maker's installation sheet for venturi and diverter tees shows supply and return connections and a marking on the body, and says only that the number of tees depends on the project. Different models mark things differently, so find the mark on your fitting and match it to the maker's drawing.

Why the last radiators run cooler

Every radiator takes heat out of the water that passes it. Because the branch rejoins the main, the cooler water coming back from each radiator mixes into the main, and the main arrives at the next radiator a bit cooler than the last. The university text says that, in a series loop, units farther from the boiler receive cooler water and must be larger to make up the difference. A maker's guide gives the same advice for tee systems: oversize the last radiators, especially when you design for a larger overall temperature drop.

Heat output also depends on water temperature. See Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs Water Temperature.

Worked example (ours)

This example is our own. The numbers are assumptions chosen to be easy to follow, not measurements from a real house.

  • Main flow: 4 gpm (assumed). Boiler supply: 180 F (assumed).
  • Six radiators, each giving off 4,000 Btu/h (assumed, all the same).

We use the common shortcut for water: Btu/h = 500 × gpm × dT. A trade-press article cautions that the 500 factor changes with fluid and temperature, so treat the answers as estimates. Rearranged, dT = Btu/h ÷ (500 × gpm).

  1. Drop in the main per radiator: 4,000 ÷ (500 × 4) = 4,000 ÷ 2,000 = 2 F.
  2. Water at the tee of radiator 1: 180 F. Radiator 2: 178 F. Radiator 3: 176 F. Radiator 4: 174 F. Radiator 5: 172 F. Radiator 6: 170 F.
  3. The last radiator sees water 180 − 170 = 10 F cooler than the first.
  4. Whole loop: 6 × 4,000 = 24,000 Btu/h, so the total drop is 24,000 ÷ 2,000 = 12 F, and the water returns to the boiler at about 168 F.

The same cooler water means the last radiator gives off somewhat less than the first, so in a real house the last units are made larger. This example ignores heat lost from the main pipe itself, which would add to the drop. A higher flow shrinks each step: at 8 gpm the same radiators drop the main only 1 F each.

Common problems

  • Tee installed backward. If the tee does not face the way the maker shows, it cannot create the pressure difference the branch depends on. Our reasoning from how the tee works is that the radiator will then get little or no flow, and the symptom looks like trapped air. Check the marking before you go bleeding.
  • Air in a radiator. Air collects at the top of the radiator and blocks the branch. A trade article suggests raising the fill pressure for a short time to push air into solution, and pumping away from the compression tank so the circulator pressure adds to the fill pressure. It also warns that sagging piping can cause air problems and that the pipe should pitch up in the direction of flow. See Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water Heating System and Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents.
  • Radiator added without a tee. A branch plumbed straight off the main with no tee has no pressure difference to drive it. Our reasoning is that it will run cold or lukewarm. Add the fitting the maker calls for.
  • Radiator removed. A trade article says not to cap the two pipes that fed it, because all the water then has to pass through the tee branch, raising resistance and cutting flow. Remove the tees as well, or join the branches with a short piece of tube.
  • Long baseboard on one branch. The same article says 50 feet of copper baseboard off two close tees adds so much resistance that water prefers the main. It suggests running long baseboard as its own zone.
  • Oversized replacement circulator. A maker's guide sizes the circulator from the total flow and the system pressure drop. Our reasoning: a bigger pump does reduce the temperature drop along the main, as the formula shows, but choosing one by guesswork instead of the heat load and drop is how mismatches start. Size it, do not guess.
  • Thermostatic heads. The maker's guide says thermostatic heads on each radiator give individual control. See Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) on Steam and Hot-Water Radiators.

Checks that cost little

  1. With the system running, feel or measure the surface of the main (use a surface thermometer on bare pipe) just upstream of each tee. It should fall steadily along the loop. A big sudden drop points to a branch taking too much or a section of main with a problem.
  2. Compare each radiator's inlet and outlet pipes. A large gap between them means a lot of heat is being removed, which is normal. Both pipes cold means the branch has no flow.
  3. If one radiator is cold at the top only, bleed it. If it is cold at the bottom too, suspect the tee or a blockage.
  4. Compare boiler supply and return. Our example gives a 12 F spread, so compare yours with the design from the original heat load.
  5. Look at the tees: count them, confirm the spacing and the marking.

Field card: One-Pipe Diverter Tee Systems

The tee makes the pressure difference. The main gets cooler as it goes.

  • Radiator above the main: one tee on the return riser. Below the main: two tees (supply and return).
  • Keep tees at least 12 inches apart on the main; match the tee marking to the maker's drawing.
  • Btu/h = 500 × gpm × dT. Our example: 4,000 ÷ 2,000 = 2 F per radiator.
  • Last radiator in the example sees water 10 F cooler than the first (170 F vs 180 F).
  • Cold radiator: check the tee first, then air, then circulation.
  • Never cap off the pipes of a removed radiator.
