Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field: Sling and Digital Psychrometers, Where to Read, and Common Errors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A charging chart is only as good as the wet-bulb number you feed it. This lesson is about taking that reading: the sling technique, what a digital probe is really doing, where to put the instrument, and the mistakes that quietly shift the answer. For the bigger moist-air picture, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

What the wet bulb is, and the one number to watch

A wet-bulb thermometer is an ordinary thermometer wearing a wet cloth wick. Air moving across the wick evaporates some water, the evaporation takes heat from the wick and bulb, and the reading drops. A University of Wisconsin weather explainer says the reading stabilizes after a few minutes and that stable value is the wet-bulb temperature. At 100 percent relative humidity the wet bulb equals the air temperature, and the drier the air, the bigger the gap.

That gap is the wet-bulb depression: dry bulb minus wet bulb. Use it as a sanity check. A depression near zero means near-saturated air; a large one means dry air. If your depression does not match how the room feels, suspect the measurement before the building.

Sling psychrometer technique

A sling psychrometer is a bare thermometer and a wicked thermometer on a swivel handle. The published procedures agree on the big points and differ on details, so follow your own instrument's instructions where they differ.

  1. Clean wick that covers the bulb. An Iowa DOT test procedure says the wick must be saturated and cover the wet-bulb reservoir, and the dry-bulb reservoir must be dry. A Colorado State University physics demonstration says to keep the wick clean. A 1970 laboratory study (Humphreys and Kronoveter, in Sources) found a wick covering only the bulb read about 1.3 F high from heat conducted down the stem, and said the wick should extend at least 0.8 inch above the bulb.
  2. Distilled water. The GLOBE atmosphere protocol says to confirm distilled water is in the reservoir and, if the wick is dry, add distilled water. The Colorado State page says room-temperature water. (Our own reasoning: distilled water avoids mineral crust on the cloth. The sources do not state why.)
  3. Move air over the wick. Iowa DOT says to spin the body two to three revolutions per second and expect the readings to settle in 1 to 2 minutes. Colorado State says whirl 15 to 20 seconds at a time. GLOBE slings for 3 minutes for its weather observations.
  4. Read the wet bulb first, immediately. Iowa DOT says to stop and read the wet bulb right away, then the dry bulb. (Our own note: the wet bulb starts to warm as soon as the air stops moving.)
  5. Repeat until it stops falling. Colorado State says to repeat the whirling until two wet-bulb readings agree at the lowest value reached. Record that lowest, steady number.

Airflow matters. The 1970 study lists published optimum velocities over a wet bulb of roughly 500 to 1,000 feet per minute and estimated about 1,050 feet per minute over a sling's wet bulb. A 2022 weather-instrument paper by Burt, citing earlier work, reports that the wet-bulb response coefficient rises steeply when airflow drops below about 1 meter per second (roughly 200 feet per minute). Slow air gives a different, higher wet-bulb reading, which is why a lazy swing is a measurement error and not just a slow one.

Digital psychrometers and their computed wet bulb

A digital psychrometer has no wick. It measures air temperature and relative humidity with a sensor and then shows a wet bulb that it calculates. The National Weather Service publishes the same kind of calculation: its weather calculator derives wet bulb from temperature, relative humidity and station pressure. So the displayed wet bulb is only as good as the humidity sensor behind it. Our humidity meter lesson covers published accuracy figures (one maker lists plus or minus 2.5 percent RH) and how to check a probe against salt.

What does that tolerance mean for wet bulb? Our own calculation, using the NWS formulas below at an assumed 78 F and 49.3 percent RH, shows RH moving 2.5 points up or down shifts the computed wet bulb by about 0.7 F (64.3 to 65.7 F). Two practical habits follow. Let the probe settle in the airstream before you read it, and keep a sling or a second trusted instrument around to cross-check a number that looks off.

Where to measure for a charging chart

A trade-magazine article on superheat charging curves places the dry-bulb reading at the return air at the air handler and the outdoor reading at the condenser air-in. For wet bulb it suggests a probe in a hole drilled in the duct, or a sling psychrometer. A trade training page likewise says to take indoor wet bulb in the return. The California Reference Appendix target-superheat table (RA3.2, Table RA3.2-2) is keyed the same way: return air wet bulb across the top, condenser air dry bulb down the side.

  • Indoor: air entering the indoor coil, in the return, upstream of the coil. Supply air or air at the grille tells you nothing about what the coil is seeing.
  • Outdoor: air entering the condenser, not discharge air. (Our own note: shade the probe from direct sun; see radiant heat below.)
  • Timing: the article says to let the system run at least 15 minutes after any charge change before rechecking, and it points you to the exact maker's charging table for the unit.

Worked example: RH and dew point from a wet-bulb reading

Assumptions (ours, for illustration): return air dry bulb 78 F, wet bulb 65 F, station pressure 1013.25 hPa (sea level). The National Weather Service formulas, with temperatures in C and pressure in hPa:

  • es = 6.112 × e^(17.67 × T / (T + 243.5)); ew is the same with the wet-bulb temperature
  • e = ew − P × 0.00066 × (1 + 0.00115 × Tw) × (T − Tw)
  • RH = 100 × e / es; dew point Td = 243.5 × ln(e / 6.112) / (17.67 − ln(e / 6.112))

Step by step: 78 F = 25.56 C and 65 F = 18.33 C, so the depression is 13 F. es at 25.56 C = 32.74 hPa. ew at 18.33 C = 21.06 hPa. The correction term is 1013.25 × 0.00066 × (1 + 0.00115 × 18.33) × 7.22 = 4.93 hPa, so e = 21.06 − 4.93 = 16.13 hPa. RH = 100 × 16.13 / 32.74 = 49.3 percent. For dew point, ln(16.13 / 6.112) = 0.970, so Td = 243.5 × 0.970 / (17.67 − 0.970) = 14.15 C = 57.5 F.

Cross-check against a printed table: the Iowa DOT procedure lists 47 percent RH for 86 F dry bulb and 71 F wet bulb. The same formulas give 47.5 percent (our own calculation), close enough to trust the arithmetic. Now see what a wet-bulb slip does: at 78 F dry bulb, a wet bulb of 63 F gives 42.6 percent RH and 67 F gives 56.2 percent (our own calculation). A 2 F wet-bulb error is roughly a 6 to 7 point swing in RH.

Common errors

  • Dry or dirty wick. The GLOBE protocol checks that the wick is wet. A wick with no water stops evaporating, so the wet bulb drifts up toward the dry bulb (our own reasoning). A crusted wick evaporates unevenly.
  • Short wick. About 1.3 F high in the 1970 study if the wick covers only the bulb.
  • Too little air. See the Burt paper above. Whirl hard enough and long enough.
  • Body heat. Hold the handle, keep the bulbs out of your breath and away from your torso, and read at arm's length. (Our own practical advice.)
  • Wrong airstream. Return air for the indoor value, condenser inlet for the outdoor value.
  • Radiant heat. In the 1970 laboratory tests with radiant panels, sling readings ran higher than an aspirated reference psychrometer in every set, with a dry bulb up to 8.5 F high and a wet bulb up to 5.1 F high in the worst heat-stress cases. A rooftop condenser in direct sun is milder than those test panels but the direction is the same, so shade the instrument.
  • Reading too early or too late. Wet bulb creeps up once the air stops. Read quickly and use the lowest steady value.

How a 2 F wet-bulb error moves target superheat

This uses a published chart, not your unit's. In the California Reference Appendix Table RA3.2-2, with condenser air at 95 F, a return wet bulb of 67 F gives a target superheat of 12.0 F, and 69 F gives 15.8 F. A 2 F wet-bulb error therefore moves the target by 3.8 F. At 82 F condenser air, 63 F wet bulb gives 12.2 F and 65 F gives 15.4 F, a 3.2 F move. The article above says to adjust charge only when superheat is more than 5 F off the chart, so a wet-bulb error of this size can use up most of that window. Always use the maker's chart for the unit in front of you.

Field card: Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field

Good wet bulb: clean wick, distilled water, fast air, lowest steady reading.

  • Wet bulb is cooled by evaporation; depression is dry bulb minus wet bulb.
  • Sling: wet the wick, whirl, read wet bulb first, repeat until it stops falling.
  • Digital probes calculate wet bulb from temperature and RH; check the probe.
  • Indoor reading: return air upstream of the coil. Outdoor reading: condenser inlet, shaded.
  • Watch for a dry wick, low airflow, body heat, the wrong airstream and radiant heat.
  • A 2 F wet-bulb error moved one published target by 3 to 4 F.
