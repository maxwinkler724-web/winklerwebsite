Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops: Freeze Protection and Testing

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Glycol keeps a water loop from freezing, but it also makes the loop move less heat and takes real upkeep. This lesson covers which glycol goes where, what the percentages mean, how to test a loop with a refractometer, and how to work out a top-up.

Why a loop gets glycol

An air-cooled chiller or a coil in an outdoor air stream can sit in freezing weather with water inside. Ice can split tubes and burst heat exchangers. Adding glycol lowers the temperature at which the water freezes. If you only want to understand the chiller itself, start with Chiller Types: Air-Cooled vs. Water-Cooled. If you are comparing it to draining a system or running pumps in cold weather, one Daikin Applied unit ventilator data sheet lists those options next to antifreeze: drain the water, keep the pump running with the coil valves open when outdoor air is below 35 °F, or use antifreeze.

Ethylene vs. propylene

Both are used in HVAC loops. Dow's DOWFROST engineering guide says both have low acute oral toxicity ratings and can be used where incidental contact with drinking water is possible, but it also says propylene glycol is preferred where toxicity matters, such as food processing or contact with drinking water, and it rates ethylene glycol as moderately toxic if swallowed. The trade-off is performance: the Dow guide says ethylene glycol is less viscous and generally gives better heat transfer and low-temperature performance. Read the safety data sheet for the exact product in the loop.

The two types also freeze at different temperatures at the same percentage. A glycol refractometer sheet from Nu-Calgon lists 30 percent propylene at 7 °F and 30 percent ethylene at 2 °F. So you must know which glycol is in the loop before reading a number off a scale.

Freeze protection vs. burst protection

These are not the same thing, and Dow draws the line clearly. For freeze protection, the fluid must stay liquid and pumpable, so Dow's guidance is a freezing point at least 5 °F below the lowest expected temperature. For burst protection, some ice crystals are allowed to form, but the fluid turns to a slush that is still pumpable and the system survives as long as there is room for the expansion. Dow's table for propylene glycol shows the difference: at an expected low of 0 °F, burst protection needs about 25 percent glycol by volume, while freeze protection needs about 38 percent. At −20 °F the figures are 31 and 48 percent. Dow notes that a 35 percent propylene glycol solution is usually adequate for burst protection.

That matters for loops with an expansion tank that was sized for water. Burst protection depends on room for expansion, so ask the designer which kind of protection the loop was built to give before you change a concentration.

What glycol does to capacity and pump flow

Glycol solutions are thicker than water and carry less heat per gallon, so the loop needs more pumping and delivers less cooling from the same coil. Dow says glycol solutions have lower film coefficients at equivalent flow, that efficiency falls as concentration rises, and that concentrations above roughly 50 to 55 percent are rarely needed. FlowCon, a balancing valve maker, says glycol changes the viscosity and the flow and that higher concentration raises pumping power. Its worked example at 30 percent glycol and 0 °C uses a correction factor of 1.045 on the flow setting.

A chiller or coil maker will publish the actual derate. The Daikin Applied unit ventilator data sheet gives capacity factors for chilled-water coils, and they are not small. At 30 percent, ethylene glycol is 0.84 and propylene glycol is 0.73. The same sheet gives pressure drop factors of 1.22 for 30 percent ethylene and 1.27 for 30 percent propylene. Those numbers are for that coil family only. For the chiller in front of you, use the chiller maker's glycol correction tables. For the flow side of the math, see Hot-Water Flow Rate Math, which shows how the flow constant changes with glycol, and Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T.

Never use automotive antifreeze

Dow's guide warns that automotive antifreeze contains silicates, which can form a thick gel on heat transfer surfaces and cut heat transfer, and that systems that have had it may need cleaning. An ACHR News article on small hydronic systems says plainly not to put automotive antifreeze in a hydronic system and to use a product made for hydronic systems with the right amount of inhibitors. Use only the fluid the equipment maker or designer specified.

Inhibitors, pH and testing

Glycol itself is not the whole product. Fluid makers add corrosion inhibitors, and a loop at too low a concentration can lose that protection. Dow's guide says inhibitor levels below 25 to 30 percent glycol may not give adequate corrosion protection, that pH between 8 and 10 is important, and that a fluid with a pH below 7.0 should be replaced. For its own fluids it lists a minimum reserve alkalinity from a titration test. Narrow-range pH paper is a simple check, and a pH tester is accurate to about 0.5. Follow the test method and limits printed by the maker of the fluid in your loop, not a general number.

Concentration is checked with a refractometer, which measures how much a drop of fluid bends light. Dow's guide says it needs only a few drops, and that diluted solutions from 0 to 60 percent glycol can be tested directly. The Nu-Calgon Glycometer instructions say to put a drop on the prism, let sample and prism reach the same temperature for 20 to 30 seconds, look through the eyepiece toward a light and read where the blue shade ends. They also say to check calibration on distilled water at the 32 °F line and that the operator must know which type of glycol is being tested, because propylene and ethylene have separate scales.

Makeup water, the feeder and backflow

If a loop loses fluid and someone tops it off with straight water, the concentration falls. A glycol feeder helps with this. A trade article from PHCP Pros describes a feeder as a self-contained unit with a pump, pressure control and a storage tank that replaces the fill valve, and says its tank should be filled with the correct water-to-glycol ratio so makeup does not dilute the loop. Dow says to mix with distilled or deionized water, limiting chlorides and sulfates to 25 ppm each and total hardness to 100 ppm as calcium carbonate. Ordinary tap water can be harder than that.

Fill connections to drinking water are also a code issue. As one example, the California Mechanical Code section on automatic makeup fluid says potable connections must follow its backflow rules, requires a pressure-reducing valve on a water-only makeup line, and for systems with additives such as glycol or inhibitors requires a pressurization unit or glycol feeder. Codes differ by state and town, so check the one that applies where you work.

Worked example: top-up from 25 to 35 percent

A loop holds 500 gal at 25 percent propylene glycol by volume. The designer wants 35 percent. You will drain some of the loop fluid and replace it with undiluted glycol concentrate (treated as 100 percent for this example; use the real strength printed on the product).

  1. Let x be the gallons to drain and replace. The glycol in the loop after the swap is the glycol left behind plus the glycol added: (500 − x) × 0.25 + x × 1.00.
  2. That should equal the target: 500 × 0.35 = 175 gal of glycol.
  3. Expand: 125 − 0.25x + x = 175, so 0.75x = 50.
  4. Solve: x = 50 ÷ 0.75 = about 66.7 gal.
  5. Check: (500 − 66.7) × 0.25 = 108.3 gal left, plus 66.7 new, which is 175 gal, or 35 percent of 500.

Freeze point from the Nu-Calgon propylene scale: 25 percent is halfway between 18 °F at 20 percent and 7 °F at 30 percent, so about 12 °F. At 35 percent it is halfway between 7 and −8, so about 0 °F. Dow's own table agrees on the trend: 9.2 °F at 30 percent and −6.0 °F at 40 percent. Treat the halfway numbers as estimates and read your fluid maker's table. Dispose of drained fluid following the maker's directions and local rules. After mixing, run the pump, then retest with the refractometer.

Where this fits in a start-up

Concentration, inhibitor condition and fill pressure belong on the pre-start list. See Chiller Start-Up and Shutdown: Sequence and Safeties for the order of events and why freeze safeties exist. Also see Air Elimination and Dirt Separation, because air and debris hurt a glycol loop just as they hurt a water loop.

Field card: Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops

Know the type, test the percent, keep the inhibitor, never use car antifreeze.

  • Propylene is preferred where toxicity matters; ethylene transfers heat a bit better but is more toxic.
  • Burst protection allows slush; freeze protection keeps it liquid. Freeze needs more glycol.
  • Glycol cuts capacity and raises pressure drop. Use the maker's correction table.
  • Refractometer: know the glycol type, calibrate on distilled water, let temperatures settle.
  • Top-up algebra: (V − x)(c1) + x(1.00) = V(c2); here 500 gal, 25% to 35% = about 66.7 gal.
  • Automotive antifreeze has silicates that can gel on surfaces. Do not use it.
  • Mix with distilled or deionized water and check local backflow rules.
