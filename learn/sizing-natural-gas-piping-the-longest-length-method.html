Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace that is clocked at the meter and found short of gas is not always a bad valve or a dirty orifice. Sometimes the pipe feeding it is simply too small for everything it has to serve. This lesson shows how gas pipe is sized: add up the load in cubic feet per hour, measure the longest run, and read a table.

Why pipe is sized by load and length

Gas moving through pipe loses pressure to friction. The longer the pipe, and the more gas pushed through it, the bigger the loss. Appliances need a minimum supply pressure to burn at their rated input. The Goodman furnace manual quoted in Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input gives 4.5 to 10.0 inches of water column at the furnace for natural gas. So the designer picks a pipe diameter that keeps the loss across the whole system small enough that every appliance still gets enough pressure when everything is running.

That gives two inputs for every sizing job: the load, in cubic feet per hour (cfh), and the length, in feet. The code text we read says the connected load comes from the appliance makers' input ratings, and that the total connected hourly load is the basis for sizing. It assumes all appliances could run at full rate at the same time unless a load diversity can be shown (International Fuel Gas Code, 2006 Chapter 4, Section 402.2).

Step 1: Convert Btu/h to cfh

Nameplates give input in Btu per hour. The tables are in cfh. To convert, divide the input by the heating value of the gas in Btu per cubic foot. An ICC teaching handout on the 2015 code gives the same rule and uses about 1,000 Btu per cubic foot as a round number. Real gas is not exactly 1,000. The U.S. Energy Information Administration says the heat content of natural gas may vary by location and over time, and tells consumers to ask their gas utility. Its 2025 U.S. average for gas delivered to end users was about 1,037 Btu per cubic foot. The worked example below uses 1,037. For a real job, ask the utility for the local number.

A good habit is to round each appliance up to the next whole cfh. That keeps the arithmetic simple and errs on the safe side.

Step 2: Find the longest length

The longest length method is in Section 402.4.1 of the International Fuel Gas Code (IFGC). Put simply, it says to size every section of pipe using the longest run from the point of delivery (usually the meter outlet) to the most remote appliance outlet, together with the load that section carries. The nearby appliances do not get a shorter row of the table. They use the same long length. That is conservative, and it is what makes the method easy: measure once, then size each section from one column of numbers.

A table row has to cover the length you actually have, so if your longest run is 65 feet and the table has 60 and 70, use 70. The ICC handout also suggests adding a fitting allowance of 50 percent of the pipe length. That suggestion was in the handout, not in the code section we read, so ask your local inspector how fittings are counted where you work. The example below uses the measured length so the table lookup stays clear.

Step 3: Read the table

The table used most for low-pressure residential and light commercial work is Table 402.4(2) in the IFGC. Its title is Schedule 40 Metallic Pipe, natural gas, with an inlet pressure of less than 2 psi, a pressure drop of 0.5 inches of water column and a specific gravity of 0.60. Rows are lengths in feet. Columns are pipe sizes. The cell shows the most gas, in cfh, that the pipe can carry over that length without exceeding the pressure drop.

These few values are from Table 402.4(2) as printed in the 2006 IFGC Chapter 4 on ICC's New Jersey code page. The 2018 Seattle Fuel Gas Code reprint of the same table showed the same numbers for these rows.

Notice how fast capacity falls as length grows: a half-inch pipe carries 65 cfh at 60 feet but 60 cfh at 70 feet. The edition your local inspector enforces may differ from the ones we read, so always size from the table in the adopted code, or the table in the manufacturer's instructions for the piping system you are using. Section 402.3 allows either, or other approved engineering methods.

Worked example: furnace, water heater and range

A house has a gas meter and one branched system serving three appliances. Gas heating value is taken as 1,037 Btu per cubic foot.

  1. Convert loads. Furnace, 100,000 Btu/h: 100,000 / 1,037 = 96.4, round up to 97 cfh. Water heater, 40,000 Btu/h: 40,000 / 1,037 = 38.6, round up to 39 cfh. Range, 65,000 Btu/h: 65,000 / 1,037 = 62.7, round up to 63 cfh.
  2. Load carried by each section. Section A (meter to first tee) carries everything: 97 + 39 + 63 = 199 cfh. Section B (first tee to second tee) feeds the water heater and range: 39 + 63 = 102 cfh. The furnace branch carries 97, the water heater branch 39, the range branch 63.
  3. Longest run. The diagram lengths are: A 20 ft, B 15 ft, furnace branch 10 ft, water heater branch 10 ft, range branch 30 ft. The farthest outlet is the range: 20 + 15 + 30 = 65 ft. The furnace run is only 30 ft and the water heater run 45 ft, but under this method every section uses the longest length.
  4. Pick the row. 65 ft is between the 60 and 70 rows. Round up to the 70 ft row: 1/2 in. = 60, 3/4 in. = 126, 1 in. = 237, 1-1/4 in. = 486.
  5. Size each section from that one row. A, 199 cfh: 3/4 in. holds only 126, so use 1 in. (237). B, 102 cfh: 3/4 in. (126). Furnace, 97 cfh: 1/2 in. holds 60, so 3/4 in. Water heater, 39 cfh: 1/2 in. (60). Range, 63 cfh: 1/2 in. holds 60, just short, so 3/4 in.

Two lessons here. First, the range misses half-inch pipe by only 3 cfh, which is why rounding loads up matters. Second, if you had wrongly used the 60 ft row, half-inch pipe (65 cfh) would have looked big enough for the range. The real run is longer than 60 feet, so it would not have been.

Who may do the work, and test it

Sizing is arithmetic, but cutting, threading and connecting gas pipe is licensed work. In New Jersey, a 2019 state consumer affairs rule-proposal notice says installing, servicing, connecting, maintaining or repairing gas piping on the load side of a meter is HVACR work under N.J.S.A. 45:16A-2, which needs a master HVACR license or a license from another state entity whose scope covers it. The Cornell LII text of N.J.A.C. 13:32-1.4 separately lists gas piping among work a licensed master plumber may do without a master HVACR license. See NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers and NJ Trade Licenses: Who Needs Which. We did not find a New Jersey source on permit rules for gas piping, so check with your local construction office and check who may do gas piping where you work. An apprentice works under a licensed person, not alone.

New or altered piping must be tested before it is put in service. A municipal policy that summarizes the 2009 IFGC says the test pressure is at least 3 psig (and 1.5 times the working pressure), air, nitrogen or another inert gas is used, never oxygen, and any pressure drop is treated as a leak. It says to find leaks with an approved detector or noncorrosive leak fluid and never with an open flame. Test details differ by edition and jurisdiction, so follow the inspector's requirements. After any disturbed fitting, leak check it, as the clocking lesson also says. Smell gas or suspect CO? Read Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Once pipe is sized and tested, the furnace still has to do its job in order; see Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation.

Field card: Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method

Load in cfh, longest run in feet, one row of the table.

  • cfh = Btu/h divided by the heating value (about 1,037 Btu per cubic foot nationally; ask your utility).
  • Round each load up. Total the connected load; do not guess diversity.
  • Longest length: meter outlet to the farthest appliance. Round up to the next table row.
  • Size every section from that same row, using the load that section carries.
  • Table 402.4(2): Schedule 40, inlet under 2 psi, 0.5 in. w.c. drop. Use the adopted edition.
  • Gas piping is licensed work and must be tested. Check who may do it where you work.
