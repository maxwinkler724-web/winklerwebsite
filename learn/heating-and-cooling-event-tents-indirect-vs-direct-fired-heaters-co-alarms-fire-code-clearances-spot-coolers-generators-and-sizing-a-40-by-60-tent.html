Heating and Cooling Event Tents: Indirect vs Direct Heaters, CO, Fire Code and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tent is a thin fabric shell with a dirt or grass floor and a lot of leaks. Heating one for a wedding, a job-site shelter or a festival is mostly a question of where the flame is: outside the tent, with only warm air ducted in, or inside it, where every pound of exhaust becomes the guests' air. This lesson walks through the choices, the fire code rules, the carbon monoxide risks and a sizing example.

Check the local rules first

Check the authority having jurisdiction. Tent permits, inspections and amendments differ by city and state. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026, not a code opinion. The fire official decides what is allowed at your event.

Indirect-fired vs direct-fired

An indirect-fired heater burns fuel in a sealed combustion chamber and passes the room air over a heat exchanger. The combustion products leave through a flue to the outdoors; only warmed air goes into the space. A direct-fired heater puts the flame in the airstream, so the products of combustion go into the tent along with the heat. We cover the same split for building make-up air in Make-Up Air Units: Direct-Fired vs Indirect-Fired.

For tents the difference is bigger. Minnesota's state fire marshal tent sheet lists portable outdoor gas-fired appliances as prohibited and names "indirect heat, where a heater outside the tent blows warm air in, and nationally listed electric heat" as the preferred options. Direct-fired units can still be used in some job-site situations, but then the federal construction rule applies: fresh air must be supplied in sufficient quantity, with mechanical ventilation where natural supply falls short (OSHA 29 CFR 1926.154(a)). A tent full of people is not a place to lean on that rule. See Temporary Heat on Construction Sites for the ventilation side.

Carbon monoxide: the risk and the alarms

CDC lists generators, grills and other gasoline-, propane- or charcoal-burning devices as sources of carbon monoxide, an odorless, colorless gas, and says never to run them in an enclosed space. The NIOSH pocket guide gives a recommended exposure limit of 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling, and an immediately-dangerous level of 1,200 ppm. Symptoms it lists include headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion and fainting, which can look like a long party night. A closed-up tent with a direct-fired heater or a stray generator can pass those numbers.

CDC's generator sheet recommends battery-operated or battery back-up CO detectors. On a tent job we place a battery CO alarm or a portable CO meter inside the tent at occupant height, away from the duct outlet, and one near any sleeping area. That placement is our own practice, not a code citation; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do for how alarms behave. An alarm is a backup. It does not make direct-fired heat in an occupied tent acceptable.

What the fire code says

The model code for this is the International Fire Code, Chapter 31, covering tents, temporary special event structures and other membrane structures. Minnesota's State Fire Marshal publishes a plain-language sheet on it, citing the Minnesota State Fire Code, which carries the tent rules in its own Chapter 31. These are the distances it gives:

  • Open-flame heaters: at least 20 feet from a tent that is open to the public, and heating equipment at least 10 feet from exits and from combustible materials.
  • LP-gas containers of 500 gallons or less: 10 feet from the tent. Larger than 500 gallons: 25 feet.
  • Flammable or combustible liquid storage and tanks: at least 50 feet away. Refueling: at least 20 feet away.
  • Generators and similar combustion power sources: at least 20 feet from the tent.
  • Tents in place more than 180 days in a 12-month period fall under the building code instead.

A city handout from Bellevue, Washington (dated 2016, citing the International Fire Code) agrees on the pattern: generators not inside tents, refueling 20 feet away, fuel storage 50 feet away, LP-gas containers of 500 gallons or less 10 feet away, and tents 20 feet from buildings, lot lines and parked vehicles. Edition and local amendments vary, so use your own jurisdiction's adopted text. The OSHA rule adds that a heater must stay at least 10 feet from tarpaulins or canvas coverings.

Cooling a tent

Portable spot coolers and portable air conditioners work on the same rule: the hot side has to leave the tent. Duct the condenser exhaust outdoors and keep the intake and exhaust from short-circuiting. A single-hose unit pulls room air for the condenser and throws it out, so the tent draws replacement air in through every gap. For the machines themselves, see Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers. DOE says evaporative coolers do best in low-humidity climates, can cool the air 15 to 40 F, add moisture, and need an open vent or window area of 1 to 2 square feet per 1,000 cfm of capacity. In a humid place, they will not do the job.

Generators, sidewalls and the ground

Keep the generator the code distance away (20 feet in the Minnesota sheet) and downwind, with its exhaust pointed away from tent openings and the heater's combustion air intake. CDC's own household advice is more than 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. Sidewalls matter because a tent loses heat through fabric with almost no insulation: close them, keep the bottoms weighted, and leave the heater's combustion air and flue clear. Floors lose heat to cold ground, so a floor cover or pallet deck helps comfort more than the thermostat does. Those are our field observations, not sourced numbers.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example, with assumptions. A 40 ft by 60 ft tent with 8 ft sidewalls. We assume a peak height of 14 ft, 2 air changes per hour, a 30 F rise (for example 40 F outdoors to 70 F inside), and a heater rated 150,000 Btu/h of output into the air. The 1.08 factor comes from air density 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb·F × 60 min/h, rounded.

  1. Box volume: 40 × 60 × 8 = 19,200 ft³.
  2. Roof triangle above the sidewalls: 0.5 × 40 × 6 × 60 = 7,200 ft³. Total volume = 26,400 ft³.
  3. Airflow at 2 air changes per hour: 26,400 × 2 ÷ 60 = 880 cfm.
  4. Air-change load: Btu/h = 1.08 × 880 × 30 = 28,512 Btu/h.
  5. Envelope loss, assuming a fabric U-value of 1.0 Btu/h·ft²·F: walls 2 × (40 + 60) × 8 = 1,600 ft²; gable ends 2 × 0.5 × 40 × 6 = 240 ft²; roof slopes 2 × 20.9 × 60 = about 2,506 ft²; total about 4,346 ft². Loss = 4,346 × 1.0 × 30 = about 130,380 Btu/h.
  6. Total = 28,512 + 130,380 = about 158,900 Btu/h. Divide by 150,000: 1.06, so we round up to 2 heaters.

If the tent is leaky and runs 4 air changes per hour, the air load doubles to 57,024 Btu/h and the total is about 187,400 Btu/h, which is still 2 heaters. Ground loss and wind are not included, so the answer is a starting point, not a design. With 2 heaters, split the load across two ducts for even heat, and put each outdoor unit and its fuel tank at the code distances above.

Set-up checks

  • Heater outdoors, level, on firm ground, at code distance from the tent, fuel tank and generator.
  • Flue terminates outdoors, away from tent openings, air intakes and people.
  • Duct sleeve through the wall is fire-resistant material and sealed; duct does not touch fabric.
  • Fuel containers secured and positioned as the code and the maker require.
  • CO alarm tested inside; fire extinguisher at hand.
  • Spot cooler exhaust ducted outside; condensate has somewhere to go.
  • Walk the permit and inspection with the fire official's checklist.
