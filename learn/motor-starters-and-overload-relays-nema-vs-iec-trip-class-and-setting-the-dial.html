Motor Starters and Overload Relays: NEMA vs IEC, Trip Class and Setting the Dial

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A motor starter is a contactor with an overload relay bolted to it. The contactor switches the motor on and off. The overload relay watches the motor current and opens the contactor if the motor is working too hard for too long. This lesson covers what the relay protects, how trip class and the dial setting work, and what to check before you press reset.

Starter, breaker and overload: three jobs

A magnetic starter pairs a contactor with an overload relay. Siemens' starter ratings material describes exactly that pairing and matches each contactor size to a specific overload relay. For the contactor itself, see Capacitors and Contactors.

The breaker or fuse and the overload relay protect different things. Siemens' overload relay manual says overload relays give inverse-time protection against overload, phase failure and (on its electronic models) unbalance, and that "fuses or motor starter protectors must be used for short-circuit protection." In plain terms, the breaker or fuse protects the circuit wiring and equipment from a short or a ground fault. The overload relay protects the motor from running too many amps for too long, which cooks the windings slowly. A motor can sit well under the breaker rating and still be overloaded, so the breaker will never save it. Motor branch-circuit and overload rules live in NEC Article 430, and your local adopted edition governs; we do not teach the code percentages here.

Thermal versus electronic overloads

Siemens describes two designs. In a bimetal (thermal) relay, motor current heats bimetal strips, they bend, and a mechanism trips the auxiliary contact; it needs no control power. Ambient-compensated bimetal strips keep a hot or cold panel from causing nuisance trips, and ABB says its compensating bimetal extends the rated ambient range. In an electronic relay, current transformers feed solid-state circuits and a small internal supply, and the relay can add features such as unbalance sensing and selectable trip class. Rockwell's electronic E1 Plus data sheet says to set up the device "by dialing the setting potentiometer to the motor FLA rating." Eaton's bimetal Freedom relay uses heater packs picked from a table, then a dial for the final match.

Trip class 10, 20 and 30

Trip class is a time rating, not a current rating. Siemens defines it by how long the relay takes to trip from a cold start at 7.2 times the setting. For its thermal 3RU11, Siemens lists class 10 as 4 to 10 seconds, class 20 as 6 to 20 seconds and class 30 as 9 to 30 seconds. ABB's table shows the same idea: a higher class number allows a longer trip time. ABB also lists maximum trip times at 1.5 times the setting when warm: 240 s for class 10, 480 s for class 20 and 720 s for class 30. A class 20 or 30 relay lets a hard-starting, high-inertia load get up to speed without a nuisance trip, but it also lets the motor carry the overload longer. The right class comes from the motor and load data and the starter maker's selection tables, so do not swap classes to stop a trip. Rockwell's E1 Plus lets you pick class 10, 15, 20 or 30 on some models, and fixes others at class 10.

NEMA versus IEC starters

NEMA starters are chosen by size, 00 through 9. Siemens' ratings material gives size 1 as 27 A continuous, 7 hp at 230 V and 10 hp at 460 V. IEC devices have no size numbers; they are rated by current and by utilization category, such as AC-3 for starting squirrel-cage motors and switching them off at speed. Each maker publishes which relay goes with which contactor. Whichever style you meet, the overload relay still has a dial or knob in amps, and the nameplate on the motor is what you set it from.

Setting the dial from the nameplate

Read the full load amps (FLA) off the motor nameplate, not the starter or the compressor label of a different machine. Makers agree on the basic rule. ABB says its setting current "is the motor nominal current and not the tripping current." Siemens says its 3RU11 is "set to the rated motor current" with a rotary knob. Eaton's technical guide says to rotate the FLA dial to the position for the motor FLA, with the relay calibrated for 1.0 and 1.15 service factor motors. For a 1.15 service factor motor Eaton's guide has you land the dial between its marked positions, and for a 1.0 service factor motor it has you turn the dial back one graduation. Eaton states the relay "will ultimately trip at 125% motor current." Always follow the instruction sheet for the exact relay in front of you; if a maker's table differs from this lesson, the table wins.

Worked example

Our own arithmetic. Nameplate: 14.0 A FLA, service factor 1.15.

  1. Dial setting, following Eaton's instruction to set the dial to the motor FLA: 14.0 A.
  2. Our own: the Eaton statement of ultimate trip at 125 percent gives 14.0 x 1.25 = 17.5 A.
  3. Our own: ABB's published points, no trip at 1.05 times setting and a trip within 2 hours at 1.2 times, give 14.0 x 1.05 = 14.7 A and 14.0 x 1.2 = 16.8 A. These are another maker's numbers; they show the idea that a dial at FLA does not trip the instant the motor goes over.
  4. Our own: the service factor amps are 14.0 x 1.15 = 16.1 A, the most the motor is designed to carry continuously on a good day.

Now the three measured leg currents: 13.2 A, 14.6 A and 13.8 A. Our own arithmetic:

  1. Sum: 13.2 + 14.6 + 13.8 = 41.6 A.
  2. Average: 41.6 / 3 = 13.87 A (13.867 before rounding).
  3. Deviations from average: |13.2 - 13.867| = 0.667; |14.6 - 13.867| = 0.733; |13.8 - 13.867| = 0.067.
  4. Maximum deviation: 0.733 A (the 14.6 A leg).
  5. Percent imbalance: 0.733 / 13.867 x 100 = 5.3 percent.

The average is under the 14.0 A nameplate and the highest leg is only 0.6 A over it, so the relay is not at risk of tripping, but 5.3 percent current imbalance is worth chasing. Current imbalance runs several times the voltage imbalance behind it, so measure the voltages next; Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance shows how, and Measuring Amp Draw covers clamp technique.

Single-phasing, reset and nuisance trips

If one phase drops out, the two remaining legs carry much more current. Eaton says single-phase protection is built into its relay, Siemens says phase-failure sensitivity limits the heating, ABB says tripping is faster on phase loss, and Rockwell lists about 3 seconds typical reaction for its enhanced phase-loss circuit. Siemens notes phase-failure protection does not work for star-connected loads with a grounded neutral.

Reset comes in manual and automatic. Eaton's relay can be set either way and shows a trip flag only in manual reset. Siemens' electronic relays wait 3 minutes before an automatic reset, and a manual reset can be done at once. Automatic reset means the motor can start again on its own after cooling, so treat a tripped machine as live until it is locked out.

Overload tripped: check before you reset

  1. De-energize, lock out and verify dead before opening the panel; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead. OSHA 1910.147 requires the authorized employee to verify isolation, and OSHA's interpretation says a starter or stop button is not an energy-isolating device.
  2. Find the cause first. Turn the shaft by hand and look for a seized bearing, a jammed fan or a dragging belt.
  3. Check supply voltage at the motor terminals and across all three phases for imbalance or a missing leg.
  4. Check connections, the contactor contacts and the wire size for heat damage.
  5. Reset, restart and read the amps on each leg against the nameplate FLA.

Repeated resets and dial bumping are the usual mistake. A relay that trips is giving you information.

Field card: Motor Starters and Overload Relays

Breaker protects the circuit. Overload protects the motor.

  • Starter = contactor + overload relay.
  • Set the dial from the motor nameplate FLA, then follow that relay's instruction sheet.
  • Trip class is time at 7.2 times setting: higher class, longer allowed.
  • Do not change class or crank the dial to stop a trip.
  • Check shaft, voltage on all three phases and leg amps before reset.
  • Lock out and verify dead first.
  • Imbalance % = max deviation from average / average x 100.
