Blood Donation and Plasma Centers: HVAC for Donor Comfort, Centrifuge and Freezer Heat, Storage Rooms and Backup Power

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A donation center is part clinic, part lab and part cold warehouse. Donors sit or lie still for a while, machines spin blood, and freezers full of plasma dump their heat into a small room. This lesson walks through what a facility tech should know about each area, what the federal rules actually say about temperatures and facilities, and one worked heat-load example.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page was built from eCFR, CDC and trade-press pages read in 2026 and is general education. The center's quality staff own product decisions, the license and the written procedures. We report readings; we do not decide whether product is usable.

What the rules say, and what they leave out

The FDA facility rule, 21 CFR 606.40 on eCFR, is short on numbers. It asks for clean, suitably sized facilities with a private donor exam area, a separate blood withdrawal area, apheresis space, and storage that keeps quarantined and finished product apart. On air it says only "adequate lighting, ventilation and screening of open windows and doors." The page we read gives no airflow rates and no room temperature. We found no ASHRAE 170 table row for a donor area in anything we could open, so we are not quoting one. Ask the facility's engineer which basis was used.

Product temperatures come from other sections. For shipping, 21 CFR 600.15 lists fresh frozen plasma at minus 18 C or colder, liquid red cells between 1 and 10 C, source plasma at minus 5 C or colder, and frozen red cells at minus 65 C or colder. Platelets ship between 1 and 10 C if the label says 1 to 6 C storage. For storage, 21 CFR 640.34 says plasma is kept at minus 18 C or colder after freezing, and liquid plasma is stored at 1 to 6 C. Under 21 CFR 640.25, platelets stored at 20 to 24 C need continuous gentle agitation, which means a platelet room is a room-temperature room with a moving shaker in it. Our own plain reading: those are product limits, not room limits, so a freezer room that gets hot is a risk because the freezers struggle, not because a rule names a room temperature.

21 CFR 606.160 requires records of storage temperature, including initialed temperature recorder charts, and records of equipment calibration and maintenance.

Donor area comfort

Donors lie still while blood is drawn or returned, so drafts and hot spots get noticed. We could not find a source in our reading that ties room temperature to fainting, so we are not claiming a number. What we did read: a 2025 hemovigilance study in Frontiers in Public Health, using Chinese national donor data from 2020 to 2022, found fainting was higher at outdoor collection sites than indoor sites, and the highest rate of vasovagal reactions in blood donation vehicles. The authors pointed to a confined space and higher personnel density, plus noise and ventilation, not to temperature. Our takeaway: keep the donor floor steady and draft-free, and do not aim supply air straight down on reclined donors.

Apheresis machines and the centrifuge lab

Apheresis machines and benchtop centrifuges turn electricity into heat, and some of it lands in the room. We have no maker numbers here, so take watts from each nameplate or manual and add them up as in the server room lesson linked below. Keep return air in the lab and the machines' own clearances open. A fault on the machine itself is a call for the maker's trained staff.

The plasma freezer room

This is where the HVAC load concentrates. A freezer is a heat pump: it moves heat out of the cabinet and rejects it, plus the compressor's own input, into the room. Over a day, all of the electrical energy the freezer draws ends up as heat in the room (our own reasoning, same as for the lab freezers in the vaccine lesson).

Three points of our own advice: put the room on its own cooling so a shared building system cannot be set back at night, give the freezers their maker's clearance on every side, and keep the room's AC alarm separate from the freezer alarms so you know which one went first. A warm room often shows up first as a freezer compressor running longer, then as a high-temperature alarm.

Worked example: a plasma freezer room

All inputs here are our own assumptions, not maker data: six upright freezers at 1,200 W each and two blast freezers at 3,000 W each, all treated as running at full power at once. That is conservative, because real compressors cycle. We ignore lights, people and the building envelope. Constants: 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu (EIA), so 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h; one ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h; sensible heat in air is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T (see our heat-transfer formulas lesson).

  1. Uprights: 6 x 1,200 W = 7,200 W.
  2. Blast freezers: 2 x 3,000 W = 6,000 W.
  3. Total: 7,200 + 6,000 = 13,200 W.
  4. Heat: 13,200 x 3.412 = 45,038 Btu/h.
  5. Tons: 45,038 / 12,000 = 3.75 tons.
  6. Airflow: assume the room is held at 75 F and the cooling air picks up 20 F as it crosses the room. Then cfm = 45,038 / (1.08 x 20) = 45,038 / 21.6 = about 2,085 cfm.

Read that last step carefully: 2,085 cfm is the air that must flow through the room to carry that heat at a 20 F rise. It is not a unit size. A real selection uses the maker's sensible capacity at the room's conditions, and for a room this critical we would want two smaller split systems rather than one big one, so one failure leaves half the cooling (our own advice, not a rule). Metering the real load beats the nameplate.

Storage room alarms and loggers

Blood and plasma storage uses the same ideas as the vaccine units in our vaccine refrigerator lesson, so we will not repeat the logger and probe details. The new points here are 21 CFR 606.160 temperature records and the CDC's current note that digital data loggers should be used with at least one backup. Our own addition: a room-air sensor that alarms before the freezers are stressed. Our building alarms lesson covers how alarms reach people; who gets the 2 a.m. call must be written down.

Backup power

For hospitals, NFPA 99 requires essential system power sources to be Type 10, Class X, Level 1 generator sets, per a trade-press summary we read. Type 10 means power is restored within 10 seconds, and Class X leaves the fuel duration to other codes or the authority having jurisdiction. Note that a donation center that is not a hospital may be under different rules, so ask who the authority is. For your work, three checks matter: the freezers, the freezer room AC and the room alarm should all be on the same backed-up circuits (a freezer on generator power with the AC not on it will cook the room); the transfer should be tested under real load, not just a start; and the generator room needs air, as covered in the generator room ventilation lesson.

Mobile blood buses, briefly

A donation vehicle has the same job in a smaller box. The hemovigilance study above noted the highest vasovagal rate in vehicles and tied it to confined space and personnel density, which is a ventilation story as much as a cooling one. Our food-truck lesson in the related list covers vehicle shore power and generator basics.

Field card: Blood and plasma center HVAC

Freezers heat the room, and the room must stay on backup power too.

  • 21 CFR 606.40 asks for adequate ventilation but gives no airflow rate or room temperature.
  • Plasma: minus 18 C or colder; platelets at 20 to 24 C need agitation.
  • Heat: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h; 12,000 Btu/h per ton.
  • Air: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T).
  • Example: 13,200 W = 45,038 Btu/h = 3.75 tons = about 2,085 cfm at 20 F.
  • Freezer room AC, freezers and alarm all go on backup power.
  • Do not judge product; report readings and times.
