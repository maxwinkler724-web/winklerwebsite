Reading a Saturated Steam Table: Pressure, hf, hfg, hg and Pounds of Steam per Hour

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steam table looks like a wall of numbers, but a plant tech uses only a few columns. This lesson shows how to read one row: what the pressure column really means, what hf, hfg and hg are, and how to turn a heat load in Btu per hour into pounds of steam per hour and boiler horsepower.

Gauge pressure versus absolute pressure

The gauge on the header reads psig, pressure above the atmosphere. Steam tables list absolute pressure, psia, measured from a perfect vacuum. Add one atmosphere to the gauge reading. NIST gives one standard atmosphere as 101,325 Pa and one psi as 6,894.757 Pa, so 101,325 / 6,894.757 = 14.696 psi. That makes 0 psig equal to 14.696 psia, 15 psig equal to 29.696 psia, and 100 psig equal to 114.696 psia. Reading a table at 15 psia when the gauge says 15 psig is a common slip, and it puts you on a row near atmospheric boiling instead of the 249.7 F row the coil really sees.

The columns that matter

A university steam table we read defines the headings this way: hf is the specific enthalpy of the saturated liquid, hfg is the specific enthalpy of evaporation (latent heat), hg is the specific enthalpy of the saturated vapor, and vg is the specific volume of the saturated steam. Plant tables in English units give them in Btu per pound and cubic feet per pound. If your table is metric, one Btu per pound is 2.326 kJ per kg (NIST).

  • Saturation temperature. The temperature at which water boils, or steam condenses, at that absolute pressure. Wet or dry, if steam and water are both present at that pressure, this is the temperature.
  • hf, the sensible heat of the liquid. Heat in each pound of water already brought to the boiling point, counted from a base near 32 F.
  • hfg, the latent heat. Heat added to turn a pound of boiling water to steam with no temperature change. It is also what each pound gives up when it condenses back to water. This is the number a coil or heat exchanger lives on.
  • hg, the total heat. hg = hf + hfg. It is the heat in a pound of dry saturated steam.
  • Specific volume. The space one pound of steam fills. It is the inverse of the density column on some tables.

For why a phase change moves so much more heat than a temperature change, see the sensible versus latent heat lesson. For what happens to the hf left in the condensate when it drops to a lower pressure, see the flash steam lesson.

The 0 psig row is the printed 212 F row, which is 14.709 psia, only 0.013 psi above the atmosphere. We got each hfg by subtracting: 1151.1 - 180.33 = 970.77; 1164.7 - 218.5 = 946.2; and for 100 psig we carried the unrounded interpolated values, which give 881.5. Specific volume is one divided by density: 1 / 0.037343 = 26.78 ft3/lb.

Why latent heat falls as pressure rises

Look at the bars. From 0 to 100 psig, hf rises from 180.3 to 309.3 Btu/lb and hg rises only from 1151.1 to 1190.7, so hfg shrinks from 970.8 to 881.5, a drop of 9.2 percent (our arithmetic: (970.8 - 881.5) / 970.8). At higher pressure the water is hotter before it boils, so more of the energy was spent in the liquid. The vapor is also squeezed closer to the density of the liquid, so there is less left to do to pull the molecules apart. The gap keeps closing at higher pressures until the liquid and vapor become the same thing at the critical point, far above any plant pressure. Two other checks: a university table lists latent heat of 2,257.9 kJ/kg at 1.0 bar, 2,201.6 at 2.0 bar and 2,046.5 at 8.0 bar. Divide by 2.326 and those are 970.7, 946.5 and 879.8 Btu/lb at about 14.5, 29.0 and 116.0 psia, in line with our rows.

The practical result is that high-pressure steam is not a more generous heat carrier, pound for pound, when it gives up its latent heat. It does carry more heat per pound in total (hg), but the coil only collects hfg unless it also cools the condensate. The DOE steam tip sheet on waste steam shows the same table use: it works an example with 40 psig saturated steam at "1,176 Btu/lb" and 70 F feedwater at 38 Btu/lb. Interpolating the NIST rows at 54.7 psia gives about 1,177, which matches within the rounding of different tables.

Our own worked example: a 1,000,000 Btu/h steam coil

Assumptions (ours): an air-handler steam coil must deliver 1,000,000 Btu/h. The steam is dry saturated. The condensate leaves the coil as saturated liquid at steam temperature, so the coil gives up only hfg. No piping losses.

At 15 psig.

  1. Absolute pressure: 15 + 14.696 = 29.696 psia.
  2. From the table above, hfg = 946.2 Btu/lb at 249.7 F.
  3. Steam flow: 1,000,000 Btu/h / 946.2 Btu/lb = 1,056.9 lb/h, about 1,057 lb/h.
  4. Volume at 13.88 ft3/lb: 1,056.9 x 13.88 = 14,670 ft3/h, about 244 ft3/min.

Boiler horsepower. One boiler horsepower is conventionally 33,475 Btu/h, which equals 34.5 lb/h of steam evaporated from and at 212 F. Our own check: 33,475 / 34.5 = 970.3 Btu/lb, close to the 970.8 hfg at 212 F in the NIST row. NIST lists one boiler horsepower as 9.809 50 kW; dividing by 0.293 071 W per Btu/h gives about 33,471 Btu/h, so the two figures agree to about 0.01 percent. The coil load is therefore 1,000,000 / 33,475 = 29.87 boiler horsepower.

Do not mix the two: 1,057 lb/h divided by 34.5 gives 30.6, not 29.87. The 34.5 figure assumes 970 Btu per pound; at 15 psig each pound delivers 946.2, which is 97.5 percent as much (29.87 / 30.6), so the pound count is higher than the horsepower alone suggests. A real boiler must also carry piping and pickup losses and heat the make-up water, so it is sized above the coil load.

The same load at 100 psig.

  1. Absolute pressure: 100 + 14.696 = 114.696 psia.
  2. From the table, hfg = 881.5 Btu/lb at 337.9 F.
  3. Steam flow: 1,000,000 / 881.5 = 1,134.4 lb/h.
  4. That is 77.5 lb/h or 7.3 percent more steam than at 15 psig (1,134.4 / 1,056.9 = 1.073).
  5. Volume at 3.89 ft3/lb: 1,134.4 x 3.89 = 4,413 ft3/h, about 74 ft3/min, roughly one third of the 15 psig volume (14,670 / 4,413 = 3.3).

The horsepower is unchanged at 29.87 because it is defined from heat, not pounds. What changes is the pound count (more), the pipe volume (less), and the condensate: at 100 psig each pound leaves with 309.3 Btu in it, 26 percent of its total heat (309.3 / 1190.7), against 15.7 percent at 0 psig (180.33 / 1151.1). That condensate heat is what a flash tank or return system recovers. Most coils are fed through a control valve or reducing station, so the heat is released at the pressure inside the coil, not at the header; see PRV station basics and steam preheat coils.

Habits for reading a table in the plant

  • Convert gauge to absolute first, every time.
  • Use hfg for coils and heat exchangers when condensate leaves at saturation; use hg minus the leaving condensate enthalpy if the condensate is subcooled.
  • Check the units of the table (Btu/lb or kJ/kg, psia or bar).
  • Tables differ in the last digit because of the reference state and the rounding. Two tables that disagree by 0.5 Btu/lb are not wrong.

Field card: Reading a Saturated Steam Table

Add 14.696 to the gauge, then read the row.

  • psia = psig + 14.696.
  • hg = hf + hfg. hfg is what a coil collects.
  • lb/h of steam = load in Btu/h / hfg.
  • Our example: 1,000,000 Btu/h needs 1,057 lb/h at 15 psig and 1,134 lb/h at 100 psig.
  • 1 boiler horsepower = 33,475 Btu/h = 34.5 lb/h from and at 212 F.
  • hfg falls as pressure rises: 970.8, 946.2, 881.5 Btu/lb at 0, 15, 100 psig.
