AC Can't Keep Up on the Hottest Days: Design Conditions and What Is Normal

Last updated: 2026-10-16

It is the hottest afternoon of the summer, the AC has run for hours, and the house is stuck a few degrees above the thermostat. Is the system broken or is it doing what it was built to do? This lesson explains design conditions, shows how much capacity a real unit loses in extreme heat, and separates normal behavior from faults.

The short answer

A correctly sized AC is sized for a design day, not for the worst day ever recorded. On days hotter than that design day, many good systems run nonstop and let the house drift a few degrees warm. That alone is not a fault. A system that cannot cool on ordinary summer days, or that struggles on a day only slightly warm, has a different problem, and the checklist below helps you tell which one you have.

What a design temperature is

The sizing method most U.S. homes use, ACCA Manual J, calculates the cooling load at an outdoor design temperature. ACCA's own technical blog explains that the cooling design value is the 1 percent value for the location, and warns that people often mistake it for "the hottest day of the year," which it is not. Energy Vanguard, a building-science site, spells out what 1 percent means: a 30-year average of the temperature the location goes above for only about 1 percent of the hours in a year, roughly 88 hours. Its Atlanta example is 91°F. An older 2002 U.S. Department of Energy sizing fact sheet used a different percentile (2.5 percent for cooling) and gave the same warning: using the annual extreme temperature leads to oversizing.

The design indoor condition is also fixed. Energy Vanguard uses 75°F inside, so the design temperature difference in that Atlanta example is 16°F. Your own number depends on your town. We did not read a published design value for Newark or northern New Jersey, so look yours up in the Manual J report your contractor ran or ask for the outdoor design temperature used.

What is normal above design

Energy Vanguard says that at design conditions a right-sized AC "should run pretty much continuously" to hold 75°F, though Manual J includes some built-in margin so it may not be quite nonstop. Above design, the unit simply runs out of reserve. A home-comfort contractor's article describes typical systems as built to hold an indoor-to-outdoor difference of about 16 to 20 degrees, so on a 100°F afternoon a house sitting at 78 to 82°F is plausible. These are rules of thumb from trade writers, not code limits. Long, steady run times on those days are normal. Short runs on those days point to a different problem; see AC short cycling.

Why a bigger AC is not the fix

It seems logical to size up so the house never slips. The DOE fact sheet explains the cost. An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat quickly and shuts off, which leaves humidity poorly controlled, adds starts and stops that wear equipment, and lowers efficiency. It cites a Florida study linking units oversized by 50 percent or more to about 9 percent more annual cooling electricity, and it says oversized units worsen summer peak demand. Energy Vanguard adds a field test: runs of 10 minutes or less on a hot afternoon suggest oversizing, about 15 minutes of running is needed before serious dehumidification starts, and indoor humidity above 70 percent is "a really serious problem." ACCA makes the same point from the other side: equipment must have enough load to run and remove moisture on mild, muggy days, not only on the hottest ones. A few warm afternoons a year are a better trade than a clammy house all summer. To see how the load is calculated, read Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J.

Worked example: capacity at design vs a hotter day

Goodman's product data sheet SS-GSXH5 includes an expanded cooling table for the 3-ton GSXH503610 with its matching coil. Conditions: indoor air at 80°F dry bulb and 67°F wet bulb, 990 CFM. The table lists the sensible-to-total ratio (S/T) rather than sensible capacity, so we multiply to get it.

Outdoor tempTotal capacity (MBh)S/TSensible (MBh)System power (kW)
85°F35.30.6723.72.51
95°F33.70.6822.92.78
105°F31.80.7122.63.08
115°F30.10.7622.93.43

Step 1, capacity loss. From 95°F to 105°F: 33.7 − 31.8 = 1.9 MBh, and 1.9 ÷ 33.7 = 5.6 percent. To 115°F: 33.7 − 30.1 = 3.6 MBh, and 3.6 ÷ 33.7 = 10.7 percent. Power rises at the same time, from 2.78 kW to 3.43 kW at 115°F.

Step 2, sensible capacity. Sensible capacity is the part that actually lowers air temperature. 33.7 × 0.68 = 22.9 MBh at 95°F, and 31.8 × 0.71 = 22.6 MBh at 105°F, a change of only about 0.3 MBh. More of the shrinking total is going to moisture removal, so the temperature-lowering part holds up better than the headline number suggests.

Step 3, the load side (illustrative, not from the data sheet). Heat leaking through walls, roof and windows scales with the temperature difference. Suppose that piece is 12,000 Btu/h at a 95°F day and a 75°F house, a difference of 20°F. At 105°F the difference is 30°F, so 12,000 × 30 ÷ 20 = 18,000 Btu/h. That one piece alone grew by 6,000 Btu/h while sensible capacity changed by about 300. The gap does not close by itself, so the house warms until the difference between inside and outside shrinks enough for the machine to catch up. That is the drift you feel. The 12,000 figure is a made-up round number to show the arithmetic; your house is different.

Real faults to rule out

If the house is warm on an ordinary hot day, look for a fault before blaming design. Our running but not cooling lesson has the full checklist; the main suspects are these.

  • Dirty filter or coils. DOE advises cleaning or replacing filters every month or two in cooling season, and more often if the system runs constantly. It says dirt on the evaporator coil cuts airflow and heat absorption, and a dirty outdoor coil needs clear space around it. ENERGY STAR adds that dirty coils weaken cooling and lengthen run times. See cleaning an outdoor condenser coil.
  • Wrong refrigerant charge. ENERGY STAR says too much or too little reduces efficiency and shortens equipment life. Checking and adjusting it takes a technician with gauges and EPA 608 certification.
  • Leaky or attic ducts. UF/IFAS Extension says typical duct systems lose 25 to 40 percent of heating or cooling energy, that leaks to a hot attic and pull-in on the return side raise the load, and that ducts in conditioned space lessen the problem. See upstairs too hot.
  • Failing capacitor. A consumer repair guide lists a hum from the outdoor unit, a fan or compressor that struggles to start, and warm air from the vents. Capacitors can keep a dangerous charge with the power off, so turn the system off at the breaker and leave this to a technician.

What owners can do

  • Block the sun. DOE says to keep window coverings closed during the day to block solar heat. Solar gain does not depend on the temperature difference, so it is a direct cut to the load.
  • Set it high, not low. DOE suggests starting at 75 to 78°F during the day. We did not find a source saying a very low setting cools faster. Our own view: a single-stage unit runs at the same output whatever number you pick, so dropping the setting only keeps it running longer.
  • Pre-cool. On a forecast heat day, let the house settle at a normal setting in the cool morning, close blinds early, and avoid ovens and dryers in the afternoon. Pre-cooling is practical advice, not a measured result; our sources did not quantify it.
  • Keep up maintenance. Replace filters, keep two feet of clearance around the outdoor unit, and book a tune-up before summer.

Field card: AC Can't Keep Up on the Hottest Days

Design day, not worst day. Rule out faults first.

  • Cooling design temperature is the 1 percent value: exceeded about 88 hours a year.
  • Above design, long steady runs and a few degrees of drift can be normal.
  • Example unit: 33.7 MBh at 95°F, 31.8 at 105°F (-5.6%), 30.1 at 115°F (-10.7%).
  • Load rises as the outdoor-indoor difference grows; capacity falls.
  • Bigger is not the fix: humidity and short cycling.
  • Rule out: filter, coils, charge, ducts, capacitor.
  • Owner steps: blinds, 75 to 78°F, pre-cool, maintenance.
