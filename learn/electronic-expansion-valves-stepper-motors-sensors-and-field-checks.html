Electronic Expansion Valves: Steppers, Sensors and Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) senses its own bulb and adjusts itself. An electronic expansion valve (EEV) does nothing until a controller tells it to, so a bad sensor, a dead coil or a lost step count can starve or flood a system that has perfectly good refrigerant. This lesson covers how EEVs work and the field checks the makers publish.

Check the maker's literature

Check the maker's literature. Coil resistances, step counts, sensor types and test modes differ by maker and model. The figures below are examples from the documents we read, not values for your valve. Use the data plate and the service manual for the exact unit. This page does not recommend a brand.

How an EEV differs from a TXV

A TXV is a self-contained mechanical loop: the bulb pressure pushes the valve open, spring pressure and the evaporator pressure push it closed. An EEV moves the loop into electronics. A pressure sensor and a temperature sensor feed a controller, the controller calculates superheat, and it drives a motor or a pulsed coil to change the opening. Copeland's superheat-control leaflet describes EEVs as stepper or pulse-width valves used for precise control of refrigerant mass flow. A side effect is that an EEV can close completely when the system is off. An ACHR News troubleshooting article notes that such systems are designed to close fully off-cycle and during defrost, so they usually have no liquid line solenoid. For the mechanical version, see Metering Devices: TXV and Piston and TXV Bulb Placement, Equalizers and Adjusting Superheat.

Two kinds of EEV: stepper and pulse width

Stepper valves. A stepper motor moves a fixed small angle for each pulse the controller sends. Danfoss describes its ETS valves as driven by a two-phase bipolar stepper motor that stays in position until driver pulses turn it, with a transmission that turns rotation into straight piston travel. Sporlan's bulletin says most of its step-motor valves have no internal position feedback: the controller counts the pulses it sent and remembers the position. That is why the count matters. Published full-travel counts include 500 for one Sporlan valve, 1596 and 3193 for others, and 2625 to 3810 for the Danfoss ETS family. The numbers depend on the model.

Pulse-width valves. Copeland describes its EX2 and CX2 as pulse-width modulated valves. Instead of holding a position, the valve is opened and closed in repeating pulses and the controller changes the share of each cycle that it is open. There is no step count to lose.

Zeroing: why the controller overdrives closed

Because a stepper valve has no position feedback, the controller needs a known reference. Sporlan says initialization drives the valve closed, past the seat (overdrive), to set the zero position, and lists suggested initialization counts larger than the full-travel count. Danfoss recommends that a non-Danfoss or custom controller overdrive about ten percent of the full step range at appropriate intervals and correct for lost steps. Danfoss also says a valve stays in whatever position it had at power failure unless a battery backup is fitted. Carel's driver manual describes a startup motor error check displayed as INIT, and a settable pre-position time.

What the controller reads

The Carel manual defines superheat as suction gas temperature minus the saturated evaporating temperature, with the saturated value calculated from the pressure probe. The two inputs are a suction pressure transducer (Carel lists ratiometric or 4 to 20 mA types) and a suction temperature sensor (an NTC thermistor by default). Danfoss's heat pump troubleshooting sheet shows a different sensor choice: a PT1000 on the suction line and a pressure transmitter that should show 5 volts between its supply terminals. Copeland's EXD controller leaflet lists R410A pressure sensors with a 0 to 18 bar range, so pick the sensor for the refrigerant and the range.

The controller then runs a control loop (Carel uses PID) to hold a superheat setpoint. Carel's manual says setpoints are almost always between 3 and 14 K, with a default of 11 K and a low-superheat protection threshold of 5 K. Treat those as that driver's defaults, not as a target for your system. For the basics of the math, see Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Worked example

All arithmetic here is Our own. The saturation value comes from the Arkema Forane R-410A pressure-temperature table (psig).

Part 1: superheat. The transducer reads 118 psig on R-410A. The suction-line sensor reads 52 F.

  1. Look up saturation. The table lists 118.4 psig vapor at 40 F. Our own rounding: 118 psig is about 40 F.
  2. Superheat = suction line temperature minus saturation temperature.
  3. Our own: 52 F minus 40 F = 12 F of superheat.

Part 2: percent open. The valve data says 480 full steps, and the controller reports 150 steps.

  1. Percent open = steps now divided by full steps, times 100.
  2. Our own: 150 divided by 480 = 0.3125.
  3. Our own: 0.3125 times 100 = 31.25 percent, about 31 percent open.

Watch the definition: Sporlan's bulletin gives a usable flow range of 48 to 448 steps for its 500-step ESX valve, with 48 as 0 percent and 448 as 100 percent. Some makers therefore count percent against the usable range, not the full count. Check how your controller defines it.

Common faults

  • Sensor out of range or wrong. ACHR News advises measuring suction pressure and suction line temperature yourself, calculating superheat, and comparing to the board. If they disagree, the sensor is the likely problem. Also confirm the refrigerant type and superheat setpoint entered in the board.
  • Transducer drift or wrong type. The same compare-with-gauges method applies. Check supply voltage first, such as the 5 volts Danfoss lists at its transmitter.
  • Coil open. Measure each winding and compare to the maker's value. The ACHR article also says to check continuity to ground.
  • Valve stuck. The ACHR article says to use the maker's magnetic tool to turn the needle valve and see if suction pressure or temperature changes. If nothing changes, the valve needs replacement.
  • Fails to close. Because the EEV is the shutoff, ACHR suggests a pump-down test, then isolating the compressor from the valve.

Field checks, in order

  1. Power and controller. Danfoss's heat pump sheet starts with confirming 24 VAC at the controller. A Fujitsu General field-test article starts with the coil, then DC supply at the board.
  2. Coil resistance, power off. Examples from the makers: Danfoss ETS, 52 ohms per phase plus or minus 10 percent. Sporlan, 46 ohms (ESX), 75 or 100 ohms (others), plus or minus 10 percent. Fujitsu General, 42 to 50 ohms on 12 V DC coils and 183 to 191 ohms on 24 V DC coils at about 20 C. Any open, short or out-of-band reading means a bad coil or harness.
  3. Supply voltage at the valve. Sporlan lists 12 VDC (minus 5, plus 10 percent) measured at the valve leads. Fujitsu General says to listen for four ticks at startup and test voltage if they are absent.
  4. Sensors against the manual. Danfoss tells the tech to check its PT1000 resistance against an ohm table. Use the table for your sensor and the line temperature from a trusted thermometer. We did not find a published table in the sources we read, so use the maker's.
  5. Mechanical movement. Fujitsu General describes removing the coil, turning the valve core with a magnet, refitting the coil and checking for a temperature difference across the valve. Danfoss lists a service driver with manual operation for its valves. Many controllers also have a manual or test mode. Follow your model's manual.

Why you never judge from superheat alone at startup

At power-up the controller may be initializing the valve, holding a pre-position, or limiting the opening on purpose. The Carel and Sporlan documents both describe initialization and pre-positioning before normal control. Our own reasoning: a high or low superheat reading in those first minutes may be the controller doing what it was designed to do, and a sensor that is wrong makes the reading itself wrong. So compare the board's pressure and temperature to your gauges, watch the valve position or step count, and wait for the controller to leave its startup routine before you call the valve bad. See Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return for the system-level view.

Field card: Electronic Expansion Valves

The controller is the brain; check inputs before blaming the valve.

  • Stepper valves count pulses; pulse-width valves open and close in repeating pulses.
  • Inputs: suction pressure transducer plus suction temperature sensor.
  • Superheat = suction line temperature minus saturation temperature at the transducer pressure.
  • Percent open = steps now divided by full steps, times 100 (check the maker's definition).
  • Coil resistance, power off, against the maker's value (examples: 52, 75, 100 ohms plus or minus 10 percent).
  • Compare board readings to your gauges and thermometer before replacing anything.
  • Do not judge the valve from superheat during startup initialization.
