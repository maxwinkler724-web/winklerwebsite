Hot Gas Defrost in Freezers: Valves, Pan Loop and Sequence

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A freezer coil can be defrosted with electric heaters, with warm air, or with the system's own hot refrigerant gas. Hot gas is the one that needs the most piping and the most care. This lesson follows the gas through the valves, the pan loop and back out, then compares it with the other two methods.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. Piping, pressures and timers differ by system. This is general education from published sources, not a service procedure.

The idea in one paragraph

An HPAC Magazine article puts it simply: gas defrost uses the system's own internal energy to defrost the evaporator. Hot vapor from the compressor discharge is sent into the frosted coil. It gives up heat to the frost, condenses to liquid, and the liquid is pushed out the other end. Because the coil is heated from the inside, an Oak Ridge National Laboratory report says less heat reaches the case and food than with heaters. For frost, timers and fan delay, see Walk-In Defrost: Termination, Fan Delay and Icing. This page covers the gas path.

The valves and what each one does

  • Hot gas solenoid. Opens at the start of defrost to admit discharge gas. A coil maker's manual describes a three-pipe system that takes gas from the discharge line between the compressor and the condenser, then sends it through this valve to the pan circuit and the coil.
  • Liquid line solenoid. De-energized (closed) during defrost, so no new liquid feeds the coil. A packaged system document we read says this also lets the evaporator pump down before gas is admitted. See Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves.
  • Check valves. Around the expansion valve and the liquid solenoid, so condensed liquid can leave the coil toward the liquid line (HPAC).
  • Suction control. In supermarket racks a suction regulator closes during defrost so discharge gas does not run straight to the compressor (HPAC).
  • Pressure difference. HPAC says about 20 psi is needed to guarantee reverse flow.

The pan loop

Melt water drains into the pan, where it can refreeze. A coil maker's manual says that in a reverse-cycle unit the suction line check valve directs the hot gas through the drain pan loop to keep condensate from freezing in the pan. A white paper on industrial defrost notes that ice melts on the coil first, then falls into the pan and finishes melting there. A packaged system document we read instead recommends an electric heat drain pan over hot gas loops. Both designs exist, so follow the unit's diagram.

The sequence, step by step

  1. The timer or controller starts defrost. The liquid line solenoid closes and the evaporator fans stop (on freezers).
  2. The coil is pumped down. The ammonia bulletin includes a pump-out phase for large coils; liquid left in the coil is a hazard when hot gas arrives.
  3. The hot gas solenoid opens. Gas flows through the pan loop and the coil, melting frost from the inside.
  4. Defrost ends. One system document we read ends on pressure rise with time as a fail-safe, and warns not to set that fail-safe above 20 minutes. A coil maker's manual says to set the fail-safe at the length of defrost plus 25 percent, so a 20-minute defrost gets a 25-minute fail-safe.
  5. The coil drips, and the liquid solenoid reopens.
  6. The fan delay holds the fans off until the coil cools. The coil maker's manual describes a control that holds the fans until the coil is about 35 F.

Liquid slugging and hydraulic shock

Gas that loses its heat becomes liquid, and that liquid has to go somewhere. HPAC says three-pipe defrost sends liquid back toward the compressor, so an adequate accumulator is usually required, and an incomplete defrost can cause floodback or oil logging. A coil maker's manual says low superheat promotes slugging. An ACHR News article lists conditions after hot gas termination among the causes of flooding. See Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging, and compare pressures with Reading a Defrost Cycle on the Gauges.

Hydraulic shock is separate. A valve maker's white paper says a liquid hammer can occur when hot gas first enters an evaporator that still holds liquid, and names slow valve opening as the main fix. An ammonia-only bulletin says thuds, slams or piping vibration are signs of pressure shock, reports short spikes of 2,000 to 4,000 psi,, with damage that accumulates over years.

Two more cautions: a packaged system document says energizing the hot gas solenoids without the compressor running can cause major defrost problems, and that lines over 100 ft and oversizing cause heavy floodback. The coil maker's manual says generally not more than one-third of a reverse-cycle system should defrost at once (25 percent for alternating evaporators), so the compressors keep a load.

How it compares with electric and off-cycle defrost

  • Off-cycle. Refrigerant flow stops and fans move air over the coil. The ORNL report says no significant energy is used for the defrost itself, but it relies on warm air, so it is limited to medium-temperature cases. A contractor article we read says the air must be above 32 F.
  • Electric. Low first cost, high operating cost. The ORNL report says about 1 percent or more of heater heat goes into melting frost, the rest heats the case and food, and heaters can use up to 25 percent of a display case's electrical energy (manufacturer data cited there range 10 to 30 percent, averaging about 20).
  • Hot gas. The report says it removes frost about 1.5 times faster than electric defrost, with 15 to 25 percent of its heat going to the frost, and that total electrical energy for low-temperature cases can be less than 20 percent of direct electric defrost.

Reverse-cycle defrost, which uses a four-way valve, cut defrost time 19 percent and energy 27.2 percent against electric heaters in one cold-room test a review paper describes. Hot gas costs more to install, and HPAC says it needs elevated winter head pressure, an energy penalty that may erase savings at some low ambient point.

Worked example: heat to melt the frost

Our own numbers. Assumptions, not measured data: 20 lb of frost on a freezer coil, starting at -10 F; specific heat of ice 0.5 Btu/lb·F; latent heat of fusion 144 Btu/lb; an electric defrost of 6 kW for 30 minutes.

  1. Warm the frost from -10 F to 32 F: 32 - (-10) = 42 F.
  2. Sensible heat: 20 lb × 0.5 Btu/lb·F × 42 F = 420 Btu.
  3. Melt it: 20 lb × 144 Btu/lb = 2,880 Btu.
  4. Total to melt the frost: 420 + 2,880 = 3,300 Btu.
  5. Heater energy: 6 kW × 0.5 h = 3 kWh. At 3.412 Btu/h per watt, 6,000 W × 3.412 = 20,472 Btu/h; over 0.5 h that is 10,236 Btu.
  6. Fraction: 3,300 ÷ 10,236 = 0.322, about 32 percent.

That 32 percent is the share the frost needs if it got every Btu. Real heaters do worse: the ORNL report's figure is about 1 percent or more reaching the frost, which would be only about 100 Btu of these 10,236. The same report says 15 to 25 percent of hot gas heat melts frost. At those shares, 3,300 Btu of melting takes about 13,200 Btu (at 25 percent) to 22,000 Btu (at 15 percent) of gas heat. That gas heat is not new wall-socket electricity, so it cannot be set against the 3 kWh directly; the point is the direction.

Field card: Hot Gas Defrost in Freezers: Valves, Pan Loop and Sequence

Gas in the pan loop and coil, liquid out through a check valve.

  • Liquid solenoid closes, hot gas solenoid opens, fans stay off.
  • Check valves let condensate bypass the expansion valve and liquid solenoid.
  • Termination is by temperature or pressure; time is the fail-safe. One maker says fail-safe = defrost length + 25 percent.
  • Example (our assumptions): 3,300 Btu to melt 20 lb versus 10,236 Btu from the heater.
