Federal Rules for Refrigerant Recovery Equipment: Testing, the 1993 Date, Labels and Low-Loss Fittings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most 608 questions about recovery ask what you must do. A smaller group asks about the machine itself: who tests it, what its label proves, why a machine made before a certain date is treated differently, and what a hose fitting must do. This lesson covers the equipment side of the federal refrigerant management rules and ends with a label-reading example.

Check the current rule text

Check the current text. We read the federal refrigerant management subpart in the government's online code of federal regulations in November 2026, plus a federal environmental agency page on the 2020 rule update. Rules change, and this is study help, not legal advice. Confirm against the live text before you rely on it.

Equipment must be tested and listed by an approved organization

The rule says anyone who makes or imports recovery or recycling equipment for servicing, repairing or disposing of appliances must first have the equipment certified. The certifying body is an approved equipment testing organization, which the federal agency approves after reviewing its test expertise, its plan for checking long-term performance and its lack of financial interest in test results. Our reading of the approval text: the agency does not keep the equipment list itself. Each approved organization must publish a list of the equipment it has certified online and update it every year. The same text says a certified model line must be retested, or its factory inspected, at least once every three years. If a maker changes a certified design in a way that could affect performance, the changed design counts as uncertified until it is retested.

The federal agency's 2020 update summary says the duty to use certified recovery equipment and to evacuate to a set level before service or disposal still applies to substitute refrigerants such as HFCs, not only the older ozone-depleting ones.

The dividing date: November 15, 1993

This date splits the equipment rules in two. Equipment made or imported before it (other than small-appliance, car air conditioning and similar machines) counts as certified if it reaches the evacuation levels in the machine standards table when checked with a properly calibrated gauge. That is the grandfathering: no testing organization was involved, and the pre-1993 column asks for less. Equipment made on or after the date must be certified by an approved organization against a published test method, and the method depends on the build date: November 15, 1993 through September 21, 2003; September 22, 2003 through December 31, 2016; and January 1, 2017 and later, when separate methods cover non-flammable and flammable refrigerants. For you, the practical point is that the machine's build date, not the appliance's age, picks the column.

Required evacuation levels by equipment date

Before opening or disposing of most appliances, the technician must evacuate to the technician table in the rule. Our own summary of it, in inches of mercury vacuum (relative to 29.9 inches of standard atmosphere), listed as machine made before November 15, 1993, then on or after:

  • Very high-pressure: 0 and 0.
  • High-pressure, full charge under 200 pounds: 0 and 0.
  • High-pressure, 200 pounds or more: 4 and 10.
  • Medium-pressure, under 200 pounds: 4 and 10.
  • Medium-pressure, 200 pounds or more: 4 and 15.
  • Low-pressure: 25 mm Hg absolute for both dates.

Two tables exist. One tells the technician what vacuum to reach, and a second sets what the machine itself must be able to do in testing. Their rows are not identical, so make sure you are reading the technician table when a question asks what you must reach. Reading each row, leaks and the major-repair shortcut are covered in the required evacuation levels lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

What the label must show

Each certified unit must carry a label. Under the rule, the label states that an approved testing organization certified the equipment to meet the federal minimum requirements for a named appliance category. It also shows the date of manufacture and, if applicable, a serial number. The label must be readily visible or accessible, made of durable material, stay legible for the life of the equipment and be attached so it cannot be removed without damaging it. A label with a date before November 15, 1993 and no approval statement is what grandfathered equipment typically looks like; our own caution is to confirm that with the maker if the label is worn.

Self-contained and system-dependent equipment

The definitions are short. Self-contained equipment can pull refrigerant out of an appliance without using the appliance's own parts. System-dependent equipment needs the appliance's own components, usually its compressor, to help the refrigerant leave. The recovery rule limits system-dependent equipment: it may not be used on appliances holding more than 15 pounds of refrigerant unless it is permanently attached to the appliance as a pump-out unit. Someone who services or disposes only of their own appliances that have pump-out units does not need separate certified self-contained equipment. For the small appliance side, equipment is certified to recover 90 percent of the test refrigerant with the compressor running or 80 percent with it not running, or alternatively to reach 4 inches of vacuum; pre-1993 small-appliance equipment counts as certified at 80 percent or 4 inches. See the system-dependent and self-contained lesson for that comparison.

Low-loss fittings and purge limits

The rule requires recovery and recycling equipment to have low-loss fittings on all hoses. The definition describes a connector used between hoses, appliances or machines that closes automatically, or can be closed by hand, when disconnected, to limit refrigerant loss. A machine with a device that purges noncondensable gases may release no more than 3 percent of the refrigerant being recycled during purging. Hose care is in the hose care lesson.

Owner equipment records

Older versions of the rules had owners send an equipment approval statement to the federal agency. When we opened the section that used to hold it, it was marked reserved with no text, and the recordkeeping section we read did not list recovery machine serial numbers or approval. So we found no current federal requirement to file or log your machine. What the rules do require is service records: for an appliance holding 50 pounds or more, the owner keeps records of the date and type of service and the refrigerant added, for at least three years, and disposal of an appliance with a 5 to 50 pound charge needs records kept three years. Our own suggestion, not rule text: photograph each machine's label and keep it with the job records, because the label is how you show the machine's date and approval status. See the records lesson.

Worked example: our own

Our own example with assumed inputs. We assume the machine label reads as in the diagram: certified by an approved testing organization for medium-pressure and high-pressure appliances, made June 2015, with an invented serial number. We assume the appliance is a medium-pressure chiller with a full charge of 230 pounds, no leak keeps the vacuum from holding, and the work is major (a compressor comes out), so the shortcut for small jobs does not apply.

  1. The label date is 2015. That is on or after November 15, 1993, so the newer column applies.
  2. The appliance class is medium-pressure and the charge is 230 pounds, which is 200 or more, so we use the medium-pressure, 200 pounds or more row.
  3. The technician table gives 15 inches of mercury vacuum in that row and column. A reading of 15 inches of vacuum is about 14.9 inches absolute (29.9 minus 15), our own arithmetic.
  4. Check against the text: we compared our reading with the regulation text of the evacuation paragraph, and the row and column match, so the answer is 15. If the label instead said 1991, the same row would give 4.

How exam questions are phrased

This advice is our own, not exam text. Questions often add facts you do not need, such as the age of the appliance. Sort what you need: which pressure class, whether the charge is under or over 200 pounds, and whether the machine was made before or on or after November 15, 1993. Four practice questions of our own:

  • Which date divides older recovery equipment from newer? Answer: November 15, 1993.
  • A system-dependent machine is proposed for an appliance holding 40 pounds that has no pump-out unit. May it be used? Answer: No. System-dependent equipment is limited to appliances with 15 pounds or less unless permanently attached as a pump-out unit.
  • What must every hose on recovery equipment have? Answer: Low-loss fittings.
  • A high-pressure appliance holds 120 pounds and the machine was made in 2018. What level? Answer: 0, from the high-pressure under 200 pounds row.

Field card: Recovery equipment rules

Date on the label, then pressure class, then charge.

  • Machines are certified by an approved testing organization before sale.
  • November 15, 1993 divides older equipment from newer.
  • Older equipment counts as certified if it reaches the required levels.
  • Medium-pressure, 200 lb or more: 4 (older machine) or 15 (newer).
  • High-pressure under 200 lb: 0 for both dates.
  • System-dependent equipment: not above 15 lb unless a permanently attached pump-out unit.
  • Low-loss fittings on all hoses; purge loss 3 percent or less.
  • Label shows approval statement, build date and serial number if used.
