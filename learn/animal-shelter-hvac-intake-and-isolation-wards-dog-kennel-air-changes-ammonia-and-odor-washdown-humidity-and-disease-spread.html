Animal Shelter HVAC: Isolation Wards, Air Changes, Ammonia and Washdown

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A shelter takes in animals of unknown health, packs them close together and hoses their floors every day. That makes the air system a disease-control tool as much as a comfort system. This lesson covers what is different in a shelter compared with a clinic or lab room: intake and isolation wards, separate air, air changes, ammonia, drying after washdown and noise.

What the shelter guidelines say about air

The Association of Shelter Veterinarians publishes the Guidelines for Standards of Care in Animal Shelters (second edition, 2022). Section 4.6 sets these points for the building air:

  • Fresh air at "between 10 and 20 room air exchanges per hour," and possibly more at full capacity because the animals themselves add heat, humidity and carbon dioxide.
  • Temperature between 64 and 80 °F, and relative humidity between 30 and 70 percent.
  • Ventilation must not compromise the recommended temperature. More air is not allowed to chill the kennels.
  • Air from isolation areas should be exhausted outside and not recirculated, because canine respiratory germs can travel through the air. For cats, separate air for isolation is called a lower priority.
  • Maintain ventilation systems per the maker; carbon dioxide monitors may help check performance.

The Guidelines do not set a room pressure in the text we read. Holding intake and isolation wards slightly negative so air leaks in from the corridor is our own design habit, not a quoted requirement, and the designer or shelter veterinarian decides it. For how pressure is checked in healthcare rooms, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare.

Federal animal welfare rules for dogs and cats (9 CFR 3.3, in the eCFR) apply to facilities covered by the Animal Welfare Act, and not every shelter is covered. Where they apply, ventilation must minimize odors, drafts, ammonia levels and moisture condensation, and fans or air conditioning must be added when the temperature reaches 85 °F. Temperature may not stay below 45 °F or above 85 °F for more than four consecutive hours with dogs or cats present. It gives no numeric humidity or ammonia limit.

Intake and isolation wards

Intake is where the newest, least known animals arrive, so germs show up there first. The Guidelines' isolation section (4.4) says isolation rooms should not open directly into other housing areas, that a corridor or vestibule can serve as the entry and as the place to put on and take off protective gear, that different species must not share an isolation room, and that animals with different highly contagious diseases need separate areas. A University of Florida shelter health text lists crowding as something that "decreases ventilation and air quality," and names poor ventilation and chronic moisture as risk factors for canine respiratory disease.

What that means for the HVAC layout, in our own words:

  • Separate air path. Intake and isolation wards get their own exhaust fans and ducts that discharge outdoors, not into a shared return. Keep the discharge away from outdoor air intakes.
  • Exhaust slightly above supply. That keeps the ward negative to the corridor, as the drawing shows.

Ammonia and odor

Urine breaks down into ammonia, and it collects in kennels that stay wet. The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists a recommended exposure limit for ammonia of 25 ppm as an 8-hour average and 35 ppm as a short-term limit. It describes ammonia as a gas with a pungent, suffocating odor that irritates the eyes, nose and throat. That limit is for workers; the shelter guidelines give no ammonia number. In practice, if staff smell ammonia strongly or their eyes sting, the kennel needs better cleaning, drying or ventilation. Barn ventilation raises the same problem at larger scale; see Poultry and Livestock Barn Ventilation.

Washdown, humidity and drying

Daily cleaning puts a lot of water into the room. The Guidelines call for drainage that prevents standing water and cross-contamination between housing units, sloped floors "for faster drying," a sealed impermeable floor such as resinous epoxy or urethane, and surfaces that withstand repeated cleaning. They also warn that housing that needs forceful equipment for daily cleaning is unacceptable, and that spot cleaning reduces fomite spread. For the HVAC side, we see three points:

  • Run the ward exhaust at full rate through cleaning and drying, not on a setback schedule. A 70 percent relative humidity ceiling is easy to pass when the floor is wet.
  • Dry the floor with squeegees and slope to the drain first, and let ventilation finish the job. Ventilation cannot dry standing water quickly.
  • Put humidity sensors where the animals are, not in the corridor. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for relative humidity basics.

Noise and cat rooms

The Guidelines say noise in animal housing must be minimized, that sudden or unpredictable sounds are a concern, and that noise and vibration sources should be as far from animal housing as possible. They give no decibel limit. For HVAC that means putting fans and compressors away from the kennels, using vibration isolators, and keeping duct velocity and diffuser noise low. Cats are the case where quiet matters most, so we keep them in rooms with their own supply and away from dog wards.

Worked example: one dog ward

These inputs are assumptions for teaching, except the 10 to 20 range (the Guidelines) and the 1.08 factor (see HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas). Our own assumptions: ward 1,200 sq ft, 10 ft ceiling, 12 air changes per hour of fresh outdoor air, outdoor air 20 °F, heated to 65 °F, all outside air with no recirculation.

  1. Volume = 1,200 × 10 = 12,000 ft³.
  2. Airflow = ACH × volume ÷ 60 = 12 × 12,000 ÷ 60 = 2,400 cfm. (At 10 ACH it is 2,000 cfm; at 20 ACH it is 4,000 cfm.)
  3. Temperature rise = 65 − 20 = 45 °F.
  4. Heat to warm the air = 1.08 × 2,400 × 45 = 116,640 Btu/h. At 10 ACH it is 97,200; at 20 ACH it is 194,400.

So about 117,000 Btu/h goes just to warm the outdoor air for this one ward, before wall losses, and before crediting the heat from the dogs. The Guidelines' warning that ventilation must not compromise temperature is a real design constraint at this size.

Savings idea. Suppose we added an energy recovery device with a labeled 60 percent sensible effectiveness, and equal supply and exhaust flow. It would recover about 0.60 × 116,640 = 69,984 Btu/h, leaving 116,640 − 69,984 = 46,656 Btu/h for the heater (assuming the exhaust leaves the ward at 65 °F). The supply air would then arrive near 20 + 0.60 × 45 = 47 °F. That is a large saving, but it comes with a catch. A wheel is not airtight and leaks toward the lower-pressure side, so a little exhaust can end up in the supply; see Energy Recovery Wheels in Commercial Units. In an isolation ward, where the Guidelines say not to recirculate air, that is our reason to avoid wheels on isolation exhaust. A system with no shared air path, such as a run-around coil loop, may be a better fit, and the shelter veterinarian should approve any recovery on infectious-ward exhaust. Latent (moisture) recovery is not counted in this example.

Field card: Animal Shelter HVAC

Separate air for the sick, dry floors for everyone.

  • Shelter guidelines: 10 to 20 fresh air changes per hour, 64 to 80 °F, 30 to 70 percent RH.
  • Isolation exhaust goes outdoors and is not recirculated; intake and isolation wards are held negative by our own design habit.
  • cfm = ACH × volume ÷ 60. Heating the air: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Our example: 12 ACH in 12,000 ft³ = 2,400 cfm = about 116,640 Btu/h at a 45 °F rise.
  • NIOSH ammonia REL: 25 ppm average, 35 ppm short-term.
  • Run exhaust at full rate during washdown and drying.
  • Noisy equipment far from housing; get veterinarian approval for energy recovery.
