Compressed Air Dryers for Facility Techs: Pressure Dew Point, Refrigerated vs Desiccant, Purge Air Cost, Condensate Drains and Common Faults

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Water in an air line is not a leak or a bad batch of air. It is physics that the dryer is supposed to handle ahead of time. This lesson covers why the water forms, how the common dryer types remove it, what the purge air on a desiccant dryer really costs, and what usually goes wrong.

The wider system (leaks, setpoints, receivers) is in Compressed Air Systems for Facility Techs, and the compressor's waste heat is in Heat Recovery From Plant Air Compressors. We do not repeat those here.

Why water forms: dew point at line pressure

Outdoor air carries water vapor. The U.S. Department of Energy compressed air sourcebook explains that cooling air, and also squeezing it into a smaller volume, makes that vapor condense. The aftercooler and separator take out most of it, but the air leaving them is still typically saturated, which means any further cooling in the piping drops out more water. That water can corrode pipe and contaminate whatever the air touches. The sourcebook gives a handy rule: cooling saturated air by 20 degrees F cuts its moisture content by roughly half.

The number that matters is the pressure dew point: the temperature at which the air, at line pressure, starts to condense. The sourcebook notes this differs from the atmospheric dew point you would read on a weather report, so a dryer spec or sensor must say which one it means. If a pipe, a tool or an outdoor drop is ever colder than the air's pressure dew point, water forms there. For the psychrometric basics behind this, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

The air path in order

Refrigerated dryers

The sourcebook calls these the most common dryer type, with relatively low first cost and operating cost. They chill the air so water condenses and drains out, then usually reheat it a bit with the incoming hot air, which lowers the refrigeration load and keeps the outside of the pipe from sweating in humid rooms. To avoid ice, the evaporator should not go below 32 degrees F, so the sourcebook says a pressure dew point of 35 degrees F or higher is what you can usually expect. Oregon State University's energy extension page gives 35 to 50 degrees F. They are not recommended in sub-freezing ambient conditions.

There are two control styles, both described in the sourcebook:

  • Non-cycling: refrigerant cools the air directly, and a hot gas bypass valve controls the leaving air temperature. The refrigerant compressor runs continuously. This is the same idea as in Hot Gas Bypass and Capacity Control: it holds the evaporator above freezing at light load.
  • Cycling: the refrigerant cools a thermal storage mass (the sourcebook lists glycol and water, metal powder, sand, steel or aluminum), and thermostats shut the compressor off when the mass is cold enough. The mass keeps it from short cycling.

The refrigeration circuit itself is the ordinary one (compressor, condenser, metering device, evaporator), covered in Refrigeration Cycle Basics. Here the evaporator is an air-to-refrigerant heat exchanger with a moisture separator behind it.

Desiccant dryers

When you need dew points below freezing, a refrigerated dryer cannot get there. Regenerative desiccant dryers have two towers: one dries the air while the other is regenerated. The sourcebook lists silica gel, activated alumina and molecular sieves as common desiccants, and says to use only the type the maker specifies. Regeneration drops the tower pressure and passes previously dried air (purge air) back through the bed. The sourcebook says the purge may be heated, inside or outside the dryer, to reduce how much purge is needed, and that cycles may run on time, on dew point, or on both.

  • Heatless: uses only purge air. Oregon State's energy extension reports that non-heated dryers need 15 percent or more of the compressed air for purge (and another table on the same page gives 10 to 15 percent).
  • Heated: the same source gives about 1 to 7 percent in one place and 3 to 5 percent for internally heated units in another. The two figures disagree, so treat the exact value as a maker spec.
  • Dew point control: a moisture sensor can start and stop regeneration so the dryer does not purge more than it must. Oregon State describes this as generally more efficient than fixed-time cycling. The sensor is also the plant's live read on whether the dryer is doing its job.
  • Heat-of-compression: the sourcebook says these use the compressor's own hot discharge air and are used almost exclusively with centrifugal or oil-free rotary screw compressors.

A coalescing filter ahead of a desiccant dryer prevents fouling, and a particulate filter after it catches desiccant fines (sourcebook).

Membrane dryers, briefly

Membranes let water vapor pass through faster than air. The sourcebook gives a typical outlet dew point of about 40 degrees F, with lower values (to minus 40 degrees F) "at the expense of additional purge air loss." Oregon State reports a 9 to 10 percent loss of system capacity. They suit small point-of-use jobs.

Worked example: what heatless purge costs (our own example)

Our own example, with assumptions we chose. These are not measured values from any plant.

  • Heatless desiccant dryer sized for 500 scfm of air, fully loaded.
  • Purge = 15 percent of dryer flow. The 15 percent comes from the Oregon State figure above ("15% or more"), so this is a typical or low case.
  • Runs 8,760 hours a year (24 hours, 365 days).
  • Our assumed cost to make compressed air: 18 kW per 100 scfm, which is 0.18 kW per scfm. At 1,000 scf that is 0.18 × 1,000 ÷ 60 = 3.0 kWh.
  • Our assumed electricity price: $0.12 per kWh, so air costs 3.0 × $0.12 = $0.36 per 1,000 scf.
  1. Purge flow: 500 × 0.15 = 75 scfm.
  2. Per hour: 75 × 60 = 4,500 scf.
  3. Per year: 4,500 × 8,760 = 39,420,000 scf, or 39,420 thousand scf.
  4. Energy: 39,420 × 3.0 = 118,260 kWh a year.
  5. Cost: 39,420 × $0.36 = about $14,190 a year.

For comparison, Oregon State lists refrigerated dryers at about 130 kWh per million cubic feet of air dried. A refrigerated dryer handling the full 500 scfm all year (500 x 60 x 8,760 = 262.8 million cubic feet) would use about 34,200 kWh, or about $4,100 at our assumed price, and it needs no purge air at all at our assumed price. If the dryer is oversized for its real load, or the plant only needs a 35 degree F dew point, that purge is mostly wasted. If a dew point sensor lets the dryer purge only half the time, the cost would halve in the same arithmetic. Change the flow, hours, kW per scfm and price to your plant's numbers before quoting any figure.

Condensate drains and oil-water separators

The sourcebook lists four drain styles. Manual drains are often left cracked open, which it says should not be tolerated. Level-operated mechanical traps waste no air when they work but can plug with sediment (inverted bucket types waste air if the condensate flow is too low to keep a liquid seal). Timed solenoid drains open on a schedule even when there is little or no condensate, and each open cycle blows air; motorized ball valves on timers are described as worse. Zero air-loss drains use a float or level sensor so they open only for liquid. Empty their reservoirs often so contaminants do not build up. Where freezing is possible, the sourcebook says heated drains may be needed. A zero-loss drain that stops draining is a different failure: the dryer floods and water goes downstream, so check them on rounds.

Condensate from lubricated compressors carries oil. The sourcebook says contaminated condensate must have the oil removed before it goes to a sewer, and to check allowable limits with the local sewer authority. An oil-water separator handles this. EPA guidance on gravity separators explains that they hold the water long enough for oil to rise and be removed, that enhanced types use coalescing media for smaller droplets, that flow above design can wash oil through, and that they need routine monitoring and prompt removal of collected oil. Emulsified oil is harder to separate, so match the separator to the compressor lubricant.

Common faults: fouling, hot inlet air and bypass

  • High inlet temperature. The sourcebook rates dryers at 100 psig, 100 degrees F inlet and 100 degrees F ambient (the "three 100s"). Hotter inlet air, hotter rooms or more flow cut capacity, and the maker supplies correction factors. If the aftercooler is dirty or its cooling is weak, the dryer sees more water and the dew point climbs. Check the aftercooler first.
  • Fouling. The sourcebook notes that filters get dirty and that dryers and filters are major pressure-drop points, worst at the highest flow and temperature. Oil carryover ahead of a desiccant dryer is the case the sourcebook warns about when it calls for a coalescing filter first. Change elements by differential pressure. On a refrigerated dryer, our own checklist (not a sourced figure) also includes cleaning the condenser, since a hot refrigeration side limits cooling.
  • Bypass problems. Our own field note, not from a source: a dryer bypass valve left open sends wet air downstream. Compare the dryer's outlet dew point or temperature with its inlet to confirm the air really goes through it, and tag the bypass valve closed after service.
  • Drains. A stuck-closed drain floods the dryer; a stuck-open one is a loud air leak. Both show up with ultrasound, see Airborne Ultrasound Testing for Facility Techs.

Picking the dew point the plant actually needs

The sourcebook is plain: drying beyond the required pressure dew point wastes energy and money. Ask what the equipment and the coldest pipe run actually need. Oregon State's advice is that where drier air is not required, a refrigerated dryer can replace a desiccant dryer, and that only the moisture-sensitive branch should be dried rather than the whole plant. If a pipe runs outdoors in winter, its coldest temperature sets the minimum. A 35 degree F dew point is fine for most indoor pipe, but freezing lines call for a lower dew point, or heat tracing. Write the chosen pressure dew point on the dryer, and check it with the dryer's own sensor on rounds. Before opening any line, relieve and verify stored air, following your site's lockout rules.

Field card: Compressed Air Dryers

Dry only as much as the plant needs.

  • Water forms when air is cooled below its pressure dew point at line pressure.
  • Refrigerated dryers: usually 35 degrees F or higher pressure dew point; non-cycling uses hot gas bypass, cycling uses a thermal mass.
  • Heatless desiccant dryers purge 15 percent or more of the air (Oregon State). Heated units purge less.
  • Our own example: 75 scfm purge is about 118,000 kWh and about $14,190 a year at our assumed $0.36 per 1,000 scf.
  • Timed drains blow air when empty; zero air-loss drains do not.
  • Check aftercooler, filters, drains and bypass before blaming the dryer.
