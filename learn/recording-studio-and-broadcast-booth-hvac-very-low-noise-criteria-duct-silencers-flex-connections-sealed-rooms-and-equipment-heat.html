Recording Studio and Broadcast Booth HVAC: Very Low NC, Silencers, Flex Connections and Equipment Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A booth where a microphone is open is the quietest room many techs will ever condition, and it is also a small box full of warm electronics with people in it. This lesson covers the published noise targets, the slow air that meets them, the parts that stop sound and shake, and the air balance of a sealed room.

What target are we trying to hit

Noise criteria (NC) curves limit the sound in each octave band, and a lower number is quieter. The general HVAC lesson HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings explains the curves and how to read a meter, so we do not repeat it. What changes in a studio is the number. The federal Whole Building Design Guide lists NC 30 to 35 for small private offices and NC 40 to 50 for computer rooms. A sound-meter maker's table of common NC levels lists a recording studio at NC 15 to 20 (cited as plain text). A trade-press article on a radio station renovation, with studios on the top floor, reports an acoustical requirement of NC 20 to NC 25. A Cornell facilities standard gives no number for audio and radio spaces; it marks the target as "to be determined" by the stakeholders with an acoustical consultant, and asks for RC Mark II to be measured and reported at commissioning. So the honest answer to "what is the NC?" is: read the drawings, because a consultant picked it. The quiet-hall version is in Theater and Auditorium HVAC.

Slow air is the first fix

The radio station article reports supply air at 250 to 300 fpm and return air at 300 to 360 fpm for its NC 20 to 25 spaces. Treat those as one project's numbers, not a code. For contrast, Cornell's standard says to keep duct velocity at 1,500 fpm or below in ordinary spaces, with transitions no steeper than a 15 degree included angle, and a New York City noise-control guidance sheet says to stay under 1,000 fpm at elbows and under 2,000 fpm in straight ducts. A very quiet booth sits well below both. A 1987 paper on airflow noise says noise climbs about 16 dB for each doubling of velocity, and that local velocity at a bend or fitting, not the average, sets the sound. Cornell also asks for turning vanes and 5 to 10 duct diameters between elbows, takeoffs and outlets. Duct sizing itself is covered in Duct Sizing Basics.

Lined duct, flex and silencers

Lined duct helps mostly at higher frequencies. A diffuser maker's acoustics guide says lined duct gives "significant attenuation of higher frequency sound," and its tables list 1 inch lining on 12 inch round spiral at 0.46 to 2.18 dB per foot across the bands it shows. Cornell discourages lining as the primary fix. The same maker guide calls flexible duct "used with moderation" an excellent attenuation element, with an 8 inch flex run 5 feet long listed at 5 to 21 dB depending on the band (AHRI 885 data in its table). Long flex also costs airflow when it sags or kinks; see Flex Duct Installation. The New York sheet adds that a flexible connector should not sit directly at a diffuser.

A silencer (sound trap) is the part for the deep part of the spectrum. Cornell caps silencer pressure drop at 0.25 in. wc including system effects; the New York sheet says not to exceed 0.3 in. wc and to watch self-generated noise. A consultant's page on soundproof openings (cited as plain text) points out that most HVAC fans have little spare pressure, so the silencer must be large with low head loss. The same page suggests balancing after install and judging quiet only then.

Floated rooms, isolators and flexible connections

The radio station studios are described as rooms within a room, with resiliently suspended ceilings under roof equipment, and with silencers where the ductwork meets the insulated shell. Cornell tells designers to isolate rotating equipment and to vibration-isolate ducts and pipes for the first 50 feet from connected equipment, and to detail supply and return so sound does not pass between spaces. A federal VA specification (published on WBDG) adds practical details: no metal-to-metal contact between fixed and floating parts, and connections to isolated equipment must allow at least the equipment's deflection. Its general equipment limit is 5 mm/s RMS vibration. See Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors for how to check deflection.

Sealed rooms still need air

A floated, gasketed room is nearly airtight on purpose. The consultant page says to treat each duct opening like a wall: the opening must stop sound while moving air. It also describes a two-stage arrangement that trades air with a neighboring conditioned room rather than ducting straight in. Our own point from the basic air balance: the air that goes in must come out. If the supply is 300 cfm and the return takes less, the difference needs its own quiet exit, often a transfer path with a sound trap, or the room pressurizes and pushes air through door seals, whistling and leaking sound. Outdoor air rates come from the code and the design; see Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions, not a design: 300 cfm supplied to the booth; two people at 250 Btu/h sensible each; 1,200 W of equipment running all session; 30 cfm of room air spilled to match outdoor air; a main duct velocity goal of 600 fpm. Effective free areas of the two diffusers are also assumed.

Step 1, small diffuser: effective free area 1.0 sq ft. 300 ÷ 1.0 = 300 fpm.

Step 2, large diffuser: effective free area 2.5 sq ft. 300 ÷ 2.5 = 120 fpm.

Step 3, compare: 300 ÷ 120 = 2.5 times the speed, which is 1.32 doublings (log base 2 of 2.5). 1.32 × 16 dB is about 21 dB. That is a rough feel, using the paper's rule for duct flow, not a diffuser rating; the maker's sound data for the real outlet decides. The 300 fpm case is already at the top of the radio station supply range.

Step 4, main duct: 300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 sq ft = 72 sq in. A round duct needs a diameter of √(4 × 0.5 ÷ π) = 0.80 ft, about 9.6 in. A 10 in round has 78.5 sq in (0.545 sq ft), so 300 ÷ 0.545 = 550 fpm.

Step 5, heat: 1,200 W × 3.412 = 4,094 Btu/h (NIST lists 1 Btu/h as 0.2930711 W). People: 2 × 250 = 500 Btu/h. Total 4,594 Btu/h. Using sensible heat Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT (our own factor), ΔT = 4,594 ÷ (1.08 × 300) = 14.2°F. With the room held at 75°F, the supply air is about 61°F, which is workable. If the gear doubled to 2,400 W, the load would be about 8,688 Btu/h and the same airflow would need a 26.8°F difference, so more air (more noise) or a machine room outside the booth would be the choice.

Step 6, quiet exit: 30 cfm ÷ an assumed 300 fpm = 0.10 sq ft, about 14 sq in of free area through the sound trap.

Field card: Studio and Booth HVAC

Slow air, trapped ducts, floated gear, balanced air.

  • Published targets differ: NC 15 to 20 (a sound-meter maker's table), NC 20 to 25 (one radio station project). A consultant sets the real number.
  • One project ran supply at 250 to 300 fpm and return at 300 to 360 fpm.
  • Silencer pressure drop: 0.25 in. wc (Cornell) or 0.3 in. wc (New York sheet), plus watch self noise.
  • Isolate ducts and pipes for the first 50 feet from isolated equipment (Cornell).
  • Air in must equal air out; give a sealed room a quiet exit.
  • Heat: watts × 3.412 = Btu/h; supply difference = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × cfm), our own method.
  • Balance and measure after install; do not promise an NC you did not measure.
