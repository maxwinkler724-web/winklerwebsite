Cooling Tower Legionella: CDC vs OSHA and Water Management Programs

Two U.S. agencies publish useful guidance on keeping Legionella out of cooling towers. They agree on the big ideas and differ on a few numbers. This lesson lays them side by side so you know what each one actually says.

Premium lesson. Planned for the premium tier; free to read while the site is in preview.

Why towers need attention

A cooling tower sprays warm water through moving air, and that makes a fine mist. If the water carries Legionella bacteria, people nearby can breathe it in. The bacteria do best in warm, stagnant water with sediment and slime. A tower can offer all of that, especially after sitting idle. For the everyday picture of how a tower works, see Cooling Tower Basics; for an awareness-level overview, see Cooling Tower Water Management and Legionella Awareness.

Where CDC and OSHA agree

  • Control starts with the water: keep the system clean, treated and moving, and keep written records.
  • Sediment, scale and slime protect bacteria and weaken disinfectants, so treating the whole system matters more than one chemical.
  • Stagnant spots, such as dead legs and idle basins, are trouble.

Where the numbers differ

Treat these as two agencies speaking in their own words, not as a conflict you must resolve yourself.

  • Growth range. CDC's cooling tower toolkit describes growth as best at roughly 77 to 113 F, and says to run towers as cold as possible, ideally below that. OSHA's page describes tower water as likely to fall in roughly 68 to 122 F, a range that supports growth. Read both as rough guides.
  • Cleaning frequency. CDC says to take the tower offline and clean and disinfect at least once a year, and also around startup, idle periods and shutdown, following the maker. OSHA's page says at least twice a year, usually before the cooling season and after fall shutdown. Many programs follow the twice-a-year pattern. Your own program and local rules decide.

Other points from the CDC toolkit

CDC also describes high-efficiency drift eliminators, siting a tower at least 25 feet from building air intakes, flushing dead legs and low-flow runs at least weekly, and a measurable disinfectant residual every day when an oxidizing disinfectant is used.

What OSHA adds

OSHA's page is guidance, not a standalone standard, but other OSHA rules can still apply to the work. It lists useful records: a description of the system and make-up water, written procedures, inspection and cleaning dates, and any test results. It cautions that dirty water with lots of dissolved solids makes biocides less effective, and that quaternary ammonium products may not kill Legionella living in slime on surfaces and can bother the lungs of some workers. Chemical figures on that page, such as chlorine levels, are examples. Your treatment supplier sets the real targets.

Protecting the people doing the work

OSHA's page describes chemical-appropriate protective gear for anyone handling biocides, and for tower cleaning a half-face respirator with N-100 cartridges. It says even voluntary respirator use brings a respiratory protection program with fit testing and medical clearance, that facial hair can break a mask seal, and that employers provide required protective equipment at no cost. Training should cover putting on, taking off and limits of the gear. If you are not trained and fitted, you are not the person to open a tower for cleaning.

What a water management program contains

ASHRAE Standard 188 is a voluntary standard for managing Legionella risk in building water systems. This summary comes from a public description by the Environmental Law Institute, not from the standard itself, which is paywalled. Buildings with cooling towers, spas or fountains need a program for those systems. The program has these parts:

  1. A designated team.
  2. System descriptions with flow diagrams and a look at where hazards could arise.
  3. Control measures, with limits for water conditions and where they apply.
  4. Monitoring.
  5. Corrective actions.
  6. Verification and validation that the program works.
  7. Documentation and communication.

Whether and how to test for Legionella is the owner's decision under the standard. A 2021 revision used firmer wording so codes could adopt it, but this lesson cannot tell you which places have done so.

State and local rules

Some states and cities require tower registration, inspection or cleaning, but which ones is not settled in the sources used here. Check your own jurisdiction.

New Jersey. A 2024 state law, P.L. 2024, c. 66, requires owners of covered buildings to have a written water management program. Any building with a cooling tower or evaporative condenser is covered, although the program may be limited to that device rather than the whole building. As we read the law, the program is due to be developed about 24 months after the September 2024 effective date (about September 2026) and put into use about 27 months after it (about December 2026). Once it is in place, a written notice saying so must be posted where occupants can see it. Penalties run up to $2,000 for a first violation and $5,000 for later ones, and more if someone is seriously hurt. Program content (such as following ASHRAE 188 or a similar standard) comes from secondary summaries and is not settled here, and no NJ cooling tower registry turned up. Dates and details can change, so check with the NJ Department of Health. Plain-English walk-through: NJ Cooling Tower Legionella Law (P.L. 2024, c. 66): Plain-English Guide.

What this lesson leaves out on purpose

Emergency disinfection after a suspected illness uses specific chemical doses and hold times. Those belong to a water-treatment professional working from ASHRAE Guideline 12, not to a lesson. If a tower smells, runs slimy or has been idle, tell the responsible person, do not open it up alone, and let the program handle the response.

Field card: Cooling Tower Legionella: CDC vs OSHA and Water Management Programs

Agencies differ on the rough numbers; your program and a treatment professional set the real ones.

  • Growth: CDC roughly 77-113 F, OSHA roughly 68-122 F. Keep water as cool as possible.
  • Cleaning: CDC at least annually; OSHA says twice a year. Follow your program.
  • Flush dead legs at least weekly; keep daily oxidizer residual (CDC).
  • Biocide and cleaning work needs chemical PPE and, where respirators are used, a fit-tested program (OSHA).
  • ASHRAE 188 program: team, system map, control limits, monitoring, corrective action, verification, records.
  • No shock-dose or hold-time recipes here: follow a water-treatment professional and ASHRAE Guideline 12.
