Erosion-Corrosion in Copper Hot-Water Piping: Pits, Velocity and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Copper pipe does not always die from rust or leaks at a bad joint. Sometimes the moving water itself wears the pipe through. This lesson explains erosion-corrosion in copper hydronic and domestic hot-water recirculation lines: what it is, where it shows up, why hot water and big pumps make it worse, what published velocity guidance says, and what a tech can do about it.

What erosion-corrosion is

Copper in potable or heating water normally builds a thin protective film on its inner wall. That film is what slows further attack. A university review of the research describes erosion-corrosion as a process where fast, turbulent water keeps stripping that film in one spot, so bare metal is exposed to the water over and over (university dissertation, 2018). It is not simple sandblasting of solid copper. It is a corrosion reaction that flow keeps restarting.

The same review points out that no single general theory explains it. Water chemistry, temperature, flow, and any particles carried in the water all matter, and failures have been reproduced in the lab in weeks when hard-water particles were added to fast flow at bends. Treat the velocity numbers below as guidance, not guarantees.

What the damage looks like

Leaks from this cause tend to show up as pinholes in areas with wavelet-type or horseshoe-shaped pits whose open ends face downstream (university dissertation, 2018). Picture hoof prints made by a horse walking upstream. The open end of the horseshoe points the way the water was going.

Where do they form? Anywhere the flow gets rough:

  • Just downstream of elbows and tees. An extension-service guide on well-water problems notes that leaks from excessive velocity are almost always in angle fittings, because water erodes the copper there (university extension).
  • Just past an unreamed cut end. A burr left inside a cut tube acts like a small obstruction. A university thesis on flow-induced copper failures says that if joints are not deburred, tiny differential-flow cells can form throughout a system, and it cites a figure of local velocity up to 300 ft/s just downstream of an obstruction (university thesis, 2009). The dissertation review adds that one empirical model flagged unreamed cut ends in hot, soft water as the worst case.
  • Near pumps, valves and reducers, wherever the stream is forced to change direction or squeeze down.

Why hot water and recirculation make it worse

Temperature. The extension guide says corrosion rate roughly triples or quadruples as water goes from 60 to 140 °F, and doubles for each 20 °F above 140 °F. That is a general corrosion statement, not an erosion-corrosion rule, but it shows why hot lines are the trouble spots. The dissertation review lists studies placing the worst erosion-corrosion near 60 to 77 °C (about 140 to 170 °F), depending on water hardness.

Continuous flow. The same review cites a 1972 study that found no erosion-corrosion in loops with intermittent flow (25 percent of the time or less) but severe damage under constant flow, which suggests the film takes time to wear away. It also says recirculation, higher storage temperatures and chlorine dosing used for Legionella control are likely to raise the risk. A pump that runs 24 hours a day gives the water 24 hours a day to scour.

Oversized or full-speed pumps. A national laboratory technology review of hot-water temperature maintenance warns that oversized pumps can induce high flow velocities that, over time, erode the pipe wall and cause pinhole leaks (national laboratory, 2021). It says the needed pump flow depends on the heat lost from the pipe and the temperature drop you will accept at the balancing valves, and that pump head comes from friction in the return piping. A pump picked for a long, leaky run and then installed on a short, well-insulated one moves far more water than it needs to.

What the published velocity guidance says

The dissertation review collects velocity limits from many documents in a table. Its preface sums up the spread: maximum velocity guidelines range from about 1.5 to 5 ft/s for copper hot-water systems. Examples from that table:

  • About 5 ft/s or less for water below 60 °C (140 °F), and about 3 to 4 ft/s or less above that.
  • 2 to 3 ft/s for hot water above 60 °C.
  • 2 ft/s or less for hot-water recirculation in one plumbing guide.
  • As low as about 1.5 ft/s for continuously circulating hot-water tubing in an older water-industry guide.

An earlier thesis from the same university group quotes design guidance of under 8 ft/s for cold water and under 5 ft/s for hot water (university thesis, 2009). The dissertation review also warns that damage can occur at much lower velocities when abrasive particles are in the water, and that the literature on code limits is inconsistent. So when a number is needed, we quote the range, then ask what the job's own documents or the pipe maker's literature require.

Worked example (our own): 1/2 inch type L at 4 gpm vs 2 gpm

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume 1/2 inch nominal type L copper with an inside diameter of about 0.545 in (an assumed catalog value; check the tube you have). Assume a hot-water recirculation branch is being compared at 4 gpm and at 2 gpm.

  1. Flow area = π/4 × (0.545 in)² = 0.7854 × 0.2970 = 0.2333 in². Divide by 144 to get square feet: 0.2333 / 144 = 0.00162 ft².
  2. Convert 4 gpm to cubic feet per second. One gallon is 0.13368 ft³. So 4 × 0.13368 = 0.5347 ft³/min, and 0.5347 / 60 = 0.00891 ft³/s.
  3. Velocity = flow / area = 0.00891 / 0.00162 = about 5.5 ft/s.
  4. At 2 gpm the flow is half, so the velocity is half: about 2.75 ft/s.

Cross-check with the common shortcut v (ft/s) = 0.4085 × gpm / d² (d in inches): 0.4085 × 4 / 0.2970 = 5.50 ft/s. It matches.

Comparing to the guidance range above. At 4 gpm, 5.5 ft/s is above even the most generous published figure of about 5 ft/s, and about double the 2 to 3 ft/s quoted for hot water above 60 °C. At 2 gpm, 2.75 ft/s falls inside that 2 to 3 ft/s band, but it is still above the 2 ft/s and 1.5 ft/s figures. Working backward, flow at each limit in this tube is gpm = ft/s × 0.727: about 3.6 gpm at 5 ft/s, 2.2 gpm at 3 ft/s, and 1.45 gpm at 2 ft/s. Halving the flow halves the velocity, so a pump that is turned down, or a balancing valve that is set, gives a direct and easy-to-calculate gain.

Fixes that work in the field

  • Ream and deburr every cut. The cited research ties unreamed ends and unremoved burrs to local high-velocity spots, so ream the inside of each cut end before assembly. See the lesson on soldering copper for joint prep.
  • Right-size the pump. Choose the circulator for the heat-loss flow and the real friction head, not for the biggest number on a catalog sheet (national laboratory, 2021).
  • Use balancing valves. They let each return branch get only the flow it needs, so one short loop does not hog the pump and run fast.
  • Lower the speed. A multi-speed or variable-speed circulator can be turned down. Because flow follows pump speed, a lower setting cuts velocity directly, as in the 4 gpm to 2 gpm comparison.
  • Do not run flow you do not need. The intermittent-flow finding above is one reason timers and on-demand controls help. See the recirculation pump lesson linked below.
  • Use long-radius turns and enough straight run where you can, so the water does not hit a sharp elbow at speed. This is our own practical advice from the geometry the research describes, not a published number.
  • Check the water. Hard-water particles, low pH and chlorine all made the damage worse in the university lab work (university dissertation, 2018).

Do not confuse it with other copper failures

Pinholes also come from galvanic corrosion at mismatched metals (see the dielectric union lesson) and from pitting caused by the water itself. Horseshoe pits that open downstream and sit next to fittings are the signature the research describes for flow damage. In our experience, pits scattered along a straight, calm run suggest a different cause. When in doubt, cut out a failed section and look at the inside wall.
