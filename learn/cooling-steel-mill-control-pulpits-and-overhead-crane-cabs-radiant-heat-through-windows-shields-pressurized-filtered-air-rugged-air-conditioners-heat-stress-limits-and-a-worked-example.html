Cooling Steel Mill Pulpits and Crane Cabs: Radiant Heat, Shields and Pressurized Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A control pulpit or crane cab in a steel mill sits near surfaces hundreds or thousands of degrees hotter than skin. The operator needs clear windows to see the work, which means the heat has a straight path in. This lesson covers what loads those small rooms, how shields, glass and pressurized filtered air help, what to look for in a rugged cab air conditioner, and the heat limits that tell you whether the cooling is enough. Related reading: radiant heat in foundries and melt shops and heat stress in attics and on roofs.

Why hot steel loads the window first

Hot surfaces send out infrared energy, and the amount climbs with the fourth power of absolute temperature. A university heat transfer lecture note gives the blackbody relation as emissive power equal to a constant times temperature to the fourth, with the constant at 0.1714 x 10-8 Btu/hr-ft2-R4, and lists window glass at a long-wave emissivity of roughly 0.9 to 0.95. So a slab at 1,800°F gives off about 44,700 Btu/hr per square foot as an ideal surface (our own arithmetic, shown below), while a pane at 100°F gives off only about 150 (same relation, emissivity 0.9). Air temperature in the bay barely matters to that exchange. The federal workplace safety heat controls page lists reflective shields to redirect radiant heat and insulation of hot surfaces, such as furnace walls, as engineering controls, and the same page says crane cabs and break rooms can be air-conditioned.

The technical manual chapter on heat stress from the same agency names foundries and boiler areas among places with radiant heat and says to use reflective shields to block it. A federal basic steel products hazards page lists heat stress as an additional concern for the industry, so the cab or pulpit is part of a larger heat program, not the whole of it.

Shields, glass and the window itself

  • Shield the line of sight, not the view. A reflective shield, panel or screen between the hot surface and the window blocks the straight path. The lecture note lists polished aluminum at an emissivity near 0.03, so a bright shield face returns most of the radiation. Leave a gap so air can move behind the shield and carry off the heat it does absorb.
  • Heat-reflecting glass. Coated or tinted glazing lowers how much radiation passes into the room. Ask the glazing maker for the transmitted and absorbed shares for the kind of radiation you have (a very hot source radiates at shorter wavelengths than a warm wall), and do not assume a sun rating applies.
  • Make the window smaller where the view allows. Every square foot of clear glass facing the source is a heat entry point.
  • Shielding is a control to design, not to improvise. Hot work and crane travel clearances matter, so involve the plant's safety people before anything is hung near moving loads.

Pressurized, filtered air against scale dust

Mill air carries fine dust and scale. Enclosed-cab studies from a federal health agency, done on mining equipment but the same idea, show the approach: bring outside air in through a filter, hold the cab at a slight positive pressure so leaks flow out, and seal the doors and openings. In two case studies the cabs reached about 0.01 inches of water gauge after sealing, and one reached roughly 0.07 to 0.12 inches after further sealing. The studies used a coarse pre-filter ahead of a finer final filter so the final filter loads more slowly, and they note that higher pressure requirements can increase filter loading and that placing the makeup air inlet away from dust sources extends the time between cleanings. A drill cab air conditioner in the study moved about 320 cfm and was rated 22,000 Btu/hr. A floor heater was found to stir up dust in one cab.

That has a cost: every cubic foot of outdoor air pulled in must be cooled. On a mill floor the intake air may itself be very hot, so the makeup air is often the largest load, as the example below shows.

Rugged air conditioners: what to check

These are our own field checks, not rules from a source. Read the nameplate or data sheet for the highest intake air temperature the unit is rated for, and compare it to the hottest air at the inlet, not the yard average. Look for vibration mounting, sealed coil and electrical sections, a filter you can reach from the floor, and a way to read filter loading. If the data sheet is silent on hot intake air, ask the maker before you quote the job.

Condensate and filter care

Our own service checklist: keep the condensate drain open and sloped to a safe drain, and check that the trap is full and not coated with scale dust, because a plugged drain on a pressurized cab can drip into the electrical or control area. Wash or replace pre-filters on a short, written interval and the final filter on a longer one, and record the pressure difference when you can. Check door seals and gaskets each visit, since a leak lets dust in and wastes the pressure.

Worker heat stress limits

The federal worker health criteria document on heat gives a worked case: for an acclimatized worker doing moderate work for a full hour, the recommended exposure limit corresponds to a wet-bulb globe temperature near 27.8°C (82°F); for an unacclimatized worker in the same case, it says metabolic heat measurement is required above 25°C (77°F). The federal technical manual gives indoor wet-bulb globe temperature as 0.7 times the natural wet-bulb reading plus 0.3 times the globe reading, and says to measure at least hourly during the hottest part of the shift. It also says acclimatization takes about 7 to 14 days with exposure increasing each day, and that re-acclimatization after a week or more away usually takes two or three days. It describes heat stroke as core temperature of 41°C (106°F) or more, reachable within 10 to 15 minutes. The controls page lists rest breaks in a cooler place, water, shorter shifts for new workers and a buddy system. An older federal health hazard evaluation at a steel plant noted high temperatures in crane cabs and recommended that operators get adequate time away from the cabs, and that crane operators spent less than four hours per day in them. A cab or pulpit that keeps the operator under these limits counts as a cool rest area; one that does not still needs the breaks.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed inputs. This covers only the window and the pressurization air; people, lights, electronics and walls are separate loads. Assumptions: window 4 ft x 5 ft = 20 ft2; steel surface 1,800°F (2,260 R) with emissivity 0.8; the steel fills only a small part of the window's view, a view factor of 0.02; 60 percent of what lands on the glass ends up as heat in the cab; glass U-factor 0.5 Btu/hr-ft2-°F; cab held at 75°F. Without a shield the outer glass is assumed to be at 140°F, with a shield the air behind it is 110°F. The shield is assumed to have an open path of 5 percent of the unshielded radiation around its edges, and its cab-side face at 200°F with emissivity 0.1. The glass outer surface is 100°F with emissivity 0.9. Pressurization air: 300 cfm at 110°F.

  1. Emitted flux. 0.1714 x 10-8 x 2,2604 = 44,700 Btu/hr-ft2; times 0.8 = 35,750.
  2. Reaching the glass. 35,750 x 0.02 = 715 Btu/hr-ft2; x 20 ft2 = 14,300 Btu/hr.
  3. Radiant heat into the cab, no shield. 14,300 x 0.6 = 8,580 Btu/hr.
  4. Conducted, no shield. 0.5 x 20 x (140 - 75) = 650 Btu/hr. Window total about 9,230 Btu/hr, which is 9,230 / 12,000 = 0.77 ton.
  5. With the shield, edge leak. 8,580 x 0.05 = 429 Btu/hr.
  6. With the shield, re-radiation from its hot face. Two-surface relation with emissivities 0.1 and 0.9: denominator 1/0.1 + 1/0.9 - 1 = 10.11. Flux = 0.1714 x 10-8 x (6604 - 5604) / 10.11 = (324.6 - 168.2) / 10.11 = 15.5 Btu/hr-ft2; x 20 = 309; x 0.6 = 186 Btu/hr.
  7. With the shield, conducted. 0.5 x 20 x (110 - 75) = 350 Btu/hr. Window total 429 + 186 + 350 = 965 Btu/hr = 0.08 ton.
  8. Pressurization air. Sensible heat = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference = 1.08 x 300 x (110 - 75) = 11,340 Btu/hr = 0.95 ton. (The 1.08 factor is air density times specific heat times 60, our own arithmetic.)
  9. Totals. No shield: 9,230 + 11,340 = 20,570 Btu/hr = 1.71 tons. With shield: 965 + 11,340 = 12,305 Btu/hr = 1.03 tons. The shield removes about 8,265 Btu/hr, or 0.69 ton, so the cab needs about 0.7 ton less cooling for the same window.

Check: 1 ton = 12,000 Btu/hr. The window heat falls by a factor of nearly ten with a shield, while the pressurization load, which depends on the hot outside air and the cfm you need to hold pressure, does not change. That is why a clean seal matters: the less leakage, the less air you need to hold pressure.

Field card: Cooling Steel Mill Pulpits and Crane Cabs

Block the radiation, seal the cab, then size the air conditioner.

  • Radiant heat goes through the window in a straight line; a bright shield stops it.
  • Heat rises with absolute temperature to the fourth power.
  • Filtered air at slight positive pressure keeps scale dust out; seal doors and gaps.
  • Pre-filter first, final filter second; check loading often.
  • Pressurization air from a hot bay can be the biggest load.
  • Check the unit's rated intake air temperature against the hottest air at the inlet.
  • Heat limits: about 82°F wet-bulb globe for acclimatized moderate work, 77°F for unacclimatized.
