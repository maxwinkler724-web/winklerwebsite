Setting Up a Sick Room at Home: Air Cleaner, Fans and Filters

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When someone at home has a cold, the flu or another respiratory illness, you cannot make the air germ-free, but you can lower how much of it the rest of the household breathes. This lesson walks through the simple pieces: one closed-off room, a portable HEPA air cleaner sized by its clean air delivery rate, fans, a window, the central system filter, comfortable humidity, and the one gadget to avoid.

The short version

  • Give the sick person one room, keep its door closed, and keep visits short.
  • Run a portable HEPA air cleaner in that room. Pick it by clean air delivery rate (CADR), not by marketing claims.
  • Bring in outdoor air when the weather allows, and use exhaust fans.
  • If you have a central system, run the fan and use the best pleated filter the system can handle.
  • Keep humidity comfortable, and do not use ozone generators.

Step 1: One room, door closed

The federal public health agency says that when you are home sick you should stay home and away from others, including people you live with, and wear a well-fitted mask around them. It also notes that extra precautions matter most when you do not have space to stay fully apart. A closed door is our own practical suggestion on top of that: it keeps the room's air from freely mixing with the rest of the house, so the air cleaner in the room has a fixed volume to work on. We did not find a federal page that states the door rule in so many words, so treat it as common sense, not a rule. If the sick person must leave the room, an older federal home-care page says they should wear a mask.

Pick a room near a bathroom if you can. Our own suggestion: keep the person's dishes, towels and tissues in or near the room, and wash hands after visits. Those habits matter as much as any air equipment.

Step 2: A portable HEPA air cleaner, sized by CADR

The federal public health agency recommends a portable high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) cleaner for cleaner indoor air. A university health and safety office makes the same point: a unit with a HEPA filter is good at catching small particles, and the prefilter protects the costlier HEPA filter.

CADR is the number to shop by. The federal environmental agency explains that the higher the CADR, the more particles a unit removes and the larger the area it can serve, and that the rating is usually measured at the unit's highest speed. The same guide says CADR describes particles only, so it says nothing about gases or odors, and its sizing chart assumes an 8 foot ceiling. With taller ceilings, choose a higher CADR.

The agency's indoor air filtration fact sheet gives a quick rule: choose a unit whose CADR for smoke is at least two-thirds of the room's area in square feet. For a 144 square foot room, that is 96 cfm. Place the unit where air can move freely around it, not tucked behind a couch, and run it all day, not just at night.

Why the rule works

Our own arithmetic shows what the two-thirds rule means. With an 8 foot ceiling, the room volume is 8 times the floor area. Two-thirds of the area as cfm, times 60, divided by 8 times the area, comes out to exactly 5 air changes per hour, whatever the floor area is. The federal public health agency's cleaner-air page names a target of 5 or more air changes per hour of clean air, in advice written for organizations. We use it only as a yardstick, not as a home requirement.

Step 3: Fresh air, windows and exhaust fans

The same federal agency says to bring as much fresh air into the home as possible by opening doors and windows or using exhaust fans, and to turn on the bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans. An older federal home-care page also says to open a window in the sick room if possible. In practice, crack the sick room window when the weather is mild and the outdoor air is clean, and run the bathroom fan while the sick person uses the bathroom and for a while after. See the related lesson on bathroom exhaust fans for how they are sized and ducted. Skip the window on smoky, very hot or very cold days when it would make the house unsafe or uncomfortable, and use the air cleaner instead.

Step 4: The central system, fan and filter

The federal public health agency suggests setting the thermostat fan to On instead of Auto so air keeps moving through the filter, using a pleated filter, and changing it every three months or as the maker directs. The federal environmental agency's fact sheet goes further and says to choose a filter rated 13 or higher on the standard filter-efficiency scale, and to run the system fan as often as you can. Our caution: a thicker, higher-rated filter can restrict airflow, and not every furnace or air handler can handle it. Check the nameplate and the maker's instructions, or ask a technician, before you step up. Our lesson on filter size and fit covers the physical side.

Step 5: Comfortable humidity

The federal environmental agency says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent. Too dry can irritate a sore throat, and too damp feeds mold. If you use a humidifier, the agency says to clean it according to the maker's instructions, change the water as directed, and use distilled water to limit mineral dust in the air. Do not leave a humidifier running in a closed room until the walls or windows are wet.

What not to do: ozone generators

The federal environmental agency says ozone generators sold as air cleaners deliberately make ozone, a lung irritant, and that no federal agency has approved them for occupied spaces. Its page adds that at safe levels ozone does little to remove contaminants, does not remove particles, and does not effectively remove viruses, bacteria or mold, and that its reactions can create harmful byproducts. The agency also says some electronic air cleaners can produce ozone, while mechanical filters do not. A university guide tells its units to avoid portable cleaners that produce ozone. A sick person with sore airways is the worst person to put next to one. For more on electronic cleaners, see our related lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with stated assumptions. Assume a 12 ft by 12 ft bedroom with an 8 ft ceiling, a portable HEPA air cleaner rated 200 cfm CADR (assumed to be the smoke rating, run at its highest speed), a closed door, and air that mixes well in the room.

  1. Floor area = 12 x 12 = 144 sq ft.
  2. Room volume = 144 x 8 = 1,152 cu ft.
  3. Air changes per hour from the cleaner = CADR x 60 / volume = 200 x 60 / 1,152 = 12,000 / 1,152 = 10.4 per hour. That is about one full pass of the room's air every 5.8 minutes (60 / 10.4).
  4. Two-thirds rule from the federal fact sheet: 144 x 2/3 = 96 cfm minimum. Our 200 cfm unit is about 2.1 times that (200 / 96 = 2.08).
  5. Check against the yardstick: 5 air changes per hour needs 5 x 1,152 / 60 = 96 cfm. The same number, as expected from the arithmetic above.

Result: the unit has about double the minimum. In real rooms, furniture, door leaks and where the person sits make the true benefit lower than 10.4, and a quieter, lower speed gives a lower CADR. With a 10 ft ceiling the volume is 1,440 cu ft, so the same 96 cfm unit would give 96 x 60 / 1,440 = 4.0 per hour, which shows why taller rooms need more CADR.

Field card: Sick room at home

  • One room, door closed, short visits, mask when leaving the room.
  • HEPA portable cleaner, CADR (smoke) at least two-thirds of the floor area in sq ft.
  • Air changes per hour = CADR x 60 / room volume in cu ft.
  • Taller than 8 ft ceilings: go up in CADR.
  • Crack a window if the weather allows; run the bathroom fan.
  • Central system: fan On, pleated filter rated 13 or higher only if the equipment can handle it.
  • Humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.
  • No ozone generators.
