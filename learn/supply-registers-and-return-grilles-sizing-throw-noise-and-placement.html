Supply Registers and Return Grilles: Sizing, Throw, Noise and Placement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The duct can be perfect and the room can still be loud, drafty or stuffy because of the last piece of metal on the end. This lesson shows how to read a maker's performance table and answer the question "how do I size a supply register?" with real numbers, then checks a return grille the same way.

Grille, register, diffuser

Trade writers use these words in a fairly consistent way. Green Building Advisor, in a series on the topic, describes a grille as a fitting with no adjustable damper, usually used over return openings. A register has a movable damper to adjust airflow. A diffuser sits in a ceiling and uses angled vanes to spread air, and round and four-way styles throw air in several directions. Floor units are heavier cast or welded pieces, while wall and ceiling units are commonly lighter stamped steel. Filter grilles are hinged so the filter can be swapped. ACCA describes its Manual T as the standard for selecting, sizing and locating supply outlets and return inlets for homes and small buildings, including floor, wall and ceiling positions.

What the catalog columns mean

Each maker prints a table for each size. The headings differ a little, but the ideas are the same.

  • CFM and core velocity. Hart & Cooley's engineering data page gives the relationship as CFM = Ak (net area, square feet) × velocity (fpm). Price lists the same idea as core area and core velocity.
  • Pressure drop. The table gives total pressure in inches of water. Hart & Cooley notes that it rises as velocity rises, and Price's table shows it climbing from 0.009 in. w.g. at 300 fpm to 0.102 at 1,000 fpm for a straight-blade setting.
  • Throw. This is how far the air travels before it slows to a stated speed. Price prints three distances, for 150, 100 and 50 fpm terminal velocity.
  • NC (noise criteria). Price bases NC on a room absorption of 10 dB with one outlet, and prints a dash when NC is below 15. Titus's sheet for its 300 and 350 series uses the same 10 dB room basis and tests to ANSI/ASHRAE 70.

Why velocity drives noise

Hart & Cooley says plainly that a higher face velocity makes more noise, and its general guidance for supply outlets is roughly 500 to 800 fpm, with 700 a common target. The Titus sheet shows how quickly it climbs: a 14-inch neck shows no NC rating (below 20) at 1,000 fpm, then 22 at 1,200 and 25 at 1,300. Price's notes add that turning the blades matters too: 22.5 degrees of deflection adds 4 NC points and 45 degrees adds 12, and the pressure drop climbs sharply as well. Many bedrooms want a quiet outlet, so the cure is a bigger outlet, not a more aggressive one.

Free area versus nominal size

A "12 x 6" register is not 72 square inches of open hole. Bars, louvers and the damper take space. Energy Vanguard notes that net free area is often about 70 percent or less of the total area and says to check the maker's data. Green Building Advisor gives 70 to 90 percent for metal grilles. In the Price table used below, the 12 x 6 has a core area of 0.39 sq ft against a nominal 0.50 sq ft (72 ÷ 144), about 78 percent. Face velocity over the nominal size therefore reads lower than the velocity the air really has through the openings. Always say which one you mean.

Worked example: pick a register for 155 CFM

A bedroom branch is designed for 155 CFM (hypothetical). We use Price's 540/640 residential supply grille catalog, straight blades (0 degrees). The table gives core area, CFM, NC and throw at each core velocity.

Option A, 12 x 6 (core area 0.39 sq ft).

  • Core velocity = 155 ÷ 0.39 = about 397, so the 400 fpm column, which lists 155 CFM.
  • Face velocity over the nominal size = 155 ÷ 0.50 = 310 fpm.
  • Total pressure at 400 fpm = 0.016 in. w.g. NC = below 15 (dash).
  • Throw (150-100-50 fpm) = 9-13-23 ft.

Option B, 10 x 4 (core area 0.22 sq ft).

  • Core velocity = 155 ÷ 0.22 = about 705, so the 700 fpm column, which lists 155 CFM.
  • Total pressure at 700 fpm = 0.050 in. w.g. NC = below 15.
  • Throw = 11-16-23 ft.

Both are silent on the NC scale as printed, but option B uses over three times the pressure (0.050 against 0.016) and, at 700 fpm, sits inside Hart & Cooley's 500 to 800 range, while option A sits below it. If the blades are turned to 22.5 degrees, Price's table lists 0.091 in. w.g. at 700 fpm and its note adds 4 NC points. For a 12-foot-deep bedroom, option A's 13 ft at 100 fpm is enough and it costs the duct system less pressure, so it is the pick. For a longer room, or a high-wall outlet that must reach across it, B's longer 100 fpm throw may win. Throw figures are lab values for isothermal air, so treat them as a guide. Do not oversize either: a DOE case study shows a 4 x 8 outlet sized for 110 CFM at 500 to 700 fpm falling below the maker's recommended range at 55 CFM.

Total pressure drop is a cost against the fan: see Duct Sizing Basics for how the friction budget is spent, and Measuring Airflow for confirming the CFM at the register.

Return grille check: CFM ÷ free area = fpm

Returns are chosen for low velocity, not throw. Green Building Advisor's author typically uses a maximum of 400 fpm for a non-filter return, and warns that the plain area formula is only a rough guide: use the maker's table. Take a system that moves 800 CFM (an assumed figure for this example) (a different system from the one in Not Enough Return Air).

  1. Open area needed = 800 ÷ 400 = 2.0 sq ft = 288 sq in.
  2. Try a 20 x 20 grille, 70 percent open: 400 × 0.70 = 280 sq in = 1.94 sq ft. Velocity = 800 ÷ 1.94 = about 411 fpm. Slightly over.
  3. Try a 24 x 20 grille: 480 × 0.70 = 336 sq in = 2.33 sq ft. Velocity = 800 ÷ 2.33 = about 343 fpm. Passes.

The 24 x 20 face-velocity figure over the whole face is 800 ÷ 3.33 = 240 fpm, which looks very comfortable and shows why the basis matters. Green Building Advisor also warns that decorative covers can cut a return's performance. A filter grille adds the filter's own resistance, so give it extra area and see Furnace Filter Size and One-Inch Filters vs Four-Inch Media Cabinets.

Placement

  • Cover the load. Energy Vanguard's summary of Manual T says supply air can be directed across windows to reduce condensation, and warns against aiming air at occupants such as a person at a desk. Long narrow rooms need good throw, and short wide rooms need more spread.
  • High-wall registers blow across the ceiling and keep fast air out of the occupied zone, according to a DOE Building America figure caption in Green Building Advisor. The same article says better windows and tighter envelopes give more freedom in outlet location.
  • Avoid short circuits. Green Building Advisor says a supply directly across from a return invites air to go straight back without mixing.
  • Do not rely on habit. The series observes that outlet placement is often decided by convenience, though it affects comfort, efficiency and noise.

Why closing registers is a poor fix

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Walker, 2003) combined lab measurements and modeling and found that closing registers raised energy use in a typical California house across a range of climates and leakage levels, because duct leakage rose. It also says not to close more than 60 percent of registers, because the added resistance can trip a furnace high-limit or freeze a cooling coil. Closing registers nearest the air handler raised pressure and leakage across the whole system. A DOE case study measured the effect of a 50 percent restriction on one register: duct pressure rose by 13 Pa at 100 CFM. Our own advice: if one room is too hot, balance with a proper design or damper adjustment and a test, not by shutting others. See Upstairs Too Hot and Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Field card: Supply Registers and Return Grilles

Size by core area, check velocity, throw and NC in the maker's table.

  • CFM = net area (sq ft) × velocity (fpm). Say whether the area is nominal or core.
  • Higher velocity means more noise and more pressure drop.
  • Example: 155 CFM, 12 x 6 at 400 fpm, 0.016 in. w.g., throw 13 ft at 100 fpm.
  • Return check: 800 CFM ÷ (480 sq in × 0.70 ÷ 144 = 2.33 sq ft) = about 343 fpm.
  • Do not close registers to rebalance; leakage and pressure rise.
