Process Analyzer Shelters: Purge, Gas Trips, Cooling and Alarms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Out in a refinery or chemical plant, small buildings the size of a storage shed hold the instruments that measure what is flowing in the pipes. They are not occupied, but they still need steady temperature, clean air and a way to tell the control room when something is wrong. This lesson explains why, and walks through a sizing example of our own.

Why analyzers want steady temperature

An analyzer is a measuring instrument with a sample pump, detectors, heated parts and electronics. Many are calibrated at one room temperature and drift when the room swings. The electronics also age faster when hot. Cold is a problem too, because a sample that cools below its own condensing point can drop liquid inside the lines and give a false reading. So the job of the shelter air conditioning and heating is to hold a narrow band, usually near a normal room temperature, around the clock. The exact band comes from the instrument maker's documents for each analyzer, so read those before you pick a setpoint.

Why the building is purged or pressurized

These shelters often sit in an area where a flammable gas or vapor could be present. The federal worker safety rule for hazardous (classified) locations says equipment must be intrinsically safe, approved for the location, or safe for the location, and that approval covers the specific gas or vapor present, not just the class of area. The same rule permits purged and pressurized protection where the equipment is approved for it. In plain terms, a purged shelter is fed clean air from a safe location so the inside stays at a slightly higher pressure than outside. Leaks then flow out of the building instead of in. Before power is turned on, the system flushes the volume with several air changes so any gas that crept in is cleared. The required number of volumes, the pressure difference and the alarm logic come from the approved purge system's instructions and the model code, not from a rule of thumb on this page.

For you as a mechanical tech, the practical points are these: the intake for purge air must stay in the safe location and must be kept clear of leaves, snow and insects; ducts must be tight; doors must close and latch; and a door left open or a plugged filter will drop the pressure and may trip the shelter off. Do not bypass a purge alarm or pressure switch to keep a unit running.

Gas detectors that trip fans or power

Many shelters add a flammable gas detector near the intake or inside. The usual idea is a staged response: a low alarm calls attention, and a higher alarm shuts down the fresh air path, stops fans or removes power to non-rated equipment. The settings belong to the plant's engineering team. An investigation report from the federal chemical safety board on a toxic release at a chemical plant is a reminder of why alarms matter: detectors alarmed only at a high level, the alarms showed up on a control board, operators passed them on by radio, and no alarm sounded in the building where field workers were. The report also says the building ventilation had been designed around flammable limits, not toxic exposure, and that fans had a work order open for about a month. Tripping equipment is only one part. People need to know about it, and the fans need to be maintained.

Heaters and wall AC units in classified areas

Whatever sits in or on a classified shelter must be approved for that location and its temperature marking must stay below the ignition temperature of the gas, according to the same federal rule. General-purpose equipment is accepted in a Division 2 location only if the employer can show it is not an ignition source in normal operation. That is why a hardware-store window unit does not go on these walls. Wall units are usually located so the part that touches the classified air is rated, or the unit sits outside the classified boundary, and the plant's electrical engineer decides which. Replacement parts must keep that rating. Never swap a heater or a wall unit with a look-alike.

Condensation on sample lines

Water vapor condenses on any surface at or below the dew point of the air around it. An extension fact sheet on moisture in food facilities puts it simply: keep the surface above the dew point, and if insulation alone is not enough, warm it. In an analyzer house, a cold sample line or a cooled coil running through warm, humid room air will sweat, and the water can drip into electronics or corrode fittings. Heated sample lines, insulation with a sealed vapor skin and lower room humidity all help. Our own calculation with a common dew-point approximation: air at 75°F and 60% relative humidity has a dew point of about 60°F, so any line colder than that will sweat. See Heat Trace Cable on Pipes for how heat trace is sized and protected.

Alarm points back to the control room

The shelter is unattended, so the control room is its set of eyes. Typical points are shelter high temperature, low temperature, loss of purge pressure or flow, a purge-mode fault, gas detector alarm, and cooling failure. The tech should know which points are hardwired and which come over a network, and who to call when one trips. Compare the alarm and lead-lag ideas in Telecom Shelters and Remote Equipment Huts, and the occupied-building side in Plant Control Room HVAC.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own teaching example with assumed numbers. It is not a design, and a real shelter is sized by the engineer of record using the instrument and purge-system documents.

Assumptions. An insulated shelter 12 ft by 8 ft with an 8 ft ceiling, so 12 x 8 x 8 = 768 ft³. Four analyzers and their electronics draw 1,600 W in total. Sample system heaters inside the shelter draw 600 W. The envelope (roof and walls) is 96 + 320 = 416 ft² with an assumed U-value of 0.06 Btu/h·ft²·°F and an assumed effective temperature difference of 35°F including sun. Purge air is delivered at 6 air changes per hour, from outdoor-temperature air at 95°F, with the shelter held at 78°F. Wall AC units deliver 85% of nameplate at the hot outdoor condition. We take a Btu/h per watt factor of 3.41, the value used in the university notes cited below.

  1. Analyzers and electronics: 1,600 W x 3.41 = 5,456 Btu/h.
  2. Sample heaters: 600 W x 3.41 = 2,046 Btu/h.
  3. Envelope and sun: 0.06 x 416 x 35 = 874 Btu/h.
  4. Subtotal, no purge air: 5,456 + 2,046 + 874 = 8,376 Btu/h.
  5. Purge airflow: 768 ft³ x 6 changes / 60 min = 76.8 cfm.
  6. Purge air heat: 1.08 x 76.8 x (95 − 78) = 1,410 Btu/h. The 1.08 comes from 0.244 x 60 / 13.5, as the university notes show.
  7. Total: 8,376 + 1,410 = 9,786 Btu/h. Add a 10% allowance: 10,765 Btu/h.
  8. Nameplate needed at 85% delivery: 10,765 / 0.85 = 12,665 Btu/h. A 12,000 Btu/h unit is a little short; an 18,000 Btu/h unit delivers 18,000 x 0.85 = 15,300 Btu/h, which covers the load.

Check. Without the purge air, 8,376 x 1.10 / 0.85 = 10,840, so a 12,000 Btu/h unit would pass. The purge air alone turned a pass into a fail, which is why purge supply conditions matter. If the purge pre-cools its air at the safe location, the shelter unit can stay smaller. Pre-start purge time at this airflow, for an assumed 4 volume changes: 768 x 4 / 76.8 = 40 minutes. Real systems can need more or fewer volumes; use the approved system's instructions. Also note a dry-bulb example only: a humid site adds latent load we did not count.

Field card: Process Analyzer Shelters

Steady air, clean purge, rated parts, alarms that someone hears.

  • Hold the temperature band the instrument maker states.
  • Purge air comes from a safe location; keep the intake and filters clear.
  • Do not bypass purge pressure or flow alarms.
  • Heaters, wall units and parts must be approved for the location and kept the same.
  • Keep surfaces above the room dew point so sample lines do not sweat.
  • Sensible load: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
  • Walk the alarm list with the control room after any work.
