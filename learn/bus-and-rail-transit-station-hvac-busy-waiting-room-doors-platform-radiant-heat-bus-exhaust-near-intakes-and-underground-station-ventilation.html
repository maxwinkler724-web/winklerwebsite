Bus and Rail Transit Station HVAC: Busy Doors, Platform Heat and Bus Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A transit station looks like a small building, but it behaves like several different spaces: a waiting room whose doors never stay shut, an open platform where heating air is pointless, a bus bay full of diesel exhaust, and sometimes an underground box that depends on fans to stay safe. This lesson walks through each one in plain terms.

Check your local rules

Check the authority having jurisdiction. Transit stations are built to model codes, local amendments and the owner's own design criteria. This page is general education built from federal and university-level sources. Confirm requirements with the project engineer and the local code official before you act on them.

The waiting room: doors that are always moving

People come and go all day, and every time a door opens, outdoor air is pulled or pushed in. A lab study for the U.S. Department of Energy (Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, report PNNL-20026) put numbers on this for several building types. It assumed door-opening rates at peak hour from 9 per hour for a small office to 153 per hour for standalone retail and 1,041 for a large secondary building, and it estimated off-peak traffic at about one tenth of peak for most types. A busy station waiting room is closer to the retail end of that range or beyond it, so the door is often the biggest single leak in the envelope.

The same report compared buildings with and without a vestibule, a small two-door airlock. In its Table 6, peak infiltration fell from 275 to 162 cfm for the small office and from 1,913 to 1,237 cfm for the quick-service restaurant. By our own arithmetic on those figures, that is about a 41 percent and a 35 percent cut. The report also notes that the larger energy savings are expected in colder climates. For a station, the lesson is practical: if the street door opens straight into the seating area, ask whether a vestibule, an air curtain or a revolving door can be added, and size the heating for the leak you cannot remove.

Worked example (our own): door infiltration and a vestibule

The inputs below are our own assumptions for teaching. They are not measured station data.

  • Assumed airflow per door opening: 30 cubic feet (our assumption; the PNNL report does not give a per-opening value, and its own figures work out to a smaller number for a restaurant door).
  • Assumed openings: 120 per hour at rush hour.
  • Inside 70 F, outside 20 F, so the temperature difference is 50 F (assumed).
  • Assumed vestibule effect: 35 percent less infiltration, within the 28 to 41 percent spread we computed from the PNNL Table 6 examples.

Step 1, average airflow. 120 openings per hour x 30 cubic feet = 3,600 cubic feet per hour. Divide by 60 minutes: 60 cfm.

Step 2, sensible heat loss. Use the common sensible-heat relation, Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference. That is 1.08 x 60 x 50 = 3,240 Btu/h.

Step 3, with a vestibule. 3,240 x 0.35 = 1,134 Btu/h saved. The remaining loss is 3,240 - 1,134 = 2,106 Btu/h.

Step 4, over a day. If rush-hour traffic lasted 16 hours (an upper-bound assumption), the saving would be 1,134 x 16 = 18,144 Btu, or roughly 0.18 therm of heat. The number is small for one door. The point is that it repeats every day, on every door, and it also removes the cold draft that makes riders sit near the heater.

Real stations also lose latent heat (moisture) in summer, and the numbers swing with wind and stack effect. Treat this as a feel for scale, not a design calculation.

Open platforms and shelters: heat people, not air

Heating the air on an open platform is mostly a way to warm the sky. Radiation moves heat through space from a warm surface to cooler objects, while convection depends on air moving (Department of Energy building science education pages). That is why electric or gas radiant heaters are mounted overhead at platforms and shelters: they warm riders and the ground directly. Our own practical notes: aim them at the waiting zone, keep clearance to combustibles and to shelter roofs as the maker's instructions say, and control them with a timer, occupancy or a train-arrival signal so they do not run all night. For gas-fired tube heaters, see the related lesson on clearances and venting below.

Bus bays: keep diesel exhaust away from the intake

An EPA fact sheet on diesel exhaust and bus idling says exposure can cause lung damage and respiratory problems and that exhaust can worsen asthma and allergies. It recommends shutting buses off as soon as they reach the loading area and limiting warm-up to the maker's recommendation, generally 3 to 5 minutes. A bus burns about half a gallon of diesel per hour of idling, which is also money going out the tailpipe.

NIOSH treats whole diesel exhaust as a potential occupational carcinogen and urges lowering exposure, but it publishes no exposure limit for it outside mining. Its method chapter for diesel particulate monitoring uses elemental carbon as the yardstick. NIOSH also lists carbon monoxide hazards in semi-enclosed spaces and gives a 200 ppm ceiling and an immediately dangerous value of 1,200 ppm. A covered bus bay attached to a waiting room is exactly that kind of semi-enclosed space.

For the HVAC design, the EPA indoor air design guidance for HVAC says intakes should not be within 25 feet of potential contaminant sources, and it names bus loading areas, loading docks and generator exhausts among them. It also says the separation may need to grow for large sources or prevailing winds, that intake bottoms should be at least 8 inches above horizontal surfaces, and that intake screens must be accessible for inspection and cleaning. That guidance is written for a different building type; the principle carries over. Put intakes high, upwind if possible, and never in the same pocket as the bay. See the lessons on intake separation and bus garages below.

Underground stations: fans are life safety

Below grade, ventilation does two jobs: ordinary air exchange and heat removal, and emergency smoke control. Smoke control for fixed-guideway systems is governed by NFPA 130, which we name here in plain text only. It sets the emergency ventilation approach for stations and tunnels; the engineer of record designs to it and the local authority enforces it.

The federal record shows why maintenance matters. The FTA research report on rail tunnel standards (FTA Report No. 0231) describes the January 2015 smoke event at a Washington, D.C. station: fans did not start until 10 minutes after the train stopped, only 2 of 4 were working, and the NTSB cited ineffective inspection and maintenance. The same report found inspection frequencies across agencies ranging from one week to six years. A separate FTA audit letter required each state safety oversight agency to review tunnel ventilation inspections and to check whether maintenance and emergency procedures match industry standards such as NFPA 130. For a technician, the takeaway is simple: emergency fans, dampers and their controls are not ordinary comfort equipment, and their test records matter.

Heat from trains and equipment rooms

Trains, brake resistors, lighting, escalators, signal rooms and electrical gear all put heat into a station. We did not find a single federal figure we can quote for station heat gain, so we do not give one. In practice, equipment rooms (signal, communications, electrical) usually need cooling all year, even in winter, and often have their own split or ducted systems. Watch for equipment rooms on the same air path as the public areas: that is a way for heat and smoke to travel.

Ticket booths and staff rooms

A booth is a small, glass-heavy, occupied box beside a draft. Small units work if they are sized for solar gain and the door and window leak, not just floor area. If a booth is inside a conditioned waiting room, a small supply and return from the main system may be simpler than a separate unit. For the same small-space ideas, including window condensation and exhaust-fume intake, see the booth lesson linked below. Staff rooms need ventilation like any other occupied room, plus a place for condensate to drain that will not freeze outdoors.

Vandal-resistant equipment and 24-hour maintenance

These are our own field notes, not sourced figures. In public areas, use tamper-resistant fasteners, heavy-gauge grilles with small openings that keep out trash and prying hands, controls behind locked covers, and condensing units or heaters mounted out of reach or inside locked cages. Run condensate and refrigerant lines inside protected chases. A site that never closes has no quiet window, so plan work around ridership: schedule filter changes and heater checks in the overnight low, use lockout and tagout, and mark safe paths away from trains and buses. Keep spare belts, filters and controls on site, and keep a log so the next shift can see what was left open. Intake screens and bay-side filters need more frequent looks than the rest of the building, since bus soot loads them faster.

Field card: Bus and Rail Transit Station HVAC

Heat people on the platform, seal the doors, and keep the intake away from the bus.

  • Door infiltration is often the biggest waiting room load; a vestibule cut it roughly 28 to 41 percent in the federal lab examples.
  • Sensible heat: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
  • Platforms: radiant heat warms riders, not the air.
  • EPA guidance: intakes at least 25 feet from bus loading areas and other contaminant sources.
  • Bus idle: shut off at the bay, warm-up generally 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Diesel exhaust: NIOSH treats it as a potential carcinogen; keep it out of intakes.
  • Underground: emergency ventilation follows NFPA 130; test the fans and keep the records.
  • 24-hour sites: plan work around ridership, lock out, log what you did.
