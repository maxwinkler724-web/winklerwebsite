Filter Driers and Sight Glasses: What They Tell You

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two small parts on the liquid line say a lot about the health of a sealed system. The filter drier cleans the refrigerant, and the sight glass shows what is flowing past it. Both are easy to misread, so this lesson covers what each one really tells you.

What a liquid-line filter drier does

A filter drier is a shell holding a porous core. Sporlan describes its Catch-All driers as using a blend of desiccants to pull out moisture, and its reversible heat pump driers as blending desiccants for water capacity and acid removal. Danfoss explains that its HFC cores are all molecular sieve (which holds water), while its older-refrigerant cores add activated aluminium oxide, which holds both water and acid. The core and mesh also act as a strainer: Danfoss says they collect particles larger than 25 microns, and Sporlan lists 25 microns for its bi-flow driers.

Why care? Water and acid corrode and sludge the inside of a system. The lesson on compressor failure modes lists contamination as a major cause, and a drier is the cheap part that guards against it. The drier sits in the liquid line, ahead of the metering device, so dirt and water are caught before they can plug a TXV or piston. Sporlan's guidance is to put it just ahead of other liquid line controls, and Danfoss says to mount it so flow matches the arrow on the label.

Bi-flow driers on heat pumps

On a heat pump the liquid line carries refrigerant in both directions, depending on heating or cooling mode. A bi-flow drier works either way. Sporlan says its reversible heat pump driers operate in either direction with low pressure drop and have check valves built into the shell, so external check valves are not needed for them. Its literature also notes that when the flow reverses, dirt already collected stays in the drier. That is the point: the debris should stay trapped, not get washed back out.

Suction-line driers after a burnout

A burned compressor motor leaves acid and sludge through the whole circuit. Sporlan says its suction line filter-driers are designed for clean-up after a burnout, with a charcoal-style core, and that the system can run during clean-up. Emerson describes its suction line driers as suited to clean-up after a burnout or major work. Sporlan's best-practice sheet calls for two parts: an oversized liquid line drier to dry the system, and a properly sized suction line drier to catch acid and debris. Keep changing both until pressure drop stops climbing and the acid test passes. Then put a standard-size liquid drier back and remove or replace the suction drier, a step the sheet says is often overlooked.

Emerson's instructions add specifics: mount the suction drier close to the compressor inlet with the arrow toward the compressor, write down the starting pressure drop, and watch it over the first 4 hours. Test the oil for acid after 48 hours, and if acid shows, replace both driers. Check again after about 2 weeks. A plugged suction drier restricts what the compressor draws through, which is why its pressure drop is watched.

Judging a restricted drier

A drier plugs slowly, so a bad one rarely announces itself. Pressure drop is the cleaner test. Sporlan's best-practice sheet says to replace a Catch-All when its pressure drop exceeds 5 psi or when liquid subcooling is lost. The ACHR News writer John Tomczyk, a trade-press source and not a manufacturer, says more than 2 psi measured with the same gauge means a drier has started to restrict. Use the maker's number for the drier in front of you and treat the 2 psi figure as an early warning.

Temperature is a weaker test. Tomczyk points out that people can only feel a difference of more than about 10°F, and that an R-134a drier at 110°F condensing would need roughly 20 psi of drop to show 10°F. A restriction can be real and still feel the same on both ends. We did not find a manufacturer-stated allowable temperature drop, so this lesson does not give one. A drier that feels cool or cold is clearly restricted, but a normal-feeling one is not cleared.

Worked example: is this drier restricted?

Put a gauge on the service tap before the drier and read it. Then move the same gauge to the tap after the drier. One gauge keeps its error out of the answer.

  1. Inlet side reads 240 psig.
  2. Outlet side reads 232 psig.
  3. Pressure drop = 240 − 232 = 8 psi.
  4. Compare with Sporlan's 5 psi replacement point: 8 is greater than 5, so replace it.
  5. Check the feel with Tomczyk's rule of thumb, about 20 psi per 10°F on R-134a: 8 ÷ 20 × 10 = 4°F. You could not feel that by hand, which is why the gauges find it and your fingers would not.

That last step is only an estimate; real values depend on the refrigerant and temperatures.

When to replace it

Sporlan says to replace the filter drier every time a system is opened for service or repair. Danfoss lists these reasons: after a major component such as the compressor is changed, each time the system is otherwise opened, when the indicator turns yellow, and when pressure drop is too high. It also says never to re-use a used drier, because it can release moisture, especially if heated. Sporlan notes that its flow ratings assume a clean system, and warns that liquid trapped in a drier between two closed hand valves can build dangerous hydraulic pressure. Recover refrigerant and follow your procedures first; see recovery step by step. After the new drier is in, pull a proper vacuum; see evacuation basics.

Sight glass and the moisture indicator

A sight glass lets you see liquid flowing in the line, and many include a chemical moisture indicator. Sporlan's See-All page says a dark green color means the refrigerant is dry and yellow means wet, with the color change set to moisture levels generally accepted as the safe operating range. Danfoss says the same for green and yellow, and adds that yellow after the drier means moisture is too high ahead of the expansion valve. Sporlan's best-practice sheet says a wet reading means the drier has captured all the water it can hold and should be replaced. Read the color chart printed on your indicator, since makers and refrigerants differ.

Why bubbles do not prove an undercharge

Sporlan's best-practice sheet says a glass solidly full of liquid shows subcooling is present, and that vapor bubbles show it has been lost, which points to an inadequate charge. That is a fair first clue. But other sources say bubbles have more than one cause. For a glass after the drier, Danfoss lists three: pressure drop across the drier too high, no subcooling, or too little refrigerant, and it adds: do not replenish refrigerant solely because of bubbles. Tomczyk explains the restricted case: liquid pressure falls as it squeezes through a clogged drier, and some of it flashes to vapor. He also warns that many systems are found with a full receiver because techs kept charging to clear the glass.

A glass can also look partly empty at low flow near the end of a cycle with no bubbles in it, and Tomczyk says not to add charge for that. So use the glass as a question, not an answer. Measure subcooling (see superheat and subcooling), measure the drop across the drier, and compare with the undercharge vs. overcharge table. Low subcooling with bubbles points to charge. A big pressure drop across the drier with bubbles after it points to the drier.

Field card: Filter Driers and Sight Glasses: What They Tell You

Gauges first, then replace; bubbles are a question.

  • A drier removes water, acid and particles above about 25 microns.
  • Replace it every time the system is opened; never re-use a drier.
  • Same gauge before and after: Sporlan says replace above 5 psi drop.
  • Your hand will not feel a small restriction.
  • Dark green is dry, yellow is wet; read the chart on the glass.
  • Bubbles: check subcooling and drier drop before adding charge.
  • Burnout: oversized liquid drier plus suction drier; watch pressure drop and the acid test.
