Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

A boiler that is checked and written down tends to stay boring. A log turns a pile of observations into evidence of what changed and when, and in New Jersey the Bureau can ask to see an operator’s log during license review.

Why the log exists

Insurance guidance says operating and maintenance logs show whether a boiler is getting attention and reveal gradual changes that are easy to miss. It also says tests should be done on a set schedule, recorded, and initialed by the person who did them. The New Jersey rule on black seal eligibility notes that the Bureau may request a copy of the operator’s log for the examiner’s review when approving an application, so honest, complete entries from your first training day are an asset.

What to look at on a visit

  • Steam boilers: pressure and water level every time you enter the boiler room, per one insurer guide.
  • Hot-water boilers: pressure and temperature every visit, plus the expansion tank cushion. The same guide mentions keeping boiler water at or below 250 degrees F as a daily check for hot-water heating boilers; treat it as an example and use your unit’s rating.
  • The flame: color, steadiness, any smoke or odd noise.
  • Leaks, drips, hot spots and strange smells.
  • Stack temperature trend. One insurer notes a rise can signal scale or soot, bad baffling or a burner out of adjustment, and gives a rough figure that a 40 degree F rise costs about one percent of efficiency.

A way to organize tests

Published guides tend to group tests by how often something can go wrong. The pattern below follows the New Jersey Department of Labor logbook guideline, with insurer guides as a cross-check. It is a guideline, and the manufacturer’s instructions and your site procedure take precedence.

  • Each shift or day: blow down the gauge glass and watch how fast the water returns. A slow return suggests plugged connections. The NJ guideline lists a daily blowdown and test of low-water cutoffs on steam boilers (once per shift on high-pressure boilers). See steam-boiler-pressure-controls-and-gauges.
  • Weekly: flush the low-water cutoff float chamber and water column with the burner running and confirm the burner stops as designed. The NJ guideline’s weekly items are flame signal strength, flame-failure cutoff and timing, fuel shutoff closing, and igniter and burner operation. The National Board’s suggested log program also has a weekly flame-safeguard check: confirm the flame-failure cutoff and its timing, and record the flame signal so you can see it drift over time. See low-water-cutoff-testing-and-maintenance.
  • Monthly: in the NJ guideline, test low-water cutoffs on hot-water boilers, manually lift safety relief valves, and test fan, air, pressure and temperature interlocks (some other guides use a quarterly lift). See boiler-safety-valves-and-overpressure-protection.
  • Semiannual or annual: the NJ guideline lists a semiannual slow-drain test of steam low-water cutoffs, gauge and instrument recalibration, and relief valve testing per ASME and NBIC, then an annual test of all controls and safety devices, an inspection by a commissioned inspector, fuel valve leak tests and an instrument-based combustion adjustment.
  • End of season: overhaul of automatic controls by a qualified person, and layup. See boiler-startup-shutdown-and-layup-basics.

How to write a good entry

  1. Date, time and your name.
  2. The reading or observation, with units.
  3. Any action taken and the result.
  4. Who you told, if anything was abnormal.

Write at the time, not afterward from memory. Never erase: draw one line through a mistake and initial it. A log that is perfectly neat and never shows a problem is less believable than one that does.

The same NJ guideline says every operator on shift should review the log and sign it at the end of the shift, and that trainees are listed by full name with the attending licensed operator and are never alone on shift. It asks that the chief engineer keep the operator logbook accessible for at least three years after the last entry, and it warns that falsifying a log can cost an operator a license. It describes instrument readings daily for low-pressure heating boilers and at least once per eight-hour shift for high-pressure boilers, with low-pressure plants monitored at least every two hours. That every-two-hours figure is the guideline's practice standard; the minimum the rule itself sets for tours is the twice-per-24-hours figure listed in the next section, so confirm which one applies to your plant in N.J.A.C. 12:90 and with the Bureau.

Water side records

Add water test results, chemical feed, blowdown times and make-up water readings. Trends in make-up use often reveal a leak long before anyone sees water. See boiler-water-treatment-and-blowdown-basics and condensate-return-and-feedwater-basics.

Handover

At shift change, write down anything not normal: a device that failed a test, a valve that weeps, a boiler locked out and reset. The next person should not have to rediscover it. Insurance guidance also warns against leaving an automatically fired boiler unattended after a start-up until it has gone through several firing cycles.

Under review. The intervals above come from an NJ DOL guideline that describes its own schedule as a suggested example. Which tests your plant must perform, and how often, still depends on the manufacturer, the inspector, your insurer and your plant’s written procedure.

New Jersey inspection, certificate and log rules

The Bureau’s information guide and the state’s reprint of the boiler law and rules (both linked below; the reprint tells readers to confirm against the official NJ text) describe these points. Treat them as a summary, and ask the Bureau about your own building.

  • Inspection cycle. Boilers get a certificate inspection at least every 12 months. The Bureau states that hot water heating boilers are inspected internally every 24 months and externally every 12; we did not read the rule text of N.J.A.C. 12:90-4 that sets this. Extra inspections can be ordered when conditions warrant. Pressure vessels that are not boilers usually run on a 3 to 5 year cycle, but the exact interval for a given vessel is not settled here.
  • Certificate. After a satisfactory inspection the certificate is framed, displayed where the equipment is or in the plant office or equipment room, and kept available on request. The Bureau’s guide lists a daily penalty for not displaying it. A jurisdiction number is permanently fixed to the equipment.
  • Logbook. Plants over 100 horsepower keep an operator’s log that each operator on shift reviews and signs at the end of the shift. Entries include the date, who was on duty, time of relief and trainee time. It can be a numbered hardbound book or an electronic record that can be printed on request (N.J.A.C. 12:90-3.10).
  • Tours. For low-pressure plants the rule's minimum is that the operator checks the plant at least twice per 24 hours, seven hours apart (the guideline's every-two-hours monitoring above is a stricter practice standard), and must log the tours when out of the boiler room.
  • After service. A 2023 Bureau advisory says boilers must be tested after maintenance or repair following ASME CSD-1, with device operation and set points recorded in the log, a maintenance record or the invoice. A “set points recorded” column is a handy habit.

Owner-side detail is in NJ Boiler Inspections, Certificates, Logbooks and Posting for Owners.

Field card: Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules

If it was not written down, it is hard to prove it happened.

  • Log pressure, temperature or level on every visit.
  • Write entries when events happen; correct with one line and initials.
  • Group tests by frequency, then confirm each interval with your plant and local rules.
  • Track stack temperature and make-up water for early warnings.
  • Hand over every abnormal condition in writing.
