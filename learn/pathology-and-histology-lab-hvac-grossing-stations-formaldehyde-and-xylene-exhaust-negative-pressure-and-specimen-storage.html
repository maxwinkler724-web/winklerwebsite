Pathology and Histology Lab HVAC

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pathology lab cuts tissue that has soaked in formalin, then runs it through solvents. The air carries formaldehyde, xylene and alcohol vapors, so the HVAC job is to catch them at the source and keep the room pulling air in. This lesson covers the grossing station, processors, stainers, specimen storage and the numbers behind them.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. Codes, standards and guidance change, and the facility's safety officer and the authority having jurisdiction decide what applies. This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It is not a design.

What is in the air

Formalin is a water solution of formaldehyde, so open specimens, waste and storage all give it off. OSHA's formaldehyde standard (29 CFR 1910.1048, eCFR) sets a permissible exposure limit of 0.75 ppm as an 8-hour average, a short-term limit of 2 ppm over 15 minutes, and an action level of 0.5 ppm. It requires employers to use engineering and work practice controls first, and its appendix calls local exhaust "designed to capture airborne contaminants as near to the point of generation as possible." It also says general dilution ventilation can be the only practical choice where sources are spread over a large area.

Xylene is the common clearing solvent. The NIOSH Pocket Guide entry for o-xylene lists a 100 ppm average limit (NIOSH and OSHA), a 150 ppm NIOSH short-term limit, a flash point of 90°F and flammable limits of 0.9% to 6.7% in air. The page covers the o-isomer only, so other xylene isomers have their own entries.

What NIOSH found in real labs

Two NIOSH health hazard evaluations show why techs hear about this room. In 1981 and 1982 NIOSH sampled 13 histology labs in one metro area and found formaldehyde from 0 to 1.9 ppm, with technicians reporting eye and throat irritation, headache and chest tightness. Its recommendations included more exhaust ventilation and periodic monitoring.

A 1992 evaluation of a hospital pathology and histology area measured a personal reading of 0.17 ppm on a resident dissecting tissue, and an area reading of 1.1 ppm during tissue disposal. It recommended local exhaust over the waste drum and storing specimens under local exhaust.

Grossing stations

Grossing is the cutting-up of fresh or fixed specimens. A grossing station is a table with a slot or perforated surface that draws vapor away from the face, usually at the back (backdraft) or through the work surface (downdraft). Because the specimen is wet with formalin, the capture point matters more than the room air change rate. The morgue lesson, Hospital Morgue and Autopsy Room HVAC, covers downdraft tables and the room balance in that setting, so we do not repeat it. For a tech, the station is a local exhaust device: check slot velocity or flow against the maker's label or the facility's test report, keep the slot clear of trays and boxes, and treat a lowered flow as an alarm, not a nuisance. The fume hood lesson, Laboratory Fume Hoods, covers face velocity and airflow monitors.

Processors and stainers

Tissue processors move specimens through formalin, alcohol and xylene baths, and stainers do the same with slides. Many units have a vent port or sit under a local exhaust hood or canopy, so the unit's own instructions and the facility's safety plan set what connection it needs. In our own practice, we treat each connected unit as one more exhaust branch: it adds flow to the room total and can be lost if a duct is crushed, disconnected or blocked.

Pressure, air changes and no recirculation

CDC's Appendix B on environmental infection control reprints a ventilation table for laboratory areas. It lists histology and pathology rooms with air moving in from adjacent areas, a minimum of 6 total air changes per hour, all air exhausted directly outdoors, no recirculation by room units, and a 75°F (24°C) design temperature. CDC notes that the table is Table 7.2 of the 2001 AIA guidelines, so a facility's current code edition may differ. We could not open the current ASHRAE Standard 170 rows for these rooms, so confirm them against the edition your facility follows. CDC's laboratory safety guidance also recommends directional inward airflow from the main lab toward work spaces, so that the cleanest areas are the ones the air flows away from. Corridor pressure monitors, anterooms and door habits are in Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare.

Specimen storage

Containers still leak vapor when lids are opened, and the 1992 NIOSH report found 0.13 ppm in a histology storage room. Store specimens under local exhaust where the facility can, keep lids on, and keep the storage room's exhaust running as part of the room balance.

Airflow monitors and alarms

A station or hood without a working flow indicator is a blind spot. We would test the alarm by throttling the exhaust (with the lab's permission) and watching the indicator and the building automation point respond, then restore flow and record it. The alarm setpoint comes from the maker or the safety plan; we found no public number to give you here.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Every input below is an assumption chosen to show the arithmetic. None is from a real building.

  • Station. A 6 ft slot at an example 150 cfm per foot: 6 x 150 = 900 cfm.
  • Room. 600 sq ft with a 10 ft ceiling: 600 x 10 = 6,000 cubic feet.
  • At the cited 6 ACH. 6,000 x 6 = 36,000 cubic feet per hour, and 36,000 / 60 = 600 cfm. The station alone (900 cfm) meets it, with 300 cfm to spare.
  • At a labeled 10 ACH (our own more cautious target, not a cited value). 6,000 x 10 / 60 = 1,000 cfm. The station alone is 100 cfm short.
  • Add the units. If a processor and a stainer each draw an assumed 50 cfm: 900 + 50 + 50 = 1,000 cfm total exhaust, which meets 10 ACH exactly.
  • Supply with a labeled 10% offset (our assumption; the test and balance report sets the real offset): 1,000 x 0.90 = 900 cfm. The 100 cfm difference has to come in from the corridor and door gaps, which is what keeps the room negative.

The lesson in the numbers: a big station can satisfy the room rate by itself, but if it fails, the room goes from 1,000 cfm of exhaust to about 100 cfm while supply keeps pushing 900 cfm, and the room can swing positive. This is why we tie supply to the station's status in the controls, and why the alarm matters.

Field card: Pathology and Histology Lab HVAC

Catch it at the source, pull the room inward, never recirculate.

  • Formaldehyde: OSHA 0.75 ppm average, 2 ppm short-term, 0.5 ppm action level.
  • Xylene (o-isomer): 100 ppm average limit, flash point 90°F.
  • CDC lab table: histology and pathology, air in, 6 total ACH, exhaust outdoors, no recirculation.
  • Grossing station, processor and stainer exhaust all count toward the room total.
  • Test the flow alarm; do not silence it.
  • Keep slots clear and lids on; store specimens under local exhaust.
  • Our example: 900 cfm station, 600 cfm at 6 ACH, 1,000 cfm at 10 ACH.
