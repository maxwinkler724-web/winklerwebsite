Whole-House Humidifier Water Use Per Day

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A homeowner asks, "How much water will this humidifier use?" The honest answer depends less on the humidifier than on the house. A drafty house throws its humid air away several times a day and has to make it again. This lesson shows how to estimate gallons per day from three things: how often the house air is replaced, how big the house is, and how much moisture each pound of air must carry.

The idea in one sentence

Whatever moisture leaves the house with the leaking air must be put back, or the indoor humidity drifts down toward the outdoor level. So the water a humidifier must add equals the weight of air that leaks through per hour, times the moisture difference between indoor and outdoor air, times 24 hours.

Step 1: air changes per hour and house volume

An air change is the whole indoor volume of air replaced by outdoor air. Air changes per hour (ACH) tells you how many times that happens each hour. A Canadian government building digest says that loose windows and doors can allow more than one air change per hour, while a house built to meet a basic ventilation need of 10 cubic feet per minute per person, at about 2,000 cubic feet per occupant, needs only about 0.3. Our own rule of thumb is to treat 0.25 as a tight house, 0.5 as typical, and 1.0 as leaky when you have no test number. A blower-door test (see the drafty doors lesson linked below) is the way to replace that guess with a measurement.

Air volume per hour is simply ACH times house volume. Volume is floor area times ceiling height for the heated space, including a conditioned basement if there is one.

Step 2: moisture content, not just relative humidity

Relative humidity changes with temperature, so it is the wrong number for a water budget. Moisture content (the humidity ratio) does not change when air is heated: it is the pounds of water vapor carried per pound of dry air. Many HVAC people use grains, where 7,000 grains equal 1 pound. An atmospheric-science teaching page from a state university gives the working relation: the mixing ratio is about 0.622 times the vapor pressure, divided by the total pressure minus the vapor pressure. The same page gives a saturation vapor pressure formula for any temperature. Our lesson on the humidity ratio goes deeper; here we only need the arithmetic.

A national measurement laboratory's online water property tables list the saturation pressure of water at 70°F as about 2.505 kPa, which agrees with the formula to within a fraction of a percent. They also give liquid water a density near 998 kg per cubic meter at that temperature, which we convert to about 8.33 pounds per gallon. That conversion is our own.

Step 3: the moisture difference

Cold winter air is very dry in absolute terms even when the weather report says the relative humidity is high. At 20°F and 60 percent RH, our own calculation gives about 9.6 grains per pound. Inside at 70°F and 30 percent RH, the same calculation gives about 32.5 grains per pound. The difference, about 22.9 grains per pound, is the moisture the humidifier must supply for every pound of air that leaks through. Colder, drier outdoor air makes the gap bigger.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions, all ours: a 2,000 square foot house with 8 foot ceilings, so 16,000 cubic feet; sea-level pressure; indoor target 70°F and 30 percent RH; outdoor 20°F and 60 percent RH; dry air at about 0.0743 pounds per cubic foot (from the ideal gas relation at 70°F, our own calculation); no moisture from cooking, showers or people, which makes this a worst case for the humidifier.

  1. Air handled per hour at 1 ACH: 16,000 cu ft x 0.0743 lb per cu ft = about 1,189 lb of dry air.
  2. Moisture gap: 0.004647 − 0.001369 = 0.003278 lb of water per lb of dry air (22.9 grains).
  3. Water per hour at 1 ACH: 1,189 x 0.003278 = about 3.9 lb.
  4. Per day: 3.9 x 24 = about 93.5 lb, which is 93.5 ÷ 8.33 = about 11.2 gallons.
  5. Scale by ACH: the answer is directly proportional, so 0.5 ACH is about 46.8 lb or 5.6 gallons per day, and 0.25 ACH is about 23.4 lb or 2.8 gallons per day.

Cross-check: the Canadian digest states that, ignoring natural moisture sources, up to 9.6 gallons per day is needed per 10,000 cubic feet at one air change per hour, 40 percent RH at 75°F, with saturated 20°F outdoor air. Scaling our own method to those conditions gives roughly 11 gallons, so the two agree in size, and their figure is somewhat lower. Real homes land lower than either for another reason: cooking, bathing, laundry and people add moisture, so the humidifier supplies only the shortfall.

Why leaky houses need so much more

Look at the table: going from 0.25 to 1.0 ACH quadruples the water. A leaky house also runs the humidifier hard on the coldest, windiest days, exactly when the outdoor air is driest and the leakage is largest. Many owners of drafty houses conclude the humidifier is "too small" or that "it never gets the humidity up." Often the fix is sealing air leaks first (see the lessons on drafty doors and on the dry-winter-air problem), then humidifying less. A tight house may need very little added water, and may even need ventilation or exhaust to shed moisture.

Window condensation sets the ceiling

More water is not always better. The Canadian digest tabulates the indoor humidity at 70°F that a window can tolerate before water forms on it. With outdoor air at 20°F, the limits are 41 percent (single glass, no wind) and 24 percent (single glass, with wind), and 61 and 53 percent for double glass. At 0°F outdoors the double-glass limits fall to 49 and 41 percent, and single glass drops to 27 and 12 percent. Extension guidance from one state university says that a storm window with at least a half inch of sealed air space warms the glass and allows higher humidity without condensation. Another university extension page lists humidity above 50 percent as the point where moisture problems may occur. A federal indoor-air page says not to humidify above 50 percent and, if water condenses on windows, to lower the humidistat setting or reduce use.

That is why we used 30 percent in the main example: on a 20°F day it is below every limit in the digest table except single glass with wind. If the table shows your windows would sweat at 30 percent, the fix is a lower setpoint, not a bigger humidifier. Outdoor-reset humidistats do this automatically (see the humidistat lesson).

What this does not tell you

  • It is the water the air needs. A bypass or fan-powered unit sends some water to the drain; the water supply it draws is larger. See the whole-house humidifier lesson linked below.
  • It uses a single steady outdoor condition. Over a heating season the average is lower than a design-cold day.
  • Evaporating water absorbs heat, about 1,050 Btu per pound by our conversion of the property tables, so humidifying has an energy side effect that we do not cost out here.

Use a hygrometer to check what the house is doing, and follow the humidifier maker's directions for water quality, cleaning and maintenance.

Field card: Whole-House Humidifier Water Use Per Day

Leaked air times moisture gap, times 24 hours.

  • Pounds per day = house volume x ACH x 0.0743 lb per cu ft x moisture gap (lb per lb) x 24.
  • Gallons per day = pounds per day ÷ 8.33.
  • Our example: 16,000 cu ft, 20°F outdoors, 30% RH indoors: about 2.8, 5.6 and 11.2 gal per day at 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 ACH.
  • Leaky houses need several times more water than tight ones.
  • Window condensation limits the setpoint; lower it before buying more capacity.
  • Cooking, bathing and people add moisture, so real need is lower.
