Refrigerators and Freezers in Unheated Garages: The Refrigerant Cycle, Cold and Hot

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A spare refrigerator in the garage is common, and so are the two calls it causes: "the freezer thawed in January" and "it runs all day in August." Both come straight from how the refrigerant cycle behaves when the room around the cabinet is far from the kitchen temperature the cabinet was built for. This lesson walks through each case, with one worked example that is our own.

The cycle in one paragraph

A household refrigerator is a small vapor-compression system. The compressor raises the pressure of the refrigerant vapor, the condenser rejects heat to the room, a capillary tube or metering device drops the pressure, and the evaporator absorbs heat from the cabinet. The condenser can only reject heat to something colder than itself, so its temperature, and the pressure that goes with it, follows the room. If you want the pressure and temperature link, see Pressure-Temperature Basics. Many household units use a small charge of a flammable refrigerant; see R-600a in household refrigerators and freezers for the label clues and handling rules.

Cold garage, part 1: the thermostat is in the wrong place

Most household refrigerator-freezers use one thermostat, located in the fresh-food compartment or on the evaporator airflow path, and a damper that sends part of the cold air to the fresh-food side. The control asks one question: is the fresh-food side warmer than its setpoint? When the garage is colder than that setpoint, the fresh-food compartment is cooled by the surrounding air through its walls and never calls for cooling. The compressor stays off. The freezer compartment, meanwhile, is colder than the garage, so it slowly gains heat and drifts up toward garage temperature. The cabinet is working exactly as designed; it was designed for a warmer room. This is our own explanation of the mechanism, and it is why a unit can look fine on the fresh-food side while the freezer food softens. A state university extension page on position and settings recommends 35 to 38°F for the refrigerator and 0°F for the freezer, which is a reminder that the freezer setpoint is far below any winter garage.

Cold garage, part 2: low head pressure and oil

Suppose the compressor does run. A cold condenser drives down the condensing temperature and pressure. A capillary tube or fixed metering device passes flow because of the pressure difference across it, so low head pressure means less refrigerant reaches the evaporator, the evaporator starves, and capacity falls just when the freezer needs it. The pressure ratio also changes, and so does the oil: oil gets thicker when cold and returns to the compressor less easily, and a compressor that starts cold may pump foam and take longer to build normal lubrication. For a deeper look at head-pressure control in commercial equipment, where the same low-ambient trouble is solved with fan cycling and flooding valves, read Low-Ambient Cooling Head Pressure Control, and for oil types see Compressor Oils. Household sealed systems do not have those control parts, which is why the maker publishes a limit instead.

Hot garage: the opposite problem

A garage in summer can be much hotter than a kitchen. Condenser air is warmer, so condensing temperature and head pressure rise, the compressor works against a bigger pressure difference, and capacity falls. Heat leaking through the walls rises too, because wall leakage is proportional to the temperature difference across the walls. Less capacity plus more load means a long run time and a unit that may never cycle off. A university extension fact sheet on freezer failures lists high room temperatures, above 85 to 100°F, among the factors that shorten how long food holds. A federal energy agency rulemaking record notes that household refrigerator energy tests are run at 90°F ambient and quotes commenters who say that the test predicts efficiency poorly below 90°F. A dirty or blocked condenser makes all of this worse; see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.

What makers do about it

  • Read the label and manual. The maker states the ambient range the cabinet is built for. A manual may say the unit is not intended for outdoor or unheated placement. We give no number here because the figure depends on the model.
  • Garage-rated models. Some models are described by their makers as built for a wider ambient range. Features described in general terms include a wider-range thermostat, a stronger or differently controlled compressor design and a heated or protected control section.
  • Heater kits. Some makers offer a small heater accessory that keeps the control sensing warm enough that the compressor still gets its call. Use only a kit the maker lists for your exact model, and install it as the instructions say. Never improvise a heater near flammable refrigerant or insulation.
  • Standalone chest freezers. A chest freezer has its own thermostat for the frozen space, so the "fresh-food side is satisfied" problem does not exist. A state university extension page says a chest freezer generally uses 10 to 25 percent less energy than an upright model. Chest freezers still have maker temperature limits, so check the label.

Worked example (our own): leak, run time and ride-through

This is our own example with assumed inputs. These are not measured values for any real unit.

Assumptions: freezer compartment at 0°F; wall and door conductance UA = 1.5 Btu/h per °F (assumed); hot garage at 95°F; cold garage at 20°F; food mass 150 lb with specific heat 0.45 Btu/lb per °F while frozen (assumed); compressor capacity to the freezer 600 Btu/h at hot-garage conditions (assumed); other loads such as door openings and defrost 100 Btu/h (assumed). Latent heat of melting and cabinet thermal mass are ignored.

  1. Hot-garage leak: 1.5 × (95 − 0) = 142.5 Btu/h.
  2. Total load to remove: 142.5 + 100 = 242.5 Btu/h.
  3. Run fraction: 242.5 / 600 = 0.40, so about 40 percent of the time. If the capacity dropped to 300 Btu/h from high head pressure, the fraction would be 242.5 / 300 = 0.81, a run time that feels "always on."
  4. Cold-garage leak: 1.5 × (20 − 0) = 30 Btu/h into the freezer, with the compressor idle. The freezer has no cooling to offset it.
  5. Ride-through after the compressor stops cycling in the hot garage: food must gain 150 × 0.45 × (32 − 0) = 2,160 Btu to reach 32°F.
  6. Leak rate falls as the cabinet warms. At 0°F it is 142.5 Btu/h; at 32°F it is 1.5 × (95 − 32) = 94.5 Btu/h. Average: (142.5 + 94.5) / 2 = 118.5 Btu/h.
  7. Time: 2,160 / 118.5 = 18.2 hours, with the door closed.

Check: 18.2 h × 118.5 Btu/h = 2,157 Btu, matching the 2,160 Btu needed to within rounding. Our figure is shorter than the 48 hours federal food safety guidance gives for a full freezer, because a real freezer also has ice, packaging, shelves and the latent heat of freezing, which this simple model leaves out. Treat our number as a rough low estimate of how a high garage temperature shortens ride-through, and follow the agency guidance for decisions.

Food safety temperatures

A federal food safety agency page says to keep the refrigerator at 40°F or lower and the freezer at 0°F or lower, and to keep an appliance thermometer in each. It says a refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours without power if the door stays closed, and a full freezer holds its temperature about 48 hours, or about 24 hours if half full. A university extension fact sheet adds that an opened door and a partly full freezer shorten the time, and that food above 40°F for more than 2 hours should generally be discarded. Another extension page warns that a garage can swing above and below 40°F within a day, and that a cold-feeling garage does not guarantee safe temperatures. So the advice for customers is simple: use a thermometer, not feel.

What to tell the customer

  1. Find the maker's allowed ambient range on the label or in the manual.
  2. If the garage will go outside the range, either move the unit or ask the maker about a heater kit or garage-rated model for that exact model.
  3. Put a thermometer in each compartment and read it after a hot spell or cold snap.
  4. Keep the condenser area clean and clear.
  5. If food has been above 40°F for more than 2 hours, discard it.

Field card: Refrigerators and Freezers in Unheated Garages

The room sets the high side. The thermostat sets the call.

  • Cold garage: fresh-food side satisfied, compressor off, freezer warms.
  • Cold garage with the compressor running: low head pressure, low flow, thick oil.
  • Hot garage: high head pressure, lower capacity, long run times.
  • The label or manual gives the maker's ambient limits; we do not guess them.
  • Heater kits and garage-rated models come from the maker, for that model only.
  • Targets from federal guidance: refrigerator 40°F or lower, freezer 0°F or lower.
  • Our own example: 150 lb of food, assumed UA 1.5 Btu/h per °F, 95°F garage, about 18 hours to reach 32°F.
