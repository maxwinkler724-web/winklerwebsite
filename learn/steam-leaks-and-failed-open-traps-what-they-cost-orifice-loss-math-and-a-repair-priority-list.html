Steam Leaks and Failed-Open Traps: What They Cost, Orifice Loss Math and a Repair Priority List

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A small hole in a steam system looks like a nuisance. Run it all year and it is a fuel bill. This lesson shows how to turn an orifice size and a pressure into pounds of steam per hour, then into dollars per year, and how to use that math to decide which repair comes first.

Why a small hole costs so much

Steam leaks through a worn packing gland, a cracked flange or a trap stuck open all behave like a hole in a pipe. The flow depends on the size of the opening and the pressure behind it, and the boiler keeps making replacement steam every hour the hole is open. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Steam Tip Sheet 1 puts a number on this for traps: it lists the discharge of a leaking trap by orifice diameter and pressure, assuming steam discharges to atmospheric pressure through a re-entrant orifice with a discharge coefficient of 0.72. Its table is where the worked example below gets its starting value.

Two patterns stand out. Going from a 1/16 in. orifice to 1/8 in. at 100 psig takes the loss from 13.2 to 52.8 lb/hr, which is four times as much (Our own arithmetic: 13.2 x 4 = 52.8). Going from 15 psig to 100 psig for the same 1/8 in. hole takes it from 13.7 to 52.8 lb/hr. Size matters most, pressure second.

Fuel cost per 1,000 lb of steam

To turn pounds into dollars we need a cost for 1,000 lb of steam. DOE Steam Tip Sheet 15 gives a method: fuel cost per 1,000 lb = (fuel price per MMBtu / 1,000,000) x 1,000 lb x enthalpy difference in Btu/lb / boiler combustion efficiency. The enthalpy difference is steam enthalpy minus feedwater enthalpy, read from the tip sheet's Table 1. Its Table 2 gives a combustion efficiency of 85.7 percent for natural gas. The sheet calls the result a first approximation that leaves out pumps, fans, blowdown, chemicals and maintenance, so a real plant's cost of steam may run higher.

Our own derivation, using inputs taken from that tip sheet: the sheet's own example uses natural gas at $8.00 per MMBtu, so we reuse that as an illustrative price, not a current quote. Table 1 has no 100 psig row, so we use its 150 psig row at 200 degrees F feedwater, 1,028 Btu/lb, as the nearest listed value. Then:

  1. $8.00 / 1,000,000 x 1,000 lb = $0.008 per Btu/lb.
  2. $0.008 x 1,028 = $8.224.
  3. $8.224 / 0.857 = $9.60 per 1,000 lb (rounded).

Your plant will have its own gas price and feedwater temperature. Swap them into the same formula.

Worked example: one 1/8 in. orifice at 100 psig, all year

The table value is 52.8 lb/hr for a 1/8 in. orifice at 100 psig (DOE Steam Tip Sheet 1). Our own arithmetic, for a system that runs 8,760 h/yr:

  1. Steam lost: 52.8 lb/hr x 8,760 h/yr = 462,528 lb/yr.
  2. In thousands of pounds: 462,528 / 1,000 = 462.5 thousand lb.
  3. Fuel cost: 462.5 x $9.60 per 1,000 lb = about $4,440 per year.

For comparison, the same tip sheet's own example prices steam at $10.00 per 1,000 lb, which would give 462.5 x $10.00 = about $4,625 (Our own). Other published figures for a 1/8 in. leak at 100 psi differ because their assumptions differ. Armstrong's steam conservation guide lists 34,500 lb per month, or $4,140 per year, at $10.00 per 1,000 lb, assuming a typical condensate load for drip trap applications. A Clemson University Industrial Assessment Center write-up cites 41 lb/hr for a 1/8 in. leak at 100 psi. Treat all of them as estimates in the same range, not as exact.

One more caution. The DOE table describes full steam flow through the opening. A real trap seldom fails fully open. A federal energy program (FEMP) steam trap guide says that, lacking better data, assuming a trap has failed at one-half of its fully opened condition is probably prudent. Our own reading: halve the example and you are still near $2,200 per year for one trap, which is why a survey tends to pay.

Ranking repairs

You rarely fix everything in one day, so rank. Our own working list, built from the loss formula and from what the makers say:

  1. Blocked or cold traps and anything affecting safety. TLV says blockage is the failure that can have the greatest impact on safety, reliability and production, so these come first.
  2. Largest estimated annual cost. Multiply the table lb/hr by hours by your $ per 1,000 lb, as above. Bigger orifices and higher pressures rise to the top, because loss climbs fast with both.
  3. Easy, cheap fixes next. A packing gland or gasket leak often takes minutes. TLV advises replacing a failed trap as soon as it is found, and Armstrong says replacement payback is always within 6 to 10 months.
  4. Low pressure, intermittent services last. They cost the least per hour, but do not leave them forever.

How often to survey

DOE Steam Tip Sheet 1 suggests testing high pressure traps (150 psig and above) weekly to monthly, medium pressure traps (30 to 150 psig) monthly to quarterly, and low pressure traps (below 30 psig) annually. It reports that systems left alone for 3 to 5 years may have 15 to 30 percent of their traps failed, while a regularly maintained system should keep leaking traps under 5 percent. Armstrong says 5 to 15 percent of traps in service fail every year. The FEMP guide describes a minimal program of one test a year and says that without a proactive program roughly 20 percent of the steam leaving a central boiler plant can be lost through leaking traps. For how to test, see our trap testing lesson linked below.

Safety when you look for leaks

Live steam is invisible, as TLV notes, and the white cloud you see is condensed vapor, not the steam itself. A high pressure steam leak can injure you before you see it. A utility safety manual advises approaching a suspected leak with caution, keeping every part of the body well away, and using a piece of cardboard or similar material to find the direction of an invisible leak. It adds that pressurized steam can penetrate clothing and skin. So we never feel for a high pressure leak with a hand, glove or rag. Follow your site's procedures, including lockout and isolation, before touching a leaking joint.
