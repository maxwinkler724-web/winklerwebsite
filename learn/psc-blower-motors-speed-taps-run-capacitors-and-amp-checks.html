PSC Blower Motors: Speed Taps, Run Capacitors and Amp Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open the blower compartment on a lot of older furnaces and air handlers and you will find a simple motor with a run capacitor strapped beside it and a bundle of colored leads. That is a PSC motor. It is easy to understand, which makes it a good place to learn how to test a motor with a meter.

What PSC means

PSC stands for permanent split capacitor. The U.S. Department of Energy describes a PSC motor as a type of induction motor with an electromagnet stator. A single-phase motor cannot start a rotating field on its own, so a second winding gets its current through a capacitor that shifts the timing. In a PSC motor that capacitor stays in the circuit the whole time the motor runs, so there is no start capacitor or switch to drop out. The general background is in Capacitors and Contactors.

Speed taps

A PSC motor does not change speed electronically. Extra taps on the winding let a technician pick a different speed, and the lead color tells you which tap is which. One technician-training article describes white as the common lead and red, yellow, blue and black as example speed leads, but colors and meanings vary by maker and model. Do not trust a color chart from memory. The wiring diagram on the equipment is the authority, so photograph the connections before you move anything.

The furnace control board has separate terminals for heat and cool speeds, plus parking spots for unused leads. A Goodman installation manual for its GMS8 family describes a single-stage furnace starting the blower on high heat speed after a fixed delay, a two-stage furnace starting on low heat speed and moving to high heat speed, cooling on the cool speed, and fan-only on the low heat speed. Goodman's service manual lists that family as having a multi-speed PSC motor. The training article above also notes that unused leads go to spare terminals on the board so a bare lead never touches ground or another conductor. If you pull a lead and have no parking terminal, cap it as the manufacturer directs.

One identification trick from the same article: with power off and the motor leads disconnected, measure resistance from the common lead to each speed lead. The lowest reading is the highest speed and the highest reading is the lowest speed. Its example read 2.6, 3.3, 4.3 and 5.3 ohms across four leads. Treat those as one example, not a spec. Compare your readings to the motor maker's data.

Choosing heat and cool speeds

Speed is not a comfort setting. It sets airflow, and airflow sets temperature rise. Goodman's manual for its GMVS80 furnace says a unit is normally run at its rated input with the factory blower speed, and if the rise is wrong, change the blower speed: lowering the speed raises the rise and raising it lowers the rise. It also says to turn power off before changing speed taps.

The target comes from the furnace rating plate. Goodman's installation manual for the GMS8 family says the rise must be within the range on the nameplate. The range depends on the model; Goodman's GM9S80 and GC9S80 spec sheet, for example, lists ranges that run from 20 to 50 degrees F up to 40 to 70 degrees F across sizes. The measuring method (thermometers in the return and supply ducts, burners running about 15 minutes, supply minus return) is in Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise. Cooling speed comes from the airflow table in the manual for your outdoor unit and coil. Setting speeds correctly matters more now because a PSC does not correct for a dirty filter or restrictive duct. When static pressure rises, it slows and airflow falls. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

The run capacitor

The capacitor is marked in microfarads (the rating plate on the motor lists the required value), and in volts. RegalRexnord's motor training note says to match the replacement to the motor rating plate, to stay within plus or minus 6 percent of the microfarad rating, and to use the same or higher voltage rating but never lower. It also says most are 370 or 440 volt parts and that the capacitor should always be replaced when a PSC motor is replaced. Do not assume two motors share a capacitor; read each plate.

Checking it means power off, locked out, proved dead, and the capacitor discharged by your approved method before the meter touches it. This page does not teach a discharge technique. The steps are in Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter. A bulged or leaking can is replaced, not tested.

Winding and mechanical checks

An ACHR News troubleshooting piece on fractional-horsepower PSC motors gives a sensible order. First, measure voltage at the motor leads, which should be within 10 percent of the data plate. Second, with power off, spin the blower wheel by hand; it should turn freely. Third, with the wiring disconnected, measure the windings with an ohmmeter: both windings should show a measurable reading, and on a multi-speed motor you check each speed tap. An infinite reading (open) or zero (shorted) means the motor is electrically defective. Fourth, test the capacitor off the motor. Fifth, check the bearings for play. The article says about 1/8 inch of horizontal shaft movement is normally acceptable and any vertical movement means replacement. We did not find a manufacturer table of ohm values for blower motors, so compare readings against the maker's data for that motor, not a rule of thumb.

Amp draw versus FLA

Clamp one lead, with the blower door on and the system running, and compare to the full-load amps (FLA) on the motor nameplate. The method and the typical range are in Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter. The amp lesson explains that a motor above FLA is overloaded and hot, and that a reading near half of FLA or less points to a serious airflow problem. A reading points toward a cause; it does not name one.

Worked example

These numbers are made up for practice. The motor plate lists a 10 microfarad capacitor and 6.4 A FLA.

  1. Tolerance: 6 percent of 10 is 10 × 0.06 = 0.6 microfarads.
  2. Allowed band: 10 − 0.6 = 9.4 up to 10 + 0.6 = 10.6 microfarads.
  3. Meter reads 9.1 microfarads, off the capacitor and discharged. 9.1 is below 9.4, so it is out of tolerance.
  4. How far off: (10 − 9.1) / 10 = 0.09, or 9 percent low. Replace it.
  5. Amps, high speed, door on: 5.1 A. 5.1 / 6.4 = 0.797, about 80 percent of FLA.

In our own judgment, a reading below FLA is not alarming by itself. Combine it with the low capacitor reading and a slow-starting motor, and the capacitor is the likely first fix. Recheck amps and temperature rise afterward.

Signs of a failing motor

  • Hum with no start, or slow to get up to speed (this can also be the capacitor).
  • A wheel that does not spin freely by hand.
  • Shaft play: vertical movement means replacement, per the ACHR News article.
  • Open or shorted winding readings on any tap.
  • Running amps above nameplate FLA.

Check the capacitor, the wheel and the wiring before blaming the motor.

PSC versus ECM, and why PSC is fading

An ECM is a different animal with a different test method; see ECM Blower Motors. The federal furnace fan rule at 10 CFR part 430 was published July 3, 2014 and applied to fans manufactured on and after July 3, 2019. It sets a limit on the Fan Energy Rating, which is normalized by airflow, rather than naming a motor type. DOE's 2014 rule explains that a BPM (ECM) motor and a PSC motor behave differently with static pressure, so airflow normalizes the comparison. DOE's 2024 final determination states that the current furnace fan standards need not be amended, and it did not adopt a request to require BPM motors in every class. The same document says some product classes use improved PSC motors as their baseline, and it estimates a 39 percent FER reduction going from an improved PSC to a BPM. So PSC is not banned, but the standard pushed many models toward ECM, and you will still see PSC in older and some current equipment.

Field card: PSC Blower Motors

Wiring diagram first, power off, discharge the capacitor, then measure.

  • PSC: the run capacitor stays in the circuit; speed comes from the tap you use.
  • Lead colors vary. Follow the unit wiring diagram, and park unused leads as directed.
  • Pick the speed to land inside the nameplate temperature rise range.
  • Capacitor: replace within plus or minus 6 percent of the rating, same or higher volts.
  • Winding check, power off: none open, none shorted, check every tap.
  • Amps: compare one lead to nameplate FLA with the door on.
  • Example: 10 uF at 6 percent is 9.4 to 10.6; 5.1 / 6.4 A is about 80 percent of FLA.
