Heat Recovery Chillers: Using Rejected Heat for Hot Water and Reheat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chiller is a heat pump pointed at the cold side. It pulls heat out of the building water, adds the heat of its own compressor, and sends the total out the condenser. A heat recovery chiller simply gives that total to a hot water loop instead of the cooling tower. This lesson covers how that works, where it pays, and the arithmetic you can do at the nameplate.

The idea in one sentence

Every chiller already makes heat. In Chiller Plant Fundamentals the condenser hands that heat to a water loop and a cooling tower throws it away. A heat recovery chiller, also called a water-to-water heat pump when it is built for heating duty, puts a useful load on the condenser side. A Daikin Applied article in HPAC Engineering describes it as reclaiming energy that would normally be rejected, and says recovery works best when heating and cooling are needed at the same time.

Where buildings need both at once

  • Hospitals and laboratories. A Consulting-Specifying Engineer article names hospitals and laboratories as typical applications for simultaneous heating and cooling. The same article reports that the 2024 IECC (section C403.11.6) calls for heat pump chillers in healthcare facilities that provide heating water.
  • Hotels, education buildings and clinics. The Daikin article puts hotels at the top of the list because of domestic hot water demand, then education buildings and healthcare. A maker brochure for an MHR-series line adds dormitories, high-rise residential buildings, data centers and food or drug plants.
  • Domestic hot water and pool water. A Trane application note lists sanitary hot water, swimming pool heating and process preheat among typical uses.
  • Reheat. In a reheat system the air is cooled to dry it out, then warmed back up for the space. That warming is a heating load that arrives exactly when the chiller is running. Our own description: treat it as the same idea, cooling and heating needed together. We did not find a source page for reheat numbers, so none are given here.

The heat balance

Energy in equals energy out. The heat leaving the condenser equals the heat picked up at the evaporator plus the work the compressor adds. The MHR brochure states it the same way: heat rejected equals evaporator cooling capacity in Btu/h plus compressor power, with kW converted to Btu/h. Compressor power is not wasted. It is part of the heat you can recover, and it is why you get more heat out than cooling in.

Condenser temperature and why lift matters

Lift is the gap between the temperature where the refrigerant picks up heat and the temperature where it gives heat up. The higher you ask the condenser to deliver water, the higher the refrigerant has to be condensed, and the harder the compressor works for the same cooling. That is our own plain-language explanation of the cycle, not a measured figure. Maker pages show the practical side. The MHR brochure describes a recovery exchanger that heats a loop to about 95 to 105 F, while recovering up to about 95 percent of available heat. A reduced hot-water flow can reach up to 140 F at a smaller share, about 20 to 25 percent, and a parallel arrangement can recover 100 percent at up to 140 F. It adds that higher hot-water temperatures may call for a refrigerant such as R-134a that tolerates higher condensing temperatures at lower pressure. The Daikin article adds that controls must keep the leaving hot water from pushing refrigerant temperatures beyond their limits. See Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend for how condenser approach is read, and the compressor comparison for what the compressor type means here.

The practical rule for an apprentice: asking for 140 F water is a bigger job than asking for 105 F water, and the machine will use more power per Btu moved. We did not find a source that gives a number for that penalty, so do not quote one. Use the maker's selection data.

Piping positions

There are two different questions, and sources use different words for them.

  • On the refrigerant side. The MHR brochure describes a series arrangement, where hot gas passes through the recovery exchanger first and then the main condenser, so surplus heat leaves at the main condenser. It describes a parallel arrangement for full recovery. The Trane note uses different names: partial recovery with a desuperheater in series with the condenser, which it says can recover up to 30 percent of cooling capacity, and total recovery with a recovery condenser and a standard condenser in parallel, which can recover up to 100 percent of the waste heat.
  • On the water side. The Consulting-Specifying Engineer article describes a heat recovery chiller placed in a "sidecar" on the main return, trimming the return water temperature for other equipment, and a layout where the heat recovery chiller gets priority and other machines cover the gap to setpoint. It also warns that too little flow can cause freeze or high-pressure lockouts, that these machines hold little water, so a buffer tank or extra piping volume may be needed to stop short cycling, and that some valve arrangements can mix glycol and plain water between loops.

Always read the manufacturer's piping diagram for the unit in front of you. For the cooling-only cousin of this idea, see Waterside Economizers.

Worked example (our own)

A heat recovery chiller makes 100 tons of cooling with an 80 kW compressor input. All of the condenser heat goes to a hot water loop that can use it. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h, and 1 kW is 3,412 Btu/h.

  1. Cooling in Btu/h. 100 tons x 12,000 = 1,200,000 Btu/h.
  2. Compressor heat. 80 kW x 3,412 = 272,960 Btu/h.
  3. Total heat available. 1,200,000 + 272,960 = 1,472,960 Btu/h, about 431.7 kW of heat.
  4. Cooling COP. 1,200,000 / 272,960 = 4.40.
  5. Heating COP. 1,472,960 / 272,960 = 5.40. It is the cooling COP plus 1, because the compressor work comes back as heat.
  6. Combined COP. (1,200,000 + 1,472,960) / 272,960 = 9.79. This counts both the cooling and the heat as useful output.

Read that last number carefully. The 9.79 is real only while the building uses both the cold and the heat at the same moment. If the hot water loop is satisfied, the extra heat has to go to the tower, and you are back to the 4.40 cooling COP. Our own arithmetic; the nameplate and the maker's data rule on real machines, which lose capacity as hot-water temperature rises.

What to watch in the field

  • Check which loop has priority, cooling or heating, and what happens when the hot loop is full.
  • Confirm design flow on both loops. The maker's low-flow lockouts exist for a reason.
  • Log the hot water leaving temperature next to the load, just as you would log approach.

Field card: Heat Recovery Chillers: Using Rejected Heat for Hot Water and Reheat

Heat out = cooling in + compressor work.

  • A heat recovery chiller puts the condenser heat to work instead of sending it to the tower.
  • Best where heating and cooling are needed at once: hospitals, labs, hotels, hot water.
  • Btu/h out = tons x 12,000 + kW x 3,412.
  • Heating COP = cooling COP + 1. Combined COP counts both outputs.
  • Hotter water means more lift and more compressor work. Check the maker's data.
  • Series or partial recovery gets part of the heat; parallel or total can get all of it.
  • Watch flow, buffer volume and which loop has priority.
