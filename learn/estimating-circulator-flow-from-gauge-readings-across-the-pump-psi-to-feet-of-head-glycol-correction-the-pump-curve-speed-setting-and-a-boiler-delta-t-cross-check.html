Estimating Circulator Flow From Gauges Across the Pump

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most circulators on a heating job have no flow meter. What you do have is a pair of gauges, a nameplate, a published pump curve and a thermometer on each pipe. This lesson shows how to turn the pressure rise across the pump into an estimated flow, then check that flow a second way. It also shows why a bad gauge or the wrong speed setting can send you the wrong way.

Check your own system first

Method, not a measurement. The flow you get this way is an estimate. Use the maker's curve for your exact pump and speed, follow the maker's instructions and your site's procedures, and do not use this page in place of a flow meter where a job needs one.

Step 1: Read the pressure rise across the pump

Put a gauge on the suction side (the inlet flange or the port just ahead of it) and a gauge on the discharge side. With the pump running, the discharge reading is higher. The difference is what the pump is adding. Subtract suction from discharge and keep the units straight.

  • One gauge moved between ports. This removes the difference between two gauges, but system pressure can drift a little between readings, so work quickly and read the suction side again at the end.
  • Two gauges. Faster, but any difference in how they read goes straight into your answer. Swap them and read again if the numbers look odd. You can learn how to check one against a test gauge in the boiler gauge lesson.
  • Gauge height. If the two ports are at different heights, the lower gauge reads higher just from the water column above it. A state water-system conversion sheet gives 0.433 psi per foot of water, so a port two feet lower adds about 0.87 psi to that gauge (our own arithmetic). Take that out before you subtract.
  • Safety. Gauge ports on a hot system can scald. Use gauges rated for the system's pressure and temperature, wear eye protection and gloves, and follow the maker's and your employer's procedures for tapping into a pressurized loop.

Step 2: Convert psi to feet of head

Pump curves plot head in feet, not psi. The same state conversion sheet lists 1.0 psi as 2.31 feet of water. So:

Head (ft) = pressure difference (psi) × 2.31 for plain water.

Head is the height the pump could lift the fluid, and a heavier fluid makes more pressure for the same height. For a glycol mix, divide by the fluid's specific gravity: head (ft) = psi × 2.31 ÷ specific gravity. A glycol mix is a little heavier than water, so the same psi means slightly fewer feet. Read the specific gravity for your mix and temperature from the antifreeze maker's table. We do not quote a figure for your mix here.

Step 3: Find the flow on the pump curve for the speed setting

Find the maker's head-flow curve for your pump. Many circulators have a speed switch or a mode setting, and each setting is its own curve. Go across from your head on the vertical axis until you meet the curve for the setting in use, then drop straight down to read gpm. If you want the full picture of curves and the piping's own curve, see Reading Pump Curves and System Curves. This lesson only uses the curve in one direction, from a measured head to a flow.

Step 4: Cross-check with the temperature difference

A second answer comes from the heat. For water, the heat carried is about 500 × gpm × delta T, in Btu/h, so gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × delta T). A federally funded ground-source heat pump case study report gives that constant as 500 for water. The same report says the constant is a factor that includes the fluid's specific gravity and heat capacity, and it used 480 for a 20 percent ethanol mix at 60 °F. For glycol, use the factor for your mix. Hot Water Flow Rate Math goes through this formula in more depth.

You need the heat the water is actually carrying: boiler output (not input), the measured supply and return temperature difference, and a boiler running steadily. A cycling boiler or one just starting up gives a delta T that is not steady. Also see Adding Up Head Loss for a Hot-Water Heating Circulator for why a system's head, and so the flow, is what it is.

Worked example (our own invented numbers)

All numbers here are our own assumptions for teaching. They are not from a real pump or a real job.

  • Assumed pump curve at speed 3: head = 20 − 0.02 × Q², with head in feet and Q in gpm. That gives 20 ft at no flow, 12 ft at 20 gpm and 2 ft at 30 gpm. This is the solid line in the diagram.
  • Assumed readings: suction 12.0 psi, discharge 15.0 psi, gauges at the same height, plain water, switch on speed 3.
  • Assumed boiler: 200,000 Btu/h output, steady firing, measured delta T of 16 °F.

Gauge method. Pressure rise = 15.0 − 12.0 = 3.0 psi. Head = 3.0 × 2.31 = 6.93 ft. Set 20 − 0.02 × Q² = 6.93, so Q² = 13.07 ÷ 0.02 = 653.5 and Q is about 25.6 gpm (point A).

Temperature method. Flow = 200,000 ÷ (500 × 16) = 200,000 ÷ 8,000 = 25.0 gpm.

Compare. 25.6 and 25.0 differ by about 0.6 gpm, or 2.4 percent. For field work that is good agreement. Both methods say the circulator is moving about 25 gpm.

Glycol variation. Say the loop holds a glycol mix and, as an assumption, its specific gravity is 1.03. Head = 3.0 × 2.31 ÷ 1.03 = 6.73 ft, so Q² = (20 − 6.73) ÷ 0.02 = 663.5 and Q is about 25.8 gpm. The head conversion barely moves the answer here. The bigger glycol effect is on the heat side: the constant is lower than 500 for a glycol mix, and the pump itself may behave differently in a thicker fluid, so ask the pump maker how the curve is affected.

Why the answer can mislead you

Gauge error

The pressure rise is a small number from two big ones. Suppose each gauge is off by 0.5 psi in opposite directions. Your 3.0 psi becomes 2.0 or 4.0 psi. At 2.0 psi the head is 4.62 ft and Q comes out near 27.7 gpm. At 4.0 psi the head is 9.24 ft and Q is near 23.2 gpm. A 1 psi gauge spread therefore moves the flow about 9 percent either way on this steep part of our curve. On a flat part of a curve, a small head error shifts the flow far more, so be wary of gauge readings when the pump is working near its low-flow end. Check gauges against each other or against a test gauge before you trust a difference.

Wrong speed setting

A curve belongs to one speed. A DOE fact sheet on pump speed says flow changes in direct proportion to speed and head changes with the square of speed, so the whole curve moves when the setting changes. Suppose, as our own assumption, that speed 2 runs at 80 percent of speed 3. Then the curve is 0.64 × 20 − 0.02 × Q², or 12.8 − 0.02 × Q². The same 6.93 ft gives Q² = 5.87 ÷ 0.02 = 293.5, so Q is about 17.1 gpm (point B). If the pump is really on speed 2 and you read the speed 3 curve, you report 25.6 gpm when the flow is closer to 17.1, which is about 50 percent too high. A sticker, a mode label or an electronic setting can be wrong or misread, so confirm the setting that is actually active. For modes on variable-speed circulators, see ECM Circulator Pump Modes.

Other traps

  • Air, a partly closed valve or a dirty strainer between the gauge ports changes the reading without changing the pump.
  • Gauge ports at a reducer or elbow can read swirling flow. Put gauges where the maker shows them.
  • The temperature method uses boiler output. Using fuel input overstates the flow.
  • If the two methods disagree by a lot, do not average them. Find out which input is wrong: gauge, speed setting, boiler output or temperature reading.

Field card: Estimating Circulator Flow From Gauges

Two gauges, one curve, one heat balance.

  • Pressure rise = discharge psi minus suction psi, after correcting for gauge height.
  • Head (ft) = psi × 2.31 for water. Divide by specific gravity for glycol.
  • Read the curve for the speed actually in use, then drop down to gpm.
  • Cross-check: gpm = boiler output Btu/h ÷ (500 × delta T) for water, with the factor for your mix if glycol.
  • A 0.5 psi gauge error each way moved our example by about 9 percent. A wrong speed setting moved it by about 50 percent.
