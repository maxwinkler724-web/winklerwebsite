PTAC Units: How They Work, Cleaning and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The box under the window in an apartment, hotel room or senior housing unit is usually a PTAC, or a PTHP if it is a heat pump. It looks like a window air conditioner but is built differently. This lesson covers what is inside, how it is wired, what to clean, and what to check when a PTAC is not cooling.

What the federal definition says

The federal rule that defines the equipment is 10 CFR 431.92. It describes a packaged terminal air conditioner as a wall sleeve plus a separate, un-encased combination of heating and cooling assemblies, meant to be mounted through a wall. A packaged terminal heat pump (PTHP) is a PTAC that uses reverse-cycle refrigeration as its main heat source, with a backup heat source that can be hot water, steam or electric resistance. The Department of Energy says these products have had federal efficiency standards since 1994.

The same rule sorts wall openings into two classes. "Standard size" means an opening of about 16 by 42 inches (a cross-section of 670 square inches or more). Anything smaller is "non-standard size." That matters when a unit is replaced, because the new chassis has to fit the sleeve already in the wall. For a manufacturer example, Friedrich lists its sleeve at 16 inches high by 42 inches wide by 13-3/4 inches deep, set in a 16-1/4 by 42-1/4 inch cut-out.

What is inside the box

Friedrich's manual lists the main parts: the wall sleeve, the chassis, the front cover, the filters, a return air grille, a discharge grille and an outdoor grille. The sleeve stays in the wall. The chassis holds the working parts: compressor, indoor coil and fan, outdoor coil and fan, controls, and the electric heater. It is the same refrigeration cycle you learn in Refrigeration Cycle Basics, packed into one sealed unit, so there are no field line sets to braze.

Sleeve, pitch and condensate

Friedrich's manual says the sleeve must be level side to side and pitched about a quarter bubble toward the outside, and it says plainly not to allow any pitch toward the inside. Amana's installation manual gives the same quarter-bubble rule for its sleeve and says the pitch is what drains condensate inside the unit. Water in the base pan on a humid day is normal in that manual, and so is the sound of water trickling.

Water can leave the unit two ways. With the optional Friedrich drain kit, it goes to the building drain through half-inch tubing. Otherwise it leaves through a rear drain hole. The manual also says to keep the four overflow slots at the back of the sleeve clear, and not to let sealant block them. Another Friedrich-brand manual lists two causes for dripping: no drain pipe kit installed (water outdoors), or a sleeve that was not installed correctly (water indoors).

Heat pump or electric heat strip

A cooling-only PTAC heats with an electric resistance strip. Friedrich offers strips of 2.0, 3.0 and 5.0 kW, with 5.0 kW on the larger models only. A PTHP heats with the refrigeration cycle run in reverse and keeps the strip as backup. See Heat Pump Basics and Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat for the principles.

Cold outdoor air is where a PTHP gives way to the strip. One Friedrich ZoneAire manual says that if the outdoor coil stays below 26 °F for 3 minutes, the unit runs the electric strip instead of the heat pump, and it returns to the compressor only after the room reaches setpoint and the outdoor coil warms to 41 °F. GE's Zoneline manual says its heat pump will not work if the outdoor temperature is too cold, and electric heat takes over. GE also notes heat pump air feels less hot than strip air, and that electric heat costs more to run. So warm-but-not-hot air from a PTHP in winter is not automatically a fault. Some Friedrich digital models also have a switch setting that lets the heater run alone if the compressor fails.

Power cord, LCDI and the receptacle

Most PTACs are cord-connected, and the voltage decides the plug. Friedrich's power cord sheet pairs breaker size, cord kit and receptacle like this for 230/208 V units: 15 A with a NEMA 6-15R, 20 A with a 6-20R, and 30 A with a 6-30R. The 265 V units use NEMA 7-15R, 7-20R and 7-30R. The heater size decides which cord fits: the Friedrich brochure pairs 2.0 kW with 15 A, 3.0 kW with 20 A and 5.0 kW with 30 A. You cannot swap in a different plug to fit an old outlet. Match the receptacle to the nameplate. See Reading an HVAC Nameplate.

The 230/208 V cords ship with an LCDI (leakage current detection interrupter), which meets UL and National Electrical Code requirements for cord-connected air conditioners. The 265 V cord is a non-LCDI type meant for a sub-base. Friedrich says to press TEST and RESET as directed before use, not to use the LCDI as an on/off switch, and to replace a failed or damaged cord with the manufacturer's cord rather than repair it. Both Friedrich and Amana say no extension cord, and use only the fuse or HACR breaker size on the rating plate. Amana adds that if a unit with an LCDI cord will not run at all, check the reset button on the cord head first. Friedrich also warns that voltage stays at the controls even when the unit is off, so unplug it or open the breaker before service. Some units, such as the GE Zoneline vertical series, are hard-wired and GE says an exposed building-mounted receptacle is not permitted by code for them. Follow Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead before opening any chassis.

Worked example: heat strip kW to amps and Btu/h

Take a 3.0 kW strip on a 230 V circuit. The Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatthour as 3,412 Btu.

  1. Heat output: 3.0 kW × 3,412 = 10,236 Btu/h, about 10,200.
  2. Current at 230 V: 3,000 W ÷ 230 V = 13.04 A.
  3. The same strip on 208 V. A resistance element has fixed resistance, so find it first: R = 230² ÷ 3,000 = 52,900 ÷ 3,000 = 17.63 ohms.
  4. Power at 208 V: 208² ÷ 17.63 = 43,264 ÷ 17.63 = 2,454 W. Current: 208 ÷ 17.63 = 11.80 A.
  5. Heat at 208 V: 2.454 × 3,412 = 8,372 Btu/h, which is 82 percent of the 230 V figure ((208 ÷ 230)²).

So the same PTAC makes about 18 percent less strip heat in a building fed at 208 V. That is one reason a room can feel weak in winter with nothing broken. This is the strip alone. The compressor and fans add to the load, and the circuit is sized from the nameplate, not from this arithmetic.

Cleaning that actually matters

  • Filter. Friedrich's PZ manual says clean at least monthly, with the unit off, in mild detergent and warm water, fully dry before reinstalling. GE says change it at least every 30 days. Friedrich's filters lift out the top without removing the front cover.
  • Outdoor coil and base pan. Friedrich says check annually or twice a year and remove lint, leaves and dirt with a soft brush, compressed air or a vacuum. It says do not use a caustic cleaner, cover electrical parts, and do not bend the fins. It allows pressure washing with care; GE says avoid high-pressure spray and acid-based cleaners. Manuals differ, so use your model's.
  • Drain. GE says to clean the drain system regularly to prevent clogging.
  • Dusty or coastal sites. Amana says to pull the chassis from the sleeve to clean the base pan and coil periodically, and yearly in coastal air.

Also, the manual warns that below about 55 °F outdoors in cooling mode the indoor coil can freeze, and suggests opening the fresh air door and running the fan only. Friedrich digital models also have an indoor coil frost sensor that shuts off the compressor when the coil gets too cold. A comparable idea for room units is in Window and Portable Air Conditioners; for no-heat strip basics see Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working.

When the chassis is the problem

If airflow, filter, coil and power are fine and the coil is still not cold, the manual's remedy is "have the refrigerant charged," which means a technician with EPA 608 certification. Our own advice, not a maker rule: because the chassis slides out of a sleeve that stays in the wall, check that a replacement fits the sleeve size class, the voltage, and the cord and receptacle type, and confirm with the chassis maker. Check those three against the nameplate before ordering.

Field card: PTAC Units: How They Work, Cleaning and Common Problems

Filter, coil, cord, then charge.

  • PTAC = wall sleeve + chassis; PTHP adds reverse-cycle heat with a strip backup (10 CFR 431.92).
  • Sleeve pitches about 1/4 bubble to the outside, never inward. Keep overflow slots clear.
  • Cord and receptacle must match the nameplate: 6-15R, 6-20R, 6-30R at 230/208 V. No extension cords.
  • Test the LCDI; replace a bad cord, do not repair it. Unplug before service.
  • kW × 3,412 = Btu/h. Resistance heat falls with the square of the voltage: 208 V gives about 82 percent of 230 V.
  • Clean filter monthly and outdoor coil once or twice a year, per the model manual.
  • Cold-weather weak heat on a PTHP can be normal. Compressor start delay is 3 minutes.
