Frozen Furnace Condensate Drain in a Cold Snap: What Homeowners Can Do

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A high-efficiency furnace or condensing boiler wrings so much heat out of its exhaust that the exhaust turns back into water. That water has to drain away, and in a deep cold snap the drain is the first thing to freeze. This lesson covers the signs, the reasons, what a homeowner can safely try and when to call a pro.

Why these units make water

Burning natural gas makes carbon dioxide and water vapor. An older furnace sends all of that vapor up the chimney hot. A condensing furnace or boiler pulls so much heat out of the exhaust that much of the vapor turns to liquid inside the unit. That liquid is called condensate, and it runs out through a small drain line, usually clear or white plastic tubing or pipe. If that line stops, the water has nowhere to go. For more on what the condensate is like inside the unit, see the lesson on secondary heat exchangers and collector boxes.

Signs the drain is frozen

  • The furnace or boiler starts, runs a short time, then shuts off and locks out, sometimes more than once.
  • You hear gurgling or bubbling from the unit or the drain line.
  • Water backs up in the unit, or you find water leaking near it or under it.
  • You see ice at the outdoor end of the drain line, or frost and ice building at the vent pipes outside.

Our own note: these signs can also come from other problems, such as a blocked vent or a failed part, so treat them as clues and not a diagnosis. A shutoff with a blocked drain is a safety feature working, since many units will stop if the water level inside rises too high.

Why it happens

Our own field experience points to a few repeat causes:

  • Drain run in the cold. The line passes outside, or through an unheated garage, crawlspace or attic, where it can drop below freezing.
  • No slope or a sag. Water should run steadily downhill. A flat spot or a low sag holds water in the line, and standing water freezes first.
  • Long idle times. A line full of trickling water stays above freezing while the unit runs. During a long off period the water sits still and can freeze solid.
  • Small flow, small pipe. The line is narrow, so a thin plug of ice is enough to close it.

The attic case has its own access and drain-pan details; see gas furnaces in attics and horizontal installs.

Worked example (our own): how fast does the water add up?

This is our own estimate, not a measured or published figure. We want to know how much water a furnace makes when it runs nonstop in a cold snap.

Assumptions (ours): a 60,000 Btu per hour input furnace; natural gas treated as pure methane; one therm is 100,000 Btu; about 100 cubic feet of gas is about 0.26 pound-mole; about 70 percent of the water vapor condenses in a condensing furnace.

  1. Gas burned: 60,000 Btu per hour divided by 100,000 Btu per therm is 0.6 therm per hour.
  2. Chemistry: methane plus oxygen makes carbon dioxide plus two water molecules, so each mole of gas makes two moles of water.
  3. Water per therm: 0.26 pound-mole of gas times 2 is about 0.52 pound-mole of water. Water weighs about 18 pounds per pound-mole, so that is about 9.5 pounds. At about 8.34 pounds per gallon, that is about 1.14 gallons, or roughly 1.1 gallons of water vapor per therm.
  4. Water vapor per hour: 0.6 therm times 1.14 gallons is about 0.68 gallon.
  5. Condensed at 70 percent: 0.68 times 0.70 is about 0.48 gallon per hour.
  6. Per day, running nonstop: 0.48 times 24 is about 11.5 gallons.

What it means: a drain that is frozen shut can hold only a small amount inside the unit. At roughly half a gallon an hour while the burner runs, the water backs up quickly, which is why the unit locks out so soon after the ice forms. The real number changes with the gas mix, the unit and how much condenses. For a bigger table by furnace size, see condensate gallons per hour and day.

Safe steps a homeowner can take

  1. Turn the thermostat down or off so the unit stops trying to fire into a blocked drain.
  2. Look at the outdoor end of the drain line. If you can see and reach it safely from the ground, check for ice at the opening. Do not climb on an icy roof or ladder to look.
  3. Warm an exposed plastic line gently. Warm, damp towels or a hair dryer on low can thaw a visible, reachable section. Keep the dryer moving and keep any plug or cord away from water.
  4. Never use an open flame or a heat gun. They can melt or warp plastic, and an open flame near a gas appliance or vent is a hazard of its own. This is our own safety rule of thumb.
  5. Keep the vent pipes clear of snow. A federal consumer product safety agency advises clearing snow away from the outside vents of fuel-burning appliances so carbon monoxide cannot build up indoors.
  6. Keep your carbon monoxide alarms working. The same agency recommends alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, with battery backup, and says interconnected alarms are best. It advises testing them monthly. If one sounds, get outside and call 911.

A federal environmental agency lists blocked or disconnected flues and poorly maintained furnaces and boilers among the common sources of carbon monoxide in homes, so a unit that keeps shutting down is a reason to take care, not to bypass any safety switch. Never jump, tape or bypass a pressure switch or float switch to keep the unit running. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do for the full routine.

When to call a pro

  • The drain freezes again after you thaw it.
  • There is water near wiring, the control board or the electrical box. Stay away and switch the unit off at the service switch only if you can do so without standing in water.
  • The lockout will not clear after the drain is open.
  • You want the drain rerouted indoors, to a floor drain or a laundry sink, so it is out of the cold.
  • You are thinking about a heat cable on the line, or a condensate pump. Both add electrical parts and need correct sizing and a proper power source. See condensate pump not working for how pumps fail.
  • You smell gas, an alarm sounds or anyone feels sick. Leave and call 911 or the gas utility from outside.

A technician can check the slope, the trap, the pipe size and the insulation and can fix the cause instead of thawing the symptom. Our own tip: write down how cold it was and how long the unit had been off when the lockout happened. That helps the technician.

Field card: Frozen condensate drain in a cold snap

Lockout plus ice at the drain means stop, warm gently and call if it repeats.

  • Signs: starts then locks out, gurgling, water at the unit, ice at the drain end or vents.
  • Causes: cold run, no slope or a sag, long idle times.
  • Do: thermostat down, check the outdoor end, warm plastic gently, clear snow from vents, test CO alarms.
  • Never: open flame, heat gun, bypassing a safety switch.
  • Estimate (our own): about 0.48 gallon per hour, about 11.5 gallons a day, for a 60,000 Btu per hour input furnace at 70 percent condensing.
  • Call a pro for repeat freezing, water near wiring, a stuck lockout, rerouting, a heat cable or a pump.
