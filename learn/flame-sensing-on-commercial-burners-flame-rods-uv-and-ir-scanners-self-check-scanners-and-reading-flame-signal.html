Flame Sensing on Commercial Burners: Rods, UV and IR Scanners

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A commercial burner is only as safe as its ability to tell whether a flame is really there. Three kinds of detector do that job, and each can be fooled in its own way. This lesson covers how they work, what the signal readings mean, and the faults behind most nuisance lockouts. The sequence and lockout logic are in Boiler Flame Safeguards, Purge and Lockout, so we do not repeat them here.

Check the maker's manual

The manual for your control and scanner governs. The numbers below come from one maker's public literature and show what such figures look like. Your amplifier, scanner and burner may use different scales and minimums. This page gives no setpoints for you to apply.

Flame rod: rectification and ground area

A flame rod is a small metal rod on a ceramic insulator, placed in a gas flame. The control puts AC voltage on it. According to Fireye's flame detection paper, the flame carries current more easily one way than the other because the grounded surface touching the flame is much larger than the rod. Positive ions reach the large ground more freely, so the net current is a small DC signal the amplifier can measure. The same paper puts flame current generally at 2 to 4 microamps and flame resistance anywhere from 100,000 to 100,000,000 ohms. A short to ground gives a steady high current in both half-cycles, and the control rejects it. For the furnace-scale basics, see Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps and Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics.

Fireye's service guide and M-Series II manual add the placement rules that matter on a commercial burner. The ground surface touching the flame should be at least four times the area of the rod touching the flame. The rod tip should reach at least half an inch into a non-luminous flame but not more than halfway through it, and the rod should stay at least half an inch from refractory. Do not run the rod wire in the same conduit as ignition wiring. Fireye describes flame rods for gas firing with a stable flame, so a rod is not the answer on every fuel.

UV tubes and why they can fail unsafe

A UV scanner has a sealed quartz tube with two electrodes; the service guide gives 400 to 1200 VAC across them. When ultraviolet light reaches the electrodes, the gas ionizes and conducts, and the control counts the pulses. Ultraviolet is only about 1 percent of a flame's spectrum, but it is strongest in the first third of the flame, which is why the guide says to aim UV scanners there.

Two weaknesses explain why UV needs care. First, the tube can respond to things that are not the flame: Fireye lists hot refractory above 2000°F, spark ignition, welding arcs and halogen light. Second, the tube's gas can degrade over time and start counting with no flame present. That is a false flame, an unsafe failure, because the control believes a flame exists when none does. A standard flame safeguard can catch this only at a restart, since the burner has to shut down and restart for the check to happen. A burner that never cycles never gets that check.

Self-check scanners for continuous firing

A self-check scanner puts a shutter in front of the tube. The shutter blocks the ultraviolet regularly, and the control expects the signal to drop each time. If the tube keeps counting through a blocked view, the control knows the tube is faulty and shuts down. Fireye's M-Series II manual says a scanner with the self-check feature is mandatory for burners designed for continuous operation, defined there as 24 hours of continuous use. The detection paper adds that the shutter interruption must stay shorter than the control's flame failure response time, which it says ranges from under one second to four seconds. Whether your jurisdiction or insurer requires a self-check type is a code and policy question, so confirm it for your plant.

IR scanners and hot refractory

Infrared makes up about 90 percent of a flame's spectrum, but steady infrared does not prove flame. The IR scanner looks for flicker, the 5 to 200 hertz modulation from combustion. The trouble is hot furnace parts. Fireye notes that refractory above 1000°F radiates infrared, and strong low-frequency background can saturate the cell so it cannot track flicker. Sight the scanner to favor the ignition zone and avoid the background. The service guide describes two refractory tests for IR detectors: watch for the signal falling as the refractory heats, and with refractory at maximum temperature, close the manual fuel valve and time the flame relay drop-out against the flame failure response time. Do these only as the maker's procedure says, with a trained burner technician.

Reading flame signal at the test jacks

Flame amplifiers usually have test jacks, and the control may also show a number on a display. Scales differ by maker and model. In Fireye's M-Series II manual, a UV amplifier should show a steady 4.0 to 6.0 volts DC with flame, a flame rectification amplifier 6 to 18 volts, and zero volts means no flame. The same manual says a microammeter in series on the S2 wire of a rectification amplifier should read 4 to 10 microamps, and that a flame rod installation should reach a minimum of 6.0 volts DC. The D-Series section of the service guide uses a different scale: normal 18 to 25 volts, with the flame relay dropping out near 5 volts. It also calls for a meter of at least 1,000 ohms per volt, or a digital meter with at least 500 kilohms of input impedance. The guide says the minimum acceptable signal is listed in the technical bulletin for the control used. Read the number, compare it with that minimum, and write it down. A steady reading matters as much as a high one. For meter habits, see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.

Sighting and cooling air

Per the M-Series II manual, a UV scanner belongs within 30 inches of the flame and sights the first third of the flame, with the flame covering the whole sight opening. It must not see the ignition spark directly, or any surface that reflects it. Heat shortens detector life: the service guide gives a maximum of 140°F for the 45UV5 UV scanner and says to keep the IR scanner below 125°F, recommending 4 SCFM of purge air for it. Those are that maker's figures for those models, so use the ones in your scanner's manual.

Common faults

  • Dirty lens. The M-Series II manual says even a small amount of contamination measurably cuts the signal reaching a UV detector, and advises routine wiping of the viewing area with a soft cloth and detergent, then removing residue.
  • Rod and insulator. The same manual says to keep the rod and insulator clean by washing with soap and water, and to replace rods as they oxidize. A cracked insulator is a leakage path, so replace it rather than clean it.
  • Poor ground. The manual's troubleshooting list says terminal S1 must be solidly earth grounded, and so must the ignition transformer secondary.
  • Ignition interference. Fireye says spark can add to or subtract from a rod signal. Remedies include more distance between the ignition electrode and the rod, checking electrode spacing, and replacing deteriorated leads.
  • Other causes of a wandering reading. The service guide lists unstable flame, wrong supply voltage, bad detector wiring, electrical noise and detector overheating.

Testing safely

Fireye's manual warns not to touch a flame rectification rod with power applied, and notes hundreds of volts across the scanner terminals when powered. Its pilot test warning says to shut off the main burner fuel manually first, and that the minimum pilot test belongs to a trained and qualified burner technician. A spark check, run with the fuel off, should show essentially no signal; the manual says no more than half a volt DC for a UV amplifier. Never jumper or bypass a scanner. Record each reading as described in Boiler Controls and Safety Devices Test Record.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These inputs are assumptions chosen for practice, not field data. The reference values come from Fireye's M-Series II manual.

Part 1: flame rod. Assume a meter on DC volts at the test jack of a rectification amplifier reads 7.2 V with flame steady. The maker's installation step asks for a minimum of 6.0 V. Margin: 7.2 − 6.0 = 1.2 V. As a share of the minimum: 1.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.20, or 20 percent above it. It passes with some room.

Part 2: dirty lens. Assume a weekly log on a UV amplifier of 5.0, 4.8, 4.5, 4.2 and 3.9 V over four weeks. First-week drop: (5.0 − 4.8) ÷ 5.0 = 0.04, or 4 percent. Total drop: 5.0 − 3.9 = 1.1 V, and 1.1 ÷ 5.0 = 0.22, or 22 percent. The maker's normal band is 4.0 to 6.0 V, so 3.9 V has slipped below it. The maker's minimum pilot test uses 3.5 V as a test target (not a stated drop-out), and only 0.4 V remains above that (3.9 − 3.5). A steady slide like this points to lens contamination. Clean the lens per the manual, retake the reading, and keep logging.

Field card: Flame Sensing on Commercial Burners: Rods, UV and IR Scanners

Read the signal, compare to the maker's minimum, log the trend.

  • Rod: ground area about four times the rod area in the flame.
  • UV tubes can fail unsafe (count with no flame); self-check shutters catch that on continuous burners.
  • IR scanners watch flicker; hot refractory can swamp them.
  • Scales differ: one maker shows 4.0 to 6.0 V for UV and 6 to 18 V for rod amplifiers.
  • Dirty lens, cracked insulator, poor ground and spark interference are the usual faults.
  • Never touch a powered rod or bypass a scanner.
