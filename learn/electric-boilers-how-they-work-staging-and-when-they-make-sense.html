Electric Boilers: How They Work, Staging and When They Make Sense

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An electric boiler is a small steel or copper vessel with resistance heating elements in the water. There is no flame, no flue and no fuel piping, so it is simple to install. The catch is the electric service it needs and what the kilowatt-hours cost. This lesson covers how the elements are staged, how to turn a heat loss into kW and amps, and a worked cost comparison with a gas boiler.

How it makes heat

Water flows through the boiler past immersed elements, much like the elements in an electric furnace (see Electric Furnace Not Heating). Every watt in becomes heat in the water. The U.S. Department of Energy calls resistance heating 100 percent efficient at the point of use, and also notes that it is often more expensive to run than combustion heat. Piping, the circulator, the expansion tank and the emitters (baseboard, radiant floor or panel radiators) are the same as on any hot-water system.

Staging: matching the elements to the load

Most of the heat load is far below the design load most days, so the boiler does not switch all its kilowatts on at once. The Electro Industries mini-boiler manual says the control reads the outlet temperature and "automatically stages the elements to match the BTU load," and that after a thermostat call the pump starts and the elements wait 30 seconds before they energize. The same maker notes that the stage lights may blink slowly near setpoint, which is normal. The Allied Engineering MSE manual describes a three-stage controller that uses PID logic to decide when to add or drop a stage, which helps prevent short cycling, and that switches stages on in sequence and off in sequence when the call ends. Staging also softens the electrical load, because a big step of full power does not hit the panel in one jump.

Outdoor reset

Electro Industries says its mini-boiler series does not include an outdoor sensor, but one can be added, and the control can tell whether it is connected. With it, the setpoint comes from the front dial and a reset curve. These sensors are 10K thermistors that you can check with an ohm meter against the maker table. Lower water temperature in mild weather means fewer kilowatts per hour, the same idea as in Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation.

Kilowatts and amps

Size the boiler like any other: take the room-by-room design heat loss. One kilowatt-hour equals 3,412 Btu, per the U.S. Energy Information Administration, so kW = Btu/h ÷ 3,412. Current on a single-phase 240 V circuit is amps = watts ÷ volts. The Allied Engineering manual table agrees: its 12 kW unit draws 50 A and its 24 kW unit 100 A at 240 V single phase. Conductor and breaker size come from the nameplate and the National Electrical Code, which both Allied and Electro Industries point to rather than giving one general rule. Always get the minimum circuit ampacity from the nameplate, and have a licensed electrician size and run the feeder. Electro Industries lists 5, 7 and 9 kW models at 240 V with 20.8, 29.2 and 37.5 A, and requires copper conductors. Allied also asks that an electrician check the current at the panel after startup and compare it with the table.

Water flow, relief valve, tank and low-water cutoff

  • Flow. Electro Industries asks for more than 0.3 gpm per kW. Hi-limit trips during operation point first to a flow problem.
  • Relief valve. Both makers ship a 30 psi valve. Electro Industries says a valve above 30 psi cannot be used on its boiler and asks for a pipe extension to prevent water damage. Allied says the valve is a code requirement, that no valve may sit between it and the boiler or in its discharge line, and that the installation follows the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section IV. See Boiler Safety Valves and Overpressure Protection.
  • Expansion tank. Electro Industries says a closed loop needs one. Its mini boiler ships with a tank pre-charged to 12 psi, and it says a pressure rise of about 4 psi or more at operating temperature suggests the tank is too small or air is in the system. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.
  • Low-water cutoff. Makers differ, and your local authority decides. Allied says a low-water cutoff must be provided when the boiler sits above the radiation or when the authority having jurisdiction requires one. Electro Industries says one is not included or factory required on its mini boilers. Do not skip it where the inspector asks for it. See Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs.

Worked example: electric vs gas, with made-up numbers

Made-up house. Design heat loss 36,000 Btu/h. Annual heating load delivered to the rooms: 60 million Btu (60 MMBtu). Both numbers are invented for teaching, not a real house.

Step 1, kW. 36,000 ÷ 3,412 = 10.55 kW. Pick the next size up from the maker line, 12 kW.

Step 2, amps. 10,550 W ÷ 240 V = 44 A. The 12 kW unit: 12,000 ÷ 240 = 50 A, matching the Allied table.

Step 3, electric cost. 60,000,000 ÷ 3,412 = 17,585 kWh. EIA's Electric Power Annual lists a 2024 U.S. residential average of 16.48 cents per kWh. 17,585 × $0.1648 = about $2,898 a year.

Step 4, gas cost. EIA's 2024 U.S. residential gas average was $14.50 per thousand cubic feet. EIA uses about 1,036 Btu per cubic foot and a therm is 100,000 Btu, so 1,000 cubic feet is 10.36 therms and the gas costs $14.50 ÷ 10.36 = $1.40 per therm. Federal law sets the minimum for new gas hot-water boilers at 84 percent AFUE (10 CFR 430.32). Fuel in: 60,000,000 ÷ 0.84 = 71.4 million Btu = 714 therms. 714 × $1.40 = about $1,000 a year.

Result. Electric is about $2,900 against about $1,000 for gas, nearly three times as much. Another way to see it: 1 MMBtu of heat costs about $48 from resistance at 16.48 cents and about $17 from an 84 percent gas boiler at $1.40 per therm. Prices vary by state and year, and this ignores install cost, maintenance and the gas boiler vent. Use your own rates.

When an electric boiler makes sense

  • A small load, such as a well-insulated addition or a home in a mild climate. DOE says resistance heat may suit an addition where extending the system is impractical, or places with few heating days.
  • No gas service, or no space for venting.
  • As a backup or booster behind a heat pump. DOE says air-source heat pumps can cut heating electricity use by up to 75 percent versus resistance heat, and describes air-to-water types that heat radiators and radiant floors. Compare options in Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Jersey Home. With a heat pump of coefficient of performance 3 (an assumed number), the same 60 MMBtu would need about 5,860 kWh, about $966.
  • If your utility offers a time-of-use rate, ask them how it is structured before you count on it.

Field card: Electric Boilers

kW = Btu/h ÷ 3,412. Amps = watts ÷ volts.

  • 12 kW at 240 V single phase = 50 A (maker table). Use the nameplate for wire and breaker.
  • Elements stage on in steps to match load; a 30 s pump lead appears on one maker.
  • Relief valve 30 psi on these makers, with a discharge pipe and nothing between valve and boiler.
  • Closed loop needs an expansion tank.
  • Low-water cutoff: follow the maker and the authority having jurisdiction.
  • Example: 60 MMBtu a year costs about $2,900 electric versus about $1,000 gas at 84 percent AFUE (2024 U.S. averages).
