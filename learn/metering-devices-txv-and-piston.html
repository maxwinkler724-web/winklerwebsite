Metering Devices: TXV and Piston

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Between the condenser and the evaporator sits a small part that decides how much liquid refrigerant gets through. It is easy to overlook and easy to blame, so it is worth understanding.

What a metering device does

A metering device creates a pressure drop between the liquid line and the evaporator. It also separates the high-pressure side of the system from the low-pressure side. When the liquid passes through the restriction, some of it flashes into vapor, which is part of how the refrigerant gets cold. Common types are capillary tubes, pistons, thermostatic expansion valves (TXVs) and electronic expansion valves (EEVs). For the bigger picture, see Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

Fixed types: piston and capillary tube

A piston (also called a fixed orifice) is a small brass piece with a hole of a set size. A smaller hole passes less refrigerant. A capillary tube does the same job with a length of thin tubing. Neither one changes its opening when conditions change, so the flow depends on the pressure difference across it and the state of the liquid arriving. Pistons are simple, can be removed and cleaned, and are common in many residential air conditioners. Some heat pumps use a piston arrangement that seats in one flow direction and unseats in the other, so each mode has a metering device in the path.

The TXV: a valve that adjusts

A TXV uses a needle and seat as its orifice, and the opening changes with the heat load on the evaporator. Three forces act on it:

  • A sensing bulb clamped to the suction line near the evaporator outlet. As the line warms, the bulb pressure opens the valve.
  • A spring that pushes the valve toward closed.
  • An equalizer line that senses evaporator outlet pressure and also pushes toward closed.

The result is a valve that tries to hold a steady superheat at the evaporator outlet. Superheat is how far the vapor temperature is above the boiling point for its pressure. An EEV does a similar job, but it uses electronic sensors and a controller to position the valve instead of a bulb. The fixed devices do not modulate, and the TXV and EEV do.

Why the difference matters

  • Load changes. A TXV adjusts as the load changes. A piston does not, so its performance shifts more with indoor and outdoor conditions. One technician source says the TXV is the more efficient choice across varying conditions, at least in theory.
  • Protection. Too little superheat can let liquid refrigerant reach the compressor. Too much starves the coil and can overheat the compressor. Sizing matters too: a valve that is too small starves the coil, and one that is too large can flood the compressor.
  • Shut-off. Some TXVs close off completely when the compressor stops. A type with a bleed port does not, so the right valve for the equipment must be used.
  • Service. A piston is simpler to service. A TXV has more parts to go wrong.

Charging and diagnosis, in general

Technician references commonly pair piston systems with the superheat method and TXV or EEV systems with the subcooling method. See Charging Methods Overview and Superheat and Subcooling Basics. A published note also points out that the TXV is often blamed unfairly for low suction pressure. Low airflow, a dirty coil or a wrong charge can look the same, so check those before replacing a valve. A TXV that seems stuck or hunting can have real causes, but confirm with measurements and the manufacturer's procedure first, and keep in mind that readers' stories online are not a diagnosis.

Safety and rules

Replacing a metering device means opening the refrigerant circuit. That requires proper recovery, the right certification and the equipment manufacturer's instructions. See Refrigerant Recovery, Recycle, Reclaim. Use the part the manufacturer specifies for that model, and do not substitute a different type or size to get around a problem.

Field card: Metering Devices: TXV and Piston

The metering device sets the pressure drop and the feed to the evaporator.

  • Fixed devices (piston, capillary tube) do not change their opening. TXVs and EEVs do.
  • A TXV works from a sensing bulb, a spring and an equalizer pressure, aiming for steady superheat.
  • Pistons are usually charged by superheat, TXVs by subcooling, but the manufacturer's method governs.
  • Check airflow, coil condition and charge before blaming the valve.
  • Use the exact specified part, and recover refrigerant properly before opening the circuit.
