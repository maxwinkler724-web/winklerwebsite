Mixed Water Temperature Math for Radiant Floors and Low-Temperature Loops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A boiler makes 180 F water. A radiant floor wants something near 110 F. Between them sits a mixing device, and the math behind it is only a weighted average plus one flow formula. This lesson works that math with numbers you can check by hand.

It assumes you already know why a floor needs gentle water and what the common mixing parts look like. If not, read Radiant Floor Heating Basics and Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics first. Here we only do the arithmetic.

The mass balance: a weighted average

When two streams of water meet, the mixed temperature lands between them, closer to the stream with more flow. Because both streams are water, the specific heat cancels out and flow in gpm can stand in for mass. The balance is:

Tmix = (Qhot × Thot + Qret × Tret) ÷ (Qhot + Qret)

Here Q is flow in gpm, Thot is boiler supply and Tret is the cooler water coming back from the loop. This treats water density as constant across these temperatures, which is a fine assumption for field math. A glycol mix changes the heat-carrying numbers; see the lesson on the 500 factor for water.

Solving for how much hot water to add

In the field you know the temperatures and want the flow split, so rearrange the balance. The fraction of the loop flow that must be boiler water is:

Hot fraction = (Tmix − Tret) ÷ (Thot − Tret)

Read it as a ratio of gaps: how far the target sits above the return, divided by how far the boiler sits above the return. A target halfway between return and boiler needs half hot water. A target only a little above the return needs a small fraction.

Injection flow straight from the load

There is a second route that skips the loop flow entirely. The heat the loop delivers must arrive with the injected water, and that water is cooled from boiler temperature down to the loop return temperature. The sensible-heat formula for water gives:

Injection gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × (Tboiler − Treturn))

The 500 is our own derivation from standard properties: a US gallon of water weighs about 8.33 pounds (a federal agency conversion appendix gives that figure), 60 minutes make an hour, and water absorbs roughly 1 Btu per pound per degree F, so 8.33 × 60 × 1 is about 500. It is a rounded constant, and it shifts for glycol mixes.

The useful insight is that the injection flow depends only on the load and the boiler-to-return gap. It does not depend on the loop flow or the exact supply target. Change the target and the loop flow changes, but the boiler water needed stays put. That is why injection pumps and valves can be small.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a design. The inputs below are assumptions, not recommendations.

  • Radiant loop load: 36,000 Btu/h (assumed)
  • Boiler supply: 180 F (assumed)
  • Loop return: 100 F (assumed)
  • Loop supply target: 110 F (assumed), so the loop drop is 10 F
  • Water properties: the 500 factor above, no glycol

Step 1, loop flow. Loop gpm = 36,000 ÷ (500 × 10) = 36,000 ÷ 5,000 = 7.2 gpm.

Step 2, hot fraction. (110 − 100) ÷ (180 − 100) = 10 ÷ 80 = 0.125, or 12.5 percent of the loop flow.

Step 3, injection flow. 0.125 × 7.2 = 0.9 gpm.

Step 4, cross-check from the load. 36,000 ÷ (500 × (180 − 100)) = 36,000 ÷ 40,000 = 0.9 gpm. The two routes agree.

Step 5, flow split. The loop circulator moves 7.2 gpm. Of that, 0.9 gpm is fresh boiler water and 6.3 gpm is recirculated return water (7.2 − 0.9). Check the mix: (0.9 × 180 + 6.3 × 100) ÷ 7.2 = (162 + 630) ÷ 7.2 = 792 ÷ 7.2 = 110 F.

Step 6, what leaves. With closely spaced tees, 0.9 gpm of 100 F water has to leave the loop and head back toward the boiler, equal to the amount injected. That is the diagram below.

Why a non-condensing boiler cares about that return

In the example the boiler would see 100 F water. That is a problem for a conventional boiler. Combustion makes water vapor, and when boiler surfaces are cooler than the flue gas dew point, the vapor condenses on them. A national laboratory evaluation of condensing boilers puts that point near 130 F for natural gas in its example, and says condensing begins when return water is below about 130 F. A federal facilities findings sheet likewise says return water must be below 130 F to reach condensing mode. Condensing boilers are built for that; many conventional boilers are not. See Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers for the maker-specific numbers, which always override the round figure here.

So a floor loop fed by a conventional boiler often needs a second mix, this time protecting the boiler. The same balance equation applies, with the boiler inlet as the mixed point. Add these assumptions to our example: a boiler protection setpoint of 130 F (assumed), and the 0.9 gpm of 100 F return arriving at the boiler.

Bypass gpm = 0.9 × (130 − 100) ÷ (180 − 130) = 27 ÷ 50 = 0.54 gpm of boiler outlet water recirculated into the inlet.

Check: (0.9 × 100 + 0.54 × 180) ÷ 1.44 = (90 + 97.2) ÷ 1.44 = 130 F. Total boiler flow is 1.44 gpm.

That total is small. A real boiler has a minimum flow and a temperature rise limit from its maker, and 1.44 gpm may be below it. If so, the boiler needs its own circulator and bypass sized to the maker's numbers, not to this example. Never use a number from this page as a setting.

Three-way valve or injection: the same fraction, different flow

A three-way mixing valve in this example would also deliver 12.5 percent hot water, but all 7.2 gpm of loop flow passes through the valve. An injection setup moves only 0.9 gpm through the control device. That is the reason smaller parts work for injection. See Two-Way vs Three-Way Control Valves for how valve authority affects control quality.

Common field mistakes

  • Mixing up which gap goes on top. The top is target minus return; the bottom is boiler minus return. Flip them and you get a fraction above 1.
  • Using the wrong return. The balance needs the actual return temperature measured at the mix point, not a design number from a drawing.
  • Forgetting the loop drop. A 10 F drop is gentle. A 20 F drop halves the loop flow for the same load, but the injection flow stays about the same.
  • Setting the mix by feel. Use the floor and tubing maker's limits, and keep the high-limit protection they call for.

Field card: Mixed Water Temperature Math for Radiant Floors and Low-Temperature Loops

Weighted average for the mix, load over 500 times the gap for the injection.

  • Tmix = (Qhot × Thot + Qret × Tret) ÷ total flow.
  • Hot fraction = (target − return) ÷ (boiler − return).
  • Injection gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × (boiler − return)).
  • Injection flow does not change with the loop target; loop flow does.
  • Our example: 36,000 Btu/h gives 7.2 gpm loop, 0.9 gpm injected, 6.3 gpm recirculated.
  • Conventional boilers need a warm return; condensing boilers want a cool one. Use the maker's number.
  • The 500 factor changes with glycol.
