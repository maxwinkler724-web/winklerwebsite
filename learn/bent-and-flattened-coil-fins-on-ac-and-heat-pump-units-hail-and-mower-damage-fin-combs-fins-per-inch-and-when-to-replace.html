Bent and Flattened Coil Fins: Hail and Mower Damage, Fin Combs and When to Replace

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The fins on an outdoor coil are thin aluminum, and a hailstone, a string trimmer, a pressure washer or a dog can lay them flat in a second. This lesson covers how much flattened fin matters, what a fin comb does, why fins per inch decides which comb fits, when straightening is worth it, and what a homeowner should and should not do.

How the damage happens

Air has to pass between fins that sit only a fraction of an inch apart. One comb maker's product page lists a tool for coils of 8, 10, 12 or 14 fins per inch, and a condensing unit maker says its units use 14 fins per inch. At 14 per inch the gap from one fin to the next is 1 ÷ 14, about 0.07 inch (our own arithmetic). Anything that presses fins together closes that gap. A support page from an equipment maker lists hail, rocks and sticks, pressure washing too close, accidental contact during maintenance, and wear from wind or animals as causes. The U.S. Department of Energy names lawn mowers and dryer vents as sources of debris and tells owners to trim foliage back at least 2 feet. Pets and string trimmers do the same job in smaller patches, though we found no study that counts them.

How much flattened face area matters

No single study matches one dented coil exactly, so we read three kinds of evidence as a range.

  • Dent studies. An engineering firm's article summarizing a laboratory hail study (IMECE2007-41518) says that with 41 percent of the fin area flattened the coils had little or no loss of capacity, while efficiency fell about 2 to 5 percent (SEER) and 3 to 6 percent (EER). With 100 percent flattened, capacity fell 23 to 27 percent and SEER fell 34 percent. Combing restored capacity to within 1 to 4 percent and efficiency to within 4 to 6 percent of the undented units. We read this only through that summary.
  • Airflow cut on a full coil. A 1999 utility-sponsored test report on five-ton rooftop units says a 35 percent cut in condenser airflow dropped EER by 24 percent in a standard unit and 19 percent in a high-efficiency unit, with power use up 18 and 13 percent. A later section reports condensing temperature up 15.8°F (standard) and 14°F (high efficiency) without stating the airflow cut for those figures, and the report also mentions a 25 percent target. The airflow was reduced by fouling the coil face with a test aerosol, not by flattening fins.
  • Blocked outlet. A conference paper on a small split unit (Setyawan and co-authors, AIP Conference Proceedings) blocked 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent of the condenser outlet. At 25 percent, capacity fell 7.7 percent, power rose 8.1 percent and EER fell 14.5 percent. That is a blocked fan outlet, not a flattened coil face, so it shows direction only.

The sources do not agree on size, which is the honest answer. A moderate patch often costs little because the fan pulls more air through the open fins, while a large share costs a lot. A national laboratory report (Berkeley Lab) studied the indoor coil and found that in a well-tuned system a 5 to 10 percent airflow drop cost 2 to 4 percent of EER and capacity, with marginal systems losing 10 to 20 percent or more. Dirt is also not always the whole story: University of Nebraska-Lincoln researchers found that washing nine end-of-life outdoor coils changed heat transfer very little.

Worked example (ours): 20 percent of the face flattened

All inputs here are our assumptions, not a measured unit. A coil face is 30 inches by 30 inches, so 30 × 30 = 900 square inches. A hail patch of 18 × 10 = 180 square inches is 180 ÷ 900 = 20 percent.

Step 1, upper bound on airflow loss. We assume the flattened area passes no air and airflow falls in proportion, so it falls at most 20 percent. Real loss is usually smaller because the fan pulls more air through the open fins.

Step 2, scale the full-coil result. The standard rooftop unit lost 24 percent of EER at a 35 percent airflow cut. Scaling by 20 ÷ 35 = 0.57 gives 24 × 0.57 = 13.7 percent EER lost. Power up 18 percent scales to 18 × 0.57 = 10.3 percent. The condensing temperature rise of 15.8°F scales to 15.8 × 0.57 = 9.0°F if that test was the 35 percent case, or 15.8 × (20 ÷ 25) = 12.6°F if it was the 25 percent target. That is a worst case of roughly 9 to 13°F more condensing temperature, which means higher head pressure. Straight-line scaling is itself an assumption.

Step 3, compare with the dent study. At 41 percent flattened the dent summary shows 2 to 5 percent SEER loss. Scaling to 20 percent gives 20 ÷ 41 × 2 = 1.0 and 20 ÷ 41 × 5 = 2.4, so about 1 to 2.4 percent.

Reading the two. The airflow method says up to about 14 percent of EER and the dent method says about 1 to 2 percent. We lean toward the small end for a patch on one face with straight tubes, and toward the large end only if fins are crushed across a whole face. No study we read settles it, so the real test is a tech comparing condensing temperature to entering air before and after. For that reading see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.

Cleaning plus combing. The dent summary says combing restored most of the loss. Washing alone gave small heat-transfer changes in the Nebraska coils, so we would expect combing to do the work here and cleaning to help only if the coil is also dirty. A cleaned but uncombed coil keeps the same 180 square inches of lost opening.

Fin combs and fins per inch

A fin comb has teeth that slide between fins to pull them back to their original spacing. A support page from an equipment maker says the comb must match the coil's fins per inch so the teeth line up. A coil-maker article says to spray the bent fins with diluted dish soap, set the comb above the damaged area and pull downward, which is along the fin direction on a typical coil with vertical fins. A tool maker's page describes working fins back over multiple passes with nylon teeth that straighten them without damaging the soft aluminum. Get the fins per inch from the maker, or count fins along a measured inch. A comb that is too fine will not enter the gap, and one that is too coarse skips fins.

Straighten or replace

  • Comb: fins are bent or leaning but not creased, tubes are straight, there is no oil stain, and the flattened share is a patch.
  • Replace the coil or unit: an equipment maker says excessively damaged sections may require replacement rather than straightening. In our view that includes fins folded flat across a whole face, a dented or cut tube, or any oil staining. Leak repair and coil replacement are technician work; see Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air for how coil leaks develop.
  • Cost: we have no cited price for either choice, so ask for both in writing.

Microchannel coils

A microchannel coil has flat tubes with small louvered fins brazed between them. The combs we found are sold for round-tube plate-fin coils, and we found no source that describes a comb procedure for microchannel. In our view, do not try one. Leak repair is also limited; see Microchannel Condenser Coils.

Homeowner versus tech

Homeowner: turn off the outdoor disconnect and the indoor breaker, wait for the fan to stop, and look. Remove loose debris by hand, keep plants trimmed back 2 feet (see Outdoor Unit Clearances), photograph the damage and call a tech. Do not poke with screwdrivers or knives, and do not use a pressure washer; owner booklets say light pressure and no high-pressure water, as summarized in Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil. One equipment maker recommends a licensed technician for combing.

Tech: lock out power, match the comb to the fins per inch, work gently along the fin direction in short passes, protect the tubes, then compare condensing temperature to entering air before and after.

Field card: Bent and Flattened Coil Fins

Power off, look, photograph, do not poke.

  • Fins per inch sets the comb size; one tool maker lists 8 to 14 per inch.
  • Dent summary: 41 percent flattened, little capacity loss; 100 percent, 23 to 27 percent loss.
  • Our example: 20 percent of 900 sq in = 180 sq in flattened.
  • Combing restored dented units to within 1 to 4 percent of capacity.
  • Replace when tubes are dented or leaking, or fins are crushed across a whole face.
  • Microchannel: no comb procedure found; do not try.
