Ground Temperature by Depth and Season: Damped Swing and Time Lag

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Air temperature can swing 60 degrees or more between January and July. Ten feet down, the ground barely notices. Understanding how soil temperature changes with depth and season explains why basements stay moderate, why buried pipes freeze only near the surface, and why a ground loop is a good place to dump or collect heat.

The surface swings, the deep ground does not

Soil is a poor conductor compared with metal, and it holds a lot of heat per cubic foot. Heat from the summer sun or the winter cold has to creep downward by conduction. Each layer smooths what the layer above passed to it. A university soil physics text describes this as short-term weather changes being damped out as measurement depth increases. The same text treats the yearly or daily temperature at depth as a wave around the average, with an amplitude that falls off exponentially and a peak that arrives later.

Two things happen at once as you go down:

  • Damping. The size of the swing shrinks. A common way to describe this is the damping depth, the depth where the swing has fallen to about 37 percent (1/e) of the surface swing, according to that same text.
  • Time lag. The warmest and coldest soil arrive later and later. At a shallow depth the soil peaks a few weeks after the surface. Deeper, the peak slides into fall or winter.

This is why deep soil is warmest in the fall, when the air is already cooling, and coldest in late winter or spring, when the air is already warming. It is also why a shallow trench and a deep trench behave so differently.

Steady temperature a few dozen feet down

A federal energy page on geothermal heat pumps says that a few feet below the surface the ground stays at a relatively constant temperature year-round, and gives a shallow-earth range of about 40 to 70 degrees F. A federal energy statistics page says the ground at 10 feet is surprisingly steady, between 50 and 60 degrees F. A university paper on soil thermal regimes states that the average annual soil profile temperature is approximately equal to the average annual air temperature, with measured differences at 21 U.S. sites ranging from -1.5 to 2.9 degrees C. The same paper says seasonal fluctuations reach about 15 meters (roughly 50 feet) in mid-latitudes, and below that the temperature stays near the annual average. So the rule of thumb is: a few dozen feet down, expect roughly the yearly average air temperature for your area, give or take a few degrees.

That match is not exact. A state university extension bulletin on western soils found yearly mean soil temperature generally ran warmer than mean air temperature, by anywhere from about 1 to nearly 6 degrees F depending on the site, and it called air temperature only a rough estimate of soil temperature (about plus or minus 3 to 4 degrees F when sites are similar). Snow cover, shade, bare ground and irrigation all move the number.

How moisture and soil type change it

How fast the wave moves depends on thermal diffusivity, which is conductivity divided by volumetric heat capacity. Higher diffusivity means heat penetrates deeper and the lag is shorter. Water matters a great deal. The extension bulletin notes that water is more than twice as effective as air at conducting heat, so wetter soil conducts better, and heavy rain or irrigation can shift soil temperatures down to 20 inches or more. A federal agricultural research paper on soil heat flux reports that for a silt loam, conductivity rose from 0.96 to 1.3 W/(m K) as water content went from 0.15 to 0.20 kg/kg, and that across commonly observed water changes conductivity can vary by a factor of two to five. Dry sand, saturated clay, gravel and rock all have different values, so a ground-loop designer measures or looks up the soil rather than guessing.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example, not a design. We use the exponential damping relation from the university soil physics text: swing amplitude at depth z equals the surface amplitude times e to the power of minus z over d, where the damping depth d equals the square root of (diffusivity times the period divided by pi). The time lag from the surface to depth z equals z divided by the square root of (4 times pi times diffusivity divided by the period).

Assumptions (ours): thermal diffusivity 0.5 x 10-6 m²/s (a moderately moist soil; the soil physics text uses this value in its own daily-wave example); surface amplitude 15 C (a 30 C or 54 F range between the yearly high and low at the ground surface); period one year, 31,557,600 seconds; surface peak around July 25; uniform soil, no snow, no freezing.

Step 1, damping depth. d = sqrt(0.5e-6 x 31,557,600 / 3.1416) = sqrt(5.02) = 2.24 m (about 7.4 ft).

Step 2, amplitude. Amplitude = 15 x e^(-z/2.24).

  • 0.5 m: e^(-0.223) = 0.80, so 12.0 C, a 24.0 C peak-to-peak swing.
  • 1 m: e^(-0.446) = 0.64, so 9.6 C, swing 19.2 C.
  • 2 m: e^(-0.893) = 0.41, so 6.1 C, swing 12.3 C.
  • 3 m: e^(-1.339) = 0.26, so 3.9 C, swing 7.9 C.
  • 5 m: e^(-2.231) = 0.11, so 1.6 C, swing 3.2 C.
  • 10 m: e^(-4.462) = 0.012, so 0.17 C, swing 0.3 C (about 0.6 F).

Step 3, time lag. The lag speed is sqrt(4 x pi x 0.5e-6 / 31,557,600) = 4.46e-7 m/s, which is 0.0386 m per day, or about 25.9 days per meter.

  • 0.5 m: 13 days, peak near August 7.
  • 1 m: 26 days, near August 20.
  • 2 m: 52 days, near mid-September.
  • 3 m: 78 days, near October 11.
  • 5 m: 130 days, near early December.
  • 10 m: 259 days, near April 10.

Check. The lag also equals (z/d) x (year / 2 pi) = 0.446 x 58.1 days = 25.9 days at 1 m. The two forms agree.

Moisture check (assumed). At 2 m, a drier soil with diffusivity 0.3e-6 gives d = 1.74 m, amplitude 4.7 C and lag 67 days. A wetter soil with 0.8e-6 gives d = 2.83 m, amplitude 7.4 C and lag 41 days. Moisture and soil type shift the answer by weeks and several degrees. Real soil has layers, snow and freezing, so measured values will differ.

What it means in the field

  • Basements. A basement wall reaches maybe 8 feet down. The upper wall sees a big seasonal swing and the lower wall a small one that lags the weather by weeks, so heat loss is largest near the top of the wall in the coldest weeks. See the related lesson on slabs and basement walls for how that is turned into heating loads.
  • Buried pipes. The coldest soil at shallow depth arrives weeks after the coldest air, and deeper pipes see a smaller, later cold. Our own example suggests that depth buys both a smaller swing and delay. Local frost depth and snow cover decide the burial depth, so use local requirements.
  • Slab heat loss. The soil under the middle of a slab is near the steady temperature, while the edge is exposed to the changing air above. That is why slab losses concentrate at the perimeter.
  • Ground heat pump loops. Deeper loops start from steadier water temperatures than shallow ones. Our example shows a 5 m loop seeing a swing of only about 3 C from the ground itself. But a running loop also changes its own soil temperature, so loop length is sized from soil conductivity and the load. See the related lessons on loop heat transfer and flushing.

Field card: Ground temperature by depth and season

Deeper means smaller swing, later peak, steadier average.

  • Surface swing shrinks exponentially with depth; damping depth is where it falls to 37 percent.
  • Lag grows with depth: deep soil is warmest in fall and coldest in late winter or spring.
  • A few dozen feet down, soil sits near the yearly average air temperature, within a few degrees.
  • Wet soil conducts better than dry, so moisture changes both swing and lag.
  • Our example (assumed 0.5e-6 m²/s): d = 2.24 m, lag about 26 days per meter.
