RV and Camper Heating and Cooling: Rooftop AC, 30-Amp Shore Power, Propane Furnaces and CO and LP Alarms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A camper has a small version of nearly every system in a house: a rooftop air conditioner, a propane furnace, a 12 volt battery, a 120 volt cord and a few alarms. They share one small, closed box, and most of them depend on the same two limits: a 30 amp shore post and a battery that is only as healthy as its last charge. This lesson explains how each piece works and why the common complaints (the breaker trips, the furnace clicks but does not light) usually have simple causes.

Check the maker's label first

Check the label. Every number here is either cited to a source we opened or marked as our own assumption. Models differ. Use the label on your unit and the maker's manual, and treat this page as general education, not a repair procedure. For the same topics in a food truck, see Food Truck HVAC and Refrigeration. For heat pump starting surge on a generator, see Running a Heat Pump on a Generator. We do not repeat those here.

The rooftop air conditioner

Most campers use a self-contained rooftop unit: compressor, condenser coil, evaporator coil and fans all in one housing on the roof, with a cutout through the roof into the living space. The sizes we read about are called 13,500 and 15,000 Btu/h. One non-ducted maker manual we read lists 12.5 amps of cooling current for the 13,500 unit and 13.0 amps for the 15,000 unit on 115 volts, with a locked rotor current of 63 amps for both. A second maker manual lists three models drawing 1,450, 1,620 and 1,720 watts, which is 13.1, 14.6 and 15.4 amps. The label on your unit is the number to use.

Those two figures matter for different reasons. Running amps decide how much of the post is left for everything else. The locked rotor figure is the current the compressor can draw for a moment as it starts, and it is several times the running figure.

Ducted versus non-ducted ceiling assemblies

The part you see inside is the ceiling assembly. A non-ducted assembly blows cooled air straight out of the ceiling grille. A ducted assembly feeds an air distribution box that sends air into ducts above the ceiling and out through several ceiling registers. The second maker manual says the box installation is the same for both types and lists duct requirements for the ducted case: total duct length 15 to 40 feet, at least 21 square inches of duct cross section, 4 to 8 supply registers, and duct insulation of at least R-7. It also gives a roof thickness range of 3.5 to 6 inches and a roof opening of about 14.25 inches square, with four mounting bolts tightened evenly to 40 to 50 inch pounds. The first manual says to check mounting bolt tightness within 3 months and then every 12 months if the vehicle is in constant use. Roads loosen things.

Filters, coils and the 3 minute wait

The two manuals disagree on filter cleaning intervals, which is a good reminder to read yours: one says every two weeks or more when in use, the other every four weeks or more, and the second recommends replacing the filter at least every 12 months. Both say to wash it in warm soapy water; the second adds that it must dry completely before it goes back. Neither manual we read gave a rooftop coil cleaning procedure. Their troubleshooting steps only mention clearing leaves and dirt from the grilles and cleaning the condensate drain openings. For general coil cleaning technique and cautions, see Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil, and unplug the shore cord before you open anything. The first manual also says the compressor waits at least 3 minutes before it starts, including after a power interruption, so a camper AC that is silent right after a breaker reset may not be broken.

Why a 30 amp post limits the camper

A 30 amp shore post at 120 volts supplies at most 30 × 120 = 3,600 volt-amps. Our own arithmetic: that total has to cover the air conditioner, the battery charger and converter, the refrigerator, lights, and whatever you plug in. A generator feeding the same 30 amp inlet has the same breaker limit, and the generator also has its own surge limit; see the generator lesson linked above. The NPS RV fire safety page warns that circuit breakers do not always prevent overloads from starting fires, advises against extension cords in an RV, and says the campground power cord should be in good condition and replaced right away if damaged. A thin or worn cord drops voltage under load, which makes a compressor start harder. See Extension Cords and GFCI for the cord-gauge side of that problem.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own example, not a measured result and not a maker rating. Every input marked "assumed" is a stand-in for the figure on your label.

  • Cooling capacity: 13,500 Btu/h (the class named above).
  • Assumed EER: 9.5 Btu/h per watt. EER is cooling in Btu/h divided by electrical input in watts. The ENERGY STAR room air conditioner specification defines the related CEER as Btu per hour of cooling output over watts of input, in Btu per watthour. We use plain EER here, ignoring standby power.
  • Assumed power factor of 1, so amps = watts / volts. Real compressors run slightly higher in amps than this.
  • Assumed starting multiplier: 5 times the running amps with no add-on. The maker figures above (63 locked rotor amps against 12.5 running amps) are a ratio of about 5, so 5 is a reasonable round number, but it is still our assumption.
  • Assumed soft-start add-on: cuts the starting multiplier to 2.
  • Assumed other loads: converter and charger 5.0 A, refrigerator control, lights and fans 3.0 A, a TV and small electronics 1.0 A, so 9.0 A in all.
  • Assumed microwave: 1,500 W input, which is 1,500 / 120 = 12.5 A.

Step 1, AC running watts. 13,500 / 9.5 = 1,421 W.

Step 2, AC running amps. 1,421 / 120 = 11.8 A.

Step 3, AC start amps. 11.8 × 5 = 59 A without a soft start; 11.8 × 2 = 23.7 A with the assumed soft start.

Step 4, AC plus other loads, no microwave. 11.8 + 9.0 = 20.8 A, which is under the 30 A rating with 9.2 A to spare.

Step 5, add the microwave. 20.8 + 12.5 = 33.3 A. That is 3.3 A over 30 A, about 111 percent of the rating. In this example, the AC plus the microwave exceeds the post. A breaker that is only slightly overloaded may not open at once, since how fast depends on its time-current curve, which we do not give. But the load does not fit, and we should expect the breaker to trip.

Step 6, the start moment. If the microwave and the other loads are already running (12.5 + 9.0 = 21.5 A) and the compressor then starts, the total is 21.5 + 59 = 80.5 A for an instant. With the assumed soft start it is 21.5 + 23.7 = 45.2 A, which is better but still above 30 A. The start surge is brief, so a post may ride through it, but combined with the microwave it is the classic nuisance trip.

Result. The AC alone with ordinary loads fits. The AC plus a microwave does not. The fixes are cheap: run the microwave when the AC compressor is off, cook another way, or add a soft-start device sized by its maker for your compressor. If your label shows a higher EER, the running amps drop; if it shows a lower one, they rise.

Propane furnaces: how they run

A typical camper furnace is a sealed combustion unit. The RV furnace service manual we read says it draws combustion air from outdoors, isolated from the room air, and vents its exhaust outside. Intake and exhaust usually share one side-wall vent cap with two tubes; the manual describes a seal between exhaust and intake, minimum tube overlaps of 1/2 inch (intake) and 1 1/4 inch (exhaust), and says not to vent into an enclosed porch or anywhere the vent can be covered or blocked. It also says to clean the main burner and inspect the venting system at least once a year, preferably before heating season, and to replace any leaking or deteriorated gaskets.

With a 12 volt control board, the manual describes this order: the thermostat calls, the blower starts, the sail switch must close to prove the blower is moving enough air for combustion, a purge of about 15 seconds follows, then the gas valve opens and the spark runs for 7 seconds. If the flame is not sensed, it tries again; after three failed tries the gas valve closes, the blower runs 3 minutes, and the board locks out. When the thermostat is satisfied the gas valve closes and a 90 second post-purge runs. If the sail switch stays open 30 seconds after the blower starts, the control board shuts the blower down.

Low battery, sail switch and no heat

The same manual gives a 12 volt DC operating range of 10.5 to 13.5 volts, and says the board goes into standby below 9.5 volts until power returns. The blower motor is the heavy load, so a tired battery or a loose connection can let the blower start slowly, which keeps the sail switch from closing, which gives you clicking and no flame. The NPS page says to check 12 volt connections before every trip because road vibration loosens them. The manual also warns that a battery charger can exceed 14.5 volts and damage the board, and that dust and lint on the blower wheel and sail switch can keep the switch from engaging. Check the battery first, then the blower and switch for lint. Do not jumper a sail switch: it is the proof that exhaust is moving.

CO alarms and LP detectors

Propane burns cleanly when the furnace is healthy, but the same manual says soot at the exhaust is a sign of incomplete combustion, which makes carbon monoxide, and that the furnace should not run until the problem is fixed. The NPS page says to make sure the RV's carbon monoxide and propane detectors are properly located and working. Those are two different sensors, though many campers use one combination alarm.

The combination alarm maker manual we read says to mount the unit on a wall between 4 and 20 inches off the floor for the LP sensor, near possible gas sources such as the furnace, refrigerator and stove, and to put the CO function outside the sleeping area, with another in each sleeping area for best protection. It says not to install within 12 inches of opening windows, exterior doors, or heating or return vents, or behind furniture or drapes. The CPSC says a CO alarm should meet the current Standard 2034 standard, should not be near heating vents or covered by furniture, and should not be placed above fuel-burning appliances. Follow your alarm's maker where it differs, since the maker's placement also fits the LP sensor. The manual also says to replace the alarm after five years, to test it after storage, before each trip and at least weekly in use, and to treat a gas alarm as an order to shut off appliances, close the tank valve, open doors and leave.

If the CO alarm sounds, the CPSC says not to search for the source: move outside to fresh air, call for help, and do not go back in until responders say so. Michigan's health department adds that a camper CO detector should be battery operated, tested before the trip, and replaced every five years or per the maker.

Heaters and generators to avoid

The CPSC says not to use fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in enclosed spaces such as tents, campers and other vehicles, and that the risk is higher at high altitude. Its CO questions page says never to run unvented fuel-burning appliances where people sleep, and not to heat with ranges or ovens. Michigan's page says the same about range burners in a camper. The CPSC generator alert says to keep a portable generator at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, and that opening doors and windows does not make indoor use safe. A camper's furnace intake, air conditioner and roof vents are all intakes. Never park a generator so its exhaust blows toward any of them, including your neighbor's.

The NPS page also says to shut propane off at the tank while driving and to turn the refrigerator and all appliances off before entering a fuel stop. For more on the general CO question, see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Winter use and condensation

People and pets breathe out a lot of moisture, and the NDSU Extension publication on winter RV living says condensation forms on cool surfaces when the air is moist and that high humidity can lead to mold. It suggests aiming for 30 to 40 percent humidity or lower depending on how much condensation you see, and ventilating periodically. It also says unvented propane heaters add moisture and should not be used as a heat source in the living space, while electric resistance heaters and vented-combustion heaters do not create that moisture problem. That is another reason a sealed-combustion furnace is a better choice than a space heater. Small electric heaters can help keep tanks and plumbing warm, but on a 30 amp post the same arithmetic as above applies: add their amps before you turn on the microwave.

Field card: RV and Camper Heating and Cooling

Count the amps, check the battery, trust the alarms.

  • 30 A at 120 V is 3,600 VA total for everything on the post.
  • Our own example (assumed numbers): 13,500 Btu/h at EER 9.5 is about 11.8 A running and 59 A starting; AC plus 12.5 A microwave plus 9 A of other loads is 33.3 A, over the post.
  • Rooftop filters: clean per the maker (two makers said every 2 or 4 weeks in use); the compressor waits 3 minutes.
  • Furnace no-heat: check the 12 V battery and connections, blower wheel and sail switch lint, then the vent cap. The maker range read was 10.5 to 13.5 V.
  • LP detector low (one maker said 4 to 20 inches off the floor); CO alarm as the maker directs, test before each trip.
  • No unvented heaters or range burners for heat; generator at least 20 ft from doors, windows and vents.
  • Winter: ventilate, aim for 30 to 40 percent humidity or lower.
