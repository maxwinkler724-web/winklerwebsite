Measuring Air-Side Cooling Capacity with Enthalpy: Wet-Bulb Readings, 4.5 x CFM, Sensible, Latent and SHR

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A coil's capacity is not printed anywhere on the jobsite. You can estimate it from three things you can measure: how much air moves, and the state of that air going in and coming out. This lesson shows the enthalpy method step by step, where the 4.5 and 1.08 come from, how to split the answer into sensible and latent parts, and why a bad airflow number spoils everything else.

What you are measuring

Air carries heat in two ways. Sensible heat shows up as temperature, which a dry-bulb thermometer reads. Latent heat is the energy tied up in water vapor, which shows up as humidity. A wet-bulb reading, taken with the sensor wrapped in a wet wick and moving air across it, responds to both, so a dry-bulb and wet-bulb pair pins down the state of the air. The federal test procedure for central air conditioners uses this same idea: it measures indoor coil inlet and outlet dry-bulb temperature plus water vapor content, which may come from a wet-bulb reading, and it uses the indoor air enthalpy method for capacity. In that procedure the wet-bulb sensor, wick removed, must be accurate to within 0.2 F. The method is also the primary capacity measurement in a national-lab fault-detection study of a residential heat pump, where a second refrigerant-side check agreed within 5 percent. The lab tools are better than yours, so treat a field result as an estimate.

Step 1: turn the readings into enthalpy

Enthalpy, in Btu per pound of dry air, is the total heat content of the air. You can read it off a psychrometric chart or calculate it. For this page we calculated it, and we show every formula so you can check us. A university agricultural-engineering text gives the working form for 70 to 150 F air as h = 0.2405 t + W (1060.5 + 0.45 t), with t in degrees F and W the humidity ratio in pounds of water per pound of dry air. For air below 70 F it uses a vapor enthalpy of 1061.7 + 0.439 t in place of the bracket.

To get W from a wet-bulb reading, the same text gives the humidity ratio as W = 0.6219 Pv / (Patm − Pv), with pressures in psia, and a standard wet-bulb relation: Pv = Pwb − (Patm − Pwb)(t − twb) / (2800 − 1.3 twb). Pwb is the saturation pressure of water at the wet-bulb temperature. We took that from a national-lab simulation library, which uses Psat = 611.657 exp(17.2799 − 4102.99 / (T − 35.719)) in pascals with T in kelvin. A chart or a phone app does the same arithmetic. What matters is that the enthalpy comes from a stated formula, not from memory.

Step 2: where 4.5 comes from

The total heat formula is Btu/h = 4.5 × CFM × (h in − h out). University notes list it exactly this way, with the enthalpy of the entering air minus the leaving air. The notes do not derive the 4.5, so here is our derivation. The federal test procedure defines standard air as dry air with a density of 0.075 lb/ft³. A CFM is cubic feet per minute, and a Btu/h figure is per hour, so:

0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h = 4.5 lb of dry air per hour for each CFM. Multiply by the enthalpy change in Btu/lb and you have Btu/h.

The sensible shortcut follows the same way. A national-lab home energy documentation page uses 0.075 lb/ft³ for sea-level air and 0.24 Btu/(lb F) for the specific heat of air. So 4.5 × 0.24 = 1.08, and sensible heat is Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × (dry-bulb in − dry-bulb out). University notes call 1.08 the air factor. Both constants assume standard air. Thinner air, from altitude or very warm air, weighs less per cubic foot, so the constants would overstate the heat moved. Correct for that, or use a density-based calculation, when the air is far from standard.

Step 3: latent and the sensible heat ratio

Total heat is the sum of the sensible and latent parts, so latent = total − sensible. The sensible heat ratio is SHR = sensible ÷ total. Those notes give the same relationship. An SHR near 0.75 to 0.80 means about three-quarters of the work went into cooling the air and the rest into drying it. The related lesson on sensible heat ratio shows how slower airflow shifts that split.

Worked example (ours)

This example is ours, not a measurement from a real unit. The inputs are assumptions: 1,200 CFM, return air at 78 F dry bulb and 65 F wet bulb, supply air at 58 F dry bulb and 56 F wet bulb, and sea-level pressure of 14.696 psia.

  1. Return air. Pwb at 65 F is 0.3059 psia. Pv = 0.3059 − (14.696 − 0.3059)(78 − 65) / (2800 − 1.3 × 65) = 0.2370 psia. W = 0.6219 × 0.2370 / (14.696 − 0.2370) = 0.01019. h = 0.2405 × 78 + 0.01019 × (1060.5 + 0.45 × 78) = 29.93 Btu/lb.
  2. Supply air. Pwb at 56 F is 0.2222 psia. Pv = 0.2115 psia. W = 0.00908. Below 70 F we use the other vapor term: h = 0.2405 × 58 + 0.00908 × (1061.7 + 0.439 × 58) = 23.82 Btu/lb.
  3. Enthalpy change. 29.93 − 23.82 = 6.10 Btu/lb.
  4. Total. 4.5 × 1,200 × 6.10 = 32,940 Btu/h.
  5. Sensible. 1.08 × 1,200 × (78 − 58) = 1.08 × 1,200 × 20 = 25,920 Btu/h.
  6. Latent. 32,940 − 25,920 = 7,020 Btu/h.
  7. SHR. 25,920 ÷ 32,940 = 0.79.

A cross-check using the grains form from the same university notes (latent = 0.69 × CFM × grains removed, with 71.4 grains in the return and 63.6 in the supply, so 7.8 grains) gives 0.69 × 1,200 × 7.8 = about 6,460 Btu/h. That is a little below the by-difference 7,020 because the 1.08 and 0.69 factors are rounded and treat the two parts separately. Expect the split to carry roughly 10 percent of slop. It is small next to what comes next.

Why airflow error dominates

Every term above multiplies CFM. A 15 percent airflow error shifts total, sensible and latent by 15 percent, and the SHR does not move at all, so nothing in your temperature readings warns you.

Assumed1,20032,94025,9207,0200.79
True flow 15% lower1,02028,00022,0325,9670.79
True flow 15% higher1,38037,88129,8088,0730.79

If you believe 1,200 CFM but the coil really sees 1,020, you report about 32,900 Btu/h while the coil delivers about 28,000. The report is high by roughly 4,900 Btu/h. A national-lab study of common contractor airflow tools at return grilles found typical errors of 5 percent or less for some devices and errors as large as 25 percent for others, and it noted that maker accuracy claims leave out many field error sources. A federal residential guideline lists 23 percent low airflow as costing about 4.7 percent of EER, which shows how common a large airflow shortfall is considered.

Temperature readings matter too. In our example, moving the return wet bulb by 1 F moves the return enthalpy to 29.17 or 30.70 Btu/lb, which changes the enthalpy difference to 5.35 or 6.88 and the total to about 28,900 or 37,200 Btu/h. That is a swing of about 12.6 percent. Scale it to the 0.2 F accuracy the test procedure asks of a lab wet-bulb sensor and the effect shrinks to about 2.5 percent, our own scaling. So good sling or aspirated technique can keep wet-bulb error well under a poor airflow measurement. Measure airflow with the best method available, and read the wet bulb only after the reading settles.

Using the number

Compare the result with the equipment's rated capacity at the conditions you measured, and watch the SHR. A low enthalpy drop with a normal temperature drop points toward airflow or a moisture problem, not a refrigerant leak by itself. A federal residential diagnostic guide calls the temperature split between return and supply the key diagnostic and lists airflow among the readings to take. Calibrate your thermometers first; see the related lesson on checking meters. Make sure the probe sits in the air that passes the coil, in the air stream only, and wait for readings to stop drifting.

Field card: Air-side capacity with enthalpy

Total from enthalpy, sensible from temperature, latent by difference.

  • Total Btu/h = 4.5 × CFM × (h in − h out). 4.5 = 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h.
  • Sensible Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × dT. 1.08 = 4.5 × 0.24.
  • Latent = total − sensible. SHR = sensible ÷ total.
  • Take dry bulb and wet bulb at return and at supply. Get enthalpy from a chart or a stated formula.
  • Constants assume standard air. Correct for altitude or very warm air.
  • Our example: 1,200 CFM gives 32,940 total, 25,920 sensible, 7,020 latent, SHR 0.79.
  • A 15 percent CFM error changes every capacity number by 15 percent and leaves SHR unchanged.
