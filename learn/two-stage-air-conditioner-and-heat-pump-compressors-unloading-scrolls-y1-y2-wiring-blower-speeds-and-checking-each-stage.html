Two-Stage AC and Heat Pump Compressors: Unloading Scrolls, Y1/Y2 Wiring and Checking Each Stage

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A two-stage outdoor unit has a low gear and a high gear. Most of the time the low gear is all a house needs, so the unit runs longer, quieter and drier. This lesson covers how the compressor makes its two capacities, how Y1 and Y2 (or a timer on the board) pick between them, how the blower follows, and how to check each stage with a meter and gauges.

Check the maker's data first

Check the maker's data first. Coil resistance, solenoid voltage and charging targets differ by compressor model and by unit. The figures below come from one compressor maker's literature and are examples, not specs for the unit you are standing at. Open the disconnect and lock it out before touching compressor leads or the solenoid plug. Line voltage on the outdoor unit can hurt you.

How the compressor makes two capacities

Unloading scroll. A scroll compressor pumps with two spiral wraps. In a two-stage scroll, a solenoid inside the compressor opens or closes bypass ports on the pumping elements. One compressor maker's literature for a residential two-stage scroll family describes a 24 volt DC coil. Energized means full load. De-energized means part load, about 67 percent of full-load capacity. The motor never stops when the unit steps between stages. In one version the solenoid moves a ring that closes the ports. In another, a small valve sends pressure to a lift ring that does the job. The maker notes that part load is the default state because most run hours are expected at low capacity, and that this lets a simple two-stage thermostat control capacity.

Two-speed reciprocating. Another design changes the motor, not the pump. A U.S. patent for a control of a two-speed single-phase compressor describes a motor with run windings plus separate start windings for each speed, and contactors that connect one set or the other, with interlocks so both cannot close together. Low speed pumps less refrigerant, so capacity falls with speed.

Inverter compressors that vary speed continuously are a different animal; see Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.

Y1 and Y2 versus a board with its own timer

With a two-stage thermostat, the thermostat decides. Y1 is the first-stage cooling call and Y2 is the second-stage call. The maker's literature says a two-stage thermostat drives the system and that Y2 energizes the unloader solenoid. In that arrangement, Y1 alone gives low stage and Y1 plus Y2 gives high stage. If a protection board sits in the line, the same literature says the Y2 signal to the solenoid can be delayed about five seconds after startup so the compressor begins at part load.

Some systems take a single Y from a basic thermostat and let the outdoor board stage on its own timer: start low, then step to high if the call lasts a set number of minutes. The delay belongs to the maker, so look in the installation instructions. With a timer, low stage only is normal until the timer expires. With a two-stage thermostat, a missing Y2 at the board is a wiring or thermostat fault. Review Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats for terminal letters and the 24 volt basics.

Matching blower speed to each stage

Less refrigerant flow at low stage means the coil needs less air. If the blower stayed on its high tap, the coil would run warmer, discharge air would be less cold, and less moisture would come out. So the indoor blower follows the stage: a lower speed (a lower tap, or a lower programmed ECM profile) on Y1, a higher one on Y1 plus Y2. How the blower learns the stage depends on the equipment: separate Y1 and Y2 terminals on the air handler or furnace board, a communicating link, or dip switches and jumpers. The taps are set in the installation instructions for each model. Our field assumption for a worked example is 350 to 400 cfm per ton of capacity being delivered; the maker's table for the matched indoor unit overrides any rule of thumb. On ECM motors, speed is set by board settings, not taps; see ECM Blower Motors.

Why low stage runs longer and dries better

A mild day does not need full capacity. A single-stage unit sized for the hot day satisfies the thermostat quickly and shuts off. A National Renewable Energy Laboratory paper on dehumidification in energy-efficient homes found that periods of high indoor humidity generally occurred when the air conditioner ran at part load, and that nearly half the hours above 60 percent relative humidity came when the unit was barely running (a run-time fraction of 0 to 0.1). It also states that an oversized system with shorter run time removes less moisture.

A Florida Solar Energy Center report for the DOE Building America program on variable-capacity heat pumps in low-load homes makes the same point from the other side. It says such units can run with extended run time, 65 to 70 percent of a typical day compared with 30 to 35 percent for a fixed-capacity system, that cycling inhibits moisture removal, and that using the lowest capacity consistently for longer periods avoids cycling and overcooling. That report studies inverter units, so it supports the principle but is not a measurement of a two-stage scroll. Low stage also gives a lower coil airflow per ton of capacity, which is how the humidity side improves; our sensible heat ratio lesson shows the arithmetic.

How a tech checks each stage

  1. Confirm the call. Read 24 V AC between C and Y1, and between C and Y2, at the outdoor control. If the system uses a timer, note the time Y1 starts, and watch whether it steps up.
  2. Solenoid voltage. The maker we read lists a control input of 18 to 28 volts AC or DC for the 24 V solenoid. With the call for high stage present, read the solenoid plug. That literature gives about 15 to 27 volts DC at the plug of a rectified plug, and says one protection board puts out less (4 to 18 volts DC). With no Y2, the plug should read near zero.
  3. Coil resistance. With power off and the plug removed, check the coil for continuity and no ground. The same literature lists 33.6 ohms for one model family and much higher values (350 to 1640 ohms) for another, so only the maker's number for your compressor counts. Open or grounded means a bad solenoid.
  4. Amps on the common leg. Clamp the compressor lead. The maker says amperage should rise when the unloader switches from part load to full load and fall when it switches back, with the size of the change depending on conditions and voltage. It gives no fixed percentage. Technique is in our clamp-meter lesson. No change in amps when Y2 comes on means the unit is stuck in one stage.
  5. Pressures. Gauges should be read in each stage, after the system has settled, and compared with the maker's charging data for that stage. As a general field note (ours, not sourced), expect lower head pressure and higher suction pressure at low stage than at high stage in the same weather, and do not charge to a number from the wrong stage.
  6. Forcing high stage. The cleanest way is to ask for it: raise the call at a two-stage thermostat, or use the board's test or service mode if the installation instructions describe one. Let the compressor start, wait through any start delay (the maker we read describes a five second delay on the Y2 signal at startup), then read. On a bench, with the compressor out of the system, that literature describes applying 18 to 28 volts to the unloader plug to hear the solenoid click. Do not jumper around a safety or a board lockout to force a stage.

Worked example (ours): a 3-ton two-stage unit on a mild day

All numbers here are assumptions, chosen for illustration, unless a source is named. Real units differ.

  • High-stage capacity: 3 tons = 36,000 Btu/h (1 ton = 12,000 Btu/h).
  • Low-stage capacity: 67 percent of full load, from the compressor maker, giving 0.67 x 36,000 = 24,120, which we round to 24,000 Btu/h (2 tons).
  • Compressor current at 230 V (our assumption): 16.0 A high stage, 10.5 A low stage. Power factor assumed 0.90.

Airflow. High stage at 400 cfm per ton: 3 x 400 = 1,200 cfm. Low stage at 350 cfm per ton of the 2 tons delivered: 2 x 350 = 700 cfm. The low-stage blower runs at 700 / 1,200 = 58 percent of high-stage airflow.

Power. High: 230 x 16.0 x 0.90 = 3,312 W, about 3.31 kW. Low: 230 x 10.5 x 0.90 = 2,174 W, about 2.17 kW. Amps fall by 5.5 / 16.0 = 34 percent while capacity falls by 33 percent, so the compressor does about the same Btu per watt in this example. Fan power is ignored.

Run time. Assume a mild afternoon load of 14,000 Btu/h averaged over 12 hours, which totals 14,000 x 12 = 168,000 Btu.

  • High stage only: 168,000 / 36,000 = 4.67 hours of run, 4.67 / 12 = 39 percent run time, or about 23 minutes on per hour.
  • Low stage only: 168,000 / 24,000 = 7.0 hours, 7.0 / 12 = 58 percent run time, or about 35 minutes on per hour.
  • Compressor energy: high, 4.67 h x 3.31 kW = 15.5 kWh; low, 7.0 h x 2.17 kW = 15.2 kWh.

Same cooling, about the same compressor energy, but low stage runs 12 more minutes each hour. That gives the coil more time wet, less startup, and steadier air, which is where the cited studies point on humidity. If the load rises above 24,000 Btu/h, low stage cannot keep up and the thermostat or timer calls the second stage.

Field card: Two-Stage AC and Heat Pump Compressors: Unloading Scrolls, Y1/Y2 Wiring and Checking Each Stage

Y2 on, solenoid on, amps up, blower up.

  • Unloading scroll: solenoid energized is full load; de-energized is about 67 percent (one maker's figure).
  • Two-speed reciprocating units change speed by switching motor windings.
  • Two-stage thermostat: Y1 low, Y1 plus Y2 high. Single Y: the board may stage on a timer.
  • Blower follows the stage: lower speed on low, higher on high.
  • Solenoid check: 18 to 28 V control input in one maker's data; coil ohms vary by model.
  • Amps should rise going to high stage and fall going back to low.
  • Read pressures per stage against the maker's chart.
  • Lock out power before touching leads or the plug.
