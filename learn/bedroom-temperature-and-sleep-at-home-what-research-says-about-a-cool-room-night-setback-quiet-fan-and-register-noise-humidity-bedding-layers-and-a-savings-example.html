Bedroom Temperature and Sleep at Home: Cool Room, Night Setback and Quiet Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many people lie awake in a bedroom that is too warm, too dry, too noisy or too cold at the far end of the house. This lesson sums up what general health sources say about a cool bedroom, then shows how a night thermostat schedule, quiet equipment settings and simple bedding changes fit together. It is general education, not medical advice.

What health sources say

A federal public health page on sleep basics lists a quiet, relaxing, cool bedroom among the steps that support good sleep. A federal medical library patient page says the same thing in plainer terms: keep the room cool, but not so cool that you wake up cold, and keep it as quiet as you can. Neither page gives a single magic number, and we will not invent one. People differ, and so do ages and health conditions. If sleep problems continue, that is a question for a clinician, not a thermostat.

A published review of the thermal environment and sleep (a journal article on a federal medical library site) adds context. It reports that the air under bed covers normally sits around 32 to 34 degrees C (about 90 to 93 F) at 40 to 60 percent relative humidity. It also reports that heat tends to increase wakefulness, and that in real homes with bedding and clothing, heat was the bigger problem than moderate cold. In the cold range it examined, 13 to 23 degrees C (55 to 73 F), sleep stages did not differ significantly when people had bedding and clothing. The authors also suggest keeping winter bedroom temperatures above 10 degrees C (50 F). Those are research findings about test subjects, not a setting for your house.

The idea: cool room, warm bed

The practical takeaway is that the bed can be warm while the room is cool. A heavier blanket, flannel sheets, warmer sleepwear or a second layer you can kick off cost nothing in fuel. Raising the whole house to make a bedroom comfortable heats every room to fix one body. Layers let each sleeper adjust. If two people disagree, separate covers on one bed often settle it better than a compromise setpoint.

Night schedules and setback

Most programmable and smart thermostats let you set a lower heating temperature for the sleeping hours and a warm-up ahead of waking. This is called a setback. In summer the same idea works in reverse: a slightly higher cooling setpoint during the day when no one is home, and a comfortable but not icy setpoint at night. Two cautions. First, a thermostat setpoint is not the bedroom temperature. The thermostat sits in one hallway or living area, so a bedroom over a garage or at the end of a long duct run can run several degrees off from it. Second, a deep setback with a heat pump can trigger backup heat during the morning warm-up in some systems, which can cost more than it saves. Smaller steps and an earlier, gentler warm-up avoid that. Our lesson on rooms over garages covers the weak-room causes.

Worked example (our own): what a 5 F setback can save

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any real house.

  • Assumption: the house loses 625 Btu/h for every degree F between inside and outside.
  • Assumption: the thermostat is set back 5 F for 8 hours each night.
  • Assumption: the furnace is 95 percent efficient, and 1 therm is 100,000 Btu.
  • Assumption: 150 heating nights in the season.
  1. Heat loss avoided per hour: 625 Btu/h per F x 5 F = 3,125 Btu/h.
  2. Per night: 3,125 Btu/h x 8 h = 25,000 Btu.
  3. Gas input to make that heat: 25,000 Btu / 0.95 = about 26,316 Btu, or 0.263 therm.
  4. Over the season: 0.263 therm x 150 nights = about 39.5 therms.

This is an upper bound. It ignores the warm-up period, when the furnace has to recover the lost temperature, and it ignores the heat stored in walls, floors and furniture, which keeps the house from cooling the full 5 F right away and then must be restored. It also assumes the outdoor temperature stays the same. Real savings are lower, and they depend on how well the house is insulated and sealed. We do not price the result, because fuel costs change and are outside this lesson.

Quiet operation

Noise wakes people and delays sleep, and the patient page above tells readers to make the room as quiet as they can. Equipment and air movement are common sources:

  • Fan speed. Many blowers can run on a lower speed setting, and a lower speed moves less air at less noise. Do not slow it so far that the system overheats or ices up; ask a technician to check airflow and the blower settings.
  • Register and grille noise. A whistle usually means air is being pushed through too small an opening. Fully open dampers, a clean filter and a larger grille can help. Closing many registers raises duct pressure and noise. See doors that slam or whistle for pressure problems with the door closed.
  • Ductless night mode. Many wall units offer a quiet or night setting that lowers fan speed and may lift the setpoint slowly. Check your unit's own manual for what its setting does.
  • Outdoor unit. A unit that sits beneath a bedroom window can be heard inside. A technician can look at mounts and fan condition.

Humidity in winter and summer

A university extension guide on moisture in the home says that relative humidity from about 25 percent in winter to 50 percent in summer is generally considered healthy, and that moisture problems can start above 50 percent. A second university extension page says that when it is below freezing outside, about 30 percent is unlikely to cause problems, while 40 percent may fog windows; it also says very low humidity can dry nasal passages. In summer, the first guide suggests a properly sized air conditioner or a stand-alone dehumidifier. Our lessons on window condensation and on night cooling with window fans go further.

Check with a cheap room thermometer

Put a basic thermometer with a humidity reading on a nightstand, away from windows and registers, and read it at bedtime and on waking for a week. Compare it with the thermostat. If the bedroom is several degrees off, the fix is usually airflow, insulation or air sealing rather than a different setpoint. Write the numbers down and give them to your technician; they are worth more than "it feels cold." These small devices vary in accuracy, so treat readings as a comparison, not a precise test.

Babies and special cases

Infants, older adults and people with health conditions have different needs. We do not give guidance here beyond what the cited sources state; see our lesson on heating and cooling a baby's nursery and ask your clinician.

Field card: Bedroom Temperature and Sleep at Home

Cool room, warm bed, quiet air, measured with a thermometer.

  • Health sources recommend a quiet, relaxing, cool bedroom, but not so cool you wake up cold.
  • Use bedding and layers before raising the whole house temperature.
  • A night setback of 5 F for 8 h at 625 Btu/h per F is at most 25,000 Btu, about 0.263 therm of gas input at 95 percent, about 39.5 therms over 150 nights. Real savings are lower.
  • Quiet comes from lower fan speed with proper airflow, open dampers and a clean filter.
  • Winter indoor humidity: keep it low enough that windows do not run with water.
  • Measure the bedroom itself; the thermostat may be elsewhere.
