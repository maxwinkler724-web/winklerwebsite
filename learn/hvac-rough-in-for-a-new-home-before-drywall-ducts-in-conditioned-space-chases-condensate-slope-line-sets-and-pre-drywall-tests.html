HVAC Rough-In for a New Home Before Drywall

Last updated: 2026-11-02

Drywall hides every duct, pipe and wire you install. Once it is up, a leaky duct joint or a backward drain slope means cutting a ceiling open. This lesson walks through the rough-in checklist for HVAC in a new home: where ducts go, how chases and returns are framed, how condensate and line sets are routed, how penetrations are sealed, and what gets tested before cover-up.

Check your local code

Check your local code. The IRC, IMC and IECC are model codes. States and cities adopt different editions and add changes, and we name them here only in general terms. This page was built from federal energy pages, national-lab construction guides and a state copy of mechanical code text, all read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. Follow the equipment installation instructions, the adopted local code and your inspector.

Put the ducts inside the envelope

The first decision is on the plans, not on the jobsite. A national-lab construction guide says ducts and air handlers in conditioned space avoid extreme attic and crawl space temperatures and need fewer holes through the building envelope (Ducts in Dropped Ceilings). For what goes wrong with ducts outside, see Ductwork Basics and Common Problems. Duct size and layout come from the design; see Duct Sizing Basics: Friction Rate and Equivalent Length.

Chases and soffits

A chase is a framed channel that carries ducts or pipes through the house. A soffit, or dropped ceiling, is a chase along the ceiling. The same guide gives a sequence worth copying. Hang drywall at the top of the planned chase first, so the chase has a continuous air barrier against the attic or floor cavity above. Hang the supply ducts from that ceiling. Then frame the chase sides and bottom without piercing the ceiling above, and drywall them with the rest of the house. It also says framers should leave a three-quarter inch gap between the wall top plate and the bottom chord of the trusses, so the ceiling drywall can run over the top plates. Plumbers and electricians should be told not to run pipes or wires through the chase or cut holes in its walls.

Build and seal as much of the duct system as you can on the ground, then lift it into place. Rigid duct is easier to hoist than flex. Support spacing is covered in Duct Hangers and Supports.

Return paths

Supply air with no way back is a rough-in problem you can fix cheaply now. With a bedroom door shut, supply air keeps coming in while the room has no good path to a central return, so the room goes positive. The Building America guide on ducted returns says typical half-inch to three-quarter inch door undercuts do not give enough return volume, especially over carpet, and that building cavities should not be used as return paths. Options are a dedicated return duct from the room, a transom opening, a transfer grille or a jump duct. For its pressure target, the guide cites a federal efficiency program target: with doors closed and the air handler at its highest design speed, a room should sit within plus or minus 3 pascals of the rest of the house (Ducted Returns). The soffit guide gives a transom rule of thumb of at least one and a half times the room's cumulative supply duct area. Rough in the transom or return duct now, while the framing is open.

Condensate: slope, trap and secondary protection

Cooling coils make water, and the drain has to carry it away by gravity. A state-published copy of the 2012 mechanical code text says the drain piping must slope at least one-eighth unit vertical in 12 units horizontal (1 percent) in the direction of discharge. It also requires an auxiliary drain pan at least 1-1/2 inches deep where overflow could damage building components, with either its own separate drain, or, if it has no separate drain, a water-level detection device that shuts the equipment off before the pan overflows. In that copy the slope rule is Section 307.2.1 and the pan rule is Section 307.2.3; numbering and details change between editions, so confirm yours (state copy of the 2012 text). The same text places the detection device upstream of the primary drain trap. Whether and where a trap goes on a given unit is in the equipment instructions, so build the trap space and cleanout access into the rough-in.

Line sets, gas and vent, and thermostat wire

  • Line sets. Plan the route and the wall or floor penetration now. Keep both lines reachable, avoid kinks and sharp bends, and leave room for insulation. Size and insulation are in Refrigerant Line Set Sizing and Refrigerant Line Set Insulation. Where possible, keep brazed joints out of closed walls and chases where nobody can reach them.
  • Gas and vent. Gas piping and flue or vent pipe go through the framing under their own rules and the vent maker's clearance table. A national-lab guide on flue shafts says to seal combustion flue penetrations with fire-rated caulk plus listed collars or sheet metal, and to use a heat-resistant air barrier around the flue (Air Sealing Duct and Flue Shafts). Keep insulation and other combustibles back from the vent by the clearances the vent maker lists.
  • Thermostat wire. Pull the cable before drywall, to a clean spot on the wall, and leave a labeled coil at the equipment. Check the conductor count the equipment and thermostat call for; see Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Seal the penetrations

Every hole you drill is an air leak until it is sealed. For wire holes, the Building America guide says to use caulk or canned spray foam on holes through top and bottom plates, to keep holes no more than 1 inch wider than the wire, and that fibrous insulation is not an air barrier and cannot seal holes. It adds that fire-rated products are needed at fire-rated assemblies (Air Sealing Electrical Wiring). The plumbing guide gives the same cut-size limit for pipes, says to seal after the pipes are in and before the drywall is completed, and says to check seals by eye before insulation and drywall go in (Air Sealing Plumbing and Piping). Duct boots are sealed to the drywall with foam or caulk, with gaps under three-eighths inch handled by sealant and larger gaps by mesh tape and mastic, which means the drywall crew must cut boot openings no more than three-eighths inch oversize on every side (Sealing Duct Boots to Floor or Drywall).

For duct joints, the metal-duct guide says to seal seams with mastic or listed metal tape, put mesh tape under mastic on gaps wider than one-eighth inch, and never rely on ordinary cloth duct tape, which it says can dry out and fail quickly (Sealed and Insulated Metal Ducts).

Keep construction dust out of the ducts

The dust guide says to store ducts and equipment clean and dry, wrap them in plastic if needed, keep the system off during drywall finishing, sanding and sawing, and cover the air handler while it is off. After the ducts are in, it says to seal every duct end, including boots and return intakes, with plastic held by painter's tape until flooring, cabinets and finish carpentry are done. A fiberglass filter catches large debris but not fine dust, so it is not enough alone. Where a boot is not yet covered by a register, cover the floor opening with plywood so nobody falls into it (Removing Construction Debris from Ducts).

Tests and inspections before cover-up

Order of work: finish duct sealing, finish penetration sealing, then test, then inspect, then insulate and hang drywall. The metal-duct guide says a rater should look over every connection (trunk-to-branch, boots, splices, return box, jump ducts) before drywall, and that a duct tester run at that stage finds leaks while they are still reachable. The federal energy codes site says the 2009 and 2012 IECC allow duct tightness to be verified either by a post-construction test or by a rough-in test, run at 25 pascals (0.1 inch water column) with all registers sealed (energycodes.gov FAQ). For 2012 it lists a rough-in limit of 4 cfm25 per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area with the air handler installed, and 3 without it. Whether a test is required for ducts sitting entirely inside the envelope depends on the adopted edition and local amendments, and the page we read does not address it, so ask the inspector. The test itself is explained in Duct Leakage Testing: Total vs Leakage to Outside and CFM25.

Also before cover-up: pour water into the condensate pan and watch it reach the drain; confirm the slope with a level; check that every chase is closed at the top; and have the framing, mechanical and energy inspections called in the order your jurisdiction uses. If the house has hydronic piping, its pressure test is in New Hydronic and Chilled Water Piping: Testing, Flushing and Cleaning.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example, made-up numbers. Part 1, condensate drop. Assume one straight 30 ft run in a floor joist bay with no rises. Assume the bay is 10 inches deep and that pipe, hangers and clearance already use 2 inches of it, leaving 8 inches for the drop.

  • At 1/8 inch per foot: 30 ft × 1/8 in/ft = 3.75 in total drop. Left over: 8 − 3.75 = 4.25 in. Fits. This is 1/8 in per 12 in, the 1 percent minimum quoted above.
  • At 1/4 inch per foot: 30 ft × 1/4 in/ft = 7.5 in total drop. Left over: 8 − 7.5 = 0.5 in. It fits on paper, but only with half an inch to spare, so a joist notch or a slightly low hanger could use it up. The slope is about 2.1 percent (1/4 ÷ 12).

The start end is the high end; the end at the drain is lower by the drop. So 1/8 inch per foot meets the minimum with room to spare, and the steeper slope eats most of the bay.

Part 2, duct leakage target. Assume a 2,000 sq ft house, a target of 4 cfm25 per 100 sq ft of conditioned floor area (the 2012 rough-in value above for a system with the air handler; your jurisdiction may differ), and a measured result of 95 cfm25.

  • Floor area in units of 100 sq ft: 2,000 ÷ 100 = 20.
  • Allowed leakage: 4 × 20 = 80 cfm25.
  • Measured, per 100 sq ft: 95 ÷ 20 = 4.75.
  • Result: 95 is above 80 by 15 cfm25, so this system fails the assumed target. To pass, the tech must find and seal at least 15 cfm25 of leaks, about 16 percent of the measured flow (15 ÷ 95).

Field card: HVAC Rough-In Before Drywall

Plan it, seal it, test it, then cover it.

  • Keep ducts, chases and the air handler inside the air barrier.
  • Drywall the chase top first; keep other trades out of the chase.
  • Give each bedroom a return path: ducted return, transom or jump duct.
  • Condensate drain: at least 1/8 in per ft, falling the whole way; 30 ft needs 3.75 in of drop.
  • Secondary pan or water-level shutoff where code requires it; follow the equipment instructions for the trap.
  • Seal holes with caulk or foam, never loose insulation; use fire-rated products where required.
  • Cover boots and the air handler until finish work is done.
  • Leakage per 100 sq ft = cfm25 ÷ (floor area ÷ 100). Test and inspect before insulation and drywall.
