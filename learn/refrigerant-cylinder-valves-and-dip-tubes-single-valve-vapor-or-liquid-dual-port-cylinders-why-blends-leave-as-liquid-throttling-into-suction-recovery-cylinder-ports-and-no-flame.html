Refrigerant Cylinder Valves and Dip Tubes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A refrigerant cylinder is not just a tank with a valve. Where the valve connects inside the cylinder decides whether you get vapor or liquid, and for a blend that choice decides whether the charge stays the same mixture. This lesson walks through single-valve and dual-port cylinders, recovery cylinder ports, and the safe way to meter liquid into a running system.

Why the port matters

Inside a cylinder that is partly full, liquid sits at the bottom and vapor fills the space above it. Whatever the open port can reach is what comes out. A port that opens into the vapor space gives vapor. A port connected to a tube that reaches the bottom gives liquid. That tube is the dip tube, and everything else in this lesson follows from that one idea.

Single-valve cylinders

A single-valve cylinder has one port at the top, opening to the vapor space. Stand it upright and it gives vapor. A technical article from a trade association says that when a cylinder has no dip tube, it must be turned upside down for liquid charging, and that not every maker fits dip tubes. Turned over, the same valve now sits under the liquid, so liquid flows out. Treat that as a handling job, not a shortcut: secure the cylinder, keep the valve protected, and follow the label and the maker's instructions for how that cylinder is meant to be used. Check the cylinder before you rely on it. A cylinder with a dip tube and two marked valves is handled differently, as the next section shows.

Dual-port cylinders with a dip tube

A trade association recovery training manual describes a recovery cylinder with a combination valve that gives access to liquid or vapor with the cylinder standing upright. One valve connects to the top of the cylinder for vapor. The other connects to a dip tube that draws from the bottom for liquid. Each valve handle is marked as VAPOR or LIQUID. A compliance plan from a college says cylinders of near-azeotropic blends are equipped with dip tubes so liquid can leave in the upright position.

Reading the valve markings

Read the handle word before you connect a hose, every time. Do not assume the left valve is liquid. Then read the cylinder itself. The same recovery manual says recovery cylinders are gray with yellow in the shoulder area, and a college plan states the guideline as gray with a yellow cap. The manual also says each cylinder is stenciled or stamped with a maximum gross weight, which is the cylinder plus its contents, and that the tare weight is usually stamped on the collar. For stamped dates, water capacity and pre-use checks, see the related lesson on refillable and recovery cylinders. A university safety program also warns that color-coding is not a reliable way to identify contents, and that labels are what identify them.

Look at the valve guard too. A federal transportation rule requires that valve protection, such as a securely attached metal cap or strong outer packaging, be in place for cylinders in shipment (49 CFR 173.301). A university safety guide says cylinders should travel with protective caps on, and that you never lift a cylinder by its cap.

Why blends must leave the cylinder as liquid

A blend is a mixture of components that boil at different temperatures. Vapor above the liquid is richer in the more volatile component. Draw vapor and you take more of that component than the nominal mix has. The same trade article recommends liquid charging rather than vapor charging to minimize composition changes. The other related lessons cover how fractionation shows up in leaks and what glide means at the gauges, so here we only do the cylinder side.

Throttling liquid into the suction side

Liquid in the compressor is a problem, so a technician who charges liquid into a running system has to keep it from arriving as liquid. The college plan says the liquid should be throttled into the low side so it vaporizes before it enters the compressor, and the trade article says to meter the refrigerant so it vaporizes before it reaches the compressor. In practice that means a gauge manifold valve cracked open just enough, a hose that is not kinked or loaded with other lines, and a hand ready to close the valve. Our own habit is to add a little, wait, and watch suction pressure and superheat before adding more. If you see frost or hear liquid noise at the compressor, close the valve.

Recovery cylinder liquid and vapor ports

The same two ports show up on a recovery cylinder. Federal rules at 40 CFR 82.156 require evacuating the refrigerant, including all the liquid refrigerant, to the required levels with certified recovery equipment, and the college plan notes that liquid recovery is preferred where the equipment allows it, because it removes many contaminants in suspension. Connect the recovery machine to the cylinder's port marked LIQUID or VAPOR as your setup calls for, and keep the cylinder on a scale. The college plan also directs technicians to monitor the weight of refrigerant in the recovery cylinder. Both sources set the limit at 80 percent of capacity: do not fill past it. Never use a standard disposable cylinder for recovery. A federal definition also lists a self-sealing valve, one that closes automatically when it is not dispensing, as a way to cut losses at the hose end.

Never use a flame on a cylinder

A cylinder that is cold and low on pressure tempts people to reach for a torch. Do not. The trade manual says never apply open flames, live steam or excessive heat to a refrigeration cylinder, and warns it can explode. One university safety guide says no part of a cylinder should be subjected to temperatures above 125 degrees F, and another sets the same 125 degrees F (52 degrees C) ceiling for flammable gas cylinders. A federal rule also requires enough outage that a filled cylinder is not liquid full at 131 degrees F (49 CFR 173.301). Overfilled or hot cylinders can fail, which is covered in the related overfill lesson along with the safe ways to warm one. A federal workplace rule (29 CFR 1910.101) also expects employers to check that cylinders in their control are safe as far as a visual inspection can show, and the transportation rule says a cylinder with fire or heat damage may not be filled and offered for shipment.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not data for any real product.

Assumptions. A two-component blend, nominal makeup 50 percent component A and 50 percent component B by weight. Component A is the more volatile one, meaning it boils at the lower temperature. Scale readings are taken before and after drawing. We assume the vapor that comes off averages 53 percent A and 47 percent B. That 53 percent is our guess to show the arithmetic; the real value depends on temperature and how the cylinder is drawn.

  1. Start: gross weight 38.0 lb, tare 13.0 lb. Net charge = 38.0 - 13.0 = 25.0 lb. Component A = 25.0 x 0.50 = 12.5 lb. Component B = 12.5 lb.
  2. Draw vapor until the scale reads 28.0 lb. Removed = 38.0 - 28.0 = 10.0 lb.
  3. Removed A = 10.0 x 0.53 = 5.3 lb. Removed B = 10.0 x 0.47 = 4.7 lb.
  4. Remaining net = 28.0 - 13.0 = 15.0 lb. Remaining A = 12.5 - 5.3 = 7.2 lb. Remaining B = 12.5 - 4.7 = 7.8 lb. Check: 7.2 + 7.8 = 15.0 lb.
  5. New makeup: A = 7.2 / 15.0 = 48.0 percent, B = 7.8 / 15.0 = 52.0 percent.

Result: the remaining 15.0 lb has drifted from 50/50 to 48/52 after one vapor draw. Compare drawing 10.0 lb as liquid: it removes the nominal 50/50 mix, 5.0 lb of each, so the remaining 15.0 lb holds 7.5 lb of each and stays 50/50. Each further vapor draw would push the mix a little further. This is why a half-used cylinder drawn as vapor is a poor source for a blend.

Field card: Refrigerant Cylinder Valves and Dip Tubes

The port decides what leaves the cylinder.

  • Single valve: upright gives vapor, inverted gives liquid, unless the cylinder has a dip tube.
  • Dual port: read the handle word, VAPOR or LIQUID, before connecting.
  • Blends leave as liquid. Vapor draws shift the mix toward the more volatile component.
  • Meter liquid into the low side so it vaporizes before the compressor.
  • Recovery cylinders: gray with yellow shoulder, weigh as you go, 80 percent maximum.
  • No flame, steam or torch on a cylinder. Keep it under 125 degrees F.
