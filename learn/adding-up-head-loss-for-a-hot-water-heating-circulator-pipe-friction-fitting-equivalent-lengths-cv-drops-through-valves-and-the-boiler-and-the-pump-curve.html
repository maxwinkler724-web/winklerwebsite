Adding Up Head Loss for a Hot-Water Heating Circulator

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A circulator does not need to lift water anywhere in a closed heating loop. It only needs to overcome friction. This lesson shows how to add every source of friction in the worst circuit into one number in feet of head, so you can put a dot on a pump curve and know whether the pump will deliver the flow.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. The friction, fitting and valve numbers below are for teaching. Real jobs use the tube, valve and boiler data for the actual products, and the circulator's own curve.

Only one circuit counts

In a closed loop with no open surface, the pump is not lifting water to a higher tank. A US Department of Energy guide on variable speed pumping says a closed circulating loop has no static head, so friction is its only resistance. The water going up a riser is balanced by water coming down the return. Height therefore drops out of the sum, and you do not add the building height.

The second rule is about which pipe to add up. If several zones run at once, each branch sees the same pressure difference across it, so the branch with the highest loss sets what the pump must produce. Add up that longest or highest-loss circuit, from the pump around through the boiler, supply main, branch, emitters and back. The shorter branches are throttled or balanced to match. Do not add all the branches together. The shared piping, such as the boiler and the main, is counted at the full system flow, and the branch piping at the branch flow.

Step 1: pipe friction

Straight pipe loss is quoted in feet of head per 100 ft. It comes from the Darcy friction method, covered in university fluid mechanics notes such as those at Purdue, where wall loss grows with length, with velocity squared, and with a friction factor that depends on flow regime and roughness. In practice you read it from a friction chart or table for your tube and flow. For flow figures and the velocity limits that go with them, see our pipe sizing and circulator head lesson instead of repeating them here.

Our own calculation for the example below: 6 GPM through 3/4 inch type M copper, with an assumed inside diameter of 0.811 in, water at 140°F, an assumed kinematic viscosity of 5.5 × 10-6 ft²/s and a smooth drawn-tube roughness of 0.000005 ft, gives a velocity near 3.7 ft/s and a loss near 6.9 ft per 100 ft. Smaller diameter or higher flow raises it fast, since loss climbs roughly with the square of velocity.

Step 2: fittings as equivalent length

Elbows, tees and valves also waste energy where the flow turns or squeezes. The Purdue notes call these minor losses, say they can dominate when straight runs are short, and show that a loss coefficient can be restated as a length of straight pipe that causes the same loss. That length is the equivalent length. You add it to the real pipe length and then multiply by the friction per foot.

Tables of equivalent length vary by maker and by tube size, so look up your own. The values we use in the example are assumptions for 3/4 inch tube: 2 ft per 90° elbow, 3 ft per tee with the flow turning through the side port, and 1 ft per full-port ball valve. A globe valve can be 10 times worse than an elbow, which is why shops avoid them as isolation valves.

Step 3: components from Cv

Zone valves, check valves, strainers and mixing valves often come with a Cv instead of an equivalent length. Cv is the flow in GPM of 60°F water that causes a 1 psi drop. Rearranged, the drop at any flow is:

ΔP (psi) = (GPM ÷ Cv)2

Because the flow is squared, doubling the flow quadruples the drop. Convert psi to feet of head with 1 psi = 2.31 ft of water. The federal unit reference from NIST lists 1 psi as 6,894.757 Pa and 1 ft of water at 39.2°F as 2,988.98 Pa, which gives 2.307 ft per psi. Hot water is lighter than cold, so at 140°F one psi is about 2.35 ft, roughly 2 percent more. That is small next to the other uncertainties, and the safety margin covers it. Our balancing valves and Cv lesson covers how Cv is used for flow checks.

Step 4: the boiler

The boiler heat exchanger has its own drop. Take it from the boiler's installation manual, usually a chart of pressure drop or head versus flow. Read it at the flow your loop will actually have, not at the maker's highest flow. If the boiler is piped primary and secondary, only the part of the boiler in your pumped path counts; see circulator placement and the point of no pressure change.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. A single zone circuit carries 6 GPM through 3/4 inch type M copper. All of the following are assumptions we chose for teaching, not measurements.

  • Straight pipe, assumed 100 ft including the emitter run, at 6.9 ft per 100 ft: 100 × 6.9 ÷ 100 = 6.9 ft.
  • Fittings, assumed: 12 elbows × 2 ft = 24 ft; 2 side-port tees × 3 ft = 6 ft; 2 ball valves × 1 ft = 2 ft. Total 32 ft equivalent. Loss = 32 × 6.9 ÷ 100 = 2.208, or 2.2 ft.
  • Zone valve, assumed Cv 6.0: ΔP = (6 ÷ 6.0)2 = 1.0 psi. Head = 1.0 × 2.31 = 2.3 ft.
  • Boiler, assumed manual figure of 3.0 ft at 6 GPM: 3.0 ft.

Total: 6.9 + 2.2 + 2.3 + 3.0 = 14.4 ft of head at 6 GPM. The picture shows how the parts stack up. Notice that the straight pipe is only about half the total. The valve and boiler together take as much as 37 percent, so skipping them is the usual way a pump ends up too small.

Step 5: a sensible safety margin

Add margin once, at the end, not at every step. A tenth on top of a careful total is plenty: 14.4 × 1.10 = 15.84, so we use about 15.8 ft. If every line was rounded up, then the pump was oversized three times before it was ordered. The same DOE guide warns that adding margin to the calculated system curve tends to produce an oversized pump that runs at excess flow or gets throttled, wasting energy and shortening pump life, and it estimates that around 75 percent of pump systems are oversized. Adding for known unknowns, such as a few extra elbows, is fair. Padding for fear is not.

Step 6: plot it on the pump curve

A pump curve shows head the pump produces at each flow. The pump runs where its curve crosses the system curve, which starts at zero in a closed loop and rises with flow. The DOE guide makes the same point: change either curve and the operating point moves. Our pump curves lesson covers reading the chart. Here is the quick check for our example.

  1. Mark the design point: 6 GPM, 15.8 ft with margin (14.4 ft without).
  2. Check that the pump's curve passes on or above that point. The point must sit under the curve, not on its far right edge, where the pump is inefficient.
  3. Draw the system curve from the origin through the point. Since both the pipe and the Cv terms rise roughly with the square of flow, we approximate H = 14.4 × (GPM ÷ 6)2.
  4. Read where it crosses the curve.

Suppose the pump chart we assume reads 22 ft at 0 GPM, 20.6 ft at 4, 18.9 ft at 6, 16.4 ft at 8 and 13.2 ft at 10 GPM. That curve is 4.5 ft above the 14.4 ft need at 6 GPM, so the pump qualifies. Our system curve, H = 0.4 × GPM2, crosses it at about 6.7 GPM. With the 10 percent margin curve it would cross near 6.5 GPM. Both are a little over the 6 GPM target. That is a healthy result: a balancing valve or a speed setting can trim the extra, which is easier than finding flow that is missing. If the crossing came in below 6 GPM, move to a stronger pump or cut the losses, for example with a larger zone valve or fewer fittings. Zone valves versus zone circulators changes which circuit counts; see zone valves vs. zone circulators.

Field card: Adding up head loss

Closed loop: friction only, worst circuit only.

  • No static height in a closed loop. Do not add building height.
  • Pipe: feet of head per 100 ft for your tube at your flow, times (length + equivalent lengths) ÷ 100.
  • Fittings: convert to equivalent length from the maker's table, then use the pipe rate.
  • Cv parts: ΔP psi = (GPM ÷ Cv)2; feet of head = psi × 2.31.
  • Boiler: read the manual's drop at your actual flow.
  • Add the margin once, about 10 percent, at the end.
  • Plot GPM and head on the pump curve and read the crossing.
