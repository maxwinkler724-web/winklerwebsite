Cooling Tower Winter Operation: Basin Heaters, Bypass, Fan Reversal

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling tower works by evaporating water into cold air, so a tower that keeps running in winter is built to make water colder. That is useful for free cooling and data centers, and it is also how ice forms on louvers, fill and fans. This lesson covers what the makers say about keeping that ice under control.

Check the maker's literature

Check the maker's literature. Numbers below come from specific documents for specific towers. Your tower's manual and the maker's technical staff decide what applies to it. This page is general education.

Why towers that run in winter ice up

EVAPCO's open-tower page says ice formation is "a subject of management, not elimination." Delta Cooling Towers says that with enough heat load the water stays above freezing, and without one it cools toward the wet-bulb temperature or freezes. Delta also warns that low flow over the fill leaves semi-dry spots that freeze quickly. SPX adds that water at the bottom of the fill can be near 32 F even when the average leaving temperature reads 40 F. So the sensor you read can be warmer than the coldest part of the tower. For the basic parts of a tower, see Cooling Tower Basics.

Keep a heat load and keep the leaving water warm

  • Minimum leaving water temperature. EVAPCO's free-cooling guidance recommends at least 45 F and calls 42 F the absolute minimum. A BAC publication says to run the leaving water as warm as the system allows "but not less than 43 F." Those are different makers and different towers, so use the number in your tower's own documents. Delta's page gives no number.
  • No load, no operation. BAC says the tower should not run for extended periods without a heat load in subfreezing weather. SPX says not to run without a load or unattended for multi-day periods.
  • Flow across every cell. SPX says cells must handle a 50 percent flow turndown and recommends holding the highest flow you can. BAC says bypass valves should not cut flow below the allowable minimum, and that when cells are taken out of service the last cell in service should be the one at the far end, to avoid freezing at the distribution header ends.

Fans: speed, cycling and starts

Practices differ by tower type, so read your manual. SPX recommends a VFD on each cell, all following the same setpoint, ramped together, and cycling from minimum speed to off only as needed. EVAPCO says single-speed on/off cycling is the least preferred method on induced-draft towers, limits fan starts to six per hour, and says a variable-speed induced-draft fan should not run below 50 percent in sub-freezing weather. EVAPCO also says forced-draft towers need fans cycled or kept at low speed so condensate does not freeze on the drive parts. Both EVAPCO and BAC suggest a vibration cutout switch, since ice on blades throws a fan out of balance. Staging and drives are covered in Cooling Tower Fan VFDs, Staging and Condenser Water Reset.

Bypass: all or nothing

When the load drops too low, SPX says to send all flow straight to the cold-water basin and not resume flow over the fill until the hot water reaches the target. SPX's wording is not to modulate the bypass, because the fill can freeze in low-flow areas. EVAPCO says to use only a full-flow bypass during free cooling and not a partial one. BAC recommends a full-flow bypass for starting and stopping in subfreezing weather. If the tower serves a heat exchanger loop, see Waterside Economizers: Free Cooling with a Plate Heat Exchanger.

De-icing by fan reversal, and its limits

Delta says to reverse the fans briefly to melt ice. The other makers are stricter. SPX says to try shutting fans off cell by cell first, then reverse at about 30 percent speed or less with VFDs, for as short a time as possible. EVAPCO limits reversal to induced-draft towers at no more than half speed, with a time delay between directions, and says forced-draft towers should not use defrost cycles. BAC calls reversal a last resort at no more than 25 percent speed, with at least a 40-second delay between directions. BAC also warns that it can throw water out of the louvers, which builds ice outside, and that ice on blades and stacks is an injury and failure risk. SPX says reversal can pull freezing air over the fan equipment.

Basin heaters and what they cannot do

A basin heater keeps the water in a stopped tower from freezing. Nothing more. The heater maker INDEECO says these immersion heaters "will not protect the fill or the piping," and that they should be interlocked with the pump so they run only in standby. It recommends a temperature control and low-level protection, and keeping the water at least 2 inches above the elements. SPX says heaters must never energize unless fully covered, and EVAPCO's stated basis is a 40 F basin at 0 F ambient, so colder sites need a larger heater from the maker. A heater cannot fix a running tower with too little load.

Heat tracing, indoor sumps and draining

EVAPCO says external piping that cannot drain by gravity, including makeup lines, equalizers, valves and level controls, needs heat tracing and insulation. SPX singles out the makeup line, which is small and freezes fast. BAC says a remote sump avoids heater-failure freeze-ups and, if designed properly, can reduce the need to heat trace piping. The University of Michigan's design guideline prefers a remote indoor sump for winter operation, and if a wet outdoor sump is used it calls for a basin heater and heat tracing on outdoor piping. For a tower shut down for winter, SPX says to drain the tower, exposed piping and makeup line, or else add heat.

Worked example: heater size and a low-load check

None of the maker documents we read printed a heater sizing table or a heat-loss rate per square foot at 0 F, and the open-tank loss tables we found start at 90 F water or higher, so we do not use them. This is a method demonstration with assumed inputs, not a design. Use the kW from the maker.

Part 1, heater kW. Our own arithmetic, with assumed inputs.

  1. Assume a basin water surface of 12 ft by 12 ft: 12 x 12 = 144 ft2.
  2. INDEECO assumes the side area is 40 percent of the top and adds a 25 percent safety factor. Total area: 144 x 1.4 = 201.6 ft2.
  3. Assume a loss rate of 100 Btu/h per ft2 for a 40 F basin at 0 F with wind. This number is our own placeholder, not from a source. Loss: 201.6 x 100 = 20,160 Btu/h.
  4. Safety factor: 20,160 x 1.25 = 25,200 Btu/h.
  5. Convert: 25,200 / 3,412 = 7.39 kW. Round up to the next heater size the maker offers.

Change the placeholder rate and the answer scales directly, which is why the real rate must come from the maker.

Part 2, low-load check. Our own arithmetic.

  1. Heat rejected at 1,000 gpm and a 6 F range: 500 x 1,000 x 6 = 3,000,000 Btu/h, about 250 tons (3,000,000 / 12,000). The 500 is 8.33 lb/gal x 60 min/h x 1 Btu/lb-F, rounded.
  2. For a 45 F leaving temperature, the entering water must be 45 + 6 = 51 F, if that load and flow hold.
  3. Assume the entering water is only 47 F at this minimum load. Then leaving is 47 - 6 = 41 F, which is below EVAPCO's 42 F floor and BAC's 43 F figure.
  4. If the fan keeps running, it pulls the water even colder. Given SPX's note that fill-bottom water can read 32 F at a 40 F average, ice is likely at the fill and louvers.
  5. The fix is to cut the fan to off (or minimum, per the maker) so the water rides up, or bypass the full flow to the basin until it warms.

Safety

EVAPCO warns that large ice buildups can damage equipment or fall on people, that ice on walkways and ladders is a hazard, and that pipe and basin leaks create icing where people walk. BAC warns of injury from ice on blades during reversal. Inspect often, with EVAPCO calling mornings the most important time, and use caution removing ice, as Delta advises. Before you work in a fan stack or on a pump, lock out and verify dead; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Fan checks are in Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks.

Field card: Cooling Tower Winter Operation

Keep a load, keep the water warm, keep flow on every cell.

  • Minimum leaving water: maker numbers seen were 42 F (absolute), 43 F and 45 F. Use your tower's manual.
  • No heat load means no operation in freezing weather.
  • Bypass: full flow or none. Do not modulate it.
  • Fan reversal: last resort, low speed, short time, with a delay between directions.
  • Basin heater: protects a stopped basin only. Needs pump interlock and low-level protection.
  • Heat trace makeup and non-draining lines, or drain them.
  • Falling ice and icy decks: inspect often, lock out before work.
