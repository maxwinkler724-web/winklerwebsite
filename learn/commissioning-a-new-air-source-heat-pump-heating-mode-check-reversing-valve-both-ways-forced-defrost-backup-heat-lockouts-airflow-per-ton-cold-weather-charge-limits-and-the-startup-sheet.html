Commissioning a New Air-Source Heat Pump: Heating Mode, Defrost, Backup Heat and the Startup Sheet

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A new heat pump is often started up in whatever season the install ends, and cooling mode gets all the attention. But the homeowner will lean on the heating side for months. This lesson is a commissioning checklist for a ducted air-source heat pump at a house: what to prove in heating mode, what to set, and what to write down for the owner.

Check the maker's instructions first

Check the maker's instructions first. Every model has its own test mode, defrost control, wiring and charging method. This page is general education built from federal, national-lab and university reports we read in 2026. Where it gives a checklist, that checklist is our own, and the maker's installation instructions win any disagreement. Follow your site safety procedures and lock-out rules when you open panels.

Prove heating, not just cooling

A system that cools well can still fail in heating: a stuck reversing valve, a miswired thermostat, a missing outdoor sensor, or backup heat that runs all the time. A federal consumer page on heat pump systems describes the heating-season job plainly: the unit moves heat from the cool outdoors into the house. So run both modes and watch each one reach steady operation. Our checklist order:

  1. Thermostat configuration. Set the thermostat type to heat pump, not conventional, and confirm the reversing valve terminal setting (O or B) matches the equipment. A wrong setting makes the unit cool when you call for heat. See the thermostat wiring lesson and the reversing valve diagnosis lesson.
  2. Valve shifts both ways. Call for cooling, let it run, then call for heating (respect the compressor's off delay). Confirm by temperature and by the line temperatures: the indoor-side vapor line should be hot in heating and cold in cooling, and the outdoor coil should flip the other way. Then switch back. A valve that shifts only one direction is a finding, not a pass.
  3. Defrost. Confirm the defrost sensor is mounted and connected and that the board can be forced into a defrost by the maker's method. Do not assume a forced defrost exists. In a lab study of nine cold-climate units, one had no way to start a defrost by hand. When it does defrost, watch the reversing valve shift, the outdoor fan stop and the backup heat behavior (see below), and confirm normal heating returns.
  4. Backup heat staging and lockouts. Test each stage of backup heat by the maker's method, then confirm the settings that keep it from running when the heat pump alone can carry the house. See emergency heat vs auxiliary heat and backup heat strips, sizing and staging.
  5. Outdoor temperature sensor. If the system or thermostat uses one, check that it reads close to a trusted thermometer in the shade. Our placement checklist: out of direct sun, away from the outdoor unit's discharge air and the dryer or exhaust vents, and away from meltwater. Mount it where the maker's instructions say.
  6. Airflow. Verify it, as below.
  7. Charge. Verify it, with the limits below.

What the field studies found about backup heat

A national-lab field validation of 13 variable-capacity ducted heat pumps in homes (12 reported) is a good reason to take the lockout settings seriously. In that study the backup-heat lockout temperatures (the outdoor temperature above which backup should not run) ranged from 20 F to 40 F, most between 25 and 35 F. Compressor lockout temperatures were disabled at several sites and set from 0 to 15 F at others. Backup heat used more than 40 percent as much energy as compressor heating at 5 of 11 all-electric sites, and at two sites it used more than the compressor did. One site's high backup use came from a control or sensor fault, and at two sites backup ran for more than five minutes after each defrost. Backup heat cut system COP by more than 30 percent at two sites and less than 7 percent at six. Those were field conditions, not recommended settings, but they show what a bad setting costs the owner. Set the lockout from the house's load and the maker's guidance, and write it down.

Airflow per ton

Heating output is proportional to airflow, so verify it before judging anything else. A university energy-research page on a test house reports a typical audit range of about 350 to 425 CFM per ton and measured 390 in its example; many real systems read lower. That page also measured airflow using the backup resistance heat, the temperature rise and the element wattage, and it advised putting the supply thermometer near the first bend or first register so the air is mixed. Low airflow does real harm in heating: in a lab study of a 2-ton split heat pump, cutting airflow to 50 percent of 800 CFM lowered heating capacity by over 30 percent and heating COP by over 50 percent. A cold-climate field study of ductless units found measured airflow only 50 to 80 percent of listed values, and setting the fan to low appeared to sharply cut capacity. For ducted units, measure at the speed the owner will use and record it.

Charge in cold weather: know your limits

Do not adjust charge from pressures at an outdoor temperature the maker's chart does not cover. The same lab study found heating-mode charge methods usable only from 37 to 47 F outdoors, because frost began building on the outdoor coil and made operation unstable. It also found coil temperature alone was a poor charge indicator and that subcooling tracked charge better. Our rule: if it is colder than the maker's charging range, do not add or remove refrigerant to chase numbers. Verify the nameplate charge was weighed in, check for leaks and airflow, record the readings and conditions, and return for a warm-day check if the maker requires it. The related lesson on charge in heating mode goes deeper.

Worked example (our own): is the heat pump delivering?

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement from a real house. A nominal 3-ton ducted heat pump has been running steadily for 20 minutes, outdoors at 35 F, backup heat locked out, not in or just after defrost. We use the sensible-heat relation and the simple COP estimate in the field COP lesson.

Assumptions. Sea-level air. Measured airflow 1,100 CFM. Return air 69 F. Supply air, averaged after the coil, 98 F. Measured total input (outdoor unit plus blower) 3,100 W. The maker's extended data table (assumed for this example) lists 33,000 Btu/h at 35 F outdoors and 70 F indoors.

  1. Airflow per ton: 1,100 CFM / 3 tons = 367 CFM per ton, inside the 350 to 425 range quoted above.
  2. Temperature rise: 98 F - 69 F = 29 F.
  3. The factor 1.08 comes from sea-level air: 0.075 lb/ft³ x 0.24 Btu/lb-F x 60 min/h = 1.08.
  4. Delivered heat: 1.08 x 1,100 x 29 = 34,452 Btu/h.
  5. Compare with the table: 34,452 / 33,000 = 1.044, or about 104 percent. Within what airflow-measurement error allows, so no capacity alarm.
  6. Input in Btu/h: 3,100 W x 3.412 = 10,577 Btu/h. The factor 3.412 is the inverse of the federal conversion-table value 0.2930711 W per Btu/h.
  7. COP estimate: 34,452 / 10,577 = 3.26, call it 3.3.

Check the sensitivity. If the true airflow were 10 percent lower, 990 CFM, delivered heat would be 1.08 x 990 x 29 = 31,007 Btu/h, which is 94 percent of the table and a COP of 2.93. Airflow error moves COP one for one, so treat 3.3 as roughly 3 and do not condemn or approve a unit on one reading. For comparison, a national-lab field study of 12 cold-climate ducted units reported an average seasonal compressor-based heating COP of about 2.5 (range 1.7 to 3.5), and measured capacity was closer to the maker's minimum reported capacity than the maximum. Different conditions, different units, so use it as context only.

What to write on the startup sheet

This is our suggested list, and it extends the general startup readings lesson. Date and outdoor temperature at the time of each test. Mode tested and how long it ran. Thermostat type setting and reversing valve terminal setting. Evidence the valve shifted both ways. Whether defrost was forced or only observed, and how it behaved. Backup heat stages tested, plus the lockout and staging settings you left. Outdoor sensor location and a reading against a reference. Airflow, method and CFM per ton. Return and supply temperatures, rise and watts. How charge was verified, or why it was not adjusted in the cold, and what needs a follow-up visit. Then tell the owner in plain words what winter operation looks like. The frost and defrost lesson covers the steam and noise they will see.

Field card: Commissioning a New Air-Source Heat Pump

If you only proved cooling, you are half done.

  • Thermostat set to heat pump, valve terminal O or B matched to the equipment.
  • Valve shifts to heating and back to cooling.
  • Defrost sensor connected; defrost forced or observed; normal heat returns.
  • Backup heat stages tested; lockout and staging written down.
  • Outdoor sensor reads true and sits in a sensible spot.
  • Airflow near 350 to 425 CFM per ton, measured at the owner's fan speed.
  • Heat = 1.08 x CFM x rise; COP = heat / (watts x 3.412).
  • Too cold for the maker's charging range: do not chase charge; schedule a follow-up.
