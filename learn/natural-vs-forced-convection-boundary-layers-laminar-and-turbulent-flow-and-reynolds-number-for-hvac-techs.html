Natural vs Forced Convection: Boundary Layers, Laminar and Turbulent Flow and Reynolds Number for HVAC Techs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Heat does not leave a coil, a tube or a radiator the same way every time. How fast it moves depends on how the fluid next to the surface is moving. This lesson explains the thin layer of fluid that controls most of that, the one number (Reynolds number) that tells you which kind of flow you have, and why it explains low-flow chiller trouble, baseboard clearance and coil face velocity.

Natural and forced convection

Our conduction, convection and radiation lesson covers the three ways heat moves. Here we only split convection in two. Colorado State's notes define natural convection as fluid motion caused by density differences, with no fan or pump, and forced convection as motion caused by a fan or pump. The same notes give rough heat transfer coefficients (h): about 10 W/m²K for gases in natural convection, about 100 for gases in forced convection, about 100 for liquids in natural convection and about 1000 for liquids in forced convection. Those are order-of-magnitude values, not design numbers. The pattern is the point: a pump or fan moves ten times more heat for the same surface and temperature difference, and liquids beat gases by about the same factor.

The boundary layer

Fluid touching a surface is slowed by it. Colorado State's notes say most of the temperature change between the surface and the fluid happens across a thin layer of fluid right next to the surface, called the boundary layer. Heat has to get through that layer, and mostly it does so by conduction, which is slow in air and water. MIT's building heat transfer notes put it as h being roughly the fluid's conductivity divided by the thickness of that thermal layer. A thinner layer means a higher h. The same notes say higher velocity shrinks the layer, and for building surfaces in air it is typically a quarter inch or less.

So the rule that connects everything in this lesson: faster or more turbulent flow thins the boundary layer, and a thinner layer raises h. MIT's notes add that turbulence can raise the heat transfer coefficient markedly, sometimes by an order of magnitude or more.

Reynolds number in plain terms

NASA describes Reynolds number (Re) as the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces. Think of it as "how much the fluid wants to keep moving and tumble" against "how much it sticks to itself and the wall." For a full pipe it is Re = density × velocity × diameter ÷ dynamic viscosity. It has no units, so every length and speed must be in matching units before you start. More speed, a bigger pipe or a thinner fluid raise it. A thicker fluid (an antifreeze mix, or colder water) has higher viscosity and lowers it.

Clarkson's notes give rough ranges for tubes: usually laminar below about 2,300, transitional from about 2,300 to 4,000, and turbulent above 4,000. Other notes use slightly different cutoffs (the Michigan Tech lecture states its laminar correlation for Re under 2,100), so treat the edges as fuzzy bands, not switches.

Why laminar flow is poor at moving heat

In laminar flow there is little mixing across the tube. The Clarkson notes say that for fully developed laminar flow in a long tube the Nusselt number (the dimensionless form of h) settles to a constant, about 3.66 for a uniform wall temperature. A constant means that speeding the water up inside the laminar band does not raise h much. Turbulence adds eddy transport across the tube, and the turbulent correlation in the same notes (the Dittus-Boelter form) has h rising with Re to about the 0.8 power. The same notes say the transitional band is hard to predict and exchangers should avoid it.

Our own estimate. If everything else stays the same, using that 0.8 exponent, doubling the velocity in turbulent flow raises h by about 20.8 = 1.74 times. Going from Re about 4,100 to Re about 24,600 (6 times) gives 60.8 = 4.2 times. We ignored changes in fluid properties, so this is only a trend.

Worked example (our own): Reynolds number in a chiller tube

Our own example, with assumptions. Plain water at 45 F inside a straight tube with a 0.75 in inside diameter (an assumption; real tubes differ, so use the maker's data). Water properties come from the NIST fluid property tool at 7.2 C (45 F is 7.2 C) and 1 atm: density 999.89 kg/m³ and dynamic viscosity 1418.4 µPa·s.

  1. Convert units. Viscosity: 1418.4 µPa·s = 1418.4 × 10-6 Pa·s = 0.0014184 Pa·s. Diameter: 0.75 in × 0.0254 m/in = 0.01905 m.
  2. Do the fixed part once. density × diameter ÷ viscosity = 999.89 × 0.01905 ÷ 0.0014184 = 13,429 (per m/s of velocity).
  3. 1 ft/s. 1 × 0.3048 = 0.3048 m/s. Re = 13,429 × 0.3048 = about 4,100. This is just above the 4,000 line, so we call it turbulent, but only barely; treat it as the edge of the transition band.
  4. 3 ft/s. 3 × 0.3048 = 0.9144 m/s. Re = 13,429 × 0.9144 = about 12,300. Turbulent.
  5. 6 ft/s. 6 × 0.3048 = 1.8288 m/s. Re = 13,429 × 1.8288 = about 24,600. Turbulent.
  6. A low-flow case. At 0.25 ft/s (0.0762 m/s), Re = 13,429 × 0.0762 = about 1,000. Laminar. The flow area of the tube is π ÷ 4 × 0.01905² = 0.000285 m², so 0.0762 m/s is 0.0000217 m³/s, which is about 0.34 gpm per tube (1 m³/s = 15,850 gpm). The 1 ft/s case is about 1.38 gpm per tube.

Reading it: with these assumptions, 1, 3 and 6 ft/s are all turbulent (1 ft/s only just). The tube only drops into the laminar band at very low speeds, roughly under 0.56 ft/s (Re 2,300 ÷ 13,429 = 0.171 m/s). Real chillers can differ: tubes have other diameters, the fluid may be a thicker glycol mix, and a loop that is throttled or partly air-bound can leave some passes at very low velocity. The take-away is to check what Re the water actually sees, not to assume it is turbulent.

What this means in the field

Low water flow in boiler loops and chiller tubes

When flow drops into the laminar or transitional range, h falls hard (our own reading of the laminar behavior above), so the same surface moves much less heat. Our chiller evaporator freeze-up lesson covers what low flow can do to a chiller. Fouling adds its own resistance; see the overall heat transfer coefficient lesson.

Baseboard and radiators: natural convection

The Department of Energy describes baseboard heat as air that warms, rises into the room, and pulls cooler air in at the bottom. It says units are usually placed under windows, where the rising warm air counteracts falling cool air from the glass, and that the unit should sit about 1.9 cm (about 3/4 in) above the floor so cool air can reach the fins. That page is about electric baseboard, but hot-water fin-tube baseboard and radiators depend on the same buoyancy-driven draft (our own reading). Because natural convection h is small, anything that blocks the inlet gap, the fins or the top outlet cuts output quickly. See radiator covers and blocked airflow and heat emitters and output vs water temperature.

Coil face velocity

Air moving across a coil is forced convection. MIT's laminar plate relation for air has h growing with the square root of velocity, so doubling the speed raises h only about √2 = 1.41 times (our arithmetic), while fan work climbs much faster; see the fan laws lesson. On a wet cooling coil there is also a limit on the high side: one federal facilities document sets 400 fpm for present design conditions on lab air handlers and says the right face velocity and drainage are essential to prevent moisture carryover. That figure is for those facilities, not a universal limit. Our cooling coil face velocity lesson goes deeper, and the fins lesson shows how fin surface adds to the air side.

Field card: Natural vs Forced Convection: Boundary Layers, Laminar and Turbulent Flow and Reynolds Number for HVAC Techs

Thin boundary layer, high h. Thick boundary layer, low h.

  • Natural convection: buoyancy only. Forced: fan or pump. Rough h: gases 10 vs 100, liquids 100 vs 1000 W/m²K.
  • Re = density × velocity × diameter ÷ viscosity. Tubes: under about 2,300 laminar, 2,300 to 4,000 transitional, over 4,000 turbulent.
  • Our example: 45 F water in a 0.75 in tube: 1 ft/s is about 4,100, 3 ft/s about 12,300, 6 ft/s about 24,600.
  • Laminar flow barely improves with speed; avoid the transitional band in design.
  • Baseboard and radiators need a clear inlet at the bottom and a clear outlet at the top.
  • Coil face velocity: faster means higher h but more pressure drop and, on wet coils, carryover risk.
