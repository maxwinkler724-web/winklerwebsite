Compressor Internal Overload Protectors: Open Readings, Reset Time and Repeated Trips

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot single-phase compressor that reads open between common and both other terminals has not necessarily lost its motor. Often a small protector inside the shell has opened and is waiting to cool. This lesson shows how to tell that case from a real open winding, how long to wait, and what keeps tripping it.

Check the unit's own literature

Check the unit's own literature. Terminal layouts, protector types and wait times differ by compressor. The figures below come from maker documents we read and are examples, not limits for your machine. Use the data for the exact compressor in front of you.

What is inside the shell

A trade-press article on compressor electrical problems explains that single-phase compressors use an internal line-break overload that senses both motor current and winding temperature. When it opens, it breaks the current path and shuts the motor down. A compressor maker's technical bulletin describes the construction: a bimetallic disc that interrupts the current, mounted on or close to the motor, and hermetically sealed because it sits in refrigerant and oil. Heat from motor current, heat from the windings and shell temperature all push the disc toward snapping open.

A maker's scroll bulletin says the overload opens the common connection on single-phase motors and the center of the Y connection on three-phase motors. That one detail explains most of the meter behavior below. A different style, the internal thermostat, senses winding temperature only and sits in the low-voltage control circuit, so when it opens the contactor drops out instead (same trade-press article).

Why a tripped protector looks like an open winding

Because the protector is in the common leg, every path that starts at C passes through it. An equipment maker's service procedures manual puts the single-phase rule this way: if start and run both read open to common, the internal overload is open. If only one winding is open to common while the other shows continuity, that winding is open. A compressor maker's mounting guide agrees from the other side: a path between the two winding terminals with open circuits to common means the protector has cut out, so wait for it to reset.

The key reading is the one that does not touch the protector. Run and start windings are joined at the junction inside, so R to S still measures through both windings. If R to S reads a sensible low resistance, the windings are intact and the problem is a hot, open protector. If R to S is open too, that does not prove a bad motor yet, but a second reading after a long cool-down is required before you say so.

How long a hot compressor can take to reset

  • A compressor maker's technical bulletin says reset may take typically around one hour, perhaps longer.
  • An equipment maker's service manual says to allow adequate time before condemning the compressor, that up to two hours may be needed if it is extremely hot, and that at least one hour of cooling is wanted before testing for a suspected open winding or defective protector.
  • A compressor maker's mounting guide gives about 5 minutes for a cold compressor and up to about 45 minutes for a warm one, with the housing above 80 degrees C (176 degrees F).
  • The trade-press article says resetting may take more than an hour, and that the motor should generally be below 115 degrees F before you check winding continuity through the overload.

The numbers differ because compressor size, shell mass, ambient and protector design differ. The practical rule from all four is the same: do not condemn a hot compressor on an open reading. A residential service manual cautions that you should be sure the internal line-break overload is not temporarily open, and says the compressor should always cool and the protector close before troubleshooting.

External pilot-duty protectors and electronic protection units

Not every protector is a line-break device carrying motor current. The service procedures manual lists internal pilot-duty thermostats and external overloads shown on the wiring diagram, and says a three-phase winding is condemned only after every external protector checks good and voltage is present at all three winding terminals. A pilot-duty device carries only control current and opens the contactor circuit, so the compressor stays quiet with no voltage on its terminals. Check the diagram and the control circuit before you reach for the compressor leads.

The same manual describes solid-state protection units on larger machines. Thermistor sensors in the windings feed a control unit that opens a control contact. Two styles are listed. In one, sensor resistance should read 60 to 150 ohms; above 90 ohms the motor is too hot to run and must cool, and above 150 ohms you check the sensor fuses first. In the other, the range is 2,000 to 45,000 ohms; above 10,000 ohms the motor must cool until it reads under 10,500, and above 45,000 ohms the sensors are damaged. The manual warns not to use a meter that applies more than 3 V DC to the sensors, and gives 21 to 30 V AC at the unit's supply terminals as a normal check. These are examples from one document, so read your own unit's data.

Why protectors keep tripping

A maker's bulletin notes that if the condition that caused the trip persists, the compressor will probably keep cycling on the overload until it is corrected. The service procedures manual lists the usual causes:

  • Voltage out of range. The manual says rated voltage plus or minus 10 percent. Low voltage also raises current and, at worst, causes locked-rotor starts. See voltage drop on HVAC circuits.
  • High head or high suction pressure. Dirty condenser, overcharge, blocked airflow, or a failed condenser fan motor or its capacitor.
  • Low charge or restrictions. High superheat means thin returning gas, so the motor loses cooling. The manual lists a compressor that runs but does not pump the same way.
  • Bad start or run capacitor, or a faulty start relay. See testing capacitors with a meter.
  • Short cycling and tight bearings. A compressor maker's bulletin lists operation outside the approved voltage or suction range and extreme loads. Short cycling is our own addition: each hard start stacks heat on the protector. See AC short cycling.

A compressor maker's bulletin adds that an external disc protector may not trip at low load until the windings are well above their limit and generally does not protect against lost charge. That is why low charge is hard on a motor even when the protector seems quiet.

What never to do

  • Never bypass a protector or safety to keep a unit running, and never jumper a pilot-duty or control-circuit thermostat. This is our own safety rule. The protector is the only thing between a stressed winding and a burnout.
  • Never cool a live compressor with water. This is our own safety rule: water on energized terminals and wiring is a shock hazard. Shut off and lock out power first, and let it cool on its own.
  • Never take readings with power on. The maker warnings we read say to turn off all power, disconnect the leads at the compressor terminals, and discharge capacitors before resistance checks. They also say never to put your face or body in line with the terminals.
  • Never keep resetting a protector that trips repeatedly. Find the cause. The trade-press article also says that if the internal overload itself fails open, the compressor must be replaced.

Worked example (ours): tripped or truly open?

These are our own invented readings on a hot single-phase compressor with power locked out and leads off the terminals. Meter set to ohms.

  1. First check: C to S reads open, C to R reads open, R to S reads 3.4 ohm.
  2. Both common paths are open, so either the protector is open or both windings are broken at the common. But R to S, which does not pass the protector, reads 3.4 ohm. A run winding and start winding that are both intact must give a reading, so the windings are good and the protector is the open item.
  3. Assumption: the compressor is extremely hot to the touch. Following the manual, we allow one hour, then retest.
  4. Retest after cool-down: C to R reads 1.4 ohm and C to S reads 2.0 ohm. Check: 1.4 + 2.0 = 3.4 ohm, matching R to S. The protector has closed; the motor is fine.
  5. Now the opposite case, also ours. If, after two hours, C to R still read open and C to S read 2.0 ohm, then R to S would read open too (the run side is broken). One winding open to common with the other showing continuity points to that winding, so the service manual says it is open.

The value of the first R to S reading: it tells you which story you are in without waiting an hour. See checking compressor windings for the full set including ground tests. Once the protector is closed and the motor tests good, find out why it tripped before you start it. Check capacitors, voltage and pressures, and take amps once it runs (see measuring amp draw).

Field card: Compressor Internal Protectors

Open to common on both windings? Check R to S before you condemn.

  • Single-phase line-break protector sits in the common leg.
  • Open protector: C to S open, C to R open, R to S still reads.
  • Example (ours): R to S 3.4 ohm, later C to R 1.4 plus C to S 2.0.
  • Wait at least one hour; up to two hours if extremely hot (one maker manual).
  • Pilot-duty and unit protectors open the control circuit instead; read the diagram.
  • Causes: voltage off by more than 10 percent, high head, low charge, bad capacitor, short cycling.
  • Never bypass, never water on a live unit, never test with power on.
