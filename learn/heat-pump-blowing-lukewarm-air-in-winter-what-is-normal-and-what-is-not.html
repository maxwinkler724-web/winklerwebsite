Heat Pump Blowing Lukewarm Air in Winter: What Is Normal and What Is Not

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You switch from a furnace to a heat pump, put your hand over a vent, and the air feels barely warm. Often nothing is broken. A heat pump delivers a lot of gently warmed air instead of a smaller stream of very hot air. This lesson shows the typical numbers, how cold weather changes them, and how to tell lukewarm-but-fine from lukewarm-and-faulty.

The short answer

Carrier says heat pump air in heating mode is typically around 85 to 100°F, while a gas furnace delivers roughly 120 to 140°F. Lennox says heat pump air may feel cooler than furnace air even when the system is running efficiently. Air in the 80s or 90s is not a defect. Cool air that lasts for hours, a house that will not warm up, or thick ice on the outdoor unit is a different story, and the sections below separate the two.

Why it feels cooler than a furnace

A furnace makes heat by burning fuel, so its heat exchanger gets hot. Energy Vanguard puts air coming off a furnace heat exchanger around 150°F, perhaps 125°F by the time it reaches the vents, and puts supply air from a heat pump coil between about 90 and 110°F. A heat pump does not make heat. As Lennox puts it, it moves heat. The result, per the Energy Vanguard figures above, is supply air that is clearly cooler than a furnace's.

Now compare that to your own body. Cleveland Clinic says anything from 97 to 99°F is considered normal body temperature. Your skin is a bit cooler than that, but a supply register in the low 90s or the 80s is close to skin temperature. Your hand finds almost no difference, so the air feels lukewarm even though it is warming the room. A furnace register at 120°F or more feels hot because it is well above skin.

A heat pump makes up for the lower temperature with volume. It moves a lot of air for a long time. That is why it tends to run for long stretches (see Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter).

How outdoor temperature changes the output

The colder the air outside, the less heat the outdoor coil can pick up, so both the capacity and the supply temperature fall. Lennox publishes this as a table. In its Merit Series 12HPB engineering data, a 2-ton 12HPB24 with an indoor air volume of 800 cfm and 70°F indoor air is listed at 23,200 Btu/h of total heating capacity at 47°F outdoors, 14,600 at 17°F and 11,200 at 5°F. The same table notes that the capacities include the effect of defrost cycles. That is roughly a 37 percent drop from 47°F to 17°F, and about half of the 47°F figure by 5°F. The same airflow carries less heat, so the air leaving the coil is cooler. (This is a 2003 data bulletin for an older model, used here only as a real example of how a maker's table looks. It is not a rating for any current product.)

The balance point idea explains why a house can feel cooler on the coldest days; see Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity. Newer cold-climate models hold up better, as covered in Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Defrost: a normal reason for a cool blast

In winter the outdoor coil runs colder than the outdoor air, and frost forms. Trane says the coil typically runs 10 to 20°F below the outdoor temperature. To melt the frost, the unit reverses and the outdoor fan stops. For a few minutes the indoor coil can blow cooler air. Trane says its cycles usually last 3 to 5 minutes, Carrier says about 5 to 10 minutes, and Lennox says usually 5 to 15 minutes. Trane adds that the AUX light can come on during defrost because the electric heat strips are keeping the house warm, and that steam from the outdoor unit is melting ice. Carrier says a frost layer under about 1/4 inch is normal. More detail is in Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat and Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter.

Worked example: what supply temperature should the table give?

You can turn a maker's capacity number into an expected supply temperature. The tool is the sensible heat equation, Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT. ACCA's HVAC blog explains where 1.08 comes from: air density of about 0.075 lb per cubic foot, times a specific heat of 0.24 Btu per lb per °F, times 60 minutes per hour (0.075 × 0.24 × 60 = 1.08). It holds for air near sea level and room temperature, and it is not truly a constant at other conditions. For a house in New Jersey it is fine.

The system: the Lennox 2-ton 12HPB24 above, moving 800 cfm (400 cfm per ton, the usual target; DOE says to aim for about 400 to 500 cfm per ton). Air returns to the coil at 70°F.

Step 1. Rearrange the equation: ΔT = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × CFM).

Step 2. The denominator is the same each time: 1.08 × 800 = 864.

At 47°F outdoors: 23,200 ÷ 864 = 26.9°F rise. Supply air is about 70 + 26.9 = 97°F.

At 17°F outdoors: 14,600 ÷ 864 = 16.9°F rise. Supply air is about 70 + 16.9 = 87°F.

At 5°F outdoors: 11,200 ÷ 864 = 13.0°F rise. Supply air is about 83°F.

What it means: on a mild day this system gives air just under body temperature, and at 17°F it gives air about 10 degrees cooler than that, well below skin temperature. Both are normal for this machine at 800 cfm. Duct losses, blower heat and the exact test setup will move a real reading a few degrees, so treat these as estimates.

Now a field check. At 17°F outdoors with the strips off, a tech reads 70°F return and 88°F supply, so ΔT is 18°F. Delivered heat is 1.08 × 800 × 18 = 15,552 Btu/h. Divided by the table's 14,600 gives 1.065, or about 107 percent of the table. That is within 10 percent, so the system is performing as published. If the supply had read only 80°F (ΔT 10°F), delivered heat would be 1.08 × 800 × 10 = 8,640 Btu/h, and 8,640 ÷ 14,600 = 0.59, about 59 percent of the table. That is a real problem to chase. The 10 percent tolerance and the method come from an ACHR News article on verifying heat pump heating output.

Normal, or a fault?

Normal: supply air in the 80s to about 100°F; a few minutes of cool air with steam outside during defrost; AUX showing during defrost or on the coldest days; a unit that runs a long time.

Worth a technician:

  • Stuck reversing valve. Carrier says a unit that heats poorly but cools normally may have a stuck valve, and Lennox says a stuck or failing valve can leave it blowing cool air in heat mode. See Reversing Valve Diagnosis.
  • Low refrigerant charge. Carrier and Lennox both say low refrigerant means the unit cannot move enough heat. DOE says too much or too little charge hurts performance. Only a certified tech can check it.
  • Failed auxiliary heat. HomeTips lists backup heat that is not activating as a cause of cold-feeling air. If the house cannot keep up on a cold day and the strips never come on, the air stays lukewarm. See Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips and Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat.
  • Dirty filter or weak airflow. Carrier suggests checking the filter every 60 to 90 days and Lennox every 1 to 3 months. DOE says performance drops when airflow falls much below 350 cfm per ton.
  • Outdoor coil iced solid. Carrier says ice thicker than 1/4 inch, or ice that remains after defrost, is not normal. Lennox says heavy ice that persists after defrost warrants a professional visit.

Simple owner checks

  • Confirm the thermostat is on Heat, not Cool, and the fan is on Auto, since Carrier lists those settings among causes of cold air.
  • Replace a dirty filter and open any blocked vents.
  • Look at the outdoor unit. A little frost with a short defrost is fine. Do not chip ice or pour hot water on it; turn the system off at the thermostat and call.
  • Clear loose snow and debris by hand, with the power off at the unit.
  • Compare how long the cool air lasts. A few minutes is defrost. Hours is not.

How techs check it

Following the ACHR News method: collect the model and find the maker's capacity table and fan table. Measure total external static pressure and read airflow from the fan table (target about 400 cfm per ton, within 10 percent). Run only the heat pump, with strips off, for 10 to 15 minutes. Measure return and supply with a fast digital thermometer and subtract. Compute 1.08 × CFM × ΔT and divide by the table's capacity at the actual outdoor temperature. Within 10 percent is good. The article calls the method imperfect: duct leaks, motor heat and humidifier bypass can skew it, and it says nothing about defrost, so do not measure during or just after defrost. A second ACHR News article says temperature split is a more useful heating indicator than in cooling because humidity does not affect it, and that low airflow raises the split. For measuring technique, see Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise and Measuring Airflow (CFM).

Field card: Lukewarm heat pump air

Warm air is not hot air, and that is normal.

  • Heat pump supply is often 85 to 100°F; furnace air is about 120 to 140°F (Carrier).
  • Capacity and supply temperature fall as the outdoor air gets colder.
  • Defrost: a few minutes of cool air, steam, AUX light. Normal.
  • Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT, near sea level.
  • Example: 800 cfm, 14,600 Btu/h at 17°F gives a 16.9°F rise, about 87°F supply.
  • Measure with strips off; within 10 percent of the maker's table is good.
  • Call for: cool air for hours, ice over 1/4 inch or not clearing, no heat in heat mode.
