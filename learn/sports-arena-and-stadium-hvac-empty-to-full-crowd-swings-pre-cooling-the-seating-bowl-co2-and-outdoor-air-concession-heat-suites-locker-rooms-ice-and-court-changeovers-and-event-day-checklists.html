Sports Arena and Stadium HVAC: Crowd Swings, Pre-Conditioning and Event-Day Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A large arena can go from a few dozen workers to a packed seating bowl in under an hour, then empty again in twenty minutes. The people alone add a huge heat and moisture load, and the building has to hold comfort and air quality at both ends. This lesson walks through how that swing is handled and what the facility crew checks on event day.

The swing is the whole problem

Most buildings are designed around a fairly steady crowd. An arena is designed around a peak that happens a few dozen times a year and a near-empty building the rest of the time. Equipment sized for the peak sits at a small fraction of its capacity on a non-event day, so controls have to modulate fans, dampers and cooling in large steps without hunting or freezing coils. The same idea shows up in smaller form in gymnasium and recreation center HVAC and in houses of worship; an arena is the same problem at many times the size.

Pre-conditioning the seating bowl

A tall, open bowl holds a lot of air and a lot of concrete. You cannot cool it in the ten minutes before doors open, so crews start hours ahead, running air through the bowl to bring the air and the surfaces toward the target before the crowd arrives. Concrete and steel hold heat, so a bowl that was pre-cooled has a cushion that slows the temperature rise once people fill it. A national laboratory report on predictive control describes the same idea: it used pre-cooling on summer nights and pre-heating in the early morning to use the building's own mass and shift load away from peak periods, and it cautions that its results came from one building in a mild climate. Treat that as a principle, not a promise for your venue.

Two cautions for the crew. Pre-cooling humid air without enough drying leaves surfaces cold and damp, so watch for condensation on cold steel, railings and seat frames, especially in humid weather. And the pre-event start time should come from experience with that particular building and weather, not a fixed clock.

Outdoor air and carbon dioxide during the event

Ventilation requirements are figured per person, so a full crowd needs far more outdoor air than an empty building. A federal energy agency ventilation guide describes demand-controlled ventilation as reducing outdoor air below the design setting when occupancy is lower than design, usually by moving outdoor air dampers based on carbon dioxide sensors or schedules. It says sensors suit unpredictable use and schedules suit predictable patterns, that sensors drift and need regular calibration, and that dampers stuck at fully closed or fully open for long periods can signal a sensor problem. For an arena, the schedule is predictable (the event calendar), but the attendance is not, so many venues use both.

A federal health agency page explains that carbon dioxide is a rough indicator of occupancy and ventilation, not a measure of infection risk. It notes that a wall sensor can read artificially low when air moves across it, and that rooms are rarely well mixed. It offers readings below 800 ppm as one possible target in a space where the system already meets code-minimum outdoor air, and suggests investigating when readings climb above 110 percent of a baseline taken under the same airflow and occupancy. A separate page from the same agency says spaces with high occupancy may need more than 5 air changes per hour, which is why a crowded bowl is a case for checking, not assuming.

Concessions and the walkway ring

The ring corridor behind the seats is where people stand in line, and where fryers, grills, warmers and ice machines dump heat. Kitchen exhaust pulls conditioned air out of the building, so make-up air has to replace it or the walkway goes negative and the doors whistle and stick. See commercial kitchen ventilation basics for hoods and make-up air. Stand-alone concession stands often have short exhaust runs that are easy to overlook on a checklist.

Suites and press boxes on their own systems

Luxury suites and press boxes are usually served separately from the bowl because their schedules and comfort expectations differ: they are small, glassy, full of electronics and used for the length of the event, and may be nearly empty on non-event days. Separate units let the crew set them back when empty and bring them up ahead of the event without running the whole bowl. Check that suite doors are not propped open to the bowl, which dumps the bowl's air into a small room and overwhelms the unit. Related ideas on dense seating and high-occupancy zones are in casino floor HVAC.

Locker rooms: exhaust and humidity

Showers, wet towels and drying gear make locker rooms the wettest spaces in the building. A state university environmental health page recommends keeping indoor relative humidity between 20 and 60 percent and sending exhaust from high-moisture areas such as showers to the outdoors. In practice, locker-room exhaust should run continuously during use and for a time afterward, and the room should be kept slightly negative to the hall so moist air does not drift into corridors. Make-up air should come from a dry source, not from the damp shower area. Check exhaust fans for belt wear and filters for lint, since a failing fan shows up as a damp room, fogged mirrors and musty odors.

Ice and court floors

Venues that switch between ice and a hard court have to manage the floor as well as the air. When the ice is down, the bowl air is held cool and dry enough to protect the surface; when it is out, the same air must be warmed and the floor made ready, and the changeover often involves covering or removing the surface. The refrigeration side is covered in ice rink refrigeration, brine and glycol floors. From the air side, the key habits are to bring humidity under control before the ice is made, to watch for fog over the surface, and to run dehumidification while a court floor is in place so wood and finishes do not move.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own example. None of these inputs come from a source; they are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. Real design data for a venue come from the engineer of record.

  • Assumed crowd: 15,000 seated people.
  • Assumed heat per person: 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent.
  • Assumed outdoor air: 15 cfm per person.
  1. Sensible: 15,000 x 250 = 3,750,000 Btu/h. At 12,000 Btu/h per ton, that is 3,750,000 / 12,000 = 312.5 tons.
  2. Latent: 15,000 x 200 = 3,000,000 Btu/h, or 3,000,000 / 12,000 = 250 tons.
  3. Total from people: 3,750,000 + 3,000,000 = 6,750,000 Btu/h, or 562.5 tons.
  4. Outdoor air: 15,000 x 15 = 225,000 cfm.
  5. Moisture: using a rounded 1,060 Btu per pound of water vapor, 3,000,000 / 1,060 is about 2,830 lb/h. At 8.34 lb per gallon, that is about 339 gallons of water per hour that the coils must remove.

The takeaway: in this assumed case the latent load is nearly as large as the sensible load, which is why a bowl cooled only to temperature, without drying, feels clammy. It also shows that the outdoor air stream, 225,000 cfm, is large enough that heating or cooling it is a separate big load, so scaling it down when the crowd is smaller matters. Our assumed per-person heat is a round figure for seated spectators; the real value depends on the event and the activity level.

After the event

When the crowd leaves, the load collapses faster than the controls can follow. Run a post-event purge to clear stale air and moisture, then return suites, concessions and the bowl to setback. Note trouble spots for the next event: hot sections, damp locker rooms, doors that fought the pressure. Door and pressure problems with crowd surges are covered in train and bus station waiting rooms and main halls. Economizer free cooling can help with purges when outdoor air is mild; see economizer basics, and demand-controlled ventilation for sensor basics.

Field card: Arena and stadium HVAC event-day checklist

Pre-condition early, watch the crowd, then purge.

  • Start pre-conditioning the bowl hours ahead, based on that building's history and the weather.
  • Check for condensation on cold steel and railings before doors open.
  • Confirm CO2 sensors read sensibly and outdoor air dampers move; stuck dampers can mean sensor drift.
  • Verify make-up air for every concession exhaust; check the walkway ring pressure and doors.
  • Keep suite and press-box doors closed; confirm their units are up before the event.
  • Locker rooms: exhaust running, room negative to the hall, humidity in a sensible band.
  • Ice or court changeover: bring humidity under control before the surface changes.
  • After the event: purge, setback, log problems.
