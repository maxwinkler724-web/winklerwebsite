Writing Clear Service Tickets and Work Order Notes as a New HVAC Tech

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The repair is only half of a service call. The other half is the ticket you leave behind. A clear ticket lets the next tech pick up where you stopped, shows the customer what was done, and lets you remember a call you made three weeks ago. This lesson covers what to write, how to keep guesses out, and how a poor ticket looks next to a good one.

Why the notes matter

Notes protect three people. The customer gets a plain record of what was found and what was done. The next tech (maybe you, maybe a coworker) does not repeat tests that were already done. And you are protected, because a note written at the time is more reliable than memory. In our own experience, a callback is much easier to sort out when the first ticket shows the readings and the work. That is our own general practice, not a rule. Your employer's ticket system and policies come first, so ask what fields are required.

What to record

These are our own suggested headings. Use them in the same order every call so the habit sticks.

  1. Complaint in the customer's words. Put quotation marks around what they said, such as "runs all day but upstairs never gets below 80." Do not translate it into a diagnosis.
  2. What you found. Things you saw, heard, smelled or measured. Where each thing was.
  3. Readings with units. A bare "12" means nothing. Write "12 °F superheat" or "16.8 A." Note the conditions too: outdoor temperature, return and supply air temperatures, and whether the system had been running long enough to settle.
  4. What you did. Each step, in the order you did it, including tests that found nothing wrong. A passed test rules something out for the next tech.
  5. Parts used. Part, size or rating, and where it went. Note any old part you left with the customer or took away.
  6. What you recommend and why. Tie each recommendation to a reading or an observation.
  7. What you told the customer. Who you spoke with, and what they were told or offered. See Talking With Customers on HVAC Service Calls for how to have that conversation.

Facts, not guesses

A fact is something anyone standing there could confirm: "Blower motor was warm to the touch and not turning," "Supply air 58 °F." A guess is "motor probably bad" or "customer let the filter go." University writing guides for note-takers make the same split: keep descriptive notes of what was observed apart from your own interpretation, be specific instead of using vague labels, and write notes up soon, because shorthand fades. They also say to put the date and time in the factual part (see the university library guide in the sources). In a service ticket, put diagnosis in its own line, worded as a conclusion tied to evidence: "Capacitor measured 31 µF, rating 45 µF, motor hums and does not start."

Avoid words that judge a person or a past worker: "lazy install," "customer neglect." Write what you saw ("filter was loaded with lint, no airflow felt at the nearest register") and let readers draw conclusions.

Safety findings as observations

If you find signs of a combustion or safety problem, write exactly what you observed, where, and what you did. For carbon monoxide that means the meter reading with units, where and when you took it, the appliance state at the time, and what you told the customer. For a suspected cracked heat exchanger, record what you saw and which test you used, and avoid the words "condemned" or "dangerous" unless your employer's procedure uses them. Note any shutoff you performed, any tag you left, and who was told. Our lessons on carbon monoxide alarms and on cracked heat exchangers cover the checks themselves. This lesson only covers the writing.

Refrigerant records, in general terms

EPA's page on stationary refrigeration recordkeeping says technicians keep a copy of their Section 608 certification at the place of business. For appliances holding 50 pounds or more of ozone-depleting refrigerant, it says the technician gives the owner an invoice showing how much refrigerant was added, and that leak inspections and the tests that verify repairs are recorded. It says owners keep the date and type of each service and the quantity added. The federal rule text (40 CFR 82.157) lists more detail for those larger appliances, including the date of the work, the parts affected, the name of the person who did it, and the amount and type of refrigerant added or removed. It says records are kept at least three years unless the rule says otherwise. Smaller appliances, disposal and substitute refrigerants have their own rules, and employers often add requirements. The simple habit that fits all of it: whenever you add or recover refrigerant, write the type, the amount, and the date. Our 608 records lesson goes deeper. Ask your employer which records they keep for you.

Photos and time stamps

Photos fill gaps that words cannot: a data plate, a burned wire, the condition of a coil before cleaning. In our own practice we shoot the data plate first, then the problem, then the finished work, and we write one line on the ticket for each photo explaining what it shows. Follow your employer's rules on where photos are stored, and keep customer faces and personal papers out of the frame. For time stamps, record arrival and departure, and note the time beside readings that depend on run time ("readings taken after 20 minutes of running"). Your phone's clock is fine for this. Our field notebook lesson covers your personal notebook. This lesson is about the official ticket.

Worked example (our own): poor ticket, good ticket

This is our own example. The readings below are made up to teach the format. They are not targets for any real system, and the refrigerant, charge values and nameplate numbers are our assumptions.

Poor ticket:

AC not cooling. Checked it, looked low. Fixed. Works now.

Nothing in it can be checked. The next tech does not know what "low" means, what was fixed, or whether anything was measured.

Good ticket (same call):

  • Time: Arrived 1:10 PM, left 2:25 PM.
  • Complaint: Customer: "It runs all day but the upstairs never gets below 80."
  • Found: Filter fully matted with lint (photo 1). Weak airflow at nearest supply register. Indoor coil face dirty (photo 2). Outdoor unit running, fan turning.
  • Did: Replaced filter. Cleaned indoor coil. Ran system 20 minutes before readings.
  • Readings at 2:15 PM, outdoor air 88 °F: suction 118 psig, liquid 350 psig, suction line temperature at the service valve 52 °F, liquid line temperature 95 °F, return air 76 °F, supply air 58 °F, compressor 16.8 A.
  • Calculated: superheat 12 °F, subcooling 10 °F, temperature split 18 °F.
  • Refrigerant: none added or removed.
  • Parts: one filter, customer's size, 1 inch.
  • Recommend: change filter on a regular schedule. Have the evaporator coil checked at the next yearly visit, because the dirt reached the coil face.
  • Told customer: Spoke with the homeowner. Showed the filter and the coil photos. Discussed filter schedule.

Short math check. Assumptions: the refrigerant is the type on the nameplate, and the saturation temperatures from our gauge-set chart are 40 °F at 118 psig suction and 105 °F at 350 psig liquid (our assumed chart values for the example).

  1. Superheat = suction line temperature minus suction saturation temperature = 52 − 40 = 12 °F.
  2. Subcooling = liquid saturation temperature minus liquid line temperature = 105 − 95 = 10 °F.
  3. Temperature split = return air minus supply air = 76 − 58 = 18 °F, which matches the 18 °F written on the ticket.
  4. Amps check: 16.8 A measured against an assumed nameplate rating of 18.5 A. It is below the rating, and the ticket shows both numbers so the next tech can compare.

If the stated split had not matched the stated return and supply readings, that mismatch would tell you a number was copied wrong. Catching it before you leave is the reason to do the arithmetic. For what superheat and subcooling mean, see our basics lesson. What counts as a good value comes from the equipment maker's instructions, not from this example.

Before you close the ticket

  • Read it as if you were a stranger. Could you repeat the job from it?
  • Check each reading has a unit and each photo has a note.
  • Make sure no guess is written as a fact, and no customer personal details appear that the system does not need.
  • Do not edit after the fact to cover a mistake. If you find an error later, add a dated correction. Our own rule of thumb: add, do not erase.
  • This lesson is general education and not legal advice. For questions about what must be kept, ask your employer and read the rule.

Field card: Writing Clear Service Tickets

Their words, your readings, your actions, your advice, in that order.

  • Customer's complaint in quotation marks.
  • Findings and readings with units, and the conditions.
  • What you did, parts used, what you recommend and why.
  • Who you told, and what you told them.
  • Facts first; conclusions in their own labeled line.
  • Safety findings as observations: what, where, when, what you did.
  • Refrigerant added or recovered: type, amount, date.
  • Photos with a line each; arrival and departure times.
  • Check the math before you leave.
