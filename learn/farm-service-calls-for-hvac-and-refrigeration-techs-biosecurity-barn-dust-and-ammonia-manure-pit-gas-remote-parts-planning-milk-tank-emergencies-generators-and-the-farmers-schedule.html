Farm Service Calls for HVAC and Refrigeration Techs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A farm call is not a regular service call with a longer drive. The customer's animals, the milking or feeding schedule, the dust and gas in the barn, and the distance from a supply house all change how you plan the day. This lesson covers what to prepare and what to watch for.

Biosecurity: you can carry disease

Your boots, tools and truck can move disease from one farm to the next. A university extension guide for professionals who visit animal farms says to talk with the owner or site contact before you schedule, to tell them if you have your own animals or have been on other livestock or poultry sites, and to aim for no more than one site per day. If you must visit several, it suggests ordering them from lowest to highest disease risk, adjusted by what the contacts tell you. The same guide says to treat your own vehicle as contaminated when you arrive, to park on the road or down the driveway unless you are directed to a clean zone, and to clean mud and manure off the vehicle before the next site.

For footwear, that guide says to wear disposable boot covers at every visit, and to use rubber boots that are easy to wash and disinfect, since patterned soles are harder to clean well. Another extension page on poultry says outside footwear and clothing stay on the dirty side of the line and barn gear goes on before you enter the animal area. It adds that boots go on before coveralls in a line-of-separation entry, and that you should never skip boots or coveralls because supplies ran short. It also says some farms need several days of downtime after a visit to another farm. A federal agriculture checklist for poultry visitors says anyone who has contact with wild birds, pet birds or their own flock may be kept away from the flock, and that tools should not be shared between farms unless cleaned and disinfected first.

So the rule for us is simple: call first, ask what the farm requires, and if the farm says not to come from another farm the same day, plan the route so you do not. Carry spare boot covers, gloves, a scrub brush and a bag for used items. Use the farm's wash station and follow its clean and dirty line. Their rules win over your habits.

Animals and moving equipment

A federal workplace safety page on agriculture lists animals, machinery, dust, noise and heat among the main hazards on farms. It describes shafts that can wrap clothing or hair, and tractor overturn and vehicle risks. For a tech, that means:

  • Ask where animals are loose or penned, and have the farmer move them or close gates before you carry tools through.
  • Do not stand behind or beside animals you do not know. Keep a clear exit path.
  • Ask the farmer to shut down feed augers, scrapers and other powered equipment near your work. Apply your own lockout procedure to the equipment you service.
  • Keep loose clothing, hoodies and long hair away from anything turning. Stay out of the paths of tractors, loaders and feed trucks, and stay visible.

Dust and ammonia in barns

The same federal page notes that organic dusts, microorganisms and endotoxins can affect breathing. Barns with litter or manure can have ammonia and dust levels you notice right away. Our poultry and livestock barn ventilation lesson covers the ventilation side. For your own protection, ask the farmer what the air is like, keep your time in the barn short, get the work done with the ventilation running when you safely can, and step out if your eyes, nose or throat burn or you feel dizzy. If a respirator is needed, it has to be the right kind, fitted and part of your employer's program; do not guess.

Manure pit gas: techs never enter pits

A federal workplace safety research page on pit deaths says methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide and ammonia can build up in manure pits and create a life-threatening risk of suffocation. That page recommends avoiding entry unless absolutely necessary, and then only with air testing, a standby person, a lifeline and respiratory protection. Another page on hydrogen sulfide calls it a colorless gas with a strong rotten egg odor and lists multiple pit deaths in its investigation reports. The page does not say whether smell is a reliable warning, so we do not treat smell as a safety check.

Our rule: HVAC and refrigeration techs never enter a manure pit, and never go in to help someone else. That page says rescuers should not enter without respiratory protection, and a rescue is not a tech job; call emergency services. Equipment near a pit, such as a fan, a pump or a heater, gets worked on from outside, and only when the farmer confirms the area is ventilated and the pit work is stopped. If you feel unsure, stop and leave.

Milk tank and walk-in emergencies

When a bulk tank or a walk-in cooler stops, the product is the clock. A university extension newsletter says milk should cool below 40 F within 30 minutes of the first milking, hold at 36 to 38 F, and not let the blend rise above 45 F when later milk is added. Those numbers are why a failed tank is urgent. Our dairy milk cooling lesson covers the system itself.

On the phone, ask the farmer what the tank temperature is now, when the next milking is, and whether there is a backup plan for the milk. What to do with the milk is the farmer's decision with the processor, not ours. Our job is a safe, quick diagnosis and an honest estimate of when the cooling will work.

Power and generators

Many farms have a standby generator and a transfer switch. Ask whether the farm is on utility power or generator before you start. Do not connect a portable generator to a building yourself; a proper transfer switch and a qualified electrician are needed, and exhaust must stay out of barns and occupied spaces. See running equipment on a generator for sizing and hookup basics.

Remote sites and parts planning

Farms are often far from a supply house, and cell service may be poor. Tell the office where you are and when you will check in; see working alone as an HVAC tech. Before you leave, get the equipment make, model and nameplate details on the phone, and ask what the symptoms are. Stock the van for the likely failures. A reasonable checklist for a tank or walk-in call:

  • Contactors and relays that match the common sizes.
  • Run and start capacitors, and a start relay.
  • Condenser and evaporator fan motors or the common sizes.
  • Thermostat or temperature control, and a probe.
  • Fuses and breakers of the sizes you see on that equipment.
  • Gauges, leak detector, recovery gear and the refrigerant named on the nameplate.
  • Meter, extension cord, work light, boot covers, gloves and water.

Sun, cold and drinking water

Condensers sit outside and barns can be hot or freezing. The federal workplace safety page lists heat illness among farm hazards and points to water, shade and rest breaks. Our lessons on heat stress and cold stress apply here. Bring your own water, because the farm may be far from a store.

Respect the farm schedule

Milking and feeding run on the clock, and animals do not wait. Ask when the quiet window is. Do not block lanes the cows or feed trucks use. Keep noise and sudden movement low near animals. Arrive when you said you would, tell the farmer early if a part is missing, and clean up before you go. A farmer who trusts you will call you the next time the tank fails.

Worked example (our own, assumptions only)

Every number below is our own assumption for teaching, not data from a source.

  • The farm is 75 minutes from the shop. We leave at 6:15 and arrive at 7:30, after an assumed morning milking ends.
  • Diagnosis takes 45 minutes, until 8:15. The compressor contactor has failed.
  • The nearest supply house is 40 minutes from the farm, one way. Counter time is 15 minutes.
  • Installing the part takes 30 minutes, a test run 30 minutes and cleanup and paperwork 15 minutes. That is 75 minutes.

Part in the van: 8:15 plus 75 minutes is 9:30. Drive 75 minutes home, back at 10:45.

Part missing: leave at 8:15, arrive at the supply house at 8:55, finish at the counter at 9:10, and return at 9:50. That is 95 minutes for the parts run (40 + 15 + 40). Add 75 minutes for the fix, so we finish at 11:05 and are home at 12:20.

The difference is 12:20 minus 10:45, which is 95 minutes. In a real case, this means a tank without cooling for 95 minutes longer, and we have to call the farmer before leaving for the part so they can plan around the next milking, assumed here to be mid-afternoon. A stocked van saves the day.

Field card: Farm Service Calls for HVAC and Refrigeration Techs

Call first, stock the van, never enter a pit.

  • Call ahead and ask the farm's biosecurity rules; avoid going farm to farm the same day when asked.
  • Boot covers, washable boots, clean gear, and use the farm's clean and dirty line.
  • Ask where animals and moving equipment are before carrying tools in.
  • Dust and ammonia: short time in the barn, step out if symptoms start.
  • Manure pits: never enter, never enter to rescue, call emergency services.
  • Failed tank: ask the temperature and next milking; the milk decision is the farmer's.
  • Bring the likely parts, water and a check-in plan.
