Sol-Air Temperature and Roof Heat Gain: How Dark and Light Roofs Run Hotter Than Air, Absorptance, Long-Wave Loss and Ceiling Heat Flow

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A thermometer in the shade says 95 F, but a black roof in the same yard can be well over 150 F. Heat flows into the house according to the roof's temperature, not the weather report's. Sol-air temperature is a single "effective outdoor temperature" that folds sunlight, the outside air film and heat lost to the sky into one number you can use with ordinary R-value math. This lesson builds it step by step, compares a dark and a light roof, and shows what it means for cooling loads and for ducts in the attic.

Why roofs run hotter than the air

Sunlight that a roof does not reflect turns into heat at the surface. A U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) guide for selecting cool roofs defines solar reflectance as the fraction of sunlight a surface reflects, and says the rest becomes heat. So the absorbed fraction (absorptance) is one minus the solar reflectance. The same guide gives typical reflectance of 5 to 20 percent for dark materials and 55 to 90 percent for light-colored ones. The DOE says conventional roofs can reach 150 F or more on a sunny summer afternoon, and a reflective roof under the same conditions could stay more than 50 F cooler. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) heat island group puts clear-noon sunlight on a flat surface at about 1000 watts per square meter.

Two other things change the surface temperature. Heat leaves by convection to the air, and a surface also radiates heat to the cooler sky. Thermal emittance, which the DOE guide describes as how efficiently a surface cools itself by emitting thermal radiation, runs from 0.80 to 0.95 for most nonmetallic roofing and is low for bare shiny metal, which therefore stays hotter. Our radiation lesson covers why emissivity matters.

The surface energy balance

The sol-air equation

A lecture on cooling-load calculation from an Illinois university gives the equation in this form: sol-air temperature = To + (a x I) / ho - (e x dR) / ho. Here To is the outdoor air temperature, a is the solar absorptance, I is the sunlight striking the surface in Btu/h per square foot, ho is the outside surface film coefficient, e is the surface emittance, and dR is the difference between the long-wave radiation arriving at the surface and what a blackbody at air temperature would emit. For horizontal surfaces the lecture uses ho = 3.0 Btu/h per square foot per degree F and dR = 20 Btu/h per square foot, and says the long-wave term is then about 7 F (with e = 1, 20 / 3.0 = 6.7, rounded to 7). Sol-air temperature is not the actual surface temperature. It is the air temperature that would, by convection alone, push the same heat into the surface that sun, air and sky together do. With any heat flowing in, the real surface is a little cooler than Te by q / ho.

Read each term as a push. The sunlight term raises the effective temperature by an amount that grows with absorptance. The long-wave term lowers it, because a roof facing the sky loses heat to it. At night, with no sun, that term alone leaves the effective temperature below the air temperature; a Canadian government building digest says an insulated roof on a clear, calm night may sit about 10 F below air temperature.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Inputs: outdoor air 95 F (assumed). Sunlight on the roof 1000 W per square meter, which the LBNL page gives for clear noon; converted at 0.317 Btu/h per square foot per W per square meter, that is 317 Btu/h per square foot. Outside film coefficient ho = 3.0 and long-wave correction 7 F, both from the Illinois lecture example above. Dark roof absorptance 0.9 (solar reflectance 0.1, in the DOE dark range); light roof absorptance 0.3 (reflectance 0.7, in the DOE light range). Indoor air 75 F (assumed). The roof/ceiling assembly total R is R-20 or R-38, including the inside air film (both assumed, and not including the outside film because ho is already in the sol-air formula). Steady heat flow, a single instant at the sunny peak.

  1. Dark roof sunlight term. 0.9 x 317 / 3.0 = 285.3 / 3.0 = 95.1 F.
  2. Dark roof sol-air. 95 + 95.1 - 7 = 183.1 F, which is 88.1 F above the air.
  3. Light roof sunlight term. 0.3 x 317 / 3.0 = 95.1 / 3.0 = 31.7 F.
  4. Light roof sol-air. 95 + 31.7 - 7 = 119.7 F, which is 24.7 F above the air.
  5. Heat flow per square foot. q = (Te - 75) / R. Dark, R-20: 108.1 / 20 = 5.41 Btu/h. Light, R-20: 44.7 / 20 = 2.24 Btu/h. Dark, R-38: 108.1 / 38 = 2.84. Light, R-38: 44.7 / 38 = 1.18.
  6. Air temperature only, for comparison. (95 - 75) / 20 = 1.00 Btu/h per square foot at R-20, and 20 / 38 = 0.53 at R-38.

What it means. On this hot afternoon the dark roof moves 5.4 times the heat that the air temperature alone predicts, and the light roof moves 2.2 times. The difference between them is (183.1 - 119.7) / 20 = 3.17 Btu/h per square foot, or about 3,170 Btu/h across 1,000 square feet at R-20 (roughly a quarter of a ton). Going to R-38 shrinks that difference to about 1,670 Btu/h, because insulation divides every case by a bigger R. A DOE guide says it directly: a well-insulated roof benefits less from a cool roof than a poorly insulated one, and cool roofs are never a substitute for sufficient insulation.

A reality check. Our dark-minus-light gap of 63.4 F (183.1 - 119.7) is a little larger than the 55 F LBNL gives for a white roof (reflectance 0.8) against a gray roof (0.2) in its standard conditions, which is the same order of size. Our example uses one fixed ho and ignores wind. A higher ho, as on a breezy afternoon, lowers both sunlight terms, since each is divided by ho. LBNL also tabulates surface temperature rise above summer-afternoon air of 69 F for a standard gray roof and 78 F for a standard red tile (reflectance 0.10), against our 88 F for the dark roof. Real conditions vary, so treat our numbers as a model of a hot, calm, sunny peak.

Why a clean white roof does not stay that way

Reflectance changes with time. In the same LBNL tabulation a bright white roof (reflectance 0.85 new) shows a rise above air of 9 F when new and 28 F after aging, as dirt and weathering cut reflectance. Rerunning our arithmetic with a lower reflectance shows the same direction: at absorptance 0.5 the sunlight term is 0.5 x 317 / 3.0 = 52.8 F, and the sol-air is 95 + 52.8 - 7 = 140.8 F. Dirt turns a light roof back toward a dark one.

What it means for cooling loads

  • Peak load. The DOE guide says a cool roof can reduce peak cooling loads enough to reduce the air conditioning capacity needed, but it describes that benefit as modest and tied to replacing equipment at the same time.
  • Time matters. The sunny peak does not hit the room at the same time, because roof construction slows and spreads the heat. Our one-instant example gives the size of the push, and a full load calculation spreads it over the day. The sol-air value is the input to that process.
  • Climate. The DOE guide says cool roofs save the most in hot climates and can raise energy costs in colder climates, where they give up winter solar heat, although heating penalties are usually smaller than cooling savings.
  • Insulation first. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) simulations of older homes with attic ducts found that raising ceiling insulation from R-19 to R-38 cut cooling by 21 percent in a hot climate, while a cool-colored shingle cut cooling by about 4.5 percent there. These are model results for one set of homes and climates, so use them as a direction, not a promise.

What it means for attic ducts

A roof deck at sol-air temperature heats the attic air, and the attic air is what the ducts see. Our supply duct heat gain lesson used an assumed 130 F attic. An ORNL field experiment on tile roofs with reflectance 0.24 (conventional) and 0.45 (cool-color coated) measured attic air about 2 C to 4 C (3.6 F to 7.2 F) cooler under the coated roof, in two matched house pairs (the authors note that occupant behavior and other equipment effects may have affected the results). Our arithmetic on the earlier example: if the attic were 7 F cooler, 123 F instead of 130 F, the R-6 run's temperature difference falls from 75 F to 68 F, so its heat gain drops from 569 to 569 x 68 / 75 = 516 Btu/h, about 9 percent less. That is helpful but small. ORNL's new-construction simulations also found duct losses in the attic at least double the ceiling load, and in one hot-climate case moving the ducts into conditioned space cut the annual attic load by 93 percent. A cooler roof helps ducts, but sealing, insulating and relocating ducts do more. See the radiant barrier lesson for another way to cut the heat that reaches the attic floor and ducts.

Series R-values and the ceiling

The last step is ordinary: heat flow equals temperature difference divided by total resistance. Our wall and ceiling R-value lesson covers adding layers and films. Sol-air temperature only replaces the outdoor temperature on the hot side. Windows use a different tool, covered in our window U-factor and SHGC lesson.

Field checks

  • Roof color and age tell you whether to expect the dark or the light case. A dirty "white" roof behaves like something between.
  • Attic temperature readings on a sunny afternoon, compared with the outdoor air, show how much of the roof's push is reaching the ducts.
  • Do not size equipment from roof color alone; follow your load calculation method and its tables.

Field card: Sol-Air Temperature and Roof Heat Gain

Sunlight, outside film and sky radiation combine into one effective outdoor temperature.

  • Absorptance a = 1 - solar reflectance.
  • Te = To + a x I / ho - 7 F (horizontal, ho = 3.0 in the lecture example). Heat flow q = (Te - Tin) / R.
  • Our example, 95 F air, 317 Btu/h per sq ft sun: dark roof (a = 0.9) 183.1 F, light roof (a = 0.3) 119.7 F.
  • At R-20 that is 5.41 and 2.24 Btu/h per sq ft, against 1.00 from air temperature alone.
  • More insulation shrinks the difference between dark and light roofs. Cool roofs do not replace insulation.
  • Dirt lowers reflectance over time. Cooler roofs lower attic and duct heat gain, but sealing and moving ducts matter more.
