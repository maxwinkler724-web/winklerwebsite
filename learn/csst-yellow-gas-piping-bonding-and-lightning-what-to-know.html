CSST Yellow Gas Piping: Bonding and Lightning, What to Know

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a basement ceiling and you may find flexible yellow pipe feeding a furnace or water heater. That is corrugated stainless steel tubing, or CSST. This lesson explains what it is, why lightning is the concern, and what "bonding" means, so you know what you are looking at and who to call.

Check the local rules

Check with the local code official. Gas and electrical codes are adopted and amended state by state, and manufacturers update their guides. This page is general education built from the sources listed below. It is not an installation guide and not legal advice. Bonding is not a do-it-yourself job.

What CSST is

CSST is a flexible gas pipe made of thin stainless steel with a ringed, corrugated wall and a plastic jacket. It is used for natural gas and propane. The New Hampshire State Fire Marshal's bulletin notes it has been installed in millions of homes since 1990 and describes it as running like a water line from the meter or propane regulator, sometimes hidden above ceilings or behind walls. Sizes in that bulletin run from 1/2 inch to 2 inches. The Maryland State Fire Marshal says it is typically covered in yellow plastic.

Not all CSST has a yellow jacket. Some products have a black jacket. For example, OmegaFlex's TracPipe CounterStrike sheet describes a black, electrically conductive jacket. Others have an insulating, arc-resistant jacket. The 2021 ICC CodeNotes summary says arc-resistant tubing is marked "AR" and listed to CSA/ANSI LC-1. So color alone does not tell you the type. Look for the printed marking on the jacket and the manufacturer name.

Why lightning is the concern

The Maryland and New Hampshire fire marshals both say that lightning striking a building or the ground nearby can send electrical energy through metal piping, and that the thin CSST wall can be damaged, causing gas leaks or fire. Because of this, codes added special bonding steps for CSST.

What bonding means

Bonding means tying metal parts together with a wire so they stay at nearly the same voltage. If everything rises together during a surge, there is less chance of a spark jumping between the gas line and another metal system. The Gastite bulletin says bonding and grounding reduce the risk of damage and fire from lightning and reduce arcing between conductive systems. It also says a full lightning protection system is a separate subject (it points to NFPA 780).

What the codes and makers say

We did not read the NFPA 54 or NEC text itself, so these are summaries and are labeled that way.

  • Fuel gas code (secondary summary). The ICC's 2021 CodeNotes summarizes IFGC section 310. Yellow (non-arc-resistant) CSST needs an extra bonding connection. The wire is 6 AWG copper or larger, runs no more than 75 feet, and goes to a grounding electrode, the grounding electrode conductor or the service enclosure. One listed clamp (UL 467) goes downstream of the gas meter or propane second-stage regulator, on rigid pipe, a fitting or manifold, or the brass CSST fitting. The clamp is never put on the corrugated tubing. A separate ground rod just for gas piping is not allowed.
  • Arc-resistant CSST. The same summary says AR tubing does not need the extra connection if every appliance has an equipment grounding conductor. If any appliance lacks one, or yellow tubing is in the same system, bond it like yellow.
  • NEC 250.104(B) (secondary summary). The North Carolina State Fire Marshal's page on the 2014 NEC says gas piping likely to become energized must be bonded, and that non-arc-resistant CSST follows the manufacturer's instructions plus the 6 AWG copper jumper from the fuel gas code.
  • Manufacturers. Gastite's 2010 bulletin also says 6 AWG copper or equivalent, and never use the tubing as the clamp location. That older bulletin gave no length limit, while the 2021 summary says 75 feet, a good example of why you follow the current guide for the exact product.

Worked example: counting the wire run

This is a made-up house. It shows the type of check an electrician or inspector makes. It is not a design.

  1. The house has yellow CSST. The gas meter is on the outside wall and the electrical panel is in the basement.
  2. The clamp location must be downstream of the meter, on black iron pipe or the brass fitting.
  3. Measure the wire path (not straight line): 22 ft along the basement wall + 12 ft up to the panel's grounding connection + 9 ft to the clamp = 43 ft.
  4. Compare to the 75 ft summary limit: 75 - 43 = 32 ft of margin. The 6 AWG copper jumper passes the length check.
  5. Now suppose the panel is at the far end, so the path measures 22 + 12 + 48 = 82 ft. That is 82 - 75 = 7 ft too long. An electrician would choose a different clamp or bonding point that keeps the run within the limit. A thinner wire is never the fix, because 6 AWG is the minimum.

Who does the work

The New Hampshire bulletin says a licensed fuel gas fitter certified in CSST or a licensed electrician makes the connection, and tells homeowners not to open an energized panel. Maryland says a licensed electrician should confirm the bonding. Rules differ by state, so ask the local code official who may do the work and whether a permit and inspection are needed.

What an HVAC tech does at the appliance

  • Recognize it. Look for ringed flexible pipe, usually yellow, sometimes black, entering the appliance shutoff or a manifold, and for brand markings printed on the jacket.
  • Look, do not alter. Note whether you can see a bond wire and clamp near the meter or manifold. Do not move, loosen or install a clamp unless you are licensed and trained for that work.
  • Protect the tubing. Report kinks, crushed spots, burn marks, or pin holes. If you smell gas, follow the steps in Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do.
  • Report. Write on the work order: "CSST present, bond not verified" or "bond wire seen at ..." and tell the customer to have a licensed electrician or gas fitter check it.

For pipe sizing, see Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method. For basic wiring ideas, see Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Field card: CSST Bonding and Lightning

See it, note it, report it, do not touch the bond.

  • CSST is flexible ringed gas pipe, often yellow; some jackets are black.
  • Lightning can send current through gas piping and damage thin CSST.
  • Summaries say yellow CSST needs a 6 AWG copper bond, 75 ft or less, clamped after the meter, never on the tubing.
  • Arc-resistant (AR, LC-1) tubing has different rules; follow the maker.
  • A licensed electrician or CSST-certified gas fitter does the bond.
  • Report "CSST present, bond not verified."
