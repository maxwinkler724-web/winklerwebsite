Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Delta-T is how hard each gallon of water is working. When it falls well below design, the plant moves lots of water for little cooling, and chillers can end up running that were not really needed.

The basic relationship

For a given load, flow and delta-T trade off: more flow means a smaller temperature difference, and less flow means a larger one. Double the flow for the same heat and the delta-T halves. The math is covered in Hot-Water Flow Rate Math. Low delta-T syndrome is the name for a chilled-water system whose real delta-T is lower than the chillers were designed for.

Why chillers care

An HVAC School article treats the evaporator delta-T as the way to judge how much load a chiller carries. In its example, a chiller designed for a 10 F difference that shows 5 F is carrying about half its capacity. A water-side article works through a plant example in which a lower delta-T leaves a 250-ton chiller delivering only about 187 tons, so a load that two machines should handle ends up needing three. These are the pages’ own example figures, and your plant’s design data decides what applies to you.

Causes the sources point to

  • Poor heat transfer at the coils, which sends water back cooler than it should be. Fouled coils or scaled surfaces are examples.
  • Oversized coils and valves, which let the water pass through too freely.
  • Poor hydronic balancing, which wastes pumping effort at part load.
  • Excess flow from improper flow settings or a pressure setpoint that is too high.
  • Bypass mixing: three-way valves or open bypasses send cold supply water straight to the return and dilute the return temperature.
  • Control valves stuck open or leaking through.
  • Bad sensors, or sensors placed where water has not mixed.

The clue to look for

If delta-T falls while flow rises, it points to a system problem rather than a chiller problem. Check the system side before opening a chiller: coil valves, bypass position, pump speed and differential pressure setpoint, and sensors. Record supply and return temperatures at the same moments from instruments you trust, together with load, outdoor conditions and which pumps are running.

Low versus high delta-T

Low delta-T means lots of water for little work. A very high delta-T is less common and can point to too little flow: a blocked strainer, air, a partly closed valve or a weak pump. A room that is too hot with a large delta-T is a flow problem until proven otherwise. Strainers and cavitation are covered in Pump Cavitation Basics.

What can be done

The sources suggest the fixes follow the causes: size coils and valves correctly, keep coils clean, balance the system, and set flow rates to match need. In a working plant these changes belong to the engineers, balancers and controls staff, and they should be made with records of valve positions beforehand. Do not rebalance to hide a fault elsewhere, and do not override pump or chiller sequencing on your own.

Why it matters

A low delta-T wastes pumping energy, starts extra chillers, and shortens equipment life. The best defense is a log that shows delta-T trending down early, so the right people can investigate before the summer peak. See Chilled-Water Pumps and Delta-T for the pump side.

Printable checklist

  • Supply and return temperature read at the same moment
  • Delta-T compared with design and with last week
  • Load, outdoor conditions and pumps running recorded
  • Differential pressure and pump speed recorded
  • Bypass and three-way valve positions noted
  • Sensor spot-check against a handheld meter
  • Coil valves checked for stuck-open or leaking-through behavior (report, do not force)
  • Strainer pressure drop noted
  • Trend reported to lead or controls staff
