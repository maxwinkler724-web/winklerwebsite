Compressed Air Receiver Tanks and Compressor Cycling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A compressed air receiver is a pressure vessel that stores air so the compressor does not have to chase every change in demand. How big the receiver is decides how long it can carry a load and how often the compressor starts, loads and unloads. This lesson gives you the arithmetic, the reasons for wet and dry receivers, and the drain and relief rules a facility tech checks.

Why a system needs a receiver

A DOE tip sheet on air storage says storage lets a system meet peak demand and helps hold system pressure without starting more compressors. It adds that the right amount depends on demand patterns, the air quantity and quality needed, and the compressor and its controls. An Oregon State University energy center page says compressors with unloading or on-off controls need a receiver to hold air for later use, and that a bigger receiver lengthens the on-off cycles. A rule of thumb on that page is about one gallon of receiver per cfm of output. A national-lab training deck calls the older one-to-three gallons per cfm rule no longer good practice and suggests about three to five gallons per cfm, with a system review as the better guide. Treat all of these as starting points, not design values.

How long can a receiver carry a load?

Air stored at higher pressure can be drawn down until pressure falls to the lowest value the equipment will tolerate. The time that supply lasts, with the compressor not helping, is:

minutes = V x (P1 - P2) / (14.7 x cfm)

  • V is the receiver volume in cubic feet. Divide gallons by 7.48 to convert.
  • P1 is the starting pressure and P2 the lowest allowed pressure, both in psig, so P1 - P2 is the usable pressure band.
  • 14.7 is atmospheric pressure in psia.
  • cfm is the air the plant draws that the compressor is not supplying. If the compressor is running and adds some air, use only the shortfall.

This matches the storage relation in the national-lab deck, which also uses atmospheric pressure of 14.7 psia and the net flow after spare compressor air. It ignores air temperature changes and the small extra volume of piping, so use it for sizing sense-checks, not guarantees. The cfm figure is free air, the volume at atmospheric conditions, which is how compressors are rated.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assumptions: one 120 gallon receiver; a compressor rated 50 cfm that loads at 105 psig and unloads at 125 psig; a steady plant demand of 30 cfm; piping volume ignored.

  1. Convert volume: 120 gal / 7.48 = 16.04 cubic feet.
  2. Usable pressure band: 125 - 105 = 20 psi.
  3. Unloaded, the receiver alone feeds 30 cfm: minutes = 16.04 x 20 / (14.7 x 30) = 320.8 / 441 = 0.73 minute, about 44 seconds.
  4. Loaded, the compressor gives 50 cfm and the plant takes 30 cfm, so the net refill is 20 cfm: minutes = 320.8 / (14.7 x 20) = 320.8 / 294 = 1.09 minutes, about 65 seconds.
  5. One full cycle is 1.09 + 0.73 = 1.82 minutes, about 109 seconds, or roughly 33 cycles per hour.
  6. Double the receiver to 240 gallons and both times double, so the cycle becomes about 3.6 minutes and cycling drops to roughly 16 per hour.

The pattern is what matters: cycle time scales with receiver volume and the pressure band, and, for a given receiver, is longest when plant demand is about half the compressor capacity. A narrower band, set to save energy, shortens the cycle unless you add storage.

Load and unload cycles

Under load and unload control, the motor keeps running. At the upper pressure the compressor unloads, and at a lower pressure it loads again. A university note on compressor controls and the national-lab deck both point out that a compressor still draws power while unloaded and that big receivers lengthen the cycle. The Oregon State page suggests timing the on-off and load-unload periods with a stopwatch for a use-factor calculation. A short cycle is a clue that the receiver is small, the pressure band is narrow, or a leak or open drain is adding demand. Check the cycle times before and after any setpoint change.

Wet and dry receivers

The national-lab deck describes two roles. A wet receiver sits at the compressor discharge, where it reduces moisture ahead of the dryer. A dry receiver sits after the dryer and filters, so a sudden demand draws already-dried air. The deck suggests splitting storage roughly 25 to 30 percent wet and 70 to 75 percent dry. For the dryer itself, see the dryer lesson.

Drains

OSHA 29 CFR 1910.169 requires a drain pipe and valve at the lowest point of each receiver to remove oil and water, allows automatic traps in addition to the drain valve, and says the receiver must be drained often enough to prevent excessive liquid buildup. It also says drains, handholes and manholes must be accessible, and that a receiver may not be buried or put where it cannot be reached. The national-lab deck recommends automatic drain traps that lose no air in place of timer or manual drains. A drain stuck open wastes air and makes the cycle shorter. A drain stuck shut fills the bottom of the vessel with water, which cuts the real storage volume.

Pressure relief and the gauge

Under the same OSHA section, each receiver needs a visible pressure gauge and one or more spring-loaded safety valves. Their combined relieving capacity must keep pressure within 10 percent above the maximum allowable working pressure. No valve of any kind may sit between the receiver and its safety valves, the safety devices must not be easy to disable, and the valves must be tested frequently at regular intervals. The rule names a specific edition of the pressure vessel code for new receivers. Receivers hold energy after the compressor stops, so follow your site lockout procedure and bleed pressure safely before opening any fitting. Related reading on relief hardware is in boiler safety valves and overpressure protection.

Field card: Compressed Air Receivers and Cycling

Bigger receiver, longer cycle, steadier pressure.

  • Minutes = V (cubic feet) x (P1 - P2) / (14.7 x cfm). Gallons / 7.48 = cubic feet.
  • Use only the shortfall cfm the compressor is not supplying.
  • Doubling the receiver doubles the time. Narrowing the band shortens it.
  • Wet receiver at the compressor, dry receiver after the dryer.
  • Drain at the lowest point, automatic traps, drained often enough.
  • Gauge plus relief valve; nothing between receiver and relief valve.
  • Isolate and bleed pressure before opening anything.
