Induction Motor Speed and Slip for HVAC Fan and Pump Motors

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A 4-pole motor on 60 Hz power never quite turns at 1800 rpm, and that small shortfall carries real information. This lesson covers synchronous speed, slip, how to read shaft speed safely with a tachometer, and how the slip method estimates motor load and where that estimate stops being trustworthy.

Synchronous speed: set by frequency and poles

A three-phase or split-phase induction motor makes a rotating magnetic field in the stator. How fast that field turns depends only on the line frequency and the number of magnetic poles. University motor notes give the relation as synchronous rpm = 120 x frequency (Hz) / number of poles. The rotor chases the field but never quite catches it, because a rotor turning at exactly field speed would see no changing flux and make no torque.

At 60 Hz the arithmetic is quick (our own math from that formula):

  • 2 poles: 120 x 60 / 2 = 3600 rpm
  • 4 poles: 120 x 60 / 4 = 1800 rpm
  • 6 poles: 120 x 60 / 6 = 1200 rpm
  • 8 poles: 120 x 60 / 8 = 900 rpm

The U.S. Department of Energy motor fact sheet lists 3600, 1800 and 1200 rpm for 2, 4 and 6 poles at 60 Hz. Pole count is fixed by how the stator is wound, so a motor does not change it on its own. Multi-speed fan motors that switch pole count have a separate winding arrangement; speed changes on a PSC blower are a different mechanism, covered in PSC blower motors.

Slip: why the nameplate says 1750, not 1800

Slip is the gap between synchronous speed and actual rotor speed. The Department of Energy fact sheet says the actual speed is always below synchronous. As a percent:

Slip % = (synchronous rpm - actual rpm) / synchronous rpm x 100

For a 4-pole motor with a 1750 rpm nameplate, slip is (1800 - 1750) / 1800 x 100 = 2.78 percent (our own example). Take two nameplate speeds for 4-pole 60 Hz motors as our own examples, 1725 and 1750 rpm, which is 4.17 percent or 2.78 percent slip. University notes put full-load slip of a typical induction motor under about 5 percent, with a lightly loaded motor under about 1 percent. So a healthy 4-pole motor lands somewhere in the 1700s, never at 1800. A 4-pole motor reading 1800 on a good tachometer is a sign you are measuring something other than the rotor, or the motor is not a 4-pole motor.

The three-phase motor nameplate lesson covers the other nameplate data; here we only need the full-load rpm. For why motor choice matters to energy use, see motor efficiency and the repair-or-replace decision.

How slip grows with load

The rotor makes torque because it slips. More load needs more torque, so the rotor falls further behind the field. In the normal working range, slip rises roughly in step with load. The Department of Energy fact sheet states it directly: a motor running at 50 percent load has slip about halfway between its full-load speed and synchronous speed. A motor with no load turns close to synchronous speed. A motor at full load turns at the nameplate rpm. An overloaded motor slows below nameplate rpm, draws more current, and heats up.

For fans this means the speed you read on a belt drive is not fixed. A fan that is moving more air (more load) turns the motor a little slower, which is why airflow math uses measured speed and not the nameplate. See the fan laws lesson for what a speed change does to airflow and power.

Checking speed with a tachometer

You can measure motor shaft speed, or fan or pump shaft speed, with three common tool types:

  • Contact tachometer. A rubber tip or wheel pressed on the shaft end or a center-drilled hole. It needs a reachable, clean shaft end and steady hand pressure.
  • Optical (photo) tachometer. A visible beam reads a strip of reflective tape on the shaft, sheave or coupling. The tape pulse count gives rpm with no contact.
  • Stroboscopic tachometer. A flashing light you tune until a mark seems to stand still. The fact sheet calls a battery-powered strobe the safest, most convenient and usually most accurate option, and notes that it avoids stopping the motor to apply paint or tape.

If you do need tape on a rotating part, apply it with the equipment stopped and locked out. OSHA's lockout and tagout rule, 29 CFR 1910.147, covers servicing where unexpected startup could injure workers. Use one small piece, press it down well, and keep it clear of the guard openings. Then restore the guards before you start the equipment.

Safety near rotating parts. OSHA machine guarding rule 29 CFR 1910.212 requires employers to protect workers from rotating parts and ingoing nip points. Belts, sheaves, couplings and open shafts catch cuffs, lanyards, gloves, hair and meter leads. Read speed through an access opening or from a safe distance whenever you can, keep leads and the tachometer cord away from moving parts, and do not remove a guard to take a reading. See belt guards, coupling guards and fan guards.

From motor rpm to fan or pump rpm

On a belt-driven fan, the motor sheave and fan sheave set the speed ratio. Fan rpm = motor rpm x motor sheave diameter / fan sheave diameter. Assumed sheaves for our own example: a 5.0 in motor sheave and an 8.0 in fan sheave. At 1770 rpm motor speed, fan speed = 1770 x 5.0 / 8.0 = 1106 rpm (1106.25 rounded). Belt slip makes the real fan speed a little lower, so measure it directly at the fan shaft when it matters. Sheave sizing and belt basics are in belt drives and sheave basics. A direct-coupled pump turns at the motor shaft speed.

Estimating load from slip

The slip method compares measured slip to full-load slip. The fact sheet states it as: load % = (synchronous rpm - measured rpm) / (synchronous rpm - nameplate rpm) x 100.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions: a 4-pole, 60 Hz motor driving a fan; nameplate full-load speed 1750 rpm; tachometer reads 1770 rpm at normal operation; supply voltage is at nameplate value. The Department of Energy fact sheet uses these same speeds in its own example.

  1. Synchronous speed = 120 x 60 / 4 = 1800 rpm.
  2. Full-load slip = 1800 - 1750 = 50 rpm (2.78 percent of 1800).
  3. Measured slip = 1800 - 1770 = 30 rpm (1.67 percent of 1800).
  4. Load = 30 / 50 = 0.60, or about 60 percent of full load.

This is an estimate, not a measurement. Multiply by the rated horsepower to get shaft output: on an assumed 10 hp nameplate, 60 percent is about 6 hp.

Limits of the slip method

The Department of Energy fact sheet says the slip method is generally not recommended for finding motor load in the field. The reasons are worth knowing:

  • Nameplate tolerance. The fact sheet says the largest uncertainty is the tolerance the motor industry allows on full-load speed, 20 percent of slip. In our example, 20 percent of 50 rpm is 10 rpm, so true full-load slip could be 40 to 60 rpm. The same 30 rpm of measured slip would then mean 30 / 40 = 75 percent or 30 / 60 = 50 percent load. The 60 percent answer could be anywhere from about 50 to 75.
  • Rounded nameplates. Makers often round full-load speed to multiples of 5 rpm. A 5 rpm difference on a 50 rpm slip changes the answer by about 10 percent: 30 / 55 = 55 percent, or 30 / 45 = 67 percent.
  • Voltage. The fact sheet says slip varies inversely with the square of terminal voltage. In our own rough math, a supply 5 percent low (0.95 of nameplate) gives 0.95 x 0.95 = 0.9025, so slip rises about 1 / 0.9025 = 1.108, or 11 percent. A motor truly at 60 percent load could then read near 66 percent. The DOE sheets give a correction using measured voltage; read it as a rough fix, not a precision tool.
  • Small slips are hard to read. Slip is the difference of two big numbers. A tachometer that is off by 5 rpm moves a 30 rpm slip by about 17 percent.
  • Rewound motors. A rewound motor can have different slip than its nameplate says (DOE).

When you can, check the motor with amps instead. Compare clamp-meter amps and volts to the nameplate as in measuring amp draw with a clamp meter, or use measured input power. The DOE sheets prefer input power readings when they are available. Use slip as a quick sanity check: it can separate a motor at 30 percent load from one at 100 percent, but it cannot tell 55 from 65 percent.

Reading the result

Speed well above nameplate rpm suggests the wrong pole count or frequency, such as a drive. Speed far below nameplate with high amps points to overload or low voltage. On a PSC blower a lower speed tap raises slip by design, so check that lesson first. ECM blower speed comes from its control board, not slip; see ECM blower motors.

Field card: Motor Speed and Slip

Induction motors always run a little below synchronous speed.

  • Synchronous rpm = 120 x Hz / poles: 3600, 1800, 1200 and 900 rpm for 2, 4, 6 and 8 poles at 60 Hz.
  • Slip % = (sync - actual) / sync x 100. A 1750 rpm 4-pole motor has 2.78 percent slip at full load.
  • Slip rises with load: halfway to full-load slip means about half load.
  • Our example: 1800 sync, 1750 nameplate, 1770 measured gives 30 / 50 = 60 percent load, an estimate only.
  • Nameplate speed tolerance, rounding and low voltage can swing the answer by 10 to 25 points.
  • Lock out before taping a shaft; keep guards on; keep leads clear of rotating parts.
  • Prefer amps or input power when you can measure them.
