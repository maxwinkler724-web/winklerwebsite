Locomotive and Rail Car Repair Shop HVAC: Tall Bays, Exhaust and Pits

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A locomotive shop is a hangar for machines that burn diesel fuel while you work around them. The bays are tall, the doors are huge, the floor has open pits, and the engines sometimes have to run indoors. This lesson covers the heating and ventilating problems that come with that, and how to size one piece of it.

Why these shops are different

Most shop heating lessons assume a car-sized vehicle. A repair bay for locomotives and rail cars may be several times that tall, with doors the width of a track and a roof that is far above anyone's head. Warm air rises to that roof and stays there. Heat leaves through the roof and through every door that opens to let a rail car in. The people are at the floor, working in or under the equipment. The ventilation has to deal with exhaust from engines that cannot simply be shut off for every task, and with pits where gases collect. For the general ideas of tailpipe capture, limits and heater height in smaller garages, read the vehicle repair and bus garage lesson. For door losses and building pressure, see the loading dock lesson. This page adds what is specific to rail shops.

What a federal look at a railroad shop found

A 1973 federal health hazard evaluation described a railroad run-and-service building that was 552 ft long, 60 ft wide and 35 to 40 ft high. When investigators arrived, only 10 ceiling exhaust fans were running. The owner added 36 more, for 46 in total, and the report noted that with all fans on, engine exhaust should almost always be under a fan. The building had been finished with no makeup air at all, so the owner cut in 46 louvered openings, each about 4 ft by 10 ft, set about 14 ft above the floor and spaced around the building. The measured contaminants in that study were below the limits of the day, but the investigators still noted slight eye burning during one 30-minute period with engines running, and recommended shutting engines off during servicing whenever possible.

Two things are worth taking from that. First, exhausting a huge building without a planned path for replacement air is a design gap, not a detail. Second, the cheapest control is often procedural: shut the engine off. Our own note: that report is old, and the exposure limits it used have changed, so use it for the building lessons, not for current limits.

Diesel exhaust: capture first, dilute second

Diesel exhaust is a mix of gases and fine particles. A 1983 federal evaluation of a city bus maintenance facility found that nitrogen dioxide was the only component that went over its criteria: six of 15 three-hour morning samples were above the federal health institute's 1 ppm 15-minute criterion, with a range of 1.01 to 1.81 ppm. The same report lists a recommended exposure limit of 35 ppm for carbon monoxide with a 200 ppm ceiling, and says there is no criterion for diesel exhaust as a whole. It also found that one diesel repair bay had no exhaust of any kind, and that several engines were running without being connected to the tailpipe system. Its recommendations included always connecting the tailpipe exhaust when an engine runs, and keeping the diesel repair area at a higher pressure than the next bay. Details are in the federal evaluation of the bus maintenance facility.

A recent federal evaluation of a fire station bay is useful for how capture systems fail in practice. It measured about 2,000 cfm total through four ceiling hoses, with each hose between 236 and 471 cfm, plus a general exhaust fan of about 5,000 cfm giving about 4.6 air changes per hour in a bay of roughly 64,000 ft³. Even so, one of four hoses was out of service, one engine's double-walled tailpipe did not fit its hose, and airflow was poorly matched to the engines. When the local system failed, fine particle levels took about 40 minutes to clear instead of about 20. Those are small vehicles, so a locomotive needs a much larger and purpose-built system. But the lessons carry over: match hoods to the stack, make capture start automatically when the engine starts, and give the technician a way to see that it is working.

  • Locomotive exhaust stacks are on the roof of the unit, so a hood or a telescoping stack connection above the locomotive is the usual capture point, with a duct run to the outdoors. Confirm the connection point and the hot-gas temperature with the equipment owner, since they vary by unit.
  • Interlocks. Start the exhaust fan whenever the hood is connected or the engine is running, and alarm if flow drops.
  • Makeup air. Every cfm exhausted must be replaced. Bring it in low enough to reach the work area and temper it, as the worked example shows.
  • Keep exhaust away from intakes and away from the ends of the shop where doors open.

Inspection pits: heavy vapors and confined-space rules

An inspection pit sits below the floor so a technician can work under the equipment. Gases heavier than air, such as propane and carbon dioxide, flow to low points and can stay there. A federal criteria document on confined-space work says that heavier-than-air gases collect in depressions such as pits and can linger for hours or days, and that some are odorless so the danger is easy to miss. Read it in the federal recommended standard for working in confined spaces.

Under the federal worker-safety agency's confined space rule, a confined space is one a person can bodily enter, with limited means of entry or exit, that is not designed for continuous occupancy. It becomes a permit-required space if it contains or may contain a hazardous atmosphere, which the rule defines to include a flammable gas or vapor above 10 percent of its lower flammable limit and oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent. The rule tests oxygen first, then combustible gases, then toxic gases, and where forced-air ventilation is used as the control, nobody enters until it has eliminated the hazard and it keeps running until everyone is out. Whether a particular pit is a permit space depends on its design and the site's own assessment, so ask the facility's safety lead, and see our confined spaces lesson.

For the HVAC design, this means three things. Put the pit exhaust intake low, near the pit floor, not at the shop ceiling. Keep it running whenever the pit is in use. And do not place a heater, a hot surface or a spark source in a pit unless the design and the local code allow it for the vapors that could be present. Pit heating is better done with warm floor-level air from outside the pit, or with radiant panels that are not an ignition source, as the authority having jurisdiction allows.

Sanding towers and fueling areas

Sanding towers (the stations where locomotives are loaded with traction sand) make dust, and fueling areas can release fuel vapor. Treat each as its own zone: dust collection at the tower fill point, and vapor-aware ventilation and equipment selection at the fuel point. The facility's fire and safety lead and the authority having jurisdiction decide what classification applies. We do not give a classification here.

Heating a very tall bay: radiant, unit heat and destratification

A federal field report on a warehouse with roughly 24 ft ceilings found warm air raising the average ceiling temperature 10 to 20°F above the thermostat set point, and found that roofs are the largest heat-loss area. A rail shop with bays far taller than that has the problem in a bigger way.

  • Radiant heaters warm the floor, the equipment and the people directly instead of heating the air. That suits spaces where doors are open often, because air heated and lost through the door is not the main supply of comfort. Radiant output must be placed and aimed with the maker's clearances to combustibles, and the flue or exhaust must go outdoors. The federal report we read does not test radiant heaters, so we give no efficiency figure for them.
  • Unit heaters heat the air and blow it down. Mount and aim them per the maker's instructions. In a very tall bay they need enough throw to reach the floor.
  • Destratification fans push the warm air under the roof back down so the heaters run less. They help most where heated air is already collecting at the roof.
  • Floor heat keeps the slab and the equipment wheels warm, and works with doors that open often. See the aircraft hangar lesson, which covers the same tall-bay, big-door problem, and the fire station bay lesson.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These inputs are assumptions for practice. They are not a design for any shop.

  • Assumed exhaust through the capture hood: 20,000 cfm.
  • Assumed outdoor design temperature: 10°F.
  • Assumed supply temperature wanted for the makeup air: 60°F.
  • Assumed heater efficiency: 80 percent.

The sensible heat formula for air is Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, where Q is in Btu/h. The 1.08 is a standard rounded factor for air near normal conditions (about 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb°F).

  1. Temperature rise: 60 − 10 = 50°F.
  2. Heat to temper the air: 1.08 × 20,000 × 50 = 21,600 × 50 = 1,080,000 Btu/h.
  3. Heater input at 80 percent efficiency: 1,080,000 ÷ 0.80 = 1,350,000 Btu/h.

That is one million Btu/h of heating just for the replacement air, before any wall, roof or door loss. If the hood runs only while an engine is in the bay, a control that starts the fan with the engine and stops it afterward avoids paying for this heat the rest of the day. If the exhaust is cut to 10,000 cfm, the heat halves, since Q is proportional to airflow. Check: 1.08 × 10,000 × 50 = 540,000 Btu/h. Real shops may allow a lower makeup air temperature than 60°F because the tall space and the radiant heat handle comfort, so the temperature is a design choice.

Field card: Locomotive and Rail Car Repair Shop HVAC

Capture at the stack, replace what you remove, pull heavy vapors from low.

  • Shut the engine off when the task allows; it is the cheapest control.
  • Connect the hood whenever an engine runs, and interlock the fan to it.
  • Every cfm exhausted needs a planned cfm of makeup air.
  • Pit vapors that are heavier than air collect low: exhaust low and test before entry.
  • Sensible heat: Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Roof air runs 10 to 20°F warmer in tall spaces; mix it down or heat the floor directly.
  • Classification of fueling and sanding areas belongs to the facility safety lead and the authority having jurisdiction.
