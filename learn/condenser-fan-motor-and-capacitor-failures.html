Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures

On a hot day, an outdoor unit with a silent or sluggish fan is a service call waiting to happen. The fan, the capacitor and the compressor are wired together closely enough that one failure can look like another. This lesson helps you separate them.

What the capacitor does

Many outdoor units use permanent split capacitor motors. HVAC School explains that the run capacitor gives the motor's start winding a phase-shifted current so the motor starts, turns the right way and has good torque. A fully failed capacitor acts like an open start winding. It is one of the most common parts to fail on a single-phase system. See Capacitors and Contactors for the basics.

Symptoms to separate

  • Capacitor weak or failed: the fan may hum without turning, spin slowly, run backward, draw high amps, or overheat. The compressor may fail to start.
  • Motor failed: shorted or open windings, or worn bearings (often from poor lubrication or contamination). HVAC School cautions that a motor that is not running has not necessarily failed. Wiring and a failed capacitor can stop it too, and replacing the motor would not fix those.
  • Normal behavior: on a heat pump the outdoor fan stops during defrost, which is not a fault.

A fan blade that spins freely by hand with the power locked off and a capacitor that tests low are a very different story from a stiff shaft.

Safe testing habits

  1. Pull the disconnect and verify no voltage with a meter you have checked: line to line and each leg to ground.
  2. Look at the capacitor. A bulged top or oil leakage means replace it. Plain surface rust is not a reason.
  3. Discharge it through a suitable resistor before touching terminals. HVAC School notes that a run capacitor in a normal system usually bleeds off through the motor windings, but do not count on it.
  4. Read the rating off the label: microfarads and voltage.
  5. Bench-test with a capacitance meter, or test under load by measuring amps on the start wire. The formula in the source multiplies the amps by 2652 and divides by the voltage across the capacitor to give microfarads. Both methods in the article use a 10 percent tolerance as the replacement guide, and readings above the rating are often measurement error. Confirm a weak result on the bench.

Replacing parts correctly

  • Capacitor: match the microfarad rating. The voltage rating can be equal or higher, never lower. Oversizing can overheat the winding. Mount it upright and photograph the wire positions first.
  • Fan motor: match frame size, pole count (RPM), voltage and phase, and do not go below original horsepower. HVAC School notes a 1075 RPM motor can replace a 1100, but an 875 RPM motor has a different pole count. Use the data tag, since wire colors vary.
  • Blade position: the blade must sit at the original depth in the shroud. A replacement motor with a different body length can leave it too high or low and cut airflow. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Rotation: check the blade turns the correct direction and clears the guard. Insulate any exposed rotation leads and tie them up, away from reach.

After the repair

Measure compressor and fan amps and compare with the nameplate. A failed fan during cooling quickly drives head pressure up and can harm the compressor, as discussed in Compressor Basics and Failure Modes. Whenever a capacitor or contactor fails, ask why: overvoltage, overheating and a pitted contactor can all shorten its life.

Field card: Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures

Hum, slow, backward, or silent: test before you replace.

  • The run capacitor shifts phase so the motor starts and runs well; failed means no start-winding current.
  • A silent fan is not proof of a bad motor; check capacitor and wiring.
  • Verify zero voltage, then discharge the capacitor safely before touching it.
  • Match microfarads; use equal or higher voltage, never lower.
  • Match motor poles, voltage and blade depth; check rotation after install.
