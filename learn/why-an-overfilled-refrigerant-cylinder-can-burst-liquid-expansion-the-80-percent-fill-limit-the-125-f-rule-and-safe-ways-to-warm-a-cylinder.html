Why an Overfilled Refrigerant Cylinder Can Burst: Liquid Expansion, the 80 Percent Fill Limit, the 125 F Rule and Safe Ways to Warm a Cylinder

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A refrigerant cylinder does not need to be defective to burst. An overfilled one can fail just from sitting in a warm van. The cause is not the usual pressure-temperature curve at all. It is a liquid with nowhere left to expand. This lesson explains why, where the fill limit comes from, why 125°F matters, and how to warm a cylinder without turning it into a hazard.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. Cylinder labels, the cylinder maker's instructions and your refrigerant's safety data sheet come first. This page is general education from federal pages read in 2026, plus our own arithmetic, and it is not a substitute for them.

Two different pressure behaviors

When a cylinder holds both liquid and vapor, its pressure is set by temperature alone. That is the pressure-temperature relationship you use on the gauges (see our pressure-temperature lesson). NIST data for R-134a show 85.8 psia at 70°F and 199.3 psia at 125°F, which is about 71.1 and 184.6 psig by our conversion. That works out to roughly 2 psi per degree on average between those two temperatures (our arithmetic). The pressure climbs, but it follows the curve, because extra heat just turns more liquid into vapor and the vapor space gives the system room.

The same NIST tables show something else. R-134a liquid is 76.24 lb per cubic foot at 70°F and 68.33 lb per cubic foot at 125°F. The density fell by about 10 percent, so the same pounds of liquid take up about 11.6 percent more room (our arithmetic). R-32 behaves the same way and is hit harder: its liquid goes from 60.99 to 51.77 lb per cubic foot over the same span, so it expands about 17.8 percent. The vapor space is whatever volume the liquid leaves unused, so as the liquid swells, the vapor space shrinks.

What happens when the vapor space is gone

Once the liquid fills the cylinder, the rules change. There is no vapor left to condense or compress. Liquid is nearly incompressible, so every further bit of warming that wants to expand the liquid has to push on the steel instead. The pressure then rises hydrostatically, far more steeply per degree than the curve for that refrigerant would predict, and it is no longer tied to the temperature on the chart. A cylinder that read a sensible 180 psi gauge pressure at one temperature can be far past that a few degrees later, and the gauge on the manifold will not warn you before the metal does. We have no cited figure for the exact slope, so we do not give one. The point is that it is steep enough to matter on a hot day.

This also explains a trap with the gauge. Reading the pressure and checking it against the chart tells you nothing about how full a cylinder is. A liquid-full cylinder and a half-full one can show the same pressure at the same temperature, until the full one warms up. Weight is the check, not pressure.

Where the fill limit comes from

The federal rule in 49 CFR 173.304(b) says the liquid part of a liquefied gas may not completely fill the packaging at any temperature up to and including 131°F (55°C). That is the real requirement: leave a vapor space at 131°F. The text of that section gives no percentage. Our existing lessons note that 80 percent of water capacity is the figure technicians use for recovery cylinders, and that it is trade practice rather than a number in that section. See the refillable cylinder markings lesson for the stamped water capacity and the push-pull recovery lesson for watching the scale while you fill.

The 80 percent figure is applied as a weight: 80 percent of the pounds of water the cylinder holds. It only protects you if that weight really leaves a vapor space for the refrigerant you are putting in, because refrigerants differ in liquid density. The stamped water capacity is a volume expressed in pounds of water, so the volume of liquid your pounds will occupy depends on the refrigerant and the temperature. If the cylinder label, a recovery machine instruction or a safety data sheet gives a lower limit for the refrigerant, use the lower one. Our cylinder labels lesson covers where those numbers appear.

Worked example (our own): R-134a at 70°F and 125°F

Our own example, with stated assumptions. The cylinder is a recovery cylinder with a stamped water capacity of 30 lb. We assume the steel does not stretch noticeably, that the cylinder starts at 70°F with the charge as saturated liquid plus vapor, and that we use water at 70°F for the capacity conversion. Liquid densities are from NIST saturated-fluid tables for R-134a and for water.

  1. Cylinder volume: water density at 70°F is 62.299 lb per cubic foot, so 30 lb ÷ 62.299 = 0.4815 ft³.
  2. 80 percent fill by weight: 0.80 × 30 lb = 24 lb of R-134a.
  3. Liquid volume at 70°F (76.240 lb/ft³): 24 ÷ 76.240 = 0.3148 ft³, which is 0.3148 ÷ 0.4815 = 65.4 percent of the cylinder. Vapor space: 34.6 percent.
  4. Liquid volume at 125°F (68.325 lb/ft³): 24 ÷ 68.325 = 0.3513 ft³, or 72.9 percent. Vapor space: 27.1 percent. Not liquid-full.
  5. Now an overfilled cylinder with 34 lb (113 percent of water capacity). At 70°F: 34 ÷ 76.240 = 0.4460 ft³, or 92.6 percent full. Only 7.4 percent vapor space is left.
  6. At 125°F: 34 ÷ 68.325 = 0.4976 ft³, or 103.3 percent. That is more than the cylinder holds, so the cylinder goes liquid-full before it reaches 125°F. A straight-line estimate between the two table points puts that near 109°F (our rough estimate).
  7. The most R-134a that keeps a vapor space at 125°F is 0.4815 × 68.325 = 32.9 lb. Any charge above about 33 lb in this cylinder goes liquid-full in that range.

The same 24 lb of R-32 tells a different story. Using NIST liquid densities of 60.988 lb/ft³ at 70°F and 51.772 lb/ft³ at 125°F, 24 lb is 81.7 percent of the volume at 70°F and 96.3 percent at 125°F. It leaves only 3.7 percent vapor space at 125°F, and the most R-32 that still leaves any vapor space at 125°F in this cylinder is 24.9 lb. So an 80 percent by weight rule gives R-134a plenty of margin and R-32 almost none, in our arithmetic. For R-454B or any other blend, get the liquid density from the maker's data and run the same arithmetic, or use the maker's stated fill limit. A cylinder used for R-32 or R-454B may also have a higher service pressure, so check the stamp.

The 125°F rule

A U.S. Department of Energy training document quotes a national compressed-gas handling pamphlet that says cylinders should not be subjected to a temperature above 125°F, should not be stored above 125°F, and should be thawed with water not exceeding 125°F. The same document quotes that a flame should never be permitted to touch any part of a cylinder, and that a user must not tamper with, paint, obstruct or remove the pressure relief device. Our own reading: 125°F is a ceiling for everything you do to a cylinder, whether storing it, warming it or leaving it in a vehicle. OSHA's compressed gas rule, 29 CFR 1910.101, requires employers to have cylinders checked for safety and points to the DOT regulations for inspection.

Safe ways to warm a cylinder

Technicians warm a cylinder to raise its pressure so vapor will flow. These are the ways that stay inside the rule:

  • Use a warm water bath or warm water poured on the shell, with the water at or below 125°F. Check the water with a thermometer rather than by feel.
  • Bring the cylinder into a warm, ventilated room and let it come up slowly.
  • Keep the valve and fittings out of the water and dry the cylinder afterward so it does not corrode.
  • Check the weight before you warm anything. A cylinder near its limit should be cooled, not warmed.

Never use a torch, open flame, heat gun, space heater, or a hot engine bay. Never apply heat directly to the valve or relief device. Refrigerants can break down into harmful products in a flame, which our refrigerant safety lesson covers. Heat is also hard to control from outside, because a flame can overheat the metal and a hot spot can fail even when the average temperature seems fine. For vehicles, keep full cylinders out of a closed hot cab or trunk; see the service van lesson.

What to do with a cylinder found overfilled

These steps are our own practical advice, not a rule, so also follow your employer's procedures and the cylinder maker's instructions.

  1. Do not heat it. Move it somewhere cool and shaded, upright and secured, and keep it out of any vehicle sitting in the sun.
  2. Weigh it on a scale and subtract the stamped tare to get the net refrigerant. Compare that with the limit for that refrigerant. In the example above, 34 lb in a 30 lb water capacity cylinder is over any reasonable limit.
  3. Do not vent the extra. Releasing refrigerant is a venting violation under EPA's Section 608 rules, and 40 CFR 82.154 says release after recovery is still a violation.
  4. Take off the excess liquid with a recovery machine into a proper cylinder with plenty of room, watching the scale on both. The push-pull setup in our linked lesson is one way to move liquid. Stop when the net weight is back under the limit.
  5. If the cylinder is bulging, leaking, corroded, hissing at the relief device or feels hot, do not handle it. Clear the area and call the supplier or emergency services.

Field card: Why an Overfilled Refrigerant Cylinder Can Burst: Liquid Expansion, the 80 Percent Fill Limit, the 125 F Rule and Safe Ways to Warm a Cylinder

Weigh it, keep it cool, never flame it.

  • Pressure follows the pressure-temperature curve only while a vapor space exists.
  • Liquid-full means a steep pressure rise from small temperature changes.
  • Federal rule: liquid must not completely fill the container up to 131°F.
  • 80 percent of water capacity by weight is trade practice, not a federal number. Check it for your refrigerant.
  • Stay at or below 125°F. Warm water bath only. No torch, no flame.
  • Overfilled? Keep it cool, weigh it, recover the excess. Never vent.
