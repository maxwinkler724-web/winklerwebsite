Protecting Your Tools and Service Van From Theft

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A service van is a rolling shop, and thieves know it. A lost meter, gauge set or recovery machine costs a tech days of work and can cost the employer a lot more. This lesson covers the habits that make a van a harder target, how to mark and list your tools, and what to do if a theft happens anyway.

Ask your employer first

Ask your employer first. Whose tools are covered, what the company expects you to do after a theft, and how any claim works are questions for your employer and its insurer. We do not give insurance or legal advice here. This page is general education built from public safety pages read in 2026.

Why vans and job sites get hit

A federal community-policing guide on construction-site theft describes sites that were often left open, with few guards and many workers and deliveries, so a thief could blend in with the crowd. It says tools and materials were constantly at risk. It also found that even when tools had serial numbers, the builders seemed not to keep records of them, which makes recovery hard.

A town police page on work-van theft adds a van-specific point: factory door locks are often easy to bypass. For a thief, a van parked in the same place every night, with tools in plain view and the same weak locks as every other van, is an easy choice. Our own plain reading: you cannot make a van theft-proof, but you can make it slower, riskier and less rewarding than the next one.

Locking habits

  • Lock every time. A police tip sheet for construction workers says to lock all doors and close all windows, and never to leave the vehicle running. Make this automatic, even for a two-minute stop at a supply counter or a customer's door.
  • Upgrade the locks. The work-van page recommends upgraded deadlocks or slam locks on all doors, since the factory locks are easy to bypass. Ask your employer before changing anything on a company van.
  • Lock the contents, not just the van. The same page suggests lockable toolboxes secured to the inside of the van, so a thief who gets in still has to deal with a second barrier.
  • Use a visible deterrent. It also lists a steering wheel lock or immobilizer as a visible warning.
  • Control keys. A city construction-site page says to issue keys to as few people as possible, keep a log of who has each key, and never hide keys on the site.

Do not leave tools visible

If a thief cannot see anything worth taking, many will move on. The work-van page suggests window grilles or opaque film to hide equipment from view. The habit is simple: put bags, gauge cases and meters in the cargo area before you park, not after, and never leave a bag or case sitting on a seat. Loading in front of a customer's neighbors tells the street what is in the van, so load when you can do it quickly.

Parking and night storage

  • Back it against something. The work-van page says to back the van against a wall or fence so the rear doors cannot be opened, and to park where the side door sits near another vehicle or structure.
  • Choose bright, busy spots over dim or remote ones.
  • Vary your pattern. It advises not using a routine parking spot that a thief can plan around.
  • Take the valuable items in at night. It says to remove tools overnight when possible, especially the high-value items. That usually means meters, gauges, cordless tools and the recovery machine. Ask your employer what is allowed, because company policy may call for a locked shop instead of your home.
  • Add alarms or tracking. The page lists an alarm or motion-activated light in the cargo area, tracking devices on the van and valuable tools, and a camera covering the parking spot.

Marking, serial numbers and a photo list

A city police page on property marking says engraving is meant to supplement, not replace, a record of serial and model numbers, and that officers should be given the serial numbers when you report a theft. It also notes that power tools should be engraved because their serial numbers may not be unique. A construction-theft page recommends marking equipment in at least two obvious places and one hidden place, using a hardened steel punch or etching tool for serial numbers. A work-van page recommends marking tools with your business name or a unique ID and keeping an inventory list with serial numbers and photos.

Put those together for a tech:

  • Mark each tool with an ID the employer assigns, or ask what to use for tools you own. Our own advice: do not engrave something personal like a government ID number without checking what your employer prefers.
  • For each item record the name, model number, serial number, where it is marked, and the date. Take a clear photo of the tool and of its serial plate.
  • Store the list somewhere other than the van, such as a copy with your supervisor.
  • Update it when you buy, borrow or retire a tool. For a start on what to own, see the tool list lesson linked below.

Refrigerant cylinders, gauges and recovery gear

Cylinders are valuable and heavy, and they are also a safety item. A federal construction rule for gas cylinders (which covers cylinders for welding and cutting) says valve protection caps stay in place and secured, cylinders carried in powered vehicles are secured in a vertical position, and cylinders are secured upright at all times except briefly while being hoisted or carried. That rule names welding gas cylinders, so we use it as the general habit for the brazing cylinders in your van. Follow the label, your employer and the maker for refrigerant cylinders; our cylinder carrying lesson covers the transport side.

Securing a cylinder in a rack or strap also helps with theft: a cylinder rolling loose is easy to grab, and a cylinder locked in a bracket in a locked cabinet is not. Practical habits, which are our own:

  • Keep cylinders upright, capped and strapped in a rack that also takes a lock or sits behind a locked partition.
  • Count full and partial cylinders at the end of the day, and log what was used on which job. A missing cylinder shows up the same day, not at the next inventory.
  • Put gauge sets, hoses, meters and the recovery machine in a locked box or take them in overnight.
  • Record the serial number of each gauge manifold, vacuum gauge, scale and recovery machine on the list. Record the cylinder tag or identification number printed on each cylinder you are responsible for, if your employer tracks them.

The parts-inventory lesson linked below covers counts and restocking; this lesson only adds the lock and record side.

Worked example: a tool list tally (our own example)

Assumptions (our own, made up for teaching): a tech's tool list has 48 items. Of those, 21 are items that carry a serial number (meters, gauges, a scale, a recovery machine, a vacuum pump, drills, drivers and saws). The other 27 are plain hand tools without one. On the list, 13 of the 21 serial-numbered items have their serial number written down.

  1. Items with a serial number recorded: 13.
  2. Items with a serial number but not yet recorded: 21 − 13 = 8.
  3. Share recorded: 13 ÷ 21 = 0.619, about 61.9 percent.
  4. Share still missing: 8 ÷ 21 = 0.381, about 38.1 percent.
  5. Check: 61.9 + 38.1 = 100 percent, and 13 + 8 = 21.

Use the right denominator. Dividing 8 by all 48 items gives 16.7 percent, which hides the problem because the 27 hand tools can never have a serial number. For those, the fix is a mark and a photo, not a serial. The goal for the 8 missing items is to read them off the tools this week, so the share missing reaches 0 percent.

After a theft

  1. Stay safe. Our own advice: do not confront anyone, and do not go back into a van you think is still being broken into. Call the police.
  2. Report it right away. A city construction-site page says to report the loss to police immediately and to have serial numbers and marking information ready for the responding officer. Ask for the report number.
  3. Give the officer the list. Hand over the serial numbers, the photos and a description of each marking.
  4. Tell the employer the same day, and give them a copy of the list and the report number. Ask whether they want to handle insurance contact; claims are between the employer and its insurer.
  5. Update the list with what was taken and replace locks or keys if any keys were in the van.

Field card: Protecting Your Tools and Service Van From Theft

Lock it, hide it, mark it, list it, report it.

  • Lock every door every time, and lock the toolboxes inside the van too.
  • Keep tools out of sight; do not park in the same dark spot every night.
  • Take high-value items in overnight when your employer allows it.
  • Mark each tool, then record model, serial number and a photo.
  • Keep a copy of the list off the van.
  • Cylinders: upright, capped, strapped, and counted daily.
  • After a theft: call police, get the report number, give the list to the officer and your employer.
  • Ask your employer or insurer about coverage and claims.
