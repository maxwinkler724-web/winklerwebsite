Water Leaking from the AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A puddle by the indoor unit is a surprise, but the cause is often a clogged drain line. Here is how to handle it without making it worse.

The short answer

Water around an air conditioner or furnace is almost always condensation that could not drain away. When cooling, the equipment removes moisture from the air, and a drain line carries it outside or to a drain. If that line clogs, the water backs up and can overflow. Many systems have a float switch that shuts the system off when this happens. A clogged drain is a common, fixable problem, but a leak can also mean something else, so be careful.

How the condensate drain works

Your cooling coil is cold, and water from the air collects on it, drips into a pan, and runs out through a drain pipe. The Department of Energy suggests running a stiff wire through the drain channels occasionally, since clogs prevent dehumidification and can stain walls or carpet. A trade article explains that slime, dirt, debris and even insects cause most clogs.

Warning signs

  • Water on the floor or wall near the indoor unit.
  • A full or overflowing drain pan.
  • The system shuts down and will not restart.
  • A musty smell from the vents.
  • Weak or no cooling.

Be aware that not all water is condensation. Water from a leaking pipe, a cracked pan, a humidifier, or a hot water boiler needs different handling. If the water is hot, smells unusual, or comes with a burning smell, treat it as more than a clogged drain.

Things you can safely check

  • Turn the system off at the thermostat and, if you can do it safely, at the equipment switch before touching anything wet. Keep water away from electrical parts.
  • Look at where the drain pipe ends outside. Is it blocked with leaves or dirt? Is water coming out?
  • Mop and protect the area. Wet insulation or carpet should be dried quickly to avoid mold.
  • Check the filter. A clogged filter can contribute to coil icing, which then melts and overflows.
  • Look for a capped T-shaped vent near the indoor unit. Some trade articles show homeowners removing the cap and using a wet/dry vacuum or flushing with a cup of vinegar (not bleach). Only do this with the power off and if you are comfortable. Otherwise, wait for a professional.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. Do not open the furnace or air handler to find the pan. A fix that breaks a pipe or leaks water into electronics costs far more than a service call.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out.
  • The water comes back after you clear the line, or the line will not clear.
  • You see ice on the coil or pipes. Switch the system off and call.
  • Water is near the furnace, electrical box, or gas equipment.
  • The float switch keeps tripping.
  • The pan, pipe, or drain looks cracked or damaged.
  • You smell gas or a CO alarm sounds: leave first, then call from outside.

Field card: Water Leaking from AC or Furnace

Condensation is normal. Standing water is not.

  • The AC makes water. The drain must carry it away.
  • Signs: puddle, full pan, musty smell, system shutting off.
  • Safe steps: power down, check the outside end, mop up, change the filter.
  • Use vinegar, not bleach, only if you are comfortable and the power is off.
  • Repeat leaks, ice, or water near electrical parts mean call a pro.
