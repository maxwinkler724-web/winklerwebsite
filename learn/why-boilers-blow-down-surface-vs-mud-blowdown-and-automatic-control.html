Why Boilers Blow Down: Surface vs Mud Blowdown and Automatic Control

Boil water and the water leaves as steam while the minerals stay behind. Blowdown is how a boiler gets rid of those leftovers on purpose, before they cause deposits, foaming or carryover.

Premium lesson. Planned for the premium tier; free to read while the site is in preview.

The basic problem

Steam leaves the boiler nearly pure, but the dissolved solids in the feedwater stay in the boiler water and become more concentrated over time. The U.S. Department of Energy's Steam Tip Sheet 9 explains that without blowdown, deposits can form that hurt heat transfer, and a high concentration of dissolved solids promotes foaming and carryover. See Wet Steam and Carryover Basics for what carryover does downstream, and Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics for the wider treatment picture.

Two kinds of blowdown

  • Surface (skimming) blowdown removes dissolved solids from near the water surface, where they are most concentrated. It can run continuously in small amounts.
  • Mud (bottom) blowdown removes suspended solids that have settled into sludge at the low point. It is done in short, intermittent bursts.

They solve different problems, so one does not replace the other.

How much water is blown down

DOE says typical blowdown runs about 4 to 8 percent of the boiler feedwater flow, and may reach 10 percent when the makeup water has a high solids content. Those are typical ranges from one sheet, not targets. Your plant's own limits come from its water-treatment specification, which depends on the boiler and its treatment program. Follow it, along with the manufacturer and your treatment supplier.

Why too much is a waste

Blowdown water leaves at about the same temperature as the steam, so it carries heat out of the plant, along with treated water and chemicals that must be replaced. That is why DOE encourages avoiding unneeded blowdown. Too little is the opposite problem, so the aim is the right amount, not the smallest amount.

DOE's worked example

This is DOE's illustration, so the numbers apply only under its assumptions. DOE considers a continuously operating, natural-gas-fired, 150 psig boiler making 100,000 lb of steam per hour, with makeup water at 60 F, 80 percent boiler efficiency, fuel at $8.00 per million Btu, and water, sewage and treatment at $0.004 per gallon. Cutting blowdown from 8 to 6 percent of feedwater saves about 2,313 lb per hour of makeup water, and DOE totals the yearly savings at about $72,631 (about $62,913 fuel plus about $9,718 water and chemicals). Your plant's savings would depend on your own numbers. The lesson to take away is that a small change in blowdown rate can matter, not that any plant will save that amount.

Automatic control

Manual blowdown depends on someone remembering and guessing. DOE describes two automatic approaches:

  • Conductivity-based control. A probe measures an indicator such as conductivity (a stand-in for dissolved solids), and a controller opens a modulating valve to hold the boiler water at its setpoint. Other measurements, such as silica or alkalinity, can also be used.
  • Proportional control. Blowdown is set in proportion to the makeup water flow.

Probes need keeping clean and checking against a lab or test-kit reading, and the setpoint comes from the plant's water-treatment specification, not from this page. The tests behind it are in Boiler Water Tests.

Hot blowdown is a hazard

Blowdown water is at boiler temperature and can flash to steam as pressure drops. Never open a blowdown valve unless you are trained and authorized, and follow the plant's written procedure. Keep people clear of the discharge, make sure the piping runs to a safe place or a blowdown receiver, and wear the protective equipment your site requires. Never defeat or modify a boiler safety, and cool any hot sample before handling it. A stuck or leaking blowdown valve is a repair and a safety report, not something to wrench on while hot and under pressure. Related: Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules.

Field card: Why Boilers Blow Down: Surface vs Mud Blowdown and Automatic Control

Blowdown removes solids on purpose; the amount comes from your plant's treatment spec.

  • Surface blowdown skims dissolved solids; mud blowdown removes settled sludge.
  • DOE: typical blowdown is about 4-8 percent of feedwater, up to 10 percent with high-solids makeup.
  • Too much wastes heat, water and chemicals; too little risks deposits, foaming and carryover.
  • Automatic control: conductivity probe with a modulating valve, or proportional to makeup flow.
  • Limits and setpoints come from the plant's water-treatment specification, not from a lesson.
