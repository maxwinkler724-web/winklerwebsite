Telecom Shelters and Remote Equipment Huts: Wall-Mount AC, Lead-Lag and Free Cooling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cell site, a rail signal hut or a pump control shelter has no one inside. The cooling has to run for months without a person looking at it, survive a failed unit, and tell someone when it cannot keep up. This lesson covers the usual answer: two wall-mount air conditioners, a lead-lag controller, an optional economizer, and alarm contacts that go out to remote monitoring.

What is different about an unmanned shelter

The load is almost all equipment. Radios, rectifiers, switches and battery chargers give off heat all day, every day, and there are no people to add or remove. The shelter is small and tight, so there is no big air volume to coast on if cooling stops. And the nearest tech may be an hour away. That is why the design leans on redundancy and alarms instead of a single bigger unit. For heat loads and equipment choices in an indoor network closet, see Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet. This lesson does not repeat that.

Equipment temperature limits come from standards such as Telcordia GR-63 and the ETSI environmental standards. We name them here but do not reproduce them; your equipment maker's data sheet and the site's design documents set the limit you work to. For a public yardstick on computing equipment, an NREL data center presentation lists a recommended inlet range of 65 to 80 F and an allowable range of 59 to 90 F for class A1 equipment, with 41 to 113 F for class A4. It also says the air entering the equipment is the number that matters, not the air leaving it. Telecom gear may be rated differently, so treat these as a comparison, not a rule for your shelter.

Two units and a lead-lag controller

A shelter usually gets two (sometimes three or four) wall-mount packaged units, each cut into the wall with its own supply and return openings. The controller is a separate box with one room sensor. It does the job a single thermostat cannot do: it decides which unit leads.

  • Rotation. One maker's controller literature says the lead role moves to the next unit at a set interval, spreading run hours evenly. The interval is selectable (that manual lists 0, 1, 3, 7, 14 or 28 days), and the controller remembers which unit was lead after a power loss. A newer controller manual from the same maker allows 0 to 30 days, default 7.
  • Staging. The first stage cooling call goes to the lead unit. The lag unit starts when the room keeps climbing: in the older controller, each later cooling stage starts 2 degrees warmer than the one before, with short built-in delays so units do not all start at once.
  • Failure. If a unit fails, that manual says to set the changeover to 0 days so the lead role does not rotate onto the dead unit, then push the button to make a working unit lead. A high room temperature is what brings the lag unit on in the first place.
  • Isolation. The controller is powered from the units but isolates each one, so losing power on one unit does not drop the controller.

In the newer controller manual the defaults are a 75 F cooling setpoint, a high-temperature alarm 1 at 85 F and alarm 2 at 90 F, and a low-temperature alarm at 50 F. Those are that maker's factory defaults, not a requirement. The same idea in a different setting, with boilers instead of air conditioners, is in Multiple Boiler Plants: Lead-Lag Rotation.

Free cooling with an economizer

When the outdoor air is cooler than the shelter, a damper can bring it in and the compressor can rest. The installation manual for one maker's telecom wall-mount unit gives enable choices of dry bulb only, temperature and humidity (its default), or enthalpy. With the default, the damper is allowed to open when outdoor air is below 70 F and outdoor relative humidity is below 60 percent, and it modulates to hold a 55 F mixed air temperature. The economizer and the compressor can run at the same time, and the damper has failure alarms (it did not open, it did not close). The older lead-lag controller manual notes that a 70 F dry bulb changeover can let outdoor air in between 60 and 70 F and recommends lowering it to 55 F. For setpoint basics, see Economizer Settings and Economizers Basics.

Two cautions for a hut in a dusty or salty place. First, filters. ENERGY STAR says economizer filters reduce the particles and contaminants the outdoor air carries in, and it cites a utility-funded study in which facilities with economizers had higher particle counts than those with minimal outside air, unless better filtration was used. A loaded filter in an unattended shelter cuts airflow quietly, so it should be on the service list and, if possible, on a pressure alarm. Second, humidity. The same page says tight humidity targets can erase savings, and it reports mixed findings on whether humid air raises equipment failures. Whether outdoor air is allowed at all is the equipment owner's call.

Cold weather and low ambient

An unmanned shelter in a cold place has the opposite problem too. Units with a low ambient control slow or stop the condenser fan to hold head pressure; the maker's manual describes a modulating fan control, and it says fan cycling at start is normal. Electric heat strips back up the lead-lag control: in that manual, heat stages come on in steps as the control value falls below the setpoint. A freezestat alarms when the coil temperature is below 30 F. The newer lead-lag controller also has a low-temperature alarm, adjustable from 28 to 65 F.

Battery strings and temperature

Many shelters hold a battery string for backup. A battery conference paper on high-temperature life gives the common rule of thumb for lead-acid: every 10 C (18 F) rise above the 25 C (77 F) reference doubles the reaction rate and halves expected life. Its table shows a 10-year design at 35 C (95 F) delivering about 5 years. That is why the cooling alarm point matters beyond the radios. For room ventilation and hydrogen, see Battery Room Ventilation.

Alarm points to the remote system

The controller reports by dry contacts. In the newer manual, the outputs are Form C (SPDT) dry contacts rated 1 A at 24 VAC, 120 VAC or 150 VDC. The list includes smoke or fire, lockout and refrigerant alarms per unit, power loss per unit, low temperature, high temperature 1 and 2, controller failure and second stage cooling. The power loss and controller alarm relays are reverse actuated, so a dead controller looks like an alarm instead of silence. Wire these to the site's remote monitoring inputs and test each one by forcing the condition. For how techs read alarms and trends in a building system, see BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them.

Worked example: our own shelter load

All inputs are our own assumptions, not measured data.

  1. Equipment: 6 kW of continuous equipment load (assumed). 6 kW x 3,412 = 20,472 Btu/h. We use the usual conversion of 3,412 Btu/h per kW.
  2. Envelope: 480 square feet of wall and roof area (assumed), an assumed U-value of 0.10 Btu/h per sq ft per F, and a 30 F difference (105 F outdoors, 75 F inside, both assumed). 480 x 0.10 x 30 = 1,440 Btu/h.
  3. Solar allowance: 3,000 Btu/h (an assumption for a sunny, uncovered wall and roof).
  4. Total: 20,472 + 1,440 + 3,000 = 24,912 Btu/h. At 12,000 Btu/h per ton, that is 24,912 / 12,000 = 2.08 tons.
  5. With one unit failed: Option A is two 24,000 Btu/h (2 ton) units. One unit alone gives 24,000 Btu/h, which is less than 24,912, so the room slowly warms and the lag unit's help is gone. Option B is two 36,000 Btu/h (3 ton) units. One unit alone gives 36,000 Btu/h, which covers the load with 11,088 Btu/h to spare.

So Option B covers the load with one unit failed, and Option A does not. We ignored lighting, battery charging heat, and any capacity loss at 105 F outdoors; real unit ratings fall in hot weather, so read the maker's capacity table at your design condition before you pick.

Field card: Telecom shelters and wall-mount units

Size so one unit carries the load.

  • Load is mostly equipment kW times 3,412 Btu/h.
  • Pair of units plus a lead-lag controller rotates the lead on a set interval.
  • If a unit fails, stop the rotation so the lead stays on a working unit.
  • Economizer: check damper, enable setpoints and filter, and the damper alarms.
  • Cold sites: low ambient control, heat stages, freezestat, low-temperature alarm.
  • Battery life falls fast above 77 F (halves per 18 F in the rule of thumb).
  • Alarm contacts: test every one by forcing the condition.
