Your First Week in a New House, HVAC Edition

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The closing is done and the boxes are everywhere. Before you unpack the kitchen, spend an hour learning where your heating and cooling equipment lives, how to turn it off, and what it needs from you. This lesson is a first-week walk-through for the day after closing, not a pre-purchase inspection.

Why the first week matters

Most people learn where the furnace shutoff is during an emergency, when the basement is dark and the water is rising or the house is cold. A short walk-through while nothing is wrong costs nothing. It also gives you a baseline: what is installed, how old it is, and what the seller left behind. If you are still deciding whether to buy, read Checking Heating and Cooling When Buying a House instead. This page picks up after that one ends.

Bring a flashlight, a phone for photos, a pen, and a roll of masking tape or labels. Do not open panels or remove covers on equipment. Everything below can be done by looking, reading labels and flipping switches that are meant for homeowners.

Step 1: Find and label the equipment

Walk the whole house once and list what you find: a furnace or boiler (often in a basement, closet, garage or attic), an air handler, an outdoor air conditioner or heat pump, a water heater, a thermostat on each zone, and any space heaters or fireplaces that burn fuel. Photograph each unit and its label. Write the room name on tape near each item so a repair visitor, a house sitter or you at midnight can find things fast.

Step 2: Shutoffs and switches

Gas shutoff. Find the shutoff valve near each gas appliance and the main shutoff near the meter. Valves are usually quarter-turn handles. Photograph them and tape a label nearby. If you are unsure which valve belongs to which appliance, write down your question for the gas utility rather than guessing. Ask the gas utility who owns what at your address. We do not cover leak response here; follow your gas utility's published instructions and keep its emergency number written down.

Furnace or boiler service switch. Many furnaces and boilers have a wall switch that looks like a light switch, mounted on or near the unit. It cuts power to the equipment for servicing. Label it. Do not assume it is a light switch and do not tape it on.

Outdoor disconnect. An outdoor air conditioner or heat pump usually has a small box on the wall beside it. Note what is in it and label it, but leave the cover closed. For how these boxes are sized and why they matter, see AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers.

Breaker panel. Find the breaker for each piece of equipment and label it in pencil. If the existing labels look wrong, note which ones to check later with a qualified person.

Water heater. Note its fuel (gas, electric or other), the shutoff for cold water going in, and its tank temperature setting. A Department of Energy guide recommends setting a water heater to 120 degrees F, which it describes as safe for most of the population. It also says most storage water heaters last 10 to 15 years.

Step 3: The filter slot and size

Look for a slot or door at the furnace or air handler, or a return grille with a hinged face. Pull the filter and read the printed size on the frame, for example 16x25x1. That nominal size is what you buy. Note the airflow arrow and slide the new one in with the arrow pointing toward the blower. Our lesson Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit explains nominal versus actual size.

The Department of Energy's consumer guide says to clean or replace furnace filters once a month or as recommended, and its air conditioner page says to check every month or two during the cooling season, more often with constant use, dust or shedding pets. If a previous owner left the filter in place with no date, replace it now and write today's date on the new frame in marker.

Step 4: Carbon monoxide and smoke alarms

Alarms are the one item here that is not HVAC equipment but is directly tied to it. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says to put battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, to prefer interconnected alarms, and not to install them in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances. It says the test button checks circuitry, not sensor accuracy, and to follow the maker's instructions on testing and on the replacement age printed in the product literature.

For smoke alarms, CPSC staff publish guidance to install an alarm on every level, outside sleeping areas and inside bedrooms, to test them once a month, and to replace batteries at least annually unless the alarm has a sealed 10-year battery. The same guidance says to replace smoke alarms that are more than 10 years old. In your first week: press the test button on every alarm, look for a date on the back, and replace any with no date or an old one. More on what to do when one sounds is in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Step 5: Read the equipment ages

Each major unit has a nameplate with model and serial numbers. Photograph it in good light. Many makers hide the build date inside the serial number, and the code differs by maker, so the pattern is not universal. Our lesson How Old Is My Furnace or AC shows how to read it and when to ask the maker. The Department of Energy guide suggests considering replacement when a furnace or boiler is more than 15 years old, which is a planning prompt, not a failure date.

Step 6: Condensate drains and pans

Cooling coils and high-efficiency furnaces make water. Look for a small plastic drain line (often PVC or clear tubing) running from the indoor coil or furnace to a floor drain, sink, outside or a small pump. Look for a pan under the coil and notice whether it holds water or stains. The Department of Energy notes that clogged drain channels keep a unit from reducing humidity and can cause moisture damage to walls or carpet. Note where the water goes and write it down. For trap and drainage details see AC Coil Condensate Traps.

Step 7: Thermostat type and batteries

Identify each thermostat: manual, programmable or smart. The Department of Energy describes a programmable thermostat as one that turns heating or cooling on at times you schedule, and a smart thermostat as a Wi-Fi device you can control remotely. Open the cover and see whether it uses batteries. If it does, write the battery type and the install date. If a previous owner left an app account on a smart thermostat, ask the seller for a transfer or reset it using the maker's instructions. If you have a heat pump, the same guide warns not to use a deep setback without a thermostat designed for heat pumps.

Step 8: Start a home records sheet

A one-page sheet is plenty. Record each item once, then add a service line whenever something happens. Keep a photo folder of nameplates. This also helps with warranties, since our lesson on equipment warranties and records explains why dates and receipts matter.

Step 9: When to book the first tune-up

CPSC advises having the heating system professionally inspected and serviced every year, including a check of chimneys and flues for blockages, corrosion and loose connections. Because you do not know the service history, our own suggestion is to book the first visit within the first month or two, before the season you will need the equipment most. Bring your sheet and ask the technician to confirm the ages and filter size. See What an HVAC Tune-Up Should Include.

Assumptions (all ours): the serial codes were read with the maker's guide; the Department of Energy 15-year planning prompt is applied to the furnace; and the household changes the 1-inch filter every 60 days.

Age check. Furnace: 2026 minus 2014 equals 12 years. It reaches 15 in 2014 plus 15, which is 2029, so about 3 years of planning time. Water heater: 2026 minus 2017 equals 9 years, still short of the 10 to 15 year range the Department of Energy gives for storage tanks, so nothing to do yet.

Filter count per year. One filter slot, replaced every 60 days: 365 divided by 60 is about 6.1, so plan on 6 filters a year. Add one spare on the shelf to cover a missed date, for 7 purchased. Checking monthly, as the Department of Energy suggests, means 12 quick looks, so some will end in "still clean, leave it."

Field card: Your First Week in a New House, HVAC Edition

Find it, label it, photograph it, log it.

  • Walk the house: furnace or boiler, air handler, outdoor unit, water heater, thermostats.
  • Label the gas shutoff, furnace service switch, outdoor disconnect and breakers.
  • Read the filter size off the frame and date the new one.
  • Test every CO and smoke alarm; check dates and replace old ones.
  • Photograph nameplates and work out equipment ages.
  • Find condensate drains and pans; note where the water goes.
  • Record thermostat type and battery date.
  • Book a first tune-up within a month or two.
