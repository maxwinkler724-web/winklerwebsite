Airflow and Static Pressure Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A system can have a perfect refrigerant charge and still perform badly if the air is wrong. Airflow is half of heat transfer, and static pressure is how we judge whether the duct and equipment are letting the blower do its job.

Why airflow matters

Heating and cooling coils only move heat if enough air crosses them. Too little air over a cooling coil lets it get too cold, which can lead to icing and poor comfort. Too little over a heat exchanger lets it overheat. The blower is the engine of the air side, and its work depends on how hard the ducts make it push.

What static pressure is

Pressure in a flowing fluid has pieces. Static pressure is the pressure on the walls of the duct, the push that would be there even if you imagine the air standing still. Velocity pressure comes from the air moving. Add them and you get total pressure. In practice, technicians mostly talk about static pressure because it shows how much resistance the system puts in front of the blower.

How it is measured

Static pressure is measured with a tube whose opening is perpendicular to the flow, so moving air does not push into it. Duct pressures are small, so they are read on a manometer in a unit called inches of water column. A common check is to measure the pressure on both sides of the equipment and compare each to the surrounding room, to see how much pressure the equipment itself uses, and how much the ductwork uses. Pick the test points and method from the manufacturer's instructions, since they differ by product.

Think of it like a straw

A blower pushing through ducts is like you sipping through a straw. A narrow straw, or one with a pinch in it, takes more effort for less drink. High static pressure means the blower is working against a lot of resistance, so it delivers less air than it was meant to. Each part adds resistance: the filter, the coil, the grilles, tight bends, and long, undersized ducts.

Common causes of low airflow

  • A dirty or too-restrictive filter.
  • A dirty evaporator coil or blower wheel.
  • Closed or blocked supply registers and return grilles.
  • Undersized or crushed ducts, and flex duct with kinks.
  • Leaks in ducts that waste air before it reaches the rooms.
  • Wrong blower speed or tap setting.
  • A failing blower motor or a slipping belt on older belt-drive units.

Reading the clues

High static pressure with low airflow suggests a restriction. Low static pressure with low airflow can suggest a leak, a blower problem or a loose connection. Temperature split across a coil is a quick check that goes along with pressure readings, because airflow changes the split. Compare what you measure to the equipment's published rating for that model.

Modern blowers

Many newer blowers hold airflow steady as resistance changes, which can hide a restriction until the motor strains. See ECM Blower Motors. A system that still delivers air may be spending extra energy and wearing out sooner.
