HVAC Career Tracks Compared: Residential, Light Commercial and Facilities

Last updated: 2026-11-04

"HVAC technician" is one job title covering three quite different working lives. A tech who spends the day in customers' homes, one who services rooftop units behind restaurants and shops, and one who walks the boiler and chiller rooms of a large building all use the same core skills. They use them in very different ways. This lesson compares the three tracks so you can picture the day, the gear and the path between them.

What the federal data says about the work as a whole

The federal labor statistics occupational page for heating, air conditioning and refrigeration mechanics and installers describes duties that cut across all three tracks: installing, inspecting, testing and repairing systems, and keeping records. It says most of these workers are full time, with schedules that may include evenings or weekends, and that they may be on call for emergencies and work overtime during peak heating and cooling seasons. It also says the occupation has one of the highest rates of injuries and illnesses of all occupations, which is why protective gear comes up in every track below. The same page says technicians may start at one site or several and travel between jobsites, and that most of them work for contractors. A separate federal page covers stationary engineers and boiler operators, the occupation closest to the facilities track.

Track 1: Residential service and replacement

A typical day. You start at the shop or at home, load the day's calls onto your phone and drive to the first house. Each stop is a conversation first and a repair second: what did the customer notice, when did it start, what has already been tried. Then you diagnose, explain the options, and either fix the problem or write up a larger job. Many calls are short, and a day can hold several stops.

Equipment. Furnaces, air handlers, split air conditioners and heat pumps, small boilers, thermostats and ductwork. These are small enough that one person can often handle a repair alone, though a full replacement usually takes a crew. See How to Become an HVAC Technician for how people start here.

Schedule and on-call. Demand follows the weather. Expect busy stretches in the first hot spell and the first cold snap, and an occasional after-hours emergency rotation. The federal page above supports the general pattern of evenings, weekends and overtime in peak seasons.

Skills. Talking with customers, clear explanations, tidy work in finished spaces, basic electrical and control troubleshooting, and combustion and refrigerant basics. Time management matters because you are the schedule.

Working alone or in a crew. Mostly alone on service, in pairs or small crews on replacements and duct work.

Track 2: Light commercial and rooftop work

A typical day. Stops are fewer but longer, and many are on roofs or in mechanical closets. You might do planned maintenance on a row of packaged rooftop units for a shopping center in the morning, then answer a no-cooling call at a restaurant at lunch rush. Restaurants and similar businesses add refrigeration, walk-in coolers and kitchen exhaust to the mix. Customers are often a manager or facility contact who wants to know when the space will be usable again. For the equipment itself, see Rooftop Unit Basics.

Equipment. Packaged rooftop units, split systems with larger air handlers, commercial refrigeration, and small to mid-sized heating plants. Parts and units are heavier, so lifting plans, ladders and roof access become part of every job. The ladder and roof lesson linked below covers setup.

Schedule and on-call. A weekday core with a rotating after-hours call, because a failed cooler or a dark dining room cannot wait. Again, the federal occupation page notes emergency on-call work as a general feature.

Skills. Three-phase power, control sequences and economizers, refrigeration circuits, reading wiring diagrams, and dealing with business customers and building staff.

Working alone or in a crew. Often alone on service, with a second person for roof lifts, brazing or a crane day.

Track 3: Industrial and facilities

A typical day. Here the equipment stays put and you come to it on a route. A shift starts with a handoff from the previous shift, then rounds: read gauges and meters, check water and fuel levels, test safety devices, log the readings, and respond to alarms. Repair and maintenance tasks fill the gaps. Our chiller plant rounds walkthrough shows what a round looks like on one kind of plant.

Equipment. Large chillers, boilers, steam systems, pumps, air handlers, cooling towers and building controls. Systems are big, run for years, and are shared by many occupants. The federal stationary engineer page says these workers typically work in boiler rooms and mechanical rooms, and that the largest employers include hospitals, educational institutions, manufacturing and government.

Schedule and on-call. The same page says facilities that run around the clock use either three 8-hour shifts or two 12-hour shifts on a rotating basis, and that places open every day of the year often need weekend and holiday work. On a day shift in a smaller building, the hours can look regular.

Skills. Controls and building automation, large chiller and boiler operation, steam and condensate systems, water treatment, record keeping, and steady attention to alarms. The federal page also says some jurisdictions license operators by equipment class and size; check the issuing agency where you work.

Working alone or in a crew. In a large plant a senior engineer may oversee a team, and in a small building one person may run everything, according to the federal page.

Tools and protective gear compared

  • Residential: a hand-tool set, meters, gauges and a refrigerant recovery setup in the van, plus shoe covers and a drop cloth for customers' floors. A tool list is in the apprentice tool list lesson.
  • Light commercial: the same, plus heavier ladders, harnesses and roof-edge protection. The federal workplace safety page on fall protection says that fall protection is required at six feet in the construction industry and four feet in general industry. See ladder and roof fall safety and heat stress on roofs.
  • Facilities: hearing protection and eye protection for noisy mechanical rooms, gloves and sleeves near hot piping, and the plant's own lockout and permit rules. The federal workplace safety heat page lists boiler rooms among indoor settings where heat illness is a concern, and recommends water, rest and shade, with extra care for new workers who have not yet built tolerance.

How people move between tracks

The federal data says openings come mostly from workers who transfer to other occupations or leave the workforce, so movement is normal. Common patterns are in the diagram: a residential tech who likes bigger equipment moves to light commercial; a commercial tech who prefers a steady site and controls work moves to a facility; a facility mechanic returns to contractor work for variety or to run a crew. Some start in a facility and never leave. The federal occupation pages also list neighboring trades (electricians, pipefitters and steamfitters, sheet metal workers, boilermakers) that people cross into. Each move usually needs new learning: customer talk and sales in one direction, controls, steam and large-plant operation in another. Moves are not one-way. If you are early in your path, use the interview and resume lessons linked below rather than treating the first job as permanent.

Worked example: one sample week in each track (our own, illustrative)

These weeks are our own invented examples, not survey data. Assumptions: each tech has a 40-hour scheduled week, one extra after-hours or overtime event, and the job counts below. Real weeks vary a lot with weather and employer.

  • Residential tech. Scheduled service calls: Monday 5, Tuesday 5, Wednesday 4, Thursday 5, Friday 4. Scheduled calls: 5 + 5 + 4 + 5 + 4 = 23, in 40 hours. One weekend emergency visit of 2 hours adds 1 job. Totals: 23 + 1 = 24 jobs and 40 + 2 = 42 hours.
  • Light commercial tech. Four 10-hour days. Stops: Monday 4 (planned rooftop maintenance), Tuesday 3, Wednesday 3, Thursday 4. Scheduled: 4 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 14 jobs in 4 x 10 = 40 hours. One after-hours restaurant callout of 3 hours adds 1 job. Totals: 14 + 1 = 15 jobs and 40 + 3 = 43 hours.
  • Facilities tech. Five 8-hour shifts, each with 2 rounds, so 5 x 2 = 10 rounds, plus 3 repair or maintenance work orders. Scheduled: 10 + 3 = 13 tasks in 5 x 8 = 40 hours. One 4-hour overtime block for a planned shutdown adds 4 hours and no new task count. Totals: 13 tasks and 40 + 4 = 44 hours.

Grand total across the three sample weeks: 24 + 15 + 13 = 52 jobs or tasks and 42 + 43 + 44 = 129 hours. Dividing hours by jobs gives 42 / 24 = 1.75 hours per job for residential, 43 / 15 = 2.87 for light commercial and 44 / 13 = 3.38 for facilities. The pattern, not the exact numbers, is the point: more stops and shorter ones at home, fewer and longer ones on commercial work, and a facility week built around routes, not calls.

Field card: HVAC Career Tracks Compared

Same trade, three rhythms: calls, stops, rounds.

  • Residential: many short calls, customer talk, small equipment, mostly solo.
  • Light commercial: fewer longer stops, rooftops and refrigeration, ladders and lifts.
  • Facilities: shifts and rounds, large chillers, boilers and steam, controls, logs.
  • On-call and overtime show up in every track; the federal data lists emergencies and peak seasons.
  • Heat, falls and lifting are the common hazards; plan water, rest and fall protection.
  • People move both ways between tracks; each move means new skills.
