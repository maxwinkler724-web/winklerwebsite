Hand-Off-Auto Switches and BAS Overrides: Finding Equipment Left in Hand

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fan or pump that was put in Hand to troubleshoot, and never put back, is one of the quietest wastes in a commercial building. It also may be running with some of its safeties out of the circuit. This lesson covers what the switch and the software override actually do, how to find the ones left behind, and what the habit should be.

What an HOA switch does

Many motor starters and some drives have a three-position selector: Hand, Off, Auto. In Hand, the starter is told to run locally, no matter what the building automation system (BAS) wants. In Off, it will not run. In Auto, it waits for a start command from the BAS or another controller. Our own plain summary: Hand makes the operator the boss, and Auto makes the controls the boss.

What Hand skips depends entirely on how the control circuit was wired. Usually it skips the BAS schedule and the BAS start contact. Sometimes the Hand path also skips safeties that were wired only in the Auto path, such as a freezestat, a smoke detector relay or a high static pressure switch. Sometimes the safeties are wired in a spot that covers both. You cannot tell from the switch label, so read the drawing and test it. See freezestats on air handlers for why that one device matters so much.

BAS software overrides and the priority array

The same idea exists in software. A BAS operator can command a fan point on, off or to a fixed speed from the screen. In BACnet, an output point holds a priority array of 16 command levels, and the active command is the non-empty entry with the lowest index number, which is the highest priority. Level 1 is Manual Life Safety, 2 is Automatic Life Safety, 5 is Critical Equipment Control, 6 is Minimum On/Off and 8 is Manual Operator, according to a protocol vendor's explainer (cited as plain text below). A schedule often writes at one of the lower-priority levels, so a Manual Operator command at level 8 beats it until someone releases that command.

Two practical points. First, the override sits there until it is released (written back to empty). Typing the schedule value again is not the same as releasing. Second, a life safety command at level 1 or 2 outranks an operator at level 8, which is why a properly built smoke or freeze response can still win over a screen override. Do not count on that unless the programming has been tested. See building automation basics.

How overrides get forgotten

The story is almost always the same. A fan will not start, so the tech flips it to Hand to see if the motor and drive work. It runs. The tech gets pulled to the next call, or the shift ends, and the switch stays where it is. A software version: someone forces a damper or valve open to chase a comfort complaint, fixes the real cause, and never releases the point. Months later, nobody remembers.

How retro-commissioning finds them

Retro-commissioning and building re-tuning are structured reviews of how an existing building really runs. The national lab re-tuning meta-analysis lists a measure called Remove Manual Overrides and Enable Automatic Control (measure A30) among its standard measures, alongside tightening schedules for air handlers and rooftop units. It reports that almost all of the buildings studied had significant potential to save energy, 5% to 30%, and that re-tuning typically yields 5% to 20%. Its measure guide tells reviewers to use trend data to confirm equipment actually shuts down overnight.

In practice, look for:

  • Override reports. Most BAS front ends can list every point currently commanded by an operator. Run it first. Anything at Manual Operator that is weeks old is a suspect.
  • Trends showing 24/7 run. Pull fan or pump status for two weeks. A flat line that ignores the schedule means an override, a broken schedule or a Hand switch. See BAS alarms and trend logs.
  • Command and feedback that disagree. A BAS shows Off but the status shows running. That is a classic sign of a switch in Hand, because the BAS output is no longer in control.
  • A walk. Look at the selector switches and the drive keypads. A software report cannot see a switch. An Oregon state retro-commissioning handbook example lists a plant with manual startup and shutdown and no automatic scheduling, so manual operation is a real finding, not a rarity.

Worked example: a 15 hp fan left in Hand

Every input here is an assumption we chose for illustration. Change them to match your building.

  • Motor: 15 hp, assumed 91% efficient, assumed running at 80% of rated load (Our own assumptions).
  • Hours: the schedule would run the fan 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday to Friday, which is 12 h x 5 = 60 h per week. There are 168 hours in a week, so Hand adds 168 - 60 = 108 unwanted hours per week, x 52 weeks = 5,616 hours per year (Our own assumption on schedule).
  • Rate: $0.12 per kWh, an assumed blended rate (Our own assumption).

Input power: kW = hp x 0.746 x load / efficiency. The 0.746 kW per hp factor and the form of the equation come from a federal motor tip sheet that uses the same relationship. So kW = 15 x 0.746 x 0.80 / 0.91 = 8.952 / 0.91 = 9.84 kW.

Energy: 9.84 kW x 5,616 h = 55,250 kWh per year, rounded.

Cost: 55,250 kWh x $0.12 = about $6,630 per year, for one fan that was supposed to be off.

Sanity check against the federal sheet, which says a continuously running 20 hp motor at $0.04 per kWh uses almost $6,000 of electricity a year. Our fan runs fewer hours at a higher rate, so the same order of size is reasonable. This leaves out the cost of conditioning the air the fan moves at night, so the real cost is higher.

Safety: never use Hand to defeat a safety

  • Hand is for testing and for emergencies approved by the site. It is not a way around a tripped freezestat, a smoke relay, a high static switch or an overload. If a safety keeps tripping, find out why.
  • An HOA selector is not a lockout device. The federal lockout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, states that push buttons, selector switches and other control circuit type devices are not energy isolating devices. Before opening a motor, drive or fan guard, lock out the real disconnect and verify; see lockout, tagout and verify dead.
  • If a drive is in Hand, remember it may start the moment the fault clears. See variable frequency drives on pumps and fans.

Good habits

  • Tag it. Put a tag on the switch or point with your name, date and reason.
  • Time-limit it. Use a timed software override if the system has one. Otherwise set a reminder for the same day.
  • Log it. Write it in the site log: what, why, when, who. A second person should be able to find it.
  • Return to Auto. Before you leave, set the switch to Auto, release the software command, and watch the equipment respond to the schedule or a command from the BAS.

Field card: Hand-Off-Auto and BAS overrides

If you put it in Hand, you own it until it is back in Auto.

  • Hand skips the BAS schedule; it may also skip safeties, depending on the wiring.
  • HOA selector switches are not lockout devices.
  • BACnet: 16 priority levels; Manual Operator is level 8; an override stays until released.
  • Find them: override report, 24/7 trends, command and status that disagree, and a walk of the switches.
  • Cost: kW = hp x 0.746 x load / efficiency; then kW x hours x rate.
  • Tag, time-limit, log, return to Auto.
