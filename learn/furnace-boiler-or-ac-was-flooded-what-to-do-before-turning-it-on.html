Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded: What to Do Before Turning It On

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your furnace got wet in a flood, the safe first move is to leave it off. Water and silt can ruin gas valves, controls and wiring in ways you cannot see, and equipment that seems to run can still fail dangerously. This lesson explains why, who to call, and how to judge whether the water reached your equipment.

The short answer

Do not turn on flooded gas or electrical equipment. That includes furnaces, boilers, water heaters, air handlers, outdoor AC units, the breaker panel and anything plugged in that sat in water. This page is general education, not a substitute for the manufacturer's instructions or for a licensed contractor and your gas utility.

  • Keep the power and fuel off. Do not touch wet electrical equipment or stand in water to reach it, as the federal ready.gov flood page advises.
  • Do not relight pilots or restore gas service yourself. Public Service Electric and Gas (PSE&G), a New Jersey gas utility, says not to restore gas service or relight appliances on your own, and offers inspections after floods.
  • Have a qualified service agency inspect everything the water touched before anything runs.

What the manufacturers say

Manufacturers put this in writing. A Trane high-efficiency gas furnace installation manual opens with a warning not to use the furnace if any part has been under water, calling a flood-damaged furnace extremely dangerous. It says a qualified service agency should inspect the furnace and replace all gas controls, control system parts and wet electrical parts, or the furnace itself if needed. Other makers print similar labels, so find the one in your own manual or on the unit. The instruction is the same: wet means inspect and replace, not dry and hope.

Why drying is not enough

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) warned flood victims in a 1993 news release that gas control valves that have been under water cannot be salvaged. Silt and corrosion inside the valve can stop it from working properly, and that can let gas leak, which risks fire or explosion. The same release says circuit breakers, GFCIs and fuses that were submerged should be thrown out and replaced, because they can look fine and still fail as safety devices. Motors, fans, wiring and venting that went under water should be checked by a qualified technician. A Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection fact sheet adapted from a U.S. Department of Energy publication agrees: flood water can corrode gas valves and controls in ways that are not visible, most experts recommend replacement over repair, and a salvaged unit would need new gas valves, controls, orifices and filter. For propane, it says regulators that floodwater reached should be replaced, since debris can plug the vent hole.

Check the water line against your equipment

The question you can answer yourself is simple: how high did the water get, and how high is the equipment? Anything the water reached is suspect. Keep in mind that the burner area, gas valve, control board and blower motor sit low in most furnaces.

FEMA publication P-348 explains how elevation is judged. The Base Flood Elevation (BFE) is the height of the one-percent-annual-chance flood. FEMA P-348 says the International Residential Code requires equipment such as furnaces and condensers to sit at or above the BFE plus 1 foot, or the community's design flood elevation if that is higher. Equipment may sit lower only if it is designed and installed to resist flood loads and keep water out. A FEMA advisory on the 2016 Iowa floods adds that Iowa's model ordinance used 1 foot of freeboard, and that a furnace should move to an upper floor or attic if possible. Your own local floodplain official sets the real number.

Worked example (made-up numbers). A basement slab is at elevation 3.5 ft. Survey marks show the flood reached 7.2 ft. A mapped BFE for the lot is 10.0 ft.

  1. Depth over the slab: 7.2 − 3.5 = 3.7 ft, which is 3.7 × 12 = 44.4 inches.
  2. An upflow furnace in this example is 34 inches tall, with the gas valve about 14 inches up and the control board about 22 inches up. The water was 44.4 inches deep, which is above all of them, so the gas valve, control board, blower and wiring were all submerged. Replace the controls, or the furnace, per the manufacturer.
  3. Required height under the code rule: BFE + 1 ft = 10.0 + 1 = 11.0 ft.
  4. Rise needed from the slab to reach that: 11.0 − 3.5 = 7.5 ft. A platform that tall is not practical in a basement, so the realistic fix is moving the furnace to a higher floor or attic.

Notice the two questions are different. Step 2 tells you what to replace now. Steps 3 and 4 tell you where to put the new equipment so this does not repeat.

Oil tanks and fuel lines

If you heat with oil, the tank and lines need attention too. North Dakota State University Extension advises that any flooded heating system be professionally inspected, cleaned and reconditioned before reuse, and that a qualified person check an oil tank for water and dirt that may have entered. It also says flooded fuel filters should be replaced and motors, fuel pumps and ignition parts be cleaned and dried by a professional. A tank that shifted or a smell of oil in the basement is a spill concern. New Jersey DEP lists signs of a leaking tank, such as oil sheen in a sump, staining and oil odors away from the burner, and says to call a DEP-certified environmental professional. A 2012 DEP advisory written after Hurricane Sandy lists the DEP hotline, 1-877-WARNDEP (1-877-927-6337), for after-hours reports. For tank basics in this state, see the home heating oil tanks lesson.

Ducts and air handlers

EPA's flood cleanup page says that if any part of the HVAC system was affected by floodwater, you should not use it to dry out the house, and that a professional should handle the cleaning and drying. The Pennsylvania fact sheet says duct insulation that touched floodwater should not be salvaged. Ducts that sat in water may need to be taken apart, cleaned, dried and disinfected, or replaced. Ordinary duct cleaning is a different question, covered in should you get your air ducts cleaned. Floodwater is a case where a professional's inspection is justified.

Carbon monoxide while you recover

After a flood the power is often out and people run gasoline-powered generators and other equipment to clear water. EPA says to run portable generators outside, at least 20 feet from buildings, and never inside a home, garage or shed, because the exhaust carries carbon monoxide that you cannot see or smell. CDC gives the same 20 feet from doors, windows and vents for generators. A cracked or flooded chimney can also vent CO into the home, which is why North Dakota State University Extension says to have flood-damaged chimneys inspected before lighting a furnace or fire. More detail is in carbon monoxide during winter outages.

For apprentices: what a tech checks

On a flood call, a tech follows the manufacturer. Shut off power and gas, note the water line, and identify what the manual says to replace: typically the gas valve, control board, ignition parts, pressure switches, wiring and any wet electrical part, or the whole furnace. The tech also has the vent checked and the breaker or disconnect replaced if submerged, as CPSC advises. Nobody restarts equipment just because it powers up.

Field card: Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded

Wet means off, inspect, replace. Never dry and run.

  • Do not turn on flooded gas or electrical equipment.
  • Do not relight pilots or restore gas yourself; call a licensed contractor or your gas utility.
  • Gas valves and controls that were under water are replaced, per CPSC and manufacturer manuals.
  • Submerged breakers, GFCIs and fuses are replaced.
  • Depth check: flood elevation minus slab elevation, compared with equipment height.
  • New equipment: at or above BFE + 1 ft under the IRC rule, or local rule if higher.
  • Oil: have the tank and fuel filters checked; report spills to NJ DEP.
  • Generators and pumps outside, 20 feet from buildings.
