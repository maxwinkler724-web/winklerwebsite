Building Pressure Control: Relief Dampers, Relief Fans and Return Fans

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every cfm of outdoor air a rooftop or air handling unit pulls in has to go somewhere. If it cannot leave through relief, it leaves through door gaps, shaft openings and walls, and the building tells you about it. This lesson covers the three relief tools, two ways to control them, and how to check the airflow arithmetic.

Why outdoor air needs a way out

When an economizer opens the outdoor air damper, more air enters the building. A national laboratory best-practice page on air-side economizers describes a relief or exhaust damper that works in parallel with the outdoor air damper, and says its job is to keep the building from becoming over-pressurized. It also says the return damper should open while the outdoor and relief dampers close, and the reverse. Setting the economizer itself is covered in Economizer Settings; here we follow the air after it enters.

A national laboratory conference paper on measured pressures in commercial buildings found that pressurization is driven mainly by a constant imbalance between outdoor air and exhaust or relief air. In other words, the pressure you see is the leftover of an airflow budget. A government building air quality guide says it plainly: if more air is supplied to a space than is exhausted, the excess leaks out; if less, air is pulled in.

The three relief tools

  • Barometric relief damper. A gravity or counterbalanced damper that opens when the building is pushed above outdoor pressure. No motor and no fan. It works when the return path is short and the pressure to push is small, and it is simple to maintain. It does nothing to pull air out, so it is limited on tall return runs or when the return duct has real resistance.
  • Powered relief (relief fan). A fan on the exhaust side of the mixing box that runs only when needed. A university facilities standard we read enables the relief fan when the supply fan runs and building pressure is above setpoint, then speeds it up or down to hold that setpoint.
  • Return fan. A fan in the return path that moves air back to the unit. The same standard has the return fan run whenever the supply fan runs. The extra fan lets the unit send more or less of that air to the relief damper without making the supply fan fight the return duct.

Our rule of thumb for the choice, labeled as our own: use barometric relief for small, low-resistance systems, a relief fan when outdoor air swings are large but return resistance is low, and a return fan when the return duct system is long or when a variable-volume supply fan changes airflow a lot. Always follow the design documents for the unit you are on.

Two ways to control it

Airflow tracking. The controller reads supply airflow and drives the return fan to supply airflow minus an offset. A university sequence of operations we read describes a return fan that follows a user-adjustable percentage offset from the supply fan or the building static pressure signal, whichever is greater. The offset represents the air that should leave by other paths: exhaust fans plus the amount you want to pressurize. Tracking is fast and does not need a pressure sensor, but it is only as good as the offset. If a toilet exhaust fan stops, or a door stays open, the offset is wrong and nobody notices.

Direct building static control. A sensor reads the pressure difference between inside and outside, and the relief damper, relief fan or return fan is modulated to hold a setpoint. A second university standard sets the relief damper and relief fan to hold a building static pressure of 0.05 in. w.c. (adjustable) in its sequences. Direct control corrects for exhaust, wind and leakage changes. The drawback is that a building static signal is tiny and noisy, so sensor placement matters (next section) and the loop should be tuned slowly.

Many sequences combine both: tracking gives a fast starting point and the pressure loop trims it.

What target should the building hold?

The national laboratory paper cites published guidance that recommends a slight positive pressure of about 1.25 to 20 Pa (our conversion: about 0.005 to 0.08 in. w.c.) to reduce infiltration. It also cites more specific guidance of about 12.5 Pa in summer (about 0.05 in. w.c., our conversion), up to 25 Pa in hot, humid climates, and 0 to negative 5 Pa in winter to limit exfiltration into the envelope. A federal workplace air quality guide recommends a slight positive pressure, described simply as air coming out when exterior doors are opened. Treat these as published ranges, not a number to set from memory. The design setpoint belongs to the project documents.

Where the pressure references go

Building static is a difference between two points, so both matter.

  • Indoor reference. In the national laboratory study, the indoor tube ended in a common area, which is a reasonable picture of a representative point. Our practical note: avoid supply diffusers, return grilles, stairwell doors and rooms with their own exhaust, where local air movement distorts the reading.
  • Outdoor reference. The same study mounted its outdoor sensor 2 ft above the roof surface. The government building air quality guide notes that wind can shift pressure relationships, especially in leaky buildings. Our practical note: follow the design for the port location and any wind shield or averaging fitting, because a steady outdoor reference keeps the signal usable. Do not leave a port near an exhaust or relief discharge.

The air quality guide also points out that even a building held positive has points, such as the outdoor air intake, that are negative to outdoors. Pressure is not one number across the building, which is why a single sensor reads one place only.

Symptoms of too much or too little

Too positive. From our own field description: exterior doors are pushed open or are hard to pull open, and air whistles through door gaps. Conditioned air is pushed into walls and ceilings, which can carry moisture into the envelope in cold weather, a concern the national laboratory paper raises by recommending a lower winter target. The same paper's modeling suggests that reducing relief airflow can improve pressurization, so relieving too much can leave a building short of positive.

Too negative. A federal workplace guide says a building under negative pressure can draw in outdoor pollutants such as particulates, vehicle exhaust, humid air and garage contaminants. Doors slam or are hard to push open, and odors appear in unexpected places. The government air quality guide adds that kitchen smells in a lobby can mean ventilation parts need adjustment. Negative pressure also pulls humid air through the envelope in summer.

Whole-building stack effect and neutral pressure plane problems are covered in Building Pressurization and Stack Effect in Tall Buildings, and how leaky a building is, which sets how much pressure a given imbalance makes, is covered in Commercial Building Air Leakage Testing.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. We assume a single air handler serving the whole building, 20,000 cfm of supply air, 30% outdoor air, 1,500 cfm of toilet and kitchen exhaust, and a goal of 1,000 cfm net positive, meaning that much air leaves through leaks in the envelope. We assume the dampers and fans are working as designed and the measured airflows are accurate.

  1. Outdoor air = 20,000 cfm x 0.30 = 6,000 cfm.
  2. Air in must equal air out: 6,000 = exhaust + relief + leakage out.
  3. Relief = 6,000 - 1,500 - 1,000 = 3,500 cfm.
  4. Check: 1,500 + 3,500 + 1,000 = 6,000 cfm out, which equals 6,000 cfm in.

With a return fan under airflow tracking, the return fan moves supply minus offset, where the offset is exhaust plus net positive: 1,500 + 1,000 = 2,500 cfm. So return = 20,000 - 2,500 = 17,500 cfm. Of that, 3,500 cfm is relieved and 17,500 - 3,500 = 14,000 cfm is recirculated. Mixed air = 14,000 + 6,000 = 20,000 cfm, which matches supply. The arithmetic closes.

Now suppose the economizer goes to 100% outdoor air. Outdoor air is 20,000 cfm, so relief must be 20,000 - 1,500 - 1,000 = 17,500 cfm, and the recirculated air is zero. That is five times the relief airflow of the 30% case, which is why a small barometric damper may be enough at minimum outdoor air but not at full economizer. The 1,000 cfm is 5% of supply. How much pressure that makes depends on how leaky the envelope is, and we do not state a pressure from it.

Checks before you change anything

  • Watch the relief damper travel while the economizer moves from minimum to full. It should open as the outdoor damper opens.
  • Confirm exhaust fans are really running before trusting a tracking offset.
  • Check the pressure sensor tubes for kinks, water and clogged ports before tuning a loop.
  • Write down the original setpoint and offset before you change them.

Field card: Building Pressure Control

Air in must equal air out.

  • Relief airflow = outdoor air - exhaust - planned leakage out.
  • Barometric relief: no fan. Relief fan: runs on demand. Return fan: moves return air to the unit.
  • Tracking: return = supply - offset. Direct control: hold a building static setpoint.
  • Published targets: slight positive, about 0.05 in. w.c. is common in summer guidance.
  • Check the indoor and outdoor pressure ports before tuning.
  • Our example: 6,000 - 1,500 - 1,000 = 3,500 cfm relief.
