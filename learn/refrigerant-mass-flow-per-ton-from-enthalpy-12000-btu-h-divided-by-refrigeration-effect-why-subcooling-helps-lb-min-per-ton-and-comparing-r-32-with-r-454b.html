Refrigerant Mass Flow per Ton from Enthalpy: Refrigeration Effect, Subcooling and lb/min per Ton

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every system has a refrigerant flow rate that matches its load, and you can estimate it with one division. This lesson shows how to turn two enthalpy readings into pounds per minute per ton, why extra subcooling lets each pound do more work, and how to compare two refrigerants fairly.

What a ton asks of the refrigerant

One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h. A university extension chapter on refrigeration explains where that figure comes from: the heat of fusion of ice is 144 Btu per pound, so melting a ton (2,000 lb) of ice per day takes 288,000 Btu per day, which is 12,000 Btu/h. The refrigerant carries that heat out of the space, and the only question is how many pounds of it must circulate each hour to do so.

The refrigeration effect

The refrigeration effect is the heat each pound of refrigerant absorbs in the evaporator. On a pressure-enthalpy chart it is the horizontal distance of the evaporator line, as the same extension chapter describes. In numbers, it is the enthalpy of the vapor leaving the evaporator minus the enthalpy of the liquid entering the metering device:

Effect (Btu/lb) = h(leaving evaporator) − h(liquid entering metering device)

Metering is a constant-enthalpy drop, so the liquid enthalpy at the inlet of the metering device is also the enthalpy of the mixture entering the evaporator. A university set of thermodynamics notes applies the first law to the evaporator and gets the same result: heat added equals mass flow times the enthalpy rise, so mass flow equals the cooling rate divided by that rise. See our pressure-enthalpy lesson for how to read the chart itself.

The formula for lb/min per ton

  1. Mass flow per ton in lb/h = 12,000 Btu/h ÷ effect (Btu/lb).
  2. Divide by 60 to get lb/min per ton.
  3. Multiply by the system tons to get total flow.

A bigger effect means fewer pounds are needed. A smaller effect means the compressor and lines must move more pounds for the same ton.

Why more subcooling raises the effect per pound

Subcooled liquid is liquid colder than its boiling temperature at that pressure, so it holds less heat. Look at the sketch: cooling the liquid moves point 3 to the left. The metering drop is straight down, so point 4 moves left with it, while the evaporator exit does not move. The line from point 4 to point 1 gets longer. Each pound of refrigerant therefore picks up more heat, and fewer pounds per minute are needed per ton. For how to measure subcooling on a job, see Superheat and Subcooling Basics. The reverse also matters: pressure drop and vertical lift in the liquid line can eat subcooling and cause flash gas, which is covered in the liquid line pressure drop lesson.

Our own worked example

This is our own example. Every enthalpy below is an assumed, round, illustrative number that we made up for teaching. They are not table values for any real operating point. For real work, look up saturation and superheat enthalpies for your refrigerant in a government or university property table; the federal standards laboratory publishes an online fluid-property tool that lists R-32 saturation data in Btu per pound, and each table states its own reference state. Because only differences matter, never mix numbers from tables with different reference states.

Assumptions (ours): an R-32 system, enthalpy of vapor leaving the evaporator 116.0 Btu/lb, enthalpy of liquid entering the metering device 52.0 Btu/lb at 10°F of subcooling. We also assume each extra 10°F of subcooling lowers liquid enthalpy by 4.5 Btu/lb.

  1. Effect at 10°F subcooling = 116.0 − 52.0 = 64.0 Btu/lb.
  2. Flow per ton = 12,000 ÷ 64.0 = 187.5 lb/h.
  3. 187.5 ÷ 60 = 3.125 lb/min per ton.
  4. Now 20°F of subcooling: liquid enthalpy = 52.0 − 4.5 = 47.5 Btu/lb. Effect = 116.0 − 47.5 = 68.5 Btu/lb.
  5. Flow per ton = 12,000 ÷ 68.5 = 175.2 lb/h, or 2.92 lb/min per ton.

Ten more degrees of subcooling raised the effect by 4.5 of 64.0, about 7 percent, and cut the needed flow by about 6.6 percent (3.125 to 2.92 lb/min per ton). For a 3-ton system that is 9.4 lb/min versus 8.8 lb/min.

Comparing two refrigerants

Now compare the R-32 case with an assumed R-454B case. Again these are our own round numbers, chosen only to show the method: leaving enthalpy 114.0 Btu/lb and liquid enthalpy 54.0 Btu/lb, so the effect is 60.0 Btu/lb.

  1. Flow per ton = 12,000 ÷ 60.0 = 200 lb/h.
  2. 200 ÷ 60 = 3.33 lb/min per ton.

In this made-up pair, the refrigerant with the smaller effect (R-454B here) needs more pounds per minute for the same ton. Do not read that as a fact about the real fluids. A fair comparison needs three things: both refrigerants at the same evaporating and condensing conditions, enthalpies from the same kind of table, and then a look at volume as well as mass, because vapor density sets how big the compressor and suction line must be. Pounds per ton is one piece of that picture, not a ranking. For the gauge side of these two fluids, see R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges.

Where this shows up in the field

  • Metering devices and line sizing are chosen for a design flow. See Metering Devices: TXV and Piston.
  • A heat exchanger that subcools the liquid raises the effect; see Liquid-to-Suction Heat Exchangers.
  • Always follow the equipment maker's charge and subcooling targets, not a calculation from this page.

Field card: Refrigerant Mass Flow per Ton

Pounds per ton = 12,000 divided by the effect.

  • Effect = h(evaporator leaving) minus h(liquid entering metering device), in Btu/lb.
  • lb/h per ton = 12,000 ÷ effect. Divide by 60 for lb/min.
  • More subcooling moves the liquid point left and raises the effect.
  • A larger effect means fewer lb/min per ton.
  • Compare refrigerants at the same conditions, from tables with one reference state.
