Low-Pressure vs High-Pressure Steam

It sounds backward, but steam at lower pressure moves through pipes faster. That surprising fact explains why home steam systems are designed to run gently, and why turning the pressure up usually makes the building heat worse.

Steam is a gas, and gases squeeze

Water turned to steam takes up a huge amount of space. At higher pressure the steam is squeezed so a pipe holds more of it in the same volume. At lower pressure it is spread thinner. Think of a crowd moving down a hallway: if everyone is packed shoulder to shoulder, they shuffle. If there is more space, they flow.

Why low pressure moves faster

The same amount of heat needs a certain amount of steam by weight. At low pressure that weight takes up more volume, so it has to move faster through the same pipe. A podcast summary of a conversation with steam author Dan Holohan states plainly that low-pressure steam moves through piping much more quickly than high-pressure steam. For a building that needs steam to reach the last radiator quickly, that is useful. It is also why designers keep the pressure low in heating systems built for it.

Why home systems run at low pressure

Residential and small-building steam heating is built to run at very low pressure, measured in ounces or a pound or so, not in the many pounds of pressure used in industrial plants. Radiators, vents and piping were sized for that. A heating-trade article on one-pipe systems gives an example: set the control that turns the burner on at about one-half pound of pressure, or about four ounces on the more sensitive type of control, so air vents do not get locked shut, and keep the shutoff point as low as possible because high-pressure steam moves more slowly. Those numbers are examples from one article, not settings for your boiler. The boiler's manual and the system designer set the real values. Low pressure also keeps stored energy low, which is a safety benefit.

What happens at higher pressure

  • Radiator air vents can close early and trap air, leaving radiators partly cold.
  • Steam travels more slowly through the mains, so far radiators wait longer.
  • The boiler works harder and uses more fuel for the same comfort.
  • Hotter steam and higher pressure raise the risk of burns, leaks and relief-valve discharge.

In plants, higher-pressure steam is used on purpose, because it carries more energy per pipe and can drive large equipment. Plants then reduce the pressure at points of use with a pressure reducing valve station.

Main venting: letting air out of the big pipes

Before steam can fill a pipe, the air in it must leave. In a building, the large supply pipes (the mains) hold much more air than any radiator. If that air leaves slowly, the pressure rises, the radiators near the boiler heat first and the thermostat may be satisfied before far radiators warm. Main venting means putting adequate vents near the ends of the mains so the air leaves quickly. The Holohan podcast summary describes this as lowering system pressure so steam moves quickly and efficiently. A trade article adds that main vents are usually fitted a little back from the end of the pipe and raised up on a short riser so water cannot reach them, and that an inline strainer helps keep dirt out. Details like those depend on the specific system, so use the manufacturer's drawings.

Who should adjust pressure controls

Pressure controls and safety valves are safety devices. Do not change pressure settings, bypass limit controls or cap a relief valve. If a steam system seems to need more pressure to heat, that points to a venting, piping or water problem. Call a qualified steam technician and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Field card: Low-Pressure vs High-Pressure Steam

Lower steam pressure means faster steam travel and gentler, more even heating.

  • At low pressure steam is less dense, so it moves faster for the same heat.
  • Home steam systems are designed for very low pressure.
  • Higher pressure can trap air in radiators and waste fuel.
  • Main vents let air leave the big pipes quickly.
  • Never change pressure controls or relief devices; follow the manufacturer.
