Inductive Reactance in HVAC Coils: Why Contactor and Relay Coils Draw Less Than Ohm's Law Predicts

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Put a meter on a 24 volt contactor coil with the power off and you might read 10 ohms. Divide 24 by 10 and Ohm's law says 2.4 amps. Wire that coil to a 24 volt AC transformer and it pulls well under half an amp. Nothing is wrong. An AC coil is an inductor, and an inductor opposes alternating current with more than just its wire resistance.

Why the DC ohms do not predict the AC amps

A coil is a long run of thin copper wound around an iron core. The copper has plain resistance, R, which is the number a meter shows. But when current changes direction 120 times a second (60 Hz), the changing magnetic field in the coil pushes back against the change. That push-back is called inductive reactance, XL, and it is also measured in ohms. University physics notes give it as XL = 2 × π × f × L, where f is the frequency in hertz and L is the inductance in henries.

Resistance and reactance do not simply add. They sit at a right angle to each other, so the total opposition, called impedance (Z), follows the Pythagorean rule: Z = √(R² + XL²). The AC version of Ohm's law then reads I = V / Z, using the RMS voltage. A Boston University physics page describes the resistor and inductor contributions as perpendicular vectors, and a university physics text from the University of Central Florida's open library gives the same Z and I = V / Z relations. Pull the numbers apart and you see the trick: when XL is much larger than R, Z is almost all reactance, and the 10 ohm wire resistance barely matters.

Worked example (our own, with assumed values)

This is our own example, not a published coil. The inputs below are assumptions chosen to be plausible for a small 24 volt AC control coil.

  • Supply: 24 V AC RMS, 60 Hz (assumed, and the coil is held at exactly 24 V).
  • DC resistance measured with power off: R = 10 Ω (assumed).
  • Sealed inductance (armature closed, gap shut): L = 0.15 H (assumed).
  • Open-gap inductance (armature out): L = 0.03 H, one fifth of sealed (assumed; real ratios vary by design).

Sealed. XL = 2 × π × 60 × 0.15 = 56.55 Ω. Z = √(10² + 56.55²) = √(100 + 3,198) = √3,298 = 57.43 Ω. I = 24 / 57.43 = 0.418 A. Apparent power = 24 × 0.418 = 10.0 VA. The heat in the wire is I²R = 0.418² × 10 = 1.75 W.

What DC ohms alone would say. 24 / 10 = 2.4 A, or 57.6 VA. That is 5.7 times the real sealed current (2.4 / 0.418). Using the DC figure would badly overstate the load on the transformer.

Open gap (inrush). XL = 2 × π × 60 × 0.03 = 11.31 Ω. Z = √(100 + 127.9) = 15.10 Ω. I = 24 / 15.10 = 1.59 A, or 38.2 VA. That is about 3.8 times the sealed 10.0 VA, and still well below the 2.4 A DC guess, because even an open coil has some reactance.

Why inrush is higher than sealed

The coil's inductance depends on how easily magnetic flux can travel through the iron path. A Rose-Hulman engineering lecture note on magnetic circuits says an air gap adds reluctance and lowers inductance (L = N² divided by the reluctance). With the armature out, the path has an air gap, so L is small, XL is small, and current is high. As the armature pulls in, the gap closes, L rises, XL climbs, and current falls to the sealed value. A solenoid-valve maker's web article describes the same sequence and says inrush for many mains-voltage AC coils is roughly two to six times the holding VA; use the figures on the actual part. Our example ratio of 3.8 falls inside that range. For how those two VA numbers feed transformer sizing, see Sizing a 24-Volt Control Transformer, which we do not repeat here.

Why a coil buzzes or burns out when it cannot close

If dirt, a bent armature, a mechanical bind or low voltage keeps the armature from seating, the gap never closes. Inductance stays low, so current stays near the inrush value instead of dropping. In our example that is 1.59 A through a 10 Ω winding, which is I²R = 25.3 W of heat in the wire against 1.75 W when sealed, about 14 times as much. The maker article above says a coil that never seats stays near its inrush current, runs hot and may burn out, often with an audible hum. The buzz itself is our plain explanation: the pull of an AC magnet rises and falls with the current, so a loose or dirty armature chatters against the core. Do not just swap the coil. Find why the armature is not closing, and check coil voltage under load, because a sagging transformer can leave a good coil half-closed.

50 Hz versus 60 Hz coils

Frequency sits in the XL formula. Run our same sealed coil at 50 Hz and XL = 2 × π × 50 × 0.15 = 47.12 Ω, Z = 48.17 Ω, I = 0.498 A, 12.0 VA. That is about 19 percent more current than at 60 Hz, and more heat. Coils are built and labeled for a frequency, so match the nameplate to the supply. Our rule of thumb: a coil labeled for a different frequency or voltage is not a safe substitute unless the part's own data says so.

What a meter can and cannot tell you

With power off and the coil isolated from other circuits, an ohm reading tells you only the winding's DC resistance. Infinity or "OL" means an open winding. A reading far lower than a known good coil of the same part suggests shorted turns. A reading near the expected value does not prove the coil is healthy: it says nothing about inductance, so it cannot show how much current the coil will draw on AC, and a few shorted turns may hide inside the tolerance. The 10 ohms in our example is the short leg of the triangle and tells you almost nothing about the 0.418 A. To judge a coil under power, qualified people compare the voltage across the coil while it is called and watch whether the armature closes. Our AC-versus-DC point is why Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter treats coil ohms as a screening check only. OSHA's hazardous energy page explains why equipment must be de-energized and locked out before servicing; prove it dead before any ohm test.

Note the difference from heating elements, where resistance is nearly all that matters; see Ohm's Law and Power for Resistive HVAC Heaters. Coil current is also a different number from contact ratings (see Reading Contact Ratings on HVAC Relays), and an isolation relay coil is one more VA load on the board (see Fan Center Relay Boards and Isolation Relays).

Field card: Inductive reactance in HVAC coils

DC ohms are the short leg; the AC amps come from impedance.

  • XL = 2πfL. Z = √(R² + XL²). I = V / Z.
  • Our example: 10 Ω DC, 0.15 H sealed, 24 V, 60 Hz gives 57.4 Ω, 0.418 A, 10.0 VA (not 2.4 A).
  • Open gap means low inductance, so inrush VA is several times sealed VA (3.8 times in our example).
  • An armature that cannot close keeps the coil near inrush current: hum, heat, burnout.
  • Match coil frequency and voltage to the supply.
  • Ohms (power off) finds open or badly shorted coils only. It cannot predict AC current.
