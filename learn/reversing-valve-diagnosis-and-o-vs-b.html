Reversing Valve Diagnosis and the O vs B Setting

Every heat pump has one part that decides whether the outdoor coil acts as a condenser or an evaporator. When that part, or the signal telling it what to do, goes wrong, the unit can heat when you ask for cooling or stay stuck in one mode. The fix starts with knowing which wire does what.

What the valve does

A heat pump moves heat in either direction by reversing the flow of refrigerant. The reversing valve makes that possible. A small solenoid on the valve uses the system's own operating pressure to shift an internal slide (often called a canoe), which sends refrigerant down one path or the other. The solenoid only steers; the pressure difference does the pushing. That is why a valve can have a perfectly good coil and still fail to shift if the compressor is not building a pressure difference.

Technician-level sources describe two broad failure families. Electrical problems involve the solenoid coil or the low-voltage control wiring. Mechanical problems are inside the valve: the slide may stick, or an internal seal may leak so refrigerant bleeds across even though the metal appears to have moved.

O or B: which wire energizes the valve

At the thermostat, the reversing valve wire lands on a terminal marked O or B. On most residential equipment the valve is energized in cooling, which is the O style. Some equipment energizes the valve in heating instead, which is the B style. Published guidance says the most common style is O, but that some manufacturers do the opposite, so the wiring diagram and the model documentation are the only reliable answer for a given unit.

Many modern thermostats let you pick O or B in a setup menu rather than rewiring. If the choice does not match the equipment, the symptoms are easy to recognize:

  • The system heats when cooling is requested, or cools when heating is requested.
  • It seems to run in only one mode.
  • Auxiliary heat runs far more than it should, because the heat pump is working against the call.
  • Outdoor coil ice or odd defrost behavior in cold weather.

A quick field clue: with cooling requested and the setpoint well below room temperature, a cold outdoor coil while the house warms up suggests the valve is energized the wrong way around for the setting. Change the setting only after reading the manufacturer documentation.

A sensible order of checks

The aim is to rule out the cheap and easy causes before condemning a valve that involves a lot of brazing in tight spaces.

  • Start with the basics. Filter, airflow, and the system temperature split. Poor airflow or charge problems can imitate valve trouble.
  • Confirm the thermostat setting and the call. Is the O/B setting right for this model, and is the valve getting the signal it should?
  • Check voltage at the valve coil. The command often passes through the outdoor control or defrost board, so measure at the coil itself.
  • Listen and watch while changing modes. Removing the low-voltage valve wire at the unit and putting it back should produce an audible shift and a change in pressures if the control side and solenoid are good.
  • Try the magnet test. Strong magnets on the outside of the valve can show whether the slide moves fully, partway, or not at all.
  • Look for signs of bypass. Low compressor amps compared with the nameplate, or head pressure problems in heat mode while cooling seems normal, can point to a leaking valve.
  • Compare line temperatures across the valve. One technician source says a healthy valve shows roughly 3 to 5 degrees F between lines that should match, and a larger difference with everything else clean points toward the valve. It also warns that a damaged compressor can heat the valve body and give false readings, so treat this as one clue, not proof.

Safety and good habits

Replacing a valve means recovering refrigerant and brazing close to wiring, coils and other parts. Follow the refrigerant handling rules where you work, protect nearby components from heat, and use the manufacturer's procedure. One source also cautions against pumping down microchannel coils, which are not designed for it. For A2L systems, the extra ignition-source precautions in the A2L lessons apply. Never bypass a high-pressure or other protective control to make the valve appear to work.

Field card: Reversing Valve Diagnosis and the O vs B Setting

Know which wire shifts the valve before you blame the valve.

  • O style energizes the valve in cooling; B style energizes it in heating. Check the model documents.
  • Wrong O/B setting looks like backwards operation, constant aux heat or odd defrost.
  • Check airflow, charge and the call before condemning the valve.
  • Measure voltage at the coil, then test the shift by changing modes and with magnets.
  • A 3 to 5 degree F difference across the valve is a published example, not a verdict.
