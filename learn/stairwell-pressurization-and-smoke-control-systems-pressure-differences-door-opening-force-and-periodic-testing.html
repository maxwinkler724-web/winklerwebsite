Stairwell Pressurization and Smoke Control: Pressure Differences, Door Force and Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a fire, the stair is where people leave and where firefighters climb. A pressurized stair uses a fan to push a little more air into the shaft than the floors have, so smoke stays out. This lesson explains the pressure targets, why door opening force is a hard limit, and what periodic testing looks like.

Who does what

This is engineered life safety work. Smoke control systems are designed by engineers, approved by the fire official, and tested against the approved design documents. A technician's job is to follow the approved test plan and the manufacturer's instructions, report what is found, and never "adjust to make it pass." If a reading is out of range, the fix goes back to the designer and the authority having jurisdiction (the local fire or building official). Write down what you saw and stop there. The design standard is NFPA 92, and the model building code that most states build on is the International Building Code. We name both here in plain text only. Your state and city may amend them, so the approved plans for your building are the rule that counts.

Why stairs are pressurized

Smoke moves through a building on the same forces that move air: fans, wind, and stack effect (see our stack effect lesson). A smoke-logged stair blocks escape and slows firefighters. A paper on smoke control capabilities hosted by the International Code Council says the aim is to keep smoke out of the stair and keep the escape route usable, and that a little smoke getting in may still leave the stair workable. The fan makes the stair the higher-pressure space, so air leaks out through door gaps rather than smoke leaking in.

The two pressure limits

Every pressurized stair has to stay inside a window: high enough to keep smoke out, low enough that people can still open the doors. A Consulting-Specifying Engineer article on smoke control design says the 2015 International Building Code sets a minimum of 0.10 inches of water column and a maximum of 0.35 inches, both with interior doors closed. Pressure is measured across the door, from stair to floor. Seattle Fire Department testing guidance lists 0.15 to 0.35 inches for a stair shaft in a mid-rise building and 0.10 to 0.35 for a high-rise. Those are one city's numbers and your jurisdiction may differ.

The same article warns that NFPA 92 also wants the minimum held with the designed number of doors open, including an exterior door that people would normally use. It says that one extra open door, especially an exterior one, can double or triple the fan size needed. How many open doors to design for is a decision between the designer and the fire official early in design, not a field decision.

Door opening force

The maximum pressure exists because pressure pushes on the door. The article gives the force equation used in the International Building Code:

F = Fdc + K W A ΔP / (2 (W − d))

  • F: total force to open the door, pounds
  • Fdc: force to overcome the door closer, pounds
  • K: 5.2 in these units
  • W: door width, feet
  • A: door area, square feet
  • ΔP: pressure difference across the door, inches of water column
  • d: distance from the handle to the latch edge of the door, feet

The ICC-hosted paper cites the Life Safety Code as saying the force to open any door in a means of egress shall not exceed 30 pounds, and says its maximum-pressure table was built on that 30 pound total. The Consulting-Specifying Engineer article says its own 3 by 7 foot door with a 10 pound closer reaches about 30 pounds at 0.35 inches, and that the value is a guide because closer force varies. A NIST paper by Klote also notes that accessibility rules in many places cap door force at 15 pounds-force, which is one more reason the designer, not the tech, picks the numbers.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own calculation, using the cited equation and a stated pressure. Assumptions: a 3 foot wide by 7 foot tall stair door; closer force 10 pounds (the value used in the cited article's example); handle 0.25 feet from the latch edge (our assumption, about 3 inches); and a measured stair-to-floor pressure of 0.30 inches. None of these are from a real building.

  1. Area: A = 3 × 7 = 21 square feet.
  2. Numerator: 5.2 × 3 × 21 × 0.30 = 98.28.
  3. Denominator: 2 × (3 − 0.25) = 5.5.
  4. Pressure part: 98.28 / 5.5 = 17.9 pounds.
  5. Total: F = 10 + 17.9 = 27.9 pounds.

That is under the 30 pound figure, with little margin. The same door at 0.35 inches comes to about 30.9 pounds, which shows how quickly it climbs, and at the 0.10 inch minimum it is about 16 pounds. Real doors vary, so this is a teaching calculation only. Measuring door force in the field is done as the approved test plan directs.

Single and multiple injection

Single injection puts the air in at one point, usually the top or bottom. Multiple injection feeds the shaft at several heights. The ICC-hosted paper says single injection can fail in tall stairs when a few doors near the supply point are open, with the greatest risk for bottom injection when a ground-floor exterior door is open. It says some designers limit top injection to eight to twelve stories and recommends multiple injection for taller stairs. Stack effect worsens this, because it pushes the pressure difference up at the bottom and down at the top. A Consulting-Specifying Engineer article on high-rise approaches says satisfactory pressurization may be impossible above about 420 feet with all doors closed, and that designers respond by splitting the stair into separately pressurized sections.

Open doors and relief

When someone opens a door, pressure falls; when it closes, pressure jumps and doors become hard to open. The ICC-hosted paper describes two answers: modulate the supply with bypass or relief dampers on pressure sensors, or relieve excess pressure outside through a barometric or motorized damper or an exhaust fan on a pressure sensor. A magazine article on integrating dampers and fans describes barometric relief and proportional control, in which dampers modulate to hold stair pressure, roughly one for every three to five floors. The same article says contractors often find fan and damper coordination problems, such as a sensor mounted downstream of a closed damper, which over-pressurizes ductwork.

Controls and the firefighter panel

A fire alarm signal normally starts the stair fan and positions the dampers automatically. The firefighters' smoke control panel is the manual side. The article above says fan status and on-off-auto override switches are required at that panel, and that it can command dampers directly. The panel is installed and labeled per the approved design. See duct smoke detectors and fire and smoke dampers for the pieces tied into it. Fan speed and static pressure are covered in our fan laws lesson; never change a smoke control fan's speed or settings without the engineer's approval, since the approved airflow was verified at commissioning.

Periodic testing

Intervals are set by the adopted fire code and the approved plan. The Seattle Fire Department testing guidance, as one public example, lists an annual confidence test and full damper testing every four years (six for hospitals), and tells testers to use the commissioning operation and maintenance test plan if it exists. It lists readings across the stair doors taken relative to the floor space at every fifth floor, checking that stair doors open and close properly with the fans running, door gaskets in good condition, signs and labels in place, and the fire alarm system placed in test mode first and returned to service after. A Consulting-Specifying Engineer article on testing says special inspectors test against the approved design documents, that it is not their job to redesign, and that airflow and pressure measurements are best done by agencies certified in test and balance. Pressure rooms use similar ideas; see pressure rooms in healthcare.

Field card: Stair Pressurization

Keep smoke out, keep doors openable, follow the approved plan.

  • Engineers design it; the fire official approves and witnesses testing.
  • Cited model code window: 0.10 to 0.35 in. water column, doors closed.
  • Door force: F = Fdc + 5.2 W A ΔP / (2 (W − d)); cited egress limit 30 lb.
  • Tall stairs: multiple injection or split sections; plan for open doors.
  • Firefighters' panel has fan on-off-auto overrides.
  • Do not change fan speed, dampers or sensor settings on your own.
