Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Boiler water is not just water. As steam leaves, the impurities stay behind and build up, so someone has to control them.

Why impurities build up

When water boils, nearly pure steam leaves and almost everything dissolved in the feedwater stays in the boiler. Over time the concentration climbs. Suspended solids settle as sludge that insulates heat-transfer surfaces. Dissolved solids can cause foaming and carryover, which sends boiler water into the steam lines. Scale and corrosion shorten boiler life. See Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

Water treatment

Most plants use a water treatment specialist and a program of chemicals and regular tests. The aim is to limit scale and corrosion and keep the water chemistry within the maker's range. Some systems add chemicals automatically in proportion to make-up water flow. Your testing routine and chemical limits come from the treatment provider and the boiler maker.

Blowdown, in two forms

  • Bottom (mud) blowdown. Removes settled solids. It is usually manual, a short burst opened at set intervals.
  • Surface (skimming) blowdown. Removes dissolved solids concentrated near the surface. It is often continuous, and may be automatic, based on conductivity.

Too little, too much

Too little blowdown lets solids pile up, leading to deposits or carryover. Too much wastes hot water, make-up water and treatment chemicals. One federal energy fact sheet puts typical blowdown at about 4 to 8 percent of feedwater flow, higher when the make-up water has a lot of solids. Its worked example shows that trimming blowdown by a couple of percentage points on a large boiler can save real money, which is why plants measure rather than guess.

Cycles of concentration

One way to describe it: compare an indicator such as silica or chloride in the boiler with the same indicator in the feedwater. If the boiler reads ten times the feedwater, the water is at ten cycles. Higher cycles mean less blowdown, up to the limit the boiler can tolerate.

Energy recovery

Blowdown water leaves hot. Plants may route it through a flash tank or a heat exchanger to recover some of that heat instead of mixing in cold water and sending it to drain.

Habits that help

  • Test and log water chemistry on schedule.
  • Record how often and how long blowdown runs, and watch make-up water meter readings for leaks.
  • Look for carryover signs, like surging water level and wet steam.
  • Open and close blowdown valves in the order and way the plant procedure says.

Safety

Blowdown discharges scalding water and steam, and water treatment chemicals can burn skin and eyes. Use the PPE and procedure your site requires, keep clear of the discharge, and lock out and tag out before opening a panel or touching a rotating part, and never bypass a safety device to keep something running. Do not defeat a low-water cutoff or any other boiler safety. See Boiler Controls.

Field card: Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics

Control the concentration, do not guess it.

  • Impurities stay behind when steam leaves, so they concentrate.
  • Bottom blowdown removes sludge; surface blowdown removes dissolved solids.
  • Too little means deposits or carryover; too much wastes heat, water and chemicals.
  • Test, log and follow the treatment provider's limits.
  • Blowdown is hot: PPE, clear discharge path, procedure.
