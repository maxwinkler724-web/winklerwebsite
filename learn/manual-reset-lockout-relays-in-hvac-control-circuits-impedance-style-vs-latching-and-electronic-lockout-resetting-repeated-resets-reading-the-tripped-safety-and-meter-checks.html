Manual-Reset Lockout Relays in HVAC Controls: How They Work, How to Reset, and Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pressure switch that resets itself is a problem when the fault is still there. The compressor starts, the switch opens, the switch recloses, and the cycle repeats. A lockout relay breaks that loop. It remembers that a safety opened and keeps the compressor or burner off until a person (or a deliberate power cycle) clears it.

What a lockout does

An ordinary safety is a switch in the 24 volt control circuit. When it opens, the contactor or gas valve drops out. When it closes again, the load comes back. That is fine for a fault that clears and stays cleared, but a high-pressure or low-pressure switch with automatic reset can open and close over and over, hammering the compressor with starts. For how those switches work and what trips them, see AC High and Low Pressure Switches.

A lockout adds memory. Once the safety has opened, the lockout holds the output off even after the safety recloses. The same idea shows up in a ladder diagram as a holding contact: an auxiliary contact on a relay, wired in parallel with the switch that started it, that keeps the coil energized after the switch is released. A university extension note on motor control describes holding contacts exactly this way for start buttons. A lockout is the same trick pointed at a fault instead of a start.

Do not confuse this with lockout and tagging for personal safety. That is a separate topic, covered in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Here "lockout" means the control circuit refusing to restart a load.

Three generations of lockout

  • Impedance-style lockout relay. An older arrangement. A relay with a fairly high-resistance coil is wired across the safety contacts, and that pair sits in series with the contactor coil. While the safety is closed it shorts the relay coil, so the contactor gets nearly the full supply. When the safety opens, current has to pass through the relay coil on its way to the contactor coil. The voltage divides, the contactor coil is starved and drops out, and the relay coil pulls in. The details differ between makers, so treat this as the general pattern and read the wiring diagram on the unit. The worked example below puts numbers on it.
  • Latching relay. A relay with a mechanical latch or a held-in coil. After it trips, a contact stays open until a reset button is pressed or control power is removed. Some oil-pressure safeties on larger refrigeration equipment work this way: a condensing-unit installation manual says the oil safety trips if net oil pressure stays below 9 psig for more than 120 seconds, is reset by hand, and the compressor cannot restart until it is.
  • Electronic lockout. On modern boards the logic is in firmware. A water heater installation manual we read for this lesson describes a 24 volt controller with a status light that flashes codes, "soft" lockouts for retry and flame-loss faults, and a roughly 2 minute soft lockout after a power outage. The board decides when to retry and when to stop. Some boards count trips within a single call for cooling and lock out after a set number, which the pressure switch lesson above covers.

How a lockout is reset

The method depends on the design. A mechanical latch has a button. Many electronic boards reset when the thermostat call is dropped and made again, or when 24 volt power is removed and restored for a moment; others need a service switch or test pins. Our rule is to read that unit's own sequence and never assume one design behaves like another. The water heater manual above, for example, describes no power-cycle reset at all and says to determine the cause of a lockout and call a qualified technician if the fault repeats.

Why repeated resets hurt compressors

A lockout is a message, not a fault. The safety opened because pressure, temperature, oil or voltage was outside what the machine can tolerate. If you reset it and walk away, you ask the compressor to run again into the same condition. Our own reasoning is that each restart repeats whatever stressed the machine in the first place, plus another start. The condensing-unit manual's troubleshooting guidance says to determine the type and cause of the shutdown and correct it before resetting a safety, and the water heater manual's troubleshooting table says to reset and determine the cause. One reset to see whether a trip repeats is reasonable. Several resets with no diagnosis is how a repairable problem becomes a dead compressor. For the timing side, see Anti-Short-Cycle Timers and Compressor Time Delays, which covers delays and does not replace a lockout.

Reading which safety tripped

  1. With the thermostat calling, look at the board status light or code chart before you touch anything. Electronic boards often name the fault.
  2. On a hard-wired circuit, use the wiring diagram to list every safety in the string. Then half-split the series string with a voltmeter: voltage across an open switch reads supply voltage, and across a closed one it reads near zero.
  3. Look at the latched device itself. A tripped latch or a lit indicator tells you which relay did the work.
  4. Then ask why the safety opened. Gauges, airflow, coil condition and electrical readings answer that, not the relay.

Meter checks on the lockout relay

These are our own working steps. Voltage checks on 24 volt controls are live work; resistance checks need the circuit off. The federal workplace safety agency's work-practice rule for electrical work says live parts must be de-energized before work on or near them, with limited exceptions, and that a qualified person must use test equipment to verify the circuit is dead. It also notes that parts under 50 volts to ground need not be de-energized where there is no added burn or arc risk. The control side is low voltage, but the transformer primary and the contactor line side are not. Our practice is to treat the whole unit as live until the disconnect is open and verified. Review verify dead first.

  • Coil resistance. Power off, one coil lead removed so nothing parallels the reading, meter on ohms. A good coil reads some steady resistance that is not zero and not open. An open coil reads infinite. A coil much lower than its sister part suggests shorted turns. Compare with the nameplate or the diagram. Real AC coils have inductance as well as resistance, which is why their running current is lower than the ohm reading predicts.
  • Contacts. Power off, leads isolated, check the contact that should be closed for continuity and the one that should be open for no continuity, in both the reset and tripped states. A closed contact should read close to zero ohms.
  • Coil voltage. Power on, meter across the coil terminals. A relay that should be latched shows voltage across its coil; one that should be idle shows almost none.
  • Across the safety. Full supply voltage across an open safety is normal. It is the clue that a lockout circuit is waiting on that switch.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions: this is our own simplified example, not any maker's circuit. Supply is 24 V. The lockout relay coil is 50 ohms and the contactor coil is 12 ohms, both treated as plain resistances (real AC coils differ, as the inductance lesson explains). The safety is wired across the relay coil, and that pair is in series with the contactor coil. The numbers were picked to make the arithmetic clean, not to match real parts.

Safety closed (relay coil bypassed). The closed switch shorts the 50 ohm coil, so only the contactor coil limits current.

  • Current: 24 V / 12 ohm = 2.0 A
  • Contactor coil voltage: 24 V, the full supply
  • Relay coil voltage: about 0 V

A 2 A, 48 VA coil is far more than real contactor coils draw. That oversize figure is an artifact of our round numbers, so the point to take away is the voltage split, not the amps.

Safety open (coils in series).

  • Total resistance: 50 + 12 = 62 ohm
  • Current: 24 V / 62 ohm = 0.387 A
  • Relay coil voltage: 0.387 A x 50 ohm = 19.4 V
  • Contactor coil voltage: 0.387 A x 12 ohm = 4.6 V
  • Check: 19.4 + 4.6 = 24.0 V, equal to the supply
  • Check by ratio: 50/62 = 0.806, and 0.806 x 24 V = 19.4 V
  • Power: relay coil 19.4 V x 0.387 A = 7.5 W, contactor coil 4.6 V x 0.387 A = 1.8 W, total 9.3 W, matching 24 V x 0.387 A = 9.3 W

The contactor coil now sees 4.6 V, about 19 percent of 24 V (4.6 / 24), far too little to hold it in. Nearly all the voltage lands on the lockout coil, which pulls in. In a real design that relay then latches, so the load stays off even if the safety recloses and shorts the coil again. This is also why a voltmeter across the relay coil reads high after a trip and near zero otherwise.

Field card: Manual-Reset Lockout Relays

A lockout is a message. Find the cause before you reset it.

  • A lockout holds the load off after a safety opens, even if the safety recloses.
  • Styles: impedance relay across the safety, latching relay, electronic board lockout.
  • Reset methods differ: button, thermostat cycle, power cycle, test pins. Read that unit's sequence.
  • Series coils split voltage in proportion to resistance (our example: 19.4 V and 4.6 V of 24 V).
  • Meter coils with power off and a lead lifted; check contacts closed and open in both states.
  • Verify the equipment dead and use test equipment before resistance checks.
  • Do not keep resetting. Fix the cause first.
