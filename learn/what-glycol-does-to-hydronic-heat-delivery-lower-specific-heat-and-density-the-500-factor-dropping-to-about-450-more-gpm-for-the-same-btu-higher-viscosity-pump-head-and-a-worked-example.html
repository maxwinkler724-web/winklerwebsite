Glycol and Hydronic Heat Delivery: Why the 500 Factor Drops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Water is the best common fluid for carrying heat in a pipe. Add glycol for freeze protection and the loop gets a little worse at that job: the fluid holds less heat per pound, it is heavier per gallon, and it is thicker. This lesson shows how to turn those property changes into a new gpm number and a pump head estimate, with arithmetic you can repeat.

Where the 500 comes from

The familiar hydronic shortcut is Btu per hour = 500 x gpm x temperature drop in degrees F. Our own unit arithmetic shows why. One gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds, a pump moves 60 minutes of flow per hour, and water takes about 1.00 Btu to warm one pound by one degree F. So 8.34 x 60 x 1.00 is about 500. Two of those three numbers belong to the fluid: its density and its specific heat. Change the fluid and the 500 changes with it. For the water side of the story, see where the 1.08 comes from and the 500 factor for water.

What glycol does to the properties

We looked for glycol-water property tables from government, university and national-lab pages. The best open data we found were for ethylene glycol: a federal measurement-laboratory data archive entry for a 2003 peer-reviewed measurement paper (cited below), which lists density of ethylene glycol and water mixtures, plus heat capacity and viscosity of pure ethylene glycol. A university water table supplies the water side. We did not find propylene glycol mixture tables in a source we could open, so the numbers below are for ethylene glycol and the direction of every trend is the same for propylene glycol. Use the maker's table for the fluid actually in the loop.

  • Density goes up. The archive lists 1037.2 kg/m3 for the mixture with 0.1105 mole fraction ethylene glycol at 20 C (293.15 K), and 1064.8 kg/m3 at 0.2248 mole fraction. Water at the same temperature is 998.2 kg/m3. Our own conversion from mole fraction to mass (using molar masses of 62.07 and 18.015 g/mol) puts those two mixtures at about 30 percent and 50 percent glycol by mass.
  • Specific heat goes down. The same archive lists pure ethylene glycol at 147.47 J/(K mol) at 20 C. Dividing by 62.07 g/mol gives about 2.376 kJ/(kg K), against 4.182 for water in the university table. Glycol carries only about 57 percent as much heat per pound as water.
  • Viscosity goes up a lot. Pure ethylene glycol measured 0.020833 Pa s at 20 C, about 21 times the 0.001003 Pa s of water. It thins to 0.006992 Pa s at 50 C, so a cold loop is the hardest case for the pump.

Computing the new factor

Our own estimate for a mixture's specific heat is the mass-weighted average of the two pure values. That ignores a small real-world mixing effect, so treat the result as an estimate, not a table value. The new factor is 500 multiplied by the ratio of (density x specific heat) for the mixture to the same product for water, all at 20 C.

The arithmetic for the 30 percent row: 3.641 x 1037.2 = 3776. Water: 4.182 x 998.2 = 4175. The ratio is 3776 / 4175 = 0.905, and 0.905 x 500 is about 452. That matches the rule of thumb of roughly 450 for a typical mix. A 50 percent mix, which is more than most heating loops need for freeze protection, falls to about 418. More glycol than the freeze point requires costs heat delivery and pump energy for no gain.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions. A zone must deliver 60,000 Btu/h at a 20 F temperature drop. Assume the loop is filled with about 30 percent ethylene glycol by mass and uses the factor of 452 from the table. Assume the loop with water would need 10 feet of head at its design flow. These are our assumed inputs, not measured field values.

  1. Water: gpm = 60,000 / (500 x 20) = 60,000 / 10,000 = 6.0 gpm.
  2. Glycol mix: gpm = 60,000 / (452 x 20) = 60,000 / 9,040 = 6.64 gpm.
  3. Increase: 6.64 / 6.0 = 1.106, so about 11 percent more flow.
  4. Friction head in a fixed piping loop rises roughly with the square of flow. Our simplification: 1.106 squared is about 1.22.
  5. Viscosity adds more. We assume a further 10 percent for the thicker fluid, which is an assumption, not a sourced number: 1.22 x 1.10 is about 1.35.
  6. Head estimate: 10 ft x 1.35 is about 13.5 ft.
  7. Pump power at the duty point scales with flow times head: 1.106 x 1.35 is about 1.49, so roughly half again as much hydraulic power.

Pipe velocity also rises by the same 11 percent, which matters for noise and erosion limits on small copper. To find where a circulator actually lands on its curve, plot the new system curve; reading pump curves and system curves shows how.

What it means on a job

  • Size flow and circulators with the factor for the real mix, not 500. Using 500 on a glycol loop makes the calculated gpm too low and the actual temperature drop too wide.
  • If you cannot change the pump, expect less flow than you planned and a larger delta-T across the emitters. Emitter output versus flow rate explains how much heat that costs.
  • Check viscosity effects at the coldest start, when the fluid is thickest.
  • For concentration testing and freeze protection, see glycol in chilled water and hydronic loops.
  • Keep the 500, 1.08 and 4.5 family straight with the heat transfer formulas lesson.

Field card: Glycol and Hydronic Heat Delivery: Why the 500 Factor Drops

Glycol holds less heat, weighs more and flows thicker.

  • Water factor: 8.34 lb/gal x 60 x 1.00 is about 500.
  • New factor = 500 x (density x specific heat of the mix) / (the same for water).
  • About 30 percent ethylene glycol by mass: factor about 452. About 50 percent: about 418 (our estimates).
  • Same Btu and delta-T: gpm = Btu/h / (factor x delta-T). Our example: 6.0 gpm becomes 6.64 gpm.
  • Head rises with flow squared plus a viscosity penalty; cold fluid is the worst case.
  • Use the maker's property table for the actual fluid and mix.
