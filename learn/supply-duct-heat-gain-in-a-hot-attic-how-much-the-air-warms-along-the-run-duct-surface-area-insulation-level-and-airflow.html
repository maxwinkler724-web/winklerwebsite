Supply Duct Heat Gain in a Hot Attic: How Much the Air Warms Along the Run, Duct Surface Area, Insulation Level and Airflow

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A supply duct in a 130 F attic is a long, thin heat exchanger that you did not mean to build. Cold air goes in at the air handler, heat soaks through the duct wall the whole way, and the register delivers warmer air than the coil made. This lesson shows how to estimate how much, with three formulas and a worked example, and why low airflow, long runs, thin insulation and leaks all push the answer the wrong way.

Heat flows from hot to cold, so the air gains

In summer the attic is hotter than the air inside a cooling supply duct, so heat flows from the attic into the duct and the air warms. The direction never flips in a cooling season: the colder the supply air and the hotter the attic, the bigger the push. A U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Building America paper on buried ducts describes attics running through 130 F, and a DOE building-science page says insulation helps prevent heat gain or loss through the duct walls and that without adequate insulation the amount lost or gained can be significant, worst in an uninsulated attic or crawlspace. We use 130 F as an assumed attic temperature in the example. Real attics vary with sun, roof color, ventilation and time of day.

Formula 1: conduction through the duct wall

Heat through a wall is Q = U x A x (Tattic - Tair), where Q is in Btu/h, U is in Btu/h per square foot per degree F, A is the outside surface area in square feet, and the temperature difference is in degrees F. U is one divided by the total resistance in series: the insulation R-value plus the air films on each side. Our series R-value lesson explains why resistances add. The thin metal or inner liner adds almost nothing, so we leave it out.

Formula 2: the surface area of a round duct

Unroll a round duct and you get a rectangle: the circumference times the length. So A = pi x D x L, with D in feet. An 8 in. duct is 8 / 12 = 0.667 ft across, so each foot of run has 3.1416 x 0.667 = 2.09 square feet of surface. Area grows in direct proportion to length and to diameter. A run twice as long has twice the surface, so twice the heat gain at the same insulation and temperatures.

Formula 3: what that heat does to the air

Heat added to moving air shows up as a temperature rise: rise = Q / (1.08 x cfm). The 1.08 is our own derivation for standard air: 0.075 lb per cubic foot x 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F x 60 minutes per hour = 1.08. Some DOE guides round the same constant to 1.1. Our heat-transfer formulas lesson covers where it comes from and when it changes. Because cfm is in the denominator, the same heat spread over half the air gives twice the rise.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Assumptions we chose, not measured values: 25 ft of straight 8 in. round supply duct; 55 F air entering the run; 130 F attic air; a combined air-film resistance of R-0.9 (inside plus outside surfaces together); the duct wall adds no resistance; insulation is exactly its labeled R-value (no compression); we use the 8 in. duct diameter for the area, which is a simplification because the wrap adds thickness; sealed duct, no leaks; sensible heat only.

  1. Area. A = 3.1416 x (8 / 12) x 25 = 3.1416 x 0.6667 x 25 = 52.4 square feet.
  2. Temperature difference at the inlet. 130 - 55 = 75 F.
  3. U for R-6. Total R = 6 + 0.9 = 6.9, so U = 1 / 6.9 = 0.145. U for R-8. Total R = 8 + 0.9 = 8.9, so U = 1 / 8.9 = 0.112.
  4. Heat gain. R-6: 0.145 x 52.4 x 75 = 569 Btu/h. R-8: 0.112 x 52.4 x 75 = 441 Btu/h.
  5. Rise at 150 cfm. 1.08 x 150 = 162. R-6: 569 / 162 = 3.5 F, so the air leaves at 55 + 3.5 = 58.5 F. R-8: 441 / 162 = 2.7 F, so it leaves at 57.7 F.
  6. Same run at 75 cfm. 1.08 x 75 = 81. R-6: 569 / 81 = 7.0 F, leaving at 62.0 F. R-8: 441 / 81 = 5.4 F, leaving at 60.4 F.

What it means. Halving the airflow did not change the heat gain, because that depends on area, resistance and temperature difference. It doubled the rise, because the same heat went into half the air. R-8 cut the heat gain by about 22 percent compared with R-6 (441 against 569, since 6.9 / 8.9 = 0.78).

A small correction. The steps above use the inlet temperature difference for the whole run, but the air warms as it goes, so the gap to the attic shrinks and the true gain is slightly lower. Solving it as a continuous run, our own calculation gives 3.4 F and 2.7 F at 150 cfm, and 6.7 F and 5.3 F at 75 cfm. The simple method runs a few percent high and is fine for a field check. The graph uses the continuous version.

How much of the cooling is that?

Suppose the room is 75 F (another assumption). Each cfm of 55 F air can absorb 1.08 x (75 - 55) = 21.6 Btu/h in the room. At 150 cfm that is 3,240 Btu/h, and the R-6 run gives up 569 of it, about 18 percent. At 75 cfm the room capacity is 1,620 Btu/h, and the same 569 is about 35 percent. With R-8 the shares are about 14 percent and 27 percent. Heat gain in the duct is heat the coil already removed, so it also lengthens run time.

Why low airflow and long runs make it worse

  • Low airflow. The rise is inversely proportional to cfm. A dirty filter, closed registers, a crushed branch, a weak blower or a damper closing most of a zone all cut cfm. An 8 in. duct at 75 cfm moves air at only about 215 feet per minute (75 / 0.349 sq ft), so it lingers in the attic.
  • Long runs. Surface area, and so heat gain, grows with length. Branches at the far end of a trunk have the most attic exposure, so those rooms often get the warmest supply air.
  • Small branches carrying little air. Heat gain follows surface area while cooling delivered follows cfm. A skinny branch with a long path and little air is where the rise is largest.

Field data are messy. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory field study of duct systems found temperature rises along ducts from 0.5 C to almost 6 C (roughly 1 F to 11 F), and the authors said the length mattered but that duct size, insulation and air velocity also differed from system to system, so ambient temperature and length alone did not predict the rise well. That matches our arithmetic: several inputs act together.

Why the insulation level matters, and why the installed R-value counts

Going from R-6 to R-8 changed heat gain by about 128 Btu/h in our example. That is real but modest next to what happens if the wrap is crushed. A University of Central Florida research report found that metal duct wrap was compressed so much that its R-value was probably near R-2.5 instead of R-6, and its simulations showed annual cooling energy 5, 8, 21 and 32 percent higher than at R-6 for R-3.5, R-2.5, R-1 and R-0.5. Rerun our example at R-2.5: total R = 3.4, U = 0.294, Q = 0.294 x 52.4 x 75 = 1,155 Btu/h, a 7.1 F rise at 150 cfm. A badly compressed wrap, a torn jacket, or bare spots at fittings and hangers can cost more than the difference between R-6 and R-8. The DOE building-science page says a federal efficiency program calls for at least R-8 on ducts in unconditioned spaces such as attics. See our duct insulation R-value lesson for the code numbers. The DOE buried-duct paper reports that burying a duct under loose-fill insulation raises its effective R-value (about R-9 to R-31 in their table, depending on depth and material) and cut peak load by half a ton in one test house.

Why leaks matter too

Our numbers assume a sealed duct. Leaks add two separate problems that conduction math does not capture:

  • Supply leaks dump cooled air into the attic, so less than 150 cfm reaches the rooms, and the same conduction gain lands on less air. See our duct leakage testing lesson and the duct sealing lesson.
  • Return leaks in the attic pull hot air straight into the system. As an example of our own, 20 cfm of 130 F attic air mixing into 75 F return air adds 1.08 x 20 x (130 - 75) = 1,188 Btu/h of sensible heat, more than double the R-6 conduction gain above.

Insulating a leaky duct and sealing an uninsulated one each fix only half the problem. Seal first, then insulate. A cold, wet duct has its own risk, covered in our will it sweat lesson.

Field checks

  • Measure supply temperature at the plenum and at the farthest register with the system running at least 15 minutes. The difference is conduction plus leakage effects, in degrees F.
  • Check the filter, registers and dampers before blaming the insulation. Low cfm magnifies everything above.
  • Look for compressed wrap, gaps at boots and hangers, and loose jackets.
  • When a long run is the problem, shortening it, burying it, or moving it inside the conditioned space beat adding another layer.

Field card: Supply Duct Heat Gain in a Hot Attic

Heat flows from the hot attic into the cold air. Same heat, less air, bigger rise.

  • Q = U x A x (T attic - T air), with U = 1 / (R insulation + R films).
  • Round duct area A = 3.1416 x D (ft) x L (ft). 8 in. duct = 2.09 sq ft per foot.
  • Air rise = Q / (1.08 x cfm). Half the cfm doubles the rise.
  • Our example, 25 ft of 8 in., 55 F air, 130 F attic: R-6 gains 569 Btu/h, R-8 gains 441 Btu/h. At 150 cfm the air arrives at 58.5 F or 57.7 F. At 75 cfm, 62.0 F or 60.4 F.
  • Longer run, less air, thinner or crushed wrap: all raise the delivered temperature.
  • Seal first (supply leaks lose air, return leaks add attic heat), then insulate.
