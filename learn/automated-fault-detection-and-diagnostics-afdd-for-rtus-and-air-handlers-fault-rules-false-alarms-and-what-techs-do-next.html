AFDD for RTUs and Air Handlers: Fault Rules, False Alarms and What Techs Do Next

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Automated fault detection and diagnostics, usually shortened to AFDD or FDD, is software that watches the data a controller already has and raises a flag when the numbers do not make physical sense. This lesson explains how the rules work, three common air handler rules, what California requires for economizers, why false alarms happen, and how a tech confirms a flag at the unit.

What AFDD is

A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) report describes AFDD as monitoring HVAC data and applying a set of rules to find equipment problems, called faults, and their underlying causes. The data is the kind a tech already sees on a controller: temperatures, flows, pressures and control signals. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) survey of commercial tools says AFDD looks for deviations from normal or expected operation. It adds that faults and operating errors are estimated to waste 5 to 30 percent of energy use in commercial buildings.

AFDD does not fix anything. It points at a symptom, and a person still has to confirm it at the unit.

Rule-based versus model-based

The same LBNL survey sorted 14 tools by method. Twelve used rule-based logic, which compares readings against limits and relationships written by experts. Two used model-based methods, which depend on a physical model of how the equipment should behave. Three used data-driven methods that learn patterns from history without a physical model. The authors say the tools need site-specific tuning, and they list avoiding false positives as an open question for users.

Rule-based is the easiest for a tech to reason about: if you can state the rule, you can check it with a thermometer.

Three common air handler rules

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published an early rule set called APAR, short for air-handling unit performance assessment rules, in an ASHRAE paper from 2001. NIST says the rules can find some fairly obvious AHU problems, and that detection depends on fault severity and operating conditions. ASHRAE Guideline 36 (named here in plain text, not linked) later wrote similar fault conditions for variable air volume air handlers. We paraphrase three families of rules.

  • Mixed air outside the range. Mixed air is a blend of outside air and return air, so its temperature cannot be higher than both or lower than both. The Guideline 36 text lists "mixed air temperature too low" and "too high" conditions that test this, and says to skip them if there is no mixed air sensor. A reading outside the range points to a sensor, a wiring problem or a badly placed probe, not to a real air condition.
  • Simultaneous heating and cooling. A PNNL re-tuning guide says an operator can spot an air handler heating and cooling the same discharge air stream by watching outdoor air temperature and coil valve signals together, and warns that leaky coil valves and simultaneous heating and cooling raise fan, heating and cooling energy use. Guideline 36 does not list it as its own numbered fault. It notes that an unknown operating state can signal this kind of problem, such as a controller failure or programming error, but can also be normal during dehumidification or warm-up.
  • Supply temperature not meeting setpoint. Guideline 36 includes a "supply air temperature too low in full heating" condition that is tested when the heating valve is at 99 percent or more, and a matching "too high in full cooling" condition with the cooling valve at 99 percent or more. If the valve is pinned open and the air still misses its target, the coil, its supply or the sensor needs a look.

Guideline 36 also says its alarms are raised after a delay that helps suppress false alarms. A single bad reading should not page anyone.

Worked example: the mixed air rule

Our own example, with assumptions stated. Assume a rooftop unit on a hot day. Outside air (OA) reads 90 °F and return air (RA) reads 75 °F. These are made-up inputs for teaching. The controller reports mixed air (MA) at 93 °F.

Step 1, the range test. Mixed air must fall between 75 and 90 °F. The reading of 93 is 3 °F above the hottest stream in the box, so the rule fails.

Step 2, the outside air fraction. The usual mixing relation is OA fraction = (RA − MA) / (RA − OA). With the faulty reading: (75 − 93) / (75 − 90) = (−18) / (−15) = 1.20, or 120 percent. No damper can pass 120 percent outside air, so the number proves the temperature is wrong, not the damper. PNNL's re-tuning guide uses the same outdoor air fraction idea and adds that, if there are no sensor errors, it is the only true indicator of the share of outdoor air entering the building.

Step 3, a valid reading. Now assume MA reads 79 °F. It sits between 75 and 90, so the range test passes. OA fraction = (75 − 79) / (75 − 90) = (−4) / (−15) = 0.267, about 27 percent outside air. Whether that is the right amount depends on the design minimum or the economizer command, a separate check.

A margin is needed. A real rule allows for sensor error. Assume (our own) each sensor is off by 2 °F, in line with the California sensor accuracy below. Combined error could reach 4 °F, so a 3 °F excess alone could hide inside tolerance. That is why tools wait and watch. A reading 10 °F over would be a clear fault.

What California requires for economizers

The California Energy Commission (CEC) posts Joint Appendix JA6.3 for economizer fault detection and diagnostics under the Title 24 building energy code. The version we read was posted in 2020, so check the current edition. It says the requirement applies to air-cooled unitary systems whose air handler has mechanical cooling capacity over 54,000 Btu per hour. Three points matter to a tech:

  • Sensors. The unit needs outside air, supply air and, where differential economizer control needs it, return air sensors, with accuracy of plus or minus 2 °F across 40 to 80 °F.
  • Faults to detect. The five are: air temperature sensor failure or fault, not economizing when it should, economizing when it should not, damper not modulating, and excess outdoor air.
  • Reporting. A fault must go to an energy management system that staff watch, or show locally within 5 feet of the zone thermostats at eye level with instructions to contact building staff or an HVAC technician, or go to a fault application that notifies a remote HVAC service provider.

The controller must also show status points such as economizer enabled and each sensor value, and let a tech manually start each operating mode. The test method imposes each fault, for example by securing damper blades or disconnecting sensors one at a time, then confirms it clears. The CEC compliance manual for the 2025 code lists economizer FDD among products that must be certified to the Commission by the manufacturer. We did not confirm from that excerpt who performs field acceptance testing, so read the current code text for your job.

False alarms and sensor errors

A tool can be wrong in two ways: flagging a fault that is not there (a false positive) or missing one that is (a false negative). PNNL researchers say AFDD records contain both and plan to compare them with manual inspections. A DOE Building Technologies Office peer review of that work lists both as a reporting risk. An NREL report adds that a no-fault range has to allow for tolerances and sensor accuracy, and that sensors must stay accurate over the equipment's life.

In practice the usual causes of a false alarm on air handlers are a sensor reading wrong, a sensor in a spot that does not represent the airstream, a rule threshold set too tight, and a normal operating state the rule does not know about, such as warm-up or dehumidification.

How a tech confirms a fault in the field

Our own sequence, built from the sources above:

  1. Read the rule. Write down which comparison tripped and the values at that moment. Note the unit's mode: heating, economizing, mechanical cooling or unoccupied.
  2. Check the sensors first. Compare each controller reading with a calibrated handheld thermometer in the same airstream. A temperature sensor that disagrees points to the sensor, its wiring or its location.
  3. Do the arithmetic. Compute the outside air fraction from the readings. A value below 0 or above 100 percent means a bad temperature, as in the example.
  4. Command the device. Use the controller's manual mode to drive the damper open and closed and watch both the linkage and the readings, as the California test method does.
  5. Look at the trend. One spike may be noise. A fault that repeats at the same time or mode is real. See the BAS trend lesson linked below.
  6. Record and report. Note what you found, what you changed and whether the flag cleared.

If no cause is found, the threshold may need adjusting. Pass that finding to whoever owns the tool rather than silencing the alarm yourself.

Field card: AFDD for RTUs and Air Handlers

A flag is a question, not a verdict.

  • AFDD applies rules to controller data to flag faults; it does not fix them.
  • Most tools are rule-based; some use physical models or learned patterns.
  • Mixed air must sit between return air and outside air, give or take sensor error.
  • OA fraction = (RA − MA) / (RA − OA). Below 0 or above 1 means a bad reading.
  • Example (our own numbers): OA 90, RA 75, MA 79 gives 27 percent; MA 93 gives 120 percent, impossible.
  • California economizer FDD: over 54,000 Btu/hr cooling, sensors within 2 °F, five fault types.
  • Check sensors with a handheld, command the damper, then check the trend.
