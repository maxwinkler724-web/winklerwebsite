Steam Headers in Multi-Boiler Plants: Sizing, Takeoffs, Drip Legs and Stop-Check Valves

Last updated: 2026-11-04

In a plant with two or three boilers, every boiler hands its steam to one common pipe, the header, and every load takes steam from that pipe. The header is easy to overlook because it is just a large pipe, yet most of the plant's wet-steam and water-hammer trouble shows up there. This lesson covers what the header is for, how to size it, how to pipe its takeoffs and drains, and what the valves between boilers and header do.

What a header does

A header is a short, large-diameter manifold. Its jobs are to collect steam from each boiler, to act as a small buffer so one boiler cutting in or out does not whip the whole plant, and to give the distribution lines a single place to start. Because it is big, steam slows down inside it. Slow steam lets droplets of water fall out of the flow and drain away instead of racing down the mains. That is why it is sized by velocity rather than by simply matching the boiler outlet.

Sizing for low velocity

Steam velocity is the volume flow divided by the pipe area. A federal steam technical brief gives the working formula in terms of flow, specific volume and internal pipe area, and uses 80 feet per second as a conservative upper limit for saturated steam, noting that velocities above 120 feet per second cause erosion and noise. The same brief says that lowering boiler pressure raises steam velocity for the same mass flow, because the steam is less dense, and that lower pressure can increase entrainment of liquid droplets. It also says wet steam can damage valves and traps. A header is usually sized well below those piping limits, because the goal is not just avoiding erosion. The goal is a velocity low enough that water settles out. The brief gives no single header target, so the target in the example below is our own assumption. One university standard bases steam pipe sizing on a handbook table. This lesson is for understanding, not for sizing a real plant.

Takeoffs from the top

Steam lines that leave a header or main should come off the top. Two university construction standards say the same thing in different words: one says to connect multiple branches to the top of the main, and the other says branches connect to the top of the pipe at a 45 degree angle. The reason is simple (our reasoning): water runs along the bottom of a pipe, so a tee on the bottom would scoop it into the branch, and a tee on top mostly takes the drier steam above it.

Drip leg and trap at the low end

Whatever water does fall out needs somewhere to go. University standards put drip legs at low points and natural drainage points, with another adding the ends of mains, the bottoms of risers, and the places ahead of control and isolation valves. One standard matches the drip leg to the main, allowing a reduction on large mains but not below a stated minimum size. Each drip leg ends in a steam trap, and a dirt pocket ahead of the trap is called for in one standard. The same standards lean toward inverted bucket traps on drip points. A federal design manual for medical facilities says drip-point traps are the inverted bucket type and sizes them for three times the line radiation loss to handle start-up. Pitch matters too: one standard slopes steam supply lines down in the direction of flow at 1 inch in 40 feet, so condensate runs to the drip points. Our diagram puts the drip leg at the low end of the header, where the slope delivers water.

Stop valves and nonreturn valves between each boiler and the header

A boiler must be able to come out of service while its neighbors stay on the line. State boiler rules say each steam outlet, except safety valve connections, needs a stop valve. For boilers with manholes connected to a common steam main, the rule calls for two stop valves per connection, with a generous open drain between them that is visible to the operator, and says the stop valves should preferably be one automatic nonreturn valve next to the boiler and a second valve of the outside screw-and-yoke type. That combination is the stop-check arrangement in our sketch. A federal design manual also expects maintenance access to the steam stop and nonreturn valves at the top of the boilers, so a platform is part of the design. Always follow the jurisdiction's current rules and the maker's drawings for the actual piping.

Warming a cold header

A cold header and its mains act as a condensing surface. A university steam and chilled-water guide notes that a main reheated after a shutdown is much colder than the entering steam and makes a lot of condensate, and that with no pressure yet to push water through traps, operators open drain valves by hand and keep them open until the system reaches working pressure. Follow your plant's written procedure for opening the boiler stop valves, the header drains and the loads, and expect to go slowly. The condensate load, pipe-weight arithmetic and slow warm-up are worked in the warm-up lesson, and venting is in steam main venting basics, so we do not repeat them.

Our own worked example: header velocity

This is our own example. Every input is an assumption, not data from a real plant.

  • Three boilers, each assumed to send 10,000 lb/h to the header, so the header carries 30,000 lb/h.
  • Saturated steam near 100 psig, specific volume assumed 3.9 ft³/lb (a rounded steam-table value).
  • Candidate pipes, using nominal inside diameters we assume for standard-weight pipe: 10 inch at 10.02 in and 12 inch at 11.938 in.
  • Assumed low-velocity target for a header: 3,000 ft/min (50 ft/s). This is our own choice, not a published limit.

Step 1, volume flow. 30,000 lb/h × 3.9 ft³/lb = 117,000 ft³/h. Divide by 3,600 s/h: 32.5 ft³/s.

Step 2, area. 10 inch: π/4 × 10.02² = 78.9 in², which is 78.9 ÷ 144 = 0.548 ft². 12 inch: π/4 × 11.938² = 111.9 in², which is 0.777 ft².

Step 3, velocity. 10 inch: 32.5 ÷ 0.548 = 59.3 ft/s, or about 3,560 ft/min. 12 inch: 32.5 ÷ 0.777 = 41.8 ft/s, or about 2,510 ft/min.

Check against limits. Both pipes are under the 80 ft/s conservative piping limit in the federal brief, so neither will erode. Against our assumed 3,000 ft/min header target, the 10 inch header (3,560) is too fast and the 12 inch header (2,510) passes. Because velocity scales with 1 over the area, a lower pressure (larger specific volume) would raise both numbers, which is why the brief says to recheck velocity whenever pressure changes. Unit check: lb/h × ft³/lb = ft³/h, which divided by 3,600 and ft² gives ft/s.

Common problems

  • Carryover. Boiler water leaves with the steam. A university water-treatment guide ties it to high dissolved solids, which make the boiler prime or foam and send droplets into the steam, and says carryover cuts the steam's heat content and can damage system parts. The federal brief suggests checking condensate conductivity near the boiler outlet to look for it. A header cannot cure heavy carryover; fix the boiler water and level first.
  • Water hammer. A federal operations and maintenance guide says the damage depends on steam velocity, not pressure, and that failed traps backing condensate into a steam main is a common cause. Undrained headers, a missing drip leg or a plugged trap are the first places to look.
  • A boiler back-fed by the header. If a boiler's steam outlet valves leak or are left open while the boiler is down or lower in pressure, header steam can flow back into it. The nonreturn valve is there to stop that (our reasoning from the valve's purpose). Follow the plant procedure before opening any boiler stop valve to a live header.

For how velocity and pressure drop are used to pick pipe sizes along the mains, see sizing steam piping by velocity and pressure drop.

Field card: Steam headers at the boiler outlet

Big pipe, slow steam, drain the low end.

  • The header collects steam from every boiler and slows it so water drops out.
  • Velocity = volume flow / area; lower pressure means higher velocity.
  • Takeoffs leave the top; drip leg and trap sit at the low end and ahead of isolation valves.
  • Each boiler gets a stop valve and a nonreturn valve ahead of the header.
  • Warm a cold header slowly, with the drains handled per plant procedure.
  • Carryover, hammer and back-feed are the common faults.
