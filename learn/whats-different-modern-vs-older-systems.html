What's Different: Modern vs. Older Systems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Back in the day a residential system was a contactor, a PSC blower, a 24V thermostat and a charge you set with gauges and a thermometer. Modern equipment still moves heat the same way, but the machine around the refrigerant circuit has changed a lot. This lesson is the map for the rest of the New systems module.

Why this module exists

If you learned on R-22 and R-410A, single-stage compressors, PSC motors and a thermostat that closes a few 24V contacts, you already understand the physics that still runs every system: pressure, temperature, heat transfer and airflow. What changed is how equipment protects itself, how it controls itself, and what you are allowed to assume when you open a panel.

Think of it as moving from a machine you could understand by following a handful of wires to one that you often have to understand by following a conversation between boards. Your diagnostic discipline still applies. Some of your shortcuts do not.

A2L refrigerants and the equipment around them

The industry is moving residential and light commercial equipment to lower global warming potential refrigerants. EPA lists several mildly flammable A2L refrigerants as acceptable for residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps, with use conditions that are spelled out in the rules. A2L means lower toxicity and mildly flammable: harder to ignite than propane-type refrigerants, but not something to treat like R-410A.

That flammability class is the reason many new units add safety hardware and why service habits are changing. The next four lessons cover this in detail. See also the existing lesson A2L and A3 Refrigerants.

Leak detection and mitigation

Many A2L systems include a refrigerant sensor that talks to a control board. If the sensor reports refrigerant, the board takes protective action, which commonly includes running the indoor blower and disabling heat and cooling outputs. What exactly happens, and whether a given product has a sensor at all, depends on the manufacturer, the model and the charge. Read A2L Leak Detection Sensors and A2L Control Boards and Mitigation.

Inverter-driven compressors

A conventional compressor is either across the line or it is off. An inverter-driven compressor is fed by a drive that rectifies incoming power, stores it in capacitors and then rebuilds an output whose frequency sets compressor speed. The result is a unit that can run slowly for long periods and ramp up when the load demands. The cost is a power electronics board that holds stored energy and cannot be checked the way a contactor and capacitor are. See Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.

ECM blowers

The PSC motor with speed taps has given way to electronically commutated motors that are told what to do by a control signal. They are efficient and respond to the control, but they are a motor plus a module, and a bad module can look exactly like a bad motor. See ECM Blower Motors.

Communicating controls

Instead of one wire per function, a communicating system exchanges digital messages on a small number of conductors. The control can see temperatures, pressures, speeds and fault history. It also means wiring quality matters in new ways, and that a replacement part may need to be recognized and configured by the system. See Communicating Controls Basics.

Two-stage and variable capacity, and SEER2

Two-stage and variable-capacity equipment can run at partial output most of the time. Research on variable-capacity heat pumps has found that long, low-output run times can be very efficient, and that the indoor fan on these systems typically slows down with demand and uses far less power than an old shaded-pole or PSC motor. A side effect is that conditioned air spends more time in ducts, so duct location and leakage matter more than ever.

On the rating side, the U.S. Department of Energy test procedure now uses SEER2, HSPF2 and EER2. These are measured with a higher minimum external static pressure on ducted systems than the older ratings used, which is a closer match to real duct systems. The newest update keeps those metrics. Take-away for the field: published efficiency numbers are rated under defined lab conditions, and your install may differ.

What to unlearn

  • Do not assume a sensor is optional. If a unit has a leak sensor, treat it as part of the safety system. Never bypass or jumper it.
  • Do not assume a 24V call equals a running component. Boards decide whether to honor the call.
  • Do not condemn a motor or compressor from one test. Find out whether the control or drive is the problem first.
  • Do not swap a board and walk away. Some parts need configuration or matching software.
  • Do not use your old recovery gear, cylinders or leak detector without checking. Compatibility is the manufacturer's call.
  • Do not guess. Reading the sequence of operation is not a sign of inexperience. It is the job.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, thresholds, sensor positions or wiring steps, and those vary by manufacturer and by model.

Field card: What's Different: Modern vs. Older Systems

Same physics, new machine: more electronics, more safety, more conversation between parts.

  • A2L means mildly flammable. Expect safety hardware and new service habits.
  • Leak sensors and boards are part of the safety system. Never bypass them.
  • Inverters and ECM modules store energy and hide faults from old tests.
  • Communicating systems can need correct wiring and configuration after any part swap.
  • The unit's own manual beats every rule of thumb, including these.
