Steam Boiler Water Columns, Gauge Glasses and Try Cocks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a plant steam boiler, the water level is read from one small vertical vessel and the glass beside it. If that reading is wrong, everything else about the boiler is guesswork. This lesson covers what the water column does, how the gauge glass and try cocks back each other up, how to blow them down, how to tell a true level from a false one, and what to write in the log.

Check your own manual first

Your boiler maker's instructions and your site procedure win. This page is general education built from public government, military and university-posted documents. Where we describe a step order, we say so and label it as our own. The ASME boiler code (Section IV for heating boilers) sets the design rules; your state boiler inspection office sets the local ones.

What the water column does

An Arkansas Department of Labor study guide describes the water column as a vessel connected at the top to the boiler's steam space and at the bottom to its water space, with the gauge glass and test cocks mounted on it. It also says the column steadies the reading and sits at the normal operating water level. The same guide puts normal level at roughly one-third to one-half of the glass.

A Washington state pre-inspection checklist, which quotes ASME Section IV, lists the plumbing rules that keep that reading honest: connecting pipes at least 1 inch, a cross or equivalent fitting at every right-angle turn so the lines can be cleaned, a drain pipe and valve at least NPS ¾ inch, and no shutoff valves between the boiler and the column. It also says the lowest visible part of the glass must be at least 1 inch above the lowest permissible water level, so the burner must be shut off before the water leaves the glass.

Gauge glass, its valves and the try cocks

The Arkansas guide says tubular glass is used up to 250 psi and flat glass above that. Each glass has an upper and a lower valve, plus a drain. A North Dakota boiler inspection training presentation says both glass valves must stay open in normal operation, and that closing either gives a false reading.

Try cocks (also called gauge cocks) are the backup. The same presentation says that, when fitted, the top one gives steam, the middle one a steam and water mix, and the bottom one water, and that you may use them to find the level if the glass breaks. The Arkansas guide lists them as the way to test the level when the glass is out of service. A Navy training manual adds a mounting point: try cocks must be in exact alignment, because misalignment strains the glass and can cause early failure. A federal rule for locomotive boilers (49 CFR Part 230, a different kind of boiler) is a useful parallel: it requires water glasses to be blown out and gauge cocks tested at the start of each day of use, and it requires a shield that keeps broken glass from flying.

True level versus false level

A false level is a reading that does not match the water in the shell. The sources point to a short list of causes.

  • A closed or partly closed glass valve. North Dakota says it gives a false reading. Our own explanation: a closed lower valve leaves the glass holding old water that never moves, and a closed upper valve lets steam condense in the glass until it fills and looks high.
  • Plugged connections. A Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry guide tells operators at the start of a shift to make sure the pipe, fittings and valves between the glass and the boiler are free and open. It says to blow down the glass and column and watch how fast water returns, and that a slow return suggests a blocked connection. A federal health-system boiler testing manual says the level should rise quickly in the glass after the drain is closed, which shows good communication with the boiler, and that if communication is poor, the lines should be cleaned. A Maine inspection list shows what neglect looks like: a gauge glass so scaled the operator could not read the level.
  • Foaming and priming. The Arkansas guide defines foaming as rapid fluctuations of the water level, caused by impurities on the water surface, that can lead to priming or carryover (water droplets going into the steam line). The level bounces, so one glance is not enough. Treat it as a water treatment problem; see the blowdown lessons linked below.

The Minnesota guide gives the rule when you cannot find the water: if water is not visible in the glass, shut the boiler off immediately until a safe level has been determined. Do not feed a hot boiler to find out.

The low-water cutoff in the column

Many columns carry the low-water fuel cutoff. The Arkansas guide says it shuts off the main burner at the lowest safe level and sits slightly below the normal level. The North Dakota presentation says cutoffs should trip while "a visible waterline barely in the glass" still shows. A New York state rule, posted by a university safety office, requires the fuel cutout to shut off fuel before water drops lower than one-quarter inch above the lowest visible level of the glass. A cutoff that trips after the water has left the glass has failed. Our full test lesson is linked below. The federal health-system manual says not to test a cutoff by rapidly blowing off water from the column, and calls for a slow drain of no more than 1 inch per minute. The Navy manual says not to adjust a cutoff yourself, since the maker sets it.

Blowdown sequence (our own step order)

A Navy public works shop procedure for a steam boiler lists the glass blowdown as a daily item: open the gauge glass drain valve, blow down the glass, close the drain, and observe the level return to the operating level. The order below builds on that and the Minnesota advice. It is our own, for study, not a replacement for your site procedure.

  1. Put on face protection and gloves. Check that the drain lines run to a safe discharge and nobody is in the path.
  2. Confirm the burner is firing normally and the level is in the normal range. Note the level and pressure.
  3. Open the glass drain valve with both glass valves open. Let it blow until the flow runs clear. The level in the glass drops.
  4. Close the glass drain valve. Watch the level come back. Note how long it takes.
  5. Open the column drain valve. Let it blow until clear. If the cutoff is in the column, it may trip the burner; that is the test. The level drops.
  6. Close the column drain. Watch the level recover. If the burner tripped, reset it and confirm it relights.
  7. Check that the level sits where it was in step 2. Record times and any odd behavior.

What the level should do: drop while draining, then recover promptly to the same line. Our own meaning for a slow recovery: partly plugged lines, valve scale, or a half-closed valve. Report it, write it down, and do not rely on the glass alone until it is cleared.

Worked example: a daily log entry (our own, assumed numbers)

These numbers are assumptions for teaching, not code limits. Say the last ten log lines show a glass recovery of about 8 seconds and a column recovery of about 10 seconds. Today the entry reads:

2026-10-16, 07:10, operator initials. Steam pressure 5 psi. Level mid-glass before blowdown. Glass blown, level dropped to bottom of glass, recovered to mid-glass in 8 s. Column blown, cutoff tripped burner, reset, burner relit, level back to mid-glass in 10 s. Try cocks: not used. Remarks: none.

Check against baseline: glass 8 s against 8 s is 8 ÷ 8 = 1.0 times normal. Column 10 s against a baseline of 10 s is 1.0 times normal. Now suppose next week the glass takes 20 s: 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5 times normal. Our own flag rule is more than 2 times baseline, or any recovery that stops short of the line, so 2.5 trips it. The entry would add: Slow recovery, 20 s, call for service, glass not trusted, verified by try cocks.

Replacing a gauge glass safely

The Navy training manual says to replace a broken or discolored glass at once, use new gaskets, avoid loose packing that could block the valve opening, and close the valves when replacing the glass. If it is replaced while the boiler is in service, open the blowdown valve, bring the glass slowly to operating temperature by cracking the gauge valves slightly, then close the blowdown valve and open the gauge valves fully. That is the warm-up step. The federal locomotive rule above also calls for a shield on tubular glass, so a guard belongs on the glass, and a broken glass can spray hot water and steam, so wear face protection; see our eye and face protection lesson. Our own advice: have the glass, gaskets and a shield ready before you start.

Daily and weekly checks, and the boiler log

Sources differ on frequency, so follow your site's schedule. The North Dakota presentation lists, for steam boilers, daily monitoring of pressure three to four times with logged readings, flushing the cutoff float bowls and testing the cutoff by draining; weekly testing of the cutoffs for burner shutdown and blowing down every blowdown valve. Its own steam section and checklist disagree on daily versus weekly cutoff testing. The Arkansas guide lists daily flushing under the water column. The Minnesota guide says the cutoff should be tested at least weekly. The New York rule above gives the glass blowdown as daily for high-pressure steam and weekly for low-pressure steam, with a slow drain test of the cutoff twice a year and annual cleaning or replacement. That rule also says operators keep logs, that the logs be available to inspectors, and that they be kept at least five years.

A good log line holds the date and time, who did it, pressure, level before and after, glass and column recovery times, cutoff result, and remarks.

Field card: Water Column, Glass and Try Cocks

Blow it down, watch it come back, write it down.

  • The column ties steam space and water space together; the glass, try cocks and often the cutoff read it.
  • No shutoff valves between boiler and column. Glass valves stay open.
  • Try cocks: top steam, middle mix, bottom water. They are the backup if the glass breaks.
  • Closed valve, plugged line, foaming: all can fool the glass.
  • Level drops then recovers fast. Slow recovery means a partly blocked line.
  • No water in the glass: shut the boiler off, do not feed it.
  • New glass: gaskets, shield, warm it slowly, face protection.
  • Our own example: 8 s baseline, 20 s today is 2.5 times, so call it in.
