Whole-Body Cryotherapy Studios: HVAC and Oxygen Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some whole-body cold studios cool a cabin with liquid nitrogen, and the nitrogen that leaves the cabin goes into the room. Nitrogen is not poisonous, but it pushes out the oxygen people need. This lesson explains how that happens, what the room's HVAC and oxygen monitor are there to do, and what we check before we work in one. It is about air and equipment only, and it makes no health claims.

Two ways to cool a cabin

Some cabins and chambers are cooled with liquid nitrogen. Others use electric refrigeration. We did not find a source we could cite on the details of each type, so do not assume. Ask the owner which type is installed, and read the maker's manual for the unit in front of you. If liquid nitrogen is on site, everything below applies. Even with an electric unit, a studio may keep a dewar for backup, so look for one.

Why nitrogen is a room-air problem

Liquid nitrogen turns into a very large volume of gas. A university safety report gives the liquid-to-gas ratio as 1 to 696.5, and another university page says one liter of liquid becomes 24.6 cubic feet of gas. Treat that as the usual "about 1 to 700" rule of thumb. The gas does not burn or smell, and your body gives no warning. What changes is the oxygen share of the air.

The federal respiratory protection rule (29 CFR 1910.134, on the eCFR site) defines an oxygen-deficient atmosphere as one with an oxygen content below 19.5 percent by volume. A federal confined-space booklet lists oxygen first in the order for testing a space. A university guide adds that a cold room can become oxygen deficient if there is not enough ventilation to remove the nitrogen gas generated.

Cold gas pools low

Nitrogen at room temperature is a little lighter than air, according to a university medical-center slide deck. Gas that has just left a cold cabin or a venting tank is far colder, and a federal technical bulletin says cold nitrogen released from vented tanks or cylinders can settle low. A UK university code of practice (cited here as plain text) says nitrogen collects in heavier concentration at low level and warns that a person who collapses may fall into denser gas. For us, the lesson is simple: floor level, pits, sumps and the area behind equipment can be worse than the air at your chest. It is the same pooling idea we cover for dry ice in the related storage-room lesson.

Oxygen monitors: what the sources say

  • Where. One federal bulletin calls for monitors with at least two sensors in the breathing zone, about 4 feet above the floor, with audible and visual alarms and a remote alarm just outside the space. A university guide says typically 4 to 6 feet for liquid nitrogen, away from doors and supply vents because incoming air dilutes the sample. Another university report says sensors usually sit in low-ventilation areas, near bulk storage and where dewars are filled. Because cold gas settles low, a designer may add low-level sensing or exhaust. Do not move a sensor on your own. Ask the owner or the monitor maker.
  • Alarm points. The sources we read name 19.5 percent as the hazard line. They do not give alarm settings, so we do not list any here. Ask for the site's written setpoints and compare the monitor to them.
  • Upkeep. A federal protocol says to calibrate every 12 months or as the maker recommends, whichever is sooner, and to check the display daily. A university guide gives the same annual and daily pattern and suggests interlocking the monitor with an emergency ventilation fan where possible.

Exhaust and room air changes

A university page says a minimum of six air changes per hour is required in liquid nitrogen areas, and another says typical lab ventilation is 6 to 12 air changes per hour. A federal bulletin advises a low-level exhaust point in addition to any other exhaust. The best exhaust is near the source: at the chamber, where gas leaves, and at the dewar. Room air changes are a backup. Check that the exhaust fan runs, that it moves air (a tissue or smoke test at the grille is enough to see flow), and that nothing blocks the makeup-air path. A fan that is off, a closed damper or a taped-over louver turns a safe room into a sealed one.

Dewar storage and filling

  • One university page says do not fill dewars and cylinders past 80 percent, fill warm dewars slowly, and store containers upright with adequate ventilation.
  • The same page says containers vent now and then, never to plug or tamper with a relief device, and that vents are best piped outside the building. It says not to keep liquid nitrogen in a container with a tight-fitting lid.
  • A university report warns that non-cryogenic tubing can burst when liquid nitrogen expands inside it, and says lines and connections should come from the supplier. It also notes some campuses use a buddy system when pouring.

Filling and storage are the studio's job, not ours. Our job is to notice when the arrangement puts a venting tank in a closet with no exhaust.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own teaching example, not a measurement of any studio. We assume the following.

  • Room: 10 ft by 12 ft by 9 ft, so volume = 10 x 12 x 9 = 1,080 cubic feet. No furniture volume is subtracted.
  • Our own assumption: one session releases 4 liters of liquid nitrogen as gas, over 3 minutes.
  • Expansion: 24.6 cubic feet of gas per liter (university page, room temperature).
  • Our own assumption: the gas warms to room temperature, mixes perfectly and instantly with room air, and air starts at 20.9 percent oxygen.
  • Our own assumption: whatever nitrogen goes in, an equal volume of mixed air leaves through leaks or the exhaust.

Step 1, gas volume. 4 L x 24.6 = 98.4 cubic feet. Release rate: 98.4 / 3 = 32.8 cubic feet per minute.

Step 2, room with no exhaust. Oxygen = 20.9 x e^(-98.4 / 1,080) = 20.9 x e^(-0.0911) = 20.9 x 0.913 = about 19.1 percent. That is already under the 19.5 percent line after one session. A second and third session without airing out would reach about 15.9 percent in the same model (20.9 x e^(-3 x 0.0911)).

Step 3, exhaust at 108 cfm. Six air changes per hour is 6 x 1,080 / 60 = 108 cfm. Using the mixing balance, the room settles toward 20.9 x 108 / (108 + 32.8) = 16.0 percent while gas is still flowing, and after 3 minutes it reaches about 19.3 percent. The six-change figure barely helps a fast release.

Step 4, exhaust at 600 cfm. Settling point 20.9 x 600 / (600 + 32.8) = 19.8 percent, and after 3 minutes about 20.0 percent. That is above 19.5, but with little margin.

What this tells us. Real air is not perfectly mixed. Cold gas pools low and near the cabin, so the real numbers near the floor are worse than these averages. Steady fans and exhaust at the source matter more than a room-wide rate. We are showing the arithmetic, not a design.

What we check before working in the room

  • Ask the owner what is on site and who is in charge of the nitrogen.
  • Read the monitor display. Look for a recent calibration label, a fault light or a bagged sensor.
  • Confirm the exhaust fan runs and the makeup-air path is open.
  • Do not work alone in the room, and keep the door open if you can.
  • Note dewar location and vents. Never block or cap a relief vent.
  • If a low-level area (pit, sump, below-floor space) is part of the job, treat it like a confined space and read our confined-space lesson first.
  • If an alarm sounds, leave and wait for the site contact. Do not go back in to silence it.

Field card: Whole-Body Cryotherapy Studios

Nitrogen takes the oxygen quietly. Check air first.

  • Liquid nitrogen expands about 696 times into gas (university sources).
  • Oxygen deficient means below 19.5 percent by volume.
  • Cold nitrogen gas can settle low; sensors usually sit in the breathing zone.
  • Ask for the site's alarm setpoints; do not guess them.
  • Check monitor calibration, exhaust running and makeup air open.
  • Dewars: no tight lids, vents never blocked, fill to no more than 80 percent.
  • Our 1,080 cubic foot example: 4 L released drops oxygen to about 19.1 percent with no exhaust.
