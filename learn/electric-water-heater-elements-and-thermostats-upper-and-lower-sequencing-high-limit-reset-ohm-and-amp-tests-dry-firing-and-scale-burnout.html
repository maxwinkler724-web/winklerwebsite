Electric Water Heater Elements and Thermostats: Upper and Lower Sequencing, High-Limit Reset, Ohm and Amp Tests, Dry-Firing and Scale Burnout

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A standard electric tank heater has two heating elements, two thermostats and one safety cutoff, and most no-hot-water calls come down to one of those parts. This lesson explains how the parts take turns, why the red reset button trips, how an ohm and amp check works with one worked example, and why a new element must never see power before the tank is full.

Safety first. Every cover on this appliance hides 240-volt wiring. Before any cover comes off: switch the heater's breaker off, then prove the wires are dead with a meter that you have first checked on a known live source. The OSHA electrical rule for qualified workers calls for testing the parts where people will be exposed, and it says control devices alone are not a means of de-energizing. Our lockout and verify-dead lesson shows the steps. Homeowners should never take readings on live wiring; if a step below needs power on, it belongs to a qualified technician.

How the two elements take turns

A college sequence-of-operation handout describes a dual-element heater as a "flip/flop" system: only one element heats at a time. The upper thermostat gets first call. On a cold start the upper element heats the top of the tank until the upper thermostat is satisfied, then power transfers to the lower thermostat, which runs the lower element until the bottom reaches its setting. When hot water is drawn, cold water enters low, so the lower thermostat usually calls first. After a big draw the upper thermostat takes priority again and then hands power down. A federal strategy guideline for water heater selection describes the same layout for a typical electric storage heater: two 4.5 kW elements interlocked so they cannot run at the same time.

Why design it that way? The top of the tank is what you draw from, so heating it first gives you usable hot water sooner, and running one 4,500 W element at a time keeps the demand on the circuit at one element's load. The same handout adds a point that matters for safety: on a 240-volt heater, there is always 120 volts to both elements, so an idle element is not a dead element. Another reason to verify dead.

The high-limit (ECO) and why it trips

The upper thermostat assembly includes the high-limit control, often called the ECO (energy cutoff). If the water gets too hot, it cuts power to both thermostats and both elements. A university building-maintenance text says most have a reset button that pops out at about 190°F, and it can be reset once the tank cools below that point. The sequence handout says the reset is the red button above the upper thermostat and that you hear a click when it resets. A city homeowner guide says the same thing: the switch must be pushed in manually.

A reset that holds is not the end of the job. The sequence handout warns that frequent tripping points to additional problems, and the building-maintenance text says a tripped button usually means the water got too hot, so the thermostats should be tested and replaced if needed. Our own list of usual suspects, from how the circuit works: a thermostat whose contacts stick closed so the element never shuts off, an element buried in scale so it overheats, a loose connection that heats a terminal, or a thermostat set far too high. To reset: breaker off, verify dead, remove the cover and insulation, press the button, replace everything, close the cover, then restore power. If it trips again, stop and test parts. For the plain no-hot-water checklist (breaker, reset, relief valve and so on) see No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks. For setting the temperature, see Water Heater Temperature Setting.

Testing an element with an ohmmeter

With the breaker off and the circuit proved dead, set the meter to ohms. The university text's test is to probe the two element terminals: a reading means current can flow, and no reading ("infinity") means the element is open and should be replaced. Two cautions from us. First, disconnect at least one wire from the element before measuring, or the thermostat and the other element will be in parallel and fool you. Second, compare the reading with what the element's own rating predicts, which is the next section.

Shorted to ground

An element can also leak to the tank. With the wires off the terminals, put one probe on an element terminal and the other on the bare metal of the tank or the element's mounting flange. A healthy element reads open (OL). Any real resistance means the element wall has failed and the heating wire is touching the tank metal. Our own explanation of the symptom: the tank can be energized, or the breaker or a ground-fault device trips right away. A meter that only reads ohms is a quick screen; a higher-voltage insulation tester gives a better view, as in Megohmmeter Insulation Testing. Replace a grounded element; do not return it to service.

Our own worked example: a 4,500 W, 240 V element

This is our own example, not a measured heater. Assumptions: the element label reads 4,500 W at 240 V; supply voltage is exactly 240 V; one element runs at a time; the tank holds 50 gallons; the water starts at 50°F and ends at 120°F; no heat is lost to the room; and we use 8.34 lb per gallon and 1 Btu per lb per °F for water, with 3.413 Btu per Wh (the factor used by the federal rule cited below).

  1. Expected resistance: R = V² ÷ W = 240 × 240 ÷ 4,500 = 57,600 ÷ 4,500 = 12.8 ohms.
  2. Expected current: I = W ÷ V = 4,500 ÷ 240 = 18.75 amps (check: 240 ÷ 12.8 = 18.75).
  3. Reading open (OL) across the terminals: the heating wire is broken. No current flows, the clamp meter would show 0 A, and the tank stays cold on that element.
  4. Reading far below 12.8 ohms across the terminals: a good element reads near 12.8. A much lower number means a damaged element, or a second path in parallel because a wire was not removed. Isolate it and measure again.
  5. Reading something (rather than open) from a terminal to the tank: shorted to ground, replace.
  6. Water mass: 50 gal × 8.34 = 417 lb. Heat needed: 417 × (120 − 50) = 417 × 70 = 29,190 Btu.
  7. Element output: 4,500 W × 3.413 = 15,359 Btu per hour (rounded).
  8. Time: 29,190 ÷ 15,359 = 1.90 hours, about 1 hour 54 minutes. Real tanks lose heat and the lower and upper elements trade turns, so the real time is longer.

One more result: power falls with the square of voltage. At 208 V (a common commercial supply) this same element makes 208² ÷ 12.8 = 3,380 W, so the same job would take 29,190 ÷ (3,380 × 3.413) = 2.53 hours. A clamp meter on one conductor of the supply to a working element should read near 18.75 A at 240 V (about 16.3 A at 208 V). That clamp reading needs the circuit live with wiring exposed, so it is a job for a qualified technician, not a homeowner step. Ohm readings are on dead wiring and are the safe screen.

Dry-firing a new element

An element is a resistance wire inside a metal sheath that depends on surrounding water to carry heat away. In air it has nowhere to send that heat and fails very quickly. The university text says never to apply power before the system is filled with water and the trapped air is removed. So after installing a new element and closing the drain: open a hot tap, let the tank fill until water runs steadily with no spitting, let it run a moment, and only then restore the breaker. The same rule applies after a tank has been drained for flushing. See Water Heater Anode Rods for other work that drains the tank.

Scale and sediment burnout

Hard water leaves mineral scale on the hot element surface. The building-maintenance troubleshooting table lists popping noises during heating as scale on the elements. A city guide for homeowners calls sediment a main cause of overheating and failure and suggests draining at least 5 gallons from the drain valve every six months in high-mineral areas. Our explanation of the burnout chain: scale insulates the sheath, the wire runs hotter, the sheath fails, and sometimes the element ends up buried in sediment low in the tank where the lower element sits. Flush on a schedule, and expect a replaced lower element to fail early if the tank is still full of deposit.

When to replace the heater instead

An element costs far less than a tank, so replace the element when the tank is sound. Replace the heater when the tank itself leaks (a city guide says a rusted-through tank cannot be repaired), when the tank is full of hardened deposit and elements keep failing, or when it is old. A city water utility gives the typical life of a tank-style heater as 10 years, and a federal rulemaking estimates average storage heater lifetime at about 15 years. Replacement also usually needs a permit, as the city guide notes. Look at the anode rod lesson if a tank is aging but dry.

Field card: Electric Water Heater Elements and Thermostats

Breaker off, verify dead, then test.

  • One element heats at a time: upper first, then power passes to the lower.
  • The red ECO reset trips on overheating; frequent trips mean a part is bad, not that the button is.
  • Our example: 4,500 W at 240 V is 12.8 ohms and 18.75 A.
  • Open reading: broken element. Any reading to the tank: grounded element.
  • Never power a new element until the tank is full and air is out.
  • 50 gal, 50 to 120 F, 4,500 W, no losses: about 1.9 hours (our example).
