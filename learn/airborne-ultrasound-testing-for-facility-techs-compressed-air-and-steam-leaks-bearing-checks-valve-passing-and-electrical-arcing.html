Airborne Ultrasound Testing for Facility Techs: Air and Steam Leaks, Bearings, Valves and Arcing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A leaking air fitting, a failed steam trap and a dry bearing all make sound far above what your ears can hear. An ultrasonic detector lets you hear it. This lesson covers how the tool works, what each probe is for, how to read the numbers, and where it fools you.

How the detector makes the sound audible

The U.S. Department of Energy's maintenance guide defines ultrasound as sound above 20 kHz and says non-contact detectors used for predictive maintenance work in roughly the 20 to 100 kHz range. The instrument translates that sound "via heterodyning" down into the audible range, and shows the strength on a meter. The detector mixes the incoming sound with an internal reference tone and keeps the difference, low enough to hear in headphones.

Our own illustration, not a spec for any tool: a 40 kHz hiss mixed with a 39 kHz reference leaves 40 − 39 = 1 kHz, an audible tone. Loudness and texture carry through, so a leak still sounds like rushing.

Airborne and contact probes

FEMP's guide says non-contact detectors pick up airborne ultrasound, and a contact-type probe picks up the same kind of sound traveling through metal. Ultrasound moves in straight lines and does not pass through solid surfaces, and it travels only a short distance from its source. That is why you scan close and aim the sensor. A parabolic dish accessory extends the normal scanning distance, and a gain control raises sensitivity.

  • Airborne: leaks, vacuum leaks, and arcing or corona in the air around electrical gear.
  • Contact: touch a rod or probe to a valve body, steam trap outlet or bearing housing and listen to what the structure carries.

Compressed air, gas and vacuum leaks in noisy rooms

The DOE compressed air tip sheet says the best way to find leaks is an ultrasonic acoustic detector, and that soapy water works but is slow and messy. The DOE sourcebook explains why it works in a loud plant: gas going from high-pressure laminar flow to low-pressure turbulence makes noise across audible and inaudible frequencies, and the detector is generally unaffected by background noise in the audible range because those signals are filtered out. Directional microphones help pinpoint the source. FEMP adds that a leak may be inaudible to a person yet still detectable, and that the method applies to oxygen and hydrogen as well as air.

Scale of the problem: the tip sheet says leaks can waste 20 to 30 percent of compressor output, and the sourcebook says a well-maintained system should lose under 10 percent. FEMP cites one day of inspection that found 64 leaks, about 295.8 cfm or 26 percent of capacity. For the system view, see Compressed Air Systems for Facility Techs.

Worked example: one 1/4 inch leak

This follows the DOE tip sheet method. Its table gives 100.9 cfm for a 1/4 in orifice at 100 psig, and says to multiply by 0.61 for a sharp-edged hole. Its example assumes about 18 kW per 100 cfm, 7,000 hours a year and $0.05 per kWh. Those are the DOE's assumptions, not measured values for your plant.

  1. Leak flow: 100.9 cfm × 0.61 = 61.5 cfm.
  2. Power: 61.5 cfm × 0.18 kW per cfm = 11.1 kW.
  3. Energy: 11.1 kW × 7,000 h = 77,551 kWh a year (using the unrounded 11.08 kW).
  4. Cost at the DOE's $0.05: 77,551 × 0.05 = about $3,878 a year. This matches the DOE example, which prices ten such leaks at $38,776.

Our own assumption for comparison: at $0.12 per kWh, the same leak costs 77,551 × 0.12 = about $9,306 a year. Use your plant's real rate, hours and measured kW per 100 cfm. Fix the biggest leaks first.

Steam traps and valve passing

The DOE steam tip sheet lists four trap test methods (temperature, sound, visual and electronic) and says systems left alone for 3 to 5 years may have 15 to 30 percent of traps failed, against under 5 percent with regular maintenance. Its example: a stuck-open 1/8 in trap at 150 psig loses 75.8 lb/hr, about $6,640 a year at $10 per 1,000 lb. FEMP says to test traps on the downstream side in contact mode, and describes what to expect: many traps fail open with a continuous rushing sound, a failed inverted bucket trap sinks and gives continuous flow noise, and failed float and thermostatic traps are normally cold and silent. Compare against a trap you know works. A passing valve is similar: a leaking seat makes turbulence you can hear at the downstream body. Confirm with temperature too. See Steam Trap Testing Methods and Steam Leaks and Failed-Open Traps.

Bearings: trend in dB and guide the grease

FEMP, citing NASA research, says a 12 to 50 times rise in amplitude of a monitored ultrasonic frequency (28 to 32 kHz) can give early warning of bearing deterioration, and that lack of lubrication is a detectable condition. In practice you record a baseline dB on the same spot with the same settings, then compare each visit. A trade-press article on bearing lubrication gives these steps relative to baseline:

  • Rise of 8 dB or less, same sound quality: no lubrication needed.
  • 8 dB rise with a uniform rushing sound: lack of lubricant.
  • 8 to 10 dB rise: add grease a little at a time, watching the meter, and stop when the sound drops and then jumps up again.
  • 12 dB rise, especially with crackling or popping: the bearing is entering incipient failure.

Our own example with assumed readings: baseline 12 dB, today 21 dB. The rise is 21 − 12 = 9 dB, in the 8 to 10 dB band, so grease slowly until the reading returns toward 12. If it stays high, recheck often. The failure line from the article would be 12 + 12 = 24 dB. Over-greasing can damage seals; see Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings and Vibration Analysis Basics. Alarm steps vary, so use your site's procedure.

Electrical corona, tracking and arcing

FEMP says arcing, tracking and corona ionize the air around equipment and make ultrasound, found around switchgear, transformers, insulators, junction boxes and breakers. The same guide warns that some scanning applications present a hazard because the range is short. Safe practice here is ours to state: scan from outside enclosures, through ports or windows made for the purpose, at a distance your site's electrical safety program allows, and by a qualified person. OSHA's 29 CFR 1910.333 requires live parts to be de-energized before an employee works on or near them unless that is infeasible, and says only qualified persons may work on energized parts. Never open energized gear to get a better signal. Report the finding and let a qualified crew handle it. See Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs and Infrared Thermography.

Limits and false readings

  • Short range: you must be near the source, and gain set too high swamps the real signal.
  • Straight-line sound: nearby surfaces can reflect or block it, so a loud spot may not be the source.
  • Other sources: a neighboring leak can sound like your target. Move closer and follow the signal rise.
  • Bearings: compare like with like. Same spot, same settings, same speed and load.
  • Training matters. FEMP says to train and practice in a controlled setting before diagnosing running systems.

Field card: Airborne Ultrasound Testing

Scan close, compare to baseline, never open live gear.

  • Detector mixes ultrasound down to an audible tone (heterodyning); DOE lists 20 to 100 kHz.
  • Airborne for leaks and arcing; contact probe for traps, valves and bearings.
  • Our own: one 1/4 in leak at 100 psig, about 61.5 cfm sharp-edged, about $3,878 a year at DOE's assumptions.
  • Steam traps: test downstream in contact mode; compare to a known good trap.
  • Bearings: trend dB from a baseline. 8 to 10 dB rise, grease slowly. 12 dB, incipient failure (trade-press steps).
  • Electrical: scan from outside enclosures, qualified person only.
