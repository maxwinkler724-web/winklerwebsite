Seawater-Cooled Marine Air Conditioners and Reverse-Cycle Heat on Boats

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A boat has no yard for an outdoor unit, but it sits in an endless supply of water. Most marine air conditioners use that water in place of outdoor air. This lesson explains how the seawater loop works, why the same unit heats poorly when the water is cold, and what to watch for with fouling, corrosion, winter freezing and shore power.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. Marine units differ in valves, pump placement and winter steps. This page is general education built from public government and university pages. Follow the installation manual for the unit and have marine electrical work done by a qualified person.

How a seawater loop replaces the outdoor fan coil

A land-based heat pump moves heat between indoor air and outdoor air. The federal energy pages describe the basic idea: a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, and a reversing valve changes the refrigerant flow so one machine can cool, heat or defrost an outdoor coil. A marine unit keeps that idea but swaps the outdoor air coil for a refrigerant-to-water heat exchanger. A small pump pulls water through a through-hull fitting and a strainer, pushes it through the coil, and sends it back overboard. In cooling the water carries away the heat that came out of the cabin plus the compressor's heat. In heating the refrigerant flow reverses and the seawater becomes the heat source.

Water is a good partner because it carries a lot of heat per gallon and its temperature is steadier than air. The same federal page notes that water-source and ground-source heat pumps exchange heat with a nearby water body or the ground and have low operating cost because those temperatures stay relatively steady. For the air-source side of the story and why cold hurts capacity, see Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Strainers, fouling and flow

Everything in the seawater path is a place for trouble. Weeds, sand, shells and small marine growth can plug the strainer, restrict the coil or starve the pump. Our own field rule of thumb: when a boat's air conditioner cools poorly or trips on high pressure, check seawater flow before touching refrigerant. Look for a clean strainer basket, a pump that primes, and a steady stream at the discharge. Low flow raises the water leaving the coil, which raises head pressure the same way a dirty outdoor coil does on a land unit. Fouling builds fastest in warm, still water, so a boat that sits at the dock all summer needs its strainer checked often. Follow the maker's cleaning steps for the coil, because acid-based cleaners are not safe for every metal.

Why reverse-cycle heat fades in cold water

A heat pump delivers heat by pulling it from a source. The federal page says air-source units were once avoided in long subfreezing spells and that newer cold-climate designs are a practical option in colder regions. The same logic applies to water. As the seawater gets colder, the refrigerant in the evaporator runs colder, suction pressure falls, and each pound of refrigerant carries less heat to the cabin. Capacity and efficiency drop together. Makers publish minimum water temperatures for heating, and many units add a protective control that stops the compressor if the coil gets too cold, because freezing inside the water coil can split it. Treat the published limit as a hard stop. In cold water a small electric or fuel-fired heater is often the practical supplement.

Condensate and bilge moisture

In cooling, the cabin coil removes moisture as well as heat, so every unit makes condensate. A boat has no yard drain, so the pan drain has to run to a sump, a gray-water path or overboard above the waterline, with a slope and no low spots, as on any land unit. See Marinas and Indoor Boat Storage HVAC for the building side of boat moisture. Our own caution: a blocked or kinked condensate hose can dump water into the bilge, and a damp bilge feeds corrosion, mildew and smells. Check the pan and hose when the strainer is serviced.

Corrosion and zinc anodes

Seawater is an electrolyte, so unlike metals that touch each other underwater form a galvanic cell. A university marine note on galvanic corrosion explains that zinc works as a sacrificial anode: it corrodes in place of the metals it protects. A state university marine extension page on zinc replacement adds two practical points: spent anodes should go to scrap metal recyclers, and zinc builds up in marina sediments, so do not let used anodes or filings end up in the water. How often to replace an anode depends on how much of it is gone, so ask the unit's maker where its anodes are and when to swap them. Salt air also attacks coils and fittings above the waterline; see Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air and Dielectric Unions and Galvanic Corrosion for the same ideas ashore.

Winterizing the raw-water lines

If a boat comes out of the water or the air chills below freezing, raw-water lines, the pump, strainer and coil can freeze and crack. The university extension page on winterizing vessels recommends propylene glycol antifreeze, usually pink, because it is less toxic than ethylene glycol, and says that applies to all systems other than closed freshwater cooling systems. Beyond that it refers readers elsewhere for engine steps, so the exact route for flushing the air conditioner loop comes from the unit's manual. Never put automotive antifreeze through a raw-water loop that discharges overboard.

Shore power and galvanic safety basics

A boat plugged into shore power ties its metal parts to the dock's grounding system, which can carry small currents that speed corrosion and, in a fault, put current into the water. A state university extension page on electric shock drowning says that leaking current from a boat or dock can electrify the water with no visible warning, that people should avoid swimming near docks with electrical service and boats on shore power, and that docks should have ground fault circuit interrupters on their feeding circuits, tested monthly. It also advises a yearly inspection of a boat's electrical system by a qualified electrician who knows marine codes. If someone is in trouble in the water, do not jump in: throw a float, cut shore power at the switch or unplug the cord, then call 911. Galvanic isolators and similar devices are electrician work, so we leave them to the marine electrical standards and to qualified people.

Worked example (our own)

These are our own assumptions, not a real product: a unit rated 16,000 Btu/h of cooling with an efficiency of 12 Btu/h per watt, and a seawater temperature rise of 10 degrees F across the coil.

  1. Electrical input: 16,000 / 12 = 1,333 W.
  2. Convert to heat: 1,333 W x 3.412 = 4,549 Btu/h.
  3. Heat rejected to the water = cooling + compressor heat = 16,000 + 4,549 = 20,549 Btu/h.
  4. With the fresh-water relation Btu/h = 500 x gpm x rise: gpm = 20,549 / (500 x 10) = 4.1 gpm.
  5. Seawater note: the 500 comes from fresh water. A university physical-oceanography text gives seawater density of 1020 to 1050 kg/m3 and specific heat of about 3850 J/(kg K), against roughly 1,000 and 4,180 for fresh water. Using about 1,025 and 3,850, the constant becomes 8.34 x 1.025 x 0.92 x 60 = about 470. Then gpm = 20,549 / (470 x 10) = 4.4 gpm.

So the pump must move about 4.4 gpm at the coil, with a clean strainer. Halve the flow and the rise doubles to about 20 degrees F, which shows why low flow quickly pushes head pressure up. The constants in step 5 are our own rounding of the cited ranges, so treat the answer as approximate.

Field card: Seawater-Cooled Marine Air Conditioners and Reverse-Cycle Heat on Boats

Check the water before the refrigerant.

  • Seawater pump and coil replace the outdoor fan coil.
  • Heat rejected = cooling + compressor input heat.
  • Seawater flow is about 470 x gpm x rise in Btu/h (our rounding), not 500.
  • Clean the strainer, check pump prime and discharge stream first.
  • Heating output falls as the water gets colder; respect the maker's minimum water temperature.
  • Condensate hose: slope, no kinks, no bilge dumping.
  • Anodes sacrifice themselves; recycle spent zinc.
  • Winterize raw-water lines with propylene glycol per the manual.
  • Stay out of the water near boats on shore power; cut power first, then call 911.
