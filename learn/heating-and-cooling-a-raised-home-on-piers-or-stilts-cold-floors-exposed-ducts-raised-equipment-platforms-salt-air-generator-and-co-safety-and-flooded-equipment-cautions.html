Heating and Cooling a Raised Home on Piers or Stilts

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many coastal and flood-prone homes are lifted on piers, posts or stilts so water can pass underneath. That helps in a flood, but it changes how the house heats and cools. The floor now touches outdoor air on its underside, and any duct or pipe hung below it is outdoors in effect. This lesson explains what to check and what to protect.

Check your local rules

Check with local officials. This page is general education. It does not cover code, permit or insurance rules, which vary by place and change. Ask your local building office before you move equipment, and ask your insurer about coverage questions.

Why the floor runs cold or hot

In a slab or basement home the floor sits against the ground or inside a buffer space. In a raised home, outdoor air moves freely under the whole floor, and wind pushes it through every gap. Our own summary of the usual complaints: a floor that feels cold in winter, rooms over the open space that lag the thermostat, and drafts at floor outlets and plumbing holes. The fix order is the same one covered in Cold Floors Over Crawl Spaces, Unheated Basements and Overhangs: seal air leaks first, then add insulation against the floor, and keep the insulation dry. In a raised home the wind exposure makes the sealing step matter even more. Our own advice is to use materials that tolerate getting damp, because wet insulation can hold water against the framing. An extension flood-recovery page notes that soaked fibrous or open-cell foam insulation should be thrown out and the cavity dried before new insulation goes in.

Ducts and pipes under the floor

A duct hung under a raised floor sees outdoor temperatures all winter. Our own rule of thumb is to treat it as outdoor equipment: seal the seams and joints, wrap it with insulation rated for the exposure, and protect that wrap from wind, rain, freezing and animals. Pests and birds tear at loose wrap, so a durable cover or enclosure is worth the cost of planning. Water pipes need the same thinking, since freezing wind can reach them from every side. The best answer, where a remodel allows it, is to bring ducts inside the heated space so they are not outdoors at all. The existing lesson on duct insulation R-values covers the insulation numbers; this lesson does not repeat them.

A federal emergency management agency advisory on protecting home utilities adds a flood point: ductwork beneath the floor system is vulnerable to flooding, flooded ducts should be removed and replaced, and ducts connected to the furnace or air handler are often the most at risk. That is one more reason to keep a duct run short, sealed and as high as the layout allows.

Worked example: what a bare under-floor duct costs

Our own example, with assumed round numbers. Assume an 8 in round supply duct, 60 ft long, hung under the floor. Assume 110 F supply air and 20 F outdoor air. Assume the only resistance to heat flow is the insulation, at R-8 in one case and R-4 in the other. The estimate is a simple one and ignores air films, wind and the air cooling along the run, so treat it as a rough size, not a measurement.

  1. Diameter: 8 in is 8 ÷ 12 = 0.667 ft.
  2. Surface area: pi × 0.667 ft × 60 ft = about 126 ft² (125.7 before rounding).
  3. Temperature difference: 110 F − 20 F = 90 F.
  4. Heat lost = area × difference ÷ R.
  5. At R-8: 125.7 × 90 ÷ 8 = about 1,414 Btu/h.
  6. At R-4: 125.7 × 90 ÷ 4 = about 2,828 Btu/h.

Halving the R-value doubles the loss, so the R-4 duct wastes about 1,414 Btu/h more than the R-8 duct in this example. That heat goes into the open air, not into the rooms you are paying to warm. With no insulation at all the loss would be larger still. Air leaking out of unsealed seams adds to it, which is why sealing comes first.

Outdoor units and equipment on platforms

The federal emergency management agency advisory says utility equipment should be raised as high as practicable, and that outdoor condensing units can sit on a platform attached to the side of the house. It says a platform that is cantilevered from the house is preferred over one on posts, because debris and scour can damage posts. It also says pads should be anchored to the floor or slab and equipment anchored to the pad, and that stacked bricks or blocks should not be used under appliances. Furnaces may be moved to upper floors or attics, and relocating equipment can mean new ductwork and moving electrical and refrigerant lines. Those are jobs for a licensed contractor, and your local office decides what is allowed. Our own reminder: the platform also has to leave room to service the unit, and the condensate and line set need a safe path.

Wind-driven rain and salt air

Coastal weather is hard on outdoor equipment. Our own note: wind pushes rain into louvers and under covers, and salt spray speeds up corrosion on coils, cabinets and fasteners. See Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air for the coil side. In practice, rinse and inspect more often than inland, check the maker's guidance for coastal installs, and keep vegetation and debris away from the unit.

Generators, carbon monoxide and alarms

Outages are common in storm country, and generators cause real harm when placed badly. A federal consumer product safety agency says portable generators belong outdoors, far from the home and well away from windows, doors and vents, and never in a garage, basement, shed or crawlspace. Its fact sheet and its information center both give a distance of at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away. The space under a raised home is open on the sides, but that is still not a safe spot: the agency lists the crawlspace among places never to run one, and exhaust can drift upward through floor gaps. A federal environmental agency's flood guidance also puts fuel-powered generators outdoors at least 20 feet from buildings and away from standing water. Our own addition: if the generator needs to be off the ground to stay dry, give it a stable stand and keep it as far from the living space as the outdoor layout and the cord allow.

The same consumer agency says to have working CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, with battery-operated units or battery backup. Its generator fact sheet says to test them monthly. If one sounds, go outside and call 911. Our own tip: test alarms before storm season. See also Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

After a flood, do not run wet equipment

Even a raised home can see water reach equipment. A federal environmental agency says to keep the HVAC system off until cleanup and drying are done, to cover vents during muck-out so debris and mold spores do not spread, and that if the ducts or equipment were affected they should not be used to dry the house until a professional says it is safe. It also says mold can grow on materials that stay wet more than 24 hours. Once the home is clean, it suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. The extension page adds not to use chlorine bleach in or near the air conditioning system. For the equipment steps, read Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded: What to Do Before Turning It On. For paperwork, see Documenting HVAC Storm and Flood Damage.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Raised Home

The underside of the floor is outdoors. Treat it that way.

  • Air seal the floor first, then insulate against it and keep it dry.
  • Seal and insulate under-floor ducts, or move them inside the heated space.
  • Protect duct wrap from wind, rain, freezing and pests.
  • Our own example: 8 in x 60 ft duct, 90 F difference: about 1,414 Btu/h lost at R-8, about 2,828 at R-4.
  • Raise outdoor equipment on an anchored platform, preferably attached to the house.
  • Generators run outdoors, 20 ft or more from the home, exhaust pointed away.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; test monthly.
  • After a flood, keep wet equipment off until it has been checked.
