Battery Power Station for a Gas Furnace or Boiler in an Outage

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas furnace or boiler heats with a gas flame, but it cannot run safely without a little electricity. A portable battery power station (sometimes called a solar generator) can supply that electricity with no exhaust, if you size it and connect it correctly. This lesson covers what actually needs power, how to read the numbers, and the hookup rules that keep you and utility workers safe.

What really needs power

The burner itself does not run on electricity. Gas flows through a gas valve and burns at the burner. That is why people are surprised when a furnace goes dead in an outage: the fuel is still there, but the parts that start, prove and move the heat are electric. (The gas-versus-electric split is our own plain-language summary of how a standard furnace works.)

  • Control board. It runs the start-up sequence and opens the gas valve only at the right moment. A furnace maker's installation manual describes the valve staying energized only if flame is sensed during the trial for ignition.
  • Igniter. A hot-surface igniter or spark device lights the gas. It draws power only briefly at each start.
  • Inducer (draft) motor. It pulls combustion gases out and proves venting before the gas valve opens. Newer high-efficiency furnaces cannot fire without it.
  • Blower or circulator pump. A furnace blower moves warm air through the ducts. A boiler uses a circulator pump (and often zone valves) to move hot water. A university extension fact sheet says hot-air systems rely on a fan and hot-water systems use circulator pumps and zone valves, and that most thermostats need electricity too.

An older furnace may give limited heat without a blower, but we do not recommend trying to bypass safety controls. See the related lessons below for igniter and flame sensor details.

Find the watts: rating plate or plug-in meter

The rating plate on the furnace lists voltage and amps. A furnace maker's installation manual points readers to the rating plate for the electrical characteristics, and also shows a 120-volt supply. To turn amps into watts for a rough estimate, multiply volts by amps (our own simplification; motors have a power factor below 1, so the real watts can be lower). For example, 3.5 A at 120 V is about 420 W.

The plate shows the maximum, not what the furnace draws every second. A plug-in watt meter on a cord-connected unit, or a clamp meter by a qualified person, shows the real running watts. Watch for a short surge when the blower motor starts. A university extension fact sheet advises adding up the wattage needed and allowing for the higher starting load of motors. Add the circulator or the inducer if they are separate loads. A federal energy document explains that nameplate amps apply at rated voltage and that a wattmeter gives direct readings, which is why a meter beats guessing.

Why polarity and ground matter

Many gas furnaces prove flame by passing a tiny current through the flame to the burner and back to ground. If hot and neutral are swapped, or ground is missing, the board may not sense flame and may shut the gas off again. A furnace maker's installation manual says proper polarity of the hot and neutral supply connections must be observed so the safety controls work, and that the furnace must be grounded under local codes or the national electrical code. Any power source you use must present correct hot, neutral and ground to the furnace. Our own advice: have an electrician check an outlet or inlet with a tester before the first outage, not during it.

Pure sine wave inverters

The inverter in a power station changes battery direct current to household alternating current. Motors and control boards are designed for a smooth sine wave. A pure sine wave inverter gives that clean waveform, while cheaper modified-sine units can make motors run hot and noisy and can upset electronics. This is our own guidance from general electrical practice; we did not find a public source to cite, so check the furnace maker's instructions and the power station's manual before you connect anything. Also confirm the station's continuous watt rating exceeds the furnace's running watts and its surge rating covers the blower start.

Safe hookup: never back-feed

Never plug a generator or power station into a wall outlet to power the house wiring. A federal product-safety alert calls this back-feeding extremely dangerous: it can electrocute utility workers and neighbors and bypasses household circuit protection. A national laboratory's building-science page says to have a licensed electrical contractor install a transfer switch that prevents back-feeding, and describes manual transfer switches, panel interlocks and socket-mounted switches. A state consumer guide notes that a battery-powered unit needs a transfer switch to run hard-wired appliances.

Two practical options, both with an electrician:

  • Inlet and transfer switch (or interlock). The electrician wires the furnace circuit so it can be fed from the station through a proper inlet while the utility feed is isolated.
  • Cord-and-plug furnace connection, where allowed. Some jurisdictions and furnace instructions allow a plug-in connection to a dedicated receptacle. Ask your local inspector and read the furnace instructions first. We do not give legal advice here.

Boilers are usually hard-wired too, so the same transfer approach applies. For a generator-based version of this topic, see the related lesson on sizing and safe hookup.

No fuel generators indoors; carbon monoxide alarms

A battery power station has no exhaust, which is its big advantage. A state consumer guide calls the battery-powered type the only generator that may be used safely indoors. A gasoline or propane generator is different: the federal alert says never to run one in a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, even with doors and windows open, and to keep it outside and well away from doors, windows and vents with the exhaust pointed away. A national laboratory page gives a distance of at least 20 feet from the home. The same federal alert recommends battery-operated or plug-in carbon monoxide (CO) alarms with battery backup, tested monthly. A gas furnace can also fail to vent during an outage, so working CO alarms matter even when you use a battery station.

Battery care in the cold

Lithium-ion batteries lose performance when cold. A national laboratory in a paper on lithium-ion cells reported that one tested cell delivered about 77 percent of its 30 °C capacity at -15 °C (about 86 °F and 5 °F; the Fahrenheit values are our own conversion). A national laboratory test of electric-vehicle charging found that at 32 °F the battery had a state of charge 36 percent lower after 30 minutes than at 77 °F, because the battery management system limits charging in cold. Those are vehicle and single-cell results, not power-station results. Our own practical tips: keep the station in a heated room if you can, do not charge a very cold station, and treat the rated watt-hours as optimistic on a cold night. Follow the maker's temperature limits.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions (ours, not measured on your furnace): a 1,000 watt-hour battery station; an inverter about 85 percent efficient; a furnace drawing 400 W while running; the furnace running about half of each hour.

  1. Usable energy: 1,000 Wh × 0.85 = 850 Wh.
  2. Average load: 400 W × 0.5 = 200 W.
  3. Runtime: 850 Wh ÷ 200 W = 4.25 hours.

For scale, 400 W at 120 V is about 3.3 A (400 ÷ 120, assuming a power factor of 1). A cold battery or a longer run time (a very cold night) shortens the answer, so treat 4.25 hours as a best case. A power station only covers a furnace or boiler; it does not heat the house by itself. A state consumer guide notes that battery-powered units run for only a few hours and take hours to recharge.

Field card: Battery Power Station for a Gas Furnace or Boiler

The flame uses gas; the controls and fans use power.

  • Needs power: control board, igniter, inducer, blower or circulator pump, thermostat.
  • Read the rating plate, then confirm with a plug-in meter; allow for motor start surge.
  • Runtime = watt-hours × inverter efficiency ÷ average watts.
  • Use a pure sine wave inverter; check correct hot, neutral and ground.
  • Never back-feed through an outlet. Use an electrician-installed inlet and transfer switch.
  • Fuel generators stay outdoors, far from openings. Keep CO alarms working.
  • Cold cuts battery capacity and slows charging.
