Open-Loop Groundwater Heat Pumps: Supply and Return Wells, Water Quality and Permits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A closed ground loop circulates the same fluid forever. An open loop does the opposite: it pulls fresh groundwater out of a well, runs it through the heat pump once, and puts it back somewhere. That drives pump energy, water chemistry and permits.

Start with Geothermal Ground-Source Heat Pump Basics and Heat Pump Basics; closed-loop purging is in Geothermal Ground Loop Flushing and Purging.

How the system works

The U.S. Department of Energy describes the open-loop option as a system that uses well or surface-body water as the heat exchange fluid, flowing directly through the heat pump. After use, the water returns to the ground through the well, a recharge well, or surface discharge. DOE adds that this option is practical only with an adequate supply of relatively clean water and has to comply with local rules on groundwater discharge.

The two-well version has a supply well with a submersible pump and a separate return well. In heating the water leaves colder than it arrived; in cooling, warmer. A Massachusetts state guideline says return flow should go back to the same aquifer when feasible and should not be altered from the withdrawn water.

Standing column wells

A standing column well uses a single deep bore for both jobs. Water is drawn from the bottom, run through the heat pump, and returned to the top of the same water column. A trade article on standing column design notes that engineers divert a portion of the water, for example 10 percent, before it returns to the well field. This "bleed" is meant to pull in fresh groundwater and keep the bore from drifting too far from ground temperature. It says to start the bleed when bore water has risen 30 to 35 degrees F from the season's start, targets depths of 800 to 1,500 feet, and says to avoid high iron, manganese and dissolved solids and to pick the exchanger material, typically stainless steel, from a water assay.

The Massachusetts guideline limits bleed to less than half of return flow on an annual average.

How much water: gallons per minute per ton

A paper on commercial open-loop design in the Geo-Heat Center bulletin gives a groundwater-side range of 1 to 2.5 gpm per ton, and 2.5 to 3.0 gpm per ton on the building loop when a separate heat exchanger is used. Its design example uses 1.75. We found no residential-specific figure. The unit's installation manual sets the required flow.

Flow matters twice. Too little water and the exchanger runs near freezing in heating, with a shutdown risk. Too much and the well pump burns energy for little gain.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions, not measurements: a 4-ton unit, 1.5 gpm per ton (inside the cited 1 to 2.5 range), water entering at 50 F and leaving at 43 F in heating. The 500 in the formula is our own arithmetic: about 8.33 lb per gallon times 60 minutes, rounded.

  1. Flow: 4 tons x 1.5 gpm per ton = 6 gpm.
  2. Temperature drop: 50 F - 43 F = 7 F.
  3. Heat taken from the water: 500 x 6 gpm x 7 F = 21,000 Btu/h.
  4. Water pumped per day if it ran 24 hours: 6 gpm x 60 min x 24 h = 8,640 gallons.

Two notes. First, 21,000 Btu/h is only the heat from the water; the compressor's work adds to the heat delivered to the house, so the two numbers are not the same thing. Second, a 7 F drop at 6 gpm extracts less than a full 4 tons (48,000 Btu/h) of ground-side heat would need. A real unit's manual will state the flow and temperature drop it was rated at. The point of the example is the shape of the math: every extra degree of drop or extra gpm moves heat in direct proportion, and the daily volume is what a return well, or a permit, has to handle. As a comparison point, the Massachusetts guideline asks applicants for a non-consumptive use determination above 100,000 gallons per day, far above our 8,640.

Entering water temperature

Well water stays near the ground's year-round temperature, which makes it a stable source. Our 50 F is an assumption, not a measurement.

Water quality: the real risk

The Geo-Heat Center paper says direct-use systems, where well water goes straight through the refrigerant-to-water exchanger, are very susceptible to water quality problems, mainly scaling. It recommends direct use only for very small applications with excellent, well-tested water. Oxygen converts dissolved ferrous iron to the less soluble ferric form, which fouls the exchanger, and open tanks add oxygen and release carbon dioxide, which raises pH and makes calcium carbonate scale more likely. The British Geological Survey review adds that cold reinjection can precipitate carbonates and clog the receiving aquifer.

  • Iron and manganese. Massachusetts lists 0.3 mg/L for iron and 0.05 mg/L for manganese among its raw-water test parameters, along with pH 6.5 to 8.5 and chloride 250 mg/L.
  • Hardness and scale. Hard water scales, and the scale insulates the exchanger. This is the same problem seen in Condenser Water Treatment.
  • Iron bacteria. The Geo-Heat Center paper calls iron bacteria the one common problem that should prompt an alternate design, even with a plate exchanger. Sand is the other concern; a strainer is the preferred separator.

Heat exchanger choices

The Geo-Heat Center paper recommends isolating the groundwater with a plate-and-frame exchanger, which largely removes the water-quality limits on the heat pump itself. Standard plates are 304 or 316 stainless steel; it recommends 316 above 150 ppm chloride and titanium above 375 ppm. A heat pump maker's technical bulletin points to the installation manual for limits, says poor water calls for a ground loop or secondary exchanger, and says a failed water sample can void part of the warranty.

Pump energy

The Geo-Heat Center paper splits well pump head into lift (static level plus drawdown), surface losses and injection head, and says lift dominates. The standing-column article puts the pump string at roughly 25 percent of project cost.

Disposal: surface or return well

The Geo-Heat Center paper says surface discharge costs less but the receiving body must accept the water long term; injection costs more but recycles the water. It says injection well failures are usually design problems, mainly sand carryover.

Permits and rules

Federal. The EPA's Class V study of geothermal direct heat return flow wells says these injection wells are authorized by rule under 40 CFR 144.26 after inventory submission, and 144.12(a) bars endangering underground sources of drinking water. That study covers hotter fluids, so use it for the legal framework only. A Massachusetts guideline classes open-loop return wells as Class V and requires registration approval before construction. A Salt Lake County guideline strongly discourages open-loop systems because of the risk of injecting contaminants into drinking-water aquifers.

New Jersey. The state's well rules are N.J.A.C. 7:9D. A DEP enforcement advisory states that no person shall construct, repair or decommission a well without a valid New Jersey well driller's license, that a well permit is required before drilling, and that a well is a hole larger than four inches in diameter or deeper than 10 feet. It names the Bureau of Water Allocation and Well Permitting for questions. The rule section we read, N.J.A.C. 7:9D-2.5, covers closed-loop geothermal wells only. We found no state page setting out open-loop supply and return well requirements, so ask the bureau before you plan.

Field card: Open-Loop Groundwater Heat Pumps

Water goes through once. Quality and permits decide the job.

  • Cited groundwater flow range: 1 to 2.5 gpm per ton; use the unit manual.
  • Heat from water = 500 x gpm x drop. Our example: 500 x 6 x 7 = 21,000 Btu/h.
  • Daily water = gpm x 60 x 24 = 8,640 gallons in our example.
  • Test iron, pH, sand and iron bacteria first.
  • Check NJ DEP well permitting before drilling.
