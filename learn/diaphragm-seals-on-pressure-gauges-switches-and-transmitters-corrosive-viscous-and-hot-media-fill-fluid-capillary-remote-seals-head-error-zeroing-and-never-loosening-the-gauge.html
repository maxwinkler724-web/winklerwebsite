Diaphragm Seals on Gauges, Switches and Transmitters

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pressure gauge wants clean, moderate fluid. Plant piping does not always oblige. A diaphragm seal, also called a chemical seal, puts a thin flexible barrier between the process and the instrument, so the instrument sees the pressure without seeing the fluid. This lesson covers why seals are used, how they work, what the fill fluid does to your reading, and the one rule every tech should memorize: do not take the gauge off the seal.

Why a seal is used

A plain gauge has a metal sensing element with process fluid inside it. That is fine for clean water, but it goes wrong in several ways when the fluid is not clean water:

  • Corrosive fluids eat the element or the socket. A seal can be built of a more resistant metal, or lined, so only the seal face meets the fluid.
  • Viscous or slurry fluids pack into small passages, set up, and plug the gauge port. The pressure then reads stale or not at all. A seal with a wide, flush face has no small port to plug.
  • Freezing fluids that sit still in a gauge port can freeze and split the element. A seal with a freeze-safe fill keeps the water out of the instrument.
  • Sanitary fluids need smooth faces with no dead pockets where product can sit. A flush seal gives that.
  • Hot media can cook a gauge. A seal on a capillary moves the instrument away from the heat.

The reasons above are our own plain-language grouping of common cases. Which seal material and fill fluid suit a given process is the instrument maker's call, so always follow the maker's data for your fluid and temperature.

How the seal passes pressure

The seal is a small chamber closed by a flexible metal diaphragm on the process side. The other side of the diaphragm, the chamber, tube and instrument, is completely filled with a liquid, called the fill fluid, with no air in it. When process pressure pushes on the diaphragm, the diaphragm moves a tiny amount and pushes the fill fluid, and the fill fluid carries that pressure to the instrument. Liquids barely compress, so the pressure shows up at the gauge element almost unchanged. The idea is the same as a gauge with an oil-filled line: a university chapter on a fluid-filled pressure probe notes that an oil-filled line carries pressure to the transducer, that no air bubbles may remain after filling, and that the probe and oil expand and contract with temperature. Another university paper describes a liquid-filled membrane gauge, where a flexible membrane and a liquid fill carry the pressure to the measuring device.

Seals fit three kinds of instrument: gauges (a pointer), pressure switches (a contact that trips at a set point) and transmitters (an electrical signal to a controller). The seal does not care which one is on top.

Direct mount and capillary remote seals

A direct-mount seal bolts to the process connection with the instrument on its back. It is compact and has a very small fill volume. A remote seal connects to the instrument through a thin armored capillary tube, also full of fill fluid. Remote seals put the instrument where a tech can read it, away from heat, vibration or a hard-to-reach vessel. The capillary must be supported, kept from kinks and crushing, and never cut or coiled up tight by hand. A damaged capillary loses fill fluid and the instrument goes dead.

Fill fluid: temperature and head

The fill fluid is a liquid with its own density and its own expansion. Two things follow, and both produce error.

  1. Head from the capillary. A tall capillary holds a tall column of fill fluid. Its weight adds to, or subtracts from, the pressure at the instrument depending on where the seal sits. The relation is the usual hydrostatic one: a federal energy department instrumentation handbook gives hydrostatic head pressure as density times height, which for a liquid of specific gravity SG is 0.433 x SG x height in feet, in psi.
  2. Temperature. The fill expands when hot and shrinks when cold, and its density changes with it. The same handbook shows the same behavior for liquid in a reference leg, where a change in liquid density changes the indicated reading unless it is compensated. For a seal this appears as a zero shift that moves as the capillary warms and cools. A long capillary near a hot pipe or in sunlight has more of this than a short one in a conditioned room.

The handbook example concerns reference legs rather than seals, so we use it only for the hydrostatic principle. The sizes of the effects for a particular seal come from the maker's data sheet.

Worked example (our own): head error from a capillary seal

This example uses our own assumed numbers and is not from a data sheet.

Assumptions: a remote seal is mounted 12 ft below the gauge. The capillary and seal are full of fill fluid of specific gravity 0.96. The gauge is at atmospheric pressure and the process is vented and empty, so the true process gauge pressure at the diaphragm is 0 psi.

Step 1. Head of the fill column: psi = 0.433 x SG x feet = 0.433 x 0.96 x 12.

Step 2. 0.433 x 0.96 = 0.4157 psi per foot.

Step 3. 0.4157 x 12 = 4.99 psi, about 5.0 psi.

What it means: the gauge sits above the seal, so the fill column weighs down on the seal's diaphragm and the gauge element feels about 5.0 psi more than the process alone. With nothing in the line, our assumed gauge would read about +5.0 psi, high by that amount. If the seal were above the gauge, the same column would pull the reading low by about 5.0 psi.

How it is zeroed out: with the process isolated, relieved and open to atmosphere (see the safety note below), we note the reading and adjust the zero. On a transmitter that is a zero trim in the device or an offset in the controller, set so the output reads 0 psi. On a gauge with a pointer adjustment it is the pointer zero. Once zeroed, the 5.0 psi fixed head is removed and the instrument reads the process pressure. The head is a constant, so a single zero setting takes it out as long as the fill and the elevation do not change.

Temperature check: suppose, as another assumption, the fill warms and its specific gravity drops from 0.96 to 0.94. Then 0.433 x 0.94 x 12 = 4.88 psi, so the head shrinks by 4.99 - 4.88 = 0.11 psi. That is the part you cannot zero away, because it moves with temperature. On a gauge with a 0 to 100 psi scale, 0.11 psi is a bit more than a tenth of one percent of span, which our example treats as small. On a low-range instrument it matters far more, which is why remote seals are a poor match for low pressures.

Never loosen the gauge from the seal

Our rule: leave the instrument and seal assembled. The seal, capillary and gauge are filled and sealed as one unit. If the gauge is unscrewed from the seal, or the capillary is disconnected, fill fluid can run out or air can get in. Without a full fill the reading is wrong or lost, and the unit usually has to go back to be refilled. If the assembly must come off the process, isolate and relieve the line and remove the whole assembly at the seal's process connection. Follow the maker's instructions for handling.

Before opening any process connection, isolate the line and relieve stored pressure. The federal lockout rule for general industry (1910.147) requires stored or residual energy, which includes hydraulic energy, to be relieved, disconnected, restrained or otherwise made safe, and requires isolation to be verified before servicing starts. A pressurized line is stored energy. Apply your site procedure and verify zero pressure before you loosen anything.

Flushing connections

Some seals have a second port on the process side of the diaphragm called a flushing connection. It lets you wash out slurry or product from the seal's chamber without disassembly. Flush only with the line isolated and relieved and with a flush fluid compatible with the process and the seal, per the maker. A flushing ring at the seal face is a common way to keep a sticky fluid from building up there.

When you do not need a seal

In clean, moderate-temperature water service, such as ordinary hot or chilled water loops, a seal adds cost, a fill that can leak or drift, and a head error to zero out. A normal gauge with a valve is the better fit there. See the related lessons for gauge protection, boiler gauges and checking meters. Use a seal when the fluid would damage, plug or freeze the instrument, or when a sanitary or hot service calls for it.

Field card: Diaphragm seals

Pressure goes through the diaphragm, the fluid stays out.

  • Use a seal for corrosive, viscous, slurry, freezing, sanitary or hot fluids.
  • Flexible diaphragm plus a full, air-free fill fluid carries pressure to the gauge, switch or transmitter.
  • Head from the capillary: psi = 0.433 x SG x feet. Our example: 12 ft at SG 0.96 is about 5.0 psi.
  • Zero the fixed head with the process vented. Temperature shifts in the fill cannot be zeroed once and forgotten.
  • Never loosen the gauge from the seal: fill fluid will be lost.
  • Isolate, relieve and verify zero before opening any process connection.
  • Clean water service usually needs no seal.
