Solid-State Relays in HVAC Controls: Leakage, Heat and Failure

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Electric heat strips, humidifier heaters and other resistive loads are often switched by a solid-state relay, or SSR, instead of a clicking contactor. An SSR has no moving contacts, which makes it quiet and fast, but it behaves differently on the bench. It leaks a little when it is "off," it makes heat when it is "on," and when it dies it usually dies closed. This lesson explains those habits so a meter reading does not fool you.

How an SSR switches with no moving parts

A federal inspection guide on electronic relays says solid-state relays are "completely different" from electromechanical ones because they have no moving parts. A university physics-department handout describes the typical build: a small input, often a few volts of control signal, lights an LED inside the relay. A light-sensitive part on the output side then turns on a semiconductor switch, so the control side and the load side are electrically isolated. A military relay reference adds that SSRs have essentially no contact bounce or chatter and generally suit long-life uses.

For alternating-current loads the output switch is usually a triac, which the handout describes as two thyristors connected back to back. It turns off within about a half cycle after the control signal drops. For direct-current loads the output is a transistor-type part, and the handout notes that you should not use a triac-type output on a DC load because it cannot turn itself off. That is the whole AC versus DC story: most SSRs are built for one or the other, so check the label before wiring. A mechanical relay can switch either; an SSR usually cannot.

Zero-cross versus random turn-on

This part is our own plain-language summary of common SSR behavior, so confirm it against the data sheet for the part in your hands. A zero-cross type waits until the AC waveform passes near zero volts before it turns on, which gives a gentle start and less electrical noise. That suits resistive heat. A random turn-on type switches at any point in the waveform the moment the input arrives, which is useful when you want to chop the waveform for fine control or switch some inductive loads. If a heater or humidifier is staged on and off by a controller, a zero-cross part is the usual match; the part's label or data sheet says which one you have.

Leakage: why an "off" load can read voltage

An SSR output is a semiconductor, not an air gap. The federal inspection guide lists excessive leakage current as one sign of a failing relay, and every SSR leaks a small amount even when healthy. The data sheet gives the off-state limit. With the relay off, that small current can flow through a high-impedance meter and make the meter show a voltage near line voltage across the open output, or from the load to ground. A mechanical contactor opened by hand does not do this.

Heat from the on-state drop

A closed SSR still drops a little voltage across its output. The handout notes that the heat is found by multiplying current by that on-state voltage for an AC SSR, and advises a quick calculation to see whether a heat sink is needed. The military reference makes the matching point for relays in general: lower the allowed continuous current from the rating, with larger reductions for harsh environments. The federal inspection guide says the maximum junction temperature limits how much power the part can shed. Putting that together, the data sheet's current rating assumes a heat sink and a cool enclosure. In a hot control panel, or with the relay mounted flat against a thin panel, we derate: run it below its rated amps. We do not give a single derating percentage here because it depends on the part.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all ours and not from a data sheet: a 240 V resistive heater strip drawing 20 A, switched by an AC SSR with a 1.5 V on-state drop and a leakage of 3 mA when off.

  1. Heat in the SSR: 1.5 V × 20 A = 30 W.
  2. Why it matters: 30 W is the output of a small soldering iron, released inside a small block that has little surface area. Without a heat sink the part climbs toward its junction limit, so it is mounted on a heat sink and the current is derated when the panel is warm.
  3. Heater resistance: 240 V ÷ 20 A = 12 Ω.
  4. Leakage through the heater with the SSR off: 3 mA = 0.003 A, so voltage across the heater = 0.003 × 12 = 0.036 V, and power = 0.036 × 0.003 = about 0.0001 W. The heater is not heating.
  5. But a high-impedance meter placed across the open SSR output draws almost no current, so the leakage path is not loaded down and the meter can show close to line voltage. That reading says "leakage," not "the load is on."

The common failure: shorted on

The federal inspection guide describes semiconductor relay failures as permanent shorts, an inability to block voltage, or excessive leakage, and says there is usually no visible sign unless the part is heat-discolored. In the field that means a shorted SSR leaves the heater or humidifier energized even though the controller has dropped its signal. Because the control circuit cannot turn the load off, any safety that depends on it is defeated. That is why heater circuits keep a separate disconnect, and why an overheated heater should never be assumed safe just because thermostat is satisfied.

Checking one safely

The federal workplace safety agency's lockout and tagout rule requires an authorized employee to verify that isolation and deenergization have been accomplished before servicing. A federal workplace safety agency interpretation letter adds that a qualified person must use test equipment to check circuit parts, including for induced voltage or backfeed, and that an LED-type indicator is not test equipment. The agency's lockout eTool also says control devices such as push buttons and selector switches are not energy-isolating devices. For an SSR, that means an "off" controller or thermostat is never your lockout: open and lock the disconnect, test for zero, then work.

  1. Input signal. With the circuit set up by a qualified person, confirm the control voltage reaches the SSR input when the controller calls, and drops to zero when it does not.
  2. Output in both states. With the call on, line voltage should drop to nearly zero across the output (the on-state drop is only about a volt or two in the example) and the load should show current. With the call off, a reading near line voltage across the output with no current in the load points to leakage, not a fault. Current flowing with no call points to a shorted part.
  3. Temperature. After a run, check that the relay and heat sink are not far hotter than nearby parts, using a non-contact thermometer. Loose mounting, missing thermal paste or a blocked panel vent show up here.
  4. Replace a suspect SSR with the same type, output (AC or DC), rating and turn-on style.

Field card: Solid-State Relays in HVAC Controls

No contacts means no click, no air gap and no free heat.

  • An SSR switches with a semiconductor; the control side is optically isolated.
  • AC loads use a triac type, DC loads a DC type. Do not mix them.
  • Zero-cross suits resistive heat; random turn-on switches at once.
  • Off-state leakage can make a meter read voltage on a dead load.
  • Heat = on-state drop × current. Our example: 1.5 V × 20 A = 30 W.
  • Use a heat sink, derate in warm panels.
  • Common failure: shorted, load stays on.
  • Lock out and test for zero before touching the load.
