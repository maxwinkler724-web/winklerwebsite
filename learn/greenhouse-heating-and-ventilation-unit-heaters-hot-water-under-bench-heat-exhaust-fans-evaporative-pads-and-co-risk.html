Greenhouse Heating and Ventilation: Unit Heaters, Under-Bench Heat, Fans and Pads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A greenhouse is a big sheet of plastic over a lot of living plants. The heat load is high, the humidity is high, and the plants are far more sensitive to combustion gases than people are. This lesson walks through sizing the heat, choosing between gas unit heaters and hot water, and sizing winter air movement and summer cooling. For a sealed indoor room with lights, see Indoor Grow Room and Vertical Farm HVAC. This page is about glazed houses.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Gas piping, venting, and combustion air are governed by the heater maker's instructions and the codes your inspector enforces. This page is general education built from university extension pages, not a design. Confirm everything with the manufacturer and the authority having jurisdiction.

Step 1: Estimate the heat loss

Extension specialists use the same basic equation you know from any building: Q = U x A x ΔT. Purdue's controlled-environment agriculture pages define Q as the heat requirement in Btu/hr, U as the Btu/hr lost per square foot per degree F, A as the exposed surface area, and ΔT as the inside-to-outside temperature difference. Purdue lists U = 0.70 for double polyethylene, 0.55 for double polycarbonate, and about 1.2 for single polyethylene. Texas A&M's page agrees on 0.70 for double-layer poly (and 1.15 for single). Note that you use the exposed covering area, not the floor area.

Air leakage adds to the load. Texas A&M puts new double-poly construction at 0.50 to 1.0 air changes per hour. The University of Florida takes a simpler route: its heat transmission factors already include normal infiltration and wind, and it says to add 10 percent if the house is windy and has many leaks. Florida also says to plan on temperatures slightly above the local 15 to 25 year lows, and to size heaters on output, not input. If a central boiler feeds the system, Florida adds at least 25 percent to cover distribution losses.

Worked example: a 30 x 96 ft house

Everything below is our own arithmetic. The inputs marked "assumption" are ours for the sake of the example and are not from a source.

  • Assumption, shape: gable house 30 ft wide and 96 ft long, 6 ft sidewalls, 6 ft of roof rise, poly on the roof, sidewalls and both end walls, no knee wall.
  • Sidewalls: 2 x 96 x 6 = 1,152 sq ft.
  • End walls: each is a 30 x 6 rectangle (180 sq ft) plus a triangle of 0.5 x 30 x 6 = 90 sq ft, so 270 each and 540 for both.
  • Roof: each slope is the square root of (15 squared + 6 squared) = 16.16 ft, so 2 x 96 x 16.16 = 3,102 sq ft.
  • Total exposed area A: 1,152 + 540 + 3,102 = 4,794 sq ft.
  • Assumption, design temperatures: 65 °F inside and 10 °F outside for a New Jersey winter night, so ΔT = 55 °F. (We chose 10 °F; check your own local design data.)
  • U: 0.70 for double poly (Purdue, Texas A&M).
  • Conduction loss: 0.70 x 4,794 x 55 = 184,569 Btu/hr.
  • Infiltration allowance: 10 percent for a leaky, windy site, the Florida rule. 184,569 x 1.10 = 203,026 Btu/hr.
  • Result: about 203,000 Btu/hr of heater output.
  • Cross-check with Florida's factors: double poly 0.75 to 1.0 gives 4,794 x 55 x 0.75 = 197,753 and 4,794 x 55 x 1.0 = 263,670 Btu/hr. Our figure sits inside that range.

Two heaters of about 100,000 to 105,000 Btu/hr output each would cover this. Two smaller units can stage (one in mild weather, both in a cold snap) and give a backup if one fails.

Gas unit heaters: why they must be vented with outside air

Gas unit heaters are the common choice. Texas A&M notes that gas is the most readily available and economical fuel there and says all combustion forms must be vented. Venting matters more in a greenhouse than in a shop because plants respond to very small doses of combustion products:

  • Ethylene. UMass Extension says levels as low as 0.01 ppm can cause malformed leaves and flowers, stunting, and bud drop. Virginia Tech's heater checklist gives about 0.01 ppm over several days or 1 ppm for a few hours as damaging, and notes ethylene is colorless and odorless. Its listed causes include a cracked heat exchanger, an unvented heater, gas leaks, and a bad flame adjustment.
  • Sulfur dioxide and other gases. UMass says as little as one-half ppm of SO2 can injure some plants. Natural gas flue gas also contains nitrogen dioxide and carbon monoxide.
  • Moisture. UMass estimates an unvented gas heater could add about 16 gallons of water vapor to a 30 x 100 ft double-poly house over a 12 hour cold night. That is a disease risk, because condensate drips on the crop.
  • Carbon monoxide for people. Our own plain statement: CO is also a hazard for anyone working in a closed house. The extension pages we read treat CO mainly as a plant-air pollutant and give no CO limits, so use a CO alarm and the maker's rules.

Alabama Extension's guidance on preventing ethylene damage says to vent exhaust outdoors through a stack that rises at least 2 feet above the top of the greenhouse and ends in at least 3 feet of vertical pipe with a cap, and not to use unvented gas burners for greenhouse vegetables. It also says tight joints and a regular check for blockages. Note how this differs from a typical shop: the stack must clear the peak, because a plastic house traps what spills.

The same Alabama page says to supply about 1 square inch of outside-air opening per 2,500 Btu of heater capacity, and NC State's extension article agrees with that figure (1 square inch per 2,500 Btu output, run by a pipe or flexible hose from outside to the burner, with a screened end). UMass cites a more generous number: at least 1 square inch per 1,000 Btu/hr of input. Because the sources differ, follow the heater maker's number and the code, and treat these as a floor for discussion. For our example heater of 105,000 Btu/hr, the 2,500 Btu rule gives 105,000 / 2,500 = 42 square inches, and the 1,000 Btu rule gives 105 square inches. NC State also warns that very tight houses can let ethylene build, and that moving to unvented heaters to save fuel led to crop losses on California farms it describes. For the general rules on combustion air in a mechanical space, see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters.

Maintenance is the other half. Virginia Tech's checklist says to inspect at least once a year plus a start-up before the growing season: check the vent for obstructions and loose joints, inspect the heat exchanger for cracks and overheating discoloration, clean the burner, test gas joints with a soap solution, and place the thermostat at crop level away from outside walls, drafts and sun. Alabama Extension adds a flame check: a clear blue flame indicates good combustion, and yellow or orange suggests a problem. NC State suggests cleaning the heater and orifice at least twice a year and growing young tomato plants as ethylene indicators. For other gas unit heaters in shops, see Garage and Workshop Heaters.

Hot water, under-bench and floor heat

A boiler can feed hot water to unit heaters, perimeter pipe, or loops under or on the benches. The University of Florida lists these pipe locations: around the perimeter, under or on benches, overhead in the trusses, or under concrete floors. Texas A&M describes under-pot heating, where water heated in a modified water heater is pumped through tubing mounted to the bench, which warms the growing media and lets growers run lower air temperatures. Neither source gave a heat output per foot of tube, so check that with the tubing maker. Hot water also keeps combustion outside the growing space entirely, which removes the ethylene concern for the plants (the boiler still needs its own venting and air). See Steam and Hot Water Unit Heaters for coil capacity and freeze concerns that carry over, and use glycol where freeze-up is possible.

Horizontal airflow (HAF) fans

UMass Extension recommends total HAF capacity of about 2 cfm per square foot of floor area, running 24 hours a day except when exhaust fans run. Put the first fan 10 to 15 feet from one end wall, space the rest 40 to 50 feet apart, aim each at the opposite end wall, and mount them about 7 to 8 feet up for bench crops. UMass says a properly installed system holds end-to-end night temperature differences to 2 °F or less, compared with 10 to 15 °F in older houses. Moving air also dries the canopy and so cuts leaf disease. For our house: 2,880 sq ft x 2 = 5,760 cfm. With the first fan at 12 ft and the rest 45 ft apart, three fan positions fit the 96 ft length, so each fan would be near 1,900 cfm (our arithmetic).

Summer: exhaust fans and evaporative pads

UMass's fan-and-pad sheet gives a design of 8 cfm per square foot of floor area at 0.1 inch of water static pressure, with adjustments for unshaded houses, altitude above 1,000 feet, and pad-to-fan distances under 100 feet. Pad area is the airflow divided by the face airflow: 150 cfm per square foot for aspen, 250 for 4-inch cellulose, 350 for 6-inch cellulose. Our house: 2,880 x 8 = 23,040 cfm, and with 4-inch cellulose 23,040 / 250 = about 92 sq ft of pad, which is a strip about 3 ft high across the 30 ft end wall. Fans go on the opposite wall. Note that the pad-to-fan distance is 96 ft, just under the 100 ft figure, so a designer would check the adjustment.

The same sheet lists pad care: screen returned water to 50 mesh, flush the sump where salts build, paint the sump and piping black to limit algae, run fans for 30 minutes after the water is shut off to dry the pads, and keep doors closed while cooling so air does not short-circuit. For the general idea of evaporative cooling, see Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers.

Controls and staging

A step controller can stage the whole house. An extension article on step controllers describes two heating stages, a setpoint stage, and several cooling stages: one heater starts first and a second joins if the temperature keeps falling, then on warming a fan runs low, then high, then a second fan joins, then the pad pump starts. In its sample table the HAF runs through the heat stages and the setpoint stage while intake shutters stay closed. It reports an on-off differential of 4 to 8 °F for a mechanical thermostat against about one-half to 1 °F for a controller, and says energy use rises about 3 percent for each degree of overheating. Put the sensor at crop level, shielded from sun, and test the stage points each season.

Maintenance summary

  • Annual heater start-up and vent inspection before the heating season.
  • Clean outside-air intake screens and check that intake pipes are not blocked.
  • Wipe HAF guards and motors a few times a year and secure the fans.
  • Service fan belts, shutters and louvers before summer; clean pads and sumps.

Field card: Greenhouse Heating and Ventilation

Size on exposed area, vent to above the peak, bring in outside air.

  • Q = U x A x ΔT on the exposed covering area; double poly U is about 0.70 (Purdue, Texas A&M).
  • Add 10 percent for a windy, leaky house (Florida); size on heater output.
  • Our example: 4,794 sq ft x 55 °F x 0.70 x 1.10 is about 203,000 Btu/hr.
  • Ethylene hurts plants at 0.01 ppm; vent the stack above the ridge and supply outside air.
  • Outside air: compare 1 sq in per 2,500 Btu with the maker's number.
  • HAF: about 2 cfm per sq ft. Exhaust: about 8 cfm per sq ft. Pads: 250 cfm per sq ft of 4-inch cellulose.
  • Controller: heat, setpoint, then cooling stages with a small differential.
