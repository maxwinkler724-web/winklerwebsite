Machinery Room Alarms: Safe Entry, Leak Math and Breathing Gear

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chiller room is a closed box with a lot of refrigerant in it. The rules around that box (detectors, alarms, a fan that starts by itself, a switch outside the door) exist for one reason: a leak in a small room can change the air faster than a person can notice. This lesson explains what each piece does and what a tech should and should not do when the alarm sounds.

Check the code edition where you work

Check the adopted edition. We describe requirements only as public summaries, code-body pages and maker literature state them. The standards involved are ASHRAE 15 and ASHRAE 34, the International Mechanical Code (IMC) and the International Fire Code (IFC). Editions differ and each state or city adopts its own. Your authority having jurisdiction and the equipment manual decide what applies to your room. This page is not a substitute for the standard.

What a machinery room is and when it matters

ACHR News, summarizing ASHRAE 15-2022, says the standard uses "machinery room" for a space that contains compressors and pressure vessels, and that a room can also qualify when the refrigerant in a circuit would exceed the refrigerant concentration limit (RCL) from ASHRAE 34. Bacharach's article on the same standard says the detector requirement applies where the total charge may exceed the RCL, with chiller rooms and walk-in coolers and freezers as examples. The RCL is a published concentration for each refrigerant. For R-134a, Bacharach lists it as 50,000 ppm, which it states is the same as 13 lb of refrigerant per 1,000 cubic feet of occupied space. The same article lists an 8-hour occupational exposure limit of 1,000 ppm for R-134a. Those two numbers are far apart, which is why the room is built around early warning.

Detectors: what they watch and where they sit

Bacharach says the standard calls for the detector to be "located in an area where refrigerant from a leak will concentrate." Honeywell Analytics notes that CFC, HCFC and HFC refrigerants are heavier than air, so a leak settles low. Its mechanical-room guide puts a sensor within 20 feet of the chiller, mounted about 18 inches above the floor, and also suggests watching pits, stairwells and trenches and, where possible, the chiller vent line. Makers differ on exact heights and counts, so follow the instructions for the model installed. For sensor technologies see Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors; this lesson is about the fixed system.

Bacharach's summary says the alarm must actuate at a value not greater than the corresponding threshold limit value (TLV-TWA). Honeywell Analytics gives example alarm pairs, such as 250 ppm and 500 ppm for R-134a and 50 ppm and 150 ppm for R-123, and notes that levels can be changed to meet customer requirements. Treat any number you see on a wall as the owner's setting, not a universal rule.

Alarms, ventilation and the switch outside the door

Bacharach says alarms must be visual and audible inside the room and outside each entrance, of the manual reset type with the reset inside the room, and that the detector must start mechanical ventilation. Honeywell Analytics adds that if boilers and chillers share the room, a leak must shut down combustion processes in or near it, and that warning signs go near each warning device and at every entrance door. ACHR News adds, for newer A2L systems, that a manual emergency shut-down controller must be located outside the door of the chiller room and that detectors must self-test and alarm on failure. The point of the outside controls is that nobody has to walk into a leaking room to stop equipment or start the fan.

Never enter on alarm without protection

Honeywell Analytics says that when the alarm sounds inside, people should leave immediately, and that outside the room the rule is "DO NOT ENTER ROOM without self-contained breathing apparatus." It describes keeping at least one NIOSH-approved SCBA outside but close to the room, with a second as backup. A university ammonia plan we read says to keep the door closed at every alarm level where safe, and that at the high alarm only properly trained and equipped people may enter. OSHA describes a confined space as one not designed for continuous occupancy, and a permit-required one as having or possibly having a hazardous atmosphere. We cannot say whether a given room is a permit space; that is a site decision. The practical rule is simple: an alarm means a bad atmosphere, a trained and equipped team decides who goes in, and you do not walk in to look for the leak.

Worked example: how little it takes

This is our own arithmetic. We use the R-134a figure Bacharach lists, 13 lb per 1,000 cubic feet, only as an example limit; always use the value for your refrigerant and your adopted edition. The 1,200 lb chiller charge is also an example.

  1. Room volume = 30 ft × 20 ft × 12 ft = 7,200 cubic feet. (Our own.)
  2. Mass at the limit = volume × limit ÷ 1,000 = 7,200 × 13 ÷ 1,000 = 93.6 lb. (Our own.)
  3. Share of the charge = 93.6 ÷ 1,200 = 0.078, about 7.8 percent. (Our own.)
  4. If all 1,200 lb escaped: 1,200 ÷ 7.2 = about 166.7 lb per 1,000 cubic feet, roughly 12.8 times the example limit. (Our own.)

Our own reading: under 8 percent of the charge would reach the example limit in a sealed, unventilated, evenly mixed room. Real rooms are not evenly mixed and ventilation helps, but the numbers show why detectors alarm well below this point and why the fan and the outside controls matter.

Daily and periodic checks

Honeywell's bump-test paper defines a function check, where gas is passed over the sensor to confirm alarms activate, and an accuracy check with a known traceable gas, typically within 10 to 20 percent of the applied value. For fixed monitors it suggests accuracy checks every 4 to 6 months at minimum and zero and span calibration at least annually, and says local rules, company policy and product manuals may take precedence. Honeywell Analytics also says to follow the manufacturer's calibration intervals. On daily rounds, look at the panel for faults; see Chiller Plant Daily Rounds. Fan tests, outside-switch tests and SCBA inspections should follow your site procedure and the maker's manual; we did not find a sourced interval for them.

How A2L and ammonia rooms differ

For A2L refrigerants, ACHR News says the detection system must shut down compressors, pumps and valves above 25 percent of the lower flammability limit, and that exhaust needs are much higher: 3,162 cfm for a 250-ton chiller on R-134a against 19,000 cfm on R-1234ze. See A2L Leak Detection Sensors. For ammonia, a summary of IIAR 2-2021 says emergency ventilation starts at 150 ppm at 30 air changes per hour, on power independent of the e-stop, with a labeled ON/AUTO switch outside the principal door. The university plan sets room alarms at 25, 50 and 250 ppm and cites 300 ppm as the immediately dangerous level from its safety data sheet; those are one owner's settings. Read Ammonia Refrigeration Basics before working near an ammonia plant. Low-pressure chillers add their own room issues; see Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units. Our existing overview, Refrigerant Machine Rooms, covers the exhaust-rate formula.

Field card: Machinery Rooms

Alarm means leave, not look.

  • Detector low and near the equipment, because these refrigerants settle.
  • Alarms inside and outside the entrance; the detector starts the fan.
  • Switch and breathing gear stay outside the door.
  • Do not enter on alarm without SCBA and a trained team.
  • Example: 7,200 cu ft x 13 lb per 1,000 = 93.6 lb (Our own).
  • Bump test and calibrate on the maker's schedule.
  • Check the code edition adopted where you work.
