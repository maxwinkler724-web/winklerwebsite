ACCA Manual S: Picking Equipment Size After the Load Calculation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A load calculation gives you a number. Buying a "3-ton" because the number is close to 36,000 Btuh is where many jobs go wrong. ACCA Manual S is the step in between: it tells you how to turn the load into a real equipment choice, using the maker's own performance data at your design conditions.

Check the current edition

Check the current edition. Manual S is a copyrighted ACCA document, and codes and programs name specific editions. This lesson relies on public summaries of its limits, not on the book itself. Confirm the exact numbers in the edition your jurisdiction or program requires. Do not use a pirated copy; buy it from ACCA.

Where Manual S sits

Manual J finds the heat the house gains and loses; see Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J. Manual S then picks equipment for those loads, and Manual D designs the ducts. The Manual J cooling answer comes in two parts: a sensible load (the temperature part) and a latent load (the moisture part). Manual S has to satisfy both.

Why "3 ton" is a label, not a capacity

Tonnage is a size class. One ton is 12,000 Btuh, so "3 ton" means roughly 36,000 Btuh, but the actual output changes with outdoor temperature, indoor air condition and airflow. The nameplate or AHRI figure is for one set of rating conditions. Energy Vanguard points out that sensible and latent capacity at your design conditions can be found only in the manufacturer's expanded performance tables, and that those tables are at the core of the Manual S procedure for air conditioners and heat pumps. The PNNL Building America Solution Center says the same thing from the other direction: use the expanded table, not the AHRI rating figures.

Makers name these tables differently (product data, expanded ratings, technical information), so look for the page with rows of outdoor temperature, indoor wet bulb and airflow.

Sensible and latent: both have to be met

Total capacity is split into sensible and latent parts, and the split moves with the conditions. Energy Vanguard stresses that the sensible heat ratio (sensible divided by total) is a result of the design conditions, not something you pick. An ICC checklist built on Manual S asks one question for each part: do the selected equipment's sensible and latent capacities meet the load's requirements? Its worked sample compares a unit's sensible and latent capacities against the house's sensible and latent gains, and reports the ratio. The checklist sets no percentage limit on the sensible or latent piece alone; it just has to meet the load. A unit that covers the total but skips the latent part will cool the air and leave the house clammy. For what oversizing does to humidity and cycling, see AC Short Cycling.

The limits, as public summaries state them

Several public summaries of Manual S give the same pattern. Check them against your edition.

  • Air conditioner, cooling: total capacity at design conditions should not exceed 115% of the design total cooling load. PNNL's Building America Solution Center states a window of 95% to 115%.
  • Heat pump, cooling: the upper limit rises to 125% in a very cold, heating-driven climate. PNNL ties that to IECC climate zones 4 through 8. The ICC checklist phrases it as a very cold climate where heating is the main concern.
  • Furnace, heating: output should be 100% to 140% of the design heat loss, per Energy Vanguard and the ICC checklist (which states the 140% cap).
  • When nothing fits the window: PNNL says the next nominal size up is allowed. Single-speed units usually come in half-ton steps; multi-stage units may come in one-ton steps.
  • Data match: if the table's conditions differ from your design conditions by more than 5%, interpolate the maker's data to your design conditions rather than pretending they match (ICC checklist).

These limits cap oversizing; going under the load is a separate failure, covered in AC Can't Keep Up on the Hottest Days.

Heat pumps: cooling and heating pull in two directions

A heat pump has one size but two jobs. PNNL says to size for the design heating or cooling load of the home, whichever is larger, and notes that standard air-source heat pump efficiency drops once outdoor temperatures fall below about 35°F. In a cold climate the heating load can be the bigger one, which is why the cooling cap is allowed to stretch to 125%. In the ICC checklist example, electric auxiliary heat covers the gap between what the heat pump delivers at its balance point and the design heating load. How that balance point is found is in Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity.

Worked example: checking a 3-ton condensing unit

Assume a Manual J result for a house. To keep the arithmetic matched to the table we have, we assume design conditions of 95°F outdoors and 80°F dry bulb, 67°F wet bulb indoors. (Many designs use 75°F indoors; then you would correct the table, as the maker's note explains below.) The assumed loads are:

  • Sensible load: 22,500 Btuh
  • Latent load: 7,500 Btuh
  • Total load: 22,500 + 7,500 = 30,000 Btuh. The load sensible heat ratio is 22,500 / 30,000 = 0.75.

The loads are our assumption. The unit data are real. Carrier's Product Data for the Performance 16 air conditioner, model 24ACC636 (nominal 3 tons, listed with an AHRI rated capacity of 34,400 Btuh), has a cooling table at 1,200 cfm. At 95°F outdoor air and 67°F indoor wet bulb it lists total capacity 34.40 and sensible capacity 25.40 thousand Btuh, with a system draw of 2.99 kW. Carrier's note says the sensible values assume 80°F entering indoor air and that the capacities are net of blower heat.

  1. Total cap check: 34,400 / 30,000 = 1.147, or 114.7% of the load. The 115% limit is 30,000 x 1.15 = 34,500 Btuh. The unit is under it, with about 100 Btuh to spare. Pass, barely.
  2. Sensible check: 25,400 is more than 22,500 by 2,900 Btuh, so the sensible load is covered (25,400 / 22,500 = 112.9%).
  3. Latent check: latent capacity is total minus sensible: 34,400 - 25,400 = 9,000 Btuh, against a 7,500 Btuh load. Covered. The unit's own sensible ratio is 25,400 / 34,400 = 0.74, close to the load's 0.75, so it handles moisture in about the proportion the house produces it.

Now see why the label misleads. On the same table at 1,200 cfm and 67°F wet bulb, total capacity is 36.25 at 85°F outdoors, 34.40 at 95°F and 32.44 at 105°F. One "3-ton" unit spans about 4,000 Btuh across 20 degrees of outdoor temperature. Airflow matters too: at 1,050 cfm and 95°F, the table gives 33.87 total and 23.70 sensible, noticeably less sensible capacity than at 1,200 cfm. And if the same house had a 28,000 Btuh load, this unit would be 34,400 / 28,000 = 122.9%, over the 115% cap, and you would look for a smaller size.

For indoor air other than 80°F, Carrier says to subtract 835 Btuh per 1,000 cfm for each degree below 80°F. At 1,200 cfm that is about 1,000 Btuh of sensible capacity per degree. A 75°F design therefore costs roughly 5,000 Btuh of sensible capacity, which is why it can change the answer. This is one maker's table and one example, not a rule for every unit.

What to take away

Manual S replaces "the closest tonnage" with a check you can show on paper: design-condition capacity from the maker's table, total within the stated window, sensible and latent each meeting their load, and the heating side checked for heat pumps and furnaces. It does not make a bad Manual J good, and it does not size ducts. Ask for the numbers behind a quote; a contractor who ran the procedure can show them. For the sizing sequence from the start, begin at Start Here.

Field card: ACCA Manual S Equipment Selection

Pick from the maker's table at design conditions, not from the tonnage label.

  • Manual S comes after Manual J and before Manual D.
  • Use the expanded performance table; AHRI ratings do not show sensible or latent capacity.
  • Cooling AC: total capacity at design conditions at most 115% of the total load (PNNL gives 95% to 115%).
  • Heat pump: cap rises to 125% in heating-driven climates; size for the larger of heating or cooling load.
  • Furnace: output 100% to 140% of the design heat loss.
  • Sensible capacity must meet sensible load, latent must meet latent.
  • Correct for indoor air other than the table's 80°F using the maker's note.
  • Example: 34,400 / 30,000 = 114.7%, under the 115% cap.
