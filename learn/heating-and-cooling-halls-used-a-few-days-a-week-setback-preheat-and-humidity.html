Heating and Cooling Halls Used a Few Days a Week

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A meeting hall, club room or house of worship may be used six to ten hours a week and sit empty for the other 158 or so. Heating it all week wastes money. Letting it go cold and hoping it warms up in time causes complaints, and sometimes damage. This lesson shows how to pick a setback, work out when the heat must start, and watch humidity.

Check the building first

Check your building. Every hall is different, and the numbers below come from general guidance and one worked example with assumed values. This page is education, not a design. Confirm settings with the equipment maker, the controls contractor and anyone who looks after sensitive contents such as an organ.

Why big halls warm up slowly

Three things work against you. First, warm air rises, so in a tall room the heated air collects near the ceiling. A Church of England diocese heating guide says this is why systems must run "for a long time until all the space warms," and that radiant heat avoids it because it warms surfaces instead of air. Second, a heavy masonry building holds a lot of cold. The same guide describes intermittent heating with long heat up times, up to 8 to 12 hours for an undersized system. Third, you must warm the walls and furnishings, not just the air, before people feel comfortable.

The guide also makes a point for halls used once or twice a week: holding a constant background temperature in such a building wastes heat while it sits empty, unless there is a specific reason to protect the building or its contents. That reason is why the decision about setback depth is not the same for every hall.

How deep should the setback be?

The U.S. Department of Energy says that turning a thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees F for about eight hours a day can save up to 10% a year on heating and cooling, and that savings are larger in mild climates. See Thermostat Setback Schedules: How Much You Really Save for the full explanation, including why recovery does not cancel the savings. DOE's figure is written for a home with an eight hour setback. A hall that sits unoccupied for most of the week is set back far longer, so treat the 10% as a rough anchor, not a forecast.

Two limits keep the setback from going too deep:

  • Equipment. DOE says heat pumps generally do poorly with standard setback, because a deep drop in heating can bring on backup electric heat, which costs more. If the hall has a heat pump, ask the installer for a heat pump thermostat and a shallow setback. DOE also says steam and radiant floor systems are slow, sometimes taking several hours, so they need to start early or use a thermostat that learns the response.
  • Contents. A pipe organ maker says the room should never fall below 60 F or rise above 80 F where control is possible, and that the room can be relaxed when the sanctuary is empty. So 60 F is a sensible setback floor if an organ is in the room. If there are no sensitive contents, ask the maker of the heating equipment and your insurer about lower setpoints and freeze protection.

Preheat: start early, not all week

The gap between "setback" and "comfortable" is the recovery time. DOE's suggestion for slow systems is a start two to three hours before people arrive, found by trial and error. A thermostat with optimum start learns this for you. One maker explains its version: the thermostat tracks a rate of change, the minutes needed to warm the space by one degree C, updates it each time it reaches the target early or late, and starts the heat early enough to hit the schedule. That maker caps the preheat at a settable maximum and suggests 3 hours for most homes. A big hall may need a longer cap, so set it from measurements.

ENERGY STAR's worship facility checklist says smart thermostats can pre-cool or pre-heat "an hour prior to occupation." That is a good starting point for a small, well insulated room. It is not enough for a tall masonry hall, as the example below shows.

Worked example: when does the heat start?

These numbers are assumed for teaching. Replace the recovery rate with one you measure by logging the room temperature on a cold morning.

  1. Comfort and setback. Comfort setpoint 68 F (a piano maker names about 68 F as ideal for its instruments). Setback 60 F (the organ maker's low limit). Drop = 68 − 60 = 8 F.
  2. Recovery rate (assumed). 2 F per hour. This is also the fastest change the organ maker says is acceptable: no more than 2 F per hour.
  3. Preheat time. 8 F ÷ 2 F per hour = 4 hours.
  4. Start time. Service starts at 10:00 a.m. 10:00 minus 4 hours = 6:00 a.m.
  5. If you measure 3 F per hour. 8 ÷ 3 = 2.67 hours, about 2 hours 40 minutes. Start at 7:20 a.m.
  6. Shut off early. ENERGY STAR suggests programming the system to stop 15 to 30 minutes before use ends, depending on outdoor temperature. Service ends at 12:00, so stop at 11:30.
  7. Heated hours. 6:00 to 11:30 = 5.5 hours that Sunday, instead of 168 a week.
  8. Savings anchor. Suppose the hall's heating and cooling bill is $3,000 a year. DOE's ceiling: $3,000 × 0.10 = $300. If you reach half of it, $300 × 0.5 = $150. Because the hall is set back much longer than eight hours a day, the real figure could differ. Compare actual bills.

Humidity and summer

Setback in summer means raising the cooling setpoint. Our own view: a cooling system dries the air as it runs, so when it runs less, moisture can build up in a closed building. EPA's mold guidance says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. A cheap hygrometer in the hall tells you if the schedule is working. If it climbs above 60 percent, shorten the summer setback or add dehumidification; our humid house lesson covers causes and fixes that apply to larger systems too.

For instruments, the sources agree on stability. The organ maker says the organ tolerates 35 to 75 percent relative humidity with occasional excursions, and calls 45 to 55 percent ideal. Changes should be gradual, supply air should not blow directly on the pipes, and the organ lags the room by several hours, so heating for a Friday event might begin the day before. A piano maker recommends 45 to 70 percent and a steady temperature of about 68 F, with the piano kept away from radiators and heating outlets. A single hall number that satisfies both and EPA's mold limit is 45 to 55 percent. If there is an instrument, loop in its caretaker before changing the schedule.

Ventilation when people are in the room

A hall can go from empty to full in minutes. Outdoor air is needed while people are there, and not during an empty setback. Rooftop units serve many halls; see Rooftop Unit Basics for the parts. A CO2 sensor can scale outdoor air to the crowd; the math is in Demand-Controlled Ventilation. Ask the controls contractor how outdoor air is handled during preheat.

Keep the schedule simple

  • Use a programmable or connected thermostat with a separate schedule for each use day and a way to add a one-time event.
  • Pick the setback from the equipment type and contents, not a rule of thumb.
  • Log start times for a few weeks, then adjust the start by 15 to 30 minutes at a time.
  • Put the thermostat away from heaters, electronics and drafts; ENERGY STAR warns they distort its reading.
  • Close exterior doors and seal gaps, which ENERGY STAR lists as basic load reducers.
  • Write down the plan for a system failure, as ENERGY STAR suggests.

Field card: Halls Used a Few Days a Week

Measure recovery, then schedule the start.

  • DOE: setback 7 to 10 F for about 8 hours can save up to 10% a year; a hall set back longer may differ.
  • Preheat time = degrees to recover ÷ recovery rate (F per hour). 8 F at 2 F per hour = 4 hours.
  • Slow systems (steam, radiant floor, heavy masonry): DOE suggests starting 2 to 3 hours early, then adjust.
  • Stop the system 15 to 30 minutes before use ends (ENERGY STAR).
  • Keep relative humidity below 60 percent (EPA); 45 to 55 percent suits most instruments.
  • Heat pumps: avoid deep setback that triggers backup heat.
  • Ventilate while occupied; see the DCV lesson.
