Water Under a Hot-Water Boiler: Finding the Leak

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A puddle under a boiler is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The water may be coming from the relief valve, a threaded fitting, a circulator flange, a drain valve, the fill valve, a condensate line, or the boiler itself. Each source has a different fix and a very different bill, so the job is to trace it before touching anything.

Safety first

Turn off the boiler's power switch and let the water cool before you reach under it. Hot-water boiler water can scald, and a leak near a hot surface or a wet electrical box adds shock risk. Never plug, cap or block a relief valve or its discharge pipe. A California boiler safety regulation says the discharge pipe of a pressure relief device cannot be readily plugged or otherwise obstructed, and that the user must keep relief devices in good operating condition. A state boiler inspection guide from Colorado calls the relief valve the most important device on a boiler. If the valve is the leaker, the answer is to find out why, not to stop it up. See Boiler Pressure Relief Valve Dripping or Discharging.

Step 1: dry it and watch

Wipe everything dry, then lay strips of paper towel on the floor and around suspect spots: under the relief valve discharge pipe, under each pump flange, under the drain valve, and directly under the boiler jacket. Run a heat call for ten to twenty minutes, then check each towel. Wet paper shows you where drops land, and tracing straight up from the first wet spot usually leads to the source. Water travels along pipes before it drips, so always look higher than the puddle. Note whether the leak happens only when the boiler is hot, only when cold, or all the time. A leak that appears only when the boiler heats up often points to pressure rising, which sends you to the expansion tank.

Step 2: read the pressure gauge

Look at the gauge cold and then hot. Use the manufacturer's stated cold fill pressure for your boiler; many homes run in the teens of psi, but we do not give a number for yours. The pattern matters more than the figure. If pressure climbs sharply as the boiler heats and the relief valve lifts, the system has nowhere for expanding water to go. The Colorado guide notes that without an expansion tank, heating water has no place to expand, so the relief valve may lift. A waterlogged or failed tank, or one with the wrong air charge, shows up this way. Our lesson on expansion tanks and fill pressure covers the test. If pressure stays low and the system keeps taking water, there is a real leak somewhere.

The usual suspects, from easiest to hardest

  • Relief valve. Water at the end of the discharge pipe. Causes include high pressure from a bad expansion tank, an overfeeding fill valve, or a valve seat damaged by debris. Dripping after the valve has lifted often means the seat is worn, and a valve that has weeped is commonly replaced rather than tapped.
  • Threaded and sweated fittings. Look for white or green crust, rust streaks, and slow beads at unions, nipples and valves. A small weep at a thread can be a tightening or a re-doping job once the section is drained.
  • Circulator flanges. Pump flange gaskets and the pump's own seal leak at the flange face or from under the motor bracket. Wet at the flange means a gasket; wet from the pump body or shaft area means the pump itself. See circulator troubleshooting.
  • Drain valves and air vents. Hose-bib style drains often drip from the cap or stem. An automatic air vent can spit water if its cap is loose or the float is stuck.
  • Fill valve and backflow preventer. The pressure-reducing fill valve can pass water to the system continuously if it fails, and the backflow device can discharge from its vent port. That pushes pressure up and may feed the relief valve. Read how the fill valve and backflow preventer work.
  • Condensate lines on condensing boilers. These boilers make acidic condensate by design. A blocked trap, a kinked or sagging tube, a full neutralizer or a failed condensate pump can back water out of the boiler's condensate outlet or the drain piping. This water is not system water, so the pressure gauge will not change. See condensing boiler annual maintenance.
  • The boiler itself. Water coming out between cast-iron sections, from a seam in the jacket or from the bottom of the heat exchanger is the serious case.

The boiler body: cracked sections and corroded exchangers

Cast-iron boilers are built of sections held together with nipples and sealant. A weep between sections can sometimes be an old gasket or sealant issue, but a crack in a section is different. Freezing can split a section, and thermal shock, such as pouring cold fill water into a hot dry or low-water boiler, can crack hot cast iron. Corroded steel or aluminum heat exchangers, especially in condensing units, leak from pinholes or seam failures. A cracked section or a leaking heat exchanger often means replacing the boiler, because repair is limited and a failed pressure boundary is a safety matter. That is our own guidance; the final call belongs to the boiler maker's instructions and the person inspecting it. Shut the boiler down, tag it out and do not keep refilling it to keep it running.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption, not a measured value.

  • Assumed drip rate: 10 drops per minute.
  • Assumed drop volume: 0.05 mL.
  • Assumed system volume: 15 gallons (about 56.8 L).
  • Assumed fresh fill water: 180 mg of hardness (as calcium carbonate) and 9 mg of dissolved oxygen per liter.

Per minute: 10 drops × 0.05 mL = 0.5 mL. Per day: 0.5 × 1,440 minutes = 720 mL, about 0.19 gallon. Per year: 720 mL × 365 = 262.8 L, about 69 gallons. That is 69 ÷ 15 = about 4.6 system volumes of fresh water a year. If the drip were 60 drops per minute, the same math gives 4.3 L a day and about 28 system volumes a year.

What comes in with it, at the assumed quality: 262.8 L × 180 mg = 47,304 mg, about 47 g of hardness minerals, and 262.8 L × 9 mg = 2,365 mg, about 2.4 g of oxygen. A 10-drop leak looks tiny but replaces the system water several times a year. A PNNL report says oxygen is the usual oxidizer that drives corrosion and speeds iron corrosion, and that calcium carbonate drops out of water as it heats, forming scale that insulates heating surfaces. So a small leak that keeps a fill valve working can slowly damage the boiler it is draining. Cheap to fix early, costly if ignored.

When to stop and call for help

If you find water between cast-iron sections, a pinhole in an exchanger, or a leak you cannot trace, shut the boiler off and get a qualified technician. Do not run the boiler with a failed pressure boundary, and never defeat the relief valve to hold pressure.

Field card: Water under a hot-water boiler

Dry it, tape paper towels, watch, and trace upward.

  • Power off, cool down, scald risk first.
  • Never cap a relief valve or its discharge pipe.
  • Pressure climbing hot points to the expansion tank.
  • Constant makeup water means a real leak or a stuck fill valve.
  • Condensate leaks do not change system pressure.
  • Cracked sections or corroded exchangers often mean replacement.
