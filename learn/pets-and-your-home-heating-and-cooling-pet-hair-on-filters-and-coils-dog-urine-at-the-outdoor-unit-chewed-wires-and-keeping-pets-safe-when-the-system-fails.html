Pets and Your Home Heating and Cooling: Filters, Coils, the Outdoor Unit, Chewed Wires and Safety When the System Fails

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Pets change how a heating and cooling system ages. Hair loads the filter faster, dander and fur reach the coils, a dog may mark or dig at the outdoor unit, and a bored chewer can find a wire. This lesson is a short care plan for each of those, plus what to do for the animals if the system quits in hot or cold weather.

Check the sources

Read your own manual first. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026. Your equipment manual, your warranty and your veterinarian come before anything here. For the contractor-side view of animals at the door, see Dogs and pets on HVAC house calls. For a broader overview, see Pets and your HVAC.

Filters: how often to look

The U.S. Department of Energy says to clean or replace an air conditioning filter every month or two in the cooling season, and says filters may need more frequent attention with fur-bearing pets in the house. The EPA says filter makers typically recommend replacement every 60 to 90 days and says to change sooner if the filter looks heavily soiled when you replace it. Put those together and a pet home has a simple rule: look every two weeks, change when the filter tells you to, and never go past the maker's longest interval. Size, direction and fit are covered in furnace filter size, direction and fit, and the general schedule is in how often to change your air filter.

Choosing a rating without choking airflow

The EPA's home air cleaner guide says to pick a filter rated at least MERV 13, or as high as your system can accommodate, and that most furnaces and HVAC systems can take a MERV 13 filter without equipment problems if it is replaced frequently. The same page gives no airflow or fan limits, and it suggests asking a professional HVAC technician about the best filter for your system. So the practical limit is your fan and your filter slot, not a number we can print. Our own advice for a shedding household:

  • Do not jump to the densest filter that fits. A dense one-inch filter loads fast with hair and can starve the fan of air.
  • If a thicker media cabinet fits, it gives more filter area for the same air, which usually means slower loading. See one-inch filters vs four-inch media cabinets.
  • Have a technician read the pressure across the filter slot once, so you know what normal looks like. Filter racks, bypass gaps and pressure gauges shows where that reading is taken.
  • Warning signs of too little air are weak airflow at the registers, a coil that ices up, or a system that cycles off on a safety. See frozen evaporator coils.

For allergy-driven filter choices and portable cleaners, we point to home HVAC for allergies and asthma and air purifiers and whole-house cleaners instead of repeating them. The EPA also notes that an upgraded furnace or HVAC filter helps but cannot remove all pollutants.

Keeping hair out of the return

Hair that never reaches the filter ends up on the return grille, in the return duct and on the blower. Vacuum the grille face every time you check the filter. Keep beds and furniture from blocking a return, and do not let a pet bed sit against it. A hair mat on the grille is a cue to open the grille and check the filter behind it. A loose filter slot lets hair and dust bypass the media; our dust lesson covers gaps. If hair is reaching the indoor coil or blower wheel, that cleaning is a professional job; see cleaning a blower wheel and indoor coil.

The outdoor unit: urine, digging and a simple barrier

We have not found a maker or federal page that quantifies urine damage to coil fins, so we give no numbers for it. Our own field note: where animals mark a unit, the fins and the coil tubing at that spot are the first places to look for corrosion, and salt and moisture make it worse. Corrosion and salt air explains why coils leak. Digging is a separate problem: a hole beside the pad can tilt the unit, strain the line set and expose wiring.

The Department of Energy says to clean the area around the coil, remove debris and trim foliage back at least 2 feet. We use the same 2 feet as our minimum clear zone for any barrier, and your manual's clearance wins if it asks for more. Put a see-through barrier, such as open fence panels or posts with wire, outside that zone, not touching the unit. A solid wall or a close fence blocks air and traps heat. Leave a gate on the service side so a technician can reach the panels.

Chewed thermostat wire and line set insulation

Low-voltage thermostat wire is thin and easy to chew, and a chewed wire usually shows up as a dead thermostat or a system that will not start. Our own guidance: if you find bare conductors, do not tape or twist them; turn the system off at the thermostat and the breaker, keep the animal away, and call a technician. For the outdoor disconnect and line voltage, see condenser disconnect switches. Chewed foam on the refrigerant line set is mostly an efficiency and condensation problem; sweating copper in summer is a cue to have it re-wrapped. Rodent damage is a different pattern, covered in mice and other pests in home ducts. Bent fins from rough play are covered in bent coil fins.

Worked example (our own): two shedding dogs

This is our own example, not a measurement from a maker or agency. The assumptions are ours:

  • One 1-inch pleated filter, dry, weighing 10.0 oz new on a kitchen scale.
  • Two shedding dogs, filter checked and weighed every 2 weeks, always dry and out of the house air for a minute before weighing.
  • Our change rule: replace the filter when it has gained 50 percent of its new weight, or at the maker's longest interval, or earlier if it looks heavily soiled. Weight also changes with humidity, so we use it as a trend, not a lab reading.

Step by step: the gain per two-week step is 1.2, then 2.8 − 1.2 = 1.6, then 4.9 − 2.8 = 2.1, then 7.5 − 4.9 = 2.6 oz. The steps grow, which is how a loading filter behaves: hair on the surface catches more dust. The 50 percent line is 10.0 × 0.50 = 5.0 oz of gain. The week 6 reading is 4.9 oz, just short, and the filter would pass the line at about 6 + (5.0 − 4.9) ÷ (7.5 − 4.9) × 2 = 6.08 weeks. In practice you change it at the week 6 check. That is 6 × 7 = 42 days, shorter than the 60 to 90 days the EPA says makers typically recommend, which is what we would expect with two dogs. If your own gain per step grows much faster than this example, the filter is loading too fast: look for a denser filter than the fan likes, a recent shedding season, or a bypass gap.

If you have a pressure gauge or a technician's reading across the filter slot, track it the same way: write the number down at the same fan speed each time and watch how it climbs between changes. We do not give a limit, because the right one is in your equipment's documentation.

When the system fails: keeping pets safe

We use federal guidance only. The CDC says never to leave a pet in a parked car, even in cool temperatures, that a cracked window is not enough, and that car interiors can rise almost 20 degrees Fahrenheit in the first 10 minutes. It also says to give pets plenty of fresh water in a shaded spot. The CDC heat page does not cover power outages or signs of heat stress, so if an animal seems ill in the heat, call your veterinarian.

In cold weather, the CDC winter storm page says to bring pets indoors, and if that is not possible, to provide adequate shelter and unfrozen water. It also says to check the temperature of your home often. For an outage, the CDC pet emergency pages suggest a kit with a 2-week supply of food, water and any medication per animal, and say that, when sheltering in place, you should choose a pet-friendly room, preferably an interior room with few or no windows. Winter outages also bring a carbon monoxide risk; see carbon monoxide during winter outages.

Pet-safe space heater habits

The Consumer Product Safety Commission says to keep all sides of a portable heater at least 3 feet from beds, clothes, curtains, papers and sofas, never to leave it running while you are away or asleep, never to power it from an extension cord or power strip, and to put it on a stable, level surface where it will not be knocked over. It also says to be mindful of children and pets around portable heaters. Our own habit for pet homes: put the heater where a tail cannot reach it, block off the 3-foot zone with furniture or a gate if you have to, and unplug it when you leave the room. Field card: Pets and your home heating and cooling

Look every two weeks, keep 2 feet clear, and plan for the pets before the outage.

  • Filter: look every 2 weeks; the maker's longest interval is a ceiling, not a target.
  • Rating: MERV 13 or as high as your fan and slot allow (EPA); ask a tech about your system.
  • Return grille: vacuum the face at every check.
  • Outdoor unit: 2 feet clear (DOE), open barrier outside that zone, service gate.
  • Chewed wire: no tape, power off, call a technician.
  • Outage: 2-week pet supplies, interior room, fresh water in shade, indoors in the cold (CDC).
  • Space heater: 3 feet clear, wall outlet, unplug when you leave (CPSC).
