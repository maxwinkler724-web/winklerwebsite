New Hydronic and Chilled Water Piping: Testing, Flushing and Cleaning

Last updated: 2026-10-16

New water piping is not ready to run the day the last joint is made. It has to be proven tight under pressure, scrubbed of construction debris, and filled with treated water before pumps and chillers see it. This lesson walks through that order using freely posted public and university specifications.

Check the job spec first

The project specification and the authority having jurisdiction win. The numbers below come from sample public specifications and a university safety manual. They show typical practice, not a rule for your job. Piping design standards such as ASME B31.9 (Building Services Piping) set the framework, and the specifier picks the test pressure and hold time for each project.

Why water, not air

Compressed gas stores far more energy than the same volume of water at the same pressure, because gas compresses and then expands violently if a joint lets go. Stanford's SLAC safety manual puts it plainly: pneumatic tests are potentially more dangerous than hydrostatic tests because of the higher level of potential energy, and hydrostatic is the preferred method. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's manual says the same and allows a pneumatic test only in limited cases, such as a system that cannot be filled with water. Water barely compresses, so a failed joint drops pressure fast and mostly leaks or sprays instead of bursting. That is why a hydronic or chilled water system is normally filled and tested with water. If air or nitrogen is truly needed, see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods for the refrigerant side.

Typical test pressure and hold time

Two freely posted specifications agree on the multiplier. The Department of Veterans Affairs hydronic piping specification (Section 23 21 13) calls for a hydrostatic test at 1.5 times design pressure, and says design maximum pressure for water systems is usually the static head or expansion tank maximum pressure plus pump head. The Penn State Office of Physical Plant testing table lists hydronic piping at 1.5 times maximum system pressure but not less than 100 psi, with 2 hours before inspection and a 15 minute inspection, and says not to exceed fitting ratings. The SLAC manual lists 1.5 times design pressure as the minimum for building services and a typical 30 minute duration for hydronic systems, with no pressure drop allowed. The VA text we read gave no hold time, so the hold is a project decision. Read the spec for yours.

What to isolate before you pressurize

  • Equipment. The VA specification says to isolate equipment where needed to avoid excessive pressure on mechanical seals and safety devices. It also says factory-tested equipment such as coils and exchangers need not be field tested. Pumps with mechanical seals, chillers, boilers and expansion tanks are common examples. Check each nameplate or manual for its maximum pressure.
  • Relief valves. SLAC says relief valves must be removed or held down with test clamps, or replaced by temporary higher-rated devices, and put back afterward. A valve set below test pressure will open and chatter.
  • Gauges and controls. Per SLAC, use a gauge calibrated within the last 12 months, with the test pressure in the middle third of its range, mounted where it is visible at all times.
  • How you isolate. Closed valves can leak by under test pressure. See HVAC Isolation and Check Valves, and use blind flanges or caps where the valve is not trusted.
  • Fittings. Test pressure must stay under the lowest rating in the section. Maker ratings for some joining methods are covered in Press-Fit Copper Fittings for Refrigerant and Hydronic Piping.

Filling, venting and temperature

Air in the pipe acts like a spring, so the gauge climbs slowly and falls on small leaks. SLAC says to fill and vent to remove as much air as practical, and the VA specification calls for air vents at all high points and drain valves at all low points. Connect the test pump low, fill from the bottom, and open the high vent until solid water flows.

Temperature changes the reading too. A pipeline test paper indexed by the U.S. Department of Energy's OSTI explains the mechanism: warming the pipe and the water makes the water volume grow, which acts like adding water and raises pressure. It recommends reaching thermal stability before the hold period starts and keeping sun, wind and rain from distorting readings. The sources we read give no psi-per-degree rule for building piping, so we do not quote one here. In practice, note the water and ambient temperatures when you start and finish. A reading that drifts upward on a sunny wall or downward as cold water warms to room temperature may be temperature, not a leak. Ask the engineer how drift is judged.

Document the test

The VA specification requires certification documentation, including all test results and detailed test procedures, delivered ten working days before requesting final inspection. Record at least the system and section tested, test pressure, gauge ID and calibration date, start and end times, start and end readings, temperatures, who witnessed it, and what equipment was isolated. Restore relief valves and remove any caps or blinds, and sign the sheet.

Flushing at velocity

A passing pressure test only tells you the pipe is tight. It does not clean it. The VA specification has you sectionalize the system to reach a debris-carrying velocity of 1.8 m/s (5.9 ft/s) where possible, install temporary strainers to protect downstream equipment, and provide temporary piping or hose to bypass coils, control valves, exchangers and other factory-cleaned equipment. Flush until the water runs clean as approved. The reason for the bypass is simple: weld slag, pipe scale, thread cuttings and sand would otherwise lodge in a coil or valve seat you cannot easily reach. Strainers should be cleaned often while debris is still coming through.

Chemical pre-cleaning and the final flush

Oil and mill coating from new steel pipe do not flush out with plain water alone. The VA specification has the system cleaned with the products and procedures in its water treatment section (23 25 00), circulated at normal temperature at not less than 5.9 ft/s, with each section circulated for at least four hours and strainers cleaned as needed. After the cleaning solution is displaced by clean makeup water, flush dead ends and isolated clean equipment for not less than one hour, working valves gently to dislodge debris. Your chemical maker's instructions govern what you can use and how to dispose of it.

Fill and treat

The VA specification says to close and fill the system as soon as possible after final flushing to minimize corrosion, then charge it with the chemicals specified for closed loops. Do not leave the pipe drained and open to air. Feeders, inhibitors and coupons are covered in Closed-Loop Water Treatment. For a retrofit where old water and sludge are the problem, see Flushing and Cleaning an Old Heating System Before a New Boiler.

Worked example (Our own)

Assumptions (ours, not from any job): a chilled water loop with a stated working pressure of 90 psi at the lowest point, new Schedule 40 steel pipe, fitting and valve ratings well above the test pressure, and flushing a 4 inch main with an inside diameter of 4.026 inches.

Test pressure. Multiplier 1.5 (VA specification, Penn State table). 1.5 x 90 psi = 135 psi. Penn State also sets a floor of 100 psi, and 135 is above it, so 135 psi governs. Our own check: confirm every component rating exceeds 135 psi or isolate it.

Flush flow. Target velocity 5.9 ft/s (VA specification). Pipe diameter = 4.026 / 12 = 0.3355 ft. Area = 0.7854 x 0.3355 x 0.3355 = 0.0884 sq ft. Flow = 0.0884 x 5.9 = 0.522 cu ft/s. One cubic foot per second is about 448.8 gpm (our own unit conversion), so 0.522 x 448.8 = about 234 gpm. A temporary pump that cannot deliver that will not scour the pipe, so sectionalize into smaller sections.

Field card: New Piping Test and Flush

Prove it tight with water, then clean it, then fill and treat it.

  • Water, not air: less stored energy if something fails.
  • Typical sample specs: 1.5 times design pressure; one table adds a 100 psi minimum and 2 hours. Read your spec.
  • Isolate or clamp anything that cannot take test pressure: seals, relief valves, some equipment.
  • Vent the high point, note temperatures, and keep the gauge in view.
  • Flush at about 5.9 ft/s with temporary strainers and bypassed coils.
  • Chemical clean per the treatment spec, final flush at least 1 hour, then fill and treat promptly.
  • Record and sign the test sheet.
