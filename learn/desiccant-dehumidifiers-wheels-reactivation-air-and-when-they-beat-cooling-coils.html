Desiccant Dehumidifiers: Wheels, Reactivation Air and When They Beat Cooling Coils

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling coil dries air by making it cold enough that water condenses out. A desiccant dehumidifier dries air by letting a spinning wheel soak the water up, then baking the water back out into a second airstream. This lesson explains how that works, why it can reach dryness a coil cannot, and how to do the moisture math.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. This page is general education built from national laboratory, federal energy program and maker literature read in 2026. Heater types, temperatures and service steps differ by model. Follow the manual and lockout rules for the unit in front of you.

How the wheel works

The heart of the machine is a round, honeycomb-style rotor coated or filled with desiccant. Munters explains that the flutes give the air a huge surface to touch, and that moisture sticks to the desiccant by adsorption, not absorption: the water vapor is held on the surface rather than soaked into the bulk. The common desiccants are silica gel and zeolite, also called molecular sieve. Seibu Giken says its standard rotor uses silica gel and offers a zeolite rotor tailored for low dew points.

The wheel turns slowly through two airstreams. Process air is the air you want dried. It goes through one part of the wheel, leaves drier, and goes to the space or the process. Reactivation air, also called regeneration air, is a smaller stream that is heated and sent through the other part of the wheel. The heat drives the water off the desiccant and carries it outdoors. That freshly dried section then rotates back into the process air, and the cycle repeats. Munters describes the reactivation stream as a smaller, heated airstream, and an NREL laboratory test paper describes the same two-stream arrangement.

Where the reactivation heat comes from

Seibu Giken's page lists five heater choices on its dehumidifiers: PTC, electric, steam, gas or hot water. The NREL test paper adds waste heat, solar and off-peak electricity as possible sources. A federal energy program alert on desiccant wheels (see Sources) says reactivation usually wants heat at about 180 to 220 degrees F, with about 250 degrees F generally the most economical when dry air is required. Lower-grade heat can work when the humidity target is looser, but the wheel then has to be larger. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory report by a maker, SEMCO, notes that regeneration energy is the main operating cost of these systems, which is why people look for waste heat.

Why a wheel can beat a coil

A coil only removes water that condenses, so the air must be cooled below its dew point. The federal alert says conventional coils can dehumidify only down to roughly a 40 degrees F dew point, because below that the condensate can freeze on the coil and moisture removal drops off. A desiccant wheel has no such limit. Bry-Air lists supply dew points of minus 40 to minus 80 degrees C for its low dew point machines, and says it designs them for lithium battery manufacturing. Munters, Bry-Air and Seibu Giken all list pharmaceutical production among their uses.

The federal alert also lists proven uses: storage spaces, ice arenas, hospital operating rooms, supermarkets and unheated warehouses. A coil system that dries air by overcooling it also has to reheat the air afterward. The alert says a desiccant system avoids that step. Munters gives a cold storage case where the dehumidifier reduced defrosts and improved staff safety. For how a building splits ventilation drying from room cooling, see the DOAS lesson.

Active wheel versus passive wheel

Do not mix this up with the energy recovery wheel on a rooftop unit. A passive wheel, also called an enthalpy or total energy wheel, swaps heat and moisture between incoming and exhaust air and needs no added heat; the energy recovery wheel lesson covers it. An active desiccant wheel is reactivated with added heat, so it can work where there is no exhaust path. The SEMCO report says passive recovery is usually preferred when an exhaust path exists, because it is less costly and saves energy all year, and it describes hybrids that put an active wheel together with a passive one.

The warm-air catch

Adsorption releases heat, so the dry air leaves warmer than it came in. The federal alert says this heat stays in the airstream, so extra cooling is usually needed before the air enters an occupied space. The SEMCO report describes the same effect, and in one example the air leaving the system was about 92 degrees F before post-cooling. Bry-Air's page for its low dew point line says no post cooling coil is required, so whether the warmth matters depends on the job.

Worked example: how much water does 2,000 cfm give up?

A wheel dries 2,000 cfm of process air from 60 grains of moisture per pound of dry air to 20 grains per pound. Seven thousand grains make one pound. The two constants below are our own working shortcuts, not quotes. Our own: 4.5 is 60 minutes times 0.075 lb per cubic foot, the standard air density that Captive Aire's published load formulas also use. Our own: 0.68 is 4.5 times about 1,060 Btu per pound of water vapor, divided by 7,000. Captive Aire publishes 0.69 for the same job, so expect small differences.

  1. Our own: grains removed per pound of air = 60 minus 20 = 40.
  2. Our own: lb of water per hour = 2,000 x 4.5 x 40 / 7,000 = 360,000 / 7,000 = 51.4 lb/h.
  3. Our own: latent load = 0.68 x 2,000 x 40 = 54,400 Btu/h. Check: 51.4 lb/h x about 1,060 = about 54,500, so the two shortcuts agree.
  4. Our own: per day = 51.4 x 24 = 1,234 lb. One pint of water weighs about 1.04 lb (8.34 lb per gallon divided by 8), so 1,234 / 1.04 = about 1,180 pints per day, roughly 148 gallons.

Our own, from the saturation tables: 60 grains per pound is about a 53 degrees F dew point at sea level, and 20 grains per pound is about 26 degrees F. That target is below freezing, past what a coil can do, which is the point of the wheel. For the dew point and grains idea, see Psychrometrics for Technicians. For a small home unit and its pints-per-day label, see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing.

Service checks

Munters' user manual for an electric-reactivation unit, and the federal alert, point to the same list.

  • Filters. The alert suggests changing filters about every two months and vacuuming the wheel face. Munters' manual has a service lamp for filter changes and tells you to let the heater cool and lock out the main power first.
  • Drive belt and seals. The Munters manual schedules drive belt and rotor gasket inspection and replacement. The alert gives seals a life of about five years and says drive parts, bearings, fans and belts need periodic service.
  • Reactivation heater. The manual has you inspect the heater coils and replace the high temperature cut-out on schedule. If the cut-out trips, find out why; do not bypass it. Switching off the main power with the heater hot can stop the reactivation fan and trip it.
  • Airflow balance. The manual says never to set airflows above the rated values, and to run the unit at full effect for about 10 minutes and check that the temperature rise across the reactivation heater is within the specified range.

Hot surfaces and a unit that restarts after a power cut are both called out in the manual. Lock out first, wait for the heater to cool, and leave commissioning to people trained on the model.

Field card: Desiccant dehumidifiers

Coils condense, wheels adsorb, and heat reactivates.

  • Process air is dried. Reactivation air is heated and carries the water out.
  • Silica gel and zeolite are the common desiccants.
  • Coils bottom out near a 40 degrees F dew point; wheels go far lower.
  • Dry air leaves warm, so plan post cooling.
  • Active wheel adds heat; passive enthalpy wheel does not.
  • Moisture removal lb/h = cfm x 4.5 x grains removed / 7,000 (our own).
  • Check filters, belt, seals, heater cut-out and airflow, locked out and cool.
