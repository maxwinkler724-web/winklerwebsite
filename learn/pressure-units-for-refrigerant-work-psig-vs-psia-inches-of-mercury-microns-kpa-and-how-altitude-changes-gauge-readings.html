Pressure Units for Refrigerant Work: psig vs psia, Inches of Mercury, Microns, kPa and How Altitude Changes Gauge Readings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A manifold gauge, a micron gauge and an EPA rule can all describe the same pressure in different units. This lesson is the translation table: what each unit measures, how to convert between them, and why a gauge reading moves when you drive up a mountain.

Gauge pressure versus absolute pressure

Absolute pressure counts from a perfect vacuum, which is zero. Gauge pressure counts from the air around you. A gauge with its needle resting at zero is not measuring zero pressure; it is measuring zero difference from the local atmosphere. The two are linked by one line: absolute = gauge + atmospheric. The units carry the clue. A trailing "g" (psig) means gauge, and a trailing "a" (psia) means absolute.

Standard atmospheric pressure at sea level is 1 atm, which NIST SP 811 lists as exactly 101,325 pascals. In the units we use at the bench that is about 14.7 psia, 29.92 inches of mercury and 760 mm of mercury. NOAA also gives 29.92 inches of mercury and 1013.25 millibars for standard sea-level pressure. Everything else in this lesson is arithmetic on that one anchor. For how pressure relates to refrigerant temperature, see Pressure-Temperature Basics; we do not repeat it here.

The units you will meet

  • psig and psia. Pounds per square inch, gauge or absolute. Positive pressures on a manifold gauge are psig. NIST SP 811 gives 1 psi as 6,894.757 pascals.
  • Inches of mercury (in. Hg). The height of a mercury column. On the vacuum side of a compound gauge, the scale counts down from zero at atmosphere, so it reads "inches of vacuum." A deeper vacuum is a bigger number. Full vacuum is about 29.92 inches of vacuum at sea level.
  • Millimeters of mercury (mm Hg). The same idea in metric, usually written as an absolute value. NIST lists the conventional mm Hg as 133.3224 pascals and the inch of mercury (32 degrees F) as 3,386.38 pascals. Dividing, one inch is 25.4 mm, as you would expect.
  • Microns. In vacuum work a micron is a micron of mercury: one thousandth of a millimeter of mercury, always absolute. Since one inch is 25.4 mm, 1 inch of mercury equals 25,400 microns. The micron gauge counts from zero up, so a smaller number is a deeper vacuum, the opposite direction of inches of vacuum.
  • kPa and bar. Metric units on newer gauges and instruments. NIST gives 1 bar as exactly 100,000 pascals, so 1 bar equals 100 kPa. A gauge may show kPa or bar as gauge or as absolute, so read the label.

Keep the direction straight: psig, kPa and bar go up as pressure rises; inches of vacuum go up as pressure falls; microns go up as pressure rises, but only at the very bottom of the scale.

Why the EPA writes evacuation levels the way it does

Table 1 in 40 CFR 82.156 gives required recovery levels in inches of mercury vacuum, "relative to standard atmospheric pressure of 29.9 inches Hg." For appliances covered after November 15, 1993, the table reads 0 inches for very high-pressure appliances and for high-pressure appliances under 200 pounds of charge; 10 inches for high-pressure appliances of 200 pounds or more and for medium-pressure appliances under 200 pounds; and 15 inches for medium-pressure appliances of 200 pounds or more. The low-pressure row is different: 25 mm Hg absolute.

The rule itself does not explain the choice, so the following is our reading. Positive-pressure systems are recovered down toward atmosphere, where the gauge a technician already holds reads inches of vacuum. A low-pressure system is already below atmosphere in normal operation, so a fixed absolute number is the cleaner way to state its target. Whichever way a question or a rule words it, convert to the same basis before comparing. For the recovery side in detail, see Recovery Machine Care and Faster Recovery.

How altitude moves the readings

Air pressure falls as you climb. NOAA says the number of air molecules drops with elevation, with about half of the atmosphere lying below roughly 18,000 feet. NASA Glenn publishes a curve-fit for the lower atmosphere: p = 101.29 x [(T + 273.1) / 288.08]^5.256 in kPa, with T = 15.04 - 0.00649 x h in degrees C and h in meters.

Because gauge pressure is measured against whatever atmosphere is present, a sealed system at fixed absolute pressure reads a higher number in psig the higher you are. The refrigerant has not changed; the reference has. In practice, zero a manifold gauge where you are working, not at the shop. For checking the instruments themselves, see Checking HVAC Meters and Gauges in the Field.

Micron gauges and absolute-pressure sensors do not have this problem. They read from zero, so 500 microns is 500 microns on a beach or a ridge. That is the reason deep vacuum is judged in microns. How to pull and verify that vacuum is in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics and Vacuum Pump Care.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not from a source. Assumptions: standard sea-level atmosphere of 14.696 psia (101.325 kPa, from NIST SP 811); 1 psi = 6.894757 kPa; 1 in. Hg = 3.38638 kPa and 25,400 microns; and, for the altitude case, an atmosphere at 5,000 ft (1,524 m) of 84.4 kPa, which we computed from the NASA Glenn curve-fit above. The fit gives T = 15.04 - 0.00649 x 1,524 = 5.15 C, then p = 101.29 x (278.25 / 288.08)^5.256 = 84.4 kPa, or 12.24 psia. That is an estimate for a standard atmosphere; real weather moves it.

  1. 500 microns to inches Hg. 500 / 25,400 = 0.0197 in. Hg absolute. As inches of vacuum at sea level: 29.92 - 0.0197 = 29.90 in. Hg vacuum.
  2. 500 microns to psia. 500 microns is 0.5 mm Hg = 0.5 x 0.1333224 kPa = 0.0667 kPa. Divided by 6.894757 gives 0.00967 psia. In round numbers, about one hundredth of a psi.
  3. 4 in. Hg vacuum to psia at sea level. 4 x 3.38638 = 13.545 kPa below atmosphere. 13.545 / 6.894757 = 1.96 psi. So 14.696 - 1.96 = 12.73 psia.
  4. 120 psig to psia and kPa absolute. 120 + 14.696 = 134.70 psia. Then 134.70 x 6.894757 = 928.7 kPa absolute. As a check, 120 psig is 827.4 kPa gauge, plus 101.325 gives 928.7.
  5. Same absolute pressure, two altitudes. Hold 134.70 psia inside a sealed system. At sea level a gauge shows 134.70 - 14.696 = 120.0 psig. At 5,000 ft with 12.24 psia atmosphere, it shows 134.70 - 12.24 = 122.5 psig. The gauge reads about 2.5 psi higher with the same pressure inside.
  6. The vacuum side at altitude. That 12.73 psia from step 3 is only 0.49 psi above the 5,000 ft atmosphere, so at 5,000 ft a compound gauge would show about 0.5 psig, a small positive reading, not 4 in. of vacuum. The same absolute pressure is a vacuum at sea level and a positive gauge reading up high. At 5,000 ft atmosphere is only about 24.9 in. Hg (84.4 / 3.38638), so no vacuum gauge can show more than about 24.9 inches there.

The takeaway for altitude work: for anything near a vacuum limit, trust an absolute scale (microns or mm Hg), and know which atmosphere your gauge is zeroed to. How the rule applies at altitude is a question for the regulator or your certifying organization, not for this page.

Field card: Pressure Units for Refrigerant Work

Absolute = gauge + atmosphere. Microns are always absolute.

  • Standard atmosphere: 14.7 psia, 29.92 in. Hg, 760 mm Hg, 101.325 kPa (NIST SP 811).
  • 1 psi = 6.895 kPa. 1 bar = 100 kPa. 1 in. Hg = 3.386 kPa = 25.4 mm Hg = 25,400 microns.
  • Inches of vacuum count down from atmosphere; microns count up from zero.
  • EPA recovery table: inches Hg vacuum for most rows; low-pressure appliances in mm Hg absolute (25).
  • Higher altitude means lower atmosphere, so the same inside pressure reads more psig and less vacuum.
  • Zero the gauge where you work. Use an absolute-reading gauge for deep vacuum.
