VAV Boxes Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

In a variable air volume, or VAV, system one central air handler feeds many zones, and each zone gets its own box that decides how much air to let through.

The big idea

Older constant-volume systems held airflow steady and changed the air temperature. A VAV system does the opposite: it keeps supply air at a steady cool temperature and changes how much air each room receives. That gives tighter control, less fan energy and quieter operation at part load.

What is in the box

A VAV box, also called a terminal unit, is basically a damper with an actuator and a controller, plus an airflow sensor at the inlet. Most boxes are pressure independent, meaning the sensor lets the damper hold the requested airflow even when duct pressure changes. The controller compares room temperature with the setpoint and modulates the damper between a minimum and a maximum airflow.

Reheat

Because every zone gets the same cool air, a room that needs less cooling, or heat, needs a way to warm it up. Many boxes include a reheat coil, either hot water or electric. The coil warms the air so the room does not overcool, while the box still delivers the ventilation air the room needs.

Why minimum airflow matters

A box should not shut completely off in an occupied space, because people still need fresh air and the room needs air movement. Conventional designs hold a minimum airflow, often somewhere between about 20 and 50 percent of design airflow depending on the design and ventilation needs, before reheat turns on. Some newer sequences use a much lower minimum and let airflow rise again when heating is needed. The right numbers come from the design, the controls sequence and the ventilation requirement, not from this lesson.

The fan side

As boxes close, duct pressure rises, and the supply fan slows to keep a steady duct static pressure. As boxes open, the fan speeds up. This depends on a working pressure sensor and a variable-speed drive. If the controls respond too slowly, ducts can be overpressurized, so sensor and tuning problems matter.

Common problems at a general level

  • A zone that is too hot or too cold: check the box's reported airflow versus its setpoint, then the damper actuator and the room sensor.
  • No heat from reheat: check for a call, hot-water flow and valve, or electric coil staging and its safety limits.
  • Noise: high velocity from a box stuck wide open, or a duct pressure setpoint set too high.
  • Flow sensor errors: kinked or plugged tubing, or a bad sensor.
  • Many boxes sit above ceilings, so be careful with ladders, ceiling tiles and unknown wiring.

Safety

Electric reheat coils run on high voltage and have limit devices that must stay in place. Lock out and tag out before opening a panel or touching a rotating part, and never bypass a safety device to keep something running. Never jumper a reheat limit or flow switch to get heat.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the equipment in front of you and the rules where you work always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It gives no setpoints, and your site's numbers are the ones that count.

Field card: VAV Boxes Basics

One air handler, many zones, one damper per zone.

  • Supply air stays cool; each box changes how much air the room gets.
  • A box has a damper, actuator, flow sensor and controller.
  • Reheat warms the air when the room needs less cooling or some heat.
  • Minimum airflow protects ventilation and comfort.
  • Never bypass a reheat safety limit.
