Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats

A thermostat is a set of switches and a small computer sitting on a 24-volt circuit. Once you know what each terminal letter asks the equipment to do, a wall full of colored wires stops being a mystery.

Terminals are calls, not colors

A low-voltage thermostat does not carry the power that runs the equipment. It simply connects 24-volt power from a transformer to different terminals, and each connection is a request: run the fan, start cooling, start heat. Manufacturer support pages stress that wire colors have no standard meaning, so identify a wire by the terminal it lands on, and when in doubt read the markings on the control board.

Common terminal letters, as typically described by thermostat makers and technician sources:

  • R (sometimes Rh or Rc): the 24-volt power side. Some systems have one R wire, others have separate heating and cooling power wires, and a small jumper between the terminals joins them for single-transformer systems.
  • C: the common, the return path to the transformer that completes the 24-volt circuit so the thermostat can be powered.
  • G: the indoor fan.
  • Y or Y1 / Y2: cooling or compressor stages. On a heat pump, Y calls the outdoor contactor.
  • W or W1 / W2: heating stages. On a heat pump, the second stage is often auxiliary or backup heat.
  • O/B: the reversing valve on a heat pump. Whether the thermostat energizes it in cooling or in heating depends on the equipment, so follow the equipment and thermostat instructions.
  • E: emergency heat on some heat pump systems.

Why the common wire matters

Older mechanical thermostats were just switches and needed no power of their own. A Wi-Fi thermostat has a screen, a radio and a processor, so it needs steady power. A C wire gives it a dedicated return path. Without one, some products try to borrow a trickle of power through the equipment wires, which can lead to odd behavior. A manufacturer's adapter kit can create a common from existing wires, but it comes with its own cautions: use it only when no spare wire exists, label every wire before disconnecting, and on a zoned system install it between the thermostat and the zone panel, not downstream.

What a smart thermostat adds

ENERGY STAR describes a smart thermostat as a Wi-Fi device that can learn your preferences, build a schedule that moves toward saving temperatures when you are asleep or away, use phone location to prepare the house, and report equipment use. Certification means the model was verified with field data to save energy, but the page does not promise a specific percentage, and neither should you. Check compatibility with the exact system before buying. Systems with communicating terminals (marked like A/B/C or 1/2/3), line-voltage heaters, multi-speed fan terminals, and dual-fuel setups often need a particular thermostat or a professional.

Replacing a thermostat safely

Turn off power to the equipment first. Take a photo of the existing wiring with the terminal markings visible, label each wire with its terminal letter (both letters if a terminal has two, such as W and O/B), and only then disconnect. Wires that go to no terminal stay unlabeled and noted. Never jumper terminals to bypass a safety or to force a call you cannot explain. Thick black or red wiring is a sign of line voltage, which is a different job with different rules.

Field card: Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats

The terminal is the truth, the color is only a guess.

  • Photograph and label every wire by terminal before removing anything.
  • R is 24-volt power, C is the return, G is fan, Y is cooling, W is heat, O/B is the heat pump reversing valve.
  • Smart thermostats need a steady common wire or a manufacturer-approved adapter.
  • Power off at the equipment before touching the wiring.
  • Do not guess on communicating, line-voltage, multi-speed or dual-fuel systems.

New Jersey note. NJ's HVACR rules (N.J.A.C. 13:32A-1.3, amended 2022) let a master HVACR contractor do electrical work up to 30 volts, so 24 volt control wiring is in scope for that license. The under-10 volt exemption belongs to electrical contractors. Whether a non-master employee may do the same wiring is not settled by the sources we read, so confirm with the Board.
