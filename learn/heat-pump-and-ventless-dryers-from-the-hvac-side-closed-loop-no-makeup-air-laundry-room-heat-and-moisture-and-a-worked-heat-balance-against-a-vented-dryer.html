Heat Pump and Ventless Dryers From the HVAC Side: Room Heat, No Makeup Air and a Heat Balance

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A vented dryer is a small exhaust fan that throws heated house air outdoors. A heat pump dryer keeps its air inside the cabinet and gives its heat to the room instead. Customers call about the laundry room, not the refrigerant circuit, so this lesson looks at the dryer the way an HVAC tech does: air balance, room heat and moisture, and a heat balance we work out ourselves.

Check the label and the manual

Check the label and the manual. Models differ in voltage, drain layout, room limits and cleaning schedules. The federal and national-lab pages we read describe dryers in general, and the installation guide for the exact model controls the job. This page is general education.

How a heat pump dryer works, briefly

A fan pulls warm, damp air out of the drum and pushes it across a cold coil, the evaporator. The air drops below its dew point, so water vapor turns to liquid and the air leaves cool and dry. The air then crosses a hot coil, the condenser, which reheats it, and goes back into the drum for another pass. A compressor moves refrigerant between the two coils, so one circuit does both jobs. The same air goes round and round, and nothing is exhausted. A federal laboratory paper on a heat pump dryer model says the same thing: no fresh air is introduced and no exhaust air is released. The sister lesson, Heat Pump Clothes Dryers, covers the loop, the lint screens and the energy use in more depth. This page is about what the machine does to the room and the house.

What a vented dryer does to the house

A vented dryer moves air out of the house every minute it runs. National-lab guidance says vented dryers contribute to depressurization of the home, which can pull unconditioned air in from the garage, attic, crawlspace or outdoors. It adds that this can make the heating and cooling equipment work harder and can encourage backdrafting of atmospherically vented combustion appliances. The air that leaves must be replaced by leaks or by a deliberate makeup air opening, and that replacement air arrives at outdoor temperature. In winter the heating system pays to warm it. For the sizing side of that, see our infiltration lesson and the combustion air lesson if a gas appliance shares the room.

A heat pump dryer has no exhaust, so the same guidance says it does not depressurize the space. The vent duct, its length limit and its lint fire risk are covered in the dryer vent lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

Where the heat and moisture go

The closed loop does not make heat disappear. The same national-lab guidance says condensing dryers release heat into the space they sit in. Nearly all the electricity the machine draws ends up as heat in the cabinet, and the cabinet gives it to the room. Water is the other half of the story: the water that leaves the clothes is condensed and drained or collected, so no vent is needed. The guidance says the condensate must be drained or emptied after each load. We found no federal figure for how much moisture reaches the room from these dryers, so we do not give a number.

  • Room size and air. The guidance says to avoid a small enclosed room with no windows and to give the machine sufficient airflow. A closet with a louvered door, or a door left open during a cycle, helps. In summer the added heat is also added cooling load, so a tiny unconditioned room can get warm.
  • Minimum room temperature. The room must stay at or above 40 degrees Fahrenheit for the dryer to work properly. That rules out an unheated garage in winter.
  • Neighbors. Do not put one next to a refrigerator, because the warm air makes the refrigerator work harder.

Cycle time and cleaning

Expect longer cycles. The national-lab guidance says condensing dryers may take as much as double the time of a conventional dryer, and a federal energy research page lists longer cycle time as one of the design challenges. A national lab paper models a standard heat pump dryer at 70 minutes for a test load. Because the coils work with the air, anything that blocks airflow costs time and energy. The guidance lists the routine: clean the primary lint screen after every load, empty a secondary lint trap monthly, and rinse and clean the condenser about once every few months. Unplug the dryer before opening a panel and follow the manual for reaching the coil. A slow dryer with a clean screen deserves a look at the drain or tank, the coil and the room temperature before anyone opens the sealed system.

Worked example (our own): heat taken out versus heat put in

This is our own example. It compares the heating load a vented dryer creates with the heat a heat pump dryer gives the room. Every input below is an assumption unless a source is named.

Assumptions (our own). Vented dryer exhaust of 150 cfm. For reference, one laboratory paper listed measured airflow of 147 cfm for a standard electric dryer; the table did not say whether this was exhaust flow, so we treat 150 cfm as our assumed round number. Run time of 45 minutes (0.75 hour) per load. Indoor air at 70°F and outdoor air at 30°F, so a temperature difference of 40°F. For the heat pump dryer, the national lab paper reports a theoretical CEF of 5.2 lb per kWh for its model; we assume a standard 8.45 lb test load, so 8.45 ÷ 5.2 = about 1.62 kWh per load. We also assume the 70 minute cycle from the same paper, and that all of that electricity ends up as heat in the room (a simplification).

Step 1, vented dryer heat to replace the exhaust air. Sensible heat in Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference. So 1.08 × 150 = 162, and 162 × 40 = 6,480 Btu/h while it runs.

Step 2, per load. 6,480 × 0.75 hour = 4,860 Btu per load. In kWh that is 4,860 ÷ 3,412 = about 1.42 kWh of heating that the furnace or heat pump must supply.

Step 3, heat pump dryer heat to the room. 1.62 kWh × 3,412 Btu/kWh = about 5,530 Btu per load. Over 70 minutes (1.167 hours) that is 5,530 ÷ 1.167 = about 4,740 Btu/h, an average electric input of about 1.39 kW, like a small space heater.

Step 4, compare. The vented dryer costs the house about 4,860 Btu per load in makeup air heating, and also sends its own heat outdoors. The heat pump dryer adds about 5,530 Btu per load to the room instead. In winter that heat offsets some of the heating system's work. In summer it adds roughly the same amount to the cooling load.

Limits. The 1.08 relation is for standard air at ordinary conditions; our formulas lesson explains it. Real leakage and real dryer heat depend on the house and the model. Treat the result as a size-of-the-effect estimate, not a measured value.

Field card: Heat Pump and Ventless Dryers From the HVAC Side

No vent means no exhaust, but the heat stays in the room.

  • Closed air loop: cold coil removes water, hot coil reheats the same air.
  • No exhaust, so no depressurization and no makeup air from the dryer.
  • The room gets the dryer's heat. Give it airflow and keep it at or above 40°F.
  • Not next to a refrigerator. Drain or empty the condensate after each load.
  • Cycles can take much longer than a vented dryer.
  • Clean the lint screen each load, the secondary trap monthly, the condenser every few months.
  • Our example: 1.08 × 150 × 40 = 6,480 Btu/h, 4,860 Btu per 45 minute load, versus about 5,530 Btu per load from the heat pump dryer.
