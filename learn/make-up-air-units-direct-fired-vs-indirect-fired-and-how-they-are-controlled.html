Make-Up Air Units: Direct-Fired vs Indirect-Fired and How They Are Controlled

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A make-up air unit (MAU) brings outdoor air into a building and heats it so the space does not freeze or sit under a vacuum. The big split among gas-fired units is where the flame sits: in the airstream (direct-fired) or inside a heat exchanger (indirect-fired). That one choice changes the venting, the safeties and what you check on a service call.

Why a building needs make-up air

Exhaust fans throw air out of the building, and that air has to come back from somewhere. Where nothing is supplied on purpose, it leaks in through doors, flues and cracks. Our kitchen ventilation lesson covers the hood side of this and the symptoms of an unbalanced kitchen, such as hard-to-open doors and disturbed pilot lights. A make-up air unit is the planned path. The kitchen lesson also notes that building pressure limits and exhaust rates come from the adopted code and the engineer, not from a tech's guess. For airflow basics see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Direct-fired: the burner is in the airstream

CaptiveAire's literature for its Model AD heater says the burner burns directly in the air stream, so combustion products mix with the make-up air that goes into the space. There is no heat exchanger and no flue to carry them away. Modine's MDB/MRB manual says combustion products enter the airstream, that its MDB units draw all their air from outdoors, and that direct-fired units must not sit downstream of a refrigerant-based cooling system. Modine's manual lists the standard for this type as ANSI Z83.4 for the 100 percent outdoor-air version, and ANSI Z83.18 for the MRB return-air version, which may recirculate room air only if that air bypasses the burner. Reznor's manual also cites Z83.18, for carbon dioxide levels in the discharge of its recirculating model. Which standard applies to a given unit is on its rating plate and in its manual.

Because the flame heats the air that people breathe, burner air velocity matters. Reznor's manual says profile plates set the velocity over the burner and that a low and a high air pressure switch watch it, and warns that too little combustion air causes incomplete combustion and carbon monoxide. Greenheck's direct-fired manual likewise says improper burner air velocity may produce elevated by-products of combustion and gives a burner static pressure to check with a manometer. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics. Reznor says not to enlarge burner ports or adjust profile plates without contacting the factory, and Modine says to clean burner ports only by hand with a drill bit, without distorting or enlarging them.

Indirect-fired: a heat exchanger and a flue

Modine's indirect-fired outdoor page describes a stainless steel heat exchanger, a burner and a power exhauster, with an 80 percent thermal efficiency rating. Trane's installation manual for its indirect-fired make-up air line adds that these units are power-vented with separate combustion air and exhaust piping, that metal pipe only is allowed (no PVC), that equivalent vent length must stay between 5 and 50 feet, and that the vent system is part of the unit and must not be altered in the field. The combustion gases leave through the vent, so they do not enter the supply air. The trade-off is a heat exchanger that can crack or foul, and a vent system to keep clear.

Indirect units also have a pressure switch at the power vent. Trane's manual says ignition is energized only when the pressure difference between the air inlet and the exhaust is correct, and that you should never fire the unit with the power vent inoperable. Compare the sequence with Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation.

How the controls fit together

Discharge air temperature control. Most make-up air units hold a discharge temperature rather than a room temperature. Modine's direct-fired manuals describe a Maxitrol System 14 with a discharge sensor and a remote dial (55 to 90°F in the MDB/MRB manual), and a System 44 that adds a room thermostat while the discharge sensor acts as high and low limit. A building automation signal of 4 to 20 mA or 0 to 10 VDC is an option. Reznor describes an automatic reset limit that shuts the burner off when discharge temperature reaches its setpoint.

Modulating gas valve. A temperature control amplifier drives the valve to hold the setpoint. Modine's MCV/MVV manual lists turndown up to 30 to 1. Reznor describes low fire passing through a mechanical bypass, with modulation from about 1/25 of full fire to full fire. Trane lists electronic modulation from 40 to 100 percent of input on its indirect units. Redundant main shutoff valves sit ahead of the modulating valve (Modine).

Airflow proving. Direct-fired burners are interlocked with a low airflow switch that proves enough air before the burner can run, and a high airflow cutoff in Modine's units. Reznor gives example switch points of about 0.25 and 0.75 inch w.c.; these are one model's numbers, so use your unit's manual.

Exhaust fan and building pressure. Modine's MDB/MRB manual describes an optional building pressure control (photohelic) that adds outside air, or raises blower speed with a VFD, as exhaust fans run. Its MCV/MVV manual says exhaust fan interlocks and fire damper end switches are wired into the safety limit circuit so they shut the heater down. If the exhaust fan runs and the make-up unit is down, the kitchen lesson above shows what you will see.

Freeze protection. Modine's optional timed freeze protection shuts the unit down if discharge air falls below setpoint once a start-up bypass timer runs out (adjustable 1 to 10 minutes on the MDB/MRB; 5 minutes on the MCV/MVV). Where a unit has none, Modine says a field-supplied low-temperature limit is required where freezing is a risk. Greenheck gives a typical setting of 5 minutes and 45°F. Trane says evaporative cooler traps should be primed with glycol in winter.

Flame safeguards. The flame safeguard controller runs pre-purge, lights the pilot or spark, proves flame, and locks out if it cannot. Modine's MDB/MRB manual gives a pilot prove time of 10 to 25 seconds before lockout and a minimum flame signal of 1.25 VDC. Reznor gives a 15-second trial, one retry, then lockout and a minimum signal of 0.5 microamp. These numbers differ by control, which is why the unit's own manual rules. Background is in Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics.

Maintenance items the makers list

  • Filters, quarterly in Reznor's schedule, and the pressure sensing tubes for the airflow switches.
  • Burner and pilot inspection and cleaning, annually (Reznor); ports cleaned only with a hand drill bit, never enlarged.
  • Belt tension and bearing lubrication (Reznor, Modine, Greenheck); avoid over-greasing, and do not mix petroleum and silicone grease (Modine).
  • Manifold and pilot gas pressures, and gas-tightness of the safety shutoff valves (Reznor, Modine).
  • Flame signal and burner profile pressure after service (Modine).
  • Winterizing coils and draining evaporative lines before freezing (Greenheck).
  • Replace wet electrical or mechanical parts instead of reusing them (Modine).

Disconnect and lock out power and gas first. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Test gas joints with leak solution, never a flame (Greenheck).

Worked example: how much heat does the air need?

The sensible heat formula for air is Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT. ACHR News explains the 1.08: 60 minutes per hour × 0.075 lb/ft³ (standard air) × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F = 1.08. CaptiveAire's toolbox uses the same constants and adds a density correction for altitude. ACHR notes the factor is a convenience value that drifts when the air is much colder or hotter than about 70°F, so treat results as estimates.

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: a unit moves 5,000 CFM, the design outdoor temperature is 10°F, and the discharge setpoint is 65°F.

  1. Temperature rise: ΔT = 65 − 10 = 55°F.
  2. Heat needed: 1.08 × 5,000 × 55 = 5,400 × 55 = 297,000 Btu/h.
  3. If it were an indirect unit at the 80 percent thermal efficiency Modine lists for its line: 297,000 ÷ 0.80 = 371,250 Btu/h input (hypothetical efficiency match).

For a direct-fired unit, the maker's maximum temperature rise matters too. Modine's MCV/MVV manual lists 131°F on natural gas for its burner, so a 55°F rise is well inside that limit. Your real design values come from the engineer's drawings and the unit's data plate.

Field card: Make-Up Air Units

Same job, different flame location.

  • Make-up air replaces what exhaust fans remove.
  • Direct-fired: burner in the airstream, no flue, combustion products mix with supply air; maker lists Z83.4 or Z83.18.
  • Indirect-fired: heat exchanger, power vent, gases leave through the flue.
  • Controls: discharge sensor, modulating valve, airflow switches, freeze stat, flame safeguard.
  • Heat estimate: 1.08 × CFM × ΔT = Btu/h (about 70°F air).
  • Never bypass an interlock. Follow the unit's own manual.
