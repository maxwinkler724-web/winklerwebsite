How CO2 Sensors Work and Drift: NDIR, Baseline Calibration, Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A wall-mounted CO2 sensor looks like a simple box, but its reading steers how much outdoor air a building conditions. This lesson explains how the sensor measures, why the number wanders over the years, what its self-calibration assumes, and how to check and place one. Control strategy is covered in the related demand-controlled ventilation lesson, so we stay on the instrument itself.

How the sensor measures

Almost every sensor sold for building controls uses non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) absorption. A small infrared source shines through a short optical cell that room air diffuses into, and a detector with an optical filter measures how much light survives. CO2 absorbs strongly near 4.26 micrometers, a band a federal space-agency technical paper on this sensor type identifies, along with a weaker one near 3.95 micrometers. More CO2 in the cell means less light reaches the detector, following the Beer-Lambert relation: the absorbed fraction depends on the gas absorption coefficient and the path length. Because the filter picks the wavelength and no prism or grating spreads the light, the method is called non-dispersive.

A national laboratory field report on classroom and office sensors found that every sensor it evaluated was this type, that most sampled by diffusion with no pump, and that default ranges ran from 0 to 2000 ppm. A separate laboratory guidance page adds that a sensor with a lower top of range is usually better for a 1000 ppm setpoint than one that reads to 5000 ppm.

Single beam, dual beam and dual wavelength

  • Single beam, single wavelength: one source, one detector band. Simple and cheap, but anything that dims the light, such as an aging lamp or a dirty cell, is read as extra CO2, so the reading creeps upward.
  • Dual wavelength: a second detector band sits where CO2 does not absorb. It sees the same dimming from source aging or dirt, so the ratio of the two signals can cancel part of it.
  • Dual beam or two lamps: a second light path or second source acts as a reference. The national laboratory report notes that some designs use two lamps or two wavelengths to correct for drift sources such as reduced lamp output.

Two-channel designs reduce slow optical drift but do not remove it. Both channels still rely on the same cell, and neither can fix an error in how the factory zero was set.

Why sensors drift

A federal laboratory review of demand-controlled ventilation points out that NDIR and similar detection can be affected by light source aging, and that particle buildup on the sensor can matter too. The field report above saw slight soiling or corrosion of optical cells and holes in the diffusion fabric of two sensors. The same report found that sensor age alone was not a consistent predictor of error, and that no facility manager it surveyed had recalibrated a sensor after installation. Maker calibration intervals it collected ranged from six to twelve months to "never needs calibration," with five years common. That wide spread is the reason to check rather than assume.

Automatic baseline calibration, and where it breaks

Many sensors reset their own zero using an automatic baseline calibration routine. The sensor watches its lowest reading over a past period, assumes that low point is outdoor air at roughly 400 to 420 ppm, and shifts its calibration so the low point reads that value. A national laboratory guidance page states the weakness directly: the method assumes the space falls to outdoor levels, so it is unsuited to buildings that are continuously occupied or spaces that stay above outdoor levels. It notes that some classrooms stay above 400 ppm overnight on weekdays.

Think of 24-hour spaces: dispatch rooms, residential care areas, a plant control room, a call center on three shifts. If the true low point is, say, 520 ppm, the routine decides 520 is 400 and shifts the whole scale down by 120 ppm. The sensor then reads low everywhere, the controller sees a cleaner room than it has, and outdoor air is cut. Ask the maker whether the routine can be turned off, how long its look-back window is, and whether it expects a recorded empty period.

Our own worked example: a sensor that reads 75 ppm high

This is our own example, not from a source. A meeting room has steady occupancy and a fixed ventilation rate, and its controller modulates outdoor air to hold the sensor reading at a control point of 1000 ppm.

Assumptions (ours): the room air is well mixed; the room is at steady state; outdoor CO2 is 420 ppm and constant; each person gives off 0.011 cfm of CO2 (an assumed round figure for a seated adult); the drift is a constant offset of +75 ppm; 20 people are in the room. The steady-state relation is outdoor air per person = G / (Cs - Co), with G the CO2 generated per person as a volume fraction per cfm, Cs the room CO2 and Co the outdoor CO2.

  1. Intended: room at 1000 ppm. Cs - Co = 1000 - 420 = 580 ppm = 0.000580. Outdoor air = 0.011 / 0.000580 = 18.97, about 19 cfm per person, or 379 cfm for 20 people.
  2. With the +75 ppm drift: the controller holds the reading at 1000, so the true room value is 1000 - 75 = 925 ppm. Cs - Co = 925 - 420 = 505 ppm = 0.000505. Outdoor air = 0.011 / 0.000505 = 21.78, about 21.8 cfm per person, or 436 cfm for 20 people.
  3. Change: 580 / 505 = 1.149, about 15 percent more outdoor air (57 cfm more for the room). The error is on the safe side for air quality but costs conditioning energy and can overload a coil in extreme weather.
  4. The opposite sign: a sensor that reads 75 ppm low lets the room reach 1075 ppm. Cs - Co = 655 ppm, so 0.011 / 0.000655 = 16.8 cfm per person, 336 cfm for 20 people. That is 580 / 655 = 0.885, about 11.5 percent less outdoor air than intended, the unsafe direction. The self-calibration failure above produces this kind of low reading.

Check: the ratio does not depend on the 0.011 assumption, since G cancels. The same 75 ppm hurts more at a lower control point. At 800 ppm the denominators are 380 and 305, so the ratio is 1.246, about 25 percent more air. Real rooms are not perfectly steady, so treat this as a size-of-the-problem estimate.

Field checks

The national laboratory field report used two methods you can copy in spirit. One compared each installed sensor with a co-located, recently calibrated reference instrument. The other exposed sensors to primary standard gases at several concentrations, with gas known to about 5 percent. It recommended checks near 1000 ppm and near zero or about 500 ppm. In that study, 40 percent of sensors checked at roughly 1000 ppm were off by more than 75 ppm, so a pass is not a given.

  1. Let the reference instrument and sensor sit in the same spot for the time the maker states, away from your own breath.
  2. Read the sensor at the controller or building system, not only on its display. The same report found building values matched sensor displays closely, but confirm yours.
  3. Compare against a gas of known value, or a reference. If the difference exceeds the allowed error, follow the maker's calibration steps and record the before and after readings.
  4. Check the outdoor reading too. A sensor near an intake or a busy road may see more than 420 ppm, and the controller's outdoor assumption may need adjusting.

Placement

A federal laboratory review advises keeping sensors away from doors, windows, air intakes and exhausts, and away from where occupants breathe on them. The field report found wall-mounted readings in meeting rooms sometimes varied by more than 200 ppm, partly because of nearby exhaled breath. A state energy code it discusses places the sensing point 3 to 6 feet above the floor, and a draft of a ventilation standard discussed there would keep sensors out of return plenums and off the plane of the return grille. We would also keep a sensor out of the throw of a supply diffuser, since moving supply air is not room air (this is our own reasoning, not a quoted source). One sensor in a shared return cannot honestly control several rooms of different occupancy.

Field card: CO2 sensors

A sensor only reports what its zero allows.

  • NDIR: infrared through a cell, CO2 absorbs near 4.26 micrometers, less light means more CO2.
  • Dual wavelength or dual beam designs cancel part of lamp aging, not all.
  • Auto baseline calibration assumes the room hits outdoor CO2 (about 400 to 420 ppm); 24-hour spaces break that and the sensor reads low.
  • Our own example: +75 ppm drift at a 1000 ppm control point gives about 15 percent more outdoor air; -75 ppm gives about 11.5 percent less.
  • Check against a reference instrument or known gas; do not trust age or a long calibration interval.
  • Mount 3 to 6 feet up, away from doors, supply diffusers, intakes and people.
