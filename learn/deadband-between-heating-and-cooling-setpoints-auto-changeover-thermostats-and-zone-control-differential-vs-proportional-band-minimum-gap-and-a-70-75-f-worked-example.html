Deadband Between Heating and Cooling Setpoints: Differential, Proportional Band and the Minimum Gap

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A thermostat that can both heat and cool needs a gap between the two jobs. That gap is the deadband. Too small, and the equipment fights itself or flips from heating to cooling and back. This lesson explains the term, separates it from two look-alike words, shows a worked example, and covers how to set and check it.

What deadband means

In an auto changeover thermostat or a building automation zone, there are two setpoints: a heating setpoint and a cooling setpoint. A national laboratory guide for small and medium commercial buildings defines deadband as the temperature range between the cooling and heating setpoints. Inside that range the zone asks for neither heating nor cooling. It is a deliberate gap, not a fault.

The same guide warns that if the occupied deadband is too small, the unit could cycle between heating and cooling. It also warns that an unoccupied deadband that is too large can make it hard to bring the space back to the occupied setpoint before people arrive. Both ends matter.

Deadband, differential and proportional band

These three words get mixed up on the job. The difference is where each one sits.

  • Deadband sits between the heating setpoint and the cooling setpoint. It separates two different modes.
  • Differential (hysteresis) belongs to one mode. A university lecture on on/off control describes hysteresis as an output that depends on the input and also on the previous state of the output. Adding it to an on/off controller creates a differential gap, such as a furnace that turns on at 72 F and off at 75 F. The gap trades a small known control error for fewer cycles. Our other lesson on thermostat cycle rate covers how differential sets cycles per hour.
  • Proportional band belongs to modulating control, where the output moves smoothly instead of snapping on and off. A university controls overview separates two-position (on/off) control from proportional control, in which energy delivered follows demand. In plain terms, the proportional band is the stretch of temperature error over which the output travels from minimum to maximum. We explain it here in our own words; see the PID lesson linked below for tuning.

A short memory aid: deadband is the gap between modes, differential is the gap between on and off within a mode, and proportional band is the width of the smooth ramp within a mode. A zone can use all three at once.

Why a small deadband causes fighting and flip-flopping

Heating and cooling bands each have their own width (the differential). If the setpoints are closer together than those widths allow, the bands overlap. Then the zone can satisfy the cooling band just after the heating band ends, and the reverse. The room temperature bounces between the two, and the equipment is started and stopped again and again. Equipment lag and overshoot make this worse, because the room keeps moving after the output changes.

The national laboratory guide also describes a related case. Adjacent packaged units with no physical barrier between zones can end up with one unit cooling while the unit next to it heats. Sensor accuracy differences or different zone setpoints can cause it, and the guide advises avoiding thermostat placement near another thermostat in that situation. A wider deadband gives the two zones more room before they disagree.

The energy code idea

Model energy codes want heating and cooling kept from running against each other. A national laboratory report on controls requirements describes the measure as a five degree thermostat deadband with setpoint overlap prevention, and lists it under the 2015 IECC. The same report quotes the requirement as being capable of a deadband of at least 5 F, and notes that this means the system must be capable of it, not that it must be set to 5 F.

In the commercial energy code of one state we read, which follows the model code's numbering, the section is C403.4.1.2. It says zone thermostatic controls must provide a deadband of at least 5 F (2.8 C) within which heating and cooling energy to the zone is shut off or reduced to a minimum. Its exceptions cover thermostats that need manual changeover between heating and cooling and applications that need precise temperature control, as approved by the code official. Section numbers and wording change between editions and local adoptions, so confirm the edition your jurisdiction uses.

The same national laboratory report found that this measure had the lowest final-testing compliance rate among the ones it discussed, 56 percent, and that owners often found 5 F too wide. All ten commissioning agents it surveyed allowed some deviation, typically down to 4 F, when the owner preferred it. That is a field finding, not a rule. It shows why checking the real setting matters.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumptions. Assume a single zone with a heating setpoint of 70 F, a cooling setpoint of 75 F, and a 1 F differential on each. Assume heating starts at its setpoint and runs until the temperature is one differential above it, and cooling starts at its setpoint and runs until the temperature is one differential below it. Other thermostats place the differential differently, so this is an assumed convention.

  1. Heat starts: 70 F (temperature falling).
  2. Heat stops: 70 + 1 = 71 F.
  3. Cooling starts: 75 F (temperature rising).
  4. Cooling stops: 75 - 1 = 74 F.

Check the logic. The deadband is 75 - 70 = 5 F. The float zone, where neither mode runs once a cycle has ended, is 74 - 71 = 3 F. The setpoint gap (5 F) is larger than the two differentials added together (1 + 1 = 2 F), so the bands cannot overlap. That is the condition to look for.

Now push the setpoints to 72 and 73. Under the same assumptions:

  1. Heat starts: 72 F. Heat stops: 72 + 1 = 73 F.
  2. Cooling starts: 73 F. Cooling stops: 73 - 1 = 72 F.

The deadband is now 73 - 72 = 1 F, and the two differentials add up to 2 F, so the gap is smaller than the bands. Heat stops at exactly the temperature where cooling starts, and cooling stops at exactly the temperature where heating starts. The float zone (cool stop minus heat stop) is 72 - 73 = negative 1 F, meaning the bands overlap by 1 F. With any lag or overshoot, the room can pass 73 F while heating coasts, call for cooling, drop to 72 F, and call for heat again. The zone alternates between modes and wastes energy. The arithmetic confirms why a minimum gap helps: it must exceed the combined differentials, and the code idea sets it well beyond that.

How to set and check it

  1. Read the heating and cooling setpoints for the zone and subtract. On a thermostat that allows only a minimum separation, try to enter a smaller gap and see whether it is refused or the other setpoint moves.
  2. Find the differential (some controls call it swing, cycle rate or throttling range) for each mode in the installer settings or the point list, and use the manufacturer's manual for the exact convention.
  3. Do the arithmetic from the example: heat stop point and cool stop point must leave a float zone, and the gap must exceed the combined differentials.
  4. On a building automation system, trend the zone temperature, the heating output and the cooling output for a day. Alternating heat and cool commands inside an hour point to a gap that is too small or to a sensor problem.
  5. Look at neighbors. If an adjacent zone runs the opposite mode, compare setpoints and sensor readings before changing anything.
  6. Check the unoccupied setback gap separately, since a very wide one can slow recovery.

Treat the results as a starting point. Owner preference, local code and the manufacturer's instructions decide the final values.

Field card: Deadband Between Heating and Cooling Setpoints: Differential, Proportional Band and the Minimum Gap

Deadband is the gap between modes; differential is the gap inside one.

  • Deadband = cooling setpoint minus heating setpoint; no heating or cooling inside it.
  • Differential (hysteresis) = on/off gap within one mode; proportional band = ramp width for modulating output.
  • Keep the setpoint gap larger than the two differentials added together.
  • Model code idea: capable of a deadband of at least 5 F, with exceptions; confirm local edition.
  • Our example: 70 and 75 F, 1 F differentials: heat 70 on, 71 off; cool 75 on, 74 off.
  • At 72 and 73 F the bands overlap and the zone can flip between modes.
  • Trend heat and cool outputs to find fighting.
