Planning HVAC for a New Walkout Basement Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A walkout basement is two houses stacked in one footprint. On one side the lower level is buried in soil, and on the other it has a full wall of doors and windows open to daylight. Heating and cooling plans that treat the lower level as "just another floor" miss that split. This lesson walks through what changes, in the order a designer meets it.

Two kinds of wall, two kinds of load

A below-grade wall faces soil, and the soil a few feet down stays close to the region's yearly average air temperature, according to a federal basement insulation guide. That is why heat loss from a basement is generally smaller than from the above-grade part of the house. The same guide warns that the top few feet of soil get much colder in winter than the soil at the bottom of the wall, so the upper part of a buried wall loses more than the lower part. The exposed walkout wall has none of that help. It sees outdoor air, wind and sun, and it carries the glass.

So the loads differ by wall, not just by floor. Our one system or two lesson covers room-by-room loads by floor. Here we add a second sort: ground-coupled surface versus above-grade surface. A load calculation should list the walkout wall, the door and each window as their own lines, and the buried walls as separate lines with their own ground-temperature assumption. See the slab and basement wall heat loss lesson for how ground-coupled loss is handled.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen to make the arithmetic clear, not a measured or sourced value, and it is not a design for any real house.

  • Both walls are 30 ft wide and 8 ft high, so 240 ft2 each.
  • Indoor air: 70°F. Design outdoor air: 5°F. Temperature difference: 65°F.
  • Exposed wall: one glass door of 6 ft by 6.67 ft, about 40 ft2, assumed U = 0.35 Btu/h per ft2 per °F. The other 200 ft2 is insulated wall, assumed U = 0.05.
  • Below-grade wall: assumed U = 0.06, which we treat as including the soil layer.
  • First pass, ground at an assumed 40°F against the whole wall. Second pass, split: top 4 ft against assumed 20°F soil and bottom 4 ft against assumed 45°F soil.

Exposed wall. Opaque part: 200 × 0.05 × 65 = 650 Btu/h. Glass door: 40 × 0.35 × 65 = 910 Btu/h. Total: 650 + 910 = 1,560 Btu/h. Per square foot of wall: 1,560 ÷ 240 = 6.5 Btu/h.

Below-grade wall, first pass. Difference: 70 − 40 = 30°F. Loss: 240 × 0.06 × 30 = 432 Btu/h, or 1.8 Btu/h per ft2. The exposed wall loses about 6.5 ÷ 1.8 = 3.6 times as much per square foot.

Below-grade wall, split. Top half: 120 × 0.06 × (70 − 20) = 360 Btu/h. Bottom half: 120 × 0.06 × (70 − 45) = 180 Btu/h. Total: 540 Btu/h, or 2.25 per ft2. The ratio drops to about 2.9, and the top half carries two thirds of the buried wall's loss. The door is 40 of 240 ft2, one sixth of the area, yet it causes 910 of 1,560 Btu/h, about 58 percent of the exposed wall's loss. The takeaway is our own: glass on the exposed wall dominates, and the buried wall's loss sits near the top.

A separate zone for the lower level

The lower level also differs in time. In summer it is the coolest floor, and in winter the glass wall can be warmed by afternoon sun while the buried rooms stay cool. A single thermostat upstairs will not see that. Many designers give the lower level its own zone, whether a second system, a damper zone or a ductless head. That choice depends on loads, equipment room space and budget; the trade-offs are in the two-story system lesson. Whatever you pick, a thermostat on the lower level needs a representative wall, away from the glass door and out of direct sun.

Return air in the basement

Every room that gets supply air needs a path for it to leave. Plan a return grille or a transfer path for each closed-door lower-level room, and size it by free area. Do not rely on the gap under a door. Returns low in a basement pull from the coolest, dampest air, so keep them away from damp areas and equipment that exhausts air.

Summer humidity

A university extension page on basement moisture says warm, humid outdoor air condenses on cool basement wall and floor surfaces, and it advises against ventilating a basement directly with warm, humid air in summer. A walkout lower level has doors that tempt people to open it up on humid days, so the plan should say not to. The same page says a dehumidifier helps with symptoms but is not a complete solution on its own. Plan for the cooling system to run long enough to dry the air, and see the basement dehumidifier lesson for sizing and drainage. The federal guide notes that insulating basement walls usually leaves the basement warmer and drier in humid summer weather.

Cold floors and registers near glass

The federal basement guide says insulating the walls raises the first-floor temperature and makes the floor surface warmer, and that insulation under the slab can raise the basement floor temperature in winter. Cold floors are mostly a building-shell question, so raise them with the builder before the slab is poured. Our own practice note: where you can, put supply registers along the glass wall so the air washes the glass and floor edge, and aim them so they do not blow on the sofa. Check throw and noise using the register placement lesson.

Equipment room and combustion air

A lower-level equipment room is common, and it is handy for duct runs to both floors. Plan door size for the largest piece to pass through, floor drains or condensate paths, and service clearances from the manufacturer's installation instructions. If any fuel-fired appliance goes there, it needs a supply of air for burning and for venting, set by the maker's instructions and the local code. A closed room with a tight envelope can be starved of air, so follow the combustion air lesson. A federal radon guide also warns that some soil gas fans can cause combustion appliance backdrafting in certain setups, so appliances and radon work have to be planned together.

Radon and soil gas, in general terms

A federal radon guide explains that indoor air pressure is typically lower than the soil around the house, which draws soil gas in. The federal builder page lists the usual radon-resistant features for new construction: a layer of clean gravel under the slab, polyethylene sheeting over it, a gas-tight vent pipe from the gravel up through the house to the roof, and careful sealing of the foundation. A fan can later turn the passive system into an active one. The guide says sealing alone does not lower radon levels significantly or consistently, tests should run at least 48 hours, and a mitigation fan must run continuously. For HVAC planning, leave a clear route for the vent pipe and a spot for fan wiring, and see the radon and HVAC lesson. We do not size or design radon systems on this page.

Planning checklist

  1. List walls by exposure: exposed, buried upper, buried lower.
  2. Split glass out as its own load line.
  3. Decide on a lower-level zone early.
  4. Place returns before the framing closes.
  5. Keep outdoor air out of the basement on humid days.
  6. Ask for floor and wall insulation to be settled before pour and drywall.
  7. Check equipment room size, drain and combustion air.
  8. Leave a path for any radon vent pipe.

Field card: Planning HVAC for a New Walkout Basement Home

Count the glass wall and the buried wall as different loads.

  • Our own example: exposed wall about 6.5 Btu/h per ft2, buried wall about 1.8 to 2.25 (assumed inputs).
  • Buried-wall loss is greatest near the top, where soil is coldest.
  • Consider a separate lower-level zone and its own thermostat location.
