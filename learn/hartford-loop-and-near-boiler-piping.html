Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The pipes right around a steam boiler are part of the boiler. The Hartford loop is a small piece of that piping with a big safety job: it helps keep water in the boiler when something goes wrong.

The pieces, in plain words

The header is the pipe that collects steam from the boiler outlets and sends it to the system mains. On a boiler piped with a Hartford loop, the end of the header drops down as a vertical pipe called the equalizer and connects into the bottom of the boiler, below the waterline. The wet return is the condensate return pipe that sits below the boiler waterline. It comes up from the floor and joins the equalizer. That joint is the Hartford loop connection.

Why it exists

Years ago, condensate was often returned straight into the bottom of the boiler. When the boiler built steam pressure, that pressure could push boiler water back out into the return pipe, leaving the boiler short of water. A check valve in the return was tried first, but sediment tended to foul it and hold it open. The equalizer replaced it. Because the equalizer carries boiler steam pressure to the return side too, the pressure on both sides stays balanced and the water stays in the boiler. A boiler insurance company required this arrangement around 1919, which is how it got the name Hartford loop (also called the Underwriters loop).

The second job: a safety backstop

If a wet return breaks or leaks, boiler water can run out of it. With the loop in place, water can only drain down to the height of the loop connection, which is set above the boiler's crown sheet (the top of the firebox surfaces that must stay covered with water). That buys the operator time. Without it, a boiler can be left dry and fired, and adding water to a hot dry boiler is dangerous. Trade writers describe the loop as inexpensive backup protection, not a replacement for a working low-water cutoff. See Steam Boiler Pressure Controls and Gauges for the cutoff side of the story.

What the drawings usually show

Published explanations of the loop (these are trade-press sources by a leading steam writer, not boiler-maker documents) describe the same few ideas. The return joins the equalizer with a close nipple or a wye fitting, not a long nipple, because a long nipple lets returning water build momentum and bang. The joint sits a short distance below the lowest normal waterline and above the crown sheet; the exact distance varies by boiler and maker. One explanation gives about two inches below the lowest operating water level, while Weil-McLain's 94 Series 3 manual says to locate the top of the Hartford loop return nipple at least 4 inches below the water line. Use the dimension on your boiler manual's piping drawing. Treat that as an example only. The boiler maker's drawing for that exact boiler governs.

Equalizer size

One published guide gives sizes by boiler rating in square feet of equivalent direct radiation (EDR). As an example only: at least 1-1/2 inch up to 900 sq ft, 2-1/2 inch from 900 to 6,400 sq ft, and 4 inch above that. An equalizer that is too small cannot balance pressure well, and the water line in the gauge glass may not hold steady. Use the manufacturer's required pipe sizes, not a remembered table.

Where the loop is and is not needed

The same trade article says the loop belongs on gravity-return systems with a wet return, and on one-pipe systems with a dry return, where it also works as a loop seal. With a condensate pump or boiler feed pump it is not needed, and the pump discharge should enter the bottom of the header drip rather than the loop connection. Whether your boiler wants it is a question for the boiler manual.

A historical note

Not every old maker used this exact arrangement. A 1929 Hoffman catalog does not show a Hartford loop at all. Its nearest device is a differential loop, with a high point that must sit a set distance above the boiler waterline (24 inches on some boiler numbers, 30 on others). That is one maker in one year, so treat it as history, not as a current rule.

Common problems

  • Connection set too high. If the water level falls below the joint, steam can enter the return and cause loud water hammer, often near the end of a firing cycle.
  • Long nipple. Increases banging at the joint.
  • Small or wrong near-boiler piping. Steam leaving the boiler too fast can drag water along with it. That is covered in Wet Steam and Carryover Basics.
  • Condensate pumps change the picture. A pump opens the returns to the atmosphere. One author says the loop is then generally unnecessary and the equalizer becomes more like a drip line, with the pump discharge piped into the bottom of the header drip below the waterline. Follow the manufacturer for pumped systems.
  • Dry returns still benefit. On systems with no wet return, the loop still keeps steam from entering the return pipe through the equalizer.

Safe habits

Never cut, cap, or rearrange near-boiler piping to fit a space or fix a noise without the boiler manual's piping diagram. Never treat the loop as permission to skip testing the low-water cutoff. Steam is hot and under pressure even at low pressure, so isolate and cool the boiler and follow lockout/tagout before opening any piping.

Field card: Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping

Near the boiler, the piping is part of the boiler.

  • Header, equalizer and wet return are part of the boiler's design, not extras.
  • The equalizer balances pressure so steam pressure does not push water out into the return.
  • The loop also limits how far water can drain if a return breaks.
  • Use a close nipple or wye at the joint; follow the manufacturer's drawing for height and pipe size.
  • It backs up the low-water cutoff. It never replaces it.
