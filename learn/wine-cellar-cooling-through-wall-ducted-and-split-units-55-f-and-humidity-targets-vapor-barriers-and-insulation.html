Wine Cellar Cooling: Through-Wall, Ducted and Split Units, 55 F and Humidity Targets, Vapor Barriers and Insulation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A wine room is a tiny cold storage box built inside a house or a restaurant. It needs a steady 55 F or so, damp-but-not-wet air, and a tight insulated shell. This lesson covers the cooling unit choices, where the hot side goes, and how to check whether a unit is big enough with simple wall math.

What the cellar needs to hold

The University of Minnesota's enology program says maturation storage should be 60 F or below, with 55 F best. A Purdue Extension fact sheet gives a long-term range of 50 to 60 F and asks that the temperature stay constant, within about 2 to 3 F either way. Neither page gave a humidity target we could use. Building Science Corporation's wine cellar paper gives 55 F at 70 percent relative humidity for bottles and says 70 percent is near the top of what corks tolerate. A maker's owner's manual for a through-wall cellar unit says the unit is designed to hold 50 to 70 percent. So the working window is roughly 55 F and 50 to 70 percent RH. Treat the humidity part as a maker and building-science figure, not a university rule.

Why a regular air conditioner is a poor fit

A room air conditioner is built for comfort cooling. The ENERGY STAR room AC fact sheet suggests a thermostat setting of about 78 F and warns that an oversized unit cools quickly but removes only part of the moisture, leaving the room damp. A cellar has two different problems. It sits 20 or more degrees below a comfort setting, and it needs the air kept from drying out or running soggy. We did not find a source that states the 55 F limit of a window or portable unit, so we make no claim on exact limits. What we can say plainly: a unit not designed for a cold room can ice up its coil (see frozen evaporator coils), and the cellar makers list their own limits on room temperature and cellar size. Use equipment the maker rates for the job.

Through-wall self-contained units

A self-contained cellar unit sits in an opening in a wall of the cellar. The cold side faces in. The hot side, with the condenser, faces a larger room and dumps its heat there. One maker's manual for a unit in this style says the room receiving the exhaust should be at least as large as the cellar. A second maker's manual says the hot-side space must be appropriately sized, or the unit recirculates its own hot air and loses capacity. The first manual lists clearances of 8 inches behind the unit and 3 inches in front inside the cellar, and says the hot-side intake air should be below 95 F. The second lists a rear operating range of 40 to 115 F and calls for 12 inches of free space in front for evaporator airflow. Do not put one in a closed closet or a hot attic.

Those same manuals say the hot side can be ducted: the second one allows a condenser duct run up to 25 equivalent feet of 8 inch duct (100 feet with an auxiliary fan) and an evaporator duct run up to 50 equivalent feet of 10 inch duct. That lets the unit sit in a closet while the air path stays short.

Split and ducted systems

For a bigger or remote room the compressor and condenser can be split from the evaporator, joined by a refrigerant line set. One maker's split manual allows the condensing unit up to 100 line feet away, calls for insulated suction line, and requires 3 feet of clearance in every direction (5 ideal). An indoor condensing unit needs ventilation, with that manual naming 500 to 600 CFM of cool air. The same manual rates the evaporator at 54 dBA and the condensing unit at 65 dBA. The manual does not say where to put the condensing unit, but the louder part belongs away from bedrooms and living spaces. Line set basics are in our pipe insulation lesson.

Shell: insulation, vapor retarder and gaskets

Two maker manuals call for at least R-11 in the walls and floor and R-19 in the ceiling, with a vapor barrier on the warm side of the insulation. R-value is resistance to heat flow, and the DOE insulation guide says a higher number is better. The same DOE guide says facing material goes on the warm-in-winter side, but warns that this is hard to apply in climates that are air conditioned in summer. A cellar reverses the usual picture: the cold side is inside, so the warm, humid side is the one facing the rest of the building. That is where the retarder goes.

The Building America report on airflow and water vapor says air leakage moves an order of magnitude more moisture than diffusion. So seal the cellar before you worry about film thickness: door sweeps, gaskets all the way around, and sealed light fixtures. The maker manuals ask for insulated, weather-stripped doors and surface-mounted lights instead of recessed ones, which can leak. Building Science Corporation adds that you should not put vapor barriers on both sides of an assembly where moisture can move both ways. In a basement where the outer wall touches soil or concrete, ask the local building official what they accept.

Condensate and humidity

A cold coil makes water, so the unit needs a drain. One split manual says a drain line is mandatory and that skipping it voids the warranty. It wants clear half-inch tubing sloped downward with the outlet above the water level. A through-wall manual asks for a water-filled trap. A condensate pump kit is offered for a low drain. See our mini-split drain lesson for the general ideas. The split manual also says its system adds no moisture, and offers fan timing changes to raise humidity a little. Dry air shrinks corks, so put a hygrometer in the cellar and check it against a known reference; see our humidity meter lesson. If the cellar sits in a wet basement, fix that first with a basement dehumidifier, and for restaurant-scale boxes see walk-in cooler basics.

Worked example: wall heat gain for one cellar

Heat flows through a surface at Q = A x delta T / R, in Btu/h, where A is area in square feet, delta T is the temperature difference in F, and R is the R-value. Every input below is our own assumption, not a measurement.

Cellar: 6 ft x 8 ft x 8 ft inside, 384 cubic feet, 55 F inside. Areas: two long walls 8 x 8 = 64 sq ft each (128 total, with one 3 x 7 = 21 sq ft door); two short walls 6 x 8 = 48 sq ft each (96 total); ceiling 6 x 8 = 48; floor 48.

Assumed: long walls border a basement room at 75 F (delta T 20). Short walls border the larger room that receives the unit's exhaust, at 80 F (delta T 25). Ceiling sees 75 F (20). Floor sits on a slab over soil at 60 F (5). Walls R-13, ceiling R-19, floor R-5, insulated door R-4.

  • Long walls, 128 minus 21 = 107 sq ft: 107 x 20 / 13 = 165 Btu/h
  • Door: 21 x 20 / 4 = 105 Btu/h
  • Short walls: 96 x 25 / 13 = 185 Btu/h
  • Ceiling: 48 x 20 / 19 = 51 Btu/h
  • Floor: 48 x 5 / 5 = 48 Btu/h

Sum: 165 + 105 + 185 + 51 + 48 = 554 Btu/h. Add lighting: two 10 W LED fixtures, assumed on all the time, 20 W x 3.412 = 68 Btu/h. Subtotal 622. Add our own 15 percent allowance for door openings, people and leakage (an assumption, not a code value): 622 x 0.15 = 93. Total about 715 Btu/h.

Compare to a rating. One maker's manual lists its smallest model at 1065 Btu/h with a 55 F cellar and says the rating must be greater than the cellar load. By our hand math 1065 beats 715. But the same manual's sizing table, which assumes R-19 everywhere and 75 F surroundings, shows about 1542 to 1744 Btu/h for 300 to 400 cubic feet at 55 F, which is more than that unit can do. The manual does not say what is inside its table. So our sum is a sanity check, not a selection. Pick equipment from the maker's table, using the real surroundings and the real R-values, and keep a margin. If the cellar has glass or no vapor retarder, the manual says the load will be much higher.

Field card: Wine Cellar Cooling

Cold box, tight shell, hot side to a big room.

  • Target about 55 F and 50 to 70 percent RH (UMN, Building Science, maker manual).
  • Use equipment the maker rates for cellars, not a room AC.
  • Hot side exhausts into a room at least as large as the cellar.
  • Walls and floor R-11 minimum, ceiling R-19 minimum, vapor retarder on the warm side.
  • Seal air leaks: gasketed door, surface-mounted lights.
  • Q = A x delta T / R, summed over every surface, then use the maker's table.
  • Drain line, trap and slope are required. Check the hygrometer.
