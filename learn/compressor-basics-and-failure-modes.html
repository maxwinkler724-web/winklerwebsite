Compressor Basics and Failure Modes

The compressor is the most expensive part in most systems. When one dies, the real question is not just how to replace it but what killed it, because a new one will die the same way if the cause stays.

What the compressor does

The compressor pulls low-pressure vapor from the evaporator and squeezes it to a higher pressure, which also raises its temperature. That hot vapor can then give up its heat in the condenser. The compressor also keeps the refrigerant circulating. It is built to compress vapor, not liquid. See Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

Common types

  • Reciprocating: a piston in a cylinder, common in refrigeration.
  • Scroll: one spiral orbits inside another fixed spiral, shrinking pockets of gas to raise pressure.
  • Rotary screw: two meshing screws trap and push vapor, used in large equipment.
  • Centrifugal: a spinning impeller adds speed that becomes pressure, used in large chillers.

Most small compressors are hermetic, meaning the motor sits in the refrigerant system and is cooled by refrigerant. If the motor burns out, the compressor must be replaced and burnt windings can contaminate the system. Variable-speed designs are covered in Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.

The usual causes of failure

Technician references group failures into a few families. They overlap, and one problem often leads to another.

  • Overheating. Named in one source as a top cause. High discharge temperatures and poor lubrication break oil down. An improper compression ratio, dirty condenser coils, or poor cooling of the compressor are common reasons.
  • Liquid floodback. Liquid refrigerant reaches the compressor and dilutes the oil, which wears bearings. Causes include a metering device out of control, poor evaporator airflow, very low load and overcharge.
  • Flooded start and slugging. Refrigerant migrates into the compressor during the off cycle and flashes off at startup. Slugging means trying to compress liquid refrigerant or oil, and valve plates, rods and shafts can break. A crankcase heater or pump-down is one way makers address migration.
  • Oil loss. Oil that leaves must return. Poor piping, short cycling, a loss of charge or inadequate defrost can leave the compressor short on oil.
  • Contamination. Moisture and acid corrode and sludge. Replace the filter drier whenever the system is opened, and keep copper chips and flux out.
  • Electrical problems. Undersized or poorly connected wiring, failed contactors, voltage trouble, or a three-phase unit running backward. See Capacitors and Contactors.

Slugging versus overheating

These two are hard to tell apart. One trade source notes many high-temperature failures are mistaken for liquid failures, since both leave damage and similar symptoms, and that the best way to know is often to open the compressor and look. In the field, that means thinking about the system-level cause: charge, airflow, load, metering device, coil condition, and electrical supply.

Before you replace it

  • Ask what the history was: short cycling, low airflow, a recent open system, a recent storm or power event.
  • Check whether the system is clean and dry, and whether the dryer was changed.
  • Fix the cause, not only the part. A checklist helps, since time pressure leads people to skip tests.
  • Follow the compressor and equipment maker's procedure, including any manufacturer replacement or acid-test steps.

Compressor work involves high voltage and refrigerant. Use lockout/tagout, follow Refrigerant Safety, and recover refrigerant properly.

Field card: Compressor Basics and Failure Modes

A dead compressor usually has a cause upstream.

  • The compressor compresses vapor. Liquid and oil in the cylinders are the enemy.
  • Main failure families: overheating, floodback, flooded start, oil loss, contamination, electrical.
  • Keep the filter drier fresh whenever the system is opened.
  • Check charge, airflow, load and metering device before and after replacement.
  • Lockout/tagout and proper recovery come first; manufacturer procedure governs.
