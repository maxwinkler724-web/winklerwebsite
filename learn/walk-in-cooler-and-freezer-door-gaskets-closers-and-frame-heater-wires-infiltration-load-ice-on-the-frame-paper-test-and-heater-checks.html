Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Door Gaskets, Closers and Frame Heater Wires

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A walk-in door is the one opening in the box that people use all day. When its gasket is torn, its closer is weak or its frame heater is dead, warm wet air flows in, ice grows where you can see it, and the compressor and coil carry a load nobody planned for. This lesson shows how to size that load with our own example and how to check each part.

Check your site rules first

Safety note. Before touching a door heater, a light or a fan circuit, shut off and lock out the power, and follow the equipment maker's instructions and your site procedures. This page is general education, not a service manual.

What the three parts do

The gasket is the soft, flexible seal around the door edge. It presses against the frame when the door is shut and blocks air. The closer is a spring or hydraulic device that pulls the door shut so the gasket can do its job. The frame heater wire is a low-wattage electric element tucked into the door frame (and sometimes the door edge) of a freezer. It keeps the gasket contact area just warm enough that moisture does not freeze the door to the frame.

Why a small gap matters so much

Air is not the only thing that comes in. Air from a warm room carries water vapor, and that vapor has to be removed by the evaporator. A university extension chapter on refrigeration load makes the point plainly: warm moist air entering a refrigerated space is a larger load than the same volume of cool dry air. It also says door size and how long the door stays open drive the load, and it recommends strip curtains on large doors.

Once the moist air meets a surface below the freezing point, the vapor turns to frost or ice. That is why the first sign of a leak is often ice around the latch side or the bottom corner of the frame. Frost on a gasket can also tear it when the door is pulled open, which widens the gap, which brings in more moisture. It feeds itself.

A national laboratory has studied how to measure this. Its 2018 paper describes a tracer gas method that times the door closing before all the cold air has spilled out, to estimate the infiltration rate. We mention it so you know the leak rate is measurable, but on a service call the simple checks below are what you will actually use.

Our own worked example: a torn gasket on a freezer

This is our own example, not a measured case. We picked the numbers to show the arithmetic. The air factors come from basic air properties (see the sensible and latent heat lesson linked below).

Assumptions (all ours):

  • Walk-in freezer inside at 0°F and 90 percent relative humidity.
  • Room outside the door at 85°F and 60 percent relative humidity, near sea level.
  • The torn gasket leaves a gap 3 ft long and 1/4 in wide.
  • Air moves through the gap at an average 5 ft per second. We assumed this value; it depends on the pressure difference across the door, which we did not measure.
  • Air density 0.075 lb per cubic foot, specific heat of air 0.24 Btu per lb per °F, and about 1,060 Btu per lb to condense water vapor. These are rounded standard values and a simplification; frost forming on cold surfaces gives up somewhat more heat, so treat our latent figure as a floor.

Step 1, airflow. Gap area = 3 ft x (0.25 / 12) ft = 0.0625 sq ft. Airflow = 0.0625 x 5 ft/s x 60 s/min = 18.75 cfm.

Step 2, moisture content. From a standard psychrometric chart or equation, the outside air holds about 108.7 grains of water per pound of dry air, and the freezer air about 4.9 grains. The difference is 108.7 - 4.9 = 103.8 grains per pound.

Step 3, sensible load. Multiplying air density (0.075), 60 minutes per hour and specific heat (0.24) gives 1.08. Sensible load = 1.08 x 18.75 cfm x (85 - 0) = 1,721 Btu/h.

Step 4, latent load. Multiplying air density (0.075), 60 minutes per hour and 1,060 Btu per lb, then dividing by 7,000 grains per pound, gives 0.68. Latent load = 0.68 x 18.75 cfm x 103.8 = 1,323 Btu/h.

Step 5, total. 1,721 + 1,323 = 3,044 Btu/h, about 0.25 ton of refrigeration, running around the clock. Over 24 hours that is about 73,000 Btu. Latent heat is 1,323 / 3,044 = 43 percent of the total, even though the air is not that humid by summer standards.

Step 6, water. Moisture removed = 18.75 cfm x 60 x 0.075 lb per cubic foot x (103.8 / 7,000) = about 1.25 lb of water per hour, or roughly 30 lb (about 3.6 gallons) per day. In a freezer that water ends up as frost on the first cold surface, then on the coil, then in the defrost drain.

Now compare an ajar door. Suppose a worn closer lets the door rest 1 in off the gasket along its 7 ft latch edge. The opening is 7 ft x (1 / 12) ft = 0.58 sq ft, about 9.3 times our torn-gasket area. Using the same assumed 5 ft/s, airflow is 175 cfm, and the same arithmetic gives about 16,070 Btu/h sensible plus 12,350 Btu/h latent, or roughly 28,400 Btu/h (about 2.4 tons). That is our scaling of an assumed case, not a measurement, but it shows why a door that does not latch is a far bigger problem than a short tear.

Closers: what the federal rule says

The Department of Energy's walk-in standards in 10 CFR Part 431 (section 431.306) require automatic door closers that firmly close walk-in doors that have been closed to within 1 inch of full closure. The text we read exempts doors wider than 3 feet 9 inches or taller than 7 feet. It also requires strip doors, spring-hinged doors or another method of minimizing infiltration when doors are open. We read the 2023 edition of the regulation and the Department's final rule; rules change, so check the current electronic CFR before you rely on the wording.

The same section limits door heater power on walk-ins with transparent reach-in doors: 7.1 watts per square foot of door opening for freezers and 3.0 for coolers when the heaters have no controls, or controls that reduce heater energy based on outside humidity or condensation. That limit is about glass door anti-sweat heaters, not the thin wire in a solid door frame, so do not assume it tells you the right wattage for the frame wire you are checking. The label or the maker's data does.

Why does this matter in the field? A closer is what makes the gasket work. A door resting a half inch ajar has no seal at all. In our experience a weak or missing closer, or a door propped open, is the usual cause. Check that the door swings freely from about 90 degrees and latches by itself every time.

Checking the gasket: the paper test

This is our own method. Close the door on a strip of paper (a dollar bill works) so the paper sticks out. Then pull it slowly. You should feel steady, even drag. If it slides out with no drag at one spot, the gasket is not touching there. Repeat every foot or so around all four sides, including the bottom corners, which are the usual failure points. A university extension article describes a related check for coolers: close the door with the lights off and look for daylight around the edge. Either way, a visible gap, a flattened or hardened gasket, or a split corner means it needs replacing or adjusting. Look also for a door that has sagged on its hinges; adjusting the hinge or latch may fix a gap that a new gasket will not.

Checking the frame heater wire

These are our own suggestions; follow the maker's instructions for the exact unit.

  • Look at the ice first. In our experience, ice or a door that sticks only along one frame side points to a dead or broken section of wire. Ice that is heaviest at the bottom corner usually points to a torn gasket.
  • De-energize before you test. With the power locked out, disconnect the heater lead and measure resistance with a meter. An open circuit (no reading) means a broken element. Compare a good reading with the label, which gives volts and watts. Resistance should be near volts squared divided by watts.
  • Check the circuit. If the element is good, look for a tripped breaker, a loose splice or a failed switch or timer in the heater circuit.
  • Feel the frame after power is restored. A working wire should leave the frame slightly warm, not hot. A very hot spot is a fault; shut it off and check the maker's instructions.
  • Do not cut into the frame to find a wire. Replacement usually needs the maker's kit and instructions.

Putting it together

Fix the cheapest, biggest problem first: make the door close and latch on its own, replace the torn gasket, then confirm the frame heater is working so the new gasket does not freeze to the frame. Then melt back the old ice and re-check after a day. The extension chapter we cited gives rule-of-thumb allowances for infiltration that range from 0 to 1 percent of box load for a door used rarely to 8 to 10 percent for big doors left open for long periods, which tells you how fast door habits can matter. Strip curtains help while the door is open; see the lesson linked below.

Field card: Walk-In Door Gaskets, Closers and Frame Heaters

Seal it, close it, heat it, then look at the ice.

  • Warm moist air in is both a sensible and a latent load; moisture becomes frost.
  • Our example: 18.75 cfm leak at 85 F/60 percent into a 0 F freezer is about 3,044 Btu/h, 43 percent latent.
  • Rule: door closers must close doors that are within 1 inch of closed (doors over 3 ft 9 in wide or 7 ft tall are exempt).
  • Paper test: close the door on paper at every foot; no drag means no seal.
  • Heater wire: lock out power, read resistance, compare with the label.
  • Ice at the bottom corner usually means gasket; ice along one side usually means heater.
