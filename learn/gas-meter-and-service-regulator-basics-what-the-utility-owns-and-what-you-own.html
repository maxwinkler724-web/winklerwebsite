Gas Meter and Regulator Basics: What the Utility Owns and What You Own

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Gas reaches a house through a chain of parts, and at some point along that chain the utility's responsibility ends and yours begins. Where that point sits matters when you smell gas, when snow piles up on the meter, and when you wonder who has to fix a leaking pipe. This lesson walks the chain and shows what the New Jersey utilities say on their own pages.

Check your utility's own page

Check your utility's own page. Rules differ between utilities and can change. Everything below is tied to the specific page named. If a utility's page is silent on a point, this lesson says so rather than guessing. Your bill, your utility's tariff and its customer service line are the final word for your address.

The gas service path

Gas travels from the utility's main in the street through a service line to the building. A service line valve sits near the meter so the gas can be shut off. A service regulator lowers the pressure, the meter measures the volume, and your house piping carries the gas to the furnace, water heater, range and dryer. NJNG uses the name "service line valve" and says its meters are typically outside, about 2 feet above the ground.

What the New Jersey utilities say

South Jersey Gas. A safety bill insert says the utility is "responsible for maintaining gas lines to the meter at your home," and that you are responsible for the lines within your home and their connections to appliances.

New Jersey Natural Gas (NJNG). Its gas meter safety page says you maintain the lines from the meter to appliances and equipment, inside and outside the home, and lists the furnace, oven, pool heater, grill and generator fuel lines. The same page says that if the service line valve is buried or hidden, you should call NJNG and say "Meter" at the prompt. NJNG says it will replace the valve and raise it to the proper height at no cost, and asks you not to dig it up yourself.

Elizabethtown Gas. Its tariff (the rulebook filed with the state) says meters and regulators are furnished and installed by the company, that company-furnished equipment stays company property, and that the customer shall not tamper with or remove meters or other equipment. It also says the gas company assumes no responsibility for the condition of the customer's gas installation, and that customer piping must be made and maintained to the referenced code. Its leak survey page does not assign ownership.

PSE&G. Its home gas safety page does not say who owns the piping on your property and sends ownership questions to customer service. Its gas appliances and piping manual is clearer: it says installation, protection, testing and maintenance of customer-owned piping, above or underground, are the customer's responsibility. It also says PSE&G has the sole right to disconnect, remove or reset gas meters, and that equipment furnished at its expense remains its property.

The pattern is the same even though the wording is not. The meter and regulator belong to the utility. The piping from the meter onward is yours to keep in good shape. Where a page is silent, as PSE&G's safety page and Elizabethtown's inspection page are on ownership, do not fill the gap from memory.

The federal notice about buried piping

A federal rule, 49 CFR 192.16, requires gas operators to notify customers in writing when the operator does not maintain the customer's buried piping. The notice must say that unmaintained buried piping may face corrosion and leakage hazards, and that it should be inspected periodically for leaks, checked for corrosion if metal, and repaired if unsafe. It also says to locate buried gas piping before digging and to dig by hand near it. That is why a grill line, pool heater line or detached garage line can be your concern even though you cannot see it.

Why the regulator vent must stay clear

The regulator holds pressure steady. PSE&G's page describes it as part of the meter assembly that releases gas if there is a malfunction, and says blocking its vent could create an unsafe condition where gas may accumulate. Snow, ice and mud can plug that vent. NJNG's meter safety tips page says to keep the meter, piping and vents clear of snow, ice and debris, because buildup can lead to dangerous gas leaks or total loss of heat. NJNG's news release says to use a broom or gloved hands and to avoid shovels, sharp tools or force. If ice is too heavy to clear gently, call the utility instead of chipping at it.

Clearances and things you never do

  • Keep the meter visible and reachable. NJNG says meters and outside vents must stay accessible so its employees and first responders can reach them.
  • Do not hang a dog leash, towels, clothes or ground wires on the meter or its piping (NJNG, PSE&G). Do not pile mulch, topsoil or rocks against it (PSE&G).
  • Do not lean ladders or metal objects against the meter, and take care with mowers (NJNG). Start snow blowers outdoors, away from the meter (PSE&G).
  • Do not remove, reset or adjust utility equipment. Elizabethtown's tariff and PSE&G's manual both reserve that to the utility.
  • Do not dig up a buried service line valve yourself (NJNG).

For new installations, PSE&G's piping manual sets spacing rules that installers follow: a regulator vent terminus not within 2 ft of an opening into a building, and a meter set at least 3 ft from the edge of a driveway or vehicle zone. NJNG says that if a meter is badly placed, such as too close to a driveway or under a deck, you can call and ask for an inspection. These are utility installation rules, not do-it-yourself instructions.

If you smell gas

Do not diagnose it yourself. Leave, avoid switches and phones inside, and call from a safe distance. PSE&G's page says outdoors to move at least 350 feet away, and South Jersey Gas says to evacuate and then call 911 and the company. Full steps are in Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do.

Delivery pressure as the utility states it

PSE&G's piping manual says standard low-pressure service varies between 4 and 7 in. w.c., and that for CSST systems gas leaves the meter at 1 psi with a secondary regulator downstream designed for 7 in. w.c. The manual says that regulator must deliver 5 to 7 in. w.c. to the branch lines. Those are PSE&G's figures; another utility may differ. Elizabethtown's 5 in. w.c. is the pressure base used to measure gas for billing, not a promise of delivery pressure. For CSST piping see CSST Yellow Gas Piping: Bonding and Lightning, What to Know.

Worked example: delivery pressure against a furnace inlet range

Goodman's GMVC96 installation manual lists natural gas inlet pressure as 4.5 minimum and 10.0 maximum in. w.c. We compare PSE&G's stated numbers to that range. NIST lists 1 inch of water as 249.082 pascals and 1 psi as 6,894.757 pascals.

  1. Inches per psi: 6,894.757 / 249.082 = 27.68 in. w.c. per psi.
  2. Top of standard low pressure: 7 / 27.68 = 0.253 psi, or 0.253 x 16 = 4.05 ounces per square inch.
  3. Bottom of standard low pressure: 4 / 27.68 = 0.145 psi.
  4. Goodman's range in psi: 4.5 / 27.68 = 0.163 psi up to 10.0 / 27.68 = 0.361 psi.
  5. Compare: 7 in. w.c. is inside 4.5 to 10.0, with 7 - 4.5 = 2.5 in. to spare. The 4 in. w.c. low end is 4.5 - 4 = 0.5 in. below the furnace minimum, before any pressure drop in your piping. That is why pipe size and the other gas appliances matter.
  6. CSST case: 1 psi x 27.68 = 27.68 in. w.c. at the meter outlet. Against the 10.0 maximum that is 27.68 - 10.0 = 17.68 in. w.c. too high, which is why the secondary regulator must bring it down to 7 in. w.c.

The takeaway: a utility number is a number at the meter, not at the appliance. Measure at the furnace as shown in Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer. For turning meter readings into therms, see Reading Your Gas Bill, and for timing the meter dial, Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.

Field card: Gas Meter and Regulator Basics

Meter and regulator are the utility's; house piping is yours.

  • Read your own utility's page; the dividing line differs.
  • NJNG and South Jersey Gas both put your responsibility from the meter onward.
  • Keep the meter, regulator vent and outside vents clear of snow, ice and debris. Clear gently by hand.
  • Never remove, reset or adjust utility equipment.
  • Buried service line valve: call the utility, do not dig.
  • Smell gas: leave, then call from a safe distance.
  • 1 psi = 27.68 in. w.c. Utility pressure is stated at the meter, not the appliance.
