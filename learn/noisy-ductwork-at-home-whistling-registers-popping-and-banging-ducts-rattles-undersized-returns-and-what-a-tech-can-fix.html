Noisy Ductwork at Home: Whistling, Popping, Rattles and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A duct system is a set of passages that move air, and moving air makes sound. Some sound is normal. A whistle, a bang or a roar usually means air is going too fast, going through too small an opening, or hitting something loose. This lesson sorts the common noises, shows what you can safely check yourself, and explains what a technician does with test instruments.

Start with how fast the air is moving

A U.S. Department of Energy national-lab guideline on air distribution says it plainly: as air speed rises in a duct system, so does the noise. It adds that an outlet run above its rated velocity will whistle or hiss, and it gives NC 30 or lower as the usual noise target for living spaces (section 2.3). NC is a standard way to rate background sound, and a lower number is quieter. So most duct noise is a speed problem, and speed comes from too much air squeezed through too little area.

Whistling registers and grilles

A whistle at a supply register usually means a lot of air through a small opening. Two common causes are a damper that was partly closed to push air to another room and a register that is simply too small for the airflow it receives. A national-lab guide on balancing homes notes that opening or closing register dampers to rebalance air can cause whistling because of the increased air velocity. The same page warns that closing too many dampers can leave too little air moving through the furnace or air handler, which in cooling season can ice up the evaporator coil. Our own tip: if you closed vents to save energy, open them back up and listen. Our lesson Does Closing Vents in Unused Rooms Save Energy? covers the tradeoffs.

Undersized or blocked returns

Every cubic foot of air a furnace blows out has to come back. When the return is too small, the return grille becomes the noisy spot: a rushing hiss, sometimes a whooshing at the grille when the blower starts. The national-lab guideline sets a target of return capacity equal to twice the supply volume, return duct velocity under 500 feet per minute (fpm), and a grille whose net free area is about 1.5 times the return duct area. It also says a room with no good return path becomes pressurized and impedes supply airflow, and that a typical door undercut of one-half to three-quarters of an inch is not adequate. A separate national-lab page on ducted returns repeats that undercuts alone do not give enough return volume, especially over carpet. The balancing page says return grilles and ducts that are too small should be enlarged if possible, or a second return path added, and that a low return grille should not be blocked by furniture or storage.

A filter that is too restrictive

A filter is part of the return path. The national-lab page on higher-efficiency filters says the filter typically accounts for 20 to 50 percent of the total system pressure drop, and that filters get more restrictive as they load with dirt. It also says a higher MERV rating does not automatically mean more resistance, because pleat depth and filter area matter. It tells readers to replace a filter right away if it makes a banging noise when the system comes on. The U.S. Department of Energy says a clogged filter restricts airflow through the unit and lowers efficiency. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV.

Popping and banging when the blower starts or stops

In our experience, a pop or bang that happens right when the blower starts or stops is most often one of two things. The first is thin sheet metal that flexes as the air pressure inside the duct rises and falls, often called oil-canning; large flat duct sides are the usual culprits. The second is metal expanding as warm air enters and shrinking as it cools, so the duct creaks or ticks against a strap, joist or neighboring duct. We did not find a federal or university page that measures these two causes, so treat this as our own field experience. Because a loaded filter or small return raises the pressure swing in the duct, checking the filter comes first.

Rumbling, roaring and rattles

A low rumble or roar from the furnace cabinet can come from the blower moving more air against more resistance than it likes, from a loose panel, or from the burner. The burner cases belong to a technician; see Furnace, AC and Boiler Noises: What Each Sound Means. A rattle in the ducts is often a loose hanger, strap or screw, or a duct touching framing. A city environmental agency's building-noise guidance sheet recommends a flexible connection where a duct meets a fan housing, so blower vibration does not travel down the metal. A tech can check whether that connection is intact or has been stiffened by tape.

What you can check yourself

  • The filter. Look at it with the blower off. Replace it if it is loaded, and make sure it is the size the slot calls for and seated flat.
  • Registers. Open supply registers fully and check that nothing, such as a rug, couch or drape, sits on one.
  • Return grilles. Keep every return grille clear and the filter grille latched. Check that closed bedroom doors are not starving a room of return air.
  • Loose parts. With the blower running, listen at the furnace for a loose access panel and for a register cover that rattles in its boot.

Stop and call a technician if you smell gas, see soot, scorching or water at the furnace, or hear a loud boom at ignition. Do not open sealed furnace compartments. For the leak side of duct trouble, see Sealing Duct Leaks: Mastic vs Foil Tape and Where to Look.

What a technician fixes

  • Static pressure test. A manometer reads pressure on the supply and return sides of the blower. The Department of Energy measure guideline for gas furnaces lists dirty or high-efficiency filters among the causes of high external static pressure and says furnace fans are designed to work against an external static pressure at rated flow, which it states as typically 0.5 to 1.0 inch of water column. The national-lab filter page says the total should be no more than about 0.4 to 0.5 inch of water column. The two figures come from different documents and may not mean the same measurement, so the tech compares readings with the data plate on your own furnace.
  • Blower speed. On a fixed-speed blower motor, the speed is set by connections on the control board, and the same Department of Energy guideline says it should be set per the manufacturer's instructions. A national laboratory report found a 30 percent airflow reduction at a typical operating pressure difference in one tested furnace, so resistance and the speed setting both matter.
  • More return. Enlarging a return grille or duct, adding a second return, or adding a transfer path for closed rooms.
  • Duct reinforcement. Adding cross-breaking, stiffeners or bracing to flexing panels, and re-strapping or replacing loose hangers. This is our own description of common practice.
  • Flexible connectors. Restoring a flexible connection at the equipment so vibration does not travel into the ducts.
  • Bigger filter area. A deeper filter cabinet adds area, which the national-lab filter page says lowers pressure drop without changing the MERV rating.

If you are thinking of replacing the furnace, noise and pressure problems often come from the existing ducts. See Replacing a Gas Furnace With a Ducted Heat Pump.

Worked example (our own): a small return grille

This example is ours. Every input marked "assumed" is a round number chosen for illustration, not a measurement of your home.

  1. Airflow (assumed): 1,000 cfm, about what a 2.5-ton system moves at an assumed 400 cfm per ton.
  2. Grille (assumed): a 16 in by 20 in return grille with 60 percent free area.
  3. Free area: 16 x 20 = 320 sq in; 320 x 0.60 = 192 sq in; 192 / 144 = 1.33 sq ft.
  4. Velocity: 1,000 cfm / 1.33 sq ft = about 750 fpm.
  5. Quiet limit (cited): a national-lab design page gives 300 fpm face velocity for return and transfer grilles. That page does not say whether it uses free area or the nominal opening, so we used free area, which gives the higher and more cautious figure.
  6. Comparison: 750 / 300 = 2.5, so this grille runs 2.5 times faster than the limit.
  7. Area needed: 1,000 / 300 = 3.33 sq ft = 480 sq in of free area. At 60 percent free, that is 480 / 0.60 = 800 sq in of grille, for example 20 in by 40 in, or two 20 by 20 grilles.
  8. Larger grille result: 1,000 cfm / 3.33 sq ft = 300 fpm, which meets the limit.

The same page says return filter grilles should be sized for the highest MERV filter likely to be used, to avoid excess pressure drop. Real grilles have their own free-area data, and a technician should use it.

Field card: Noisy Ductwork at Home: Whistling, Popping, Rattles and Fixes

Noise is speed, restriction or something loose.

  • Whistle at a register or grille: air too fast through too small an opening, or a half-closed damper.
  • Hiss at a return: return too small or blocked. Cited target: grille face velocity near 300 fpm.
  • Check first: filter, fully open registers, clear return grilles.
  • Bang when the blower starts: check the filter, then call a tech.
  • Tech fixes: static pressure test, blower speed, more return, duct bracing, flexible connector.
  • Gas smell, soot or boom at ignition: stop and call a technician.
