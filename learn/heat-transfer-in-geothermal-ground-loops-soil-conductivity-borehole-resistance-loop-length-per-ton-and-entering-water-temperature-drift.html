Heat Transfer in Geothermal Ground Loops: Soil Conductivity, Borehole Resistance, Loop Length per Ton and Entering Water Temperature Drift

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A ground loop does not make heat. It is a long, slow heat exchanger buried in dirt, and the dirt is the slowest part of the chain. This lesson follows one pulse of heat from the soil to the loop fluid, and explains why loops are sized by length, why the water coming back to the heat pump changes with the seasons, and how a thermal response test measures what the ground can do.

Check the design data

Check the design data. Numbers from sources below are what those reports measured, not rules for your site. Real loops are sized with design software and site data by the designer. This page is general education. For loop types see Geothermal (Ground-Source) Heat Pump Basics, and for filling and purging see Geothermal Ground Loop Flushing and Purging.

The path heat takes

The Department of Energy describes a closed vertical loop as bores about four inches across, roughly 20 feet apart and 100 to 400 feet deep, with two pipes joined at the bottom by a U-bend. A water or water-glycol blend circulates through the pipes and passes heat to the refrigerant in the heat pump. In heating mode the heat moves one direction through four steps in series, as the figure shows:

  1. Through the soil toward the borehole, by conduction.
  2. Through the grout that fills the space between the pipes and the borehole wall.
  3. Through the pipe wall, a thin layer of plastic.
  4. Into the moving fluid, through the thin film of liquid against the inside wall.

In cooling mode every arrow reverses: heat goes from the fluid out to the soil. Heat only moves because there is a temperature difference, so the fluid has to run colder than the soil to collect heat, and warmer than the soil to dump it. Each step uses up part of that difference. The more resistance in the chain, the bigger the temperature gap the heat pump has to live with. For why a thin plastic wall rarely limits heat flow, see Thermal Conductivity of HVAC Materials.

Soil conductivity and moisture

Soil is the largest and slowest piece of the chain, because heat spreads outward through a growing volume of ground. How well the soil conducts heat sets how much length a loop needs. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) reported measured ground conductivities of about 1.2 to 2.2 Btu/h·ft·°F across twelve test cases, and 1.02 to 2.47 W/m·K across nine others. Those are from specific tested sites, not a universal range.

Moisture matters because water conducts heat far better than the air that fills dry pores. A Purdue geothermal symposium presentation on field soil measurements called soil moisture the dominant control on conductivity. It reported that a three-fold rise in conductivity, such as dry sand to saturated sand, could cut the required loop length by about 30 percent. That study looked at horizontal trench loops, so treat the 30 percent as a direction and a rough size, not a bore figure. The same study found measured values between about 0.8 and 2 W/m·K across its sites, and that seasonal soil temperature can sit several degrees off a simple model. For slab and basement ground temperature, see Heat Loss from Slabs on Grade.

Borehole thermal resistance

Borehole thermal resistance is the combined resistance between the circulating fluid and the borehole wall: the fluid film, the pipe wall and the grout. A designer multiplies it by the heat flow per foot of bore to get the temperature difference it eats. A published laboratory and field study, indexed by the U.S. Department of Energy's OSTI service, tested grout conductivity, borehole diameter, pipe size and pipe layout. It found resistance fell as grout conductivity rose, with only very small gains above about 1.0 Btu/h·ft·°F. In the field, raising grout conductivity from 0.43 to 0.85 Btu/h·ft·°F cut the overall fluid-to-earth resistance by 15.3 to 19.5 percent. The takeaway for the field: voids, poor grout and pipes pressed against the bore wall add resistance that the ground cannot make up for. The study also reported that assuming equal spacing of the pipes from the borehole wall conservatively accounted for the grout.

Why loops are sized by length per ton

Heat transfer to the ground happens along the surface of the pipe and bore, so capacity grows with length of bore, not with the size of the heat pump. Designers think in heat per foot of bore. Because soil, grout, climate and the cooling-to-heating load ratio change that heat per foot, the feet-per-ton number changes with them. There is no one figure to memorize; the Purdue trench study, for example, listed designs of 100 to 400 feet of trench per ton, where 1 ton is 12,000 Btu/h. Bore lengths come from the software run for the actual site.

Why entering water temperature drifts

Entering water temperature (EWT) is the loop fluid temperature arriving at the heat pump. It follows the season, falling in a long cold spell as the ground around the bore is drawn down, and rising in a hot spell as heat is pushed in. Over years the trend depends on balance. If a building takes out more heat in winter than it puts back in summer, the ground slowly cools; if it rejects more in summer than it extracts in winter, the ground slowly warms. A journal article indexed by OSTI, on a hybrid system in a cooling-dominated climate, says imbalance may degrade the loop's ability to absorb or reject heat, and that pairing the field with a supplemental boiler or cooling tower balances the loads and lets the well field be smaller. An ORNL review likewise recommends hybrid designs where heat rejected far exceeds heat extracted. Neither source gave a rate of drift, so we give none. Low EWT in heating is also why antifreeze is used.

Thermal response tests

A thermal response test (TRT) measures the ground on site, in one test bore, before the whole field is drilled. A heater and pump add (or remove) a known heat rate to the loop fluid while the fluid temperature is logged. ORNL describes a heat flux applied for a certain period, usually 48 hours. The average fluid temperature plots as a straight line against the natural log of time, and the slope gives ground conductivity (k = q / 4πm in its line-source equation). Early data is discarded. ORNL says the industry standard calls for 36 to 48 hours, and its newer method cuts the test time by roughly 40 to 60 percent and tolerates unstable power. Borehole resistance appears as a term in the same line-source equation, so it is part of what the test result reflects.

Worked example (our own example)

These inputs are assumptions, not a design: a heating load of 36,000 Btu/h delivered by the heat pump, an average heating COP of 3.5, 450 feet of total bore, 9 gpm of water-antifreeze flow, and a flow factor of 480 in place of 500.

  1. Ground heat extraction. The compressor power ends up in the delivered heat, so the ground supplies the rest: Qground = heat delivered × (1 − 1/COP) = 36,000 × (1 − 1/3.5) = 36,000 × 0.7143 = about 25,714 Btu/h. The remaining 10,286 Btu/h (36,000 ÷ 3.5) is electrical input.
  2. Per foot of bore. 25,714 ÷ 450 = about 57.1 Btu/h per foot. Heat flows from the soil into the loop.
  3. Fluid temperature drop. Q = 480 × gpm × ΔT, so ΔT = 25,714 ÷ (480 × 9) = 25,714 ÷ 4,320 = about 5.95°F, call it 6°F. Fluid returns from the ground at EWT and leaves the heat pump about 6°F colder, then warms again in the ground.

Why 480 and not 500? The 500 in the common water formula comes from water, about 8.33 pounds per gallon times 60 minutes per hour times 1 Btu per pound-degree. Antifreeze blends carry less heat per gallon-degree than plain water (our reasoning, from the lower heat capacity of glycol), so the factor drops. The exact value depends on the antifreeze type and strength, so 480 is our assumption; use the figure for your fluid. With 500 the same load gives 25,714 ÷ 4,500 = 5.71°F, so the lower factor makes the real drop a little larger. As a rough sense of scale, 450 ft for 36,000 Btu/h is 150 feet of bore for every 12,000 Btu/h delivered, but this is arithmetic on assumed numbers, not a sizing rule, and cooling load can govern.

Field card: Heat Transfer in Geothermal Ground Loops

Heat moves soil, grout, pipe wall, fluid. Every layer costs temperature.

  • Heating: heat flows from soil to fluid. Cooling: fluid to soil.
  • Soil conductivity and moisture set how much bore length is needed.
  • Borehole resistance is fluid film plus pipe wall plus grout.
  • Better grout lowers resistance, with small gains past about 1.0 Btu/h·ft·°F in one study.
  • Unbalanced yearly loads make the ground slowly cool or warm.
  • A thermal response test heats the loop for about 36 to 48 hours and reads conductivity from the slope.
  • Our example: 36,000 Btu/h at COP 3.5 means about 25,714 Btu/h from the ground.
