Undercharge vs. Overcharge: A Symptom Table

Gauges rarely hand you the answer. They hand you a pattern. This lesson lays out the patterns that service sources describe for a residential cooling system so you can compare what you see, and know when a pattern is ambiguous.

The five readings

HVAC School's refrigerant diagnosis article uses five readings: suction pressure, head pressure, superheat, subcooling, and the temperature split across the evaporator. Its reference points, which are rules of thumb and not a replacement for the unit's chart: suction saturation about 35 degrees below return air temperature, head about 15 to 20 degrees above outdoor temperature, TXV superheat near 10 and subcooling near 10, and an air split of roughly 16 to 22 degrees. Learn the readings in Superheat and Subcooling Basics and Pressure-Temperature Basics.

Where the table misleads

  • Low suction is not proof of low charge. HVAC School's overcharge page says low suction more often points to a restriction or poor airflow. Adding refrigerant because suction looks low is a classic way to create an overcharge.
  • Overcharge and a dirty condenser look alike. Both can show high head and high subcooling. Check the coil visually and compare liquid line temperature to ambient.
  • A restriction does not usually give high head. Another HVAC School page says clogged driers, screens, or kinked lines produce low suction and normal-to-low head. A restriction plus an overcharge from someone adding charge can give high head, though.
  • Airflow problems change the numbers too; see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

How to avoid the trap

  1. Check airflow (filter, coil, blower) and the condenser before anything else.
  2. Look at the whole pattern, not one gauge.
  3. Know the metering device type; see Metering Devices: TXV and Piston.
  4. Confirm a low charge with a leak search, and fix the leak before adding any refrigerant; see Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.
  5. HVAC School recommends weighing in a charge with a scale rather than relying only on pressures, and letting the system equalize before adding.
  6. Charge by the manufacturer's method for that unit; see Charging Methods Overview.

Refrigerant handling is regulated. Never vent refrigerant, and recover what you remove; see EPA 608 Basics.

Field card: Undercharge vs. Overcharge: A Symptom Table

Read the pattern, not one gauge.

  • Low charge: low suction and head, high superheat, low subcooling.
  • Overcharge: high suction and head, low superheat, high subcooling.
  • Metering or liquid line restriction: low suction, high superheat, high subcooling.
  • Dirty condenser and overcharge can look alike; check the coil first.
  • Low suction alone is not proof of low charge; do not add refrigerant by guess.
