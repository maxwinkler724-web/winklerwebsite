Heating and Cooling a Straw-Bale Home: Small Loads and Dry Walls

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A straw-bale house has walls a foot and a half thick or more, and that mass of insulation changes the whole heating and cooling job. Loads are small, so it is easy to install equipment that is too big. The bigger danger is not cold or heat at all. It is water getting into the bales and staying there.

Why the loads are small

Heat loss through a wall follows one simple relation: area times temperature difference, divided by the wall R-value. A thicker wall means a bigger R and less heat moving through. A federal housing research text on adobe, rammed earth and straw-bale walls lists whole-bale values from roughly R-26 to R-33.5, depending on bale size and how the bales are laid. That text also says its own figures for two-string bales are inconsistent, so treat any single number as an estimate. Our worked example below uses a round number of our own instead.

Walls are only part of the load. A tight, well-insulated shell still loses heat at windows, doors and the roof, and the roof matters just as much as the walls. When the wall load drops, the remaining load shifts toward windows, air leaks and ventilation, which is why the next sections matter.

Worked example (our own): bale wall vs framed wall

Our own example, with assumed round numbers. The R-values are assumptions for teaching, not test results. Compare 1,200 ft2 of wall at a 50 F difference between indoors and outdoors (for example 70 F inside and 20 F outside).

  • Bale wall: 18 in x an assumed R-1.45 per inch = R-26.1, call it about R-26.
  • Framed wall: an assumed whole-wall R-15 (the whole-wall value is lower than the cavity R because framing conducts heat; see the heat-loss lesson linked below).

Heat loss in Btu/h = area x temperature difference / R.

  • Bale wall: 1,200 x 50 = 60,000; 60,000 / 26.1 = 2,299, about 2,300 Btu/h.
  • Framed wall: 60,000 / 15 = 4,000 Btu/h.
  • Difference: 4,000 - 2,299 = 1,701 Btu/h. 1,701 / 4,000 = 0.425, so the bale wall loses roughly 43 percent less heat through the wall area (check: 0.57 x 4,000 = 2,280, which matches within rounding).

The saving is about 1,700 Btu/h on this wall area. In our own judgment, even the smallest common equipment can exceed the whole-house load of a tight bale home, which is the oversizing trap. This example covers walls only. Windows, roof, air leaks and ventilation still need to be added to get a real load. Our log-home and earth-sheltered lessons show how those other pieces change the answer.

Oversizing: the quiet problem

Equipment sized by rule of thumb or by the old house it replaced will be much too big for a tight bale wall. An oversized cooling unit satisfies the thermostat before it has run long enough to pull water out of the air, so humidity stays high. In a bale home that is more than a comfort issue, because damp indoor air is one of the moisture sources we want to avoid. Size equipment from a real room-by-room load calculation, then pick the smallest unit that covers the result. See our short-cycling lesson for what oversizing does to a system.

Moisture is the real risk

The federal housing text says plainly that preventing moisture penetration is the most important factor in a straw-bale building. It gives several specifics: bales should be under 20 percent moisture by weight when installed and dry further before plastering; the bales should sit at least 8 inches above finished grade on a damp-proofed foundation; roof overhangs should reach at least two to three feet past the wall face; and the plaster, at least an inch thick, must breathe so that vapor can leave. It describes earth and lime plasters as better than cement for breathability and compatibility. It does not mention a vapor barrier, and its emphasis is on letting the wall dry.

  • Plaster that lets walls dry. A plaster skin keeps liquid water and fire out, while still passing vapor.
  • Roof overhangs. Wide eaves keep rain off the wall face, which is cheaper than repairing wet bales.
  • No vapor-tight interior finish. A paint, tile or sheet layer that traps vapor can hold water inside the wall. Ask the builder what finish the wall was designed for before adding anything.
  • Keep indoor humidity moderate. A federal environmental agency guide recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and suggests a basic hygrometer to check.
  • Fix leaks fast. The same guide says mold usually does not grow if wet materials are dried within 24 to 48 hours. In a thick bale wall a slow plumbing or roof leak can hide for a long time, so act at the first stain or smell.

Air leaks at joints

Thick walls do not help if air moves around them. Look at the window and door frames, where the plaster meets the frame, and at the roof-to-wall joint. Our own caution: moist indoor air that leaks outward through a joint may condense inside a cold wall. Sealing those joints is a good first job when a bale home feels drafty, and it is cheaper than adding capacity. Our attic-hatch and entrance lessons have more on leaks.

Equipment that fits a small load

  • Small ductless heads. One or two small heat pump heads can heat and cool a tight home. They need no duct runs, and a thick bale wall has no cavity for ducts anyway. Our ceiling-cassette lesson is linked in the related list.
  • A small direct-vent heater. A sealed-combustion unit takes air from outside and vents outside, which keeps combustion gases and moisture out of the room. Install it by the maker's instructions.
  • Spot ventilation. Run a bathroom fan when showering and a kitchen fan when cooking, ducted outdoors. The environmental agency guide gives the same advice, and also says to vent moisture-producing appliances outside where possible. See our bathroom-fan lesson.

Carbon monoxide and fire safety

A tight house holds combustion gases, so any fuel-burning heater needs a working CO alarm. A federal consumer product safety agency says to put alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, to avoid spots near heating vents or covered by furniture, and not to put them in kitchens or directly above fuel-burning appliances. The test button checks the circuit, not the sensor, and alarms have a replacement age. If one sounds, get outside to fresh air and call for help; do not stay to look for the source. The same agency says never to run unvented fuel-burning appliances where people sleep and to have heating systems inspected each year.

For stoves, our own advice is general: keep clearances the maker lists, use a noncombustible hearth, and keep the flue and chimney clean and clear. Plaster helps resist fire on the wall surface, but it does not replace clearances.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Straw-Bale Home

Small load, big risk: size down, keep it dry.

  • Heat loss = area x temperature difference / R. Thicker wall, smaller load.
  • Our assumed example: R-26 bale wall vs R-15 framed wall, about 2,300 vs 4,000 Btu/h, roughly 43 percent less.
  • Size from a real load calculation. Oversized cooling leaves humidity high.
  • Moisture: breathable plaster, wide overhangs, no vapor-tight interior finish, humidity 30 to 50 percent.
  • Fix leaks within 24 to 48 hours, and seal window and roof joints.
  • Small ductless heads or a small direct-vent heater; bath and kitchen fans ducted outdoors.
  • CO alarm with any fuel-burning heater; keep stove clearances.
