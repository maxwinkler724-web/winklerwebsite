Egg Washing Rooms and Egg Coolers on Farms: Temperature, Humidity, Sweating and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Eggs leave the hen at about body temperature and spend the next day moving through a wash room, a dryer and a cooler. Each step is a small HVAC problem: warm wet air in one room, cold dry-ish air in the next, and a shell that sweats whenever it meets air that is too moist for its surface temperature. This lesson walks through the targets, the physics and a sizing example.

Where the heat and moisture come from

A state university extension fact sheet says an egg is laid at about 105°F, the normal body temperature of the hen. Every dozen therefore carries heat that the cooler must remove, and the wash room adds warm water and vapor on top of it. Neither room is a comfort space. Both are process spaces with a temperature, a humidity and a time target.

Storage temperature: the federal rule in general terms

A federal food-safety guide for small egg producers says eggs from farms with 3,000 or more laying hens must be refrigerated beginning 36 hours after they are laid, and that the maximum for holding and transporting eggs at least 36 hours old is 45°F ambient (air) temperature. Note the wording: it is an air temperature, not an egg temperature. A cooler that holds 45°F air still needs hours to pull the eggs themselves down to that level. Whether the rule applies to a given farm depends on its size and the details of the regulation, so confirm with the agency; this page is general education, not legal advice.

A federal egg-grading manual adds practical targets. It says coolers for unprocessed eggs should be able to hold 60°F or lower, coolers for processed eggs should hold 45°F air or lower, and all coolers should be able to hold 70 to 85 percent relative humidity. A state extension fact sheet gives a similar 40 to 45°F with 70 to 85 percent. For the equipment, that means a box set a little under 45°F with a coil that does not dry the room out. Evaporator Td and humidity are covered in the linked box-load and small-farm cooler lessons.

Why eggs sweat

Sweating is condensation on the shell. The federal grading manual says every reasonable precaution should be exercised to prevent it and notes it usually happens when eggs move from a very cool area into a moderate-temperature one. A university poultry fact sheet adds that condensation lets microbes move through the shell pores and says to keep egg temperature fairly constant until the eggs are washed.

The cause is the dew point. Water vapor condenses on any surface colder than the dew point of the air touching it. Using a standard psychrometric relation, our own calculation puts the dew point of 75°F air at 60 percent relative humidity at about 60°F. A shell at 45°F is 15 degrees below that, so it fogs the moment it leaves the cooler into that room. See psychrometrics for technicians for the method. Practical consequences for design: keep the loading and packing area drier and cooler than a wash room, do not open a cooler directly into steam, and let eggs warm slowly in a closed container when they must come out.

Wash water and egg temperature

Both the grading manual and a state extension fact sheet say to wash with water at least 90°F and at least 20°F warmer than the eggs. The reason is that warm water keeps the contents expanding outward so dirt is pushed away from the pores rather than pulled in. A university poultry fact sheet also warns that water much warmer than the eggs can cause thermal cracks and suggests not exceeding the egg temperature by more than 40°F. Eggs should not soak: the same sheet says soaking for only one to three minutes can let microbes in. The manual also says wash water must be corrected quickly if it drops below the minimum.

The HVAC takeaway is a conflict: do not rapidly chill eggs before washing (the extension sheets say so), yet do cool and dry them quickly after. So the wash room should be a steady, moderate space, and the cooler should be the place the eggs go once dry. The grading manual says eggs should be reasonably dry before packaging, and the university sheet says wet eggs should not be packed into cartons because they stick.

Humid wash room exhaust and makeup air

Wash water at 90°F or more evaporates into the room air. Our own reasoning, not a code rule: the moisture has to leave by exhaust or be removed by a dehumidifier, and the exhaust needs replacement air so the fans can move what they are rated for. If that replacement air is cold, it cools the wash room and may make window and ceiling surfaces sweat. Direct exhaust outdoors from the wash machine hood or the room, and keep the air path moving from the dry, clean side toward the wash room rather than the other way. Do not let wash room air drift into the cooler vestibule.

Condensation and mold in the cooler

The grading manual says cooler rooms should be free of objectionable odors and mold, and lists high storage humidity among the factors that encourage mold. That is why the top of the 70 to 85 percent range is a ceiling, not a goal. Condensation shows up on door frames, ceiling panels, unsealed penetrations and cold coil lines, and on the eggs themselves when warm wet eggs enter. Fixes are mostly mechanical: sealed gaskets, a vestibule or strip curtain, drying eggs before they go in, and a coil that can recover. See the air versus product temperature lesson for probe placement.

Sizing from egg heat and wall gains

Product load is mass times specific heat times temperature drop. Wall and door gains are a separate, continuous load; the box load lesson shows how to estimate them. A cooler must be sized for the pull-down of a collection peak and the steady gains together, because the product heat arrives in batches.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example; the inputs marked as assumptions are not from a source.

  • Eggs: 300 dozen, all large. The grading manual's minimum for large is 24 ounces per dozen, so we use 1.5 lb per dozen.
  • Laying temperature 105°F, as the extension sheet states. Storage target 45°F, the air temperature in the federal guide. Assumption: eggs are cooled to the target value.
  • Specific heat: a federal egg pasteurization manual gives 0.88 Btu/lb°F for whole egg above freezing, from the relation (percent water + 0.5 × percent solids) / 100. We apply it to the whole shell egg as an approximation; the shell is a small share of the mass, so treat the result as rough.
  • Assumption: the cooler has 9,000 Btu/h gross capacity at the running condition and 3,000 Btu/h of wall, door and fan gains, leaving a net 6,000 Btu/h for pull-down.

Step 1, mass: 300 dozen × 1.5 lb = 450 lb.
Step 2, temperature drop: 105 − 45 = 60°F.
Step 3, heat removed: 450 lb × 0.88 Btu/lb°F × 60°F = 23,760 Btu.
Step 4, time at net capacity: 23,760 Btu ÷ 6,000 Btu/h = 3.96 hours, about 4 hours.

Real eggs also lose heat unevenly: cartons and flats slow it, so use open racks and air movement. The result tells you a coil sized only for steady wall gains will fall hours behind during a collection peak.

Alarms

Our own practice suggestions, not a rule: a high-temperature alarm with a delay long enough to ignore door openings, a high-humidity alarm, and a power-loss alarm that reaches a phone. Check that the alarm reads the same place you care about, and test it with the box warm. Record the temperature at least daily; a log helps show that eggs stayed cold.

Field card: Egg Washing Rooms and Egg Coolers

Warm water in, dry eggs out, cold air that is not wet.

  • Federal rule in general: 45°F ambient air for eggs at least 36 hours old, for larger flocks; confirm with the agency.
  • Cooler humidity target in the grading manual: 70 to 85 percent.
  • Sweating happens when shell surface temperature is below the dew point of the air.
  • Wash water: at least 90°F and at least 20°F warmer than the eggs.
  • Product heat = lb × 0.88 × temperature drop (our example input).
  • Mold and wet cartons are signs of excess moisture. Fix the source.
