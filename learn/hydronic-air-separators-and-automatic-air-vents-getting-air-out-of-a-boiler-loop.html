Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents: Getting Air Out of a Boiler Loop

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A closed hot water loop is supposed to be full of water and nothing else. Air gets in anyway, and it comes out of the water itself when the boiler heats it. This lesson is about the hardware that catches that air: air separators and automatic vents, which kind to pick, where the makers say to put them, and how the vent cap works.

Where the air comes from

Caleffi's free idronics journal lists the usual entry points: weepage at valve packings and circulator flange gaskets, air left behind after filling, air pulled in by a vent or packing where pressure falls below atmospheric, and oxygen diffusing through PEX and other plastic tubing. Bell & Gossett adds the one that surprises people: fresh make-up water carries dissolved air, which its air management manual puts at about 2 percent by volume. Every fill, and every top-off after a leak, brings in more.

Dissolved air stays invisible until conditions change. Water holds less gas as it gets hotter and as pressure drops, so the boiler is where gas leaves solution. Caleffi says the amount of dissolved gas water can hold depends on its temperature and pressure, and shows that water at 65 F can hold about twice as much as at 170 F at the same pressure.

Why it matters

  • Noise and cold spots. B&G says free air can create noise, reduce terminal output or even stop flow. The symptom lesson is Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water System.
  • Pump damage. Caleffi lists lost lubrication in circulator bushings, and B&G warns that low pressure at the pump brings noise, loss of circulation and often serious damage. See Pump Cavitation Basics.
  • Corrosion. Caleffi describes magnetite forming and possibly converting to hematite when oxygen keeps entering. B&G notes that a leaking automatic vent can bring in oxygen-bearing make-up water.

Air scoop or microbubble separator

An air scoop or purger relies on buoyancy. Water slows down inside it, bubbles that are already formed float up into a chamber, and a float vent lets them out. Caleffi says purgers do not capture microbubbles well. A coalescing or microbubble separator adds a mesh or media. Caleffi's Discal data sheet describes concentric mesh surfaces that create a whirling motion; microbubbles stick to the mesh, join into larger bubbles and rise when buoyancy beats the grip of the mesh. A tangential (vortex) type spins the water so lighter gas collects in the middle. B&G's manual claims combining that spin with a velocity drop removes more air per pass than either idea alone, though it gives no test data in that passage.

Velocity decides how well any of them work. B&G says about 2 ft/s keeps air carried along in the flow, while separating it needs the water to slow to 0.5 ft/s or less. Caleffi says optimal Discal performance is at or below about 4 ft/s, with 10 ft/s as the maximum at the connections. Taco's 4900 manual covers air (4900A) and air-and-dirt (4900AD) models, but its instruction sheet does not describe the internal mechanism, so check the maker's data for the exact model. Spirotherm calls its Spirovent an air eliminator and says it has no moving parts except the vent head. A separator can only remove gas that has already come out of solution. It does not pull dissolved gas out of cold water, so the system keeps cycling water past it until the dissolved air is gone.

Where to put it

The makers agree on the idea, with different wording. B&G: when possible pick a point with both the highest temperature and the lowest pressure, and put the point of no pressure change, where the expansion tank joins, on the pump suction side so the pump adds to the static pressure everywhere. Spirotherm: install at the point of lowest solubility, which it describes as after the boiler or heat exchanger and before the pump suction, and "pump away" from the expansion tank. Caleffi's Discal data sheet: preferably after the boiler and on the pump suction side, mounted vertically on horizontal pipe. Taco: as close as possible to the boiler exit and upstream of the pumps. Caleffi also recommends at least 12 pipe diameters of straight pipe between a separator outlet and the circulator inlet. For what the tank and fill valve do, see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure and Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer. With a primary loop and secondaries, see Primary-Secondary Piping Basics. For dirt and magnetite, the sister page is Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.

Automatic air vents: caps and leaks

A float vent works like a toilet tank in reverse. Air collects and lowers the float, which opens the valve; incoming water lifts the float and closes it. The cap is where problems start.

  • The cap must be loosened for service. Caleffi says a fully closed cap blocks operation. B&G's Number 97 vent sheet says to open the cap one full turn, and that fast release can let dirt foul the vent.
  • Closed during fill, then opened. B&G says to keep the cap closed while the system is filling so debris stays out. Taco's 4900 sheet closes its vent screw for the first fill and reopens it 1 to 1½ turns afterward.
  • Leaks. B&G says to inspect the vent periodically and replace it if it leaks or is corroded. Dirt, scale or pipe compound in the seat is the usual cause, so a dripping vent is a part to replace, not a nuisance to tighten.
  • Special caps and vents. Caleffi describes anti-siphon caps that block air intake when pressure falls below atmospheric, and hygroscopic vents with a disc that swells when wet and needs replacing about every three years. Caleffi's Discal data sheet suggests a hygroscopic safety vent where inspection is difficult.
  • Pressure at the top. Caleffi calls for at least 5 psi static pressure at the top of the system so vents work.

Purging

Hardware catches what is left; the first fill should push out the bulk of the air. Caleffi describes flow-through purging with a drain hose and the feed valve open, running until the discharge shows no visible air for at least 30 seconds. With several branches, purge one at a time, then open them together. With primary-secondary piping, purge the primary, then each secondary. Follow your boiler manual's purge steps.

Worked example

Part 1: how much air is in the water. Caleffi gives a case at 30 psi absolute, about 15 psi on a gauge. Its chart shows water at 65 F can hold up to 3.6 percent dissolved gas by volume, and at 170 F only 1.8 percent. For a 100 gallon system:

  1. Gas held at 65 F: 100 gal × 0.036 = 3.6 gal.
  2. Gas held at 170 F: 100 gal × 0.018 = 1.8 gal.
  3. Released as the water heats: 3.6 − 1.8 = 1.8 gal, which matches the 1.8 gallons of air per 100 gallons of water that Caleffi states.

That is about 416 cubic inches (1.8 × 231) that must be vented, more than enough to block several baseboard runs. Chart readings are approximate.

Part 2: pick a separator size. A boiler puts 150,000 Btu/h into the water with a 20 F temperature drop across the loop. Water moves about 500 Btu/h per GPM per degree (8.33 lb per gallon × 60 minutes × 1 Btu/lb F).

  1. Flow: 150,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 15 GPM.
  2. Caleffi's Discal table at 4.0 ft/s lists brass sizes: 1¼ in at 15 GPM, 1½ in at 22 GPM, 2 in at 39 GPM.
  3. The 1¼ in size is exactly at the rating. The 1½ in size leaves margin: velocity is roughly 4.0 × 15 ÷ 22 = 2.7 ft/s, scaled from the maker's rating.

Choose the 1½ in unit, then confirm it against the data sheet for the exact model and your pipe size. Not a design for your system.

Field card: Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents

Hot, low pressure, after the boiler, and keep the vent cap loose.

  • Air enters with make-up water (about 2 percent by volume per B&G), leaks and low-pressure spots.
  • Heating drives gas out of solution; a separator catches only gas already out.
  • Scoops use buoyancy; microbubble types add mesh; check the model.
  • Mount near the boiler outlet, with the expansion tank at the pump inlet.
  • Cap about one full turn open (B&G), closed during fill; a leaking vent gets replaced.
  • Flow in GPM = Btu/h ÷ (500 × temperature drop).
  • Purge until no visible air for 30 seconds (Caleffi).
