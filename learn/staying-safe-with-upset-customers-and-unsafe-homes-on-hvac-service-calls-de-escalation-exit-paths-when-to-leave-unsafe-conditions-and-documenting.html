Staying Safe With Upset Customers and Unsafe Homes on HVAC Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most service calls end with a working system and a customer who says thanks. A few do not. A customer may be angry about a bill, or the home itself may be unsafe. This lesson covers what federal safety agencies say about violence risk for people who work in other people's homes, how to keep a calm conversation calm, how to keep a way out, and when to simply leave.

What OSHA and NIOSH say

OSHA defines workplace violence as any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation or other threatening behavior at the work site. Its workplace violence page cites federal data showing 740 fatal workplace injuries in 2023 from violent acts, which it ranks as the third-leading cause of fatal occupational injuries. The page lists risk factors that include working alone or in isolated areas, dealing with volatile or unstable people, and providing services. It also says there is no specific OSHA standard for workplace violence, and that employers can reduce the risk with a written prevention program, training, and a zero-tolerance policy. NIOSH describes workplace violence as the act or threat of violence, from verbal abuse to physical assault, and says it can affect any worker in any setting.

OSHA's guidance for home health and social service workers is the closest match to a service tech walking into a stranger's house. It lists unfamiliar residences, possible firearms or knives, no way to call for help, and little backup as risk factors. Its suggestions carry over well: assess each home for exit routes, keep a way to communicate, use a buddy when safety is in question, and give workers discretion to decline to start or to continue a visit if they feel threatened or unsafe. For the solo-work side of this, such as check-in plans, see Working Alone as an HVAC Tech.

Calm de-escalation basics

De-escalation is about lowering the temperature before anything physical is possible. A university primer written for home-visiting social workers gives habits any field tech can borrow:

  • Speak more quietly and slowly than usual, in a friendly, steady tone.
  • Keep your hands visible and relaxed, keep a comfortable distance, and turn partly sideways instead of squaring off.
  • Set limits without attacking. The primer's example is a plain statement that you still want to help and also need everyone to feel safe.
  • Offer help without promising what you cannot deliver.

Most anger on HVAC calls is about cost, delay, or a house that is too hot or too cold. Listening first and stating findings in plain words helps; see Talking With Customers on HVAC Service Calls. If the person keeps escalating, stop trying to win the conversation. A calm voice is a tool, not a guarantee.

Keep an exit path

The same primer says to arrange yourself so you always have a clear route to the door and never let the client or anyone else box you in. On an HVAC call the work often pulls you the other way: furnaces sit in basements, air handlers in attics and closets, and the customer may stand at the only stairway. Before you set up, note where the door is, keep your toolbag and body on the exit side of the equipment when you can, and keep the walkway clear of your own gear. When to leave and call your office

The primer says to leave at the first sign of danger from the client or anyone else in the home. Its red flags are intoxication, verbal escalation, invading your space, ignoring limits, any weapon, and impulsive or reckless behavior. It adds that you do not have to explain why you are leaving. Once you are out, get to a reasonably safe place, then call 911 if you are in danger or call your supervisor. OSHA's guidance likewise says employers should have a policy letting staff refuse service in clearly hazardous situations. Follow your company's policy, and if you are in danger, call 911.

Unsafe homes

  • Hoarding. A fire-prevention office at a university says clutter can block doors and entrances, put flammable items near stoves, expose or damage wiring, and slow firefighters, and that heavy loads raise the chance of structural collapse. For a tech that means blocked exits and an equipment area you cannot reach or escape from. If you cannot see a clear route in and out, do not go in.
  • Drug labs. A Justice Department drug intelligence fact sheet lists signs that may occur together: chemical odors such as ether, ammonia or acetone, covered or foil-lined windows, chemical containers and waste, heavy security, and secretive occupants. It warns of fire, explosion, toxic vapors and burns, and says not to investigate or confront occupants because of possible booby traps. If you suspect a lab, leave, do not touch anything, and report it to the police from a safe place.
  • Weapons. Seeing a weapon is a red flag in the primer above. Do not touch it or comment on it; leave calmly.
  • Other conditions. Animals are covered in Dogs and Pets on HVAC House Calls, and gas or carbon monoxide hazards in Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Document the visit

OSHA's guidance asks employers to have workers report all threats and assaults to a supervisor, and to keep logs so patterns can be spotted. It also says workers can report concerns without retaliation. After a bad call, write down the time, address, what you saw and heard in plain words, who you told, and when you left. Do not guess motives. The ticket is the place for this; see Writing a Useful HVAC Service Ticket. Ask your employer how photos and notes should be handled.

Company policies that help

OSHA's guidance points to a written, zero-tolerance policy, training on warning signs and response, specific check-in and check-out steps with a code word to signal trouble, a way to request backup or police help, and a right to decline or stop a visit. Ask which of these your company has, and ask for the missing ones.

Worked example: a call that goes wrong (our own, hypothetical)

Assumptions. This is an invented scenario for study, not a real event. A no-heat call at 6 p.m. in winter. The tech works alone, the furnace is in the basement, and the customer is already angry about an earlier visit. Company policy lets techs stop any visit they feel is unsafe.

  1. Arrival. The customer shouts from the porch. Choice: the tech stays outside the door, speaks quietly, and says what the first step will be. The tech does not argue about the earlier visit.
  2. Going down. The customer follows to the stairs. Choice: the tech asks the customer to wait upstairs, and gets a yes or a no. A no is noted. The tech goes down only if the stair side stays clear, and sets the bag by the stairs, not by the furnace.
  3. Escalation. The customer comes down, stands at the foot of the stairs and raises their voice about the price. Choice: the tech stops work, keeps hands visible, uses a limit statement, and offers to explain the findings upstairs.
  4. Red flag. The customer blocks the stairs and the tech notices what looks like a weapon on a shelf. Choice: no more discussion. The tech says calmly that this needs a call to the office, and asks to step past.
  5. Leaving. If the way is clear, the tech leaves with the bag if it is easy to carry and leaves the tools if it is not. If the way is not clear, the tech calls 911 right away.
  6. Afterward. From a safe spot the tech calls the office, then writes the ticket notes while they are fresh.

Pre-call checklist (our own). Read the call notes for warnings. Tell the office the address and expected time. Charge the phone and know the code word. Park facing out. On entry, spot the door, stairs and any blocked paths. Set up on the exit side. Decide beforehand what would make you leave.

Field card: Staying Safe With Upset Customers and Unsafe Homes

Calm voice, clear exit, leave early.

  • Violence at work includes threats and intimidation, not only assault.
  • Speak slowly and quietly, keep hands visible, and set limits kindly.
  • Know where the door and stairs are before you set up, and stay on the exit side.
  • Leave at the first sign of danger. You do not owe an explanation.
  • Hoarding, drug labs and weapons mean leave and report from a safe place.
  • Call 911 if you are in danger. Follow company policy.
  • Write what happened on the ticket the same day.
