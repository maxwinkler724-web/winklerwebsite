Why Is My Heating or Cooling Bill So High? What to Check

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A high bill has three possible causes that people mix up: you used more energy, you paid a higher rate for it, or the weather was harsher. Sort those out first, and then look at the equipment. This lesson walks through the order a careful tech would use, and you can do most of it from your kitchen table with a few bills.

Step 1: Separate weather from everything else

Heating and cooling are the biggest part of a home's energy use. EIA reports that space heating and air conditioning made up 52% of household energy consumption in 2020, and it notes that use varies with climate, home size and equipment. Because the weather drives so much of it, a cold month will raise a bill even when nothing in the house changed.

The standard way to account for weather is the degree day. EIA explains that you take the day's mean temperature (the high plus the low, divided by 2) and compare it to a base of 65°F. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center uses the same 65°F base and describes heating degree days as the sum of how far the daily mean falls below it. EIA's own example: a high of 33°F and a low of 25°F gives a mean of 29°F, which is 65 minus 29, or 36 heating degree days (HDD). Cooling degree days work the other way, counting degrees above 65°F.

EIA uses the same idea for its winter bill forecasts. In its 2019 outlook it used NOAA's forecast of 3,635 heating degree days for the winter and noted that seven of the previous ten winters came within 10% of the forecast. So winter-to-winter swings are normal. Utilities, weather sites and the National Weather Service publish monthly HDD totals for your area, and some utilities print them on the bill.

Worked example: usage per degree day

Say you compare November and January. All numbers below are made up to show the arithmetic.

  1. Pull the energy used (not dollars) from each bill: November 900 kWh, January 1,700 kWh.
  2. Look up the heating degree days for each billing period: November 600 HDD, January 1,000 HDD.
  3. Estimate the non-heating baseline (lights, appliances, hot water) from a mild month, say 300 kWh. Subtract it: November 900 − 300 = 600 kWh; January 1,700 − 300 = 1,400 kWh.
  4. Divide by degree days: November 600 ÷ 600 = 1.00 kWh per HDD. January 1,400 ÷ 1,000 = 1.40 kWh per HDD.
  5. Compare: 1.40 ÷ 1.00 = 1.4, so the house needed 40% more energy per degree of cold in January.

January was colder, and that explains part of the jump: HDD rose 67%, from 600 to 1,000. But usage rose 133% on the heating part (600 to 1,400 kWh), far more than the weather alone. Something changed. The remaining steps are how you hunt for it. If the per-HDD number had stayed near 1.00, you could stop and blame the weather. This is a rough screen, not a measurement. Billing periods rarely match calendar months, and a baseline guess adds error.

Step 2: Read the rate on the bill

Next, divide the bill into quantity and price. Your bill lists usage (kWh, therms or gallons) and a rate or per-unit charge, and the rate can change with the season, a utility rate case or your supplier. EIA publishes average residential electricity prices by state and month in its Electric Power Monthly, so you can see whether your area's prices moved. In EIA's winter outlooks the expected expenditure change differs by fuel: in the 2020 outlook EIA expected propane households to spend about 14% more and heating oil households about 10% less, while gas and electric households saw slightly higher bills. If your usage per degree day held steady but the bill rose, the rate is the culprit and no equipment visit will fix it.

Step 3: Thermostat settings

DOE says turning the thermostat back 7°F to 10°F for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling. Check that schedules did not reset after a power outage, that a hold or vacation mode is not stuck, and that nobody raised the setpoint. One caution from the same DOE page: programmable thermostats are generally not recommended for heat pumps, because a deep setback in heating mode can make the system run inefficiently and cancel the savings. Smart thermostats made for heat pumps are the exception DOE notes. See Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Step 4: Filter and airflow

DOE states that replacing a dirty, clogged filter with a clean one can lower an air conditioner's energy use by 5% to 15%. A restricted filter also means less air moves through the system. Check the filter first: it costs the least and takes a minute. Also look for closed or blocked supply registers and furniture over returns. Sizes, MERV ratings and change intervals are in How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics.

Step 5: Heat pump backup and emergency heat

DOE explains that a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, and says it can cut electricity use for heating by up to 75% compared with electric resistance heating. That is why it matters when a heat pump leans on backup resistance strips. If your thermostat shows an indicator for auxiliary or emergency heat, note how often it comes on. Running on emergency heat all winter can raise the bill sharply, and so can a heat pump that cannot keep up. Read Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures and Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter: Is That Normal? before you decide it is a fault.

Step 6: Duct leakage and air sealing

ENERGY STAR says that in a typical forced-air home, leaks, holes and poorly connected ducts can waste roughly 20 to 30 percent of the conditioned air, and it lists signs such as high summer and winter bills, rooms that are hard to heat or cool, and ducts running through attics or crawlspaces. Its benefits page gives a smaller figure, saying leaky ducts can cut system efficiency by as much as 20 percent, so treat both as upper-range estimates, not a promise for your house. ENERGY STAR names mastic sealant and metal tape for sealing and advises against standard duct tape because it does not last. Whole-house air leaks (attic hatches, rim joists, gaps around penetrations) do similar harm. See Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Step 7: Equipment efficiency ratings

An older or lower-rated system uses more energy for the same comfort. Ratings such as SEER2, HSPF2, AFUE and EER2 tell you how a unit performs under test conditions; What Does a SEER2 Rating Mean? explains each one. A rating does not tell you your bill, because installation, duct condition and weather all change real results.

Short tech section: what a service visit adds

If steps 1 to 6 do not explain it, a technician can check airflow, refrigerant charge and control settings against the manufacturer's data, test the ducts for leakage, and verify that backup heat is staged correctly. Those are measured checks rather than guesses, and the findings belong in writing so you can compare next season's usage per degree day.

Field card: Why Is My Heating or Cooling Bill So High? What to Check

Weather first, rate second, house third.

  • Compare energy used (kWh, therms), not dollars.
  • Heating degree day: 65°F minus the day's mean temperature, if positive.
  • Usage per degree day = (usage − baseline) ÷ HDD. A jump means something changed.
  • Read the per-unit rate on the bill before blaming equipment.
  • Check thermostat schedule, filter, registers, heat pump backup heat.
  • Ducts: ENERGY STAR cites roughly 20 to 30 percent of air lost in a typical leaky system.
  • Ratings are test-condition numbers, not a bill prediction.
