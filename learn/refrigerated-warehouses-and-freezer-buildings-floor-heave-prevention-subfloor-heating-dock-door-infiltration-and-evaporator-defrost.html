Freezer Warehouses: Floor Heave, Subfloor Heat, Dock Doors and Defrost

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A big freezer building has the same parts as a walk-in, just scaled up, and three problems that a walk-in rarely has: the floor can lift because the ground under it freezes, the dock doors let in a lot of warm moist air, and the evaporators grow frost fast. This lesson walks through each one. Our walk-in lessons cover the small-box version, so we link to them instead of repeating them.

Check your own site

Check your own site. Numbers here come from public pages we read in 2026 and are for study. Real floor heating, door and defrost designs belong to the engineer of record and the equipment makers. Ammonia and high-pressure hot gas work needs site training and lockout rules, not a web page.

Why a freezer floor heaves

Even a well-insulated slab leaks a little cold downward. Plant Services explains that when the wet soil under the slab drops below 32°F, the water in it freezes, and capillary action pulls more water toward the frozen zone, where it builds an ice mass. That growing ice can lift columns, crack the slab and tilt racks. The same article lists early signs: small cracks and bulges, uneven grade, tilted racks, sticking doors and sinking corners. Repair means shutting the room down for weeks, so prevention is cheap by comparison. A Gresham Smith design article makes the point that even the best slab insulation cannot always keep ground moisture from freezing when the room is around minus 20°F. A UK builder of cold stores notes that chiller rooms that never drop below zero do not have this problem; freezers do.

Plant Services also gives a feel for scale: one inch of insulation is thermally about two feet of soil. Insulation slows the cold, but only heat stops the freeze.

Subfloor heating methods

  • Glycol loops. Plant Services describes propylene glycol warmed to about 65°F and pumped through pipes under the insulation. Watts, a maker of radiant tubing, gives 55 to 75°F fluid, inhibited propylene glycol at about 50 percent (never automotive antifreeze) and a design target of a 40°F sub-slab temperature. The warm fluid can come from heat reclaimed off the condenser side, so it can run year-round at low cost. The drawback is that a leak under a slab is hard to find. Gresham Smith advises designing redundant loops and pressure-testing the piping before the concrete pour.
  • Electric cable. Self-regulating cable in conduit under the slab is described by Plant Services as perhaps the best electric option, because the output rises as the cable gets colder and the conduit lets a failed cable be pulled and replaced. Utility and agency guidance leans away from resistance heat, though. Energy Trust of Oregon recommends a glycol slab system over electric resistance for new construction, and the California Energy Commission fact sheet for the 2022 energy code requires electric resistance underslab heat to be thermostat controlled and disabled during the summer on-peak period set by the local utility.
  • Ventilated pipes. Older designs run air through tubes under the insulation. Plant Services lists natural airflow (no power, but prone to frost and hard to design) and fan-forced airflow (controllable, but prone to blockage and condensation ice).

A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory demand-response guide abstract recommends removing under-floor resistance heaters and reusing condenser heat through a heat exchanger instead.

How techs monitor it

The sources we read are thin on probe placement, so we only report what they say. The Watts design aims to hold 40°F under the slab, so the control has to read a temperature there and cycle or modulate the heat source to hold it. Watts also lists a flow switch with an alarm that tells maintenance when a circulator stops, plus optional temperature display, pressure and power alarms. Plant Services mentions controllers with remote alarm contacts for cable zones. In our own practice terms, a tech watches for a sub-slab reading drifting down toward 32°F, a stopped pump or a tripped heater, and treats any of them as urgent because ice lenses grow slowly and are hard to undo. How deep the probes sit and where is not given in these sources, so use the plans and the engineer's trend alarms for the building in front of you.

Dock doors and infiltration

An ACHR News case study quotes the ASHRAE refrigeration handbook saying air infiltration through open doorways is the leading cause of extra refrigeration load in cold storage. Warm moist air that leaks in also leaves frost on coils and ice on floors and tracks. An ACEEE paper on one older refrigerated warehouse found the dock doors mostly stayed open all day; replacing them with insulated doors and sealing the dock cut the cooling loads of a freezer and a produce cooler by 36 and 59 percent in that building. It is one site and a 2002 paper, so treat the percentages as an example, not a rule.

The usual layers, from outside in:

  • Dock seals and shelters. Energy Trust of Oregon says shelters reduce warm air entering, and that foam dock seals are cheaper but perform worse than shelters. Worn or torn seals leak.
  • Fast doors. Energy Trust recommends high-speed doors to cut outside air entering the dock.
  • Vestibules and anteroom spaces. Gresham Smith suggests tight-sealing doors, strips that interrupt airflow, and intermediate-temperature rooms that act as air locks. An intermediate room means the cold space never sees full outdoor conditions.
  • Strip or air curtains. The California fact sheet requires barriers (strip curtains, an automatically closing door or an air curtain) at passageways between freezers and warmer spaces. Energy Trust also says to repair damaged strip curtains. For how an air curtain works and is sized, see our air curtain lesson linked below.

The ACHR case describes a 20,000 square foot freezer with a 12 by 12 foot opening where a fast door was already installed. After an air curtain was added and sequenced to start before the door opened, the installer reported temperature swings dropping from 8°F to under 2°F. That is an installer-reported result, not a study.

Worked example: dock door sensible load

All inputs below are our own assumptions for practice, not measured values.

  • Assumed average infiltration through the door while it cycles: 2,000 cfm.
  • Assumed outdoor air 75°F and freezer air 0°F, so dT = 75 − 0 = 75°F.

Sensible load: Q = 1.08 × cfm × dT = 1.08 × 2,000 × 75 = 162,000 Btu/h.

In tons: 162,000 ÷ 12,000 = 13.5 tons. In kW: 162,000 ÷ 3,412 = 47.5 kW of heat for the system to remove.

The 1.08 factor is the common sensible rule of thumb for standard air; very cold air is denser, so treat the result as an estimate. This counts only sensible heat. Humid outdoor air also carries moisture that freezes onto the coil, which adds latent load and frost on top, so the real number is higher.

For comparison, Watts gives 3 to 7 Btu/h per square foot of floor for heating, with 5 at minus 20 to 0°F. For an assumed 20,000 square foot floor, 20,000 × 5 = 100,000 Btu/h, or 29.3 kW of heat. The floor heat is real, but one busy door in this example can be bigger.

Evaporator frost and defrost in big rooms

Frost is moisture from infiltration and product turned to ice on the coil. As it builds, airflow and capacity fall, so coils must be defrosted. In the ACEEE paper's facility, electric defrost was about 5 percent and evaporator fans about 14 percent of refrigeration electric demand, and the retrofit switched to hot gas defrost with higher-efficiency fans. A Danfoss white paper on ammonia air coolers says hot gas defrost is the most common method because the heat comes from inside the system. Its outline: hot gas goes to the evaporator and builds pressure to about 50°F saturation, condensate drains to a separator, defrost ends once the ice is gone, and pressure is equalized before returning to cooling. It recommends a soft-opening step on the wet return so the large pressure difference does not push liquid slugs. Its field example used one 40 minute defrost a day on a 41 kW evaporator. The walk-in side of defrost, with heaters and fan delay, is in our walk-in lessons.

Why this matters

Floor heat protects the building, doors protect the load, and defrost protects the coil. A tech who sees icing at a dock, drifting sub-slab readings or long defrosts is looking at the same moisture and heat story from three angles. Ammonia plants add safety rules; see the ammonia lesson below.

Field card: Freezer Warehouses: Floor Heave, Subfloor Heat, Dock Doors and Defrost

Keep the ground above 32, the door shut, the coil clean.

  • Heave: wet soil under the slab freezes and lifts the floor.
  • Fix: glycol loops, self-regulating cable or ventilated pipes under the insulation.
  • Watts design target: 40°F sub-slab; fluid 55 to 75°F; 3 to 7 Btu/h per sq ft.
  • Watch: sub-slab temperature, circulator flow alarm, heater trips.
  • Doors: seals or shelters, fast doors, air locks, strip or air curtains.
  • Sensible load: 1.08 × cfm × dT; latent adds more.
  • Hot gas defrost: equalize pressure slowly before returning to cooling.
