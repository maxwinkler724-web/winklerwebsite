VFD Harmonics in Buildings: THD, Line Reactors, DC Chokes and Filters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A drive saves energy on the motor side, but on the power-line side it pulls current in lumps instead of a smooth sine wave. Those lumps are harmonics. This lesson explains where they come from, how they are measured, what they can do to a building, and what a tech can look at on a service call.

Check the maker and the engineer

Check the maker and the engineer. Harmonic numbers vary with the drive, the transformer and the rest of the building. Everything below is general education from maker and standards-group literature we read in 2026. Reactor sizing, filters and compliance studies belong to the design engineer and the drive maker's manual. Lockout/tagout and the drive's capacitor discharge wait apply before you touch any wiring.

Why a standard drive draws lumpy current

The front end of a common six-pulse drive is a rectifier feeding a large capacitor on the DC bus. A Control Techniques (Nidec) white paper explains that current flows only while the rectified line voltage is higher than the voltage the capacitor has charged to. So the drive takes short gulps of current near the voltage peaks, not a smooth sine. Any repeating, non-sinusoidal wave can be broken into a 60 Hz fundamental plus higher frequencies that are whole-number multiples of it. The Siemens drive harmonics white paper tabulates the 5th (300 Hz), 7th (420 Hz), 11th and 13th for six-pulse drives; those are the ones to know.

THD, TDD and why there are two numbers

Current THD (often written THDi) compares the harmonic content to the fundamental. The Siemens paper defines it as the rms of the harmonics divided by the fundamental, as a percentage. Because it uses the fundamental at that moment, a drive at light load can show a scary percentage on a small current. TDD (total demand distortion) uses the same harmonic current but divides by the maximum demand load current at the point being studied. The Siemens example: 40 percent THD at 50 percent load equals 20 percent TDD. Voltage THD applies the same ratio to the voltage waveform; both papers list IEEE 519 voltage limits separately from the current limits.

IEEE 519 and the point of common coupling

IEEE 519 is a standard on harmonic limits for power systems; we name it here and do not link a copy. The Siemens paper says its limits apply to the whole installation at the point of common coupling (PCC), not to each load alone, and describes the PCC as the point between the system owner and a user where service could be offered to other users. The Control Techniques paper adds that, inside an industrial plant, the PCC is the point between the nonlinear load and other loads, and warns that measuring at a drive's own input terminals misapplies the standard and has led to unneeded hardware. Both papers give a rule of thumb: nonlinear loads under about 30 percent of the transformer load rarely need special study. Who defines the PCC on a given job is a design decision, so ask the engineer of record.

What harmonics can do

  • Transformer heating. The Siemens paper says harmonics add core, copper and stray-flux losses, so a transformer runs hotter than its kVA suggests.
  • Neutral heating. Triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th and so on) do not cancel in a shared neutral under unbalanced loads. Siemens notes the neutral can carry a large current at 180 Hz.
  • Nuisance trips. Thermal-magnetic breakers respond to rms current, so they may trip early. Electronic trip units can misread distortion as overcurrent, and MTE says its 3 percent reactors prevent nuisance tripping of drives or breakers in most applications.
  • Other equipment. An Eaton presentation lists distribution transformers, power factor correction banks, standby generators, communications gear, switchgear, relays and computers as equipment that can be affected.

Ways to reduce it

AC line reactor. An inductor ahead of the drive. Siemens gives a typical 2 percent to 7.5 percent impedance range for reactors and chokes. MTE offers 1.5, 3 and 5 percent units, calls 3 percent the general choice for spikes and nuisance trips, and calls 5 percent best for reducing harmonic currents. MTE also notes that effective impedance changes with current: a 5 percent reactor acts like 3 percent at 60 percent of its rated current. DC choke. An inductor inside the drive on the DC bus. A Schneider Electric data bulletin tested a 20 hp, 480 V drive and found most of the benefit comes by about 5 percent for a DC choke or 3 percent for a line reactor, with little gain beyond. It also reports that its internal chokes average about 4.0 percent. Passive filters. Tuned inductor-capacitor networks aimed at the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th; Siemens warns they are sensitive to source impedance and can resonate. Active filters. Electronics that inject corrective current; Control Techniques says one can serve several drives and gives distortion below 4 or 5 percent. 12- and 18-pulse drives. Control Techniques gives input current distortion of about 10 percent and 5 percent, at a much higher first cost, and warns that a supply imbalance of 3 percent can cancel nearly all the benefit. That ties back to three-phase voltage imbalance. Active front end drives. A second inverter pulls near-sinusoidal current from the line; Siemens lists input THD around 1.2 to 1.3 percent.

Worked example: 40 A fundamental, with and without a reactor

Siemens Table 4.1 gives typical harmonic current as a percent of fundamental for a six-pulse front end on a 3-phase, 480 V system with a short-circuit ratio of 20 to 50. We take a drive with a 40 A fundamental line current and use the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th values only. Siemens lists its own totals (101.5 and 43.6 percent) that include higher orders, so our results land slightly lower. Everything below is Our own arithmetic.

Case A, no line reactor (80, 58, 18, 10 percent).

  1. Harmonic amps: 5th = 0.80 x 40 = 32.0 A; 7th = 0.58 x 40 = 23.2 A; 11th = 0.18 x 40 = 7.2 A; 13th = 0.10 x 40 = 4.0 A.
  2. Squares: 1024 + 538.24 + 51.84 + 16 = 1630.08.
  3. Harmonic rms = square root of 1630.08 = 40.37 A.
  4. Current THD = 40.37 / 40 = 1.009, or about 100.9 percent.
  5. Total rms = square root of (40 squared + 1630.08) = square root of 3230.08 = 56.8 A.

Case B, 2 to 3 percent line reactor (40, 15, 5, 4 percent).

  1. Harmonic amps: 16.0, 6.0, 2.0, 1.6 A.
  2. Squares: 256 + 36 + 4 + 2.56 = 298.56.
  3. Harmonic rms = square root of 298.56 = 17.28 A.
  4. Current THD = 17.28 / 40 = 0.432, or about 43.2 percent.
  5. Total rms = square root of (1600 + 298.56) = square root of 1898.56 = 43.6 A.

Case C, 5 percent line reactor (32, 9, 4, 3 percent): amps 12.8, 3.6, 1.6, 1.2; squares sum to 180.8; harmonic rms 13.45 A; THD about 33.6 percent; total rms about 42.2 A.

What it means. The reactor cut the total rms from 56.8 A to 43.6 A, about 23 percent less, for the same 40 A of useful fundamental current. Heating in wiring and transformers follows current squared, so that is roughly 0.59 of the heat in the same conductors (43.6 / 56.8 squared). Remember: these are typical table values, not a measurement of your drive.

Output side, briefly

Harmonics also have a motor-side cousin. MTE says reflected waves on long leads can push motor terminal voltage to 2 to 4 times the drive DC bus voltage, and warns that even very short runs can exceed some motors' insulation ratings; its dV/dt filter is guaranteed to 1,000 feet. Eaton notes peaks up to about 2 times bus voltage, worse on longer leads, and that a lower carrier frequency permits longer runs. ABB's bearing current note says shaft voltage that breaks down the bearing lubricant film erodes the bearing, and lists shaft grounding brushes, insulated bearings, common mode filters and symmetric shielded cabling as options by motor size; ABB also says bearing currents cause only a small share of bearing failures. Eaton likewise lists grounding rings or chokes. For pump and fan background, see VFDs on pumps and fans.

What a tech can check

  • Reactor nameplate. Look for percent impedance, amp rating and inductance (mH). MTE lists amps and mH in its tables. Compare amps to the drive input current.
  • Carrier frequency parameter. Find it in the drive manual; Eaton notes a lower carrier frequency allows longer motor leads. Change it only as the manual allows.
  • Power quality meter. Read current THD, voltage THD and individual 5th and 7th values, and note the load at the time. A high percent at light load is expected. Record where you measured; the PCC is not the drive terminals unless the engineer says so.
  • Clamp meter. Drive input current is not a clean sine. See measuring amp draw with a clamp meter; check the meter's specifications for non-sinusoidal current.
  • Overloads. See motor starters and overload relays for how settings relate to motor current.

Field card: VFD Harmonics in Buildings

Smooth the input current, then protect the output.

  • Six-pulse drives take current in short pulses near voltage peaks, producing 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th harmonics.
  • Current THD divides by the present fundamental; TDD divides by maximum demand current.
  • IEEE 519 limits are judged at the point of common coupling, not at one drive terminal.
  • Our own example: 40 A fundamental, 100.9 percent THD and 56.8 A rms without a reactor; 43.2 percent and 43.6 A with a 2 to 3 percent reactor.
  • Options: line reactor, DC choke, passive or active filter, 12/18-pulse, active front end.
  • Output side: reflected wave on long leads, bearing currents, carrier frequency.
  • Check: reactor nameplate, carrier frequency parameter, power quality meter.
