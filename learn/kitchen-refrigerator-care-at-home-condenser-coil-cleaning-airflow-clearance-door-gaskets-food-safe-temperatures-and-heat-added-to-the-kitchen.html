Kitchen Refrigerator Care: Coils, Gaskets, Temperatures

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A kitchen refrigerator is a small refrigeration system: a compressor, a condenser coil, a metering device and an evaporator, sealed and running on its own. Homeowners cannot open the sealed system, but they can keep it clean, keep its airflow open and keep the door sealed. This lesson covers those jobs and the signs that mean it is time for a qualified technician.

Where the heat goes: the condenser coil

A refrigerator does not make cold. It moves heat out of the food compartment and dumps it into the room, and the condenser coil is where the dumping happens. A university extension page on coil cleaning says that in most models the coils are at the back of the appliance, and that on a bottom-mounted freezer model they may be underneath. On many units the underneath coil is reached by taking off the grille along the bottom front; check your owner manual for how yours comes off.

The same page explains that dirty coils cannot shed heat efficiently, which raises energy use, and that a dirt buildup can cause temperature problems. Our own plain-language version: dust and pet hair act like a blanket over the coil. The coil runs hotter, the compressor has to run longer to move the same heat, and the food compartment can end up warmer than it should. That is why a unit with a furry coil is both a bigger energy user and a less reliable cooler.

Cleaning the coil, step by step

  1. Unplug the refrigerator first. The extension page says to unplug before you begin. You will be near a fan, wiring and a sharp-edged cabinet, so take power off.
  2. Gather the tools. The same page lists a vacuum with a brush or crevice attachment, a coil brush, a soft cloth, gloves, eye protection and a flashlight.
  3. Open the way in. Remove the bottom grille, or pull the unit out gently from the wall if the coil is on the back. Move it straight out so you do not kink or stress the water line to an ice maker, if one is fitted.
  4. Brush, then vacuum. Loosen dust with the brush, always working so you do not bend the thin fins, and vacuum it up as you go. Do not use a hose or spray water on the coil area.
  5. Look at the floor under and behind the unit while it is out. Lint and pet hair collect there and get pulled back into the airflow.
  6. Put it back, plug it in and wait. The compartment takes hours to settle. Check it with a thermometer, covered below.

Our own rule of thumb: look at the coil every few months in a home with pets, and at least once a year otherwise. We offer that as our own habit, not a figure from a source. If the coil is a different design or you cannot get to it safely, leave it to a technician. An outdoor air conditioner coil has its own cleaning rules; see the related lesson on that below.

Keep room to breathe

A federal home energy guide (2022 edition) says to leave a few inches of space between the wall and the unit. It also says to place the refrigerator away from an oven or dishwasher and out of direct sunlight. Both ideas are the same idea: the coil needs cool room air to dump heat into. Our own practical addition: do not stack boxes on top or push the unit into a tight cabinet with no gap for air to leave. Check the owner manual for the exact clearances your model needs, since models differ.

The drain pan and defrost water

Most frost-free models melt the frost off the evaporator now and then, and the water runs down a drain to a shallow pan near the compressor, where warm air from the coil helps it evaporate. This is general appliance design, offered here as our own explanation, so your owner manual is the guide for your model. If you move the unit out for coil cleaning, look for a pan and check it. A pan that is dirty, sour smelling or overfull points to a blocked drain, and a puddle on the floor is a reason to look sooner. Do not pour in cleaners that your manual does not allow, and keep the pan from tipping when you slide the unit back.

Door gaskets and the paper test

The door gasket is the rubber seal around the door. A leaky seal lets warm, humid room air into the box, which means more running and more frost. The federal energy guide describes the test: close the door over a dollar bill, and if the bill pulls out easily, the latch may need adjusting or the seal may need replacing. A strip of paper works the same way. Our own habit is to test at several points around the door, top, bottom, hinge side and latch side, because a gasket can fail in one spot. Clean the gasket with mild soap and water so it does not stick or tear, and look for cracks, gaps or a rolled edge.

Temperatures: use a thermometer, not the dial

The dial on a refrigerator is a setting, not a measurement. A federal food safety chart gives 40 F or below for the refrigerator and 0 F or below for the freezer. A federal drug and food agency page on power outages tells households to keep an appliance thermometer in both the refrigerator and the freezer and to check that the freezer is at or below 0 F and the refrigerator at or below 40 F. The 2022 federal energy guide suggests settings of 35 F to 38 F for the refrigerator and 0 F for a standalone freezer used for long-term storage. Together that gives a sensible target: a refrigerator reading in the mid to upper 30s and a freezer at 0 F, and never above the 40 F and 0 F limits.

Put the thermometer in a glass of water in the middle of the shelf for a steadier reading (our own tip), wait several hours, and read it at the same time of day. After moving the dial, wait a day before adjusting again, since the box responds slowly.

The heat a refrigerator adds to the kitchen

All the electricity a refrigerator uses ends up as heat in the room: the compressor motor, fans and lights all end up there, and the heat pulled out of the food is released too. This is the same idea used for other appliances in the related heat gain lesson below. Here is a worked example.

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume a refrigerator uses 400 kWh per year. This is an assumed round number for the example. It is not a measured or labeled value for any real unit, so your own number will differ. Your own unit will have its own figure in its paperwork.
  1. Hours in a year: 365 days x 24 hours = 8,760 hours.
  2. Average watts = kWh x 1000 / 8,760 = 400 x 1000 / 8,760 = 400,000 / 8,760 = 45.66 W, about 46 W.
  3. Heat to the kitchen = watts x 3.412 = 45.66 x 3.412 = 155.8 Btu per hour, about 156 Btu/h.
  4. Check: 156 Btu/h divided by 3.412 gives 45.7 W, and 45.7 W x 8,760 h / 1000 gives 400 kWh. The numbers agree.
Now suppose a dirty coil and a leaky gasket push the use up by 10 percent, which is our own assumption, not a measured result: 440 kWh gives 440,000 / 8,760 = 50.23 W, and 50.23 x 3.412 = 171.4 Btu/h. The refrigerator would then add about 16 Btu/h more heat to the room. This is small next to an air conditioner, but it never stops, and it adds to the cooling load in a small kitchen on a hot day.

The average is only an average. The compressor runs in cycles, so while it is running the draw is higher than 46 W and when it is off the draw is close to nothing.

When to call a refrigeration tech

  • Warm refrigerator with the fan or compressor running. If the unit runs and the box is still above 40 F after you have cleaned the coil and checked the gasket, there may be a fault in the sealed system, the evaporator or the defrost parts.
  • Frost building up on the back wall or around the evaporator cover, especially after you have checked the gaskets.
  • Clicking from the back, with the compressor starting and stopping, or a hum without cooling.
  • Oily spots, a hissing sound or a chemical smell. These can point to a leak.
  • Anything involving the sealed system. Refrigerant work needs a qualified technician with the right equipment. Homeowners should never cut, pierce or open the refrigerant lines.

Some newer household units use a flammable refrigerant in a very small charge; the related lesson on that explains how to read the labels. Until a technician arrives, move food to another cold place and keep both doors shut so the cold lasts.

Field card: Kitchen Refrigerator Care: Coils, Gaskets, Temperatures

Clean coil, open airflow, tight door, thermometer in the box.

  • Unplug before cleaning. Brush, then vacuum the coil, and keep the fins straight.
  • The coil is underneath behind a grille or on the back. Check the owner manual.
  • Leave a few inches behind the unit and keep the top open for air.
  • Door seal test: a bill or paper strip should grip when the door is closed on it.
  • Targets: refrigerator 40 F or below, freezer 0 F or below, checked with an appliance thermometer.
  • Our own example: 400 kWh/yr is about 46 W, or about 156 Btu/h into the kitchen.
  • Call a qualified technician for a warm box with the fan running, frost buildup, clicking or any sealed-system work.
