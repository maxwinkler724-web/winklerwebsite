Gas Station Convenience Store HVAC and Refrigeration: Beer Cave, Coolers, Food Equipment Heat and Fuel Vapor

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A gas station store is a small building with a grocery store's refrigeration, a restaurant's hot equipment and a door that opens all night. Add a fuel island a few steps away and the rooftop unit has two jobs: carry a heavy internal load, and never pull fuel vapor inside. This lesson walks the floor plan and shows how to add up the heat.

Check the exact site

Check the exact site. The wattages in the example below are our own assumptions, not data for any real store. Use nameplates and the equipment documents on the job, and follow the fire code and the fire authority for anything near fuel. This is general education, not design advice.

Cooler wall and walk-in: where the heat goes

Most stores put glass-door coolers along one wall with a walk-in cooler directly behind, so staff restock from the back and customers pull from the front. Two designs share that wall. A self-contained cabinet has its compressor and condenser inside the cabinet, so every watt it draws ends up as heat in the sales floor. A remote design pipes refrigerant to a condensing unit outside the sales area. EPA describes remote condensing units as typically 1 to 20 kW, or 0.3 to 5.7 refrigeration tons, with the condenser outside the cooled space, usually rejecting heat to outdoor air. Moving a condenser outside takes its heat off the rooftop unit, but the evaporator fans and lights stay in the box. Our glass-door merchandiser lesson covers the cabinet itself, and our walk-in lesson covers defrost and door heaters. The glass-door fog problem is in the anti-sweat heater lesson.

ENERGY STAR small-business guidance says refrigeration can use up to 40 percent of a convenience store's energy and suggests refrigerator settings of 35 to 38 F and freezer settings of -14 to -8 F. A beer cave is a walk-in run as a cooler, so it follows the cooler range. Colder than needed wastes energy and loads the coil.

Hot equipment, drinks and ice

Roller grills, hot-food cases, coffee brewers with warmers and microwave ovens turn nearly all their electricity into heat in the same room as the cooler condensers. A fountain dispenser has its own small chiller, and many are self-contained. An ice merchandiser outside is a freezer in the weather: it is not a load on the building, but its door area and condenser sit in direct sun and summer heat, so it is a common call for a dirty condenser or a door that will not seal. We do not publish wattages for these because makers vary a lot. Read the nameplate, or clamp the actual draw, then use the arithmetic in the example below.

Doors, infiltration and the iced evaporator

Every opening of a cooler door or the front door exchanges air. Warm humid air that enters a cold box condenses and freezes on the evaporator. A university refrigeration best-practices guide says ice buildup blocks airflow and insulates the coil, that a stopped evaporator fan is a common cause of icing, that worn gaskets and latches let heat in, and that a door left open overnight can raise product temperature. It recommends a door alarm. A university energy page for stores says strip curtains let a walk-in door stay open for convenience while minimizing cooling loss. For gaskets, the ENERGY STAR tip is simple: if a dollar bill slides easily into the seal, have it adjusted.

In a 24-hour store the beer cave sees its heaviest door traffic on summer evenings and weekends, which is also when outdoor heat and indoor humidity are highest. If the box was borderline in the morning, it fails at dusk.

Sizing the rooftop unit for internal loads

A store like this has a high internal load and a lot of sensible heat, so the unit runs for long hours even in spring and fall. Count the equipment heat first (the example below), then people, the envelope and outdoor air on top. Door traffic adds outdoor air in an uncontrolled way, so a vestibule or an air curtain helps the unit hold humidity. Keep the supply from blowing straight onto the cooler glass, which can bring fog back. A tune-up each year and a monthly filter check are the ENERGY STAR minimums; ENERGY STAR says to replace filters at least every three months.

Fuel vapor and the air intake

Gasoline is flammable and its vapor is heavier than air. The CDC emergency-response guidance lists vapor density at 3 to 4 (air = 1), a flammable range of 1.4 to 7.4 percent in air, and warns of asphyxiation in enclosed, low-lying areas. Vapor therefore sinks and pools, which is why a low intake, a floor-level return or a pit near a dispenser is a bad idea.

Several numbers apply to the equipment around the intake. OSHA's flammable liquids standard (29 CFR 1910.106) says vent pipes for the more volatile liquids must discharge upward, at least 12 ft above adjacent ground. An older EPA design document for service stations repeats the 12 ft vent height, attributing it to OSHA and the national fire code for flammable liquids. The same OSHA standard sets a Division 1 hazardous area extending 5 ft in all directions from points of vapor liberation. EPA emission factors say underground tanks "breathe" daily and release vapor during delivery and vehicle refueling, so the vent pipe is a real source, not a theoretical one.

We found no federal rule giving a minimum distance between a fuel dispenser or vent pipe and a rooftop unit's outdoor air intake. Do not invent one. The practical rules are: put the intake on the side of the building away from the island and the vent pipe; keep it well above grade; never put it near a delivery fill point; and get the required distance from the fire authority and the code adopted where the store sits. The general idea of distance from sources is in our outdoor air intake separation lesson. Do not add heaters, outlets or other possible ignition sources near the island unless the site's hazardous-area classification allows them.

24-hour operation and maintenance windows

The store never closes, so plan around the traffic. Our own habit is to do coil cleaning and gasket work on a weekday morning and to tell the clerk before cutting power. Lock out the unit, protect the product with a temporary fan and keep the beer cave door shut for the shortest time possible. Ask whether the store has a monitoring alarm; a call at 2 a.m. often starts with a high-temperature alert. Work near the island needs the owner's hot-work rules, so ask before you bring in tools that spark.

Worked example: adding up the internal heat (our own)

Assumptions (ours, not measured). Average electrical draw, not nameplate: roller grill 1,200 W; two coffee brewers with warmers, 1,500 W combined; fountain dispenser 800 W; hot-food case 1,000 W; lighting 3,000 W; registers and electronics 600 W; four self-contained glass-door coolers at 900 W average each. The beer cave uses a remote condensing unit outside, so it adds nothing to the store in this example. We treat all draw as heat in the store, since the heat each cabinet pulls from its box originated in the room and the net addition is close to the electrical input. This ignores people, envelope, outdoor air and pulldown after restock.

  1. Hot and lighting equipment: 1,200 + 1,500 + 800 + 1,000 + 3,000 + 600 = 8,100 W.
  2. Self-contained coolers: 4 x 900 = 3,600 W of condenser and fan heat into the store.
  3. Total: 8,100 + 3,600 = 11,700 W.
  4. Convert to Btu/h. NIST lists 0.2930711 W per Btu/h, so 1 W is about 3.412 Btu/h. 11,700 x 3.412 = 39,920 Btu/h.
  5. Convert to tons. NIST lists a ton of refrigeration as 12,000 Btu/h. 39,920 / 12,000 = 3.33 tons.

The coolers alone are 3,600 x 3.412 = 12,283 Btu/h, or 1.02 tons. Moving those four to remote condensing units would take about one ton off the rooftop unit in this example, and the cooler wall would still need its own condensing capacity outdoors. Equipment is only part of the load, so the full calculation adds people, the envelope and outdoor air. Whatever size comes out of that, the internal load alone takes up 3.33 tons of capacity.

Common service calls

  • Beer cave not holding temperature in summer. Check the condenser first. A university best-practices guide says as little as 0.01 inch of buildup can cost about 5 percent of capacity (an expert estimate). Then check the door gasket, the strip curtain, the evaporator fan and the defrost.
  • Iced evaporator from door traffic. Find out why: a stopped fan, a door held open, a failed defrost, or a bad gasket. Melt the ice safely, then fix the cause. Do not chip the coil.
  • Dirty condenser coils. A state university energy page recommends cleaning store condenser coils quarterly. Roadside dust and cooking grease can mean more often. ENERGY STAR also says to clean the cooling coils on the backs of units and remove any ice found on them.
  • Store hot and sticky. Add up the heat as above, check the filter and the outside air, and ask what equipment was added since the unit was sized.

Field card: Gas Station Convenience Store HVAC and Refrigeration

Count every watt in the room, and keep the intake away from the island.

  • Self-contained cabinets put their full draw into the store; remote condensers put it outside.
  • Watts x 3.412 = Btu/h; divide by 12,000 for tons.
  • Gasoline vapor is heavier than air (density 3 to 4); keep intakes high and away from the island and vent pipe.
  • Vent pipes discharge upward at least 12 ft above ground; get the intake distance from the fire code.
  • Iced evaporator: find the cause (fan, door, defrost, gasket).
  • Clean condensers quarterly, and sooner in dusty or greasy spots.
