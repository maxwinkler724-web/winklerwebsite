Pump Cavitation Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Cavitation sounds like gravel or small rocks rattling through a pump. It is not just noise: it is vapor bubbles forming and collapsing inside the pump, and it can wreck an impeller.

What is happening inside

A centrifugal pump lowers pressure at the eye of the impeller to pull water in. If the pressure there falls below the liquid’s vapor pressure, bubbles form. They then collapse as they travel into higher-pressure areas, and that collapse is the source of the noise and the damage. Pump people describe the margin that prevents this as net positive suction head, or NPSH. Cavitation usually means there is not enough of it on the suction side.

Symptoms

A pump maker’s explainer lists the common signs:

  • Noise. A rattling sound, likened to pumping rocks.
  • Vibration. Collapsing bubbles can shake the pump.
  • Lower performance. Less flow and pressure than the pump should make.
  • Damage. Pitting or chipping of the impeller, and early failure of seals and bearings.

Common hydronic causes

The pump-maker page lists running the pump too fast, an undersized suction path, and placing the pump too far from the liquid source. In a building loop, the things to check are usually more everyday:

  • A dirty strainer on the suction side. A strainer with a high pressure drop starves the pump and is a very common cause. Compare pressure before and after it; Caleffi puts the cleaning point near 5 to 6 psi differential and a Watts manual says 5 to 10 psi, but the strainer maker and your site procedure govern.
  • A partly closed suction valve or a valve that is not fully open after service.
  • Air in the system. Air pockets and entrained air cause noise and false readings. See Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.
  • Low system pressure. An expansion tank or fill problem can leave suction pressure too low. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.
  • High water temperature, because hotter water boils at a lower pressure.
  • Running far out on the curve, meaning flow much higher than the pump was selected for.

What to do when you hear it

The pump-maker page recommends shutting the pump down at the first sign of noise, vibration or lost performance and getting the cause fixed. In a plant that is a decision for the operator in charge, so report it and follow your site procedure rather than letting the pump run for days. Before opening anything, use lockout/tagout, relieve pressure, and let hot water cool, since heating water can scald instantly. Stand to the side when opening strainers or vents.

Cavitation or something else?

Bearing noise, air in the pump and cavitation can sound alike, so confirm with pressures, the strainer pressure drop, the pump curve and the manufacturer’s guidance rather than guessing. Cavitation can also damage seals, so see Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits.

Prevention

Keep strainers clean, keep systems full and vented, keep fill pressure correct, avoid closing suction valves, and do not run pumps far beyond the flow they were chosen for. If you suspect the pump is wrong for the job, report it so a qualified person can review the selection.

Printable checklist

  • Listen at each pump: any gravel or rattling sound?
  • Compare suction and discharge pressure with the usual numbers
  • Strainer pressure drop before and after, against the clean baseline
  • Suction valve fully open
  • System pressure and expansion tank normal
  • Air vents and separators working
  • Bearing temperature and vibration normal
  • Report noise instead of letting the pump run
  • Lockout/tagout and pressure relief before opening anything

Field card: Pump Cavitation Basics

If it sounds like rocks, check the suction side.

  • Cavitation: vapor bubbles that form and collapse inside the pump.
  • Sounds like gravel; causes vibration, lost flow and damage.
  • Check the suction strainer, valves, air and system pressure first.
  • Report it early; do not run a rattling pump for days.
