Thermostatic Radiator Valves on Hot-Water Heating: How TRV Heads Work, Two-Pipe vs Series Loops, Bypass Needs and Room-by-Room Control

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostatic radiator valve, or TRV, lets each radiator hold its own room temperature without a wall thermostat or a zone valve. It is a simple part, but it changes how the whole piping loop and pump behave. This lesson covers how the head works, where it fits, and why closing a few valves can upset the rest of the system.

How the head throttles flow

A TRV has two parts: a valve body in the radiator piping and a sensing head on top. The head holds a sealed element that expands as the air around it warms. A U.S. Department of Energy Building America evaluation describes this as a fluid-filled capsule in the operator that expands as room temperature rises and closes the valve (source 1). In our own plain words, some heads use wax and some use a liquid or gas, but the job is the same: as the room warms, the element pushes a pin that squeezes the valve toward closed, and as the room cools the pin backs off and flow returns. The valve does not shut off like a switch. It modulates, passing less water as the room nears the setting.

The head is marked with a numbered scale rather than degrees. In the DOE evaluation, a hydronic simulation found about a 12.4% heating reduction when settings stayed at 2 to 3 (source 1, citing a 2008 simulation study). Maker charts tie each number to a room temperature, so use the maker's chart for your head.

Remote sensors and covered radiators

The head measures the air at the valve, not the middle of the room. Our own rule of thumb: if the head sits behind a curtain, inside a radiator cover, or in the rising heat from the radiator itself, it reads too warm and closes early, leaving the room cold. The DOE evaluation lists possible sensor placement problems among the issues in its steam field test (source 2). Many makers offer a remote sensor head, with the sensing bulb on a capillary tube, so the valve stays at the radiator while the bulb sits in open room air. Mount a remote bulb away from drafts, sunlight and the radiator itself.

Where TRVs fit: two-pipe, one-pipe and series loops

Two-pipe systems. In a two-pipe system, each emitter is fed from a supply main and drains to a return main, so each radiator is its own branch. DOE describes direct-return and reverse-return layouts and notes that balancing valves are still needed in practice (source 4). This is the natural home for a TRV: closing one branch does not starve the others.

Series loops. In a series loop, the same water passes through every emitter in turn. DOE says this arrangement cannot be zoned and balancing issues are hard to resolve (source 4). Our own reading: a TRV that closes in a series loop would stop flow to every emitter downstream, so the usual answer is a TRV with a bypass leg around the radiator, or not using a TRV at all. Follow the maker's instructions for series use.

One-pipe hot-water systems. Here the radiators tap off a single main through diverter tees. See One-Pipe Diverter Tee Hot-Water Heating Systems. A TRV on a branch just throttles that branch, but the tee depends on main flow, so check the maker's instructions for the valve and the tee before fitting. Much of the DOE TRV work was on one-pipe steam (sources 1 and 7), where the valve sits at the air vent; steam TRVs exist, but they are a different job from the hot-water valves in this lesson.

What the research says about savings

  • A 1995 study of eight New York multifamily buildings on one-pipe steam found about 9% less fuel with partial TRV installs and about 15% with full installs (source 1, summarizing earlier work). That is steam, not hot water.
  • The DOE team's own field test in a one-pipe steam building found no measurable building-wide savings (source 1) and no savings at the unit or building level (source 2).
  • One DOE expert-meeting speaker said that average apartment temperature drops of 3 to 6 degrees F from resident control are optimistic, and that evidence so far showed an average drop of 0 to 3 degrees F (source 3).
  • A DOE low-rise multifamily report said under- or overheating can easily occur as TRVs age, cited a study in which over 65% of tenants set TRVs higher than required, and noted many apartment valve actuators have failed wide open (source 5).
  • That same report says vendor claims of 25% to 40% savings are not verified by independent studies (source 5).
  • Installed cost for hot-water TRVs in the DOE evaluation was $250 to $315 each, not including draining (source 1).

The takeaway for an apartment retrofit: a TRV stops overheated rooms, but it saves fuel only if the boiler and distribution respond. Balance and boiler controls come first. Fixing an overheated apartment with an open window is not a TRV job.

Pumps, bypasses and pressure control

Every closed TRV shrinks the number of open branches, so the pump sees more resistance. On a fixed-speed pump, the operating point slides up its curve: less total flow, more head. The open radiators then see higher pressure and more flow, which can cause water noise and unequal heating. Two fixes are common. A differential-pressure bypass valve opens when head rises above its setpoint and lets water short-circuit from supply to return, so the pump keeps moving. Or a variable-speed pump holds pressure by slowing down.

One DOE field study describes an ECM circulator with seven settings: three constant-speed, three constant-pressure and one automatic (source 6). For detail on these modes see ECM Circulator Pump Modes, and for larger systems Hydronic Differential Pressure Control. A bypass has a cost: the water that skips the radiators returns to the boiler hot, so the return runs warmer than it would with full flow. Constant-pressure pumping avoids that and cuts pump power. The same DOE study measured one pump dropping from 40 W to 18 W when zone flow was cut from 1.5 to about 1.0 gpm (source 6). Zone valves do the same pump-loading job at a larger scale; compare Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators.

Worked example (our own): four rooms, two TRVs close

Assumptions, all ours. Four identical radiator branches in parallel on a two-pipe loop, mains with negligible loss. At design, each branch carries 2 gpm and the loop needs 9 ft of head, so total flow is 8 gpm. Assumed pump curve: head = 14 − 0.078125 × Q² (ft, gpm), which passes through 9 ft at 8 gpm.

  1. Each open branch follows flow = c × √head. At 2 gpm and 9 ft, c = 2 / 3 = 0.667.
  2. Two branches open: Q = 2 × 0.667 × √H = 1.333 √H, so H = 0.5625 × Q².
  3. Set the pump head equal to the system head: 14 − 0.078125 Q² = 0.5625 Q², so Q² = 14 / 0.640625 = 21.85 and Q = 4.67 gpm.
  4. Head: 0.5625 × 21.85 = 12.3 ft, up from 9 ft (+37%).
  5. Each open room: 4.67 / 2 = 2.34 gpm, up from 2.0 (+17%). Total flow fell from 8 to 4.67 gpm (−42%).

With a bypass set to open at 9 ft: the pump holds near 9 ft and 8 gpm, the two open rooms keep 2 gpm each, and the other 4 gpm passes through the bypass. With a constant-pressure pump set for 9 ft: flow settles at 4 gpm, rooms stay at 2 gpm, and no hot water is wasted around the radiators. Real mains have some loss, so this is a model, not a design.

Check before you fit TRVs

  • Does the pump have a bypass, a constant-pressure mode, or enough curve margin?
  • Is the head free of covers and curtains, or is a remote sensor needed?
  • Is the loop two-pipe, one-pipe or series, and does the maker allow TRVs on it?
  • For steel panel radiators, see Steel Panel Radiators: Sizing at Lower Water Temperatures and Valve Options.

Field card: TRVs on hot-water heating

Close a TRV and the pump sees more resistance.

  • The head holds a sealed element that expands with room air and closes the valve; it modulates.
  • Numbers on the head are a scale, not degrees; use the maker's chart.
  • Head behind a curtain or cover reads warm; use a remote sensor.
  • Two-pipe is the natural fit; series loops cannot be zoned; one-pipe needs maker approval.
  • Our example: 8 gpm falls to 4.67 gpm, head rises 9 to 12.3 ft, open rooms gain 17% flow.
  • Fix with a differential bypass or a constant-pressure pump.
