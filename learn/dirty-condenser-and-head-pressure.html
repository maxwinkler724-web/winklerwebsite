Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The condenser's job is to get heat out of the refrigerant. When air cannot move through it, the heat has nowhere to go, and everything in the system pays for it.

The chain reaction

HVAC School describes the effect simply. A dirty coil restricts airflow, so less heat leaves the refrigerant, which raises the condensing temperature and the head pressure. The compression ratio goes up, the compressor moves less refrigerant, amps rise and capacity falls. Axial fans lose airflow quickly as pressure builds, which makes it worse. On fixed-metering systems suction pressure can rise too. TXV or EEV systems are hit harder, according to the article, because the valve throttles to hold superheat and pushes the compression ratio higher still. For the cycle itself see Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

What it looked like in one test

HVAC School shares a labeled example from a 2-ton R-22 heat pump. Cleaning the condenser coil lowered liquid (head) pressure from about 279 to 217 psig, lowered compressor amps from 11.4 to 10.3, and raised measured capacity and EER. The author notes the unit was also slightly low on refrigerant, so some of the improvement is not from cleaning alone. It is an example of direction, not a number your unit should match.

Reading the clues

High head pressure with the liquid line hot is the first sign. A rule-of-thumb reference from HVAC School says head pressure for many modern systems runs about 15 to 20 degrees of saturation above outdoor ambient, give or take a few. A reading far above that range calls for a look at the condenser. Common causes of high head: high load, overcharge, and poor heat rejection from a dirty or blocked condenser. A separate HVAC School page says clogged driers and kinked liquid lines usually give low suction and normal-to-low head, not high head. Overcharge and dirt produce similar readings, so compare the symptom table in Undercharge vs. Overcharge and look at the coil before touching the charge. Also check the fan (see Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures) and that the blade sits correctly in the shroud.

Cleaning steps in outline

From HVAC School's step-by-step: shut off and verify power, wear gloves and eye protection with caustic cleaners, unwire the fan motor and lift off the top, clear debris and drain ports, protect controls, pre-rinse the coil from the inside out, apply foam bottom to top, let it dwell for the time on the cleaner's label (the article says 5 to 10 minutes), then rinse from the inside out, top to bottom. Reassemble without pinching wires and let the coil dry while running before you take readings.

  • Use no more water pressure than needed. High pressure bends fins.
  • Avoid stiff brushes.
  • Microchannel coils need extra care with mild cleaners or water only.
  • Use only cleaners the manufacturer allows.

Why you wait to measure

A wet coil gives false readings, according to HVAC School. Let the unit run until dry, and do not change the charge during a before-and-after comparison. See Superheat and Subcooling Basics for the numbers to take afterward. Keeping plants and debris back from the unit is part of Preventive Maintenance Checklist Habits.

Field card: Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure

Dirty coil, hot liquid line, high head.

  • A blocked condenser raises condensing temperature and head pressure.
  • Higher compression ratio means more amps and less capacity.
  • Dirt and overcharge both give high head; look at the coil first.
  • Clean from the inside out, with the lowest pressure and approved cleaner.
  • Let the coil dry before measuring; do not change the charge in a comparison.
