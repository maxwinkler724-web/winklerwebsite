Compressor Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE, Which Refrigerant Uses Which

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The oil in a compressor is chosen to suit the refrigerant, not the other way around. Put the wrong oil in, or let a good oil soak up room air, and the compressor pays for it later. This lesson sorts the four oil families, shows what pairs with what, and walks through topping off a system after a repair.

Check the maker first

The compressor nameplate and the maker's service literature decide which oil goes in. What follows is general education built from government and university sources and one maker service guide. It does not replace the label on your compressor.

The four oil families

Mineral oil is refined petroleum. Alkylbenzene (AB) is a synthetic hydrocarbon. Polyolester (POE) and polyvinyl ether (PVE) are synthetic oils made for the newer refrigerants. A 2018 Purdue conference paper says the old CFC and HCFC refrigerants, such as R-12 and R-22, were used with mineral oil and alkylbenzene, and that R-22 had trouble mixing with mineral oil at low temperature. A 1996 Purdue paper adds that mineral oils are generally considered unsuitable for HFC refrigerants because they do not mix well with them.

For HFCs, a Department of Energy research report says the newer HFC systems pair with POE and are much more thermally stable than the older CFC and mineral oil systems. A 1996 Purdue paper lists R-134a, R-404A and R-407C as refrigerants tested with a POE. Conference papers on R-410A call polyol esters, in ISO 32 and ISO 68 grades, the commercial oils used with it today. A 2014 Purdue paper tested both POE and PVE with the low-GWP HFO refrigerants R-1234yf and R-1234ze(E) and a blend that includes R-32, so PVE is also in use there.

Why POE drinks water

A 1994 Purdue paper reports that esters in general are more hygroscopic than mineral oils. In its test the oils sat at 25°C and 50 percent relative humidity for one week, and the esters saturated at about 900 to 1,100 ppm of water. A 1996 Purdue paper adds that POEs tend to accumulate moisture even in storage. We did not find a government or university source that gives a minutes-per-ppm uptake rate, so we do not give one. What we can cite is a maker's service guide for an R-410A water-source product line, which says POE absorbs moisture very easily, limits exposure to ambient air to no more than 15 minutes, and warns that a vacuum pump will not adequately remove moisture from POE.

Acid and moisture

Water does more than sit in POE. The Department of Energy report says acidity in HFC and POE systems comes from hydrolytic decomposition of the POE with water, and it names carboxylic acids as the products. A 1996 paper describes the same reaction, an ester plus water giving an acid and an alcohol. In its long test, oil without a moisture absorbent rose in total acid number from 0.01 to 0.15 mg KOH/g after 12,000 hours, while oil with one stayed below 0.05. For the mineral oil systems, the DOE report notes a total acid number of 0.05 mg KOH/g was set as a limit, and says no consensus limit exists yet for POE systems. For testing and cleanup, see Compressor Burnout Cleanup and Chiller and Compressor Oil Analysis.

Keeping oil clean in the shop

  • Keep containers closed except while you are pouring. The sources above show why: POE picks up water from air in storage and in service.
  • The maker guide we read says to store POE in glass or metal, never plastic, and to use a pump to move it.
  • Open the refrigerant circuit for the shortest time you can, and replace the filter drier whenever the system is opened. See Filter Driers and Sight Glasses.
  • Evacuation and leak work are covered in Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection.

Think of it as a habit: pour, pump, cap, and note the time the circuit was open. A technician who writes that time on the ticket can answer later questions about a wet system.

Retrofit flushing: mineral to POE

When a system built for mineral oil is converted to an HFC, the old oil has to be mostly removed, because mineral oil and HFCs mix poorly. A 1996 Swedish university study (KTH) gave the industry target as 2 to 5 percent residual mineral oil. It found too much leftover oil may reduce evaporator heat transfer, and that 75 percent of its field samples were at roughly 7 percent or less, above the target. Separately, a refrigerant maker's retrofit presentation hosted on an EPA site says to check oil return and the oil analysis first, measure how much oil you remove, and confirm with the refrigerant maker how many flushes are needed. We found no government or university figure for a required number of flushes, and the 2 to 5 percent target is a 1990s study target, not a rule, so follow the maker's retrofit procedure.

Never mix oils on your own

Do not blend oil types or grades unless the compressor maker approves it. The maker guide we read says the oil must be POE, mineral oil should never be used, and approved POE oils are not all interchangeable. That guide did not address mixing different POEs, so ask the maker.

Worked example (ours): oil after a condenser coil swap on an R-410A system

This example is our own. The numbers marked as assumptions are made up to show the steps, not typical values.

  1. Choose the oil type. The system is R-410A, so we look for the compressor label for the oil type. Sources above name POE as the oil used with R-410A. The label governs; if it says PVE, use PVE.
  2. Choose the viscosity. Read the ISO grade on the compressor nameplate or in the maker's literature. Assumption: the label says POE, ISO 32. Published R-410A studies used ISO 32 and ISO 68 grades, so the grade has to match the label and not a guess.
  3. Figure how much oil left. Assumption: we recover the refrigerant and find 2.5 fl oz of oil in the recovery cylinder or catch, and drain 1.5 fl oz from the old coil. Lost oil: 2.5 + 1.5 = 4.0 fl oz.
  4. Measure the replacement. Add back about what came out, 4.0 fl oz, unless the maker gives a different amount. Pour from a fresh, sealed metal or glass container into a clean measuring cup, then pump it in.
  5. Handle it dry. Reseal the container at once. Keep the system open no longer than the maker's 15 minutes, then fit a new filter drier and evacuate.
  6. Check the moisture headroom. Assumption: new oil starts at 25 ppm. Against the 900 to 1,100 ppm saturation range, that leaves about 875 to 1,075 ppm of room. Poured and left open for a week at 50 percent humidity, it could soak up nearly all of it.

Field card: Compressor Oils

Match the oil to the label, and keep POE dry.

  • CFC and HCFC: mineral or alkylbenzene. HFC: POE. HFO and blends: POE, PVE in some designs.
  • Read the oil type and ISO grade from the compressor nameplate.
  • POE soaks up water; one lab test saturated esters near 900 to 1,100 ppm.
  • Wet POE can form acid; replace the filter drier when the system is opened.
  • Keep containers closed; limit open-circuit time to the maker's limit.
  • Retrofit: flush to the maker's procedure; one study target was 2 to 5 percent residual mineral oil.
  • Never mix oils without the maker's approval.
