HVLS Fans and Destratification in Warehouses and Gyms: Heat Recovery, Cooling Effect and Sprinkler Interlocks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a gym or warehouse with a 30 or 40 foot ceiling, the heaters can run all day while the air under the roof is far warmer than the air at your feet. A high-volume low-speed (HVLS) fan is a big, slow ceiling fan that stirs that warm layer back into the occupied space. This lesson covers why stratification wastes heat, what the fan does in winter and summer, spacing, and the sprinkler and mounting checks a tech should respect.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. We name NFPA 13 (sprinkler systems), NFPA 72 (fire alarm) and ASHRAE 55 (thermal comfort) in plain text only, since the standards are copyrighted. Editions and local rules vary, so the sprinkler contractor, the building authority and the fan maker decide each job.

What an HVLS fan is

An HVLS fan is a large-diameter fan, commonly 8 to 24 feet across, that turns slowly and moves a lot of air gently. A utility program page from Enbridge Gas limits its rebate to HVLS fans 12 feet or larger in buildings with ceilings at least 22 feet high, and says, "the higher the ceiling, the worse the problem gets."

Why warm air piles up at the roof

Warm air is lighter, so it rises and collects under the roof deck. An HPAC Engineering article puts the floor-to-ceiling difference in heated large spaces at roughly 5 F to 30 F. A maker guide from Continental Fan offers a rule of thumb of about 1 F per 3 feet of height and notes that comfort control matters mostly in the first 6 feet above the floor. The thermostat sits low, so heaters run until the floor is comfortable while the top stays warm.

That warm layer costs money because the roof is where heat leaves. Heat loss through a surface follows Q = U x A x dT: the U-value of the assembly, its area, and the temperature difference across it. The same roof loses more heat when the air under it is 75 F than when it is 57 F. Enbridge says savings potential is highest where stratification differences are large. Radiant heat builds less of a hot air layer: Enbridge excludes radiant heaters, and a Carbon Trust note reposted on a maker site calls fans rarely cost-effective with them.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

All inputs are our own assumptions, not a real building.

  • Building: 200 ft by 200 ft floor, 40 ft clear height, so the roof area is 40,000 sq ft.
  • Stratification before fans: an assumed 0.5 F per foot, so 40 ft x 0.5 = 20 F. The thermostat holds 55 F at the floor, so the roof deck air is 75 F. (That sits inside the 5 F to 30 F range HPAC reports.)
  • Roof U-value: assumed 0.05 Btu/h per sq ft per F, a well-insulated roof.
  • Outdoor air: assumed 30 F.
  • After mixing: floor still held at 55 F, with an assumed 2 F of leftover gradient, so the roof deck air is about 57 F.

Before the fans. The roof temperature difference is 75 - 30 = 45 F. Q = 0.05 x 40,000 x 45 = 2,000 x 45 = 90,000 Btu/h.

After the fans. The difference is 57 - 30 = 27 F. Q = 2,000 x 27 = 54,000 Btu/h.

Reduction. 90,000 - 54,000 = 36,000 Btu/h, which is 36,000 / 90,000 = 40% of this roof's loss. With an assumed 80% efficient heater, the fuel input saved is 36,000 / 0.80 = 45,000 Btu/h, or 0.45 therm per hour. At an assumed $1.20 per therm that is about $0.54 per hour.

Cost of running the fans. Assume four fans at 0.5 kW each in this slow mode: 4 x 0.5 = 2 kW. At an assumed $0.14 per kWh, that is $0.28 per hour. Net: $0.54 - $0.28 = about $0.26 per hour in cold hours.

Limits: this counts roof loss only, not walls, doors or leakage, and assumes mixing really erases most of the gradient. Enbridge excludes buildings with night setbacks from its rebate. Treat the result as an order-of-magnitude picture, not a prediction.

Summer: cooling effect, not cooling air

In warm weather the fan does not lower the air temperature. It moves air across skin, so people feel cooler. The Center for the Built Environment (CBE) at UC Berkeley says an air speed of 0.5 m/s (100 fpm) gives about 2 C (4 F) of cooling effect under its stated conditions: 76 F operative temperature, 50% humidity, light clothing and light activity. CBE says the ASHRAE 55 elevated air speed method lets a designer credit that effect, and lists a 0.8 m/s (160 fpm) average air speed limit when occupants cannot control the fan. Our own note: a fan speed that suits a gym floor may not suit a packing bench where loose paper blows around. CBE adds that fans may not help in extreme heat; air conditioning is safer where available.

In winter CBE says the occupied-zone air speed must stay very low to avoid a draft feeling, and that running the fan to push air upward uses more power than downward for the same mixing. Our own advice: set winter speed low and check floor-level air movement before you leave.

Sizing and spacing: what the maker guides say

Start with the maker's coverage chart, since spread varies by design, then check minimums. Two maker documents we read agree (numbers are for those products; read yours):

  • Fan blades at least 10 ft above the floor.
  • Center-to-center spacing of at least 2.5 times the diameter. For a 20 ft fan that is 50 ft.
  • Clear space around the blade path from walls, lights, ducts and conduit: one manual says at least 15% of the diameter, another at least 2 ft.

Enbridge caps rebated fans at length x width x 0.75 divided by a constant, 8,056 for a 20 ft fan. Our own arithmetic: 40,000 x 0.75 / 8,056 is about 3.7, which is why we assumed four fans. That is a rebate rule, not a design method.

Working with unit heaters and tube heaters

Enbridge excludes fans with radiant heaters or high-velocity vertical-throw unit heaters in the fan's influence area, and says its eligible buildings have heaters at roof level with thermostats at the floor. One maker guide tells installers to check combustible clearances for HVAC equipment and radiant heaters near the fan. Our own checklist: keep the fan stream off the heater's discharge, ask the heater maker whether moving air could disturb a flue or combustion air, and recheck thermostat readings. Sprinkler interlocks

The National Fire Sprinkler Association's article, quoting the 2013 NFPA 13, lists these rules for HVLS fans in sprinklered buildings: maximum fan diameter 24 ft; fan centered approximately between four adjacent sprinklers; 3 ft (36 in) of vertical clearance between the fan and the sprinkler deflectors; and every HVLS fan interlocked to shut down immediately on a waterflow signal. With a building fire alarm, the interlock follows NFPA 72. A fan maker's blog restates those points and says to verify the current edition, and one manual says a fire suppression specialist installs the external relay. We cannot say what your edition requires. If you find the shutdown bypassed, report it to the owner and the fire alarm contractor.

Mounting and safety cable checks

  • Structure. Both maker documents say not to mount to single purlins, trusses or bar joists. One lists I-beams or angle iron only, approved by a structural engineer. A 20 ft fan can generate about 300 ft-lb of torque, and one manual says to use twice the hanging weight when checking the structure.
  • Safety cable. One manual says it is required for the warranty; the other has the installer wrap it around the beam with as little slack as possible. Check that it is present, tight and not frayed.

Maintenance

The maker manuals give checklists, not intervals: mounting and blade bolts, safety cable, terminations, cleaning, and one adds an annual cable inspection. A seller's guide suggests monthly visual checks, quarterly torque checks and an annual structural review. Torque values are product specific. Before touching a fan, isolate power: OSHA's 29 CFR 1910.147 requires lockout where the energy source can be locked, and one manual says to wait 3 minutes for capacitors to discharge, noting that dark indicator lights do not prove it is safe.

Field card: HVLS fans and destratification

Tall room, warm roof, cold floor: mix it gently and keep the sprinkler shutdown intact.

  • Q = U x A x dT: a warmer roof layer loses more heat.
  • HPAC range: about 5 F to 30 F floor-to-ceiling difference in large heated spaces.
  • CBE: 100 fpm gives about 4 F cooling effect (stated conditions); it cools people, not air.
  • Maker minimums: 10 ft blade height, 2.5 x diameter between fans.
  • NFPA 13 as summarized by NFSA: 24 ft max, centered between four sprinklers, 3 ft below deflectors, shutdown on waterflow.
  • Not on radiant heat or vertical-throw heaters without maker guidance.
  • Lock out before service; check cable, bolts, wobble.
