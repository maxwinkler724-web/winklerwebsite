Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom: Relief Valve, Drain Valve or Tank

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Water on the floor under a tank water heater has several possible sources, and they range from harmless to "replace the heater this week." This lesson helps a homeowner find where the water is really coming from, explains why a relief valve discharges, and ends with a short section for technicians.

First, make it safe

Your heater's label and manual rule over this page. Here is what the makers we read agree on.

  • Shut off the energy. A. O. Smith's electric heater manual says to turn off the breaker or remove the fuses and confirm with a non-contact tester before working. Bradford White's gas manual gives the draining order as gas off, power off, then cold supply closed.
  • Close the cold water supply valve above the heater if water is actively running. A. O. Smith also says that if wiring, thermostats or insulation got wet, shut the heater off and have a qualified person inspect it, and that a heater that flooded or had thermostats submerged should be replaced.
  • Hot water can scald. The Consumer Product Safety Commission lists about 2 seconds to a third-degree burn at 150°F, 6 seconds at 140°F and 30 seconds at 130°F, and recommends a 120°F setting. Keep people and pets away from any discharge.
  • Smell gas? Leave and call the gas utility from outside. Do not troubleshoot a leak by the pilot.

Find the source before you decide anything

Dry the floor and the heater with a towel, then watch for a few minutes with a flashlight. Where the drip appears tells you most of what you need.

  1. Condensation. Rheem's manual says moisture often forms when a tank is first filled with cold water and is normal; it should disappear as the water warms. If it keeps happening, Rheem says to look for leaks in the piping and fittings. Bradford White also warns to keep cold water pipes from sitting directly above the gas control, so humid-weather drips do not land on it.
  2. Fittings and connections. A. O. Smith says most leaks occur at connections rather than in the tank itself. Look at the hot and cold nipples on top, the relief valve fitting, and the element gaskets on an electric heater. Water can run down the jacket and pool at the bottom, so a leak that looks like it comes from underneath may start at the top.
  3. Drain valve. Bradford White lists the drain valve, along with the relief valve and hot and cold connections, as places to check for leaks after filling. A drip at the little valve near the floor is a repairable part, not a failed tank. Do not force it; have a plumber replace it.
  4. T&P (relief) valve discharge. Look for water running from the side-mounted valve or from the pipe that runs from it toward the floor. That is covered in the next section, because the cause is not the valve itself.
  5. The tank body. If the water comes through the jacket or seeps from the bottom and no fitting is wet above it, the tank has probably corroded through.

Why a relief valve discharges, and why you never cap it

The temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve is the heater's last line of defense. It opens if the water gets too hot or the pressure gets too high. Three things commonly make it open.

  • Thermal expansion. Water takes up more room as it heats. Rheem's manual says a check valve, backflow preventer or pressure reducing valve in the cold inlet line can make the plumbing a closed system, which traps the extra water. Rheem and Bradford White both say periodic discharge can be thermal expansion in a closed system, and Rheem adds that replacing the valve will not fix it.
  • High supply pressure. A. O. Smith says most codes cap incoming pressure at 80 psi, recommends 50 to 60 psi, notes that some homes see over 100 psi, and says a pressure reducing valve is required above 80 psi. High pressure can make the valve drip and cause early leaks.
  • Excess temperature. A thermostat or control that lets the water overheat can open the valve. That is a safety problem a technician must find. Do not respond by turning the valve down or plugging it.

Never cap, plug or restrict the T&P valve or its discharge pipe. A. O. Smith warns that an explosion could occur if the valve or its pipe is blocked. Bradford White says not to thread, plug or cap the discharge line, and Watts says never to plug a safety relief valve. Do not put a valve, reducer or cap in that pipe either.

Expansion tanks on closed systems

The usual cure for thermal expansion is a small thermal expansion tank. It has a rubber bladder with an air cushion on one side. As the heater warms, extra water squeezes into the tank instead of pushing the pressure up. Rheem says to put it on the cold line between the heater and the check valve. A. O. Smith notes that codes generally call for one in most homes and says it should be sized to the heater and pressurized to match the incoming water pressure. Amtrol's instructions describe mounting it on the cold supply between the heater and any backflow preventer, check valve or pressure reducing valve, with the tank vertical. Bradford White's manual does not specify a method and tells you to ask the water supplier or local plumbing inspector.

Rheem points out that swapping the relief valve will not cure expansion discharge, so if the valve keeps dripping, ask a plumber whether the system is closed and whether a tank is present and charged. For the same idea on a heating boiler, see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.

When the tank itself is leaking

A. O. Smith's manual puts it plainly: because of the normal corrosive action of water, the tank will eventually leak. A leak from the tank body is not repairable. Shut off power or gas and the cold water, drain it if you can do so safely, and plan on a replacement heater. The Department of Energy says storage water heaters last about 10 to 15 years and advises researching a replacement as yours nears that age. If the heater is in a finished space, A. O. Smith suggests a drain pan piped to an adequate drain and an automatic leak detection and shutoff device. Compare costs with How Long Do HVAC Systems Last: Repair or Replace.

If the problem is a lack of hot water rather than a leak, use Water Heater No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks.

Tech section: test, size and set it right

  • Annual relief valve test. A. O. Smith, Rheem and Bradford White all say to lift and release the lever once a year. Rheem says to let several gallons flow; Bradford White says to lift it several times until it seats. Make sure the discharge is routed somewhere safe first. If water does not flow freely, A. O. Smith says to turn the heater off and call a qualified person. A. O. Smith also asks for a qualified inspection of the valve and pipe, and says to follow the valve label.
  • Discharge pipe. A. O. Smith: at least 3/4 inch inside diameter, sloped to drain, ending no more than six inches above a floor drain or outside, made of material rated for 250°F such as copper or CPVC, with no valve or reducer. Do not pipe it into the drain pan.
  • Precharge. Amtrol's Therm-X-Trol instructions say to adjust the precharge with the tank empty of water to match the cold supply pressure, at ambient temperature, and not to re-pressurize except at the initial installation. Watts' tank instructions also say to bring the air side to system pressure before installing.
  • Pressure reducing valve. Measure static pressure with a gauge on a hose bib. If it exceeds 80 psi, a reducing valve is required; set it near the 50 to 60 psi A. O. Smith recommends, then set the tank precharge to match.

Worked example: a mismatched precharge

A house has 62 psi static pressure (gauge). The new expansion tank shipped with 40 psi in its air side and nobody changed it. Air volume follows Boyle's law, P1 × V1 = P2 × V2, using absolute pressure, which is gauge plus 14.7.

  1. Precharge, absolute: 40 + 14.7 = 54.7 psia.
  2. House pressure, absolute: 62 + 14.7 = 76.7 psia.
  3. When 62 psi water pushes on the bladder, the air shrinks to V2 = V1 × 54.7 ÷ 76.7 = 0.713 × V1.
  4. The tank has already lost about 29 percent of its air cushion before the heater ever fires, so it has less room for expansion and the relief valve opens sooner.

Set to 62 psi, the same tank would start at 62 psi with its full cushion. That is why makers say to match the precharge to the incoming pressure.

Field card: Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom

Find the wet spot first; never cap the relief valve.

  • Shut off power or gas and the cold water supply if water is running.
  • Light moisture on a new tank can be normal condensation (Rheem); persistent water is a leak.
  • Check fittings and the drain valve before blaming the tank.
  • Relief valve discharge usually means thermal expansion, high pressure or overheating.
  • Never plug, cap or restrict the T&P valve or its pipe.
  • Closed system: add a thermal expansion tank with precharge matching supply pressure.
  • Water through the tank body means replacement.
  • Test the relief valve yearly with the discharge routed safely.
