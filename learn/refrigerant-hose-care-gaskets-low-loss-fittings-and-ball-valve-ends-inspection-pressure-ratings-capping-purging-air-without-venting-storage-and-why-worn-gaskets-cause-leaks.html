Refrigerant Hose Care: Gaskets, Low-Loss Fittings, Inspection, Capping and Storage

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Hoses are the cheapest tools on the truck and the ones that quietly ruin the most jobs. A tired gasket leaks refrigerant on a pressure job and leaks air into a vacuum. A capped, inspected, correctly rated hose does neither. This lesson covers hose care from gasket to storage rack, and it keeps the federal rules on releases in view the whole way.

Gaskets: the small part that decides if a hose seals

Most service hoses seal at the swivel nut with a flat, soft gasket (the seat) that is squeezed between the hose end and the service port or valve. Tightening by hand is normally enough because the gasket does the sealing, not the threads. Over time the gasket flattens, nicks or hardens. Our field rule is to look at the gasket every time you attach a hose, and to replace it when you see a flat spot, a tear, a gouge, a groove worn by a damaged port face, or any hardness or cracking. A spare gasket costs little compared with a repeated vacuum pull. If you cannot decide whether a gasket is good, treat that as a replace decision; the same plain rule appears in a university extension hose guide, which says that if you have to stare at a hose and wonder whether it needs replacing, change it.

Why does a worn gasket matter so much? On a pressurized system, a tiny gap at the nut sheds refrigerant to the air. Under vacuum the flow reverses: air and moisture leak in. That leak shows up as a vacuum reading that will not fall as low as it should or that creeps back up when you close the valve to isolate the pump. A tech who blames the system can chase a leak that is really sitting at a hose end. Replace the gasket and recheck before you tear into the equipment. The test that separates a pump-side leak from a system leak is in the vacuum decay lesson.

Low-loss fittings and ball-valve ends

A low-loss fitting is a device that makes the connection between hoses, appliances or recovery machines and closes, either automatically or by hand, when it is disconnected, so that the refrigerant in the hose stays in the hose. That is how the federal rule defines it (40 CFR 82.152). The recovery-equipment section of the same rules (82.158(d)(7)) says recovery equipment must be equipped with low-loss fittings on all hoses. Many techs put the same fittings on their service hoses because they cut the puff of refrigerant that escapes when a hose comes off.

A ball-valve end is one form of low-loss fitting: a small valve built into the hose end that you close with a quarter turn while the hose is still connected. Close it first, then remove the hose. Stems and seals in these valves can wear, so exercise them, look for weeping at the handle and keep dirt out of the bore. Hose-end valves and core-removal tools also change how freely gas flows during evacuation; that effect is covered in the hose conductance lesson, so we do not repeat it here.

Inspect before every job

  • Cuts and scrapes. Replace a hose whose outer cover is cut through or scraped to the braid. The extension guide we read lists cuts, exposed braid and worn spots as reasons to replace a hose.
  • Bulges and blisters. A bulge or blister at a fitting or along the cover means the hose has been weakened. That guide lists blisters, bubbles and bulging ends as replace items. A bulge is not a repair job.
  • Kinks. A kink that permanently pinches the hose wall is a replace item in the same guide. A kinked hose also restricts flow and gives false gauge readings.
  • Fittings. Look for cracked or loose crimped collars and for swivel nuts that no longer spin freely. The extension guide notes that crimped fittings are permanent and belong to the maker or a proper shop, so we do not recommend field splices.
  • Inside. A hose that feels stiff, has soft spots or sheds particles inside should be retired. If the hose is old or has an unknown history, plan on a new one.

Pressure ratings suited to the refrigerant

Every hose has a working pressure and a higher burst pressure, and the extension guide points out that ratings apply to new hoses and fall as hoses wear and when temperatures climb. Choose a hose whose printed working pressure is above the highest pressure you can see on the job, using the equipment data plate and the maker's instructions for the refrigerant in the system. Higher-pressure refrigerants and test gases need hoses made for them. If the rating is missing or unreadable, retire the hose. Do not mix a hose designed for one refrigerant or oil type with another without checking the maker's instructions, and keep any hose that has carried a different refrigerant or oil type labeled so it does not cross-contaminate a system.

Cap the ends, keep moisture and dirt out

Refrigerant systems are hurt by moisture and grit. An open hose end acts like a straw for both, and the hose wall itself can hold moisture. Cap or plug the ends when a hose is not in use, and cap the unused ports of the manifold. If a hose has been left open or you know it is wet, expect a longer pull-down, because moisture that boils off slowly under vacuum is what makes readings stall. For the connection-and-removal sequence at the gauge set, see the manifold gauge hookup lesson.

Purging air from hoses without venting

A hose that has been in the truck is full of air. If that air is pushed into a system, it becomes a noncondensable that raises head pressure, and if it is mixed into a fresh charge it holds moisture. That is why many techs get the air out first. The cleanest method is to evacuate the hoses with the vacuum pump before opening to the system, which releases nothing at all. The alternative, pushing the air out with a brief flow of gas, has to be done with care: federal rules prohibit knowingly venting refrigerant while servicing equipment, and the rule that allows de minimis releases covers small amounts released while making good-faith attempts to recapture refrigerant (40 CFR 82.154). The federal environmental agency's venting page lists releases during the connecting and disconnecting of service hoses among those small releases. We could not find a federal page that approves using system refrigerant as a purge gas, so we do not teach it as an approved step. A pure nitrogen purge carries no refrigerant, but a hose that still holds refrigerant should be recovered, not vented, as far as we can tell from the rules above. Follow your employer's procedure and the equipment maker's instructions, keep any release to the smallest possible amount, and recover what is in the hoses with approved equipment. The wider rules are in the venting prohibition lesson.

Storage

The extension guide recommends storing hoses indoors, away from sun, heat and moisture, hung or coiled so that they keep their shape, without kinks or stretching, and with nothing heavy stacked on top. In our own practice we add: caps on, gaskets in place, ball valves closed, and the hose not left lying on a rooftop in the sun. A short, honest inspection when you hang the set up saves a bad surprise at the next job.

Worked example: how much refrigerant can a hose hold?

This is our own example, not a published figure. It shows why hoses matter to the "minimal release" goal. Assumptions, all ours: each hose is 60 inches (5 ft) long; the inside diameters are 1/4 inch, 3/8 inch and 1/2 inch; the hose is completely full of liquid (a worst case, since most service hoses carry vapor); the liquid density is 1.1 g/cm³, a round number chosen for the example and not for any particular refrigerant. Unit conversions: 1 in³ = 16.387 cm³, 1 oz = 28.35 g.

Volume of a round hose: V = (π/4) × d² × L.

  1. 1/4 in: V = 0.7854 × 0.0625 × 60 = 2.945 in³ = 48.3 cm³. Mass = 48.3 × 1.1 = 53.1 g = 1.87 oz.
  2. 3/8 in: V = 0.7854 × 0.1406 × 60 = 6.627 in³ = 108.6 cm³. Mass = 108.6 × 1.1 = 119.5 g = 4.21 oz.
  3. 1/2 in: V = 0.7854 × 0.25 × 60 = 11.781 in³ = 193.1 cm³. Mass = 193.1 × 1.1 = 212.4 g = 7.49 oz.

Volume goes with the square of the diameter, so doubling the diameter from 1/4 to 1/2 inch holds four times as much (7.49 divided by 1.87 is 4.0). Three hoses of the largest size, full of liquid, could hold about 22 oz in total. Vapor-filled hoses hold far less, but the lesson stands: a closed hose end or low-loss fitting keeps that refrigerant in the hose instead of in the air.

Field card: Refrigerant hose care

Look, seal, cap, recover.

  • Check the gasket at every connection; replace if flat, nicked, hard or cracked.
  • Replace hoses with cuts, bulges, blisters, kinks or an unreadable pressure rating.
  • Use a hose rated above the highest pressure you will see.
  • Close the low-loss or ball-valve end before you disconnect.
  • Cap hose ends and unused manifold ports.
  • Evacuate hoses first when you can; keep any release to the smallest amount, and do not vent refrigerant.
  • Our example: a 5 ft liquid-full hose holds about 1.9 oz (1/4 in), 4.2 oz (3/8 in) or 7.5 oz (1/2 in) at an assumed 1.1 g/cm³.
