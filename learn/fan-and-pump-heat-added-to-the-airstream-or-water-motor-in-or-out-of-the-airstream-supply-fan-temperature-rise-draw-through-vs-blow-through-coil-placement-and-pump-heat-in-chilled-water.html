Fan and Pump Heat: Supply Fan Temperature Rise, Coil Placement and Chilled Water

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every watt a fan or pump motor draws ends up as heat somewhere. In an air handler it warms the supply air, and in a chilled water loop it becomes extra load on the chiller. This lesson shows where the losses go, how to estimate supply fan temperature rise from brake horsepower, why coil placement changes the supply temperature you read, and how pump heat shows up in chilled water.

Where fan and motor losses go

A motor turns electric power into shaft power, and the rest is loss. A fan turns shaft power into airflow and pressure, and the rest is loss again. A U.S. Department of Energy fan systems sourcebook defines fan efficiency as the power imparted to the airstream divided by the power delivered by the motor, and it says the power of the airflow is pressure times flow, corrected for units. A U.S. Department of Energy motor systems fact sheet adds that energy lost in an inefficient system is dissipated as waste heat and flow energy, and that this heat usually has to be removed by the HVAC system.

The useful part is not really saved, either. The pressure the fan builds is used up as air rubs through filters, coils, ducts and terminals, and that friction also becomes heat. In a supply-only system, our own energy-balance reasoning says essentially all the shaft power ends up in the air that flows past. The only open question is where along the path the heat arrives.

Motor in the airstream or outside it

Motor losses (winding, core and bearing losses) leave the motor as heat. If the motor sits in the airstream, as with a direct-drive plug fan or a motor mounted inside the fan section, that heat goes into the supply air. If the motor is outside the cabinet and drives the fan with a belt, the motor loss goes into the mechanical room instead, and only the shaft power reaches the air. The belt drive has its own small loss, which also lands where the drive sits.

That gives you two numbers to keep apart. Brake horsepower (bhp) is the shaft power the fan needs. Motor input power is bhp divided by motor efficiency. For a motor inside the airstream, count the input power. For a motor outside, count only bhp. Part-load efficiency matters too: the DOE fan sourcebook says motor efficiency and power factor fall significantly when a motor runs below half its rating, and the DOE motor fact sheet says motors usually run most efficiently at about 70 to 80 percent load.

Supply fan temperature rise math

A federal conversion-factor table lists 1 electric horsepower as 746 watts and 1 Btu per hour as 0.2930711 watt. Dividing gives about 2,545 Btu/h per horsepower, the number in the field formula. The 1.08 comes from the standard sensible heat relation for air near standard conditions; where the 1.08 comes from covers its limits at altitude and temperature.

Temperature rise (°F) = bhp × 2,545 / (1.08 × cfm)

If the motor is in the airstream, replace bhp with motor input horsepower (bhp divided by motor efficiency).

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not from a manufacturer. Assumptions we chose: a variable-air-volume supply fan moving 20,000 cfm at design; 25 bhp at the shaft; a motor that is 93 percent efficient at that load; mixed air entering the unit at 80.0°F; and a target duct supply temperature of 55.0°F.

  1. Fan with the motor outside the airstream: 25 × 2,545 = 63,625 Btu/h.
  2. Air capacity: 1.08 × 20,000 = 21,600 Btu/h per °F.
  3. Rise: 63,625 / 21,600 = 2.95°F, about 2.9°F.
  4. Motor in the airstream: input = 25 / 0.93 = 26.88 hp. Total = 26.88 × 2,545 = 68,413 Btu/h, so the rise is 68,413 / 21,600 = 3.17°F, about 3.2°F.
  5. The motor loss alone is 1.88 hp, or about 4,790 Btu/h, which adds only 0.22°F.

So belt-driven and plug-fan designs differ by a few tenths of a degree in this example, while the fan shaft power does most of the work. We use the 3.2°F motor-in-airstream value below.

Fan speed and fan heat

The DOE fan sourcebook says airflow changes in direct proportion to fan speed, pressure with the square of speed, and power with a third-order relationship. In our example, a fan slowed to 80 percent speed would need about 0.8 cubed, or 51 percent, of the shaft power, but also only 80 percent of the airflow. That is our own arithmetic, and the rise in degrees falls to roughly 0.512 / 0.8 = 64 percent of the design value. Part-load fan heat is smaller, which is one reason variable speed helps. Draw-through vs blow-through coil placement

In a draw-through unit the fan sits after the coil and pulls air across it. In a blow-through unit the fan sits before the coil and pushes air through it. Fan heat is added at the fan in both. What changes is whether the coil sees it.

  • Draw-through: the coil sees the mixed air at 80.0°F. The fan then adds 3.2°F. To deliver 55.0°F to the ducts, the coil has to leave the air at 51.8°F. A temperature probe right after the coil reads 51.8°F, not the 55.0°F that the zones receive.
  • Blow-through: the fan raises the air to 83.2°F first. The coil cools it to 55.0°F, so a probe after the coil reads the supply temperature directly.

Sensible cooling at the coil is the same in this example either way: 28.2°F × 21,600 = 609,120 Btu/h. The coil is simply working to a different leaving temperature. A draw-through coil runs colder, which tends to remove more moisture, and the fan warms the air afterward. This is our own reasoning from an energy balance, so check unit data before relying on it for a design. Condensate handling also differs between the two; see Air Handling Unit Basics for unit layouts.

Pump heat into chilled water

A pump gives the water pressure, and that pressure is spent as friction in pipe, valves and coils, so it also becomes heat. In a closed chilled water loop, the shaft power is the heat added to the water. A DOE pump systems tip sheet gives fluid power as head (ft) × flow (gpm) × specific gravity / 3,960, and defines pump efficiency as fluid power divided by pump shaft power. It reports efficiencies ranging widely from 35 percent to more than 90 percent. The factor 500 in the water formula is our own arithmetic: about 8.33 lb per gallon × 60 minutes × 1 Btu per lb per °F. A U.S. Geological Survey page gives water's specific heat as 4,184 joules per kilogram per °C, which is the same as 1 kilocalorie per kilogram.

Our own example. Assumptions we chose: 600 gpm in a closed loop, about 158 ft of head, 80 percent pump efficiency and a 94 percent efficient motor in the open mechanical room.

  1. Fluid power: 600 × 158 / 3,960 = 23.9 hp.
  2. Shaft power: 23.9 / 0.80 = about 30 bhp.
  3. Heat to the water: 30 × 2,545 = 76,350 Btu/h, about 6.4 tons that the chiller has to remove.
  4. If it were not removed, the loop would warm by 76,350 / (500 × 600) = 0.25°F per pass.
  5. Motor loss: input 30 / 0.94 = 31.9 hp, so the loss is 1.9 hp, or about 4,870 Btu/h, and it goes into the pump room air, not the water.

A quarter of a degree is small against a design coil temperature difference, but the 6.4 tons is real chiller load, and it is larger when pumps run at high head with poor efficiency. See Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T for how the loop temperature difference is read, and motor efficiency for fan and pump motors.

Field checks

  • Find out where the supply temperature sensor sits relative to the fan before you judge coil leaving temperature.
  • Note whether the motor is in the airstream. It decides whether motor losses count in the air or in the room.
  • Use measured amps and volts or drive readings for real bhp where you can, because nameplate horsepower overstates a lightly loaded fan.
  • Remember that fan and pump heat adds to the cooling load in summer and offsets heating in winter.

Field card: Fan and Pump Heat

All shaft power ends up as heat in the air or water.

  • Rise (°F) = bhp × 2,545 / (1.08 × cfm).
  • Motor in the airstream: use bhp / motor efficiency.
  • Draw-through: probe after the coil reads colder than supply.
  • Blow-through: the coil sees the fan heat and can be read as supply.
  • Pump heat to water = shaft bhp × 2,545 Btu/h; rise = that / (500 × gpm).
  • Our own example: 20,000 cfm and 25 bhp gives 2.9°F; 600 gpm and 30 bhp gives 0.25°F.
