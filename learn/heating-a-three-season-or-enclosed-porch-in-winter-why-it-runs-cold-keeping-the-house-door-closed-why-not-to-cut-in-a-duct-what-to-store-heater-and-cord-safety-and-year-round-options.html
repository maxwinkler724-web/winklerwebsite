Heating a Three-Season or Enclosed Porch in Winter: Why It Runs Cold

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A three-season or enclosed porch feels great in October and miserable in January. It was built to be a covered place to sit, not a heated room, and the house heating system was never sized for it. This lesson explains why the porch runs cold, what to keep out of it, how to use a portable heater without starting a fire, and what your real options are if you want to use it all year.

Why a porch runs cold

Most three-season porches have single-pane glass, or screens with plastic panels. They usually have little or no insulation in the walls, floor or roof, and often no heat source at all. A federal window guideline lists whole-window U-factors for single clear glass of about 0.88 to 1.29, against about 0.52 to 0.83 for double clear glass, and says double glazing cuts heat loss by more than half compared with single glazing. U-factor is the number of BTU per hour that flow through one square foot of window for each degree of temperature difference, so a lower number means a better window. A porch with a lot of single glass is therefore a big, leaky wall between your heated air and the outdoors.

Why not just cut a supply duct into the porch

This one is our own reasoning, not something we found a source for, so treat it as a rule of thumb from the trade. Cutting a branch off the house duct system for a porch usually causes four problems:

  • Unbalanced air. The system was designed for the rooms it serves. A new branch steals air from them, and with no return path from the porch the air has nowhere to go.
  • Cold ducts. The porch is cold, so a duct running through it, or ending in it, carries heat out of the system and can be cold to the touch.
  • Condensation. Warm, humid house air meeting cold duct metal or a cold porch surface can condense water.
  • An overworked system. A furnace or heat pump sized for the house now has to heat a poorly insulated space too, so it runs longer and may struggle on the coldest days.

A federal home HVAC guide makes a related point: it says adding ducts to an existing home for an addition may not be worth it, and that a ductless mini-split heat pump may be a good alternative. See our sunroom and addition lesson for that comparison.

Keep the house door closed and sealed

The door between the house and the porch is the one part you control. A federal energy page says weatherstripping can reduce the energy lost to air leakage around exterior doors, and recommends checking it every year and replacing it when it wears out. The same idea applies to this door. If the porch is unheated, treat that door like an exterior door: close it, and make sure the weatherstripping and the bottom seal are in good shape. Leaving it open lets house heat flow into a space that cannot hold it. See our lesson on drafty doors and windows.

Frost and condensation on porch glass

Glass is a poor insulator, so in winter a porch pane is far colder than the walls around it. A university extension fact sheet explains that cooler air holds less water vapor, so humid air touching a cold pane leaves liquid on it, and that air leaking around windows keeps bringing moisture to the glass. Another extension page says frost or condensation can be a sign of too much indoor moisture or a cold window, and that water and ice on windows can encourage mold on frames. Both suggest lowering indoor humidity, and one notes that covering a window with plastic warms the glass surface. Porch glass will frost more than house windows because it is colder, even with normal humidity. Do not hang wet laundry or run a humidifier out there. Our lesson on winter window condensation goes deeper.

What to store on the porch, and what to keep out

  • Water lines and anything with water in it. An extension article says supply lines in unheated areas are more susceptible to freezing, and that freezing water builds pressure that can break pipes. Do not run or leave water lines, hoses, or water-filled items on a cold porch.
  • Paint. An extension guide says to store paint in dry areas that will not freeze, and that latex paint is no longer usable if it has been repeatedly frozen and thawed.
  • Houseplants and pots. An extension page notes that roots of above-ground container plants can be the same temperature as the winter air, and that clay, ceramic or glazed pots left exposed may crack. Bring tender plants indoors.
  • Batteries in deep cold. Our own caution: very cold weather can weaken batteries and some devices. Keep spare batteries and electronics you rely on inside the house.

Portable electric heaters and extension cords

A portable electric heater is the usual quick fix for a porch, and the fire-safety agencies agree on the basics. A federal consumer-product-safety agency says to place heaters at least three feet away from drapes, furniture and bedding, to plug electric space heaters directly into a wall outlet and never into an extension cord or power strip, and to turn heaters off when sleeping. A federal fire-safety agency also says to keep a heater at least three feet from anything that can burn, to put it on a solid, level surface, to check the cord and plug for cracks or damage, and to plug it straight into a wall socket. Porches are full of cushions, wicker, curtains and stored boxes, so that three-foot space needs to be real. Read more in our space heater safety lesson.

No fuel-burning heaters or grills in an enclosed porch

Do not bring a gas, propane, kerosene or charcoal heater or grill onto an enclosed porch, even with the door cracked. A federal consumer-product-safety agency says fuel-burning heating equipment can produce carbon monoxide, an odorless, colorless and deadly gas, and says to install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, with battery backup. For charcoal, it says never to use it to cook or provide heat inside enclosed areas, naming homes and garages among the places, and to use charcoal grills outside only. An enclosed porch is an enclosed area attached to your house, so the same rule is the safe one. See our winter carbon monoxide lesson.

Worked example (our own): what a 1,500 W heater can hold

This is a rough, our own example. The assumptions are ours: a porch with 120 sq ft of single glazing at a U-factor of about 1.0 (inside the range of the federal guideline above), a 40 F difference between the porch and outdoors (for example 60 F inside and 20 F outside), and we ignore the walls, floor, roof, door and air leaks, which would all add to the loss.

  1. Heat loss through the glass = area x U-factor x temperature difference = 120 x 1.0 x 40 = 4,800 BTU/h.
  2. A 1,500 W heater makes 1,500 x 3.412 = 5,118 BTU/h, since one watt is about 3.412 BTU/h.
  3. Surplus = 5,118 - 4,800 = 318 BTU/h. So the heater can just about hold a 40 F difference, and no more. The most it can hold through the glass alone is 5,118 / (120 x 1.0) = about 43 F.

On a 10 F night, holding the porch at 60 F means a 50 F difference, and the glass alone would lose 120 x 1.0 x 50 = 6,000 BTU/h, more than the heater makes. In real life the other losses and leaks mean the porch will sit well below the number we calculated. That is why a portable heater makes a porch tolerable for an hour, but does not make it a room. Also, running a heater all day moves the porch out of the "occasional use" category and into extra electric load, so ask a qualified electrician about the circuit if you plan to do that.

If you want to use it all year

If the owner wants a true year-round room, the porch has to be treated like an addition. In general terms, that means insulating the floor, walls and roof, replacing single glazing with better windows, air sealing, and then heating it with something sized for that room. A federal weatherization page lists insulation, air sealing, storm windows and window film among shell measures, and the federal home HVAC guide describes ductless mini-split heat pumps as a fit for additions and single rooms. A ductless head should be sized for the room after the shell is improved, not for the leaky porch you started with, and sized and installed by a qualified installer. A trained contractor should determine the heating and cooling loads, as the guide says. Our sunroom and addition lesson covers the options in more detail.

Field card: Heating a Three-Season or Enclosed Porch

A porch is mostly glass. Seal the door, skip the duct, keep the heater simple.

  • Single glass at U about 1.0 loses about 1 BTU/h per sq ft per degree F. Our own example: 120 sq ft at 40 F is 4,800 BTU/h.
  • A 1,500 W heater makes 5,118 BTU/h: it barely holds that example.
  • Do not cut a house duct into the porch (our own rule of thumb).
  • Keep the house door closed and weatherstripped.
  • Store nothing that freezes: water, paint, tender plants.
  • Heater 3 feet from burnables, plugged straight into the wall, no extension cord.
  • No fuel-burning heaters or grills on an enclosed porch (carbon monoxide).
  • Year-round use: insulate, upgrade windows, then a ductless head sized for the room.
