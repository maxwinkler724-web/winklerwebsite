Writing a First Resume for an Entry-Level HVAC Technician or Helper Job

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A first resume has one job: get a hiring manager to say "let's talk." You do not need years of HVAC history for that. You need one clean page that shows you are reliable, can learn, and have started building real skills. This lesson walks through each part and ends with a worked example.

What a hiring manager is looking for

The federal occupational outlook page for heating, air conditioning and refrigeration mechanics and installers says technicians typically learn through postsecondary instruction or on-the-job training, and that some enter apprenticeships. It also lists the qualities the work calls for: mechanical skills, customer-service skills (friendly, polite and punctual) and physical stamina. Your resume should point at those same three things with true examples. It adds that technicians may need a driver's license to reach jobsites.

Layout: one page, plain and readable

A university career-center guide we read advises one page for most new job seekers, and says to cut anything not closely tied to the target field. It suggests a larger name at the top, then a phone number, an email address and your city and state; a full street address is not needed. It also suggests separating skills with commas or similar punctuation rather than columns, because many employers scan resumes with software that misreads columns. A state labor department page says to customize the resume for each job, using key skills and duties from the posting. We suggest this order for a first HVAC resume:

  1. Name and contact line
  2. Short summary (two lines)
  3. Training and certification
  4. Work history
  5. Skills and tools
  6. License and driving record, if you have one

Contact line and summary

Use a phone you answer, a plain email address built from your name, and your city and state. A summary is two lines: who you are, what you are aiming for, and one proof point. Example: "Dependable warehouse associate with a clean driving record, finishing HVAC training and EPA 608 Type I certification, seeking a helper position." Keep it factual and drop words like "expert" or "passionate."

List EPA 608 accurately

The federal outlook page says the EPA requires nearly all technicians to be certified in refrigerant handling and that certification requires passing an exam. Write exactly what you hold: Type I, Type II, Type III or Universal, with the year earned. If you have studied but not yet taken the exam, write "EPA 608 exam scheduled" only if it truly is, or leave it off. Never write "Universal" when you hold one type. See EPA 608 Certification Basics for what each type covers. List other credentials only once earned; a credential you are still working toward goes under training as "in progress," with no date implied.

Transferable work from other jobs

Most first resumes lean on non-HVAC work, and that is normal. Match each past job to what the outlook page names:

  • Customer service: being polite, on time and calm with upset people.
  • Warehouse: lifting, reading labels, counting stock, using hand trucks and following safety rules.
  • Driving: delivery routes, hitting time windows, loading a vehicle.
  • Mechanical hobbies: rebuilding an engine, fixing bikes, wiring a project. Say what you did, for example "replaced a window air conditioner's fan motor."

Training, tools, license and driving record

Describe training plainly: the subject, how long, and what you did hands-on, such as "brazed copper joints, read wiring diagrams, used a multimeter and manifold gauges in a lab." Do not name more than you did. In a skills list, include only tools you can use without help, and mark the others "learning." If you have a driver's license, state the class and write "clean driving record" only if your own record shows it; you may be asked to prove it. Our apprentice tool list lesson covers what to buy first.

Honest dates, no inflation

List month and year for each job, newest first, and keep them true. Gaps are better explained in a single honest line than hidden. The university guide above is direct that everything on a resume must be truthful. In our own view, a single stretched claim makes everything else on the page doubtful, and an employer may ask to see your certification card. If you did something with a coworker's help, say "assisted."

References and the cover note

Ask people before you list them: a former supervisor, a teacher or instructor, a coach. A government archive of older career guidance recommends a separate reference sheet with each person's name, title, relationship and contact details. Do not put "references available on request" taking up space on the resume. For the cover note, the university guide suggests three or four short paragraphs, the same font as the resume, and information that is not already on the resume. The state page suggests this order: why you are writing and how you found the opening, how your skills fit the role, then a confident close that asks for an interview. Tailor it to the posting. Worked example (our own): three warehouse duties become bullets

Our own example. The numbers below are assumptions we made up to show the method; use your real numbers. Suppose a person worked at a distribution warehouse. The rule: action verb, what you did, a count or time frame.

Plain dutyAssumed factResume bullet
Loaded trucksAbout 30 pallets per shift, 5 shifts a weekLoaded about 30 pallets per shift onto delivery trucks while following posted lifting and safety rules
Helped customers at the counterAbout 15 pickups a dayServed about 15 customers per day at the pickup counter, confirming orders and resolving mix-ups
Checked stockOne weekly count of 200 itemsCounted and logged about 200 items weekly, reporting shortages to the supervisor

Check the arithmetic before you write: 30 pallets x 5 shifts = 150 pallets a week, so "about 30 per shift" is safe, but "150 pallets daily" would be wrong. Use the smaller true number, and write "about" when you are estimating.

Sample skills section (same assumptions):

Skills: hand and cordless tools, tape measure, multimeter (basic), manifold gauges (training use), copper brazing (training use), reading wiring diagrams (learning), customer communication, pallet jack, valid driver's license.

Field card: Writing a First Resume for an Entry-Level HVAC Technician or Helper Job

One page, true claims, numbers where you have them.

  • Order: contact, summary, training and certification, work, skills, license.
  • Write the exact EPA 608 type and year; never round up to Universal.
  • Use transferable work: customer service, warehouse, driving, hobbies.
  • Bullet: action verb + what you did + a count or time frame.
  • Honest dates; credentials only once earned.
  • Ask references first; tailor the resume and a short cover note to each posting.
