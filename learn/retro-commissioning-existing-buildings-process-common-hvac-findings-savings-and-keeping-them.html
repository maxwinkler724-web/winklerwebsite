Retro-Commissioning an Existing Building: Process, Findings, Savings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most buildings drift. Schedules get overridden, sensors wander, valves and dampers wear, and nobody writes it down. Retro-commissioning (RCx) is the structured way to find that drift and fix it. This lesson explains what RCx is, the usual steps, the problems it finds most often, what published studies say about savings, and where a facility tech fits.

What RCx is, and what it is not

The Whole Building Design Guide (WBDG) commissioning committee page on existing building commissioning (EBCx) defines retro-commissioning as commissioning an existing building that has never been commissioned before. Recommissioning repeats the process on a building that was already commissioned, often after a change in use or ownership or when problems appear. New-building commissioning confirms the design through design reviews, functional testing, documentation and operator training, according to the FEMP Operations and Maintenance Best Practices Guide, chapter 7. EBCx differs because it happens in an operating, often occupied building, so scope and order of work change. WBDG says it starts and ends with a Current Facility Requirements document, the existing-building counterpart of the Owner's Project Requirements, and the scope can range from a basic tune-up to replacing systems.

Terms vary by author. FEMP also lists continuous commissioning (an ongoing process built into normal O&M) and value recommissioning, described as the lowest cost option, focused on the most common opportunities and usually done by in-house staff. WBDG adds monitoring-based commissioning (MBCx) and ongoing commissioning (OCx). PNNL separates RCx from re-tuning: it calls RCx an equipment-by-equipment examination to repair and recalibrate equipment, and re-tuning a data-driven look at building automation system (BAS) trends aimed at low-cost and no-cost fixes. PNNL says re-tuning does not replace RCx where equipment has substantial component problems.

The usual phases

FEMP chapter 7 gives a four-step retrocommissioning process, attributed to Haasl and Sharp (1999).

  1. Planning. Set objectives, form the team, and gather building and equipment documents.
  2. Investigation. Survey the site, run monitoring and functional tests, build a deficiency list, and estimate savings for the fixes.
  3. Implementation. Make repairs, retest and re-monitor, fine-tune, and update the savings estimates.
  4. Hand-off and integration. Document the work and write a plan for future commissioning, including test frequency, reporting and budget.

WBDG splits the same work into five phases: planning, initial assessment (a walk-through, staff and occupant interviews, work-order review), investigation, implementation and hand-off, with ongoing commissioning sometimes added as a sixth. The University of Illinois SEDAC page describes a similar path: utility bill analysis and document review, then assessment with the BAS, data loggers or handheld instruments, then costs and savings for each measure. It says the building owner decides what to implement and who does the work, and the provider verifies results afterward. Our trend log lesson covers the data side of investigation, and our functional testing lesson covers the test side.

Common findings, as the sources list them

FEMP chapter 7 lists the most frequently cited opportunities, drawing on several earlier studies: adjusting reset, setback and setpoint temperatures and recalibrating sensors; optimizing staging and sequencing of boilers, chillers and air handlers; repairing dampers and economizers; changing controls so equipment does not run 24 hours a day without need; eliminating simultaneous heating and cooling; rebalancing air and water distribution; and verifying controls, schedules and overrides. SEDAC lists failed sensors, actuators or valves, equipment in alarm, suboptimal run hours or settings, labeling errors, and design issues such as outdated zoning. PNNL's re-tuning page describes manual setpoint and schedule changes that stay in place, outdoor air dampers closed by hand to fight humidity, heating coils left running when freeze protection fails, and damper or valve faults. One of its clues is temperature data that does not match the damper signal.

Several of these have their own lessons here: economizer settings, duct static pressure reset, and PID tuning and hunting. The sources we read do not break findings out by how much each one saves, so we do not rank them.

What the studies say about savings and payback

Numbers differ by study, building mix and decade, so treat each as that study's result, not a promise.

  • LBNL, 643-building update (Mills): for existing buildings, median commissioning cost $0.30 per sq ft, median whole-building energy savings 16%, median simple payback 1.1 years. The same page reports over 10,000 energy-related problems found.
  • FEMP chapter 7: case studies reporting 5% to 30% improvements, and paybacks typically under 2 years. It cites an LBNL 2004 analysis of 224 buildings at a $0.27 per sq ft median cost, 15% savings and a 0.7-year payback.
  • LBNL later analysis (Crowe and others, 2020): over 1,000 existing-building projects, median cost $0.26 per sq ft, median savings 6.4%, median simple payback 1.7 years. Medians by project type were 5% for utility EBCx and 14% for EBCx outside utility programs. Their 25th to 75th percentile savings range was 3.4% to 12.4%.
  • PNNL re-tuning: typical building savings of 5% to 25%, about $0.185 per sq ft per year, and simple payback of 0.3 to 3.5 years.
  • SEDAC: median identified savings of 16% across more than 100 RCx projects, and about a 1 year average simple payback.

The gap between 16% and 6.4% is worth noticing. The sources we read do not fully explain it. The later paper covers many more projects, including utility programs whose median was lower (5%), and it spans 1984 to 2018. Also note that SEDAC's figure is identified savings, not measured savings.

Worked example

Our own arithmetic. A 100,000 sq ft building uses 2,000,000 kWh per year at $0.15 per kWh. We apply two cited studies. One assumption is ours: the studies report whole-building energy, and we apply the percent to this building's kWh as if all its energy were electric.

Case A, LBNL 643-building medians (16%, $0.30 per sq ft):

  1. Savings in kWh: 2,000,000 × 0.16 = 320,000 kWh per year.
  2. Savings in dollars: 320,000 × $0.15 = $48,000 per year.
  3. Project cost: 100,000 sq ft × $0.30 = $30,000.
  4. Simple payback: $30,000 ÷ $48,000 per year = 0.625 year, about 0.6 year (roughly 7.5 months).

Case B, Crowe and others medians (6.4%, $0.26 per sq ft):

  1. Savings in kWh: 2,000,000 × 0.064 = 128,000 kWh per year.
  2. Savings in dollars: 128,000 × $0.15 = $19,200 per year.
  3. Project cost: 100,000 × $0.26 = $26,000.
  4. Simple payback: $26,000 ÷ $19,200 = 1.35 years, about 1.4 years.

Both land under two years, but the savings differ by a factor of 2.5 (320,000 ÷ 128,000). Our paybacks differ from the studies' own medians (1.1 and 1.7 years) because each study takes medians across its own buildings, which is not the same as dividing one median cost by one median savings. Simple payback also ignores the cost of keeping the savings in place.

Keeping the savings

FEMP chapter 7 says recommissioning is a point-in-time event and that performance degradation is continuous afterward, as sensors drift and needs change. It describes continuous commissioning as the approach where savings persist, and notes PNNL sustains its own through ongoing monitoring of energy data, periodic reviews and monthly reports. LBNL's 2004 work proposed a persistence index, post-commissioning energy use in a year divided by pre-commissioning use. The 2020 Crowe paper does not report measured persistence; it says more MBCx data could show whether persistence is greater. WBDG says hand-off should include preventive maintenance requirements, training and performance tracking, and that OCx gathers data continuously, usually under a separate contract.

What a facility tech contributes

Our own reading of the sources: the people who know which override was set last winter and why a valve actuator was swapped are the people in the building every day. FEMP says value recommissioning is usually done in-house, and its PNNL case study was an in-house RCx. SEDAC lists operator training and maintenance procedures among the ways to sustain gains. Practical habits that fit: keep a log of manual overrides and remove them when done, check that BAS readings agree with a handheld meter, and review trends after every repair.

Field card: Retro-Commissioning an Existing Building

Find the drift, fix it, then watch it.

  • RCx: commissioning a building never commissioned. Recommissioning: repeating it.
  • Four steps (FEMP): planning, investigation, implementation, hand-off.
  • Commonly listed findings: schedules and overrides, setpoints and reset, sensors, dampers and economizers, simultaneous heating and cooling.
  • Cited medians: LBNL 16% and 1.1 years; later LBNL 6.4% and 1.7 years.
  • Simple payback = project cost ÷ annual savings.
  • Savings fade without monitoring and follow-up.
