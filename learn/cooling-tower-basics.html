Cooling Tower Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

A cooling tower is a big, simple idea: let some water evaporate to cool the rest. This lesson explains how towers work, why their water needs care, and why Legionella awareness is a core safety topic.

The basic idea

Air conditioning and many industrial processes produce heat that has to go somewhere. In many large buildings, a chiller moves heat into a loop of condenser water. That warm water goes to a cooling tower on the roof or beside the building, where it is spread out over fill material while air moves through. A small part of the water evaporates, and evaporation takes heat from the rest. The cooled water returns to the plant for another trip.

Main parts you will hear about

  • Fill: surfaces that spread water into thin films or droplets so more of it touches air.
  • Fans: move air through the tower.
  • Basin or sump: collects the cooled water.
  • Drift eliminators: catch droplets so less mist leaves the tower.
  • Make-up water supply: replaces water lost to evaporation and other losses.
  • Pumps and piping: circulate water between plant and tower.

Why the water needs treatment

A tower is open to the outdoors. It collects dust, pollen, leaves and living organisms. As water evaporates, dissolved minerals stay behind and get more concentrated. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that open recirculating systems commonly face four problems: corrosion, scaling, fouling and microbiological growth. Operators manage these with water treatment, filtration and periodic removal of some of the water, called blowdown, which is replaced with fresh make-up water. Treatment programs should be designed and monitored by qualified people. Never mix or handle treatment chemicals without training, safety data sheets and the protective gear they call for.

Legionella awareness

Legionella are bacteria that live naturally in water. They can grow in human-made water systems when conditions allow, including in poorly maintained cooling towers, and they can cause Legionnaires' disease, a serious pneumonia, as well as the milder Pontiac fever. According to OSHA, people can be exposed by breathing in small droplets or mist that contain the bacteria. The disease is not spread from person to person in the usual way.

The widely recommended approach is a water management program. In general terms, that means someone describes the water system, identifies where the bacteria could grow, sets and watches control measures, plans what to do if controls fail, and documents all of it. OSHA and the CDC both provide background on this, linked below.

Practical safety for workers

  • Know what your site's water management program requires before working on or near a tower.
  • Ask whether respiratory protection is needed for tasks that create mist, such as cleaning or spraying. Do not assume. Follow your employer's hazard assessment.
  • Report standing water, slime, odd smells or visible biological growth.
  • Climbing, ladders, fans and confined basins are hazards in themselves. Use proper fall protection, lockout and confined space procedures.
  • Cold weather brings ice hazards and freezing risks, so follow site procedures.

How towers are rated

Tower capacity is often quoted at a standard rating condition: 3 gpm of water per ton, cooled from 95 °F to 85 °F with 78 °F wet-bulb air. That is a 10 °F range (water in minus water out) and a 7 °F approach (water out minus wet bulb). One trade article notes that a tower ton is 15,000 Btu/hr, against 12,000 for a chiller ton, because the condenser also rejects the heat of compression. Real plants run at other conditions, so treat the rating as a yardstick.

Operators also talk about cycles of concentration, how many times the make-up water’s minerals have been concentrated. A U.S. DOE best-practice guide says many systems run at about two to four cycles and six or more may be possible, depending on make-up water quality and the treatment program. Its example is that going from three to six cycles cuts make-up water by about 20 percent and blowdown by about 50 percent. (A simple mass balance with no drift, blowdown equals evaporation divided by cycles minus one, gives a 60 percent cut from three to six cycles, so the exact figure depends on the assumptions; use the DOE guide's number as an example, not a rule.) Make-up equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift. For a feel for the size of evaporation, Baltimore Aircoil’s formula is flow times range times 0.001, which works out to about 1 percent of the circulating flow for every 10 °F of range (a rule of thumb of about 0.03 gpm per ton). Check your tower maker’s manual for exact figures.

Why trends matter

A tower that is dirty, scaled or poorly maintained rejects heat less well. That makes the chiller work harder and use more energy. Keeping logs of water quality, temperatures and inspections lets staff notice slow changes before they become failures or health concerns.

Field card: Cooling Tower Basics

A tower cools by evaporation and needs clean, managed water.

  • Towers cool water by letting a little of it evaporate.
  • Open water collects dirt and minerals; treatment is required.
  • Legionella can grow in poorly managed systems; mist is the exposure route.
  • A written water management program is the standard approach.
  • Follow site rules for fall, lockout, chemical and respiratory safety.
