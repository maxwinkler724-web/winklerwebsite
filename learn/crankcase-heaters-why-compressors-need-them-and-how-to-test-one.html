Crankcase Heaters: Why Compressors Need Them and How to Test One

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A compressor that sits idle can fill itself with liquid refrigerant, and the first start afterward can wash the oil off the bearings. A crankcase heater is a small, cheap part that prevents that. This lesson covers why it works, the three common styles, what manufacturers say about when you need one and how long to power it before a first start, and how to test one with a meter.

Check the unit's own manual

Check the unit's own manual. Warm-up times, charge limits and heater ratings differ by maker and by model. This page quotes several sources to show the pattern, but the data plate and installation manual on the job in front of you govern.

Why refrigerant ends up in the crankcase

When a system stops, refrigerant moves toward the coldest, lowest-pressure spot. An ACHR News article explains that the crankcase often sits at lower pressure than the evaporator because of the oil it holds, and that cold ambient air makes this worse. Refrigerant condenses, dissolves into the oil, and on long off-cycles liquid can settle under the oil because it is heavier. Goodman's long-line-set bulletin makes a related point: a long line set raises the charge the system needs, which makes off-cycle migration more likely.

Why it matters at startup: Copeland's technical information on scroll heaters says refrigerant in the oil dilutes it, and that the first start stresses the bearing surfaces. The HVAC trade training article we read describes the result as oil that foams at startup, washes bearings, and can lead to slugging that breaks valves, pistons or scroll sets. Trane's support page adds that oil temperature itself is not the point. The point is that warm oil drives off refrigerant so the bearings get oil rather than a refrigerant mix. See Compressor Basics and Failure Modes for floodback and flooded starts, and Refrigerant Oils for how oil and refrigerant mix.

How crankcase heaters work

ACHR News describes the goal as holding the oil at a temperature higher than the coldest part of the system, which raises crankcase pressure slightly so refrigerant boils off and returns toward the suction line. The common styles, from the trade article and the maker literature we read:

  • Belly band (wrap-around or belt). A resistance element in a band strapped around the lower shell. Danfoss's instructions describe belt heaters mounted low on the shell where oil collects, and say that belt heaters are not self-regulating: control must energize them once the compressor has switched off. Copeland's wrap-around listings include 40 W at 120 V or 240 V up to about 150 W on larger models.
  • Insertion (well). A heater slid into a well in the crankcase to warm the oil directly. Copeland lists insertion heaters of 100 W and 200 W and says certain part numbers must be used with specific wells.
  • Self-regulating PTC. A distributor listing for a PTC crankcase heater describes a self-regulating device that draws more current as the compressor cools and only enough electricity to hold temperature. Because its resistance changes with temperature, a single ohm reading is not a pass or fail by itself (more below).

When makers require one

  • Charge limits. Copeland's scroll heater document says a crankcase heater is not required when the system charge does not exceed the limits in its Table 1, but always required for its ZO/ZOD CO2 compressors. In Copeland's ZP K6 application guidelines, three-phase compressors need a heater when system charge exceeds the compressor charge limit (for example 8 lb for ZP10-31K6), and single-phase models need one above the system limit (9.6 lb for that family), with a heater only recommended between the two limits.
  • Long line sets. Goodman's bulletin TP-106C recommends a crankcase heater, 50 watt minimum, for any long line application.
  • Cold or idle equipment. Goodman's heat pump installation instructions describe the heater as warming the crankcase to prevent liquid migration, and the ACHR article warns that shutting off outdoor-unit power over winter is a common way to defeat it.

Danfoss's instructions also say the oil in the sump should be kept 10 K above the saturated low-pressure temperature. A heater helps, but ACHR News cautions it can be overpowered near 0°F or in cold wind, will not fix slugging from liquid floodback, and calls automatic pump-down the only sure remedy. See Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves.

The warm-up instruction, in the makers' words

Goodman/Amana installation instructions for the A/GPHH3 packaged heat pump (IOG-3038A, 02/2025) say in the installer section: "If a crankcase heater is used, the unit should be energized 24 hours prior to compressor start up," and "Compressor damage may occur if this step is not followed." Trane's support page says crankcase and oil heaters "must be energized for at least 24 hours prior to starting a compressor" on its listed chiller models. Copeland's scroll heater document gives a shorter figure: the heater "must be turned on a minimum of 12 hours prior to starting the compressor" and must stay energized during off cycles. ACHR News suggests restoring outdoor-unit power 24 to 48 hours before cooling season. After an outage or a winter shutdown, see restart checks after an outage.

One more quirk: on the Goodman unit above, the heater has a thermostat that closes at 67°F and opens at 85°F, so a warm compressor on a warm day may legitimately have the heater off.

How to test one

Safety first. Resistance checks need the heater circuit dead. Open the disconnect, lock out, and verify with your meter before touching the leads; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Both Copeland documents we read warn never to apply power to a heater in free air or before it is mounted on the compressor, because it can overheat and burn out.

  1. Look. The trade article lists cracks, burn marks, frayed leads and moisture under sound blankets.
  2. Voltage. With the heater supposed to be on, check voltage at its leads. No voltage points to the wiring or control circuit.
  3. Continuity and ohms. With power off, disconnect a lead and read across the element. The trade article says a failed one reads OL (open), and gives the expected value as R = V2 / P, from the heater's rated voltage and wattage.
  4. Ground. See Checking Compressor Windings for the ground-check habit. The trade article says any reading other than OL between the heater and ground may mean a short. Danfoss specifies an insulation resistance of at least 100 megohms at 2500 V for its belt heaters and calls for an insulation test before start-up.
  5. Amps. With the heater energized, current should be about P / V.

PTC caveat. A self-regulating heater changes resistance as it warms, so compare cold and warm readings to the maker's data rather than to R = V2 / P. The maker's data governs.

Worked example

Danfoss's belt heater instructions list a 230 V, 65 W model at 814 ohms. Check it with the formula from the trade article:

  • R = V2 / P = 230 × 230 / 65 = 52,900 / 65 = 813.8 ohms. That rounds to the 814 ohms Danfoss lists, so the formula matches the maker.
  • Expected current at 230 V: I = P / V = 65 / 230 = 0.283 A. Check: 230 / 813.8 = 0.283 A.

Now a hypothetical service call. You have locked out and verified dead, and the meter reads 842 ohms across the leads; ground reads OL. Difference from 813.8: 842 − 813.8 = 28.2 ohms, and 28.2 / 813.8 = 0.035, or 3.5 percent high. The element is intact and not shorted to ground, so the heater is not the problem.

After restoring power, you measure 0.14 A. That is about half of 0.283 A. The element reads fine, so check the supply. If the heater sees 115 V instead of 230 V, I = 115 / 813.8 = 0.141 A, and power = 115 × 0.141 = 16.2 W, a quarter of 65 W. A heater fed half its rated voltage makes a quarter of its heat. Copeland says to connect heaters only to their rated voltage.

Common failures

  • Open element (OL reading) or a short to ground, from the trade article.
  • Wrong voltage or wattage, which the trade article says leaves a heater ineffective or burned out.
  • Power switched off for the season, per ACHR News.
  • Poor placement. The trade article says a band over welds or seams can cause early failure. Danfoss and Copeland both say to mount it low on the shell.
  • Control problems: no voltage at the leads, or a heater thermostat that is open. Adding a hard start kit to a scroll that stalls from liquid migration is not recommended, per the trade article.

Field card: Crankcase Heaters

Keep the sump warmer than the coldest point.

  • Off cycle: refrigerant migrates to the coldest spot and dilutes the oil.
  • Styles: belly band, insertion well, self-regulating PTC.
  • Makers' warm-up: Goodman 24 hours, Trane 24 hours, Copeland scroll 12 hours. Use the unit's manual.
  • Long lines: Goodman recommends at least 50 W.
  • Test dead: lockout, verify, then read ohms. Expected R = V2 / P; amps = P / V.
  • Example: 230 V, 65 W is 813.8 ohms and 0.283 A.
  • PTC resistance changes with temperature; use maker data.
  • Never power a heater in free air.
