Hydraulic Separators vs Closely Spaced Tees: Flow Cases and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-10-26

A hydraulic separator and a pair of closely spaced tees do the same job: they let the boiler pump and the system pump each move their own water without fighting. What differs is what happens inside when the two flows do not match. That is where supply temperature gets lost, and it is the subject of this lesson.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. Sizing and sensor rules differ by boiler and separator model. This page is general education built from the trade articles and maker documents listed below. Follow the instructions that ship with the equipment you are working on.

What hydraulic separation means

If you already know the pump-and-loop layout, see Primary-Secondary Piping Basics. Here we stay with the idea behind it. A trade article on the subject describes hydraulic separation as keeping the pressure drop of the piping shared by all circuits as low as possible, so one circulator does not disturb the others. A separator or a tee pair is simply a short, nearly resistance-free piece of piping that two or more circulators share. Because almost no pressure difference exists across it, the boiler pump cannot push water through the system, and the system pump cannot pull water through the boiler. Each pump sees only its own loop.

That matters most on modern boilers with compact heat exchangers, which have higher resistance and a required flow range. One maker bulletin requires separation on its condensing boilers and warns that without it the heating system can see high pressure losses and low flow. A trade article also points out that an old system often ran one pump for everything, so a retrofit may need a new boiler pump sized to the boiler's own flow requirement.

Separator or tees

Closely spaced tees are cheap and easy to insulate, but a maker comparison notes they are not always cheaper to install, and they still need separate air and dirt devices. A hydraulic separator is a larger vessel that adds those jobs. A trade article says coalescing media in the upper part separates microbubbles, and media in the lower part catches dirt that settles into the bottom and is flushed through a drain valve. See Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents and Hydronic Dirt Separators and Magnetic Filters for those functions.

Sizing rules of thumb

  • Tees: a trade article says to space them four pipe diameters apart, center to center (4 inches for 1 inch pipe), and warns that wider spacing causes unwanted flow in the secondary loop. A UK maker bulletin gives 150 mm and adds that the pipe between the tees can go up one size.
  • Separator body: one trade article says the barrel is typically 2.5 to 3 times the connected pipe diameter; a maker page says 3 to 4 times. Treat that as a range and use the maker's flow rating.
  • By flow, not pipe size: a trade article says to size by flow using the higher of the boiler-side or system-side flow, and that a larger body is not a problem while an undersized one is.
  • Low velocity inside: a UK maker says header flow should stay under 0.5 m/s (about 1.6 ft/s, our own conversion). A trade article uses 2 ft/s as the ceiling for the short, fat headers it describes.

The three flow cases

Call the flow through the boiler loop B and the flow through the system loop S. The separator only has to make up the difference. The cases below use our own energy-balance reasoning (flows follow mass balance, temperatures follow mixing), checked against a UK maker's statement that primary flow should be equal to or greater than secondary, or secondary water flows back into the header and mixes with hot primary water.

  1. B greater than S. The extra boiler water cannot enter the system, so it turns down through the separator and rejoins the boiler return. The system gets full boiler supply temperature. The cost is a warmer boiler return, because hot supply water is recirculating into it. For a condensing boiler that works against return temperature and condensing.
  2. B equal to S. Nothing moves vertically in the barrel. System supply equals boiler supply and boiler return equals system return. This is the ideal match, but it is rare in practice and does not hold when zones open and close.
  3. B less than S. The system wants more water than the boiler sends, so the shortfall is drawn from the system return and mixes into the supply. System supply temperature falls below boiler outlet temperature. The boiler return stays equal to the system return, so condensing is helped.

This explains why the first rule is to keep boiler flow at least equal to the largest likely system flow, and why a boiler pump that is too small looks like a "cold supply" problem.

Worked example: system flow exceeds boiler flow

All numbers here are our own assumptions for illustration, not field data.

  • Boiler flow B = 10 gpm, boiler outlet 180 F.
  • System flow S = 15 gpm, system return 150 F.
  • Water factor: about 500 Btu/h per gpm per degree F (8.33 lb/gal times 60 min/h, rounded; our own).

Step 1. Shortfall = S - B = 15 - 10 = 5 gpm, drawn from the system return side.

Step 2. Mixed supply temperature = (10 x 180 + 5 x 150) / 15 = (1,800 + 750) / 15 = 2,550 / 15 = 170 F.

Step 3. Boiler return = system return = 150 F, so the boiler delta T is 180 - 150 = 30 F.

Check: boiler heat = 10 x 500 x 30 = 150,000 Btu/h. System heat = 15 x 500 x (170 - 150) = 150,000 Btu/h. The two match.

The emitters see 170 F, not 180 F. If a controller is reading the boiler outlet, it will think the system is hotter than it is.

For contrast, reverse the flows with the same load: B = 15 gpm, S = 10 gpm. The system receives 180 F, and the boiler return is (10 x 150 + 5 x 180) / 15 = 160 F. The supply is higher, but the return is 10 F warmer than the system return.

Where the system supply sensor goes

A trade article describes a boiler staging control whose supply sensor sat upstream of the closely spaced tees. With the boilers off, it still read warm water from the injection loop, so the boilers waited about an hour to fire. The fix was to move the boiler-control sensor to the hot riser and the mixed-supply sensor to downstream of the distribution circulator. A boiler maker's manual puts its system supply sensor in the common system loop after the outlet tee from the heater. The pattern: the system supply sensor reads the water that actually goes to the emitters, on the system side of the separation point. See Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics for how mixed supply is controlled.

Common mistakes

  • Tees spaced too far apart or too little pipe straight run. Wide spacing lets flow wander into the secondary loop. A UK maker also asks for straight pipe of 8 pipe diameters upstream and downstream of the tee connection.
  • Sizing the separator by pipe size. Size by flow, then check the maker's rating.
  • Separator but no boiler pump. A separator is not a pump. One UK maker says that if the boiler has no integral pump, one must be added; the system loop needs its own pump too.
  • Supply and return crossed. A trade article says reversed connections let hot and cold water mix in the barrel, lowering emitter supply and raising boiler return, and that supply should enter the top and return the bottom.
  • Fill valve or expansion tank on the drain. Debris settles on the tank diaphragm and the fill valve can stir it up. Connect them at the supply or return side instead.
  • Boiler flow below system flow with no one watching. This is the mixing case above, and it often shows up as "the boiler is fine but the radiators are lukewarm."

Field card: Hydraulic Separators and Closely Spaced Tees

Match the boiler flow to the system and read the supply after the mix.

  • Separation means the shared piping has almost no pressure drop, so each pump sees only its own loop.
  • Tees: about four pipe diameters center to center. Separator: size by flow, not by pipe.
  • B greater than S: full supply temperature, warmer boiler return.
  • B equal to S: ideal, no vertical barrel flow.
  • B less than S: system return mixes in, supply falls below boiler outlet.
  • Example: (10 x 180 + 5 x 150) / 15 = 170 F (assumed values).
  • Supply on top, return on the bottom. Pump away from the separator.
