What Happens on HVAC Replacement Day: Prepare, Startup Checks, Paperwork

Last updated: 2026-10-30

Replacement day is mostly noise, strangers in your yard and a long list of steps you never see. This lesson walks through a typical day for a central air conditioner, heat pump or furnace so you know what is happening, what to prepare, which readings you can ask about, and which papers to keep.

How to read this page

Crews differ in order and method, and the equipment maker's installation manual always has the final say. Where a number comes from a public source, we name it. Where we describe ordinary practice without a source, we call it our own description. Nothing here replaces the manual, local rules or your written agreement.

Before the crew arrives

These are our own practical suggestions, not code requirements.

  • Clear a path. Move items from the route between the door, the indoor unit, the outdoor pad and the driveway. Clear the area around the furnace or air handler, and the attic or crawlspace entry if the equipment is there.
  • Pets and children. Doors will be open and tools will be running. Plan a quiet room, a crate or a friend's house for pets.
  • Parking. Leave driveway or curb space for the work vehicle and for the haul-away of old equipment.
  • Power and gas. The crew will switch off the breaker for the old equipment and the gas valve at the furnace. Show them where the panel and the shutoff valve are. If you ever smell gas, leave the house and call your gas utility from outside. Do not try to fix gas piping yourself.

Ask in advance whether a permit is needed locally and who pulls it. We make no claim about any state's rules here, and requirements differ by town.

The day in order

1. Recover the old refrigerant

Before anything is cut, the crew pulls the old charge into a recovery cylinder. The EPA says refrigerant in equipment that is usually dismantled on site, with central residential air conditioning named as an example, must be recovered under its servicing requirements before disposal. It does not belong in the air. If your old system is the R-22 type, see our R-22 lesson.

2. Remove the old equipment and set the new pad

The old outdoor unit, coil, furnace or air handler comes out. The new outdoor unit sits on a level pad. Ask how they check that it is level.

3. Flush or replace the line set

The two copper lines between indoor and outdoor units are the line set. A bid sheet from a federal program says new refrigerant lines should be installed rather than reusing existing ones. If a crew plans to reuse them, ask for the reason and for the maker's cleaning method, because old oil and debris can stay in the tubing.

4. Braze, then pressure test with nitrogen

Joints are brazed with a torch. Ask whether nitrogen is flowing through the lines while they braze. A national laboratory resource guide describes the next step: purge the lines with nitrogen, pressurize to the maker's recommended pressure (it cites roughly 250 to 600 psig for residential systems), valve off and hold for 30 to 60 minutes. Any drop means a leak, and joints are then checked with bubble solution or an electronic detector.

5. Evacuate to a micron target

Evacuation pulls air and moisture out of the lines and coil so only refrigerant goes in. The same guide says to pull the vacuum below 500 microns with a gauge made for the job and large, vacuum-rated hoses, close off the pump, and watch for at least 10 minutes. A pass is a rise typically under 50 microns that stays below 500. If the number climbs steadily, there is a leak or moisture left in the system.

6. Charge, wire, drain and, for furnaces, gas and combustion

The system is charged by weight or by superheat or subcooling readings, per the maker. Low-voltage control wiring and the line-voltage connection are made, and the condensate drain is run to a proper termination. A float switch in the drain line or pan shuts cooling off if water backs up; that is our own description, so ask where yours is. For a furnace or boiler, the crew checks every gas joint they touched. An Alabama extension article describes a soap-solution test at fittings: a few bubbles over a minute or two show a small leak, and many bubbles in under a minute show a large one. A federal technical brief on combustion appliances says to use a calibrated gas leak detector and to confirm any suspected leak with soap bubbles, because some pipe-joint compound can trigger the detector. The same brief says to check venting and measure carbon monoxide (CO) at the appliance.

7. Startup, cleanup and haul-away

The crew runs the system and takes the readings below. They should then remove packaging and scrap, take the old equipment, show you the thermostat and filter location, and hand over manuals. The bid sheet above says you should receive all manuals and documentation of installation procedures and records of measurements and tests, and that the contractor should show you how to replace the air filter.

Startup readings you can ask about

  • Micron reading. The final hold number from step 5.
  • Measured static pressure. Total external static pressure is the sum of the return and supply readings, compared to the maker's acceptable range (national laboratory guide). A federal commissioning checklist asks that test holes be made, marked and accessible.
  • Airflow. The same checklist passes a system when measured airflow is within plus or minus 15 percent of design airflow.
  • Superheat or subcooling. That checklist passes subcooling within 3°F of the maker's goal for systems with an expansion valve, and superheat within 5°F of the goal for fixed-orifice systems. It says to run the system 15 minutes first, and below 55°F outdoors it marks the check not applicable for a system with an expansion valve.
  • Temperature split or rise. Air conditioners are judged by the drop across the coil, furnaces by the temperature rise, which must fall inside the nameplate range. A federal research review notes that a high rise points to low airflow. We do not give a general cooling split here, because it varies with humidity and airflow; ask for the maker's chart.

If a reading falls outside its band, the next question is "what do you change, and will you measure it again?" In our own experience, low airflow is fixed before charge is adjusted, because charge readings depend on airflow. Federal lab research also reports that a 15 percent airflow reduction costs noticeable efficiency and a 20 percent undercharge does too.

Paperwork to keep

  • Model and serial numbers for the outdoor unit, indoor coil and furnace or air handler. Photograph the data plates.
  • Warranty registration. Ask who registers the equipment and whether registration affects coverage. Keep the confirmation.
  • Permit and inspection. Ask whether a permit is needed locally. If one was pulled, keep the permit and the final inspection sign-off.
  • Test records and manuals. The bid sheet says you should receive load calculations, the matched-system rating document and records of measurements and tests. Ask for the micron, static pressure and charge readings in writing.
  • Carbon monoxide alarms. CPSC says to use alarms meeting UL 2034, on every level and outside sleeping areas. The EPA calls alarms a backup, not a replacement for proper installation and maintenance.

After the day, keep up the routine in the tune-up checklist and the seasonal steps in the cold-snap checklist. For planning the decision itself, see how long systems last.

Field card: What Happens on HVAC Replacement Day: Prepare, Startup Checks, Paperwork

Ask for the numbers, not just "it works."

  • Prep: clear paths, secure pets, leave parking, show panel and gas shutoff.
  • Old charge is recovered, not vented.
  • Nitrogen pressure test, then evacuate below 500 microns with a hold under 50 microns rise (lab guide).
  • Static pressure: return plus supply, compare to the maker's range.
  • Airflow within 15 percent of design; subcooling within 3°F; superheat within 5°F of the goal.
  • Furnace: gas joint leak test, rise in nameplate range, CO reading.
  • Keep serial numbers, warranty confirmation, permit papers if any, and the test records.
