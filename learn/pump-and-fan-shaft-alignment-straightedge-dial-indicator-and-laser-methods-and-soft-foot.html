Pump and Fan Shaft Alignment: Dial Indicator, Laser and Soft Foot

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pump or fan that is driven through a coupling has two shafts that must turn as if they were one. When the motor shaft and the driven shaft do not line up, the coupling is asked to absorb the difference every revolution. This lesson explains the vocabulary, the checks that come first (soft foot and pipe strain), the main measuring methods, and a worked example with real numbers.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Alignment limits belong to the pump, coupling and motor makers. The tables below come from a federal tip sheet and a laser-tool manual, and the makers themselves say no single industry standard exists. Use the numbers to learn the scale of the problem, then follow the instructions that came with your equipment.

Safety first: lock out before touching a coupling

A coupling can turn the moment the motor is energized. OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, lays out the order: prepare, shut down, isolate every energy source, apply a lock and tag to each isolating device, relieve or restrain stored energy, and verify the machine is de-energized before work starts. Bell & Gossett's pump manual says it plainly: always disconnect and lock out power to the driver before any installation or maintenance task. Our lockout and verify-dead lesson covers the steps in detail. Rotating shafts by hand to take dial readings is done only with the power locked out.

Why alignment matters

The U.S. Department of Energy's motor tip sheet says forces from flexing a coupling and shaft can cause early bearing and seal failure, that leaning on coupling flexibility to hide a large error can fail the coupling, and that misalignment shows up as extra vibration. SKF's alignment page lists the same family of effects, adding loose or failed coupling and foundation bolts and, in its words, increased friction and energy use. DOE is more careful on energy: its sheet states that misalignment has no measurable effect on motor efficiency, though it does affect how smoothly power reaches the driven machine. So we teach alignment as a life-and-reliability job (bearings, seals, couplings, bolts, noise) and we do not promise an energy bill drop. DOE's pumping maintenance sheet also lists checking whether motor and pump alignment is within the pump's service limits as a routine preventive task.

Two kinds of error

Easy-Laser's manual defines the two. Offset (parallel misalignment) means the shaft center lines are parallel but not in one line. Angular misalignment means the center lines are not parallel. Real machines usually have both, and in both the vertical and horizontal planes, which is why a complete alignment has four numbers to correct.

Check these first: soft foot, pipe strain and heat

Soft foot means a machine foot does not sit flat on the base, so tightening the bolt bends the casing. Acoem's soft foot article gives causes such as a warped base, wrong shim thickness, dirt under a foot, dents, and jack bolt tension. It describes a feeler gauge check: with bolts loose, try a 0.005 in. gauge at three corners of each foot as a gross check; then with everything tight, loosen one foot at a time and recheck with a 0.002 in. gauge. Fix it with the fewest shims you can, clean and flat, rather than a stack. Easy-Laser's manual says to shim the foot showing the highest value, and its tool measures the same thing by loosening each foot in turn. Do this before any alignment readings, since a soft foot makes the readings change as you tighten.

Pipe strain is the second trap. The Bell & Gossett manual says suction and discharge pipes should be supported on their own hangers near the pump, and warns that coupling and bearing wear result if lines are forced into position. If the readings drift when you unbolt a flange, pipe is pulling the pump. Our isolators and flexible connectors lesson covers the connector side.

Thermal growth is the third. DOE says to recheck alignment once equipment reaches operating temperature, because growth often pulls a cold-aligned machine out, and that makers' thermal offset values can be applied during the first alignment. Easy-Laser gives a steel rule of thumb of about 0.01 mm per meter per degree C: a 1 m tall machine warming from 20 to 50 degrees C grows about 0.3 mm.

Methods, from simplest to finest

  • Straightedge and feeler gauge. Bell & Gossett describes laying a straightedge across the two coupling hubs to find parallel offset, and measuring the gap between hubs with calipers or feelers at several points around without turning the shafts to find angularity. It is quick and cheap but coarse, and fine for a first rough set before finer work.
  • Rim-and-face dial indicator. One indicator reads the rim (offset) and one reads the face (angle) of the other coupling half. Rotate both shafts together and read at the four clock positions. The manual notes you set zero, turn both halves together, and confirm the index marks stay matched.
  • Reverse dial. Our own plain description: two indicators, each reading the other shaft's rim, let you work out the offset at the coupling center without face readings, which avoids face-reading errors from shaft end float.
  • Laser systems. SKF lists minimum shaft rotation of only 40 degrees for its higher models, against 180 degrees for the simplest, and Easy-Laser's tool also has the soft foot routine and a tolerance check built in. Lasers calculate the shim values for you.

For belt-driven fans, sheave alignment is a different job; see Belt Drives and Sheave Basics.

Tolerances by rpm

DOE's table for direct-coupled shafts (values it attributes to a 2008 Ludeca source) is below. Offset is for short flexible couplings, in mils (thousandths of an inch). Angular is for spacer couplings, in mils per inch.

Easy-Laser's manual has its own general table that differs. For 1,000 to 2,000 rpm it gives offset 2.0 mils excellent and 4.0 mils acceptable, and angular 0.5 and 0.8 mils per inch. Two makers, two sets of numbers, and DOE states that no industry standard exists. Higher speed always means tighter limits.

Shims and the order of corrections

Bell & Gossett says to correct alignment by moving or shimming the motor only, to keep bolts tight while measuring, to recheck every direction after each correction because one move can disturb another, and to do a final check at operating temperature. Easy-Laser's manual shims first for the vertical values and then moves sideways for the horizontal values. DOE adds a sequence: secure the driven machine, put the coupling on it, then bring the motor to it.

Worked example: a 1,800 rpm pump and motor

All arithmetic here is Our own; the tolerances are the cited ones above.

Step 1, vertical offset. An indicator on the pump hub reads the motor hub rim, and the total indicator reading (TIR), top to bottom, is 0.012 in. Our own: the indicator sees the rim move twice the actual offset, so offset = 0.012 ÷ 2 = 0.006 in. = 6.0 mils. (This ignores indicator bracket sag; a laser has none.)

Step 2, pass or fail. DOE at 1,800 rpm: acceptable offset is 3.0 mils. Our own: 6.0 is greater than 3.0, so it fails, at twice the acceptable limit. Easy-Laser's 4.0 mils acceptable also fails. Verdict: fail. Fix the vertical offset before anything else.

Step 3, angular case. The face readings differ by 0.004 in. across a 4 in. diameter. Our own: gap per inch = 0.004 ÷ 4 = 0.001 in. per in. = 1.0 mil per inch.

Step 4, pass or fail. DOE's 1,800 rpm acceptable angular value is 1.0 mil per inch, so 1.0 sits right at the limit (acceptable, not excellent, and not much margin). Against Easy-Laser's 0.8 acceptable it fails. We would correct it.

Step 5, shim at the rear foot, similar triangles. Our own: angle grows in proportion to distance, so a 0.001 in. per in. wedge needs a rear-foot change of 0.001 × 10 in. = 0.010 in. if the rear foot is 10 in. from the coupling. A front foot 4 in. from the coupling would change 0.001 × 4 = 0.004 in. Whether you add or remove the shim depends on which side the faces open. Recheck all readings after the move.

Field faults to look for

  • Coupling bolts or rubber elements wearing fast.
  • Repeated bearing failures: see greasing intervals and amounts.
  • Seal leaks that return: seal leaks and strainer habits.
  • Readings that change as bolts tighten (soft foot) or when a flange is loosened (pipe strain).

Field card: Pump and Fan Shaft Alignment

Lock out, check feet and pipes, then measure.

  • Lock out, tag out and verify before touching the coupling.
  • Soft foot first: feeler 0.005 in. gross, 0.002 in. final check, fewest shims.
  • Check pipe strain: loosen a flange and watch the readings.
  • Offset = half the total indicator reading.
  • Angular = face difference divided by diameter (mils per inch).
  • Tolerances tighten as rpm rises; DOE 1,800 rpm: 3.0 mils offset acceptable, 1.0 mil/in. angular.
  • Shim vertical first, then move horizontally; recheck everything; check again hot.
