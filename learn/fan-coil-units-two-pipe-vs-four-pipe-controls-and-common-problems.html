Fan Coil Units: Two-Pipe vs Four-Pipe, Controls and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fan coil is the small unit in a hotel room, apartment or office perimeter that heats or cools a space with hot or chilled water. The piping layout, two-pipe or four-pipe, decides what that unit can do on any given day, and most complaint calls trace back to that choice or to a clogged drain.

Check the manual first

Check the manual first. This page is general education built from manufacturer literature and engineering articles read in 2026. Models differ. Follow the installation and service manual for the exact unit, and have only qualified people service equipment. Trane's manual for one cassette model says improperly installed, adjusted or altered equipment by an unqualified person could cause death or serious injury. Shut off and lock out power before opening a unit.

What a fan coil is

A fan coil has a water coil, a fan, a filter and a condensate drain pan, all in one cabinet. Water from a central boiler or chiller flows through the coil, and the fan moves room air across it. Trane's fan coil catalog shows the family: cabinet units, concealed units, vertical and horizontal types. Because the water does the heavy lifting, each unit needs only power, a control signal, water connections and a drain.

Two-pipe vs four-pipe

In a two-pipe system, one supply pipe and one return pipe carry hot water in winter and chilled water in summer. An engineering article by Mark Rockwood of Elara Engineering explains that central valves and equipment are adjusted to send hot or chilled water, so the whole building is in one mode at a time. A two-pipe system needs roughly half the piping and one coil for both jobs, which lowers installation and maintenance cost.

In a four-pipe system there are separate hot and chilled supply and return pipes, so each room can choose heating or cooling. Trane describes its four-pipe units as having two hydronic circuits, and notes that the heating and cooling connections sit on the same side of the unit. Hot and chilled water can also be served by a coil with separate rows for each (Trane lists options such as 2-row cooling with 1-row heating).

Changeover on a two-pipe system

Changeover is the switch of the whole loop between heating and cooling. Rockwood says it can be fully manual, manual with building automation assist, or fully automatic, and that changing over too fast can harm equipment: water that is too warm can over-pressurize a chiller, and water that is too cool can thermally shock some boilers. He also notes the system is often temporarily unable to condition spaces during the switch.

Some units also watch the water themselves. Trane's catalog describes a factory-mounted thermistor that senses the entering water temperature on changeover cooling/heating coil units, which removes the need for bleedlines. Daikin Applied's wiring schematics show "2-Pipe With Automatic Changeover" and list a changeover switch that can be manual or automatic. Which sensor or switch your building uses, and where, comes from the unit wiring diagram.

Why two-pipe buildings have "no heat" calls in spring and fall

This is the classic two-pipe complaint. Rockwood notes that on one day a sunlit side may need cooling while a shaded north side needs heat, but a two-pipe building cannot do both. The University of Iowa facilities group explains the same limit to occupants: its buildings cannot provide heating and cooling at the same time, all areas must operate in the same mode, and it switches to cooling when daytime highs are consistently between 60 and 65 F. It also expects occupants to feel a stretch of warm afternoons before the switch.

When you get a no-heat call in a two-pipe building during these weeks, first ask which mode the loop is in. If the plant is sending chilled water, the unit may be working exactly as designed. Check the mode before you take a valve or a thermostat apart. The permanent fixes (a four-pipe layout, or electric heat on a two-pipe coil, which Trane offers as an option) are design decisions for the owner and engineer.

Control valves and thermostats

Trane's catalog describes two kinds of valves. Two-position valves respond to a 24 V signal and either fully open or fully close; they come normally open or normally closed, in two-way or three-way types. Modulating valves respond to a controller signal, and analog types follow a 2 to 10 V signal. A two-way valve simply throttles flow through the coil. A three-way valve can send water through the coil or around it.

Fan speed is the other control. Trane's low-voltage wall thermostat can control hydronic cooling, heating, electric or steam heat, and automatic changeover, with continuous or thermostat-controlled fan speeds. Daikin Applied's four-pipe schematic uses a sequencing thermostat, meaning one thermostat calls for heating or cooling as needed. Always match the replacement thermostat to the valve type and voltage.

A valve you suspect is stuck is a common find. The Titus installation manual shows a lever on the valve motor to open a 2-way or 3-way valve manually, which lets you confirm water moves through the coil. Daikin Applied's troubleshooting table lists an electric control valve that is not started, and also cold water being supplied continuously after the fan has stopped. For the way delta-T reveals these problems, see Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T. For flow setup, see Hot-Water Balancing Basics.

Condensate pans, drains and overflow switches

In cooling, the coil makes water. Daikin Applied's manual calls for inspecting and cleaning the condensate drain pan monthly to avoid clogging by dirt and dust, and says the trap and piping should be free of debris. Its trap is clear plastic and removable for cleaning. Titus says periodic cleaning of drain pans is essential, especially in high humidity. First Co. asks for drain piping sloped away from the unit at least 1/8 inch per foot and checked before summer operation. Daikin says the pan is designed with a slight gradient, so do not level it; Titus says to level the unit itself for proper drainage, so read the manual for your model.

A float switch is a backstop. Trane supplies one for the auxiliary drain pan, and after a high-level trip the controller must be manually reset before normal operation. If a switch trips, find out why the drain backed up before you reset it. The same ideas appear in Water Leaking From AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain Fixes.

Filters

A dirty filter lowers airflow and performance, Daikin Applied says, and it asks for monthly checks. First Co. says to clean or replace the filter every 30 days or more often in severe conditions, and never to run the unit without a filter. Titus recommends at least every three months. Intervals vary by maker and by how dusty the building is; start with the manual and shorten it for hotels and apartments with heavy lint.

Air in the coil

Trapped air blocks flow and leaves part of the coil dead. Titus describes removing the cap of the coil air-vent valve and depressing the needle to release trapped air, and First Co. includes confirming all air has been vented in its pre-startup checks. Trane offers manual or automatic coil vents. For central removal, see Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.

Noisy fan motors

Daikin Applied's troubleshooting lists abnormal noise and vibration with two causes: a damaged motor bearing (replace the motor) and loose fan and motor screws (tighten them). First Co. says to keep wheels clean to avoid imbalance and vibration, and that a motor with oiling ports takes a few drops of oil per bearing, while a Carrier DX unit manual says its motors are permanently lubricated and external lubricant must not be used. Check the data plate before you oil anything.

Worked example: coil capacity from GPM and delta-T

This is a hypothetical unit for practice, not a real building. For water, heat moved in Btu per hour is about 500 x GPM x delta-T (the temperature difference in degrees F). The 500 comes from 8.33 pounds per gallon x 1 Btu per pound per degree F x 60 minutes. It fits plain water, not glycol, where actual fluid properties should be used (Captive-Aire's calculator page).

  • Chilled water flow: 2.0 GPM. Entering water 44 F, leaving 56 F.
  • Delta-T = 56 - 44 = 12 F.
  • Capacity = 500 x 2.0 x 12 = 12,000 Btu/h.
  • If the valve is partly stuck and the same coil returns water at only 49 F, delta-T is 5 F, and 500 x 2.0 x 5 = 5,000 Btu/h. That is a flow-and-temperature picture, not a diagnosis, but it shows why a reading across the coil is worth taking.

The Red Seal formulas sheet from Canada gives the same Btuh = 500 x gpm x delta-T relationship for water.

Field card: Fan coil units

Which mode is the loop in? Then check valve, drain, filter.

  • Two-pipe: one pair of pipes, whole building in one mode, changeover needed.
  • Four-pipe: separate hot and chilled loops, any room can heat or cool.
  • Spring and fall no-heat call on two-pipe: check the loop mode first.
  • Valves: two-position (full open or closed) or modulating (2 to 10 V analog is one type).
  • Clean the drain pan and trap; reset a tripped float switch only after finding the cause.
  • Filters monthly to quarterly per the manual; never run without one.
  • Release trapped coil air at the vent; noisy motor means bearings or loose screws.
  • Plain water only: Btu/h = 500 x GPM x delta-T.
