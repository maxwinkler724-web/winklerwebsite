Boiler Start-up, Shutdown and Layup Basics

Most boiler damage comes from going too fast, forgetting a valve position, or leaving a boiler idle without protection. A calm checklist habit prevents all three.

Before you fire

Published start-up checklists begin with the water side. Insurance guidance calls the water level the most important item to check before firing, along with float chambers and electrical connections. Confirm valve positions, that the vent is open on a steam boiler, that the fuel supply is available, and that the room is clear of people and tools. Check the low-water cutoff and trim according to your procedure, and make sure the gauge glass isolating valves are open.

Lighting off

The burner control runs its own safe sequence of proving air, purging and proving flame, described in boiler-flame-safeguards-purge-and-lockout. Your job is to not interfere with it. Fire at low rate and raise pressure or temperature gradually so metal heats evenly. Thermal shock is a real hazard, especially for cast iron. Never add cold water to a hot boiler.

Bringing steam to the system

On a steam boiler, air must leave before steam fills the space, so the vent stays open until steam appears. The main stop valve is opened slowly so the header warms and condensate drains instead of slamming through pipe. Fast opening is a classic cause of water hammer. See water-hammer-in-steam-and-hot-water.

After light-off

Test the controls after firing as your procedure requires: low-water cutoff, feed control, safety devices and the combustion safeguards. One insurer guide recommends not leaving an automatically fired boiler unattended until it has completed several firing cycles and the controls are confirmed working. Watch level, pressure, flame and noise, then log the start. The NJ DOL logbook guideline expects tests, blowdowns, abnormal events and valve positions changed from normal to be entered in the operator log.

Shutting down

  • Reduce load, then drop to low fire and stop the burner.
  • Keep the water level right while the boiler cools; do not leave it unattended with an unknown level.
  • Close the main stop when the procedure says to, and let the boiler cool before draining. Draining a hot boiler stresses the metal.
  • Record the shutdown, any problems, and what the next person must know.

Layup

Layup protects idle metal from corrosion. A published insurer guide describes two approaches. For long idle periods, dry storage is preferred: cool, clean, drain and flush, dry the surfaces, and use a desiccant or dry air. A steel boiler left empty should be posted with a clear sign that it is empty and not to be fired. For short standby, an accepted wet-storage method may be used, but the same guide says not to use wet storage where freezing can occur. Treatment chemistry and procedure should come from your water treatment supplier and the manufacturer.

Do not forget the fire side: soot and moisture corrode too, so clean it before a long idle period.

Restarting after layup

Treat a restart like a cold start plus an inspection. Remove the empty-boiler sign only after refilling, checking for leaks, and confirming that every protective device is back and working. See boiler-water-treatment-and-blowdown-basics for the water side.

Under review. The detailed New Jersey start-up and shutdown procedure, and any legal requirement for layup practice, were not confirmed; the NJ DOL guideline covers logging and test intervals, not a start-up sequence. Use your plant’s written procedure and manufacturer instructions; the sequence here is general.

Field card: Boiler Start-up, Shutdown and Layup Basics

Slow, checked and written down.

  • Check water level first; confirm valve positions and open vent on steam.
  • Raise pressure and temperature gradually; open the main stop slowly.
  • Never add cold water to a hot boiler.
  • Shut down by reducing load, burner off, controlled cooldown, no hot drain.
  • Dry layup for long idle periods; wet only by an accepted method and never where it can freeze.
