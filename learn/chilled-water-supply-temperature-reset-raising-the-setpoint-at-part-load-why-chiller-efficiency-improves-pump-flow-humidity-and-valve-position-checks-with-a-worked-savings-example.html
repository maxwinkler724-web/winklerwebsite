Chilled Water Supply Temperature Reset at Part Load

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A chiller does not need to make 44 F water on a mild day. When the coils are only half asked, a warmer supply setpoint still does the job, and the chiller burns less power making it. This lesson covers why that works, what it costs elsewhere in the plant, and what a tech checks before and after the setpoint moves.

What a reset is

The chilled water supply setpoint is the leaving water temperature the chiller controls to. Many plants hold it at a fixed value such as 44 F. A reset lets the control system move that value up when the building needs less cooling and back down when it needs more. Three triggers are common.

  • Outdoor air temperature. A national laboratory training guide for central plants gives a sample sequence in which the setpoint falls from 52 F to 42 F as outdoor air rises from 60 F to 100 F.
  • Cooling demand from valve position. The same guide says to raise the setpoint when the most open coil valve is below about 90 to 95 percent open, and to lower it when more than one valve sits at 100 percent. A university controls appendix describes a trim-and-respond method: valves that open past a threshold (95 percent in its example) send requests, many requests push the setpoint down, and quiet valves let it creep up. Its example range is 45 F to 55 F.
  • Schedule or load. Some plants simply step the setpoint by time of day or by measured plant load.

The training guide also suggests small steps of 0.5 F so the chiller does not trip, and a cap of no more than 5 F above the design value. Your site sequence and the chiller maker set the real limits.

Why efficiency improves

A chiller moves heat from the evaporator, where the water is cold, up to the condenser, where heat is rejected. A warmer leaving water temperature raises the evaporator refrigerant temperature and pressure, so the compressor lifts the refrigerant through a smaller pressure difference. Less lift means less compressor power for the same cooling. Water that is only a few degrees warmer does not change the load on the building; it changes how hard the machine works.

Published figures vary with the machine. A federal energy department fact sheet lists chilled water reset savings of about 0.5 to 0.75 percent per degree F for fixed-speed drives and 2.0 to 3.0 percent per degree F for variable-speed drives. A national laboratory best-practice guide for chilled water systems says each 1 F rise in supply temperature gives roughly 1 to 2 percent better efficiency on centrifugal chillers. Your own chiller's data matters more than either range.

The trade-offs

  • More pump flow. A warmer supply with the same return temperature shrinks the temperature difference across the coils. To carry the same load, flow must rise. On variable-flow systems the pumps speed up. The laboratory guide says the pumping penalty should be weighed against the chiller gains, and the federal fact sheet warns that resets can raise energy use elsewhere. Pump power climbs steeply with speed, so savings can shrink or vanish if the pumps run hard. See Chilled Water Pumps and Delta T.
  • Less dehumidification. Coils condense moisture only when the coil surface is colder than the air dew point. Warmer water means warmer coil surfaces and less moisture removal. The laboratory guide notes that a higher setpoint is most feasible when the air needs little dehumidifying, such as dry nights. On humid days, space humidity can drift up. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.
  • Valves open wider. Warmer water carries less cooling per gallon, so coil valves open further to hold the same discharge air temperature. If the setpoint goes too high, valves reach full open and the coil can no longer meet the load. The training guide says to lower the setpoint when the discharge air temperature is not being met.

Checks techs make

  • Coil valve positions. Look at the trend of the most open valves. If several sit near 100 percent open, the reset has gone too far or a coil is starved. If none opens past about 90 percent, there may be room to raise the setpoint.
  • Space humidity. Compare zone humidity readings before and after a reset. Check the sensor first if a reading looks odd.
  • Pump speed and differential pressure. Watch whether the pumps ramp up after a reset. Pair this with the chiller kW trend to see the real net result.
  • Chiller limits. Read the maker's allowed leaving temperature range and keep reset steps small. The federal fact sheet recommends that the maker's set points stay the safest option and that you contact the manufacturer for specific temperatures.
  • Return and supply difference. If the difference across the plant collapses, review low delta T syndrome.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. The 1.5 percent per degree figure below is our assumption, chosen inside the published ranges above; it is not a measured result for any real chiller.

  • Reset: supply setpoint rises from 44 F to 48 F, which is 48 - 44 = 4 F.
  • Assumed improvement: 1.5 percent of chiller energy per degree F.
  • Chiller energy trimmed: 4 × 1.5 = 6 percent.
  • Assumed chiller load: 200,000 kWh per year.
  • Savings: 200,000 × 0.06 = 12,000 kWh per year.

That is the gross chiller number. Subtract any added pump energy from the flow increase, and keep in mind that the reset applies only during the hours when the load is light, so a real estimate would use hours at part load rather than the whole year.

Field card: Chilled Water Supply Temperature Reset

Warmer water, less lift, but watch flow, humidity and valves.

  • Reset triggers: outdoor air, coil valve position, schedule or load.
  • Sample ranges seen in guides: 42 F to 52 F, or 45 F to 55 F.
  • Raise in small steps; keep inside the chiller maker's limits.
  • Trade-offs: more pump flow, less dehumidification, valves open wider.
  • Check: valves near 100 percent, space humidity, pump speed, chiller kW.
  • Our own example: 4 F x 1.5 percent assumed = 6 percent; 200,000 kWh x 0.06 = 12,000 kWh.
