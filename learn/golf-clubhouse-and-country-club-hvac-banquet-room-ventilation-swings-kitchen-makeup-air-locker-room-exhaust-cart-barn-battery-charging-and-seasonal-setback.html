Golf Clubhouse HVAC: Banquet Swings, Kitchen Makeup Air, Locker Exhaust and Cart Barn Charging

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A golf clubhouse looks like one building, but it is really five or six different ventilation jobs under one roof, plus a cart barn out back. The banquet room goes from empty to packed in an hour, the kitchen needs its air replaced as fast as the hood throws it out, and the lead-acid cart chargers make hydrogen. This lesson walks the building room by room.

The building at a glance

Banquet and event rooms: the big swing

Ventilation codes set outdoor air by two parts: an amount for each person and an amount for each square foot of floor. The people part is what swings. The International Mechanical Code table, as adopted in one state code and reproduced in a college handout, lists dining rooms at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 70 people per 1,000 square feet. The same tables list a dance floor at 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, and conference or meeting rooms at 5 cfm per person plus 0.06. A banquet room with a dance floor can therefore land between those rows, so ask the designer which row the plans used.

Two controls make this workable. Demand-controlled ventilation uses a CO2 sensor in the room to trim outdoor air when few people are present; the basics are in the demand-controlled ventilation lesson linked below. Many clubs also run an event schedule that starts the unit early. Pre-cooling before an event means running the banquet unit ahead of the guests so the structure is cool before the people load arrives. A few hundred guests add heat quickly, and a unit that starts cold from the first guest cannot catch up. Pre-cool with outdoor air held at minimum, or closed, until guests arrive, so you are not pulling hot humid air into a cold room.

Worked example: banquet room, full event versus quiet afternoon

Our own example, with assumed inputs. We assume a 2,400 square foot banquet room used as a dining room, with 200 guests at the event and 10 people on a quiet afternoon. Rates are the dining-room rates above (7.5 cfm per person, 0.18 cfm per square foot). This is the breathing-zone amount before any air distribution adjustment, so treat it as a first estimate.

  1. Area part, both cases: 2,400 x 0.18 = 432 cfm.
  2. Event people part: 200 x 7.5 = 1,500 cfm. Event total: 1,500 + 432 = 1,932 cfm.
  3. Afternoon people part: 10 x 7.5 = 75 cfm. Afternoon total: 75 + 432 = 507 cfm.

The event needs about 3.8 times the outdoor air of the quiet afternoon (1,932 / 507), a difference of 1,425 cfm. Note that 200 guests in 2,400 square feet is about 83 per 1,000 square feet, above the 70 default, so the designer should confirm the unit can deliver it. A fixed damper set for the event wastes energy all week; one set for the afternoon leaves the room stuffy at the event. That is the case for sensors or a schedule.

Kitchen hood and makeup air

The mechanical code table the college handout reproduces lists a commercial kitchen at 0.7 cfm per square foot of exhaust, with a note that mechanical exhaust is required and recirculation is prohibited. A Department of Energy guidance document explains the pairing: the hood throws out a large volume of air, and makeup air replaces it with outside air. Too little makeup air gives suction on kitchen doors and weak capture of cooking fumes. The same document says most sites use a dedicated makeup air unit, and that the building should sit at slightly higher pressure than outdoors, with air moving from the dining area toward the kitchen so grease and odors stay out of the dining room. That is the small arrow in the sketch.

That document also describes demand control for kitchen hoods: sensors detect cooking, and fan speeds follow. It reports site results such as a 61 percent average fan energy reduction at one restaurant and 62 percent at another site, and says those are site-specific, not a promise. At a golf club the grill is often idle between lunch and the evening, so it fits. More on hoods is in the kitchen ventilation lesson linked below.

Locker rooms and shower exhaust

The same table gives locker and dressing rooms an exhaust rate of 0.25 cfm per square foot, with no outdoor air rate, which means air is drawn in from the lobby (the transfer air arrow) and thrown out. Shower rooms are listed at 50 cfm per showerhead where the exhaust runs intermittently, or 20 cfm per showerhead when it runs continuously. Our own example: a 1,000 square foot locker area needs 1,000 x 0.25 = 250 cfm. The makeup path matters: if the exhaust fan has no way to draw air in, doors whistle and moisture stays. More is in the locker room lesson linked below.

Pro shop

The pro shop is retail. The handout table lists retail sales floors at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.12 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 15 people per 1,000 square feet. It is lightly occupied, so the area part dominates, and it often has the best natural light and the worst solar gain. Keep that in mind when you read a complaint about a hot corner.

Pool or snack-bar building, if present

If the club has a pool, the Washington State ventilation code table lists swimming pool and deck at 0.48 cfm per square foot of pool and deck area. Pool rooms need dehumidification, which is a bigger topic than ventilation alone. A snack bar with cooking follows the kitchen rules above.

Cart barn battery charging

Lead-acid cart batteries give off hydrogen while charging. A Department of Energy safety document lists hydrogen's flammable range as 4.00 to 74.2 percent by volume in air, and gives it a specific gravity of 0.0696 relative to air, so it is far lighter than air. It also advises ventilation in roof areas where hydrogen might collect. That is why the sketch puts the exhaust fan high and the inlet low.

OSHA's powered industrial truck rule, 29 CFR 1910.178(g), requires battery charging installations to be in designated areas, with facilities for adequate ventilation to disperse fumes from gassing batteries. It also says smoking is prohibited in the charging area and that precautions shall be taken against open flames, sparks and electric arcs. OSHA does not put a number on the ventilation in that paragraph, so size the fan from the battery room lesson and the charger maker data, and treat the exhaust fan motor and any controls as a spark question. Ask the electrical side whether the fan and switching suit the area. Keep the barn exhaust on a separate path from the clubhouse so hydrogen never reaches the building.

Seasonal buildings and winter setback

Many clubs close in winter. We have no cited numbers here, so treat these as our own general practice items, not rules: set back or shut down only what the owner has agreed to; keep enough heat on to protect plumbing, kitchen water lines and sprinkler piping from freezing; shut makeup air dampers tight so wind does not push cold air into the kitchen; drain or protect pool and snack-bar water lines; and write a spring startup list so the first banquet is not the first test.

Field card: Golf Clubhouse HVAC

Banquet air follows headcount; hydrogen goes up.

  • Dining rooms: 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot (model code table).
  • Dance floor: 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot.
  • Kitchen exhaust 0.7 cfm per square foot; locker rooms 0.25 cfm per square foot.
  • Showers: 50 cfm per head intermittent, 20 continuous.
  • Hood exhaust needs matching makeup air; keep the building slightly positive.
  • Cart barn: high exhaust, low inlet, no ignition sources, per OSHA 1910.178(g).
