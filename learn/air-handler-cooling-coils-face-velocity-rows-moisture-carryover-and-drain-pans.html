Air Handler Cooling Coils: Face Velocity, Rows, Carryover, Drain Pans

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chilled water cooling coil does two jobs: it cools the air and it pulls water out of it. If the air moves too fast across the coil, some of that water leaves with the air instead of running down to the drain pan. This lesson explains face velocity, why limits exist, how rows and fin spacing change the picture, and what to look at in the field.

What face velocity is

Face velocity is the speed of air as it meets the coil face. We get it by dividing airflow in cubic feet per minute (cfm) by the face area of the coil in square feet, which gives feet per minute (fpm). Face area is the height times the width of the finned section, not the cabinet opening. The University of Michigan design guide for air handling units makes the same point: it bases velocity on the actual coil face area with no allowance for the blank metal (safing) around the coil.

Why designers limit it

There are three reasons we can source. First, carryover. A white paper from a manufacturers' representative firm, Airreps, says carryover happens when the air is fast enough to pull condensed moisture off the trailing edge of the coil, and that it can harm downstream filters, fans and the unit itself. It adds that taller coils are more prone to it above 500 fpm, with a rule of thumb that a coil over 48 in. is tall, and it suggests stacked coils with intermediate drain pans to manage height. Second, the NIH Office of Research Facilities says proper face velocity and drainage are essential to prevent moisture carryover. Third, air-side pressure drop rises with face velocity, and the Airreps paper names that as the main limit on heating coils, which have no carryover issue. A higher pressure drop means more fan work.

The limits themselves differ by source, so we do not treat any one as a law:

  • HPAC Engineering (Rowland and Wendel, 2005) calls 500 fpm typical industry practice and says higher velocities are acceptable if moisture eliminators sit downstream of the coil.
  • The Airreps paper says 500 fpm is the typical maximum, sometimes as low as 400 fpm in specifications.
  • The NIH article sets a nominal 400 fpm for present design conditions and 480 fpm for future growth capacity.
  • The University of Michigan guide caps cooling coil face velocity at 450 fpm for custom and semi-custom units, and allows for future capacity.

Aerofin's installation manual ties the drain pan to velocity. At 500 fpm it says to extend the pan at least 8 in. beyond the coil base, at 525 fpm 12 in., and at 550 fpm 16 in., and it says moisture eliminators can limit carryover at higher velocities. Always use the limit in your own project specification; these are examples of what published sources say.

Rows and fin spacing

More rows and more fins per inch give more heat transfer area, but they also close up the airway. The Airreps paper compares two coils of the same size and airflow, one at 6 fins per inch and one at 14. The 6-fin coil has more free area, so its face velocity is lower, and it advises keeping fin density down so velocities do not cause carryover. It also notes that adding rows or slowing the air increases condensation, a trade designers weigh before asking for colder water. Dense fins also gather dirt faster. The Michigan guide caps cooling coils at 6 rows and 10 fins per inch and requires two coils piped in series if those limits cannot be met. The NIH article caps depth at 8 rows and 10 fins per inch so the coil can be cleaned.

Piping and circuiting

Daikin's water coil manual describes a counterflow connection: supply enters at the bottom on the leaving-air side and return leaves at the top on the entering-air side. It says this gives counterflow heat exchange and positive drainage. The Airreps paper says manufacturers design coils for counterflow, so a coil piped backwards will miss its rated capacity. Daikin says to vent air at start-up, and Aerofin says to drain from the bottom of both headers for full drainage and to put the control valve in the return piping. The Airreps paper gives about 3 ft per second as a safe water velocity to avoid laminar flow in a circuit, noting that the Reynolds number should be checked per tube. For the shut-down side, see Winterizing Chilled Water Coils.

Drain pans and traps

The NIH article describes a stainless steel pan with positive slope to a bottom drain connection. It attributes a slope of at least 10 mm per meter (1/8 in. per foot) to an ASHRAE 62 addendum, and a pan that extends downstream of the coil by at least 12 in. It says to extend the bottom drain at least 18 in. downstream and trap it, with trap height calculated using static pressure that accounts for dirty filters. The Michigan guide asks for above-floor stainless pans (plastic only below 2,000 cfm) and a detail showing trap height for every coil. On a draw-through unit the fan pulls the coil section under negative pressure, so the trap has to hold that pressure; the details are in AC Coil Condensate Traps. Why the airflow also changes dehumidification is covered in Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.

Worked example: face area and face velocity

Our own: a 20,000 cfm air handler has a coil 48 in. high by 120 in. wide.

  1. Our own: face area in square inches = 48 × 120 = 5,760 sq in.
  2. Our own: divide by 144 sq in. per sq ft: 5,760 ÷ 144 = 40 sq ft.
  3. Our own: face velocity = 20,000 cfm ÷ 40 sq ft = 500 fpm.

That sits right at the HPAC "typical" 500 fpm figure but above the 450 fpm Michigan cap. Suppose a project uses that 450 fpm limit.

  1. Our own: required area = 20,000 cfm ÷ 450 fpm = 44.4 sq ft.
  2. Our own: convert to square inches: 44.4 × 144 = 6,400 sq in. (44.44 × 144 = 6,400).
  3. Our own: width = 6,400 ÷ 48 in. high = 133.3 in., about 11.1 ft.

Our own: the coil would need to be about 13 in. wider, or taller if the unit allows. Keeping 48 in. height also keeps us at the Airreps tall-coil threshold rather than past it. Check: 20,000 ÷ (48 × 133.3 ÷ 144) = 20,000 ÷ 44.4 = 450 fpm.

Cleaning and pressure drop trending

Dirt lowers free area, which raises air-side pressure drop. Where the unit has a differential pressure gauge or port across the coil, we can log the reading at known airflow each season and watch for a climb; that is our own suggestion, since the sources we read did not set a trend limit. Daikin says to clean from the leaving-air side so debris washes out of the coil, and not to let the solution dry on the coil. Aerofin says to avoid direct high-pressure blast on the fins. Coils need access for this: the NIH article requires access doors and removable coils, and the Michigan guide requires upstream and downstream access. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics for reading pressure.

Field checks

  • Face velocity: divide measured or design cfm by the face area, as above. Daikin states airflow anywhere across the coil face should not vary by more than 20 percent, so a traverse of readings is more telling than one point.
  • Carryover: the EPA HVAC inspection checklist asks, for the cooling coil, whether it is clean, whether there is water carryover, and any sign of condensation problems. Wet insulation or liner downstream, or water in the fan section, points the same way.
  • Drain pan: the same checklist asks whether pans are accessible, clean, free of standing water, leaks and visible growth, and whether drains and traps are clear and working.

Field card: Cooling Coil Face Velocity and Drain Pans

Fast air carries water; a slow, sloped, trapped pan sends it out.

  • Face velocity (fpm) = cfm ÷ face area (sq ft).
  • Published limits vary: 400, 450 and 500 fpm all appear in the sources we read. Use your spec.
  • Rows and fins per inch raise capacity and raise pressure drop and dirt capture.
  • Check pans for standing water, growth and clear traps.
  • Look for wet insulation downstream of the coil.
  • Clean from the leaving-air side; avoid high-pressure blasts.
