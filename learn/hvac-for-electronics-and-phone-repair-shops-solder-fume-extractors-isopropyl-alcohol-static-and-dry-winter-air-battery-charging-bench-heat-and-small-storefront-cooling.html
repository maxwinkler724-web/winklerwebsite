HVAC for Electronics and Phone Repair Shops: Solder Fume, Static, Batteries and Bench Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A phone and electronics repair shop looks gentle on the load sheet: a few benches, a sales counter, and shelves of small parts. In practice it mixes hot soldering irons, solvent wipes, charging batteries and customers walking in the front door. This lesson covers what the HVAC has to handle in a small storefront with a back bench.

Solder fume and why capture at the bench matters

Most repair benches use solder with a rosin flux core. When the flux heats, it gives off smoke. The federal workplace safety agency's chemical data page for rosin core solder pyrolysis products says no federal permissible exposure limit has been established, carries a potential carcinogen note for the products in the presence of certain aldehydes, and flags skin and respiratory sensitization. It advises reducing exposure to the lowest feasible concentration. We read that as a plain rule for shop design: do not rely on the room air to dilute the smoke. Take it off the bench.

The same agency's technical manual explains why. Local exhaust captures a contaminant at or near its source before it spreads, and it is the better choice for higher-hazard emissions. General dilution fits low-hazard materials. The manual says a hood should sit close to the source, within about 1.5 duct diameters, because capture speed falls off fast with distance. Its example is a 6-inch duct, where the source should be no more than 9 inches away, with capture velocity of at least 50 feet per minute. It also warns that strong cross-drafts can cut a hood's effectiveness by 75 percent. That is a storefront problem: a front door that opens every few minutes and a supply diffuser aimed at the bench can pull the plume away from a small extractor.

Two types of bench extractors exist. A recirculating unit filters the air and returns it to the room. A ducted unit sends it outdoors and creates a makeup air need. A filter-type unit only works while its filter is fresh, so the shop needs a change schedule and the technician needs to know when it is overdue. Cleaning solvents

Isopropyl alcohol is the usual board and screen cleaner. The federal chemical data page lists an 8-hour permissible exposure limit of 400 ppm and a flash point of 53 degrees F, with explosive limits of 2.0 to 12.7 percent. A flash point that low means the liquid gives off enough vapor to ignite at ordinary room temperature, so the practical rules are small containers, lids closed, no open flame or hot work near wipes, and exhaust that sweeps vapor away from the bench. A spray bottle used all day adds vapor to the room even when no single task smells strong. If a shop adds ultrasonic cleaning or bulk solvent, that deserves its own review.

Static control and very dry winter air

Static can damage electronic parts, and heated buildings in winter can be very dry. A university research abstract on humidity and static in equipment rooms says low humidity allows and enhances the buildup of electrostatic charge on isolated conductors and makes charge easier to retain, while higher humidity may reduce discharge events but raises energy cost. That page gives no safe humidity number, so we do not give one here. General shop practice is to control static at the bench with grounded mats, wrist straps and handling habits, and to treat humidity as a supporting measure. Before adding a humidifier, remember that moisture can condense on cold surfaces and wet air can hurt the rest of the building, so a humidifier needs a controlled setpoint and a drain plan. Lithium batteries: charging, swelling and storage

Repair shops handle many lithium-ion cells, and swollen phone batteries are routine. The federal workplace safety agency's lithium-ion battery publication says to follow the manufacturer's instructions for storage, use, charging and maintenance, and lists improper charging, mechanical damage and excess heat or cold as failure causes. It describes thermal runaway as a chain reaction in which one failing cell heats its neighbors, and names a rise in battery temperature and venting of gas, vapor or smoke as warning signs. It advises storing batteries in dry, cool locations and limiting the quantity stored. A federal fire agency's consumer page says not to charge lithium-ion batteries below 32 degrees F or above 105 degrees F, to store them at room temperature when possible, to keep them out of direct sunlight, and to stop using a battery that shows an odor, a change in color, too much heat or a change in shape.

For HVAC, that means a cool, steady parts and charging area, not a closet above a warm ceiling or a window shelf in the sun. Our own practice note: keep swollen cells out of the room's main air path and away from the sales floor, using whatever approved container the shop's fire plan names.

One small unit, two very different spaces

Many repair shops are 800 to 1,500 square feet with one rooftop unit or one split system serving the sales counter in front and the bench area behind (those sizes are our rough description, not a sourced statistic). The two areas need different things. The front has door openings, daylight and customers. The back has steady equipment heat and fume capture. One thermostat in the front will not see the back bench getting hot. Good practice is to put the return where it can pull from the back, give the bench area its own supply, and consider a ceiling cassette or ducted ductless head for the back (see cassette and ducted mini-splits). A ducted extractor also changes the pressure balance, as the next example shows.

Our own worked example: four benches

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measured shop. Assumptions: four benches. Each bench has a computer and monitor (150 W), a soldering or rework station (200 W) and a microscope light with test chargers (100 W), so 450 W connected per bench, running at a 60 percent duty cycle. There is also a front counter computer (150 W, 100 percent duty) and a charging shelf (200 W connected, 50 percent duty). We assume all electric power ends up as heat in the room. We also assume four ducted extractors at 60 cfm each, a figure we chose for the example.

  1. Bench heat: 4 benches x 450 W x 0.60 = 1,080 W.
  2. Counter computer: 150 W x 1.00 = 150 W.
  3. Charging shelf: 200 W x 0.50 = 100 W.
  4. Total: 1,080 + 150 + 100 = 1,330 W = 1.33 kW.
  5. Convert: 1.33 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 4,538 Btu/h.
  6. Tons: 4,538 / 12,000 = 0.38 ton from equipment alone.
  7. Exhaust: 4 x 60 cfm = 240 cfm. Makeup air should be close to that, about 240 cfm, or the room goes negative and the extractors move less air.
  8. Makeup air sensible load in summer, using the usual 1.08 factor (our own simple method) and an assumed 20 degrees F difference: 1.08 x 240 cfm x 20 = 5,184 Btu/h.
  9. Sum: 4,538 + 5,184 = 9,722 Btu/h, about 0.81 ton, before people, lights, walls, windows and door traffic.

The surprise is that bringing in makeup air for 240 cfm of exhaust adds more load than all the bench electronics. In winter, the same 240 cfm with an assumed 50 degree F difference needs 1.08 x 240 x 50 = 12,960 Btu/h of heat. Recirculating extractors avoid that load but need filter care. A real shop still needs a proper load calculation by a qualified designer.

Filter loading and the parts room

Repair shops load filters fast: paper, packaging, lint and cardboard dust, plus solder smoke residue near the bench. A loaded filter cuts airflow, so the supply gets weaker exactly when bench heat and door traffic peak. Check filters often at first and set the change interval from what you see, not the calendar. A pressure gauge or a visual check against light helps; see filter racks and bypass gaps.

A small parts room has little internal heat, so the risks are a warm location (shared wall with a mechanical space or the roof), summer humidity and the stored batteries. The simplest fix is to give it a supply path from the main system or a small ductless head set to a mild cool setpoint, with a door that stays closed. Keep the lithium stock on a low shelf away from heat and sun, in line with the storage advice above. Field card: Repair shop HVAC

Capture fume at the bench, keep batteries cool, balance the makeup air.

  • Rosin flux smoke: capture close to the source; keep the hood within about 1.5 duct diameters.
  • Cross-drafts from doors and diffusers can ruin capture.
  • Isopropyl alcohol: 400 ppm federal 8-hour limit, flash point 53 degrees F. Keep lids on.
  • Dry winter air raises static risk; bench grounding comes first.
  • Lithium cells: do not charge below 32 or above 105 degrees F; store cool and out of sun.
  • Our example: 4,538 Btu/h of equipment heat; 240 cfm exhaust needs about 240 cfm makeup.
