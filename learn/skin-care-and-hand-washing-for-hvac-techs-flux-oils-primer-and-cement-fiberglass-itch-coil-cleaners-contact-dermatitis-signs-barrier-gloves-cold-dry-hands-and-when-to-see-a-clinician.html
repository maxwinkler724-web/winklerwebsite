Skin Care and Hand Washing for HVAC Techs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Your hands are your most-used tool, and they touch flux, oils, cement, insulation fibers and cleaners all day. Most skin trouble in the trades is slow: dry, then cracked, then sore. This lesson covers the habits that keep hands working, using federal workplace safety and occupational health guidance.

Why skin gets its own lesson

The federal occupational health institute says the skin's outer layer is the main barrier between a chemical and your body, and that how much gets through depends on skin condition, body site, the chemical, its concentration, how long it stays on and how much skin is covered. It also says contact dermatitis, which is inflammation caused by contact with a chemical, makes up roughly 90 to 95 percent of occupational skin disease cases in the United States, citing research. So hand care is not fussiness. It is how you avoid the most common work skin problem.

What touches an HVAC tech's hands

  • Brazing flux. Paste on copper joints gets on fingers while you prep and after you quench. See brazing alloys, flux and joint prep for the joint itself.
  • Cutting oil and refrigerant oil. Thread cutting, drilling and opening a compressor or line set leave oil on skin and sleeves.
  • PVC primer and cement. Drain and condensate work means solvent-based liquids on fingertips.
  • Fiberglass and insulation fibers. Duct liner, wrap and board leave fibers that can itch.
  • Coil cleaners. Cleaners may be strong acids or bases, and spray or runoff reaches wrists and forearms.
  • Water and soap. Even repeated wetting counts, which we cover below.

We are not going to guess how risky each product is. The safety data sheet for the exact product you hold tells you, and that is the first habit on our list.

Read the safety data sheet

The federal workplace safety agency says a hazard communication rule requires labels and safety data sheets for workers who may be exposed to hazardous chemicals, with a specified 16-section format, and that employers must train workers on them. Its brief on the sheets says Section 4 gives first-aid measures for each exposure route, including skin contact, and Section 8 covers personal protection, including the type of glove material, such as PVC or nitrile rubber gloves, and the glove's breakthrough time. Section 7 covers general hygiene such as no eating, drinking or smoking in work areas.

For a new primer, cleaner or oil, look at Sections 4, 7 and 8 before you open it. If the sheet names a glove material, match your glove to that, not to what is in your pocket. Ask your employer for the sheet if it is not in the truck binder.

Contact dermatitis: the signs

The occupational health institute describes two types. Irritant contact dermatitis is direct damage to the skin. Causes include acids, bases and prolonged exposure to mild irritants such as water, detergents or weak cleaning agents, and the damage usually stays where the skin touched it. Allergic contact dermatitis is an immune reaction in a person who has become sensitized, and it can spread beyond the contact site. Common allergen sources it lists include metals, epoxy and acrylic resins and rubber additives. The two look similar, and testing by a clinician is often needed to tell them apart.

Signs it lists: itching, pain, redness, swelling, small blisters or wheals, and dry, flaking, scaly skin that may crack. It also says severity depends on temperature or humidity and on whether skin is healthy, damaged, dry or wet. Cracked skin is easier to hurt again, so catch it early.

Washing: when and how

The federal public health agency lists key times to wash, including before and after eating. For you that means: before lunch, before you touch your phone or drink, and at the end of the job before you get in the van or go home. It says to scrub for at least 20 seconds, and that soap and water is the best way to remove germs in most situations, with sanitizer containing at least 60 percent alcohol as a backup when soap and water are not available. The page does not say sanitizer removes oil or flux, so we do not rely on it for that; on the job, a sanitizer is a stopgap, and soap and water comes next.

Wet skin is also a risk: the occupational health institute counts prolonged water and detergent exposure as an irritant. Wash, rinse well, dry thoroughly and do not scrub harder than needed.

Barrier gloves versus work gloves

The federal workplace safety agency's hand protection guide says gloves must be designed for the hazards and tasks, and groups them into leather, canvas or metal mesh; fabric and coated fabric; chemical- and liquid-resistant; and insulating rubber. Thicker material generally resists chemicals better but costs grip and dexterity. It says to check the maker's recommendations, especially for immersion.

In shop terms: leather or coated work gloves are for cuts, heat and grip, and fabric ones soak up primer, cleaner and oil. A thin chemical-resistant glove goes on for the wet task, and if you need both, wear the barrier glove under or over as the task and sheet suggest. Details on cut levels, frostbite and gloves that make a job more dangerous are in work gloves for HVAC techs; we do not repeat them here.

The same guide says to inspect gloves before each use for tears, punctures or damage, notes that a visual check can miss pinholes (one test is filling the glove with water and rolling the cuff toward the fingers), and says discolored or stiff gloves can mean wear or chemical degradation. Discard gloves with impaired protection.

Cold, dry hands in winter

Cold air and low humidity are the conditions the occupational health institute lists as affecting how severe skin reactions get, and repeated wetting adds to it. Our own practical habits: dry hands fully after washing, put on a moisturizer after the last wash of the day, and wear a lined work glove for cold work. For frostbite and warm-up breaks, see cold stress for HVAC techs. Cold hands with numbness, tingling or lost grip are covered in protecting HVAC hands and wrists.

Fiberglass itch

We did not find a federal page we could open that gives numbers for insulation fiber itch, so we keep this practical and unnumbered: read the product's sheet, cover skin where it allows, and wash the exposed area when the task ends. If an itchy rash does not clear, treat it like any other skin sign below.

When to see a clinician

The occupational health institute says the two kinds of dermatitis look alike and clinical testing is often needed. So get a clinician to look when you have a rash that keeps coming back, cracks or blisters, spreads past where the product touched, or does not settle after you stop the exposure. A chemical splash or burn is a first-aid and medical matter, and first aid for HVAC jobsite injuries covers it. Bring the product name and sheet to the visit. We cannot diagnose you, and this page is not medical advice.

Worked example (our own): a glove supply for one week

This is our own example. All numbers are our assumptions, not figures from any source.

  • One tech works 5 days a week.
  • Each day starts with 1 fresh pair of thin barrier gloves, plus 5 glove changes (after primer work, after a cleaner job, after a tear, after lunch and after a dirty crawlspace job).
  • So pairs per day = 1 + 5 = 6.
  • Hand washes per day = 9 (arrival, before lunch, after lunch, after 3 messy tasks, before the drive home, and at home).
  • We add 20 percent spare for tears.

Pairs per week = 6 x 5 = 30. Spare = 30 x 0.20 = 6. Order target = 30 + 6 = 36 pairs, or 72 single gloves. If the supplier packs 100 single gloves in a box (an assumption), that is 72 / 100 = 0.72 box, so one box covers the week with 28 gloves left over.

Washes per week = 9 x 5 = 45. At the minimum 20 seconds each, that is 45 x 20 = 900 seconds, or 15 minutes of scrubbing a week. Change the assumptions to fit your own day; the method stays the same.

A short daily routine

  1. Check the sheet for any new product.
  2. Glove for the wet task, and inspect before each use.
  3. Take gloves off before you touch your face, phone or food.
  4. Wash with soap and water for at least 20 seconds before eating and when the job ends.
  5. Dry hands, then moisturize at the end of the day.
  6. Report a rash that will not settle.

Field card: Skin Care and Hand Washing for HVAC Techs

Read the sheet, glove the wet task, wash before you eat.

  • Safety data sheet Sections 4, 7 and 8: first aid, hygiene, glove material.
  • Dermatitis signs: itch, redness, swelling, blisters, dry cracked skin.
  • Wash at least 20 seconds with soap and water, before eating and after the job.
  • Inspect gloves each time; discard torn, stiff or discolored ones.
  • Dry well, moisturize, and protect hands from cold.
  • Rash that persists, spreads or cracks: see a clinician.
