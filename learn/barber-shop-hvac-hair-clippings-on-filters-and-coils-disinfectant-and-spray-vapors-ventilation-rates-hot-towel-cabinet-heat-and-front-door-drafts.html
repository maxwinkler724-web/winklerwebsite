Barber Shop HVAC: Hair on Filters, Spray Vapors, Door Drafts

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A barber shop looks like an easy job: a storefront, a small rooftop unit or a few ductless heads, and a handful of chairs. The service calls say otherwise. Clipped hair rides the room air to the return and mats the filter, aerosol and disinfectant vapors build up between the chairs, a towel cabinet and dryers dump heat next to the customer, and the front door never stays shut. This lesson walks through each of those, then sizes the outdoor air and the equipment heat for one made-up shop.

What makes a barber shop different

Two neighboring trades overlap with this one, and we keep them apart on purpose. Nail salons are driven by vapors at the table, so they are about source capture; see nail salon and beauty shop ventilation. Pet grooming is about wet fur and humidity; see pet grooming HVAC. A barber shop is dry fine hair, light aerosol use, modest equipment heat and a customer count that swings all day.

Hair on filters, return grilles and coils

Short clippings are light enough to stay airborne for a while, then settle where the air slows down: on the return grille, on the filter face, and on the leading edge of the evaporator coil. A film of hair mixed with styling powder and spray residue acts like a blanket on a filter. The Department of Energy says a clogged filter swapped for a clean one can cut energy use by 5% to 15%, and it suggests cleaning or replacing filters about every one to two months in cooling season, more often when the air is dusty or pets shed. It also says to inspect the evaporator coil once a year and clean it as needed, because dirt reduces airflow and insulates the coil so it absorbs less heat.

For ductless heads, the national-lab building-science guide says a typical manufacturer recommendation is to clean the standard mesh filters every two weeks, and that wall-hung heads need a deep cleaning every 1 to 3 years that is generally not a do-it-yourself job. The same guide notes that wall-hung heads can collect moisture on the blower wheel in cooling mode, and that dust, mold and biofilm collect in the condensate tray. Hair on a damp blower wheel or in a dirty tray is a likely reason a ductless head in a barber shop starts to smell or throw water.

Our own starting point for a busy shop: look at the filter or mesh every month and write the date on it, then let what you find set the interval. If the return grille is low, a washable screen over it catches long strands before the filter. The pet grooming lesson covers hair on coils in a wetter setting.

Disinfectant and spray vapors

Barber work uses alcohol-based aerosols, spray disinfectants and styling products at the chair. The best public measurement we found is old and from a larger hair salon: a federal health hazard evaluation of a salon with 33 employees measured ethanol during hair spray use at 120 and 106 ppm in two short samples, roughly a tenth of the long-term criterion it used (1000 ppm), and found no single measured exposure above its criteria. Yet 13 of the 33 workers reported two or more asthma-like symptoms in the prior month, and the authors suggested the combined irritant effect of many products may not be covered by single-chemical limits. So the numbers can look small while people still complain, and general ventilation matters even when each chemical is under its limit. It also recommended keeping exhaust a little below outside air so the shop does not go negative.

The federal workplace safety agency's salon page is narrower: if air tests show formaldehyde from smoothing products over the limit, it says to install and maintain ventilation where those products are mixed and used. It gives no barber shop airflow rates. Follow the product label and safety data sheet, and ask what chemical services the shop offers; smoothing or color work moves it toward the beauty salon case.

Ventilation rates for barber shops

The ventilation standard we have in mind is ASHRAE Standard 62.1, which sets outdoor air for the breathing zone as a people part plus an area part. We could not find the standard's own barber shop row on a free government or university page, so we do not print its numbers here. Instead, the Washington State mechanical code, Section 403, publishes a ventilation table for specialty shops. It lists barber at 7.5 cfm of outdoor air per person and 0.06 cfm per square foot, with a default occupant density of 25 people per 1000 square feet, and a mechanical exhaust figure of 0.5 cfm per square foot. Beauty salons in the same table are 20 cfm per person, 0.12 cfm per square foot, with mechanical exhaust required and recirculation prohibited. The barber row carries no such footnote. Your area adopts its own code, so use your inspector's table.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own made-up shop, not a real building. All inputs below are assumptions, and the rates come from the Washington State table above.

  • Assumption: 20 ft by 50 ft, so floor area Az = 1,000 ft².
  • Assumption: four chairs, four barbers, four customers in the chairs and four people waiting, so Pz = 12 people at the busiest time.
  • Assumption: zone air distribution is perfect (Ez = 1.0), which a real calculation would check.
  • Assumption: a rooftop unit that moves 1,200 cfm (our own round number).

Step 1, people part: 7.5 cfm per person × 12 people = 90 cfm.
Step 2, area part: 0.06 cfm per ft² × 1,000 ft² = 60 cfm.
Step 3, outdoor air: 90 + 60 = 150 cfm, which is 150 ÷ 1,200 = 12.5% of the supply air.
Check with the default density instead: 25 people × 7.5 = 187.5, plus 60, gives 247.5 cfm. Know which headcount the plans use.

Exhaust column: if your local code applies the table's 0.5 cfm per ft², 0.5 × 1,000 = 500 cfm, which is 500 ÷ 150 = about 3.3 times the outdoor air. That gap would have to be made up with makeup air, or the door will provide it for you.

Equipment sensible heat (all wattages and duty cycles are our own assumptions): conversion is 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h.

  • Hot-towel cabinet: 400 W average, so 400 W.
  • Two hair dryers: 2 × 1,500 W × 0.25 duty = 750 W.
  • Four clippers: 4 × 15 W × 0.5 duty = 30 W.

Total 400 + 750 + 30 = 1,180 W × 3.412 = about 4,026 Btu/h, or 4,026 ÷ 12,000 = 0.34 ton. Steam from a cabinet is a latent load we left out. For comparison, cooling the outdoor air from an assumed 90°F to an assumed 75°F with the standard-air relation (1.08 = 60 × 0.075 × 0.24) gives 1.08 × 150 × 15 = 2,430 Btu/h. Equipment plus outdoor air is 6,456 Btu/h. This is not a full load calculation: people, lights, envelope and door infiltration are not included.

The plan view

Heat sources and the front door

The towel cabinet and dryers are small beside a summer load, but they sit at the chairs. A thermostat on the wall beside the cabinet will read hot and run the unit hard for everyone.

The door is the bigger swing. A national-lab report on entrance vestibules calls the number of people using a door per hour a key parameter for infiltration, and in its example a small office with an automatic door at a 21 ft² opening peaked around 275 cfm, nearly double our 150 cfm of required outdoor air. That is a different building, so use it only for scale. The report also describes vestibule exemptions for small entrances, so a storefront door may have no vestibule. The practical fix is pressure: if exhaust fans run harder than the outdoor air the unit brings in, the door makes up the difference. The salon evaluation above recommended keeping exhaust roughly 5% to 10% below outdoor air intake, and that same idea works here.

RTU or ductless?

A packaged rooftop unit can bring in outdoor air through its damper and filters it with its own filter, so the 150 cfm in our example is something you can set and measure. A ductless system by itself brings in no outdoor air; the national-lab guide lists the ventilation (fresh air) setup as a factor in choosing between ductless and ducted.

Comfort complaints at the chairs

  • Cold neck: a diffuser throwing straight at the chair.
  • Warm at the chair, cool at the door: thermostat reading the towel cabinet, or the door draft overpowering the supply.
  • Weak airflow: hair on the grille, filter or coil.
  • Water or musty smell from a ductless head: dirty condensate tray or damp blower wheel; see the guide cited above.

Field card: Barber Shop HVAC

Hair loads the filter, the door loads the room.

  • Filter: check monthly in a barber shop (our own starting point); DOE says every one to two months in cooling season, more if dusty.
  • Ductless mesh filters: a typical maker recommendation is every two weeks.
  • Evaporator coil: inspect yearly and clean as needed.
  • State table used here: barber 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per ft².
  • Our example: 7.5 × 12 + 0.06 × 1,000 = 150 cfm outdoor air.
  • Equipment heat, our example: 1,180 W × 3.412 = about 4,026 Btu/h.
  • Keep exhaust a little below outdoor air so the door does not feed the shop.
  • Ductless has no outdoor air on its own; plan it separately.
