Refrigerant in Crankcase Oil: Dilution, Start-Up Foaming and Oil Return

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Oil and refrigerant are not strangers in a compressor. They mix. When a unit sits off and cold, the crankcase oil can soak up refrigerant like a sponge, and the first seconds of the next start can look more like a boiling pot than a lubrication system. This lesson explains why that happens and what we can do about it.

Why refrigerant goes into the oil

Many refrigerant and oil pairs are miscible, which means they dissolve into each other instead of separating like oil and water. How much refrigerant a given amount of oil can hold depends on temperature and pressure. In general, cold oil under higher pressure holds more. A compressor that has been off for hours has a cold crankcase, and the pressure inside has settled toward the pressure of the coldest spot in the system. Those are the conditions that favor dissolved refrigerant. A federal energy department sponsored report on refrigerant and lubricant mixtures measured exactly this kind of mixture and found that viscosity drops as refrigerant dissolves into the oil [2].

The oil types themselves are covered in Compressor Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE. Here we only care about what dissolved refrigerant does to the oil you already have.

What dilution does to the oil

  • The oil gets thinner. Lower viscosity means a thinner film on bearings and cylinder walls. Thin oil protects less until the refrigerant has left the sump [2].
  • The apparent oil level rises. The refrigerant adds volume to the liquid in the crankcase, so the sight glass can show more than the oil charge you put in. Do not drain oil because the glass reads high on a cold, long-off compressor.
  • The mixture is ready to flash. Dissolved refrigerant is held in the liquid only by the pressure and temperature around it. Change those quickly and it comes out as vapor.

What happens at start-up

When the motor starts, the compressor pulls the crankcase pressure down fast. The refrigerant that was dissolved in the oil is suddenly above its boiling point at the new lower pressure, so it boils out of the oil through the whole depth of the sump. The result is foam. University researchers who filmed oil and refrigerant flow during cold starts reported severe oil foaming inside the compressor and at the discharge, and they saw a difference between cold starts and starts after only a short idle period, with no foaming after the short idle [1]. A university conference paper on shutdown and startup likewise links the sharp drop in dissolved refrigerant at a cold start to phase change and foaming [3].

Foam is a problem for two reasons. First, foam is mostly gas, so the oil pump can pick up bubbles and lose pressure instead of feeding the bearings. Second, foam travels. It can be carried out with the discharge gas, so the crankcase can end up short of oil right after a start, which is why a level that looked fine before the start may look low during it. How long the foaming lasts depends on how much refrigerant was in the oil, which brings us to the numbers.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measured value from any compressor.

  1. Assume a crankcase with an oil charge of 50 oz.
  2. Assume the cold oil has absorbed refrigerant equal to 20 percent of the oil charge by weight.
  3. Refrigerant in the sump: 50 oz x 0.20 = 10 oz.
  4. Total liquid in the crankcase before the start: 50 + 10 = 60 oz by weight. Refrigerant is then 10 / 60 = 16.7 percent of the mixture.
  5. So 10 oz of refrigerant is waiting to flash when the pressure falls on start-up.

Now assume a crankcase heater keeps the oil warm enough that it holds only 5 percent of the oil charge: 50 oz x 0.05 = 2.5 oz. That is one quarter of the first case. These percentages are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. Real solubility depends on the refrigerant, the oil and the temperature, and the maker's data for your compressor is the place to look.

How heaters, pump-down and the sight glass help

  • Crankcase heaters. A heater keeps the sump warmer than the coldest part of the system, so refrigerant tends to stay out of the oil instead of dissolving into it. See Crankcase Heaters for testing, and note that many makers ask for the heater to be energized for a set time before the first start of a season. Follow the maker's instructions.
  • Pump-down. A pump-down control closes a liquid line valve and lets the compressor pull the low side down before it shuts off. With less refrigerant left on the low side, there is less to dissolve into the oil during the off period. It does not replace a heater on every system, and it only works if the valve seals.
  • Sight-glass checks. Read the glass at the time the maker's procedure says to read it, usually when the compressor has run for a while and conditions are steady. A reading taken cold, or in the middle of start-up foam, tells you little about the true oil charge. The method is in Checking Compressor Oil Level in the Crankcase Sight Glass.

Liquid refrigerant arriving at the compressor from the outside is a separate issue from refrigerant that was dissolved in the oil all along; that topic is in Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging.

Miscibility and oil return in the evaporator

Oil leaves every compressor with the discharge gas, and it has to come back. In the evaporator and suction line, the oil travels with refrigerant vapor and with liquid. When the oil and refrigerant are miscible, oil that reaches the cold evaporator stays mixed in the liquid refrigerant and moves along with it. When the pair is only partly miscible, a lower temperature can make the mixture separate, and the oil can form a thick layer that clings to the coil and moves poorly. The federally sponsored report notes that a pair is considered miscible over a range, and that at one very low test temperature the fluid became immiscible above about 9 percent refrigerant, which stopped the measurement [2]. That is a lab temperature far below normal air conditioning use, but it shows why low evaporating temperatures are where oil return gets harder. The report treats miscibility as something that affects oil return; it does not cover compressors directly, so what we say about oil return in coils is a general summary and the maker's piping rules still apply.

For vertical lines and low load, see Suction Risers, Oil Traps and Double Suction Risers.

Quick checks for the field

  1. Oil level high on a cold, long-off unit: wait for a steady run before judging it.
  2. Heater cold or dead on a unit with a history of noisy starts: test it before blaming the compressor.
  3. Level drops right after the start but recovers: that points to foam and dilution, not a leak.
  4. Level does not recover and the system has long piping or low loads: think oil return.

Field card: Refrigerant in Crankcase Oil

Cold oil holds refrigerant, and start-up pulls it out as foam.

  • Off and cold: oil soaks up refrigerant, gets thinner, and the glass reads high.
  • At start: pressure drops, refrigerant boils out, oil foams.
  • Our own example: 50 oz oil x 20 percent = 10 oz refrigerant to flash.
  • Heaters, pump-down and correct sight-glass timing all reduce the problem.
  • Partly miscible oil can cling in a cold evaporator and return poorly.
  • Follow the maker's instructions for heater time and oil level.
