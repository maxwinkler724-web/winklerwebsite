Wet Steam and Carryover Basics

Steam that leaves the boiler carrying drops of liquid water heats less, pounds the pipes and shows up in the sight glass as a bouncing water line. Here is how to tell the terms apart and where to look.

The vocabulary

  • Dry steam: steam with little liquid in it. For heating boilers, one trade article uses 98 percent steam quality as the goal, meaning no more than about 2 percent water.
  • Wet steam: steam that carries liquid water along. That water adds no heat, so fuel is wasted.
  • Carryover: liquid water leaving the boiler with the steam.
  • Priming: water thrown into the piping in a surge.
  • Foaming: froth on the boiler water surface that traps steam bubbles and sends water out with them.

What you see

The classic clue is a water line in the gauge glass that bounces or surges. Other signs trade articles mention include water hammer, uneven heating, higher fuel bills, and a low-water cutoff or automatic feeder reacting to the swinging level. A water line that swings is not a gauge glass problem. It is a message from the boiler.

Common causes

  • Dirty water or oil. Clean water lets bubbles escape easily. Oil, grease or sediment make a film or froth that traps them. Oil can come from pipe threading during installation. Cleaning the boiler per the manufacturer's instructions is the usual fix.
  • Water chemistry. High pH (very alkaline water) promotes foaming. One article gives pH 7 to 9 as a desirable range. See Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics.
  • Water level too high. Water near the steam outlet is easy to pull out. A feeder or controller may have been raised, sometimes to cover a domestic hot water coil.
  • Near-boiler piping too small or wrong. The article stresses that in a steam system the piping is effectively part of the boiler and helps dry the steam. Undersized piping makes steam leave fast and drag water with it. A drop header is one way to improve this. See Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping.
  • Over-firing or oversizing. A boiler fired too hard, or too big for the load, can prime. When zones close under pressure the boiler is in effect oversized for what is left, and opening a zone can release a rush of steam.
  • Flame problems. A flame that touches metal can overheat one area and cause surging.

A sensible first look

Start with what is easiest and safest. Look at the water level against the manufacturer's mark. Look at the water: is it clear, oily or milky? Ask what changed recently, such as a new boiler on an old dirty system, a chemical addition, or a rebuilt piping job. One article notes that a new boiler can scour an old system and push solids back to the boiler, so temporarily wasting condensate may be needed while the source is found. Then compare the piping to the manufacturer's near-boiler drawing.

Gauge glass tricks that hide the problem

A surge column is a pipe-based arrangement that steadies the gauge glass and low-water cutoff reading. It does not fix surging, so use it only as a symptom helper.

Safety

Steam and boiler water are scalding. Do not blow down, skim or drain a hot boiler unless the manufacturer's procedure says how, and always follow lockout/tagout. Never raise the water level or defeat the low-water cutoff to quiet a problem. Water treatment chemicals have their own safety data sheets and handling rules.

Field card: Wet Steam and Carryover Basics

Dry steam heats. Wet steam hammers.

  • Wet steam carries liquid water. It heats less and pounds pipes.
  • A bouncing gauge-glass water line points to foaming, priming, dirt, oil, level or piping.
  • Check water level and water condition first, then pH.
  • Near-boiler piping must match the manufacturer's drawing.
  • Never raise the level or bypass the low-water cutoff to hide surging.
