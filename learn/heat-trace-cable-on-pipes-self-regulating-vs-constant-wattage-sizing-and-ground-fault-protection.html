Heat Trace Cable on Pipes: Sizing and Ground-Fault Protection

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Heat trace is an electric cable strapped to a pipe so it does not freeze. It looks simple, but a wrong cable, a missing layer of insulation or a skipped ground-fault device turns it into a fire or shock problem. This lesson covers how makers size it, how it is wired, and how it is checked.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Every cable family has its own tables, limits and test values. This page uses published manuals from a few makers to show how the pieces fit, and it names no favorite. The cable you are holding comes with its own instructions, and those win over anything here. Electrical code and your local inspector also apply.

Two kinds of cable

Self-regulating cable has a conductive core that passes less current as it warms. One maker's guide says its output rises as heat loss increases and falls as it decreases, along the whole length. A cold spot gets more heat and a warm spot gets less. A maker's trade guide adds that this type can be overlapped, and needs no temperature controller to keep it from cooking itself.

Constant-wattage cable makes heat from wire resistance and does not respond to the temperature around it. That same guide says it cannot be overlapped and needs a control device, or it keeps heating until it passes its maximum exposure temperature. This is why a crossed constant-wattage cable is a classic failure. Another maker's quick reference allows several crossovers for self-regulating cable but only one for power-limiting cable. Read the crossing rule for your exact cable before you wrap a valve.

How makers size watts per foot

You do not guess. A maker's chart uses three inputs: pipe size, insulation thickness and the lowest air temperature you expect. One manual says its charts are based on holding the pipe at 40°F with a 10 percent safety factor. A design guide from another maker bases its charts on fiberglass insulation, a stated wind speed and a 10 percent safety factor, and says larger pipes and colder climates may need more than one pass of cable. Plastic pipe has its own chart with different results.

As a feel for the numbers, one install manual's metal-pipe chart for 0°F air and 1/2 in insulation lists 3 W/ft for pipe from 1/2 in to 1 1/4 in, 5 W/ft for 1 1/2 in to 4 in, and 8 W/ft for 6 in to 8 in. Colder air or thinner insulation pushes you toward a higher number. The point is the method: look up your three inputs in your maker's chart.

Straight run or spiral?

It depends on the cable. One install manual says do not spiral and run the cable straight, at the 8 o'clock position for one cable and at 4 and 8 o'clock for two, with extra cable allowed for valves, elbows, unions and hangers. Other design guides cover multiple passes or loops for big pipes and short branches, and say little about spiraling. A spiral uses more cable per foot of pipe, which also eats into your circuit length. Follow the one for your product.

Why insulation must cover it

Heat trace does not warm the pipe so much as it replaces what the pipe loses. The chart assumes insulation, so bare cable on a bare pipe in a cold wind is under-sized by design. One install manual says never leave the cables exposed, to cover the pipe, cables, connections and valves, and to use a vapor barrier outdoors. Another says insulation should be closed only after the cable is complete and has passed its test, and that small pipes need insulation one size larger to fit the cable. Keep cable away from combustible surfaces and from being buried in insulation material, as that manual describes.

Thermostats and controllers

A thermostat saves energy and protects constant-wattage cable. One design guide lists two styles: ambient-sensing, which reads outdoor air and can run many circuits together, and pipe-wall sensing, which reads the pipe itself and is the more efficient choice when flowing water needs little help. If the cable load is bigger than the thermostat contact rating, a contactor is used. Some controllers have the ground-fault device built in. One install manual for a self-regulating cable simply does not mention a thermostat, so the control scheme comes from the maker, not from habit.

Circuit length, breaker size and ground-fault protection

Cold cable draws extra current when it first powers up, so the maker publishes a maximum circuit length for each cable output, voltage, breaker size and start-up temperature. One design guide stresses the limit applies to one continuous length of cable, not the sum of pieces. A longer run needs a bigger breaker with matching wire, or another circuit. Do not size from the running amps alone.

All the sources we read call for ground-fault equipment protection, with a 30 mA trip level named in the manuals. One maker's sheet says conventional breakers may not clear sustained arcing, and another says the ground-fault device is required by the National Electrical Code. One sheet cites NEC Article 427 for breaker sizing; we name it here in plain text only, so read the current code and your local rules. Test the device as the maker says, and check it during yearly inspections.

Worked example: 60 ft of 1 in pipe

Suppose a 1 in pipe run needs a 5 W/ft self-regulating cable, and we put it on a 60 ft straight run. This figure is a stated assumption. The sample chart above lists 3 W/ft for that pipe at 0°F with 1/2 in insulation, so your own chart may say otherwise. Cable ratings are quoted at a set temperature (one maker uses 50°F), so the wattage below is the nameplate figure, not what it draws on a cold night.

  1. Total watts. Our own: 60 ft × 5 W/ft = 300 W.
  2. Amps at 120 V. Our own: 300 W ÷ 120 V = 2.5 A.
  3. Amps at 240 V. Our own: 300 W ÷ 240 V = 1.25 A.
  4. Circuit length check. One install manual's table, for 5 W/ft cable started at 0°F on a 15 A breaker, allows 140 ft at 120 V and 285 ft at 240 V. Our own: 60 ft is under both. Other makers publish their own tables, and the limits differ between products. Use your maker's table for your cable.

Running amps look tiny, but the table exists because of the cold start. Higher voltage halves the current, which is why the 240 V length is about double.

Failures that come up

  • End seals. One manual says to install all end seals and the power connection before turning on power, and to protect exposed ends from moisture. A wet end or splice is a common ground-fault source.
  • Damaged jacket. A crushed, cut or overheated jacket lets water reach the conductors. Another manual says to follow lockout rules before removing insulation to work on a line.
  • Tape. One manual says no vinyl tape or wire to hold the cable; use the fiberglass or aluminum tape it names.

Checking with a megger

An insulation resistance test finds a damaged jacket before power does. One design guide recommends 2,500 Vdc (500 Vdc minimum acceptable) and says the minimum acceptable reading is 20 megohms regardless of length, tested at least three times: on the reel, after the cable and kits go on but before insulation, and after insulation but before power. A different maker's quick reference lists a target above 1000 megohms at 2,500 Vdc for its cable. These do not agree, which shows why your cable's own manual sets the pass line. For how the instrument works, see Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing.

Field card: Heat Trace Cable on Pipes

Size it from the chart, insulate it, protect it, test it.

  • Self-regulating can overlap; constant-wattage cannot and needs control.
  • Chart inputs: pipe size, insulation thickness, lowest air temperature.
  • Straight or spiral: follow your cable's manual.
  • Cover cable, kits and valves with insulation and a vapor barrier outdoors.
  • Watts = feet × W/ft. Amps = watts ÷ volts.
  • Check max circuit length for breaker size and start-up temperature.
  • 30 mA ground-fault protection named in the manuals read.
  • Megger: 20 megohms is one maker's floor; follow your cable's number.
