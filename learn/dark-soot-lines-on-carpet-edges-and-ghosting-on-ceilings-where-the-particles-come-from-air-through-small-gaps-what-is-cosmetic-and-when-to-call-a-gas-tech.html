Dark Soot Lines and Ghosting at Home: Causes, Cleanup, When to Call

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gray or black line along the carpet under a door, dark streaks around a supply register, or a ceiling that shows the shape of the framing above it: these are common service-call questions. Most of the time the cause is ordinary household soot and the stain is cosmetic. Sometimes it is a clue that a fuel-burning appliance is not venting well. This lesson helps you tell the two apart.

Safety first. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave the house and call 911. Do not stay to look for the cause. A stain alone is not an emergency, but soot at an appliance is not cosmetic. Nothing here is a repair procedure.

What the marks look like

  • Dark lines on carpet. A gray or black band along the edge of the carpet under a door, along baseboards, or where carpet meets a wall.
  • Streaks around supply registers. Dark halos or streaks on the wall or ceiling near a register where air blows out.
  • Ghosting. Faint dark outlines on a ceiling or wall that follow the framing behind the drywall.
  • Smudges near open flames. Dark patches on the wall or ceiling above candles, oil lamps and fireplaces.

Where the particles come from

The federal environmental agency lists the usual indoor sources of fine particles: outdoor air (car exhaust, smoke and road dust), cooking, candles, fireplaces, unvented space heaters, tobacco and ordinary dust. Most dark marks in a home trace back to that list, not to the furnace.

Candles are the classic case. In the agency's research-house tests of five candle types, most had low fine-particle emission rates, but two sooted heavily, with concentrations approaching about 1,000 micrograms per cubic meter when six or nine wicks were burning. Smoldering after a flame was put out produced more fine particles than several hours of normal burning. So a pillar candle trimmed and burning cleanly is a minor source, while a multi-wick candle that flickers, or one that is snuffed and left smoking, is a bigger one.

Cooking and fireplaces add particles every day. The agency suggests a range hood that vents outdoors and running it for 10 to 20 minutes after cooking. Outdoor smoke reaches the inside through leaks and open windows, which is why the agency also suggests keeping windows closed when outdoor pollution is high.

A misadjusted furnace or water heater is the less common cause, but it is the one that matters for safety. We cover that below.

Why a small gap draws a dark line

Our own plain-language explanation: air in a house is always moving. Fans, the stack effect, wind and the furnace blower push air from one place to another, and some of it takes the path of least resistance, which is often a thin gap under a door or along a baseboard. The gas passes through, but the carpet fibers and edge of the gap act like a coarse filter and hold on to the fine particles. A little at a time, over weeks, they build into a visible line. The line is a record of where the air leaks, which is why the same stain pattern tends to show up in the same places every year.

The agency's measurements of indoor particle deposition give the general picture: in a test home, particles from cooking, combustion and cleaning settled out at rates that depend on particle size, with the slowest settling for middle-sized particles (roughly 0.4 per hour) and faster loss for the smallest and the largest. Those are initial estimates, so we use them only as a reminder that particles do not stay in the air forever.

Ghosting over framing and dark streaks around registers follow the same idea in a different place: air that carries particles meets a surface and leaves them there. Registers move a lot of air past one spot. Ceilings over cold framing can look different from the rest because cooler spots are where we usually see deposits collect. We have not found a government source that measures this in homes, so treat that second reason as our working explanation.

Worked example (our own): soot mass at a door gap

Our own example with assumed inputs. These are not measurements. We want to see whether a visible line is plausible and to check the units.

  • Assumed leakage through the gap: 5 cubic feet per minute (cfm).
  • Assumed particle concentration in the room air: 20 micrograms per cubic meter (ug/m3) of soot-bearing particles.
  • Assumed running time: 12 hours a day for 30 days, so 360 hours.
  • Assumed share caught by the carpet edge: 50 percent.

Step 1, convert airflow. 1 cfm is about 1.699 cubic meters per hour (m3/h). So 5 cfm is 5 x 1.699 = 8.5 m3/h.

Step 2, mass carried per hour. 8.5 m3/h x 20 ug/m3 = 170 ug/h. The cubic meters cancel and leave micrograms per hour.

Step 3, mass over the month. 170 ug/h x 360 h = 61,200 ug, which is 61.2 milligrams (mg).

Step 4, share caught. 61,200 ug x 0.50 = 30,600 ug, or about 30.6 mg.

Step 5, spread it out. Suppose it lands in a band 0.9 m long and 5 cm wide, which is 90 cm x 5 cm = 450 square centimeters. 30.6 mg / 450 cm2 = 0.068 mg per cm2. Soot is very dark, so even a thin film shows against light carpet.

Compare with one heavy candle evening. Using the same airflow and catch share but a sooting candle at 1,000 ug/m3 for 2 hours: 8.5 x 1,000 x 2 x 0.5 = 8,500 ug, or 8.5 mg. That is about 28 percent of the 30.6 mg in the month at the assumed background level, from one evening. Real leakage, concentrations and catch rates vary a lot, so use this as a sense of scale only.

Cosmetic or combustion trouble?

Use location to decide. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's guide for responders lists sooting, and debris or soot falling from a chimney, fireplace or appliance, among the carbon monoxide clues you can see. It also lists rusting or water streaking on a vent or chimney. It treats improper burner adjustment as a hidden clue that you cannot see.

What you seeUsuallyAction
Gray line on carpet edge, baseboard or under a doorCosmetic: filtered household sootClean and look for the air leak
Smudges above candles or lampsCosmetic: candle or lamp sootChange habits, then clean
Dark streaks at registers or ghosting on ceilingsUsually cosmetic, but a new heavy pattern deserves a lookCheck filter fit; call if it appears fast
Soot or staining at the furnace, water heater draft hood or flue pipePossible combustion or venting troubleStop using the appliance and call a gas tech
Carbon monoxide alarm soundsTreat as realLeave, call 911, do not re-enter

For a carbon monoxide alarm, the Commission says to leave immediately for fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, count heads, and not go back in until responders say it is safe. If an appliance is suspected, do not use it until trained personnel have serviced it. If the alarm sounds again within 24 hours of re-entering, repeat those steps and have a qualified technician inspect all fuel-burning equipment. For more on alarms, see the related lessons below.

The Commission recommends an annual professional inspection of fuel-burning appliances, with the inspector checking chimneys and flues for blockages, corrosion and loose connections. An oil burner that puffs back is a different event, with greasy soot over many rooms at once; see the puffback lesson linked below.

Cleaning tips

  • Test first. A university extension page on smoke and soot stains says to pretest every cleaning agent on a hidden spot and to be careful with commercial products or combinations of cleaners.
  • Dry methods first. The federal environmental agency recommends vacuuming carpets weekly, a damp cloth for hard surfaces, and a vacuum with a HEPA filter. Light surface soot lifts best before it is wetted. This is our own practical note: avoid rubbing wet soot into porous paint or carpet.
  • Wood and painted trim. The extension page suggests a cloth dampened with mild sudsy water, then a clear-water wipe and prompt drying for stained wood.
  • Fix the cause. Cleaning a carpet edge without addressing a leak or a heavy soot source means the line comes back. Burn fewer or cleaner candles, use the range hood, and keep the filter seated.

When to call a gas tech

  • Soot, staining or rust streaks at the furnace, the water heater draft hood or the flue.
  • A carbon monoxide alarm that sounded and has not been explained.
  • Dark marks that appear fast, or in many rooms at once.
  • Headache, nausea or dizziness that eases when you leave the house.

The agency's guidance is to have heating systems inspected yearly and to vent combustion appliances outdoors. A technician can check burner operation, venting and draft, which a homeowner cannot judge by looking at a stain.

Field card: Dark soot lines and ghosting

Gray line at a gap is usually filtered soot. Soot at an appliance is a call.

  • Common sources: candles, cooking, fireplaces and outdoor smoke.
  • Air through a small gap leaves particles at the edge of the carpet.
  • Sooting at the furnace, water heater or flue is not cosmetic.
  • A sounding CO alarm means leave and call 911.
  • Test cleaners in a hidden spot first.
