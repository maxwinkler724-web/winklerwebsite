Setting V-Belt Tension by the Force-Deflection Method

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A belt that is too loose slips and squeals. A belt that is too tight loads the bearings on both shafts. The force-deflection method gives you a repeatable way to land between the two: measure the span, press the belt a set distance, and read the force it takes. This lesson covers that one job on fan and pump drives.

Where this fits

We assume you already know what a sheave is and how a drive is laid out. If not, start with Belt Drives and Sheave Basics. Changing sheave sizes is a different job, covered in Changing a Fan Sheave on a Belt-Driven Air Handler, and belt styles are compared in V-Belts vs Cogged and Synchronous Belts. Here we only set tension on a standard V-belt drive.

Lock out first

Belt tension is adjusted with the fan or pump off and the disconnect locked out. A fan maker's installation manual says the same: the adjustment is made with the unit off and its service disconnect locked out. The federal energy control rule in 29 CFR 1910.147 lays out the order: know the energy, shut the equipment down, operate the isolating device, apply your lock and tag, release or restrain stored energy, and verify the equipment is isolated before you start. A coasting wheel or a fan that can windmill from another fan is stored energy. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC for the full habit. Also, the federal machine guarding rule, 29 CFR 1910.219, covers guards on belts and pulleys within reach and says belts and pulleys must be kept aligned and in good repair. Put the guard back before you restart.

Step 1: Check alignment before tension

Tension cannot fix a drive that is out of line. A fan maker's engineering bulletin says to lay a straightedge across the two sheaves; when they are aligned it touches the whole face of both. A belt distributor's installation guide adds that all four contact points of the straightedge should touch, and that you should test in more than one position to catch both angular error and offset. Fix alignment first, then set tension, and look again after you tighten, because moving the motor can pull the sheaves out of line. A national laboratory report on fan system tune-ups lists three types of misalignment (vertical angle, horizontal angle and axial offset) and notes they can cause early bearing failure, vibration and noise. For a deeper look at those symptoms, see Vibration Analysis Basics for Fans and Pumps.

Step 2: Measure the span

The span is the straight length of belt between the two points where it leaves one sheave and meets the other. An extension service newsletter on fan belts describes it that way. For two sheaves of about the same size it is close to the center distance; with unequal sheaves it is a bit less. Measure the actual free length with a tape, on the tight side of the belt if the drive has one.

Step 3: Find the target deflection

The common rule is 1/64 in of deflection for each inch of span. A fan maker's installation manual states it as deflection equals belt span divided by 64, measured at the middle of the span, and the extension newsletter gives the same 1/64 in per inch. Divide your span in inches by 64 and you have the distance to press the belt.

Step 4: Read the force on a tension tester

A belt tension tester (a small spring gauge with a sliding scale) lets you press the belt at mid-span and read both distance and force. Set the deflection ring or scale to your target, press until the belt just reaches that depth, and read the force. The right force depends on the belt cross section, the smaller sheave's diameter, and the belt type, which is why the extension newsletter's example is for one specific belt and sheave size. Use the force range printed in the belt maker's table or on the tester's instructions for your belt. Check the middle belt of a multiple-belt set and compare belts to one another; they should feel alike.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, with assumed numbers. Suppose a belt-driven exhaust fan has a measured span of 30 in. We assume the belt maker's table for this belt and small sheave size gives 4.5 to 6.5 lb for a belt that has already run, and 6.5 to 9 lb for a new belt. Those two force ranges are assumptions for the example only; always use the table for your own belt.

  1. Target deflection = span / 64 = 30 / 64 = 0.469 in, which is a little under 1/2 in (about 15/32 in).
  2. Set the tester to 15/32 in and press at mid-span.
  3. New belt: the gauge should read 6.5 to 9 lb at that deflection. If it reads 4 lb, the belt is too loose; tighten the motor mount and measure again.
  4. After run-in: with the same deflection, an already-run belt should read inside the lower range, 4.5 to 6.5 lb. A reading of 5 lb is fine; do not chase the new-belt number.

New belts and the run-in recheck

New belts are set higher on purpose, because they stretch and seat in the grooves early on. The extension newsletter says belts stretch substantially in the first 24 hours and, without an automatic tensioner, should be re-tensioned to roughly 70 to 90 percent of the initial force (the newsletter's own example is a small A-section belt, so treat the percent as a pattern, not a rule for every drive). Fan makers give a shorter schedule: one installation manual calls for checks twice in the first 24 hours and again at 24 hours, and a belt distributor's guide says to recheck after a 24 to 48 hour run-in. A fan maker's bulletin says to recheck after a few days. Where those differ, follow the maker's instructions for your drive, and write the date and the reading on the equipment record.

Reading the symptoms

  • Loose: a squeal at startup or under load. A distributor's guide calls squeal an indication of belt slip, most often from low tension (contamination, misalignment or overload can also be the cause). A fan maker's bulletin says to tension the belts just enough to stop squealing at startup; a brief chirp is acceptable, a squeal that lasts seconds is not. Slip also makes heat.
  • Glazing: shiny sidewalls and cracks are signs the belt has been damaged by heat, which can come from slip, overload or high ambient temperature. Replace the belt and find the cause.
  • Too tight: a fan maker's bulletin says excess tension overloads both the belts and the bearings. A distributor's guide says it shortens both belt and bearing life. Hot or noisy bearings on a belt drive are a reason to recheck tension and alignment, not only to add grease; see Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings.

Worn or loose belts waste power, and the laboratory tune-up report suggests checking belt tension and sheave alignment about twice a year as a low-effort task. Use the deflection check as part of that routine.

Field card: Setting V-Belt Tension by the Force-Deflection Method

Lock out, align, measure, press, read, recheck.

  • Lock out and verify the drive is isolated before you touch it.
  • Straightedge across the sheaves should touch the full face of both.
  • Span = free belt length between sheaves; deflection = span / 64 (1/64 in per inch).
  • Press at mid-span with a tension tester and read the force; compare to the belt maker's table.
  • New belts run higher initial tension; recheck after the run-in period the maker gives.
  • Squeal means slip; glazing means heat damage; hot bearings can mean over-tight or misaligned.
  • Replace the guard before restart.
