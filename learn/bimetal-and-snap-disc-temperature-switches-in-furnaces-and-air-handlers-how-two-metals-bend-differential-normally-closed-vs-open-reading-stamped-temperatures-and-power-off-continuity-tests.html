Bimetal and Snap-Disc Temperature Switches in Furnaces and Air Handlers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A furnace has no brain in its simplest safety circuit, only a small metal part that bends when it gets hot. This lesson explains how two metals bend a switch open, why a domed disc jumps instead of creeping, what the stamped numbers mean, and how to test one with the power off.

Two metals, two expansion rates

Almost every solid grows a little when it warms up. The size of the growth for a given length and temperature change is set by the material's coefficient of linear expansion. A university physics text gives the relation as change in length equals the coefficient times the original length times the temperature change. The same text lists brass as expanding more than steel for the same warming, and explains that a strip made of two bonded metals curves toward the metal that expands less. A university physics site names brass and steel as a typical pair for this and notes that the same effect trips electrical breakers when excess current heats the strip.

That is the whole idea behind a bimetal switch. One end of the strip is fixed. The free end carries a contact. When the strip warms, the brass-side layer wants to be longer than the steel-side layer, they are bonded together, so the strip bows. Cool it and it straightens. In a furnace the bowing moves the contact away from its mate and breaks a circuit, or moves it into its mate and makes one.

Worked example (our own): why a tiny difference bends a strip

Our own example, with assumed inputs. Take two loose strips, one steel and one brass, each 2 inches long, and heat each by 100 F. We assume typical coefficients of about 6.5 x 10-6 per F for steel and about 10.5 x 10-6 per F for brass. These are our assumptions. As a cross-check, the university text lists steel at 12 x 10-6 per C and brass at 19 x 10-6 per C. Dividing by 1.8 gives about 6.7 and 10.6 per F, close to our figures.

  1. Steel: 6.5 x 10-6 x 2 in x 100 F = 0.00130 in.
  2. Brass: 10.5 x 10-6 x 2 in x 100 F = 0.00210 in.
  3. Difference: 0.00210 - 0.00130 = 0.00080 in, about eight ten-thousandths of an inch.

Eight ten-thousandths of an inch is invisible. But when the two layers are bonded, that mismatch cannot show up as two different lengths. The longer-wanting layer is held back and the shorter-wanting layer is pulled along, so the strip bends. The longer and thinner the strip, the more that tiny strain turns into end movement, which is why a bimetal element is a long, thin piece. Even so the motion is slow and small, which is the problem the snap disc solves.

Why a disc snaps

A creeping contact is a bad switch. It arcs, chatters and wears. A snap-disc switch uses a small, domed bimetal disc, shaped like a shallow dish. Think of the lid of a jar or a metal clicker toy. The dome is stable in one direction. As the disc warms, stress builds in the bonded layers. At a particular temperature the stress is enough to flip the dome the other way in a fraction of a second. The contact moves with a click rather than a crawl.

The flip back does not happen at the same temperature. The disc has to cool past a lower point before the dome flips again. The gap between the opening temperature and the closing temperature is the differential. It keeps the control from buzzing open and closed around one temperature. In a furnace it also means the part may stay open for a while after the heat source stops.

Where they are used

  • High limits. Mounted in the heat exchanger area or plenum, they open the burner circuit if the air or metal gets too hot. See High Limit Trips and Airflow.
  • Rollout switches. Mounted near the burner opening, they open if flame or hot gas spills out. Many are one-shot or manual reset. See Furnace Flame Rollout Switch Tripped.
  • Fan switches. Some older furnaces use a temperature-closing switch to start the blower after the plenum warms and stop it after it cools. See Combination Fan and Limit Controls on Older Furnaces.
  • Auxiliary limits on electric heat. Electric heat sections often carry their own limit discs in addition to the main one. See Electric Furnace Not Heating.
  • Blocked-vent shutoff. A federal consumer product safety test report describes a furnace with a bimetal disc thermostat near the flue outlet that shuts the furnace off when its set temperature is exceeded, and says it must be manually reset before the furnace can run again.

Normally closed versus normally open

The word "normally" means the state at normal room temperature with the part cool. A normally closed disc passes current when cool and opens on a temperature rise. High limits, rollout switches and auxiliary limits are normally closed, wired in series with the circuit they protect, so any one opening stops that circuit. A normally open disc is open when cool and closes on a temperature rise, which is how a fan switch works: the blower circuit stays open until the plenum is warm.

Whether a switch resets by itself or needs a button press is a separate question. For that, read Manual Reset vs Automatic Reset Safety Controls.

Reading the numbers on the disc

Most discs have temperatures stamped or printed on the case or label. Read them as a pair: the temperature at which the contacts change state on a rise, and the temperature at which they return on a fall. For a normally closed limit, the larger number is where it opens and the smaller is where it recloses. For a normally open fan switch, the larger number is where it closes. Sheet metal screws, a bent bracket or a moved mounting point change what temperature the disc actually sees, so replace a part with one that has the same stamped values and the same style of mounting, and follow the furnace maker's parts list. Our own illustration: a limit stamped with two numbers a few dozen degrees apart opens at the upper one and recloses near the lower one; the stamped pair on the actual part is the only source of truth.

Testing continuity with the power off

  1. Turn off power at the furnace switch or breaker and verify with a meter that it is dead. Close the gas valve if you will be near burners.
  2. Let the part cool to room temperature. A hot disc may still be open and give a false failure.
  3. Disconnect one wire from the switch, or the switch from the board, so no parallel circuit gives a false reading.
  4. Set the meter to continuity or low ohms. A good normally closed switch reads near zero ohms across its terminals. A normally open switch should read open when cool.
  5. If a normally closed switch reads open while cool, the disc has failed or it has tripped and is waiting for a manual reset. Check the label for a reset button first.
  6. Replace a failed switch with an exact match. Never jumper, tape over or bypass a limit or rollout switch. A tripped switch is a symptom to be explained; see the related lessons for airflow and burner causes.

A federal energy measure guideline from a national lab says to check the performance of the high limit and pressure safety switches after installation and to follow the manufacturer's safety-testing instructions. This lesson only covers the power-off meter test; heat-up tests belong in the maker's procedure.

Field card: Bimetal and Snap-Disc Switches

Two metals bend, the dome snaps, the gap between open and close is the differential.

  • Two bonded metals bow because they grow by different amounts; the strip curves toward the one that grows less.
  • A domed disc flips with a click at its opening temperature and flips back only after cooling to a lower one.
  • Normally closed: limits and rollout switches in series. Normally open: fan switches.
  • Read the stamped pair of temperatures and match them exactly on replacement.
  • Test cool, power off, one wire lifted: normally closed should read near zero ohms.
  • Never jumper a limit or rollout switch.
