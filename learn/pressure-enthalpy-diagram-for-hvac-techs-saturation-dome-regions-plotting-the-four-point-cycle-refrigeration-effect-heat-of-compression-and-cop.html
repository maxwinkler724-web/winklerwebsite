Pressure-Enthalpy Diagram for HVAC Techs: Reading the Dome, the Cycle and COP

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pressure-enthalpy (P-h) chart looks like a wall of curves, but it is a map of one idea: where the refrigerant is, and how much energy each trip around the system moves. This lesson shows how to read the map, plot a basic cycle, and turn two distances on the chart into capacity, compressor work and COP.

What the two axes mean

Pressure runs up the side, usually on a log scale so low and high pressures both fit. Enthalpy runs across the bottom, in Btu per pound of refrigerant. Enthalpy is the heat content of one pound of the stuff, measured from an arbitrary zero. Because the zero is arbitrary, only differences matter, and a difference in enthalpy read across the chart is a number of Btu per pound. That is the whole trick: horizontal distance is energy per pound.

If you want the plain four-component walk-through first, read the refrigeration cycle lesson. For how to measure the two temperatures on a job, see Superheat and Subcooling Basics. This lesson is about drawing those same states on a chart.

The dome and its three regions

The bell-shaped curve is the saturation dome. Its left side is saturated liquid, its right side is saturated vapor, and the two meet at the top at the critical point. Everything else on the chart is one of three regions.

  • Subcooled liquid is to the left of the dome. The liquid is colder than its boiling temperature at that pressure. Horizontal distance left of the liquid line is a measure of subcooling.
  • Mixture is under the dome. Liquid and vapor coexist, and temperature stays fixed at the saturation temperature for that pressure. Across the dome, the fraction of vapor (quality) goes from 0 on the left line to 1 on the right line.
  • Superheated vapor is to the right of the dome. The vapor is hotter than its boiling temperature at that pressure, and distance to the right of the vapor line is a measure of superheat.

A horizontal line on the chart is a constant-pressure process, and a vertical line is a constant-enthalpy process. Those two shapes cover three of the four legs of a basic cycle.

Plotting the four legs

  1. Evaporation (4 to 1). Constant pressure, moving right. The refrigerant absorbs heat, boils across the dome, then picks up a few degrees of superheat. The length of this line is the refrigeration effect.
  2. Compression (1 to 2). The line climbs up and to the right. Pressure rises and enthalpy rises, because the compressor adds work to the vapor. The horizontal length is the heat of compression.
  3. Condensing (2 to 3). Constant pressure, moving left. The hot vapor sheds its superheat, condenses across the dome, then loses a few more degrees as subcooled liquid. The length is the heat rejected.
  4. Metering (3 to 4). A straight vertical line down. The TXV or fixed orifice drops pressure without adding or removing heat, so enthalpy stays constant. This is why the line is vertical, and why some liquid flashes to vapor on the way into the dome.

A university thermodynamics note (Purdue, listed below) writes the same relations: the evaporator heat per unit mass is h1 minus h4, compressor work per unit mass is h2 minus h1, and throttling is a constant-enthalpy process. Our four-point labels match that note.

Turning distances into numbers

  • Refrigeration effect = h1 − h4 (Btu per lb). It is the cooling each pound of refrigerant delivers.
  • Heat of compression = h2 − h1 (Btu per lb). It is the work the compressor puts into each pound.
  • Heat rejected = h2 − h3, which equals the sum of the two above if there is no loss to the surroundings.
  • COP (cooling) = refrigeration effect divided by heat of compression.
  • Flow per ton = 200 Btu/min per ton divided by the refrigeration effect, in lb/min.

Worked example (our own): R-134a, one pound at a time

This is our own example. We chose the conditions, then read the properties from the NIST Chemistry WebBook for R-134a (listed below). R-134a is a long-standing, current refrigerant that is easy to find in tables; the method is identical for R-32 or any other pure fluid. The WebBook values were set to Btu/lb and psia; because its zero point is arbitrary we use only differences.

Assumptions (ours): evaporating temperature 40°F, condensing temperature 120°F, 10°F superheat at the compressor inlet, 10°F subcooling at the liquid line, and an isentropic efficiency of 0.70. The 0.70 is our own assumed figure for teaching, not a measured or published value for any compressor. Line losses and motor losses are ignored.

Properties from NIST:

  • Saturation at 40°F: 49.741 psia, liquid enthalpy 88.623, vapor enthalpy 172.59 Btu/lb.
  • Saturation at 120°F: 185.86 psia.
  • Point 1: vapor at 49.741 psia and 50°F: h = 174.77 Btu/lb, s = 0.41655 Btu/lb-R.
  • Point 3: liquid at 185.86 psia and 110°F: h = 112.45 Btu/lb.
  • Vapor at 185.86 psia: 135°F, h = 186.26, s = 0.41543; 140°F, h = 187.64, s = 0.41773.

Step by step:

  1. Point 4 has the same enthalpy as point 3: h4 = 112.45 Btu/lb.
  2. Refrigeration effect = 174.77 − 112.45 = 62.32 Btu/lb.
  3. Ideal (isentropic) compression keeps entropy at 0.41655. We interpolate between the two vapor rows: (0.41655 − 0.41543) / (0.41773 − 0.41543) = 0.487, so h2s = 186.26 + 0.487 × (187.64 − 186.26) = 186.93 Btu/lb. The interpolation is ours.
  4. Ideal work = 186.93 − 174.77 = 12.16 Btu/lb.
  5. Actual heat of compression at efficiency 0.70 = 12.16 / 0.70 = 17.37 Btu/lb, so h2 = 174.77 + 17.37 = 192.14 Btu/lb.
  6. COP = 62.32 / 17.37 = 3.59.
  7. Heat rejected = 192.14 − 112.45 = 79.69 Btu/lb, which matches 62.32 + 17.37.
  8. Flow per ton = 200 / 62.32 = 3.21 lb/min.
  9. Compressor input per ton = 3.21 lb/min × 17.37 Btu/lb = 55.7 Btu/min, or about 3,344 Btu/h, which is about 0.98 kW per ton at the shaft. This is also 12,000 / 3.59.
  10. Quality after metering = (112.45 − 88.623) / (172.59 − 88.623) = 0.284, so about 28 percent of the liquid flashes to vapor at point 4.

Points 3 and 4 use the NIST liquid value at 110°F and 185.86 psia, so the pressure effect on liquid is already included.

What higher head pressure and more superheat do

Higher condensing temperature. The top line rises and the dome's liquid side sits farther right, so point 3 moves right and the vertical drop to point 4 starts at a higher enthalpy. The refrigeration effect gets shorter, while the compression line gets longer because it must reach a higher pressure. Both changes lower COP. Our data show how fast liquid enthalpy climbs with temperature: saturated liquid is 101.93 Btu/lb at 80°F and 116.12 at 120°F, so each degree of extra liquid temperature costs about 0.35 Btu/lb of refrigeration effect.

Subcooling. In our example, 10°F of subcooling puts point 3 at 112.45 instead of the 116.12 for saturated liquid at 120°F. That is 3.67 Btu/lb more refrigeration effect, about 5.9 percent more than the 62.32 we found. For why subcooling can vanish before the metering device, see the flash gas lesson.

More superheat. Point 1 slides right along the low-pressure line. From our rows, saturated vapor at 40°F is 172.59 and vapor at 50°F is 174.77, so 10°F of superheat adds 2.18 Btu/lb, about 0.22 Btu/lb per degree. If that heat was picked up inside the evaporator it is real cooling. If it was picked up in a warm suction line outside the cooled space, the compressor still pays for it but you get no useful cooling. More superheat also means the vapor is thinner (our 50°F point is 0.98 ft³/lb), so a given compressor pumps less mass per stroke, and discharge gets hotter; see discharge line temperature as a diagnostic.

Limits of this sketch

Real compressors lose energy to motor heating, friction and valve flow, and real lines have pressure drop, so field COP is lower than a chart value. The 0.70 efficiency is a teaching assumption. Charts also assume a pure fluid; blends have temperature glide, which we cover in R-454B and R-32 at the gauges. For how metering devices set the pressure drop at leg 3 to 4, see Metering Devices: TXV and Piston.

Field card: Pressure-Enthalpy Diagram

Horizontal distance on a P-h chart is Btu per pound.

  • Left of the dome is subcooled liquid, under it is a mixture, right of it is superheated vapor.
  • Evaporation and condensing are horizontal lines; metering is a vertical line.
  • Refrigeration effect = h1 − h4. Heat of compression = h2 − h1.
  • COP = refrigeration effect / heat of compression.
  • Pounds per minute per ton = 200 / refrigeration effect.
  • Our R-134a example (40°F evaporating, 120°F condensing, 10°F superheat and subcooling, 0.70 assumed efficiency): 62.32 Btu/lb, 17.37 Btu/lb, COP 3.59, 3.21 lb/min per ton.
  • Higher head shortens the effect and lengthens the compression. Subcooling lengthens the effect.
