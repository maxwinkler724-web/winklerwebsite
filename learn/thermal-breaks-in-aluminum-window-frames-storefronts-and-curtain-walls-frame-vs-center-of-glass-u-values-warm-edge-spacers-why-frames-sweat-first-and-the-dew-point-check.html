Thermal Breaks in Window Frames, Storefronts and Curtain Walls

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Walk a cold storefront in January and the first places to fog, drip or grow frost are the metal frame and the strip of glass right beside it. That is not bad luck. Metal moves heat fast, and the frame is the weak link in the window. This lesson explains why, what a thermal break does about it, and how room humidity and surface temperature decide whether a frame sweats.

Why solid aluminum frames conduct so well

A research paper from a national lab lists the conductivity of aluminum at 160 W/(m·K) and a glass-fiber-filled polyamide thermal break material at about 0.17 W/(m·K) (source 1). Dividing one by the other (our own arithmetic), aluminum carries heat roughly 900 times better than the plastic strip, for the same size and temperature difference. A solid aluminum frame therefore acts like a fin that reaches from the warm room to the cold outdoors. Heat flows in through the inside face, runs through the metal almost without resistance, and leaves on the outside face.

That is why the same paper says frame performance is usually the limiting factor in lowering a whole window's U-factor. Good glass can reach very low U-factors, but the frame usually cannot keep up (source 1). The glass is not the problem; the metal around it is.

What a thermal break is

A thermal break is a low-conductivity link placed inside the metal frame so the inside metal and the outside metal are no longer one piece. The lab paper describes frames that use low-conductivity materials as thermal breaks inside the structural frame to cut what would otherwise be a high U-factor (source 1). Two common forms are a plastic strip, such as the polyamide above, that is rolled or snapped in place, and a pocket that is filled with liquid polyurethane, cured, and then has the metal bridge that held the two halves together machined away. Trade people call that second method poured and debridged. The first frames in the same lab study used polyurethane foam for the break and a polyamide strip in another design (source 1).

A break does not stop heat. It slows it. A university lecture slide set on windows says a small plastic break around three-eighths of an inch helps, but deeper breaks, three-quarters of an inch or more, are usually needed to lift the frame to an insulation value of about R2 or R3. The same slides call poor-quality breaks far too common and a usual cause of heat loss and condensation trouble in curtain walls, and say curtain walls need deep breaks (source 4).

Frame, edge and center of the glass

Glass is rated three different ways, and the numbers differ. The center-of-glass U-factor ignores the edges. A whole-window U-factor includes the frame, and a band at the glass perimeter in between. A lab review of spacers describes that band as running from the frame edge to the point where the glass surface reaches the center-of-glass temperature, and puts its width at about 2.5 inches in early lab work and closer to 4 inches in later studies (source 2). The university slide set adds that center-of-glass values flatter a window, since the frame and glass edge are the most critical places for condensation (source 4).

A typical aluminum frame with a standard break was reported at frame U-values of about 1.0 to 2.0 Btu/hr-ft²-F, and a premium aluminum frame as low as about 0.5. The same report calls the frame the weakest part of a window and notes that high conductivity makes condensation a major concern for aluminum systems (source 3).

Spacers and the cold glass edge

An insulating glass unit has a spacer holding the panes apart around the edge. The lab review explains that aluminum and galvanized steel spacers form a thermal bridge on the inside glass surface at the edge, and that this cold edge raises the risk of condensation, mold and damage to frames and seals in cold climates (source 2). A warm-edge spacer uses a lower-conductivity material, such as stainless steel or a foam, so the glass edge stays warmer. The review gives effective conductivities of about 0.4 to 1.0 W/(m·K) for stainless steel and 0.15 to 0.18 for some foam spacers, against about 1.8 to 7.6 for aluminum, and reports raised edge-of-glass surface temperatures in lab tests (source 2). The same review found that swapping an aluminum spacer for an insulating one cut the whole-window U-value by about 6 percent in a basic double-glazed wood frame and about 12 percent in a high-performance unit. A modeling paper reached a similar conclusion: windows should use the best spacers available (source 1).

Condensation resistance in general terms

Condensation resistance is how well a frame and glass edge keep their inside surfaces above the dew point. Makers can publish a rating from a standard lab test, and designers can ask for it alongside the system U-factor (source 4). It is not a promise for your building: it depends on the room air, and on how well the installed frame is insulated from the wall. The same slides advise keeping the frame's inside face warm by placing as much mass as practical inside the break line, and note that tight curtains or coverings that block room air from the window can leave the glass cold (source 4). For homeowner-level fixes, see the related lesson on condensation on windows in winter.

How humidity and surface temperature combine

Water vapor condenses on a surface that is colder than the dew point of the room air. The room supplies the dew point; the frame supplies the surface temperature. Raise indoor humidity and the dew point climbs. Cool the frame and the surface drops. They meet, and drops or frost appear. The surface temperature of a part with heat flow through it follows a simple series balance: the heat that crosses the frame equals the heat that leaves the room air into the inside surface through the film of still air. That film coefficient is why even a good frame stays colder than the room.

Worked example (our own, with assumed values)

This is our own teaching example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for illustration, not a measured value or a product rating. We use a simple lumped model: the frame has one U-factor, and the same heat flow passes through the inside air film.

  • Indoor air: 70°F. Outdoor air: 10°F. Difference: 70 − 10 = 60°F.
  • Inside film coefficient, h: 1.46 Btu/hr-ft²-F (assumed; about 0.68 hr-ft²-F/Btu of film resistance).
  • Frame A, bare aluminum with no break: U = 1.30 (assumed). Frame B, broken aluminum frame: U = 1.00 (assumed, within the 1.0 to 2.0 range reported in source 3). Frame C, a deeper-break frame: U = 0.80 (assumed).

The balance is U × (indoor − outdoor) = h × (indoor − surface). So surface = indoor − U × 60 ÷ h.

  1. Frame A: heat flux = 1.30 × 60 = 78.0 Btu/hr-ft². Drop across the film = 78.0 ÷ 1.46 = 53.4°F. Surface = 70 − 53.4 = 16.6°F.
  2. Frame B: 1.00 × 60 = 60.0. 60.0 ÷ 1.46 = 41.1°F. Surface = 70 − 41.1 = 28.9°F.
  3. Frame C: 0.80 × 60 = 48.0. 48.0 ÷ 1.46 = 32.9°F. Surface = 70 − 32.9 = 37.1°F.

Next we ask at what indoor relative humidity (RH) the room air at 70°F has a dew point equal to each surface temperature. We used a standard saturation-pressure approximation for water (the Magnus form) to find saturation pressure at the surface temperature and at 70°F, and divided them. RH at dew point = saturation pressure at surface ÷ saturation pressure at 70°F.

  • Frame A (16.6°F): about 13 percent RH. Because the surface is below 32°F, the moisture arrives as frost.
  • Frame B (28.9°F): about 22 percent RH. Also frost.
  • Frame C (37.1°F): about 30 percent RH, and liquid water.

As a check, 70°F air at 30 percent RH has a dew point of about 37°F (Frame C's surface), and at 40 percent RH about 44.6°F. So in this assumed 10°F weather, a humidifier set at 40 percent would put moisture on all three frames, while at 30 percent only the weakest two clearly would, and Frame C would sit right at the line. Lowering the frame U-factor moves the sweat point to higher humidity, but it does not remove it. Real frames have corners, glazing pockets and gaps, so surface temperatures vary across the frame; this example gives the idea, not a design number.

What HVAC techs see in the field

  • Perimeter heat. Fin-tube along the base of a storefront, or heat discharged up the glass, warms the glass and frame so that the surface stays above the dew point. If it is off, undersized or blocked by displays, the frames fog first.
  • Humidifier setpoints. A setting that works in mild weather can cause dripping frames on the coldest days. Many systems let you lower the setpoint as the outdoor temperature falls. Check the logic before blaming the humidifier.
  • Where it shows. Corners, the bottom rail, mullion joints, and a band of glass near the spacer. These match the cold edge and frame described above.
  • What a tech cannot fix. Failed breaks, damaged strips and cold edges are building issues. Report them with photos. Use an infrared camera carefully, since reflections can mislead; see heat loss through walls for the same bridging idea in walls.

Water on a frame can run into wall cavities, so repeated sweating is worth reporting, not just wiping. Also compare with the ideas in window U-factor and solar heat gain, which covers glass.

Field card: Thermal Breaks in Window Frames, Storefronts and Curtain Walls

Cold metal plus humid air equals sweat.

  • Aluminum is about 900 times more conductive than a plastic break strip (our arithmetic from source 1).
  • A break slows heat, it does not stop it; deeper breaks do more.
  • Frames and glass edges are the coldest spots, so they sweat and frost first.
  • Warm-edge spacers keep the glass edge warmer.
  • Surface = indoor − U × temperature difference ÷ film coefficient.
  • Dew-point check: compare surface temperature to room dew point.
  • If frames drip on cold days, check humidifier setpoint and perimeter heat first.
