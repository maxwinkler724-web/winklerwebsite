Ventilating Rabbit Barns and Small Livestock Barns

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rabbit shed or a six-goat barn looks simple: a roof, some walls, a few fans. The air still has to do three jobs, and the animals have little room for error. It must carry heat out in summer, carry ammonia and moisture out in winter, and never blow across the animals. Rabbits are the hardest case, because they handle heat poorly. This lesson covers the airflow basics and a worked example, and leaves the large-barn details to the related lessons.

Know the animal limits first

Extension publications put the rabbit problem plainly. One university fact sheet says rabbits do not cope well above 90 °F, that humidity makes it worse, and that the ideal range is 50 to 60 °F. It adds that heat stress can make bucks sterile, cause does to miscarry or neglect newborns, and kill animals outright. Another state bulletin notes that buck fertility may decline when temperatures stay above 85 °F for several days. A winter care sheet from a third university puts the comfort zone, where rabbits feed and gain best, at 60 to 65 °F, and says 20 to 60 °F is usually manageable if the animals are protected from wind, snow and rain. Different sources give slightly different bands, so treat 85 to 90 °F as the point where the building design is failing.

Sheep and goats tolerate more. One extension article gives a normal body temperature range of 101.5 to 103.5 °F and warns that poor ventilation traps heat and moisture. A small-ruminant article puts the target air temperature for adults at 50 to 60 °F and for young animals at 54 to 65 °F. These are animal-care targets, not equipment settings. They tell you why the airflow numbers below matter.

Natural ventilation: ridge out, eaves in

Most small barns rely on thermal buoyancy (warm air rising) and wind. A Pacific Northwest ventilation publication says the outlet should be at the ridge so warm, moist air can leave, and the inlets should be adjustable by season with curtains or weighted flaps. A Kentucky goat-barn publication describes air leaving through an open ridge on a gable roof and entering along the eaves, with sidewall curtains or roll-up doors closed down on cold nights and opened wide on hot days.

A goat-facility article gives opening areas per animal: for a doe, about 0.1 square feet of exit area and 0.2 square feet of entry area, and for a buck, about 0.13 and 0.26 square feet. Notice that the entry area is double the exit area, the usual layout for a natural system. Cold drafts, the Kentucky publication adds, usually mean badly designed inlets, such as openings that are too large or blocked by trusses just inside them.

The same goat-facility article sets the air speed at animal level: no more than about 1.6 feet per second for adults and about 0.65 feet per second for kids. It suggests a candle-flame test, where the flame must stay upright. For rabbits, a winter care sheet asks for gentle air movement around each animal, with no drafts, pockets of stale air or sudden temperature changes. Rabbit cages and pens sit low, so put the cold inlets above the animals and let the air mix before it drops.

Fans, evaporative cooling and misting: cautions for rabbits

The Alabama rabbit fact sheet says not to aim fans directly at rabbits. It suggests mounting box or ceiling fans above the cages, since only slight airflow is needed, and notes that moving air also helps repel flies. It calls air conditioning impractical for most operations. On wet cooling, it says misting systems are costly and only work if the rabbits stay dry, and that swamp coolers are cost-efficient but excess moisture can cause respiratory problems. A Mississippi extension page tells rabbit builders not to rely on natural air movement for air quality and cooling, and recommends mechanical ventilation, with insulation mainly to keep summer heat out.

For sheep and goats, a Nebraska publication allows sprinkling short-wool sheep and goats with room-temperature water, but says to wet only the lower legs and inside flanks of sheep with wool over an inch long, and to avoid cold water, which can cause temperature shock. An Alabama small-ruminant article says to watch that misting does not leave things overly wet. In practice: fans and shade first, wetting only with a plan for keeping the barn dry, and no fogging in a rabbit house. Humidity pushes the dew point up, and wet air takes away less heat from an animal that cools mainly by breathing.

Ammonia and manure

Urine ammonia irritates the airways, and a Maine bulletin links good ventilation and no strong ammonia smell to lower disease risk in small rabbitries. A Mississippi page says cages need drainage for urine and waterer leakage, and that solid waste should be removed promptly. Goat articles use the nose as the test: if you can smell ammonia, ventilation is not enough. We do not use a measured ppm figure here, because the sources we read did not give one. The practical rule for a technician is that a smell at animal height, a damp bedding pack, or condensation on the walls means more air or less moisture, not a bigger heater.

Winter: minimum ventilation and moisture

In winter the goal flips. Heat is precious, but animals still breathe out moisture and bedding gives off ammonia. A Kentucky publication says that in winter the controlling concern may be moisture or ammonia, and that fan speed matters most in cold weather when balancing moisture loss against heat retention. A Vermont-attributed rate quoted by two goat sources is about 20 cfm per animal, or 4 to 15 room air changes per hour. For sheep, one extension document uses a minimum of 25 cfm per 1,000 pounds of animal weight, and the Pacific Northwest table lists 25, 100 and 200 cfm per 1,000 pounds for cold, mild and hot weather. A 100-pound goat is only a tenth of that weight unit, so a few cfm each. Wool matters too: one sheep-moisture publication notes that wool can hold up to 30 percent of its weight in water.

Rabbit sources say it in words: reducing heat loss can also cut airflow and leave pens damp, and breath moisture can condense on nest fur in very cold weather, so replace wet nest material. At or below 32 °F, the Michigan sheet says to refill water two to three times a day, since large pans freeze before the rabbits drink much.

Heated waterers, small heaters and power loss

A South Dakota extension page calls heaters a top fire hazard in barns. It says to keep them away from straw and hay, to blow dust out of them before use, and to check heated waterers daily in cold months. Cords on tank heaters need regular inspection and must be out of animals' reach, floating heaters belong in automatically filled tanks, and plastic tanks need a wire guard on the element because a tank running dry is a fire risk. The page also says extension cords should not be used in the barn. A Pennsylvania page adds that heating equipment was the leading cause of barn fires in a five-year fire-loss data set and that dust and cobwebs on heat sources should be removed.

Fuel-burning heaters add combustion moisture and carbon monoxide. Burning propane releases 1.64 pounds of water per pound of fuel and natural gas 2.25 pounds, according to the sheep-moisture publication, which is why unvented heaters and winter ventilation conflict. Rabbit and goat barns are small, so a single heater is a big share of the load. See our carbon monoxide lesson in the related list for alarms and venting.

Power loss in hot weather is the quiet danger. A powered-fan barn that loses its fans has lost its main heat removal, and rabbits do not tolerate temperatures above 90 °F. Our own planning suggestion, not a sourced rule: have a way to open the building by hand, a backup power plan for the fans, and an alarm that reaches the owner.

Worked example: how much air for a 10-doe goat barn

Our own example. A small gable barn houses 10 adult does. These are our assumptions, not measured data. We assume each doe gives off 500 Btu/h of sensible heat. That is a round teaching number, because the sources we read gave no sensible heat figure per goat, so use a measured or published value before you design anything. We want the barn to run no more than 5 °F above outdoor air.

  1. Total sensible heat: 10 does × 500 Btu/h = 5,000 Btu/h.
  2. Use the sensible heat equation: Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  3. Solve for airflow: cfm = 5,000 ÷ (1.08 × 5) = 5,000 ÷ 5.4 = about 926 cfm.
  4. Cross-check the openings. Using the per-doe areas above, exit area = 10 × 0.1 = 1.0 sq ft at the ridge, and entry area = 10 × 0.2 = 2.0 sq ft at the eaves.

Compare 926 cfm with the Cornell summer rate of 150 to 200 cfm per animal, or 1,500 to 2,000 cfm for ten goats. The published rate is higher because a real roof also adds solar and radiant heat, which our animal-only 5,000 Btu/h leaves out. The lesson: the equation shows the airflow needed to carry away the animals' own heat, and it is a minimum, not a design value. Now apply the same limit to rabbits. If it is 85 °F outside and the barn is 5 °F warmer, rabbits are at 90 °F. Rabbits have no margin, which is why shading, insulation and fans mounted above the cages matter more than the arithmetic.

Field card: Ventilating Rabbit Barns and Small Livestock Barns

Ridge out, eaves in, no wind on the animals.

  • Rabbits: trouble above about 85 to 90 °F; comfort bands in the sources run 50 to 65 °F.
  • Natural ventilation: open ridge for exit, adjustable eave and sidewall openings for entry.
  • Goat opening areas per doe: about 0.1 sq ft exit, 0.2 sq ft entry; air speed at goat level about 1.6 ft/s or less.
  • Sensible heat: Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Winter minimum: about 20 cfm per goat in one quoted rate; watch moisture and ammonia smell.
  • Fans above rabbits, not aimed at them; misting and swamp coolers add moisture risk.
  • Heaters and heated waterers: daily checks, no extension cords, keep away from straw and hay.
  • Plan for a hot-weather power loss before it happens.
