Heat Pump Pads, Stands and Wall Brackets: Snow Height and Drainage

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump makes meltwater all winter, and it works best with open air around the coil. That is why installers lift the outdoor unit off the ground. This lesson covers how high, what to lift it on, and where the water goes. For plain clearance numbers see Outdoor Unit Clearances; this page is about the support itself.

Why height matters

In heating mode the outdoor coil runs colder than the air, so frost builds and the unit melts it off in a defrost cycle (see Heat Pump Iced Up). Two things go wrong at ground level. Snow blocks airflow to the coil, and meltwater has nowhere to go, so it refreezes under the unit. Writer Jon Harrod, in a 2023 GreenBuildingAdvisor article, says raising a heat pump is necessary for performance and durability, and cites one blizzard where defrosts went from about once every four hours to about once an hour. A cold-climate heat pump installation guide hosted on a U.S. Department of Energy site says to leave clearance above the historical average maximum snow depth, using risers if needed.

Makers print their own minimums. Carrier's 25HNB6/25HNB9 instructions say to elevate the unit to clear the estimated snowfall level and not to let water or ice build up in the base pan. Trane's 2TWZ9C guide says to raise the unit 3 to 12 inches above the pad, depending on local weather, and to keep the base pan drain holes open. Daikin's condensing unit instructions (IOD-4038D, March 2024) list minimum base elevation by design temperature: 2 1/2 in. at +15°F and above, 8 in. from -5°F to +14°F, and 12 in. below -5°F. Those are drainage minimums. Snow depth is a separate check, so use whichever number is larger.

Pad, stand or wall bracket

Ground pad. Daikin says to set a ground unit on a solid, level foundation that will not shift or settle, and that the slab should not touch the building foundation, to cut sound transfer. Trane likewise says the pad must not be in direct contact with any structure. Carrier asks for the unit to be level, within 3/8 in. per foot. The same installation guide adds that ground under a pad should drain well and not heave with frost. A pad alone only gives a few inches, so in snow country it is usually paired with a stand.

Stand. A stand gets the base higher than a pad can. The cost is stability. A reader of the Harrod article reported using a 24 in. stand and anchoring it with four ground screws because the raised unit was top-heavy in wind. Mitsubishi's manual for the MSZ-FD09/12NA says that when a unit sits high, secure the legs. Carrier says to locate the unit so it is stable in all circumstances, including bad weather.

Wall bracket. The same installation guide prefers wall mounting where it can be done, using brackets designed for the foundation wall where ground clearance allows. A bracket gets the unit up out of the snow and keeps the ground under it free. The limits are weight and what the wall is made of. DiversiTech lists its Quick-Sling QSWB3000 steel bracket at a 300 lb allowable load, with four anti-vibration points that use 50 durometer rubber washers. A distributor listing for the QSWB2000 says up to 350 lb, anchored into structural framing, concrete or masonry and never into siding. RectorSeal lists its WBB500 at a 500 lb maximum. Rubber isolators cut noise, but they do not make a weak wall strong. Mitsubishi says to pick a rigid wall or support to limit noise and vibration. Fujitsu and Bosch both say simply to install on a location that can carry the unit's weight.

Where the water goes

The same installation guide says to keep the unit away from walkways where defrost meltwater could refreeze into a slip hazard, and to avoid spots under roof drip lines or overhangs. If you cannot, and there is no working gutter, use drip caps or shields that the maker approves. Carrier says to position the unit so water, snow or ice from the roof or eaves cannot fall directly on it. Harrod notes a metal roof can drop a large mass of snow at once and prefers a separate roof or awning where snow is heavy. Mitsubishi says in heavy-snow areas to add a canopy, a pedestal and/or baffle boards. Do not box in the base: Daikin and Trane both say the base drain holes and cutouts must stay open. For where the water lands, aim it at a gravel bed or a slope away from steps, not at a path.

Worked example: height and bracket load

Assumptions, labeled. The National Weather Service Newark almanacs (1931 to present) list a record one-day snowfall of 24 in. (January 23, 2016) and a greatest January total of 37.4 in. (2011). Those are snowfall, not snow depth on the ground, and Newark is not your yard. So we assume a design snow depth of 24 in. for a northern New Jersey yard. We also assume a 99 percent design temperature of 10°F and a 3 in. ground pad. Confirm both for a real job.

Stand height.

  1. Daikin row for -5°F to +14°F: 8 in. minimum base height.
  2. Snow check: 24 in.
  3. Larger of the two: 24 in.
  4. Our own advice, not a maker figure: add 4 in. for drifting and for the unit settling into snow: 24 + 4 = 28 in. from grade to the underside of the unit.
  5. The pad already supplies 3 in., so the stand needs 28 - 3 = 25 in.

A 25 in. stand is tall, so it needs anchoring and a snow or wind plan.

Wall bracket load. Mitsubishi's MUZ-FS18NA specification lists the outdoor unit at 118 lb. We add assumed allowances: 15 lb for hardware and line set weight carried by the bracket, and 30 lb for ice or snow on the top and a technician leaning on it during service.

  1. Load: 118 + 15 + 30 = 163 lb.
  2. Our teaching margin (not a code): rated load of at least 2 times the load. 2 x 163 = 326 lb.
  3. QSWB3000: 300 lb. 300 is less than 326, so it fails the 2x margin. (300 / 163 = 1.84 times.) On the bare unit it would be 300 / 118 = 2.5.
  4. WBB500: 500 lb. 500 / 163 = 3.07 times, which passes.

The result does not say the 300 lb bracket is unsafe, only that it uses up the margin we chose. The bracket rating is half the story: the wall, the fasteners and the stud or masonry they bite into must carry the same load. Check the bracket maker's instructions for that.

Wind and final checks

Mitsubishi says not to expose the air inlet or outlet directly to wind, and to face the inlet toward a wall and add a baffle board on the outlet side in cold weather. Carrier says roof-mounted units in winds above 5 mph may need baffles. Before you leave: unit level, drain holes open, nothing aimed at a walkway, stand or bracket anchored to structure, and the maker's clearances still met. If the bracket or stand has a weight or wall limit, the maker's instructions win over this page.

Field card: Heat Pump Stands and Brackets

Lift it above the snow, drain it away from people, anchor it to structure.

  • Base height: take the larger of snow depth or the maker's drainage minimum (Daikin: 2 1/2, 8 or 12 in. by design temperature).
  • Stand height = base height - pad height.
  • Keep base pan drain holes open; keep meltwater off walkways.
  • Avoid roof drip lines and sliding roof snow.
  • Level within the maker's tolerance (Carrier: 3/8 in. per foot).
  • Bracket load = unit + hardware + snow or service allowance; compare the rating with a margin.
  • Rubber isolators help noise; the wall still has to be strong.
