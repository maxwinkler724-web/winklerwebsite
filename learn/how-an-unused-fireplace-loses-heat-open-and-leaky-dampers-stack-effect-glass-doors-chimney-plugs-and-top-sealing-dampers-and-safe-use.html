How an Unused Fireplace Loses Heat: Open and Leaky Dampers, Stack Effect, Glass Doors, Chimney Plugs and Safe Use

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A wood-burning fireplace you never light can still be one of the biggest holes in your home. The flue is a tall, open shaft, and in winter it can carry heated room air straight outdoors around the clock. This lesson explains why, how to check your own fireplace, and which fixes are safe.

Why a cold fireplace still costs you

A flue is a vertical opening about as wide as a stove pipe or bigger, running from your living room to the sky. The damper is a metal plate that is supposed to close it. Two things go wrong. Some homes leave the damper open all winter because it is stuck or nobody thinks about it. And even a damper that looks shut often is not tight. Colorado State University Extension notes that air can leak through even a closed damper and that many dampers warp over time with fireplace use. The same extension service reports a table of average air leaks in a U.S. home in which fireplaces account for 14 percent, and Purdue Extension lists the same 14 percent for the fireplace. Those are averages across many homes, not a measurement of yours.

Stack effect: the chimney-sized air leak

Warm air is lighter than cold air, so in winter it rises through the house and escapes at the top. Colorado State University Extension describes this as the stack effect: hot air rising out through gaps in the ceiling pulls cold air in through gaps near the floor. A flue is the biggest, straightest gap in many houses, and the pull is strongest when the outdoor air is coldest. For every cubic foot of heated air that goes up the flue, a cubic foot of cold outdoor air has to sneak in somewhere else: under doors, around sill plates, through outlets and window frames. You feel that as a cold floor and a draft along the baseboard, even though the leak is overhead.

For a deeper look at the same physics in tall buildings, see our lesson on stack effect in tall buildings; the idea is the same at house scale.

What an open fire changes

When you actually burn wood the picture shifts. A fire needs a lot of air, and that air comes from your room. an EPA wood-burning page says a wood-burning fireplace is generally an inefficient way to heat a home, that fireplace drafts can pull warm air up the chimney so other rooms are cooler, and that the central heating system has to work harder while the fireplace runs. Our own reading of that: much of the heat the fire gives off goes up the flue with the smoke, and the room air drawn in is already heated. Whether a particular fire adds more warmth than it removes depends on the fire, the house and the weather, so treat the fireplace as a mood feature, not a furnace.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a measured result. We use the common sensible-heat relation Q = 1.08 x CFM x delta T, where Q is in Btu per hour, CFM is airflow in cubic feet per minute, and delta T is the indoor minus outdoor temperature in degrees F. The 1.08 comes from 60 minutes x 0.075 lb per cubic foot x 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F for ordinary air.

Assumptions: the damper is closed but leaky; the leakage airflow is 20 CFM (we picked a modest figure; real fireplaces vary widely); the house is 70 F; the outdoor temperature is 30 F all day; the air heated and lost is the only loss counted, with no credit for any warming of the air as it passes the chimney walls.

  1. Delta T = 70 F - 30 F = 40 F.
  2. Q = 1.08 x 20 x 40 = 864 Btu per hour.
  3. Per day: 864 x 24 = 20,736 Btu.
  4. In gas terms: 20,736 / 100,000 Btu per therm = about 0.21 therm per day.
  5. In electric terms: 20,736 / 3,412 Btu per kWh = about 6.1 kWh per day.

If every day of a 100-day stretch were that cold, the loss would be 20,736 x 100 = 2,073,600 Btu, about 21 therms or about 608 kWh. Real winters are milder on most days, so a season figure would be smaller, but the loss still adds up. Compare an open damper: if we assume 150 CFM, Q = 1.08 x 150 x 40 = 6,480 Btu per hour, or 155,520 Btu per day, about 1.6 therms or 46 kWh per day. That is roughly seven and a half times the leaky-damper case. These are heat losses at the house; your cost depends on your equipment efficiency and fuel price.

Fixes that work, from simple to better

  • Close the damper whenever there is no fire. US DOE tips say to keep the fireplace damper closed unless a fire is burning, and to check the seals around the damper. Make it a habit.
  • Glass or metal doors. Purdue Extension advises fireplace doors that can be closed when not in use. DOE suggests tempered glass doors. Doors cut room air from reaching the opening, but they do not seal the flue.
  • A tight damper or a top-sealing chimney damper. A top-sealing damper is a gasketed lid on the chimney crown operated by a cable from the firebox. Because it sits at the top, it stops cold air from falling down the whole flue. A pro should fit it so it matches your flue size and the cable works. Our own caution: it must be open for every fire, and with a cable damper you can open it from inside the room, which makes it easier to remember than a plug.
  • An inflatable chimney plug. Colorado State University Extension tested a chimney balloon and reported that it sealed leaks and that the author could not feel drafts afterward, but that it lost some air over a few weeks and may need removing and reinstalling before each fire. It is useful for a fireplace you truly never burn.
  • If you will never use it, DOE says to consult an expert and have the flue plugged and sealed. That is a permanent job for a qualified chimney or masonry pro.
Plug rule. Any plug, balloon, board, rag or blanket in a flue must be taken out and the damper opened before you light anything. CPSC advises opening the fireplace damper before lighting a fire and keeping it open until the ashes are cool. Hang a tag on the screen or tools so a guest or a family member does not light a fire against a blocked flue. Follow the maker's instructions for the exact product.

Checking for drafts: incense and the back of your hand

Colorado State University Extension describes a simple test: pick a windy day, turn combustion appliances off, close windows, exterior doors and fireplace flues, run exhaust fans, then hold a lit incense stick near the suspect spot and watch whether the smoke bends. It also notes that a damp hand held near a door or outlet on a windy day can reveal leaks. Purdue Extension says to hold an incense stick or smoke pen next to windows, doors, electrical boxes and similar places. For a fireplace, check around the damper handle, along the edge of the damper plate, and at the firebox opening with the damper both closed and open.

Be safe doing it: keep the stick away from curtains, never put a flame into a flue, and do not do the test just after ashes were removed, because embers can hide in them. Never use a lit incense stick near a gas smell. If you smell gas, stop, leave, and call the gas utility from outside.

Carbon monoxide and backdraft cautions

Treat the fireplace as part of a system. Furnaces, boilers and water heaters in the same house also use the chimney or a flue, and exhaust fans, dryers and range hoods pull air out of the house. When the house is made tighter, or fans pull hard, a flue can reverse and draw exhaust gases down into the room instead of letting them rise. That is backdrafting, and it can put carbon monoxide in the living space.

  • Colorado State University Extension warns that sealing around combustion appliances can leave them without enough fresh air and that incomplete combustion makes carbon monoxide. If you are unsure, it says not to seal and to have a qualified technician run a combustion appliance zone test.
  • CPSC recommends an annual professional inspection of fuel-burning heating systems, including fireplaces, water heaters, chimneys, flues and vents, and checking chimneys for leakage and blockage by creosote or debris.
  • CPSC also says to install carbon monoxide alarms and check that the batteries are fresh. Put alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.
  • Do not plug a flue shared with a water heater or furnace. Our own caution: a plug in a shared flue could trap exhaust. Ask a pro.
  • Do not close a gas-log damper fully if your logs need it open. See the vented gas logs lesson linked below.

Field card: How an Unused Fireplace Loses Heat

A flue is a chimney-sized leak. Close it, seal it, and never burn against a plug.

  • Stack effect: warm air goes up the flue, cold air comes in low.
  • Even a closed damper can leak; many warp with heat and age.
  • Our own example: 20 CFM at 40 F delta T is 864 Btu/h, about 0.21 therm or 6.1 kWh per day.
  • Fixes: close the damper, glass doors, top-sealing damper, inflatable plug, or a permanent seal by a pro.
  • Before any fire: remove any plug, open the damper, keep it open until the ashes are cool.
  • Test: incense stick or damp hand on a windy day, appliances off.
  • Have flues and chimneys inspected yearly; keep working CO alarms.
