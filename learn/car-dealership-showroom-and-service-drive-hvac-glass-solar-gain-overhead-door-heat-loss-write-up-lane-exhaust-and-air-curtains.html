Car Dealership Showroom and Service Drive HVAC: Glass Solar Gain, Overhead Door Heat Loss, Write-Up Lane Exhaust and Air Curtains

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A car dealership is two buildings under one name. The showroom is a tall glass box that fights the sun. The service drive is a row of overhead doors that open all day while engines run. This lesson puts numbers on both, then covers the customer lounge and the detail bay that sit between them.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Exhaust rates, detector rules and door rules come from the mechanical and energy codes your town adopts, often with changes. This page is general education from public sources read in 2026, not an engineering design. We name standards in plain text and do not link copies of them.

The showroom: glass is the load

The U.S. Department of Energy's window guide says SHGC measures how much of the sun's heat comes through a window, and that the lower the SHGC, the less solar heat the window lets in. Its shopping table points to low SHGC for west-facing glass in warmer climates. A showroom wants clear glass so the cars show, which is why the glass choice and the shading matter more here than in most buildings.

Instant heat gain and cooling load are not the same number. A National Research Council of Canada building digest says sun on east and west surfaces peaks near 75 W/sq ft in Ottawa, that a single sheet of ordinary glass passes about 87 percent at a low sun angle, and that the peak cooling load runs about 60 percent of the peak instant gain because floors and interior surfaces soak up and release the heat later. Our own reasoning: that soaking delays the gain but does not remove it.

Stratification and the cold-glass side

A tall room sorts itself into layers. In summer the top fills with hot air under the sun. In winter, a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory window comfort paper estimates a downdraft of about 0.25 m/s (0.8 ft/s) one meter from a large window at cold design conditions, enough to cause cooling discomfort on bare skin. It lists inside surface temperatures of about 16.9°F for single clear glass and 45.3°F for clear double glazing, and it describes radiant asymmetry, the difference in radiant temperature between the window side and the other side of a person, which it says ASHRAE 55 keeps under 10 K for vertical surfaces. Our own conclusion: do not park a sales desk or a customer chair right at the glass in January.

For perimeter heat, layered air and fans that push warm air back down, we do not repeat the full treatment. See Atrium and Glass Lobby HVAC and HVLS Fans and Destratification.

Worked example, part 1: west glass

Every input below is our own assumption, not a measured value for any building. Use Q = area × SHGC × incident sun.

  • Assumed west glass: 1,200 sq ft.
  • Assumed SHGC: 0.40.
  • Assumed incident sun: 200 Btu/h per sq ft.

Gain: 1,200 × 0.40 × 200 = 96,000 Btu/h. A ton is 12,000 Btu/h, so 96,000 ÷ 12,000 = 8.0 tons of instant solar gain. If the digest's 60 percent figure held for this room, which we cannot promise, the peak load from the glass would be about 96,000 × 0.60 = 57,600 Btu/h, or 4.8 tons. A real load calculation counts conduction, people, lights, cars and the site's own sun data. This only shows scale: the glass alone can ask for several tons.

The service drive: doors open all day

Cold outdoor air pours in at the floor and warm air leaves at the top, so open doors on a winter morning are a heating load that never finishes.

The sensible heat equation is Q = 1.08 × cfm × delta T. The 1.08 is our own rounding of air density, specific heat and 60 minutes per hour at standard air.

Worked example, part 2: one open overhead door

  • Door: 12 ft wide by 14 ft tall, 168 sq ft (our own).
  • Assumed infiltration with the door open: 1,500 cfm (our own illustrative figure; the real number depends on wind, building pressure and how many doors are open).
  • Outdoor 35°F, indoor 68°F, so delta T = 68 − 35 = 33°F.

Q = 1.08 × 1,500 × 33 = 53,460 Btu/h. That is 53,460 ÷ 12,000, about 4.5 tons of heating equivalent, from one door. Three doors open at once would triple it under the same conditions, so door habits matter before a heater is sized.

Cars running indoors: carbon monoxide

Customer cars, test drives and shop vehicles run indoors. The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists carbon monoxide at a NIOSH limit of 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling, an OSHA permissible limit of 50 ppm as an 8-hour average, and an immediately dangerous level of 1,200 ppm. An OSHA training outline on engine hazards warns that engines run in enclosed spaces can reach dangerous levels, that shutting doors against the cold traps fumes, and it lists mechanical ventilation and a CO monitor among controls. Our Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC lesson covers tailpipe capture, NO2 and the unit heater height rules, so we only add the dealership angles here.

  • The write-up lane is where idling piles up. Cars wait with engines running while the writer finishes the order. A "shut it off while we talk" habit costs nothing.
  • Exhaust fans for the shop must have makeup air, and in winter that air is cold. Minnesota's labor department garage guidance says an equal amount of tempered fresh supply air is to be provided.

Worked example, part 3: exhaust and its makeup air

Minnesota's repair garage rule (a state adoption of an International Mechanical Code section) sets a minimum exhaust rate of 0.75 cfm per square foot, and its labor department guidance excludes isolated areas such as parts rooms and showrooms from the floor area. Other states and editions differ, so check yours. As an assumption, our own shop floor is 3,000 sq ft.

Exhaust: 0.75 × 3,000 = 2,250 cfm. Heating that makeup air from 35°F to 68°F: 1.08 × 2,250 × 33 = 80,190 Btu/h. That is more than the 53,460 Btu/h of the open door, and it runs on the fan schedule, not the door schedule. Makeup air and door losses are separate lines on the heater sizing sheet.

Air curtains on the service door

An air curtain across the 12 ft by 14 ft door slows the exchange in the example. Maker mounting-height limits and width rules are in our Air Curtains Over Commercial Doors lesson. The door is 14 ft tall, so check the unit's rated mounting height first. A curtain reduces infiltration but does not stop it, and a stuck door switch either runs the fan all day or leaves the opening bare. Our own advice: size the shop heater as if the curtain earns only part of the credit.

Customer lounge comfort

The lounge is where customers wait, often against the service drive wall. Three checks. Keep it out of the write-up lane's door drift. Make sure its supply is not sharing a return with the shop, so shop fumes do not travel. And watch the radiant side: if the lounge has a glass wall to the showroom or outdoors, the same cold-glass and sun effects apply, so put the seating where the afternoon sun and the cold-glass downdraft do not reach.

The detail bay: humidity

Washing and detailing put water in the air, and a bay with a cold concrete floor and glass can drip and fog. We do not run a moisture number here because it would be invented. The method to use is in our Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load lesson: find the water added, then compare it with how much moisture the ventilation air can carry out. Our own checks: exhaust the bay outdoors, keep wet cars out of the showroom, and look for cold surfaces below the dew point.

Field card: Dealership HVAC

Glass loads the showroom, doors load the shop.

  • Solar gain Btu/h = area × SHGC × incident sun; divide by 12,000 for tons.
  • Our example: 1,200 × 0.40 × 200 = 96,000 Btu/h, 8.0 tons instant.
  • Door loss Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × delta T. Our example: 1.08 × 1,500 × 33 = 53,460 Btu/h.
  • CO limits (NIOSH Pocket Guide): 35 ppm TWA, 200 ppm ceiling; OSHA 50 ppm; IDLH 1,200 ppm.
  • Check the repair garage exhaust rate in your adopted code; exhaust needs tempered makeup air.
  • Air curtain: rated height at least the door height, door switch working, fan on only when open.
