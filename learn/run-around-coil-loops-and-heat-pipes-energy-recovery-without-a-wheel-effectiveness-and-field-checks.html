Run-Around Coil Loops and Heat Pipes: Energy Recovery Without a Wheel

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A wheel is not the only way to pull heat out of exhaust air. In labs and other places where exhaust must never leak into the supply, designers often pick a run-around coil loop or a heat pipe instead. This lesson explains how each works, what effectiveness numbers the sources give, how frost and summer bypass are handled, and what to check in the field.

Why not a wheel here

A rotating wheel carries a small amount of exhaust air over to the supply side. A university facilities design standard we read (Yale's section 23 72 00) says wheels should not be used where any leakage, even a small amount, is unacceptable. The same standard excludes plate exchangers from laboratory use and says heat pipes and run-around loops may be used where zero leakage is required, including labs. For wheels, see Energy Recovery Wheels in Commercial Units. The NREL and DOE Laboratories for the 21st Century guide on energy recovery agrees on the run-around side: it says a loop has no cross-contamination issues. For heat pipes it gives air transfer of 0 to 1 percent, and it notes that the building code section IMC 514 prohibits energy recovery of all types, heat pipes and run-around loops included, in hazardous exhaust systems, so fume hood exhaust may need a code variance. See Lab Fume Hoods for the exhaust side.

How a run-around coil loop works

A run-around loop is two finned coils joined by piping. One coil sits in the exhaust duct, one in the outdoor air stream. A pump circulates a water and glycol mix between them. In winter the fluid picks up heat from the warm exhaust and drops it into the cold outdoor air. The NREL guide calls it sensible-only recovery, notes that the two airstreams do not need to be next to each other, and says the loop needs space for a pump. Yale's standard likewise says loops fit separate supply and exhaust air handlers and cannot move moisture.

How a heat pipe works

Heat Pipe Technology describes its heat pipe as passive: no energy input, with heat moved as the working fluid changes between liquid and vapor. The tubes are copper, built as charged circuits and expanded into partitions that seal the two airstreams apart. Its page states the product is certified for zero exhaust air transfer rate and that the only recommended upkeep is periodic cleaning, since there are no moving parts. The NREL guide says heat pipes recover sensible energy only, with the supply and exhaust normally side by side, and lists tilting as one way to control part-load output (the others are a bypass damper or bypass duct). Yale's standard allows only passive heat pipes, built complete at the maker, with copper tubes and no field assembly except approved retrofits.

Fixed plate exchangers are a third option. Yale recommends them where no leakage is acceptable but excludes laboratory use, and requires a face and bypass damper for operation below freezing. We did not find a typical effectiveness range for them in the sources we read, so we give none.

Typical effectiveness

The NREL guide gives a sensible effectiveness of 45 to 65 percent for heat pipes and a theoretical 55 to 65 percent for run-around loops. It adds that a loop in a cold, humidified building must be controlled to prevent freezing and condensation, which drops effectiveness to around 30 percent on a design day near 0 F outdoors. It says the limit applies mainly below about 20 F. Heat Pipe Technology's loop specification gives no number and says scheduled effectiveness comes from the project schedules. Treat catalog figures as design values, not what you will measure on a dirty coil.

Frost control and summer bypass

Exhaust leaving the coil can fall below freezing and frost over. The loop spec from Heat Pipe Technology holds the exhaust air above a minimum temperature, described as typically 36 F and adjustable, by throttling the loop. For a two-airstream loop it asks for separate inline and bypass valves for winter and summer, and says a single three-way valve will not be accepted there. It also turns the pump off when recovery is not wanted, such as during economizer operation. Engineered Air's glycol coil page warns that exhaust coils dehumidify, so fluid below 32 F can frost them, and that the system then runs at lower efficiency, so savings may fall short. For summer or mild weather, the NREL guide says heat pipe bypass placed in the supply section suits frost control, but in moderate weather it belongs in the exhaust section or the unit needs a full exhaust bypass. One case it cites had 55 F outdoor air and 70 F exhaust, and the supply air rose 6 to 7 F while mechanical cooling ran needlessly. Yale's standard also asks that frost control never interrupt ventilation during occupied hours.

Pump and glycol upkeep

The Heat Pipe Technology loop spec describes lead and lag pumps that rotate on run time and a standby that takes over on failure, an air separator with blowdown and flush valves, and a 5 micron filter on seal flush lines. It writes the fluid as up to 50 percent propylene or ethylene glycol by volume. That spec does not give a test schedule, so for concentration testing and inhibitor care see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops and follow the maker's manual for the unit in front of you.

Worked example

Outdoor air is 10 F, exhaust is 72 F, and both sides move 5,000 cfm. The NREL guide gives 55 to 65 percent for loops, so we use 0.55, the low end of that range.

  1. Our own: temperature difference = 72 - 10 = 62 F.
  2. Our own: temperature rise = 0.55 x 62 = 34.1 F.
  3. Our own: air leaving the recovery coil = 10 + 34.1 = 44.1 F.
  4. Our own: recovered heat = 1.08 x 5,000 x 34.1 = 5,400 x 34.1 = 184,140 Btu/h.

Now the frost-limited case. Our own: at 0.30, the rise is 0.30 x 62 = 18.6 F, the leaving air is 28.6 F, and heat recovered is 5,400 x 18.6 = 100,440 Btu/h. With equal flows and no losses, Our own: the exhaust would leave the coil at 72 - 34.1 = 37.9 F in the first case, close to the 36 F minimum the spec mentions, so a cold day pushes the controller toward the second case. The 1.08 factor is the standard sensible-heat constant for standard air, and these numbers ignore fan heat and coil losses.

Field checks

  • Temperatures across both coils. Read air in and out of each coil and compare with the design numbers; a low rise can mean a dirty coil, low flow or air in the loop.
  • Glycol concentration. Test the mix and compare with the design percentage in the maker's manual.
  • Valve stroke. Heat Pipe Technology lists functional testing of valves and variable-speed drives in start-up. Command the valve through its range and confirm it actually moves and the loop temperatures respond.
  • Pump. Check rotation, differential pressure and the standby changeover.
  • Cleaning. The NREL guide says cleaning is still critical to performance.

Also compare with ERV vs. HRV and Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems.

Field card: Run-Around Loops and Heat Pipes

No wheel, no air mixing, sensible heat only.

  • Loop: exhaust coil, outdoor coil, pump, glycol mix.
  • NREL: loops 55 to 65 percent, about 30 percent when frost-limited; heat pipes 45 to 65 percent.
  • Frost: hold exhaust above the minimum setpoint (spec: typically 36 F).
  • Mild weather: bypass or pump off, or heat is added when not wanted.
  • Check coil temperatures, glycol percentage, valve stroke, pump changeover.
  • Heat recovered = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise.
