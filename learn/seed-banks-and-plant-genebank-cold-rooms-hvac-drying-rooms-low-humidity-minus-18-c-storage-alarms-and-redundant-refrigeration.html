Seed Bank Cold Rooms: Drying Rooms, Minus 18 C Vaults and Alarms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A seed bank is a building whose product is time. Seeds that are dried well and kept cold can stay alive for decades, and the HVAC and refrigeration plant exists to protect that. This lesson walks through the two rooms where the work happens, a dry room at cool temperature and a freezer vault, and what the equipment around them has to do.

What the standards ask for

The FAO Genebank Standards (revised edition, 2014) are voluntary guidance for gene banks around the world. For drying, Standard 4.2.1 says samples go to equilibrium in a controlled environment of 5 to 20 C and 10 to 25 percent relative humidity, depending on the species. For long-term (base collection) storage, Standard 4.2.3 calls for -18 C plus or minus 3 C and 15 percent plus or minus 3 percent relative humidity. Active collections that are used more often sit in refrigerated storage at 5 to 10 C and the same 15 percent humidity (Standard 4.2.4).

A federal genebank describes the same pattern: new samples spend a few weeks in an equilibration room at 23 percent relative humidity, then go into foil bags and a -18 C vault. Its vault area has a separate air handling unit and a standby generator.

Why low humidity matters

Seeds are hygroscopic. They trade water with the air until their moisture matches the air's relative humidity. A state university extension guide shows the extremes: seed in dry air at 15 percent RH can drop to about 6 percent moisture and store safely for several years, while seed held at 90 percent RH stays wet and, the guide warns, germinates poorly after a year. The same guide's plain rule of thumb is that the drier the seed, the longer it keeps.

This is a psychrometrics problem; for a refresher, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

The drying room

In our description, a drying room is a small, tight, insulated space with a closing door, and its load is mostly latent. Cooling at 15 C is easy; getting the humidity ratio that low is the hard part. A cooling coil would have to run below the freezing point of water to reach a dew point that low (the room's vapor pressure, worked out below, is under the 0.61 kPa saturation pressure at 0 C in the NIST table), and a coil that cold frosts over. That is why drying rooms usually use a desiccant dehumidifier, which pulls moisture out chemically and does not depend on coil temperature. See Desiccant Dehumidifiers: Wheels, Reactivation Air, and When They Beat Cooling Coils for how the wheel and reactivation air work.

Our own field notes: the unit dries only the air that passes through it, so the room needs a good air path. Outdoor air tends to be the biggest moisture source, as the example below shows, so we assume the room is kept slightly pressurized with makeup air limited to what the occupants need. Humidity probes drift; see Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes.

Worked example: moisture to remove from a drying room

Everything below is our own example with assumed inputs. Saturation pressures come from the NIST Chemistry WebBook water table (15 C: 1.7058 kPa; 30 C: 4.2470 kPa). We use W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw) with p = 101.325 kPa, and lb/h = 4.5 x cfm x delta W.

Assumptions (ours): room held at 15 C and 15% RH; 100 cfm of outdoor air at 30 C and 50% RH; 2 workers at an assumed 0.25 lb/h each; a 400 lb lot of seed at 12% moisture (wet basis) dried to 6% over 7 days (168 h).

  1. Room air. pw = 0.15 x 1.7058 = 0.2559 kPa. W = 0.622 x 0.2559 / (101.325 - 0.2559) = 0.001575 lb water per lb dry air.
  2. Outdoor air. pw = 0.50 x 4.2470 = 2.1235 kPa. W = 0.622 x 2.1235 / (101.325 - 2.1235) = 0.013314.
  3. Outdoor air load. delta W = 0.013314 - 0.001575 = 0.011740. lb/h = 4.5 x 100 x 0.011740 = 5.28 lb/h.
  4. People. 2 x 0.25 = 0.50 lb/h (assumed).
  5. Seed moisture. Dry matter = 400 x 0.88 = 352 lb. At 6% wet basis the lot weighs 352 / 0.94 = 374.5 lb, so 25.5 lb of water leaves. 25.5 / 168 h = 0.15 lb/h.
  6. Total. 5.28 + 0.50 + 0.15 = 5.93 lb/h. Those are the only three sources we counted; we treat door and wall leakage as zero because the room is pressurized, and the outdoor air figure stands in for it.

Sizing the airflow. Suppose the unit dries room air to 15 C and 5% RH. pw = 0.05 x 1.7058 = 0.0853 kPa and W = 0.000524. delta W = 0.001575 - 0.000524 = 0.001051. Airflow = 5.93 / (4.5 x 0.001051) = about 1,255 cfm through the unit. As Btu/h, 4,840 x 1,255 x 0.001051 is about 6,380 Btu/h of latent load.

What it teaches: outdoor air is 89 percent of the load here (5.28 of 5.93 lb/h). Cut it to 25 cfm and its share falls to 1.32 lb/h, for a total of 1.97 lb/h. Controlling outdoor air does more than a bigger dehumidifier.

The cold vault

Inside, the seeds are sealed in foil or glass, and the standard says moisture-proof containers are what hold the seed's moisture after drying. The standard adds that containers leak over time and suggests self-indicating silica gel sachets to show moisture ingress.

The standard says storage temperature should stay within 3 C of the set point and that time outside that band should total under a week a year, with temperature deviations recorded. For evaporator and defrost behavior at this temperature, see Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Defrost, Evaporator Fan Delay and Icing.

Keeping coils from frosting on humid days

An industry article on freezer coils says the largest buildup of frost and ice forms at access doors and conveyor openings, that warm humid air entering through openings is the main moisture source, and that coils should be placed away from the openings. It adds that some moisture never freezes on the coil and frosts the ceiling, walls and fans instead, and that extra defrost costs energy. On a humid day each door opening brings in far more water; our example below has 0.0133 outdoors against 0.0016 in the room.

The practical answers are the ones the sources point to: fewer and shorter door openings, a vestibule so the two doors are never open at once, an evaporator away from the door, and a defrost schedule you confirm on a humid day, not only in winter. For the dock-door side, see Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings.

Door discipline and vestibules

A vestibule is a small buffer room between the dry room, or receiving, and the vault. Staff close the first door before opening the second, so the air that leaks in is the vestibule's. A vestibule is our own layout suggestion, not a requirement of the standards; it supports the access-point moisture control the sources describe. A university risk-management page also notes that an accurate inventory reduces unnecessary freezer openings.

Redundant refrigeration and alarms

The standard says generators with adequate fuel should be available on site, that monitoring devices should track essential parameters over time, and that storage conditions fail when mechanical controls fail. The federal genebank pairs its vault with its own air handling and a generator. The sources we read do not prescribe how many compressors to use. In our diagram we show the vault served by two condensing units that alternate as lead and lag. That is our own illustration, not something we found in the standards.

For alarms, two university freezer guides give workable practice. One says alarms should signal to a place where a reliable response is ensured at all hours, that the most critical freezers should get their own circuit, and that emergency power should be confirmed. The other says alarms give early warning of mechanical failure, door seal problems or power loss, that the alarm's backup battery needs to be replaced when low, that remote monitoring should be used where feasible, and that the contact list must be kept current. The first guide also suggests keeping marked empty space in a backup freezer.

Our own takeaways: test the alarm and the call-out chain, not only the sensor; check the battery; and set the high-temperature alarm inside the standard's 3 C band so someone has time to act. For loggers on cold units, see Vaccine and Pharmaceutical Refrigerators and Lab Freezers.

Field card: Seed Bank Cold Rooms

Dry first, then cold, then protect both with alarms.

  • FAO drying range: 5 to 20 C and 10 to 25% RH. Base storage: -18 C plus or minus 3 C, 15% RH plus or minus 3.
  • Humidity ratio: W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw). Moisture: lb/h = 4.5 x cfm x delta W.
  • In our example, outdoor air was 89% of the drying room load.
  • Desiccant units dry air without a cold coil.
  • Frost forms mostly at doors: limit openings, use a vestibule, keep coils away from the door.
  • Test the alarm, the battery and the call-out chain, and know whether the panel is on emergency power.
