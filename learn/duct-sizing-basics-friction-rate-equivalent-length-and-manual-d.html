Duct Sizing Basics: Friction Rate, Equivalent Length and Manual D

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "how to size ductwork friction rate," here is the short version: the blower can only spend so much pressure on the ducts, and friction rate is that pressure spread over the longest run. This lesson walks the steps in the order a Manual D design follows them, with one worked example.

What Manual D does

Manual D is the residential duct design standard from ACCA (Air Conditioning Contractors of America). ACCA describes it as an ANSI-recognized set of duct sizing principles and calculations that applies to every duct material. It includes worksheets, a table of maximum air velocities, default fitting lengths, friction charts, and example problems for both rigid and flexible duct. It works after the load calculation, which gives room-by-room airflow; see Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J. ACCA also says its Speed-Sheets and approved software can support the manual, but neither replaces it.

Step 1: Find the available static pressure

Start with the blower. The manufacturer's blower performance table lists how much air the fan moves at each external static pressure (ESP), by speed or tap. Read the ESP that goes with your design airflow. If static pressure is new to you, read Airflow and Static Pressure Basics first.

Not all of that ESP is yours to spend on duct. ACCA's Manual D overview says to total the pressure drops of devices in the airstream, such as the coil, filter, heaters, humidifier, supply outlets, return grilles and balancing dampers, and subtract them from the ESP. What is left is the available static pressure (ASP): the pressure the straight duct and fittings can use up.

ASP = ESP minus component losses

Use the manufacturer's data for coil and filter drops, not a guess. A trade blog article on this step points out that a coil and a throwaway filter count against an air handler, and for a furnace the indoor coil counts. ACCA's blog also warns that blowers are only nominally rated at 0.50 inch of water column on high speed. In real houses the blower often works at a different pressure, so airflow can fall short of the design number.

Step 2: Add up the total effective length

Every fitting costs pressure. Manual D handles that by giving each fitting an equivalent length: the number of feet of straight duct that would cause the same pressure drop. Green Building Advisor's rigid duct article shows Manual D elbow values from roughly 10 to 75 feet, and the author designs with 35 feet for a three-piece elbow.

The total effective length (TEL) is the straight duct plus the fitting equivalent lengths along the longest supply run, plus the same for the longest return run. You size to the most restrictive path, not to the total of all the ducts in the house.

Step 3: Calculate the friction rate

Friction rate is the pressure loss allowed per 100 feet of effective length. ACCA's overview and the trade blog both give the same formula:

Friction rate = ASP × 100 ÷ TEL

The result is in inches of water column per 100 feet. ACCA's blog article on the revised Manual D describes an acceptable range, "the wedge," of 0.06 to 0.18 inch per 100 feet. A result outside that range is a signal to recheck your inputs or the equipment choice, not something to ignore. The same blog shows a case where the math came out at 0.013, far below the wedge, and the author treated that as a red flag.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

These component losses are invented for teaching. They are not from any manufacturer's data. In a real job, take them from the equipment and part literature.

  1. Blower ESP at design airflow, read from the blower table: 0.80 inch (assumed).
  2. Component losses (assumed): coil 0.15, filter 0.08, supply outlet 0.03, return grille 0.03, balancing damper 0.01. Total: 0.15 + 0.08 + 0.03 + 0.03 + 0.01 = 0.30 inch.
  3. Available static pressure: 0.80 − 0.30 = 0.50 inch.
  4. Total effective length (assumed): 250 feet, from the longest supply run plus the longest return run, each with its fitting equivalent lengths.
  5. Friction rate: 0.50 × 100 ÷ 250 = 50 ÷ 250 = 0.20 inch per 100 feet.

That answer is just above the 0.18 top of the wedge in ACCA's blog. A high friction rate means small ducts and fast air, so the next step would be to check velocity and noise carefully, and to look at whether a shorter run or fewer fittings would bring TEL down or the pressure budget up. For comparison, ACCA's overview material works a different example (0.70 inch ESP, 0.50 inch of losses, 200 feet TEL) and gets 0.10 inch per 100 feet.

Step 4: Turn the friction rate into duct sizes

With a friction rate and a room's design CFM, you read the duct size from a friction chart or a duct calculator (often called a ductulator), or from approved software. Pick the chart that matches the duct material, since flex duct and sheet metal have different friction. Manual D's appendices include friction charts, and ACCA lists a set of approved calculation tools. Green Building Advisor describes the same last step: a duct calculator or software converts the friction rate into duct size.

Step 5: Check velocity

A duct that passes the friction check can still be too fast and too loud. The Manual D overview lists recommended and maximum velocities. For supply trunks it gives 700 feet per minute (fpm) recommended and 900 fpm maximum for rigid duct, and 600 and 700 for flex. For supply branches it gives 600 fpm recommended for either, with 900 (rigid) and 700 (flex) as maximums. For return branches the recommended value is 400 fpm and the maximum is 700. It also lists grille face velocities: 500 fpm for return grilles and 300 fpm for filter grilles. The blog's example found a 1,000 CFM supply trunk running at over 1,400 fpm, above the 900 maximum, which made its equivalent lengths invalid.

Velocity is airflow divided by duct area. A 6-inch round branch carrying 100 CFM, as a quick check (the airflow is assumed):

  1. Radius: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 inches = 0.25 foot.
  2. Area: 3.14 × 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.196 square foot.
  3. Velocity: 100 ÷ 0.196 = about 510 fpm, under the 600 fpm recommended supply branch value.

Flex duct: sag, compression and length

Flex duct is not forgiving. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Abushakra, Walker and Sherman, HVAC&R Research, 2004) tested 6, 8 and 10 inch flexible ducts. Moderate compression of the kind seen in field installs raised pressure drop by about a factor of four, and further compression raised it by close to a factor of ten. They also found that the published correction factor was too low, because it ignored duct size.

The Building America Solution Center (DOE and PNNL) says to keep flex out of cavities where it would be compressed, to keep bend radius at least equal to the duct diameter, to pull it taut and support it so it does not sag, and to plan short, straight runs. It adds that longer runs add friction, which in retrofits can be offset by using a larger diameter. ACCA's table of contents for Manual D includes an appendix on flex duct sag and excess length, and the 2016 edition added equivalent lengths for flex duct junction boxes. The takeaway: design for flex as it will really be installed, and see Ductwork Basics and Common Problems and Sealing Duct Leaks for the field side.

Rules of thumb vs. proper design

A single friction rate used on every job is a shortcut. Energy Vanguard's Allison Bailes, writing in ACHR News, concludes that "there is no one friction rate you can use across the board," because the right number depends on the equipment, fittings, register locations and available space. The blog says undersized systems are common when a rule of thumb replaces this process. The reason is plain from the arithmetic above: ASP and TEL change from house to house. Return side matters too; see Not Enough Return Air.

Field card: Duct Sizing Basics

Spend the blower's pressure on the longest run.

  • ASP = blower ESP minus coil, filter, grille, damper and other component losses.
  • TEL = straight length plus fitting equivalent lengths, longest supply plus longest return.
  • Friction rate = ASP × 100 ÷ TEL, in inches per 100 feet.
  • ACCA blog wedge: 0.06 to 0.18. Outside it, recheck inputs.
  • Then size from a friction chart or duct calculator and check velocity.
  • Velocity = CFM ÷ area in square feet.
  • Flex compression can multiply pressure drop several times (LBNL).
  • No single friction rate fits every house.
