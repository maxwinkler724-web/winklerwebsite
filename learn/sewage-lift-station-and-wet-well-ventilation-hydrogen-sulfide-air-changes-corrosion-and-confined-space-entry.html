Lift Station and Wet Well Ventilation: H2S, Air Changes and Entry

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A sewage lift station is a pit that collects waste and pumps it uphill. The air above the sewage can contain hydrogen sulfide, a toxic and flammable gas. This lesson covers how those wells are ventilated, what the numbers in state standards mean, and why a running fan is not permission to climb in.

Check the rules that apply to your site

This page is general education from public sources we read in 2026. Ventilation rates, fan ratings and entry procedures for a real station come from the engineer of record, the state wastewater agency and the employer's written program. Confirm them before you rely on anything here.

What is in the air

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) occurs naturally in sewers. OSHA says it is heavier than air, collects in low-lying and enclosed spaces, and cannot be trusted to announce itself by smell: after a while at low concentrations, or sooner at high ones, you can no longer smell it. OSHA also describes four workers who died in an underground lift station where no permit entry system was in place.

Exposure numbers come from two agencies. OSHA's general industry limit is a 20 ppm ceiling, with a 50 ppm peak allowed for up to 10 minutes under the conditions on its page. NIOSH recommends a 10 ppm ceiling over 10 minutes and lists 100 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health. NIOSH also lists H2S as flammable, from 4.0 percent to 44.0 percent in air, so both toxic and fire hazards matter. The entry test described below checks oxygen first for that reason.

Air change rates the standards cite

An air change means moving a volume of air equal to the space's volume. The widely used Recommended Standards for Wastewater Facilities, known as the Ten States Standards (2014 edition, hosted by the Minnesota Department of Health), give these safety ventilation rates in its pumping station chapter:

  • Wet well: at least 12 air changes per hour if ventilation runs continuously, at least 30 if intermittent.
  • Dry well: at least 6 continuous or 30 intermittent. A two-speed option starts at 30 for 10 minutes and then drops to 6.
  • Air should be forced into the wet well by mechanical means rather than only exhausted from it.
  • Intermittent ventilation is interconnected with the pit lighting, switches are clearly marked and conveniently located, and a manual switch overrides automatic controls.

States adopt their own versions. Texas (30 TAC 217.60) requires continuous wet well mechanical ventilation at 12 changes per hour, and 6 continuous or 30 intermittent for dry wells. Virginia (9VAC25-790-380) lists 12 continuous or 30 intermittent for wet wells and says the wet well exhaust and dry well ventilation must not be interconnected. These are state wastewater design rules, not building HVAC code. NFPA 820, Fire Protection in Wastewater Treatment and Collection Facilities, is the standard usually named for hazardous area classification in these spaces; we name it here without reproducing it, and the Ten States text we read does not cite it.

Fans, motors and corrosion

The Ten States Standards call for electrical equipment in wet wells to meet the National Electrical Code requirements for Class I, Division 1, Group D locations, for fan wheels of non-sparking material, and for equipment suited to corrosive conditions. For the HVAC tech, that means a standard commercial fan, motor or damper actuator is not an automatic substitute, and you do not swap one in without the engineer's approval.

EPA's report to Congress on hydrogen sulfide corrosion lists air conditioning and ventilation units among equipment vulnerable at pumping and lift stations, describes direct attack on copper, iron and steel by the gas, and names wet wells among sites with severe corrosion. A City of Columbus project page says the gas corrodes metals, especially the copper in electrical equipment. A Pumps and Systems article explains the headspace mechanism: bacteria convert H2S into sulfuric acid on moist surfaces. The practical lesson is to expect copper, steel and electrical contacts in or near the well to fail early, and to plan inspection. Our lesson on salt-air coil corrosion covers coil protection in general.

Gas detection

Ten States says that where a two-speed system is paired with gas detection, the designer should consider raising ventilation automatically when hazardous concentrations are detected. For sensor types, alarm setpoints and bump tests, see our lesson on gas and CO detection in boiler and mechanical rooms. A permanent detector does not replace testing before entry.

Confined space entry

Under 29 CFR 1910.146 a permit space is one with at least one listed hazard, such as a hazardous atmosphere. The permit program covers hazard evaluation, testing before entry and monitoring during entry, an attendant outside, rescue arrangements and a written permit. Atmosphere testing goes in a set order: oxygen first, then combustible gases and vapors, then toxic gases and vapors. Alternate procedures under 1910.146(c)(5) apply only if the sole hazard is atmospheric and continuous forced-air ventilation alone keeps the space safe, supported by monitoring data and documented. Our lesson on confined spaces in HVAC work covers the testing order and permit steps. Here the point is that the station fan is one control, not the whole program.

Worked example: sizing our own wet well exhaust

All inputs are our own assumptions for teaching, not a real station.

  1. Assume a round wet well 6 ft in diameter with 10 ft of air space above the liquid at high level. We treat only the headspace as the air to change; this is our assumption, so confirm how the engineer defines the volume.
  2. Area = 3.1416 x 3 ft x 3 ft = 28.3 sq ft. Volume = 28.3 x 10 = 283 cu ft.
  3. Continuous at 12 air changes per hour: 283 x 12 = 3,396 cu ft per hour. Divide by 60 = 57 cfm.
  4. Intermittent at 30 air changes per hour: 283 x 30 = 8,490 cu ft per hour. Divide by 60 = 141 cfm. That replaces the headspace volume about every 2 minutes.
  5. Check an assumed dry well beside it, 8 ft by 8 ft by 10 ft = 640 cu ft. Continuous 6 changes: 640 x 6 / 60 = 64 cfm. Intermittent 30 changes: 640 x 30 / 60 = 320 cfm.

We left out duct, louver and damper losses and any safety margin. The fan must deliver that cfm at the actual static pressure; see the fan laws lesson linked below.

Field card: Lift Station and Wet Well Ventilation: H2S, Air Changes and Entry

Fan on does not mean safe to enter.

  • H2S: OSHA ceiling 20 ppm, NIOSH ceiling 10 ppm, IDLH 100 ppm. Smell is not a detector.
  • Ten States wet well: 12 air changes per hour continuous, 30 intermittent. Dry well: 6 or 30.
  • Volume x air changes per hour / 60 = cfm.
  • Ten States wet well electrical: Class I, Division 1, Group D; fan wheel non-sparking.
  • Test oxygen, then combustibles, then toxics, before entry.
  • Expect copper and electrical contacts near H2S to corrode early.
