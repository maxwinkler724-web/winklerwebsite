Why Is My Furnace Blowing Cold Air?

Your furnace is running, you can hear the blower, but the air coming out feels cold. Here is how to sort the harmless causes from the real ones.

The short answer

Cold air from the vents can be harmless or a sign of trouble. A brief puff of cool air at start-up is common. Continued cold air is not. The easy suspects are a fan set to On, a dirty filter, a tripped breaker, or blocked vents. An ignition problem, a dirty sensor, or ductwork leaks need a professional.

Why a furnace blows cold air

  • The fan is set to On. The blower then runs constantly, and between heating cycles it pushes room-temperature air around that feels cool.
  • A dirty filter. Restricted airflow overheats the furnace, the high-limit switch turns the burners off, and the blower keeps going to cool it down.
  • Ignition failure. If the burners cannot light, the blower runs without heat. Older furnaces may have a pilot light that went out.
  • A dirty or faulty flame sensor. The furnace may start and then shut down soon after.
  • Blocked vents or returns. Less air makes the furnace work poorly and can trigger overheating.
  • Duct leaks. These more often cause uneven or reduced heat than truly cold air.

Notice the safety theme again: when a furnace cannot light or gets too hot, it shuts the flame off on purpose. Do not try to override that. Cold air during a very cold spell also means the house loses heat quickly, so plan for safe temporary heat, but never burn charcoal indoors or use camp stoves or flameless chemical heaters inside. The CDC lists them as carbon monoxide dangers.

What is normal and what is not

Bryant says a brief period of cool air at start-up can be normal while the furnace goes through its ignition sequence. That short wait is normal, though the page gives no exact time. What is not normal is air that stays cool, heat that comes and goes, or a blower running with no sign of the burners. Pay attention to which of those you have, because it points the technician toward the right part. A furnace that shut its burners off after overheating keeps the blower running, which pushes out room-temperature air.

Things you can safely check

  • Thermostat: set to Heat, the temperature set above the room temperature, and the fan on Auto.
  • Filter: replace it if it is dirty or clogged.
  • Power: the furnace has a switch that looks like a light switch on or near it. Make sure it is on, and check the breaker has not tripped.
  • Airflow: make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not blocked.
  • Listen and watch from outside the cabinet: does the burner start? Is a status light blinking? Write down the pattern.
  • Check your CO alarms have fresh batteries while you are at it.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. If you have an older furnace with a pilot light, relighting it is a step many manuals describe, but only follow your own manual's exact instructions, and if you smell gas, do not try at all.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out.
  • The air stays cold after the checks above, or the furnace runs but never gets warm.
  • It short cycles, or you hear banging, rattling, or squealing.
  • The pilot will not stay lit or ignition keeps failing.
  • A breaker trips repeatedly.
  • You smell rotten eggs, hear hissing, or a carbon monoxide alarm goes off.

Field card: Furnace Blowing Cold Air

Brief cool air at start-up can be normal. Staying cold is not.

  • Set thermostat to Heat and fan to Auto.
  • Replace a dirty filter and open all vents.
  • Check the furnace switch and breaker.
  • Cold air plus ignition problems or odors means call a pro.
  • Gas smell or CO alarm: leave, then call from outside.
