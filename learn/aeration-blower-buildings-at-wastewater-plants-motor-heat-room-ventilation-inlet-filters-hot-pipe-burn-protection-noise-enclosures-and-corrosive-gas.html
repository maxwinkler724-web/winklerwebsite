Aeration Blower Buildings: Motor Heat, Ventilation, Filters, Hot Pipe and Noise

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An aeration blower building at a wastewater plant is a plain-looking room with a very large electric motor in it. Every kilowatt that motor wastes becomes heat in the room, and the blower, its piping and its noise cover all add more. This lesson walks through where that heat goes, how to size ventilation for it, and the other hazards that share the room: dirty inlet filters, burn-hot pipe, noise and corrosive gas.

Why the blowers matter

Aeration pushes air into the biological tanks, and it is the biggest electric load at many plants. A federal wastewater energy evaluation says aeration uses 25 to as much as 60 percent of total plant energy, and a federal local-government energy guide says aeration typically accounts for about half of a plant's energy use. The same evaluation notes that the common blower types are positive displacement and centrifugal, and that air intakes are typically fitted with filters to protect the blowers and diffusers from airborne particles. A university aeration chapter adds that positive displacement units suit small plants (efficiencies of 70 to 75 percent), while centrifugal units with inlet guide vanes and outlet diffusers approach 80 percent. A big electric load means a big heat load in the building around it.

Where the heat comes from

  • Motor losses. No motor is 100 percent efficient. The part of the electric input that does not become shaft power is released as heat at the motor, right in the room. This is the most reliable number to size on.
  • Casing, gearbox and drive losses. Gearboxes, couplings and bearing oil coolers shed heat to the room. So does the hot blower casing.
  • Heat of compression. Squeezing air raises its temperature. Most of that heat leaves in the discharge air, so it is not a room load until the air gives it up through the hot pipe walls. A federal compressed-air sourcebook (cited as plain text below) says more than 80 percent of the electrical energy going into a compressor becomes available heat. For a blower building, the practical point is that the discharge piping inside the room acts like a long radiator.
  • Other sources. Lighting, control panels and the sun on the roof. For panels see cooling control panels in hot plant rooms.

Ventilating or cooling the room

For most blower rooms the first tool is outside air: a louver with a filter, and a fan or louver to exhaust warm air. You size it with the sensible heat formula for air, Btu per hour = 1.08 x cfm x temperature rise in degrees F. A university heating and cooling note gives that formula and shows where 1.08 comes from: air's specific heat of 0.24 Btu per pound per degree F times roughly 4.5 pounds of air per minute for each 60 cfm. Mechanical cooling comes up when outdoor air is too hot or too dirty to carry the load, or when the room has to be kept sealed. Place the inlet and exhaust so the cool air passes the motor before it leaves, and keep the makeup air path open, because the blower itself also pulls air from the room if it is not ducted to an outside intake.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions for teaching, not plant data. The numbers use the standard conversion 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h.

  • Assumed motor electrical input at the operating point: 100 kW.
  • Assumed motor efficiency: 95 percent.
  • Assumed casing, gearbox and exposed pipe heat to the room: 4 kW.
  • Assumed allowed temperature rise, room over outside air: 15 degrees F.

Step 1, motor loss. 100 kW x (1 - 0.95) = 5 kW. In Btu/h: 5 x 3,412 = 17,060 Btu/h.

Step 2, other heat. 4 kW x 3,412 = 13,648 Btu/h.

Step 3, total sensible load. 17,060 + 13,648 = 30,708 Btu/h.

Step 4, airflow. cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature rise) = 30,708 / (1.08 x 15) = 30,708 / 16.2 = about 1,896, so call it 1,900 cfm.

Check. 1.08 x 1,900 x 15 = 30,780 Btu/h, which covers the 30,708 load. Motor loss alone would need 17,060 / 16.2 = about 1,053 cfm, so leaving out the other heat would undersize the fan by nearly half. If the room measured 40 x 30 x 15 ft (18,000 cubic feet, also assumed), 1,900 cfm is 1,900 x 60 / 18,000 = about 6.3 air changes per hour. Allowing a 10 degree rise instead raises the need to 30,708 / 10.8 = about 2,840 cfm, so the allowed rise matters a great deal.

Inlet filters and their pressure drop

A blower moves air by making pressure, and the filter sits in front of it. A loaded filter makes the blower work against more resistance on the suction side. In our own reasoning, that means more power for the same air mass, more heat released, and for some centrifugal units a lower flow before the controls respond. Treat the filter differential gauge or transmitter as a maintenance clue and compare it with the maker's limit on the nameplate or in the manual. The federal compressed-air sourcebook says to compare inlet pressure to atmospheric and check differentials against manufacturer specifications; the same habit applies here. Never run a blower with the filter removed, because the particles go to the diffusers.

Hot discharge piping and burn protection

The discharge pipe is the hottest surface a person is likely to touch. A federal space agency touch-temperature brief gives a skin-injury limit of 49 degrees C (about 120 degrees F). Pipe that carries hot compressed air is often well above that. A federal worker-safety interpretation letter (archived, so check current practice) explains that exposed hot surfaces are a hazard when a person can reach them, that exposed steam and hot-water pipes near work areas are to be insulated or guarded under certain general standards, and that PPE and lockout rules still apply when thermal energy is present. In practice, that means insulation with a durable jacket, or a guard, on piping within reach of walkways, and a warning label. Insulation also trims the room load. Before work, let the pipe cool, lock out and verify.

Noise enclosures trap heat

Blowers are loud. A university aeration chapter notes that the low-frequency thud of positive displacement blowers is harder to dampen than the higher-frequency whine of centrifugal units. The federal worker-safety noise rule sets a permissible exposure of 90 dBA for 8 hours, with a hearing conservation action level of 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours; the allowed time halves for roughly every 5 dBA above 90 (95 dBA for 4 hours, 100 dBA for 2 hours). An acoustic hood or enclosure can bring levels down, but it is also a box around a heat source. If it has no cooling air path, the motor and gearbox run hotter than their ratings assume. Ask whether the enclosure has its own fan, how its air is ducted, and whether the room air is being counted twice.

Corrosive gas from nearby process tanks

Blower buildings often sit next to headworks, digesters and process tanks that give off hydrogen sulfide. The federal worker-safety hydrogen sulfide page lists a general industry ceiling of 20 ppm and an immediately dangerous level of 100 ppm, and notes that the gas can destroy the sense of smell, so odor is not a warning. Our own practical point is that any outside-air louver should be placed away from tank vents, and a gas detector in the room is worth asking about. Corrosion of coils, motors and panels in that environment is covered in sewage lift station ventilation, hydrogen sulfide and corrosion; the same intake placement ideas apply, and a coil in the airstream is the first thing to suffer.

Field card: aeration blower buildings

Motor loss plus casing and pipe heat sets the airflow; the allowed rise sets how big it is.

  • Motor heat = input kW x (1 - efficiency) x 3,412 Btu/h per kW.
  • cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x allowed rise in degrees F).
  • Add casing, gearbox and exposed pipe heat, not just the motor.
  • Watch the inlet filter differential; a loaded filter costs power.
  • Hot discharge pipe within reach needs insulation or a guard.
  • Noise enclosures need their own cooling air.
  • Keep intakes away from tank vents; ask about gas detection.
