HFO and HFO-Blend Refrigerants: What They Replace, Safety Classes, GWP, Glide and Oil

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The labels on newer equipment keep showing refrigerant names that did not exist when many of us started: R-1234yf, R-513A, R-448A, R-454B. This lesson sorts them out: what an HFO is, which older refrigerants these tend to replace, how their safety classes and GWP numbers compare, and what changes at the gauges and with the oil.

Check the label and the current rule

Check the label and the current rule. The equipment data plate, the manufacturer instructions and the federal rules control what you may charge. This page is general education built from federal and national-lab pages read in 2026. Rules are being reconsidered, so confirm dates on the EPA pages below before you rely on them.

What an HFO is

An HFO is a hydrofluoroolefin. EPA describes HFOs as unsaturated HFCs, meaning the molecule has a carbon-carbon double bond. Their GWP (global warming potential) is very low compared with the older HFCs. Two pure HFOs show up in our trade: R-1234yf and R-1234ze(E). EPA's 100-year table lists both at a GWP of 1. Other EPA fact sheets list slightly different single-digit values for them (for example 4 and 6). Say which table you used when you quote a GWP.

Most of what you will meet in stationary equipment is not a pure HFO. It is an HFO/HFC blend: HFO molecules mixed with HFC molecules (and sometimes others) to hit a target pressure, capacity and GWP. R-513A, R-448A, R-449A, R-454B and R-452B are all examples.

What each one tends to replace

  • R-448A and R-449A are low-GWP replacement candidates for R-404A (and R-507A, R-22 and others) in commercial refrigeration. A national-lab measurement report calls R448A, R449A and R452B potential low-GWP replacements for R404A, and an EPA webinar calls the first two a long-term replacement for R-404A, R-507, R-22, R-408A and R-402A.
  • R-452B and R-454B are listed by EPA as acceptable, with use conditions, in residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps, the market that R-410A has held. The national-lab report calls R452B a proposed replacement for R410A.
  • R-513A is listed by EPA as an acceptable substitute in centrifugal, reciprocating and screw chillers, and in supermarket systems. It sits with R-134a-type applications; we kept the exact pairing off the page because we did not read a government page that says it in one line.
  • R-1234ze(E) is listed in chillers (EPA says it is commercially available in small and medium positive-displacement chillers) and for supermarket systems in new equipment. R-1234yf is listed for supermarket systems in new equipment as well.

Remember that listed for new equipment is not the same as a drop-in. Whether an existing system can be converted is a manufacturer question; see Retrofitting R-404A Refrigeration to Lower-GWP Blends.

Safety classes in plain text

The refrigerant safety standard (Standard 34) gives each refrigerant a letter and number. The class is a letter and a number, and the number tracks flammability. EPA's flammable-refrigerant webinar places A1 at the low-flammability end and describes A2L as a flame that exceeded the test angle with slow flame spread, and says A2Ls are harder to ignite than A3 refrigerants such as propane.

EPA SNAP listings give A1 for R-404A, R-410A, R-448A, R-449A and R-513A, and A2L for R-1234yf, R-1234ze(E), R-452B, R-454B and R-32. The A1 versus A2L split is the practical headline: a pure HFO or an A2L blend needs the flammable-refrigerant handling covered in A2L Refrigerants Guide, and the wider A2L and A3 picture is in A2L and A3 Refrigerants. R-448A and R-449A are A1, so they do not bring that.

Glide, and why blends are charged as liquid

A blend with more than one component can boil and condense across a range of temperatures at one pressure. That range is temperature glide. A NIST report measured glide at its average test conditions: R404A about 0.5 K, R449A 5.39 K, R448A 5.74 K and R452B 1.24 K. In Fahrenheit that is roughly 1, 10, 10 and 2 degrees (our own conversion, multiplying by 1.8). Those are test-condition numbers, not a charging chart. For field work, use the manufacturer's pressure-temperature chart for the blend, and use the one for the exact blend.

Why it matters: glide changes how you read superheat and subcooling. An EPA webinar on the 448A/449A blends tells technicians to learn glide before the transition and to use proper superheat and subcooling methods.

Charging as liquid follows from the same behavior. The components of a blend do not have the same volatility, and EPA's technician test topics list the "fractionation problem", the tendency of blend components to leak at different rates. In our own plain explanation, pulling vapor from a cylinder would draw off more of the lighter component first and shift the mix, while liquid leaves the cylinder as the blend. So charge from the liquid side with the cylinder oriented the way its label says, and meter it in. EPA's webinar adds that for these two blends the system effects of fractionation are small, but the habit stays the same.

Oil

Blends like R-448A and R-449A are used with polyol ester (POE) oil. The EPA webinar on R-448A and R-449A lists "change oil from mineral to POE" as a retrofit step. A national-lab report on HFC/POE systems explains why moisture matters: the POE lubricant decomposes with water, forming acids, and driers reduce the free water that drives that reaction. Handle POE with the system open as briefly as you can, and use the compressor maker's oil specification for the exact grade. The full oil picture is in Compressor Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE.

Federal context: the HFC phasedown rules

EPA's Technology Transitions program restricts the use of high-GWP HFCs in new products and systems by sector. EPA's sector page, which it says was last updated August 28, 2026 and is informational only (the rule text is 40 CFR Part 84, Subpart B), lists examples such as a GWP limit of 700 for residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump products and systems from January 1, 2025, and a limit of 150 for stand-alone retail food refrigeration units from the same date. For supermarket systems and remote condensing units it shows interim and later limits (1,400 first, then 150 or 300 from January 1, 2032, depending on charge size and configuration). Several of those entries carry footnotes to a reconsideration rule, so dates may move. When you recover and reuse refrigerant, see Refrigerant Recovery, Recycle and Reclaim.

Worked example (our own example)

Compare the CO2-equivalent of one charge. Assumption (ours): a unitary system holds 10 lb of refrigerant. The GWP values come from EPA's Technology Transitions GWP reference table (100-year): R-410A 2,088, R-454B 465, R-452B 698. CO2e is charge in pounds times GWP.

  1. R-410A: 10 lb × 2,088 = 20,880 lb CO2e.
  2. R-454B: 10 lb × 465 = 4,650 lb CO2e.
  3. R-452B: 10 lb × 698 = 6,980 lb CO2e.
  4. Difference, R-410A to R-454B: 20,880 − 4,650 = 16,230 lb CO2e, about 78 percent less (16,230 ÷ 20,880 = 0.777).
  5. Difference, R-410A to R-452B: 20,880 − 6,980 = 13,900 lb CO2e, about 67 percent less (13,900 ÷ 20,880 = 0.666).

This is a per-charge comparison on one assumed charge size. Real systems differ in charge, and a leak releases only part of a charge. It does show why GWP, not charge alone, drives the size of the footprint.

Field card: HFO and HFO-blend refrigerants

Check the class, then check the glide, then charge liquid.

  • HFO means unsaturated HFC (a carbon double bond); pure HFOs: R-1234yf and R-1234ze(E), GWP 1 on EPA's 100-year table.
  • R-448A and R-449A: A1, replace R-404A-type refrigerants; R-454B and R-452B: A2L, R-410A-type air conditioning and heat pumps.
  • A1 is the low-flammability end; A2L is lower flammability with slow flame spread.
  • Glide: learn it first and use the maker's pressure-temperature chart for superheat and subcooling.
  • Charge blends as liquid; expect POE oil and keep moisture out.
  • GWP: R-404A 3,922; R-410A 2,088; R-448A 1,386; R-513A 630; R-454B 465.
