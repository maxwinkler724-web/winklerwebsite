Coffee Roaster Exhaust: Chaff Collectors, Afterburners and Roastery Ventilation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A small coffee roastery looks like a gas-fired drum in a room, but the air path behind it does most of the work: it carries away smoke, chaff and heat, and it is where fires and smell complaints start. This lesson follows that path from the drum to the roof, then puts a rough number on the fuel an afterburner burns.

Check the sources

Check the sources. Roaster makers, air agencies and fire officials set the real requirements for a given site. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. It is not an engineering design or legal advice.

The exhaust path, in order

EPA's air emission factor chapter on coffee roasting (AP-42 section 9.13.2) describes roasters as horizontal rotating drums that tumble beans in hot combustion gases, run in batches or continuously, with gas temperatures in the roasting range of about 698 to 1004 degrees F. It says particulate from roasting and cooling is typically ducted to cyclones. A cyclone spins the air so heavy chaff, the papery skin that flakes off the bean, drops into a bin while the air leaves the top. After that, the chapter says, roaster exhaust is typically sent to a thermal oxidizer, which most people call an afterburner. Then an exhaust fan pushes the cleaned air up a stack. The figure shows the order we use in this lesson.

Not every layout puts the fan in the same spot, and some roasters carry the fan and the oxidizer inside the machine. One maker's small-roaster page shows a gas afterburner that mounts directly above the exhaust of the chaff collector, with a 10 inch exhaust connection and a ceramic insulation rating up to 1200 degrees C. Follow the manual for the exact unit; we only use these as examples of what exists.

Smoke, odor and the afterburner

AP-42 says separate oxidizers are preferred and that they typically run between 1200 and 1500 degrees F. It also says catalytic oxidizers can reach equivalent control at lower temperatures, which is the reason to ask about them: lower temperature means less gas burned. The chapter lists filterable particulate of 0.12 pound per ton of green bean for a batch roaster with a thermal oxidizer. Most of its factors are based on limited test data.

Air agencies write temperatures into permits. A permit review report from a regional air agency in Oregon for a small gas-fired roaster (design maximum 100 pounds per hour) required exhaust gas held at 1,400 degrees F for at least 0.5 second, or another temperature and time the agency approves. The same document required a continuous final-chamber temperature monitor, calibrated yearly, and monthly records of green coffee roasted, fuel use and afterburner temperature, kept five years. It also set an opacity limit of 20 percent for no more than three minutes in an hour. That one is a basic permit for a source roasting under 30 green tons per year. It is one agency's rule, not New Jersey's.

For New Jersey, NJDEP's General Permits 101 slides describe a general permit as a pre-approved permit for a class of sources under N.J.A.C. 7:27-8.8. Coffee roasting is not named in them. We could not open the NJDEP page that lists exemptions, so we cannot say whether a small roaster needs a permit. Ask the department before the roaster is bought, since the oxidizer is easiest to specify at the start.

Chaff and duct fires

Chaff burns easily, and oil and smoke residue coats the duct walls. Roaster makers say so plainly. One maker warns that improper or insufficient cleaning or maintenance can cause fire or damage to equipment. Another says blocked airflow leads to uneven roasting, system malfunction or even fire. Their cleaning schedules, from their own pages:

  • Empty the chaff bin about every 10 batches (one maker says every 30 kg roasted) and at the end of each roasting day.
  • Weekly: open the exhaust inspection hatch with the machine cool and brush and vacuum the exhaust; clean the cyclone and chaff outlet.
  • Monthly or longer: pull the duct apart and clean the cyclone from above, the drum exhaust and the cooler exhaust duct.
  • Clean more often with bigger batches or darker roasts, says one maker.

Duct material matters too. One maker requires smooth-walled metal duct with seams sealed because its system runs at positive pressure, rated at least 260 degrees C (500 degrees F) on the drum exhaust and 150 degrees C on the bean cooler. Another recommends neutral or slight negative pressure in any duct attached to the roaster and says not to join several roasters to one duct. OSHA's combustible dust page lists food dusts such as sugar and flour among materials that burn rapidly as fine dust. It does not name coffee chaff, so we treat chaff as a fire hazard by maker warning, not as a labeled explosive dust. Our welding fume and dust collector lesson covers dust collectors in general.

Stack height and roof clearance

We did not find a single national number for how high a roaster stack must go. The height, distance from air intakes and clearance to the roof come from the maker's manual, the mechanical code the building official enforces, and any air permit. The point of the stack is to carry exhaust above the roof so it does not drift back into a window or makeup air intake. Our laboratory exhaust and stacks lesson explains discharge velocity and re-entrainment, and the same idea applies here. One maker suggests simple rain caps on vertical terminations.

Combustion air, makeup air and room heat

A gas drum burner and an afterburner both need combustion air. The sizing methods are in our combustion air lesson, and the burner nameplate and manual rule for these machines. Whatever the fan and burner pull out of the room has to come back in. A maker of a small electric roaster lists maximum drum exhaust of 130 m3/h (77 cfm) and cooling tray exhaust of 380 m3/h (224 cfm); gas-fired production roasters will be larger, so use your own machine's figures. For makeup air equipment and controls, see our makeup air unit lesson; for how open-flame cooking spaces handle it, see commercial kitchen hoods and makeup air.

Roaster heat goes to the room too. We found no maker figure for heat released into the room, so size the cooling or ventilation from the nameplate input and the manual, not from our example.

Roasting and grinding exposure

NIOSH says diacetyl and 2,3-pentanedione form naturally when coffee is roasted, and that grinding roasted beans adds surface area for off-gassing. Its coffee workers bulletin links them to obliterative bronchiolitis, an irreversible lung disease, found in five workers at one facility. It proposes 8-hour limits of 5 ppb for diacetyl and 9.3 ppb for 2,3-pentanedione, and 15-minute limits of 25 ppb and 31 ppb, and names three source areas: flavoring, grinding and packaging of unflavored coffee, and storage of roasted coffee while it off-gasses. A NIOSH health hazard evaluation of one roasting facility found all 13 full-shift samples above the recommended limits, with grinding, flavoring and packaging the highest, and called local exhaust on the grinders the main engineering control. For the HVAC side, that means capture at the grinder and the storage area, not just a bigger roof fan.

Worked example: afterburner fuel use

The sensible heat equation for standard air is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. We use these inputs, and every one is an assumption unless a source is named:

  • Exhaust flow: 400 cfm through the afterburner. Assumption, a round number for a small gas roaster; use the maker's figure for a real machine.
  • Air entering the afterburner: 350 degrees F. Assumption.
  • Oxidizer temperature: 1,400 degrees F, the figure in the Oregon permit review report above. AP-42 gives a 1200 to 1500 degrees F range.

Step 1: delta T = 1,400 - 350 = 1,050 degrees F.

Step 2: Btu/h = 1.08 x 400 x 1,050 = 432 x 1,050 = 453,600 Btu/h.

Step 3: one therm is 100,000 Btu, so 453,600 / 100,000 = about 4.5 therms per hour of burner operation.

Check: at 1,250 degrees F, delta T is 900, which gives 432 x 900 = 388,800 Btu/h, or about 3.9 therms per hour. The factor 1.08 is for standard air, and hot gas is less dense, so treat 4.5 as a rough upper bound. Burner efficiency, the heat the roast itself gives off, and any heat recovery are not counted. For the same 400 cfm of makeup air with a 20 degrees F outdoor day and a 70 degrees F room, our own estimate is 1.08 x 400 x 50 = 21,600 Btu/h to warm it.

Field card: Coffee roaster exhaust

Drum, cyclone, fan, afterburner, stack. Keep it clean, sealed and replaced with makeup air.

  • AP-42: roaster exhaust usually goes to cyclones, then a thermal oxidizer at about 1200 to 1500 degrees F.
  • Catalytic units can reach similar control at lower temperatures.
  • One Oregon permit set 1,400 degrees F for 0.5 second, plus temperature and fuel records.
  • Chaff and duct residue burn; makers call for daily bin emptying and weekly duct cleaning.
  • Fuel estimate: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T; 100,000 Btu = 1 therm; a rough upper bound.
  • Grinders and roasted coffee storage need local exhaust for diacetyl and 2,3-pentanedione.
  • Ask the air agency before buying the roaster.
