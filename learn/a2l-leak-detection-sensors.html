A2L Leak Detection Sensors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the A2L Refrigerants Guide

A growing number of residential and light commercial systems that use A2L refrigerants include a refrigerant sensor. It is a small part with a big job, and it deserves more respect than the average thermistor.

Why the sensor is there

A2L refrigerants are mildly flammable. They are difficult to ignite, but in an enclosed space a large enough leak could reach a concentration that matters. R-32, for one, is heavier than air according to its safety data sheet, so a leak tends to pool low rather than rise. A leak sensor gives the equipment a way to notice refrigerant in the air handler or coil area and respond before a small problem becomes a bigger one. Whether a particular product has a sensor, how many it has, and where it lives are manufacturer and model decisions, driven by the equipment standards and the refrigerant charge.

How the sensor works, in general

Published technician-level descriptions explain that these sensors react to a change in the surrounding air. One common family senses a change in how well the air mix conducts heat. Another uses a heated element whose electrical behavior changes when certain gases reach it. Both are very sensitive, which is good for catching small leaks, and also means they can react to things that are not refrigerant. Many are communicating sensors that wire back to a control board with a small harness and are installed at the factory in the coil section of the air handler.

What the system does when the sensor trips

The details differ, but the general pattern is consistent:

  • The sensor sends a signal to the control board.
  • The board enters a mitigation response. The indoor blower is commanded on to move air and dilute any refrigerant.
  • The board typically stops cooling and heating outputs and may block the call for heat, so the compressor and heat sources are not energized while the condition exists.
  • Some systems can also open zone dampers, send a signal to a building controller, or sound an alarm.
  • The system holds that response for a period and then checks the sensor again. The timing and how it resets are manufacturer specific.

Because the response needs the blower, these systems are generally designed to stay powered when idle so the protection is available. Do not assume the disconnect is a harmless thing to leave off for days. Follow the product's instructions.

At install and service, in general terms

  • Follow the manufacturer's installation and service instructions for that exact unit. Sensor location, orientation, harness routing, and the mitigation board it pairs with are all specified by the maker.
  • Verify the leak detection system works after install or any service on it, using the method the manufacturer describes. Some products include a way to trigger a test and confirm each response. Others, including at least one manufacturer's commercial sensor, have no test button, so use whatever verification method the manual gives.
  • Use only the replacement sensor the manufacturer specifies. A generic part may not be recognized.
  • Treat the harness gently. Keep it away from brazing heat, sharp edges and moving parts, and avoid over-tightening mounting hardware that can crack the sensor housing.
  • Never bypass, jumper out, unplug to silence, or otherwise defeat the sensor. A nuisance trip is a diagnostic clue, not an obstacle.

Common failure modes, at a general level

  • False alarms from other vapors. Technician literature points out that fumes from curing sealants, adhesives and solvents in or near the equipment can look like refrigerant to a sensitive sensor. Products meant for outdoor use applied in a closed mechanical space, with poor ventilation, are a known concern. Before blaming the sensor, ask what was recently built, painted or sealed nearby, and ventilate.
  • A real leak. The first assumption on a sensor alarm should be that refrigerant may really be present. Treat it that way until the manufacturer's procedure and a proper leak search say otherwise.
  • Wiring and connector problems. A loose plug or damaged harness can produce a sensor communication or sensor error on the board.
  • Sensor end of life or fault. Some sensors report their own errors. What counts as a failed sensor, and how long one lasts, is a manufacturer question.
  • Contamination or damage during service. Dirt, moisture, or a broken mounting ear can change how a sensor behaves.

Use the board's indicator lights or codes, which most makers document in the manual, to tell which case you have.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, thresholds, sensor positions or wiring steps, and those vary by manufacturer and by model.

Safe habits on an alarm

If you arrive to a unit in an active alarm, do not look for an ignition source to hide. Ventilate, avoid creating sparks, keep open flame away, and follow the manufacturer's process to find out whether refrigerant is truly present. A leak detector made for the refrigerant in the system, used the way its maker describes, is part of that. More on tools in A2L Service Safety and Tools.

Field card: A2L Leak Detection Sensors

The sensor is a safety device, not a comfort option.

  • Many A2L systems use a sensor that signals the control board.
  • A trip commonly runs the blower and stops cooling and heat. Details vary by maker.
  • Verify detection works after service, using the manufacturer's method.
  • Never bypass, jumper or unplug the sensor to make an alarm stop.
  • Check for off-gassing vapors, wiring faults and real leaks, in that order of thought, not guesswork.
