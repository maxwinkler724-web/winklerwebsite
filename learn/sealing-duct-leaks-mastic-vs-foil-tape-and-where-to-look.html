Sealing Duct Leaks: Mastic vs Foil Tape and Where to Look

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "how to seal air ducts," the short answer is: find the leaks that matter, seal them with mastic or a UL 181 listed tape, and test the result. The details below cover what to use, where to look, how leakage is measured, and the two safety issues that can turn a simple job into one for a professional.

How much air do ducts lose?

ENERGY STAR says that in a typical house about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through the duct system is lost to leaks, holes and poorly connected ducts. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America program puts it another way: the efficiency of air distribution systems has been found to be 60 to 75 percent or less in many houses. Leaks in ducts running through attics, crawl spaces, unfinished basements and garages can also let contaminants into the home and create pressure imbalances, according to the same DOE material. For the basic parts of a duct system, see Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Where the leaks usually are

  • Plenum and air handler. Building Science Corporation's Information Sheet 603 (a Building America document) says sealing the supply system includes the supply plenum and its attachment to the furnace or air handler, and that joints near the equipment get extra attention because of vibration and flexing.
  • Takeoffs and flex connections. The same sheet says the inner liner of insulated flex duct should be sealed where it connects to other ducts, plenums and boots.
  • Boots and registers. It says connections between grilles, registers and ducts at ceilings, floors and knee walls typically leak, and attic, basement or crawl space air can leak in or out where ducts meet the boot. The Building America Solution Center also checks that boots are durably sealed to the subfloor.
  • Panned returns. The Building America Solution Center says building cavities used as return plenums are probably the biggest duct leakage culprits, and that with a panned floor joist the suction pulls outside air in through the construction joints. Its guidance calls leakage in these cavities very difficult, if not impossible, to prevent.

Mastic, foil tape or duct tape?

Mastic is a thick paste brushed over joints. Information Sheet 603 says supply systems should be sealed with mastic to be airtight. DOE's mastic job aid describes the method for small gaps: clean the surface, press fiberglass mesh tape over the gap, then cover the mesh with mastic, extending at least 1 inch past the edge of the tape in all directions.

UL 181 foil tape. DOE's Building Science Education Solution Center says ducts should be sealed with mastic or metal tape that meets UL 181, 181A or 181B. Information Sheet 603 says tapes meeting UL 181A or 181B may provide reasonable performance, and that the surface must be clean, dry and dust-free. Look for the printed listing on the tape itself.

Cloth duct tape. ENERGY STAR's duct sealing page says never to use duct tape because it is not long-lasting. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory tested many sealants and reported that only duct tape failed: eleven products failed within days and some fell off the joint, while clear tapes, foil-backed tapes, mastics and an aerosol sealant held for the test. The researchers suspected heat degrades the glue. Information Sheet 603 gives the field version: after hot and cold cycling the adhesive dries out and fails.

Measuring leakage: duct blaster and CFM25

A duct blower is a calibrated fan with a manometer that pressurizes or depressurizes the duct system to 25 pascals (0.10 inch water column), according to the Building America Solution Center. The airflow needed to hold that pressure is written CFM25. A total leakage test counts leaks from all the connected ductwork. A leakage-to-outside test uses a blower door to bring the house to the same pressure so leaks into the house are not counted, which is the number that matters most when ducts run through an attic or crawl space. The test needs registers sealed off, and a smoke machine can pinpoint the leak spots. See What Happens in a Home Energy Audit for where this fits in an audit.

For new homes, ENERGY STAR's limits as listed by the Solution Center are total leakage of 4 CFM25 per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area (or 40 CFM25) at rough-in, and 8 per 100 square feet (or 80 CFM25) at final, whichever is greater. A DOE Building America sheet lists a typical maximum of 6 percent of design airflow.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

Assume a 2,000 square foot home with a system whose design airflow is 1,600 CFM, and a total duct leakage reading of 240 CFM25. These numbers are invented for teaching.

  1. Leakage as a share of airflow: 240 / 1,600 = 0.15, or 15 percent.
  2. Compare to the 6 percent figure: 6 percent of 1,600 = 0.06 x 1,600 = 96 CFM.
  3. New-home final limit for this size: 8 x (2,000 / 100) = 8 x 20 = 160 CFM25. That is greater than 80, so 160 applies. The 240 reading is 80 CFM25 over it.
  4. After sealing, suppose the retest reads 90 CFM25: 90 / 1,600 = 0.056, about 5.6 percent, and under 160.

Caution: this is a rough yardstick. A CFM25 reading is taken at a test pressure of 25 pascals, which is not the pressure the ducts see while running, so dividing it by airflow shows relative size, not exact air lost. The 160 limit applies to new construction. Your local program may use different numbers.

Safety: two stop signs

  • Backdrafting. Building America's guidance says to conduct a combustion safety test after ducts are sealed to ensure there is no backdrafting of gas or oil-burning appliances. Follow your employer's or the manufacturer's procedure; see also the combustion safety step in the audit lesson above.
  • Asbestos. EPA says to inspect heating and cooling systems before work to be sure there are no asbestos-containing materials, and that those materials require specialized procedures and should not be disturbed except by trained, equipped contractors. If you suspect asbestos anywhere in the system, stop and have a trained contractor assess it before you touch anything.

When to call a pro

Call a qualified contractor if you suspect asbestos, if gas or oil appliances sit near the return you want to seal, if the leaks are inside walls or joist bays you cannot reach, or if you want a measured result. Aerosol sealing is one option for hidden leaks: the Building America sheet says Aeroseal seals ducts from the inside and can reach inaccessible areas, and LBNL reported its aerosol sealant showed no significant change in tightness over two years of cycling. That is a contractor job. EPA also notes that encapsulating duct interiors is a different practice than sealing duct air leaks. For the cleaning question, see Should You Get Your Air Ducts Cleaned?

Field card: Sealing Duct Leaks

Mastic or listed tape. Never cloth tape. Test after.

  • Typical house loses about 20 to 30 percent of duct air (ENERGY STAR).
  • Check: plenum and furnace joints, takeoffs, boots, panned returns.
  • Mastic with mesh on gaps, or tape marked UL 181A or 181B on clean, dry metal.
  • CFM25 is duct leakage at 25 pascals. Percent of airflow = CFM25 / system CFM x 100.
  • Combustion safety test after sealing. Suspect asbestos: stop and call a pro.
