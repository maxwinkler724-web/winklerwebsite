Building Entrances and Infiltration: Doors, Vestibules and Stack Effect

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The front entrance is the biggest planned hole in a building envelope. Every time someone walks through, outdoor air moves in or out, and the amount depends on the door type, how long it stays open, and how much pressure is pushing on it. This lesson covers why entrances leak, how revolving doors and vestibules cut that leakage, what unit heaters and air curtains add, and why doors sometimes whistle or fight you.

Why air moves through an entrance

Three things push air through a doorway: temperature, wind and fans. A federal-lab report that models door infiltration builds its pressure input from stack effect and wind together, using an assumed 15 mph design wind and a neutral pressure plane at mid-height. A federal standards lab review of infiltration describes the heating-season pattern: indoor air is warmer and lighter, so air enters through low openings and leaves through high ones. With uniform leakage the neutral level sits at half the building height, and it moves up if the leaks are concentrated high.

That is why the ground-floor doors of a tall building pull air in on a cold day. In summer the same buoyancy argument runs the other way: cool indoor air is denser than the hot air outside, so the pattern reverses and air is pushed out at the ground floor (our explanation of the same physics). For the full tall-building story, including the neutral plane and elevator problems, see our stack effect lesson. This page stays at the entrance.

A closed door leaks little; an open door leaks a lot

A modeling guide from a national lab lists leakage inputs for closed doors at a test pressure of 0.30 in. w.c.: 1.00 cfm per square foot for swinging or revolving glass doors and vestibules, and 0.40 cfm per square foot for opaque doors and for sliding glass doors. It also uses a flow exponent of 0.65 for envelope leakage. Those are small numbers. The same guide notes that doors and windows are not the main leak paths in a building; the federal standards lab review says the same about joints and penetrations. The entrance matters because people open it. The worked example below shows an open door moving over a thousand times more air than the closed one.

The door types

  • Swinging doors. The simplest door and the leakiest in use. Each person holds the door open for a few seconds, and in a busy entrance the door is open a large share of the time.
  • Automatic sliding doors. They open wide and close on a timer. The opening is large while open, so a lot of air moves, but they stop leaking when they close. The federal-lab door model treats all exterior doors as automatic and says that likely overstates infiltration for buildings with manual doors.
  • Revolving doors. The wings form a sealed compartment, so at any moment there is a wall between inside and outside. Think of them as an entrance that is always closed. A model energy standard, as summarized in a state agency training deck, exempts entrances with revolving doors from the vestibule rule. A university master's project on six public buildings in a cold city found revolving doors can technically save energy, but not enough to justify the added cost in most of those buildings, because of low traffic, building height and wind. It also found people at existing revolving-door entrances often prefer the adjacent sliding door. A door nobody uses does not seal anything.

Vestibules: two doors, one airlock

A vestibule works by making sure the outside is never directly connected to the building. The same state training deck summarizes the model standard this way: an enclosed vestibule with self-closing devices, revolving doors, or a combination, with interior and exterior doors that cannot be open at the same time. It gives a minimum of 7 ft between the doors when closed, rising to 16 ft where the vestibule opens into a space of 40,000 ft² or more and both doors are automatic and self-closing. The deck lists exceptions by climate zone, building size and entrance type. Local codes adopt these rules differently, so confirm with the local building department.

Why depth matters is our own reasoning: a person, a cart or a stroller has to clear the first door and let it start closing before reaching the second. A shallow vestibule means both doors are open at once and you have built a short hallway, not an airlock. The federal-lab modeling report ran numbers for buildings with and without vestibules. Its table of peak infiltration shows a small office dropping from 275 cfm to 162 cfm and a medium office from 2,210 cfm to 1,438 cfm. By our arithmetic those are cuts of about 41 percent and 35 percent, and the largest building in the same table drops about 29 percent. The report also says yearly energy savings vary widely by building type, from a fraction of a percent to a few percent in the most entrance-heavy types, and are generally larger in colder climates. It found door-opening frequency was the most sensitive input.

Entrance heaters and air curtains

Even a good vestibule gets cold. Cabinet unit heaters in the vestibule warm the entry and keep a wet floor from icing near the door; our vestibule unit heater lesson covers fan control and coil freeze risk. Air curtains blow a sheet of air across the opening; sizing and use are covered in our air curtain lesson, so we do not repeat them here. For big truck doors see our loading dock lesson. Neither a heater nor a curtain replaces keeping both doors from opening together.

Doors that are hard to open or whistle

A researcher report on tall buildings lists lobby entrance doors that are hard to open, elevator doors that will not close, and cold drafts on lower floors as stack-effect problems. It adds that in leaky buildings the pressure across fire exit doors could exceed the limits on the force needed to open them. Its suggested fixes: keep the envelope airtight above the lobby, add airtight vestibules around entrance, elevator and stairwell doors, or add automatic door openers. It found that pressurizing floors differently to cancel stack effect was inappropriate for most buildings.

Whistling is our reading of the same cause: a high pressure across a small gap makes fast air through it. Seal the gap, fix the door closer, or reduce the pressure.

How air handlers help or hurt

The federal standards lab review says that with no temperature difference or wind, net supply air raises building pressure and net exhaust lowers it. A positive building pushes air out at the doors; a negative one pulls it in. It also says unbalanced design or poor testing and balancing can cause unplanned infiltration, and individual zones can be off even if the building total balances. The modeling guide says all infiltration is subject to the pressure the building HVAC system maintains, with the windward side usually seeing infiltration and the other sides exfiltration. In practice, a small amount of positive pressure from the air handlers (more outdoor air in than relief and exhaust out) reduces entrance drafts in winter. Too much makes doors hard to open outward and pushes moist air into walls. Relief and return fan control is in our building pressure control lesson.

Worked example (our own): one open door in a tall building

This is our own example with assumed inputs. Assumptions: a 300 ft tall building with a neutral plane at mid-height, so the ground-floor door is 150 ft below it; indoor 70°F, outdoor 20°F; one door open, 3 ft by 7 ft = 21 ft²; orifice coefficient 0.6 (a common assumed value); every other leak in the building ignored. The door is open 5 percent of the time (assumed).

  1. Air densities from the ideal gas relation, density = 39.67 / T (lb/ft³, T in °R). Outdoor: T = 20 + 459.67 = 479.67 °R, so 0.0827. Indoor: 529.67 °R, so 0.0749.
  2. Stack pressure = (0.0827 − 0.0749) lb/ft³ × 150 ft = 1.17 lb/ft². Divide by 5.2 to get inches of water: 0.225 in. w.c. (about 56 Pa). Check: the federal standards lab review gives about 0.02 Pa per meter per kelvin; at 45.7 m and 27.8 K above and below the neutral plane that scales to roughly 55 Pa. It agrees.
  3. Velocity with the standard-air relation V = 4005 × √(0.225) = 4005 × 0.474 = 1,900 fpm. Times the 0.6 coefficient = 1,140 fpm.
  4. Airflow = 1,140 fpm × 21 ft² = 23,940, call it 24,000 cfm.
  5. Heat to warm it = 1.08 × 24,000 cfm × (70 − 20) = 1,296,000 Btu/h while the door is open. At 5 percent open that averages about 65,000 Btu/h. (The 1.08 factor is 0.075 × 0.24 × 60 for standard air.)
  6. Compare with closed. Closed leakage from the lab guide: 1.00 cfm/ft² × 21 ft² = 21 cfm at 0.30 in. w.c. At 0.225 in. w.c.: 21 × (0.225/0.30)0.65 = 21 × 0.83 = about 17 cfm. The open door moves roughly 1,400 times more air.
  7. Door force. Pressure on the door = 1.17 lb/ft² × 21 ft² = 25 lb total. It acts at the door center, so the pull at the handle edge is about half, 12 lb, before closer or latch effects. A closer set for light pressure will not hold at this load.

Reality check: the modeling report's peak without a vestibule for a medium office is 2,210 cfm, far below 24,000. Our number is a deliberate ceiling: a real building has many other leaks that raise the inside pressure at the door and cut flow, and doors do not stay open. It shows why the pressure and open time, not the door leaf, set the load.

Field card: Building Entrances and Infiltration

Keep both doors from being open at once.

  • Stack effect pulls air in at low doors in winter; summer reverses it.
  • Revolving doors act as an always-closed entrance, but only if people use them.
  • A vestibule works only if its depth keeps both doors from opening together.
  • Open door load: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Hard-to-open or whistling doors point to pressure: check stack effect, then fan balance.
  • Unit heaters and air curtains help comfort; they do not replace an airlock.
