Refrigerant Blend Fractionation: Vapor Leaks vs Liquid Leaks, Liquid Charging, Topping Off and Shifted Charge Signs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A blend is not one fluid. It is a recipe, and the recipe can change after the system has left the factory. This lesson covers how that happens, which kind of leak does the most damage, why you pull blends from the cylinder as liquid, and what to do about a system whose charge may have shifted. We use R-407C and R-404A as examples of existing systems.

Why a blend separates at all

Each part of a zeotropic blend has its own boiling point. At the same temperature, the lower-boiling part has the higher vapor pressure, so it is over-represented in the vapor sitting above the liquid. A university chemistry handout on boiling diagrams puts it plainly: at any given pressure the vapor is richer in the more volatile component, and the liquid left behind is richer in the other one. That is the whole principle behind distillation, and it is also the reason a refrigerant blend can drift. Fractionation is just distillation happening by accident.

The NIST leak and recharge model for refrigerant blends describes the same cause: composition changes come from preferential evaporation of one or more components, so vapor and liquid do not share a composition. For the names and numbers on the nameplate, see How Refrigerant Numbers Work. For bubble and dew points, see Refrigerant Temperature Glide. We will not repeat those here.

Vapor leaks vs liquid leaks

The NIST guide states the key rule: during a vapor leak the escaping refrigerant has the same composition as the vapor inside the system, and during a liquid leak it has the composition of the liquid. So the two leaks are not equal even when the same weight escapes.

  • Liquid-side leak. The escaping mix matches the liquid, which is close to the nameplate recipe. The charge is lighter, but its composition stays close to the original.
  • Vapor-side leak. The escaping mix is richer in the lower-boiling part. What stays in the system is slightly richer in the higher-boiling parts, and each leak-and-top-off cycle can push it further.

Real systems hold liquid and vapor in several places (receiver, evaporator, suction line, condenser), so the real picture is more complicated than either case. A 1996 conference paper from an equipment maker's engineers tested R-407C in a split heat pump with simulated leaks. It reported that leaks in the two-phase region after the expansion device lost vapor richer in R-32, and that composition changed most in the first leak simulations. R-407C is nominally R-32, R-125 and R-134a at 23, 25 and 52 percent by mass, and R-404A is R-125, R-143a and R-134a at 44, 52 and 4 percent, both per the NIST guide's blend list.

Why blends are charged as liquid

The same effect happens inside the cylinder. A cylinder holds liquid with vapor above it. Draw vapor and you pull the lower-boiling part first, and the liquid left behind slowly turns into a different blend. The cylinder you charged from will then be off-recipe for the next job, too. A maker's R-407C service manual says to use liquid refrigerant to charge, to stand cylinders with a siphon pipe upright for liquid, and warns that charging with gas changes the composition in the cylinder and performance may drop. A trade-press article on liquid charging says technicians commonly take 400-series blends in the liquid phase for this reason.

Throttling liquid in safely

Liquid straight into a compressor can damage it, so liquid charging means metering. The trade-press article describes partly opening the manifold valve so the low side sees roughly 10 psi above the current suction pressure, and closing the valve often so too much liquid does not arrive at once. Go slowly, watch the suction line, and weigh the charge in when you can; see Charging by Weight. On a system with a receiver and king valve, the same article describes adding liquid straight to the liquid line, but notes it is harder to judge the amount, so check condenser subcooling. Follow the equipment maker's instructions where they differ.

Worked example (our own, simplified)

This is our own two-component illustration. It is not real composition data for any refrigerant. We assume ideal behavior, and the assumptions are stated so you can check the arithmetic.

Assumptions. Component A is the lower-boiling part, B the higher-boiling part. At the system temperature, pure A has a vapor pressure of 300 kPa and pure B has 100 kPa (assumed). The system is liquid-dominated, 100 mol total, starting at 50 mol percent A. Vapor holds negligible moles. The vapor leak removes 10 mol, and we top off with 10 mol of the original 50/50 mix.

Step 1, vapor above the liquid (Raoult's law). Partial pressure of A = 0.50 x 300 = 150 kPa. Partial pressure of B = 0.50 x 100 = 50 kPa. Total = 200 kPa. Vapor fraction of A = 150 / 200 = 0.75, so the vapor is 75 percent A against 50 percent in the liquid.

Step 2, vapor leak of 10 mol. A lost = 10 x 0.75 = 7.5 mol. B lost = 2.5 mol. Left: A = 50 - 7.5 = 42.5 mol, B = 50 - 2.5 = 47.5 mol.

Step 3, top off with 5 mol A and 5 mol B. A = 47.5, B = 52.5, total 100. Composition = 47.5 percent A. One cycle moved A from 50.0 to 47.5 percent.

Liquid leak for comparison. The 10 mol lost is 5 A and 5 B (the liquid mix), and the top-off adds the same back. Composition stays at 50.0 percent A.

Repeating the vapor case. Each pass recalculates the vapor mix from the new liquid. Our arithmetic gives A at 47.5, 45.2 and 43.1 percent after cycles one, two and three.

A near-azeotropic comparison. Now assume pure A is only 110 kPa and B is 100 kPa (assumed). Vapor fraction of A = (0.5 x 110) / (0.5 x 110 + 0.5 x 100) = 55 / 105 = 0.524. A 10 mol vapor leak removes 5.24 mol A and 4.76 mol B, and the top-off gives A = 44.76 + 5 = 49.76 mol of 100, or 49.8 percent. After three cycles it is 49.4 percent, compared with 43.1 percent for the wide-split blend. That is why a low-glide, near-azeotropic blend shifts much less than a high-glide blend: its parts are close enough in volatility that the vapor looks almost like the liquid. It does not make topping off a free pass, only a smaller error per cycle. The glide lesson explains how R-404A and R-407C differ on this scale, and the HFO blend lesson covers newer blends.

Topping off vs recovering the whole charge

Guidance varies by maker, so read the manual for the exact unit. What we found: one maker's R-407C manual describes the leak procedure as recovering all the refrigerant first, repairing, then recharging the specified amount as liquid. It does not describe topping off. The 1996 R-407C paper also leans toward recovering the remaining charge and recharging to the nameplate recipe, and cautions against relying on superheat or subcooling alone to decide how much to add. If the manual allows a top-off on a near-azeotropic blend such as R-404A, that choice is the maker's to give; it is not ours.

The EPA rules still apply either way. EPA states you must evacuate equipment to the required vacuum levels when opening it for service, and that recovery equipment must be used per its manufacturer's directions. Recovered refrigerant can be reused without restriction in the same owner's equipment, but if ownership changes it must be reclaimed. See Retrofitting R-404A Refrigeration for existing R-404A systems.

Signs of a shifted charge

None of these signs proves a shifted charge by itself. They are our own reasoning from how blends behave, and each one has other possible causes, so check the usual suspects first.

  • A system with a history of leaks and repeated top-offs, especially a vapor-side leak on a wide-glide blend.
  • Capacity that stays low while pressures and temperatures look reasonable on the chart.
  • Superheat or subcooling that will not settle at believable values when you use the correct dew and bubble columns.
  • Suction and head pressures that disagree with the saturation temperatures you measure.
  • Refrigerant identifier or lab results that do not match the nameplate recipe.

The usual remedy is to recover the charge, repair the leak, and recharge by weight with liquid from fresh refrigerant. Whether the recovered mix is reused or sent for reclamation depends on the reuse rules above.

Recovering into the right cylinder

EPA defines reclamation as reprocessing to the specifications in the rule's appendix, verified by its analytical method. Our reasoning is that a mixed cylinder is hard to bring back to specification, so keep each refrigerant in its own marked recovery cylinder and never mix. Use a cylinder rated for the refrigerant, check its markings and fill limit, and label it before you start. See Refillable and Recovery Cylinders and Push-Pull Liquid Recovery.

Vapor carries the light end. Liquid carries the recipe.

  • The lower-boiling part dominates the vapor, so a vapor leak removes more of it.
  • A liquid leak removes the recipe mix, so composition stays close to nameplate.
  • Draw blends from the cylinder as liquid; throttle in slowly at the suction side.
