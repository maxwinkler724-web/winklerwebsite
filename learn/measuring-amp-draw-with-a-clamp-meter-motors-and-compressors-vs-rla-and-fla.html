Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter: Motors and Compressors vs RLA and FLA

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You clamp a compressor lead and the meter says 11.4 amps. The nameplate says RLA 13.1. Is that good? This lesson shows how to take the reading correctly and what to compare it to, because the answer is not as simple as "stay under the nameplate."

Clamp meter basics

A clamp meter reads the magnetic field around a conductor, so you never break the circuit. The key rule from Fluke's guidance is to clamp one conductor at a time. If the jaws enclose two or more conductors, their fields cancel and the display is wrong. A flexible clamp is one way to reach a crowded bundle without pulling live wires aside.

Motors draw far more current for a moment at start than they do running. Fluke explains that a digital display has trouble catching a number that changes that fast, so many clamp meters have an inrush button. You set the dial to inrush, put the jaws on the live conductor, press the button to arm the meter, then start the motor. Fluke says the meter takes about 400 samples over roughly 100 milliseconds to calculate the surge. It also says inrush can run about 4 to 10 times the running current depending on the motor, and that a brief surge above a breaker's rating does not necessarily trip it, because breakers follow a time-versus-current curve.

Your meter must also be rated for the circuit you are on. See Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings. Measuring with the power on means working near live parts and moving fans. Review Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC so you know when to de-energize and when a live reading is the job.

What RLA, LRA and FLA mean

These are covered line by line in Reading an HVAC Nameplate. In short: RLA is the rated-load amps for a compressor, LRA is the locked-rotor amps (the current with the rotor stalled, close to what a start asks for), and FLA is the full-load amps for a motor such as a fan or blower. Carrier's product data table for its N4A3 condensing units lists all three side by side, plus the acceptable voltage range.

Why running amps below RLA is normal

RLA is not a prediction of what the compressor draws today. ACHR News columnist John Tomczyk writes that RLA is a calculated value used to get UL approval, found by dividing the maximum continuous current by 1.56 (some makers use 1.44). It sets the overload trip point and helps size wire, fuses and contactors. He warns that it should not be used to judge whether a compressor is good or bad, since the nameplate number does not change as temperatures and pressures change.

What changes the real draw? Tomczyk lists ambient temperature, condensing and evaporating temperatures, return gas temperature, liquid temperature and voltage. A compressor working against a mild head pressure on a cool day will draw less than RLA, and that is healthy.

The better comparison is the manufacturer's performance data. Dave Bell, a Copeland training specialist, wrote in ACHR News that you should read suction and discharge pressure, find the expected amps on the compressor's curve sheet, and accept about plus or minus 10 percent. His example is a Copeland compressor with RLA 59.6 at 43 psig suction and 260 psig condensing: the curve predicted about 44 amps and he measured 43.

What high or low amps can point to

  • Higher than the curve predicts. Bell says this suggests extra mechanical load such as worn bearings, and on a single-phase compressor a bad run capacitor can do it too. His point was that 59 amps at those pressures is too high even though it is under the 59.6 RLA.
  • Much lower than the curve predicts. This suggests less mechanical work is being done. He estimates a broken connecting rod would drop the draw about 30 percent. Tomczyk's example lists a bad valve, worn rings, a leaking gasket, a restriction, an undercharge or a weak motor as reasons for a reading far below expected.
  • Pressure also matters. Bell warns against condemning a compressor because suction pressure is high. If the amps match the curve at those pressures, find what raised the pressures.

Amps alone do not name the fault. Use them with pressures and the lessons on compressor failure modes, capacitors and contactors and checking compressor windings.

Blower and condenser fan motors against FLA

Fans are simpler because the nameplate FLA is the number to compare. Rob Falke, writing in Contracting Business, describes clamping one wire only, with the blower door on (removing it makes the fan work harder), the system in cooling and the fan on high. He reports that 80 to 90 percent of FLA is common on direct-drive residential units, and that most motors draw 70 to 100 percent of FLA on high speed at 400 CFM per ton. Above FLA means an overloaded, hot motor. Around half of FLA or less points to a serious airflow problem. Neil Simon in ACHR News adds that the motor should be measured in its normal setup, with blower and enclosure in place, or the number can read misleadingly low. For failing fan motors, see Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures. ECM motors behave differently; see ECM Blower Motors.

Voltage drop under load

Amps and voltage move opposite ways. Bell says a 5 percent change in voltage produces about a 5 percent opposite change in amps, so a sagging supply makes a motor pull more current. Measure voltage at the unit with it off and again while it runs. A big difference means a problem in the wiring or supply. The National Electrical Code recommends, in informational notes, no more than 3 percent drop on a branch circuit (5 percent for feeder plus branch), but EEPower notes these are recommendations, not mandatory rules. Also check the range the maker allows. Carrier's table gives 197 to 253 volts for a 208/230 volt unit, and Tecumseh's voltage bulletin lists minimum and maximum voltages per nameplate rating, noting that out-of-range supplies need extra protection.

Worked example

Carrier's N4A348GHB (three-phase, 208/230 volt) lists compressor RLA 13.1 A, LRA 83.1 A and fan FLA 1.4 A. The readings below are hypothetical.

  1. Clamp one compressor lead while the unit runs: 11.4 A.
  2. Percent of RLA: 11.4 / 13.1 = 0.870, or 87 percent.
  3. Start surge ratio from the plate: LRA / RLA = 83.1 / 13.1 = 6.34, so a start asks for roughly six times rated load.
  4. Clamp the condenser fan lead: 1.2 A. 1.2 / 1.4 = 0.857, or 86 percent of FLA, in the range described above.
  5. Voltage with the unit off: 232 V. Running: 221 V. Drop is 11 V. 11 / 232 = 0.047, or 4.7 percent. That is inside Carrier's 197 to 253 volt range but worth chasing.

The 87 percent figure alone says "below RLA." The honest next step is to read pressures and look up the compressor's expected amps, accepting about 10 percent either way. The band around RLA would be 11.8 to 14.4 A, but the true expected value depends on conditions and may be lower.

Field card: Measuring Amp Draw

Running amps are not RLA.

  • Clamp one conductor only. Two conductors cancel.
  • Use inrush mode to catch the start surge.
  • Compare compressor amps to the maker's curve, about plus or minus 10 percent.
  • Fans: compare to nameplate FLA, with blower and enclosure in place.
  • Check voltage off and running. About 5 percent low voltage gives about 5 percent more amps.
  • Amps point toward a cause. They do not name it.
