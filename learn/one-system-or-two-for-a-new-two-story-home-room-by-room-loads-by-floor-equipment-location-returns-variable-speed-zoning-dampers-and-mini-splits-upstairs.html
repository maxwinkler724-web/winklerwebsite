One System or Two for a New Two-Story Home: Loads by Floor, Returns, Zoning and Mini-Splits

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A new two-story house gives you a choice that a retrofit rarely does: decide before the walls close whether one system serves both floors or each floor gets its own. The right answer comes from the room-by-room numbers, so this lesson walks through how the loads split by floor and what that means for equipment, returns and ducts.

Why the floors want different things

Heat rises, and the top floor sits under the roof, so in summer the upper rooms usually carry more cooling load per square foot than the rooms below. In winter the opposite tends to hold: the lower floor loses more heat, because it has the larger exposed footprint, the slab or crawlspace or basement below it, and the entry doors. A single thermostat on one floor cannot see both problems. For the symptoms side of this, see Upstairs Too Hot: Uneven Temperatures in a Two-Story House. This lesson is about preventing it at design time.

Start with the room-by-room load

Both options begin the same way: a room-by-room load calculation using the national residential load method, followed by equipment selection from the maker's performance data at design conditions. The DOE Building America mini-split guide on the Solution Center says to do the load calculation for each zone and for the whole house, and to size from the manufacturer's expanded performance tables rather than from rating-label data. It also warns that oversized equipment short-cycles, wastes energy and controls humidity poorly. Ask the designer for the room loads on paper, with cooling and heating shown separately and summed by floor.

One zoned system or two systems

A DOE Building America guide to home HVAC says that in large homes, zoning is usually done better with multiple systems, one unit per floor, than with dampers in a single system. The same guide notes that damper zoning affects system efficiency and balance. The mechanics of dampers, bypass and zone panels are covered in Forced-Air Zoning Explained; here the question is only whether you need them.

  • One system with dampers. Fewer pieces of equipment and one outdoor unit. The fan and trunk have to serve a summer split and a winter split that are different (see the example below), so duct design and damper control matter a great deal.
  • Two systems. Each fan, duct run and return only has to match its own floor. Cost is more equipment and more places for it to live, which is a design issue before it is a purchase issue.

A third path is a hybrid: central ducted equipment for the lower floor and ductless or short-duct mini-splits for the upper floor.

Where to put the equipment

The DOE guide recommends putting the furnace or air handler and the ducts inside the conditioned space where possible, such as a utility closet inside the house, because that saves energy and helps air quality. In a two-story plan that often means a closet or chase on each floor, or a centrally stacked chase so duct runs stay short. Two systems are easier to lay out when the plan reserves a small closet upstairs; one system is easier when a single mechanical room sits in the middle of the floor plan. Ducts between floors inside open-web floor trusses are another conditioned-space option the guide describes. Equipment and line routes are covered in the pre-installation site survey lesson.

Return air on each floor

The same DOE guide lists undersized central returns as a common problem and suggests larger returns, jump ducts or transfer grilles as fixes. In a new two-story home, we would plan a return path on each floor from the start, so the upstairs does not depend on air finding its way down a stairwell, and so closed bedroom doors do not starve a room. Sizing is in Sizing Return Air and Filter Grilles.

Variable-speed equipment and dampers

DOE guidance notes that variable-speed blower motors match airflow to the need and that most systems rated at higher efficiency levels use them, but it cautions that poor ducts can cancel out the savings. The mini-split page adds that inverter-driven compressors can ramp down to as little as 10 percent of rated capacity. Variable speed is what lets one floor's equipment run at a low, steady output in mild weather. It does not remove the need for a duct system that fits the load; see Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Furnaces and Air Handlers.

Mini-splits for the upper floor

The Solution Center page makes several points a designer should weigh. Each indoor unit runs independently, so rooms can be set to different temperatures. Ductless systems have no ducts, which removes duct losses; the page says those losses can exceed 30 percent of heating and cooling energy when ducts run through unconditioned areas. In a multi-split, all indoor units on one outdoor unit must be in the same mode, and turning units off saves little and can cause cycling if the load falls below the outdoor unit's minimum. Ductless units typically have poorer filtration and dehumidify less well than ducted equipment, and ceiling-mounted units pull warm air from the room, which hurts heating. A ducted mini-split air handler with short duct runs is the middle option. See Ceiling Cassette and Ducted Mini-Splits vs Wall-Mounted Heads. A Building America field study of a ductless retrofit found a bathroom without an indoor unit ran wetter than the rooms with them, which is a reminder to plan for rooms that have no head. Heating-dominated climates need more care; see Sizing a Heat Pump for a New Home in a Heating Climate.

Worked example (our own, made-up numbers)

Assumptions. A 2,400 square foot house, 1,200 square feet per floor. The room loads below are invented for illustration and are not from any real calculation. We use 400 cfm per ton for cooling, 12,000 Btu/h per ton, and for heating a 30 degree F supply air temperature rise with the sensible-heat relation cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × rise). All airflow is rounded to the nearest cfm.

Floor-to-floor split. Cooling: upstairs 20,700 ÷ 35,400 = 58.5 percent, downstairs 41.5 percent. Heating: downstairs 28,000 ÷ 45,400 = 61.7 percent, upstairs 38.3 percent. Per square foot, cooling is 20,700 ÷ 1,200 = 17.3 Btu/h upstairs versus 14,700 ÷ 1,200 = 12.3 downstairs; heating is 28,000 ÷ 1,200 = 23.3 downstairs versus 17,400 ÷ 1,200 = 14.5 upstairs. The split flips between seasons.

Airflow each floor needs. Cooling tons: upstairs 20,700 ÷ 12,000 = 1.725 tons, so 1.725 × 400 = 690 cfm; downstairs 14,700 ÷ 12,000 = 1.225 tons, so 490 cfm; together 1,180 cfm. Heating at a 30 degree rise: 1.08 × 30 = 32.4, so upstairs 17,400 ÷ 32.4 = 537 cfm, downstairs 28,000 ÷ 32.4 = 864 cfm, together 1,401 cfm.

One system. The trunk and dampers set for summer send 58.5 percent of the air upstairs. If the same split held in winter at 1,401 cfm, upstairs would get 1,401 × 0.585 = 819 cfm, which carries 819 × 32.4 = 26,550 Btu/h against a need of 17,400 (153 percent), while downstairs would get 582 cfm, or 18,850 Btu/h against a need of 28,000 (67 percent). To fix that, the dampers must flip the split to about 38/62 each season, and the downstairs duct must carry 864 cfm in winter versus 490 in summer, about 1.8 times. That is possible, but it must be designed in, with the fan and static pressure checked at both extremes.

Two systems. The upper unit moves 690 cfm in cooling and 537 in heating; the lower unit moves 490 and 864. Each fan only tracks its own floor, with no damper fighting the trunk. The catch shows in the ratios: heating to cooling is 28,000 ÷ 14,700 = 1.90 downstairs, 17,400 ÷ 20,700 = 0.84 upstairs, and 45,400 ÷ 35,400 = 1.28 for the house. A lower unit picked from cooling alone would fall short on heat. That is the point to raise with the designer, along with backup heat and capacity at design temperature.

What to ask the designer

  • Can we see the room-by-room cooling and heating loads, summed by floor?
  • What is the upstairs-to-downstairs split in summer and in winter, and how does the design handle both?
  • If one system, how are the dampers, bypass or other means sized, and what happens to airflow and noise when only one zone calls?
  • If two, where do the two air handlers and the return paths go, and what is the plan for the lower floor's heating load?
  • Are ducts and equipment inside the conditioned space?
  • Will each floor have its own return or a transfer path?
  • If mini-splits serve the upper floor, how many heads, which mode rules apply to a multi-split, and how are filtration and humidity handled?

Field card: One system or two for a new two-story home

Size each floor, then decide how many systems.

  • Upstairs usually leads in summer load; downstairs usually leads in winter load.
  • Get room loads by floor in writing, cooling and heating separately.
  • DOE guidance says one unit per floor often zones large homes better than dampers.
  • Keep equipment and ducts inside the conditioned space.
  • Plan a return path on each floor.
  • Variable speed helps, but duct design still has to fit the load.
  • Multi-split heads on one outdoor unit must share a mode.
