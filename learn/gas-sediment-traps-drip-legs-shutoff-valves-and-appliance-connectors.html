Gas Sediment Traps, Drip Legs, Shutoff Valves and Appliance Connectors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The last few feet of gas pipe before a furnace, boiler or water heater are small parts with big jobs: catch dirt, give a way to shut the appliance off, allow it to be unhooked, and carry gas safely the last stretch. This lesson explains each part, what the makers say, and how to check a proposed hookup against a manual.

Licensed people only. Gas piping and appliance hookups are work for qualified, licensed people in New Jersey, with the permits and inspections the town requires. This page is for learning to read a manual and spot a bad layout. It is not a how-to for doing the work yourself. If you smell gas, leave and call from outside; see what to do if you smell gas.

What a sediment trap is

Gas line can carry rust flakes, pipe-thread chips, and sometimes moisture or oily residue. A sediment trap, also called a drip leg, is a short dead-end pocket low on the pipe. Gas turns toward the appliance, while heavier debris drops into the pocket instead of reaching the gas control. The Goodman/Amana installation manual for its MES80 and CES80 furnaces says the drip leg is there to trap dirt and moisture before it can enter the gas valve.

How the trap is built

The fuel gas code text, in a 2006 International Fuel Gas Code section 408 copy posted by the Village of Schiller Park, describes the common form: a tee fitting with a capped nipple in the bottom opening. The gas run goes in one side of the tee and out the other, and the nipple and cap hang below. The same text puts the trap downstream of the appliance shutoff valve and as close to the appliance inlet as practical. A California code handout posted by the City of Shasta Lake shows a 3 inch minimum on the nipple in its mechanical code figure, and the Goodman horizontal-furnace drawing labels its drip leg "3 in. minimum." Check the edition adopted where you work, because local amendments vary.

One common mistake is putting the gas through the bottom of the tee. Flow has to pass straight through the run, with the stub pointing down as a dead end. The cap must be gas-tight, which you prove at the leak check.

Which appliances need one

Two sources answer this in different words. The code text says a trap is required where the appliance does not have one built in, and it excludes illuminating appliances, ranges, clothes dryers and outdoor grills; the California handout lists similar exemptions plus decorative and fireplace appliances. The makers speak for their own units. The Lennox ML193 furnace instructions say to install a drip leg in vertical runs. The Goodman MES80/CES80 manual calls for a drip leg. A. O. Smith and a second residential water heater manual, hosted by Lochinvar, both say to install the trap ahead of the gas control valve, as close to the inlet as practical, as a tee with a capped nipple. Weil-McLain's 94 Series 3 boiler manual says the supply piping must include a manual shutoff and a sediment trap. So furnaces, water heaters and boilers in these manuals all get one. Do not decide from habit; read the manual for the exact unit.

Shutoff valve and union

The code text calls for a shutoff valve for each appliance, in the same room, no more than 6 feet away, with access, upstream of any union or connector, and never in a concealed space or furnace plenum. The Goodman MES80/CES80 manual says the same in its own terms: manual valve between meter and unit, within six feet, with a union downstream of the valve. It also labels the union a ground joint pipe union installed ahead of the gas valve. The Lennox manual notes that in some localities codes require a manual main shutoff and union furnished by the installer, outside the unit. A. O. Smith allows a flexible connector or a ground joint union between the shutoff and the control valve so the heater can be serviced. Weil-McLain says a ground joint union must be installed to allow servicing.

BrassCraft's connector sheet adds that its shutoff must be within 6 inches of the appliance for the kit it covers, a reminder that the kit instructions can be stricter than the 6 foot code outline. Follow whichever is stricter.

Flexible appliance connectors

Dormont's installation instructions say its connector meets ANSI Z21.24, the standard for metal connectors for gas appliances. Both Dormont and BrassCraft say the connector must not be hidden in or run through a wall, floor or partition, and Dormont says the gas outlet must be in the same room as the appliance so the connector can be seen. Dormont also says never to reuse a connector, not to kink or twist it, and to keep bends above a minimum radius. BrassCraft says not to reuse it for another appliance and gives a minimum bend radius of 10 inches. Goodman's manual says connectors must be fully inside the room with the furnace. Lennox says that where a flexible connector is allowed, black iron pipe should leave the gas valve and extend outside the cabinet, and the connector goes between that pipe and the supply. In plain words, the connector is an exposed last link in the same room, not a way to reach through a wall or cabinet.

Leak checking

Every manual here says the same thing: no flame. Lennox says to use a commercial leak detecting solution made for the purpose and never an open flame. Goodman says approved chloride-free soap solution or an electronic combustible gas detector. A. O. Smith and Weil-McLain say soapy water, and A. O. Smith says to rinse and wipe it dry. Dormont warns against household detergent or soap that may contain chlorides, and BrassCraft says test solutions may corrode, so rinse after. Bubbles at a joint mean the joint is not done. The pressure side of testing belongs with checking inlet and manifold pressure.

Worked example: checking a proposed furnace hookup

Standard used: Goodman/Amana MES80/CES80 installation manual IOG-2021G. It asks the installer to supply a drip leg with a minimum length of three inches, a manual shutoff valve within six feet of the unit, and a ground joint union downstream of the valve and ahead of the furnace gas valve. The proposal below is hypothetical.

Proposed hookup, from the supply side: ball valve, close nipple, tee with a 2 inch nipple and cap, short nipple, ground joint union, then the furnace.

Step 1, count the parts. Valve 1, close nipple 1, tee 1, trap nipple 1, cap 1, union 1, short nipple 1 = 7 pieces. The manual's three installer-supplied items (valve, drip leg, union) are all present. Pass for presence.

Step 2, valve distance. The valve will sit 4 ft 2 in from the furnace. Convert: 4 x 12 + 2 = 50 inches. Limit is 6 x 12 = 72 inches. 50 is less than 72, with 72 - 50 = 22 inches to spare. Pass.

Step 3, trap length. Proposed nipple 2 inches against a 3 inch minimum. 3 - 2 = 1 inch short. Fail. Fix: a longer nipple, at least 3 inches, and more if the local code asks for more height. The manual's figure labels it "height required by local code."

Step 4, union position. The proposal puts the union in the line upstream of the valve. The manual puts the valve ahead of the union. Fail. Fix: move the union to the furnace side so the valve can shut gas off before the union is broken.

Step 5, leak check method. Plan says approved soap solution on every joint, including the cap. Pass.

Score: 3 pass, 2 fail out of 5. The corrected hookup uses the same 7 pieces after swapping the nipple length and moving the union. One caution: the manual's figure labels the drip leg and the union but, as extracted, does not say which of those two is closer to the furnace, so the sketch above is one common arrangement and the manual's figure itself decides. A licensed installer makes the final call and the inspector approves it.

Field card: Gas Sediment Traps, Drip Legs, Shutoff Valves and Appliance Connectors

Valve, trap, union, appliance. Soap, never flame.

  • Trap = tee with a capped nipple pointing down, downstream of the shutoff, close to the appliance.
  • Goodman example: 3 in. minimum drip leg; valve within 6 ft; union after the valve.
  • Shutoff in the same room, accessible, never in a plenum or concealed space.
  • Flexible connector: same room as the appliance, not through a wall, floor or partition, never reused.
  • Leak check with a solution made for the job, rinse after, no flame.
  • Gas work is for licensed people in New Jersey; follow the manual and the local inspector.
