Cooling Water for Welders, Induction Heaters and Lasers: Chillers, Conductivity, Condensation and Flow Switches

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Spot welders, induction heaters and industrial lasers all turn a lot of electric power into heat in a small place, and plain water carries it away. If the water slows, warms or gets dirty, parts fail in seconds or slowly corrode. This lesson is for facility techs who keep that water healthy. For the plastics and machine shop side of process chillers, see Process Chillers for Plastics and Machine Shops; we do not repeat it here.

Why these tools need steady water

The heat is made inside parts that cannot shed it to air. An induction heater's copper work coil and its power supply, a welder's electrodes and transformer, and a laser's diode or resonator are all cooled by water passing through them. An induction equipment maker's public page says its equipment typically wants water between 75 and 105 degrees F at a steady pressure of 30 to 70 PSIG, and that a brief pressure drop will de-energize the equipment. A laser chiller maker's leaflet rates its units for coolant temperature stability within 0.1 K. Those are maker figures for particular equipment, so read the data plate and manual for the tool you actually have.

In a particle-accelerator plant, the Stanford linear accelerator report describes low-flow switches that shut a modulator off at 80 percent of normal flow. That is the same idea you will find on a small welder: flow is the thing worth proving, not just pump power.

Recirculating chiller or plant water

Tying a tool into plant water (a tower loop or chilled water main) costs less equipment but ties the tool to that water's temperature, pressure swings and chemistry. A small recirculating chiller gives the tool its own closed loop, so you set its own temperature and water quality. The University of Vermont safety page recommends avoiding tap water as the coolant for lab equipment because it greatly increases flood risk, and advises a refrigerated recirculator instead.

A middle path is a heat exchanger: plant water on one side, clean closed-loop water on the other. The Stanford report describes exactly this, with low-conductivity water on one side of shell-and-tube exchangers and tower water on the other, so the tool never sees tower water. If you do tap plant water directly, ask who treats it and what the tool maker allows.

Water quality

Conductivity. Water that conducts well can carry stray current. The induction maker page says resistance should be tightly controlled to prevent ground readings and electrochemical corrosion, and recommends starting a closed loop with deionized or distilled water, calling low conductivity essential for solid-state power supplies. A laser chiller leaflet we read is designed for deionised water, with optional conductivity monitoring and a deionizing cartridge in a bypass. The SLAC report held specific resistance above 1 megohm with roughly 1 percent bypass flow through demineralizer cartridges. A university laser lab maintenance sheet we read says to refill with distilled water and to hold conductance between 0.1 and 1 microsiemens, and notes that readings settle over hours. The takeaway: use what the maker names, never top off with tap water out of habit, and expect the deionizer bed to wear out and need changing.

Hardness, solids and corrosion. The induction maker page says hardness above 100 PPM as calcium carbonate impedes flow and cooling, particles above 10 PPM hurt cooling, and pH is best between 7.0 and 7.5. Dissolved oxygen promotes corrosion and biological growth. A closed loop that never touches air stays cleaner. For the chemistry side see Closed-Loop Chilled and Hot Water Treatment.

Biocide. The DOE FEMP cooling tower page says treatment programs typically combine corrosion and scale inhibitors with biological fouling inhibitors, and that conductivity is measured in microsiemens per centimeter. Slime in a tank blocks small coil passages. A sealed, dark closed loop with a filter grows less, but check for algae whenever the tank lid is open, and ask the maker before adding any treatment to a low-conductivity loop.

Condensation when the water is too cold

Oklahoma State University Extension defines dew point as the temperature at which dew begins to form and says moisture condenses on any surface at or below the dew point of the surrounding air. Cold supply water makes cold hoses, manifolds and tool bodies. The induction maker page warns that water that is too cold causes condensation, which can short-circuit electrical components. The fix is to keep supply water above the room dew point, insulate lines, or lower the room humidity. In summer, a setpoint that was fine in January can sweat in July.

Flow switches, strainers and interlocks

A flow switch proves water is moving before the tool is allowed to make heat. The Stanford report notes that many early switches were unreliable and were replaced with sealed types, and that strainers were added upstream of each switch after solder, chips and resin were found while flushing. The same report used 5-micron filters, with a 100-mesh strainer after the demineralizer. A laser lab sheet says to swap a filter when flow through it falls under 5 gpm, where a new one flows over 10 gpm. A laser chiller leaflet shows a default flow trip of 2 to 2.5 liters per minute. Never jumper a flow switch to keep a tool running. OSHA 1910.255 requires doors and access panels on resistance welding machines to be kept locked and interlocked. That standard does not specify cooling water, so follow the tool manual for the flow interlock. See Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits for pump-side care.

Hoses, quick-connects and leaks

The University of Vermont page says hoses can come off when water pressure fluctuates or when hose material degrades with prolonged use, and recommends clamping connections or using locking plastic disconnects. Leaks near electrical gear are a shock risk. The laser lab sheet says to look for water on the floor under the chillers and filter, and to assess electrical danger before touching anything. Check hose ends, crimps and quick-connect o-rings on a routine round, and replace stiff, cracked or swollen hose. A low tank level that keeps falling is a leak report.

Winter and freeze protection

An outdoor chiller, or a line crossing an unheated dock, can freeze and split a coil. The University of Vermont page says a water and ethylene glycol mixture keeps refrigeration coils from freezing, and the DOE FEMP solar alert says glycol inhibitors break down and fluid should be replaced every 5 to 10 years. A tool that needs low-conductivity water may not allow a glycol mix, so check with the maker, and read Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops before changing a fluid.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These inputs are assumptions chosen for practice, not data from any tool. The 500 constant is the standard water relation used in the ORNL process cooling material (8.33 lb per gallon times 60 minutes, specific heat 1.0). Our own rule: Btu/h = 500 x gpm x delta T.

  1. Assume a 100 kW induction heater at 80 percent efficiency, so 20 percent of input power, 20 kW, ends up in the cooling water.
  2. Convert: 20 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 68,243 Btu/h.
  3. Assume we allow a 10 degree F rise through the tool.
  4. Flow = 68,243 / (500 x 10) = 13.6 gpm.
  5. If a clogged strainer cut flow to 10 gpm, the rise would be 68,243 / (500 x 10) = 13.6 F, about 3.6 F more. That is how restricted flow shows up in return temperature.

Dew point check (assumed). Assume the room is 75 F and 55 percent relative humidity. Using a standard Magnus approximation, we calculate a dew point near 57.7 F, call it 58 F. If the chiller supply is set at 55 F, the hoses are below the dew point and will sweat. Raise the setpoint to about 65 F (7 F of margin) or insulate and dehumidify. If the tool's maker says it needs colder water, insulation and room humidity control are the options.

Field card: Cooling water for welders, induction heaters and lasers

Prove flow, keep it clean, keep it above dew point.

  • Heat to water in Btu/h = 500 x gpm x delta T (our own rule of thumb for water).
  • Use the water type the maker names; do not top off with tap water.
  • Keep supply water above the room dew point or insulate.
  • Never bypass the flow switch; fix the cause of a trip.
  • Strainers and filters get cleaned on a schedule; a falling flow is a warning.
  • Check hoses, clamps and quick-connects on rounds; water near electrical gear is a hazard.
  • Ask the maker before putting glycol or biocide in a low-conductivity loop.
