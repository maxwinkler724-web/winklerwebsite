Bare Pipe Heat Loss in Mechanical Rooms: Btu per Foot, Bare Valves and Flanges, Removable Jackets, Burn Risk and Annual Cost

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A bare steam or hot water pipe is a long, thin radiator that nobody asked for. In a mechanical room it heats the room, makes the boiler burn extra fuel, and can burn a hand. This lesson puts numbers on the loss from sourced Department of Energy tables, shows why bare valves and flanges count for more than their size suggests, and ends with a simple yearly cost estimate you can repeat for your own plant.

We keep this page to the "how much" and "what to do about it." For materials, vapor retarders and chilled water, see Pipe Insulation: Steam Heat Loss, Chilled Water Condensation and Vapor Retarders. For leaks, see Steam Leaks and Failed-Open Traps. For pipe movement, see Pipe Thermal Expansion.

How much heat does bare pipe lose?

The U.S. Department of Energy steam tip sheet on insulating distribution and condensate lines gives a table of heat loss from uninsulated horizontal steel steam pipe, per 100 feet, in million Btu per year. Its stated conditions are 75°F still air and 8,760 operating hours a year. Here are the 15 psig and 150 psig columns:

Pipe size15 psig, MMBtu/yr per 100 ft150 psig, MMBtu/yr per 100 ft
1 in.140285
2 in.235480
4 in.415850
8 in.7401,540

Our own conversion to Btu per hour per foot: divide by 8,760 hours, multiply by 1,000,000, then divide by 100 feet. For 2 in. pipe at 150 psig, 480 × 1,000,000 ÷ 8,760 ÷ 100 = about 548 Btu/h per foot. For 4 in. pipe at 150 psig, 850 gives about 970 Btu/h per foot. A single foot sounds small. A 120 foot run does not.

Hot water lines

A University of Massachusetts Amherst extension fact sheet lists steel pipe carrying 180°F water in 70°F room air. A bare 2 in. pipe loses 147 Btu/h per foot, and the same pipe with 1 in. of foam insulation loses 23. A bare 4 in. pipe loses 279, and insulated, 37. A bare 6 in. pipe loses 409, insulated, 51. The sheet works out $3.72 per foot per year saved on 2 in. pipe at $1.05 per therm, using 2,000 heating hours and a 70 percent efficient system. Those are the sheet's assumptions, not ours.

Why bare valves, flanges and strainers add up

A valve body, bonnet and the two flanges bolted to it present far more outside surface than the same length of pipe. The DOE tip sheet notes that a 6 in. gate valve can have more than 6 square feet of surface area. A companion DOE tip sheet on removable insulation gives the energy saved by insulating a flanged valve (bare loss minus insulated loss, with a 1 in. pad, in 65°F still air). For a 4 in. valve it lists 2,300 Btu/h at 300°F and 3,400 at 400°F.

The equivalent-length idea turns that into pipe feet so you can add valves to a pipe count. Our own worked comparison: steam at 150 psig is about 366°F (our steam-table value, not from the tip sheet). Interpolating the 4 in. valve figure to 366°F gives 2,300 + 0.66 × (3,400 − 2,300) = about 3,030 Btu/h. A foot of bare 4 in. pipe at 150 psig loses about 970 Btu/h. So one bare 4 in. flanged valve behaves like 3,030 ÷ 970 = about 3.1 feet of bare pipe. That is slightly low, because the table is a savings figure with an insulated remainder, so treat it as a floor. Strainers, unions, meters and pump casings are the same kind of case. We did not find sourced values for them, so count them as extra surface on your walkdown and size jackets to each one.

Removable insulation jackets

The DOE tip sheet on valves and fittings describes removable covers made with a noncombustible inside cover, insulation, and a noncombustible outside cover, held on with wire laced through grommets or with buckled straps. It says the material is designed for temperatures up to 1,600°F. Covers are made for valves, flanges, traps and other fittings. They come off for service and go back on, which is the main reason valves get left bare in the first place. Two cautions from DOE: on steam traps, install the jacket as the trap maker directs so the trap keeps working, and check for asbestos under OSHA rules before removing any old insulation. Tell the supervisor if old lagging looks suspect.

How thickness cuts loss

Insulation adds thermal resistance in series with the pipe wall and the air film on the outside, which is how the ORNL heat-loss formula page frames both the bare and the insulated case. More resistance means less heat per foot. The sources we opened give only one thickness, 1 in., and show how large that first step is: 147 down to 23 Btu/h per foot on 2 in. hot water pipe, an 84 percent cut (our arithmetic). DOE says insulation can typically cut steam line losses by 90 percent. We did not open a table that compares multiple thicknesses, so we give no numbers for thicker layers. Use the thickness in the job specification or the energy code. A PNNL Building America page summarizes that the 2012 IECC calls for R-3 on piping carrying fluid above 105°F.

Burn safety

DOE says any surface over 120°F should be insulated, and the removable-insulation sheet adds that this protects personnel. An archived OSHA letter says OSHA treats exposed heated surfaces as a hazard where injury is possible, that OSHA has no specific touch-temperature number, and that two industry standards, 1910.261(k)(11) and 1910.262(c)(9), call for hot-water and steam pipes near floors and work platforms to be insulated or guarded. The letter notes that if controls do not remove the hazard, suitable thermal protection falls under 1910.132(a), and it points to a consensus test standard (C 1055) for deciding acceptable surface temperatures. The letter is marked archived, so confirm current OSHA policy for your site. In the field: a bare valve you must reach for is a burn waiting to happen, and a missing jacket is a reportable condition.

Worked example (our own) and a simple annual cost

Our own example, with assumptions called out. We have 120 ft of bare 2 in. steam pipe at 150 psig in a mechanical room. Assumptions: the pipe runs 6,000 hours a year at that pressure, the boiler is 80 percent efficient, and gas costs $1.20 per therm. The loss rate comes from the DOE table (480 MMBtu/yr per 100 ft at 8,760 h). Scaling it to fewer hours is our approximation.

  1. Loss rate: 480 × 1,000,000 ÷ 8,760 = 54,795 Btu/h per 100 ft, so 120 ft loses 54,795 × 1.2 = 65,753 Btu/h.
  2. Heat lost per year: 65,753 × 6,000 = 394,520,000 Btu = 3,945 therms (100,000 Btu per therm).
  3. Fuel burned: 3,945 ÷ 0.80 = 4,931 therms.
  4. Cost: 4,931 × $1.20 = about $5,918 per year.
  5. With insulation cutting the loss by an assumed 90 percent: therms saved = 4,931 × 0.90 = 4,438, and dollars saved = about $5,326 per year. The remaining $592 is the insulated loss.

Add the bare valves. Our own: a bare 4 in. valve at 3,030 Btu/h for 6,000 hours is 18.2 MMBtu, or 22.7 MMBtu of fuel at 80 percent, about 227 therms, about $272 per year. The DOE valve sheet does the same sum for a 6 in. gate valve on 250 psig steam at 8,760 hours and finds about $525 per valve per year at its $8.00 per MMBtu. Multiply by your valve count.

Field card: Bare Pipe Heat Loss

Find it, count it, cover it.

  • DOE: insulate any surface over 120°F.
  • DOE table: bare 2 in. steam pipe at 150 psig loses about 548 Btu/h per foot (our conversion).
  • One bare 4 in. flanged valve is roughly 3 ft of bare 4 in. pipe (our estimate).
  • Our example: 120 ft, 6,000 h, 80 percent boiler, $1.20 per therm: about 4,931 therms and $5,918 a year; 90 percent savings about $5,326.
  • Removable jackets: follow the trap maker for traps; check for asbestos first.
