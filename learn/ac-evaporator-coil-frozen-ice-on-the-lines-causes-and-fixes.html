AC Evaporator Coil Frozen: Ice on the Lines, Causes and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You find frost on the copper line by the indoor unit, or a block of ice on the coil, and the house is getting warm. This lesson explains what the ice means, what usually causes it, what you can safely do, and how a technician sorts out the cause.

What the ice is telling you

The indoor (evaporator) coil is the part that gets cold. Warm house air blows across it, the refrigerant inside boils and absorbs that heat, and moisture in the air condenses on the coil and drips into the drain pan. That only works as liquid water if the coil surface stays above 32°F. When the coil, or the refrigerant inside it, drops below 32°F, the condensation freezes instead of draining. Ice builds up, blocks more air, and the coil gets colder still. That is why a small problem can snowball into a solid block of ice.

Ice on the larger suction line (the cold, insulated copper pipe) tells the same story. Trane's homeowner troubleshooting page lists ice on the coil or its cabinet, frozen lines at the outdoor unit, warm air from the vents, and water pooling on the floor as signs of a frozen coil. A frozen coil does not move much air or heat, so warm air from the registers is a common first clue.

The common causes

Nearly every freeze-up comes from one of two things: too little warm air crossing the coil, or too little refrigerant pressure (and so too cold a coil). Carrier's guide and Trane's page both name restricted airflow and refrigerant problems as the main groups.

  • Dirty filter. The easiest one to check. Lennox says a clogged filter restricts airflow. The Department of Energy also warns that clogged filters cut airflow. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter.
  • Closed or blocked registers and returns. Lennox lists vents blocked by furniture, rugs or curtains. Trane lists blocked return vents. Closing too many supply registers to save a room can have the same effect.
  • Blower problems. Carrier lists a malfunctioning fan as a cause. A weak or failing blower moves too little air even when the filter is clean. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Dirty evaporator coil. The DOE says dirt on the coil reduces airflow and insulates the coil. Trane's blog says dust acts as an insulator and restricts airflow.
  • Low refrigerant charge or a leak. Less refrigerant means lower pressure in the coil and a colder coil. Lennox calls it a serious issue that usually needs a professional. See Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.
  • Cool outdoor weather. Lennox says running the AC when it is too cool outside, below about 60°F, can cause a freeze-up. Overnight freezing can also point to a thermostat that is not shutting the system off, as Trane's blog notes.
  • Restrictions. A blocked metering device or restricted liquid line starves the coil of refrigerant. It looks different at the gauges from a plain low charge; the symptom table shows how.

This lesson does not repeat the full not-cooling checklist; for that, see Air Conditioner Running but Not Cooling.

Homeowner steps that are safe

  1. Turn cooling off. Set the thermostat from Cool to Off. Both Carrier and Trane say running a frozen system can damage the equipment, and Carrier specifically warns about the compressor.
  2. Run the fan only. Set the fan to On so room air moves across the coil without cooling. Carrier and Lennox both describe this as the way to speed thawing.
  3. Check the filter. Replace it if it is dirty. Make sure supply and return vents are open and not covered.
  4. Be patient. Carrier says light frost may take roughly 1 to 3 hours to thaw and heavy ice 4 to 8 hours or longer.
  5. Watch the drain pan. All that ice becomes water. Carrier tells you to check that water is not overflowing from the drain line during thaw. If it is, see Water Leaking From AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain.
  6. Restart only when airflow is normal and the coil is dry. Then watch it.

What not to do. Trane is direct: do not chip the ice, because it can damage the coil. Carrier adds not to use a hair dryer, space heater or hot water, since rapid temperature swings can crack or warp parts. If the unit freezes again after you fix the filter and airflow, stop and call a technician. Lennox says persistent icing needs professional diagnosis. Adding refrigerant is not a homeowner task, and Carrier notes the cause has to be repaired before any recharge.

For techs: airflow first, then refrigerant

An ACHR News article on iced evaporators describes a useful pattern after you fully thaw the coil and check again. A low charge tends to ice the coil mostly at the inlet, with lower than normal suction and discharge pressures and high superheat. An airflow problem gives lower than normal suction pressure with low superheat, and the coil tends to ice more fully. Another ACHR News article on restricted evaporator airflow walked through a dirty filter case: suction pressure fell, the evaporating temperature dropped to 26°F, superheat collapsed to about 1°F, and the coil iced over. The ACHR article that discussed low charge also warns that adding refrigerant will not fix an airflow problem.

Manufacturers say the same. Rheem's split system installation instructions tell the installer to verify the indoor blower is running and airflow is not restricted, and to make sure air filters are clean and in place before operating. Their charging chart notes also tie lower than design suction pressure to low indoor airflow, so a low suction reading alone does not prove a low charge.

So the order is: filter, coil, registers, blower and static pressure first. Then measure superheat and subcooling (see Superheat and Subcooling Basics) and compare to the manufacturer's targets. Do not add refrigerant by guess.

Worked example: is the coil below freezing?

A tech has a thawed, running R-410A system and reads suction pressure at the service port. The plan is to turn pressure into a saturation temperature with a P/T chart (see Pressure-Temperature Basics). Arkema's Forane 410A chart lists vapor saturation at 93.1 psig for 28°F and 97.0 psig for 30°F, and 101.1 psig for 32°F.

  1. Gauge reads 93 psig on the suction side.
  2. From the chart, 93.1 psig is 28°F, so the refrigerant is boiling at about 28°F.
  3. Compare to freezing: 28°F is 32 − 28 = 4°F below 32°F.
  4. For contrast, the chart lists 118.4 psig at 40°F, a more typical evaporating temperature, so 93 psig is a low reading.

Result: the coil is boiling refrigerant below 32°F, so moisture on it will freeze. That matches frost, but it does not say why. Next check airflow.

Airflow check. A 3-ton system at a nominal 400 cfm per ton should move about 3 × 400 = 1,200 cfm. Carrier's installation manual says airflow should generally be 350 to 450 cfm per ton in normal cooling, which for 3 tons is 3 × 350 = 1,050 to 3 × 450 = 1,350 cfm. Suppose you measure 780 cfm. That is 780 ÷ 3 = 260 cfm per ton, and 780 ÷ 1,200 = 0.65, only 65 percent of nominal. It is well below the band, so low airflow is the lead suspect, and a dirty filter, blocked coil or weak blower is where to look. An ACCA blog notes that the 400 figure is a rule of thumb and the manufacturer's tables control, so use the equipment's own data.

Field card: Frozen Evaporator Coil

Ice means the coil went below 32°F. Find out why before you restart.

  • Cooling off, fan on, wait for the thaw.
  • Check the filter, registers and returns.
  • Do not chip the ice or use heat guns, hair dryers or hot water.
  • Watch the drain pan for overflow.
  • Freezes again: call a technician.
  • Tech order: airflow, then superheat and subcooling.
  • Low airflow: low suction, low superheat. Low charge: low suction, high superheat.
