Supermarket Refrigeration Racks: Parallel Compressors, Suction Groups and Leak Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk into a supermarket and every case, freezer and cooler may be fed by one machine room. That machine room is a parallel compressor rack. This lesson explains how a rack is laid out, how it is controlled, why it holds so much refrigerant, and how federal leak-repair triggers apply to the big charges.

What a parallel rack is

In a parallel system, several compressors sit side by side on a common suction line and a common discharge line. Hussmann describes its parallel compressor systems as multiple compressors working together, custom designed for each store, with a choice of compressor types and optional heat reclaim. The idea is simple: instead of one small condensing unit per case, one shared set of compressors serves many evaporators. If you have not yet read how a single loop works, start with Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

The rack's compressors run in stages to match the load. An HVACR trade podcast summary says parallel rack compressors work together in stages, and that 30 to 80 evaporators may share one suction line. Danfoss describes its pack controllers as optimizing suction and condensing pressure according to load and outdoor temperature, covering up to 12 compressors and 8 condenser fans. We did not find a maker page that spells out the staging logic, so treat the next sentence as the general idea: the controller watches suction pressure, and when pressure climbs because the load rose, it brings on another compressor or speeds one up; when pressure falls, it backs off.

Suction groups: low temp and medium temp

A store has very different cold needs. Frozen food wants a far lower evaporating temperature than produce or dairy. So a store usually has at least two racks, or two suction groups: a low-temperature group for freezer cases and a medium-temperature group for coolers. An HVACR trade podcast summary notes that racks are named by their saturated suction temperature, such as a 13 degree rack or a minus 13 degree rack, tied to the coldest evaporator on that line. Each suction group has its own set of compressors and its own suction header.

Common liquid supply: receiver and header

All the condensed liquid collects in a receiver and then goes out on a liquid header to the cases. The same podcast summary adds that a liquid receiver removes the natural subcooling of the liquid, and that mechanical subcoolers restore it before the expansion device. Hussmann lists mechanical and ambient subcooling as options. The point for a tech: a rack is not one loop but a trunk with many branches, and a problem in one case can show up in the whole group.

EPR valves for warmer cases on a shared suction

What if one suction group serves cases that need slightly different temperatures? An evaporator pressure regulator, or EPR, sits in the suction line of a case or branch and holds that evaporator's pressure up so it runs warmer than the coldest case on the line. The same podcast summary says EPRs let cases from about minus 13 F to about 24 F share one rack, and that without them cases would cycle constantly, causing efficiency, oil and food-safety problems. Mechanical types add roughly 0.5 to 2 psi of pressure drop depending on style, and electronic EPRs can control by temperature. The same summary says EPRs must be overridden open to 50 to 100 percent when you set TXV superheat. Hussmann also lists electronic EPRs as an available suction-control option. See Reach-In and Display Case Defrost, Gaskets, Drains and Coil Care for the case side.

Heat reclaim and floating head pressure

A rack rejects a lot of heat at its condenser. Hussmann lists heat reclaim as an optional feature, and Danfoss lists heat recovery for tap water and space heating among its controller features. Neither page we read explains the piping, so for that follow the maker's drawings for the store.

Condensing pressure is the other big lever. Danfoss says its controllers optimize condensing pressure according to outdoor temperature, which is the idea behind floating head pressure: let condensing pressure drop on cool days instead of holding it high, so compressors use less power. Limits, such as the minimum pressure the expansion valves need, come from the maker's settings. We did not find a source page that states how much energy this saves, so we give no number.

Why racks hold big charges and leak a lot

A rack has long runs of liquid and suction line out to every case, plus a receiver, a condenser, and hundreds of joints and valves. EPA GreenChill says traditional centralized direct-expansion systems carry 3,000 to 4,000 pounds of refrigerant and can leak more than 20 percent of their charge each year. It says advanced centralized systems reach 1.75 pounds or less of refrigerant per MBTU/hr of evaporator load and keep annual leak rates under 15 percent, while self-contained hermetic systems average under 1 percent. More joints, more pipe and vibration all mean more places to leak. For leak-finding methods see Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.

EPA leak-repair triggers for commercial refrigeration

Two federal rules matter, and which applies depends on the refrigerant. EPA says that since April 10, 2020, the Section 608 leak repair provisions of 40 CFR 82.157 apply only to ozone-depleting refrigerants (CFC and HCFC), so appliances with 50 pounds or more of substitutes such as HFCs are no longer subject to them. The eCFR text of 82.157 shows a 20 percent trigger for commercial refrigeration and a 30 day repair window after refrigerant is added.

HFC racks fall under 40 CFR 84.106, which applies to appliances with a full charge of 15 pounds or more of a refrigerant with a GWP above 53, from January 1, 2026. Its triggers are 20 percent for commercial refrigeration, 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other appliances. The owner or operator must calculate the leak rate each time refrigerant is added, and leaks must be repaired within 30 days (120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required) of adding refrigerant to an appliance over its trigger. Part 84 defines commercial refrigeration as appliances used in the retail food and cold storage warehouse subsectors, which is where racks live. The page we read for 84.106 does not show every detail, such as extensions and verification tests, so confirm the full text on eCFR.

Worked example: a rack with a 2,000 lb charge

Suppose a rack has a stated full charge of 2,000 lb and needed a stated 400 lb top-off over 12 months. The Part 84 definitions describe two methods. In the annualizing method you divide the pounds added by the full charge, then scale by 365 divided by the days since the last addition. The page we read describes this in words and did not print the formula itself, so the steps below follow that description.

  1. Pounds added divided by full charge: 400 / 2,000 = 0.20.
  2. Days since last addition: 365 (12 months), so the scale factor is 365 / 365 = 1.
  3. 0.20 / 1 = 0.20, and times 100 is 20 percent.

The rolling average method gives the same answer here: pounds added over 365 days divided by full charge, times 100, is 400 / 2,000 x 100 = 20 percent. Compare that with the 20 percent commercial refrigeration trigger. The rack sits right at the trigger, not clearly above it, and the repair duty applies to an appliance exceeding the trigger, so a real case this close would be a judgment call to settle with the owner and the rule text. Our own note: that comparison is our reading, not an EPA ruling. Also, the first calculation after January 1, 2026 uses special transition rules.

Our own carbon arithmetic: EPA's Technology Transitions GWP reference table lists R-404A at a 100-year GWP of 3,922, labeled "Calculated." The page cites the IPCC 2007 report (AR4) for many substances, but does not name the assessment report for that blend directly, so treat AR4 as the likely basis, not a confirmed one.

  1. 400 lb x 3,922 = 1,568,800 lb CO2-equivalent.
  2. Convert to metric tons: 1,568,800 / 2,204.62 = about 711.6 metric tons CO2e.

One year of top-offs on one rack equals roughly 712 metric tons of CO2e, if the GWP is right.

Newer systems in brief

EPA GreenChill describes distributed systems with compressors near the cases, using considerably less piping and a smaller charge, and secondary loop systems that use a much smaller refrigerant charge with a pumped fluid through the store. It also notes CO2 transcritical systems use CO2 as the sole refrigerant and need specialized controls and components for the high pressures. Details differ by maker, so follow the manual for each system. Danfoss says it has pioneered CO2 systems for over 10 years.

Field card: Supermarket Refrigeration Racks

One machine room, many cases, big charge.

  • Parallel rack: several compressors on a common suction, serving many cases.
  • Separate low-temp and medium-temp suction groups, named by suction temperature.
  • EPRs let warmer cases share a colder suction; override them open when setting superheat.
  • Heat reclaim and lower condensing pressure on cool days save energy; follow maker settings.
  • GreenChill: traditional racks hold 3,000 to 4,000 lb and may leak over 20 percent a year.
  • Commercial refrigeration trigger: 20 percent; HFC systems of 15 lb or more follow 40 CFR 84.106.
  • Our own: 400 / 2,000 = 20 percent; 400 lb R-404A is about 712 t CO2e at GWP 3,922.
