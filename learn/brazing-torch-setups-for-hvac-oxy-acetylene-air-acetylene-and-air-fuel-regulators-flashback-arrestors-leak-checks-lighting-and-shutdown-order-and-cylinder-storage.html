Brazing Torch Setups for HVAC: Regulators, Arrestors, Leak Checks and Cylinder Storage

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every brazed joint on a refrigerant line starts with a gas setup that has to be safe before it is good. This lesson walks the chain from cylinder to flame, what each torch type is for, how to leak-check and light up, and how to store and move the cylinders. Rules cited here come from federal workplace safety regulations and university safety guidelines.

What this lesson covers, and what it leaves to others

We do not repeat filler metals, flux or joint prep; see Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines. Flowing nitrogen inside the tube is in Nitrogen Purge Flow While Brazing. Fire watch and the 35-foot rule are in Hot Work Permits and Fire Watch. Shades and goggles are in Eye and Face Protection. Here we cover the torch and its gas.

Three torch families

  • Oxygen-acetylene. Two cylinders, two regulators, two hoses. Oxygen mixed with acetylene gives the hottest and most controllable flame of the three, so it is the usual choice when a lot of heat must reach a large copper joint quickly.
  • Air-acetylene. One cylinder and one hose. The torch draws in room air instead of using a second cylinder. It is lighter to carry and has fewer parts to check, with less heat than an oxygen setup.
  • Air-fuel with a propane-type gas. One fuel supply, often a hand-held cylinder, sometimes with a regulator and hose. This is the lightest kit and the coolest flame.

Our own rule of thumb, not a sourced figure: the larger the copper line and the thicker its fittings, the more heat you need, so the lighter kits suit small line sets and the oxygen kit suits large lines. The torch maker's tip chart, not a rule of thumb, tells you what a given tip can heat. Whatever kit you use, the acetylene and storage rules below apply to the gases you use.

The setup chain

Regulators

OSHA's oxygen-fuel gas standard, 29 CFR 1910.253, says regulators may be used only for the gas and pressure they were made for, and oxygen regulator gauges must be marked "USE NO OIL." The construction standard, 29 CFR 1926.350, requires oxygen and fuel-gas regulators and gauges to be in proper working order. The University of Pittsburgh guideline adds that an oxygen regulator goes only on an oxygen cylinder and a fuel-gas regulator only on a fuel-gas cylinder A working-pressure gauge needle that creeps is a sign of a faulty regulator, per the same guideline.

Hoses

Under 1926.350(f), oxygen and fuel-gas hoses must not be interchangeable, must be inspected at the start of each shift, and defective hose must be removed from service. Couplings must not release by a straight pull without a turning motion. 1910.253 also says hose showing leaks, burns or worn areas must be repaired or replaced.

Check valves and flashback arrestors

A flashback is a flame traveling back into the equipment. A check valve stops gas from flowing backward; a flashback arrestor is meant to stop a flame. The Pittsburgh guideline calls for reverse-flow check valves and flashback arrestors on torches and regulators, and the Dalhousie guideline asks for an arrestor between regulator and hose if the torch lacks one built in. The UK Health and Safety Executive leaflet says arrestors are not a substitute for safe working practice and that a non-return valve "will not stop a flashback once it has occurred." Treat them as backup, inspected regularly, not as permission to be careless.

Cracking the cylinder valve and setting pressure

  1. Back the regulator adjusting screw out until it is loose before opening any cylinder valve (Pittsburgh guideline).
  2. Before the regulator goes on, briefly open and close the cylinder valve to blow out dirt, standing to one side of the outlet. 1926.350(d)(1) says not to do this with a fuel-gas valve where gas could reach sparks, flame or another ignition source.
  3. Open the valve slowly. 1926.350(d)(2) says fuel-gas cylinder valves are not opened more than 1 1/2 turns. 1910.253 prefers no more than three-quarters of a turn for acetylene, and the wrench stays on the stem during use so you can shut it off fast.
  4. Acetylene must not be used above 15 psig (1910.253(a)(2)). University guidelines repeat the limit and note that the regulator gauge shows pressures at or above 15 psig in red. Set working pressures from the torch maker's chart for the tip you are using.

Leak-checking with soap solution

Check cylinder valve and regulator connections, then every hose and torch connection, with a liquid soap and water mixture (Dalhousie guideline; Virginia Tech guideline). Bubbles mean a leak. The Pittsburgh guideline wants the torch valves and connections examined daily before lighting. Never look for a fuel-gas leak with a flame.

Lighting and shutdown order

1926.350(g)(3) says torches are lit with friction lighters or other approved devices, not with matches or from hot work. Three university guidelines agree on the order for oxy-acetylene: open the acetylene torch valve a little, light it, then add oxygen and adjust the flame (Virginia Tech; Pittsburgh; Dalhousie). To shut down, close the acetylene torch valve first and then the oxygen (Virginia Tech; Dalhousie), then close the cylinder valves, bleed each line until the gauges read zero, and back the adjusting screws out. When work is finished, 1926.350(a)(8) requires cylinder valves to be closed. Note one exception: after a flashback, the Health and Safety Executive leaflet and the Pittsburgh guideline say to close the oxygen side first, which is the reverse of normal. Air-fuel torches have the same idea with a single valve.

Storing and moving cylinders

  • Upright. 1910.253 requires acetylene cylinders stored valve end up and fuel-gas cylinders valve end up whenever in use. 1926.350(a)(9) says cylinders are secured upright at all times. A university fact sheet says the gas is not stored as free compressed gas but dissolved in a liquid held in a porous mass inside the cylinder, and that acetylene cylinders must always be transported, stored and used upright (University of Pennsylvania).
  • Oxygen away from fuel gas. In storage, oxygen and fuel-gas cylinders must be at least 20 feet apart or separated by a noncombustible barrier at least 5 feet high with a fire-resistance rating of at least half an hour (1926.350(a)(10); 1910.253(b)(4)(iii)).
  • Oxygen away from oil and grease. Cylinders, valves, regulators and hose must be kept free of oily substances. Never handle oxygen equipment with oily hands or gloves, and never aim an oxygen jet at an oily surface or greasy clothing (1910.253; 1926.350(i)).
  • In a vehicle. Cylinders are secured vertically, caps on and valves closed (1926.350(a)(1), (a)(4), (a)(8)). Remove regulators first unless on a special carrier. Dalhousie says to use a well-ventilated, preferably open, vehicle and never carry acetylene in a closed compartment. The Health and Safety Executive says a closed van needs ventilation and that even then a large release can be dangerous. Refrigerant cylinders are in Carrying Refrigerant Cylinders in a Service Van.
  • Heat and sparks. Keep cylinders away from sparks and slag, or behind fire-resistant shields (1926.350(b)(1)). Indoors, 1926.350(a)(11) bars unventilated lockers and cupboards.

Worked example, our own: how long does the cylinder last?

This is our own example with assumed numbers. OSHA's 2009 Federal Register notice reports that an older gas-industry edition set an acetylene flow rate of one-seventh of cylinder capacity per hour, and a 2003 edition set one-tenth per hour for intermittent use and one-fifteenth per hour for continuous use; OSHA calls these provisions advisory. Taking the 2003 values:

  • Assumption A: the cylinder holds 60 cubic feet of acetylene when full (not a sourced size).
  • Assumption B: the tip draws 5 cubic feet per hour while the flame is lit.
  • Assumption C: the flame is lit 40 percent of the working time, and we use every cubic foot.

Intermittent limit: 60 / 10 = 6 cubic feet per hour. Continuous limit: 60 / 15 = 4 cubic feet per hour. Our 5 cubic feet per hour is under 6, so intermittent brazing is within the limit. It is over 4, so holding the flame lit nonstop would exceed the continuous value and the tip should be smaller or a second cylinder manifolded. Lit time: 60 / 5 = 12 hours. At 40 percent: 12 / 0.40 = 30 working hours. Real cylinders are not drained to zero, so expect fewer hours.

Field card: Brazing Torch Setups for HVAC

Right gas, tight connections, upright, apart.

  • Regulators only for their own gas; oxygen kept free of oil and grease.
  • Back the regulator screw out, crack the valve standing to the side, open slowly.
  • Acetylene: no more than 15 psig; valve no more than 1 1/2 turns, wrench on.
  • Soap solution on every connection; bubbles mean a leak.
  • Light with a friction lighter; acetylene first, then oxygen. Shut down acetylene first, then oxygen.
  • Oxygen and fuel gas 20 feet apart or behind a 5-foot barrier; secure cylinders upright with caps on.
