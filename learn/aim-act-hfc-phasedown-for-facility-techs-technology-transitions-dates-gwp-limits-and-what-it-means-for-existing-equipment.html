AIM Act HFC Phasedown for Facility Techs: What It Means for Existing Equipment

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you work on chillers, rooftop units or split systems, you will hear that "HFCs are going away." That is half true. The AIM Act limits how much new HFC gets made and which refrigerants can go into new equipment. It does not tell an owner to pull out a working machine. This lesson sorts out what the law does, what it leaves alone, and what it changes when a unit nears replacement.

Check EPA for current dates

These rules have been revised more than once. The dates below come from EPA pages and the eCFR as we read them in 2026, including a May 2026 final rule that changed several deadlines. This is general education, not legal advice. Before you plan a project, check EPA's HFC restrictions by sector page and 40 CFR Part 84 for the current text.

What the AIM Act does

The AIM Act of 2020 directs EPA to cut HFCs three ways. First, a phasedown of production and consumption, run through allowances. EPA says it is phasing regulated HFCs down to 15% of their historic baseline in steps by 2036 [1]. Second, the Technology Transitions program, which limits the GWP of refrigerants and other HFCs in new products and systems by sector [2][3]. Third, the Emissions Reduction and Reclamation (ER&R) rule, which covers leak repair, leak detection and reclaimed refrigerant [6]. The first affects the supply of new refrigerant. The second affects what manufacturers can build and what can be installed. The third affects how you service big equipment.

The phasedown steps

EPA lists the allowance caps as a percent of baseline: 90% for 2020 through 2023, 60% for 2024 through 2028, 30% for 2029 through 2033, 20% for 2034 and 2035, and 15% for 2036 and after [1]. Each step is a smaller pool of new HFC that producers and importers may supply. Our own reading: smaller caps mean less new HFC supply, so expect pressure on virgin high-GWP refrigerant as the steps drop.

Technology Transitions: limits on new equipment

EPA's final Technology Transitions rule was published October 24, 2023, with restrictions starting January 1, 2025 for certain technologies and limits that vary by sector [3]. EPA's sector table, last updated August 28, 2026, shows these examples [2]:

  • Residential and light commercial AC and heat pumps (mini-splits, unitary systems): GWP limit 700, installation date January 1, 2025 [2]. Window units and portable room AC: 700, January 1, 2025 [2].
  • Variable refrigerant flow (VRF): 700, installation date January 1, 2027 [2]. A December 2024 rule lets VRF equipment made or imported before January 1, 2026 be installed until January 1, 2027, and projects with a building permit issued before October 5, 2023 until January 1, 2028 [3].
  • Comfort-cooling chillers: 700, January 1, 2025 [2].
  • Process chillers: 700 above -30 °C exiting fluid, January 1, 2026; 700 from -50 °C to -30 °C, January 1, 2028; below -50 °C not covered [2].
  • Data center cooling: 700, January 1, 2027 [2].
  • Cold storage and retail food: interim limits first, then 150 or 300 depending on charge size, starting January 1, 2032 [2].

Compliance dates for semiconductor-related chillers and process refrigeration of 100 lb or less moved to January 1, 2030 in the May 2026 final rule [4]. The sector page also notes that sale and distribution of products are prohibited three years after the manufacture and import date [2]. Because the 2026 reconsideration is recent, treat any older table, including a printed one, as possibly out of date [3][5].

Existing equipment can keep running

This is the point owners most often get wrong. EPA's FAQ says the 2023 rule "does not limit the use of any existing product or system," and that you can continue to repair your existing refrigeration, air conditioning and heat pump system [1]. EPA's fact sheet says such systems can be serviced and repaired throughout their useful life, including replacing components as needed [5]. The sector table adds that components used to repair existing systems are not subject to the restrictions [2]. The regulation itself says the Technology Transitions requirements do not apply to equipment in existence in the United States before December 27, 2020, and requires a "For servicing existing equipment only" label on certain components for higher-GWP refrigerants [8].

For residential and light commercial AC, the May 2026 rule removed the January 1, 2026 installation date for systems made or imported before January 1, 2025, so that older inventory can still be installed [4][10].

What changes for replacement planning

  • Ask what refrigerant the replacement uses. A new comfort-cooling chiller or rooftop unit must meet the sector GWP limit that applies on its installation date or manufacture date [2].
  • Expect different refrigerants in new units. EPA lists R-32 and R-454B as A2L [9]. See A2L and A3 Refrigerants and R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges.
  • Do not assume a retrofit. The limits in the sector table apply to new products and systems [2]. Ask the manufacturer before changing refrigerant in an old machine.
  • Weigh service cost. The phasedown steps above affect virgin supply over time, so ask your supplier about future refrigerant cost when you plan [1].

The ER&R rule: leak repair and reclaimed refrigerant

Under 40 CFR 84.106, leak repair applies, from January 1, 2026, to appliances with a full charge of 15 lb or more of a regulated substance or a substitute with a GWP above 53, and it excludes residential and light commercial AC and heat pump appliances [6]. Trigger leak rates in the eCFR text are 20% for commercial refrigeration, 30% for industrial process refrigeration, and 10% for comfort cooling and other appliances. Repair is due within 30 days of adding refrigerant above the trigger, or 120 days if an industrial process shutdown is needed, followed by verification tests [6]. Automatic leak detection applies to industrial process or commercial refrigeration appliances with a full charge of 1,500 lb or more [6]. For reclaimed refrigerant, the same section says that from January 1, 2026 reclaimed refrigerant may not contain more than 15% by weight virgin regulated substance [6]. For the calculation method, see Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances. EPA has proposed, but not finalized, an exemption for road refrigerated transport appliances from the leak repair requirements [10].

Worked example: CO2-equivalent of a 500 lb R-410A charge

EPA's SNAP table lists a GWP of 2,090 for R-410A, 675 for R-32 and 470 for R-454B [9]. EPA's Appendix A to Part 84 lists 675 as the exchange value for HFC-32, which matches [7]. GWP is a 100-year multiplier, so charge times GWP gives CO2-equivalent. Our own arithmetic:

  1. R-410A: 500 lb × 2,090 = 1,045,000 lb CO2e.
  2. Convert to short tons: 1,045,000 ÷ 2,000 = 522.5 tons CO2e.
  3. R-454B at the same 500 lb: 500 × 470 = 235,000 lb CO2e.
  4. Difference: 1,045,000 − 235,000 = 810,000 lb CO2e. As a share: 1 − (470 ÷ 2,090) = 1 − 0.225 = about 0.775, or 77.5% lower.
  5. R-32 at 500 lb: 500 × 675 = 337,500 lb CO2e.

Our own caution: a real replacement unit rarely has the same charge, so this compares the refrigerants, not two specific machines. The unit's nameplate charge is what counts.

Field card: AIM Act HFC Phasedown for Facility Techs: What It Means for Existing Equipment

Limits are on new equipment. Existing equipment can be run and serviced.

  • Phasedown caps (percent of baseline): 90, 60, 30, 20, 15 by period, ending 2036 [1].
  • Many new comfort cooling systems and chillers have a GWP limit of 700 [2].
  • Existing systems can be repaired and serviced [1][5].
  • Leak repair rules start at 15 lb and a GWP above 53; triggers 10%, 20%, 30% [6].
  • Rules change. Check EPA before you quote a date.
