Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Both methods let different areas call for heat separately. Which one you have changes how you wire it, what noises it can make and what to check when one zone will not heat.

Zone valves

One circulator serves every zone. Each zone has an electrically operated valve on its circuit. Caleffi describes the common type as a motorized valve with a spring return: powering the actuator opens it and winds a spring, and when power goes away the spring closes the valve. Two actuator styles exist: a wax heat motor that takes about 2 to 3 minutes to open, and a geared motor that opens in about 2 to 3 seconds.

Many valves have an end switch that closes once the valve is fully open and is used to start the circulator and boiler. A 3-wire valve shares the actuator supply, while a 4-wire valve has an isolated dry contact that can be used with a separate transformer. Wiring is typically 24 VAC. For the control basics, see Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

Transformer sizing

The Caleffi article says valves draw roughly 2 to 7 VA each, and recommends a transformer rated at least 10 VA above the total load. Check the actual valve and transformer labels.

Zone circulators

Each zone has its own circulator. Each also needs a flow-check valve so water does not drift backward into zones that are off. Advantages listed: no zone valves and small circulators that are inexpensive. JLC adds that a small circulator per zone limits a failure to one zone and avoids flow noise. Disadvantages from Caleffi:

  • Circulators sharing common piping can push on each other, so the common piping should be low in resistance (flow velocities of 2 to 4 ft/s there help) or the zones should be hydraulically separated.
  • Total electrical use is typically higher. In Caleffi's example, zone circulators used about 3.4 times the electricity of the zone valve system for the same heat.

The pressure problem with valves

When zones close, the single circulator pushes against fewer open paths, and the pressure difference across the headers rises. That can cause noise and wear. Caleffi lists velocities above 4 ft/s as noisy and above 7 ft/s as able to erode copper. Common fixes:

  • A differential pressure bypass valve that opens when the header pressure rises, usually about 0.5 to 1 psi above the all-zones-open value.
  • A flat-curve circulator.
  • A variable-speed, pressure-regulated circulator, which can remove the need for a bypass valve. Caleffi describes these as well suited to zone valve systems.

Related noise problems are in Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.

Troubleshooting thoughts

  • Zone will not heat: is the thermostat calling, is the valve getting 24 V, does it open, does the end switch start the circulator?
  • Zone heats when it should be off: a leaking valve or a missing or stuck flow-check valve.
  • Noise when only one zone is open: check bypass or pump setting.
  • Always isolate power before working on valve wiring or actuators, and drain or isolate the zone only per the maker's instructions.

Field card: Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators

Valves share one pump and need pressure control; circulators need check valves.

  • Zone valve: one circulator, spring-return valve, end switch starts the pump and boiler.
  • Wax-motor valves open in minutes; geared ones in seconds.
  • Transformer: add up valve VA and leave margin (Caleffi suggests 10 VA above the total).
  • Zone circulators need flow-check valves and low-resistance common piping.
  • Closing zones raises pressure; use a bypass or pressure-regulated pump.
  • Velocity above about 4 ft/s gets noisy.
