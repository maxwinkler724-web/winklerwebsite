Liquid Line Pressure Drop, Vertical Lift and Flash Gas: Why Subcooling Disappears Before the Metering Device

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The condenser can make 10°F of subcooling and the expansion valve can still be fed a mix of liquid and vapor. The gap is the liquid line: every foot of lift, every drier, solenoid and fitting takes pressure, and pressure is what keeps the liquid a liquid. This lesson shows how much each one takes, works one example, and covers how to check it.

Why pressure loss eats subcooling

Liquid leaving the condenser is at its saturation pressure, minus whatever subcooling it picked up. Subcooling is a safety margin. If the pressure drops until the liquid is at its boiling point for that pressure, some of it flashes to vapor. That vapor is flash gas. Anything that lowers pressure before the metering device spends the margin: friction in the pipe, a drier, a solenoid valve, a sight glass, bends and fittings, and the weight of a column of liquid lifted upward. A maker's piping manual says subcooling is the only way to prevent flashing from line pressure drop, and it also says to size liquid lines so the total loss is no more than about 2 to 3°F of saturation temperature. Our subcooling basics lesson covers how to measure the margin itself.

Vertical lift: the loss you cannot size away

Friction depends on pipe size, but the weight of the liquid column does not. It depends on the liquid density and the height. Liquid pressure rises by density divided by 144 per foot of depth (lb/ft³ to psi), so lifting liquid up a riser costs that much per foot. A refrigerant maker's R-410A property table lists liquid density of 61.73 lb/ft³ at 100°F, which gives 61.73 ÷ 144 = 0.43 psi per foot. A commercial refrigeration maker's line-sizing guide says to add 0.5 psig for each foot of liquid riser, and trade press says the same, about 1 psig for every 2 ft of lift. A maker's piping manual tabulates the figure by refrigerant at 100°F liquid, with values from about 0.43 to 0.5 psi per foot. So 0.5 is a fair round number for most common refrigerants, and R-410A is a little lower. When the condenser is below the evaporator, the lift is a pure loss. When the condenser is above the evaporator, the same column adds pressure, which helps.

Pressure per foot is small, but a 30 ft riser costs about 13 to 15 psi. In saturation temperature, on R-410A around 110°F, that is about 2.5 to 3°F.

The other losses on the line

  • Filter drier. A clean drier should cost little. A partly plugged one costs more, and the pressure falls inside it. See Filter Driers and Sight Glasses for replacement limits.
  • Solenoid valve, sight glass, service valves. Each has its own rated drop at flow. Read the maker's data for the size on the line.
  • Fittings and bends. Count them as extra pipe length. See line set sizing and pump-down and liquid line solenoids.
  • Heat gain. A liquid line run through a hot attic or mechanical room warms the liquid. That lowers the subcooling by raising the liquid temperature, on top of the pressure loss. Insulating the line helps.
  • Velocity. Trade press gives 300 feet per minute as a ceiling for liquid line velocity.

What flash gas does to the metering device

A thermostatic expansion valve is sized to pass liquid. Vapor takes far more room per pound, so a mixed stream passes much less refrigerant by weight and the evaporator is starved. Trade press describes flashing at the expansion valve when subcooling is lost or the liquid line drop is too high, and warns of a severe drop in capacity. The symptoms look like low charge: high superheat, low suction pressure and a warm box. Adding charge does not fix it, and it can overfill the system. The maker's piping manual asks for 4 to 6°F of subcooling at the expansion valve when the valve maker gives no number.

Bubbles in the sight glass that are not low charge

Bubbles tell you vapor is in the liquid line at the glass. They do not tell you why. A glass just after a plugged drier shows flashing because of the drop across the drier. A glass at the top of a long riser can show bubbles from lift while the condenser outlet is solid liquid. A trade-press article on restricted liquid lines adds that blends such as R-410A can flash in the glass even when the charge is correct, that start-up and sudden load changes can cause bubbles, and that a low-flow glass at light load can look partly empty. Its advice is not to charge to a clear glass. Check subcooling at both ends first.

Worked example (ours)

These numbers are our own example, not a field measurement. R-410A, outdoor condenser below the indoor evaporator. We use the maker's property table: 110°F saturation is 381.1 psia (366.4 psig, after subtracting 14.7), 105°F is 341.9 psig, and liquid density at 100°F is 61.73 lb/ft³.

Assumptions: condensing at 110°F, liquid leaves the condenser at 100°F (10°F subcooling), 30 ft vertical riser, drier 3 psi, solenoid 2 psi, fittings and pipe friction 2 psi, no heat gain.

  1. Condenser outlet pressure: 366.4 psig. The 100°F saturation pressure is 318.5 psig (333.2 − 14.7), so the margin is 366.4 − 318.5 = 47.9 psi, or 10°F.
  2. Static head: 30 ft × (61.73 ÷ 144) = 30 × 0.429 = 12.9 psi.
  3. Other losses: 3 + 2 + 2 = 7 psi.
  4. Total loss: 12.9 + 7 = 19.9 psi.
  5. Pressure at the expansion valve inlet: 366.4 − 19.9 = 346.5 psig.
  6. Saturation temperature at that pressure, between 105°F (341.9) and 110°F (366.4): 105 + (346.5 − 341.9) ÷ (366.4 − 341.9) × 5 = 105 + 0.94 = about 106°F. Check: 24.5 psi per 5°F is about 4.9 psi per degree, and 19.9 ÷ 4.9 is about 4°F lost.
  7. Liquid temperature is still 100°F, so subcooling at the valve is 106 − 100 = about 6°F.

Result: 10°F at the condenser becomes about 6°F at the valve, which is inside the 4 to 6°F range the maker's manual gives. If the condenser had made only 5°F, almost nothing would be left (about 1°F), and a plugged drier or a hot line would flash the liquid. Charging by weight on this job also has to count the liquid in the line; see charging by weight and line set length.

How to check it

  1. Subcooling at both ends. Measure it at the condenser outlet with a pressure and a pipe temperature. Then measure again just ahead of the metering device, using a liquid pressure tap there if one exists. If the second number is much lower, the line is losing it. If only the condenser end is low, look at charge or condenser airflow.
  2. Pressure drop across a part. Use the same gauge on both sides of the drier or solenoid. Compare with the maker's limit.
  3. Temperature drop across a drier. This is a weak test. The trade-press article puts a feelable 10°F difference at roughly 20 psi of drop for R-134a at 110°F and 30 psi for R-22, so a small restriction can feel identical on both sides. It suggests a sensor about 12 inches ahead of the expansion valve should not read colder than the surroundings.
  4. Compare lift with the maker's limit. Check the maker's installation data for a maximum liquid lift on the model, and stay inside it.

Fixes follow the cause: shorten the lift or move the unit, size the line to the maker's table, replace a plugged drier, pick low-drop valves, insulate the line, or add subcooling with a liquid-suction heat exchanger or a subcooling circuit.

Field card: Liquid line pressure drop and flash gas

Pressure keeps liquid liquid. Every psi lost spends subcooling.

  • Lift costs about 0.43 psi/ft for R-410A (our arithmetic from density) and about 0.5 psi/ft as a common round number.
  • On R-410A near 110°F, about 4.9 psi equals 1°F of saturation temperature (our arithmetic).
  • Ask for 4 to 6°F of subcooling at the expansion valve when the maker gives no number.
  • Bubbles are a question: measure subcooling at the condenser and at the valve before adding charge.
  • A hand cannot feel a small drier restriction. Use gauges.
  • Example: 10°F out, 19.9 psi lost, about 6°F left.
