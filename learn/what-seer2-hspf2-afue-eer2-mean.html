What Does a SEER2 Rating Mean? HSPF2, AFUE and EER2 Too

Shopping for a new air conditioner, heat pump, or furnace means meeting a pile of acronyms. Here is what each one measures and what it leaves out.

The short answer

These are report-card numbers for heating and cooling equipment. SEER2 and EER2 score cooling. HSPF2 scores heat pump heating. AFUE scores furnaces and boilers. For all of them, a higher number means the equipment turns more of its energy into comfort. A higher number usually costs more up front, and the right choice depends on your climate and your house.

Each rating in plain English

  • SEER2 (cooling, whole season). It averages how well an air conditioner or heat pump cools across a range of outdoor temperatures through a summer. Trane says it averages from about 65 to 104 degrees F outside. It replaced the older SEER in 2023 when the testing method changed.
  • EER2 (cooling, hot day). It measures cooling efficiency at one hot condition: 95 degrees F outside, 80 inside, and 50 percent humidity, according to Trane. It matters more where it is very hot for long stretches. Manufacturers usually advertise SEER2, so you may need to ask for EER2.
  • HSPF2 (heat pump heating, whole season). It compares the heat a heat pump delivers across a winter with the electricity it uses. Colder places benefit more from a higher number.
  • AFUE (furnace or boiler fuel use). It is the percentage of fuel that becomes heat for the house. A 97 percent AFUE means roughly 97 cents of each fuel dollar heats your home, and the rest goes up the vent.

What the minimums are in New Jersey

Federal minimums depend on the type of equipment, and for air conditioners they also depend on the region. New Jersey is in the North region on Goodman's regional chart, and the Department of Energy defines the North as states with about 5,000 or more heating degree days. For equipment manufactured on or after January 1, 2023, the Department of Energy's FAQ lists these minimums:

  • Split-system air conditioner: 13.4 SEER2 (the North figure).
  • Split-system heat pump: 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2. Heat pumps are not split into regions.

In the North, the date the unit was made decides, not the install date, so a unit built before 2023 that met the old rules can still be installed. The Department of Energy also says SEER2 numbers are similar to the old SEER numbers but not directly comparable, because the test changed. A new rating can look lower than an old one for the same kind of machine because of the test, not because the machine got worse. Trane lists a federal AFUE minimum of 80 percent for furnaces, and a utility page calls an HSPF2 above 8.0 highly efficient. Treat these as examples and check the current rules and the EnergyGuide label.

What the ratings do not tell you

A rating describes the equipment under lab conditions. It cannot tell you whether the system is the right size, whether the ducts leak, or whether it was installed with the correct refrigerant charge and airflow. The Department of Energy points out that a split system charged in the field can end up with too much or too little refrigerant, and that the wrong amount or poor airflow lowers performance. A very efficient unit that is too big or badly installed can feel worse than a modest unit installed well. Sizing is why we suggest asking any contractor how they calculated the load (see our hiring guide linked below).

Things you can safely check

  • Find the nameplate and EnergyGuide label on equipment you already own. The outdoor unit usually has a model number you can read from the ground.
  • Compare like with like. SEER2 to SEER2, AFUE to AFUE. Old SEER numbers are not the same scale.
  • Ask for both SEER2 and EER2 if you live somewhere very hot, and HSPF2 if you are shopping for a heat pump in a cold place.
  • Look for the ENERGY STAR mark and ask about rebates from your utility, since offers vary.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. Reading a label is fine. Opening the equipment to find it is not needed.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out. None of these terms is an emergency; they are shopping tools.
  • You are replacing equipment and want help choosing a rating that fits your home.
  • You notice your bills climbing or comfort dropping on an older system.
  • A contractor quotes ratings but cannot explain how they sized the equipment.

Field card: Efficiency Ratings

Higher is better, but fit and installation matter just as much.

  • SEER2: cooling across a whole season. EER2: cooling on a hot day.
  • HSPF2: heat pump heating across a winter.
  • AFUE: percent of furnace or boiler fuel that becomes heat.
  • NJ is in the North: 13.4 SEER2 minimum for split AC; 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2 for split heat pumps (made 2023 or later).
  • Ratings do not fix a wrong size or poor installation.
