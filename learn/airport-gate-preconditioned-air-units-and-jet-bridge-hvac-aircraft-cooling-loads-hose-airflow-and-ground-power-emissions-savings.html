Gate Preconditioned Air Units and Jet Bridge HVAC: Cooling Loads, Hose Airflow and APU Savings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A parked airliner is a hot or cold box with no cooling plant of its own once the engines are off. Most of the time the small engine in the tail, the APU, fills that gap. A preconditioned air unit, or PCA, does the same job from the gate with electricity. This lesson covers how the unit, its hose and the jet bridge fit together, one sizing calculation of our own, and what the federal agencies actually claim about the savings.

What a PCA does

The FAA describes PCA as air heated or cooled at the gate and sent to the parked aircraft, paired with a ground power converter that supplies electricity for lighting and navigation systems. Its Voluntary Airport Low Emissions (VALE) program page lists gate electrification among the projects it can fund, and its brochure says these systems cut emissions from aircraft auxiliary power units. A GAO review of the first VALE projects defines the APU as a small engine that supplies cabin power when the main engines are off.

The unit's job is narrow. It does not recirculate room air like a rooftop unit (see Rooftop Unit Basics). A trade-press article by an airline ground equipment engineer says PCA uses 100 percent outside air and must deliver a set volume at a set static pressure, because the aircraft's own ducts are already sized.

Where the equipment sits

A design guideline from a state aviation administration says PCA and 400 Hz units go on the underside of the jet bridge when possible, may move to the top if they block the bridge from lowering to a regional jet door, and that ground-mounted units are not permitted. An industry association paper describes two main layouts. Point-of-use units sit on the bridge or apron, are the most common type worldwide, and mean each gate has its own chiller to maintain. Central plants feed piped air or water to many gates, which the paper rates as more efficient at scale and easier to maintain in one place. Heat or cooling lost through hoses and pipes hurts performance either way. One airport authority memo we read describes a central design with chillers and ice storage serving about 73 bridges; for how that storage works, see Ice Thermal Energy Storage for Commercial Cooling and Stratified Chilled Water Storage Tanks. We do not repeat that material here.

Hose, airflow and supply temperature

The same ground equipment engineer writes that a PCA discharges through a 14 inch hose to an 8 inch coupler on the aircraft. He aims for a steady discharge of 34 to 40°F for hours, warns against sub-freezing discharge because a frosting coil blocks airflow and forces defrost cycles that push warm air into the cabin, and says nameplate tonnage can mislead: a 25 ton PCA compressor would not remove 25 tons of heat in most outdoor air conditions. He also says bridge conditioning units are typically 5 to 7.5 nominal tons, and that elbows and kinks matter: citing duct design guidance, he notes a 90 degree elbow can resist airflow like 30 feet of straight duct of the same size, and that a long hose kinked most of the time performs worse than a hose of the right length.

A maker data sheet for a bridge-mounted line lists airflow of 330 lb/min on its smaller models and 460 lb/min on its larger ones, a minimum discharge of 32°F that depends on ambient conditions, and 14 inch outlets. The data sheet does not state the rating conditions for its tonnage, so we do not compare its tons with the engineer's figures. A trade-press article on a central system adds that the jet bridge control has settings for wide-body, narrow-body and regional aircraft, and that a probe in the open aircraft doorway feeds temperature back to the PCA.

Worked example: tons for an assumed gate

This is our own calculation, with assumptions we chose. It is a sensible-heat estimate only. Latent load from humid air is left out, so a real unit on a muggy day needs more (see Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load).

  • Assumed airflow: 330 lb/min, taken from the data sheet above for a smaller model.
  • Assumed air density: 0.075 lb/ft³ (standard air, our assumption).
  • Assumed outdoor air: 95°F. Assumed supply to the hose: 40°F. Assumed cabin air: 75°F.

Step 1, airflow. 330 lb/min ÷ 0.075 lb/ft³ = 4,400 cfm.

Step 2, coil load. The unit cools 95°F outdoor air to 40°F, so ΔT = 95 − 40 = 55°F. Btu/h = 1.08 × 4,400 × 55 = 261,360 Btu/h. Tons = 261,360 ÷ 12,000 = 21.8 tons.

Step 3, cabin side. Once in the cabin the air warms from 40 to 75°F, so ΔT = 35°F. Btu/h = 1.08 × 4,400 × 35 = 166,320 Btu/h, or 13.9 tons of cabin heat the stream can absorb.

The gap between 21.8 and 13.9 tons is the outdoor air pulled down before it ever reaches the cabin. The 1.08 rule only gives a first check. Measure real flow at the hose end; see Measuring Airflow (CFM).

What the agencies claim about savings

We only repeat claims as stated. The FAA VALE page says grants are expected to reduce ozone emissions by 1,768 tons per year for the next 5 years. The VALE brochure says one central PCA project, serving 73 gates, is expected to avoid over 2,900 tons of nitrogen oxides over the equipment's useful life. GAO reported estimated lifetime reductions for two small projects, for example 11.1 tons of nitrogen oxides for 3 PCA units at 3 gates, and quoted FAA cost-effectiveness of $7,000 per ton for ozone and $6,000 per ton for carbon monoxide, excluding electrical upgrades and assuming at least 4 hours of daily gate occupancy. GAO also noted that FAA calls these reductions a small portion of total airport emissions, and that one airport would have installed its gate systems without a grant.

A 2010 airport authority memo estimated cuts of more than 69,000 metric tons of CO2 and other emissions per year, about 2 percent of aircraft emissions at that airport, with about $800,000 per year of added operations and maintenance cost. A trade-press article on the finished project reports the airport expects about 5 million gallons of fuel saved and 40,000 metric tons less CO2 per year. The figures differ; treat both as projections, not measured results. The EPA's West Coast Collaborative page lists plugging in at the gate as a focus area but gives no savings numbers that we could use.

A National Academies report notes that these systems are not always optimally used, so savings depend on crews connecting the hose and shutting down the APU.

Ground power at the gate

The electrical half is a converter and cable. The aircraft side uses 400 Hz power. The state aviation guideline we read requires power factor of 90 percent or higher at 50 percent load and above for such units, and asks for separate metered circuits for bridge power, PCA and 400 Hz. Electrical work belongs to the licensed electrician. A PCA also draws large three-phase current: the maker sheet lists 120 to 145 A at 480 V on its smaller 60 Hz models.

Maintenance: hoses, filters and coils

  • Filters. The ground equipment engineer calls cleaning intake filters, condenser coils and evaporator coils an easy way to protect performance. Dirty condenser coils raise head pressure; dirty filters and evaporator coils lower discharge quality and risk liquid refrigerant reaching the compressor. The maker sheet lists a MERV 11 equivalent filter with a clogging alarm. Our coil-cleaning lesson is Commercial Duct and Air Handler Cleaning.
  • Hoses. Look for kinks, crushed sections and torn ends. Match hose length to aircraft type. We did not find a published leak-rate or replacement interval, so follow the maker's manual.
  • Drain pan and plenum. The maker sheet says its plenum and stainless drain pan can be cleaned in under 2 hours once the cooling sections are out, and that those sections can be swapped in about 20 minutes.
  • Mounting. Do not alter a bridge or its warranty; the state guideline requires installers to coordinate with the bridge maintenance contractor.

Field card: Gate PCA and Jet Bridge HVAC

Cool the cabin from the gate, keep the APU off.

  • PCA is 100 percent outside air through a 14 inch hose to an 8 inch aircraft coupler (per one engineer).
  • Sensible tons = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT ÷ 12,000. Our example: 4,400 cfm, 55°F drop, 21.8 tons.
  • Avoid sub-freezing discharge; frosted coils block air and force defrost.
  • Clean filters, condenser coil and evaporator coil; check hose for kinks and tears.
  • Savings figures are agency or airport projections, not measured results.
