Hydronic Differential Pressure Control: Sensor Location, Setpoint and Pump Speed Reset

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chilled water loop with two-way control valves changes its flow all day, so the pump needs something to follow. Most of the time that something is a differential pressure (DP) signal. Where you put the sensor and what number you give it decide whether the pump saves energy or just runs fast. This lesson covers the sensor, the setpoint, the reset, and the usual ways it goes wrong.

Why the pump follows differential pressure

With two-way valves, each coil valve closes as its space is satisfied, and the water that is no longer needed has nowhere to go. Pressure in the piping rises. A pump that holds a pressure difference instead of a fixed speed slows down as valves close and speeds up as they open. For how two-way valves differ from three-way, see Two-Way vs Three-Way Control Valves. For the pump itself, see Reading Pump Curves and System Curves. Grundfos says DP sensors are the most common choice for circulating pumps, citing reliability and cost-performance, and claims savings of up to 50 percent or more depending on application and pump type. Treat that as a maker claim, not a prediction for your building.

Where the sensor goes

The Deppmann hydronics article describes a common method: a DP transmitter at the hydraulically farthest terminal unit. California's acceptance test NA7.5.9 (Reference ACE site) says that on systems without DDC to individual coils, the sensor sits at or near the most remote heat exchanger or control valve, or at the one needing the greatest differential pressure. With DDC to the coils there is no location restriction, because the controls can watch the valves themselves.

The other choice is the pump discharge and suction. Deppmann's example uses 35 ft of friction plus a 25 ft control head, 60 ft in total at the pump. A sensor across the pump has a control head of 60 ft. Sensors placed farther out had lower values: 55 ft, 45 ft and 35 ft, depending on which zone they served. The article states that sensor location has a direct effect on energy cost, and its modeled example saved almost ,000 per year when the feedback was placed correctly. That figure comes from the author's software and loading assumptions, not from a field measurement.

How the setpoint is chosen

The setpoint is the pressure the sensor must keep so the load it serves gets design flow. In Deppmann's example the control head downstream of a sensor was built as 25 + 5 + 5 = 35 ft. The author notes that the DP setpoint at maximum should deliver balanced design flow at full capacity, which may be below full pump or motor speed, and that the sensor is most useful during balancing and startup. The Continuous Commissioning chapter on chilled water adds a constant of typically 1 to 5 psi to its setpoint calculation, and says a value above that range that also exceeds design may mean a blocked coil. See Balancing Valves and Pressure Independent Control Valves for how design flow is verified.

Resetting the setpoint from valve position

A fixed setpoint is set for peak load, so at part load most valves are throttling pressure the pump made. Reset lowers the setpoint until a valve is nearly wide open. Deppmann quotes ASHRAE 90.1-2013 section 6.5.4.2: with DDC and DP control, the setpoint is reset downward based on valve positions. The Belimo article calls the zone whose valve is nearly fully open the critical zone and cites the 2016 edition of 90.1 and Title 24, which keeps the valve needing the most pressure at no less than 80 percent open. The Continuous Commissioning chapter uses 85 percent. This is the same idea as the fan lesson: VAV Duct Static Pressure Reset.

Deppmann cautions that controls react almost instantly, but pumps and drives cannot, so limit how often and how much the speed signal changes. It also notes that valve signal and position can differ because of slippage, so build in tolerance. Pump context is in Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T.

Worked example

Our own arithmetic, with simplifying assumptions: a pump at 600 gpm design and 60 ft total head. All of the head not held at the remote sensor is friction in the piping between pump and sensor, and friction scales with (Q/Qdesign) squared. The remote sensor holds a constant 15 ft. Pump power is compared as flow times head only (hydraulic power), so efficiency, motor and drive losses are ignored and treated as equal.

  1. Flow ratio at 300 gpm: 300 / 600 = 0.50.
  2. Friction part at design: 60 ft - 15 ft = 45 ft.
  3. Friction at 50 percent flow: 45 x 0.50 squared = 45 x 0.25 = 11.25 ft.
  4. Remote sensor case, required head: 15 + 11.25 = 26.25 ft.
  5. Pump-sensor case: the pump holds 60 ft, so the valves throttle away 60 - 26.25 = 33.75 ft.
  6. Relative power, design: 600 x 60 = 36,000 (gpm x ft).
  7. Pump-sensor case at 300 gpm: 300 x 60 = 18,000, which is 50 percent of design.
  8. Remote sensor case: 300 x 26.25 = 7,875, which is about 22 percent of design.
  9. Ratio of the two: 7,875 / 18,000 = 0.4375, about 44 percent. The remote sensor needs roughly 56 percent less hydraulic power at this point.

This is a bounding illustration, not a prediction. Real pumps shift efficiency as speed drops, and real loops are not one fixed branch. The Continuous Commissioning chapter shows a field case where fixing a setpoint that kept the pump at full speed cut flow by 50 percent and pump power by about 20 kW, a measured result in one building.

Common problems

  • Sensor at the pump. It forces the pump to hold the full head, as the example shows. Armstrong's FAQ goes the other direction for remote comfort complaints, saying a DP sensor may need re-setting or placing closer to the pump (Q2). Our own reading: that is the trade-off between a safer sensor near the pump and the energy saved by a remote one.
  • A rogue zone or stuck-open valve. The Continuous Commissioning chapter warns that one malfunctioning valve may cause the pump to run at full speed, and a valve still fully open at setpoint often indicates a problem with the valve or coil. Belimo says one anomalous feedback signal can disrupt the whole system, and that valves commanded open for freeze protection while the air handler is off should be dropped from the logic.
  • Plugged taps. A trade article on pressure measurement says narrow nipples (one half or three quarter inch) are more easily bridged and plugged. That article is about process piping, so use it as a general caution only.

Field checks

  • Under the California acceptance test, with flow at or below 50 percent of design, pump speed should fall and pressure should stay within 5 percent of setpoint, stabilizing within 5 minutes. At 90 to 100 percent flow, speed rises to 100 percent and the new setpoint is higher than the first. Sensors should read within 10 percent of a calibrated reference.
  • Our own: read the sensor's value on the controller and a gauge across the same taps; a large difference points to a plugged tap or bad calibration.
  • Our own: list each valve position when the setpoint stops falling; the one pinned near 100 percent is the zone to inspect.

Field card: Hydronic differential pressure control

The sensor tells the pump what the far coil needs.

  • Two-way valves make flow vary, so the pump holds DP and changes speed.
  • Remote sensor: at or near the most remote or critical coil or valve.
  • Sensor across the pump needs the full head, the highest pump energy of the choices in the example.
  • Reset lowers the setpoint until one valve is nearly open (80 to 85 percent in the sources here).
  • One bad valve can pin the pump at full speed.
  • Check taps and calibration against a gauge.
