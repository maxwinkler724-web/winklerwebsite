Cardiac Cath and Interventional Labs HVAC: Procedure Room Air Changes, Pressure, Equipment Room Heat and Cold-Room Comfort

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cardiac cath lab or interventional suite looks like an operating room with a big x-ray system in it, and the air system is somewhere in between an OR and a normal clinic. This lesson covers what the room has to do, the heat that runs all day in the control and equipment rooms, and why the staff and the patient never agree on the thermostat.

The facility's rules come first

The facility's rules come first. The infection control team, the engineer of record and the commissioning papers decide what each room must do, and the adopted code edition may differ from the public summaries below. Never change airflow, pressure, setpoints or filters in a procedure room without permission and a window when no case is running. This page is general education.

How a procedure room differs from an operating room

Our operating room lesson covers unidirectional diffusers, humidity history and setbacks in detail, so we do not repeat them. The short version for cath labs is that they are usually a step down in air changes but still positive.

A Facility Guidelines Institute (FGI) summary of the 2018 guidelines lists cardiac catheterization and angiography rooms as Class 2 imaging rooms, with 15 total air changes per hour (ACH) and positive pressure for catheterization. It lists operating rooms and hybrid operating rooms (Class 3 imaging) at 20 total ACH, positive. A general procedure room is listed at 15 ACH, positive. A public 2017 addendum to ASHRAE Standard 170 shows the same idea in its ventilation table: an interventional imaging procedure room row for x-ray in surgery, critical care and catheterization, with positive pressure, 3 outdoor ACH and 15 total ACH. The CDC's 2003 environmental infection control guideline asks for operating rooms to be positive to corridors and adjacent areas with at least 15 ACH, at least 3 of them outdoor air. A cath lab that is built as a hybrid operating room is different: FGI's hybrid OR design note says it must meet the Standard 170 operating room parameters, at least 20 ACH, and be positive to every adjacent space, including the control room.

So the first question on any call is which row the room was designed to. Look at the drawings and the room label before you assume 15 or 20.

Pressure, filters, temperature and humidity

  • Pressure. Positive to the control room, equipment room and corridors, as the drawing below shows. The same Standard 170 table has notes about pressure when a room is unoccupied and during door movement. We could not confirm how they apply to a given room, so ask the engineer before you let a cath lab go neutral at night.
  • Filters. The addendum lists two filter banks for the interventional row, MERV 8 and then MERV 14, and a procedure room row with MERV 13. It also says banks rated above MERV 12 need a manometer or differential pressure device. Read it, log it, and change filters on the schedule.
  • Temperature and humidity. The interventional row lists 70 to 75 degrees F and a maximum of 60 percent relative humidity. The procedure room row lists 20 to 60 percent. The addendum's turndown note says humidity must stay at or below 60 percent even when air changes are reduced.
  • Setbacks. Both rows list unoccupied turndown as "Yes", and the addendum says the minimum air changes, temperature and humidity must be re-established when the room is occupied. Whether a given suite uses it is the facility's decision, so do not install a schedule on your own.

Control room and equipment room heat

The x-ray generator, power cabinets, computers and monitors do not turn off when the room is empty. FGI's hybrid OR note says a system component room may be separate for heat load, infection control, noise or serviceability reasons, and that it should be sized to the maker's recommendations for installation, service and maintenance. An Australasian health facility planning guide for imaging units says rooms with heat-generating equipment may require special air-conditioning and that reporting areas need adequate ventilation and temperature control to reduce the risk of monitor failure. Neither source gives a heat load, so the number comes from the maker's site planning sheet, which you should get from the facility's imaging group or biomedical department.

The same FGI note says a control room with no door has to meet the same ventilation requirements as the operating room itself. A control room with a window and a door is a different case, but a leaky door still lets procedure room air out, so that is where the pressure arrows point.

In our experience these rooms are heat loads all day, much like the equipment rooms in our small server room lesson. Before any setback, find out whether the equipment room shares an air handler with the procedure room, and what the imaging group says the equipment needs. Our MRI and imaging suite lesson covers chillers and shielded penetrations, so we leave those out here.

Cold rooms, lead aprons and patients

A 2026 modeling study of one operating room found that at 20.5 degrees C (68.9 degrees F) and 0.28 m/s air speed, up to 75 percent of male surgeons wearing lead aprons would feel pronounced heat stress, while about 22 percent of female anesthesiologists would be dissatisfied, mostly from feeling too cool. Aprons averaged 7.5 kg. The authors say uniform temperature settings cannot give everyone comfort, and favor role-specific measures like lightweight cooling for surgeons and insulation for anesthesiologists. It is a model, with relative humidity fixed at 50 percent and no staff present, so treat the numbers as a direction, not a design rule.

The patient side matters just as much. The same paper notes that anesthesia impairs thermoregulation and that awake patients often feel cold, and NICE guidance says staying warm before surgery lowers the risk of complications. The standard range is 70 to 75 degrees F in the interventional row, and a room set colder for staff can fight the patient warming that the team is using. If staff ask for colder air, the answer is to pass the request to the charge nurse or clinical engineering, and not to drop the setpoint. The facility, not the thermostat on the wall, picks the compromise. Air that dumps cold on the table is a diffuser question for the engineer, not a field adjustment. Federal hospital rules (42 CFR 482.41) also require proper ventilation, light and temperature controls in appropriate areas.

Worked example (Our own)

Assumptions, all ours: a procedure room of 600 square feet with a 10 foot ceiling, designed to 15 total ACH and 3 outdoor ACH (the cited rows above); a 10 percent flow offset; an equipment room with 8 kW of electronics running all day; and a 20 degree F air temperature rise. A real room may be designed higher, and the drawings govern.

Procedure room. Volume = 600 x 10 = 6,000 cu ft. Total supply = 6,000 x 15 / 60 = 1,500 cfm. Outdoor air = 6,000 x 3 / 60 = 300 cfm, which is 3 / 15 = 20 percent of supply. With the assumed 10 percent offset, return plus exhaust = 1,500 x 0.90 = 1,350 cfm, and about 150 cfm leaves through the doors and cracks, which is what keeps the room positive. If you were told the room is a hybrid OR at 20 ACH, the supply becomes 6,000 x 20 / 60 = 2,000 cfm.

Equipment room. 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h (standard conversion), so 8 x 3,412 = 27,296 Btu/h. Tons = 27,296 / 12,000 = 2.27 tons. Airflow at a 20 degree F rise with the sensible heat formula Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T: cfm = 27,296 / (1.08 x 20) = 27,296 / 21.6 = 1,264 cfm. That is close to the whole procedure room supply, so a unit sized for "a little electronics" can fall well short. Heat from other lights, people or the generator cooling loop would add to this.

Field card: Cath Lab and Interventional Room HVAC

Positive pressure, quiet heat, and no change without the owner.

  • Find the design row: cath lab at 15 total and 3 outdoor ACH, hybrid OR at 20 total, from the cited summaries.
  • Procedure room stays positive to the control room, equipment room and corridors.
  • Interventional row: 70 to 75 degrees F, humidity at or below 60 percent, MERV 8 then MERV 14.
  • Equipment room heat runs 24 hours: kW x 3,412 = Btu/h; cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T).
  • Our example: 8 kW is 27,296 Btu/h, 2.27 tons, 1,264 cfm at 20 F.
  • Staff in lead feel hot, patients feel cold. Do not lower setpoints on request; route it to the facility.
  • Setbacks only where the facility approves and the room recovers before cases.
