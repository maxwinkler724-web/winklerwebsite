Battery Room Ventilation: Hydrogen, Exhaust Rates and Lithium-Ion Risks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A battery room looks like an electrical room, but the air has a job the transformer room never gives it: keeping hydrogen from collecting near the ceiling. This lesson covers where the hydrogen comes from, how the codes frame the limit, how to estimate the airflow, and why lithium-ion rooms are a different problem.

Check the code your jurisdiction uses

Check the adopted code. Fire and mechanical codes are adopted and amended locally, and the battery rules have moved between editions. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. The design belongs to the engineer and the authority having jurisdiction, not to this page.

Why batteries give off hydrogen

A lead-acid cell on charge splits some of the water in its electrolyte into hydrogen and oxygen. A battery maker's support page puts the yield at about 0.42 liters of hydrogen per amp-hour of overcharge, per cell, and says the oxygen adds half that volume again. Gassing is heaviest at the end of a charge or during an equalize charge, when more current goes into splitting water than into storing energy. Flooded cells vent that gas straight into the room. Valve-regulated (VRLA) cells recombine most of it inside the case, so they gas far less on float, but they still have relief valves and can gas hard when overcharged or failing. A Battcon checklist example puts a healthy 240-cell, 400 Ah VRLA string at only 0.086 cubic foot of hydrogen per day on normal float. Heat and a high charger setting are what turn that into a real problem.

OSHA's general rule for electrical installations, 29 CFR 1910.305(j)(7), asks for enough diffusion and ventilation of battery gases to prevent an explosive mixture. The construction rule, 1926.441, says unsealed batteries belong in enclosures with outside vents or in well ventilated rooms. Neither gives a number. The numbers come from the codes below.

The 1 percent idea

Hydrogen burns in air at roughly 4 percent by volume. Codes keep the room well under that by capping it at 1 percent of the room volume, which is 25 percent of the lower flammable limit. An ICC code change proposal we read quotes the older International Mechanical Code wording: ventilation designed to hold hydrogen to 1.0 percent of the room volume, with continuous ventilation of not less than 1 cfm per square foot of floor area. That proposal moved the details into Section 608 of the International Fire Code. Trade articles describe the same two paths today. Path one is continuous exhaust at 1 cfm per square foot of floor. Path two is a hydrogen detector that alarms and runs the exhaust at the threshold, sized with the IEEE 1635 and ASHRAE Guideline 21 method. Ask the authority which path and which edition apply, because we did not read the current adopted text.

Where the exhaust goes

  • High. Hydrogen rises. An ACHR News design article says wall exhaust fans go as close to the ceiling as possible and discharge outdoors, and that the ceiling should be smooth so gas does not pool in pockets.
  • Makeup low. The same article's case study put makeup air dampers about 12 inches off the floor, so air sweeps across the racks and up.
  • Detectors high. A monitoring-vendor article puts hydrogen sensors at the highest point or above the racks.
  • Watch the fan. A seized fan on a continuous-ventilation room goes unnoticed unless someone watches it, so the fan run status should be wired to an alarm on stoppage. Detector health needs the same supervision.

Worked example: a small UPS string

This estimate is our own. The 0.42 liter per amp-hour yield comes from the battery maker page above; everything else is an assumption we chose to show the arithmetic, so replace it with the maker's data for a real string.

  • Assumed string: 240 flooded 2 volt cells (a 480 volt nominal UPS string), 400 Ah.
  • Assumed gassing current: 4 A into the whole string during an equalize charge (about 1 A per 100 Ah). On a series string the same current passes through every cell.
  • Assumed room: 20 ft by 14 ft by 10 ft, so 2,800 cubic feet and 280 square feet of floor. Assume the air mixes evenly, which hydrogen will not do on its own.

Step 1, hydrogen per hour: 0.42 L/Ah × 4 A × 240 cells = 403.2 L/h.
Step 2, convert: 403.2 ÷ 28.317 L per cubic foot = 14.24 cubic feet per hour, or 0.237 cubic foot per minute.
Step 3, time to reach 1 percent with no ventilation: 1 percent of 2,800 is 28 cubic feet. 28 ÷ 14.24 = about 2.0 hours.
Step 4, airflow to hold 1 percent: 0.237 ÷ 0.01 = about 24 cfm. Adding a 25 percent blower safety factor, a figure the maker page lists, gives about 30 cfm.
Step 5, compare to the continuous rule: 1 cfm per square foot × 280 = 280 cfm. At 280 cfm the room would settle at 0.237 ÷ 280 = 0.085 percent hydrogen.

The lesson in the numbers: the 1 cfm per square foot rule is generous for this string, which is why designers sometimes choose the detector path. But the rule does not depend on guesses about gassing current, and the 30 cfm answer only holds if the charger behaves. Compare 14.24 cubic feet per hour on equalize with the 0.086 cubic foot per day float figure above: gassing during a charge problem can be thousands of times a healthy float rate, although the two examples are different cell types. That gap is why a stuck charger is the case to design for.

Temperature and battery life

The Battcon checklist asks whether the room is held at or below 77°F (25°C) around the clock. A Sandia-hosted survey paper on valve-regulated cells says design life is cut about in half for every 15°F the ambient temperature rises above the optimal temperature. Heat also raises float current, which raises gassing. A hot battery room therefore needs cooling as well as exhaust; see cooling electrical rooms and UPS heat for the heat side, and keep in mind that continuous exhaust drags warm outdoor air in, which a monitoring-vendor article names as a cost of the first path.

What is different about lithium-ion

Sealed lithium-ion cells do not off-gas hydrogen in normal operation. The risk is an abnormal event. A Sandia slide deck from the national lab's storage safety program describes a first stage of cell venting, mostly electrolyte solvent vapor with small amounts of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, followed by thermal runaway, which produces mostly carbon monoxide, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and organic vapors at a far larger rate. Early off-gas detection is aimed at that first stage, paired with isolating the battery from charge or load. Because the lithium risk is an event, not a steady trickle, a fixed 1 cfm per square foot rule does not describe it.

Lithium rooms are governed by NFPA 855, the standard for stationary energy storage systems. A Jensen Hughes engineering article says NFPA 855 and the fire code require explosion protection above certain energy thresholds. It describes two approaches: deflagration venting under NFPA 68, which needs an ignition to open the panels, and exhaust that holds battery gas below 25 percent of the lower flammable limit under NFPA 69, which can fail if backup power or maintenance is lacking and can be overwhelmed by local pockets. The article suggests the two together. The release rate, it says, is the hardest design input. That is engineer territory, with a hazard analysis, listed equipment and fire department input. A technician's job is to recognize which kind of room this is and not apply lead-acid rules by habit.

Field card: Battery Room Ventilation

Hydrogen rises: exhaust high, makeup low, watch the fan.

  • Charging splits water; about 0.42 L of hydrogen per Ah of overcharge per cell (maker page).
  • Limit: 1 percent of room volume, which is 25 percent of the lower flammable limit.
  • Continuous path: 1 cfm per square foot of floor, exhausted outdoors.
  • Detector path: detector trips exhaust; supervise the detector.
  • Alarm on fan stoppage.
  • Keep the room near 77°F; heat shortens life and raises gassing.
  • Lithium-ion is an event hazard under NFPA 855; do not reuse lead-acid rules.
