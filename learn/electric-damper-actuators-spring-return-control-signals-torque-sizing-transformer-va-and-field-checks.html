Electric Damper Actuators: Spring Return, Signals, Torque and VA

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A damper actuator is a small motor and gearbox that turns a damper shaft. Picking the wrong one causes three kinds of calls: a damper that will not close, a damper that stalls halfway, and a transformer that runs hot. This lesson covers what to specify, how to size it, and what to test before you leave.

Spring return or not

A spring-return actuator stores energy in a spring while powered and drives the damper to a set position when power is lost. A non-spring-return actuator stays where it was when power drops. The choice is about what you want the damper to do in a failure.

  • Outdoor air: the PNNL economizer best-practices page says the fail-safe position for an outdoor-air damper is normally closed when the electric actuator is not powered, and that some actuators use a mechanical spring to get there. A university control-devices standard (University of Colorado Anschutz) goes further and requires spring return to closed on every damper that opens to the outdoors. A university lab specification (University of Maryland, Baltimore) requires spring return for freeze, fire, moisture and occupant safety applications and notes that fail-in-place actuators have no spring.
  • Freeze protection: a closed outdoor-air damper on power loss keeps cold air off a hydronic coil when the fan and controls are dead. Pair it with a freeze sensor wired as the sequence calls for.
  • Where spring return is not needed: a university BAS specification (University of Central Florida) requires a spring or capacitor return only where the drawings indicate a power-failure or safety need. Mixed-air or return dampers inside the unit often use a plain actuator.

The same UCF specification tells the installer to verify during checkout that the fail-safe direction is correct for every actuator. Do that on every job: cut power and watch.

Control types and feedback

  • Two-position (open/close): power on one wire opens, remove it and the spring closes, or a second wire drives it closed. Good for isolation and minimum-ventilation dampers.
  • Floating (tri-state): one input drives open, another drives closed, and neither holds. The controller does not know the position unless it counts run time. The Colorado standard allows "proportional or 3 point floating" for terminal valve actuators, and the Maryland specification limits floating control to terminal hot and chilled water.
  • Modulating: the UCF specification has proportional actuators accept 0 to 10 VDC and operate across a 2 to 10 VDC range, so 2 V is closed and 10 V is open. Below 2 V the actuator sits at its closed stop. Current-loop versions use 4 to 20 mA the same way.
  • Feedback: UCF requires proportional actuators to return 0 to 10 VDC or 4 to 20 mA position feedback, shown as a percent on the BAS graphic. Colorado requires a position indicator on each primary damper actuator. Feedback is the actuator reporting where it thinks it is. It does not prove the blades moved.

The UCF specification also says a controller output span should match the actuator travel time. Its example is a 90-second damper with a 90-second full-span output, so the loop does not outrun the motor. See BAS Point Types and Reading the Graphics for analog input and output basics.

Coupling, stroke and manual override

UCF says to mount actuators directly on the damper shaft or jackshaft unless the drawings show a linkage. Direct coupling uses a clamp that grips the shaft, so there is no linkage slop. Linked installations use a crank arm and rod, as in many packaged rooftop units where one actuator drives both the outdoor-air and return-air dampers (PNNL). Gear-driven dampers have less slop and close better.

Set the stroke so the damper reaches both stops without the actuator pushing hard against them. Many actuators have adjustable end stops. UCF adds a procedure for tight-sealing dampers: power a normally closed spring-return actuator to about 5 degrees open, close the damper by hand so the seals compress, then tighten the clamp or linkage, and leave at least 5 degrees of travel for that purpose.

For manual override, UCF requires non-spring-return actuators to have an external manual gear release and spring-return actuators above 60 in-lb to include a manual crank. Colorado requires a manual override release on modulating actuators. Use it to test blade travel with the power off.

Torque sizing

Torque is the turning effort the actuator must supply to move the blades against seals, friction and air pressure. Damper makers publish a torque requirement in in-lb per square foot of damper face area, and it rises with air velocity, pressure and seal type. We found no government or university standard that gives a single per-square-foot number. Colorado requires pneumatic actuator torque ratings to be 150 percent of the application requirement, and Maryland and Kentucky specifications require damper schedules that list torque requirements and actuator capacities. So the per-square-foot value in the example below is an assumption, and the real number comes from the damper maker's data for your damper.

Worked example (our own example)

An outdoor-air damper is 36 in. wide by 24 in. high. Assumptions: the damper data sheet calls for 5 in-lb per sq ft (our assumed value), we apply a 1.5 safety factor (our choice, in line with the 150 percent Colorado uses for pneumatic actuators), and actuators are sold in 35, 60 and 90 in-lb ratings (assumed).

  1. Area: 36 in. x 24 in. = 864 sq in. 864 / 144 = 6.0 sq ft.
  2. Required torque: 6.0 sq ft x 5 in-lb/sq ft = 30 in-lb.
  3. With safety factor: 30 x 1.5 = 45 in-lb.
  4. Choose: 35 is too small, so pick the 60 in-lb spring-return actuator (outdoor air, fail closed). Check that its spring-return torque, not only its powered torque, meets the 45 in-lb need if the damper data requires it to close against wind.

Transformer VA for four actuators (our own example)

Assume each actuator is labeled 10 VA (our assumed value; use the nameplate). Four actuators: 4 x 10 VA = 40 VA. The UCF specification limits connected loads to 80 percent of transformer rating, so the minimum rating is 40 / 0.80 = 50 VA. That leaves no room for a relay or sensor, so we would choose the next size up, an assumed 75 VA Class 2 transformer, and keep damper actuators on their own transformer or branch from the controller. UCF requires 24 VAC and 24 VDC actuators to use Class 2 wiring, and Colorado requires Class 2 current-limiting transformers. If the sum of all loads on one transformer exceeds its rating, the secondary voltage sags and actuators slow or stall. See Relays, Transformers and 24V Control for how VA ratings work.

Field checks

  1. Command vs feedback: command 0, 50 and 100 percent from the BAS and compare the feedback. A university sequence (Virginia Tech) defines a damper fault as commanded open while the status reads closed, and also flags the reverse as a hand-operated damper.
  2. Look at the blades, not the screen: a matching number with a closed damper means a slipping clamp or broken linkage. Mark the shaft and the clamp with a paint pen to see slip.
  3. Binding blades: with power off, use the manual release to move the blades. If they are stiff or rub, fix the damper first. A bigger actuator on a bound damper bends linkage.
  4. Stroke and stops: confirm full travel in both directions and a tight close at the end stop.
  5. Fail position: remove power and confirm the damper goes where the drawings say.
  6. Supply voltage: read 24 VAC at the actuator while it moves, not only at the transformer.

For economizer-specific faults, see RTU Economizer Fault Diagnosis and Damper Actuator Checks and Economizer Settings. For older pneumatic equipment, see Pneumatic HVAC Controls in Older Buildings.

Field card: Electric Damper Actuators: Spring Return, Signals, Torque and VA

Size it, power it, then watch it move.

  • Outdoor-air dampers normally fail closed; spring return gets them there.
  • Modulating: 2 to 10 VDC, 2 V closed, 10 V open (UCF).
  • Torque: area x in-lb per sq ft from the damper data x safety factor.
  • Our example: 36 x 24 in. = 6.0 sq ft, 30 in-lb, 45 in-lb with 1.5, choose 60 in-lb.
  • Transformer: add VA, keep loads to 80 percent of rating.
  • Test command vs feedback, binding, clamp slip and fail direction.
