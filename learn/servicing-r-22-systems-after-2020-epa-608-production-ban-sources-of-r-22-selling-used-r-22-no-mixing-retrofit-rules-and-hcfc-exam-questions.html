Servicing R-22 Systems After 2020: EPA 608 Rules on Supply, Resale, Mixing and Retrofits

Last updated: 2026-11-03

R-22 is still in a lot of attics, rooftops and back yards, and a technician still gets called to service it. Since January 1, 2020 nobody in the United States may make or import new R-22, so every pound you add has to come from somewhere else. This lesson is the technician side: where that R-22 can come from, what you may and may not do with it, and the HCFC questions that show up on the exam.

Check the sources

Check the sources. This page is general education built from EPA web pages and the federal regulation text read in 2026. Rules, supply and EPA pages change. Confirm everything on the EPA site and the eCFR before you act, and follow the equipment maker's instructions. Nothing here is legal advice.

What changed, and what did not

EPA's phaseout table for HCFC-22 (R-22) has two steps. From January 1, 2010 there was no production or import except for use in equipment manufactured before that date. From January 1, 2020 there is no production or import at all. EPA also says some uses continue, such as servicing refrigeration and air-conditioning equipment. The regulation (40 CFR 82.15) lists refrigerant use in equipment manufactured before January 1, 2010 among the permitted uses of the 2010 step.

What did not change: owning, running and servicing an existing R-22 system is still legal. EPA's residential fact sheet says owners may keep using HCFC-22 in an existing system for as long as necessary, and its technician FAQ says existing equipment can still be serviced, including replacing failed components. The owner side of that decision is in My AC Uses R-22: Repair or Replace?, and the treaty background is in Ozone Rules and the Montreal Protocol.

Where R-22 can still come from

EPA's residential fact sheet names the supply for existing systems: recovered and reclaimed HCFC-22, plus stock produced before 2020. That gives four practical sources, and each has a different rule attached.

  • Recovered from the same owner's equipment. EPA's technician brochure says refrigerant recovered from equipment can be recharged into the same appliance, or into another appliance owned by the same person, without first being recycled or reclaimed.
  • Recycled. The definition in 40 CFR 82.152 is cleaning recovered refrigerant for reuse in the same owner's equipment, usually by oil separation and filter-driers, without meeting the full reclamation specification.
  • Reclaimed. Reclaiming means reprocessing recovered refrigerant to the specification in Appendix A of the same subpart and verifying it. EPA's technician FAQ says reclaimed refrigerant can be sold and used to service any existing equipment.
  • Existing stock. R-22 produced before 2020 is still legal to use for servicing. EPA's technician brochure says supplies will tighten and it is hard to predict when they run out.

Buying is limited too. EPA's consumer FAQ says refrigerant for stationary appliances is sold only to certified technicians or the companies that employ them. See The EPA Refrigerant Sales Restriction.

Selling used R-22: reclaim first when ownership changes

This is the rule people trip over. Under 40 CFR 82.154(d), no one may sell, distribute or offer for sale, for use as a refrigerant, a Class II substance such as R-22 that is wholly or partly used refrigerant, unless one of the listed conditions is met. The one that matters most in the field is that the refrigerant has been reclaimed by a reclaimer certified under 40 CFR 82.164. EPA's technician FAQ puts it plainly: recovered refrigerant cannot be sold to a new owner for use as a refrigerant until an EPA-certified reclaimer has reclaimed it.

The listed exceptions are narrow. One is a transfer between a parent company and its subsidiaries, another a transfer within one federal agency's own facilities. An independent contractor selling a cylinder of recovered R-22 to another contractor is not covered by either. Owners, the same-owner reuse rule and what reclaimers must do are covered in Reusing Recovered Refrigerant, so we do not repeat them here.

The 2010 line for new equipment

EPA's commercial FAQ says virgin HCFC-22 is not allowed in refrigeration systems manufactured on or after January 1, 2010. A system counts as manufactured when its refrigerant circuit is complete and ready for its intended purpose, and expanding an existing system to increase cooling capacity can make it "new." The FAQ adds that recovered or reclaimed HCFC-22 must be used any time refrigerant is added to such an expanded system. EPA's technician brochure also says EPA has banned manufacturing new air-conditioning equipment that contains R-22. For the technician the takeaway is simple: no new R-22 equipment is being built, and a project that adds capacity to an old system needs a second look before anyone reaches for a cylinder.

Do not mix refrigerants or top off with a different one

We want to be exact here. The EPA pages we read do not contain a single sentence reading "never mix refrigerants." The rule is the sum of several things they do say:

  • EPA says there are no drop-in replacements for HCFC-22. A change of refrigerant is a retrofit with an approved substitute, and it usually needs component changes.
  • EPA asks owners to have leaks found and repaired rather than adding refrigerant to a leaking system, and its technician brochure says to repair leaks rather than top off.
  • EPA warns that "R-22a" is a flammable hydrocarbon blend of propane and butane, is not legal in equipment designed for HCFC-22, and is not an acceptable alternative. Never add it to a system.
  • Some blends that include R-22 (EPA lists R-401A, R-402A, R-408A, R-409A, R-414B and R-502A) fall under the same rules because they contain a Class II substance. Do not assume a blend with a different number can be added to a straight R-22 system.

A mixed charge also makes the next job harder. Recovered refrigerant that is a mixture cannot simply be reused, and it is a problem for reclamation; see the mixed-cylinder section of Reusing Recovered Refrigerant. If a system is low, find the leak. If you are changing refrigerants, recover first and treat it as a retrofit.

Retrofitting to an approved substitute

EPA does not publish retrofit procedures because equipment varies; it says the system manufacturer is the best source on how a substitute will perform. What the EPA pages do say, and what we add as shop practice, fits into a short sequence:

  1. Fix the leak first. A retrofit into a leaking system wastes the new refrigerant.
  2. Recover the R-22. EPA says the technician should recover the remaining HCFC-22 before a retrofit. Evacuate to the level in 40 CFR 82.156 Table 1; for a high-pressure R-22 appliance under 200 lb that table lists 0 inches Hg, and you must verify the level was reached. Details are in the recovery lessons linked below.
  3. Choose a substitute on EPA's list of acceptable substitutes for this use. The table for residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps, as we read it in 2026, lists several acceptable retrofit refrigerants, for example R-407C, R-417A and R-422D, and marks some as retrofit only. Flammable Class 3 refrigerants such as R-290 and R-600a, and products sold as "22a," are unacceptable for retrofitting split air conditioners and heat pumps (as of January 3, 2017). An EPA substitutes listing covers ozone depletion, global warming, flammability and toxicity, not performance or component fit, so ask the maker.
  4. Follow the equipment and refrigerant maker on oil and parts. Oil type, filter-drier, metering device and seals are the maker's call. EPA notes that HCFC-22 and some retrofit refrigerants can be incompatible with certain desiccants and hoses. Our compressor oils lesson covers flushing.
  5. Label the system. The EPA pages we read set no general label rule for these retrofits, but tag the unit with the new refrigerant, the oil, the date and your company, plus any label the substitute's conditions call for. The next technician has to know what is inside.
  6. Tell the owner. EPA says to explain how the new refrigerant may change performance and energy use.

EPA also says technicians who use substitutes should be trained in retrofit installation and servicing, and that some alternatives are not allowed in retrofits.

Worked example (our own): a 20-year-old R-22 split system

This is our own made-up example, not EPA data. Assumptions: a residential split system installed 20 years ago, nameplate factory charge 7.0 lb of R-22 (we assume this matches the installed line set), cooling poorly. The owner says it was last charged about 200 days ago. We recover everything into a cylinder and weigh it: 5.5 lb.

Is 82.157 in play? No. Section 82.157 applies to appliances with a full charge of 50 lb or more, and this system holds 7.0 lb, so its leak-repair triggers do not apply. We still calculate a loss rate as a gauge, using the annualizing steps taught in Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate:

  1. Pounds missing: 7.0 - 5.5 = 1.5 lb. Divide by full charge: 1.5 / 7.0 = 0.214.
  2. Days since last addition, 200, divided by 365: 200 / 365 = 0.548.
  3. 0.214 / 0.548 = 0.391.
  4. Times 100: about 39 percent per year.

For comparison, the trigger EPA uses for comfort cooling on large appliances is 10 percent, but that is a reference only here. At roughly 39 percent a year this system is leaking something real.

Option A: repair and recharge. Find and fix the leak, pressure test, evacuate, then weigh in 7.0 lb. The 5.5 lb you recovered can go back into this owner's system under the same-owner rule. The other 1.5 lb must be reclaimed R-22 or pre-2020 stock, and it must not come from another customer's recovered cylinder unless a certified reclaimer has processed it (82.154(d)). Do not top off with another refrigerant.

Option B: retrofit. Fix the leak, send or hold the 5.5 lb under the same-owner or reclaimer rules, pick a substitute EPA lists as acceptable from the maker's guidance, change oil and parts as the maker says, charge, label, and tell the owner about capacity and efficiency effects. A 20-year-old coil and line set are worth a careful look first.

Option C: replace. Recover the R-22 to the 0 inches Hg level, verify, and handle the cylinder the same way as above. New equipment will not be an R-22 design, since virgin R-22 is not allowed in systems made after January 1, 2010. We do not compare costs here.

HCFC exam questions that go with this

  1. Is it legal to service an existing R-22 system after 2020? Yes. Production and import ended, but servicing continues with recovered, reclaimed and previously produced R-22.
  2. When did production and import of R-22 end? January 1, 2020.
  3. May virgin R-22 be used in a system manufactured on or after January 1, 2010? No.
  4. A contractor recovers R-22 from a customer's unit and wants to sell it to another contractor. What must happen first? A certified reclaimer must reclaim it.
  5. May recovered R-22 go back into the same owner's system? Yes. It may also go into another appliance owned by the same person.
  6. Is there a drop-in replacement for R-22? No. A retrofit needs a substitute on EPA's list of acceptable substitutes for that use.
  7. What is "R-22a"? A flammable hydrocarbon blend that is not an acceptable substitute for R-22 in split air conditioners and heat pumps.
  8. Before retrofitting, what happens to the original R-22? It is recovered.
  9. Who may buy refrigerant for stationary equipment? Certified technicians or the companies that employ them.

Field card: Servicing R-22 After 2020

Existing stock and reclaim only; recover before you change anything.

  • Production and import of R-22 ended January 1, 2020; servicing existing equipment is still allowed.
  • Supply: same-owner recovered, recycled for same owner, reclaimed, pre-2020 stock.
  • Used R-22 to a new owner must be reclaimed by a certified reclaimer first (82.154(d)).
  • No virgin R-22 in systems made on or after January 1, 2010.
  • No drop-ins. Do not mix or top off with another refrigerant. Never use "22a."
  • Retrofit: fix leak, recover R-22, substitute EPA lists as acceptable, maker's oil and parts, label, tell the owner.
  • Under 50 lb: 82.157 leak repair does not apply, but leaks still matter.
