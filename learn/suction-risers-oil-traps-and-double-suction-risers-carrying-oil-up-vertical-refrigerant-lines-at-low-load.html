Suction Risers, Oil Traps and Double Risers: Carrying Oil Up at Low Load

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A compressor sends a little oil out with the discharge gas, and every drop has to come back. On a horizontal line, moving gas pushes the oil along. In a vertical riser, the gas has to lift it. This lesson is about how to make that happen at full load and, harder, at low load: riser velocity, traps, double risers, loops and pitch.

Check the maker's document for your system

Check the maker's document for your system. Published minimum velocities, trap rules and pitch differ from one source to the next, and we show that spread below on purpose. The installation manual or piping guide for the exact equipment you are working on governs. This lesson is general education. For tube sizing and pressure drop on long runs, see Line Set Sizing for Long Runs; here we stay on risers and traps.

Why oil has to be carried up

Oil that leaves with the discharge gas travels through the condenser, the liquid line and the evaporator, and it must return through the suction line. A trade-press article on oil return says the compressor can be left with too little oil to lubricate properly, which leads to premature failure, and that oil pooling in the evaporator reduces heat transfer and can make operation unstable. A maker's piping guide adds that when velocity is too low, oil stays in the piping and the sump can run dry.

Research backs up the riser problem. A University of Illinois paper presented at the Purdue refrigeration conference tested R410A with POE oil in clear tubes. Below a flow level called the Jacobs limit, a vertical line stopped behaving smoothly: the oil film could run back down, oil collected at the bottom, and the flow turned to churning slugs. Near that limit the vertical tube held about twice as much oil as the horizontal one, and getting back to smooth flow took more gas than it took to lose it. The authors recommend staying above the limit.

Riser versus horizontal velocity

Every source we read asks for more speed in a riser than in a horizontal run. The numbers are not the same, so treat them as a pattern:

  • A maker's piping design guide for applied systems: 1,000 feet per minute (fpm) minimum in vertical suction risers and 500 fpm in horizontal lines.
  • A trade-press oil return article: 1,500 fpm vertical and 700 fpm horizontal.
  • A maker's commercial split guideline: 1,200 fpm minimum in risers (1,500 preferred) and 800 fpm horizontal, with velocity kept at or below 2,000 fpm for noise.
  • A trade-association piping handbook (metric): 5 m/s in risers and 2.5 m/s horizontal, which is roughly 1,000 and 500 fpm.
  • A maker's engineering newsletter gives a table by tube size. For 2-1/8 in. tube it lists 980 fpm in a riser and 735 fpm horizontal; for 3-1/8 in. it lists 1,180 and 885. The same table says to stay below 4,000 fpm.

The tube-size table shows why one number is not enough: larger tube needs more velocity to move the oil film.

Traps: what they do and where sources disagree

A P-trap is a short dip in the line where oil collects until the gas can push it up the next riser. The sources that call for traps agree on the idea but not on spacing. The trade-press article says to put a trap at the base of a riser when the compressor sits more than 3 to 4 feet above the evaporator, then add another about every 20 feet of rise, keeping traps shallow with short horizontal legs. A maker's commercial split guideline wants a trap at the bottom of every suction riser, a second halfway up for cooling-only lifts of 50 to 100 feet, and traps at one-third intervals above 100 feet. The metric handbook says one trap drains up to a 7.5 m riser, with another for each additional 7.5 m.

Two sources push the other way. A maker's engineering newsletter says riser traps are unnecessary when the riser is sized to hold velocity, because a trap only adds pressure drop. A maker's design guide says intermediate traps are unnecessary in a properly sized riser and that a trap at the base mainly catches condensed refrigerant. The lesson: do not add traps by habit. Follow the maker's rule for that unit, and keep any trap you do add small.

The double riser

When a system can unload far, a riser sized for full gas flow becomes too slow at low load. A maker's commercial split guideline suggests a double riser when the unit can unload more than 50 percent and minimum-load velocity is too low to return oil. The fix is two risers in parallel:

  • The small riser is sized to keep velocity high enough for oil at minimum load.
  • The large riser is sized so the two together carry full load. In one maker's method, large riser area equals the single-riser area minus the small riser area.
  • A trap sits at the base of the large riser. At low load, oil does not climb the large riser, runs back, and fills the trap. The trap seals that riser and all the gas goes up the small one.
  • When load rises, the greater gas flow clears the trap and both risers flow. A maker's guide says the second riser should enter the main from the top.

Worked example: velocity at full and reduced load

Assumptions (our own, for practice only): a commercial suction line carries 100 cubic feet per minute (cfm) of suction gas at full load and 25 cfm at 25 percent load. A single 3-1/8 in. riser has a flow area of 6.812 square inches (area from a maker's design guide). We use the 1,000 fpm riser minimum from that same guide.

  1. Convert area: 6.812 in² ÷ 144 = 0.0473 ft².
  2. Full load: 100 cfm ÷ 0.0473 ft² = about 2,114 fpm. That is above 1,000 and below the 4,000 fpm ceiling in the newsletter table.
  3. 25 percent load: 25 cfm ÷ 0.0473 ft² = about 528 fpm. That is well under 1,000, so oil would fall back in a single riser.
  4. Add a small riser of 2-1/8 in. (3.095 in² in the same guide, or 0.0215 ft²). With the trap sealing the large riser at low load: 25 cfm ÷ 0.0215 ft² = about 1,163 fpm. That clears 1,000.
  5. Full load with both open. The guide's method wants a large riser of 3.717 in², and the closest standard size is 2-5/8 in. (our own area figure for that size: about 4.77 in²). Total area: (3.095 + 4.77) ÷ 144 = 0.0546 ft². So 100 cfm ÷ 0.0546 = about 1,830 fpm, still above the minimum.

Notice the margin at low load is thin. Against the 1,200 fpm riser minimum in a different maker's guideline, 1,163 fpm would miss. That is why we check the chart for the equipment and, as one maker's guide says, design without a double riser if a single riser works. That guide also notes that undersizing the riser by one pipe size can help oil management in many air conditioning applications.

Loops, risers above the evaporator and pitch

One maker's guide requires an inverted trap when the evaporator sits above the compressor: the suction line must rise above the top of the evaporator before going to the compressor, so liquid does not drain down the line during the off cycle. It is not needed when the compressor is below the coil and the line is properly sloped. Another maker's guideline says connections to a suction main should loop over and enter the top of the main, so oil does not drain into idle risers.

Pitch also varies by source. One maker says 1/8 in. per foot in the direction of flow. The trade-press article says at least 1/2 in. per 10 feet toward the compressor. The metric handbook says at least 12 mm per 3 m toward the compressor when the evaporator is above the compressor. One engineering newsletter instead says 1 in. per 10 feet toward the evaporator, to keep condensed refrigerant from draining to the compressor when off. These are different design choices, so use the maker's rule, and avoid dips and low spots that collect oil.

Signs of poor oil return

  • Low oil in the compressor sump or sight glass while the system runs, especially after low-load periods.
  • Oil logging in the evaporator, with weak capacity and unstable operation (the trade-press article ties oil pooling to reduced heat transfer and instability).
  • Short compressor life from poor lubrication (the same article says premature failure).

See Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return for the field checks, and Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging for the opposite problem. Oil analysis can show wear that points back to lubrication trouble: Chiller and Compressor Oil Analysis. Capacity control that keeps gas flow up is covered in Hot Gas Bypass and Capacity Control.

Field card: Suction Risers, Oil Traps and Double Risers: Carrying Oil Up at Low Load

Oil rides the gas. Low load is where the riser fails.

  • Risers need more velocity than horizontal runs; every source we read says so.
  • Check riser velocity at minimum load, not just full load.
  • Double riser: small riser carries oil at low load, trap fills and seals the large one.
  • Trap spacing and need vary by maker; follow the document for your unit.
  • Evaporator above the compressor: rise above the coil top first, per the maker.
  • Pitch rules differ; avoid dips and low spots.
  • Warning signs: low sump oil, oil in the evaporator, short compressor life.
