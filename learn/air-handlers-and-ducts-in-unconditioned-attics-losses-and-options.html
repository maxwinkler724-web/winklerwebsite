Air Handlers and Ducts in Unconditioned Attics: Losses and Options

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "air handler in attic efficiency loss," the short answer from U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Building America material is that ducts in an unconditioned attic can lose an estimated 10 to 45 percent of the energy used for heating and cooling. This lesson covers why systems end up there, what research measured, the fixes in order of ambition, and what a serviceable attic install needs.

Why systems end up in attics

A DOE Building America profile on high-performance ducts gives the plain reason: an attic has room for ductwork, and putting the air handler there frees floor space in the living area. A DOE page on unvented attics adds that much of the U.S. housing industry prefers attic HVAC. The cost is that a vented attic is very hot in summer and very cold in winter, so the equipment and every foot of duct sit in the worst air in the house. We did not find a source giving how common this is in New Jersey specifically, so this lesson makes no such claim.

What the research says is lost

Two separate things go wrong, and the sources keep them apart. Air leaks out of (or into) the ducts, and heat soaks through the duct walls even when no air leaks. Our duct insulation lesson covers the second path, and the duct sealing lesson covers the first. Here is what the studies we read found:

  • DOE Building America. Thermal losses for ducts in unconditioned attics are estimated at 10 to 45 percent, and properly installed buried ducts can cut that to 3 percent or less. A second DOE profile says duct leakage commonly exceeds 20 percent of airflow and that heating and cooling energy typically rises about 10 percent with ducts in a vented attic.
  • Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC), 1990. Cummings and coauthors monitored 24 Florida homes before and after duct repair. Average cooling use fell from 41.3 to 34.2 kWh per day, an 18 percent drop, with individual homes ranging from 3.5 to 44.6 percent. A later FSEC paper (McIlvaine and others, 2002) says mesh-and-mastic sealing saves about 15 percent of heating cost or 20 percent of cooling cost.
  • Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL). In 1999 field tests of two new houses, delivery effectiveness (the share of conditioned air energy that reaches the rooms) was about 75 to 89 percent as found. The authors wrote that duct efficiency can matter as much as differences in SEER rating, and the capacity measured at the registers was about one third below nameplate.
  • NREL (ACEEE 2010). Roberts and Winkler ran ASHRAE 152 calculations for Houston, Phoenix and Las Vegas homes and found seasonal cooling distribution efficiency of 79 to 82 percent, a roughly 20 percent loss. These are hot-climate models, not New Jersey data.
  • Building Science Corporation (BSC). Joseph Lstiburek's analysis puts summer attic air near 120 to 130°F and roof-deck undersides at 170 to 180°F. His own modeling, which he says is not independent measurement, shows airtight attic ducts costing about 10 percent more energy than ducts inside, and a typical install 30 percent or more.

These numbers vary because the studies use different climates, houses and methods. Treat them as a range, not a promise for your house.

Options, from small fix to big change

1. Seal and insulate the ducts

Cheapest and least disruptive. FSEC (2002) put sealing at about $200 installed and a payback under four years, though those are old dollars. Sealing does not remove the conduction loss, which is why BSC still rates an airtight attic duct system below the inside options.

2. Bury the ducts

The DOE profile describes laying ducts on the attic floor and covering them with several inches of loose-fill insulation. In dry climates that can work. In humid and mixed climates it describes wrapping ducts in about 1.5 inches of closed-cell spray foam first, to prevent condensation, then burying them. It quotes about $600 to $1,000 for a standard buried-and-encapsulated install and warns against unencapsulated buried ducts in moist or marine climates. It also says to air-seal the ducts before encapsulating. Spray foam in an attic needs an ignition barrier, per the same profile. New Jersey is humid in summer, so ask a contractor and your code official which method applies.

3. Make the attic unvented (conditioned)

DOE describes moving the insulation and air barrier from the attic floor to the roof deck, so the ducts sit inside the thermal boundary. Its profile says the roof deck must be exceptionally airtight, that humid and cold climates need foam above or below the deck, and that interior foam needs a fire-rated cover such as half-inch gypsum board. BSC notes a well-done conditioned attic still costs about 10 percent more than ducts and equipment fully inside, because of the larger insulated surface.

4. Move ducts and air handler inside

BSC ranks this first, and calls a closet-mounted air handler workable. NREL lists ways to get ducts inside: drop ceilings or soffits, space between floors in two-story homes, a cathedralized attic, or special trusses. Its models show about a 14 to 17 percent cut in annual cooling electricity and a 22 to 24 percent smaller required cooling capacity, with reported costs from a saving of $800 to about $4,000 for truss plenums, depending on method. Often this is a new-construction or major-remodel choice.

5. Go ductless

The Building America Solution Center says duct losses can exceed 30 percent when ducts are in unconditioned space, and that ductless mini-splits avoid them because indoor units sit in the living space. The same guide lists limits: weaker dehumidification, filters that need regular cleaning, and a need for load calculations per zone. Ducted systems avoid the loss too if everything sits inside the insulated boundary.

Worked example: what a loss range costs

Invented numbers, for practice only. Annual cooling use: 3,000 kWh (invented). Electricity price: $0.18 per kWh (invented). The cited range is the DOE 10 to 45 percent loss and the 3 percent buried-duct figure. Simplification: we apply the percentages to the cooling energy only, though the source applies them to heating and cooling together.

  1. Low loss: 3,000 × 0.10 = 300 kWh. 300 × $0.18 = $54 per year.
  2. High loss: 3,000 × 0.45 = 1,350 kWh. 1,350 × $0.18 = $243 per year.
  3. After burying (3 percent): 3,000 × 0.03 = 90 kWh. 90 × $0.18 = $16.20 per year.
  4. Savings if you start at the low end: 300 − 90 = 210 kWh. 210 × $0.18 = $37.80 per year.
  5. Savings if you start at the high end: 1,350 − 90 = 1,260 kWh. 1,260 × $0.18 = $226.80 per year.

So the same upgrade is worth about $38 or about $227 a year in this made-up house, depending on where the real loss falls. That spread is why a duct leakage test comes before a big job. With a $600 to $1,000 job cost, simple payback is roughly 16 to 26 years at the low end ($600 ÷ $37.80 and $1,000 ÷ $37.80) and about 2.6 to 4.4 years at the high end ($600 ÷ $226.80 and $1,000 ÷ $226.80). Cooling-only savings understate the benefit in a house that also heats through the same ducts.

Service access and safety

An air handler in an attic is a place someone must work in, in heat, often on a ladder. A local permit tip sheet based on the 2012 International Residential Code lists these requirements for attic equipment: an access opening at least 22 by 30 inches and large enough to remove the biggest appliance; continuous solid flooring at least 24 inches wide from the opening to the equipment; no more than 20 feet of travel; a level service area at least 30 by 30 inches in front; a light with a switch near the opening; a receptacle outlet; and a disconnect. Editions and local amendments differ, so confirm with your code official.

Water is the other risk. Condensate overflow from a unit above a ceiling can ruin drywall quickly; see secondary drain pans and float switches. Attic insulation and air sealing also interact with the ducts; see attic insulation for New Jersey homes. FSEC also warns that later cable and security installers are a major risk to interior duct chases, because those jobs are almost never inspected.

Field card: Air Handlers and Ducts in Unconditioned Attics: Losses and Options

Attic ducts lose to leaks and to heat soak; fix the leaks first, then pick a location strategy.

  • DOE Building America: attic duct thermal losses estimated at 10 to 45 percent; buried ducts 3 percent or less.
  • Seal with mastic or UL 181 listed tape, then insulate.
  • Buried ducts in humid climates need encapsulation; check local code.
  • Best case: ducts and air handler inside the thermal boundary.
  • Ductless avoids duct loss but has dehumidification and filter limits.
  • Attic equipment needs a walkway, a 30 by 30 inch service area, light, outlet and a secondary drain plan.
