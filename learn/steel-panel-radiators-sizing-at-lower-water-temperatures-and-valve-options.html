Steel Panel Radiators: Sizing at Lower Water Temperatures and Valve Options

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A steel panel radiator looks simple: a flat, water-filled steel plate on the wall. The part that trips people up is the number on the chart. That number is output at one hot water temperature. Drop the water temperature, as a condensing boiler with outdoor reset will, and the same panel gives a lot less heat. This lesson shows how to read a maker's chart, apply its correction factor and see how much bigger the panel has to get.

What the type numbers mean

Panel radiators come in a few standard builds. Stelrad, a UK maker, describes them by the number of steel panels and rows of convector fins (the folded metal on the back that helps air flow through). Its pages say a Type 11 has one panel and one set of fins and has the lowest output of the four. A Type 21 has two panels and one set of fins. A Type 22 has two panels and two sets of fins, and a Type 33 has three panels and three sets, the highest output. Purmo's catalog lists 11, 21S, 22 and 33 in the same order, and its tables show output, weight and water content all rising from Type 11 to Type 33. More steel and more fins means more heat from the same height and length, but also a deeper unit that sticks farther into the room.

How makers publish output

Every chart states the conditions behind its numbers. They differ by maker, so read them before comparing two panels.

  • European style. Purmo rates output to the EN 442 standard at 75/65/20 C (supply, return, room), which is a 50 C difference between average water and room air. Zehnder says most radiator outputs are published at that same "delta T 50" and defines delta T as the average of flow and return temperature minus room temperature.
  • North American style. John Siegenthaler writes in Plumbing & Mechanical that panel radiator ratings assume about 180 F average water and 68 F room air, a 112 F difference, and that no American or Canadian standard covers panel radiators, so designers borrow the European approach. Runtal's North American catalog lists its wall panel output in Btu/hr per foot at 180 F, though the excerpt we read gave no correction table.
  • The Buderus design manual lists Model 21 and Model 22 panels at four supply temperatures (194, 176, 158 and 140 F) in a table, and then gives one table of correction factors for any other temperature difference.

The correction factor, in plain words

Heat moves from water to room in proportion to the gap between them, and it does not move in a straight line. The same Siegenthaler article scales output by the temperature difference raised to the 1.3 power, an exponent and not a multiplier. A maker's correction table does that math for you. The idea is simple: find the difference between the panel's average water temperature and the room, look up the factor, and multiply the chart output by it.

Here is part of the Buderus Table 9. The factors are the same in the older design manual and in the maker's December 2025 panel radiator applications manual.

The full table runs from 10 F up to 150 F, and 108 F gets the factor 1.000. The manual's own example takes a 7,000 Btu/hr panel from the 194 F column and multiplies by 0.569 to get 3,983 Btu/hr. Other makers print their own tables in the same style. Stelrad's, for example, is based on delta T 50 C and runs from delta T 20 to 65 C, with a factor of 0.513 at delta T 30 C. Always use the table that belongs to the panel in front of you.

Worked example: a 4,000 Btu/hr room at 140 F water

This is an invented room. A bedroom has a heat loss of 4,000 Btu/hr at design conditions. The room is held at 68 F. Wall space is limited, so we stay with 20 inch high panels from the Buderus Model 21 and 22 chart.

  1. Pick a panel at the hot rating. In the 194 F column, a Model 21, 20 in high by 36 in long, is rated 4,200 Btu/hr. That covers 4,000. So on a hot-water system the 36 inch panel works.
  2. Find the new difference. The designer now plans on 140 F average water. The difference is 140 - 68 = 72 F.
  3. Get the factor. The table has 0.569 at 70 F and 0.677 at 80 F. 72 F is 2 F into the 10 F step, so by our own straight-line interpolation the factor is 0.569 + (2 / 10) x (0.677 - 0.569) = 0.569 + 0.0216 = 0.5906, about 0.59.
  4. Test the panel you picked. 4,200 x 0.5906 = 2,480 Btu/hr. That is only 62 percent of the 4,000 Btu/hr load, so the room would run cold.
  5. Work backward. Required chart output = load / factor = 4,000 / 0.5906 = 6,772, call it 6,770 Btu/hr at the 194 F rating.
  6. Choose a bigger panel. In the same column, a Model 21, 20 x 48 is 5,600 (too small). A Model 21, 20 x 59 is 7,000. Check: 7,000 x 0.5906 = 4,134 Btu/hr, which beats 4,000. A Model 22, 20 x 48 at 7,500 also works: 7,500 x 0.5906 = 4,430 Btu/hr. It is shorter on the wall but 4 inches deep instead of 2.5.
  7. Compare. The Model 21 grows from 36 in to 59 in, 64 percent longer, and its chart rating rises from 4,200 to 7,000, about 67 percent more. That is close to 1 / 0.5906, which is 1.69.

Cross-check: the exponent method gives (72 / 108) to the 1.3 power, about 0.59, the same answer. If you round down to the 70 F row (0.569) you get 3,983 for the 7,000 panel, just under the load, which is why interpolating matters near the edge.

Why this matters with condensing boilers and reset

A condensing boiler gets its extra efficiency when return water is cool enough for the flue gas to condense. Buderus says in its manual that a condensing system should be designed so the return makes the boiler condense except on the coldest days, and that panel radiators may allow return water below 130 F with natural gas. See Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers and Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation. A Steven Winter Associates presentation hosted on energy.gov calls outdoor reset a must for condensing performance, and says oversizing the emitters is fine. Bigger panels run cooler for the same heat, which is the point of the example. For baseboard, see Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature.

Valves, connections, bleeding and mounting

  • Integral valve body. Purmo's Plan Compact Ventil sheet says the radiator has a Kv-adjustable thermostatic valve body built in, with two bottom connections 50 mm apart and four side connections. Buderus stock panels have a flow setter valve on the same side as the 20 mm bottom tappings. Its dial rings run 1 to 7 and N for fully open, and a thermostatic head fits on it only when it is in the N position.
  • Supply and return are not reversible. Buderus says they cannot be swapped. Purmo's Ramo Ventil page warns that reversed connection cuts output by more than 30 percent.
  • Series loops. Buderus says thermostatic heads cannot be used on a series loop because they shut off all flow. It recommends constant circulation with outdoor reset for thermostatic head systems. For how heads work, read Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) on Steam and Hot-Water Radiators.
  • Bleeding. Buderus panels have a manual 1/2 inch air vent opened with a flat screwdriver, and a removable drain plug. Leave 2 to 4 inches of access on the side opposite the flow setter. Purmo includes one air vent and two blank caps.
  • Mounting. Buderus says to fasten brackets to studs or other strong supports and never to drywall alone, use at least two screws per bracket, keep brackets at least 4 inches in from each end, and use two brackets for panels 71 inches or shorter.

Field card: Steel Panel Radiators: Sizing at Lower Water Temperatures and Valve Options

Chart output is for hot water. Correct it before you trust it.

  • Type 11 = 1 panel; 21 = 2 panels and 1 fin set; 22 = 2 and 2; 33 = 3 and 3.
  • Read the rated condition first: about 180 F average water in North America, 75/65/20 C in Europe.
  • Delta T = average water minus room. Look up the maker's factor.
  • Needed chart output = load / factor.
  • Example: 4,000 / 0.5906 = 6,770 Btu/hr, so a 36 in panel became 59 in at 140 F water.
  • Supply and return are not reversible. Thermostatic heads do not belong on series loops.
