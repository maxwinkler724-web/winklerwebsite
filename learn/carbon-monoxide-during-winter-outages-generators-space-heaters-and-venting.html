Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages: Generators, Space Heaters and Venting

When the power goes out in winter, the ways people try to stay warm can be the dangerous part. Here is what federal safety agencies say to do and not do.

Why outages raise the risk

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas made when fuels burn incompletely. NFPA describes it as odorless and colorless, and the CDC says you cannot see, smell or hear it. Winter outages push people toward generators, fuel-burning heaters and grills they do not normally use indoors, and that is when CO poisonings happen. The CPSC says that, as an example, nearly 100 people in the U.S. die each year from CO poisoning linked to portable generators.

Safety first. Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or any gasoline, propane or charcoal device inside a home, garage or carport. If a CO alarm sounds, get outside right away, then call 911.

Generators: outside and away

CPSC says never to run a portable generator indoors, including in a garage, basement, shed or crawlspace, and that opening doors or windows does not give enough airflow. The CDC adds that generators belong outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. CPSC also says not to put one on a porch or in a carport, to point the exhaust away from buildings, and to follow the maker's guidance for wet or snowy weather. Both agencies point to a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm in the house, and CPSC suggests looking for generator models with a CO shut-off feature.

Heaters and heat sources

The CDC says never to heat a home with a gas oven, never to burn charcoal indoors, and not to use portable gas camp stoves or flameless chemical heaters indoors. It also says not to burn anything in an unvented stove or fireplace. Appliances meant for indoor use need proper venting, and the CDC recommends yearly service of furnaces, water heaters and other gas, oil or coal appliances by a qualified technician, plus a yearly chimney check.

For electric space heaters the main danger is fire rather than CO. CPSC advice for outages includes keeping all sides at least three feet from beds, furniture, curtains and paper, setting the heater on a stable, level surface, never leaving it running unattended in a confined space, and plugging electric heaters straight into a wall outlet instead of a power strip.

Venting still matters when the power is out

CPSC says to clear snow from the outside vents of fuel-burning appliances such as furnaces so CO cannot build up inside. If you smell or hear gas, CPSC says to leave immediately and contact the gas authorities from outside, without first switching lights or electronics. For how furnace venting and sensors work, see the related lessons below.

Vehicles

The CDC says never to run a car or truck in an attached garage, even with the garage door open, and to have a mechanic check the exhaust system every year.

Alarms and symptoms

CPSC recommends battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas, with interconnected alarms best, and monthly testing. The CDC says to check batteries when you change clocks and to replace detectors on the maker's schedule. Symptoms the CDC lists include headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, often mistaken for the flu. The CDC notes that people who are asleep or impaired can die before showing symptoms. Infants, older adults and people with heart, blood or breathing conditions are more likely to get sick. Field card: Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages: Generators, Space Heaters and Venting

Outside, away, alarmed, and vents clear.

  • Generator: outside only, more than 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, exhaust pointed away.
  • Never use a garage, carport, porch, basement or shed, even with doors open.
  • No gas oven, charcoal, grill, camp stove or flameless chemical heater for indoor heat.
  • Electric heaters: 3 feet clear, wall outlet, never unattended in a confined space.
  • Clear snow and ice from outside vents of fuel-burning appliances.
  • Battery-backup CO alarms on every level and near sleeping areas; alarm sounds, get out and call 911.
