Gas Furnaces in Attics and Horizontal Installs: Access, Condensate, Pans and Venting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An attic is a hard place to own a furnace. It is hot in summer, cold in winter, hard to reach, and a water leak lands on a ceiling. This lesson walks through how a horizontal gas furnace is set up in an attic, what the model codes ask for in access, why a condensing unit makes a freeze and drainage problem, and what to check on a service call.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Cities and states adopt different editions of the International Residential Code, the International Mechanical Code and the International Fuel Gas Code, and many amend them. The numbers below come from government-hosted copies we read, and they are examples, not your local rule. Ask the local building department which edition applies, and follow the equipment's installation manual.

What "horizontal" means

A horizontal furnace lies on its side so it fits where height is short, such as an attic or a crawl space. A federal housing-rehab guide notes that horizontal furnaces are the type used in attics (HUD, The Guide, Vol. 8). Air still moves in a straight line through the cabinet: return air enters at one end, passes the blower and the heat exchanger, and leaves at the other end into the supply duct. Many horizontal models can be set up so air travels left to right or right to left, but the manual for the exact unit says what is allowed, and a unit built only for upflow is not a horizontal furnace just because it fits on its side. The unit sits on a platform, or hangs from the rafters on rods with a pan below it, according to the manual.

Condensing models add a second concern. The same guide says condensing furnaces can reach efficiencies as high as 97 percent, which means they pull so much heat from the flue gas that water forms and must be drained. A PNNL resource guide on combustion furnaces says that condensate is fairly acidic (pH of 3 or lower is mentioned) and that the line should be a gravity drain to an indoor drain where possible, with a condensate pump when gravity will not work.

Access rules for attic appliances

The model codes treat an attic furnace as something a tech must be able to reach, service and remove. Here is what the government-hosted copies say.

  • Passageway. A City of Newcastle, Delaware, copy of International Mechanical Code section 306.3 (2006 edition, noted there as unchanged in 2015) calls for an unobstructed passageway at least 30 inches high and 22 inches wide, with continuous solid flooring at least 24 inches wide, no more than 20 feet long measured along the centerline. An exception allows a longer passageway, up to 50 feet, if it is at least 6 feet high and 22 inches wide for its whole length. A Butte County, California, handout states the same 24-inch flooring and 20-foot limit where headroom is under 6 feet.
  • Platform or service space. The Mechanical Code text asks for a level service space at least 30 inches deep and 30 inches wide at the front or service side of the unit. The Butte County handout calls this a level platform of the same size.
  • Opening. The 2006 Mechanical Code text gives 20 by 30 inches as a minimum opening, large enough to remove the largest appliance. A City of Gig Harbor, Washington, tip sheet based on the 2015 International Residential Code with state amendments lists 22 by 30 inches. Editions differ, so read your local text.
  • Light, switch and receptacle. The Gig Harbor sheet says a lighting and receptacle outlet must be provided (International Residential Code M1305.1.3.1) with the light switch near the access opening, and Butte County asks for a permanent 120-volt receptacle and a light near the unit with the switch at the opening.

These rules exist for a practical reason: a tech who must crawl on bare joists carrying a heavy part will step through a ceiling sooner or later.

Condensate and freeze risk in a cold attic

A condensing furnace drains water whenever it runs. In a heated basement that is easy. In an attic the line is surrounded by the coldest air in the house, and water that sits in a trap or in a low spot can freeze. The PNNL guide says to protect condensate lines from freezing, to insulate or heat-trace a line that drains outdoors, and that in cold climates the condensate should go to an indoor drain. Our own habits follow from that: keep the run short and sloped, keep the trap inside the insulated envelope or inside the furnace cabinet where the manual allows it, and use a heat cable only if the manufacturer lists it for that line.

We cover cleaning and neutralizing in Condensate Neutralizers for High-Efficiency Furnaces and Boilers, so here we stay on the freeze.

Secondary pans and float switches

The PNNL guide also says to provide a secondary (emergency) drain pan made of durable material. In an attic, the pan catches water from a blocked or cracked primary line before it reaches drywall. A float switch in the pan opens the control circuit when water rises, so the unit stops making more water. We go through pan sizing, the extra drain and the wiring in Secondary Drain Pans and Condensate Float Switches for Attic and Closet Units. Remember that a float switch only helps if the pan is level and the switch is tested.

Combustion air and venting in an attic

Condensing (Category IV) furnaces have low exhaust temperature, and the PNNL guide says PVC is allowed because exhaust gas stays under 140 degrees F, as long as the pipe is suited to contact with condensate. It says the horizontal portion of the exhaust pipe should slope slightly toward the furnace so condensate drains back, and that manufacturers do not recommend installing these furnaces as non-direct-vent units: a second pipe brings in outside air. In cold climates, it says, keep the intake separate from the exhaust to avoid frost on the intake pipe. For a Category I furnace, the same guide says to verify that the minimum combustion and dilution air is available. Attics add a trap for the unwary: roof-top air openings, soffit vents and insulation can block or change airflow, so the attic's actual openings must be checked against the manual and code. See Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters for the sizing ideas.

Energy lost to attic equipment and ducts

The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America guide on duct efficiency says typical duct systems lose 25 to 40 percent of the heating or cooling energy put out by the central furnace, and that duct repairs may be the most important energy improvement if the ducts are in the attic. It also says ducts inside the conditioned space avoid those losses. A furnace that sits in a 20 degree attic loses a little through its cabinet too, but the long duct runs are where the money goes. Our lesson Air Handlers and Ducts in Unconditioned Attics: Losses and Options goes into the choices.

Worked example: how much water, and why a frozen trap stops the furnace

Our own calculation, with assumptions. We are not quoting a manufacturer or a federal condensate figure. We could not find a government or university source giving gallons per therm, so we derive it from basic chemistry.

  1. Assume the input. 80,000 Btu per hour is an assumed furnace input, or 0.8 therm per hour (1 therm = 100,000 Btu).
  2. Water made per therm. Treat natural gas as methane, which makes 2 moles of water per mole burned. The national measurement institute's data page lists a heat of combustion near 890 kJ per mole of methane (we treat this as the higher heating value, an assumption). One therm is about 105,500 kJ, so 105,500 / 890 = about 118.5 moles of methane. Water: 2 x 118.5 = 237 moles. Water weighs 18.015 grams per mole, so 237 x 18.015 = about 4,270 grams, or 4.27 kg. A gallon of water is 3.785 kg, so 4.27 / 3.785 = about 1.13 gallons per therm if every bit of vapor condensed.
  3. Assume capture. A real furnace does not condense everything. We assume 70 percent. Water per hour: 0.8 x 1.13 x 0.70 = about 0.63 gallon per hour.
  4. Per minute. 0.63 gal x 231 cubic inches per gallon = about 146 cubic inches per hour, or about 2.4 cubic inches per minute.
  5. Freeze. Assume the trap and the pipe ahead of it hold a total of 12 cubic inches (assumed). If the trap freezes solid, water has nowhere to go. Even with the same 2.4 cubic inches per minute, the reservoir fills in about 12 / 2.4 = 5 minutes of run time.

What happens then depends on the design. In many condensing furnaces, blocked drainage lets water back up into the collector box and the vent side. That can open the pressure switch circuit, because the inducer can no longer move air through a flooded passage, or trip a separate overflow switch. The furnace stops with a lockout or a pressure switch fault, even though nothing is wrong with the controls. Read the pressure switch lesson below if you see this code. Water in a pressure switch tube is a clue.

Service checks for an attic furnace

  • Check the access: solid walkway, light works, switch at the hatch, outlet has power and a disconnect is within sight of the unit.
  • Look for water in the pan. Any water means the primary drain has a problem.
  • Test the float switch by lifting it by hand and watching the unit shut off.
  • Pour water into the condensate trap and watch it drain. Feel the line on a cold day for ice.
  • Check that the vent slopes toward the furnace, joints are tight, and the intake is clear of insulation and frost.
  • Check the input on the meter, per the manual. Our lesson Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input covers how.
  • Diagnose a pressure switch fault only after clearing water from tubes and traps: Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis.

Field card: Gas Furnaces in Attics and Horizontal Installs

Reach it, light it, drain it, vent it, and keep the water from freezing.

  • Example code text: solid floor 24 in wide, passageway 30 x 22 in, 20 ft limit, level 30 x 30 in service space; check your edition.
  • Light, receptacle and a switch at the opening are part of the attic setup in the codes we read.
  • Condensing furnaces make water every cycle; ours estimates about 0.63 gal per hour at 80,000 Btu per hour with assumed 70 percent capture.
  • A frozen trap backs water up and can trip a pressure or overflow switch.
  • Use a secondary pan and a float switch, and test both.
  • Vent slopes toward the furnace; keep the intake clear of frost.
  • Typical duct systems lose 25 to 40 percent of output (DOE); attic ducts are the worst place.
