Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Jersey Home: How to Compare

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Both can heat a New Jersey house well, and the honest answer for your house depends on your rates, your ducts and your electrical service. This lesson gives you a fair way to compare the two and a formula you can run with your own bills. It does not pick a winner.

How each one heats

A gas furnace burns fuel and sends the heat into the air that moves through your ducts. A heat pump does something different. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) says a heat pump transfers heat rather than converting it from a fuel, and that a properly installed unit can deliver up to three times more heat energy to a home than the electrical energy it consumes. Those two ideas explain most of the comparison: the furnace is limited to about the energy in the fuel, while the heat pump can beat 100 percent because it moves heat that already exists outdoors. See Heat Pump Basics for the cycle itself.

Reading the efficiency numbers

DOE defines AFUE as the ratio of a furnace's or boiler's annual heat output to the fossil fuel energy it consumes. An AFUE of 90% means 90% of the energy in the fuel becomes heat for the home. DOE groups furnaces roughly as 80% to 83% for mid-efficiency and 90% to 98.5% for high-efficiency. It also points out that AFUE leaves out losses from the duct system or piping, which can reach 35% of the furnace's output when ducts run through unconditioned space.

Heat pumps are scored differently. HSPF2 is a seasonal heating rating, and COP (coefficient of performance) is heat delivered divided by electric energy used, at a given outdoor temperature. The two systems are scored on different scales, so do not compare an AFUE number to an HSPF2 number directly. Use What Does a SEER2 Rating Mean? for the rating names, then compare cost, as the worked example below does.

Cold-climate heat pumps and the NEEP list

Heat pump output drops as outdoor air gets colder, and the house needs more heat at the same time. The lesson on balance point and low-temperature capacity shows how those lines cross. Cold-climate models are built to hold their output longer. NEEP (the Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships) keeps a cold-climate air-source heat pump specification and a public product list used by some utilities and state programs for rebate eligibility. NEEP's page lists version 4.0 as effective January 1, 2023. The older version 3.1 I read required, for ducted systems, an HSPF above 9 and a COP above 1.75 at 5°F at maximum capacity, with listings giving capacity and COP at 47, 17 and 5°F. NEEP's document adds that meeting the spec does not necessarily mean a product is right for every cold-climate application, so a load calculation still matters (see Heat Load Calculation: Intro to Manual J). NEEP's consumer guide also says cold-climate heat pumps are designed to run almost continuously at a very low level, which is why a long run time is not alarming (Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter). For more on these models, see Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Dual-fuel: both at once

DOE describes dual-fuel or hybrid systems as a heat pump combined with a gas furnace: the heat pump handles most heating in milder weather and the furnace takes over when temperatures drop. This keeps a familiar backup, but you own two systems and the controls must be set so each runs when it should. NEEP's guide, which covers keeping an existing system as backup, says to use the backup only when needed and keep the heat pump running as much as possible. Electric strip backup is a separate option, covered in Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat.

Ducts and electrical service

Ducts. DOE says homes with existing ductwork often find it easier to replace a furnace with a heat pump or add a hybrid, and that ductless mini-splits are generally the best retrofit for homes without ducts. DOE also gives about 400 to 500 cfm of airflow per ton for heat pumps, notes that efficiency falls when airflow is well under 350 cfm per ton, and says leaky ducts hurt performance. A furnace duct system that was sized for another purpose is worth having checked either way; see Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Electrical panel. A DOE building-science document calls panel capacity a key barrier to electrification, and says electricians size loads from nameplate data and recommend that load never exceed 80 percent of the service capacity. It lists load-management devices as alternatives to a panel upgrade in some cases. Whether your house needs an upgrade depends on its existing loads, so this is a question for a licensed electrician; this page makes no code claims.

Worked example: cost per delivered MMBtu

An MMBtu is one million Btu. To compare fuels fairly, find the cost of one million Btu of heat delivered into the house: the fuel's cost per MMBtu divided by the efficiency. EIA gives the conversions: 1 therm is 100,000 Btu and 1 kilowatthour is 3,412 Btu.

The rates below are made-up examples, not current prices. Replace them with the numbers on your own bills.

Gas, example rate $1.60 per therm, AFUE 95%:

  1. 1 MMBtu is 1,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = 10 therms.
  2. Fuel cost: 10 × $1.60 = $16.00 per MMBtu.
  3. Delivered: $16.00 ÷ 0.95 = $16.84 per MMBtu.

Electric heat pump, example rate $0.20 per kWh, COP 2.5:

  1. 1 MMBtu is 1,000,000 ÷ 3,412 = 293.1 kWh.
  2. Electricity cost: 293.1 × $0.20 = $58.61 per MMBtu (this is also the price of resistance heat, COP 1).
  3. Delivered: $58.61 ÷ 2.5 = $23.44 per MMBtu.

At these example rates the gas furnace is cheaper per delivered MMBtu. Now find the break-even: $58.61 ÷ $16.84 = 3.48. A heat pump would have to average a COP of about 3.5 to match gas at these rates, and a COP of 3 gives $58.61 ÷ 3 = $19.54. If electricity were cheaper or gas pricier, the answer would move the other way. The formula is the point: change the two rates and the two efficiencies and redo the division. Also remember that a heat pump's COP is not one number. It changes with outdoor temperature, which is why NEEP's tables list it at three temperatures, so use a COP that fits the weather in your heating season. This example also leaves out equipment price, maintenance, cooling, incentives and any backup heat.

If your gas bill is in ccf (hundred cubic feet), EIA gives 1 cubic foot of natural gas as 1,036 Btu, so one ccf is about 103,600 Btu, or roughly 1.04 therms. Check the units and any delivery charges on your own bill.

Questions to ask for any quote

  • What load calculation was used, and what outdoor temperature does it assume?
  • What are the rated capacity and COP at 17°F and 5°F (heat pump), or the AFUE (furnace)?
  • Have the ducts been checked for leaks and airflow?
  • Has an electrician looked at the panel and service?
  • What is the backup plan on the coldest days, and who sets the controls?

Field card: Heat Pump or Gas Furnace Comparison

Compare cost per delivered MMBtu, then check ducts and panel.

  • Gas: 10 therms = 1 MMBtu; delivered cost = (price per therm × 10) ÷ AFUE.
  • Electric: 293.1 kWh = 1 MMBtu; delivered cost = (price per kWh × 293.1) ÷ COP.
  • Break-even COP = electric cost per MMBtu ÷ gas delivered cost.
  • COP changes with outdoor temperature; ask for it at 17°F and 5°F.
  • AFUE ignores duct losses; DOE says they can reach 35%.
  • Dual fuel pairs a heat pump with a furnace backup.
  • Panel capacity is an electrician's call.
