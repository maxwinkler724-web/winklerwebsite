Hot Gas Reheat for Dehumidification in Rooftop Units: How It Works, Modes and Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A rooftop unit can hold 72 F in a room and still leave it clammy. Hot gas reheat is one way makers let the coil run cold enough to dry the air, then warm that air back up using heat the refrigerant circuit already has. This lesson explains the idea, the valves and modes, and what to look at on the roof.

Check the unit's own literature

Check the unit's own literature. Reheat packages differ by maker and model. This page describes how maker literature explains the concept. Your unit's installation and service manual controls the sequence, settings and procedures. We do not recommend any brand.

Why dehumidifying needs reheat

Water comes out of the air at the evaporator, and the coil has to be cold enough to do it. At part load a constant-volume unit cannot simply slow its fan to compensate. A Penn State architectural engineering thesis (a student document, so we use it only for the basic idea) describes a single-zone unit whose thermostat watches dry-bulb temperature: the unit has to deliver warmer supply air to avoid overcooling the room, and humidity stays uncontrolled. In its example the room held temperature but sat about 11 percent above the target relative humidity.

The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America guideline on warm-humid dehumidification makes a related point: when equipment is off for long stretches, indoor humidity builds up, and it states that latent capacity is generally 20 to 25 percent of total capacity at summer design conditions. For why airflow and load change that split, see Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.

Reheat solves the problem by letting the evaporator keep drying the air, then adding sensible heat so the supply air does not overcool the space. AAON's reheat page says this "prevents over cooling of the space" and that the reheat coil controls supply air temperature after the cooling load has been satisfied.

How the hot gas circuit is laid out

AAON describes the reheat coil as part of the refrigerant circuit, sitting downstream of the DX evaporator. A three-way valve sends some of the hot gas that would otherwise go to the condenser into the reheat coil, which warms air that has already been cooled and dried. The reheat coil has to be downstream of the evaporator because all moisture removal happens at the evaporator. Put the heat before the coil and you would only warm the air you are about to cool, undoing the point.

Valves and styles

AAON's reheat page separates two styles. On/off reheat uses a simple valve, usually with coils piped in series, so reheat either runs or is bypassed. Because it cannot modulate, supply temperature swings inside the controller's deadband. Modulating reheat uses a three-way modulating valve that diverts a varying share of hot gas to hold the discharge air at the dehumidification setpoint, with coils piped in series or parallel. AAON also notes that series reheat typically recovers only part of the heat of rejection, while a parallel design can be sized for all of it, subject to limits such as oil return and suction temperature, and that head pressure control is required with modulating reheat.

Daikin's brochure for its modulating system describes modulating valves on both the condenser coil and the reheat coil, and says the hot gas feed is modulated to hold a steady supply air temperature, since supply temperature otherwise swings as compressors cycle. Heil's literature, held at a distributor, describes a different layout on its light commercial line: a hot gas solenoid valve and a liquid line solenoid valve that select normal cooling, a sub-cooling mode, or hot gas reheat. In sub-cooling mode the brochure says supply air runs about 5 to 7 degrees warmer than normal. Check valves and other piping details vary, and we did not find a maker page that explains them in general terms, so use your unit's refrigerant circuit diagram.

Control modes and inputs

  • Cooling. AAON says reheat is inactive in plain cooling, and cooling stops when the dry-bulb setpoint is met unless humidity calls.
  • Dehumidification with reheat. In AAON's controls article a 24 VAC dehumidistat signal starts the sequence in standalone control. In "priority" mode it runs whenever humidity exceeds the enable setpoint; in "non-priority" it runs only after cooling and heating demands are met. The reheat coil then warms the air toward the supply setpoint.
  • Combined. Heil's sub-cooling mode applies when temperature and humidity are both above setpoint; its hot gas reheat mode handles a humidity call with no cooling call.

Inputs vary. Trane's announcement of its rooftop reheat option describes a humidity sensor with a humidity setpoint, and AAON's article lists space, return or outside air humidity sensors on its controller plus a duct supply air sensor for modulating reheat. Daikin's brochure says compressor control holds a constant dew point leaving the cooling coil. Whether your unit uses a dehumidistat, a humidity sensor or a dew point strategy is a question for its manual. For the base cooling sequence see RTU Cooling Sequence of Operation and the Safety Chain.

Charge and oil return

A reheat coil adds refrigerant volume and piping. Daikin's brochure says its hot gas reheat adds 0.2 lb of R-410A per ton on one model line and 0.4 lb per ton on another. AAON points to oil return and suction temperature as limits on parallel reheat sizing. Trane's reheat coil replacement guide gives oil to add when a reheat coil is changed (4 oz for 6 to 12.5 ton units and 8 oz for 15 to 25 ton units in its Table 3) and warns not to add or remove oil from a new compressor. That is a replacement-specific rule for that model family, not a general one. How a particular unit purges or manages oil in reheat is in its own manual; we did not find a general maker statement we can repeat here.

Energy: is the heat free?

Makers say it largely is. Daikin's page states that reheat "costs no energy." Trane's engineers newsletter says hot gas reheat reuses compressor heat that would otherwise be rejected, so it can reheat "without the use of additional new energy," and says the ASHRAE 90.1 reheat limit has an exception when at least 90 percent of the annual reheat energy is site-recovered energy, with condenser heat including hot gas reheat counted as recovered. Note the limit: that same newsletter says hot gas reheat often increases annual energy use, and argues that humidity control, comfort and moisture-damage protection often outweigh the cost. The Penn State thesis criticizes the idea because compressor energy cools the air that is then warmed again. AAON's article rates modulating hot gas reheat as low energy, on/off hot gas as medium, and auxiliary electric or gas reheat as high energy and "typically against energy codes." Read these as maker positions. The code text itself (ASHRAE Standard 90.1 and your adopted energy code) governs your job.

Worked example: 4,000 cfm

All arithmetic below is Our own, using the sensible heat equation covered in HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas. Given: 4,000 cfm, air leaving the evaporator at 52 F, stated target supply air of 68 F.

  1. Temperature rise: 68 F - 52 F = 16 F.
  2. Reheat heat flow: 1.08 x 4,000 cfm x 16 F = 1.08 x 64,000 = 69,120 Btu/h.
  3. Convert to tons of rejected heat: 69,120 Btu/h ÷ 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 5.76 tons.
  4. Electric reheat for the same duty: 69,120 Btu/h ÷ 3,412 Btu/h per kW = about 20.3 kW.

So the reheat coil must take about 69,000 Btu/h, roughly 5.8 tons of compressor heat, from the refrigerant. A resistance heater doing the same job would draw about 20.3 kW of new electric power while the compressor still runs. The 1.08 factor assumes standard air; actual altitude and temperature change it slightly. Whether a given unit can actually move 5.8 tons of heat out of the refrigerant is a capacity question for its data, since AAON says series arrangements recover only part of the heat of rejection.

Field checks

Our own checklist, built from the behaviors above. Follow your manual first.

  • Confirm a real dehumidification call exists (humidity above setpoint, or the dehumidistat contact closed) before judging the unit.
  • Compare supply air temperature in reheat mode to the unit's stated supply setpoint. A modulating system should hold it fairly steady; an on/off system will swing, as AAON says.
  • Compare air leaving the evaporator to air leaving the reheat coil. A temperature rise across the reheat coil shows it is working, as in the 52 F to 68 F example. Use a clamp-on or probe thermometer, not a guess.
  • Check the reheat coil is warm and the valve is receiving its signal. Verify the valve command with the controller, not by touch alone, and mind hot discharge piping.
  • Check head pressure and the usual superheat and subcooling readings, as in Superheat and Subcooling Basics. Reheat changes how the circuit loads the condenser, so readings in reheat mode differ from plain cooling.
  • If supply air is cold during a humidity call, suspect the valve, its signal, or the sensor before blaming charge.

For another refrigerant-side load-control device, see Hot Gas Bypass and Capacity Control. For a design that avoids the reheat problem by treating outdoor air separately, see Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems (DOAS).

Field card: Hot Gas Reheat in Rooftop Units

Dry it at the evaporator, warm it after.

  • Reheat lets the coil stay cold enough to dry air without overcooling the space.
  • The reheat coil sits downstream of the evaporator.
  • A three-way valve sends compressor discharge gas to the reheat coil; on/off swings, modulating holds a setpoint.
  • Inputs vary: dehumidistat, humidity sensor, supply air sensor, per the manual.
  • Sensible heat: 1.08 x cfm x dT. Example: 1.08 x 4,000 x 16 = 69,120 Btu/h = 5.76 tons = about 20.3 kW electric (Our own).
  • Check supply air in reheat mode, rise across the reheat coil, and the valve command.
  • Charge and oil return notes are model specific. Use the unit manual.
