Window Air Conditioners: Sizing, Installation, Cords and Upkeep

Last updated: 2026-10-28

A window air conditioner is the simplest cooling a homeowner can buy, and it is also the easiest to get wrong. Most trouble traces back to three choices: the wrong size, a sloppy install, or a power setup the unit was never meant for. This lesson walks through each one, with a worked sizing example.

Start with the room, not the box

The federal ENERGY STAR program publishes a room air conditioner sizing table. It matches the floor area you want to cool with the cooling capacity you need, in Btu per hour. A few rows from the table: 150 to 250 square feet needs 6,000 Btu/hr, 300 to 350 needs 8,000, 350 to 400 needs 9,000, and 450 to 550 needs 12,000. The table assumes an 8-foot ceiling.

Measure the room in feet. Multiply length by width for a rectangle. For an odd shape, split it into rectangles and triangles and add the areas.

The four adjustments

The same program lists adjustments to apply to the table number:

  • Heavy shade: reduce the capacity by 10 percent.
  • Very sunny room: increase it by 10 percent.
  • More than two regular occupants: add 600 Btu/hr for each extra person.
  • Kitchen: add 4,000 Btu/hr.

The program also notes that a unit near a room corner should be able to direct its airflow where it is needed.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example, not a program case study. The assumptions are ours: a bedroom measuring 350 square feet, an 8-foot ceiling, strong afternoon sun, no kitchen, and two people who sleep there.

  1. Base capacity. At exactly 350 square feet the room sits on the line between two rows, 300 to 350 (8,000 Btu/hr) and 350 to 400 (9,000 Btu/hr). The table does not say which row owns the boundary, so we take the higher row: 9,000 Btu/hr.
  2. Sun. The room is very sunny, so add 10 percent: 9,000 × 0.10 = 900. Then 9,000 + 900 = 9,900 Btu/hr.
  3. Occupants. Two people is not more than two, so add 0.
  4. Kitchen. None, so add 0.
  5. Result. The target is about 9,900 Btu/hr. Shop for a unit whose labeled capacity is close to 10,000 Btu/hr, and check the room's actual shade and ceiling before you buy.

If you took the lower row instead, the same steps give 8,000 + 800 = 8,800 Btu/hr. That is why we say to treat a boundary room as a judgment call and lean on the sun and use rather than the exact row.

Why bigger is not better

ENERGY STAR warns that an oversized unit cools the room before it can remove humidity. The room can feel damp and clammy. Slower airflow removes more moisture, so on humid days the program suggests a low fan speed. Our lesson on sensible heat ratio and latent load explains the mechanism.

Reading CEER and the label

Federal efficiency standards for room air conditioners are written in CEER, the combined energy efficiency ratio. The Department of Energy describes it as cooling provided per unit of energy used, in Btu per watt-hour, and it counts cooling mode plus low-power modes. A higher number means less electricity for the same cooling. ENERGY STAR sets minimum CEER values by capacity band and by whether the unit has louvered sides, so compare units only within a similar size. The Federal Trade Commission notes that room air conditioners carry an EnergyGuide label, which shows how the unit compares with similar models and gives an estimated yearly operating cost based on typical use and a national average energy price. Your own cost will differ.

Installing it so it drains and seals

Follow the manual that came with your unit. Two manufacturer installation manuals we read both ask for a slight tilt toward the outside so that condensate, the water the cooling coil pulls from the air, runs out the back instead of into the room. One says a level should show roughly a third of a bubble. The other says a very slight downward tilt toward the outside. One of them adds that water dripping indoors usually means the unit is not tilted outward. ENERGY STAR's consumer guidance puts the same goal differently, saying to make sure the unit is level so drainage works. Treat that as a reminder to check the slope; the unit's own manual sets the exact angle.

Water collecting in the base pan and dripping from the outdoor side in humid or rainy weather is normal, according to one of the manuals.

  • Support bracket. Both manuals call for a bracket or sill support attached to the case and the window, using the screws supplied.
  • Side panels. The accordion panels attach to the unit and the sash with screws. ENERGY STAR says a poorly sealed unit can leak air equal to a 6-square-inch hole. Federal energy-lab guidance suggests replacing accordion panels with rigid foam cut to fit, plus backer rod between the sashes and tape around the edges. It estimates five to ten percent cooling savings from such sealing, with roughly $15 of materials.
  • Storm windows. One manual warns that a storm window frame can block the outward tilt and gives a shim fix, so check before you buy.
  • Off season. Take the unit out or cover and seal it, or air leaks through it all winter.

Plugs, cords and circuits

The U.S. Fire Administration says to plug major appliances directly into a wall outlet and never use an extension cord with them, because the cord can overheat and start a fire. Its page does not name air conditioners, but a window unit draws a lot of power, and the manufacturers say the same thing. One manual strongly recommends against an extension cord and forbids one on its 230 and 208 volt models. The other says to use a dedicated circuit and no extension cord. Our practical reading: plug it into a grounded outlet, and move the unit or have an electrician add an outlet before you reach for a cord.

Circuit size comes from the nameplate. In the manuals we read, 115 volt models want a 15 amp time-delay fuse or breaker. One 230 volt model calls for 20 amps, and the other manual says such models belong on their own single branch circuit. Do not change the plug or cut off the ground prong. Larger units can need a different outlet shape, so match the receptacle to the plug, and have a licensed electrician add or alter a circuit.

Cleaning the filter and coil

  • Filter: unplug the unit first. The manuals say to check it every two to four weeks and to wash it in lukewarm soapy water, then dry it. Never run the unit without the filter. A clogged filter cuts airflow and efficiency, and the Department of Energy says a clean filter can lower energy use by 5 to 15 percent.
  • Coils: the Department of Energy says to check the evaporator coil yearly, straighten bent fins with a fin comb, and pass a stiff wire through the drain channels to stop clogs. One manual advises a professional cleaning for outdoor coils clogged with dirt or soot. The same energy-lab guidance adds periodic cleaning of the grills on both sides.

For the mini-split version of these habits, see mini-split cleaning and maintenance habits.

Saddle and U-shaped styles

A standard window unit is boxy and blocks much of the glass. ENERGY STAR's room heat pump report describes saddle designs that straddle the sill and leave the window usable, and notes that U-shaped and box styles exist too. The report lists minimum window opening sizes for the saddle models it describes, so measure your window before you shop. The sizing steps above still apply.

When a heat pump or mini split is the better choice

A cooling-only window unit does nothing in winter. ENERGY STAR says a mini split heat pump heats and cools from one system, needs no ductwork, and can replace window units. Its cold climate certification requires verified performance down to 5 degrees F. The program says the higher purchase price is recovered over time through lower energy bills. A window heat pump is a middle step that the program's report covers. If you are cooling several rooms every summer, heating with electric baseboards, or tired of removing units each fall, get a quote for a mini split. For a single small room used a few weeks a year, a correctly sized window unit is hard to beat. Our PTAC lesson covers the through-wall cousin, and portable spot coolers are a different tool.

Field card: Window Air Conditioners

Size it, tilt it, seal it, plug it straight in.

  • Find the row in the ENERGY STAR area table, then adjust: shade minus 10%, sun plus 10%, each person over two plus 600 Btu/hr, kitchen plus 4,000.
  • Our example: 350 sq ft, sunny, two people gives 9,000 + 900 = about 9,900 Btu/hr.
  • Too big cools before it removes humidity, so the room feels clammy.
  • Slight tilt to the outside for condensate; follow the unit manual for the angle.
  • Use the support bracket and seal the side panels.
  • No extension cord; match circuit and outlet to the nameplate.
  • Clean the filter every few weeks; check coils yearly.
