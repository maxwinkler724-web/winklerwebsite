Storing Family Photos and Papers at Home: Heat, Humidity and Leaks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Family photos, old letters, scrapbooks and framed art last longest in the same kind of place people are comfortable: the conditioned part of the house. The places most of us use for storage, such as the attic, basement and garage, are the worst places for paper. This lesson explains why in heating and cooling terms and gives a simple way to pick a spot.

Heat speeds aging

A federal archives agency's home-storage page says to keep the temperature below 75 degrees Fahrenheit, and explains that cooler storage lasts longer because it slows chemical decay and reduces insect activity. Its general guidance adds that heat can fade colors, melt plastics and scorch photographs. A summer attic is the extreme case: it can be far hotter than the rooms below it. Even without a measurement, any space that is not heated or cooled follows the weather, which is why that agency says to keep items out of hot attics.

Damp spaces bring mold and stuck prints

The same archives page says to keep relative humidity below 65 percent to prevent mold growth and reduce insect activity, and warns that very dry air, under 15 percent, can make items brittle. Its general guidance says dampness makes photos stick together and encourages mold, and that a finished basement is often cool but too damp for photos unless it is dehumidified. A national library preservation page says basements, attics and garages have poor temperature and humidity control. A federal environmental agency recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, to limit mold growth in a home.

Big swings curl and crack

This part is our own explanation, not a quoted rule. Paper, prints and the glue in bindings take up and release moisture as the air changes. When a space swings from a damp, warm day to a dry, cold night, the layers do not move at the same rate, which tends to curl prints, ripple pages and crack emulsion. Steady conditions avoid that stress, so a stable room beats a slightly better room that cycles. Our related lessons on museum, library and archive climate control show how professionals manage drift.

Where to put things

  • Use the conditioned living space. The archives agency's general guidance suggests the coolest, driest spot that stays that way all year, and names an above-ground interior closet as having fairly steady temperatures.
  • Pick an interior wall. A closet on an outside wall can run cold in winter and collect moisture on the cold surface; see cold closets and musty spots. This reasoning is our own.
  • Get boxes off the floor. The archives agency says to store items on a shelf so they do not get wet and to keep them away from pipes, windows and known roof leaks. A low shelf also stays away from any floor-level water.
  • Stay away from heat sources and sun. Our own advice: keep boxes clear of supply registers, radiators, baseboard heat and sunny windows. Those spots are warmer and drier at times, and the sun adds heat and light, so the temperature there swings more than in the rest of the room.
  • Do not overstuff. The archives page says to size containers so items lie flat or stand without bending, and not to overstuff.

Measure with a simple hygrometer

The environmental agency says relative humidity can be measured with a moisture or humidity meter, a small inexpensive instrument sold at many hardware stores. Put one where the boxes are and glance at it through the seasons. A reasonable target is steady and moderate: a national library page gives 35 to 55 percent for areas where mold stains are a concern and 35 to 50 percent to discourage pests, and the archives agency says to stay under 65 percent. If the reading keeps climbing, see basement dehumidifier sizing, setpoint and drainage, and condensation on windows in winter for the other side of the problem.

Worked example (our own): why a box gets damper in the basement

Relative humidity compares the water vapor in the air to the most the air could hold at that temperature. Cooler air holds less, so the same water gives a higher reading. Assumptions, all our own: the living room is 70 F at 50 percent relative humidity, and the box moves to a 55 F basement where the air has the same water content (no added moisture, no dehumidifier).

  1. Saturation water vapor pressure from a standard approximation (our own calculation): about 2.50 kPa at 70 F and about 1.47 kPa at 55 F.
  2. Room vapor pressure: 50 percent x 2.50 kPa = 1.25 kPa.
  3. Dew point: the temperature where saturation equals 1.25 kPa, which works out to about 50 F. Surfaces at or below that temperature collect liquid water.
  4. Basement relative humidity: 1.25 kPa / 1.47 kPa = 0.85, about 85 percent. A check by ratio: 1.47 / 2.50 = 0.59, and 50 percent / 0.59 = 85 percent.
  5. If the room were drier, say 40 percent, the same steps give a dew point near 45 F and about 68 percent in the basement, still above the 65 percent limit the archives agency gives.

Result: with a modest room humidity, a 15 degree drop pushes the box from 50 percent to about 85 percent, well into mold range. Real basements are often also damp from the ground, so the reading can be higher. A box on a concrete floor touching a wall at 50 F or less could reach the dew point and get wet outright. This is a simplified example, not a measurement of any house.

After a leak or flood: act fast

  • Time matters. A federal emergency management page says mold can form in about 48 hours and to freeze photos, papers, books and textiles you cannot dry in that window. A national library page says to respond within 24 hours if possible, and that wet paper items that cannot air dry within two days should be frozen.
  • Air-dry what you can. The emergency management page says to dry indoors if possible, to avoid hair dryers, irons, ovens and long sunlight, and to improve airflow with fans, open windows, air conditioning or dehumidifiers. It suggests paper towels between pages of wet books and drying photos flat or hanging by a corner with plastic clothespins, keeping the image from touching other surfaces. Do not rub or scrub a wet photo; the archives agency notes water swells the image layer.
  • Freeze for later. Freezing buys time when there is too much to dry at once.
  • Know when to stop. The emergency management page says not to try a do-it-yourself rescue on items that touched sewage or chemicals, and to wear protective gear if mold is extensive. Protect your health first and ask a conservator about valuable items.
  • Dry the house too. The environmental agency says to dry wet building materials and furnishings within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. If heating or cooling equipment was under water, read what to do before turning it on.

Sealed plastic bins in a damp space

A tight plastic bin feels protective, and against a drip it is. But a national library page says plastic bags or bins add only secondary water protection and do not guard against environmental extremes, and that in a humid area plastic storage can increase the chance of mold. Our own explanation: a bin closed on damp air holds that damp air against the contents, and when the bin cools, its inner surface can reach the dew point. A sealed bin works best in a conditioned room, with dry contents.

Field card: Storing Family Photos and Papers at Home: Heat, Humidity and Leaks

Store paper where people are comfortable.

  • Keep items out of hot attics, damp basements and garages.
  • Choose an above-ground interior closet or shelf, not an outside wall.
  • Keep boxes off the floor and away from registers, radiators, baseboard heat and sunny windows.
  • Keep relative humidity under 65 percent at the very most; indoor humidity of 30 to 50 percent is the environmental agency's ideal.
  • Cooler air at the same moisture means higher relative humidity (our own example: 50 percent at 70 F becomes about 85 percent at 55 F).
  • After a leak: begin within 24 to 48 hours, air-dry or freeze, and skip DIY salvage after sewage or chemicals.
  • Sealed plastic bins can trap moisture in humid spaces.
