Static Shocks and Dry Winter Air at Home: Why Heated Outdoor Air Gets So Dry

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Doorknob zaps, itchy skin and cracked lips all show up when the heat comes on. The cause is usually not a broken furnace. Cold outdoor air carries very little water, and when it is heated to room temperature the same small amount of water makes the air read as desert dry. This lesson shows the arithmetic, then covers targets, window limits, measuring and fixes.

Why static shocks get worse in winter

University of Maryland, Baltimore County physicists explain that dry winter air is the main cause: without moisture, charge stays trapped on a surface, builds up, and discharges as a shock. Their simple fixes are dryer sheets on clothing and a humidifier. A University of Wisconsin weather-science explainer links the same dryness to itchy skin and clinging clothes. Neither source gives a humidity threshold for shocks, so we do not claim one here. What we can do is explain why the air is dry and how to raise it safely.

Same water, higher temperature

Relative humidity (RH) compares the water vapor in air with the most the air could hold at that temperature. The New Mexico State University atmospheric science encyclopedia writes it as 100 times the actual vapor pressure divided by the saturation vapor pressure. The Wisconsin explainer puts it plainly: warming outside air that enters a home raises its temperature but not its dew point, so the gap between them grows and RH falls. Our humidity ratio lesson and Psychrometrics for Technicians go deeper on the theory.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Outdoor air at 20 F and 70% RH leaks in or is brought in, and is heated to 70 F with no humidifier running. Find the new RH.

Assumptions (ours): no moisture is added or removed by people, cooking, plants or a humidifier; total pressure is standard sea-level pressure, 101.325 kPa, throughout; saturation pressure at 20 F is taken over liquid water, as weather RH is reported.

  1. Convert 20 F to Celsius. (20 − 32) / 1.8 = −6.67 C.
  2. Saturation pressure at 20 F. The NMSU encyclopedia gives an approximation over water in millibars, 6.11 × 10[7.5 T / (237.3 + T)], with T in C. Exponent = 7.5 × (−6.67) / (237.3 − 6.67) = −0.2169. 10−0.2169 = 0.6070. So 6.11 × 0.6070 = 3.709 mbar = 0.371 kPa.
  3. Actual vapor pressure outside. 0.70 × 0.3709 = 0.2596 kPa. With no water added, this stays 0.2596 kPa after heating.
  4. Saturation pressure at 70 F. 70 F = 21.11 C = 294.26 K. The NIST water property table lists 2.5051 kPa at 294.26 K.
  5. Indoor RH. 0.2596 / 2.5051 = 0.1036, about 10 percent. (Using the NMSU formula at 70 F, 2.505 kPa, gives 10.4% as well.)
  6. Dew point, before and after. Solving the same formula for the temperature where saturation equals 0.2596 kPa gives about −11.2 C, or 11.8 F, in both places.
  7. How much water would a 30% house need? At 70 F, 30% RH is 0.30 × 2.5051 = 0.7515 kPa. Using the humidity-ratio relation W = 0.622 × pv / (p − pv) from the NMSU encyclopedia and 7,000 grains per pound, that is 32.5 grains per pound of dry air. The heated outdoor air holds only 11.2 grains per pound (W = 0.001598). The house needs about 21 more grains per pound of air, which is why heated leakage air keeps pulling the number down.

Result: about 10 percent RH, with a dew point near 12 F. Rounding in the exponential formula moves the answer by a few tenths of a point, so treat it as about 10 percent, not a precise figure.

Why air leaks make it worse

Every cubic foot of outdoor air that gets in must eventually be heated, and it arrives carrying only a fraction of the water the house wants. The example shows the gap is about 21 grains per pound at a 30% target. Missouri University Extension says holes and cracks let warm air and moisture through, and recommends sealing all openings with long-lasting caulk, sealant or gasket material. North Dakota State University Extension also advises sealing openings in walls and ceilings. For finding leaks, see Drafty Doors and Windows. Sealing first means you need to add less water later. Keep ventilation: NDSU lists 0.35 air changes per hour, but not less than 15 cfm per person, as the recommended rate.

Sensible indoor humidity targets

Sources give a range, not one number. The EPA mold guidance says keep indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. EPA's humidifier page says not to go above 50 percent. Missouri Extension suggests about 35 to 50 percent in the heating season. NDSU Extension recommends about 30 to 40 percent in winter, and University of Minnesota Extension calls 25 percent in winter up to 50 percent in summer healthy and comfortable. A practical winter target from these sources is roughly 30 to 40 percent, adjusted downward when windows show moisture. Pianos and other wood instruments want steadier numbers; see that lesson.

Why the upper limit drops when it gets colder

The inside surface of a window is the coldest surface in the room. If it is colder than the room air dew point, water forms. NDSU Extension gives an example: at 0 F outdoors, the inside glass of a double-glazed window is about 43 F, and room air at 70 F and 40% RH reaches near 100% RH at that surface. NDSU says such a window condenses at about 40% RH at zero or colder, and at 30% condensation waits until below about −20 F. Our own check agrees: 70 F air at 40% has a dew point near 45 F, above 43 F, while 30% has a dew point near 37 F.

A 1960 Canadian government building research digest published maximum indoor RH values that avoid window condensation, assuming 70 F indoors and no sun (an old digest, so modern glass does better):

Outdoor temperatureDouble glazing, no windDouble glazing, 15 mph wind
40 F73%68%
20 F61%53%
0 F49%41%
−20 F39%32%

Those values are higher than the NDSU example because they model different windows and conditions, so use them to see the trend (colder outside, lower limit), not as a promise. The glass is the early warning; the same condensation can form in wall cavities where you cannot see it. University of Minnesota Extension lists window condensation as a symptom of excess moisture, and see Condensation on Windows in Winter for fixes.

Measure it with a hygrometer

EPA says a hygrometer, sold at local hardware stores, can measure indoor humidity. NDSU Extension adds that hygrometers drift, should be calibrated annually, and digital ones tend to be more accurate; its salt check in a sealed bag should read about 75%. Our humidity meter lesson covers that test. Our suggestion: check two rooms, and on cold nights look at the windows too. For how a wall control switches a humidifier, see humidistats and dehumidistats.

Reducing shocks and dry skin, in a sensible order

  1. Seal leaks first. Less cold air in means less water needed.
  2. Add moisture carefully. A humidifier raises the dew point, as the Wisconsin explainer says. Pick a modest target and watch the glass. Whole-house types are in Whole-House Humidifiers.
  3. Keep humidifiers clean. EPA advises emptying, wiping dry and refilling portable units daily, and scrubbing off scale and film every third day. Use low-mineral water; EPA names bottled distilled water as the most effective choice. EPA warns microorganisms often grow in tanks with standing water. Follow the maker's instructions for console units.
  4. Back off if it sweats. EPA says if water appears on windows, walls or pictures, move the unit, lower its humidistat setting, or run it less.
  5. Small helps. UMBC physicists mention dryer sheets for clothing.

When too much humidity causes mold

EPA's mold guidance says relative humidity above 60 percent is likely to cause condensation in a building and promote mold, and that mold often appears in cold corners behind furniture where condensation forms. EPA also says humidifiers should be set to stay under 60 percent. University of Minnesota Extension says problems may occur above 50 percent. Chasing a comfortable skin feel by cranking a humidifier can trade dry skin for damp walls, which is why the colder-weather limit matters. Mold on HVAC parts is covered in Mold on the AC Coil or in Ducts.

Field card: Static Shocks and Dry Winter Air

Heating adds no water. Seal first, humidify gently, watch the glass.

  • Our own example: 20 F and 70% RH heated to 70 F is about 10% RH, dew point about 12 F.
  • Typical sources: about 30 to 50% (EPA), about 30 to 40% winter (NDSU), under 60% always.
  • Colder outdoors means a lower safe limit because the glass gets colder.
  • Measure with a hygrometer; calibrate yearly.
  • Humidifier: empty daily, scrub every third day, low-mineral water.
  • Water on windows or walls means turn it down.
