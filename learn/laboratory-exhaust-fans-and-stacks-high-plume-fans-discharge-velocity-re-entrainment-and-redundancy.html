Laboratory Exhaust Fans and Stacks: High-Plume Fans, Discharge Velocity, Re-Entrainment and Redundancy

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A lab fume hood only protects people if what it pulls out of the room goes somewhere harmless. That job belongs to the exhaust fan and the stack on the roof. This lesson covers what the published guides say about stack height, discharge velocity, rain caps, fan types, redundancy and upkeep, and how a tech can check a stack with simple math.

Check your project documents

Check your project documents. Stack heights and velocities come from the engineer's dispersion study and the owner's design standard. The numbers below are published guidance from the sources listed at the bottom, not a rule for your building. ANSI/ASSP Z9.5 (laboratory ventilation) and the ASHRAE handbook chapter on laboratories are named here in plain text only; read your own copies.

Why the exhaust has to clear the roof wake

Wind hitting a building does not slide cleanly over it. It separates at the roof edge and leaves a pocket of slow, swirling air on the roof and behind the building. Anything released inside that pocket gets carried back down and around the building, including to the outdoor air intakes. D'Cruz, in an ASHRAE Transactions paper on lab exhaust, describes a geometric method that starts by calculating the length of the recirculation zone downwind of the building, and gives an example of a 60 ft tall, 100 ft wide building with a zone of about 71 ft. The goal is to push the plume up and out of that zone before it comes back down.

The I2SL guide (written by two wind engineers at CPP) lists a minimum stack height of 10 ft to protect rooftop workers, and then adds a warning in plain words: that 10 ft minimum is specifically not meant to be enough to avoid re-entrainment. The D'Cruz paper says the same about the 7 ft state minimums and the 10 ft Z9.5 figure. Taller stacks, higher exit velocity and good intake placement do the real work.

Discharge velocity

Velocity at the stack tip gives the exhaust momentum, so it rises as a jet instead of drooping. The guides agree closely:

  • I2SL says Z9.5-2012 suggests an exit velocity of at least 3,000 fpm, and also quotes Z9.5 Appendix 3 saying that 3,000 fpm should not be assumed safe. Dispersion modeling should confirm it for systems that may carry toxic emissions.
  • The University of Michigan design guideline calls for a minimum of 3000 fpm unless a dispersion study shows a lower tip velocity meets the dilution target. Stanford EH&S and the NIH technical bulletin also give about 3,000 fpm.
  • D'Cruz says variable-volume systems should never let exit velocity fall below 3,000 ft/min.
  • I2SL passes along a rule of thumb from the ASHRAE handbook: to avoid stack-tip downwash, exit velocity should be at least 1.5 times the wind speed at the stack top. It also notes that for exhausts of 30,000 cfm or more, studies show exit velocities as low as 1,000 fpm can give acceptable plume rise, judged case by case.

Never put a rain cap on a lab stack

A cap or cone over the stack turns the jet sideways or down. I2SL recommends avoiding rain caps and other devices that limit plume rise, and says conical caps often fail to keep rain out anyway because rain does not fall straight down. Michigan prohibits rain caps, including deflector types, and wants tips that discharge straight up without obstruction. Stanford prohibits rain caps that divert exhaust toward the roof. Rain is handled with a drain at the fan scroll and trapped drains at the plenum, which Michigan requires.

High-plume fans versus centrifugal fans with bypass air

Two ways to hold stack velocity are common.

Centrifugal fan with bypass damper. The fan runs at a steady speed. When the hoods close down and the building needs less exhaust, a damper opens and mixes outdoor roof air into the fan inlet so the stack still sees full flow. I2SL says adding flow through a bypass damper at the base of the stack is generally preferred over raising exit velocity, because high velocity brings noise, vibration and energy cost. Michigan describes this as normally-closed bleed-in dampers that fail closed, and says VFDs used just to hold stack velocity are generally not used there.

High-plume (induced-flow) fan. A nozzle on the fan throws a fast core jet and pulls extra air in through a wind band around it. The NIH bulletin lists these as a more compact, efficient alternative. I2SL is more cautious: it says manufacturers' effective stack height figures can mislead, the induced air adds no momentum, and the benefit may be only a few feet of height. In its 30,000 cfm example at 4 in. w.g., the induced-flow system needed 32 to 42 bhp against about 27 bhp for a conventional one. Michigan requires direct-drive high-plume fans above 5000 cfm when no redundancy is provided, so what the owner's standard demands matters more than what a catalog claims.

Manifolds and N+1 redundancy

Manifolding means several hoods and rooms share a common plenum served by more than one fan. NIH lists increased redundancy, more fume dilution and fewer fan installations among the benefits, and notes that a single-fan manifold gives no redundancy. D'Cruz explains N+1: with N fans needed for the load plus one spare, any one fan can fail and the system is still fully backed up, though two failures at once cut capacity. Michigan puts numbers on it for its campus: over 10,000 cfm, provide 100% redundant standby fans; at or below 10,000 cfm, consider two fans at half capacity each. Note that I2SL warns tightly clustered stacks plume well only if all run together, which may not hold when one fan is the standby.

Constant velocity with bypass versus VFD turndown

If a VFD slows the fan, stack velocity drops with it, so the floor of 3,000 fpm sets how far you can slow it. I2SL describes constant-volume exhaust as the norm and offers three variable-volume strategies: simple turndown, wind-responsive control and chemical-monitored control. Its advice for keeping velocity adequate at turndown is adjustable bypass makeup air or staging several stacks on and off. If your fans do run on VFDs, see the fan laws lesson for how power falls with speed, and the VFD harmonics lesson for drive side concerns.

Re-entrainment into outdoor air intakes

I2SL defines the problem as exhaust re-ingested into the building through closed-circuiting between exhaust stacks and air intakes. It adds that concentrations at nearby intakes can exceed those at the fume hood face, and that occupants cannot easily escape them. Putting intakes on a sidewall instead of the roof can cut concentrations by half to a full order of magnitude. As a tech, treat any change near the roof as a possible intake or stack change: a new louver, a relocated unit or a screen wall can alter the airflow. I2SL also says a solid architectural screen can cut effective stack height by up to 80%.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own example. These are assumed inputs for teaching, not from a real building: one round stack, 24 in. inside diameter, serving 12,000 cfm at full load. The 3,000 fpm floor is the cited guidance above.

  1. Area of a 24 in. (2 ft) stack: 3.1416 x (2 ft)² / 4 = 3.14 ft².
  2. Velocity at 12,000 cfm: 12,000 / 3.14 = about 3,820 fpm. That clears 3,000 fpm.
  3. Flow needed to hold 3,000 fpm at the stack: 3.14 x 3,000 = about 9,420 cfm.
  4. Suppose hoods close and the building only needs 6,000 cfm. The bypass damper must add 9,420 - 6,000 = about 3,420 cfm of roof air, so the fan still moves 9,420 cfm and the stack stays at 3,000 fpm.
  5. Downwash check, assuming a 15 mph wind at the stack top: 15 mph x 88 = 1,320 fpm. Times 1.5 gives about 1,980 fpm, well under 3,000 fpm, so the 3,000 fpm floor is the controlling number here.

Because velocity pressure grows with the square of velocity, a stack that is too small for the flow gets noisy and costly fast. That is why designers size the stack so full flow is not far above the floor.

Maintenance a tech can do

  • Belts. D'Cruz says belt failure is the most common failure mode on belt-driven fans and that direct-drive fans remove it. Inspect tension, wear and guards on schedule.
  • Bearings. The same paper recommends longer-life bearings on exhaust fans. Lubricate and check for heat and noise per the maker's manual.
  • Dampers. D'Cruz recommends isolation or backdraft dampers so a stopped fan does not backflow. Michigan wants motorized control dampers that prevent backdraft, with actuators outside the airstream. Stroke them and confirm they open before the fan is proved.
  • Lead and standby fans. D'Cruz says rotating lead and backup fans weekly helps keep them in working order. A standby fan that has never run may not start.
  • Drains. Michigan requires scroll drains on centrifugal fans; keep them clear.

Field card: Lab Exhaust Fans and Stacks

Tall, fast, uncovered, and backed up.

  • Published guides give about 3,000 fpm at the stack tip; Z9.5 itself warns that this is not automatically safe.
  • 10 ft stack height is a floor, not proof against re-entrainment.
  • No rain caps on lab stacks.
  • Bypass damper holds stack flow at turndown; a VFD alone lowers stack velocity.
  • Our own math: velocity = cfm / area; flow to hold 3,000 fpm = area x 3,000.
  • Rotate lead and standby fans; check belts, bearings and dampers.
