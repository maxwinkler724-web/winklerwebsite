Secondary Coolant Refrigeration: Glycol and Pumped CO2 Loops in Supermarkets

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A secondary loop keeps the refrigerant in the machine room and sends a pumped liquid out to the display cases instead. That changes what a technician carries to the job: less refrigerant piping to chase, more fluid to test, balance and keep air out of. For the direct-expansion rack side, see Supermarket Refrigeration Racks. Here we cover the loop.

How the loop is built

An EPA GreenChill page on advanced refrigeration describes the layout: the primary refrigerant chills a secondary fluid in the machine room, and that fluid is pumped through the store. The same page says these systems typically use two to four chiller systems and that the secondary fluid can be water or glycol, with CO2 (R-744) listed as one possible primary refrigerant. A maker white paper hosted in an EPA archive adds that the primary side is a direct-expansion circuit chilling a heat exchanger, and a pump then circulates a single-phase fluid to the cases and unit coolers.

Why stores use it: charge and leaks

The same EPA page says secondary loop systems "use a much smaller refrigerant charge" and have significantly lower total refrigerant emissions. It gives a comparison point: standard centralized direct-expansion systems typically hold 3,000 to 4,000 pounds of refrigerant and can leak more than 20 percent of the charge each year. A maker white paper, which says its goal is to promote these systems, states that traditional systems can hold up to 5,000 pounds while secondary loops hold under 500 pounds. A technical paper by industry authors, also hosted in an EPA archive, models a drop from 1,500 pounds for an HFC direct-expansion baseline to 600 pounds for a CO2 secondary system. Those figures come from different designs and stores, so treat them as examples, not guarantees. The reason is simple: refrigerant stays in a compact machine room circuit, and the long field piping carries coolant, where a leak is easier to find and clean up (a point the maker paper makes).

If you need the leak-rate math that applies to the refrigerant that remains, see Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances.

The energy penalty

Two things cost energy. The heat exchanger needs a temperature difference, so the refrigerant must run colder than the coolant it chills. The pump also adds work and, in the end, its heat goes into the coolant. The industry paper says concerns about higher energy use "have largely been overcome through proper design practice." Its model shows CO2 secondary low-temperature systems using 3 to 12 percent less energy than an HFC direct-expansion baseline, depending on climate, and 0 to 5 percent less if the direct-expansion condensing pressure floats down to a 50 F condensing temperature. The maker paper cites a nine-month field trial with 4.9 percent savings. These are results for particular designs. A poorly balanced or over-pumped loop can lose them.

Coolant choices

  • Propylene glycol. The maker paper calls it the most suitable secondary fluid, and the industry paper says medium-temperature propylene glycol systems have been in use since 1996. A maker data sheet lists freeze points by weight percent: 20 percent freezes at 19.2 F, 30 percent at 9.2 F, 40 percent at -6.0 F and 50 percent at -28.3 F. The same sheet shows why strength matters at low temperature: viscosity for 40 percent glycol is about 7.2 mPa·s at 50 F but 48.9 mPa·s at -4 F, and for 50 percent it is 172.8 mPa·s at -22 F. Thick fluid means more pump head and poorer heat transfer, so low-temperature loops use pumped CO2 or a different fluid.
  • Potassium formate. A US patent document describes formate salt fluids for secondary refrigeration. It reports a freeze point of -36 C (about -33 F, our conversion) at 35 weight percent and -54 C at 72 percent, and says the solutions keep low viscosity down to -40 C. Formate fluids are a salt solution, and the patent stresses corrosion control with a buffered pH of about 8 to 12, so use only the fluid and inhibitor the system maker specifies.
  • Pumped CO2. The industry paper says CO2 was chosen as a two-phase secondary fluid over single-phase potassium salts, crediting lower pumping power, smaller pipe and good heat transfer. Its drawback is higher operating pressure. Nine low-temperature CO2 secondary systems were operating in the U.S. and Canada by the end of 2008. Pressure and safety basics are in CO2 (R-744) Systems: Pressures, Gas Coolers and Safety.

Worked example (ours): 20 tons, 6 F rise versus 10 F

Assumptions, not field data: a medium-temperature loop carrying 20 tons, with a 30 percent propylene glycol mix. For specific heat we use the maker data sheet value of 3.821 kJ/(kg·K) for 30 percent by volume at the cold end of its table (listed at 50 F, though the sheet's label is garbled, so we treat it as an assumption). Converting, 3.821 / 4.1868 = 0.913 Btu/lb-F. For density we use the sheet's 63.65 lb/ft³ at 104 F, which is 63.65 / 7.4805 = 8.51 lb/gal. Real density is a little higher in a cold loop, so our flows are slightly high.

Load. 20 tons x 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 240,000 Btu/h.

Heat carried per gallon per degree. 8.51 lb/gal x 0.913 = 7.77 Btu/gal-F.

Flow. gpm = Btu/h / (60 min/h x 7.77 x rise).

  • 6 F rise: 240,000 / (60 x 7.77 x 6) = 240,000 / 2,797 = about 86 gpm.
  • 10 F rise: 240,000 / (60 x 7.77 x 10) = 240,000 / 4,662 = about 51 gpm.

The 10 F rise needs 51 / 86 = 0.60 of the flow. For comparison, the maker paper says older designs used a 2 F rise and current practice uses 4 to 8 F, and that the lower flow cuts pump horsepower sharply. Under the pump affinity relation (our own physics, assuming the same piping), power goes roughly with flow cubed: 0.60 cubed is about 0.22, so the 10 F design could need about a fifth of the pump power. The trade-off is that a higher rise means warmer coolant returning and a higher average case coil temperature, so case capacity and product temperature must still be checked. Match the rise to the case design, not to this example.

Service items

The checks below are our own routine, with the sources noted where one exists.

  • Coolant concentration. Take a sample from the loop, and use a refractometer. A maker instruction for a glycol refractometer says to calibrate with distilled water (it should read 32 F), put a few drops on the prism, wait 20 to 30 seconds, then read the edge of the blue shade. Use the scale for the correct glycol type; the instruction says the operator must know which type is in the system. Record the freeze point each visit and compare with the design. Formate fluids and CO2 need their own maker test methods.
  • Pump checks. Look for unusual noise, seal weeping, motor amps against nameplate, and the pressure difference across the pump compared with the start-up record. Gravel noise can mean air or cavitation.
  • Air in the loop. A university design guide for hydronic systems calls for air separators and vents at all high points. Air causes noisy flow, low flow at the far cases and cycling pump pressure. Vent at high points and check the fill and expansion tank pressure.
  • Case valve balancing. The same guide says every system needs a means to measure and balance flow at each piece of equipment and advises against closing a balancing valve more than 60 percent. A valve nearly closed is a sign of a pump that is too big or a branch that was left unbalanced. Balance to the flow on the start-up sheet.
  • Coolant-side delta-T. Measure supply and return temperature across the heat exchanger and each case branch. If the rise is much lower than design, flow may be too high or the case is not loading; if it is much higher, suspect low flow from air, a strainer or a partly closed valve.

For freeze-protection basics and mixing, see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops, and for another glycol chiller application see Brewery and Winery Glycol Chillers.

Field card: Secondary loops

Small charge out back, pumped coolant out front.

  • Refrigerant stays in the machine room; coolant goes to the cases.
  • Gpm = Btu/h / (60 x lb per gal x specific heat x rise).
  • 30 percent propylene glycol: about 0.91 Btu/lb-F (maker sheet).
  • Doubling the rise roughly halves flow; pump power falls much faster.
  • Cold, strong glycol is thick. Check strength, not just freeze point.
  • Test with a refractometer set for the right glycol.
  • Vent high points, balance each case, log delta-T.
