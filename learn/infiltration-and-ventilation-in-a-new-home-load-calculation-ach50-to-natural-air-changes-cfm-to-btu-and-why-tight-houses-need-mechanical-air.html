Infiltration and Ventilation in a New-Home Load Calculation: ACH50 to Natural Air Changes, CFM to Btu, and Why Tight Houses Need Mechanical Air

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new house can have a very small infiltration load and still need a lot of outdoor air, because the air that leaks in by accident is no longer enough. This lesson shows how a blower door number becomes an air-change estimate, how that becomes CFM and Btu per hour, and why a tight home moves load from random leaks to a fan you choose.

What the blower door number means

A blower door holds the house at 50 pascals of pressure difference with the outside. CFM50 is the airflow the fan needs to hold that pressure. ACH50 is that airflow expressed as air changes per hour: (CFM50 x 60) / building volume in cubic feet, according to the Building America Solution Center, which is run by a national lab for the U.S. Department of Energy. The test is normally done after the electrical, mechanical and plumbing work is finished. Our lesson on what happens in a home energy audit covers the test steps, so we do not repeat them here.

The key point is that 50 pascals is a strong, artificial pressure. A house does not breathe at ACH50 on a normal day. The lab author of the method we use below says ACH50 is a leakage indicator, not an infiltration rate.

From ACH50 to a natural air change rate

A load calculation needs the air that really leaks in at ordinary pressures. The common shortcut divides ACH50 by a number, often called N. Natural ACH = ACH50 / N. In a 1987 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory paper, Max Sherman reports that N runs from about 15 to 30 across the U.S. and Canada, with most of North America between 17 and 23. He notes that an earlier rule of dividing by 20 "was close to the mark." This lesson uses 20.

Limits of the divisor

  • It is an annual average. The paper is meant for rough, large-dataset estimates and advises against short-term or single-building use. A design-day wind and temperature can push real leakage above the average, so treat the result as an estimate, not a measurement.
  • Height changes N. The paper lists correction factors of 1.0 for one story, 0.9 for 1.5 stories, 0.8 for two stories and 0.7 for three. A smaller N means more infiltration.
  • Shielding changes N. The factors are 1.2 for a well-shielded site, 1.0 for normal and 0.9 for exposed.
  • Leak type changes N. Small cracks use 1.4, normal 1.0 and large holes 0.7.
  • The author expects large uncertainty. He says the correction factors alone suggest roughly 10% to 40%, and he called for field validation.

CFM to Btu per hour

Convert air changes to airflow first: CFM = ACH x volume / 60. Then heating load from that air is sensible only: Q = 1.08 x CFM x dT. The Oak Ridge National Laboratory weatherization engineering manual explains that 1.08 is 60 x density x specific heat, which turns cubic feet per minute into Btu per hour per degree F. The same manual gives the latent (moisture) term for cooling as Q = 4840 x CFM x dW, where dW is the difference in humidity ratio, pounds of water per pound of dry air, between outdoors and indoors. It builds 4840 from 60 x 0.075 lb/ft3 air density x 1076 Btu/lb. Use the first term for winter design and both for summer. Picking the outdoor temperature is its own topic; see outdoor design temperatures for new HVAC systems, and see the heat load calculation intro for where infiltration sits among walls, windows and ducts.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This is our own example, not a real home. Assumptions: a 2,000 square foot house with 8 foot ceilings, so the volume is 2,000 x 8 = 16,000 cubic feet. Two tests: 3 ACH50 and 7 ACH50. We divide by N = 20. The design temperature difference is 55 F. The mechanical ventilation is an assumed 60 CFM, running continuously, and we add it straight to the leakage airflow. That simple sum is a cautious simplification we chose for teaching; a real procedure may combine the two flows differently.

  1. Blower door flow. CFM50 = ACH50 x volume / 60. At 3 ACH50: 3 x 16,000 / 60 = 800 CFM50. At 7 ACH50: 7 x 16,000 / 60 = 1,867 CFM50.
  2. Natural air changes. 3 / 20 = 0.15 ACH. 7 / 20 = 0.35 ACH.
  3. Natural CFM. 0.15 x 16,000 / 60 = 40 CFM. 0.35 x 16,000 / 60 = 93.3 CFM.
  4. Heating load. 1.08 x 55 = 59.4 Btu/h per CFM. Tight: 59.4 x 40 = 2,376 Btu/h. Leaky: 59.4 x 93.3 = 5,544 Btu/h.
  5. Add 60 CFM of ventilation. The ventilation alone is 59.4 x 60 = 3,564 Btu/h. Tight: 100 CFM, 5,940 Btu/h. Leaky: 153.3 CFM, 9,108 Btu/h.

What the numbers tell us

  • Tightening from 7 to 3 ACH50 cuts the infiltration load by 3,168 Btu/h, a little over half. The leakage that remains is unplanned and uncontrolled.
  • In the tight house, the fan accounts for 3,564 of the 5,940 Btu/h, about 60%. Most of the air load is now air you chose to bring in.
  • The tight house with ventilation (5,940) lands close to the leaky house with no ventilation at all (5,544). The tight house has a known, filtered, ventilated air supply for about the same load.
  • Sensitivity. If the house is two stories, Sherman's 0.8 factor gives N = 16 (our arithmetic). Then 3 ACH50 gives 0.1875 ACH, or 50 CFM, or 2,970 Btu/h, and 7 ACH50 gives 0.4375 ACH, or 116.7 CFM, or 6,930 Btu/h. A small change in N moves the load noticeably.
  • Recovery. If the 60 CFM went through a balanced ventilator and we assume 70% sensible recovery (an assumption for illustration), the fan load would drop to about 3,564 x 0.30 = 1,069 Btu/h. See ERV vs. HRV.
  • Cooling moisture. Assume a humid summer day with dW = 0.006 lb/lb. Latent load is 4840 x 40 x 0.006 = about 1,162 Btu/h for the tight house and 4840 x 93.3 x 0.006 = about 2,710 Btu/h for the leaky one.

Why tight houses need mechanical air

The Building America ventilation guide for new homes says houses with natural air change rates below 0.35 ACH benefit from added mechanical ventilation, partly to lower aldehydes such as formaldehyde, and that tight homes tend to accumulate more moisture. In our example the 7 ACH50 house estimates at exactly 0.35 ACH and the 3 ACH50 house at 0.15. The same guide says the national residential ventilation standard (numbered 62.2) gives minimum rates from floor area and bedrooms, and it describes three system types: exhaust-only, supply, and balanced with heat recovery. It also says the model mechanical code requires mechanical ventilation in dwelling units tighter than 5 ACH50. Confirm which code edition your jurisdiction adopted. Our 60 CFM is an assumption for the example, not a computed required rate. When ventilation is treated as its own system, see dedicated outdoor air systems.

Code air-leakage targets

A U.S. Department of Energy report prepared with a national lab states that since the 2012 edition the IECC has required a tested leakage rate of no more than 5 air changes per hour in Climate Zones 1 and 2, and 3 air changes per hour in Climate Zones 3 through 8. The IECC text does not itself name the 50 pascal test pressure, per that report, though the test is run at 50 pascals. A 7 ACH50 house would miss those numbers; a 3 ACH50 house meets the 3 limit. The DOE Zero Energy Ready Home program lists tighter limits, from 3 ACH50 in Zones 1 and 2 down to 1.5 in Zone 8. State and local amendments differ, so check the adopted code.

Field card: Infiltration and ventilation in a new-home load

Tighter shell, smaller leak load, bigger share from the fan.

  • ACH50 = CFM50 x 60 / volume. It is a test number at 50 pascals, not a real air change rate.
  • Natural ACH is about ACH50 / N, with N near 20 (typical range 17 to 23, wider in extremes). It is an annual-average estimate.
  • CFM = ACH x volume / 60.
  • Sensible load = 1.08 x CFM x dT. Latent load = 4840 x CFM x dW.
  • Our example: 3 ACH50 = 40 CFM, 2,376 Btu/h; 7 ACH50 = 93.3 CFM, 5,544 Btu/h, at 55 F.
  • Adding 60 CFM of ventilation adds 3,564 Btu/h at 55 F. Recovery reduces it.
  • IECC: 5 ACH in Climate Zones 1 and 2, 3 ACH in Zones 3 through 8 (since the 2012 edition). Confirm the adopted code.
