Keeping a Well Pump House from Freezing: Insulation, Heaters and Alarms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A private well has no utility crew behind it. If the pump house, the well pit or the pressure tank room freezes, the household loses water, and a cracked fitting can follow. This lesson walks through where freezing starts, why insulation comes first, which heat sources are safer than others, what to do after a freeze, and when to hand the job to a pro.

Where freezing happens first

Four spots do most of the damage, and the small ones go first.

  • The pressure switch and its small tube. A university extension article on winterizing wells says the small line from the manifold to the pressure switch is especially at risk, and that pressure gauges can be destroyed in a single night below freezing.
  • Exposed pipe. The same article says the tank itself often needs little insulation but the plumbing entering and leaving it does, and that smaller, lower-lying pipe is the likeliest to freeze.
  • Pumps and fittings. It also warns that cast-iron booster pumps can split in a hard freeze because the ice expands.
  • The pitless adapter area. This is where the water line leaves the well casing and heads for the building. It is a transition point where pipe, fitting and cold ground meet, so inspect it with the rest. Whether a given adapter is deep enough is a question for the well contractor.

Insulation first, then a little heat

Insulation does not make heat, it slows the loss. A university extension page on freezing pipes says plainly that insulation will not protect pipes from prolonged freezing unless there is some source of heat. A pump house usually has a little: the pump motor and the ground. A different extension page says that in a small enclosed space such as a well pit or pump house, a small electric heater may be enough, and that insulating the house or pit cover helps conserve the heat already there. So the order is: seal drafts and insulate the walls, roof, door and pit cover, then add a modest controlled heat source. The extension article on winterizing wells also advises against covering electrical control parts with foam or draping them with blankets, so keep insulation off the pressure switch, wiring and motor vents. It adds that loose coverings left in place after a freeze invite rodents and insects.

Thermostat heaters versus heat lamps

A thermostatically controlled heater is the better tool because it runs only when the space is cold and can be set just above freezing. A heat lamp has no thermostat, runs all night, and puts a very hot bulb close to wood, insulation, dust and cobwebs. That is our own reasoning, and it is why we do not recommend heat lamps or makeshift heaters (a work light, a hair dryer left on, a portable heater with no controls) in a pump house, even though one extension page mentions a lamp as a possible heat source for a small enclosed space. A federal fire agency page reports that portable heaters were only 3 percent of home heating fires in 2017 to 2019 but 41 percent of fatal heating fires. Heaters are not harmless just because they are small.

If you use a small electric heater in a pump house, a federal product safety agency page says to keep portable heaters at least three feet from drapes, furniture, bedding and other combustibles, and to plug electric space heaters directly into a wall outlet, never an extension cord or power strip. The federal fire agency page uses the same three-foot rule for anything that can burn. In a pump house that means stored paint, boxes, rags and loose insulation too. Choose a heater that is marked as listed for the intended use, protected by a ground fault circuit interrupter where the code requires one, and have an electrician run a proper circuit rather than stringing cords.

Heat tape: listed for the use, installed to the instructions

Heat tape (an electric resistance cable) is made for pipes. Use a product marked for the pipe material and the location, and follow its instructions exactly. Two extension pages give the key rules: never wrap it over itself, and put it directly on the pipe, not on top of the insulation. One adds that over-insulation can cause a fire and that heat tapes used improperly can start fires. Thermostat-controlled tape switches on and off by temperature and saves electricity. Another extension page notes that tape alone is a fallback because the pipe can freeze if the power is off for several hours. Replace any tape that is cracked, scorched or damp.

Alarms and remote sensors

A heater can fail without any sign. A temperature alarm with a remote sensor in the pump house, set above freezing (we suggest around 35 F, our own number), gives you time to act before pipes freeze. Choose one that works when the power is out, since an outage is when heat and the alarm are most needed, and test it each fall. Our lesson on settings for homes left empty covers remote sensors in more detail.

Worked example: sizing a heater (our own example)

Everything below is our own example with assumed numbers, not a rule or a sizing recommendation. It uses the standard heat-loss relation: heat loss in Btu/h equals area divided by R-value, times the temperature difference.

Assumptions. A pump house 4 ft by 4 ft, walls 6 ft high, flat roof. Walls R-13, roof R-19, floor R-10 over ground assumed at 35 F, door 2 ft by 5 ft at R-5. Air leakage of 2 air changes per hour. Design outdoor temperature 0 F. Target inside 40 F, so the temperature difference is 40 F.

  1. Wall area: perimeter 16 ft times 6 ft is 96 sq ft; subtract the 10 sq ft door to get 86 sq ft. UA = 86 / 13 = 6.62 Btu/h per F.
  2. Door: 10 / 5 = 2.00. Roof: 16 / 19 = 0.84.
  3. Air leakage: volume is 4 x 4 x 6 = 96 cu ft. At 2 air changes per hour that is 96 x 2 / 60 = 3.2 cfm. Using the usual 1.08 factor, UA = 1.08 x 3.2 = 3.46.
  4. Total above-ground UA = 6.62 + 2.00 + 0.84 + 3.46 = 12.91 Btu/h per F.
  5. Loss through the air and envelope: 12.91 x 40 = 516 Btu/h. Floor: (16 / 10) x (40 - 35) = 8 Btu/h. Total about 525 Btu/h.
  6. Convert: 525 / 3.412 = 154 W.
  7. Add a 50 percent safety margin (our own choice): 154 x 1.5 = 231 W. A heater of about 250 W would be the nearest size up.

Check. At an assumed design of minus 20 F the difference is 60 F: 12.91 x 60 = 775 plus the 8 floor term is 783 Btu/h, or 229 W, or 344 W with the same margin. Cold snaps, wind and a leaky door change this a lot, so the real answer comes from your own building and a contractor. Note that a low wattage like this shows why leak sealing matters: the air leakage term alone was about a quarter of the load.

After a freeze: what to do

  1. Power off. Turn off the pump at its breaker before opening anything. One extension page says to turn off the well pump when you shut off water. Do not touch wet or iced electrical parts.
  2. Look before thawing. Check for split pipe, cracked fittings, a bulged tank and a damaged switch tube. Thawed splits will leak.
  3. Thaw gently. Extension pages say heat gradually, work from the faucet side, and avoid open flames and boiling water. One page says an electric hair dryer is usually effective; keep any electrical thawing tool dry and away from standing water.
  4. Check the pressure switch. Look for a cracked tube, frost damage and moisture inside the cover. Do not reset or adjust it yourself if you see water in it.
  5. Restore power slowly and watch. Turn the pump on, listen for odd sounds, and look for leaks and short cycling.

When to call a pro

Call a well contractor for a damaged pump, a pitless adapter or casing problem, a tank that bulged or leaked, or a pump that runs but gives no water. Call an electrician for wet or scorched wiring, a heater circuit, a tripped breaker that will not hold, or any new outlet or heater supply. A federal public health page notes that private well water is not covered by the national drinking water law and may not be tested regularly, so ask your contractor about checking the water if the system was opened or the tank contaminated.

Field card: Keeping a Well Pump House from Freezing

Insulate, then add small controlled heat, then add an alarm.

  • First to freeze: the switch tube, small exposed pipe, then the pump and fittings.
  • Insulation slows loss; it needs some heat source to work.
  • Prefer a thermostat-controlled heater over a heat lamp or makeshift heater.
  • Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from a heater; no extension cords.
  • Heat tape: listed for the use, on the pipe, never overlapped, instructions followed.
  • After a freeze: power off, look, thaw gently, check the switch, restore power slowly.
  • Our example: about 525 Btu/h, 154 W, about 250 W with margin (assumptions stated).
