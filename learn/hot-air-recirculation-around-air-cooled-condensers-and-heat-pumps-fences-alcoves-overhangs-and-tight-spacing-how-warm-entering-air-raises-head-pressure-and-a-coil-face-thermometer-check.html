Hot Air Recirculation Around Condensers and Heat Pumps

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An outdoor unit can be clean, correctly charged and still run hot, because the air it breathes is partly its own exhaust. This lesson explains how that loop forms, what it does to head pressure, power use and capacity, how to check for it with a thermometer, and how to fix it.

What recirculation is

An air-cooled condenser or heat pump outdoor unit pulls outdoor air through the coil, picks up heat from the refrigerant, and blows the warmer air away. Recirculation means some of that warmed air gets drawn back into the coil inlet instead of drifting off. The coil then sees entering air that is hotter than the true outdoor air. Researchers who studied air-cooled condensers at a large power plant in a wind-tunnel test reported the same pattern at that scale: part of the hot exhaust air returns to the inlet region, which forces a significant rise in inlet air temperature and cuts condenser efficiency (see source 3). A home unit is much smaller, but the physics is the same.

What causes it

  • Privacy fences and tight wall alcoves. A solid fence or a three-sided nook traps the discharge air and gives it a short path back to the coil.
  • Overhangs and low covers. A roof, deck or cover over a top-discharge unit blocks the plume, so it spreads sideways and is drawn back in.
  • Units packed side by side. One unit's discharge feeds its neighbor's inlet, and a row of equipment can feed itself along the row.
  • Rooftop screens. Solid screens around rooftop condensers can act like a fence on a roof.
  • Prevailing wind. Wind that pushes the plume back toward a coil, or that swirls behind a wall, can turn a marginal layout into a bad one. The wind-tunnel study above found the worst recirculation when wind hit the buildings from certain directions, so wind direction matters as much as layout.

Heat pump outdoor units are condensers too whenever they run in cooling mode, so everything here applies to them. Discharge direction and nearby walls matter for them as well.

Why warm entering air hurts

The condensing temperature has to stay above the entering air temperature for heat to flow out of the refrigerant. If the entering air rises, condensing temperature and head pressure rise with it. A university vapor-compression lecture shows the same idea: compressor power depends on the pressure ratio, and condenser performance is driven by the difference between condensing temperature and entering air temperature (source 2). Higher pressure ratio means more compressor work for less cooling, so power goes up and capacity goes down.

How big is the effect? A Southern state university research center measured condenser inlet air at three homes and cites a rule of thumb that efficiency improves about 1.2 percent for each degree F the condenser inlet air is cooled (source 4). At one of its sites, horizontal exhaust air mixed into the inlet and pushed inlet temperature above ambient. The same study also found that poorly placed plantings can cut efficiency by restricting airflow. A national lab field study on a rooftop unit found condenser inlet air only about 0.3 C warmer than a far reference point over a hot roof, and a cool roof coating removed that rise; with the fan running alone the rise was not significantly different, which suggested discharge air was not being recirculated at that site (source 1). The size of any one effect depends on the site, so measure instead of guessing.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measurement of any real unit.

  • Assumption 1: the true outdoor air is 95 F.
  • Assumption 2: the condenser discharges air 20 F warmer than the air entering it. (This is the 20 F figure we were given for our example.)
  • Assumption 3: 25 percent of the air entering the coil is its own discharge air. The other 75 percent is true outdoor air.

Step 1. Mixing is a weighted average, so the rise in entering air is the recirculated fraction times the discharge warming: 0.25 x 20 F = 5 F.

Step 2. New entering air temperature: 95 F + 5 F = 100 F.

Step 3. Condensing temperature follows entering air, so we expect it to climb by roughly the same 5 F (our own simplification, and the exact change varies by unit).

Step 4. Capacity impact, as an assumption only: if the 1.2 percent per degree F rule of thumb in source 4 applied here, 5 F x 1.2 percent = about 6 percent worse efficiency. That figure is an illustration, not a prediction, because it came from other equipment and other sites. Check the maker's performance tables for your model.

How to check it with a thermometer

  1. Safety first. Keep hands and probes out of the fan and away from moving parts. Take readings from outside the guard, and follow the maker's instructions and your site procedures.
  2. Get the true outdoor reading. Use a shaded spot upwind, well away from the unit, a wall or hot pavement. Direct sun on the sensor will read high.
  3. Get the coil face reading. With the unit running and steady, hold a second thermometer in the air stream just in front of the coil, not touching metal and shaded from sun. Take several points around the coil, including the sides that face walls or neighbors.
  4. Compare. Entering air that reads clearly above the true outdoor air, especially after the unit has run for a while, points to recirculation or a hot local surface. Compare with the unit off or the fan only: a gap that appears only when the compressor runs points to discharge heat.
  5. Use two thermometers, calibrated against each other, read at the same time, since the outdoor air changes over minutes.
  6. Look at the gauges too. If head pressure is high but the coil is clean and the charge and airflow check out, recirculation is a suspect. See the dirty condenser lesson linked below for the other causes.

Fixes

  • Spacing. Follow the maker's clearance and spacing in the installation instructions, and see the clearances lesson below. Keep units apart so one does not feed the next.
  • Open the screen or fence. Use open slats, louvers or lattice with generous free area instead of a solid panel, or lower or move the panel. A federal energy department maintenance page says to trim foliage back at least 2 feet around a condenser (source 5).
  • Discharge direction. Turn the unit so its discharge points away from the wall, the fence and neighboring units. Never add a duct or hood that is not approved for that unit, since extra restriction can hurt airflow.
  • Remove the lid. Take away or raise an overhang or cover so a top-discharge plume can rise.
  • Raise or relocate. The wind-tunnel study found that raising the platform and raising a windbreak both cut recirculation (source 3). On a building, that can mean a stand or a different location.

For a roof or yard job, a quick re-test after the change shows whether it worked: repeat thermometer comparison under the same conditions.

Field card: Hot Air Recirculation

If the coil breathes its own exhaust, head pressure follows.

  • Recirculation is discharge air pulled back into the coil inlet.
  • Causes: solid fences, alcoves, overhangs, tight spacing, rooftop screens, wind.
  • Warmer entering air means higher condensing temperature, more power, less capacity.
  • Mixing math: rise = recirculated fraction x discharge warming (our example: 0.25 x 20 F = 5 F).
  • Check: shaded true outdoor air versus air at the coil face, both read at the same time.
  • Fix: spacing, open screens, discharge direction, remove lids.
