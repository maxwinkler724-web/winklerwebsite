Hospital Central Sterile Processing HVAC: Sterilizer Heat and EtO Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hospital central sterile department is the ambulatory surgery sterile processing room made much bigger, with more steam and sometimes an ethylene oxide (EtO) sterilizer. The tech's new concerns are chase heat, dedicated gas exhaust and alarms.

Start with the facility's design and safety rules

Check the site documents. Design rows and hospital policies decide what applies where you work. This page is general education, not a design basis. For the basic pressure map and humidity logs, see the ambulatory surgery center lesson.

The department in one picture

A public slide deck from the Facility Guidelines Institute reproduces the sterile processing rows of an ASHRAE 170-2013 addendum. It lists decontamination as negative with 6 total air changes per hour (2 outdoor), exhausted directly outdoors and not recirculated. It lists the clean workroom as positive with 4 total (2 outdoor), and sterile storage as positive with 4 total (2 outdoor). Both clean spaces show a 60 percent maximum relative humidity, and all three spaces show 72 to 78 F. Editions differ, so confirm the row in your project's edition. ASHRAE interpretation IC 170-2013-7 confirms the 72 to 78 F range for those three space types.

Steam sterilizer heat and the equipment chase

In many layouts (our general observation, not a cited rule) large steam sterilizers are recessed so the door faces the clean prep and pack room and the machine body sits in a chase or service space behind it. We found no public maker-neutral figure for how much heat a sterilizer releases, so the example below uses a labeled assumption. The real number comes from the equipment submittal.

  • The CDC guideline describes prevacuum sterilizer cycles at 132 C (270 F) for wrapped supplies, so surfaces and doors run hot.
  • The CDC guideline says the ideal steam is dry saturated steam with a dryness fraction of at least 97 percent. Wetter steam is a boiler plant and piping problem, not a ventilation problem. See wet steam and carryover for the causes. A tech who sees wet packs should tell the sterile processing lead and check traps and separators on the supply line.
  • The chase should exhaust to outdoors by its own path. Do not send it into the clean prep and pack room, which would be heating the room it is meant to serve and could push the pressure the wrong way.
  • Chase exhaust takes air from somewhere. Know which room it pulls from so it does not drain the positive rooms. Our own rule of thumb: pull makeup from a corridor or mechanical space, not through the sterile side.

Washer-disinfectors and the decontamination side

Washers add heat and moisture where staff load soiled items. The OSHA hospital eTool notes personal protective equipment for staff removing heated items from washer-decontaminators, which tells you the surfaces and exhaust run hot. Keep washer exhaust connections tight and check that the washer vent has airflow, since in our experience a failed vent lets steam into the room. Decontamination stays negative, exhausted outdoors, and unattended doors stay closed. See negative and positive pressure rooms for monitors and checks.

EtO sterilizers and aerators: the dedicated exhaust

EtO is a toxic gas, and where a hospital still uses it, the HVAC rules are stricter than for steam. NIOSH publication 89-115 (older, so check it against current maker and facility instructions) says to route EtO directly outdoors by keeping net suction on all the exhaust ductwork, with a dedicated system serving only the sterilizer area, including the sterilizer, cylinders and aerator. Discharge points should be placed so prevailing winds do not carry the gas toward populated areas, open windows or HVAC intakes.

  • Door-area hood. NIOSH says to put a local exhaust hood as close as possible to the top of the sterilizer door and size it for the worst case, when the door first opens and hot air rising from the door carries gas with it. It also describes cracking the door to the latched position or 2 inches, whichever is less, and waiting 15 to 20 minutes.
  • Room airflow. NIOSH describes net inflow into the loading room and mechanical access room, air entering upper openings at 50 to 100 ft/min, and a cylinder area kept below 100 F. It gives no air change rate for those rooms.
  • Alarms. NIOSH recommends flow sensors with alarms for fan failure or poor exhaust performance, a sterilizer that will not run if exhaust is not working, airflow sail switches, and room EtO sensors with alarms. OSHA's hospital eTool also lists alarms for inadequate ventilation with automatic shutdown, and detector systems to signal leaks.
  • Leak checks. NIOSH calls for regular checks of door gaskets, valves, tubing and piping connections, and sensor and alarm checks at the maker's intervals. OSHA's eTool says to keep a written log of detected leaks and service.
  • Aeration. The CDC guideline says items must be fully aerated before patient use, giving 12 hours at 50 C or 8 hours at 60 C for polyvinylchloride tubing. NIOSH says aeration typically takes 12 hours.

Alarm setpoints and gas monitoring belong to the facility safety program and the equipment maker. A tech keeps the fan, duct and controls working, never bypasses an interlock, and treats an exhaust alarm as a stop condition.

EtO exposure limits

OSHA's standard, 29 CFR 1910.1047 on eCFR, sets a permissible exposure limit of 1 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average and an excursion limit of 5 ppm over 15 minutes. The action level is 0.5 ppm as an 8-hour average. Medical surveillance covers employees at or above the action level for 30 or more days a year. Periodic monitoring repeats at least every 6 months between the action level and the limit, and every 3 months above the limit. The standard's ventilation appendix is non-mandatory.

EPA has also written air emission rules for commercial EtO sterilization facilities. That rule is about outdoor emissions and is in flux (a March 2026 EPA fact sheet describes a proposal to repeal the 2024 rule). It does not set indoor exposure limits, and the fact sheet does not mention hospitals, so we use it for context only.

Humidity limits in sterile storage

The CDC disinfection guideline says sterile storage should be well ventilated and protected from dust, moisture, insects, and temperature and humidity extremes, and that a torn, wet or punctured pack must be repacked and reprocessed. The 60 percent maximum from the design table above is the number to log against. If readings run high, check coil operation, reheat and the economizer, the order of work in the ambulatory surgery lesson.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions, all ours: an equipment chase with 4 steam sterilizers, each releasing an example 6,000 Btu/h to the chase, and 2 washers releasing an example 4,000 Btu/h each. The allowed air temperature rise in the chase is 15 F. Real release rates come from the submittals and are not cited values. We use the sensible heat equation, Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T.

  1. Sterilizers: 4 x 6,000 = 24,000 Btu/h.
  2. Washers: 2 x 4,000 = 8,000 Btu/h.
  3. Total: 24,000 + 8,000 = 32,000 Btu/h.
  4. Exhaust: cfm = 32,000 / (1.08 x 15) = 32,000 / 16.2 = 1,975 cfm.
  5. If the chase can only tolerate a 10 F rise, cfm = 32,000 / (1.08 x 10) = 2,963 cfm. A smaller allowed rise means much more airflow.

That 1,975 cfm leaves the building and must be made up by supply or transfer air.

EtO exposure line. Suppose a personal sample gives 0.3 ppm as an 8-hour average (an example, not a measured value). Against the 1 ppm limit, 0.3 / 1 = 30 percent. Against the 0.5 ppm action level, 0.3 / 0.5 = 60 percent. The reading is below both, so on this one result the action level is not reached. That is not a pass on the 5 ppm 15-minute excursion limit, which is a separate test, and any change in equipment or work practice calls for new monitoring under the standard.

What a tech checks

  • Pressure monitors for decontamination, prep and pack, and storage match the design direction, and alarms work.
  • Chase exhaust fan runs, and its makeup path is known.
  • EtO exhaust flow alarm and room sensors are not disabled, and nothing was changed without the safety officer.
  • Steam supply traps and separators are on the preventive maintenance list. See measuring airflow for verifying exhaust.

Field card: Central Sterile Processing HVAC

Soiled side negative, clean side positive, gas and heat go outdoors.

  • Decontamination: negative, 6 total ACH, exhausted outdoors, no recirculation (ASHRAE 170-2013 addendum row via FGI deck).
  • Clean workroom and sterile storage: positive, 4 total ACH, 60 percent maximum RH.
  • Temperature 72 to 78 F in the design table; IC 170-2013-7.
  • Steam dryness: at least 97 percent (CDC).
  • EtO exhaust: dedicated, net suction, discharge away from intakes (NIOSH 89-115).
  • OSHA EtO: 1 ppm TWA, 5 ppm 15-minute, 0.5 ppm action level.
  • Our example: 32,000 Btu/h, 1,975 cfm at 15 F rise; 0.3 ppm is 30 percent of the limit and 60 percent of the action level.
