Packinghouse Cooling: Hydrocoolers, Forced-Air Tunnels and Room Cooling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Fruit and vegetables come off the field warm and still alive, giving off heat as they breathe. A packinghouse has a short window to pull that field heat out. This lesson covers the three common ways to do it, how cooling time is described, how to turn a cooling job into refrigeration tons, and what goes wrong at the dock.

Why the speed matters to the refrigeration side

A storage room is built to hold a temperature. A packinghouse cooler has a different job: it has to remove a large amount of heat in a few hours, over and over, all season. That is a pull-down load, not a holding load. Our produce cold storage lesson covers the storage side and our farm stand cooler lesson covers the small version, so here we stay with the packinghouse equipment.

Room cooling

With room cooling, boxes or bins sit in a refrigerated room and the room air slowly takes their heat. A state university extension chapter on apples describes it as needing little labor and little electrical demand, but as slow and sometimes uneven, with cooling taking several days or longer. A university lecture handout says forced-air cooling takes roughly one quarter to one tenth as long as room cooling. Because the heat leaves slowly, a room cooler needs the least refrigeration capacity of the three methods.

Forced-air cooling tunnels

In forced-air cooling, a fan makes a pressure difference so that cold room air goes through the vent holes of the boxes rather than around them. The extension chapter on forced-air cooling describes the common tarp setup: two parallel rows of pallets with a gap (the plenum) between them, a cover over the top of the plenum and down the back, and a fan that pulls air out of the plenum. Because the plenum runs at negative pressure, the cover is pulled inward, so slats or small pipes hold it open. That chapter gives a rule of thumb of about 2 to 3 cfm per pound of produce at about 0.10 inch of water static pressure, and about 24 to 26 pallets per fan.

Two numbers show why airflow matters. The same chapter works an example in which a load starting at 95°F in a 41°F room reaches its seven-eighths point at 47.75°F, and shows that point arriving in about 30 minutes at 5 cfm per pound versus about 90 minutes at 2 cfm per pound. The university handout adds that doubling airflow gives roughly 40 percent faster cooling but needs about four times the pressure and about six times the fan power. That fan power ends up as heat in the room, so an oversized fan adds to the refrigeration load.

The extension chapter also says air leaving the produce can be 20 to 30 F° above room temperature at the start, and that each fan should have a thermostat that shuts it off once the produce nears room temperature, because extra running wastes energy and dries the produce. Forced-air cooling moves heat much faster than room cooling, so an existing room may need more refrigeration capacity before it can run this way.

Hydrocoolers

A hydrocooler moves cold water over or around the produce. The extension chapter on hydrocooling describes a large conventional type with a conveyor under a shower (active cooling commonly 10 to 30 minutes or more), a batch type for one or more pallets, an immersion tank, and flooding a truck or trailer, which it flags for uneven flow and contamination risk. Most large units use a vapor-compression system with a heat exchanger in the water reservoir. The same chapter gives flows up to 20 gallons per minute per square foot of cooling area, and the handout lists 7 to 10 for a single layer. A federal agricultural research handbook notes that hydrocooling does not dry produce, but packages must pass water and tolerate wetting.

The apple chapter cautions that apples are seldom hydrocooled below 45°F and that removing all the heat by hydrocooling alone is not advisable, so a room or tunnel often finishes the job. The handout says only about half of the refrigeration effort in hydrocooling typically goes to cooling the crop, and the hydrocooling chapter puts that figure lower still, at 10 to 15 percent of capacity, so the load on the plant is bigger than the product heat alone.

Water sanitation, in general terms

Recirculated hydrocooler water can spread disease from one lot to the next. The extension chapter says it needs continual treatment, that chlorine-based products should be registered and used as the label directs, that chlorine acts on surfaces, and that wastewater may be regulated and need a discharge permit. The federal handbook says the water must come from a clean source and be treated to limit decay organisms. For refrigeration techs, the practical points are corrosion and cleanliness: sanitizer residue attacks metal, and dirty water fouls spray nozzles and heat exchangers. Treatment levels belong to the packinghouse food safety plan, not to the refrigeration tech.

Half-cooling and seven-eighths cooling

The federal handbook defines the terms this way: a product is half cool when it has dropped half of the difference between its starting temperature and the temperature of the cooling medium. Another half-cooling period makes it three-quarters cool, and cooling is usually finished at seven-eighths or fifteen-sixteenths. The extension chapter defines the seven-eighths point the same way (87.5 percent of the difference). The time is not the same for every product; it falls with colder cooling medium, faster airflow or water flow, smaller pieces and good contact with the product. The sweet corn example in the hydrocooling chapter shows why the tail is slow: the first 30 F° of drop took about 28 minutes, and the next 13 F° took about twice that long.

Sizing: from product heat to refrigeration tons

The federal handbook builds the product-cooling load as product weight times specific heat times temperature change, adds respiration heat separately, then adds a margin and divides by the operating hours. We follow that method below. One ton of refrigeration is 12,000 Btu per hour, a standard unit definition.

Worked example (our own example, with assumptions)

Assumptions, all ours except the specific heat: 40 bins of pears, 900 lb per bin; field temperature 85°F; target pulp temperature 40°F (the apple chapter stops forced-air cooling at 38 to 40°F); cooling air at 34°F; the job must finish in 8 hours; specific heat of pears 0.86 Btu/lb·F, the value in the federal handbook's heat-load example. We leave out bin wood, respiration and pallet heat, and cover them with a 25 percent allowance (our own assumption).

  1. Product weight: 40 bins × 900 lb = 36,000 lb.
  2. Temperature drop: 85 − 40 = 45 F°.
  3. Heat to remove: 36,000 × 0.86 × 45 = 1,393,200 Btu.
  4. Per hour over 8 hours: 1,393,200 ÷ 8 = 174,150 Btu/h.
  5. Tons: 174,150 ÷ 12,000 = 14.5 tons.
  6. With the 25 percent allowance: 174,150 × 1.25 = 217,688 Btu/h, or about 18.1 tons.

Check against the definitions: with 34°F air, half cool is 85 − (85 − 34) ÷ 2 = 59.5°F, and seven-eighths cool is 34 + 51 ÷ 8 = 40.4°F. So the 40°F target is about the seven-eighths point, which is why the last few degrees take the longest. If the same load were hydrocooled, remember the handout figure that only part of the refrigeration reaches the crop. The tonnage would be higher. In this example we assume the new chiller or condensing unit is charged with a refrigerant other than R-410A; confirm the actual refrigerant on the nameplate.

The key lesson is the swing. Run the same 1,393,200 Btu over 24 hours and the need is about 4.8 tons; over 8 hours it is 14.5. The handout says inadequate capacity lets the room air temperature rise as new product comes in, which slows every box in the room. Match the coil, compressor and fan to the peak, not the daily average.

Condensation on packed fruit at the dock

The apple chapter says chilled apples moved suddenly into warm air can sweat, meaning water vapor condenses on them, and that wetting encourages fungi and bacteria. It advises letting fruit warm gradually inside the storage before going into open air. In our own general practice, at the dock that means short door-open times, a cooled staging area or dock seal where practical, and no opening of pallets until they have warmed. Our refrigerated warehouse lesson covers dock door infiltration, and the box load lesson shows how infiltration adds to load.

Worker comfort and refrigeration safety

This part is our own general guidance, not drawn from the sources above. Packing line staff often stand in cool, wet, drafty areas for long shifts, and the forced-air tunnel puts cold air through the stacks, not onto people. Keep fan exhaust and wet spray off walkways, keep floors drained, and report puddles under coils and drains. Wet floors and ice are a slip hazard for crew and for techs carrying tools.

Plants that use ammonia need extra care. A federal workplace safety agency's ammonia refrigeration page says ammonia is a high health hazard that corrodes skin, eyes and lungs, that 300 ppm is immediately dangerous to life and health, that the odor threshold is 20 ppm, and that it can burn or explode in an enclosed space with an ignition source. If you are not trained and authorized for the plant, do not enter an ammonia machinery room, especially during an alarm. Follow the site's lockout and emergency plan, and see our potato and onion storage lesson for related air-handling ideas.

Field card: Packinghouse Cooling

Size for the pull-down, not the average.

  • Room cooling is slowest and needs the least capacity; forced-air is several times faster; hydrocooling is fast but only part of the refrigeration reaches the crop.
  • Forced-air rule of thumb from extension: about 2 to 3 cfm per pound at about 0.10 in. of water.
  • Half cool = half the gap to the cooling medium; seven-eighths cool = 87.5 percent of the gap.
  • Load = pounds × specific heat × degrees dropped, divided by hours, divided by 12,000 for tons.
  • Our example: 36,000 lb of pears, 85°F to 40°F in 8 hours is about 14.5 tons before allowances.
  • Let cold fruit warm gradually before it meets warm dock air to limit sweating.
  • Do not enter an ammonia area without training and authorization.
