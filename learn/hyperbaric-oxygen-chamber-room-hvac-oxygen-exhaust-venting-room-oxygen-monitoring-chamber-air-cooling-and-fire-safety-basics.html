Hyperbaric Oxygen Chamber Room HVAC: Oxygen Exhaust Venting, Room Oxygen Monitoring, Chamber Air Cooling and Fire Safety Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hyperbaric oxygen chamber is a pressure vessel where a patient breathes oxygen at higher than room pressure. For a facility tech the room around it matters most: oxygen must never pile up there, and nothing in it should start a fire. This lesson covers the HVAC side. It does not teach chamber operation, which is not a tech's job.

Check the issuing authority

Check the issuing authority. Chamber rules come from the chamber maker, the facility's hyperbaric safety staff, the local fire authority and the standards they adopt. This page is general education from public sources read in 2026. Follow the maker's documents and your site's procedures over anything here.

Why oxygen and rooms do not mix

The FDA's letter to health care providers about these devices says it has seen reports of fires with serious injuries and deaths, and describes a heightened fire risk when oxygen is used at high concentration. It also says patients breathe 100% oxygen in a pressurized chamber. The Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society (UHMS) page on safe design says the most common cause of clinical chamber fires is an ignition source brought inside, and that it counts about one clinical chamber fire every two years worldwide. UHMS also points to NFPA 99, the Health Care Facilities Code, whose Chapter 14 covers hyperbaric facilities. We name that code only; read it through your site's safety staff, not from us.

Your part is the room. In an oxygen-enriched room, ordinary materials burn harder. The OSHA Technical Manual says oxygen-enriched atmospheres present a fire and explosion hazard and that meters are usually set to alarm below 19.5 percent and above 23.5 percent oxygen, with normal air about 20.9 percent. OSHA's shipyard guidance defines enriched as more than 22 percent, and a federal shipyard definition says 22.0 percent or more. Sources differ a little, so we use 23.5 percent as the alarm line in the example and treat any climb toward it as a stop-and-call event.

Oxygen exhaust piped outdoors (our example setup)

In our example, a monoplace chamber (one patient) vents its spent oxygen through a dedicated line that runs through the wall to outdoors. This is our own assumed layout to teach the idea; your chamber's installation documents decide the real one. The tech's job is to leave that line alone. Do not tee anything into it, cap it, or let a duct or a mechanical-room exhaust fan share it. If the line's outdoor end is blocked, leaking at a joint inside the room, or disturbed during other work, oxygen goes into the room instead of outside. UHMS notes a 1998 fire that began in a recently maintained oxygen supply line, likely from improper installation or cleaning, so work on any oxygen piping belongs to qualified people only.

Room oxygen monitoring

A wall-mounted oxygen monitor tells staff when the room air has drifted. The alarm points in the OSHA manual above are the usual idea: low alarm near 19.5 percent, high alarm near 23.5 percent. Do not move, silence, or recalibrate it yourself unless the owner's procedure and the monitor maker's documents assign you that task. If it alarms, the answer is to follow the site's alarm plan and call the people who run the chamber. Do not open doors to "air it out" while a treatment is running without their direction.

Room ventilation and cooling

Ventilation is the dilution that keeps small oxygen leaks harmless. The supply and exhaust grilles should stay unblocked, the fan should run whenever the chamber is in service, and a lost-airflow alarm to the building system is worth asking about. Chambers, their controls and any equipment room also give off heat, and multiplace chambers (several occupants) use a compressor and air supply that need cooling and clean intake air. We have no public load figures here, so use the maker's heat data for sizing, as in cooling electrical rooms. Compressed air basics are in compressed air for facility techs. Keep intake air for any chamber air supply away from exhausts and fumes; ask the maker where the intake may be located.

Worked example: how much leak is too much

All numbers here are our own assumptions for teaching, not data from any chamber. Assume a monoplace chamber exhausts 400 L/min of oxygen, the room is 12 x 14 x 9 ft, and it has 100 cfm of ventilation.

  1. Convert exhaust to cfm. One cfm is 28.3168 L/min, so 400 / 28.3168 = 14.13 cfm.
  2. Assume 5 percent leaks into the room: 0.05 x 14.13 = 0.706 cfm.
  3. Extra oxygen fraction at steady state = leak / (leak + ventilation) = 0.706 / (0.706 + 100) = 0.00701, or 0.70 percentage points.
  4. Room oxygen, starting from 20.9 percent: 20.9 + 0.70 = 21.6 percent. A more exact mix balance, (20.9 x 100 + 100 x 0.706) / 100.706, gives 21.45 percent. Either way it is far below 23.5.

The room is 12 x 14 x 9 = 1,512 cubic feet, so 100 cfm is about 4 air changes per hour (100 x 60 / 1,512 = 3.97). Now stop the ventilation in your head. The same 0.706 cfm adds about 0.047 percent of the room volume per minute, so a simple (no mixing losses) estimate to climb 2.6 points to 23.5 is about 56 minutes. That is why airflow matters and why a lost-fan alarm is valuable. A bigger leak shortens it quickly; 100 percent of the exhaust (14.13 cfm) would give 14.13 / 114.13 = 12.4 points extra at steady state, which is far above any alarm line.

No heat, no sparks, hot work permits

FDA recommends proper grounding, keeping static-generating and prohibited items out of chambers, and cotton-type clothing for patients because wool and synthetics create more static. For a tech this means asking before bringing in tools, radios, phones or space heaters, and never running temporary power or heat in the room. UHMS adds that a 1997 suppression system failed because it had been disabled during maintenance. Do not impair a fire suppression system or detection without the owner's approved impairment procedure.

Brazing, cutting or grinding is hot work. Federal rule 29 CFR 1910.252 describes a written permit as preferred, an inspection before work starts, a fire watch where needed, and a watch for at least 30 minutes after work ends. It sets 35 feet as the basic combustible clearance distance and says not to weld or cut in explosive atmospheres. OSHA's shipyard guidance says an enriched space should be labeled not safe for hot work. See A2L service habits for the same ignition-control mindset, and confined spaces for air-testing order.

Where the tech's role stops

Techs do not operate chambers, do not handle patients and do not decide treatment. Your work is room air, cooling, ductwork and controls, done under the site's permits, with the hyperbaric safety staff informed. Anything that touches the oxygen supply, chamber exhaust, fire suppression or monitors is for trained, authorized people. A similar hydrogen-monitoring mindset appears in battery room ventilation.

Field card: Hyperbaric chamber room

Oxygen out, air in, nothing that can ignite.

  • Chamber exhaust oxygen goes outdoors on its own line. Never share, cap or cut it.
  • Normal air is about 20.9 percent oxygen. OSHA's manual cites alarms near 19.5 and 23.5.
  • Keep grilles clear and room airflow running; ask about a loss-of-airflow alarm.
  • Example: 400 L/min is 14.13 cfm; 5 percent leak with 100 cfm gives about 0.70 points extra.
  • No hot work without the site permit, fire watch and owner sign-off.
  • Do not disable detection or suppression without an approved impairment procedure.
  • Techs do not operate chambers. Call the chamber staff.
