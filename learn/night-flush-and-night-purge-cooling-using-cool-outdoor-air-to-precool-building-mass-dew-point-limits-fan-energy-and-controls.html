Night Flush and Night Purge Cooling: Precooling Building Mass with Cool Outdoor Air

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A concrete slab, block walls and furniture hold a lot of heat. If the air handler runs at night with the outdoor air damper open, cool air can pull that stored heat out for the price of fan power instead of compressor power. Done wrong, it brings in wet air, overcools the space or burns more fan kWh than it saves.

How night flush works

Night flush (also called night purge or night precooling) runs the supply fan while the building is empty, opens the outdoor air and relief or exhaust dampers, and lets cool outdoor air sweep through. The air picks up heat from the space and the mass, then leaves through relief. A nonprofit sustainability workshop describes the passive version the same way: closed by day, open at night, with exposed floors and walls absorbing the next day's heat. It suggests nights below about 20 to 22°C (68 to 71°F) and timer or thermostat control.

In a mechanical building this is an economizer job run at night. A regional energy-efficiency technology review describes it as an air-side economizer that opens at night so the structure and furnishings are cooled and less direct-expansion cooling is needed the next day. Without it, the space in that review drifted from about 72°F to 80°F overnight. With it, the space was cooled down to a lower limit of about 67°F.

When it pays

Two things have to be true: the nights must be cool enough, and the building must have mass that can see the air.

  • A big day-night swing. The Northwest review calls for swings above 25°F and fairly dry air. A UC Berkeley thesis summarizing the literature says that with a 15 to 20°C swing (27 to 36°F), indoor peak temperatures can run 6 to 8°C (11 to 14°F) below the outdoor peak. In mild climates the same thesis found the buildings tended to overcool and night ventilation changed little.
  • Heavy, exposed mass. A national lab summary of precooling field tests concluded that night precooling and night ventilation can cut peak demand and energy in heavy-mass buildings but not in light ones. A suspended ceiling or carpet over the slab hides the mass from the air stream.
  • A good nighttime air temperature. One optimization study (DOE-2 plus a genetic algorithm) found outdoor temperatures of 10 to 18°C (50 to 64°F) and an outdoor-indoor difference above 8°C (about 14°F) suited night ventilation. Treat that as one study's result, not a code.

The payoff is modest. The Northwest review gives a typical 10 percent of cooling energy, with a 5 to 15 percent range there, and cites other studies at 5 to 18 percent in less temperate cities. In an Oak Ridge simulation of apartment buildings, moderate climates did best and hot or cold ones saved less.

Dew point and humidity limits

Temperature alone is not enough to decide whether to flush. Night air can be cool and still carry a lot of moisture. That moisture can condense on cold slab surfaces or ductwork, raise indoor humidity, and land on the cooling coil the next day as extra latent load. The ORNL report did not address humidity, and the Berkeley thesis lists it as future work, though it notes night ventilation did not beat daytime ventilation in studies from Shanghai and Singapore. So the guard below is our own judgment, not a published setpoint:

  • Read outdoor dew point (or humidity ratio), not just dry bulb, and lock out the flush when it is too high. Our rule of thumb: keep it well below the coldest surface temperature the air will touch and below the indoor dew point you want to hold. ASHRAE Standard 55-2013 intends indoor dew points below 62°F, according to a Florida Solar Energy Center paper.
  • Watch rainy and foggy nights. A 59°F night at 94 percent humidity (below) is a bad flush even though it is cool.

Worked example: heat out, heat stored, kWh in and out

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption for illustration, not a measurement.

Assumptions: a 12,000 cfm air handler flushes 8 hours (10 pm to 6 am). Average return-to-outdoor temperature difference is 10°F. Exposed slab is 20,000 sq ft, 6 in thick, concrete at 150 lb/ft³ and 0.21 Btu/lb·°F. Fan power is 0.6 W per cfm at flush speed. Next-day cooling costs 1.2 kW per ton (12,000 Btu/h per ton).

Concrete values: a masonry paper gives normal-weight concrete as 130 to 150 lb/ft³ and 0.21 to 0.22 Btu/lb·°F (citing ACI 122). A thermal-properties chapter gives 0.22 to 0.24, and an MIT report uses about 0.24 at about 144 lb/ft³. We used 150 and 0.21.

  1. Heat the air can remove: 1.08 × 12,000 cfm × 10°F × 8 h = 1,036,800 Btu. (The 1.08 factor is the sensible heat constant covered in Psychrometrics for Technicians.)
  2. Slab mass: 20,000 sq ft × 0.5 ft × 150 lb/ft³ = 1,500,000 lb.
  3. Heat stored per degree: 1,500,000 lb × 0.21 = 315,000 Btu per °F. Cooling the slab 3°F (our assumption for what the air can reach) = 315,000 × 3 = 945,000 Btu. That is 91 percent of step 1, so the numbers hang together. A thick slab cools unevenly; the surface layer responds first.
  4. Compressor kWh avoided next day: 945,000 ÷ 12,000 = 78.75 ton-hours × 1.2 kW/ton = 94.5 kWh.
  5. Fan kWh spent: 12,000 cfm × 0.6 W/cfm = 7,200 W = 7.2 kW × 8 h = 57.6 kWh.
  6. Net: 94.5 − 57.6 = 36.9 kWh saved for the night, a ratio of about 1.6 to 1.

The fan is the swing factor. At 1.0 W/cfm the fan uses 96 kWh and the savings vanish. The Berkeley thesis noted a medium-weight office that used more energy with mechanical night ventilation because of fan operation, and the Northwest review cites an older study with no net savings for the same reason. Fan law savings from speed reduction are in Fan Laws.

Wet-air check (psychrometric table values). Take 15°C (59°F) night air. ASHRAE saturation pressures: 1.7055 kPa at 15°C, 1.2281 kPa at 10°C, 1.5987 kPa at 14°C, with total pressure 101.325 kPa. Humidity ratio W = 0.622 × pw ÷ (p − pw).

  • Dry night, dew point 10°C (50°F): W = 0.622 × 1.2281 ÷ 100.097 = 0.007631 lb/lb (about 53 grains/lb). RH = 1.2281 ÷ 1.7055 = 72 percent.
  • Muggy night, dew point 14°C (57°F): W = 0.622 × 1.5987 ÷ 99.726 = 0.009971 lb/lb (about 70 grains/lb). RH = 1.5987 ÷ 1.7055 = 94 percent.
  • Difference: 0.002340 lb/lb. Moisture = 4.5 × 12,000 × 0.002340 = 126 lb/h, or about 1,010 lb over 8 h. As latent heat: 4,840 × 12,000 × 0.002340 = about 136,000 Btu/h, or about 1.09 million Btu over the night.

That is more than our sensible benefit of 945,000 Btu. If even part of that moisture stays in the building, the cooling coil pays it back next day. Same dry bulb, opposite result.

Low limits, so the morning is not too cold

More cooling is not better. If the flush chills the space below where occupants are comfortable, the building may need reheat or complaints start at 8 am. The Northwest review puts the space lower limit at about 67°F and warns against overcooling. A reviewer there reported a large energy increase from night flushing in cold weather, and the method is for cooling only. Stop the flush at the low limit and skip the heating season. In ORNL's simulations, uncontrolled whole-house fans roughly doubled fan energy and added a heating penalty. The mixed-air low limit (freeze protection) is a separate setting; see Economizer Settings.

Tie-in with economizer controls

  • Enable: occupied schedule is off, space or return temperature is above the night low limit, outdoor temperature is a set amount below return (the difference test), and outdoor dew point is below the lockout.
  • Disable: space reaches the low limit, outdoor temperature rises or the difference closes, dew point rises, or morning start time arrives.
  • Hand-off: the normal economizer takes over at occupancy. Coordinate with optimal start.
  • Fan speed: if the drive allows, run lower speed. Fan power drops faster than airflow. The Northwest review says variable-speed drives may change the fan-energy picture.
  • Commission it. Check damper stroke, sensor accuracy and the schedule. See Economizers Basics for the daytime logic.

Field card: Night Flush and Night Purge Cooling

Cool air is not enough: check mass, dew point and fan kWh.

  • Best with heavy exposed mass and a night-to-day swing above about 25°F in dry air.
  • Lock out on outdoor dew point, not just dry bulb.
  • Fan kWh can erase the savings. Estimate both sides before enabling.
  • Set a space low limit near 67°F as a starting point and skip the heating season.
  • Confirm dampers, sensors and the night schedule after enabling.
