Sweating Ducts and Vents: Condensation in Attics and Basements

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your AC ducts are sweating, water is forming on a surface that is colder than the dew point of the air around it. That is the whole story. This lesson shows how to check whether your duct can sweat, what usually causes it, why wet insulation is a mold problem, and what a tech does to fix it for good.

Dew point in plain words

The National Weather Service describes dew point as the temperature to which a parcel of air, at constant pressure and moisture content, must be cooled to become saturated. Put simply, it is the temperature where the air can no longer hold its water as vapor. Touch that air to anything colder, and water appears as liquid. The NWS notes that summer dew points often run in the 60s and 70s, which it ties to very humid conditions. Our psychrometrics lesson covers the basics, and the humid-house lesson covers how humid air gets into a home in the first place.

Worked example: will this duct sweat?

Say a supply duct in a basement or attic has a metal surface at 55°F. That temperature is an assumption for the example, not a rule. The NWS heat index page gives a matched pair: air at 80°F with a 60°F dew point is 50 percent relative humidity. Use that as the room air around the duct.

  1. Surface temperature: 55°F.
  2. Dew point of the surrounding air: 60°F.
  3. Compare: 55 − 60 = −5°F. The surface is 5 degrees below the dew point.
  4. Result: water condenses on the duct. It sweats.

Notice that 50 percent humidity is considered fine by the EPA, which advises keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent and ideally between 30 and 50 percent. A bare cold duct can still sweat at a humidity level that feels normal. Now try the NWS "very humid" range. If the dew point were 70°F, the gap would be 70 − 55 = 15°F, and the sweating would be heavy. If the dew point were 50°F, then the duct is 55 − 50 = 5°F above the dew point, so it stays dry. The fix is never to chase the humidity alone. It is to keep the duct's outer surface warmer than the dew point, which is what insulation does.

Why ducts sweat

Missing or damaged insulation

Insulation keeps the outside skin of the duct close to the room air temperature. ENERGY STAR advises insulating all accessible ducts in attics, crawlspaces, unfinished basements and garages. A torn jacket, a gap at a joint, or a squashed section lets the cold metal show through, and that bare patch sweats first. EPA also says ducts in non-air-conditioned spaces should be sealed and insulated so condensation does not enter the system.

Air leaks at seams

ENERGY STAR says about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through a typical duct system is lost to leaks, holes and poor connections. Leaks also matter for sweating. Building Science Corporation's article "No Sweat" says duct leakage should be kept tight, because leakage can carry humid air to cold surfaces.

Humid attics, basements and crawlspaces

Building Science Corporation reports that in a vented attic, outside air enters at the soffits and leaves at the ridge, and that humid summer air is what causes the ducts to sweat. A basement can have the same problem when the air is damp. EPA suggests keeping indoor humidity between 30 and 50 percent, and our basement dehumidifier lesson covers one way to get there.

Sweating registers and vents

A register or grille is just another surface. If cold supply air chills it below the dew point of the room, it sweats. That points to either very humid room air or very cold supply air. Our humid-house lesson describes how airflow settings and oversized equipment affect both. If registers drip often, have a technician check airflow and humidity.

Why it is not just a mess

EPA says condensation can be a sign of high humidity, and that controlling moisture is the most effective way to keep mold from growing in air ducts. If duct insulation gets wet or moldy, EPA says it should be replaced, because it cannot be cleaned effectively. EPA also says that no antimicrobial products or biocides are approved by EPA for use on lined ductwork. Before paying for a cleaning, see Should You Get Your Air Ducts Cleaned?. A cleaning does not fix the moisture source.

What a homeowner can check

  • Look for drips, water stains, or dark spots on ceilings under the duct runs.
  • Look for a torn or missing outer jacket on duct insulation, or insulation that is damp or sagging.
  • Keep indoor humidity in the EPA's 30 to 50 percent range, and use a dehumidifier in a damp basement.
  • Do not wrap a duct with household tape or plastic. Call a technician. Attics are hot, tight and have live wiring, so do not crawl around in one without proper protection.

For technicians: fixing it right

Seal first, then insulate. A Building America guide from DOE says ducts should be thoroughly sealed before insulating, and that insulation will not reduce duct leakage. The same guide says conventional cloth “duct tape” is not acceptable for sealing, that UL 181A or 181B listed tapes are the acceptable tapes, and that many energy experts consider mastic superior to tape in most situations. DOE's mastic job aid applies mastic over all mesh tape and gaps, and on gaps from 1/4 to 3/4 inch it extends mastic at least 1 inch past the tape. ENERGY STAR also recommends mastic or metal tape and says duct tape is not long-lasting.

Vapor retarder facing goes outside. A NAIMA article reprinted by ACHR News says fibrous glass duct wrap comes factory-laminated to vapor retarder facing, and that the facing goes on the outside, away from the duct. Where a vapor retarder is required, the seam is sealed with pressure-sensitive tape, or with mastic and glass fiber fabric. The wrap should not be compressed below 75 percent of its nominal thickness, and the article warns that the R-values in the 1989 edition of ASHRAE 90.1 may not be enough for condensation control. For heavy-duty cases, follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Buried ducts and R-values. The Building Science Corporation attic newsletter lists R-13 duct insulation for climate zones 1A to 4A and R-8 elsewhere, with an outer layer under 1 perm, for ducts buried in attic insulation. PNNL's resource guide on encapsulated ducts calls for at least R-8 duct insulation with a vapor barrier cover. Check your local adopted energy code for the minimum that applies to your job.

Why it matters for study

Sweating duct calls combine heat transfer, psychrometrics and building science. Learn to name the dew point, compare it to the surface, and look for the cause: bare metal, leak, or humid air. Then you can explain the fix to a customer in one sentence. See also Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Field card: Sweating Ducts and Vents

Sweat means surface below dew point.

  • Compare duct surface temperature to the dew point of the air around it.
  • Example: 55°F surface vs 60°F dew point (80°F air, 50% RH) sweats.
  • Causes: bare or damaged insulation, leaky seams, humid attic or basement air.
  • Wet or moldy duct insulation should be replaced, per EPA.
  • Seal first with mastic or listed tape, then insulate with the vapor retarder facing out.
