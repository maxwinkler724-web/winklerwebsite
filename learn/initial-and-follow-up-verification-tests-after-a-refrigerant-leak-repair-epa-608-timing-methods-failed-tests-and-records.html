Initial and Follow-Up Verification Tests After a Refrigerant Leak Repair: EPA 608 Timing, Methods, Failed Tests and Records

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Fixing a leak is only half the job under the federal leak repair rules. The owner must also prove the fix worked, with two separate checks called the initial verification test and the follow-up verification test. This lesson covers what each one is, when each is due, what a failed test means and what gets written down.

Check the current rule text

Rules change. The leak repair requirements have been revised and extended over the years, and which appliances they reach depends on the refrigerant and the charge size. This page was built from eCFR and EPA pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal advice. Before you rely on any number here, read the current text of 40 CFR 82.157 and the definitions in 40 CFR 82.152, and the EPA page for the rule that covers your refrigerant.

Which appliances this applies to

Section 82.157(a) says the leak repair provisions apply only to appliances with a full charge of 50 or more pounds of any class I or class II refrigerant (ozone-depleting types), as of April 10, 2020, and not to appliances containing only substitute refrigerants. EPA's technician Q&A makes the same point: verification tests are not required for appliances under 50 pounds, for appliances that do not leak above the threshold, or for appliances holding substitutes such as HFCs.

That is not the whole picture. An EPA fact sheet dated January 2026 describes a newer rule under the AIM Act, with leak repair provisions at 40 CFR 84.106, that applies beginning January 1, 2026 to appliances with a charge of 15 pounds or more containing an HFC or certain HFC substitutes. It excludes residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump appliances. The fact sheet repeats the same two verification tests and the same 10, 20 and 30 percent thresholds. So the habit of testing twice is the same under both rules; the thresholds that trigger it come from whichever rule covers the charge in front of you. Start with the lesson on calculating the leak rate to see how the percentage is found. We do not repeat that math here.

What the two tests are

The definitions in 82.152 are short. An initial verification test is a leak test done after the repair is finished, to confirm the repair before refrigerant is added back. A follow-up verification test checks the repair after a successful initial test, once the appliance is back at normal operation. Normal operating characteristics and conditions are defined by the absence of atypical conditions affecting how the appliance runs. The rule in 82.157(e) says the owner or operator must do both tests on each leak that was repaired.

When each one is due

  • Initial test. Due within 30 days of the appliance exceeding its leak rate (120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required), unless more time is granted. If the repair did not need the appliance opened or evacuated, the test comes after the repair work and before any more refrigerant is added. If the repair needed evacuation, the test also comes before any refrigerant is added.
  • Follow-up test. Due within 10 days of the successful initial test, or, if the appliance was evacuated for the repair, within 10 days after it reaches normal operating characteristics and conditions. If testing under normal conditions is unsafe or impossible, the rule says to test before returning to those conditions where practicable.

Which methods are allowed

This is where students often expect more than the rule gives. The text of 82.157(e) does not name a particular method, such as a pressure check or a vacuum check, for either test. It describes what each test must show and when it happens, and the records paragraph asks for the type of test used. EPA's technician Q&A likewise lists no methods for verification tests, though it does say that standing pressure or vacuum decay checks show whether a leak exists but not where it is, so they do not count as the leak inspection that finds the leak. The lessons on nitrogen pressure testing and evacuation and deep vacuum cover how those checks are done on the bench; whether a given method satisfies the rule for a given appliance is a question for the current rule text and the owner's compliance plan.

What happens if a test fails

A failed initial test is not a violation by itself. Paragraph (e)(1)(iii) lets the owner make as many additional repairs and initial tests as needed inside the original time period. A failed follow-up test works the same way: further repairs and tests to get the leak rate below the threshold within the applicable period. EPA's leak repair page adds that an owner may be relieved of retrofit or retirement duties if a second repair of the same leaks succeeds, or if they determine within 180 days of the failed follow-up test that the rate is below the trigger rate, and they must notify EPA of how they will make that determination within 30 days of the failed test. If the appliance is still above the threshold, the owner is looking at a retrofit or retirement plan, due within 30 days and finished within one year per that page. See leaks and leak repair basics for the full picture of repair choices.

How it ties to thresholds and deadlines

The thresholds are 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, 20 percent for commercial refrigeration and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other appliances, measured over 12 months. Crossing one starts the 30-day repair clock (120 days with a required shutdown). The initial test must fit inside that clock. The follow-up test then runs on its own 10-day clock from the passed initial test or from normal operation. Extensions exist for specific reasons, such as unavailable parts, but they are requested and documented, so check the current text before counting on one.

Records to keep

Paragraph (l)(5) says to keep the dates and results of all initial and follow-up verification tests. The record includes the appliance location, the test dates, the locations of the repaired leaks that were tested, the test types and the results. Technicians must give the owner or operator documentation containing this when the service is complete. The retention period in the lead-in to paragraph (l) is at least three years, in paper or electronic form, unless otherwise specified. EPA's technician Q&A notes that technicians do not have to hand over the full-charge or leak-rate calculations, since owners keep those. See what technicians keep for the wider record set.

Worked example (our own, invented)

This example is our own and is made up. It is not a real site, appliance or record. Assumptions: a comfort cooling chiller with a full charge of 200 pounds of an ozone-depleting refrigerant (so the 50-pound rule applies); the owner's calculation when refrigerant was added gave a leak rate over the 10 percent comfort cooling threshold; the leak was a failed flare-type fitting that is replaced after the section is isolated and recovered, so the repair needs evacuation; day 0 is the date of that refrigerant addition. We count days simply here, so confirm how the rule counts days.

  1. Day 0. Leak rate calculated over 10 percent. The 30-day repair window opens and ends on day 30.
  2. Day 9. Fitting replaced and the repair finished.
  3. Day 10. Initial verification test, done before any refrigerant is added back. It passes. Because the unit was evacuated, the follow-up clock waits for normal operation.
  4. Day 12. Refrigerant charged and the chiller returned to service.
  5. Day 13. Unit reaches normal operating characteristics and conditions. The 10-day follow-up window runs through day 23.
  6. Day 18. Follow-up verification test at normal conditions. It passes.

Record entries for this made-up job: appliance location (invented: Plant room B, Unit 2); initial test dated day 10, repaired leak location is the replaced fitting, test type and result pass; follow-up test dated day 18 at normal conditions, same leak location, test type and result pass. We deliberately wrote no test type here because the rule leaves the method to current text and the owner's plan.

Field card: verification tests after a leak repair

Test after the repair, test again at normal operation, write both down.

  • Applies to covered appliances that exceeded the leak rate; check which rule covers your refrigerant and charge.
  • Initial test: after repair, before refrigerant is added back, inside the 30-day window (120 with a required shutdown).
  • Follow-up test: within 10 days of a passed initial test, or of normal operating conditions after evacuation.
  • Failed test: more repairs and retests inside the period; still leaking means a retrofit or retirement plan.
  • Record dates, locations of repaired leaks, test types and results; keep at least three years.
  • Thresholds: 10 percent comfort cooling, 20 percent commercial refrigeration, 30 percent industrial process.
