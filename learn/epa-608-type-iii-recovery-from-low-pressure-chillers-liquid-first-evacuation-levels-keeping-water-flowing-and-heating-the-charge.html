EPA 608 Type III Recovery From Low-Pressure Chillers: Liquid First, Evacuation Levels, Keeping Water Flowing and Heating the Charge

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Recovering refrigerant from a low-pressure chiller is a different job from pulling a charge out of a split system. The charge is large, the shell is thin, and the tubes are full of water that can freeze. This lesson walks through the order of the job and the Type III recovery topics EPA lists for the exam.

Check the current rule text

Check the current rule text. This is general study help built from the eCFR and EPA pages read in 2026. It is not a procedure for your machine. Rules are amended, so confirm every number against the current rule text, and let the chiller maker's literature and your site rules come first.

Which machines this covers

The federal definition in 40 CFR 82.152 calls an appliance low-pressure when its refrigerant has a liquid phase saturation pressure below 45 psia at 104 °F. That is the group a Type III exam focuses on. For how the three certification types divide up, see Type I, II and III Explained. For why these shells run below atmospheric pressure and need a purge unit, see Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables. We do not repeat either one here.

Liquid first, then vapor

EPA's Type III test topics under Recovery Techniques say that recovering liquid at the beginning of the recovery process speeds it up, and that you still need to recover vapor in addition to the liquid. Both halves matter. The rule itself, 40 CFR 82.156(a), says technicians must evacuate the refrigerant "including all the liquid refrigerant" to the levels in Table 1.

Here is why the order makes sense, in our own words. A recovery machine moves a lot more mass per minute when it is pulling dense liquid than when it is pulling thin vapor. A big chiller holds most of its charge as liquid in the bottom of the shell, so emptying that liquid first removes most of the pounds quickly. Once the liquid is out, only vapor and a little remaining liquid are left. The machine then runs on vapor to bring the shell down to the required level.

Whether a particular chiller has its liquid connection on the evaporator, the condenser or a storage vessel depends on the design, so use the maker's recovery hookup instructions for yours. The sketch only shows the idea: liquid low, vapor high.

The evacuation level in the rule

We opened Table 1 in 40 CFR 82.156(a) on the eCFR. For a low-pressure appliance, the table lists 25 mm Hg absolute, and it lists the same value for equipment made before and on or after November 15, 1993. Table 2 in 40 CFR 82.158, the equipment table, also lists 25 mm Hg absolute for low-pressure appliances in both columns. The other rows in Table 1 are written differently, in inches of mercury vacuum relative to standard atmospheric pressure (the table header gives 29.9 inches Hg). For comparison, the post-1993 values are 0 in. Hg for very high-pressure appliances and for high-pressure appliances under 200 lb, 10 in. Hg for high-pressure appliances of 200 lb or more and for medium-pressure appliances under 200 lb, and 15 in. Hg for medium-pressure appliances of 200 lb or more. Check the current rule text before you rely on any of these.

The rule also covers a different job: a non-major repair where the appliance will not be vented afterward. For a low-pressure appliance, 82.156(a)(1)(ii) says it must be pressurized to a pressure no higher than 0 psig before it is opened. If the refrigerant's boiling point is above 85 °F, heat is used first to raise the pressure as much as possible, and nitrogen may then be used to reach atmospheric pressure. For a refrigerant with a boiling point at or below 85 °F, the rule says not to use methods such as nitrogen that require subsequent purging. Do not confuse that opening pressure with the recovery level above. The leak-test side of this is in Type III Low-Pressure Chiller Leak Testing.

If leaks make the Table 1 level unattainable, 82.156(a)(2) lets the technician isolate the leaking parts, evacuate the rest to Table 1, and take the leaking parts as low as can be reached, not above 0 psig. The rule says you must verify the level before opening the appliance.

Worked example (our own): what 25 mm Hg absolute means

This is our own example. We use the rule's 25 mm Hg absolute and the conversion 29.92 in. Hg = 760 mm Hg (standard atmosphere). The rule's table header rounds atmosphere to 29.9, so a result written that way may differ in the last digit.

  1. Inches of mercury absolute: 25 mm Hg ÷ (760 mm Hg ÷ 29.92 in. Hg) = 25 ÷ 25.40 = about 0.98 in. Hg absolute.
  2. Vacuum, which is measured down from atmosphere: 29.92 − 0.98 = about 28.94 in. Hg vacuum (about 28.9 with the rule's 29.9).
  3. Compare with atmosphere: 25 ÷ 760 = 0.0329, so the shell is at about 3.3 percent of atmospheric pressure, or about 96.7 percent of the way to a perfect vacuum. In psi, 14.696 × 25 ÷ 760 = about 0.48 psia.
  4. Compare with the other rows: 28.94 in. Hg vacuum is far deeper than the 10 or 15 in. Hg vacuum levels for the medium- and high-pressure rows. A lower absolute number means a deeper vacuum, so 25 mm Hg is a harder target than any inches-of-vacuum row.

Time saved by taking liquid first. Assumptions (ours, not from any source): 800 lb of liquid, a liquid recovery rate of 10 lb/min, and a vapor-only rate of 1.5 lb/min. Real rates depend on the machine, hose size, cylinder temperature and the refrigerant, and the equipment rules only require that the liquid and vapor rates be measured (82.158(d)(8)); they give no number.

  1. As liquid: 800 lb ÷ 10 lb/min = 80 min, about 1 hour 20 minutes.
  2. As vapor only: 800 lb ÷ 1.5 lb/min = 533 min, about 8 hours 53 minutes.
  3. Time saved: 533 − 80 = about 453 min, roughly 7 hours 33 minutes. The liquid route is about 6.7 times faster.

The vapor-only figure is, if anything, optimistic, because boiling that much liquid off cools the shell and slows the process. Either way the direction is the same: liquid first is much faster.

Why the water must keep moving

EPA's Type III topics include the need to circulate or remove water from the chiller during refrigerant evacuation to prevent freezing. The eCFR text of 82.156 has no wording on water or freezing, so this is an exam topic and a practice point, not a rule number. Our explanation: as recovery lowers the shell pressure, the remaining liquid boils at a lower and lower temperature, and the boiling pulls heat from the tubes. Water that sits still in a chilled tube can reach freezing, and ice can split a tube and push water into the refrigerant. Flowing water carries heat in, or you remove the water altogether. We did not find a temperature figure in the sources we opened, so check the maker's instructions. The related freeze-up lesson is Chiller Evaporator Freeze-Up.

Heating the charge to speed things up

Liquid boils faster when it has heat to boil with, and a cold shell is slow. Warm water in the tubes gives the refrigerant heat, which helps in two ways at once: it keeps the tubes above freezing and it keeps the vapor coming. That is our explanation. The EPA topics page does not list "heating the charge" for recovery. It does list heating the oil to 130 °F before removing it to reduce refrigerant release, and the rule uses heat to raise pressure before opening, as above. If the machine has a built-in heating device, the maker's instructions say how to use it.

High-pressure cut-outs on the recovery unit

EPA lists the high-pressure cut-out level of recovery devices used with low-pressure appliances as a Type III topic, but the page gives no number, and we did not find one in 82.156 or 82.158. So we teach no value here. What we can say is the purpose: a cut-out shuts the recovery unit off if its discharge pressure climbs too high, which protects the unit and the cylinder. Never bypass it. Equipment has to be certified under 82.158, and the unit's manual gives its setting. For machine care and other speed factors, see Refrigerant Recovery Machine Care and Faster Recovery.

What the exam usually asks

  • Is liquid or vapor recovered first, and why (liquid, because it is faster)?
  • Do you still need vapor recovery after the liquid is out (yes)?
  • What evacuation level applies to a low-pressure appliance (25 mm Hg absolute in the table we read)?
  • Why circulate or remove water during evacuation (to prevent freezing)?
  • What highest pressure is allowed before opening a low-pressure appliance for a non-major repair (0 psig, per 82.156(a)(1)(ii))?
  • Does the answer change for equipment built before versus after November 15, 1993 (the table values for low-pressure are the same)?

These are our reading of EPA's published topic list, not a list of actual questions.

Field card: Type III recovery from low-pressure chillers

Liquid first, vapor next, water moving the whole time.

  • Take liquid first because it is much faster, then recover the vapor.
  • Rule table value for low-pressure appliances: 25 mm Hg absolute, same before and after Nov. 15, 1993.
  • 25 mm Hg absolute is about 28.9 in. Hg vacuum, about 3.3 percent of atmosphere (our conversion).
  • Keep water circulating or remove it, so the tubes do not freeze.
  • Opening a low-pressure appliance for a non-major repair: no higher than 0 psig.
  • Never bypass the recovery unit's high-pressure cut-out.
  • Check the current rule text and the maker's instructions.
