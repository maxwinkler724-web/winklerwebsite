Centrifugal Chiller Surge: Lift, Low Load, Guide Vanes and VFDs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A centrifugal compressor does not squeeze gas the way a piston does. It throws gas outward and turns speed into pressure. When the pressure it must make gets too high for the flow it is moving, the gas can briefly run backward. That is surge. This lesson explains lift, why hot condenser water and light loads push a machine toward surge, and what the makers say about guide vanes, drives and hot gas bypass.

What surge is

Daikin Applied's Centrifugal Chiller Fundamentals application guide describes surge as a repeating cycle: refrigerant flows backward through the compressor wheel every few seconds until pressure builds up and the gas moves forward again. The guide adds that surge is more damaging than a stall because it reverse loads the thrust bearings on the compressor shaft. The same guide says a properly selected chiller will not surge at the conditions it was designed for, and that Daikin controllers carry software that protects the compressor from stalls and surges. Surge is a limit of the compressor, not a refrigerant leak or a bad part.

Lift: the number behind it

Lift is the pressure rise the compressor must produce, from evaporator pressure up to condenser pressure. An ACHR News article by Chris Miller, P.E., defines it as the pressure difference, or the temperature difference, of the refrigerant. In the field, Trane's Engineers Newsletter says the colder the evaporator refrigerant and the warmer the condenser refrigerant, the higher the lift, and that you can approximate it as the leaving condenser water temperature minus the leaving chilled water temperature. Daikin's guide puts a pressure on it for one design: a required lift of 81.0 psig for R-134a. Your machine's nameplate and submittals set your own numbers.

Because lift is the thing the wheel has to deliver, anything that raises it moves the machine toward surge. Trane's catalog says reducing the load or increasing the head conditions will cause the compressor to move toward a surge condition.

What pushes a chiller toward surge

  • Warm condenser water. Daikin's guide says raising the tower water supply temperature can lead to surge. Carrier gives an illustration in a comparison paper: a rise in entering condenser water from 85 F to 88 F is shown tipping a centrifugal chiller into surge. That is the maker's example, not a rule for every machine.
  • Very cold chilled water. The Daikin guide also lists lowering the chilled water supply temperature beyond design points.
  • Low load. Daikin says centrifugal chillers are vulnerable at part load because, with less flow, the discharge area is too large for the reduced flow. Miller's article puts a figure on it: chillers below about 30 percent of capacity may hunt, cycle and surge, while at 30 percent or higher a chiller may never approach surge under normal conditions. He also points out that an oversized chiller cannot turn down and may surge.
  • Fouling and non-condensables. The maker pages we read do not call these surge causes by name. A YORK operating manual says trouble from fouled tubes usually shows as a steady rise in head pressure, and that foul gas buildup can cause similar symptoms and is removed by purging. Our own reasoning: higher head pressure is higher lift, so the same pull toward surge applies. See the lessons on tube cleaning and purge units.

Worked example: what a 5 F tower change does to lift

These approach values are examples we chose, not nameplate data. Use your own submittal values on a real machine.

Case A: 85 F entering condenser water. (Our own arithmetic.)

  1. Saturated evaporating temperature = leaving chilled water 44 F minus evaporator approach 2 F = 42 F.
  2. Leaving condenser water = entering 85 F plus a 10 F range = 95 F.
  3. Saturated condensing temperature = leaving condenser water 95 F plus condenser approach 2 F = 97 F.
  4. Temperature lift = 97 F minus 42 F = 55 F.

Case B: 90 F entering condenser water, same range and approach. (Our own arithmetic.)

  1. Evaporating temperature is unchanged at 42 F.
  2. Leaving condenser water = 90 F + 10 F = 100 F.
  3. Condensing temperature = 100 F + 2 F = 102 F.
  4. Lift = 102 F minus 42 F = 60 F.
  5. Change = 60 minus 55 = 5 F, and 5 divided by 55 = 0.0909, so lift rises about 9.1 percent.

Our own reading: every degree the tower water rises adds a degree of lift here, because the evaporator side did not move.

How the makers extend the range

Inlet guide vanes. YORK says its prerotation vanes modulate capacity from 100 percent down to as low as 15 percent of design. Trane says vanes throttle the gas to match part-load needs. Daikin presents vanes as a capacity control method, not a surge cure. Miller adds that repeated vane movement from fast-changing loads can cause premature wear, so slow load changes are better.

Variable speed. Daikin's guide explains that lowering compressor speed lowers the lift the wheel can make, so speed control only works when lift is also low enough. Trane's catalog says its drive controller reduces speed until a surge boundary is reached and that vanes and speed modulate together inside that boundary. A YORK variable-speed manual says speed is kept above the minimum needed for the current head. A paper by Joost Brasz of Danfoss Turbocor, presented at the Purdue compressor conference, notes that even a variable-speed compressor will surge if forced to deliver low capacity against head, and argues for a different stabilizing method than guide vanes. That is the author's position, offered for context.

Hot gas bypass. A YORK manual says optional hot gas bypass is used to eliminate compressor surge during light load or high head operation. Carrier's controls manual says the controller energizes the hot gas relay in low load, high lift conditions. Daikin warns that hot gas bypass is inefficient and should be avoided whenever possible, and Trane says its multistage design virtually eliminates the need for it. Adjustment belongs to a qualified service technician, per YORK.

What surge looks and sounds like on the machine

The maker documents we read are thin on sound. What they do give is how controls notice it. A Trane manual says its surge protection is based on motor current fluctuation, enabled by default, and shows a message that the unit is running in a surge condition. Carrier's controls list surge prevention and surge protection routines, with an alert history entry for surge prevention. Our own plain advice: pair the alarm history with your log sheet, watch for motor current that swings in a repeating pattern, and ask a senior tech to walk you through it live. We could not source a description of the actual sound from a maker or public page, so we leave it out here.

On damage, Daikin names the thrust bearing. Carrier's paper says repeated surging can hurt maintenance and the life of the machine. Carrier also notes that technicians sometimes soften the controller's response to repeated surge, which costs efficiency.

Field responses

  • Check condenser water temperature. Daikin calls lowering condenser water temperature the most common way to reduce lift. See tower fan staging and condenser water reset.
  • Check condenser water flow, but do not cut it blindly. Trane's newsletter warns against reducing condenser flow at points that cause surge. Miller says temperature and flow both change lift.
  • Confirm the tower is working. Miller suggests verifying tower selection on design wet-bulb days with about 3 F of margin for fouling.
  • Look at head pressure trends, purge activity and tube condition, using the YORK guidance above.
  • Work within the protections. Our own advice: follow the maker's start-up and safety procedures; see chiller start-up, shutdown and safeties. For the compressor types themselves, see centrifugal, screw and scroll compared.

Field card: Centrifugal Chiller Surge

Lift up, flow down: that is the road to surge.

  • Surge: gas flows backward through the wheel in a repeating cycle (Daikin).
  • Lift is condensing minus evaporating, or leaving condenser water minus leaving chilled water as a field estimate (Trane).
  • Warm tower water, very cold chilled water and low load raise surge risk (Daikin, ACHR).
  • Example (our own): 85 F to 90 F entering condenser water takes lift from 55 F to 60 F, about 9.1 percent.
  • Vanes, speed control and hot gas bypass widen the range; each has limits.
  • Check tower, condenser flow, head pressure trends, purge and tubes.
