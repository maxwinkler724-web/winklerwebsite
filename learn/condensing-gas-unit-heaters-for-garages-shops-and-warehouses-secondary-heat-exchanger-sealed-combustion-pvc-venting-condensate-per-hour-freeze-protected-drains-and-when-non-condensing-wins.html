Condensing Gas Unit Heaters for Garages, Shops and Warehouses

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A standard gas unit heater sends its flue gas up a chimney still hot, and with it a lot of heat. A condensing unit heater adds a second heat exchanger to catch most of that heat, and the price of catching it is water: a steady trickle of acidic condensate that has to go somewhere that does not freeze. This lesson explains how that works in a garage, shop or warehouse and when the simpler unit is the better choice.

How the secondary heat exchanger works

Natural gas is mostly methane. A university combustion chapter gives the reaction as one methane molecule plus two oxygen molecules making carbon dioxide and two water molecules, which works out to 2.25 pounds of water for every pound of methane burned. That water leaves as vapor, and the vapor carries latent heat, the heat that was needed to turn liquid into gas. The same chapter puts the flue gas dew point for natural gas at about 124.6 °F with dry combustion air. If the gas is cooled below that temperature, the vapor turns back to liquid and hands its latent heat to whatever is cooling it.

In a condensing unit heater, the flue gas first passes through the primary exchanger, where it is hot and stays a gas. It then enters a cooler secondary exchanger, where the metal is below the dew point. Water condenses on the surface and runs to a drain. A federal field report on high-efficiency gas heaters lists condensing heat exchangers at above 90 percent thermal efficiency, against about 78 to 82 percent for conventional gravity-vented units. For a closer look at the heating-value math, see Higher vs Lower Heating Value.

Why low return air temperature helps

This part is our own reasoning from the dew point. The secondary exchanger can only condense water if its surface is cooler than the dew point. The air it is heating is the coolant. In a cold shop the air entering the unit may be 40 or 50 °F, far below 125 °F, so the surfaces run cool and a large share of the vapor condenses. In a warm space the entering air is warmer, the surfaces run warmer, and less condenses. The university chapter makes the same point from the other side: the flue products must stay warm enough to heat the return air, and the lowest practical product temperature it gives is about 90 to 100 °F. A unit heater that mostly re-heats cold room air is close to an ideal condensing application. A unit that sees warm return air will condense less and save less.

Sealed combustion and two-pipe plastic venting

Many condensing units are sealed combustion, also called separated combustion. One pipe brings outdoor air to the burner and a second pipe carries the flue gas out, so the burner does not use shop air. That helps in a garage or shop where dust, solvent vapor or a tight building would trouble an open burner. Because the flue gas leaves cool, plastic pipe is used. A federal measure guideline on high-efficiency furnaces says PVC, CPVC and ABS are generally approved for this kind of venting, that schedule 40 PVC is generally recommended, and that vent piping should slope back toward the unit and its condensate drain at one-quarter inch per foot. Pipe type, length, number of elbows and termination spacing differ by model, so we leave those to the maker's installation manual and the local code. Vehicle bays have additional vapor and exhaust rules, covered in Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC.

Worked example (our own): how much condensate?

This is our own example with assumed inputs. We assume a unit with an input of 100,000 Btu/h on natural gas, and that half of the water vapor condenses (50 percent, an assumption, because real units vary with return air temperature and firing rate). We assume it fires at full rate for 10 hours a day. We use the university chapter's 2.25 pounds of water per pound of methane and 23,857 Btu per pound for methane's higher heating value, and a federal energy agency's 1,036 Btu per cubic foot for natural gas. We treat the gas as pure methane, which is a simplification.

  1. Gas burned: 100,000 ÷ 1,036 = 96.5 cubic feet per hour.
  2. Fuel mass: 100,000 ÷ 23,857 = 4.19 pounds of methane per hour.
  3. Water if all of it condensed: 4.19 × 2.25 = 9.43 pounds per hour. Per cubic foot of gas that is 9.43 ÷ 96.5 = 0.098 pound, about 0.1 pound.
  4. Water actually condensed at 50 percent: 9.43 × 0.5 = 4.72 pounds per hour.
  5. Convert to gallons at 8.34 pounds per gallon: 4.72 ÷ 8.34 = 0.565 gallon per hour.
  6. Per day at 10 hours: 0.565 × 10 = about 5.7 gallons per day.

Check: 9.43 pounds of water times about 1,050 Btu per pound (the chapter's heat of vaporization) is about 9,900 Btu/h, or 9.9 percent of the input. Half of that, near 4,950 Btu/h, is the heat the secondary exchanger recovers from the water alone. About 0.57 gallon per hour is a trickle, which is why a drain line fails from freezing or blockage rather than from volume. The wider gallons-per-day picture is in Condensate Quantities: Furnace, Boiler and AC Coil Gallons per Day.

Why the drain freezes in a cold garage

Condensate is nearly plain water with a little dissolved acid, so it freezes close to 32 °F. In a heated house the trap and drain stay warm. A unit heater often hangs in a space that is held only just above freezing, or that is not heated at all when the heater is off, and the drain pipe may run through an unheated wall or outdoors. A trap holds a small plug of water, and a trickle of 0.5 gallon an hour leaves little heat to keep a pipe open. When the unit stops, the water in the trap and low spots can freeze, block the line, and then the secondary exchanger fills and a pressure or water-level safety shuts the unit down on a cold night, which is when you need it.

The federal measure guideline recommends short condensate lines ending in conditioned space, with insulation and heat tape on the trap and lines where a furnace sits outside conditioned space in a cold climate. It also notes that heat tape can use 40 to 100 W around the clock and recommends a controller that turns it off above 40 °F. It says that in cold climates condensate should be drained into the sanitary drain system and never outdoors, and that local codes may add limits. Good routing, in our words: slope the line continuously downhill, keep it as short as you can, avoid outside walls and unheated ceilings, and end it inside a heated space. Where that is not possible, a condensate pump in a heated spot is one option. Check the pump and any freeze-protection devices against the maker's instructions and the local code.

Neutralizers

A national laboratory page says furnace flue condensate is acidic with a pH near 3, more acidic than typical acid rain. Many places want that water raised toward neutral before it enters a drain, so units are often fitted with a neutralizer: a small container of media the condensate trickles through. The media is used up over time, and the more gallons that pass, the sooner it is spent. At our example 5.7 gallons per day, a 120-day heating season passes about 680 gallons through it, so ask the maker how many gallons their media handles and plan to check it at least yearly. Whether a neutralizer is required depends on the local rules, so confirm them. See Condensate Neutralizers for details.

Mounting height and throw

Hot air is lighter than cold air, so a heater hung too high lets warm air collect at the ceiling while the floor stays cold. A university extension guide on greenhouse heating says unit heaters should be spaced and directed to blanket the whole area, with each unit blowing toward the next so the air circulates. Exact mounting heights, clearances to combustibles and throw distances depend on the model and its airflow, so use the maker's installation manual and the local code instead of rules of thumb. Our own practical point: in a tall warehouse, a secondary exchanger gains nothing if the warm air never reaches the people working below it. The companion lesson, Garage and Workshop Heaters, covers sizing and placement more broadly.

When a non-condensing unit heater is the better choice

The federal field report says milder climates may not burn enough gas for the extra efficiency to pay off, and that condensing equipment can need complicated condensate drainage. From that and the lesson above, our judgment is that a non-condensing unit heater is often better when:

  • there is no practical way to route the drain to a heated space or a sanitary drain;
  • the heater runs only a few hours a week, so little water and little savings are involved;
  • the space is warm enough that return air stays above the dew point much of the time;
  • nobody will do the yearly drain and neutralizer checks.

A condensing unit makes the most sense in a large space that is heated for many hours in cold weather, with a drain location that stays above freezing.

Yearly service checks

These are our suggested checks. Follow the maker's service instructions first, and shut off gas and power before opening anything.

  • Clear and flush the trap and drain line. Slime, sagging pipe and kinks block a drain more than volume does.
  • Check the neutralizer media and replace it as the maker directs.
  • Look at the vent and intake pipes for leaks, sags, slope back to the unit, and blocked terminations from snow, ice, nests or stored items. See High-Efficiency Furnace Vent Pipes Blocked by Snow or Ice for the furnace version of this problem.
  • Inspect the secondary exchanger and the burner for dirt, corrosion and cracks, and test the pressure switches and water-level safeties as the maker instructs.
  • Check the heat tape or freeze protection before the first cold snap, if any is fitted.
  • Test combustion with a calibrated analyzer and confirm the unit starts and runs cleanly.

Carbon monoxide

A federal worker safety agency fact sheet lists fuel-burning equipment among the sources of carbon monoxide and says it must be kept in good working order, with effective ventilation and a regular check of workplace air. It lists early symptoms as headache, fatigue, dizziness, drowsiness, nausea and chest tightness. A blocked vent or intake, a cracked exchanger or a frozen drain that makes a unit cycle badly are all reasons to treat a unit as suspect until a qualified person checks it. Alarms are a backup, not a substitute for service.

Field card: Condensing Gas Unit Heaters

A second exchanger turns flue gas water into heat, and into a drain job.

  • Natural gas makes about 2.25 lb of water per lb of methane burned; the dew point is near 125 °F.
  • Our example: 100,000 Btu/h, 50 percent condensed, 10 h/day gives about 0.57 gal/h and 5.7 gal/day.
  • Cooler return air means more condensing.
  • Sealed combustion uses one pipe in and one out, in plastic pipe slope back to the unit.
  • Condensate pH is near 3. Neutralize where required.
  • Drain to a heated space or sanitary drain, short and sloped, never outdoors in cold climates.
  • Check trap, neutralizer, vent ends and exchangers each year.
