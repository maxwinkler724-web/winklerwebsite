Sidewall Vent Terminations: Clearances From Windows, Meters and Snow

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condensing furnace or water heater usually sends its exhaust straight out the wall through plastic pipe. Where that pipe ends is not a matter of taste. This lesson explains what a sidewall termination is, the kinds of distances a maker's manual sets around it, where makers differ, and how to check a proposed spot one distance at a time.

The manual and the code decide

The manual and the code decide. This is general education, not a ruling on any job. The numbers below come from the specific manuals named in the text. Your appliance, your fuel, your input rating and your town may call for something else. If a code number and a manual number differ, ask the code official which one governs.

What a sidewall termination is

A sidewall (direct vent) termination is where the exhaust pipe, and usually a combustion air intake pipe, pass through an outside wall instead of the roof. The vent is sealed from the room, so the appliance takes in outdoor air and sends exhaust outdoors. For the category rules behind this, see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained, and for pipe materials, Plastic Vent Pipe for Furnaces and Boilers.

There are two layouts. A two-pipe kit uses separate exhaust and intake pipes. Goodman's installation instructions for its CVC96 and MVC96 furnaces (IOG-2008, 08/14) show the pipes 10 inches minimum and 24 inches maximum center to center. Lennox's EL296UHV instructions (507264-05, 07/2023) call for intake and exhaust to sit close together, no more than 6 inches apart on a side wall, so the exhaust does not blow across the intake. A concentric kit puts the intake pipe around the exhaust pipe in one fitting and needs only one hole. Lennox counts a concentric termination as the equivalent of 5 feet of pipe when you add up vent length.

Why the plume and frost matter

Exhaust from a condensing appliance is cool, wet and visible. A.O. Smith's water heater instruction manual (100348176) warns against venting into dead spaces such as alleys, atriums and inside corners, because flue gas can recirculate, and says to take special care near walkways, windows and building entrances. Goodman says a termination must not sit over a public walkway or where condensate or vapor could be a nuisance or hazard, or could harm regulators and relief valves. Trane's concentric kit guide (18-CH34D1-8B, 06/2024) says not to stack kits one above the other, to prevent condensate freeze-up, and suggests a 24 inch square corrosion shield behind the terminal if flue moisture can reach the wall. Frost and ice on the pipe ends are a separate risk; see High-Efficiency Furnace Vent Pipes Blocked by Snow or Ice.

The clearance categories

Manuals group the distances in a lettered table or figure. The usual categories:

  • Grade and snow. A minimum height above the ground and above the expected snow line.
  • Operable windows and doors. A horizontal or vertical gap, sometimes stepped by appliance input.
  • Meter, regulator and regulator vent. A gas-side clearance, because the regulator vent can release gas.
  • Mechanical air intakes. Fresh-air or ventilation intakes, usually a rule about being above them.
  • Corners, soffits and overhangs. Inside corners trap exhaust; outside corners rarely matter.
  • Decks, porches and balconies. Above them, or under them with conditions.
  • Walkways. Public sidewalks and driveways, mainly.

The gas side is covered in Gas Meter and Regulator Basics. The utility may set its own distance too. South Jersey Gas's meter handout (GD-01, 10/26/17) lists 3 feet, measured radially, from a regulator relief vent to a furnace air intake or exhaust, and 10 feet to a mechanical building air intake.

Code in New Jersey

New Jersey's Department of Community Affairs lists the International Fuel Gas Code under the Fuel Gas Subcode (N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.22) and the International Residential Code, New Jersey edition, for one- and two-family dwellings (N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.21); its page showed 2024 editions at the time we read it. That page does not name NFPA 54. The makers' manuals do: Rinnai and Lennox cite ANSI Z223.1/NFPA 54, the National Fuel Gas Code, for clearances their tables do not list, and say to follow local code and the gas supplier for the rest. Ask your local code official which edition applies on the day of your job.

Worked example: one wall, eight checks

Invented house. Back wall, gas furnace, two-pipe sidewall kit, pipes about 3 inches across with the elbow. Rule set: Lennox EL296UHV Figure 32, U.S. column, as read in the 07/2023 form. Proposed spot: bottom of the lowest pipe opening 30 inches above grade. Expected snow depth on this side: 24 inches.

  1. Snow and grade. Rule: 12 inches above grade or 12 inches above average snow. Snow is 24 inches, so 24 + 12 = 36 inches. Grade alone needs only 12. The larger is 36. Proposed: 30. 30 < 36, short by 6 inches. Fail.
  2. Window. The operable window is 5 feet = 60 inches from the nearest pipe. Our own check: the window tiers did not come through clearly in the copies we read, and the largest window figure we could read in the Lennox row is 36 inches. 60 ≥ 36, a margin of 24 inches. Pass at any input tier.
  3. Meter. Rule: 3 feet each side of the meter/regulator center line extended upward, within 15 feet above. The meter top is 24 inches high; the terminal bottom is 30 inches, so it is above the meter. Horizontal offset: 2 feet = 24 inches. Limit: 3 feet = 36 inches. 24 < 36, short by 12 inches. Fail.
  4. Regulator vent outlet. Rule: 3 feet. Measured straight line: 5 feet. 5 ≥ 3, margin 2 feet. Pass. The utility may ask more (SJG: 3 feet radially to a furnace exhaust), and 5 passes that as well.
  5. Mechanical air inlet. A fresh-air intake is 14 feet away horizontally. The rule applies only within 10 feet. 14 > 10, so it is not triggered. Pass.
  6. Outside corner. 6 feet away; Lennox lists no minimum to an outside corner. Pass.
  7. Deck. Two parts. Height: the deck underside is 8 ft = 96 inches; the pipe top is 30 + 3 = 33 inches; gap 96 − 33 = 63 inches against 12. 63 ≥ 12. Opening: the rule allows an under-deck termination only if the deck is open on at least two sides beneath the floor. This deck has lattice on three sides, so only one is open. 1 < 2. Fail. Lennox also says to avoid the spot if possible.
  8. Public sidewalk. The street sidewalk is 40 feet away. The 7 foot rule is a height above a public sidewalk the terminal is over; it is not over one. Not triggered. Pass.

Result: three fails (snow, meter, deck) and five passes. Our own suggested fix for all three, with margin we chose: move the terminal out from under the deck, put it at least 4 feet (48 inches) sideways from the meter center line (48 ≥ 36), and raise the bottom of the pipes to 42 inches (42 ≥ 36). Then recheck every row, including the window distance from the new spot, against your model's manual.

Field card: Sidewall Vent Terminations: Clearances

Check each distance, then the manual, then the code.

  • Sidewall termination: sealed vent through an outside wall; two-pipe or concentric kit.
  • Grade and snow: the makers we read say 12 inches above the higher of grade or anticipated snow.
  • Windows, meters, air inlets, decks and walkways each get their own distance in the maker's table.
  • Makers differ and some leave rows blank; blank means look elsewhere, not no rule.
  • Do not stack kits; frost and plume reach what is above.
  • The utility may add its own regulator vent distance.
  • New Jersey: fuel gas subcode and IRC; makers cite NFPA 54. Local code official has the last word.
