Heat Pump Ideal COP and Temperature Lift: Why Every Degree Costs Efficiency

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A heat pump does not make heat, it lifts heat from a colder place to a warmer one. The bigger that lift, the more work the compressor has to do. Physics sets a ceiling on how well any heat pump can do, and that ceiling falls every time the outdoor air gets colder or the supply gets hotter. This lesson shows the ceiling, the math, and what a tech or designer can do to keep the lift small.

The ceiling: ideal heating COP

Coefficient of performance (COP) is heat delivered divided by electric energy used. For the best possible machine operating between two fixed temperatures, the heating COP is the hot temperature divided by the difference between hot and cold, with both temperatures on an absolute scale. A university physics reference on heat pumps says the same thing in words: the theoretical maximum comes from the Carnot cycle, and the temperatures must be in kelvins. In Fahrenheit work the absolute scale is Rankine, which is degrees F plus 459.67.

Ideal heating COP = Thot / (Thot − Tcold), where the denominator is the lift. A federal energy department technical brief on industrial heat pumps gives the same relation, with temperatures in Rankine, and notes that the savings advantage of a heat pump erodes as the lift grows.

Notice what the formula says. Shrink the lift and the COP climbs fast. If the lift went to zero, the ideal COP would be unlimited, which is why a tiny lift is so valuable. Raise the lift and the ceiling drops, no matter how good the equipment is.

Why real machines reach only a fraction

The ideal machine has no pressure drops, no temperature differences across the coils, and a perfect compressor. A real unit needs its evaporator colder than the outdoor air, so heat can flow in, and its condenser hotter than the supply, so heat can flow out. That widens the true refrigerant lift beyond the air or water temperatures you read. Add compressor losses, fan and pump power, and frost on the outdoor coil, and the real COP lands well under the ceiling.

How far under? A national laboratory report on alternatives to vapor compression says vapor compression reaches about 70 to 80 percent of the ideal cycle for ideal cycles, about 60 percent for large water-cooled centrifugal chillers at rating conditions, and about 40 percent for smaller air-cooled equipment. The federal energy department brief puts industrial machines at 65 to 75 percent of ideal. For this lesson we assume 45 to 50 percent for a small air-source heat pump. That is our own assumption, picked to sit between the small-equipment and large-equipment figures, and not a measured value for any machine.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with stated assumptions. We treat the outdoor air temperature as the cold side and the condensing temperature as the hot side. That is a simplification, because the real evaporator runs colder than the air. We assume the real COP is 45 to 50 percent of the ideal.

Case A: 35 F outdoor, 105 F condensing.

  • Tcold = 35 + 459.67 = 494.67 R
  • Thot = 105 + 459.67 = 564.67 R
  • Lift = 564.67 − 494.67 = 70 R (the same 70 F)
  • Ideal COP = 564.67 / 70 = 8.07
  • Real COP at 45 percent: 8.07 × 0.45 = 3.63. At 50 percent: 8.07 × 0.50 = 4.03.

Case B: 5 F outdoor, 120 F condensing.

  • Tcold = 5 + 459.67 = 464.67 R
  • Thot = 120 + 459.67 = 579.67 R
  • Lift = 579.67 − 464.67 = 115 R
  • Ideal COP = 579.67 / 115 = 5.04
  • Real COP at 45 percent: 5.04 × 0.45 = 2.27. At 50 percent: 5.04 × 0.50 = 2.52.

Check. The lift grew from 70 to 115 degrees, a 45 degree increase. The ideal COP fell from 8.07 to 5.04, which is about 37.5 percent lower (5.04 / 8.07 = 0.625). The assumed real COP fell from roughly 3.6 to 4.0 down to roughly 2.3 to 2.5. As a rule of thumb from the same math, one extra degree of lift at Case A costs about 0.11 of ideal COP (564.67 / 71 = 7.95), or about 1.4 percent. Those are our own figures from our own assumptions, not a rating for any product.

Real field results move the same direction. A national laboratory field-validation report on cold-climate heat pump prototypes found that heating COP rose steadily with outdoor air temperature, with median COPs below 30 F between 1.6 and 2.7, and a median of 1.9 in the 0 to 5 F bin. It also notes that a conventional heat pump's capacity drops below its rating once outdoor air falls under 40 F.

Where lift comes from, and how to shrink it

Lift has two ends. You usually cannot change the weather, but you can change almost everything else.

  • Keep the outdoor coil working. Dirt, bent fins and frost force the refrigerant colder to pull in the same heat, which widens lift. See frost as insulation for the mechanism, and keep the unit clear of obstructions.
  • Get the right airflow. Low indoor airflow raises the condensing temperature, and low outdoor airflow lowers the evaporating temperature. Both stretch the lift. Measure airflow instead of guessing; our field COP estimate lesson covers the method.
  • Use low-temperature emitters. Larger radiators, panels or low-temperature coils let the building stay warm with cooler supply water or air. A designer who picks 100 F supply instead of 140 F removes tens of degrees of lift in the way the formula rewards most.
  • Use outdoor reset. Run the supply temperature only as high as the day requires. On mild days the lift stays small. See outdoor reset and weather compensation.
  • Avoid unneeded backup heat and big setbacks. Recovery that calls for a high supply temperature or resistance heat gives up the efficiency gains above.

Why this matters for study

When a customer asks why the unit "uses more power when it is cold", the honest answer is lift. The compressor works harder to move each unit of heat, and the ceiling itself is lower. Knowing the formula lets you check any claimed COP against the temperatures it was measured at.

Field card: Heat Pump Ideal COP and Temperature Lift

Smaller lift, higher COP.

  • Ideal heating COP = Thot / (Thot − Tcold), in Rankine (F + 459.67) or kelvins.
  • Real machines reach only a fraction; our example assumes 45 to 50 percent.
  • Our example: 35 F out and 105 F condensing gives ideal 8.07; 5 F out and 120 F gives 5.04.
  • Shrink lift: clean coils, correct airflow, low-temperature emitters, outdoor reset.
  • Never use Fahrenheit directly in the formula.
