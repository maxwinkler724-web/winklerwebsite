Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter: Is That Normal?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Your furnace used to blast for ten minutes and shut off. Your heat pump seems to run for hours, and the air from the vents feels merely warm. In most cases that is how a heat pump is supposed to behave in cold weather. This lesson explains why, which sights and sounds are normal, and which signs mean it is time to call someone.

The short answer

Long run times in winter are usually normal. A heat pump is working against two things at once: the colder it gets, the more heat your house loses, and the less heat the machine can move. NEEP, the Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships, says in its consumer guide that cold-climate heat pumps are designed to run almost continuously at a low level, and that running longer at low speed does not mean higher costs. What is not normal is long running together with a cold house, a thick coat of ice, or a big jump in your electric bill.

Why it runs so long in the cold

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that a heat pump moves heat from outdoor air into the house instead of making it. Cold air has less heat to give, so the capacity of the machine falls as the outdoor temperature falls. Manufacturers publish this as a table. Newer variable-speed and cold-climate models hold more of their capacity at low temperatures than older ones, Trane and Carrier both say, but every model gives up something eventually.

Meanwhile your house loses heat in proportion to how big the gap is between inside and outside. At some outdoor temperature the heat the machine can deliver equals the heat the house loses. That is the balance point. Above it the heat pump keeps up and cycles on and off. Below it the machine runs flat out and the backup heat covers the shortfall. The full explanation is in Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity; the example below uses one datasheet.

Why the air feels cooler than a furnace

A heat pump delivers a large amount of warm air, not a small amount of very hot air. Carrier's troubleshooting page puts supply air from a heat pump at roughly the upper 80s to 100 degrees Fahrenheit in heating mode, and Energy Vanguard describes it as normally around 100 degrees. A gas furnace's air is clearly hotter. Lennox says heat pump air may feel cooler than furnace air even when everything works. Air in the 90s is still warmer than skin, but it can feel cool, so people report "cold air" when nothing is wrong.

Normal behaviors you might notice

  • Defrost cycles. In heating mode the outdoor coil runs colder than the air, so frost forms. Trane says the coil can sit roughly 10 to 20 degrees below outdoor air temperature. The unit periodically reverses to melt the frost, and the outdoor fan stops while it does. Carrier says a defrost cycle typically lasts about 5 to 10 minutes and that a light frost layer, under about a quarter inch, should clear in a cycle. Trane says its demand-defrost cycles usually run 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Steam from the outdoor unit. Trane describes a cloud of steam from the outdoor unit as the ice melting and evaporating. It looks alarming and is normal during defrost.
  • Aux heat showing on the thermostat. Trane says the electric heat strips may come on during defrost to keep the house warm, and may also run when the heat pump falls behind in cold weather. DOE notes that strip heaters are usually more expensive to run than the heat pump.

Worked example: capacity against heat loss

We need two lines. The first is the machine. Mitsubishi Electric's submittal for a multi-zone Hyper-Heating outdoor unit, model MXZ-3D24NLHZ, lists maximum heating capacity for the non-ducted configuration of 30,600 Btu/h at 47°F, 25,000 at 17°F, 25,000 at 5°F and 20,400 at −13°F. Mitsubishi notes that actual capacity varies with the number of indoor units running, so treat these as one datasheet example, not a promise for a house.

The second line is the house. This part is a made-up house, not a measurement. Say a load calculation says it loses 30,000 Btu/h at 5°F, and it needs no heat at 65°F outdoors.

  1. Temperature difference at the design point: 65 − 5 = 60°F.
  2. Heat loss per degree: 30,000 ÷ 60 = 500 Btu/h per °F.
  3. Heat loss at 47°F: 500 × (65 − 47) = 500 × 18 = 9,000 Btu/h. At 17°F: 500 × 48 = 24,000. At −13°F: 500 × 78 = 39,000.
  4. Between 17°F and 5°F the datasheet capacity is 25,000 at both ends, so we assume it stays 25,000 between them. Set heat loss equal to capacity: 500 × (65 − T) = 25,000, so 65 − T = 50 and T = 15°F.
  5. Check: at 17°F the unit (25,000) beats the house (24,000), so it keeps up. At 5°F the house needs 30,000, the unit gives 25,000, and the gap is 5,000 Btu/h. At −13°F the gap is 39,000 − 20,400 = 18,600 Btu/h.

What it means: in this house the balance point is about 15°F. Below that the heat pump runs at full output without a break, which is exactly the "it never shuts off" the homeowner sees. That is normal as long as the house holds temperature and the backup share is small.

Signs of a real problem

  • The outdoor unit is iced and not clearing. Lennox says to call when ice does not clear after a defrost cycle. Carrier gives ice thicker than about a quarter inch, or defrost happening unusually often, as reasons to call. Trane says to call if the unit seems to be constantly switching into defrost. See Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter.
  • Aux or emergency heat seems to run all the time, and the bill jumps. Trane lists aux heat staying on above 40°F, or running for more than an hour or two in mild weather, as signs of a heat pump or thermostat issue. Lennox lists sudden, unexplained spikes in energy bills as a reason to call. Trane calls emergency heat a setting for true emergencies. More in Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat.
  • Short cycling. Lennox lists short cycling without reaching the set temperature as a call-a-pro sign.
  • Cold air for long stretches. Defrost air is cool for a few minutes. Cool air for a long time, or an indoor temperature persistently 2 to 3 degrees or more off the thermostat setting (Carrier), is not defrost.
  • A setback that wakes the strips. Trane says raising the setpoint by more than about 3 or 4 degrees can bring on aux heat so the house recovers faster. DOE advises not setting back a heat pump thermostat if that causes the backup heating to come on.

What a homeowner can check

  • Thermostat mode. Make sure it says Heat, not EM Heat or Emergency, unless the heat pump is actually down. Carrier lists confirming the heat setting as a first step.
  • The filter. DOE says to clean or change filters about every three months or as the manufacturer recommends. NEEP suggests every 2 to 6 weeks for its heat pump types, depending on need, and Lennox says every 1 to 3 months. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter.
  • Space around the outdoor unit. NEEP says to keep snow and debris away from it, and Lennox and Carrier give about two feet of clearance. DOE says to turn off power to the fan before clearing around the unit. Clear loose snow only. If there is ice on the coil or fan, leave it and call.
  • Steady settings. NEEP says modest nighttime changes are fine but deep setbacks may cost more with cold-climate heat pumps. Trane suggests raising the setpoint gradually.

For techs: what to check

  • Compare to the table. Pull the manufacturer capacity table, the load calculation and the outdoor temperature. If the unit is running flat out above the balance point, look for undersizing, duct leakage, or a load higher than calculated. See Heat Load Calculation Intro.
  • Airflow first. DOE says to aim for roughly 400 to 500 cfm per ton and that performance drops when airflow is far below 350 cfm per ton. Start with the filter, coil and ducts. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Charge. DOE says incorrect charge reduces heat pump performance and efficiency. Verify against the manufacturer's data.
  • Supply air. Measure supply temperature and compare it to the manufacturer's expected range; a low reading with the compressor running points to airflow, charge or defrost trouble.
  • Defrost. Confirm the control starts and ends defrost, not just starts. Trane names low refrigerant, faulty defrost controls or a faulty sensor as the causes of persistent ice. See Heat Pump Defrost Control Boards and Sensors.
  • Aux logic and thermostat. Check outdoor lockout settings, staging, and how the thermostat handles setback recovery, then confirm the stat is not in EM Heat. See Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Field card: Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter

Long and steady is normal. Long and cold, or long with strips on, is the question.

  • Capacity falls and house loss rises as it gets colder; they cross at the balance point.
  • Below the balance point the unit runs flat out and backup heat covers the gap.
  • Heat pump supply air is around the 90s, so it feels cooler than furnace air.
  • Normal: defrost, steam, brief cool air, aux during defrost.
  • Problem: ice that stays, aux above 40 F for hours, short cycling, cold air for long periods.
  • Check: thermostat mode, filter, clearance, steady settings.
  • Tech: airflow, charge, defrost control, aux logic, load versus capacity table.
