Water Quality for Modern Hot-Water Boilers: pH, Hardness, Sludge and Inhibitors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An old cast iron boiler shrugged off bad water for decades. A condensing boiler has thin, compact heat exchanger passages and the maker's manual puts limits on what flows through them. This lesson explains why those limits exist, shows how three makers differ, and checks one invented water test against each.

Check the manual in front of you

Check the manual in front of you. Limits differ by maker, model and manual revision. The numbers below come from the manuals we opened in 2026 and are for study. Your boiler's own installation manual is the rule for that job.

Why the limits exist

Lochinvar's Knight manuals give the plain reasons. Mineral build-up in the heat exchanger cuts heat transfer and can let a stainless steel exchanger overheat and fail. Oxygen carried in by makeup water causes internal corrosion, and the manual says continual fresh makeup water reduces boiler life. Sediment can also damage the exchanger. Caleffi's idronics issue 18 adds that hot surfaces such as boiler heat exchangers are the places scale forms first, and that hardness comes from calcium and magnesium salts.

So there are three separate enemies: scale (hardness), corrosion (oxygen, chloride, wrong pH) and sludge (dirt and iron oxide carried around the loop). Each limit in a manual points at one of them.

What the makers publish

The makers do not agree, and some publish very little. Weil-McLain's AquaBalance Series 2 manual (550-100-326/0122) asks for pH of 7.0 to 8.5, hardness below 7 grains, and chloride below 50 ppm. It says to consult a local water treatment company if pH is out of range or hardness is above 7 grains, and it allows pH to be checked with litmus paper or chemical analysis. Navien's NHB manual (section 3.4) lists maximums: total hardness 200 mg/l (which it also writes as 12 grains per gallon), chloride 250 mg/l, iron 0.3 mg/l, and pH 6.5 to 8.5. It tells the installer to confirm the water meets those limits before filling. The Viessmann Vitodens 100-W instructions say far less: fill with potable water, and soften fill water harder than 150 ppm temporary hardness. We found no pH or chloride figure in that document.

The Lochinvar Knight installation manuals we opened gave no numeric limits in the parts that loaded. They do require a thorough flush without the boiler connected, ban petroleum-based cleaning or sealing compounds, and the Knight service manual says to clean the system water with an approved system restorer if the water test calls for it. NTI's Trinity Tft manual gives no numbers in the section we could read, but says to treat all boiler feed water as hard water, test the fill water, use chemical inhibitors on every job, and flush old and new systems before commissioning.

Softened water and fill water

Softening sounds like the obvious cure for hardness, and Viessmann does tell the installer to soften hard fill water. But Caleffi's idronics 18 explains that a softener swaps hardness ions for sodium, so the water stays conductive, and says softened water is not recommended for hydronic systems; it can speed galvanic corrosion. Caleffi prefers demineralized fill water. It suggests keeping dissolved solids between about 10 and 30 ppm, because some ions are needed for low-water cutoffs to sense water, and it warns that demineralized water can start with a pH near 5 to 6 from dissolved carbon dioxide, rising as the system runs. Read your maker's text before choosing, and test after any treatment.

Whatever the source, keep the system closed. Weil-McLain says to use its boiler only in a closed-loop system, and a Navien manual states that makeup water adds oxygen and minerals, so leaks should be fixed at once. A fill meter that keeps climbing is a clue.

Sludge, magnetite and dirt separators

Magnetite is the black iron oxide that forms when iron or steel corrodes. Caleffi describes it as small, often microscopic magnetic particles. Navien tells the installer to flush the whole system before connecting the boiler, to clean sludge and scale from retrofit systems, and to fit a return filter. NTI recommends magnetic dirt separators on systems with a lot of iron. For the full cleaning routine on an old system see Flushing and Cleaning an Old Hot-Water Heating System Before a New Boiler, and for how separators work see Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.

Cleaners and inhibitors

A cleaner removes deposits; an inhibitor protects clean metal afterward. Caleffi describes film-forming treatments as extremely thin films on metal surfaces. Makers want products they have accepted. Weil-McLain says never to use automotive or standard glycol antifreeze, only antifreeze it lists as suitable for boilers. Navien says to use cleaning products formulated for boiler systems and only inhibited propylene glycol, and not to exceed 35 percent glycol. Lochinvar rejects homemade cures and boiler patent medicines. So the habit is: find the approved-products text in the manual, follow the product label for dose, and write down what went in. Glycol adds its own testing; see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops.

Testing the water

Test the fill water before it goes in, and again after the system has run. NTI says water quality must be checked at installation and every year, and that digital meters are highly recommended. Caleffi shows a meter that reads TDS, pH and temperature. Weil-McLain accepts litmus paper for pH. Record date, pH, hardness, chloride and anything added. Next year's technician, see Condensing Boiler Annual Maintenance, can then compare.

Worked example: one test, three manuals

An invented water test reads pH 8.9, hardness 9 grains per gallon (gpg), chlorides 120 ppm. By the usual conversion (our own note, not a Caleffi figure), 1 gpg is about 17.1 ppm. Milligrams per liter and ppm are treated as equal for water here.

Step 1, convert hardness. 9 gpg × 17.1 = 153.9 ppm.

Step 2, Weil-McLain. The pH band is 7.0 to 8.5. 8.9 − 8.5 = 0.4 above the top: fail. Hardness must be below 7 gpg. 9 − 7 = 2 gpg over (7 × 17.1 = 119.7 ppm, and 153.9 − 119.7 = 34.2 ppm over): fail. Chloride must be below 50 ppm. 120 − 50 = 70 over, and 120 ÷ 50 = 2.4 times the limit: fail. Result: three fails.

Step 3, Navien. pH band 6.5 to 8.5. 8.9 − 8.5 = 0.4 over: fail. Hardness max 200 mg/l. 200 − 153.9 = 46.1 under: pass (the manual's 12 gpg is 12 × 17.1 = 205.2, close to 200). Chloride max 250. 250 − 120 = 130 under: pass. Result: one fail, two passes.

Step 4, Viessmann. No pH or chloride limit was found, so those two cannot be graded against this document. For hardness the text says to soften fill water harder than 150 ppm temporary hardness. 153.9 − 150 = 3.9 ppm over, but a hardness test usually reports total hardness, and temporary hardness is only part of it, so the result is cannot confirm. Ask the maker or a water treatment company.

The same water fails one maker three ways and another only on pH. That is why you read the manual for the boiler on site. A pH of 8.9 is a problem under both numeric manuals, so treat or replace the water before filling.

Field card: Water Quality for Modern Hot-Water Boilers

Test the water, read the maker's limits, flush, treat, record.

  • Limits target scale (hardness), corrosion (oxygen, chloride, pH) and sludge.
  • Makers differ: Weil-McLain pH 7.0 to 8.5, under 7 gpg, under 50 ppm chloride; Navien pH 6.5 to 8.5, 200 mg/l hardness, 250 mg/l chloride.
  • 1 gpg is about 17.1 ppm: gpg × 17.1 = ppm.
  • Softened water: Caleffi advises against it for hydronics; Viessmann says soften hard fill water. Read your maker.
  • Flush, add a dirt or magnetic separator, use only maker-accepted cleaners, inhibitors and glycol.
  • Fix leaks; makeup water brings oxygen and minerals.
  • Record date, pH, hardness, chloride and additives.
