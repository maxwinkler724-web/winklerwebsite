Esports and Gaming Lounge HVAC: Computer Heat, Dense Occupancy, CO2, Noise and Zoning per Room

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gaming lounge packs dozens of high-power computers and a young, loud, sitting-still crowd into one room for hours. The equipment heat does not care what month it is, the crowd drives CO2 up fast, and the fans and the HVAC both make noise. This lesson shows how to size the load, ventilate it, and zone it so the PC room does not fight the lobby.

Why this room is different

Almost every watt a computer draws ends up as heat in the room. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study of gaming computers measured a base test system peaking at 512 W during active play, with a mode-weighted average of 212 W while on, and reported measured displays between 15 and 77 W. It also says gaming PCs need dedicated cooling and that large commercial buildings may need extra air conditioning to remove the heat. Its average gamer played 4.4 hours a day, and an extreme gamer 7.2 hours. A lounge runs many machines at once, so the room behaves like a small data room with people in it. Our server room lesson covers the equipment-heat side; here we add people and air quality.

The LBNL tests also saw a graphics processor reach 91 °C in the base setup, and nameplate ratings about 50 percent above measured draw. So do not size from the power supply label. Use measured or planning watts per seat, and decide whether you size for the peak or the average. A lounge with every seat in a tournament is a peak case.

Heat from watts to Btu/h

The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatthour as 3,412 Btu. So one watt is 3.412 Btu/h, and the sensible heat from equipment is watts times 3.412. Divide Btu/h by 12,000 for tons. For airflow, the sensible rule is Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × delta T; see sensible heat ratio and latent load. This is a cooling-dominant room: the equipment heat is there on a 20 °F morning as much as on a 95 °F afternoon. In winter, outdoor air is often a free source of cooling, so a controls plan with an economizer or cold-weather outdoor air mode matters more than the heating system. Keep any heat on the perimeter or in the lobby on its own zone so it never runs against the PC room.

People and CO2

An ASHRAE Standard 62.1 addendum extract (named here in plain text) lists, in Table 6-1, game arcades at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per ft² with a default of 20 people per 1,000 ft², and broadcast stages and studios at 10 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per ft² at 70 per 1,000 ft². That extract is a text conversion of a PDF, so check the published table before design. A seated crowd packed around machines will often exceed the default density, and the per-person term scales with every added head.

ASHRAE's 2025 position document on indoor carbon dioxide says 1,000 ppm is at best an indicator of outdoor air, linked to about 16 cfm per person, and that 62.1 gives no limit value for indoor CO2. It reports a global outdoor level of 426 ppm for 2024 and notes that urban areas can briefly reach 600 ppm or more. So a room with only 7 to 13 cfm per person can legitimately read above 1,000 ppm at full house; that is a clue, not a violation. EPA says indoor CO2 can give information about ventilation but readings need careful interpretation. The CDC says CO2 mostly reflects how many people are present and how much outdoor air comes in, and that rooms are rarely well mixed. It suggests under 800 ppm as one possible target and says to investigate if readings exceed 110 percent of a baseline with airflow and occupancy unchanged.

Sensors and control

A CO2 sensor is how you scale outdoor air with the crowd. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory looked at 208 sensors in 34 commercial buildings and found an average absolute error of 138 ppm (14 percent) at 1,010 ppm, with 19 percent off by more than 200 ppm. Setpoints it found ranged from 500 to 1,100 ppm. Its authors suggest calibrating after installation and periodically, and that return grilles may be a better location than wall points near occupants, where readings varied by more than 200 ppm. For a lounge: put the sensor in the room, away from doors and diffusers, away from a single hot seat, and verify it against a calibrated portable meter. See demand-controlled ventilation and CO2 sensors and measuring airflow. ASHRAE also warns that CO2-removing air cleaners can confuse a CO2-based control.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions, not measurements: 40 stations, each with a PC at 400 W (between the LBNL base average and peak) and two monitors at 30 W each; 50 people in the PC room (40 players plus 10 others); 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent per person (the same planning values we use elsewhere, not from a table we opened); a 1,500 ft² room; summer outdoor air at 95 °F with a water vapor pressure of 0.33 psia and indoor 75 °F with 0.215 psia, both at 14.696 psia; ventilation from the 62.1 extract above. Lights, envelope and fan heat are left out.

  • Equipment: 40 × 400 = 16,000 W; 40 × 2 × 30 = 2,400 W; total 18,400 W. 18,400 × 3.412 = 62,781 Btu/h, all sensible.
  • People: 50 × 250 = 12,500 sensible; 50 × 200 = 10,000 latent.
  • Outdoor air: 50 × 7.5 = 375 cfm, plus 1,500 × 0.18 = 270 cfm, total 645 cfm, or 12.9 cfm per person.
  • Humidity ratios, W = 0.622 pw/(p − pw): outdoor 0.622 × 0.33 / 14.366 = 0.0143; indoor 0.622 × 0.215 / 14.481 = 0.0092. Delta W = 0.0051.
  • Outdoor air sensible: 1.08 × 645 × (95 − 75) = 13,932 Btu/h. Outdoor air latent: 4,840 × 645 × 0.0051 = 15,921 Btu/h (moisture 4.5 × 645 × 0.0051 = 14.8 lb/h).
  • Sensible total: 62,781 + 12,500 + 13,932 = 89,213 Btu/h. Latent total: 10,000 + 15,921 = 25,921 Btu/h. Together 115,134 Btu/h, or 9.6 tons (115,134 / 12,000).
  • Supply air for the room itself: room sensible 62,781 + 12,500 = 75,281 Btu/h. With a 20 °F rise (supply near 55 °F), cfm = 75,281 / (1.08 × 20) = 3,485 cfm, about 2.3 cfm per ft². That is high for a 1,500 ft² room, which is why diffuser layout and noise need attention.
  • Winter check: with 30 °F outdoor air and the same 645 cfm, 1.08 × 645 × (75 − 30) = 31,347 Btu/h of free cooling, about half of the 62,781 Btu/h of equipment heat. More outdoor air through an economizer, or a cold-air mode, covers the rest.

The 12.9 cfm per person is below the roughly 16 cfm ASHRAE ties to 1,000 ppm, so expect readings above that on a full night. Equipment is 54 percent of the total load (62,781 of 115,134). The stage room is separate: our own assumption of 3,000 W of lighting is 3,000 × 3.412 = 10,236 Btu/h before any other gear, so it gets its own zone and its own schedule. See the theater lesson for stage heat.

Noise

PC fans, a crowd and the HVAC all add to the sound level. At 3,000-plus cfm in a small room, supply noise is a design limit before capacity is. Slow the air by using more diffusers at lower velocity, and keep the fan and box noise out of the room; see HVAC noise in commercial buildings. A streaming room is a different case: a microphone picks up diffuser hiss, so that room needs a low NC target and its own air path (our own advice). Never trade away airflow to hush a diffuser if the room then overheats.

Zoning per room

Our own rule of thumb: zone by load and by schedule. The PC room runs hot and long, the stage runs in bursts with lights, the lobby sees doors and weather. One thermostat for all three means the lobby sets the temperature for rooms that have nothing in common. Separate zones, ideally separate units or VAV boxes with reheat and a fixed minimum, let the PC room cool while the lobby is neutral. Check that a return from the PC room does not dump hot air into the lobby, and that room pressure stays slightly positive to the outdoors. Put thermostats away from racks of machines. See zoning basics and VAV boxes.
