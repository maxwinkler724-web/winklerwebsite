AC Runs Constantly and Won't Shut Off: Normal or Not?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An air conditioner that seems to run all day is sometimes doing exactly what it should, and sometimes telling you something is wrong. The one case that is not normal is an outdoor unit that keeps running well after you turn the thermostat off. This page helps you tell the difference, and ends with a short tech section.

Quick answer

Long run times on the hottest days are normal. Long run times on mild days, or a unit that keeps going after the thermostat is set to Off, are not. If the outdoor unit will not stop with the thermostat off, switch the air conditioner's breaker off and call a licensed technician. Lennox lists a faulty contactor among the electrical causes of an AC that will not turn off and says to contact an HVAC expert as soon as possible. Trane says a welded contactor is an electrical issue and to call a professional to replace the switch.

When long run times are normal

Very hot days. Trane says it is normal for an AC to run longer than usual in the hottest months, and that continuous running outside that period usually points to a system problem. Lennox says a system may cycle a bit longer in high outdoor temperatures but should not run constantly. Both describe a typical cycle in the range of 15 to 30 minutes with two to three cycles an hour, so treat any single number as rough.

Systems are sized for a design day, not for the worst heat ever recorded. An Energy Vanguard article explains that a location's 1 percent summer design temperature is exceeded about 1 percent of the hours in a year (about 88 hours for Atlanta at 91 F) and argues for sizing to design loads rather than extremes. On a day well above design, a correctly sized system can run steadily and still fall behind. See Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J for how design conditions are chosen.

Variable-speed and inverter systems. These are built to run long at low output. Carrier's page for its Infinity variable-speed air conditioner says the compressor can step down to as low as 25 percent of capacity, calls the lower speeds the unit's most efficient range, and ties longer low-speed running to removing more humidity. If you have one, "it never shuts off" can simply be how it works. Check your manual, and read Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.

When it is not normal

  • Dirty filter or coil. The Department of Energy says a clean filter in place of a dirty one can cut an air conditioner's energy use by 5 to 15 percent, and that evaporator and outdoor condenser coils collect dirt that should be cleaned. Trane lists dirty filters and dirty evaporator coils as reasons cooling cycles run long. See filters and MERV and Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Low refrigerant. Refrigerant does not get used up; low charge means a leak. ACHR News lists low compressor amps, high evaporator superheat and low condenser subcooling among the signs on a gauge set. Only a technician with EPA 608 certification should handle it.
  • Undersized system. Lennox and Trane both say a unit that is too small runs constantly or works overtime.
  • Leaky ducts. A DOE Building America guide uses 15 percent leakage per side (supply and return) as a typical benchmark and says ducts in attics or crawl spaces can lose 25 to 40 percent of the energy that passes through them. In its Orlando example, repairing the ducts cut the equipment load by 20 percent. See Ductwork Basics.
  • Thermostat trouble. Lennox and Trane list wrong settings, dust on sensors, loose connections and damaged wiring. DOE says a thermostat belongs on an interior wall away from direct sun, drafts, doorways and windows, with no furniture blocking it. A very low setpoint also makes the system run longer; see Thermostat Blank or Wrong Temperature.
  • Frozen coil. Ice means switch the system off; see Frozen Evaporator Coil.
  • A stuck contactor. The outdoor unit keeps running even with the thermostat off. This is the urgent one.

Safe checks for a homeowner

  1. Thermostat mode and fan. Set the mode to Cool and the fan to Auto. Lennox says a fan set to On keeps the fan running between cycles, and Trane notes the air will not be cool then. Confirm the setpoint is reasonable and no schedule is overriding you.
  2. Filter. Replace a dirty one. Trane suggests replacing filters every 30 to 90 days depending on pets and household factors.
  3. Vents. Open supply vents and clear returns.
  4. Outdoor unit. Clear loose debris and trim plants so there is at least 2 feet of space around it, as DOE advises. Do not open the cabinet.
  5. The off test. Set the thermostat to Off. Wait a few minutes, then go listen to the outdoor unit. A fan or compressor that is still running with the thermostat off points to the contactor. PickHVAC describes a stuck compressor contactor as the most probable explanation in that case.
If the outdoor unit keeps running with the thermostat off: turn the air conditioner's breaker off at the electric panel and call a licensed technician. Do not pull the wires, tap the contactor, open the cabinet, or reach inside. PickHVAC notes that shutting off the breaker only stops the unit while you wait. It does not fix the part.

Where the fault sits

Tech section: reading the temperature split

This is education, not a repair procedure. A technician working on a long-running system compares the temperature split across the indoor coil with what the equipment should produce, after confirming airflow. The split is return air temperature minus supply air temperature, measured with a good thermometer.

Worked example. Return air is 78 F and supply air is 60 F.

  1. Split = 78 - 60 = 18 F.
  2. Many technicians quote 15 to 20 F as a typical split. An ACCA blog article calls that rule of thumb "not a one size fits all" and says the right target depends on sensible capacity, temperatures and airflow.
  3. ACCA's formula is split = sensible capacity ÷ 1.08 ÷ CFM. Its example uses 17,900 Btu/h of sensible capacity at 1,000 CFM with 85 F outdoors, 75 F dry-bulb and 67 F wet-bulb entering air.
  4. 17,900 ÷ 1.08 = 16,574. Then 16,574 ÷ 1,000 = 16.6 F. (The article states 16.5 F; the difference is rounding.)
  5. Our 18 F reading is about 1.4 F above that example. Different equipment and conditions would give a different target, so read your unit's data, not this example.

The same formula works backward. At 1,000 CFM, an 18 F split carries about 1.08 × 1,000 × 18 = 19,440 Btu/h of sensible heat. A low split with long run time suggests weak capacity such as low charge or a dirty coil. A high split suggests low airflow. Airflow has to be verified first; see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics and Superheat and Subcooling Basics. ENERGY STAR's installation guidance lists proper sizing, sealed ducts, correct charge and adequate airflow as the pillars of a quality install.

Contactor. For a unit that runs with the thermostat off, the tech proves power off, then checks whether the contacts are pitted, burned or welded, and whether the coil is being energized when it should not be. Lock out and verify dead first, and never jumper a contactor closed. The full control-voltage sequence is in Outdoor AC Unit Not Running but the Indoor Fan Is, and the general symptom list is in Air Conditioner Running but Not Cooling.

Field card: AC Runs Constantly and Won't Shut Off

Long on a very hot day can be normal. Running long after the thermostat is off is not.

  • Normal: hottest days; variable-speed systems that run long at low speed.
  • Not normal: mild weather, dirty filter or coil, low charge, undersized unit, leaky ducts, bad thermostat setting or location.
  • Homeowner: mode Cool, fan Auto, new filter, open vents, clear outdoor unit.
  • Outdoor unit runs with thermostat Off: breaker off, call a licensed technician.
  • Tech: verify airflow first, then compare the split to the unit's data. 15 to 20 F is only a rough rule.
  • Split = sensible capacity / 1.08 / CFM.
