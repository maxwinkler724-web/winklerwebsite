Steam Condensate Line Corrosion: Carbonic Acid, Grooving, Oxygen Pitting and Amines

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Steam itself does not eat pipe. The wet, acidic water that condenses from it does. A return line can look fine on the outside and then leak at a thread one winter. This lesson explains where the acid comes from, how to spot the damage, what amines do, and how cutting makeup water alkalinity beats chasing the problem with chemicals.

Where the acid comes from

Makeup water carries carbonate and bicarbonate alkalinity. A federal facilities criteria document says these break down in the boiler into hydroxide and carbon dioxide gas. The CO2 leaves with the steam, and when the steam condenses, the CO2 dissolves and forms carbonic acid. That lowers condensate pH, and the document calls it corrosive to most metals, especially mild steel. A U.S. Army technical note says the same thing for bicarbonate alkalinity. A university conference paper on kiln boilers adds that return condensate is naturally corrosive because of its purity.

That is why a plant with a lot of makeup water (poor condensate return, or steam used up in process) usually has more trouble than one that returns most of its condensate. More returns means less makeup, and the university paper names makeup as the main CO2 source. For the return side, see Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

Two kinds of damage

  • Carbonic acid thinning. The federal criteria document lists wall thinning at the condensate liquid level and at pipe threads. Condensate runs along the bottom of a sloped line, so the acid water keeps contact with the same strip of steel, and the result is a groove. Threads are thin to start with, so they fail first.
  • Oxygen pitting. The same document says oxygen corrosion shows as pitting and tubercles (lumpy rust mounds). Air gets in through feedwater that was not fully deaerated, and through intermittently used lines that cool and pull a vacuum, drawing air past valve seals. The Army note adds that pitting in return piping points to oxygen or to potable water leaking in, often from heater tubes. See Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics.

Field signs

  • Leaking threaded returns. Drips at unions and nipples are the classic sign of thread thinning.
  • Red or rusty water in condensate or a flash tank. Our own note: this means pipe metal is going into the water, so it is worth a pH and iron check instead of a patch.
  • Pinholes or a groove when a section is cut out. The groove runs along the six o'clock position.
  • Traps that fail or plug. Our own inference, not a sourced limit: corrosion debris can foul trap seats, and leaking traps push steam and air through lines that are meant to carry water, so a spate of trap failures on one return run is a reason to test that line.

Measuring condensate pH and iron

The sources do not agree on one number, which is itself a lesson: follow the plant's written water program. The university kiln paper targets a return condensate pH of 8 to 9. The federal criteria document says to hold pH between 7.5 and 9.0, ideally 7.5 to 8.5, and that corrosion occurs below 7.5. The Army note says to keep it at 7.5 to 8.0 where possible and never below about 7.6 anywhere. The criteria document says to check pH at several places along the system, not only at the receiver, and to adjust the amine blend based on those readings and to resurvey after a change.

Iron is the other half. Our own reasoning: pH shows what the water is doing, while iron in the condensate shows what the pipe has already lost. We did not find a federal numeric iron limit for building steam, so trend your readings at the same sample points and ask the water treatment provider for a site target. A rising trend with a steady pH points to a problem the pH test cannot see, such as oxygen.

Neutralizing and filming amines

Neutralizing amines are volatile: they travel with the steam and dissolve in condensate to raise pH. The criteria document names morpholine, diethylaminoethanol (DEAE) and cyclohexylamine for military boilers and says to feed continuously. The criteria document feeds them directly to the steam drum, and the Army note to the drum or main steam header, upstream of the lines that need protecting. The criteria document lists vapor-liquid distribution ratios of 0.4 for morpholine, 1.7 for DEAE and 4.7 for cyclohexylamine, so morpholine drops out of the steam early and cyclohexylamine rides far down the line. Long systems may use a blend, with a starting point of 1 part cyclohexylamine to 3 parts morpholine. Cyclohexylamine is not for feedwater alkalinity above 75 ppm, because its bicarbonate can deposit at the end of the line.

Filming amines work differently. The criteria document says 0.7 to 1.0 ppm of a filming amine such as octadecylamine can control CO2 corrosion by coating the pipe so CO2 stays off the wall, and it may help where air leakage is high. The university paper describes filming amines the same way. Oxygen scavengers such as sulfite work upstream; the university paper says to feed sulfite as far upstream as possible, ideally at the deaerator storage section. Basics of the boiler side are in Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics.

Federal limits when steam touches food or humidifies air

The FDA rule at 21 CFR 173.310 (read on eCFR) covers boiler water additives for steam that will contact food. It says an additive must not exceed what its purpose requires. For amines it sets: cyclohexylamine, not to exceed 10 ppm in steam; diethylaminoethanol, 15 ppm in steam; morpholine, 10 ppm in steam; octadecylamine, 3 ppm in steam; hydrazine, zero in steam. For all of these but hydrazine, the rule also excludes steam that contacts milk and milk products. The section we read does not separately address humidifier air, so we do not claim a federal number for it. The Army note says neutralizing amines are not authorized for steam used in direct-contact cooking or humidification and points to a steam-to-steam heat exchanger for amine-free steam, and a unified facilities manual says treated steam should not contact food directly. For a humidifier or kitchen, get the water treatment provider and the authority having jurisdiction to confirm what applies before any amine goes in the boiler.

Fix the alkalinity first

The cleanest answer is less CO2 at the source. A unified facilities manual lists dealkalization by chloride ion exchange among makeup treatments for removing alkalinity, and the kiln paper says dealkalizers swap carbonates for chloride and are regenerated with salt, sometimes with sodium hydroxide too. Reverse osmosis is another route; see Makeup Water Softeners and Reverse Osmosis. A DOE sourcebook says reducing makeup water also cuts chemical use. A deaerator removes CO2 from feedwater too, and receivers matter for hot returns: Boiler Feed Pumps and Condensate Receivers.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Method. The federal criteria document gives an estimate of CO2 in steam from feedwater alkalinity: CO2 (ppm) = bicarbonate x 0.79 + carbonate x 0.35, with alkalinity in ppm as CaCO3. We use only the bicarbonate term. Assumptions (ours, not from a site): makeup water has M alkalinity of 120 ppm, all bicarbonate (P = 0); returned condensate carries no alkalinity; the boiler makes 10,000 lb/h of steam; losses and blowdown are ignored; the plant returns 60 percent of its condensate.

  1. Feedwater alkalinity: 40 percent is makeup, so 0.40 x 120 = 48 ppm.
  2. CO2 in steam: 48 x 0.79 = 37.9 ppm.
  3. CO2 mass: 37.9 / 1,000,000 x 10,000 lb/h = 0.379 lb/h, about 9.1 lb per day running around the clock.

What changes it. Raise returns to 80 percent: feedwater 0.20 x 120 = 24 ppm, CO2 = 24 x 0.79 = 19.0 ppm (half). Or assume a dealkalizer brings makeup to 20 ppm (our assumption) at 60 percent returns: feedwater 8 ppm, CO2 = 6.3 ppm, an 83 percent cut.

Amine check. The criteria document says holding pH 8.0 in water with 10 ppm CO2 takes 15 ppm cyclohexylamine, 22 ppm DEAE or 37 ppm morpholine. If we scale those in simple proportion (a rough assumption of ours; real demand depends on the system) to 37.9 ppm CO2, we get about 57, 83 and 140 ppm. These are a different measurement basis than the "in steam" limits, so they are not a direct comparison, but the size of the gap shows why steam that touches food or air cannot lean on amine to cover high alkalinity. Reduce the CO2 first.

Field card: Steam Condensate Line Corrosion: Carbonic Acid, Grooving, Oxygen Pitting and Amines

Fix the alkalinity, then neutralize what is left.

  • Alkalinity in makeup becomes CO2 in the boiler, then carbonic acid in the condensate.
  • Thinning shows at the bottom of the pipe and at threads; oxygen shows as pits.
  • Check condensate pH at several points, and trend iron at the same points.
  • Neutralizing amines go to the drum or header; filming amines coat the pipe.
  • 21 CFR 173.310 limits amines in steam that contacts food; do not assume it covers humidifiers.
  • More returns and less alkalinity mean less CO2.
