Air Handler Condensate Recovery: Reusing Cooling Coil Water for Cooling Tower Makeup and Irrigation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling coil that dehumidifies outdoor air makes a steady stream of clean, cold water, and most buildings send it to the sewer. This lesson covers how much water forms, what is in it, how to collect it, and what the plumbing and Legionella rules mean for each reuse.

Why this water is worth catching

When a coil cools air below its dew point, water vapor condenses on the fins and drips into the drain pan. A federal case study calls the result "nearly distilled-quality" water, and a university case study reports low conductivity and cold temperature, which suits cooling tower makeup. Timing helps too: the DOE page on cooling tower management says condensate is generated in the greatest quantities when tower loads are highest. That is the same pattern we cover in cooling tower cycles of concentration, blowdown and makeup, where low-mineral makeup lets a tower run more cycles. One campus tower moved from five cycles to an average of nine after adopting condensate.

How much forms: rules of thumb from the sources

  • An EPA WaterSense guide gives 3 to 10 gallons per day per 1,000 square feet of air-conditioned space, and says output depends on load, humidity and outdoor air volume.
  • An EPA water-recovery summary says a 100,000 square foot building in a humid zone can produce about one million gallons of condensate per year.
  • A federal lab in Georgia averaged about 120,000 gallons per month in hot, humid weather.
  • A hospital-campus cancer center cut its tower water by about 900,000 gallons per year, per an EPA regional guide.

To size your own, calculate from the air.

Worked example: a 10,000 cfm outdoor air unit

Our own example. Dedicated outdoor air units carry the biggest latent load, because every cubic foot they move is humid outdoor air; see dedicated outdoor air systems. The inputs below are our assumptions, not measurements.

Assumptions (our own): 10,000 cfm of 100% outdoor air at 85°F dry bulb and 70°F wet bulb; sea-level pressure of 14.696 psia; leaving air 55°F saturated; 10 hours a day; 120 cooling days; every pound of water removed from the air is collected.

Table values used. A saturated-steam table (English units, abridged from the 1967 ASME steam tables) lists saturation pressure of 0.21404 psia at 55°F and 0.36292 psia at 70°F. We converted wet bulb to vapor pressure with the Carrier equation as printed in a PE exam study guide, then used W = 0.622 pw / (p − pw).

  1. Entering vapor pressure: pw = 0.36292 − (14.696 − 0.36292)(85 − 70) / (2800 − 1.3 × 70) = 0.36292 − 214.996 / 2709 = 0.2836 psia.
  2. Entering humidity ratio: W = 0.622 × 0.2836 / (14.696 − 0.2836) = 0.01224 lb water per lb dry air.
  3. Leaving humidity ratio, saturated at 55°F: W = 0.622 × 0.21404 / (14.696 − 0.21404) = 0.00919.
  4. ΔW = 0.01224 − 0.00919 = 0.00305.
  5. Water removed: lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW = 4.5 × 10,000 × 0.00305 = 137 lb/h. (The 4.5 is 60 minutes times about 0.075 lb of standard air per cubic foot.)
  6. Gallons per hour: 137 / 8.34 = 16.5 gal/h.
  7. Per day: 16.5 × 10 h = 165 gal. Per season: 165 × 120 days = about 19,800 gal.

That is about 20,000 gallons a year from one unit. Part-load hours and drier days will lower it, so treat it as a ceiling for these assumptions. A tower usually needs more, so keep a potable supply; an EPA regional guide says supplemental domestic water is still needed.

Water quality

  • Minerals: low. The EPA WaterSense guide rates condensate low for sediment, dissolved solids, hardness and pathogens.
  • Copper: the same guide notes it may contain copper when coils are cleaned. Keep coil-cleaning rinse out of the collection line, and the guide says to segregate it.
  • Biology: condensate can grow bacteria picked up from indoor air, and an EPA summary of tests on condensate from 13 air handlers found potential for microbial growth and metals leaching in untreated collections. Quality can change once it sits in a tank.
  • Treatment: the WaterSense guide says condensate is generally fine without added treatment for a tower using biocide control, or for subsurface irrigation. It says to filter and disinfect when people could breathe or touch it, such as spray irrigation, and that disinfection is recommended for water used in toilet flushing.

Collection, tank, pump and overflow

Start with the drain pan and a trap on each unit; the trap depth for draw-through units is in AC coil condensate traps. The EPA regional guide describes a gravity-fed system: a drain from each air handler to a central point in the penthouse, then one line to the towers. Each unit gets a two-way isolation valve, and a three-way valve on the tower makeup line picks condensate or domestic water. At a central plant the longer route needs a pump, which adds cost.

A federal case study shows the tank-and-pump version: pipes run from the bottom of each air handler to a collection basin, and a pump moves the water when the basin reaches a set level. A flow meter totals the gallons sent to the tower, which is how you prove the savings. Standing water is where biology grows, so a small tank that turns over beats a large one that sits (our own advice).

The overflow is for the day the pump fails or the tower is full. Run it to a visible drain so a failure shows. An EPA regional guide notes that excess condensate sometimes goes to the sewer.

Backflow and plumbing code

Condensate is non-potable water, so the pipe must never be a path back to drinking water. We draw the pipe ending above the basin water level with an air gap, and the same idea applies to any potable makeup into the tank. See backflow prevention on HVAC makeup water for how protection devices and hazard levels are chosen. The sources we read do not set an air gap size or pipe labeling rule. The WaterSense guide points to the IAPMO green plumbing and mechanical code supplement for alternative water systems, and the Whole Building Design Guide page tells designers to check with local jurisdictions. The Whole Building Design Guide page lists condensate as a candidate for irrigation and toilet flushing, but the WaterSense guide does not name condensate for flushing. Permits, pipe marking and signs are set locally, so ask before you design.

Legionella and water management

Reuse does not remove the tower's water management duties. CDC says warm water between 77 and 113°F, sediment, biofilm, stagnant water and no disinfectant residual favor growth, and it says to use potable water for makeup, with reclaimed or condensate sources appropriately managed if used. The EPA summary says Legionella and other opportunistic pathogens can be controlled in condensate collection systems and that they should be managed like other premise water systems. Put the tank, pan treatment and tower in the written plan from cooling tower water management and Legionella awareness, and keep blowdown running, because makeup still equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift.

Field card: Air handler condensate recovery

Clean and cold, but not drinking water.

  • Water removed (lb/h) = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW; gal/h = lb/h / 8.34.
  • Our own example: 10,000 cfm, 85/70 to 55 saturated gives about 16.5 gal/h and about 19,800 gal per season.
  • Best use: tower makeup, since supply peaks when the tower is busiest.
  • Small tank, visible overflow, air gap at the tower.
  • Disinfect for spray irrigation and flushing; follow the tower's Legionella plan.
