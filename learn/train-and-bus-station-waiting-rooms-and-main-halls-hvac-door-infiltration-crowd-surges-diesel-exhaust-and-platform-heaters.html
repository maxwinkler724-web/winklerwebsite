Train and Bus Station Waiting Rooms: Doors, Crowd Surges, Diesel Exhaust and Platform Heaters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A station waiting room is a lobby with a bad reputation: doors that never stay shut, a crowd that arrives all at once, and vehicles outside that burn diesel. This lesson walks a facility tech through each of those problems and ends with a worked example in numbers.

Check first

Check the adopted code and the design documents. Ventilation and fire rules for stations vary by owner, jurisdiction and edition. NFPA 130 is the standard for fixed guideway transit and passenger rail systems, and its publisher describes it as covering fire protection and life safety in those systems. We did not find a free public summary of its station ventilation rules, so we do not repeat any here. Ask the owner which edition applies. This page is general education, not engineering advice.

What makes a station different

  • Big, frequent door openings to the platform or bus bays.
  • Crowd surges. The room may hold a handful of people for hours, then a full train load for ten minutes.
  • Vehicle exhaust just outside, drawn in at doors and outdoor air intakes.
  • Open platforms where only a shelter and a heater stand between riders and the weather.

Ventilation for the people in the room

ASHRAE 62.1 sets the breathing zone outdoor air as Vbz = Rp x people + Ra x area. The 2022 edition, as revised by Addendum w, lists a "Transportation waiting" space in Table 6-1 with Rp = 7.5 cfm per person, Ra = 0.06 cfm per square foot, a default density of 100 people per 1,000 square feet, and air class 1. The standard is copyrighted and we do not reproduce it, so confirm the row in the edition your jurisdiction has adopted. See our 62 lesson for the method.

The people term dominates. That is why a crowd needs far more outdoor air than the same room empty, and why a fixed design for the full crowd wastes energy the rest of the day.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions, not field data: a waiting room of 4,000 sq ft with a 14 ft ceiling (56,000 cu ft), a rush-hour count of 200 people, and a quiet-hour count of 20 people.

  1. Area part: 0.06 x 4,000 = 240 cfm.
  2. Rush-hour people part: 7.5 x 200 = 1,500 cfm. Vbz = 1,500 + 240 = 1,740 cfm.
  3. Quiet hour: 7.5 x 20 = 150 cfm. Vbz = 150 + 240 = 390 cfm.
  4. For comparison, the default density of 100 per 1,000 sq ft would be 400 people: 7.5 x 400 + 240 = 3,240 cfm. Our 200 is an assumed count, half of that.

Next, the door. Assume (our assumption) that the platform doors leak an average of 2,000 cfm of 20 F outdoor air into a 68 F room. The sensible heat formula is Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. ACHR News explains that 1.08 is 0.075 lb per cu ft x 60 x 0.24, for dry air near 70 F, so at 20 F it is a good estimate rather than an exact one. Our heat transfer formulas lesson covers it.

  1. Delta T = 68 - 20 = 48 F.
  2. No vestibule: 1.08 x 2,000 x 48 = 103,680 Btu/h.
  3. With a vestibule, using an example reduction of 35 percent (our assumption): 2,000 x 0.65 = 1,300 cfm, and 1.08 x 1,300 x 48 = 67,392 Btu/h.
  4. Difference: 103,680 - 67,392 = 36,288 Btu/h.
  5. For scale, heating the rush-hour ventilation air: 1.08 x 1,740 x 48 = 90,202 Btu/h.

So an ordinary door problem can add a heat load as large as the code ventilation for a full crowd. Where does 35 percent come from? PNNL modeled infiltration with and without vestibules for 12 building types and gave peak cfm for each. Dividing its with-vestibule numbers by its without-vestibule numbers (our arithmetic) gives reductions from about 28 to 41 percent, so 35 is a mid-range pick. Those models assumed a 21 sq ft door and automatic doors, not a station with wide, constantly used openings, so treat the example as a teaching number.

Vestibules, revolving doors and air curtains

PNNL's report describes the code rule behind vestibules: the older ASHRAE 90.1 text required an enclosed vestibule with self-closing doors at many entrances, with exceptions by climate zone and building size, and interior and exterior doors at least 7 feet apart that do not open at the same time. It also lists entrances with revolving doors as an exception. In a station, a vestibule only helps if people do not prop its doors, and a bus-bay door that stays open all day defeats it. Check closers, sweeps and door-hold wiring. Our air curtain lesson covers when an air curtain is accepted instead of a vestibule.

PNNL also found that door-opening frequency changed infiltration much more than outdoor temperature did, so a log of how often doors cycle is worth more than a guess.

Crowd surges and CO2 control

When occupancy swings from 20 to 200 people, demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) with CO2 sensors can follow the crowd instead of holding 1,740 cfm all day. Our DCV lesson covers sensors and 62.1 limits. Station points to remember:

  • CO2 rises slowly. A crowd that stays ten minutes may leave before the sensor responds, so ask the designer about a pre-open schedule or a train-arrival signal.
  • A CO2 sensor measures people, not diesel exhaust. A clean CO2 reading does not mean the air is clean at the intake.
  • Sensor location matters. Do not mount it near a door.
  • If the fan and damper ramp up with the crowd, confirm the damper reaches the airflow in the example above when the sensor says so.

Diesel exhaust at entrances and intakes

EPA's bus idling pages say diesel exhaust can enter buildings through air intakes, doors and open windows, that its fine particles can lodge deep in the lungs, and that continuous idling for more than three minutes emits more particulate matter than a restart. EPA's diesel information also says fine particles can aggravate asthma and cause lung damage. For a tech, that turns into a checklist:

  • Find where buses or locomotives idle and where the plume goes in wind.
  • Look at every outdoor air intake and door near those spots. Move an intake, or ask whether the exhaust or the idling can move instead. Treat moving anything as a design question.
  • A vestibule and positive pressure in the room keep exhaust from being pulled in at the door, but only if the room is not running negative.
  • For source capture where vehicles park indoors, see our bus garage lesson.

Ticket office and restroom exhaust

Exhaust fans pull air out of the room, and that air has to come from somewhere. If the restroom and ticket office fans remove more air than the system brings in, the waiting room runs negative and the doors pull in unfiltered outdoor air, and anything outside, through every crack. That is our reasoning from basic airflow, not a code value. Check that makeup or transfer air is provided, and that the fans were balanced to the design numbers on the test and balance report. Exhaust rates for restrooms come from the adopted code and design, so do not guess them.

Platform shelter radiant heaters

Radiant heaters warm people and surfaces rather than the air, which is why they suit an open platform where air heat would blow away. A university poultry paper puts it plainly: the heater gives off infrared rays that transfer heat to objects nearby, and the air is warmed only by those objects. DOE explains the underlying idea as warm objects giving off heat waves that cooler objects absorb. DOE's gas heater field report lists infrared heaters as indirect units, where combustion products do not enter the building, with the radiation reflected to the space. For a shelter, check:

  • Clearances to the shelter roof, benches and signs are in the maker's manual. The Auburn paper says to observe them strictly.
  • Reflectors are clean. Dirty reflectors can overheat the surface above them.
  • Aim and mounting height match the manual, since the warmest spot is directly under the unit.
  • Gas or electric, venting and combustion air details differ by model, so follow the nameplate and instructions. Our infrared tube heater lesson covers the common checks.

Field card: Train and Bus Station Waiting Rooms

Doors, crowds, exhaust, shelter heat.

  • 62.1: Vbz = Rp x people + Ra x area. Transportation waiting row: 7.5 cfm per person, 0.06 cfm per sq ft (confirm your edition).
  • Our example: 200 people, 4,000 sq ft gives 1,740 cfm; 20 people gives 390 cfm.
  • Door loss: Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. 2,000 cfm at 48 F is 103,680 Btu/h; with an example 35 percent reduction, 67,392.
  • Check vestibule closers and propped doors first.
  • CO2 sensors follow people, not diesel exhaust.
  • Keep intakes away from idling bays and plumes.
  • Exhaust fans need makeup air or the room goes negative.
  • Radiant heater: manual clearances, clean reflectors.
