HVAC for Taxidermy Shops and Natural History Specimen Prep Rooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A taxidermy shop or specimen prep room mixes jobs that fight each other: solvent and tanning-chemical fumes, grinding dust, deep-freeze pest control, gentle drying, and a colony of beetles that eats flesh. Each one wants its own air handling. This lesson walks through the pieces, then works one exhaust and makeup-air example of our own.

Start with the rule of the room

Treat the building as zones. Messy and smelly work (fleshing, grinding, chemicals, beetles) should sit at lower pressure than clean areas, and finished collections should be the cleanest and most protected space. For the clean side, see Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control; we do not repeat setpoints here.

Fumes: capture them at the bench

A federal shipyard ventilation guide says local exhaust is the preferred control for solvent work because it removes vapor at the source, and that dilution alone has limits and cannot be used for highly toxic chemicals. It also says to place the hood close to the emission source, and to put makeup air behind the worker so contaminated air moves away from the breathing zone. A federal worker safety page on salon chemicals makes a similar point from a different trade: ventilation is the best way to lower chemical levels, and tests showed exhaust ventilation may cut worker exposure by at least half. Those pages are about other trades, so we use them for the principle, not as taxidermy rules.

The federal technical manual adds a warning that matters at a bench: a hood must sit close to the source because capture velocity falls off fast with distance. For a simple hood with 3,000 fpm in the duct, it says a source one duct diameter away sees about 300 fpm, and at two diameters about 30 fpm. It also says strong cross-drafts can cut a hood's effectiveness by 75 percent. So a supply diffuser blowing across the bench can undo a good hood.

Dust: grinding needs its own hood

A university lecture note, abstracting a published ventilation manual, lists capture velocity ranges by how the contaminant is released: 50 to 100 fpm for vapor drifting into still air, 100 to 200 fpm for slightly moving air, 200 to 500 fpm for active generation, and 500 to 2,000 fpm for grinding and abrasive blasting. Use the low end of a range for a large hood, minimal room air movement and intermittent use; use the high end for a small hood, drafts and heavy use. Our reading: a bench hood sized for vapor is far too weak for a grinding wheel or a buffing wheel. Put a hood or enclosure right at the tool, with duct velocity high enough to carry the dust. One federal battery-plant exhaust page shows transport velocities of 4,200 to 4,500 fpm for dust and fume in its own designs; it is a different industry, so treat it as an example of the order of magnitude, not a rule for your shop. Fleshing scrapings are wet and smelly rather than dusty, so a flexible arm near the fleshing beam is often the better fit.

Specimen freezers and walk-in freezers

A national park service collection care leaflet on freezing says freezing is for pest activity that is evident or suspected, not routine, and suits dry natural history specimens not stored in fluid. It says to hold at least -20°C (-5°F), prefers a chest freezer without a frost-free cycle (a frost-free unit only in an emergency), and notes that walk-in freezers can reach -30°C. It recommends sealing objects in polyethylene bags, leaving air space around bags, freezing at least 48 hours, and doing two cycles with a 24-hour warm-up between. It also says to run the thermometer lead through the door so the lead is thin enough that the gasket still seals. The park service museum handbook gives times that depend on temperature: at least one week at -4°F, 72 hours at -22°F, and 48 hours at -40°F.

What this means for a refrigeration tech: a defrost cycle that warms the box, a worn gasket or a door left ajar is a pest-kill problem as well as an energy problem. Log the box temperature with a probe near the load, not just the controller's air sensor. See Walk-In Door Gaskets, Closers and Frame Heaters and Walk-In Defrost, Fan Delay and Icing.

Drying rooms: steady and gently dry

The handbook says to keep relative humidity below 65 percent to discourage moisture pests, and the freezing leaflet says to condition objects near 50 percent RH, within 35 to 65 percent, before freezing. A drying room should therefore hold a steady, modest humidity rather than swinging. Our own advice: use a dehumidifier or cooling coil with a humidity control, keep air gently moving, and keep it away from the beetle room's air. A national museum prep-lab page (plain-text citation) describes a 0°C cold room used to dry specimens before they go to the beetles.

Beetle rooms: warm, ventilated and separate

The same national museum page describes walk-in beetle chambers heated to 81 to 85°F at 70 percent relative humidity, kept dark and isolated from the rest of the building. A university zoological museum page says its colony sits in its own building, about two city blocks from collections storage, and that constant vigilance over temperature, humidity and cleanliness is needed. Our own design rules from those facts: give the beetle room its own exhaust to outdoors, keep it negative to neighbors, put 20-mesh screens (the handbook's figure for vents) on any openings, never share return air with collections or the drying room, and use a self-closing door with sweeps. Warm moist air plus decaying tissue is a hard odor load, so the exhaust should discharge well away from any outdoor air intake.

Odor control and negative pressure

The federal technical manual says an exhaust system must have makeup air; without it the room goes slightly negative and exhaust airflow drops, and it lists cracks, gaps under doors and roof vents as unacceptable sources. Those leaks can pull odor and pests toward the rest of the building. The sound approach is a dedicated, tempered makeup air unit interlocked with the exhaust fan, set so the room stays slightly negative without whistling doors. We have no sourced pressure number, so the commissioning target belongs with the designer.

Keeping pests away from collections

The park service handbook says new arrivals should be inspected, isolated and monitored in a well-sealed room outside the collections area for at least a month, since insect eggs can stay dormant for years. It also calls for sticky traps checked weekly at first, gaskets and sweeps for doors, sealing of pipe, conduit and HVAC penetrations, a high-efficiency vacuum at least monthly, and action on even a single larva or adult. In HVAC terms: keep the prep room, freezer anteroom and beetle room off the collections' air system, and treat the doorway as the filter.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example. The numbers are assumptions, not a design for any real shop.

Assumptions: a bench opening 4 ft wide by 2 ft high under a back-draft slot hood with side baffles; solvent vapor released into still air with little heat; we choose 100 fpm, the top of the 50 to 100 fpm vapor range, because the hood is small. The slot runs the full 4 ft width at 2,000 fpm slot velocity, the value a federal exhaust page uses for its workbench slot hoods. Outdoor air is 15°F (assumed); makeup air is heated to 65°F, borrowing the entry temperature a federal spray-finishing rule requires for makeup air. That rule covers spray booths, so we use it only as a comfortable target here. Room air is at sea level.

  1. Face area: 4 ft × 2 ft = 8 ft².
  2. Exhaust airflow: 100 fpm × 8 ft² = 800 cfm.
  3. Slot area: 800 cfm ÷ 2,000 fpm = 0.4 ft² = 57.6 in². Slot height: 57.6 in² ÷ 48 in = 1.2 in.
  4. Makeup air equals exhaust: 800 cfm. Temperature rise: 65 − 15 = 50°F.
  5. Sensible heat: 1.08 × 800 cfm × 50°F = 43,200 Btu/h. The 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F = 1.08 (standard air density and specific heat).
  6. In electric terms: 43,200 ÷ 3,412 Btu/h per kW = about 12.7 kW.

Check: 800 cfm over 8 ft² gives back 100 fpm; 800 cfm through 0.4 ft² gives back 2,000 fpm; the heat arithmetic repeats as 864 × 50 = 43,200. A grinder at the same shop needs its own tool hood and more airflow, so this 800 cfm is only the bench. Because the heat load is large, many shops use a smaller hood opening or run the fan only while working; that is a design choice, not a rule.

Field card: HVAC for Taxidermy Shops and Specimen Prep Rooms

Capture at the source, replace what you exhaust, keep dirty zones negative.

  • Put hoods close to the source; cross-drafts can cut capture by 75 percent.
  • Vapor 50 to 100 fpm; grinding 500 to 2,000 fpm, so grinding needs its own hood.
  • Our example: 4 ft by 2 ft opening at 100 fpm is 800 cfm; heating 800 cfm by 50°F is 43,200 Btu/h.
  • Pest freezing: at least -20°C, no frost-free cycle if possible, bagged, two 48-hour cycles.
  • Defrost swings, worn gaskets and open doors undo a pest-kill freeze.
  • Beetle room: warm and humid, own exhaust, negative, screened, off the collections air.
  • Isolate new arrivals for a month; act on a single sighting.
