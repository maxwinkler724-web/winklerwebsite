Automatic Vent Dampers on Boilers: How They Work and Common Faults

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas boiler with a draft hood has an open path up the chimney, even when the burner is off. An automatic vent damper closes that path between cycles and opens it before the burner lights. If a boiler will not fire and the damper looks closed, the damper is doing its job as a safety interlock. This lesson explains how it works and where it fails.

Check the manual first

Check the manual first. Every damper and boiler pair has its own wiring and instructions. This page is general education built from maker manuals and federal pages read in 2026. It is not a repair procedure for your equipment.

What the damper does

An atmospheric gas boiler breathes through its draft hood. When the burner is off, warm air from the boiler room and from the boiler's own hot iron can keep drifting up the chimney. The U.S. Department of Energy's page on gas-fired boilers puts it simply: a vent damper prevents heat loss by closing off the boiler's vent when it is not firing. A motor-driven damper in the vent pipe does this. Field Controls says its damper sits in the vent after the draft hood, stays closed in the off cycle, and turns 90 degrees to open when there is a call for heat. Its GVD manual gives about 15 seconds to open and 15 seconds to close.

Some dampers are thermally operated rather than motor driven. A federal public housing page from HUD prefers tight mechanical dampers and says thermal ones are not recommended because they do not allow accurate timing. The rest of this lesson is about the motor-driven kind with an end switch, which is what the maker manuals below describe.

The proving end switch

The key part is the end switch inside the damper operator. It is wired in the burner control circuit, so the burner cannot run until the damper has proven itself open. The Slant/Fin damper guide says the end switch acts as a proving interlock, and that if the damper does not rotate to the open position the burner circuit will not be activated. Field Controls describes the same order: the damper must be fully open before the gas valve opens and stay open while the valve is open, and the valve must close before the damper starts to close. Weil-McLain's control supplement says the damper must be fully open before the main burners light.

Burnham's Series 3 manual shows how this looks on a modern control. Its IQ control displays a status while it waits for the damper to open with the end switch to close, and a separate error for an end switch whose contacts are stuck closed and one whose contacts are stuck open. So a "no heat" call can be the control correctly refusing to fire.

The harness plug

The damper connects through a wiring harness with a plug. Weil-McLain tells the installer to plug the damper harness receptacle into the boiler's damper harness plug and to apply the harness plug warning label where it can be seen. Its troubleshooting chart asks whether the harness is securely plugged in at both ends. The Slant/Fin guide says not to operate the boiler until the damper harness receptacle is plugged into the male plug. Field Controls warns not to cut the plug off the damper motor, which also voids the warranty.

An unplugged or loose harness is therefore a simple cause of a boiler that will not start. It is also a reminder that the plug is part of the safety circuit, not a convenience.

Common faults and safe checks

  • Damper closed, will not open. The Slant/Fin guide lists a blocked damper, wiring errors and a bad operator. Field Controls points to a loose connection or defective motor, and tells you to check for 24 volts at the damper terminals named in its manual.
  • Stops short of full travel. Slant/Fin says travel should reach 90 degrees. Look for a blockage, a missing roll pin stop, or a broken coupling or return spring.
  • Burner runs with the damper closed, or lights before it opens. Slant/Fin says to recheck wiring and replace the operator if the switch is faulty. This is a safety failure. Do not run the boiler.
  • Intermittent operation. Slant/Fin suggests checking the coupling, the ground, and the switch.
  • Control codes. On Burnham's IQ control, a damper end switch status or error points you to the damper circuit.

Safe habits: shut the boiler off before you touch the damper or its wiring, label wires before you remove them, and check operation after the work. Slant/Fin says never to jump safety controls. Weil-McLain warns that bypassing or jumpering the vent damper will let flue products such as carbon monoxide escape into the house. Field Controls describes a jumper test only as a troubleshooting step for the manual's own procedure, and still says the damper must be open before combustion can take place. Apprentices should follow the maker's procedure with a supervisor and not improvise.

A recall shows why the end switch matters. In 2008 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall of about 45,000 Effikal RVGP-PC gas vent dampers, sold from August 2007 through July 2008 and installed in atmospheric boilers from several brands including Burnham and Weil-McLain. The concern was that a damper could fail and, if its blocked-vent switch also failed to activate, carbon monoxide could leak. Effikal offered a free replacement damper.

Listed for the appliance, installed to the instructions

A damper is not a universal part. Field Controls says to use its GVD only with a listed gas-fired appliance with a draft hood, with the draft hood outlet no larger than the damper inlet. It must serve a single appliance, not go on standing pilot systems, and not be installed where the appliance lacks a redundant gas valve unless one is added. The manual also says the damper must be wired into the appliance burner control circuit and installed by a qualified agency under the maker's instructions and local codes. Burnham states that unlisted vent dampers in the vent pipe are prohibited and that the supplied damper must not block another appliance's vent. Weil-McLain says the damper must sit directly on the draft hood so that it serves only that boiler. For how venting is classified, see the vent categories lesson linked below.

This lesson does not cover water heater dampers beyond this: the same rules of listing and maker instructions apply, and a water heater with a draft hood is a separate backdraft question, covered in the water heater lesson linked below.

What savings can you expect?

The makers are careful here. The Slant/Fin guide gives no percentage and lists factors that change fuel savings: local climate, plant size compared with the home's heat loss, chimney height and diameter, thermostat settings, wind and the number of zones. The Field Controls manual says only that a damper will significantly reduce off-cycle venting losses, with no figure. The HUD page says vent dampers reduce heating costs by approximately 5 to 8 percent, with better results on larger, older, oversized boilers.

Worked example (invented numbers)

Suppose an old atmospheric boiler uses 900 therms of gas a year for heat, and gas costs $1.60 per therm. Use the HUD range of 5 to 8 percent.

  1. Low end: 900 therms x 0.05 = 45 therms saved.
  2. High end: 900 therms x 0.08 = 72 therms saved.
  3. Low dollars: 45 therms x $1.60 = $72.00 per year.
  4. High dollars: 72 therms x $1.60 = $115.20 per year.

So the invented savings range is about $72 to $115 a year. A different boiler, climate or chimney can land outside this range, as the maker's list of factors says. The motor and end switch also have to keep working, since a failed one costs a service call.

Field card: Automatic Vent Dampers

No open damper, no burner. Never jump it.

  • A damper closes the vent in the off cycle to cut heat loss up the chimney (DOE).
  • Order: call for heat, damper opens, end switch closes, burner fires. Burner stops first, then the damper closes.
  • Typical motor damper travel time is about 15 seconds each way (Field Controls).
  • Check the harness plug at both ends before suspecting parts.
  • Use only a damper listed for the appliance, installed to the maker's instructions.
  • Bypassing the damper can let carbon monoxide into the house.
  • Savings: HUD says about 5 to 8 percent; makers give no figure.
