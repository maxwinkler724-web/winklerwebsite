Commercial Building Air Leakage Testing: Whole-Building Pressure Tests, CFM75 and HVAC

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house gets a blower door. A large commercial building gets a bank of big fans in its doorways, a long list of sealed openings, and a number written as CFM75 per square foot. HVAC techs are often the people who close the dampers and seal the intakes that make that number honest. This lesson explains the test, the targets, and what the result means for the systems you service.

Check the project documents

Check the project documents. Test boundaries, pass limits and shutdown steps come from the project specification, the test plan and the testing agency. Targets change with each edition of a code or owner standard. This page is general education built from public documents read in 2026. Follow the written test plan for your job.

Why leakage matters to HVAC

Every crack in the envelope is an uncontrolled outdoor air opening. That air arrives unfiltered, unconditioned and not counted in the ventilation design. A 2005 NIST report (NISTIR 7238) ran annual energy simulations on an office, a retail building and an apartment building in five climates. At a target airtightness of 1.2 L/s per square meter at 75 Pa, the heating and cooling energy cost savings ranged from 3 percent to 36 percent, with the smallest savings in Phoenix and Miami. The gas savings were the biggest in cold climates. In the apartment building, tightening raised electric use by about 9 to 14 percent in cooler climates, because less outdoor air meant less free cooling against internal gains. Leakage is not always a pure penalty, but it is never controlled.

The same report found that the office and retail buildings, whose systems brought in outdoor air whenever the zone was occupied, saw average infiltration fall by roughly 83 percent at the target level, while the apartment building, with no mechanical outdoor air, fell only about 64 percent and kept more infiltration. Our reading: a building with a working outdoor air system is partly pressurized by it, and that changes how much air the envelope leaks. The NIST report does not model humidity. It does mention moisture damage to envelope parts as a general concern tied to infiltration. In humid climates, our own reasoning is that every cubic foot of outdoor air that leaks in carries its moisture with it, which the cooling coil then has to remove.

How a whole-building test works

The method behind most commercial tests is ASTM E779 (or the similar ASTM E1827). Fans pressurize or depressurize the building to a series of induced pressure differences across the envelope, and the technician records airflow at each step. The result is reported at 75 Pa, about 0.3 inches of water column, which is why you see the label CFM75. Divide that airflow by the area of the envelope and you get cfm per square foot at 75 Pa.

The City of Fort Collins publishes a commercial test protocol based on the Army Corps of Engineers approach and ASTM E779. Its main points:

  • Test fans are usually mounted in exterior doors. Interior pressure differences must stay within 10 percent of the induced envelope pressure, so at 75 Pa no two points inside may differ by more than 7.5 Pa. Interior bottlenecks can force fans to be spread around the building.
  • The minimum induced pressure for a valid test is 75 Pa, and the protocol says not to exceed 85 Pa.
  • Both pressurization and depressurization are run, with at least 12 points each way, unless the building needs more than 125,000 CFM at 75 Pa, in which case one direction may be used. The result is the average of the two directions.
  • Wind and baseline pressure matter. The starting baseline pressure must not exceed 30 percent of the lowest induced pressure.
  • The data must have an r-squared above 0.98 and a pressure exponent between 0.45 and 0.80, and the 95 percent confidence interval must be 0.02 or less for results of 0.25 or less.

The envelope area in the denominator is the surface enclosed by the continuous air barrier, including roof or ceiling, walls and floor, according to the Army Corps of Engineers bulletin ECB 2009-29. The designer of record sets the boundary, so ask where it is drawn before you do any math.

What HVAC techs do during a test

The test measures the envelope, not the mechanical system, so the system has to be taken out of the picture. The Fort Collins protocol says to:

  • disable the HVAC system and exhaust fans, and disable recirculating air handlers if needed;
  • seal, mask or close dampers at outdoor air and makeup air intakes and at ventilation and exhaust outlets;
  • turn off vented combustion appliances or set them to pilot;
  • fill plumbing traps with water;
  • remove one ceiling tile per 500 square feet in suspended ceiling plenums, and prop open interior doors inside the boundary.

The Army Corps bulletin takes a design view of the same issue. It calls for dampers and controls that close all ventilation or make-up air intakes and exhausts when leakage can occur during inactive periods. The point for a tech: a damper that does not seal tight is part of the envelope leakage, so a stuck or leaky outdoor air damper can sink an otherwise good test. Work from the written test plan and tag every item you shut down so it is restored afterward. Reopen dampers, restore fan and combustion appliance settings, and check that nothing was left in test mode.

Targets from public sources

  • Army Corps of Engineers: ECB 2009-29 requires leakage not to exceed 0.25 cfm per square foot of envelope at 0.3 inches of water (75 Pa). It also calls for infrared thermography and corrective work to meet the whole-building rate. Fort Collins uses the same 0.25 limit.
  • GSA: a 2024 GSA P100 training deck on the building enclosure shows a 0.17 cfm per square foot enclosure airtightness baseline, tighter than the previous 0.25. The slides state no test pressure or test method, so check the current P100 for the details. A 2022 Building Enclosure magazine article lists the 2021 GSA P100 at 0.25.
  • Energy codes: the same article lists 0.40 cfm75 per square foot for ASHRAE 90.1-2019 and the 2021 IECC (International Energy Conservation Code), which name ASTM E779, E1827 and, for the IECC, ASTM E3158 among accepted methods. Its rough guide: about 0.60 is typical of a leaky building, and 0.25 is excellent.
  • Measured buildings: NIST holds data on 228 U.S. commercial buildings with an average of 25 m3/h per m2 at 75 Pa. That is a little over 10 percent tighter than the average reported in 1998. By our own conversion that is about 1.37 cfm per square foot, several times the codes above.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assumptions: a one-zone warehouse office building with an envelope area (roof, walls and floor inside the air barrier) of 60,000 square feet, a target of 0.25 cfm75 per square foot, and test fans that each deliver 6,000 cfm at 75 Pa. The fan rating is an assumption for the example, not a product rating.

  1. Allowed leakage = 60,000 sq ft x 0.25 cfm/sq ft = 15,000 cfm at 75 Pa.
  2. The Fort Collins protocol suggests planning fan capacity at 0.30 cfm per square foot for a single-zone building: 60,000 x 0.30 = 18,000 cfm.
  3. Fans needed = 18,000 / 6,000 = 3 fans, before allowing for door locations and interior bottlenecks.
  4. For comparison, a building at 0.60 would leak 60,000 x 0.60 = 36,000 cfm at 75 Pa. That is 6 fans at the same rating, and the building fails the 0.25 limit by 21,000 cfm.

Our own note: if the fans can only reach 12,000 cfm at 75 Pa, the test cannot show whether the building meets the 15,000 cfm limit, so more fan capacity or another door location is needed.

Pressure balance: outdoor air versus exhaust

In normal operation, the pressure inside depends on the difference between supply and exhaust airflow. When outdoor air plus transfer air exceeds exhaust, the building goes slightly positive and air leaks out through cracks. When exhaust exceeds outdoor air, the building goes negative and the envelope pulls air in. That is our general explanation. The NIST apartment case fits it: with no mechanical outdoor air there was no pressurization effect, and infiltration stayed higher. Stack and wind effects add their own pressures; see Stack Effect and Building Pressure in Tall Buildings. A tight envelope makes pressure control easier, because small airflow differences then produce a measurable pressure difference you can set and hold. Ventilation airflow itself is covered in Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics, and economizer behavior in Economizer Settings.

Using IR scans to find leaks

If the building fails, the Fort Collins protocol has the testing agency run a diagnostic evaluation with infrared thermography, smoke, or both. The Army Corps bulletin requires infrared thermography as part of its testing section. With the building under test pressure and a real indoor to outdoor temperature difference, our own field experience is that leaks usually show up as cold or warm streaks on inside surfaces, such as roof deck joints, wall to slab edges, penetrations and damper frames, depending on the season. The GSA deck also lists IR imaging and smoke pencil checks during construction inspections to catch defects early. Fixing gaps while the wall is open is far cheaper than after finishes go up. The home version of this process is described in What Happens in a Home Energy Audit, and ducts are tested separately; see Duct Leakage Testing.

Why this matters for study

Expect to hear CFM75, air barrier and envelope area on commissioning jobs. Know the three jobs of the HVAC trade at a test: close and seal what the test plan names, keep a list so everything is restored, and understand that a tight envelope changes the loads, humidity and pressure behavior of the systems you set up later.

Field card: Commercial Building Air Leakage Testing

Seal the openings, hold 75 Pa, divide by envelope area.

  • Result is CFM75 per square foot of envelope area at 75 Pa (about 0.3 in. w.c.).
  • Army Corps limit is 0.25 cfm75/sq ft; codes name 0.40 in the 2021 IECC and ASHRAE 90.1-2019.
  • Fans go in doors; interior pressure differences stay within 10 percent of the induced pressure.
  • HVAC tasks: shut down fans, seal outdoor air, makeup air and exhaust openings, handle combustion appliances per the test plan.
  • Restore everything after the test and note it on the checklist.
  • More outdoor air than exhaust pushes the building positive; the reverse pulls infiltration in.
  • Find leaks with IR and smoke, then repair and retest.
  • Always follow the written test plan and the current standard.
