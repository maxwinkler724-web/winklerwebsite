Rabbits, Guinea Pigs and Hamsters at Home: Heat Limits, Cage Placement, Ammonia, Outages and a Warm-Up Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Small caged pets live in one spot, at one height, in whatever air the house gives them. They cannot walk to a cooler room. That makes the heating and cooling system part of their care. This lesson covers heat limits, where to put a cage, cage air, filters, cords, and a plan for the day the AC quits.

Why small pets need a plan

A federal agriculture agency's animal welfare sheet on guinea pigs says to keep them between 60 and 85 degrees F, says young animals regulate body temperature less well, and warns that heat stroke can come on fast and be fatal. It names heat vents, direct sun, radiators and heat lamps as places to keep them away from. A state extension article on hot weather and rabbits says rabbits may show signs of overheating above 85 degrees F, and notes that a rabbit kept indoors with continuous air conditioning needs no extra cooling steps. The key words are "continuous" and "indoors." A house that is warm when no one is home, or a room with a sunny window, is not the same as steady AC.

We did not find a federal or extension sheet with numbers for hamsters and gerbils. Our own rule of thumb: treat 85 degrees F at cage height as a ceiling for all four animals, and treat anything near it as a reason to act, not to wait. Ask your veterinarian for limits for your animal, especially for young, old, or ill pets.

Where the cage goes

Look at the room the way a cage sees it, from about knee height or lower, and check these four things.

  • Sun. A sunny window can heat a cage in minutes. Sun through glass can leave a room feeling fine to you while it is hot where the cage is. The worked example below puts numbers on it.
  • Supply registers. A register blows air that is much warmer or cooler than the room. Keep the cage out of that stream and do not point a register at it. See the reptile tank lesson for the same idea.
  • Radiators and space heaters. The agency sheet above says to keep guinea pigs away from radiators. A federal consumer product safety agency says to keep a space heater about three feet from bedding, curtains, furniture and other things that burn. A cage with bedding and hay counts, so give heaters at least that much room.
  • Drafts. A state extension publication on domestic rabbits says housing should be dry and free of drafts. A leaky door, an old window or a gap under an exterior door can cool one side of a cage on a cold night. The agency sheet also notes that opening windows in cold weather can cool a room quickly.

An interior wall, out of direct sun, away from all of the above, is usually the best spot. Moving a cage is an easy fix that costs nothing. Our own advice: move it gradually if the animal is nervous, and never carry a hot animal through a hot house; move it to the cooler spot first.

Ammonia smell, ventilation and air fresheners

Urine in bedding releases ammonia. The state extension rabbit publication says that if you notice an ammonia smell, or moisture condensing on walls and windows, the ventilation rate must go up. The same logic applies at home. A cage that smells sharp is telling you that bedding is wet and the air around it is not moving enough.

Our own advice is that cleaning and fresh air beat fragrance every time. Remove wet bedding and soiled corners, keep the cage dry, and give the room some air movement (a cracked window in mild weather, a bath fan run in the next room, a ceiling fan on low that does not blow on the cage). A federal environmental agency lists air fresheners among household products that give off volatile organic compounds, and advises more ventilation when using such products, following label directions, and keeping products away from pets. A scent covers the smell and adds chemicals. It does not fix the wet bedding, so skip aerosols and plug-ins in the pet room.

Bedding dust, fur and the return filter

Hay, shavings and fur become airborne when you clean or when animals dig. Some of it ends up on the return grille or the filter. That part is our own idea, not from a source: a cage in the same room as a return grille tends to load the filter faster. Plan to check the filter more often (monthly is a fair habit when you keep pets), vacuum around the return grille, and keep the cage out of the path of airflow into it. Our dog and cat lesson covers hair on filters and coils in more detail, and the general pets lesson covers more of the system.

Chewed cords and wires

Rabbits, hamsters and gerbils gnaw. Our own advice: route every cord of a fan, heater, lamp or thermostat out of reach, behind furniture or in a rigid cover, and do not leave a loose animal alone in a room with cords on the floor. If you find a chewed cord, unplug it at the outlet only if you can do so without touching bare wire; otherwise turn off the circuit at the panel, and replace the cord or the device. Do not tape over damage and keep using it.

A plan for AC failures and outages

Write the plan before the hot day. This is our own checklist.

  1. Know the coolest room in the house, usually an interior room on the lowest floor, and clear a spot there for the cage.
  2. Keep a thermometer at cage height, and read it when you leave and when you return. A thermometer with a high alarm or a phone alert is better, and a simple min and max thermometer shows what happened while you were out.
  3. Set the thermostat while away to a temperature that keeps cage height well below 85 degrees F, not to an energy-saving setback that lets the house climb on a hot afternoon. If you use a smart thermostat, set a high-temperature alert if it has one.
  4. Shade the south and west windows before you leave on a sunny day.
  5. In an outage or failure, move the cage to the cool room, open shaded windows if outdoor air is cooler than indoor air, and put frozen water bottles next to the animal, as the extension article suggests for rabbits (our own tip: wrap them in a towel). Give fresh water and check it often. Do not mist a rabbit heavily or soak it.
  6. Have a plan for someone to check on pets if you are away, including how to reach your veterinarian.

When power returns, see restart checks after an outage, and call a technician if cooling does not come back. If an animal is limp, panting hard or very hot to the touch, move it somewhere cooler and call a veterinarian right away.

Worked example (our own): how fast a closed room warms after the AC stops

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement and not a source. It is a rough estimate to show the size of the effect.

Assumptions. A bedroom is 10 ft by 12 ft with an 8 ft ceiling and starts at 76 degrees F with the AC off. Outdoor air is 95 degrees F. One unshaded south window is 3 ft by 4 ft, and we assume it lets in about 75 Btu per hour per square foot of glass at midday (an assumed incoming sun value times an assumed glass factor of 0.5). The room's walls, ceiling and door leak heat in at 40 Btu per hour for each degree F of difference from outdoors. The air, furniture, bedding and cage together act like 150 Btu per degree F of heat storage. The danger line is 85 degrees F at cage height.

Step 1, sun through the window. Glass area = 3 x 4 = 12 sq ft. Solar gain = 12 x 75 = 900 Btu per hour.

Step 2, heat through the room's surfaces. At the start the difference is 95 - 76 = 19 degrees F, so the gain is 40 x 19 = 760 Btu per hour. At 85 degrees F the difference is 10, so the gain is 400 Btu per hour. Average of the two = (760 + 400) / 2 = 580 Btu per hour.

Step 3, total. Average gain = 900 + 580 = 1,480 Btu per hour.

Step 4, heat needed to climb from 76 to 85. Rise = 9 degrees F. Storage = 150 x 9 = 1,350 Btu.

Step 5, time. 1,350 / 1,480 = 0.91 hour, about 55 minutes.

Check with shade. If a blind or shade cuts the window gain in half (an assumption), solar = 450 Btu per hour. Average gain = 450 + 580 = 1,030 Btu per hour. Time = 1,350 / 1,030 = 1.31 hours, about 79 minutes.

What it means. With the assumed room, the room can reach the 85 degree line in under an hour with the blinds open, and in about an hour and 20 minutes with them half-shaded. A real cage in the direct sun beam is hotter than the room, so the estimate is the best case for that spot. A bigger room, more thermal mass, or cooler weather would slow the rise; a smaller room or hotter day would speed it. The point is the order of magnitude: a "short" outage on a sunny afternoon can matter to a small animal.

Field card: Small Caged Pets and Your Heating and Cooling

Cage height is the temperature that counts.

  • Guinea pigs: 60 to 85 degrees F per a federal animal welfare sheet. Rabbits can overheat above 85 degrees F per an extension article.
  • Our own rule: 85 degrees F at cage height is the ceiling for all four animals.
  • Keep cages out of sun, supply air, radiators, space heaters (three feet from things that burn) and drafts.
  • Ammonia smell means wet bedding and too little air. Clean and ventilate; skip air fresheners.
  • Check the return filter more often when pets share the room.
  • Route cords out of reach. Replace chewed cords.
  • Put a thermometer at cage height and write the outage plan before summer.
