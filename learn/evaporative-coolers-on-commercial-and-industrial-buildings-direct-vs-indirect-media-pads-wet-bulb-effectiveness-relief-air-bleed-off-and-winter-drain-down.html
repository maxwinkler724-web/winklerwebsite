Evaporative Coolers on Commercial and Industrial Buildings: Direct vs Indirect, Media Pads, Wet-Bulb Effectiveness, Relief Air, Bleed-Off and Winter Drain-Down

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fixed evaporative cooler on a warehouse, shop or plant uses water turning to vapor to take heat out of a moving airstream. It costs little to run and works well in the right weather. It also has hard limits, and they are set by the wet-bulb temperature, not the dry-bulb reading on the thermostat. This lesson covers the fixed, building-mounted kind. For portable units, see Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers in Warehouses and Server Emergencies.

Wet-bulb depression and effectiveness

Evaporating water pulls heat out of the air, and the air cannot get colder than its wet-bulb temperature this way. The gap between dry bulb and wet bulb is called the wet-bulb depression. It is the room the cooler has to work in. For help taking the readings, see Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field.

Effectiveness (also called saturation effectiveness) says how much of that depression the cooler actually captures. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building-science guide writes it as (entering dry bulb minus supply air temperature) divided by (entering dry bulb minus wet bulb). A cooler at 100 percent would deliver air at the wet-bulb temperature. Real ones do not get there. The same guide reports 75 to 93 percent for newer thick-media units, and treats an aspen-fiber pad as roughly 70 percent in its chart.

Direct, indirect and two-stage

  • Direct. A fan pulls outdoor air through wet media and sends it straight into the space. The air gets cooler and also gains moisture. The Department of Energy says direct units typically lower the air temperature about 15 to 40 degrees F.
  • Indirect. Outdoor air passes through one side of an air-to-air heat exchanger while a separate wet airstream cools the other side. The supply air gains no moisture, and the wet exhaust stream is vented outside. Because the exchanger is not perfect, a federal simulation reference uses 0.67 as a reasonable default effectiveness for a dry-coil indirect stage, lower than a direct pad.
  • Two-stage (indirect/direct). The air is first cooled in the indirect stage without adding moisture, then passes through a wet pad. The second stage starts with cooler air, so the final supply can be colder than a direct unit could make it. The PNNL guide gives 95 percent or greater for this design and says it adds less humidity.

Rigid media pads versus aspen pads

The PNNL guide lists treated cellulose, fiberglass, plastic foam and shredded aspen fibers as common media, usually 8 to 12 inches thick on a direct cooler, with 12-inch media reaching 90 percent or better. Aspen is the loose, inexpensive pad that sags and needs frequent changes. Rigid media is a stiff, corrugated cellulose or synthetic block that holds its shape, gives more wetted surface per inch of depth and resists sagging. Our own plain reading: rigid media suits a building that runs all summer and wants a steady airflow resistance, while aspen suits a small, low-hour shop where first cost matters. Check the maker's data for pressure drop and effectiveness at your face velocity.

Why they struggle on humid Northeast days

The depression shrinks when the air is already moist. The DOE says evaporative coolers are best in low-humidity areas and not suited to humid climates because they add moisture to indoor air. PNNL ties suitability to the 1 percent summer design wet-bulb: at or below 70 degrees F is appropriate, and where it climbs to about 74 degrees F performance suffers and indirect cooling may be better. On a muggy summer day in the Northeast, the wet bulb can be only a few degrees under the dry bulb, which is exactly when you want cooling most. A direct unit then delivers air that is cooler by a few degrees but nearly saturated, which feels clammy. The worked example below shows the numbers.

Relief air: the open exhaust path

A direct cooler pushes 100 percent outdoor air into the space, so the same amount must leave. The DOE advises roughly 1 to 2 square feet of window or vent opening per 1,000 cfm on the downwind side. PNNL's retrofit guidance recommends 2 square feet of unrestricted relief per 1,000 cfm of delivery, bigger if screens or louvers cut the free area, with dampers closed in heating season. In a warehouse that means roof relief hoods, wall louvers or open dock doors. Too little relief raises building pressure, slows airflow and lets humidity build up. If the building also has exhaust fans or makeup air systems, think about how the two interact; see HVLS Fans and Destratification in Warehouses and Gyms for moving air inside a big room.

Bleed-off and water use

Water leaves a cooler two ways: evaporation (the useful part) and bleed-off (water drained to keep minerals from concentrating on the pad). PNNL says a bleed-off valve can add 10 to 50 percent to water use, and describes timed dump-pump systems that empty and refill the pan, for example every 8 to 12 hours of operation, as more efficient. An agricultural extension publication on pad systems suggests starting bleed-off at about 1 gallon per hour per foot of pad length unless the maker says otherwise, with a flow of about three-quarters of a gallon per minute per foot, and says scale cuts evaporation efficiency, so raise the bleed rate if scale shows. The same concentration idea is worked out for towers in Cooling Tower Cycles of Concentration, Blowdown and Makeup Water.

Water treatment and Legionella

A pad system is a warm, wet surface with a fan blowing across it. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says Legionella grows best from about 77 to 113 degrees F, that scale and sediment use up disinfectant and shelter the bacteria, and that stagnation encourages biofilm. Its toolkit lists cooling towers, air washers, misters and humidifiers among devices that can spread droplets, and says such devices should have a water management program even if the building does not. That list does not name pad coolers, so we treat them as a similar case rather than a listed one. Practical habits: keep the sump clean and the pads free of slime, do not let water sit during idle weeks, and follow the maker's treatment advice. The Illinois extension page warns that bleach or bromine can shorten cellulose pad life, so ask the maker before dosing. Compare Cooling Tower Legionella: CDC vs OSHA and Water Management Programs.

Winter drain-down

The City of Portland water bureau lists the steps: disconnect power, shut off the water supply at season end, empty the reservoir (standing water can grow Legionella and freezing can destroy components), and disconnect and drain tubing at its lowest point so it does not freeze and burst. PNNL adds cleaning the pan, removing mineral deposits, closing dampers on ducted units and covering the unit. Inspect before spring restart.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assumptions: a direct cooler with 85 percent effectiveness and 10,000 cfm; an assumed dry day of 95 degrees F dry bulb and 65 degrees F wet bulb; an assumed humid day of 90 degrees F dry bulb and 76 degrees F wet bulb. Formula: supply air temperature = dry bulb minus effectiveness times (dry bulb minus wet bulb).

  • Dry day. Depression = 95 - 65 = 30. Drop = 0.85 x 30 = 25.5. Supply = 95 - 25.5 = 69.5 degrees F.
  • Humid day. Depression = 90 - 76 = 14. Drop = 0.85 x 14 = 11.9. Supply = 90 - 11.9 = 78.1 degrees F, and that air is close to saturated.

Same cooler, but the drop falls from 25.5 to 11.9 degrees F, less than half as much. Using the other types with the same assumptions (indirect 0.67, two-stage 0.95): indirect gives 95 - 0.67 x 30 = 74.9 and 90 - 0.67 x 14 = 80.6; two-stage gives 95 - 0.95 x 30 = 66.5 and 90 - 0.95 x 14 = 76.7. The indirect and two-stage figures treat the effectiveness as applying to the whole unit, which is a simplification.

Water (our own rough estimate, assuming about 1,050 Btu per pound of water evaporated, 8.34 pounds per gallon and the common 1.08 sensible factor): dry day, 1.08 x 10,000 x 25.5 = 275,400 Btu/h, which is about 262 lb/h or 31 gallons per hour of evaporation. Humid day, 1.08 x 10,000 x 11.9 = 128,520 Btu/h, about 122 lb/h or 15 gallons per hour. Add 10 to 50 percent for bleed-off per PNNL. The humid day uses half the water and removes half the heat.

When they suit a warehouse or shop

They fit large, leaky buildings with open doors, high ceilings, heat from processes, workers who need relief from heat more than tight temperature control, and mostly dry summer weather. They fit poorly in tight, humidity-sensitive buildings (paper, electronics, food), in rooms with no relief path, and where summer wet bulbs are high for weeks. Where it is humid, a mix of ventilation, fans and conventional cooling is usually the safer plan.

Field card: Evaporative coolers on commercial and industrial buildings

Supply air = dry bulb minus effectiveness times (dry bulb minus wet bulb).

  • Cooling is limited by wet bulb, not dry bulb.
  • Direct adds moisture; indirect does not; two-stage cools further with less added humidity.
  • Aspen pads sag and need changing; rigid media holds shape and wets more surface.
  • Plan roughly 2 sq ft of free relief area per 1,000 cfm unless the maker says otherwise.
  • Bleed-off can add 10 to 50 percent to water use.
  • Keep sumps clean and do not leave water standing; Legionella grows in warm stagnant water.
  • Winter: power off, water off, drain reservoir and low points, cover.
