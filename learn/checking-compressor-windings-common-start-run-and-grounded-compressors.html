Checking Compressor Windings: Common, Start, Run and Grounded Compressors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Ohming out a compressor means measuring the resistance of its motor windings with the power off. Three readings tell you which terminal is which, whether a winding is open, and whether the motor has shorted to its shell. The meter work is easy. The safety steps before it are the part that keeps you out of trouble.

Check the manufacturer first

Check the manufacturer first. This page is general education built from published service material read in 2026. Resistance values differ by model, so the compressor maker's data for your exact model always wins over anything here.

Step 1: Power off, locked out, proven dead, capacitors discharged

Do this before you touch a terminal. Open the disconnect, lock and tag it, and prove the circuit dead with your meter. Copeland's terminal venting guidance says to confirm every power leg is open, because a system can have more than one power supply, and to use lockout/tagout so power is not restored until the terminal cover or molded plug is back on. Then deal with stored energy. A capacitor can hold a charge after the power is gone, so treat every one as charged until it has been discharged by the method your employer and the manufacturer approve. This lesson does not teach a discharge technique. The full sequence is in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, and the capacitor background is in Capacitors and Contactors. For meter selection and safe habits, see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.

Step 2: Get the wires off the compressor

An ACCA article on motor checkout says to pull the disconnect and remove all wires from the compressor terminals before testing. This matters. Leave the capacitor, relay or contactor connected and you are measuring a parallel path through them, not the winding. It also helps to label each wire as you take it off, because you will need to put it back on the same terminal.

Step 3: Use a real ohmmeter

The same article recommends a good dedicated meter on its lowest resistance range and warns against the ohms scale on a clamp-on ammeter, which it calls not precise enough. Compressor windings are often a few ohms or less, so a small error matters. Touch the probes firmly to clean terminals. Hold the probes only by the insulated parts. Poor contact gives a wrong reading, and a wrong reading leads to a wrong compressor swap.

Step 4: Find common, start and run

On a single-phase compressor the three terminals are Common (C), Start (S) and Run (R). If the terminal markings are missing or you are not sure, measure all three pairs and write the numbers down. Run to start goes through both windings in series, so it reads the highest. Two rules follow, both from the ACCA article:

  • The largest reading is across R and S. The terminal left out of that pair is Common.
  • From Common, the higher of the two remaining readings is Start and the lower is Run.

Then run the sum rule as a check. On a healthy motor, C to S plus C to R equals R to S, within meter error. If the numbers do not add up, one winding is suspect or a contact was poor, so re-measure before you decide.

Worked example: three labeled readings

The ACCA article gives an example with three unmarked terminals called A, B and C, measured in ohms: A to B is 2.5, B to C is 3.0, and A to C is 0.5. Work it step by step.

  1. Find the largest reading. B to C is 3.0, so the pair is R and S, and the terminal left out, A, is Common.
  2. From A, compare the other two readings. A to B is 2.5 and A to C is 0.5. The higher one is Start, so B is Start. The lower one is Run, so C is Run.
  3. Check the sum rule. C to S plus C to R is 2.5 + 0.5 = 3.0, which equals R to S at 3.0. The windings pass the sum check; still compare each reading to the maker's data and test to ground.

These numbers come from the article's example. They are not specifications for any compressor. Compare real readings with the published values for the model, which Copeland's scroll bulletin AE4-1331 points readers to the online product information to find.

Open winding versus an open internal overload

Many compressors have an internal overload that opens the circuit behind Common when the motor gets too hot. If the overload is open, C to R and C to S both read infinity (or OL on a digital meter), exactly like a burned-open winding. The ACCA article says an open overload can take many hours to reset. Copeland's bulletin says that when the internal overload has opened, the compressor must be allowed to cool enough to reset, and it gives no fixed time. So do not condemn the compressor from an open reading on a hot shell. Let it cool, follow the manufacturer's guidance, and measure again. If the readings stay open after a full cool-down, you have found a real open. The ACCA article suggests adding liquid refrigerant to speed cooling. Skip that idea; it is not a manufacturer procedure and is not taught here.

Also ask why it was hot. A tripped overload is a symptom. See Compressor Hums and Won't Start for the causes that trip it, and Compressor Basics and Failure Modes for what failed windings look like.

Winding to ground

Next check that no winding touches the shell. With all wires off, put one probe on bare metal on the compressor (clean the spot if needed) and touch the other to each terminal in turn. The ACCA article checks Start and Run to ground and calls any continuity a short to ground. Check Common too, since the three windings share it. A reading of continuity means the motor is grounded and the compressor is done.

A megohmmeter applies a higher test voltage and can find weaker insulation that a plain ohmmeter misses. Do not make up your own pass number. Copeland's AE4-1331 bulletin refers readers to its separate bulletin AE4-1294 for megohm testing recommendations, so use that or the service manual for your compressor, and follow its test voltage and any precautions. Other makers, such as Tecumseh and Danfoss, publish their own service data. Use the one for your brand.

Terminal venting: why a grounded compressor can hurt you

Copeland describes terminal venting as what happens when a compressor's electrical terminal pin is damaged, weakened or loses its seal. Pressurized oil, refrigerant and debris spray out, and electrical arcing can ignite the spray. Copeland says flames can travel a significant distance and cause serious or fatal burns, and that it can happen even with non-flammable A1 refrigerants. Causes include internal or external electrical faults and shorts. Copeland adds that venting can occur without warning sounds, though sizzling, sputtering or popping means get away at once.

The practical rules, from Copeland: do not reset a breaker or replace a fuse without electrical testing first, because energizing a faulted compressor can cause venting. Do not power the compressor without the terminal cover or molded plug in place, and keep clear while power is applied. Before re-energizing, check the terminal connector for wear, rust or discoloration, check that connections are tight, confirm the compressor is grounded and confirm the breaker or fuse size is right. A winding that tests grounded is a compressor you should not energize.

Three-phase and quick notes

The ACCA article says the windings of a three-phase motor should read equal. Copeland's AE4-1331 allows leg-to-leg differences of up to 30 percent for its three-phase ZP20K5 to ZP31K5 models, so check the maker's allowance for your model.

Field card: Checking Compressor Windings

Lock out, discharge, label, then measure.

  • Open, lock, tag, prove dead. Discharge capacitors by your approved method.
  • Remove every wire from the compressor terminals before measuring.
  • Largest pair is R-S; the odd terminal is Common. Higher from C is Start, lower is Run.
  • Sum rule: C-S plus C-R equals R-S.
  • Open on both C-R and C-S: could be the internal overload. Let it cool and retest.
  • Any continuity from a terminal to the shell means grounded. Do not energize.
  • Use the maker's resistance and megohm data for the exact model.
  • Never run a compressor without its terminal cover or plug.
