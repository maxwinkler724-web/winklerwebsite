Reading Heat Pump Extended Performance Data: Capacity and COP at 47, 17 and 5 F, Balance Point Charts and Sizing Backup Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump's tonnage tells you what it does at a mild outdoor temperature, mostly in cooling. It does not tell you what it delivers on the coldest night. The extended performance table does, and with one chart you can see where the heat pump stops keeping up and how much backup heat has to cover the gap.

Why nameplate tons are not winter capacity

One ton is 12,000 Btu/h, so a 3-ton unit is nominally 36,000 Btu/h. That label is a size class. A heat pump moves heat from outdoors to indoors, and the colder the outdoor air, the less heat each pound of refrigerant can pick up and the less the compressor can move. So heating capacity falls as outdoor temperature falls, while the house needs more heat. Two lines on one chart, one falling and one rising toward each other, are the whole idea of this lesson.

The federal test points

The federal test procedure for central heat pumps is 10 CFR Part 430, Subpart B, Appendix M1 (see Sources). DOE's own guidance on 5 F performance names the heating tests this way: at 47 F, test H1 for single-speed units, H12 for two-capacity units, or H1N for variable-speed units; at 17 F, H3 for single-speed units and H32 for two-capacity or variable-speed units; and an optional test at 5 F, called H42. DOE's cold-climate challenge specification also lists a 35 F test and a 5 F test that measures maximum capacity. The "N" in H1N refers to the nominal speed, and that nominal capacity is the number the challenge specification compares everything against.

Variable-speed units therefore have more than one capacity at each temperature. Read the labels:

  • Rated or nominal capacity: output at 47 F at the nominal compressor speed. This is the label size.
  • Maximum capacity: output with the compressor at its top speed at a given outdoor temperature. This is the line you use for sizing against a design day.
  • Minimum capacity: output at the lowest speed. DOE's challenge specification checks that capacity at 47 F can turn down to at most 70 percent of nominal. Minimum capacity matters for comfort and cycling in mild weather, not for the balance point.

HSPF2 and what it does not tell you

HSPF2 is the seasonal heating efficiency rating measured with the revised DOE test procedure required since January 1, 2023, according to a DOE standards FAQ. The same FAQ lists 7.5 HSPF2 as the minimum for split-system heat pumps. It is a seasonal average built from several test points and an assumed climate. It is useful for comparing efficiency, but one HSPF2 number cannot show you capacity at 5 F or whether the unit meets your house load on a design day. For that you need the capacity and COP values at each temperature.

COP (coefficient of performance) is heating output divided by electrical input in the same units. DOE's 5 F guidance divides capacity in Btu/h by input power converted with 3.412 Btu/h per watt. A COP of 2.4 means 2.4 units of heat for each unit of electricity.

What the cold-climate benchmark asks for

DOE's cold-climate heat pump challenge specification (version 1.2) asks for units with 24,000 to 65,000 Btu/h nominal capacity to show, at 5 F, maximum heating capacity of at least 100 percent of nominal 47 F capacity and a COP of at least 2.4 (2.1 above 48,000 Btu/h). It also sets low-temperature compressor cut-out no higher than -10 F. A unit that only meets a minimum federal efficiency standard has no such requirement, so its 5 F capacity can be well below the label. Always read the actual table.

Field data gives a reality check. A PNNL report on 22 monitored challenge prototypes in occupied homes found lab-tested capacity at or above nominal at 5 F, and a field median COP of 1.9 in the 0 to 5 F bin. It also noted that average heat pump output in the field was much lower than the maximum capacity at many sites, because most hours are milder than the design day, and it observed switchover to auxiliary heat around 30 to 35 F, which the report attributes mainly to control settings and occupant behavior rather than unit limits. Those are prototype units in a limited number of homes, so treat them as an example, not a promise.

Drawing the capacity line and the heat loss line

  1. Plot the capacity points. From the table, put maximum heating capacity at 47, 17 and 5 F on a chart with outdoor temperature across the bottom and Btu/h up the side. Connect the points with straight lines. This is a simplification: the real curve is smooth, and DOE's own 5 F guidance also uses straight-line interpolation between 17 and 47 F when no 5 F test exists.
  2. Plot the house heat loss line. A room-by-room heat loss calculation gives the load at the outdoor design temperature. Heat loss is roughly proportional to the temperature difference, so draw a straight line from that design point up to zero load at the indoor temperature you assume. This ignores solar and internal gains, so the real load line bends toward zero a little lower.
  3. Find the crossing. Where the capacity line meets the loss line is the balance point. Warmer than that, the heat pump alone covers the house. Colder, the house needs more than the heat pump can deliver, and backup heat (electric strips, or a furnace in a dual-fuel setup) makes up the difference.
  4. Size the backup at design. The shortfall at the design temperature is load minus heat pump capacity. Convert Btu/h to kW by dividing by 3,412.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is an example we made up to show the arithmetic. It is not data for any real unit or house.

Assumed heat pump (3-ton nominal, variable speed), maximum capacity: 38,000 Btu/h at 47 F, 34,000 Btu/h at 17 F, 30,000 Btu/h at 5 F. Assumed house: 36,000 Btu/h heat loss at a 5 F outdoor design temperature with 70 F indoors, and zero load at 70 F.

Step 1, heat loss slope. The design temperature difference is 70 - 5 = 65 F. 36,000 / 65 = 553.8 Btu/h per degree F. The loss line is 553.8 x (70 - T). Check: at 17 F it is 29,354 Btu/h, and at 47 F it is 12,738 Btu/h.

Step 2, which capacity segment. At 17 F the heat pump (34,000) beats the load (29,354). At 5 F the heat pump (30,000) is short of the load (36,000). So the crossing lies between 5 and 17 F.

Step 3, capacity slope there. (34,000 - 30,000) / (17 - 5) = 333.3 Btu/h per degree F. Capacity at temperature T is 30,000 + 333.3 x (T - 5).

Step 4, set them equal. 30,000 + 333.3 x (T - 5) = 553.8 x (70 - T). That gives 28,333 + 333.3 T = 38,769 - 553.8 T, so 887.1 T = 10,436 and T = 11.8 F. Check: capacity 32,254 Btu/h and load 32,254 Btu/h.

Step 5, backup at design. 36,000 - 30,000 = 6,000 Btu/h. 6,000 / 3,412 = 1.76 kW. So the example house needs about 1.8 kW of backup heat at the 5 F design day, before any margin, and in practice the installed strip size is a step in a standard kit. Note the unit is also short of the challenge benchmark at 5 F (30,000 is under 36,000 nominal), which is why the gap exists.

The gap is zero at 11.8 F, then grows about 887 Btu/h for each degree colder, reaching 6,000 Btu/h at 5 F. Below the 5 F point the capacity table has no data, so do not extend the line without the manufacturer's numbers.

Where to find cold-climate data

  • DOE Cold-Climate Heat Pump Challenge specification for the 47 F, 17 F and 5 F test conditions and the capacity and COP targets.
  • DOE's heat pump data repository, which publishes minute-by-minute and hourly field data from an NREL study of 12 ducted variable-speed heat pumps, most in Washington State, with calculated capacity, COP and operating modes such as auxiliary heating and defrost.
  • National lab field reports from NREL and PNNL on cold-climate field performance, and the NREL study's own comparison of calculated design heating load against manufacturer maximum capacity.

Always use the performance table for the exact model and the exact indoor coil and airflow, not a generic curve. For strip kW, breakers and staging see Heat Pump Backup Heat Strips. For a gas furnace as backup, see Dual-Fuel Heat Pump Changeover. For how some designs hold capacity in the cold, see the vapor injection lesson.

Field card: Reading Heat Pump Extended Performance Data

Plot capacity, plot load, read the crossing.

  • Nameplate tons describe the 47 F nominal size, not capacity at 5 F.
  • Variable-speed tables list nominal, maximum and minimum capacity. Use maximum for design day sizing.
  • Federal tests use 47 F and 17 F points, with an optional 5 F test. HSPF2 is a seasonal average only.
  • Balance point: where the capacity line meets the house heat loss line.
  • Backup needed at design = load minus heat pump capacity, then divide Btu/h by 3,412 for kW.
  • Example (assumed): balance point 11.8 F, backup 1.76 kW at 5 F.
  • Use the table for the exact model and airflow.
