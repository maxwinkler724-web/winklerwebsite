Heating and Cooling a Barndominium or Steel Post-Frame House

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A barndominium is a house built in a steel and post-frame shell: tall rooms, big doors, lots of glass and metal panels on the roof and walls. It heats and cools like no other house. This lesson is about the living space, not the shop. The order matters: stop the metal from sweating first, then deal with the tall air volume, then pick equipment.

Why steel panels sweat and drip

Warm indoor air carries water vapor. When that air touches a surface colder than it can stay dry against, the vapor turns to water. A federal environmental agency page on moisture in homes says condensation on cold surfaces is a sign of high humidity and suggests drying the surface quickly and cutting the moisture source. An extension guide on controlling condensation explains that warm, moist air moves toward cold spots and condenses when it reaches them.

A bare steel roof is about the coldest surface in a house on a winter night, and in summer a hot roof can drive moisture the other way. If warm indoor air can reach the underside of the panels, you get drips, wet insulation, rust and mold. That is why the first job is a continuous layer of insulation with an air and vapor control layer on the warm side. The same extension guide says a vapor barrier belongs on the warm side and that barriers on both sides can trap moisture. Gaps, staples and unsealed seams let air carry vapor through, so air sealing matters as much as the insulation thickness. A related steel cabin lesson covers the same idea at small scale, and the shop planning lesson covers it before the panels go up.

Plan this before the walls are closed. Fixing a sweating roof after the interior is finished usually means tearing the interior out.

Tall great rooms: stratification and air volume

Warm air is lighter than cool air, so in a tall open room it collects near the ceiling while the floor stays cooler. This is called stratification (our own plain-language description). In winter the thermostat on the wall reads the cool air near the floor, so the equipment runs while the heat pools overhead. Tall rooms also hold a lot of air. Every cubic foot of that air leaks and has to be heated or cooled.

Worked example (our own, assumed round numbers)

This is our own example, not a published figure. A common rule of thumb for heat lost with leaking air is: heat loss (Btu/h) = 0.018 x volume (ft3) x air changes per hour x temperature difference (F). The 0.018 comes from air density times the heat it takes to warm air (our own arithmetic: about 0.075 lb/ft3 x 0.24 Btu/lb-F). Assumptions: a 40 x 60 ft great room, a floor of 2,400 ft2, the same 0.5 air changes per hour in both cases, and a 50 F difference between inside and outside.

  • Case A, 14 ft ceiling: volume = 40 x 60 x 14 = 33,600 ft3. Loss = 0.018 x 33,600 x 0.5 x 50 = 604.8 x 0.5 x 50 = 15,120 Btu/h.
  • Case B, 8 ft ceiling: volume = 40 x 60 x 8 = 19,200 ft3. Loss = 0.018 x 19,200 x 0.5 x 50 = 345.6 x 0.5 x 50 = 8,640 Btu/h.

Difference: 15,120 minus 8,640 is 6,480 Btu/h. Case A loses 6,480 / 8,640 = 0.75, or 75 percent more than case B. Said the other way, case B loses about 43 percent less (6,480 / 15,120 = 0.4286). The check: volume goes up by 14 / 8 = 1.75, and every other input is the same, so the loss must go up by 1.75 times, which is 75 percent. Real houses will differ: the leak rate depends on how tight the shell is, and tall rooms with large doors often leak more, not the same. Treat this as a way to see the effect, not a sizing method. A proper load calculation is still needed before choosing equipment.

Big doors and glass

Large overhead or sliding doors and big glass areas are weak spots for both leakage and temperature swings. Specify tight gaskets, insulated doors and glass suited to your climate, and keep these openings in mind when you place supply air, so a cold glass wall does not leave you with a cold seating area. A ceiling fan lesson covers how fans move air in tall rooms: on a low speed in winter a fan can pull warm air down gently, and in summer it adds a breeze that makes the room feel cooler (our own summary; read the lesson for details).

Equipment options

  • Ducted heat pump with ducts inside the insulated space. Ducts run in cold roof spaces or unconditioned areas lose heat and can sweat too. In a barndominium, keep the duct work inside the insulated and air-sealed envelope, for example in a dropped soffit. Place returns low as well as high so the system picks up cool air from the floor level too (our own guidance).
  • Ductless heads, one per zone. Good for a great room, a bedroom wing or a loft that is hard to duct. Each head serves one open area, so plan the air path to rooms that have no head.
  • Radiant slab. Tubing in or under the slab warms people and surfaces from below, which suits tall rooms because it does not depend on moving air. An extension publication on farm shops notes that hydronic tubing in the slab needs good perimeter insulation because the soil draws off heat. Radiant floors do not cool, so many homes pair them with a ductless or ducted cooling system.

No option is best for every house. Climate, insulation, floor plan and budget decide it, and a load calculation should come first.

Fresh air, kitchen and bath exhaust

A tight, well-insulated shell traps moisture and cooking and bathing byproducts. The federal environmental agency moisture page advises running bathroom fans when showering and kitchen exhaust when cooking, and keeping indoor humidity between 30 and 50 percent, and below 60 percent at most. A second federal page on indoor air says to use a range hood that vents outdoors when cooking on a gas stove. Vent these fans outside, never into the roof or wall space; moist exhaust blown into the shell defeats everything above.

When fans pull air out, the same amount must come in. In a tight house, plan that supply on purpose, for example a ducted fresh-air intake or balanced ventilation, rather than letting make-up air leak in through the roof and wall panels. See bathroom exhaust fans and whole-house ventilation choices.

A shop or garage under the same roof

Many barndominiums put a shop or garage under the same roof as the bedrooms. Treat it as a separate space: an air-sealed, insulated wall and a tight, self-closing door between the shop and the living space, no supply or return duct that serves both, and no shared air path to the bedrooms (our own guidance). Vehicles and fuel-burning equipment make carbon monoxide. A federal consumer product safety page says CO alarms should be on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and that they should be tested monthly. The federal indoor air page lists gas appliances, fireplaces, space heaters and flues with problems among CO sources. Our own advice: if any fuel-burning equipment or vehicles are in the building, put alarms in the living space even if you think the separation is good. See attached garages and your home for the details.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Barndominium or Steel Post-Frame House

Seal and insulate first, then equipment.

  • Steel sweats when warm, moist room air reaches it; continuous insulation plus an air and vapor control layer come first.
  • Warm air stratifies under a tall ceiling; tall rooms also hold more air to condition.
  • Our own example: 14 ft ceiling vs 8 ft, same floor: 15,120 vs 8,640 Btu/h, 75 percent more.
  • Keep ducts inside the insulated space, or use ductless heads or a radiant slab.
  • Vent kitchen and bath fans outdoors and plan fresh air supply.
  • Separate the shop or garage from bedrooms; put CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas.
