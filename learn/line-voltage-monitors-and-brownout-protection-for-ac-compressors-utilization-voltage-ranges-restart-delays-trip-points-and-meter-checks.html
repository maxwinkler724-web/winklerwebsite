Line Voltage Monitors and Brownout Protection for AC Compressors: Utilization Voltage Ranges, Restart Delays, Trip Points and Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A brownout does not blow anything up. The lights dim, the line voltage sags, and a compressor keeps trying to work on power that is too weak to do it comfortably. A line voltage monitor is a small control that watches the supply and opens the contactor coil circuit when the voltage leaves a safe window. This lesson covers why low voltage hurts a motor, what the standard voltage ranges say, how the monitor is wired, how its settings work, and how to check it with a meter.

Why low voltage raises current and heat

A motor is asked for a certain amount of work. If the voltage drops, the motor still has to deliver that work, so it draws more current to make up the difference. A federal motor tip sheet states the result for general-purpose motors run at 90 percent of nameplate voltage: full-load current rises about 5 to 10 percent, efficiency falls about 1 to 3 percent, and starting and maximum running torque fall about 19 percent. It adds that torque drops with the square of the voltage ratio and that higher current raises winding losses and can cause overheating at rated load (Source 1). A second tip sheet says undervoltage can produce increased currents, reduced starting torque and lower efficiency (Source 2).

Heat in a winding follows the current squared, so a modest rise in current is a larger rise in heat. These sheets describe general motors, not hermetic compressors, so use them for the direction of the effect and not as compressor limits. The unit's nameplate and the maker's literature give the real operating range.

The standard voltage ranges

The U.S. standard for nominal system voltages, ANSI C84.1, defines two ranges. Range A is the normal window within which service voltages are supposed to be held. Range B is slightly wider and, in the words of a federal rural utilities bulletin that reproduces the table, can be tolerated only for limited frequency and duration (Source 3). The standard separates service voltage (what the utility delivers at the meter) from utilization voltage (what arrives at the equipment, after the wiring in the building has taken its share). The bulletin lists these values for a 120/240 volt single-phase system (Source 3):

ANSI C84.1 limits, 120/240 volt single-phase, 240 volt side
RangeService voltageUtilization voltage
Range A228 to 252 V216 to 252 V
Range B220 to 254 V208 to 254 V

For a 120 volt load the same table gives a utilization window of 108 to 126 volts in Range A and 104 to 127 volts in Range B (Source 3). A utility bulletin says that when voltages sit in Range B during normal conditions or for an extended time, the utility should correct it promptly, and voltage outside Range B calls for correction as soon as it is found (Source 4). Lowest-allowed Range A utilization on a 240 volt circuit is 216 volts, which is 10 percent below nominal (our own arithmetic).

What the monitor does and how it breaks the circuit

A single-phase voltage monitor measures the line voltage at the condensing unit. It does not carry compressor current. Instead it has a small relay contact wired in series with the 24 volt control circuit, usually in the Y (cooling call) wire between the thermostat and the contactor coil. When the line voltage is acceptable the contact is closed and the thermostat call passes through. When the voltage falls below the trip point, the contact opens, the coil loses its 24 volts, the contactor drops out and the compressor stops. Installation instructions for one plug-in style protector say to splice the thermostat control wires into Y1 and that the relay output opens on undervoltage until the line voltage returns to the accepted level (Source 5). Hard-wired units work the same way; they are simply mounted in the disconnect or the unit's control section instead of plugging in.

Anti-short-cycle delay after power returns

When power comes back after a brownout or outage, the monitor should not close its contact at the instant the voltage recovers. It waits first. The protector instructions we read give a fixed delay of 3 minutes, with no adjustment, and say the device does not have to be reset after a brownout event (Source 5). An adjustable three-phase monitor datasheet shows a different style: trip delay adjustable from 1 to 30 seconds and restart delay adjustable from 1 to 500 seconds (Source 6). Our own explanation of why the delay matters: a compressor that restarts immediately must start against whatever pressure difference is still in the system, and a supply that is bouncing around can trip and restore repeatedly, which would cycle the compressor over and over. The delay stretches those events into a single, slower restart. For starting trouble see Compressor Won't Start: Hard Start Kits and Potential Relays.

Setting trip points

Many single-phase protectors have fixed trip points. The one we read trips at the Range B utilization minimums: 104 volts on a 120 volt nominal supply, 180 volts on 208 and 208 volts on 240 (Source 5). The adjustable three-phase monitor datasheet lists undervoltage tripping at 90 percent of setpoint, plus or minus 1 percent, resetting at 93 percent, with overvoltage tripping at 110 percent and resetting at 107 percent (Source 6). The gap between trip and reset is intentional: it keeps the contact from chattering when the voltage hovers near the line. If your monitor is adjustable, our own guidance is to set it from the unit's nameplate voltage range and the maker's instructions, not from a guess, and to confirm the setting against the actual supply with a meter.

How this differs from surge protection and phase monitors

Surge protection deals with brief, high-voltage spikes by diverting them. A monitor deals with sustained low or high voltage by opening a contact. The two jobs differ, and the protector we read combines them in one housing while stating that it is not intended to protect against voltage swells (Source 5). See Surge Protection for AC, Heat Pumps and Furnaces. Three-phase phase monitors check more: phase loss, phase sequence and imbalance among the three legs. A single-phase supply has no sequence and no imbalance to measure, so its monitor watches only the voltage level. See Phase Loss and Single-Phasing on Three-Phase HVAC Motors.

Worked example (our own)

These numbers are assumptions for teaching, not a field record. A condensing unit has a nameplate rating of 240 volts single-phase. A meter at the disconnect reads 208 volts with the compressor running.

  1. Drop from nominal: 240 − 208 = 32 volts.
  2. Percent below nominal: 32 ÷ 240 = 0.1333, or 13.3 percent.
  3. Compare with the ranges in Source 3: the Range A utilization minimum is 216 volts, so 208 volts is 8 volts below Range A. The Range B utilization minimum is 208 volts, so this reading sits exactly at the edge of Range B and has no margin left.
  4. Current, as a rough ceiling: if the motor holds the same power, current scales by 240 ÷ 208 = 1.154, about 15 percent more. We state this as an upper-bound simplification. The tip sheet figure of 5 to 10 percent more at 90 percent voltage (Source 1) is for a general motor at a milder sag, so do not read 15 percent as a measurement.
  5. Heat: winding heat follows current squared, 1.154 × 1.154 = 1.33, so up to about 33 percent more heat in the winding under the same simplification.
  6. Torque: the voltage ratio is 208 ÷ 240 = 0.867, and squaring it gives 0.751, so starting torque falls about 25 percent (using the square relation in Source 1).

What it means: the compressor is running at the bottom of the standard's allowed window, drawing more current, running hotter and starting with about a quarter less torque. A monitor with the Range B trip point we described would be right at its trip line, so it may drop the unit in and out. The fix is not to loosen the monitor. Find out whether the sag comes from the utility or from voltage drop in the building wiring; see Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits.

Field checks with a meter under load

These checks are live electrical work. Use a properly rated meter, the required protective equipment and your site's procedures, and stop if you are not trained for it.

  1. Read the nameplate voltage and the stated voltage range.
  2. Measure line to line at the line side of the disconnect with the compressor off, then again while it starts and while it runs. The start reading is the lowest, so measure during the start attempt.
  3. Compare the running reading with the 216 volt Range A and 208 volt Range B marks above, and with the nameplate.
  4. Repeat at the contactor lugs. A big difference between disconnect and contactor points to a connection or wire problem inside the run.
  5. On the 24 volt side, with the thermostat calling and the monitor satisfied, a meter across the monitor contact should read near zero; a reading near 24 volts means the contact is open.
  6. Write down the readings with the time and what else was running. Utility sags often follow the neighborhood's load.

Field card: Line Voltage Monitors and Brownout Protection

Low voltage means more current, more heat, less torque.

  • At 90 percent voltage a general motor draws about 5 to 10 percent more current and loses about 19 percent torque.
  • 240 V circuit, utilization: Range A 216 to 252 V, Range B 208 to 254 V.
  • The monitor contact sits in series with the Y wire and opens the contactor coil circuit.
  • Expect a restart delay after power returns; one protector uses a fixed 3 minutes.
  • A monitor is not a surge protector and is not a three-phase phase monitor.
  • Measure voltage during the start attempt and while running, at the disconnect and at the contactor.
  • Fix the cause of a sag; do not widen the trip point to stop nuisance trips.
