HVAC Isolation and Check Valves: Where Each Fits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every pump room has the same cast of valves: the ones that shut a branch off, the ones that trim flow, and the ones that only let water go one way. Mixing up the jobs is how you get a gate valve chewing itself apart, a pump that slams at shutdown, or a drop in head nobody can find. This lesson sorts the common types by job and puts a real number on what each one costs a pump.

Two jobs: isolate and throttle

Isolating means fully open or fully closed so you can work on equipment downstream. Throttling means holding a partly open position to trim flow. A valve that is good at one job is not automatically good at the other. NIBCO's valve selection guide for building services lists "starting and stopping the flow (isolation)" and "regulating (throttling) the flow" as separate principal functions and rates each valve type against them. Keep that split in mind for the rest of the page.

How the types compare

Gate valve. A wedge or disc slides across the flow. NIBCO's guide calls it a good choice for on-off service with full flow, low pressure drop and flow in either direction, and the "best choice for steam service." It also says gate valves are "not for throttling; use fully opened or fully closed." The University of Colorado Anschutz design standard for general-duty valves goes further for balancing: use eccentric plug, globe or angle valves, "do not use gate valves." The same standard requires gate valves as isolation valves on steam supply and condensate return. Crane's iron valve catalog lists the outside-screw-and-yoke (OS&Y) gate as a rising-stem design with backseats.

Globe valve. The flow turns through the body and passes a seat, so the disc can be positioned finely. NIBCO's guide recommends globe valves for throttling and good for frequent operation, and flags "significant pressure drop due to flow path." Crane says its globe valves suit services needing frequent operation and throttling.

Ball valve. A ball with a hole turns a quarter turn. NIBCO's guide lists bubble-tight shutoff, quick operation and, for full-port versions, pressure drop close to the equivalent length of pipe. It says a ball valve can be throttled depending on the application but is a poor choice for steam flow control. A Milwaukee Valve installation manual warns that throttling a ball valve in the near-closed position "can destroy the valve seats." The Colorado standard accepts ball valves for isolation in hot water and other low-pressure systems on pipe sizes of 2 inches or less.

Butterfly valve. A disc rotates on a stem in the middle of the flow. NIBCO's guide describes resilient-seated butterfly valves as having a broad throttling range and less pressure drop than a globe valve, but it lists steam as a drawback and says sizes above 6 inches need gear operators. Bray's technical manual recommends sizing control valves between 20 and 70 degrees of disc travel, with 60 degrees preferred.

Plug valve. The Colorado standard names eccentric plug valves for balancing and throttling duty on heating and chilled water. We did not find a maker page in this research that explains the mechanism, so this lesson stays with that use.

Lug versus wafer, and dead ends

Both styles sit between two pipe flanges. NIBCO's selection guide says a wafer-body valve held between flanges "cannot be used as isolation valve" in that arrangement, while a lug body has tapped lugs that match the flange bolt circle, so cap screws can go in from either side. That is why the Colorado standard asks for lug-style butterfly valves with cap screws "so the valve can stay in place with one flange removed." Milwaukee Valve's application manual states that its high-performance butterfly valves are suitable for bidirectional dead-end service at full rated pressure. Dead-end rating is a feature of a specific valve, so check the maker's data sheet for the model on the pipe before you pull a downstream flange with the line live.

Check valves and pump discharge

A check valve opens with flow and closes when flow reverses. The Colorado standard says to install check valves on each pump discharge. NIBCO's guide sorts them this way: a swing check has the least pressure drop and closes automatically, but slow closing can cause hydraulic shock. Lift checks (in-line or globe-style) cost more pressure drop, and some are spring-actuated. The spring-actuated in-line lift check is called the "silent check" because the spring closes it before gravity and reversing flow can slam it. A double-door (wafer) check uses two spring-loaded discs and flows better than a lift check.

Watts' water hammer literature describes the problem the same way: reverse flow makes a swing check disc slam on its seat, creating noise and piping stress. It says silent check valves were developed to fix this because the spring brings the disc closed before reverse flow begins, and it suggests mounting a check five to ten pipe diameters from pumps and elbows. For the wider story of steam and hot-water hammer, see Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.

Steam service

NIBCO's guide gives gate valves the "best choice for steam" rating and says ball and resilient butterfly valves are poor fits for steam control. The Colorado standard requires stainless steel gates and seats on steam gate valves and a bonnet drain if a steam valve must be mounted inverted. The bigger hazard is how you open them. A U.S. Department of Energy operating experience summary describes a steam line restart where condensate sitting upstream of an isolation valve flashed when the valve opened, and a flange failed. Its advice: do not introduce steam into piping without verifying no liquid water is present, warm cold lines slowly with trap blowdown valves open, and be careful when cracking valves open. It also records a 1986 fatal incident where steamfitters used an in-line gate valve to drain condensate instead of the installed drains, and a case where a quarter-turn ball valve closed quickly caused a pressure wave.

What each one costs in pressure drop

Makers publish Cv, the gpm of 60°F water that passes with a 1 psi drop; our Cv lesson covers it. A bigger Cv means less loss. For 4 inch valves, Crane's iron valve catalog lists Cv of 1,314 for gate, 202 for globe and 526 for swing check, labeled "for estimating purposes only." Bray lists 1,282 for its 4 inch Series 20/21 butterfly valve fully open. NIBCO's Cv chart (the set used below) lists 945 for an F-619 gate, 195 for an F-718 globe, 790 for an F-510 ball and 660 for a LD/WD-2000 butterfly, plus 2,350 for the F-515 ball. NIBCO's chart warns these are calculated approximations that cannot be used for highly critical flow or pressure drop calculations, and that angle valves use the globe value times 1.25.

Worked example: a 4 inch pump discharge at 300 gpm

Water horsepower is gpm times head in feet divided by 3,960, and head in feet is psi times 2.31, both as given in the University of Nebraska-Lincoln PASSEL irrigation energy lesson. The EPA wastewater math slides give brake horsepower as water horsepower divided by pump efficiency. Our assumption is a 70 percent pump efficiency, chosen for the example. Our own arithmetic follows, for fully open valves and water, using the NIBCO Cv values above.

  1. Pressure drop, dP = (gpm / Cv)².
    • Gate, Cv 945: 300 / 945 = 0.3175; squared = 0.101 psi.
    • Globe, Cv 195: 300 / 195 = 1.538; squared = 2.367 psi.
    • Ball, Cv 790: 300 / 790 = 0.380; squared = 0.144 psi.
    • Butterfly, Cv 660: 300 / 660 = 0.4545; squared = 0.207 psi.
  2. Head, feet = psi × 2.31.
    • Gate: 0.101 × 2.31 = 0.233 ft. Globe: 2.367 × 2.31 = 5.47 ft.
    • Ball: 0.144 × 2.31 = 0.333 ft. Butterfly: 0.207 × 2.31 = 0.477 ft.
  3. Water horsepower, 300 × ft / 3,960.
    • Gate: 300 × 0.233 / 3,960 = 0.0176 hp. Globe: 300 × 5.47 / 3,960 = 0.414 hp.
    • Ball: 300 × 0.333 / 3,960 = 0.0252 hp. Butterfly: 300 × 0.477 / 3,960 = 0.0362 hp.
  4. Shaft horsepower at 70 percent, divide by 0.70.
    • Gate 0.025 hp, ball 0.036 hp, butterfly 0.052 hp, globe 0.59 hp.

The globe valve fully open costs about 5.5 ft of head and roughly 0.59 hp of shaft power at this flow, about 23 times the gate valve. That is why a globe belongs where you need the throttling, not as a casual isolation valve. With the full-port F-515 ball valve (Cv 2,350) the same steps give 0.016 psi, 0.038 ft and about 0.004 hp. Port size matters as much as type. Throttling a valve to trim flow adds head on purpose, so that loss is the price of control.

Keeping them working

A Milwaukee Valve installation manual says if a valve develops a packing leak, adjust the packing nut to increase pressure on the stem packing, about a quarter turn clockwise or until the leak stops, and that valves should not be repacked while under pressure. It lists routine maintenance as packing adjustment and cycling open to closed, and calls periodic cycling of butterfly valves "highly recommended." Work out an interval with your site; we found no maker or agency page that sets one for in-service valves. Bray's manual recommends opening and closing stored valves once every three months. Pump seals and strainers fail in their own ways, covered in Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits.

Locking out valves

A closed valve is only one barrier. An OSHA letter of interpretation defines double block and bleed as closing and locking or tagging two in-line valves and opening and locking or tagging a drain or vent between them. OSHA says it encourages that method and that double valve isolation is not required under the lockout/tagout standard, but it notes energy is more likely to reaccumulate when a single valve controls the line, so verification must continue. Field card: HVAC Isolation and Check Valves

Isolate with the right valve, throttle with the right valve, never mix them up.

  • Gate: open or closed only, not throttling.
  • Globe: throttles, but costs the most head.
  • Ball and butterfly: low loss; ask the maker about throttling and steam.
  • Lug body with cap screws can stay with one flange off; check the data sheet for dead-end rating.
  • Silent check closes before reverse flow; swing check can slam.
  • dP = (gpm / Cv)²; feet = psi × 2.31; whp = gpm × ft / 3,960.
  • Steam: drain, warm slowly, crack valves open.
  • Packing leak: about a quarter turn on the nut, not under pressure to repack.
