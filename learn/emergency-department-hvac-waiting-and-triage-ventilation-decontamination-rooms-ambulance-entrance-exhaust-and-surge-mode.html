Emergency Department HVAC: Waiting, Triage, Decontamination, Ambulance Bay and Surge Mode

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An emergency department never closes, never knows who is coming through the door, and has to change its airflow on short notice. This lesson is the facility tech's view: where the exhaust goes in the front of the department, how a patient decontamination room is held negative, why ambulance fumes find their way inside, and how to switch a wing negative in a surge and prove it worked.

Check the design documents

Check the design documents. Rules differ by state and by the code edition adopted. This page is general education from public pages we read. The drawings, the commissioning report and the hospital's infection-control and emergency-planning staff decide what your building must do. Never change a pressure relationship without their sign-off.

What the standards tables say, and what we could not confirm

ASHRAE/ASHE Standard 170 is the ventilation standard for health care. The standard is not linked here; we name it in plain text. In the public 2021 errata text we read, the Table 8-1 row for a decontamination room reads negative pressure, 2 outdoor air changes per hour (ACH), 6 total ACH, exhausted to the outdoors, not recirculated. Be careful: in that table the room is the instrument-cleaning room, not the place where a contaminated patient is washed. We use it as a floor for the idea, not as the patient decontamination rate.

We could not open the published emergency department rows for waiting, triage, trauma or patient decontamination rooms. We do not state their air change rates here; look them up in the edition your state adopted. What we did read, in a trade-press report on a proposed addendum, is that public waiting areas, triage areas and decontamination rooms in the emergency department would not be allowed to turn down air when unoccupied. Treat that as a reason to leave their schedules alone until the design documents say otherwise.

Waiting and triage: exhaust and air changes

The front of the department is where the sickest unknown patients sit together. CDC's tuberculosis guideline says triage protocols should identify and separate people with suspected infectious disease, and that they should be kept apart from other patients and not in an open waiting area. That is a policy, but it only works if there is somewhere to put them: a room built as an airborne infection isolation room. CDC lists those at 12 or more ACH for new or renovated construction and a negative differential of at least 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 Pa). We cover those rooms, anterooms and monitors in our isolation room lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

For the waiting room itself, the tech's job is simple: keep the supply and exhaust on their own schedule, check the exhaust is actually moving air, and keep doors and vestibules from short-circuiting the pressure. The waiting room, triage and the isolation rooms should be tied to the equipment the design says, so a rooftop unit on a night setback does not leave an exhaust fan pulling the department negative to the outdoors. Our urgent care lesson works the outdoor-air arithmetic for a waiting room and shows what portable HEPA units can and cannot do (CDC says they do not supply fresh air).

Decontamination rooms

OSHA's best-practices guide for hospital first receivers says the hospital decontamination zone typically ends at the department door and that victims should disrobe in the decontamination area, not in the department. It calls for constant fresh air through the decontamination and waiting areas, and says air in enclosed systems should come from a clean source and not be recirculated. It also says contaminated waste water must not become a secondary source of exposure and calls for belongings in a hazardous waste container isolated outdoors. It sets a minimum of 5 minutes under running water. The pages we read did not give an air change rate, a pressure value or a drain design, so those come from the hospital's own plan.

What follows for the tech, as our own reasoning:

  • The room is held negative to the corridor and the department, with its exhaust fan ducted outdoors and no recirculation.
  • The exhaust discharge sits well away from outdoor air intakes. A state health department's guidance on improvised negative rooms asks for at least 10 ft from intakes, operable windows and public walkways. A permanent fan needs the design distance.
  • The room has its own floor drains and collection, not tied to the ordinary sanitary drain unless the plan says so. Plumbing and environmental staff own that, not the HVAC tech.
  • The outside door and the inside door are never open together. Interlocks and door closers are part of the pressure system.

Worked example: sizing a decontamination room

These are our own assumptions, not code values. The room is 250 sq ft with a 10 ft ceiling, and we use a labeled 12 ACH example because it matches the rate CDC gives for isolation rooms.

  1. Volume: 250 x 10 = 2,500 cu ft.
  2. Exhaust at 12 ACH: 12 x 2,500 / 60 = 500 cfm.
  3. Supply with a labeled offset of 10 percent (our own assumption): 500 x 0.90 = 450 cfm.
  4. Offset: 500 - 450 = 50 cfm that must come in from outside the room through door gaps. That inward leak is what makes the room negative.
  5. Comparison, 6 ACH from the instrument-room row above: 6 x 2,500 / 60 = 250 cfm. Half the airflow, and a different room.

A pressure result needs a tight room. A bigger offset only helps if the gap under the door is small enough for the difference to register as pressure. The NIOSH ventilation FAQ says pressure differentials are created by offsetting exhaust and supply flow rates, and its own example uses a 15 cfm difference to make a room negative.

Ambulance entrance: exhaust and intakes

An ambulance can sit at the department door for a while. A hospital facilities engineer wrote in a regional trade society newsletter that an ambulance spends about 20 minutes docked on average, that idling engines send fumes into the building's ventilation, and that exterior doors opening constantly pull in outside air. This matches what we see in fire stations; our apparatus bay lesson covers source capture of diesel exhaust.

What to check, as our own list:

  • Where the outdoor air intakes are compared with where vehicles park and idle. Wind direction changes the answer.
  • Whether the ambulance entrance vestibule is positive or negative to the waiting room. Fumes should not be pulled into the department every time the door opens.
  • Whether any exhaust discharge from a decontamination or isolation room could blow toward the bay or its intakes.

Surge mode: switching a wing negative

ASPR's technical assistance center described a health system converting a 24-bed unit half at a time, using HEPA-filtered air scrubbers that exhaust outdoors, then converting whole units, with fire and life safety review at each change. It also noted a wish to make the pressure change from the building automation system instead of opening ceilings and ductwork. We found no published offset figure there, so the offset below is ours.

Worked example: a 20-bed wing on an 8,000 cfm air handler

  1. Supply to the wing: 8,000 cfm (our assumption).
  2. Exhaust 10 percent more than supply: 8,000 x 1.10 = 8,800 cfm.
  3. Offset: 8,800 - 8,000 = 800 cfm of air that must come into the wing from outside it.
  4. Per bed: 800 / 20 = 40 cfm.

That 800 cfm has to come from somewhere. If the wing is leaky it enters through doors and walls from the neighbors, and those areas lose air and may swing negative too. The added exhaust fan also has to have the capacity and the discharge location to carry it. Both are questions for the engineer before the surge, not during it.

Testing it

The CDC environmental guideline says airflow direction can be checked qualitatively with smoke tubes or flutter strips, and calibrated equipment giving a numerical reading daily is preferred. The state guidance asks for the door to stay closed, leaks to be found and sealed, and the room checked on a schedule in the emergency plan. Our own routine: smoke at the door, reading with a calibrated manometer, and a written log. Return the controls to normal only on the engineer's word. Field card: Emergency Department HVAC

Negative where it is dirty, no shortcuts at the door.

  • Isolation rooms: at least 0.01 in. w.c. negative, 12 ACH or more (CDC); see the isolation lesson.
  • Decon room: negative, exhaust outdoors, no recirculation, own drains.
  • Our example: 250 sq ft x 10 ft, 12 ACH = 500 cfm exhaust, 450 cfm supply.
  • Surge example: 8,000 supply, 8,800 exhaust, 800 cfm offset, 40 cfm per bed.
  • Keep exhaust discharge 10 ft or more from intakes (state guidance for improvised rooms).
  • Check airflow with smoke and a manometer; log it.
