Emergency Shelters and Warming or Cooling Centers: HVAC for a Surge of People

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When the power fails or a heat wave hits, a community gym or parish hall can become a shelter overnight. The building was sized for a basketball game or a dinner, not for a hundred people sleeping and staying for days. This lesson walks through what changes for the HVAC: outdoor air, heating or cooling load, temporary heat, backup power and air quality.

Check the plan first

Check with the people in charge. A county emergency manager, the facility owner and the licensed contractors on the job decide what is allowed at a given site. This page is general education from public guidance, not a design. NJ 211 says its cooling center listings come from state agencies, county offices of emergency management and local governments, and that each operator sets its own hours and site rules.

Warming center, cooling center, shelter

A warming or cooling center is usually a daytime refuge from bad weather. A shelter adds sleeping, sometimes for days. The Red Cross notes that communities often open these centers during outages. The distinction matters for HVAC because sleeping people stay put, so every system runs around the clock. FEMA's Mega-Shelter Planning Guide lists airflow and extra ventilation in high-density areas, and equipment running outside its normal range, as 24 hour concerns, and it plans dormitory floors at a minimum of 40 square feet per person. Red Cross standards, as reproduced in a Florida state plan, give 20 square feet for 24 to 48 hour emergency shelters and 40 to 60 or more for post-disaster shelters.

Surge occupancy and ventilation

Code-minimum ventilation assumes a typical headcount. CDC's ventilation guidance warns that code minimum may be insufficient in crowded spaces, and suggests more outdoor air than code where the weather and equipment allow, at the cost of more energy. Its steps: run the system fan on rather than auto while people are in the building, and turn off demand-controlled ventilation that cuts supply during occupied hours.

Carbon dioxide is a rough clue, not a pass or fail test. ASHRAE's 2025 position document calls 1,000 ppm at best an indicator of outdoor air per person, tied to about 16 cfm per person, says Standard 62.1 sets no CO2 limit, and says a reading means little unless it has reached steady state. It also says to measure outdoors too, since outdoor CO2 was about 426 ppm in 2024. For rate basics, see our lesson on ventilation rates.

Worked example: 120 cots in a gym

These inputs are our own assumptions, chosen to be easy to follow.

  • Gym floor: 6,000 square feet with 120 cots, which is 50 square feet per person (our assumption, inside the 40 to 60 range above).
  • Outdoor air design temperature 20 F and indoor setpoint 68 F, so delta T is 48 F (assumed).
  • We ignore heat from people and the building envelope, to isolate the ventilation load.

Step 1, two outdoor air targets.

  • Code-style rate: an engineering article from a maker lists 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot for multiuse assembly space, from an older ASHRAE addendum. The current standard may differ, so confirm it. Then 7.5 x 120 = 900 cfm, and 0.06 x 6,000 = 360 cfm, for 1,260 cfm.
  • CO2 benchmark: 16 cfm per person from the ASHRAE position document. Then 16 x 120 = 1,920 cfm.

Step 2, heat to warm that outdoor air with sensible heat = 1.08 x cfm x delta T (the factor is explained in our heat transfer formulas lesson).

  • 1.08 x 1,260 x 48 = 65,318, about 65,300 Btu/h.
  • 1.08 x 1,920 x 48 = 99,533, about 99,500 Btu/h.

Step 3, in electric terms, at 3,412 Btu/h per kW: 65,318 / 3,412 = about 19.1 kW and 99,533 / 3,412 = about 29.2 kW. That is only the ventilation air, before the building's own losses. The point is the size: ventilating a crowd in winter is a real heating load, and in a heat wave the same outdoor air arrives hot and humid. A rooftop unit that handled a game-night crowd for two hours may not hold setpoint when that air runs all night, so plan a wider acceptable range or a smaller occupied zone.

Temporary heat and carbon monoxide

CDC says never to run a generator in a garage even with the doors open, to keep it outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, never to heat with a gas oven, and not to use flameless chemical heaters indoors. The Red Cross gives the same 20 feet and says not to use outdoor stoves or heaters indoors. CPSC adds that portable heaters need 3 feet from anything that can burn, should never run unattended in a closed room, and should plug straight into a wall outlet. Fuel-burning heaters of any kind are a question for the fire official and a licensed contractor. In a shelter full of sleeping people CO alarms matter: CPSC says interconnected alarms are best and that an alarm means get outside and call 911. Our full lesson on carbon monoxide during winter outages covers this in detail, so we do not repeat it here.

Backup power in general terms

FEMA's generator job aid says to make a load list, match the generator to the maximum anticipated demand, and size it for critical functions rather than the whole building. It also says large motors in HVAC and refrigeration need extra starting capacity, so both starting and running power count, and it asks how long on-site fuel will last and how it will be refilled. For a shelter, critical loads usually include air handler or furnace fans and controls, lighting, and outlets for medical equipment; electric resistance heat of 20 to 30 kW, as in the example, can dominate the list, which is why gas, hot water or heat pump heat is easier to back up. Let an electrician or engineer finalize the size and the transfer switch. Siting and airflow around the unit are in the lesson on emergency generator rooms. Keep the exhaust away from every air intake and door, including the rooftop unit's.

Airborne illness: filters and air cleaners

CDC says respiratory viruses can spread in the air indoors, especially in crowded spaces. Its guidance for buildings: upgrade central filters to MERV 13 or the highest efficiency the system can handle, check that filters fit without gaps so air does not go around them, and add portable HEPA cleaners sized for the space. It suggests aiming for 5 or more air changes per hour of clean air, and also says five does not guarantee safe air. EPA agrees that portable cleaners supplement ventilation and do not replace it, advises a HEPA unit or one with a high smoke CADR, and says not to use ozone generators in occupied spaces. For a DIY box-fan unit, EPA says a MERV 13 or higher filter, and only when commercial units are unavailable. For ceiling fans, CDC advises low speed so air does not blow from one person onto another.

Example, same gym with an assumed 20 foot ceiling: 6,000 x 20 = 120,000 cubic feet. For 5 air changes per hour, 120,000 x 5 / 60 = 10,000 cfm of clean air, counting outdoor air and filtered air together. Subtract the 1,920 cfm of outdoor air and about 8,000 cfm is left for air cleaners. If each unit delivers an assumed 500 cfm of CADR, that is 16 units, so a smaller occupied zone may be the better answer. For more on filters and cleaners, see air purifiers and whole-house air cleaners.

Heat illness at a cooling center

CDC's heat guidance names older adults, young children and people with chronic conditions as high-risk groups. Staff should know the signs. For heat exhaustion CDC lists headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, thirst, heavy sweating and decreased urine output, and says to move the person out of the heat, give cool water, apply cold compresses and get medical evaluation. Heat stroke is an emergency: altered mental status, loss of consciousness, seizures or a very high body temperature, and CDC says to call 911, cool the person fast with cold water or wet cloths and stay until help arrives. CDC's cooling center review adds practical points: put up clear signs, have drinking water, and train staff to recognize heat illness. An MMWR report on Arizona centers found transportation and awareness were barriers for older adults. Our lesson on nursing home heat emergencies covers the care-facility side.

Field card: Shelters and Warming or Cooling Centers

More people, same building, longer hours.

  • Plan 40 sq ft or more per person for sleeping (FEMA, Red Cross).
  • Heating outdoor air: 1.08 x cfm x delta T.
  • Generators outside, 20 ft or more from openings (CDC, Red Cross).
  • CO alarms where people sleep.
  • MERV 13 or best the unit takes, plus sized HEPA cleaners; 5 ACH is a guide, not a guarantee.
  • Heat stroke: call 911 and cool fast.
