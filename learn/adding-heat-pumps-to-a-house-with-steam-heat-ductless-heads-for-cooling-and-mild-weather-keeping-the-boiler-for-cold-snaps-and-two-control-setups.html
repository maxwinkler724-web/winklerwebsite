Adding Heat Pumps to a House with Steam Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house with a steam boiler and radiators often has no air ducts, which makes central air conditioning hard to add. Heat pumps can fill that gap. This lesson covers how ductless equipment fits a steam house, how to let the heat pump carry mild weather while the boiler keeps the cold snaps, and how to set the two controls so they do not work against each other.

Why many steam homes have no ducts

Steam heat sends vapor through pipes to radiators. Nothing in that design needs a duct, so many older steam houses were built without any. That is why a cooling upgrade is a bigger decision there: a central system means finding room for supply and return ducts in walls and ceilings that were never planned for them. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that air-source heat pumps are available for homes without ducts in a ductless form called a mini-split, and that ductless installs need little construction, since the outdoor and indoor units connect through a small hole in the wall. See Adding AC to a House Without Ducts for the comparison with other options.

What the heat pump adds

A heat pump moves heat instead of making it from fuel. The Department of Energy says a properly installed unit can deliver up to three times more heat energy than the electrical energy it uses, and that newer designs, especially those built for cold regions, are now a legitimate option in colder areas. For a steam house, that gives you two things: cooling in summer, and an efficient source of heat in spring and fall when the boiler would otherwise fire for a few minutes and shut down.

The choice of equipment is usually ductless heads on the walls of the rooms you use most, or a small ducted or cassette unit if a closet or soffit can hold one. The lesson on cassette and ducted mini-splits versus wall heads covers that choice.

Keep the boiler for the coldest days

Heat pump output falls as the outdoor temperature drops. Cold-climate models are built to hold more of their capacity, but whether one can carry your whole house on the coldest night depends on the house and the model. Our own advice is to leave the steam system fully intact and treat it as the backup that covers the cold snaps, rather than removing it. The boiler already heats every radiator room, and keeping it avoids a design gamble. The lesson on cold-climate heat pumps and the one on balance point explain how capacity drops with temperature.

Two control systems, set so they do not fight

The heat pump and the boiler will each have their own thermostat. Nothing ties them together. If both are set to 70, both can call at once, and the boiler may fire while the heat pump is already doing the job. Our own guidance, not a code rule or a maker requirement: set the steam thermostat a few degrees below the heat pump setpoint. For example, heat pump at 70, steam at 67. On mild days the heat pump holds 70 and the room never drops to 67, so the boiler stays off. When the heat pump cannot keep up, the room drifts down, reaches the lower setpoint, and the boiler takes over. Check the actual behavior on a cool day and adjust the gap if the house is unevenly heated.

Two cautions. First, avoid deep night setbacks on the steam side if you want the heat pump to carry the load, since steam needs time to bring a cold system back up. Second, follow the maker's instructions for both appliances; this setup does not change what either needs to run safely.

Boiler short cycling and slower warm-up when it runs less

When the boiler only runs in the coldest weather, each call may start from a fully cooled boiler and cold pipes. The boiler has to heat the water, make steam, and push that steam through the pipes until the radiators warm. That can take a long time compared with a boiler that runs often, so a room may be slow to respond even though the system is fine. If the boiler is oversized for the load, a heat pump taking most of the load can leave it firing in short bursts. The steam boiler short cycling lesson covers causes and fixes. Measuring your radiators with the method in steam radiator EDR sizing tells you how big a boiler the radiators actually need, which helps if the boiler is replaced later.

What stays the same with steam upkeep

A boiler that runs less is not a boiler that needs less attention. The water level still has to be right before it fires, so keep checking the gauge glass; see steam boiler sight glass and when to add water. The low water cutoff still has to work when the boiler is called after a long idle, so keep it on your test routine from low water cutoff testing and maintenance. Radiator vents, pipe pitch and the pressure control are unchanged too.

Radiator rooms versus head placement

Radiators sit under windows and heat the room they are in. A ductless head heats by moving air from one wall, so it serves the room it is in best and neighbors only as far as air gets through open doorways. A Department of Energy field study of two ductless homes in Austin put indoor units in common areas and bedrooms but none in bathrooms, and the authors tied higher humidity in those rooms partly to that. It is a small case study, so treat it as a caution, not a rule. In practice: put heads in the rooms people occupy most, expect closed-door rooms and bathrooms to stay on the radiators, and do not assume one head will heat the whole floor.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own illustration. The numbers are assumptions, not measurements of any house or product.

  • Assumed seasonal heat delivered to the house: 60 MMBtu.
  • Assumed switchover temperature: 25°F outdoors. Assume the heat pump covers the part of the season above it, which is 70% of the seasonal load. The boiler covers the other 30%.
  • Assumed heat pump seasonal COP above the switchover: 2.5.
  • Assumed steam boiler seasonal efficiency: 80%.

Boiler only. Fuel in = 60 / 0.80 = 75 MMBtu.

Hybrid. Heat pump share = 0.70 x 60 = 42 MMBtu of heat. Electricity in = 42 / 2.5 = 16.8 MMBtu. Using 1 MMBtu = 293 kWh, that is about 4,900 kWh. Boiler share = 0.30 x 60 = 18 MMBtu of heat. Fuel in = 18 / 0.80 = 22.5 MMBtu. Total energy in = 16.8 + 22.5 = 39.3 MMBtu.

Comparison. 39.3 / 75 = 0.524, so the hybrid uses about 48% less site energy in this example. This compares energy only. It does not compare operating cost or emissions, because those depend on local fuel and electric prices and the power supply. If the real COP is 2.0, the heat pump share becomes 42 / 2.0 = 21 MMBtu and the total is 43.5 MMBtu, still about 42% less. If the heat pump covers only half the season, the total changes again, so test your own numbers.

Field card: Adding Heat Pumps to a House with Steam Heat

Heat pump first, steam boiler for the cold snaps, two thermostats a few degrees apart.

  • Steam homes often have no ducts, so ductless heads or small ducted units are the usual add.
  • Keep the boiler: it covers the coldest days and every radiator room.
  • Our guidance: steam thermostat a few degrees below the heat pump setpoint.
  • A boiler that runs less may warm up slowly or short cycle; see the linked lessons.
  • Keep up the gauge glass check and low water cutoff test.
  • Heads heat their own room best; closed-door rooms may stay on radiators.
