Refrigerant Recovery Machine Care and Faster Recovery

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A recovery machine is a pump you trust with the refrigerant, your cylinder and the next job. This lesson covers what federal rules say about the machine and the vacuum level you must reach, then how we keep the machine healthy and the job moving. Step-by-step hookup is in our recovery how-to.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page was built from federal regulation text and EPA pages read in 2026. It is general education. Your machine's manual, the cylinder markings and the current EPA rules come first.

What the rules require of the machine

For appliances other than small appliances and motor-vehicle systems, 40 CFR 82.156 requires you to evacuate the refrigerant, including all the liquid, to the levels in its Table 1 using a certified recovery or recycling machine, and to verify that the level was reached before you open the appliance. EPA's service practice page applies those levels to appliances with more than five pounds of charge. Section 82.156 also says recovery equipment must be used in accordance with the manufacturer's directions unless they conflict with the rule.

Under 40 CFR 82.158, equipment used on appliances made or imported on or after January 1, 2017 must be certified by an EPA-approved testing organization, under appendix B3 for non-flammable refrigerants (a test appendix based on the AHRI 740 standard, 2016 edition) or appendix B4 for flammable ones. A label must show the certification, the date of manufacture and the serial number. Altered designs that could affect certification must be retested, so do not swap parts for non-matching ones.

Evacuation levels by appliance type

Table 1 of 40 CFR 82.156 gives inches of mercury vacuum, measured from 29.9 inches Hg atmospheric pressure. For appliances made or imported on or after November 15, 1993:

  • Very high-pressure: 0 inches.
  • High-pressure, full charge under 200 lb: 0 inches. High-pressure, 200 lb or more: 10 inches.
  • Medium-pressure, under 200 lb: 10 inches. Medium-pressure, 200 lb or more: 15 inches.
  • Low-pressure: 25 mm Hg absolute.

Older appliances have a lower requirement (4 inches for the 200 lb and over and medium-pressure rows). If leaks make the table level unreachable or would badly contaminate the recovered refrigerant, 82.156 lets you isolate leaking parts and take them only to the lowest attainable level, not above 0 psig. Section 82.158 also lists vacuum levels that the equipment itself must be able to reach (its Table 2), and those differ from the appliance table, so read the right one for the question.

Machine care that pays off

The federal test appendix gives some hard clues about what the designers expect. Certified equipment must indicate when a filter drier needs replacement, and the maker must state a maximum quantity or a change interval. If a machine purges noncondensables, it may lose no more than 3 percent of the refrigerant by weight doing it. Multi-refrigerant equipment must come with a method to complete connections and clearing within 15 minutes. The practices below are our own field habits, in line with those requirements, and your manual overrides them.

  • Inlet filter drier and screen. Debris, acid and moisture go straight to the compressor. Keep the inlet screen clean and change the drier by the machine's indicator or the maker's interval, sooner after a burnout.
  • Oil-less compressors. Many machines use compressors that do not rely on a crankcase oil charge, which cuts oil changes and cross-contamination. That does not remove the need for clean filters and purging.
  • Purge after each job and before switching refrigerants. Run the machine as the manual directs to clear refrigerant from the machine and hoses into the recovery cylinder, so the next refrigerant is not diluted. Our mixed refrigerant lesson shows why a mixed cylinder is expensive.
  • High-pressure cutoff and float switch. The cutoff shuts the machine down on excess discharge pressure. A cylinder float switch, wired to the machine, stops the job before the cylinder is overfilled. Test the cable and plug, and never bypass either device.
  • Routine maintenance. Keep hoses and gaskets in good order, store the machine with caps on, wipe off oil and dirt, and follow the maker's schedule for service.

Speeding up recovery

These are our own field practices; none is a federal requirement. Always keep the machine's manual and the regulation's vacuum levels as the rule.

  • Short, large-diameter hoses. Less restriction between the system and the machine inlet means more flow.
  • Remove valve cores with a core tool. A Schrader core is a restriction. A removal tool lets you pull the core without losing the charge, recover through the open port, and put the core back when finished.
  • Liquid first. Moving liquid takes far less time than waiting for it to boil off. Our push-pull lesson covers when that works, and you still finish with vapor to reach the table level.
  • Keep the recovery cylinder cool. A cooler cylinder holds lower pressure, so the machine pumps against less. Never heat a cylinder with a flame.

Cylinder fill limit and weighing

The federal cylinder rule, 49 CFR 173.304(b), says the liquid portion of a liquefied gas may not completely fill the container at any temperature up to and including 131 °F (55 °C). It gives no 80 percent figure for refrigerant. The 80 percent limit is common trade practice, and the federal equipment test appendix uses the same figure for its test cylinder, saying the filling cylinder is not filled over 80 percent by volume. Put the cylinder on a scale and weigh it; do not trust a gauge. Our van lesson covers carrying it afterward.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions. Refrigerant is R-134a. The cylinder's stamped water capacity is 30 lb (assumed). Empty (tare) weight is 14 lb (assumed). It already holds 4 lb of R-134a (assumed). The job's charge is 22 lb (assumed).

Density source. The NIST Chemistry WebBook gives a saturated liquid density for R-134a of about 11.83 mol/L at 77 °F and a molecular weight of 102.0309. Then 11.83 × 102.0309 = 1,207 g/L, about 1.21 times the density of water (we take water as 1.00 kg/L, an assumption).

  1. 80 percent of volume, in water-weight terms: 30 × 0.80 = 24 lb.
  2. Convert to refrigerant: 24 × 1.207 = 29.0 lb maximum.
  3. Maximum gross weight: 14 + 29.0 = 43.0 lb.
  4. Room left: 29.0 − 4 = 25.0 lb.
  5. Does the job fit? 22 lb is less than 25.0 lb, so yes, with 3.0 lb to spare. Finished weight: 4 + 22 = 26 lb of refrigerant, 40 lb gross.

A 28 lb charge would not fit. Use a second cylinder of the same refrigerant. If the cylinder's own markings give a lower limit, use the lower one.

Avoiding contamination when switching refrigerants

Purge the machine and hoses, change the filter drier if the maker says so, and use a cylinder dedicated to the refrigerant. Check the machine's label: a machine certified under appendix B3 is for non-flammable refrigerants, and flammables need one certified under B4 and marked for them.

Field card: Recovery Machine Care and Speed

Clean machine, short hoses, cool cylinder, scale on.

  • Evacuate to Table 1 of 40 CFR 82.156 and verify before opening.
  • Post-1993 levels: high-pressure under 200 lb 0 in. Hg; 200 lb or more 10; medium-pressure 10 or 15; low-pressure 25 mm Hg absolute.
  • Use certified equipment and follow its manual.
  • Check the drier and screen, purge after each job and before a different refrigerant.
  • Never bypass the cutoff or float switch.
  • Fill limit: trade practice 80 percent; weight = water capacity × 0.80 × liquid density ratio.
