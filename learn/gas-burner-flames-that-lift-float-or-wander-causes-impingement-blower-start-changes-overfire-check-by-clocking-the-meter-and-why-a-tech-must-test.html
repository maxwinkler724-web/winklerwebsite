Gas Burner Flames That Lift, Float or Wander: Why Each Needs a Combustion Test

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Flame color is only one clue. A flame can be blue and still be wrong: standing off the burner ports, drifting lazily, touching the heat exchanger, or changing shape the moment the blower starts. This lesson explains what each behavior usually points to, and why the fix is a combustion test by a qualified technician, never a knob turned by the owner.

Safety first. This page is for understanding, not for adjusting equipment. If you see any of these flames, turn thermostat down, and call a qualified technician. If anyone has headaches, nausea or dizziness, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call emergency services.

What a normal flame does

An atmospheric-style gas burner mixes some air with the gas inside the burner tube (primary air). The rest of the air the flame needs arrives around the outside of the flame (secondary air). A healthy flame sits steady on its ports, the flames look alike from port to port, and they burn quietly. The related lesson on blue versus yellow flame color covers color, so we do not repeat it here. Our focus is shape, position and motion.

Lifting flames: leaving the ports

A lifting flame stands off the burner port with a visible gap, or blows away and goes out. A federal lab study of burner stability reports that when primary air is raised past a limit the flame can lift away from the ports, and that natural gas is particularly prone to lifting. The same study says the air reaching the flame from outside helps keep it attached. In practice, a technician looks at four causes:

  • Too much primary air. The gas-air mix leaves the port too fast for the flame to hold on.
  • Overfiring. More gas than the rating means more mixture velocity at each port.
  • High manifold pressure. Pressure past the burner rating pushes gas through the orifices faster. See checking gas inlet and manifold pressure.
  • A wrong orifice. A drilled-out or mismatched orifice, or one for a different gas, changes both gas flow and air drawn in. The same lab study notes that orifice size and pressure together set how much air the burner draws in.

One more cause surprised us. A federal product-safety lab tested a furnace with its vent disconnected and saw several flames lifting off the burners, to the point of going out. The report notes that this was an observation and that more testing would be needed to find the exact cause, and it links lifting to combustion air contaminated by combustion products. A lifting flame can therefore mean a venting or air problem, not only a gas-side one. That is one reason guessing is a bad plan.

Floating or lazy flames: wandering

A lazy flame is soft, long and unsteady, and it may drift or reach toward one side. The lab study above says too little primary air makes a flame lazy, and below a lower limit the tip turns yellow and carbon forms. Causes a technician checks include not enough combustion air to the furnace room, a blocked or restricted flue or vent, dirty or partly blocked burner ports, and concerns about the heat exchanger itself. A restricted vent matters because the flame needs the combustion products to leave so that fresh air can come in. For the heat exchanger side of the story, see how technicians inspect for a cracked heat exchanger. A federal product-safety report found that a furnace shut down within 1 to 3 minutes when its vent was blocked beyond about 47 percent, which shows a safety device working, but not every restriction trips one.

Flame impingement

Impingement means the flame touches the heat exchanger surface instead of ending inside the tube or clamshell opening. Our own plain reasoning: metal that is touched by flame runs hotter in that spot, and a flame that is too long, too forceful or pointed off center is the usual reason. Likely contributors are overfiring, high pressure, misaligned or bent burners, and a shifted burner box. We did not find a federal or extension page that quantifies impingement, so we describe it only in general terms and we give no limits. Someone with a mirror, a light and the manufacturer's instructions needs to look at where the flames land. Flame rollout out of the heat exchanger is a separate topic; see the rollout switch lesson.

Flames that change when the blower starts

Watch the flames when the main blower starts. A small, brief flicker can come from the airflow change around the cabinet. A flame that jumps, shrinks, leans, or goes out each time the blower runs is different. Our own reasoning is that a change tied to the blower start means pressure or air movement near the burners has shifted, and the possible reasons include a leak between the blower side and the combustion side, a pressure change in the furnace room, or a draft problem. We found no federal or extension source that gives a test for this, so we make no diagnosis claim. The takeaway is simple: a flame that reacts to the blower is a reason to stop and call, not a reason to keep watching.

Why owners should not adjust, and what a tech does

Federal consumer-safety pages say to have fuel-burning heating equipment inspected by a professional every year, and to never service fuel-burning appliances without the knowledge, skill and tools. They also say not to run an appliance that is operating wrong until trained personnel have serviced it. A university extension page on heating systems likewise assigns adjustments and combustion checks to a heating specialist. The reason is that the flame is only the visible part. Carbon monoxide has no color or smell, and a flame that looks acceptable can still make it.

A federal energy technical brief on combustion appliance testing lists the usual steps. The tech runs the appliance to steady state, measures flue gases for carbon monoxide and efficiency, and checks gas pressure and primary and secondary air if carbon monoxide is high. The same brief suggests action levels for a gas furnace, boiler or water heater of 100 ppm as measured or 200 ppm air-free, and says to refer the unit onward if the readings stay high after adjustment. It also says input that differs from the nameplate by more than 10 percent (5 percent for condensing units) should go to an HVAC technician. A turned screw without that testing is a guess with a hidden risk.

Worked example (our own): is it overfired?

This is our own example with assumed numbers. A tech clocks the meter while only the furnace runs, as in the meter clocking lesson.

Assumptions: natural gas at 1,000 Btu per cubic foot (assumed; the gas supplier gives the real heating value), a nameplate input of 80,000 Btu/h, a test dial that is one cubic foot per revolution, and one timed revolution of 40 seconds.

  1. Gas flow per hour: 1 cubic foot x 3,600 seconds per hour / 40 seconds = 90 cubic feet per hour.
  2. Input: 90 cubic feet per hour x 1,000 Btu per cubic foot = 90,000 Btu/h.
  3. Difference from nameplate: 90,000 - 80,000 = 10,000 Btu/h.
  4. Overfire percentage: 10,000 / 80,000 = 0.125, or 12.5 percent over.

That is beyond the 10 percent the federal brief gives for referral, so it goes to a qualified tech to find out why: pressure, orifice, or something else. For comparison, one timed revolution of 47 seconds gives 3,600 / 47 = 76.6 cubic feet per hour, or about 76,600 Btu/h, which is 4.3 percent under. A federal product-safety lab report measured a furnace rated 110,000 Btu/h running about 119,000 Btu/h as received, roughly 8 percent over. Overfire can happen in real units. Note that a clock test shows input only. It cannot tell you whether the flame touches the heat exchanger or whether carbon monoxide is high.

Field card: Gas Burner Flames That Lift, Float or Wander

Blue is not the same as right.

  • Lifting: look for too much primary air, overfiring, high manifold pressure, wrong orifice, or contaminated combustion air.
  • Lazy or wandering: look for too little combustion air, a restricted vent, dirty ports or heat exchanger concerns.
  • Flame touching the heat exchanger: overfire or misaligned burners; needs a tech.
  • Flame changes when the blower starts: stop and call.
  • Input check: cubic feet per revolution x 3,600 / seconds x 1,000 Btu per cubic foot.
  • Over 10 percent off nameplate (5 percent for condensing): refer to an HVAC tech.
  • Owners do not adjust. Combustion test, then fix.
