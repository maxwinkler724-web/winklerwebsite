Reading Temperature Control Drawings and a Written Sequence of Operations: Points Lists and Tracing a Sequence

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A controls submittal gives you three papers that describe one air handler: a schematic drawn on the airflow, a points list, and a written sequence. When a unit misbehaves, the fastest question is "what did the sequence say it should do right now?" This lesson shows how to read all three together and trace one sentence of a sequence down to a wire.

The three documents

The control schematic is a one-line picture of the unit with the airflow path running left to right and every sensor, actuator and safety drawn where it sits in the air stream. The points list is a table of every point the controller sees or drives. The sequence of operations is the written rulebook that says what the points do. Design standards from universities treat them as a set: the UConn building automation standard asks for a descriptive points list for each control panel and for graphics that show the listed points and carry a written sequence, and the Virginia Tech sequence appendix is organized as one written sequence per unit type.

Basic point types are covered in BAS Point Types and Reading the Graphics, so we only use them here as labels: AI (analog input, a sensor reading), AO (analog output, a modulating command), BI (binary input, a status or safety contact) and BO (binary output, an on/off command).

Reading the schematic

Follow the air and name each device as you pass it. Outdoor and return dampers (OAD, RAD) are AO points. The mixed-air sensor (MAT) and discharge-air sensor (DAT) are AI points. The filter differential pressure (dP-F) is an AI that drives a "change filter" alarm. The heating and cooling valves (HCV, CCV) are AO points. The freezestat (FS) and duct smoke detector (SD) are BI points, usually wired in series with the fan starter so they stop the unit even if the controller fails. The fan has a BO command and a BI status. If a device is on the schematic but not on the points list, or the reverse, that is the first mismatch to chase.

Reading the points list

A points list row normally gives a name, the type (AI, AO, BI, BO), the engineering range or signal, and any alarm limits. UConn's standard also says points must be identifiable by name and gives an example style of a room prefix plus a description. The range matters because it tells you the scaling: a mixed-air sensor might be listed as -40 to 140 °F and a valve as 0 to 100 percent. For how those ranges get proven in the field, see BAS Point-to-Point Checkout.

What a sequence contains

The university standards we read follow the same order. The Virginia Tech single-zone unit sequence has run conditions and modes (occupied, standby, unoccupied), setpoints marked adjustable, interlocks that enable heating or cooling only under stated outdoor conditions, safeties, and an alarm list. Its freezestat and smoke detector items both say the unit shuts down and alarms. The Duke Facilities sequence set is split by function, including economizer, unoccupied warm-up, supply air temperature reset, low temperature limit and alarming. Two details to notice. First, freeze protection is often handled twice: UConn describes a hardwired low-limit thermostat that stops the fan at 38 °F and needs a manual reset at the unit, plus a separate averaging sensor that alarms below 40 °F. Second, resets are written as slow, stepped moves: UConn steps the discharge-air setpoint up 0.5 °F every 5 minutes inside a 55 to 60 °F band, and Duke resets between 55 and 65 °F every 5 minutes. These are examples from those documents, not values for your job. The Virginia Tech document itself says its sequences are not meant to be used word for word. The national guideline named ASHRAE Guideline 36 is the usual starting text for many newer projects, and the sequence on your job may be an edited version of it.

Tracing one sentence to a field device

Pick one sentence and walk it down three levels. Take this sentence from our own sample sequence below: "When the mixed-air temperature falls below 45 °F, the outdoor-air damper closes and a low-mixed-air alarm is generated."

  1. Points. Input: MAT (AI). Output: OAD command (AO). Output: an alarm flag in the controller (software point). Setpoint: 45 °F, adjustable.
  2. Controller terminals. From the points list or the panel drawing, find which universal input holds MAT and which output holds OAD, and what signal each uses (for example a thermistor or a 0 to 10 V command).
  3. Field devices. MAT is a sensor in the mixing box, ideally an averaging element, and OAD is an actuator on the outdoor-air blades. To test the line, read MAT on the screen, compare it to a handheld meter at the sensor, then command OAD closed and watch the blades move.

If any level breaks, the sentence cannot be true. A wrong scaling number, a loose wire or a detached linkage all look the same on the screen until you trace down.

Worked example: one sequence against trend readings (all numbers ours)

Our own made-up sequence, for teaching only. (1) Occupied: supply fan runs, outdoor-air damper holds a 20 percent minimum. (2) Economizer: when outdoor temperature is below 65 °F, the damper modulates to hold mixed air at 55 °F. (3) Above 65 °F, economizer is locked out and the damper returns to minimum. (4) Mixed-air low limit: below 45 °F, the damper closes and an alarm is raised. (5) Cooling valve modulates to hold discharge air at 55 °F. Assumption: we ignore fan heat to keep the arithmetic simple.

The tool is the mixed-air relationship. The fraction of outdoor air is (RA - MA) / (RA - OA), with all three temperatures in °F. This is our own application of a standard mixing balance, and it works best when return and outdoor temperatures differ by at least 10 °F, because a one-degree sensor error swings the answer a lot when they are close. The PNNL building re-tuning guidance says to trend mixed-air, outside-air, return-air and discharge-air temperatures, damper position, valve positions and fan status, at 5-minute intervals if possible. Our made-up trend rows:

TimeOARAMAOAD cmdCCVFan
09:0052 °F74 °F55.3 °F85%0%On
13:0066 °F76 °F68 °F100%40%On
07:00 (other day)30 °F72 °F36 °F20%0%On

09:00. To hold 55 °F mixed air, the sequence needs (74 - 55) / (74 - 52) = 19 / 22, about 86 percent outdoor air. The reading shows 85 percent command and 55.3 °F. The cooling valve is closed, so free cooling is doing the job. Matches sentences 2 and 5.

13:00. Outdoor air is 66 °F, above the 65 °F lockout, so sentence 3 says minimum position. At 20 percent outdoor air, expected mixed air is 76 - 0.2 x (76 - 66) = 76 - 2 = 74 °F. We see 68 °F and a 100 percent command. The measured outdoor fraction is (76 - 68) / (76 - 66) = 8 / 10, or 80 percent, so the damper really is open, which suggests the actuator is following its command and the fault is upstream of it. Candidates in order: the lockout setpoint in the controller is not 65, the outdoor sensor reads low (compare to a handheld), or the damper output is overridden. The cooling valve is working 40 percent to remove heat that the sequence says should not be admitted. For setting changeover correctly, see Economizer Settings.

07:00. The damper is commanded to 20 percent, so expected mixed air is 72 - 0.2 x (72 - 30) = 72 - 8.4 = 63.6 °F. The reading is 36 °F. The measured outdoor fraction is (72 - 36) / (72 - 30) = 36 / 42, about 86 percent, with the damper commanded to 20 percent. The blades are stuck or detached while the command says minimum. Worse, mixed air is below the 45 °F limit, so sentence 4 says the damper should have closed and an alarm should exist. Two faults, one on the damper and one on the logic or alarm routing. Stop and check the coils for freeze risk first. See Freezestats on Air Handlers and, for what the alarm record should show, BAS Alarms and Trend Logs.

Using the sequence to troubleshoot

The habit is simple: write the sentence, write the expected value, compare to the reading, then decide whether the fault is in the logic, a sensor, an output or the device. PNNL's re-tuning guide gives a real example of this thinking: its lockout check says heating lockout is typically around 50 °F and cooling lockout around 55 °F, that the two should not overlap, and that overlapping coil valves on a trend chart suggest simultaneous heating and cooling. It also suggests feeling the coil pipe where it enters the unit to check for a leaking valve. A field study of Guideline 36 retrofits hosted by the Minnesota Department of Commerce used the same method: its evaluation was based on reviewing the trended data from the building automation system against the sequence checklist.

Control loops that hunt while the sequence is correct are a tuning problem; see PID Control Loops in Building Automation.

Field card: Reading Temperature Control Drawings and a Sequence

Sentence, points, terminals, device.

  • Read the schematic along the airflow and name every device you pass.
  • Every drawn device needs a points-list row, and the reverse.
  • AI is a sensor, AO a modulating output, BI a status or safety, BO an on/off command.
  • Sequences cover modes, setpoints, interlocks, safeties, alarms and reset.
  • Trace one sentence: points, then controller terminals, then field device.
  • Compute what the reading should be, then compare with the trend.
  • Outdoor fraction = (RA - MA) / (RA - OA), best when RA and OA differ by 10 °F or more.
  • Hardwired safeties stop the unit regardless of the controller; do not bypass them.
