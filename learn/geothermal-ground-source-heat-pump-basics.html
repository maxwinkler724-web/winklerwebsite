Geothermal (Ground-Source) Heat Pump Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An air-source heat pump works against a temperature that swings with the weather. A ground-source heat pump, often called geothermal, works against the earth, which stays far steadier.

The idea

Below the surface, ground temperature stays fairly constant through the year. The Department of Energy lists a range of about 40 to 70 degrees F depending on location. A ground-source heat pump uses the same refrigeration cycle as other heat pumps, but its outdoor heat exchanger is a buried loop of pipe. In winter, fluid in the loop picks up heat from the ground and the unit raises it to a useful temperature for the house. In summer the cycle reverses, moving heat out of the house into the loop and the earth. Because the ground does not drop to a very cold level on a cold morning, performance is less affected by outdoor air temperature than an air-source unit.

Loop types

The loop is the biggest design decision. Common types described by the Department of Energy and the AHRI are:

  • Closed horizontal: plastic pipe in trenches. DOE describes it as usually the most cost-effective for homes with enough land.
  • Closed vertical: deep bored holes with U-bend pipe. Used where land is limited, such as schools and commercial sites. DOE gives typical bore depths as 100 to 400 feet.
  • Pond or lake: pipe coils sunk in a suitable body of water, where depth, volume and quality allow.
  • Open loop: water drawn from a well or other source, passed through the system and returned, only where water supply is plentiful and rules allow.
  • Hybrid: ground loop combined with another heat source or sink, useful when cooling needs greatly exceed heating needs.

In general practice, in a closed loop the fluid is water or a water-based mix that moves heat through a heat exchanger to the refrigerant. Some designs instead run refrigerant directly through buried tubing. Which loop fits depends on soil, land, climate and local cost.

Trade-offs

DOE says installation can cost several times as much as an air-source system of similar capacity, with energy savings possibly recovering the extra cost over about 5 to 10 years depending on energy prices and incentives. It lists expected life up to about 24 years for indoor components and over 50 years for the ground loop. Those are program-level estimates, so a real project needs its own numbers.

What the technician sees

Indoor equipment looks like a water-to-air or water-to-water heat pump in a closet, basement or mechanical room, and it is protected from weather. In general practice, service work adds the loop side: pump, flow, loop pressure and fluid temperatures, in addition to normal refrigerant-side checks. Loop design, sizing and permits are specialist work done by qualified installers. Follow the manufacturer's commissioning steps, and treat refrigerant and electrical work with the same safety rules as any other heat pump.

Field card: Geothermal (Ground-Source) Heat Pump Basics

The earth is a steadier heat source than the air.

  • Buried loop exchanges heat with the ground instead of outdoor air.
  • Closed horizontal, closed vertical, pond and open are the main loop types.
  • Land, soil and water availability steer the loop choice.
  • Higher upfront cost, long expected life of the buried loop.
  • Service adds loop flow, pressure and fluid temperature checks.
