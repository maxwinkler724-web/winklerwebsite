Battery Storage Container HVAC: Temperature Windows, AC Units and Venting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A lithium-ion battery container is a steel box full of cells, power electronics and fire protection, and the HVAC is one of its safety systems. This lesson covers why the cells want a narrow temperature window, how much heat a container makes, and why the AC has to cooperate with gas detection and the fire system.

Check the authority having jurisdiction

Check first. Energy storage rules are changing quickly. This page is general education from public agency documents read in 2026. The local fire official and the equipment maker decide what applies to a real site.

Why the cells want a narrow window

An NREL presentation on battery thermal management says temperature affects the life, performance, safety and cost of lithium-ion cells. It says higher temperatures degrade cells more quickly and low temperatures reduce power and energy. A DOE Vehicle Technologies Office review page agrees. Neither page gives one ideal number for every cell, so we do not either. The window is on the maker's data sheet, and the container controls are set to it.

The same NREL material makes a second point that matters to HVAC: uneven temperature is a problem too. A DOE page cites a U.S. DRIVE target of less than 3°C cell-to-cell difference, and describes one vehicle pack that exceeded 12°C. Those are vehicle packs, not storage containers, but the lesson carries over. Air that bypasses some racks leaves hot and cold cells in the same box. NREL modeling also suggests cooling can pay back: for a Phoenix case, liquid cooling let a design reach a 10-year life with about 12 percent fewer cells than no cooling.

Cold is not free either. NREL lifetime-model slides list cycling at low temperature and fast charging among the conditions that wear cells faster, and they list high temperature with high depth of discharge as another. Our own reading: a New Jersey container should be checked for winter heating as well as summer cooling, so ask whether the AC units include heat.

Where the heat comes from

Cells and power electronics are not perfectly efficient, and the lost energy becomes heat inside the box. An NREL study of a stationary lithium-ion system found conversion round-trip efficiency in the range of 70 to 80 percent for the systems it studied, and that losses in the power electronics outweigh battery losses at low power. It also reports that auxiliary power use, which includes cooling, dominates when a system is lightly used.

The cooling equipment is usually wall-mount or packaged AC units on the container, much like the wall-mount units in telecom shelters. Sizing losses from power electronics is covered in cooling electrical rooms.

Worked example: heat load of one container

Every input below is our own assumption for teaching, not a measured or published value for any product.

  • Assumed rating: 4 MWh container delivering 2 MW at the AC terminals, so a 2-hour discharge.
  • Assumed round-trip efficiency: 88 percent, including cells and power conversion. This is higher than the NREL range above on purpose, to represent a newer design. Use the maker's number on a real job.
  • Assumed to be split evenly: each direction is the square root of 0.88, which is 0.938.
  • Assumed auxiliary load inside the box: 10 kW for controls, fans and lights.
  • Assumed worst case: all conversion losses land inside the container.

Step 1. Discharging 2 MW out means the battery side supplies 2 ÷ 0.938 = 2.132 MW.

Step 2. Loss = 2.132 − 2.000 = 0.132 MW = 132 kW. (Charging at 2 MW in loses 2 × (1 − 0.938) = 124 kW, so discharge is the worse case.)

Step 3. Add the auxiliary load: 132 + 10 = 142 kW of heat.

Step 4. Convert: 142 kW × 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 484,504 Btu/h, call it 485,000 Btu/h.

Step 5. Tons: 484,504 ÷ 12,000 = 40.4 tons.

Check: about 40 tons of cooling for a 2 MW box, running for hours, is why container AC is a multi-unit job. Add solar gain through the walls and roof on top of this. If the power conversion equipment sits in a separate enclosure, part of the 132 kW leaves the battery container, so find out where the inverters actually are before you size anything.

Codes you will hear named

Two documents come up on almost every job: NFPA 855, the standard for installing stationary energy storage systems, and Section 1207 of the International Fire Code (IFC), the energy storage section. We name them in plain text and do not reproduce them; buy or view them through the publishers. A New York State working group report on fire codes cites IFC 1207 subsections and NFPA 855 sections by number. A DOE Office of Electricity report calls NFPA 855 the bellwether standard for storage projects, and says the IFC is the model fire code in 42 states, with NFPA 1 in the rest.

In New Jersey, minutes of the state Fire Safety Commission from March 2026 say the 2018 fire code was still in use for battery storage and that the proposed rule adopting the 2024 International Fire Code, NJ Edition, had been published in the NJ Register on March 2, 2026, with a 60-day comment period. The minutes do not name NFPA 855 or IFC 1207. Ask the local fire official which edition governs your site today.

Explosion control and venting

When a cell fails and vents, the gases include hydrogen, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and hydrocarbons, according to a DOE fire safety presentation, and an enclosed box can hold a lot of hydrogen. The DOE Office of Electricity report says venting can happen before full thermal runaway and may not trip smoke or heat sensors first, which is why many designs add gas sensors. It also says passive-only devices on an enclosure will not prevent the potential for an explosion.

The New York State working group report names two accepted approaches: explosion prevention under NFPA 69 or deflagration venting under NFPA 68. The DOE presentation describes containers with deflagration panels that direct a blast upward, and says ventilation is activated by a high gas alarm. A PNNL technology page describes a different idea for cabinets: a controller that reads smoke, heat or gas detector inputs and opens the doors so gas dilutes. Vent path, fans and detectors are designed together, and the AC has to work with them.

Why the HVAC must interlock

A New York State guidebook for local governments, quoting that state's 2020 fire code, says gas detection must start mechanical ventilation when flammable gas reaches 25 percent of the lower flammable limit, and keep it running until the level falls back under that. It also requires at least 2 hours of standby power for the detection and the exhaust, and a trouble signal if detection fails. The same guidebook says gas sensors may not be tied to the fire alarm system unless that is approved and done per the fire alarm maker's instructions, so who connects what is a code question, not a wiring shortcut.

  • Gas alarm: exhaust runs, and the plan has to say what the AC does. Our own caution: a recirculating AC unit can keep stirring gas through the box, which is why the AC state during a gas alarm should be written in the design, not guessed by a tech.
  • Fire alarm or smoke: the same guidebook says storage systems on rooftops or in open parking garages must sit at least 50 feet from building HVAC air inlets, or 25 feet if a fire alarm shuts off the intake. Compare the duty of duct smoke detectors.
  • Loss of power: detection and exhaust have standby power; the AC may not. The controller still has to do the safe thing when it comes back.

What an AC unit does in each state is for the system designer and the fire official to set. A tech should find the written sequence, test it with the designer present, and not bypass an interlock to clear a nuisance alarm.

Field card: Battery Storage Container HVAC

The AC protects the cells, and the interlocks protect people.

  • Cell temperature window comes from the maker's data sheet; heat and cold both age cells.
  • Even temperature across racks matters, not just the average.
  • Heat load = conversion losses + auxiliary load. Our example: 142 kW, about 485,000 Btu/h, about 40 tons (assumed inputs).
  • Convert: kW x 3,412 = Btu/h; Btu/h / 12,000 = tons.
  • NFPA 855 and IFC 1207 are the codes to name; the local fire official decides the edition.
  • Gas detection starts exhaust at 25 percent of the lower flammable limit in the New York code the guidebook quotes.
  • Never bypass a gas or fire interlock to clear a nuisance alarm.
