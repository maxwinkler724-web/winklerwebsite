Heating and Cooling a Houseboat or Floating Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A floating home lives on the water, not cruising across it. That changes the comfort problem. The floor and lower walls touch water that stays near one temperature all season, so the floor can feel cold in winter, yet the water also steadies your heating and cooling loads. The bigger trouble is usually moisture. This lesson walks through the order we would work in: air and moisture first, then a small heat source, then safety.

Why the floor feels cold but the load stays steady

Heat moves from warm to cold through a floor at a rate set by the area, the temperature difference and the insulation value (R-value). Water under a floating home is often much warmer in winter than the air over a house on land, and it changes slowly. That means a cold floor surface is about the water, not about wind. The same slow swing helps in summer, when the water is usually cooler than the air. For a plain look at why a floor over a cold space feels cold, see the lesson on cold floors over crawl spaces and overhangs; the same logic applies, with water in place of cold air.

Worked example (our own, assumed round numbers)

Our own example. These inputs are assumptions, not measurements: a 600 ft2 floor, an effective R-10, a 68 F room, 40 F water under the hull, and for comparison a floor over 20 F winter air. The simple conduction relation is heat loss in Btu/h = area x temperature difference / R.

  1. Over water: temperature difference = 68 - 40 = 28 F. Loss = 600 x 28 / 10 = 16,800 / 10 = 1,680 Btu/h.
  2. Over 20 F air: temperature difference = 68 - 20 = 48 F. Loss = 600 x 48 / 10 = 28,800 / 10 = 2,880 Btu/h.
  3. Difference = 2,880 - 1,680 = 1,200 Btu/h. As a share of the air case, 1,200 / 2,880 = 0.4167, so about 42 percent less loss.

The floor still loses heat, and it still feels cool underfoot, but it is a smaller and steadier load than a floor hanging in cold air. Real floors have joists, hull framing and air gaps, so a real number needs a proper survey. Treat this as a teaching figure only.

Moisture is the first problem

Living over open water adds humidity, and a small, tight home adds more: cooking, showers, breathing and drying clothes. A federal environmental agency guide to mold and moisture says the key to mold control is moisture control, and it gives an indoor relative humidity target below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. It also says condensation on windows, walls and pipes is a sign of high humidity, and that you prevent it by cutting humidity, moving air, raising the surface temperature and insulating cold surfaces. Hull sides, window frames and closets on an outside wall are the usual cold spots on a floating home.

  • Ventilation first. Run the bath and galley fans, and vent them outdoors. The same guide says to vent moisture-producing appliances outside where possible, and notes that combustion appliances add water vapor.
  • Dehumidifier next. The guide says to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. A small unit that drains overboard through a proper hose, or into a pump, is easier to live with than one you empty by hand. See our lessons on dehumidifier sizing and drainage and window condensation.
  • Air movement in closets. Leave doors cracked or add a grille so air reaches cold outside walls.
  • Measure it. A hygrometer tells you if you are inside the target range. Keep it where the air is stagnant, such as a closet, since that is where trouble starts.

Heat sources that make sense

Small ductless or reverse-cycle units. A compact ductless head can heat and cool one open living space with little ductwork. A water-source unit uses the water as the place it gets heat from in winter and dumps heat to in summer. Because the water changes slowly, such a unit has a steady source. Strainers, fouling, anodes, winterizing and shore power for these systems are covered in our lesson on marine reverse-cycle systems, so we do not repeat them here. Very cold water lowers capacity, so check the rating at your water temperature.

Electric heaters. Use them only on properly rated shore circuits. A heater is a continuous load, and a dock pedestal, cord and plug can be marginal. Check the circuit rating against the heater's nameplate, use a cord and plug sized for it, and have a qualified electrician look at the dock supply. Our own rule of thumb: if a plug or cord is warm to the touch, stop and fix it. Do not run an extension cord that is thinner than the heater's cord.

Fuel-burning heaters. Any fuel-burning heater must be vented to the outside by its listed vent, with makeup air as the maker requires. Never use an unvented fuel-burning heater to heat a sleeping space. See the next section.

Carbon monoxide around docks

A federal consumer product safety agency says to install a CO alarm on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, to prefer interconnected alarms, and to use battery-operated alarms or alarms with battery backup. A federal disease-control agency page lists fuel-burning engines, stoves, lanterns, grills and furnaces as CO sources, says headache, dizziness and weakness are common signs, and says sleeping people can die before showing symptoms. Both say never to run a portable generator inside or in a garage, and to keep it well away from windows, doors and vents; the disease-control page says more than 20 feet.

Docks add a hazard that a house on land does not have: exhaust from your own boat, a neighbor's boat or a nearby generator can drift into a cabin. A federal research report on houseboat generators found that older gasoline generators without exhaust controls often showed carbon monoxide in the exhaust stack above 10,000 ppm. It also found that catalyst and fuel-injection controls cut on-board readings, and that a worn catalyst lost effectiveness. Put differently, a generator that has run for a long time can still be dangerous to people nearby. So we place CO alarms in the sleeping cabin, near any fuel-burning appliance and on every level, and we test them when we change the clocks. Read our lesson on CO alarms and what to do, and the thin-walled cousin of this problem in heating a yurt.

Freeze protection for water lines

Our own practice is simple: the floor and hull stay near water temperature, so supply lines that run along the hull or under the floor are the likeliest to freeze when the water gets close to freezing or ice forms. Insulate exposed lines, keep the home heated, and know how to drain the dock hose and the lines in a long absence. If you leave for weeks, ask a qualified tech about winterizing rather than relying on one heater. A frozen dock supply hose is a common first sign.

What to remember

  • The water under the floor makes the load smaller and steadier, but the floor still feels cool.
  • Fix moisture first: vent, then dehumidify, then heat.
  • Water-source ductless or reverse-cycle units are a good fit; see the marine lesson.
  • Electric heat only on rated shore circuits.
  • CO alarms on every sleeping level, and keep generator and boat exhaust away.
