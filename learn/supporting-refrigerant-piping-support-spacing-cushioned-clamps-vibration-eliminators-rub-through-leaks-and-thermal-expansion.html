Supporting Refrigerant Piping: Spacing, Clamps, Vibration and Expansion

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most refrigerant leaks we chase on older systems are not bad brazing. They are a line that moved: a clamp that let copper saw against steel, two lines that rubbed together, or a joint near the compressor that flexed a few thousand times too many. This lesson covers how far apart to support copper lines, what to put between copper and the strut, where a vibration eliminator goes, how much a hot line grows, and what to look at on a maintenance visit.

Check the code and the maker

Check the code and the maker. The adopted mechanical code in your jurisdiction and the equipment installation manual govern. The numbers below come from the documents under Sources and from our own worked example. They show the pattern. They do not replace the manual on your job.

Why lines move and what it does

A compressor rocks at every start and stop and pushes gas pulses into the lines while it runs. A compressor-maker application bulletin on minimizing line vibration says structural resonance is the most common cause of line vibration, followed by forced vibration and acoustic resonance, and that flexibility has to be designed into the piping to absorb the motion. It also warns that a weight that can move independently of the line may cause wear and possibly a leak, so it has to be rigidly attached. A university conference paper on field-made refrigerant joints (an ASHRAE research project) ran fittings through a vibration test of about 30 Hz for two million cycles. Larger compression and flare fittings failed at 50 percent or higher, and one brazed joint also failed, likely from too little braze in the gap. So vibration does break joints, and the fix is in the support, not in tightening later.

How far apart: the code spacing

Mechanical codes set an interval of support for piping. In the International Mechanical Code that is section 305.4, and the 2012 Seattle Mechanical Code, which follows that numbering, points to a pipe hanger and support standard under it. We could not open the mechanical code table itself, so for the numbers we used two model plumbing codes that state copper tube spacing and that state governments publish:

  • International Plumbing Code, 2024, Table 308.5 (as reproduced in a Connecticut state building inspector document): copper or copper-alloy tubing, 1-1/4 in and smaller, 6 ft horizontal and 10 ft vertical; 1-1/2 in and larger, 10 ft in both directions.
  • Uniform Plumbing Code Table 313.3 (as amended in Minnesota rules): copper, 1-1/2 in and smaller, 6 ft horizontal; 2 in and larger, 10 ft horizontal; risers at each floor, not over 10 ft.

Both tables give 6 ft horizontal for small copper, so we use 6 ft as the cited maximum for the sizes in this lesson. Refrigerant tube is sized by outside diameter, so 7/8 in and 3/8 in lines are well under the 1-1/4 in line either table draws. A Michigan State University facilities specification adds that hangers go within 1 ft of fittings and valves and at every change of direction. For other published tables, some tighter than 6 ft on small tube, see our hanger spacing and saddles lesson.

Cushioned clamps and the copper-to-steel problem

Two things go wrong where copper meets a steel strut: abrasion and galvanic action. Abrasion is the common one. Copper is soft, the line vibrates a few thousandths of an inch at every start, and a bare steel clamp slowly thins the wall. That is why the clamp needs a soft lining that stays in place, and why the sketch gives every line its own clamp.

Galvanic action needs five things, according to Naval Academy corrosion class notes: an anode, a cathode, an electrolyte, an electrical connection and a potential difference. Remove any one and it stops. Copper on steel supplies two metals in contact. Our reading of the notes' tabulated potentials is that iron is the more active metal, so the steel would be the one that corrodes. What is missing in a dry space is the electrolyte. In a damp or sweating location it is not missing, which is why the Michigan State specification says to use copper-plated or plastic-coated supports where they touch copper tubing. A lined clamp solves both problems because the lining is also an insulator. Do not let the clamp crush the foam on a cold line; the insulation lesson covers saddles.

The same compressor bulletin says a clamp must be fastened to something more rigid than the line, because a discharge line clamped to light channel can make the channel ring. It also calls a single-bolt clamp ineffective against vibration in either plane and says a clamp works best placed in the direction of maximum motion.

Vibration eliminators near the compressor

A braided vibration absorber is a short flexible section brazed into the line. Its maker's installation sheet says to mount it as close to the compressor as possible, at a right angle to the motion, in a straight line with the piping. It is not meant to correct misaligned pipe, and there must be room so it is not in compression or tension after brazing. Anchor the piping at the end farthest from the source, to a solid member. For severe vibration, use two in series at right angles to each other. The compressor bulletin agrees: on spring-mounted compressors the absorber runs parallel to the crankshaft, with one end on the moving base and the other on the solid frame, while on a solidly mounted compressor both ends are clamped rigidly. Our takeaway: put the first rigid support past the eliminator, never on it.

Rub-through at lines that touch

Two lines in contact, or a line resting on a stud or joist, will wear through at the touch point over seasons of start and stop cycles. Fix the cause: separate the lines with a gap, give each its own lined clamp, and keep copper off framing with an isolating hanger or sleeve. A leak at a rub spot is a repair of the tube, not a patch on the outside; see the leak lesson.

Expansion and contraction on long runs

A hot line grows and a cold line shrinks. The National Bureau of Standards paper on copper alloys (Hidnert and Krider, 1947) lists electrolytic copper at about 16.8 millionths per degree C from 20 to 100 C; the scan is hard to read, so treat the third digit as approximate. We convert that to about 9.3 millionths per degree F (divide by 1.8). The compressor bulletin does not name expansion loops, but it says flexibility must be built in. Our suggestion for a long hot gas run is an offset or a loop at a change of direction, with the clamp on each side allowed to hold the tube without pinching it.

Worked example: a 60 ft run (ours)

This is our own example. Assumptions, not field data: a 60 ft horizontal run carries a 7/8 in suction line and a 3/8 in liquid line on one strut, the strut has support points we can place anywhere, and a separate 60 ft hot gas line of the same length sees 70 F at install and 170 F running.

Support count, using the 6 ft cited maximum. 60 ft divided by 6 ft is 10 spans. Ten spans need 9 intermediate supports. If both ends are already held by the equipment or a wall anchor, 9 clamps per line is the minimum. If you also need a support at each end, count 11 positions. Both lines share the same support points on the strut, so the strut has 9 stations and 18 cushioned clamps, one per line per station. Supports within 1 ft of each elbow, valve or tee add to the count.

Growth of the hot gas line. Change in length equals coefficient times length times temperature change. Length is 60 ft times 12, which is 720 in. Temperature change is 170 F minus 70 F, which is 100 F. So 9.3 times 10 to the minus 6 per F, times 720 in, times 100 F, is 0.67 in. The line grows about two-thirds of an inch. As a second assumption, a suction line that goes from 70 F to 40 F running shrinks 9.3 times 10 to the minus 6, times 720 in, times 30 F, which is 0.20 in.

What to check on a maintenance visit

  • Chafe points. Look where lines cross framing, strut edges and each other. Shiny bare copper, worn insulation or a groove in the foam is a warning.
  • Clamps. Check for loose bolts, lost cushion liners, and clamps that crush the insulation.
  • Brazed joints near the compressor. Look for cracks, oil stains and frost patterns at the service valves and at the eliminator ends. Leak check any joint that is oily.
  • Vibration eliminator. It should be in line, not stretched, bent, or carrying the line's weight, and its braid should be dry, not corroded.
  • Sags and spacing. Compare actual spacing to the 6 ft maximum and close it up where lines droop.

Field card: Supporting Refrigerant Piping: Spacing, Clamps, Vibration and Expansion

Support it, cushion it, keep it apart, leave it room to grow.

  • Cited maximum for small copper tube: 6 ft horizontal (two model plumbing codes); check your adopted mechanical code.
  • Hangers within 1 ft of fittings and valves.
  • Lined clamps on every line, one line per clamp, no bare steel on copper.
  • Vibration eliminator close to the compressor, in line, first rigid support beyond it.
  • Copper grows about 9.3 millionths per F: 0.67 in per 60 ft per 100 F in our example.
  • Maintenance: chafe points, loose clamps, cracks and oil at brazed joints near the compressor.
