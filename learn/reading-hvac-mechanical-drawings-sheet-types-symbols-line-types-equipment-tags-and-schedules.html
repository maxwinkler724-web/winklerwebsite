Reading HVAC Mechanical Drawings: Sheets, Symbols, Tags and Schedules

Last updated: 2026-10-26

A mechanical drawing set is a filing system. Once you know where each kind of information lives (notes and legend up front, plans in the middle, schedules near the back), you can answer most field questions in a minute. This lesson walks through the sheet types, the common symbols, how an equipment tag leads to a schedule row, and how to check scale and revisions before you cut or hang anything.

Check the project set

Every set is different. There is no single national rule for how a designer numbers sheets or draws symbols. Each owner or agency publishes its own standard, and each set carries its own legend. This page is general education built from public drafting standards read in 2026. On a real job, the legend, notes and specifications in your set win over anything here.

Sheet numbering: where things live

Many sets use a letter for the discipline and digits for the type: M for mechanical, E for electrical, P for plumbing. One state drafting manual we read lists M as the HVAC letter and says the first digit after it identifies the drawing type, such as plans, sections, elevations, schedules or details, with the designer assigning those numbers. A university standard lists a fuller pattern: M001 is the legend, abbreviations and general notes sheet; M100s are HVAC floor plans; M200s are HVAC piping plans; M300 is sections; M400 is mechanical room part plans; M500s are details; M600s are controls and schematic diagrams; M700s are schedules. Treat that as one example, not a rule. A federal agency's procurement guidance we read uses a different system, with classification symbols such as MH for heating, ventilating, air conditioning and refrigeration, and the building number in front of it.

So the first move is always the same: find the drawing index on the cover or first sheet, then find the sheet titled legend or general notes. Read it before you read any plan.

General notes, legend and abbreviations

The legend sheet defines the symbols this set uses, and the abbreviations list tells you what S/A, R/A, O/A, E/A, AHU, CFM and FD mean. One state bid set we read lists S/A as supply air, R/A as return air, O/A as outside air and E/A as exhaust air, and notes that FD means fire damper in one list and floor drain in another. That is a good reminder: an abbreviation only means what this set says it means. General notes often cover things a tech must act on, such as field-measuring existing conditions and telling the engineer about deviations before construction.

Plan, section, detail, riser: what each one tells you

  • Plan view: looking down from above. It shows where equipment, ducts, pipes and air devices sit horizontally. Separate sheets often show air (HVAC) and piping. A county plan-review checklist we read expects a plan scale of at least 1/8 inch = 1 foot, and a graphic scale on every plan.
  • Section: a vertical cut through the building. It shows heights, ceiling space and what crosses what.
  • Detail: an enlarged view of one connection, such as a flexible duct connection at a diffuser. One bid set's detail limits that flexible duct to 5 feet long. Details are often drawn larger, 1/4 inch = 1 foot in the same county checklist.
  • Riser diagram: a vertical, not-to-scale schematic of piping showing pipe sizes and fittings floor to floor. The same checklist asks for riser diagrams with pipe sizes and fittings.
  • Controls and schematics: flow diagrams and sequences of operation, separate from the plans.

Line types and common symbols

Line types are the designer's choice within an owner's standard. One state manual says designers may use standard CAD line types or their own for each layer, with colors and weights set by that agency's pen chart. So read the legend rather than memorizing dash patterns. What is more consistent is the information: ducts are drawn with their size in a tag, and the legend tells you which air stream a duct belongs to. The bid set we read has tags for round duct (diameter), square or rectangular duct (width by height) and oval duct (width over height), and symbols for supply, return and exhaust duct rises, both round and rectangular. It also has separate tags for fire damper, smoke damper, combination fire and smoke damper, motorized damper, backdraft damper and manual balancing damper, and for valves such as balancing, ball, check, three-way mixing, motorized control and solenoid valves. A federal drafting layer list we read even splits ductwork by service: supply, return and exhaust ducts and supply diffusers each have their own layer, and schematics and riser diagrams have their own group. That tells you how the drawing is built: the same duct can be hidden or shown by service.

Equipment tags and schedules

A tag such as AHU-1 on the plan is a pointer to a row in the equipment schedule. A university design guide we read says equipment numbers are unique within a building, that drawings use the short designator (such as AHU-1), and that existing numbers continue in sequence, so a new air handler after AC-1 through AC-3 would be AC-4. It also says that if a project's first item is not numbered 1, the schedule must say the earlier equipment is existing. Duct labels can combine a letter code and the unit number, such as S/A (AHU-1). Small units like VAV boxes get a unique number on the drawings but need not be labeled in the field.

The schedule is where numbers live. A county checklist we read asks that an air handling schedule show total cooling and heating load, total supply air and outdoor air cfm, entering and leaving coil temperatures, the basis of design and installation details. Electrical data (voltage, phase, MCA, MOCP) usually sits in the same row or a nearby electrical column. To use the electrical numbers, see Reading an HVAC Nameplate. Diffuser tags tie in the same way: the legend in the bid set we read shows a grille, register and diffuser tag with cfm and neck size, so a tag reads like type, neck size and airflow.

Keyed notes

A keyed note is a number in a symbol (a circle, hexagon or diamond) on the plan, matched to a numbered list on the same sheet. It points to one spot, so it usually carries a job-specific instruction, such as verifying an existing opening. General notes apply everywhere; keyed notes apply where the symbol is. Read both.

Worked example: one schedule row and one measured run

Everything in this example is made up by us. The numbers are assumptions for practice, not from any real project or standard.

Assumed schedule row, AHU-1: supply air 400 cfm, outside air 80 cfm, 460 volts, 3 phase, MCA 6.0 A, MOCP 15 A. Assumed plan: scale 1/8 inch = 1 foot, supply trunk tagged 10x8, diffusers A-8/150, A-8/150 and B-6/100.

  1. Add up the air. 150 + 150 + 100 = 400 cfm. That matches the 400 cfm supply on the schedule, so the tags agree with the row. If they did not, you would ask before installing.
  2. Check duct size against airflow. A 10x8 inch duct has 10 x 8 = 80 square inches, which is 80 / 144 = 0.556 square feet. Velocity = cfm / area = 400 / 0.556 = about 720 feet per minute. That is a quick sanity number, not a design check; for friction and sizing, see Duct Sizing Basics.
  3. Check an 8 inch round neck. Area = 3.14 x (4/12)2 = 0.349 square feet. 150 / 0.349 = about 430 feet per minute at the neck. See Supply Registers and Return Grilles for throw and noise.
  4. Scale the run. At 1/8 inch = 1 foot, 1 inch on the sheet is 8 feet. You measure the trunk from the unit to the first branch at 2-3/8 inches (2.375). 2.375 x 8 = 19 feet.
  5. Compare with the tape. Suppose you measure 19 feet 10 inches in the field, which is 238 inches. The plan says 19 feet, 228 inches. The difference is 10 inches, and 10 / 228 = 4.4 percent. Is that acceptable? It depends on the fitting and what the notes say. The right move is to measure from structure, check a larger scale detail if there is one, and call the engineer if it affects clearance or fit.

Scale cautions. A sheet reduced to half size no longer matches its labeled scale, so use the graphic scale bar, which shrinks with the print, or the written dimension. One university drafting standard says larger-scale drawings take precedence for dimension control. Never assume; ask.

Check revisions before you work

Look at the title block first: the sheet issue date, revision block, and the latest revision. Standards vary. One university standard marks revised areas with a revision cloud and a triangle containing a number in final design and construction sets, and requires each revision to be dated with day, month and year. A state manual we read dropped the cloud-and-tag requirement and tracks revisions in a revision block instead. A federal agency's procedure says a replaced sheet keeps its number plus an R suffix and the sheet carries the amendment label above the title block. So: match the delta number to the revision block, confirm the date, make sure your printed copy is the newest sheet, and check that the schedule sheet and the plan sheet are from the same issue. If a cloud surrounds the area you are working in, read the revision description first.

Field card: Reading HVAC Mechanical Drawings

Index, legend, plan, schedule, then revisions.

  • Find the sheet index, then the legend and general notes, before the plans.
  • Sheet numbers vary by owner. Do not assume M100 is always plans.
  • Tag to schedule: AHU-1 on the plan is a row in the equipment schedule.
  • Add diffuser cfm and compare with the schedule total.
  • Scale: at 1/8 in = 1 ft, 1 in on paper is 8 ft. Use the graphic bar on reduced prints.
  • Check delta number, cloud, date and issue before you cut or order.
  • When plan and detail disagree, ask the engineer in writing.
