Mold on the AC Coil or in Ducts: What the EPA Says and What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Mold is a moisture problem that happens to show up on HVAC parts. The U.S. EPA's advice is consistent across its pages: find and fix the water first, do not run a system you know is contaminated, and know which materials can be cleaned and which must be thrown out. This lesson walks through that guidance for a homeowner or a new tech.

Why the system grows mold: moisture

EPA's mold guide for homes says plainly that the key to mold control is moisture control. Its list of moisture sources includes clogged or dirty drip pans and obstructed drain lines, cold surfaces where water condenses, and poor air movement. CDC adds that mold is always present in the environment and can enter a home through vents and HVAC systems, so the question is never whether spores are around. The question is whether something stays wet enough to let them grow.

EPA's duct page ties this to equipment. It names condensation on or near cooling coils as a major factor, along with standing water in drain pans that do not slope toward the drain, humidifiers that are not maintained, ducts in attics and crawl spaces that are poorly sealed or insulated, and an oversized air conditioner that cycles on and off without pulling much moisture out. High relative humidity is called an indicator of potential mold growth on any duct type. Those causes are covered in our lessons on a house that feels humid with the AC running, condensate drains and sweating ducts, so this page does not repeat them.

Do not run a system you know is contaminated

EPA's home mold pages say not to run the HVAC system if you know or suspect it is contaminated with mold, because it could spread mold through the building. They say to suspect contamination when there is an identified moisture problem in or near the system, or mold near the air intake. EPA's duct page also warns that spraying products into an operating system can spread them through the home. For a tech, the practical step is to turn the system off at the thermostat, tell the occupant why, and look at the coil, drain pan and plenum before anything else.

Confirming it is mold

EPA's duct page says only an expert can confirm that a substance is mold, and a lab analysis may be needed. It says some growth on a settling plate (a petri dish left out in the air) is normal, so that is not proof of duct contamination, and it calls other testing methods unreliable. EPA's home guide says sampling is usually unnecessary when mold is visible, and notes there are no federal limits for mold in indoor air. CDC goes further and does not recommend routine mold testing; it says to remove the mold and fix the moisture whether or not you know the type. EPA also warns that some parts of a system are out of sight, so if a provider says there is mold, ask to be shown.

What can be cleaned and what must be replaced

EPA says cleaning ducts is warranted when there is substantial visible mold growth inside hard-surface (sheet metal) ducts or on other system components. For a bare metal surface, scrubbing with detergent and water and drying it fully is the approach in EPA's home guide, and CDC lists soap and water among its options for hard surfaces. Porous material is different. EPA's duct page says wet or moldy insulation cannot be effectively cleaned and should be removed and replaced, and that once fiberglass duct liner is contaminated with mold, cleaning is not enough to prevent regrowth. EPA, NADCA and NAIMA all recommend replacing wet or moldy fiberglass duct material. Accessible flex duct can be cleaned with soft brushes, but EPA notes replacement may be more economical. See our flex duct lesson for what replacement involves, and our duct cleaning evidence lesson for when cleaning is worth paying for.

Fix the cause first. EPA says that if the conditions causing the growth are not corrected, it will come back, and that underlying causes must be corrected before ducts are cleaned, retrofitted or replaced.

Biocides and UV lamps

EPA's duct page says no chemical biocides are currently registered by EPA for use in internally insulated duct systems. A small number of products are registered for sanitizing the inside of bare sheet metal ducts. If one is used, EPA says it should go on only after visible growth is proven and cleaned, only on uninsulated surfaces, and strictly by the label, since some label steps such as rinsing with water can encourage mold. EPA's home guide does not recommend bleach as routine practice and says to never mix bleach with ammonia-containing cleaners. For UV lamps near the coil, see our UV lesson.

When to bring in a professional

EPA's home guide says you can usually handle a moldy area of less than about 10 square feet, roughly a 3 ft by 3 ft patch. For larger areas, or significant water damage, EPA points to its separate remediation guidance for bigger jobs. It also advises a professional for sewage or contaminated water and for suspected hidden mold, and says to check a contractor's mold experience and references. For HVAC contamination it sends you back to the duct page. Use the 10 square foot line as a rule for visible mold on surfaces. EPA does not say how to apply it to the inside of a duct, so a duct with mold in more than one run is a job for a pro.

PPE basics

EPA's home guide lists: an N-95 respirator that fits properly (OSHA requires fit testing at work), gloves reaching mid-forearm, and goggles without ventilation holes. Avoid touching mold with bare hands. Ventilate if you use any disinfectant. Dead mold can still cause allergic reactions, so it needs to be removed, not just killed. People with health concerns should talk to a health professional before cleanup, per EPA.

Worked example: is the house wet enough to feed mold?

A homeowner finds fuzzy growth on the drain pan and runs a pocket meter. The living room reads 75°F and 68% relative humidity. The coil and the first stretch of supply duct measure 55°F while cooling.

Step 1, compare to EPA. EPA's home guide says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60%, ideally 30% to 50%. 68 − 60 = 8 points over the ceiling. CDC's own figure is no higher than 50%, so the gap there is 68 − 50 = 18 points.

Step 2, estimate the dew point. Mark Lawrence's 2005 paper in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society gives a rule of thumb: the dew point falls about 1°C for every 5% below 100% RH, accurate to roughly 1°C in humid air (above about 50% RH) between 0 and 30°C. Dew point depression = (100 − 68) ÷ 5 = 6.4°C. In Fahrenheit, multiply by 1.8: 6.4 × 1.8 = 11.5°F. Dew point ≈ 75 − 11.5 = 63.5°F.

Step 3, compare to the cold surface. The 55°F coil is 63.5 − 55 = 8.5°F below the dew point, so it will collect water while the system runs. That is normal on a coil, which is why the pan and drain must work. But the same room air touching an uninsulated or damaged duct at 55°F would also sweat. Even at EPA's 50% ceiling of the ideal band, the rule gives (100 − 50) ÷ 5 = 10°C = 18°F, so the dew point is about 57°F. That is at the edge of where the rule works, so treat it as roughly at the coil temperature: a cold coil still sweats, which is normal, as long as the water drains away. Bringing the humidity down helps, but insulation and drainage still matter.

Area check. A 3 ft by 3 ft patch is 3 × 3 = 9 square feet, under the 10 square foot line. A coil face of 20 in by 24 in is 480 ÷ 144 ≈ 3.3 square feet per side.

Fixing the moisture, in order

  1. Turn the system off if mold is known or suspected.
  2. Clear the drain line and clean the pan; check that the pan slopes to the drain. See the condensate lesson.
  3. Check the humidity with a meter (EPA says they cost about $10 to $50) and bring it below 60%. See the dehumidifier lesson and the psychrometrics lesson.
  4. Seal and insulate ducts that sweat; see the duct sealing lesson.
  5. Replace wet or moldy liner and insulation. Clean hard surfaces and dry them.
  6. Confirm the finish as EPA describes: the moisture problem is fixed, visible mold and odor are gone, and later visits show no water damage or growth. EPA calls it ultimately a judgment call.

Field card: Mold in the HVAC System

Fix the water, then clean the metal and replace the porous parts.

  • Moisture control is mold control. Find the water first.
  • Known or suspected contamination: do not run the system.
  • EPA humidity target: below 60%, ideally 30% to 50%.
  • Wet or moldy fiberglass liner and insulation: replace, do not clean.
  • No EPA-registered biocides for internally insulated ducts; use only registered, labeled products on bare metal.
  • About 10 sq ft is the EPA line for doing it yourself; sewage and hidden mold go to a pro.
  • PPE: fitted N-95, long gloves, unvented goggles.
