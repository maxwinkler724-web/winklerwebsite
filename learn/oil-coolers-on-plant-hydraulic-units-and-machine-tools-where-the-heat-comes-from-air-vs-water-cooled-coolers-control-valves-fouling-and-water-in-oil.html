Oil Coolers on Plant Hydraulic Units and Machine Tools

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hydraulic power unit on a press, molding machine or machine tool turns electric power into oil flow. Whatever part of that power is not turned into useful work ends up as heat in the oil. The oil cooler is how the plant gets that heat out. This lesson covers where the heat comes from, the main cooler types, the valve that controls the water, and the problems facility techs find most often.

Check the machine maker first

Follow the maker. Every machine maker sets its own oil temperature limits, oil type and cooling water requirements. This page gives no oil temperature limit for your equipment. Use the nameplate, the manual and the site lockout procedure before you open any oil or water line, and treat the oil side as pressurized until it is proven otherwise.

Where the heat comes from

A University of Florida lecture set on hydraulic systems says power lost in valves is converted to heat, and gives relief valve pressure override as one example. It notes that a pilot-operated relief valve has less override than a direct-acting one, so it wastes less power. The same notes show that pump and motor efficiency is the product of volumetric and torque efficiency, so the input power that does not come out as useful hydraulic power is the loss. In practice that means pumping oil over a relief valve, throttling flow through a valve, or holding pressure while nothing moves all make heat. A unit that sits at pressure on idle can heat the oil as fast as one that is working.

The reservoir sheds some heat to the air, and the notes say it should be large enough, with baffles and port placement, so returning oil has time to cool. When the tank alone cannot hold the temperature, the notes say an oil cooler is needed. Temperature matters beyond wear: the same notes say maker-recommended viscosity is about 12 to 48 mPa·s at operating temperature, and that pump and motor efficiency depend on viscosity, which falls as the oil heats.

Air-cooled and water-cooled coolers

Air-cooled (fan cooler). Oil runs through a finned core and a fan pushes room air across it. It needs no water piping, so it suits machines far from the cooling water loop. Its capacity depends on room air temperature, so a hot shop in summer is its worst case. It also needs clear air in and out, and the fins load up with lint, oil mist and chips.

Water-cooled (shell-and-tube or plate). Oil passes one side and plant water the other. These are compact and not tied to room air temperature, but they need a water supply, a return, and water that will not foul the passages. The US Environmental Protection Agency lists hydraulic equipment among the machines that are often cooled by single-pass water, and its water-efficiency guide says to minimize use, recirculate, or replace with air-cooled equipment, after checking that the added energy does not cancel the water savings. In many plants the tie-in is to an existing closed cooling loop or chilled water system, which brings its own rules for pressure, strainers and treatment; see Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach.

The water regulating valve

Most water-cooled oil coolers have a valve on the water side so water flows only as hard as the oil needs. A common form senses oil temperature with a bulb or sensor and opens as the oil warms, as in the figure. The Department of Energy water guidance says to keep the water control valve maintained so it adjusts flow to demand, to compare entering and leaving water temperatures and flow with the maker's recommendations, and to keep flow near the maker's minimum to save water. The EPA guide adds that where cooling water cannot be eliminated, an automatic control should stop flow when the equipment is idle. For a valve of this kind on a different service, see Water Regulating Valves on Water-Cooled Condensers.

Fouling: dirty fins and dirty water passages

Air side. A Purdue literature review of air-side fouling says fouling raises air pressure drop more than it hurts the heat transfer coefficient, so a dirty fin core cuts airflow and raises fan load before the core itself looks bad. It reports fouling is worse on the front face of a coil. Cleaning methods were not covered in that review, so follow the cooler maker's cleaning instructions, and do not bend fins.

Water side. The Department of Energy cooling tower guidance explains that as water evaporates, dissolved solids such as calcium and magnesium concentrate and can form scale, and that treatment programs usually combine scale and corrosion inhibitors with biological control. A once-through plant line with poor quality water, or a tower loop with weak treatment, can lay scale or slime inside cooler passages. Signs are the same: higher oil temperature at the same load, water leaving the cooler cooler than it used to, or a valve that sits wide open. A university chilled-water design guideline adds basket strainers on condenser water pumps and solids separators, which is the kind of protection a cooler tied into a tower loop depends on. See Condenser Water Treatment.

Signs the oil is running too hot

  • Oil temperature gauge or alarm above the maker's limit, or the machine going into a high-temperature fault.
  • Darker oil, a burnt smell, varnish on valves, sticking spools, or noisy and slow actuators. These are general shop signs, so confirm with an oil sample against the maker's guidance.
  • Pump and motor running warmer, with slower cycle times, as viscosity falls.

As a reference only, the US Bureau of Reclamation lubrication guide says that for turbine oil in a hydropower plant, the oxidation rate doubles for every 18 °F (10 °C) above 150 °F (66 °C), which roughly halves oil life. That number is for turbine oil, not your hydraulic fluid. It does show why a cooler that is slightly low on capacity matters over months.

A leaking cooler: water in the oil or oil in the water

A tube, plate or gasket leak lets one fluid into the other. Which way depends on which side is at higher pressure at that moment, and it can change when the machine stops and the oil pressure falls while the water is still up. The Reclamation guide says water is the most common contaminant in hydropower lubricating oil, that it promotes oxidation, corrosion, sludge and foaming, and that hazy or cloudy oil may mean water. A crackle test, which gives an audible crackle if water is present, is qualitative, so it recommends a laboratory water test. Removal options it lists are centrifuge, coalescing filters and vacuum dehydration, and it notes a desiccant breather may be needed if water persists.

Oil in the water loop shows up as a sheen or oil at drains, strainers or the tower basin. The EPA guide says single-pass effluent contaminated by hydraulic equipment must not be reused in boilers. If you find either problem, isolate the cooler per procedure, find the leak by a pressure test of the cooler on the maker's method, and fix the loop and the oil together, since flushing one side alone brings it back.

Freeze protection

An idle unit with cold water standing in an outdoor or unheated line can freeze and split a cooler. A university design guideline calls for heat trace on outdoor piping that stays water-filled in winter and for glycol in chillers exposed to outside air; that is that campus's design practice, not a general rule. In your plant, ask who owns the loop and whether it is protected, drain or isolate coolers on units shut down in the cold, and see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops.

Our own worked example: heat load and water flow

This example is our own. The inputs below are assumptions we picked for teaching, not data from any machine. Heat-load rule: Btu/h = 500 × gpm × ΔT (°F). The 500 is our own rule of thumb: 8.33 lb per gallon × 60 min per hour × 1 Btu per lb per °F, rounded.

  1. Assume the pump motor delivers 30 hp to the pump.
  2. Convert: 30 hp × 2,545 Btu/h per hp = 76,350 Btu/h of input power.
  3. Assume 70% of that ends up as heat in the oil: 76,350 × 0.70 = 53,445 Btu/h. We assume the cooler must take all of it, which ignores the reservoir.
  4. Assume the cooling water may warm 10 °F. Flow = 53,445 ÷ (500 × 10) = 10.7 gpm.
  5. If the loop allows a 20 °F rise: 53,445 ÷ (500 × 20) = 5.3 gpm.

Why it matters: if the unit ran single-pass at 10.7 gpm for 16 hours a day, 250 days a year, using the flow-times-time method in the EPA guide, that is 10.7 × 60 × 16 × 250 = about 2.57 million gallons a year. That is why the regulating valve, and shutting water off when the unit is idle, are worth checking. The EPA and DOE guides say single-pass systems use about 40 times more water than a cooling tower run at five cycles of concentration to remove the same heat.

Field card: Oil Coolers on Plant Hydraulic Units and Machine Tools

Losses become heat; the cooler moves it to air or water.

  • Valve and pump losses turn into heat in the oil, even at idle pressure.
  • Follow the machine maker for oil temperature limits and cooling water needs.
  • Air coolers: clear fins and room air. Water coolers: clean passages and a working regulating valve.
  • Hazy oil may mean water; sheen in the water loop may mean oil. Test and find the leak.
  • Water flow (gpm) = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). Our own rule of thumb.
  • Protect idle outdoor water lines from freezing.
