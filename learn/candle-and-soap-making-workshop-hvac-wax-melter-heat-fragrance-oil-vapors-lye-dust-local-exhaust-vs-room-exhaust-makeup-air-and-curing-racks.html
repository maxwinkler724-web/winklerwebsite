Candle and Soap Workshop HVAC: Melter Heat, Fragrance Vapors, Lye Dust and Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A small candle and soap workshop looks gentle, but the air in it is not. Wax melters add heat, fragrance oils add vapor, burn tests add soot, and cold-process soap adds lye dust and mist. This lesson shows how a technician sorts those problems into heat, vapor, dust and fire, and sizes the exhaust and makeup air for each.

Sort the hazards first

Four things happen in these rooms, and each needs a different fix. Heat from melters and pouring pots is a load, and a fan can carry it away. Vapor from fragrance and essential oils should be captured at the source. Dust and mist from lye must not be pulled across a worker's face. Soot from test burning needs filtration and a place to go. Mixing these up is the usual mistake: a big room fan does not capture a vapor, and a hood does not cool a room.

Fragrance oils, flash points and fire

Fragrance and essential oils are mixtures of volatile chemicals. A state public health report on workplace essential oil use describes them as concentrated mixtures of volatile chemicals and notes that safety data sheets list some, such as bergamot and lemon, as flammable liquids and others, such as lavender and peppermint, as combustible liquids. That report gives no flash point numbers, so read the safety data sheet for each oil you stock.

The federal flammable liquids rule, 29 CFR 1910.106, defines a flammable liquid as one with a flash point at or below 199.4 °F (93 °C), and defines flash point as the minimum temperature at which a liquid gives off vapor. It treats ventilation as adequate when it keeps vapor-air mixtures below one-fourth of the lower flammable limit, and it says to eliminate or control ignition sources such as open flames, hot surfaces and sparks. A university safety guideline adds that heating raises the hazard and that heaters used with liquids near their flash points need automatic overtemperature shutoffs. For a wax shop, that means no open flame or exposed element under the fragrance bench, and a thermostat limit on every melter.

General practice (our own, not a cited figure): add fragrance to wax only after the pot is off the heat source, keep oil bottles capped and away from melters, and keep a fire extinguisher rated for the hazards on hand near the pour area.

Wax fume and soot

The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists paraffin wax fume with a recommended exposure limit of 2 mg/m3 as a time-weighted average, and shows no OSHA limit for it. It describes paraffin wax as a white to slightly yellowish, odorless solid made of heavy hydrocarbons. Overheated wax smokes, so the cure is a thermostat that stops well short of smoking, not more fans.

Soot from test-burning candles is a general-practice concern, not a number we can cite here. Burn tests belong in one spot under a small hood or near an exhaust inlet, away from curing racks and finished stock, because soot settles on everything and loads filters. We did not find a figure we could open and cite, so none appears on this page.

Lye dust and mist in cold-process soap

Sodium hydroxide is a white solid sold as flakes, beads or granules. The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists a recommended ceiling of 2 mg/m3 (never to be exceeded), an OSHA limit of 2 mg/m3 as a time-weighted average, and an immediately dangerous level of 10 mg/m3. A ceiling limit matters for ductwork: the exhaust must work every moment lye is being weighed or mixed, not just on average.

Mix lye and water at a station with local exhaust drawing away from the worker, and pull the capture air across the container, not past the person. Lye mixing also heats the solution, so the vapor it gives off rises with the temperature. Keep exhaust air moving while the mix cools. General practice: weigh lye in a still corner of the hood, not at the front edge where room drafts can push dust out.

Local exhaust versus general room exhaust

Local exhaust is a hood or capture inlet placed right over the melter, mixing bowl or burn-test spot. It takes a little air from a lot of source. General room exhaust dilutes whatever escapes the hood and carries off room heat. Use local exhaust for vapors, lye and soot; use general exhaust for heat and for odor you cannot capture. Duct material and fan choice for fragrance vapors should consider fire risk, so ask for a fan rated for the airstream when vapors are near their lower flammable limit.

Keep makeup air coming in

Every cubic foot you exhaust must come back in. If it does not, the room goes negative, doors are hard to open, and flame-type appliances can backdraft. Bring makeup air in through a filtered opening or louver on the side away from the hood, so it sweeps across the room toward the capture point. In winter, tempering the makeup air avoids a cold spot at the soap bench and keeps wax from setting too soon.

Curing racks: steady temperature, moderate humidity

Soap and candles on curing racks do best in a room that stays steady. General practice: avoid supply air blowing straight on the racks, keep the rack room out of the melter exhaust path, and use a dehumidifier or a humidifier with a humidity sensor rather than guessing. Large swings in temperature or humidity cause sweating, uneven curing and cracks, so a thermostat that follows the workshop schedule helps.

Filter loading from wax and soot

Wax vapor condenses on cool surfaces, and soot is sticky. Both coat filters, coils and fan blades faster than ordinary dust. General practice: use a cheap pre-filter you can change often, check it more often than the normal schedule, and never run a hood filter until it is clogged with wax, because a loaded filter lowers airflow and is itself fuel. Keep heaters and electric melters clear of stacked boxes, paper and cardboard.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions we chose. A shop runs three electric melters: two at 1,500 W and one at 1,000 W, for 4,000 W connected. Assume the melters cycle on 60 percent of the time. We want to hold the room to an 8 °F rise over the air coming in. We assume all melter power ends up as heat in the room, and use 3.412 Btu/h per watt and the standard sensible-air relation Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.

  • Average power: 4,000 W x 0.60 = 2,400 W.
  • Room heat: 2,400 W x 3.412 = 8,189 Btu/h.
  • In tons: 8,189 / 12,000 = 0.68 ton.
  • Exhaust cfm for an 8 °F rise: 8,189 / (1.08 x 8) = 8,189 / 8.64 = about 948 cfm, so call it 950 cfm.
  • Peak check, all melters on: 4,000 W x 3.412 = 13,648 Btu/h; 13,648 / 8.64 = about 1,580 cfm.

This sizes the heat only. The hood over the melters and the lye station still need their own capture rates, set by the hood design and the safety data sheets, and those may take more or less than the heat requires. The 950 cfm must also be replaced by makeup air, which adds a heating or cooling load of its own.

Field card: Candle and Soap Workshop HVAC

Heat, vapor, dust and fire each need their own fix.

  • Capture vapors, lye and soot at the source; use general exhaust for heat.
  • Read each oil's safety data sheet; some are flammable liquids, some combustible.
  • Lye: NIOSH ceiling 2 mg/m3; keep exhaust running through the whole mix.
  • Paraffin wax fume: NIOSH 2 mg/m3 time-weighted average.
  • Heat: Btu/h = watts x 3.412; cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature rise).
  • Every cfm out needs a cfm of makeup air in.
  • Thermostat limits on melters; no open flame near fragrance oils.
