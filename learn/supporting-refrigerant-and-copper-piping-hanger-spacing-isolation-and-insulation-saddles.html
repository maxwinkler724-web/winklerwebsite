Supporting Refrigerant Lines: Hanger Spacing, Isolation and Saddles

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Copper refrigerant tube is soft, light and easy to bend, which also makes it easy to leave sagging, rattling or squashed into a clamp. This lesson covers why lines need support, how far apart the hangers go according to published tables, how to keep compressor vibration out of the building, and how to hang a line without crushing its insulation.

Check the maker and the code

Check the maker and the code. Spacing tables differ by source, and your local mechanical code and the equipment manual govern. The numbers here come from the documents listed under Sources. They show the pattern and are not a substitute for the manual on your job.

Why lines need support

Three problems show up when copper hangs on too few supports. First, vibration. An ACHR News column by a Heatcraft field support engineer says compressor vibration travels directly down the suction and discharge lines, and that a rigid clamp lets the copper rub on the hanger until the wall thins and leaks. Second, fatigue. Copeland's application bulletin for 7 to 15 ton scroll compressors tells designers to keep tubing stress below 9.5 ksi, which it calls the endurance limit of copper tubing. A line that flexes every start and stop, over thousands of cycles, is the kind that cracks at a fitting. Third, sags. The same column says a sagging horizontal suction line can cause poor oil return, because oil settles in the low spot. The sizing lesson linked below explains why oil needs moving gas to get home.

How far apart: the published tables

No single table covers every job, so here are the three I could read. They agree closely on the small sizes.

  • ACHR News column on ACR tubing (by outside diameter, horizontal): under 5/8 in, support at least every 5 ft; 3/4 in through 1-1/8 in, at least every 7 ft; 1-1/4 in and larger, no more than 8 to 10 ft apart. It adds a support within 12 to 18 in of a major bend or tee, and near heavy items such as filter driers and solenoid valves. Vertical risers are supported at every floor or every 10 ft.
  • Hill Phoenix piping guide (a refrigeration system maker): 5/8 in, 5 ft; 1-1/8 in, 7 ft; 1-5/8 in, 9 ft; 2-1/8 in, 10 ft. It says a straight run is supported at each end, and that intermediate supports are typically not more than 8 ft apart.
  • Copper Development Association (CDA) guide specification, by nominal plumbing size: up to 3/4 in, 5 ft; 1 in, 6 ft; 1-1/4 in, 7 ft; 2 in, 8 ft. The page says these values come from MSS SP-69 and that local plumbing, mechanical or building codes set the required spacing. It is written for water piping, so treat it as a cross-check, not the main table.

I did not find a table in the equipment manuals themselves. Carrier's, Trane's and Mitsubishi's installation documents we read give routing and isolation rules but no hanger distances, and the Copeland bulletin gives no support spacing either. For refrigerant lines, the spacing normally comes from the system designer, the code in force, or a trade source like those above.

Isolation near the compressor

Copeland's bulletin says a scroll compressor rocks and twists, so the suction line needs enough flexibility to stop vibration from passing into attached lines. For split systems it calls minimizing vibration at the service valve the most important goal, and its recommended layout uses a shock loop in the tubing. The same idea appears in a Mitsubishi mini-split manual, which notes that a loop in the piping near the outdoor unit can reduce vibration passed on from it. So near the compressor, do not clamp the line stiffly. Leave the loop, and put the first hanger beyond it.

Farther along, the manuals care about noise in the building. Trane's condensing unit installer's guide says to use isolation-type hangers where lines are fastened to joists or framing and in stud spaces or enclosed ceilings, to insulate and isolate lines where they pass through a wall or sill, and to isolate them from all ductwork. A Carrier air conditioner manual says to avoid direct tubing contact with water pipes, ductwork, joists, studs and walls, and not to suspend tubing from joists or studs with rigid wire or strap. Hill Phoenix adds rubber grommets between pipe and clamp to prevent chafing, individual clamps for condenser pipes, and a warning not to over-support piping attached to a compressor rack, which has to stay free to move.

Saddles: do not crush the insulation

A hanger that bites into foam squeezes out the insulating air, and the cold line then touches a warm clamp. The ACHR column calls crushing insulation in a standard hanger a common error on suction lines and recommends rigid pipe shields or saddle inserts at every support point. A Carrier manual asks for hanger straps about 1 in wide that follow the shape of the insulation, and metal sleeves so the strap stays off the foam. An Armacell white paper says crushed or torn insulation lets thermal bridging occur, and recommends saddles that give extended support, at least 12 in long, with curved ends so the edge does not cut into the foam. Keep the vapor seal intact at every hanger; the insulation lesson linked below covers seams and thickness.

Dissimilar metals

The CDA guide specification says that where excessive moisture is expected, the piping or the support must be wrapped with an approved tape or otherwise isolated, to prevent contact between dissimilar metals and inhibit galvanic corrosion. A bare steel strap on a cold, sweating copper line is the classic case. Insulation, a plastic-lined clamp or an inert wrap between the two keeps them apart. Hill Phoenix says the same in plain words: line supports should isolate the lines from contact with metal.

Slope on suction lines

Hill Phoenix's piping guide says horizontal suction lines should slope 1/2 in per 10 ft toward the compressor to aid oil return. A trade blog article gives the same 1/2 in per 10 ft as a general guideline. Mini-split and condensing unit manuals we read do not give a slope. The hangers have to hold that slope, which is another reason not to stretch the spacing.

Worked example: 48 ft trapeze run

Invented job: a 48 ft horizontal run carrying a 7/8 in suction line and a 3/8 in liquid line on one common trapeze. How many hangers, and how much does the run drop?

  1. Pick the spacing for each line. Using the ACHR table: 7/8 in falls in the 3/4 to 1-1/8 in group, so 7 ft. 3/8 in is under 5/8 in, so 5 ft.
  2. Let the tighter line govern. Both lines sit on the same bar, so the bar must be close enough for the weaker line: 5 ft. (Cross-check: the CDA table also gives 5 ft for sizes up to 3/4 in, and Hill Phoenix gives 5 ft at 5/8 in.)
  3. Count spans, rounding up. 48 ft / 5 ft = 9.6, which rounds up to 10 spans.
  4. Count hangers, including both ends. Hangers = spans + 1 = 10 + 1 = 11 hangers.
  5. Find the real spacing. 48 ft / 10 spans = 4.8 ft (4 ft 9.6 in), under the 5 ft limit.
  6. Compare. If the suction line were alone at 7 ft: 48 / 7 = 6.86, rounded up to 7 spans, so 8 hangers. The 3/8 in liquid line on the trapeze costs 3 more.
  7. Slope. At 1/2 in per 10 ft: 48 ft / 10 = 4.8; 4.8 x 0.5 in = 2.4 in total drop toward the compressor. Between hangers: 4.8 ft / 10 x 0.5 in = 0.24 in, about 1/4 in per span. Cut the trapeze rods so each hanger sits about 1/4 in lower than the one before.

Add extra supports within 12 to 18 in of any bend or tee and at heavy valves, per the ACHR column. Those do not replace the count above.

Field card: Supporting Refrigerant Lines: Hanger Spacing, Isolation and Saddles

Support it, isolate it, do not crush it.

  • Published spacing (ACHR column): under 5/8 in every 5 ft; 3/4 to 1-1/8 in every 7 ft.
  • A common trapeze follows the smaller tube.
  • Hangers = spans (rounded up) + 1.
  • Suction slope: 1/2 in per 10 ft toward the compressor (Hill Phoenix).
  • Keep a shock loop at the compressor; use isolation hangers at framing.
  • Saddle or shield at every hanger; keep steel off bare copper.
  • Manual and local code come first.
