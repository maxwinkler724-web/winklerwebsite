Unit Ventilators in Classrooms: Outdoor Air, Cycles, Freeze Protection and Noise

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk into almost any older classroom and you will find a cabinet along the exterior wall with a grille on top. That is usually a unit ventilator. It heats, it brings in outdoor air, and it is easy to break in small ways that the room's teacher notices first. This lesson explains the parts, the control ideas, and the checks, using maker literature and federal pages.

Check the maker and the drawings

Check the maker and the drawings. Control sequences differ by model and by the job's design. This page is general education built from the sources listed below. Follow the unit's own manual and the project's sequence of operation.

What a unit ventilator is

EPA's indoor air quality design page groups unit ventilators with other single-room units that serve one room without ducts, and notes they do not recirculate air between rooms. Daikin Applied's classroom unit ventilator catalog describes a floor cabinet with a draw-thru fan section, a single full-length filter, a face and bypass damper on some models, optional controls, and an insulated double-wall outdoor air damper. Its installation manual lists hot water, chilled water, direct expansion, steam and electric heat coil options, plus a drain pan. Another maker's controls document describes the same idea in system-modeling terms: an outdoor air mixer, a fan, a heating coil and a cooling coil, like a fan coil with built-in outdoor air.

The control cycles, as sources name them

You will see "ASHRAE Cycle I," "Cycle II" and "Cycle W" in control submittals. We found only fragments of how they work in public maker pages, so we state just those. A Siemens unit ventilator controller document says that for Cycle I the outdoor air damper minimum position is set to 100 percent, and for Cycle II it is set to a value below 100 percent to satisfy the minimum outdoor air requirement. It says that in both cases the damper sits at its minimum while the coil provides heat. A Johnson Controls tool page notes that Cycles I, II and W need a discharge air temperature input for the damper logic. Daikin Applied's catalog names ASHRAE Cycle II as one that its controls provide. We did not find a public maker page describing Cycle W's behavior, so we leave it at that.

Minimum outdoor air can also follow the fan. The Daikin catalog says one option adjusts the damper's minimum position with fan speed to bring in a constant amount of outdoor air, and demand control ventilation can target a rate such as 15 cfm per person from occupancy. Daikin's controls manual says the damper is at minimum in the heat state unless the unit has CO2 control, and that the damper stays closed when the room is unoccupied. See Economizers Basics and Economizer Settings for the free-cooling side.

Freeze protection, only as sources state it

Daikin's catalog says units with hydronic coils get a factory low-temperature freezestat whose capillary senses air leaving the coil, and that constant hot water flow through the coil in face and bypass units further reduces freeze risk. The controls manual says a low-limit state can override the outdoor air damper toward closed to hold a ventilation low limit, and that a valve freeze-protect condition sends the valve full open. The Siemens document describes a fail-safe on a low temperature detection thermostat trip: outdoor air damper closes, heating goes full on (electric heat turns off), cooling goes off, the fan stops, and the face and bypass damper goes to face. The installation manual adds that piping must be protected from outside air and freezing, and that water flow must be maintained through the heating coil in freezing conditions.

Freezestat testing and capillary placement are covered in Freezestats on Air Handlers. One cost to remember: EPA's appendix on HVAC systems and indoor air quality says some central designs close the outdoor air damper when a freezestat trips, which can leave occupied areas short of fresh air. We did not find a unit ventilator page that says the same, so treat it as a general caution.

Worked example: heating the mixed air

Our own: a 1,000 cfm unit ventilator at 30 percent minimum outdoor air, 0 F outdoor air and 70 F return air. We want 70 F supply. The 1.08 factor is explained in HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas.

  1. Mixed air temperature: 0.30 × 0 F + 0.70 × 70 F = 0 + 49 = 49 F.
  2. Temperature rise needed: 70 F − 49 F = 21 F.
  3. Heating: 1.08 × 1,000 cfm × 21 F = 22,680 Btu/h.

Our own note: this ignores fan heat and any air leakage, and a real damper may not deliver exactly 30 percent. Mixed air of 49 F is why the freezestat setting and coil water flow matter in cold weather.

Common field problems

  • Stuck dampers. The DOE ventilation assessment guide lists dampers stuck closed or open that do not move when commanded, and suggests cycling dampers, actuators and linkages fully open and closed during maintenance. Stuck closed means no ventilation. Stuck open in winter means cold air on the coil.
  • Filters and coils. The same guide says dirty coils waste energy (it cites up to 37 percent more for dirty condenser coils). Daikin's catalog says the single-filter design makes changes quick. Its installation manual warns that some cleaning agents can damage aluminum coils and electronics, and says to spray water into the drain pan high end to flush debris to the drain end.
  • Blocked discharge. The DOE guide lists furniture blocking supply vents and impeding circulation as a fix staff can often make themselves. EPA's appendix also notes that movable partitions, shelving or furnishings can interfere with airflow.
  • Noise and drafts. EPA's design page says central systems are quieter and less drafty than unit ventilators, and that quieter systems are more likely to be left on by teachers. Daikin lists lower fan speeds and its fan design as aids to quiet operation.
  • Moisture. EPA says unit ventilators add moisture risk through the wall penetration, and that drain pan problems are another source.

For ventilation target rates and measurement, see Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics.

Field card: Unit Ventilators in Classrooms

Air path, damper position, coil water, then furniture.

  • Parts: damper section, filter, fan, coil, discharge, drain pan, controls.
  • Dampers should move fully open and closed when commanded; check actuators and linkages.
  • Heat state: damper at minimum, unless the controls call for something else.
  • Freeze: confirm the low-limit device and hot water flow per the unit's manual.
  • Clear the discharge and intake of furniture and papers.
  • Mixed air = %OA × OAT + %RA × RAT. Heat = 1.08 × cfm × dT.
