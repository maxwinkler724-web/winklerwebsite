Oil Separators and Oil Management on Commercial Refrigeration Racks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every compressor sends a little oil out with the hot gas. On a single compressor the oil finds its way home. On a rack of several compressors sharing headers, it needs help: a separator, a reservoir and a level control on each machine. This lesson follows the oil and shows what goes wrong when part of that loop fails.

Why oil leaves the compressor

Oil lubricates the compressor, and some of it leaves as a mist with the discharge gas. An ACHR News article on separators says a small amount of oil is always discharged, and that poorly sized or poorly configured lines can trap it until the compressor is starved and bearings are damaged. Another ACHR News piece puts oil pumping at about 3% to 15% of total mass flow, depending on compressor design, operating conditions, oil and refrigerant miscibility and wear. That 2000 article was written by a separator maker's employee, so treat its numbers as a rough guide. For line sizing and the piping side of oil return, see Line Set Sizing for Long Runs and Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return.

The separator in the discharge line

The separator sits in the discharge line near the compressors. Inside, the gas slows down and passes baffles and screens, so oil drops out and collects at the bottom. A float watches the level. When oil builds up, the float opens a needle valve and the pressure difference sends the oil onward. On a single compressor that oil goes back to the crankcase. On a rack it goes to a reservoir, as ACHR News describes.

Two designs are common. An impingement type uses centrifugal force and screens, which works mainly on larger droplets. A coalescing type uses a fine filter matrix that merges tiny droplets into big ones that drain by gravity. The older ACHR News article gives about 80% to 85% efficiency for impingement units and 95% to 99% or more for coalescing units, and notes that coalescing units cause less pressure drop. The 2009 article says some reach about 98%. No separator catches everything, so some oil always travels through the system and still has to come back through the suction line. A separator does not cure bad piping.

Reservoir and level controls

On a rack, oil from the separator goes to a reservoir that holds the surplus. A spring-loaded valve ties the reservoir to the suction header and holds it above suction pressure. ACHR News gives 5 to 20 psi in one article and about 20 psi in another. A trade-training article by Jeremy Smith also gives 20 psi as the most common setting. That difference is what pushes oil toward the compressors, and it keeps the reservoir from foaming oil or overfilling crankcases. Oil then passes a filter, and each compressor has its own level control at the crankcase sight glass. ACHR News describes an adjustable float regulator on each machine, and electronic controllers as an alternative.

Why one per compressor? Compressors on shared headers do not return oil evenly. Run time, size and wear differ, so one machine can run low while another floods. ACHR News says a well-balanced system returns most oil through suction and the level controls cover the moderate differences. An electronic sensor described in ACHR News waits 15 seconds, then pulses oil in, holds about half a sight glass, and raises a low-oil alarm after two minutes of feeding without reaching that level. The alarm contacts can be wired to shut the compressor down if oil is completely lost.

Low oil, logged oil and a stuck float

Low oil in a crankcase is a symptom, not a diagnosis. ACHR News says a tripped oil pressure control is a warning of another problem, and that low oil may be trapped in the suction line or evaporator, so adding oil can do more harm than good. On low-temperature systems, a defrost cycle warms the evaporator and can help move oil. The same article says to check piping slope and risers. Oil logging in an evaporator also insulates the coil surface, which the coalescing article says can raise head pressure and lower evaporating pressure.

A failed separator float can go two ways. ACHR News says the oil line leaving the separator should run only a little above room temperature most of the time; if it stays hot, the float may be stuck open and passing hot discharge vapor into the crankcase, which can cause high crankcase pressure, overheating and serious damage. Smith suggests a logging thermometer on the feed line for 10 to 15 minutes: the temperature should rise and fall as the float feeds, and no change points to a float problem. If a float sticks closed, oil stays in the separator, crankcases run low and level controls run dry.

  • Check every compressor and the reservoir level first. Consistently low levels point at the oil management system; too much oil is also a problem (Smith).
  • If the reservoir is empty but its differential is right, look for oil elsewhere before adding any (Smith).
  • A crankcase running more than about 2 psi above suction can mean blowby from worn rings, which hurts oil feed (Smith).
  • Screens, filters, the separator float, the pressure-reducing valve and regulator screens can all clog with debris (Smith).
  • The GreenChill guide says oil seepage at racks, piping and valves can mean a leak.

Checking and adding oil safely

Read each sight glass against the maker's level mark. Before adding any oil, rule out oil trapped elsewhere, as above. Use the oil grade the compressor or system maker specifies, since ACHR News notes POE grades differ in lubricity, viscosity, additives and moisture content. Rack oil systems hold pressure, so follow the maker's procedure and your site lockout rules before opening any part.

POE moisture handling

POE is more hygroscopic than mineral oil. One ACHR News article reports a figure of about 10 times the moisture uptake, free water at about 1,500 ppm, and an open system possibly contaminated within an hour (all quoted from a contractor interview, not a lab test). Moisture plus heat can form acid. So keep containers closed except while pouring, store oil in its original container, since many plastic packages pass moisture, cap open system ends during long pauses, and use high-water-capacity driers. See Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE and Filter Driers and Sight Glasses.

Worked example: how fast oil can log (our own numbers)

All inputs below are our own assumptions for practice, not field data. The 2% is below the 3% to 15% range quoted above, so real racks can be worse.

  1. Assumed rack: 6 compressors, refrigerant flow 1,800 lb/h, oil circulation rate 2% of refrigerant mass flow.
  2. Oil leaving the compressors: 1,800 x 0.02 = 36 lb/h.
  3. Assumed separator efficiency 95%, so 5% passes through: 36 x (1 - 0.95) = 36 x 0.05 = 1.8 lb/h reaches the system. The other 36 - 1.8 = 34.2 lb/h goes back through the float to the reservoir.
  4. Level check: the reservoir sight glass shows an assumed 4 gal. With an assumed POE density of 8 lb/gal, 4 gal = 32 lb and 1 gal = 8 lb.
  5. If nothing returned, 1.8 lb/h would log about 1 gal in 8 / 1.8 = 4.4 hours. The whole 4 gal would take 32 / 1.8 = 17.8 hours.

A small percentage adds up fast, so the suction side must bring that 1.8 lb/h home. A failed separator passing more than 5% shortens the time.

Field card: Rack oil management

Find the oil before you add oil.

  • Reservoir sits about 20 psi above suction through a differential valve.
  • Hot separator return line can mean a float stuck open.
  • Low crankcase oil may be trapped in the suction line or evaporator.
  • Check all sight glasses and the reservoir before adding oil.
  • Use the specified oil grade; keep POE containers closed and sealed.
