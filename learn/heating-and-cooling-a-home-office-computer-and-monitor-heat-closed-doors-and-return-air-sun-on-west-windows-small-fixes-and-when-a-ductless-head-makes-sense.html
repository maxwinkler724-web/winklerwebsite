Heating and Cooling a Home Office: Computer Heat, Closed Doors, West Windows and Small Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A spare bedroom turned office is often the hottest room in the house by mid-afternoon and the stuffiest by evening. The cause is usually not one big problem. It is a handful of small ones stacking up: heat from the equipment and the person, a closed door that blocks air from coming back, sun on a west window, and a supply register sized for a bedroom. This lesson walks through each one, shows the arithmetic for a sample room, and ends with the simple fixes to try first.

Where the heat comes from

Almost every watt a computer draws ends up as heat in the room. A researcher at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, a U.S. Department of Energy lab, listed typical office loads in a 2011 presentation: a standard desktop PC at about 400 W, an efficient one at about 300 W, typical 19 to 24 inch monitors at 30 to 50 W each, and a multifunction printer at about 100 W. Those are older figures and today's equipment varies a lot, so treat them as a range rather than a rule. The same presentation says that putting monitors and computers into standby instead of running a screensaver would save about 70 W per person. The Department of Energy's Better Buildings program also notes that electronics keep drawing power even when they are turned off, which is one reason an idle desk is never a zero-heat desk.

People add heat too. We use an assumed 100 W per seated person in the example below. That is our own round number, not a published figure, and it is meant only to show the method.

Closed doors and missing return air

A supply register pushes air into the room, and that air has to leave somehow. If the door is shut and the room has no return grille, no transfer grille and no gap under the door, the room slowly pressurizes. The register then delivers less air than it would with the door open, because it is pushing against a room that cannot take more. Less air means less cooling in summer and less heat in winter. This is our own plain explanation of the effect; the details are in our lesson on transfer grilles and jump ducts.

The usual fixes are a transfer grille through the wall or door, a jump duct over the ceiling, or a door undercut. A bare gap under the door is often too small to matter for a room with a full-size supply, so ask a tech to size the opening. If you keep the door closed for privacy on calls, this is the first thing to fix.

Worked example (our own): why this room runs warm

This is our own example with assumed numbers. It is not a measurement of any real house.

Assumptions:

  • Desktop computer working: 200 W (assumed)
  • Two monitors at 40 W each: 80 W (assumed)
  • Printer on standby: 5 W (assumed)
  • One person: 100 W (assumed)

Step 1, add the watts. 200 + 80 + 5 + 100 = 385 W.

Step 2, convert to Btu/h. One watt is about 3.412 Btu/h, so 385 x 3.412 = 1,314 Btu/h (rounded).

Step 3, find what the register can carry away. The usual sensible-heat rule for air near sea level is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta-T. Assume the office register delivers 50 cfm with the door closed and no return path, and that supply air is 58 F while we want the room at 75 F, so delta-T is 17 F. Then 1.08 x 50 x 17 = 918 Btu/h.

Step 4, compare. The equipment and person add 1,314 Btu/h, and the register can remove only 918 Btu/h. That is a shortfall of about 396 Btu/h, and we have not yet counted sun through a window, heat through walls and ceiling, or warm attic air. The room warms until the air leaving it is warm enough to carry the extra heat. To carry 1,314 Btu/h with 50 cfm, delta-T has to be 1,314 / (1.08 x 50) = 24.3 F, which puts the room near 58 + 24.3 = 82 F. That is why the office drifts well above the thermostat setting.

What changes if we fix the airflow. With a return path that lets the register deliver 80 cfm at the same 17 F, it can carry 1.08 x 80 x 17 = 1,469 Btu/h. That covers the 1,314 Btu/h of internal heat with a little left over for sun and walls. Switching off the second monitor and the idle desktop when you step away trims the load further.

Sun on west-facing windows

The Department of Energy's Energy Saver page on windows advises closing curtains on south- and west-facing windows during the day in warm weather, and says awnings on those windows provide shade. It also says white or light-colored shades, drapes or blinds reflect heat away, and that exterior shades are preferred when possible. For an office, the west window is the one that matters most in the afternoon. Our lesson on window shades, blinds, films and awnings goes into the choices. If glare on the monitor is also a problem, a light-colored shade pulled partway helps with both.

Simple fixes first

  • Close the blinds before the sun arrives, not after the room is already hot.
  • Turn off idle equipment. A second monitor, a printer that stays warm and a desktop left running all add watts, and each watt is 3.412 Btu/h.
  • Use a fan for people, not for the room. A fan moves air across your skin so you feel cooler, but it does not remove heat from the room. Switch it off when you leave.
  • Open the door when you are not on a call. It restores the return path in seconds.
  • Adjust registers carefully. Opening the office register fully is fine. Closing many registers elsewhere to push air to the office can raise duct pressure and cause noise. If the office needs more air, ask a tech to check the duct balance. Our lesson on upstairs too hot and uneven temperatures covers this.

When a ductless head or room unit makes sense

If the fixes above are not enough, the room probably needs its own cooling. A window air conditioner is the cheapest route if a window fits one; see window air conditioners. A single ductless head, a wall-mounted indoor unit linked to an outdoor unit, serves one room without using the house ducts, so it works with the door closed and does not depend on return air. Our lesson on adding AC without ducts compares the options. A small office has a small load, so a common mistake is a head that is too big, which cycles on and off and removes moisture poorly. Have the load worked out rather than guessed.

Noise for video calls

A window unit can be loud on a call, while a ductless indoor unit is usually quieter because the compressor is outdoors, though fan speed still matters. Whistling or rattling registers and ducts are a separate issue; see noisy ductwork at home. Closing the door for sound makes the return-air problem worse, which is one more reason to fit a transfer grille rather than rely on a gap. These noise points are our own general advice.

Fresh air and CO2 in a small closed room

People exhale carbon dioxide. The Environmental Protection Agency's fact sheet on ventilation in offices says CO2 can accumulate when too little outdoor air is brought in, and that excess CO2 can indicate inadequate ventilation. It adds that outdoor air rates are based mainly on controlling odors and CO2. The EPA's introduction to indoor air quality says pollutants can build up to levels that cause health and comfort problems when too little outdoor air comes in, and that opening windows and doors is one form of natural ventilation. In a small office with the door shut and windows closed for hours, stuffiness is a signal. Cracking the door or a window when the weather allows helps. We give no target CO2 number here because the sources we read for this lesson did not set one.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Home Office: Computer Heat, Closed Doors, West Windows and Small Fixes

Equipment, people and sun add heat; a closed door takes airflow away.

  • Every watt of equipment becomes heat: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Air carries away about 1.08 x cfm x delta-T Btu/h (a standard rule near sea level).
  • A closed door with no return path cuts the supply air reaching the room.
  • Shade west windows before the sun arrives.
  • Turn off idle monitors and printers; use a fan for people only.
  • Do not close many registers elsewhere to feed the office.
  • Window unit or one ductless head if the load is still too much.
  • Open the door or a window now and then for fresh air.
