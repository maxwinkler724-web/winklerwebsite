Keeping Noise Off Control and Sensor Wiring: Twisted Pair, Shields, Ground Loops and Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A temperature reading that jumps a few degrees, or a pressure value that shifts every time a fan motor starts, is often not a bad sensor. It is electrical noise riding on the wire. This lesson covers how low-voltage signal and communication wiring picks up noise, how twisted pair, a shield, and sensible routing keep it out, and the quick meter checks that tell you which problem you have.

How noise gets onto a signal wire

A long sensor cable is an antenna. Wires carrying line voltage, motor current, or the rapidly switching output of a variable frequency drive (VFD) throw off electric and magnetic fields, and any nearby signal wire picks up a little of that as a small unwanted voltage or current. A university guide to field wiring for analog signals notes that current signal sources are more immune to this kind of noise than voltage sources, because the induced voltage shows up in series with the source rather than across it. That is one reason 4-20 mA loops are favored for long runs. A 0-10 V signal has a much smaller span to protect, so the same pickup shows up as a larger share of the reading. Network wiring is affected too: noise can corrupt data, which looks like dropped devices or intermittent comms errors. See BACnet, Modbus and LonWorks in Plain English for how those links work.

Twisted pair: cancel the magnetic pickup

When the two conductors of a signal pair are twisted together, each small loop picks up an induced voltage opposite to its neighbor, and the pickup largely cancels. A national-laboratory-hosted shielding tutorial describes it the same way: twisting shrinks the loop area, so magnetic pickup roughly sums to zero. Twisting does not stop electric-field (capacitive) pickup, which is what a shield is for. The university guide recommends a shielded twisted pair for each analog input pair. For the field tech, that means: use the cable type the control maker specifies, keep the pair twisted right up to the terminal, and do not strip back more insulation or untwist more length than the terminal needs.

The shield: ground it at one end

A shield is a foil or braid around the pair, usually with a bare drain wire. It intercepts electric-field noise and carries it to ground. The common field rule for low-frequency noise such as 60 Hz is to ground the shield at one end only, normally at the controller end. The university guide states that significant ground currents flow in a shield that is grounded at both ends, and gives an illustration: a 1 V difference between two ground points across a shield with 0.5 ohm of resistance would drive 2 A through the shield.

That is a ground loop. Two grounds in two parts of a building are rarely at exactly the same potential, especially when large motors are running. Tie the shield to both, and the difference pushes current along the shield, which sits right next to your signal pair and couples that current into it. The national-lab tutorial says the same: ground-potential differences, usually at line frequency, drive current through a shield grounded at both ends and can cause an error at the receiver. A floating shield that touches nothing is worse than none, according to the university guide, so the shield must go to ground somewhere.

There are honest exceptions. The same tutorial says that above about 1 MHz grounding both ends with a full-circumference bond is recommended, and a hybrid approach grounds one end directly and the other through a small capacitor. A federal space agency test report found that in its high-frequency injection tests, grounding both ends reduced noise. Those are lab and electronics settings. For HVAC controls, follow the control maker's wiring instructions for the exact cable and device in front of you, and when the instructions say one end, do one end.

Keep signal wiring apart from power

The university guide advises that signal cables not be run close to AC or other power cables. A drive maker's installation manual (hosted by a manual distributor, so we cite it as plain text) gives practical routing rules for a VFD: keep control cables on separate trays from the motor cable and input power cable, avoid long parallel runs with the motor cable, and where control cables must cross power cables, cross them at an angle as close to 90 degrees as possible. Crossing at right angles keeps the wires from running alongside one another, so there is little length over which one can couple into the other. The national-lab tutorial explains the physics: magnetic pickup is smallest when the circuit is perpendicular to the field, and field strength falls off quickly with distance.

In practice: never share a conduit or tray section between a signal pair and a VFD output cable; route signal wiring on its own path; and keep splices in a dedicated control junction box. The VFD output is the nastiest wiring on the job because it is fast-switching. A federal energy-department publication on motor and drive systems warns about power quality problems from drives, and a national-lab best-practices page suggests keeping the drive-to-motor cable short; both support treating the drive output wiring as the noisiest cable in the building. Read the lesson on variable frequency drives on pumps and fans for the drive itself.

Symptoms that point to noise

  • Jumpy readings. The value bounces by a steady amount around the true value, faster than the process could really change.
  • Values that change when a motor starts. A shift on a start, or whenever a VFD ramps, is the strongest clue. Real temperatures do not move on a contactor pulling in.
  • Better with a load off. The reading settles when a nearby motor, drive or relay is switched off.
  • Intermittent network errors. Devices drop off and come back at about the times equipment starts.

Compare these with a sensor that has drifted: drift is slow and steady, noise is fast and tied to events. Use the sensor checks in RTDs and thermocouples in HVAC controls to rule out the sensor itself.

Meter checks

  1. AC volts on a DC signal line. Set a meter to AC volts and measure across the signal pair at the controller (and then each wire to ground). A clean DC signal should read very little AC. A reading that rises when a motor or drive starts is noise. Note that a meter averages and is slow, so a low reading does not prove the line is clean; a high one does prove it is not.
  2. Shield continuity. With the wiring de-energized and the shield lifted from the controller ground, measure resistance end to end on the shield or drain wire. It should read low, with no breaks at splices or junction boxes. A shield that is broken at a splice no longer protects the cable.
  3. Shield to ground. With the far end lifted, measure from the shield to building ground. A one-end-grounded shield should read open at the open end; a low reading there means the shield touches ground somewhere it should not, such as a metal box or conduit, and you may have a second ground point. Then check the grounded end for a good connection.

Always follow your lockout procedure and the maker's instructions, and keep the signal check separate from the point-by-point scaling check in BAS point-to-point checkout; the signal basics are in BAS analog signals.

Worked example (our own): what 0.4 mA of noise looks like

This is our own example. Assumptions: a 4-20 mA temperature transmitter scaled 0-100 F, so 4 mA is 0 F and 20 mA is 100 F. The noise is a steady 0.4 mA peak, picked up from a nearby unshielded drive output cable. These are our assumed numbers, not measurements.

  1. Signal span: 20 mA - 4 mA = 16 mA.
  2. Temperature span: 100 F - 0 F = 100 F.
  3. Sensitivity: 16 mA / 100 F = 0.16 mA per F.
  4. Jump from the noise: 0.4 mA / 0.16 mA per F = 2.5 F.

So a true 50 F reading (12 mA) would wander between about 47.5 F and 52.5 F if the 0.4 mA swings both ways. A control loop will chase that, hunting a valve or damper for no reason. For comparison, assume the same 100 F span on a 0-10 V signal: 100 F / 10 V = 10 F per volt, so 0.1 V of noise would be 1 F, and 25 mV would be 0.25 F. The two signals respond differently to the same wiring faults, which is why shield and routing care matters most on long and low-level runs.

Field card: Keeping Noise Off Control Wiring

Twist it, shield it, ground one end, keep it away from the drive.

  • Use twisted pair, and shielded twisted pair where the maker or the environment calls for it.
  • Ground the shield at one end, usually the controller end, unless the maker says otherwise.
  • Both ends grounded can carry ground-loop current along the shield.
  • Never share conduit with VFD output or line-voltage wiring; cross it at 90 degrees if you must.
  • Symptom: jumpy values or a shift when a motor starts.
  • Meter: AC volts on the signal pair, shield continuity, shield to ground at the open end.
  • Example: 0.4 mA noise on 0-100 F over 4-20 mA is 2.5 F.
