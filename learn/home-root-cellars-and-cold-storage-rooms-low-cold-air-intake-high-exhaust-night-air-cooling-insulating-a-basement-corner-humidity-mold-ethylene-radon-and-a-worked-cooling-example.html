Home Root Cellars and Cold Rooms: Vents, Humidity and Night Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A root cellar is a cold, damp, dark room that keeps garden crops for months with no refrigeration. Many homes can make one from a corner of the basement. The trick is to let cold outdoor air in on cold nights, keep the house heat out, and hold humidity where the crop wants it.

How the room works

A root cellar is a ventilated box. Cold air is heavier than warm air, so we bring it in through a vent low on an outside wall. Warm air rises, so we let it leave through a vent high on the room. A university extension guide on root cellars notes the same layering inside the room: warm air rises, so the top of the cellar will be warmest, and thermometers at several heights are worth having. The intake-low, exhaust-high layout is our own explanation of that effect; the guides we read did not give vent heights. Screen both vents to keep out rodents, as the same guide advises, and fit each with a damper you can close.

Pick the right corner

A northern state extension guide suggests putting the room in a northeast or northwest basement corner and insulating it well. A second guide says basements with furnaces are generally too warm, and suggests partitioning a north or east side, preferably one without heating pipes or ducts. We would add three habits. Build an insulated wall and door between the room and the furnace area. Keep ducts, hot-water pipes and the water heater out of the room, or insulate them. Close or block any supply register that serves the room. The idea is to keep the room off the house heating completely. Furnaces and water heaters need their own air, so do not wall off their combustion air; see the related lesson on combustion air in basements below.

Target temperature and humidity

Different crops want different conditions, which is why one small room usually has a cold end and a milder end. Two extension charts we read agree closely:

  • Beets, carrots, parsnips, turnips, cabbage family: about 32 to 40 degrees F at 90 to 95 percent relative humidity.
  • Potatoes: about 38 to 40 degrees F at 80 to 90 percent. One guide warns that potatoes held above 40 degrees F will sprout.
  • Onions and garlic: about 32 to 35 degrees F, drier, at 55 to 70 percent depending on the chart.
  • Winter squash and pumpkins: about 50 to 55 degrees F at 60 to 75 percent. One guide warns that squash will quickly mold in a moist spot.
  • Apples and pears: 32 to 40 degrees F at 80 to 90 percent.

Since onions and squash want dry, and carrots and potatoes want wet, keep them at opposite ends or in different rooms. Fruits and vegetables should not be allowed to freeze.

Using outdoor air at night

In fall and spring, days are warm and nights are cold. One extension bulletin says to close the outlet and the cold-air damper during the day in those periods, open them at night when outside air is cooler, and close them once the room reaches about 35 degrees F. Another says to close the vents tightly whenever it is warmer outside than the storage temperature, and notes that outdoor temperatures well below 32 degrees F are needed to cool the storage air to 32 degrees F and hold it there. A small exhaust fan in the high vent can speed night cooling, then be removed for winter. A thermometer outside and one inside make the open-and-close rule easy to follow.

Humidity from the floor

A dirt floor is the easiest way to keep humidity high, according to one extension guide. If the floor is concrete or wood, the same guide suggests setting several pans of water on it. Another suggests large pans of water under the fresh-air intake. Both approaches add moisture; neither is needed for onions or squash. A damp gravel bed under the shelves is a common variation, and we would only use it for the crops that want 90 percent or more.

Condensation and mold

Moist cold rooms are where condensation and mold show up. Our explanation: when warmer, humid air touches a colder surface, such as an uninsulated wall or the cold-air duct, water drops out of the air. Insulating the walls and ceiling, as the extension guides advise, keeps those surfaces closer to the room temperature. Ventilation can exhaust the excess moisture that encourages mold, and the room can be aired out when not in use. Remove any crop showing decay promptly. If moisture collects on the inside of a storage bag, one guide says to punch a few more holes.

Ethylene: keep apples away from potatoes

Apples and pears give off ethylene gas, which shortens the storage life of vegetables. One guide says potatoes are especially affected and can sprout early when stored near certain fruits. Store fruit apart from vegetables, ideally in a separate room. Cabbage, rutabagas and turnips also give off strong odors, so they get their own space too. For commercial rooms, see the produce cold storage lesson.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers. The sensible heat that moving air carries is Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference, a standard relation for ordinary air, with Q in Btu per hour.

  • Assumed room: 8 ft by 10 ft by 8 ft high, so 8 x 10 x 8 = 640 cubic feet.
  • Assumed night air change rate: 6 air changes per hour with the vents open.
  • Assumed temperatures: outdoor air 30 degrees F, room air 45 degrees F.

Step 1, airflow: 640 cubic feet x 6 changes per hour = 3,840 cubic feet per hour. Divide by 60 minutes: 3,840 / 60 = 64 cfm.

Step 2, temperature difference: 45 - 30 = 15 degrees F.

Step 3, cooling: Q = 1.08 x 64 x 15 = 1,036.8, or about 1,040 Btu per hour of sensible cooling.

Over an assumed 8 hour night, that is about 1,040 x 8 = 8,300 Btu removed. Two cautions. The cooling shrinks as the room approaches the outdoor temperature, since the difference shrinks. And the walls, floor and stored crops hold far more heat than the air does, so the room cools over several nights, not one. This example ignores moisture, heat from the crops and heat leaking in from the house, so use it as a feel for scale, not a design.

Radon and basement air

A federal radon guide says radon moves up through the soil and into the house, entering through things like cracks in solid floors and gaps around service pipes. It says testing is the only way to know your level, to put the test in the lowest lived-in level, and to fix the home if the result is 4 picocuries per liter or higher. A dirt floor is a direct opening to the soil, so if you plan a cellar with an exposed floor, test first and ask a qualified radon professional about the result. Do not rely on cellar vents as a radon fix, because the guides above do not present them that way. Also note that a storage room is not a place to spend time for long; treat it like a pantry.

When a small cooler with a controller makes sense

In warm climates, or when fall stays mild, nights may never get cold enough. Some people then put a small air conditioner or a small insulated cooler room on an add-on controller that turns the cooling unit on and off at a lower setpoint than the unit's own thermostat allows. The controller reads a probe in the room, and the unit cycles to hold it. This is our own description of the general idea, and it needs a few cautions. Keep the room well insulated and sealed, as the cooler removes moisture and may dry the air, so check humidity. Run the unit only as the manufacturer allows, since many were not built for near-freezing air. Have a licensed electrician handle any new circuit. Use the outdoor-air method first, because it costs almost nothing in power.

Field card: Home Root Cellars and Cold Rooms: Vents, Humidity and Night Air

Cold air in low, warm air out high, house heat kept out.

  • Pick a north or east basement corner, away from the furnace, pipes and ducts.
  • Insulate walls, ceiling and door; screen and damper both vents.
  • Open vents at night when it is colder outside than inside; close them in the day.
  • Roots and cabbage: cold and wet. Onions and squash: drier. Potatoes: about 38 to 40 degrees F.
  • Keep apples and pears apart from potatoes because of ethylene.
  • Our example: 640 cubic feet at 6 changes per hour is 64 cfm; at 15 degrees F difference that is about 1,040 Btu per hour.
  • Test for radon in the lowest lived-in level; the federal action level is 4 pCi/L.
