Hydro-Air Systems: Hot Water Coils in an Air Handler

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hydro-air system heats a house with hot water but delivers the heat with a fan and ducts. If yours is "not heating," the cause could be water, air in the pipes, controls, or the fan. This lesson shows how the parts fit together so you know which to check first.

What a hydro-air system is

A hydro-air system puts a hot water coil inside an air handler. A boiler or water heater makes the hot water. A circulator or zone valve sends it to the coil, and the blower pushes house air across the coil and into the ducts. Manufacturers describe their units this way. Weil-McLain's WMWFM hydronic air handler gets its hot water from an external heat source. First Co. says its Aquatherm air handlers heat a home using a properly sized gas or oil water heater. Rinnai's hydronic air handler is made to pair with its tankless water heaters or boilers. Some models also hold a refrigerant coil, so the same blower and ducts handle air conditioning. That is why you may see a cooling thermostat on a heating system.

The control sequence

Every brand does it a little differently, so read the manual for the unit in front of you. The manuals we read show three patterns.

  • Fan waits for hot water. On the Weil-McLain unit, a call for heat (R to W) starts the pump relay. The fan relay only closes once an entering water thermostat senses at least 120°F.
  • Fan waits on a timer. On the Rinnai unit, a 60-second delay follows the heating call before the blower starts, and the blower runs 30 seconds after the call ends. First Co. describes a blower delay relay on certain models that runs the pump 20 to 45 seconds before the fan starts and keeps the fan going 20 to 45 seconds after.
  • Thermostat type matters. First Co. says a gas furnace thermostat will not work with its units, and Weil-McLain lists a wrong thermostat type as a reason the fan will not run on heat while it runs on cooling.

The fan wait exists so the system does not blow cool air through the ducts while the coil is still warming. For the pump and valve side of the call, see Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators. For how a boiler's own aquastat limits work, see Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs.

Worked example: how much heat is the coil giving?

Water carries heat. A standard physics relationship for water is Btu per hour = 500 × GPM × temperature drop. John Siegenthaler gives it in ACHR News and warns that the 500 changes with the fluid and temperature, and that it works best with measured flow and temperature, not assumed ones. First Co. says its pumps move about 3 to 6 GPM and that water typically leaves the heater at 135 to 140°F and returns at 115 to 125°F. Take round numbers inside those ranges:

  1. Flow: 4 GPM.
  2. Supply to coil: 140°F. Return from coil: 120°F.
  3. Temperature drop: 140 − 120 = 20°F.
  4. Heat: 500 × 4 × 20 = 40,000 Btu/h.

Now see what a problem does. If air or a restriction cuts the flow to 2 GPM and the same 20°F drop held, the result would be 500 × 2 × 20 = 20,000 Btu/h. In practice, the drop changes as flow changes, as Siegenthaler notes, so treat this as a rough check, not a promise. The useful habit is the comparison against what the manual expects. Weil-McLain says the return line should run 15 to 20°F cooler than the supply during normal heating. Rinnai publishes ratings at 120, 140 and 160°F entering water, and the same coil gives noticeably less heat at lower water temperature (about 23,000 Btu/h at 120°F and 31,000 at 140°F for one model at medium speed).

Common reasons it is not heating

Air in the coil

Air blocks water flow. First Co. lists trapped air as a cause of insufficient heat and says a noisy pump usually means air remains in the loop, so the system should be purged again. Weil-McLain says the same about a noisy pump. Rinnai says to flush and purge the coil at start-up, and warns not to run the pump until flushing is done because running it dry can damage it. Rinnai also notes that air bubbles can travel down pipes when water moves slower than about 2 feet per second.

Water that is not hot enough

The coil can only deliver what the water carries. First Co. lists a low water heater setpoint as a cause of insufficient heat. On the Weil-McLain unit, if the leaving pump water is under 120°F, the fan is not supposed to start. If water is above 120°F and the fan still will not run, the manual says to test the entering water thermostat with an ohmmeter and replace it if it is open, noting that it is wired to high voltage. Disconnect power first.

The pump or valve is not moving water

Weil-McLain and First Co. both say a pump can stick from sitting unused, and describe freeing the shaft through the motor plug. Whatever moves the water, the no-heat steps in Hot-Water No-Heat Diagnosis apply here too.

Piping and sizing

First Co. also lists reversed piping, restrictions and undersized equipment. Weil-McLain warns that units with internal pumps will not heat if piped backwards.

Dirty filter

Weil-McLain says to change the filter every 30 days or when dirt shows, and says a dirty filter can reduce airflow and damage the unit. See Air Handling Unit Basics for how coils and filters sit in an air stream.

Fan runs, air is cool

This is a common version of "hydro air system not heating." Check which delay style your unit uses, then check water temperature at the coil. Rinnai's manual says to set a connected boiler's parameter to continuous run to prevent cold air, which shows how a heat source setting can cause the complaint.

Freeze protection in attics and cold spaces

A coil full of water in a cold space can freeze. Weil-McLain says not to install the unit where the temperature can fall below 32°F and to insulate all piping, with a low limit control on the coil if the piping passes through a space that can freeze. First Co. offers a field-installed freeze kit, recommended for attics and crawl spaces, whose sensor starts the pump if the coil drops below 38°F, even with the thermostat off. Rinnai's control board starts freeze protection at 40°F and ends it at 70°F, and its manual puts the duty on the installer to protect the water piping. Those are three different set points from three makers, so follow the one for your unit. If antifreeze is considered, read Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops, and note that Rinnai gives a capacity correction for glycol (for example, multiply by 0.95 at 20 percent).

Water-heater-fed versions and drinking water

Some units are fed straight from a water heater that also makes domestic hot water. First Co.'s FWA-HW sheet says the water source must be NSF/ANSI certified for use with domestic water. Rinnai's manual lists its unit as suitable for potable water systems, requires lead-free piping and no-lead solder on open domestic loops, and says its pump timer runs one minute every six hours to prevent water stagnating in the system. First Co. notes that Massachusetts code requires a similar pump timer for its FWA-HW models. Rinnai also describes a domestic priority switch that shuts off the air handler when hot water demand is high. If the loop is a closed boiler loop, see Indirect Water Heaters on a Boiler and Combi Boiler: No Hot Water or No Heat.

Field card

  • Fan runs, air cool: check water temperature at the coil and the delay style.
  • Noisy pump: air in the loop, purge again.
  • Fan will not start on heat but runs on cool: thermostat type or entering water thermostat.
  • Cold space: confirm freeze protection before winter.
  • Return water 15 to 20°F cooler than supply is a normal reference (Weil-McLain).
