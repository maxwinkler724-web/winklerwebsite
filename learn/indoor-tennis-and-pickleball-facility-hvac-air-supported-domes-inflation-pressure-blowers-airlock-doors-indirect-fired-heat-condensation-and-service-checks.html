Indoor Tennis and Pickleball Facility HVAC: Air-Supported Domes, Inflation Pressure, Airlock Doors and Indirect-Fired Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tennis or pickleball building is a huge box with a few people in it. An air-supported dome is the same box with no structure at all: the roof is fabric held up by air pressure. This lesson covers what the HVAC tech needs to know about both, and why the dome changes some basic rules.

Big volume, few people

Courts need a lot of height and floor, but a handful of players. Heat load from people is small, so the load is mostly the envelope, outdoor air and (in summer) humidity. Ventilation is set by the code and the occupancy, not by the volume. Our own rule of thumb: size the heating for the envelope and the air that leaks in, not for the size of the room. For the comfort side of tall spaces, see HVLS Fans and Destratification in Warehouses and Gyms.

How a dome stays up

An air-supported structure holds its shape because the air inside is at a slightly higher pressure than outside. The National Research Council Canada building digest on these structures says they need only small pressures, about 1 inch of water static pressure, or 0.036 psi, and says that for a design wind of 60 or 70 mph in U.S. practice, about 1 inch of water differential is needed. The digest also says lower pressures can be acceptable for many uses. Normal running pressure is therefore a fraction of an inch to about an inch of water column. Your own building's design pressure and control setpoints come from its design documents and the blower control panel; we do not give a single number here. Check the project's inflation specification.

Inflation blowers, backup and generator

  • Blower capacity. The digest says blower capacity must replace leakage at the design pressure. It uses an allowance of 10 cfm per linear foot of base perimeter, 200 cfm per door assembly and 2400 times the vent area (sq ft) in cfm, all at 1 inch of water, and says blowers should provide at least twice the calculated airflow.
  • Two blowers. The digest says the Canadian code requires two blowers for assembly occupancy, each able to supply the required air. The standby unit starts on failure, and "an automatic starting power generator is normally provided."
  • U.S. code text. The 2021 Seattle Building Code, Chapter 31, requires primary and auxiliary inflation, an auxiliary system for buildings larger than 1,500 sq ft, and approved standby power. It also requires automatic pressure control, backdraft check dampers on blowers and a weather-protecting housing. The design standard it names is ASCE 55. Check the code your jurisdiction actually adopted.
  • Backdraft dampers and controls. The digest recommends backward-curved centrifugal blowers with variable air volume at a set pressure, so the building is not over-pressurized.

Doors: revolving and airlock

The digest says revolving doors are the most widely used for high or continuous traffic and that their resistance to rotation does not noticeably increase with internal pressure. It describes airlocks as a two-door system where only one door can open at a time, so pressure is not lost. It also says that holding 1 inch of water with an open 3 ft by 7 ft door would need about 48,000 cfm (a 10-hp fan) and calls that impractical. Exit doors must still meet the exit rules of the code in force.

Worked example (our own, with an assumed pressure)

Assumptions: the dome is held at 0.25 in. w.c. (our assumption, picked to make the arithmetic clear, not a design value); one door leaf is 3 ft by 7 ft.

  1. Convert pressure. Water weighs 62.4 lb per cubic foot, so 1 ft of water is 62.4 psf (the Indiana environmental agency formula sheet lists 1 cu ft of water as 62.4 lb and 1 ft of head as 0.433 psi). 62.4 / 12 = 5.2 psf per inch. This agrees with the digest: 0.036 psi x 144 = 5.2 psf.
  2. Pressure on the door: 0.25 x 5.2 = 1.3 psf.
  3. Door area: 3 x 7 = 21 sq ft.
  4. Force: 1.3 x 21 = 27.3 lb pushing on the whole leaf.
  5. Our simplification: that load acts near the middle of the leaf, so a hand at the latch edge feels about half, roughly 14 lb, before door weight, closer and friction. At 1 in. w.c. the same math gives 5.2 x 21 = 109 lb.
  6. Open-door leakage. The digest's 48,000 cfm is at 1 in. w.c. Airflow through an opening scales with the square root of pressure, so at 0.25 in. the airflow is about half, or roughly 24,000 cfm (our calculation).

Why airlock doors: a door opened into a dome lets out air at a rate far beyond one blower. Two doors with a small room between them mean the pressure stays on one door while the other is open. The force numbers also show why a person can swing a door at 0.25 in. but not comfortably at higher pressures, and why you should never prop a dome door open.

Heating with indirect-fired units

An indirect-fired unit keeps combustion products inside a heat exchanger and vents them outdoors, so the room gets clean warmed air; see Make-Up Air Units: Direct-Fired vs Indirect-Fired. Our own reasons it suits a dome: the flue carries combustion products out, and the fabric shell leaks, so you do not want combustion air drawn from a space where occupants breathe it. The digest says heaters and air conditioners are acceptable provided nothing increases leakage or disrupts continuous inflation, equipment is not normally hung from the fabric, and openings in the envelope need reinforcing. Install flue and combustion air penetrations as the manufacturer and the structure's maker instruct. Heaters often sit outside and discharge through ducts to the dome, which keeps the penetrations few.

Condensation and dripping

Our own explanation: in winter, indoor air meets a cold fabric. If the surface is below the air's dew point, water forms and drips on the court. Fixes are keeping indoor humidity low, keeping the shell warm (insulated liner or better fabric), and keeping air moving across the roof surface. Ventilating with dry outdoor air in cold weather helps, and so does limiting any humidifying. Dripping on a hard court is also a slip hazard. See Indoor Pool Dehumidification for the dew point logic in a worse case.

Stratification, glare and summer

  • Stratification. Warm air rises into the peak. Destratification fans or low-velocity ducted air return it to the floor; players should not feel a draft on the ball path, which is our own comfort guidance.
  • Glare-free comfort. Supply diffusers and fans must not sit where a high lob goes. Keep fixtures out of the sightline and avoid bright, hot spots on the ceiling. Our own advice: involve the lighting designer before placing grilles.
  • Summer dehumidification. A full-size dome or hall may not be air conditioned at all, or only partly. Where it is, the cooling coil needs enough run time to remove moisture. Desiccant units are one option; see Ice Rink Refrigeration for another big-volume space with moisture control issues. Take any capacity targets from the design documents.

Snow load and heat

The digest says little or no snow normally accumulates on the shape except for heavy wet snow in calm periods, and that snow load does not add stress in the fabric and may relieve it. Gradual sagging gives advance warning. Removal methods it names include heating the structure until snow slides off, deflating by 5 to 10 percent of volume so ice cracks and then reinflating, and, on small structures, running a rope over the top. Plan heater capacity and blower control for snow events with the structure's maker, and keep people clear below sliding snow.

Service checks

  • Test the standby blower and the generator transfer on a schedule. The Seattle code text asks for automatic transfer within 60 seconds and at least 4 hours of running, so check the transfer and fuel.
  • Check inlet screens, belt guards, backdraft dampers and the weather housing the digest lists.
  • Confirm the pressure sensor reads true and the alarm works.
  • Check door sweeps, door closers and the airlock interlock so only one door opens at a time.
  • On indirect-fired units, follow the maker's combustion, flue and heat-exchanger inspection steps.
  • Log humidity, and look for wet spots on the fabric after cold nights.

Field card: Indoor Tennis and Pickleball Facility HVAC

Dome air is kept in by pressure, so every leak is a blower job.

  • Design pressure is small, about an inch of water or less; get the number from the design documents.
  • 1 in. w.c. = 5.2 psf. Door force = psf x door area.
  • Our example: 0.25 in. at 3 x 7 ft = 27 lb on the leaf.
  • Primary and standby blowers plus standby power; test transfer.
  • Airlock: only one door open at a time.
  • Indirect-fired heat keeps combustion products out of the room.
  • Condensation: keep humidity low and the shell warm.
  • Keep heater and diffuser placement out of ball paths and sightlines.
