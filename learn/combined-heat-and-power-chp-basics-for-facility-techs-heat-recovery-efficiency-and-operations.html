Combined Heat and Power (CHP) Basics for Facility Techs: Heat Recovery, Efficiency and Operations

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A power plant throws away most of the fuel energy as hot exhaust and warm water. A combined heat and power (CHP) system puts that heat to work in the building. If you are a facility tech who inherits a CHP skid next to the boiler room, this lesson explains what the machine is doing and what to keep an eye on.

What CHP is

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) describes CHP, also called cogeneration, as making electricity or mechanical power and useful thermal energy (heating, cooling or both) from a single energy source, at or near where the energy is used. The EPA CHP Partnership adds that the recovered heat can serve process heating, steam, hot water or chilled water, and that a CHP system can also provide backup power during a grid outage.

The prime movers

The prime mover is the machine that turns fuel into shaft power for the generator. EPA's Catalog of CHP Technologies lists five, with these approximate size and electric efficiency ranges (higher heating value basis):

  • Reciprocating engines: about 5 kW to 10 MW, roughly 27 to 41 percent electric.
  • Gas (combustion) turbines: about 500 kW to 300 MW, roughly 24 to 36 percent.
  • Microturbines: about 30 to 250 kW per unit, packaged up to about 1,000 kW, roughly 22 to 28 percent.
  • Fuel cells: about 5 kW to 2 MW, roughly 30 to 63 percent.
  • Steam turbines: about 50 kW to several hundred MW, run from a boiler, with a very wide efficiency span.

EPA also notes that packaged systems of about 500 kW or smaller typically use an engine, microturbine or fuel cell. A DOE fact sheet says reciprocating engines are about 58 percent of U.S. CHP sites, which is why most facility techs meet an engine first.

Where the heat comes from

DOE's reciprocating engine fact sheet names four recoverable streams: exhaust, jacket cooling water, lubricating oil, and turbocharger intercooler or aftercooler fluid. Exhaust can make steam or hot water. The other three make hot water only. DOE puts exhaust at roughly 700 to 1,000°F and says it is about half of the recoverable heat; the other streams are under 300°F and usable as hot water below about 200°F.

Efficiency: what the agencies say

EPA defines total CHP efficiency as net electric output plus net useful thermal output, divided by fuel input. The DOE CHP basics page says CHP applications can operate at about 75 percent and that 65 to 75 percent is a reasonable expectation, against a national average of about 50 percent when heat and power are bought separately. EPA's catalog gives a worked comparison: CHP needs 100 units of fuel for 75 units of useful energy, while separate systems need 147 units (about 51 percent). Its baseline assumes 35.7 percent efficient electricity and 80 percent efficient heat. For engines the catalog lists overall efficiency near 77 to 80 percent, and the DOE engine sheet says engine systems are near 80 percent with hot water only. The EPA CHP Partnership page says CHP can exceed 80 percent in good cases.

Worked example (Our own arithmetic)

Take a stated engine CHP: 1,000 kW electric output, 9.5 MMBtu/h fuel input, 4.0 MMBtu/h recovered heat. These are stated numbers for practice, not a real machine.

  1. Our own: electric output in MMBtu/h = 1,000 kW × 3,412 Btu per kWh = 3,412,000 Btu/h = 3.412 MMBtu/h.
  2. Our own: electric efficiency = 3.412 ÷ 9.5 = 0.359, or 35.9 percent.
  3. Our own: thermal efficiency = 4.0 ÷ 9.5 = 0.421, or 42.1 percent.
  4. Our own: total efficiency = (3.412 + 4.0) ÷ 9.5 = 7.412 ÷ 9.5 = 0.780, or 78.0 percent. That sits inside the 77 to 80 percent engine range EPA lists.

Now compare with separate production using EPA's baseline (35.7 percent power, 80 percent heat):

  1. Our own: fuel for the same power = 3.412 ÷ 0.357 = 9.56 MMBtu/h.
  2. Our own: fuel for the same heat = 4.0 ÷ 0.80 = 5.00 MMBtu/h.
  3. Our own: separate total = 9.56 + 5.00 = 14.56 MMBtu/h, a blended efficiency of 7.412 ÷ 14.56 = 50.9 percent, which matches the roughly 50 percent figure DOE and EPA quote.
  4. Our own: fuel saved = 14.56 − 9.5 = 5.06 MMBtu/h, about 34.7 percent less fuel than separate systems.

The saving only exists while the 4.0 MMBtu/h of heat is actually used. Dump the heat and you are back to the 35.9 percent electric figure.

Loads that suit CHP

EPA says CHP works best where power and heat are needed year-round at the same time. Its list includes hotels, nursing homes, multifamily buildings, colleges and universities, hospitals, prisons, wastewater plants and K-12 buildings. EPA's example hospital uses recovered heat to drive absorption chillers in summer so the heat has a use when heating demand is low; DOE's microturbine sheet says direct-exhaust absorption chillers can push total system efficiency to roughly 85 to 90 percent. See Absorption Chillers for that side.

Grid interconnection and outages, in brief

A CHP unit normally runs paralleled with the utility. Whether it can keep a building running during an outage depends on how it is built. DOE's engine sheet says synchronous generators and inverter-based systems can black start, while induction generators cannot and will not operate in an outage. EPA's catalog says an engine needs only minimal auxiliary power to start, generally batteries or compressed air. Do not assume a CHP skid is a backup generator: ask whoever commissioned it. Backup hookups are a separate topic; see Running a Furnace or Boiler on a Generator.

What facility techs watch

  • Recovery temperatures. EPA's catalog gives jacket coolant hot water of about 190 to 230°F (up to 265°F in ebullient systems), and says exhaust heat recovery units are typically designed for a 250 to 350°F outlet to avoid condensation corrosion. A falling return-water margin or a high exhaust outlet temperature points to fouling or flow trouble.
  • Dump radiator operation. EPA says an excess heat exchanger sends heat to a cooling tower or radiator when the building cannot absorb it, and small skid packages include a radiator and fan. Check that the fan, louvers, valve and controls actually move when the building loop is hot, and log how often the dump runs. Frequent dumping means wasted fuel.
  • Maintenance intervals. EPA's catalog gives routine service (oil, filters, coolant, spark plugs) typically every 500 to 2,000 hours, top-end overhauls at 8,000 to 30,000 hours and major overhauls at 30,000 to 72,000 hours, and says oil analysis is common. Those are ranges from a survey; your engine maker's schedule governs. Follow it and the service contract.
  • Emissions. EPA lists NOx, CO and VOCs as the main pollutants. Rich-burn gas engines typically use a three-way catalyst; lean-burn engines use an oxidation catalyst and often SCR (DOE engine sheet). Know your site air permit limits and keep catalyst and sensor records. A lean-burn engine tuned for top efficiency makes about twice the NOx of a low-NOx tuning, per EPA.
  • Rounds and logs. Treat the skid like boiler plant equipment: see Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules. For fuel economics, see Comparing Heating Fuel Costs per Million BTU.

Lockout, hot exhaust, hot water and electrical hazards all apply. Follow the maker's manual and your site procedures. New here? Try Start Here.

Field card: CHP basics

One fuel, two outputs; the heat only counts if it is used.

  • Total efficiency = (electric + useful thermal) ÷ fuel input.
  • 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu.
  • DOE: CHP about 65 to 75 percent vs about 50 percent separate.
  • Engine heat: jacket water, exhaust, oil cooler, aftercooler.
  • Watch: recovery temperatures, dump radiator duty, service hours, emissions records.
  • Black start depends on generator type; confirm with the commissioning record.
