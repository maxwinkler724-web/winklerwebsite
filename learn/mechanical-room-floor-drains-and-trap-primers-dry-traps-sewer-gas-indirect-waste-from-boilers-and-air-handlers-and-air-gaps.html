Mechanical Room Floor Drains, Trap Primers and Air Gaps

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler room floor drain looks like the least interesting thing in the room, until it dries out and the room smells like a sewer, or a relief valve discharge lands where it should not. This lesson covers why the drain is there, how its trap fails, and how boiler, air handler and cooling tower water reaches it legally.

Check your local rules

Check your local rules. Plumbing codes differ by state and city, and editions change. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026, not legal advice. Confirm with your local plumbing authority before you rely on it.

Why mechanical rooms have floor drains

Boilers drain down, relief valves discharge, pump seals leak, coils overflow and tower basins get emptied. A floor drain gives that water somewhere to go besides the floor and the room below. The drain connects to the building sanitary system, so it needs a trap, a U-shaped bend that holds water and blocks sewer gas from entering the room.

How a trap fails

A trap only protects while it holds water. A floor drain in a mechanical room may see no water for weeks, so the seal slowly evaporates. A trade magazine article on floor drains says traps on drains not tied to a supplied fixture tend to evaporate, and describes the seal as a barrier of 2 to 4 inches of water. Once the seal is gone, air moves freely between the sewer and the room.

Hydrogen sulfide is the part of sewer gas people notice first. A New Jersey Department of Health fact sheet describes it as flammable and colorless with a rotten egg odor, says many people smell it at 10 parts per billion, and lists eye, nose or throat irritation at low exposure, with possible nausea, headache and breathing trouble for people with respiratory problems. A sewer smell in a plant room is a report to make, not a mystery to air out.

Trap primers and seal protection

A trap primer adds a little water to the trap so the seal does not vanish. The same article lists three kinds. A pressure-drop primer opens when supply line pressure falls by 3 psi, as when a nearby fixture is used. A flush-valve primer takes a small amount of water from a toilet or urinal flush valve through a tube. Multiple and electronic primers serve several drains from a manifold, and the electronic type runs on a timer that releases enough water each day to hold the seal. Other seal protection devices exist, such as deep-seal traps and mechanical valves, but we found no public code text on when each is accepted, so ask your authority.

What can an HVAC tech do? Notice a dry trap and report it. Do not assume a primer works because one is present: the supply, the cartridge and the small tube to the trap can each clog or leak.

Indirect waste, air gaps and air breaks

Equipment discharge usually does not connect straight to the drain. It ends above a receptor so sewage cannot back into the equipment. The 2022 New York City plumbing code, which follows the model code, says an air gap must be at least twice the effective opening of the indirect waste pipe (section 802.2.1). An air break instead sits between the indirect waste pipe and the trap seal of the receptor (802.2.2). That code calls for an air gap where relief valves discharge potable water (802.1.3) and allows an air break or an air gap for nonpotable waste from boilers (802.1.5). Never cap or plug a relief discharge; see the relief valve lesson below.

Hot discharge, blowdown and condensate

Drains have temperature limits. The same city code says water above 150 F may not go into any part of a drainage system and that steam pipes may not connect to one (803.1). A California code page we read gives 140 F for pressurized discharge, and says boiler blowdown goes through indirect waste to a condenser or sump, with a deep-seal outlet trap and a full-size vent (810.1 and 810.3). The limits differ by code, so find out which applies. Air handler condensate and cooling tower drain water normally reach a drain through an air gap or air break; blowdown detail is in the blowdown and tower lessons linked below.

Worked example 1: how long a trap seal lasts

Assumptions (Our own, for teaching; real rates vary with room temperature, air movement and humidity): a 2 inch seal that loses 1/8 inch per day.

  1. Days to empty = 2 in / 0.125 in per day = 16 days.
  2. In a hot room at an assumed 1/4 inch per day: 2 / 0.25 = 8 days.
  3. A 3 inch seal at 1/8 inch per day: 3 / 0.125 = 24 days.

A drain that sees water once a month can sit dry for weeks. A primer or a scheduled top-up closes that gap.

Worked example 2: cooling hot blowdown before the drain

Assumptions (Our own): 10 gallons of blowdown water at 212 F, cold water at 55 F, target 150 F (the city code limit above), equal specific heat, no steam flash loss. Energy balance: 10 gal x (212 - 150) = x gal x (150 - 55).

  1. Left side: 10 x 62 = 620.
  2. Cold water needed: x = 620 / 95 = 6.5 gallons.
  3. For a 140 F target: 10 x 72 = 720, divided by (140 - 55) = 85, gives 8.5 gallons.

A real blowdown receiver is sized by the boiler maker and the code, so this only gives a feel for scale.

Who does the work

The New Jersey rule on the plumbing subcode named the National Standard Plumbing Code 2021 edition, and the state codes page lists it as in place before newer model codes were adopted on August 17, 2026, so confirm which edition applies now. The state master plumbers board FAQ says plumbing is performed by a master plumber, an authorized plumbing contractor, or their W-2 employees. Our own reading, for you to confirm: connecting a relief or condensate line to a drain, adding a trap, or tapping a water supply for a primer is plumbing, so expect a licensed plumber and a permit. An HVAC tech can usually inspect, test, clean and report. See the HVACR license lesson below for what that license covers.

Field card: Mechanical Room Floor Drains, Trap Primers and Air Gaps

No water in the trap means no barrier against sewer gas.

  • Floor drain traps hold a 2 to 4 inch seal; rarely used drains dry out.
  • Primer types: pressure-drop (3 psi), flush-valve, electronic timer.
  • Air gap: at least twice the effective opening of the waste pipe (model code text).
  • No steam to a drain; hot water limit is 150 F or 140 F depending on code.
  • Never cap a relief discharge or tie it solidly to the drain.
  • In NJ, plumbing connections belong to a licensed plumber. Check local rules.
