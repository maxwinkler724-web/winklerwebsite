Material Takeoff for a Split-System Install From a Sketch

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A good install day starts in the shop. A takeoff turns a rough sketch of the house into a list of lengths and counts, so the truck leaves with what the job needs and nobody makes a trip for one elbow. This lesson walks through a takeoff for a residential split system: line set, fittings, insulation, condensate pipe, wire and conduit, a waste allowance, and a last check of the list against the job.

What a takeoff is

A university project management text describes a quantity takeoff as breaking a project into items whose quantities are measured, or taken off, from the plans. A university lecture on material takeoff shows the same idea in a different trade: quantities come from the drawings, and waste is added as its own step. That lecture adds 10 percent to reinforcing steel for overlaps and cut waste, which is a figure for that material only. For a split system, the "plans" are usually your own sketch from the site visit, so the sketch has to be good enough to measure.

Start with a sketch you can measure

Draw the route the pipe and wire will actually take, not the straight line between the two units. Mark each straight segment and each direction change. Write down the length of every segment as you measured it on site. Note anything that changes the route: a joist you must go around, a wall you must penetrate, a roof edge, an obstruction in the attic. Our rule is one segment, one number, one label. If a number is a guess, write it with a question mark and measure it again before you load the truck.

Pre-job planning is also a safety step. OSHA's job hazard analysis guidance says to identify hazards before a job begins and at each step in the job. While you list segments, note the hazards that go with them, such as a roof or attic access, a long ladder carry, or a heavy coil of pipe. Those notes belong on the same sketch. See Pre-Installation Site Survey for a New Home HVAC System for what to look at during the visit.

Line set length

Add the segments along the path. Then add slack for each end, because you need enough tubing to reach the service valves and the coil connections without tension. Then add the waste allowance. Check the equipment installation manual for the longest line set the unit allows without extra steps, and for how the charge changes with length. See Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments. A longer route than the manual covers is a design question to settle before you buy tubing.

Fittings and elbows

Walk the sketch from one end to the other and mark every direction change. Each change is one elbow on each of the two refrigerant lines, so count them twice. Add couplings wherever the tubing run is longer than a single piece, and add the fittings for any sleeve, strap or support the route needs. Count what the sketch shows first, then add a small spare count for anything you may need to redo after a bad cut or a misjudged bend.

Insulation, condensate pipe, wire and conduit

  • Insulation. The suction line needs insulation over its whole length, plus pieces to cover each fitting. The line set insulation lesson covers thickness and sun damage; see Refrigerant Line Set Insulation: Thickness, UV Damage and Sweating Lines. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the liquid line.
  • Condensate pipe. Measure the drain path from the coil pan to the discharge point, including the trap, any cleanout and the drop outside. Count every elbow and tee. Plan the fall of the pipe along its length, and keep it on the sketch so you do not run short of pipe or hangers.
  • Low-voltage wire. Measure the path from the air handler to the thermostat and from the air handler to the outdoor unit. Count conductors from the equipment wiring diagram and the control plan, then add spare conductors; see Installing and Splicing Thermostat and Control Cable.
  • Power wire and conduit. Measure from the panel or existing disconnect to the new disconnect, and from the disconnect to the unit. The unit nameplate and the electrical code where you work set the wire size and the protection, so take those from the nameplate and the code, not from memory. Conduit comes in fixed stick lengths, so round up.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not from a real job. Assumptions: the sketch in the figure; 2 ft of slack at each end of the line set; a 10 percent waste allowance on every length (our own working figure, not a published rule); conduit and drain pipe sold in 10 ft sticks; line set tubing available in 25, 50 and 75 ft lengths. Change any of these to match your shop.

  1. Line set route. A 12 + B 9 + C 18 + D 3 = 42 ft. Add 2 ft of slack at each end: 42 + 4 = 46 ft.
  2. Line set with waste. 46 x 1.10 = 50.6 ft. That is over a 50 ft length, so under our assumptions the order is the 75 ft length, or we re-measure and tighten the route first. Settle that before you leave, and check the manual for what the longer length does to the charge.
  3. Elbows. The route turns three times: along the ground to up the wall, up the wall to the attic run, and the attic run down to the air handler. That is 3 turns x 2 lines = 6 elbows, plus 2 spares = 8.
  4. Suction line insulation. The route is 46 ft. 46 x 1.10 = 50.6, so order 51 ft, plus 3 extra fitting covers for the suction line elbows.
  5. Condensate pipe. Assume the drain runs 20 ft along the attic, 9 ft down the wall and 4 ft outside: 20 + 9 + 4 = 33 ft. 33 x 1.10 = 36.3 ft, which is 4 sticks of 10 ft. Count 3 elbows, 1 trap and 1 cleanout tee.
  6. Low-voltage wire. Assume 30 ft from the air handler to the thermostat and use the 46 ft route to the outdoor unit: 30 + 46 = 76 ft. 76 x 1.10 = 83.6, so order 84 ft of cable.
  7. Conduit. Assume 35 ft from the panel to the disconnect and 6 ft from the disconnect to the unit: 35 + 6 = 41 ft. 41 x 1.10 = 45.1 ft. At 10 ft per stick that is 4.51, so order 5 sticks.
  8. Power conductors. Each conductor needs the 41 ft run plus 3 ft of slack at each end: 41 + 6 = 47 ft. 47 x 1.10 = 51.7, so 52 ft each. Assume 3 conductors (two hot and one ground, per the nameplate): 52 x 3 = 156 ft. The wire size comes from the nameplate and code, not from this lesson.

Why add a waste allowance

Pipe gets cut short, a kinked section is thrown away, tape and hangers get dropped on a roof. The lecture notes above treat waste as a visible line in the takeoff instead of a hidden fudge, and we do the same: the allowance is its own multiplication, so the next person can see it and change it. A bigger allowance fits a messy attic route or a first-time crew; a smaller one fits a short, straight run you measured twice. Never add waste to a number you only guessed. Fix the number first.

Check the list against the job before you leave the shop

  1. Read the list back against the sketch, segment by segment, and tick each one.
  2. Check units: feet versus sticks, conductors versus total wire length.
  3. Have a second person re-add the segments, or add them again yourself in the reverse order.
  4. Compare the equipment nameplates and installation manuals to the list: line sizes, wire size, protection, and the longest route allowed.
  5. Check the safety items from the sketch: ladders, a way to carry the coil, hangers, and personal protective equipment.
  6. Walk the truck and see that each item is physically there, not just on paper.

Field card: Material Takeoff for a Split-System Install

Measure the path, count the turns, add waste in its own step, check before you drive.

  • Sketch the real route; label each segment with a measured length.
  • Add end slack, then the waste allowance. State the percent you used.
  • Elbows: one per turn, per line. Add a few spares.
  • Round up to stock lengths: pipe sticks, conduit sticks, tubing lengths.
  • Take wire size and protection from the nameplate and local code.
  • Check the manual for the longest allowed line set and the charge change.
  • Re-add the numbers, then tick the list against the truck.
