Power Factor on HVAC Motors: kW, kVA, kVAR and Correction Capacitors Explained

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A motor nameplate gives amps, but amps are not the same as work done. Power factor is the gap between the two, and it explains why a utility may care about a motor that is not even broken. This lesson covers kW, kVA and kVAR in plain words, a full worked capacitor calculation, and the rules that keep correction capacitors from causing trouble.

Three kinds of power

A DOE Motor Challenge fact sheet defines power factor as the ratio of real power to apparent power. Real power (kW) does the work: it turns the shaft. Reactive power (kVAR) is the magnetizing part. It moves back and forth between the motor and the line and does no work. Apparent power (kVA) is the total the wiring and transformer must carry. The three form a right triangle, so kVA squared equals kW squared plus kVAR squared, and power factor is kW divided by kVA. A Washington State University Extension fact sheet gives an example of 100 kW with 102 kVAR, which comes to about 143 kVA and a power factor of 0.7.

Why lightly loaded motors have poor power factor

The WSU fact sheet says an induction motor's power factor is "dramatically worse" when it runs well below nameplate horsepower, and that even an unloaded motor still draws more than half of its full-load reactive power. An EC&M article gives the same reason: the magnetizing field, and so the reactive current, stays roughly constant as load falls, while the kW falls. It puts a typical standard motor's rated power factor around 0.85. The DOE sheet's advice follows: avoid idling or lightly loaded motors. Our own takeaway for a tech: an oversized or lightly loaded blower or pump motor is a poor-power-factor motor. See Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter for why running amps below the nameplate are normal.

Why a utility cares

The DOE sheet says utilities usually charge a penalty to customers whose power factor is below 0.95. The WSU sheet adds that utilities with such penalties usually meter and bill on displacement power factor. Billing varies by utility and rate class, and some bill on kVA demand instead. Read the tariff for the account; we are not quoting any rate here.

Worked example: 460 V motor, 52 A, 0.78 power factor

Our own arithmetic, using the standard three-phase formulas and the capacitor relation (kW times the difference of two tangents) used in the DOE and WSU examples.

  1. Apparent power. kVA = 1.732 × 460 × 52 / 1000 = 41.43 kVA.
  2. Real power. kW = kVA × PF = 41.43 × 0.78 = 32.32 kW.
  3. Reactive power. kVAR = √(41.43² − 32.32²) = √(1716.5 − 1044.4) = 25.93 kVAR.
  4. Tangents. acos(0.78) = 38.7 degrees, tan = 0.8023. acos(0.95) = 18.2 degrees, tan = 0.3287.
  5. Capacitor. kVAR needed = 32.32 × (0.8023 − 0.3287) = 32.32 × 0.4736 = 15.3 kVAR. Leftover reactive power is 25.93 − 15.30 = 10.62 kVAR.
  6. New current, same kW. kVA = 32.32 / 0.95 = 34.02 kVA. Amps = 34.02 × 1000 / (1.732 × 460) = 42.7 A, about 18 percent less line current (52 to 42.7).

The WSU sheet's own example shows the same effect, cutting current about 27 percent when going from 0.70 to 0.95. Nothing here changes the work the motor does. Only the current in the supply side of the capacitor drops.

Do not oversize the capacitor

Step 5 is not the end. Schneider Electric's Electrical Installation Guide limits a capacitor connected directly across a motor to Qc ≤ 0.9 × Io × Un × √3, where Io is the motor's no-load current and Un is line voltage in kV. The reason is self-excitation: if a high-inertia motor keeps spinning after the supply opens, the capacitors can feed the windings and push terminal voltage dangerously high. For illustration only, suppose a 460 V motor had a no-load current of 20 A (an assumed number, not from the example). Then 0.9 × 20 × 0.46 × 1.732 = 14.3 kVAR, which is under our 15.3 kVAR. In that case the motor-mounted capacitor would be capped near 14 kVAR and the power factor would land a little under 0.95. Use the real no-load current or the capacitor maker's table. The same guide notes that the motor's overload relay current reading is reduced once a capacitor is at the motor, so the relay setting needs attention; always follow the capacitor and starter instructions.

At the motor or at the service

The Schneider guide says correcting at an individual motor makes sense when that motor's kVA is large compared with the installation, and a central bank is the option for many smaller loads. Our own reasoning: correction at the motor also cuts current in the branch wiring, while correction at the service helps only upstream of it. A motor-side capacitor that stays on when the motor stops is the case that causes the self-excitation risk, so we wire it to switch with the motor (Schneider states this for larger banks).

VFDs, ECMs and harmonics

Do not put capacitors on a drive's output. ABB's ACS550 manual includes, in its installation checklist, "NO power factor compensation capacitors are in the motor cable." Schneider Electric's FAQ for its Altivar drives goes further and does not recommend power factor correction capacitors with its drives, citing resonant currents on systems with 6-pulse drives. The WEG capacitor manual lists variable speed drives among non-linear loads whose harmonics can push capacitor current above its thermal rating, and the WSU sheet warns that harmonics can create harmful resonance currents in capacitors. The EC&M article says a PWM drive with a diode bridge front end presents a "near unity" displacement power factor to the line; its example reads 0.96 at the drive input while the motor itself was 0.89. That is displacement power factor only, and the article cautions that harmonics mean true power factor is not the same. For how drives work, see Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans and ECM Blower Motors.

Safety: capacitors hold charge

WEG's safety manual says to always discharge and short-circuit capacitor terminals before handling, even after disconnection, and says residual voltage must be no more than 10 percent of rated voltage before re-energizing. It notes that units with built-in resistors are rated to reach that level within 30 seconds, and that motor capacitors usually discharge through the windings but the discharge time should be checked. Our own practice for a tech: lock out and tag out, wait, discharge through a proper method, then verify with a meter rated for the voltage before touching. Never switch capacitors with fuses (WEG), and keep them protected by correctly rated overcurrent devices. Related unbalance trouble is covered in Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance.

Field card: Power Factor on HVAC Motors

kW works, kVAR magnetizes, kVA is what the wire carries.

  • PF = kW / kVA. kVA = 1.732 × V × A / 1000.
  • Lightly loaded motors have poor PF; the magnetizing current stays.
  • Capacitor kVAR = kW × (tan acos PF1 − tan acos PF2).
  • 460 V, 52 A, 0.78 to 0.95: 15.3 kVAR; current 42.7 A.
  • Do not exceed the motor's no-load magnetizing limit at the motor.
  • No capacitors on drive outputs.
  • Discharge, then verify with a meter before touching.
