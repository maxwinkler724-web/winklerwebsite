Pressure-Pan Testing on Ducts: Reading Register Pressures and Targeting Leaks

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A leakage test gives you one number for the whole duct system. A pressure-pan test gives you eight or ten small numbers, one per register, and those numbers tell you which runs are tied to the outdoors. That makes it the tool for deciding where to put the mastic before anyone sets up the full test. This lesson covers how the pan readings work, how to read them, where leaks tend to hide, and how a zonal check of an attic or garage fits in.

What the test measures

A pressure pan is a shallow cover that seals over a register or grille, with a tap for a hose to a gauge. A national-lab building-data glossary describes the test this way: with a blower door pressurizing or depressurizing the house, the gauge on the pan shows a pressure difference that identifies which duct runs leak to the outside, but it does not put a number on the leakage.

An Oak Ridge National Laboratory field report describes the usual procedure. The blower door pulls the house to 50 Pa below the outdoors, then the supply and return registers are blocked off one at a time with a shallow pan, and the pressure difference across each register is read. Here is why that works, in our own words. The pan isolates one run from the room. Whatever pressure the duct sees from its own leaks is what the gauge shows. A run whose leaks open into the conditioned house stays near the house pressure, so the pan reads close to zero. A run whose leaks open to the outdoors, or to an attic or crawl space that is itself tied to the outdoors, gets pulled toward the outdoor pressure, so the pan reads higher.

A simple setup checklist (our own)

  1. Finish the blower door setup and bring the house to 50 Pa below outdoors, as in the field report above.
  2. Leave the air handler off for the test.
  3. Zero the gauge, and run the pan hose to the duct side of the gauge with the other side open to the room.
  4. Seat the pan on one register at a time, leave the other registers open, wait for the number to settle, and write it down with the register name.
  5. Repeat for every supply and every return. Do the returns too: they are the registers people skip.

Treat sign conventions loosely. Different gauges show the same difference as plus or minus, so record the size of the reading and keep the same hose setup for every register and for the after test.

Reading the numbers

Federal and state weatherization documents give several rules of thumb. None of them is a code limit, and they do not agree exactly, so use them as a guide to priority.

  • The Oak Ridge report says a difference under 1.0 Pa suggests any duct leaks are far from that register, while a larger difference, 2 to 5 Pa or more, points to a duct leak near the register. It adds that some agencies count the readings above a chosen cutoff, 1.0 Pa as an example, as a measure of how leaky the whole system is.
  • A Texas state housing agency guide says 1.0 Pa indicates a small hole and 7.5 Pa a large hole, and that any register above 0.8 Pa should be considered for more sealing. The same guide stresses that the test does not tell you how many cfm are lost.
  • A North Dakota state commerce department field form says that for mobile homes, work may be accepted when the readings average 1 or less and every visible penetration in the trunk is sealed. Otherwise the crew must document why the ducts were left as they are.

Put those together and you get a practical picture. Near 0 means tight, or at least not connected to anything cold or hot. Around 1 Pa is the edge. Several pascals means a leak close to that register. High single digits means a big opening. Because the sources give no single pass line for all houses, the 1.0 Pa line in our example below is our own working threshold, not a code or program requirement.

Where the leaks hide

The Oak Ridge report lists the leakage sites that crews typically find and seal first: disconnected ducts, panned floor joists with missing end plates and poor joints, junctions between register boots and the wall or floor surface, filter slots and equipment panels. It also says the pans help find leaks hidden in walls and floors that nobody can see.

  • Boots. The boot-to-drywall or boot-to-subfloor gap is a classic. The pan reading at that register jumps because the leak is right there.
  • Panned returns. A return that uses a floor joist bay or wall cavity as the duct is a long, leaky path with many seams. That is why return registers often read the highest.
  • Attic runs. Long flex or metal runs in a vented attic that have a cracked collar or loose trunk joint pull outdoor-connected air. Pair this with our lesson on air handlers and ducts in unconditioned attics.

A high pan number tells you the run to look at. It does not tell you the exact joint. After the pan points to a run, you find the joint by sight and by feel, then seal it. Our lesson on sealing duct leaks with mastic or foil tape covers the materials and the places to look, so we do not repeat that here.

Zonal checks: is the attic or garage tied to the house?

A pan reads the duct against the house, so the zone the duct sits in changes what a reading means. The Oak Ridge team saw this with basements. They opened and isolated the basement from the house for the test, and noted that if the basement had been treated as part of the house, most pan readings would have been near zero.

Zone pressure diagnostics go one step further. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building-science page describes the method: you measure the house against the outdoors and the house against the zone (an attic, basement or attached garage) with the blower door running, ideally before and after a known change to one boundary. The pressure from the house to the zone must lie between 0 Pa and the house-to-outdoor pressure. The tool then estimates how much air leaks between the house and the zone and between the zone and the outdoors. A large house-to-zone share means the living space is tied to that attic or garage. A large zone-to-outdoor share means the zone is open to outside.

Our shorthand for field use, drawn from that rule: with the house at -50 Pa, a zone that reads near 0 Pa against the house is strongly tied to the house, and a zone that reads close to the full -50 Pa against the house is strongly tied to the outdoors. Anything in between is a split. If a pan run sits in a zone that is half tied to outside, its pan reading will land partway up the scale even when the duct itself is only a little leaky.

Using it on a new home before the total leakage test

On a new home the duct system is open and often easy to reach before the drywall goes up or the attic insulation goes in. Our suggested order, not a code procedure:

  1. Do the pan test while you can still get at the joints.
  2. Seal the worst run first, then the next, and re-pan the ones you touched.
  3. When the pans are quiet, set up the total duct leakage test, which is the one with a pass or fail limit. See duct leakage testing, total versus leakage to outside, and code limits.

The reason to do it this way is simple: a failed total test only says the system leaks, and pan readings say where. Our rough-in lesson on HVAC rough-in for a new home covers the pre-drywall timing. For what a blower door visit looks like, see what happens in a home energy audit.

Worked example (our own example)

These numbers are invented for teaching. They are not from a real house.

Assumptions: a house with six supply registers and two return grilles. Blower door at 50 Pa below outdoors. Our own working line: any register above 1.0 Pa gets sealed and re-checked.

Before sealing, pan readings in Pa: Kitchen 0.4, Living 0.6, Master 1.2, Bed 2 0.5, Bed 3 3.8, Bath 0.3, Hall return 7.5, Great-room return 0.9.

Total of the readings: 0.4 + 0.6 = 1.0, plus 1.2 = 2.2, plus 0.5 = 2.7, plus 3.8 = 6.5, plus 0.3 = 6.8, plus 7.5 = 14.3, plus 0.9 = 15.2 Pa. Average = 15.2 / 8 = 1.9 Pa.

Which runs to seal first: the three above 1.0 Pa, in order of reading. The hall return at 7.5 Pa is first (a large opening, and it is a panned joist bay). Bedroom 3 at 3.8 Pa is second (the long attic run). The Master at 1.2 Pa is third and is probably just a boot gap.

After sealing: Master 0.7, Bed 3 0.9, Hall return 1.1; the other five did not change. Total = 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.7 + 0.5 + 0.9 + 0.3 + 1.1 + 0.9 = 5.4 Pa. Average = 5.4 / 8 = 0.675, which rounds to 0.68 Pa. Change in the total = (15.2 - 5.4) / 15.2 = 9.8 / 15.2 = 0.645, or about 65 percent lower.

Result: seven of eight registers meet our 1.0 Pa line. The hall return, at 1.1, is still just above it, so we go back for one more look at its end caps and boot before calling the total test. The pan numbers do not convert to cfm. The total test is what shows how much air the system actually loses.

Limits of the test

  • It ranks runs. It does not give cfm, and a Texas state guide says so directly.
  • Readings depend on the zone around each duct, so pans on runs in the basement, attic or garage need the zone checks above.
  • It does not replace the total duct leakage test where a code or a rating requires one.

Field card: Pressure-Pan Testing on Ducts

Near zero is tight. Higher means tied to outside.

  • House at 50 Pa below outdoors; pan one register at a time; read the gauge.
  • Under 1.0 Pa suggests leaks are far from that register; 2 to 5 Pa or more suggests a leak nearby (national-lab field report).
  • Check boots, panned returns, filter slots, equipment panels and attic runs.
  • Seal the highest reading first; re-pan the runs you touched.
  • Pans rank runs. They do not give cfm. Follow with the total leakage test.
  • Check the zone (attic, garage, basement) before trusting a reading on a run inside it.
