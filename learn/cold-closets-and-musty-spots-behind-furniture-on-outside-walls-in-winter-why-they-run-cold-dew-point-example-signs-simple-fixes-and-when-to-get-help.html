Cold Closets and Musty Spots Behind Furniture in Winter

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You open a bedroom closet in January and it smells stale. The shoes on the floor feel clammy, and the wall behind the dresser has gray spots on the paint. The rest of the room is comfortable. This lesson explains why those spots run colder than the room, how that turns into moisture, and what you can do about it yourself.

Why a closet or a furniture-covered wall runs cold

A heated room is warmed by air from supply registers or by radiators, and that air keeps moving. A closet usually has no register of its own and a shut door, so little warm air reaches it. The same is true behind a dresser, a couch or a bookcase pushed flat against the wall. The furniture blocks both the moving air and the heat that would otherwise reach the wall surface.

Add an outside wall and the wall surface starts out colder than the room. A closet on an exterior wall also tends to have the least insulation in the house: a thin spot in the wall, insulation that has settled down inside the cavity and left the top bare, or a gap near a corner or framing. These are common causes in our experience, and a quick look from a pro can tell you which one applies. The result is a wall surface that can be many degrees below the room temperature. Our own example below uses 50 F and 44 F surfaces in a 70 F room as assumptions.

How cold surfaces turn into moisture

Indoor air always carries some water vapor. Cool that air enough and it cannot hold the vapor any longer, and liquid water appears. The temperature where that happens is the dew point. The federal mold guide says mold needs moisture to grow, that mold spores are already in indoor air, and that condensation on windows, walls or pipes is a sign of high humidity that should be dried quickly. A state university extension page on home moisture lists mold in a closet corner and winter window condensation as symptoms of the same problem.

Air next to a cold wall is cooled by the wall, and that air has little chance to mix with the drier room air when a closet door is shut or a couch is in the way. So the surface can be damp even when the middle of the room feels fine. You do not need visible drops of water. A surface that stays damp or very humid for days is enough to feed mildew on cloth, leather, cardboard and paint.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measurement of any real home. Assumptions: indoor air at 70 F and 40 percent relative humidity; two possible closet wall surface temperatures, 50 F and 44 F. We found the dew points with a standard saturation-vapor-pressure relation and rounded them to the approximate values below, which match what a psychrometric table gives for these conditions.

  1. Dew point of 70 F air at 40 percent: about 45 F (our calculation gave 44.6 F).
  2. Wall at 50 F: 50 is above 45, so about 5 F of margin and no liquid water on the surface. It stays dry to the touch.
  3. Wall at 44 F: 44 is below 45, so the surface is colder than the dew point, and it can collect moisture.
  4. Now drop the room to 30 percent at the same 70 F. The dew point falls to about 37 F (our calculation gave 37.1 F).
  5. The 44 F wall is now about 7 F above the dew point (44 minus 37), so it no longer collects liquid water.

One more caution, also our own arithmetic. Even without liquid water, the air touching a cool surface has a higher relative humidity than the room. In the same method, the 70 F, 40 percent air cooled to 50 F is at about 83 percent, and the 30 percent air cooled to 44 F is at about 76 percent. That is still damp enough that cloth and cardboard can stay humid in a closed, still space. The federal mold guide says to keep indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. So lowering humidity helps a great deal, but air movement and a warmer wall matter too. The extension page gives a winter target of about 25 percent, so in a very cold spell aim for the lower part of the range.

Signs to look for

  • A musty or stale smell when you open the closet, even if you see nothing.
  • Gray, black, green or white spots on drywall, paint, caulk or baseboard, often in a corner or low on the wall.
  • Damp or clammy shoes, boxes, bags and stored clothes, especially items pressed against the wall.
  • Peeling paint or a cold, slightly damp feel on the wall surface behind furniture.
  • Window condensation in the same room. See Condensation on Windows in Winter: Indoor Humidity and What to Do.

A musty smell with no visible source can mean hidden mold. The federal guide lists that as a reason to get help rather than guess.

Simple fixes to try first

  • Pull furniture a few inches off outside walls. Even three or four inches lets room air flow behind it and warm the wall. This is our own rule of thumb, not a published figure.
  • Keep closet doors open a good part of the day, or swap a solid door for a louvered one, so the closet shares room air.
  • Do not pack a closet tight. Leave a gap between clothes and the exterior wall, keep boxes and shoes off the floor against the wall, and avoid sealed plastic bins of damp items.
  • Keep winter indoor humidity moderate. Follow the federal range above, and use a simple humidity gauge. If you run a humidifier, turn it down when windows or closets show moisture.
  • Use bath and kitchen fans. The federal guide says to run the bathroom fan when showering and a fan or open window when cooking or running a dishwasher. Vent dryers outdoors as well. For sizing and ducting, see Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting, and Moisture Control.
  • Dry damp items quickly. The federal guide says water-damaged items should be dried within 24 to 48 hours.

Small mold cleanup, and when to stop

The federal cleanup guidance says a moldy area smaller than about 10 square feet, about a 3 ft by 3 ft patch, can usually be handled by the homeowner. Fix the moisture cause first and dry the area. Scrub hard surfaces with detergent and water, then dry them fully. Do not paint or caulk over mold. The mold guide suggests an N-95 respirator, gloves and goggles. Ceiling tiles and carpet that get moldy may have to be thrown away, because mold grows into them. Check the federal pages for how to treat other porous items. For valuable or sentimental items, ask a specialist.

Get help instead of doing it yourself if mold covers more than about 10 square feet, there was significant water damage, the water was contaminated, hidden mold is suspected, or someone in the home has health concerns (talk to a health professional first). If you suspect the HVAC system itself is contaminated, the guide says not to run it until it has been checked.

When to have insulation or air leaks checked

If the same spot grows damp or musty every winter even after you move furniture, open the door and lower humidity, the wall itself is probably the problem. Ask a qualified contractor to look for missing or settled insulation and for air leaks that let cold air reach the wall cavity or let warm, moist air get into it. The extension page advises sealing openings that let warm, moist air into walls, ceilings and the attic. For background on insulation, see Home Insulation Types Explained. A thermal camera or a simple surface thermometer can show cold spots, and a blower-door test shows leaks, but you do not need either to start with the fixes above.

Field card: Cold Closets and Musty Spots Behind Furniture in Winter

Cold surface plus humid air equals moisture.

  • Closets and furniture-covered outside walls get less warm air and can have thin or settled insulation.
  • A surface below the air's dew point can collect moisture. Our own example: 70 F and 40 percent has a dew point of about 45 F; at 30 percent it is about 37 F.
  • Signs: musty smell, spots on drywall or baseboard, damp shoes and boxes.
  • Try first: furniture off the wall, doors open, loose packing, moderate humidity, fans.
  • Under about 10 square feet you can usually clean it; larger, hidden or contaminated, get help.
  • Repeat problems: have insulation and air leaks checked.
