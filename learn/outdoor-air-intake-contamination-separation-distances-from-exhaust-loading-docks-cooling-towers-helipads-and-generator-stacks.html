Outdoor Air Intake Contamination: Separation Distances from Exhaust, Docks, Towers, Helipads and Generators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A building can have a perfect exhaust system and still smell like its own exhaust. The fan works, the duct is tight, and the odor comes back in through the outside air intake. This lesson is about the distances that keep intakes away from trouble, and about how to trace a complaint when they did not.

Check the edition and the local code

Check the edition. Separation distances change between code editions and between jurisdictions. The table numbers below come from the 2007 edition of ASHRAE Standard 62.1, which we read in a state-posted copy, and from the International Mechanical Code (IMC) as adopted in Washington State. Your jurisdiction may use a later edition with different numbers. Use this page to learn the idea, then read the code your inspector enforces. This is general education, not design advice.

What re-entrainment means

Re-entrainment is exhaust air, or a nearby smell, being pulled back into a building through an outdoor air intake. The ASHRAE Exhaust Re-Entrainment Guide lists layout traits that raise the risk: a direct line of sight between exhaust and intake, both sitting inside one screened enclosure, a recirculation zone on the roof, an intake mounted above the exhaust, and exhaust that discharges sideways, downward or under a rain cap. Our lab exhaust stack lesson covers stack height and discharge velocity in depth, so we do not repeat it here: see Laboratory Exhaust Fans and Stacks. The fire station lesson covers diesel exhaust capture at the source: Fire Station Apparatus Bay HVAC.

What the codes say about distance

Two documents do most of the work. ASHRAE 62.1 has a table of minimum separation distances (Table 5-1 in the 2007 edition). The IMC, section 401.4, sets intake opening location rules.

Selected Table 5-1 entries, ASHRAE 62.1-2007
Object near the intakeMinimum distance
Truck loading area or dock, bus parking or idling area25 ft (7.5 m)
Cooling tower exhaust25 ft (7.5 m)
Cooling tower intake or basin15 ft (5 m)
Significantly contaminated exhaust (strong odor or irritation)15 ft (5 m)
Noxious or dangerous exhaust30 ft (10 m)
Combustion vents, chimneys and flues15 ft (5 m)
Garbage pick-up area, dumpsters15 ft (5 m)
Roof or grade directly below the intake1 ft (0.30 m)

The table notes matter. Distance for vehicles is measured to the closest place where vehicle exhaust is likely to be, not to the building wall. Where snow is expected, the below-intake distance grows by the expected average snow depth.

The IMC in the Washington State version we read (WAC 51-52-0401) says intake openings must be at least 10 ft from lot lines or buildings on the same lot, and at least 10 ft horizontally from hazardous or noxious contaminant sources. The City of Seattle text of the same section names vents, streets, alleys, parking lots and loading docks as examples. An intake may sit closer than 10 ft horizontally to a street, alley, parking lot or loading dock if it is at least 25 ft vertically above it. When a contaminant source is within 10 ft of the opening, the intake must be at least 3 ft below it. The Seattle text also sets 1 ft above the roof or grade below an intake.

Cooling towers, helipads and generators

Cooling towers. The CDC cooling tower page says to place towers at least 25 ft from building air intakes so the drift plume is not pulled into the ventilation system, and to use high-efficiency drift eliminators. It adds that during emergency cleaning, nearby intake vents should be closed, especially those downwind. If Legionella is present, drift aerosols can carry it long distances. Our cooling tower Legionella lesson covers the program side.

Helipads. The FAA heliport advisory circular (paragraph 2.7.7.5.2) says helicopter engine exhaust can affect building air quality if a heliport is too close to fresh air vents. For an existing building it suggests moving the vents or, if that is not practical, using charcoal filters or a fresh air intake bypass louver system. It gives no separation distance, so we do not either. Jet fuel smell on landing days is this problem. We found no number to quote for rotor wash, so the fix is a design-team decision.

Generator stacks. We found no permanent-stack separation number to cite. The NIOSH carbon monoxide page tells workers to keep gasoline engines outside, at least 20 ft from building openings, and to keep their exhaust away from air intakes. That advice is for portable engines, not a roof stack, but it shows the same idea. NIOSH lists a ceiling limit of 200 ppm for CO. In our experience, complaints cluster on generator test days. Our generator room ventilation lesson covers the room side.

Security and height

The NIOSH building protection guidance points the same direction for a security reason. It says intakes at or below ground level are most at risk and the lowest edge should be as high as feasible, ideally on a secure roof or high sidewall. It also says not to seal intakes permanently, because that harms indoor air quality.

Worked example: a dock and a tower

Assumptions (our own example, not a real building). A rooftop intake is 18 ft horizontally from the spot where trucks idle at a loading dock. The cooling tower exhaust is 40 ft away. We use the 2007 table entries above.

  1. Dock. Table minimum is 25 ft. Actual is 18 ft. 25 - 18 = 7 ft short, so it does not meet the table. Under the IMC alternative, an intake at least 25 ft vertically above the dock is acceptable at a shorter horizontal distance, so the fix could be height, not distance.
  2. Tower. Table minimum for tower exhaust is 25 ft. Actual is 40 ft. 40 - 25 = 15 ft of margin, and the CDC 25 ft guidance is also met.
  3. Dilution at the intake. Say a tracer in the exhaust is at 500 ppm CO2-equivalent (assumed). Say the dilution factor between the exhaust and the intake is 50 (assumed). 500 / 50 = 10 ppm above the outdoor background at the intake.
  4. Doubling distance. The ASHRAE re-entrainment guide gives a rule of thumb that doubling the distance increases dilution by about 4 times. Doubling 18 ft to 36 ft: 50 x 4 = 200. Then 500 / 200 = 2.5 ppm above background. This is a screening rule from the guide, not a prediction for your roof. Wind, building shape and exhaust velocity can change it a lot.

The same guide calls external dilution near 75:1 low risk and below 10:1 high risk, so 50:1 sits in between.

Finding the source of a complaint

  • Match the smell to the clock. Note the time, the wind direction and what was running: dock trucks, a generator test, a helicopter, tower fans, a plumbing vent. In our experience, a pattern that follows the wind points to re-entrainment.
  • Look at the intake itself. Leaves, debris and drifted snow can block the louver and starve the unit. Snow also lowers the effective height of the opening, which is why Table 5-1 adds the expected snow depth. Checking this is our own field habit, not a code rule.
  • Walk the roof. Check for exhaust that points sideways or has a cap, a plumbing vent near the louver, and a dock exhaust fan. Plumbing vents count as contaminant sources under the IMC wording we read; we did not confirm a plumbing code distance, so read your local plumbing code.
  • Do a controlled release. The ASHRAE re-entrainment guide describes an odor test: release a detectable substance in the exhaust and walk the area to find where the odor appears, at target dilutions from 1:10 to 1:100. Smoke checks follow the same logic. Our own habit is to tell the fire alarm contractor and the occupants first, so a smoke test does not trip a detector, and to use a smoke product made for building airflow work, never a combustible. We found no standard tracer gas procedure for intakes, so follow your engineer or industrial hygienist.

Field card: Outdoor air intake separation

Source, distance, wind, then fix.

  • ASHRAE 62.1-2007 Table 5-1: dock 25 ft, tower exhaust 25 ft, tower intake or basin 15 ft, combustion vents 15 ft.
  • IMC 401.4 (Washington version): 10 ft from contaminant sources, or 25 ft vertically above streets, lots and docks.
  • CDC: towers at least 25 ft from intakes.
  • Dilution example: 500 ppm / 50 = 10 ppm above background.
  • Rule of thumb: doubling distance gives about 4 times the dilution.
  • Log time, wind and operations before guessing.
  • Use the edition your jurisdiction adopted.
