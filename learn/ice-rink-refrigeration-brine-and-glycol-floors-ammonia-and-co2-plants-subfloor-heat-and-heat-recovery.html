Ice Rink Refrigeration: Brine and Glycol Floors, Ammonia and CO2 Plants, Subfloor Heat and Heat Recovery

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An ice rink is a very large, very flat evaporator with people standing on it. The plant under the floor is ordinary refrigeration, but the loads, the fluids and the heat that has to go somewhere else make it a good case study. This lesson follows the heat from the ice to the condenser and back into the building.

The floor, layer by layer

An ACHR News article on rink design describes the common build: a 5 or 6 inch reinforced concrete pad with a grid of 1 inch polyethylene or steel pipe on 3 to 4 inch centers, fed from 6 to 10 inch headers. Ice is frozen directly on that slab. Below it sit insulation and a vapor barrier, then a second, warmer pipe grid about a foot lower for subfloor heating. The same article says the cooling grid usually carries calcium chloride brine, and the heating grid carries a brine of its own. The diagram below is our own simplified section.

Indirect and direct systems

In an indirect system the refrigerant cools a secondary fluid, and a pump sends that fluid through the floor. OxyChem, a calcium chloride maker, describes the brine as a closed loop: it is chilled, circulates to cool the surface, then returns to be chilled again. In a direct system the refrigerant itself boils in the slab piping. ACHR News says direct floors are more efficient but that the larger refrigerant leak exposure has reduced their popularity, and that they were used in few U.S. systems and rarely in Canada. A Natural Resources Canada (NRCan) arena study lists both secondary-fluid options (calcium chloride, ethylene glycol and methanol) and a CO2 option that vaporizes CO2 directly in the slab piping. The study's experts flagged the large CO2 charge under the ice as a concern. The glycol side of this is covered in Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops.

Temperatures: ice, brine and the gap between them

The NRCan study uses an ice temperature of 21°F (-6°C). A University of Illinois energy page says hockey wants harder ice and figure skating softer ice, so brine temperature is adjusted for each. It adds that raising ice temperature by one degree can save 6 percent of annual refrigeration cost, and that thicker ice needs colder brine. A Leddy Arena refrigeration audit also used 21°F in its heat-transfer calculation and noted the ice can drift up 4 to 6°F when the rink is unoccupied.

For brine numbers, NRCan's CanmetENERGY paper on multi-pass brine networks simulated a -9°C supply, and its measured four-pass return was -7.3°C with an ice surface near -5.5°C. That is a brine temperature rise of only about 1.7°C across the floor, which is why rink loops move a lot of fluid. The paper adds that the brine pump often accounts for over 15 percent of the plant's energy use. Those numbers are for one test rink, so treat them as an example, not a design value.

Worked example: brine flow

Our own example. The load, temperature rise and brine are all assumptions. We chose them to be close to the figures above.

  • Assumed refrigeration load: 100 tons. ACHR News gives a typical range of 45 to 300 tons for rinks.
  • Assumed brine temperature rise: 3°F (about 1.7°C).
  • Assumed brine: calcium chloride with a specific gravity of 1.20. OxyChem's table lists 1.189 at 20 percent and 1.228 at 24 percent, so 1.20 is roughly 21 percent.
  • Specific heat: a 1910s National Bureau of Standards paper lists 0.708 at 0°C and 0.702 at -10°C for a density of 1.20 (cal per g per °C, which equals Btu per lb per °F). At about -8°C we use 0.70.

Step 1, load in Btu/h. 100 tons × 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 1,200,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, heat carried per gpm per degree. Water weighs about 8.33 lb per gallon, so 8.33 × 60 min = about 500 lb/h per gpm. For this brine: 500 × 1.20 × 0.70 = 420 Btu/h per gpm per °F.

Step 3, flow. gpm = 1,200,000 ÷ (420 × 3) = 1,200,000 ÷ 1,260 = about 952 gpm.

Check. At a 6°F rise the same load needs about 476 gpm, half the flow. As a loose sanity check, the NRCan test rink's two-pass network ran 56.9 L/s, which is about 900 gpm, the same size of flow. The NRCan paper found that halving flow with a four-pass layout let a smaller pump motor cut about 93,000 kWh per year for that rink, which is the trade the designer is making: bigger temperature rise and less pumping against an ice surface that is less even.

Plants: ammonia, HFC and CO2

The NRCan study compares ammonia (GWP 0), CO2 (GWP 1) and HFCs such as R507A (GWP 4000) and R410A (GWP 1725 in that study; EPA's SNAP table lists 2,090 for R-410A). Its listed ammonia charges run from 45 to 320 kg, or 0.6 to 4.1 kg per tonne of capacity, against 6.5 and 20.7 kg per tonne for the two CO2 designs. The authors note an ammonia plant needs a Class T mechanical room under the Canadian code and describe ammonia as a dangerous product. They note that CO2 rink plants had less than ten years of operating history and can run transcritical at pressures above 7 MPa. Read the hazard rules in Ammonia Refrigeration Basics and the pressure side in CO2 Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems before you touch either.

Subfloor heat

If nothing warms the ground, the cold slab slowly freezes the soil below it. The NRCan study lists "heating the slab under the ice to prevent soil from freezing" as a use of recovered heat. The Leddy audit assumed a 55°F ground temperature and 60 percent heat exchange efficiency, and estimated about 850,000,000 Btu per year for this duty. ACHR News says the heat usually comes from rejected plant heat, with boilers or electric heaters used occasionally, and the heating grid is spaced much wider (12 to 24 inch centers) than the cooling grid.

Resurfacing water

The resurfacer lays a thin layer of hot water. Leddy's audit assumed 110°F water and 60 gallons per resurfacing, with 10 resurfacings a day on weekdays and 16 on weekends. The University of Illinois page notes that switching to unheated demineralized water removes the heating step and reduces the refrigeration load.

Fog and condensation

Summer humidity causes fogging and ceiling condensation, which can rust ceilings and grow stalagmites on the ice, according to the Minnesota Center for Energy and Environment (CEE). CEE names desiccant dehumidifiers as one fix; the Leddy audit shows an installed desiccant unit. For how those units work, see Desiccant Dehumidifiers. The NRCan study used 50 percent relative humidity over the ice.

Heat recovery

An arena guide says an ice plant gives off on average about 7.2 million Btu of heat per day, more than 2,000 kWh, mostly from the condenser. NRCan lists the uses: shower and resurfacing water by desuperheater, heating the stands and locker rooms, slab heat, and melting the shavings scraped off the ice. ACHR News adds fresh-air preheat and snow melt pits. The NRCan authors say full recovery can cut energy cost by over 40 percent. Cautions from ACHR News: colder outdoor weather means less heat available, so do not make recovered heat the only heat for an occupied area, since a plant shutdown in cold weather causes real trouble. Domestic hot water reclaim must be double-walled and vented. The general method is in Heat Recovery Chillers.

Field card: Ice Rink Refrigeration

Cold floor on top, warm floor below, and the condenser heat has a job.

  • Indirect: refrigerant cools brine or glycol, which flows through the slab. Direct: refrigerant boils in the slab.
  • Brine flow (US units): gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × SG × specific heat × temperature rise).
  • Example: 100 tons, 3°F rise, SG 1.20, 0.70 specific heat gives about 952 gpm (our own assumptions).
  • Ice at about 21°F in the NRCan study; one degree warmer can save about 6 percent.
  • A warm subfloor loop keeps the soil from freezing; recovered heat often feeds it.
  • Summer fog and ceiling condensation point to humidity control.
  • Do not rely on recovered heat as the only heat for an occupied space.
