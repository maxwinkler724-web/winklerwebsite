Learning From an Experienced HVAC Tech as a New Helper

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Your first months on a truck are a long, paid chance to watch a working tech. How much you learn depends less on talent than on habits: how you show up, when you ask, what you write down, and whether you speak up when something looks unsafe. This lesson covers those habits.

Arrive ready

Being ready means the lead never has to wait on you. Be on time, wear the protective gear your employer requires, and bring the basic hand tools and meter your lead asked for. If you are not sure what to carry, ask the day before. Our apprentice tool list covers what to buy first and what to borrow. Bring a pocket notebook and a pen that works in a cold or damp van.

A federal workplace safety page says workers can get safety and health training in a language they understand, and that employers must keep the workplace free of known hazards. Ask for the training you were promised. Do not wait for a hazard to teach you the lesson.

Watch hands and meters

On a ride-along, most of the teaching is silent. Watch where the lead puts their hands first: what gets shut off, what gets checked, what gets touched last. Watch the meter too. When the lead clamps a wire or probes a terminal, note which function is selected and what number appears. Then guess what the next test will be before it happens. Over a few weeks your guesses will get better, and the misses show you what you do not yet understand.

Ask at the right moment

Good questions are fact-seeking. A university extension publication on workplace mentoring says facts and information are far better than assumption and speculation. That fits our trade: "Is it the capacitor?" is a guess, while "What reading told you it was the capacitor?" gets you the reasoning.

  • Not mid-diagnosis. When the lead is holding probes, counting a sequence or thinking, hold the question. Write a short note and ask during a pause.
  • Not in front of an upset customer. A question that sounds like "do you know what you are doing?" hurts trust. Save it for the van.
  • Good times: while loading up, on the drive, over a break, and right after a job when the details are fresh.
  • Safety is the exception. If you think someone could get hurt in the next few seconds, say so now.

A state apprenticeship mentoring guide suggests bringing a list of questions to one-on-one meetings and using the answers to plan what to work on next. A running list in your notebook does the same job.

Keep a pocket notebook

Write down what you were shown, not just what you did. One line per task is plenty: the date, the task, the key steps in order, one reading with units, and one question for later. Keep customer details out of it. Our field notebook lesson goes deeper on readings and baselines.

Repeat the steps back

After the lead shows you a task, say the steps back in your own words: "So we shut off the disconnect, verify it is dead, pull the panel, then check the wire." If you left something out, you find out while it is cheap to fix. Repeating back also tells the lead that you are listening, which makes them more willing to hand you the next task.

Ask to try, with supervision

Watching only gets you so far. Ask for a turn once you have seen a task a couple of times: "Can I do the next one while you watch?" Supervised practice is the middle step in the show, watch, do, then alone pattern in our training-a-helper lesson. Do not start a task alone that you have only watched. Some jobs involve electrical, gas or refrigerant hazards that need a trained and qualified person on the spot; your employer decides what you may do and when.

Accept correction

You will be corrected, often quickly and not always gently. The state mentoring guide says apprentices should seek advice and feedback and be committed to accepting it and making needed changes. In practice: say "got it," fix it, write the correction down, and do not argue on the roof. If something seems wrong or confusing, ask about it later in private. The extension publication also notes that mentors may encourage you to learn from difficult experiences, so your own mistakes are part of the process. Our lesson on owning mistakes shows how to report them.

Speak up on safety, even as the newest person

Being new makes this harder and more important. A federal research institute page on young and new workers says little experience and a lack of safety training contribute to injuries, and that workers should speak with a supervisor about all safety concerns. A federal workplace safety page says it is illegal for an employer to retaliate against a worker for complaining to the agency, and that when a condition clearly presents a risk of death or serious harm, there is no time for an inspection and the employer has been told where possible, a worker may have a legal right to refuse the work. That is a narrow situation, so ask your employer on day one what its rule is for stopping a job over a safety concern, and who to tell.

On lockout, the federal hazardous energy page says each worker must be trained so they know, understand and can follow the applicable energy control procedures. Workers in the area where a procedure is used are to be instructed in its purpose and use, especially the ban on restarting or re-energizing locked or tagged equipment. So as a helper: never turn on, reset or restore power to equipment that has a lock or tag on it, and ask the lead to show you the procedure. See Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

When two techs teach different methods

It will happen. One tech brazes with nitrogen flowing and another skips it, or two techs charge a system in different ways. Do not pick a side and do not argue. Ask each one, politely, why they do it that way. Then check the maker's installation or service instructions for that equipment, since they are the written reference both techs work under. If the instructions are clear, follow them and tell your lead what you found. If they are silent or conflict with what you were shown, ask your lead or supervisor to decide. The extension publication's advice to prefer facts over assumptions applies here.

Worked example (our own): a four-week task log

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a new helper keeps a tally in a pocket notebook. Each distinct task type gets one line and counts at its highest stage reached by Friday. Percentages, each count divided by tasks logged and rounded to one decimal:

  • Week 1: 8 / 9 = 88.9% shown only, 1 / 9 = 11.1% tried with help, 0 / 9 = 0% alone.
  • Week 2: 7 / 16 = 43.8% shown only, 7 / 16 = 43.8% tried with help, 2 / 16 = 12.5% alone (rounding makes the total 100.1%).
  • Week 3: 7 / 21 = 33.3%, 9 / 21 = 42.9%, 5 / 21 = 23.8%.
  • Week 4: 8 / 24 = 33.3%, 10 / 24 = 41.7%, 6 / 24 = 25.0%.

What the numbers show: the "done alone" share climbed from 0% to 25.0% in four weeks, while 33.3% of tasks were still only shown. That is normal in our example, because new task types kept arriving (9 in week 1, then 7, 5 and 3 more, which sum to 24). Another way to read it: 16 of 24 tasks, or 66.7%, had been tried by the helper at least with help. If your own log shows a stack of "shown only" lines, that is the list to take to your lead: "Can I try these next?"

Field card: Learning From an Experienced HVAC Tech as a New Helper

Ready, curious, written down, and willing to stop a job.

  • Arrive on time with the gear and tools your lead asked for, plus a notebook.
  • Watch hands and meters; guess the next test before it happens.
  • Ask fact-seeking questions in pauses, never mid-diagnosis or in front of an upset customer.
  • Write down the steps, one reading with units, and one question per task.
  • Repeat the steps back, then ask to try with supervision.
  • Accept correction, note it, and fix it.
  • Never restore power to anything locked or tagged. Speak up on any safety concern right away.
  • Two methods? Ask why, then check the maker instructions.
