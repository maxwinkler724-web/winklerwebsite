Centrifugal Fan Types and Fan Curves: FC, BI, Airfoil and Plenum

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a blower housing and you will see one of a few wheel shapes. The shape decides how much air the fan moves at a given pressure, how its power draw behaves, and whether a duct change can overload the motor. This lesson sorts the main types, shows how to tell them apart in the field, and runs one energy comparison with made-up numbers.

The wheel types and where each is used

Forward-curved (FC). Twin City Fan's Aerovent literature describes small, numerous blades whose concave side faces the direction of rotation, used for high airflow at modest pressure, mostly 3 in. w.g. or less. Loren Cook's catalog counts 24 to 64 shallow blades and ties FC fans to low-pressure HVAC equipment such as residential furnaces. The Department of Energy (DOE) pump and fan report says FC wheels are common in central circulation fans and room air conditioners, handle clean air only, and can overload the motor if static pressure drops.

Backward-inclined (BI). The blades lean away from the direction of rotation. Loren Cook counts 10 to 12 single-thickness blades. They come flat, curved, or as an airfoil shape. DOE's report puts BI wheels in commercial and industrial HVAC and larger HVAC-specific equipment.

Airfoil (AF). A BI wheel with airfoil-shaped blades. The DOE sourcebook, Improving Fan System Performance, calls it the most efficient of the family but notes that, because the blades rely on lift, it is highly susceptible to unstable operation from stall. Greenheck groups airfoil, BI and backward-curved wheels as the most efficient centrifugal designs, best for clean air.

Radial. Six to twelve flat blades pointing straight out, rugged and tolerant of dirty or particle-laden air. Aerovent describes them as lower in efficiency and louder than FC or BI, used for material conveying and combustion air.

Plenum (plug) fans and arrays. These are unhoused wheels, usually BI or airfoil, that sit inside the air handler casing and discharge into the plenum. Aerovent says BI wheels suit plug, plenum and in-line fans without a housing. DOE's report says unhoused fans are used only in larger commercial air handling and estimates that 43 percent of commercial centrifugal fan shipments are unhoused. Twin City Fan's plenum fan guide covers arrays: divide the plenum into equal cells and center each inlet in its cell.

Fan curves and power curves

A fan curve plots the pressure a fan can make against the airflow it moves at one speed. A power curve shows the shaft horsepower at those same points. The shapes differ by wheel type.

  • FC: Aerovent says horsepower rises toward free delivery, which makes it an overloading design. The DOE sourcebook says the same: power rises steadily, so the driver must be sized carefully. Its performance curve also has a dip that DOE calls a stall region at low airflow, and Aerovent also flags the dip as a region to avoid.
  • BI and airfoil: DOE says brake horsepower climbs with airflow, then drops at high flow. That is the non-overloading shape, and DOE says it is often chosen when high-flow behavior is uncertain. A motor sized near the peak will not overload as the system opens up. Aerovent gives a normal selection range of about 40 to 85 percent of free delivery for BI wheels.
  • Radial: Aerovent says horsepower rises with flow like FC, and the type tolerates operation well left of peak.

Carrier's application paper adds a field warning: in new construction or remodeling, incomplete or changed ductwork could push an FC fan into its overloading range. Link this to the fan laws and to static pressure basics.

Efficiency: the sources do not agree, and that is normal

Typical peak numbers vary by source and by how efficiency is defined. The DOE sourcebook gives 55 to 65 percent for FC and above 85 percent for airfoil. Aerovent gives about 82 percent for flat BI, 86 for curved BI and 90 for airfoil. DOE's pump and fan report says FC is around 60 to 65 percent and curved BI can reach 80 percent or more. Carrier's paper gives stand-alone static efficiency of 60 to 80 percent for FC and 75 to 80 for airfoil. Greenheck says airfoil static efficiencies peak near 80 percent at about 60 percent of free delivery. Treat these as ranges, and read the maker's own data for a real fan.

System effect: the duct can rob the fan

Fans are rated with smooth, straight airflow in and out. The DOE sourcebook defines system effect as the change in system performance from the interaction of components, caused by swirls and non-uniform airflow, and says it moves the system curve up. It cites an elbow placed close to a fan outlet cutting delivered flow by as much as 30 percent. Carrier notes that housed fans need straight duct after the outlet, and that a coil within about 24 inches upstream of a plenum fan sees uneven velocity. Twin City Fan's plenum guide tells designers to add estimated system effect (it names AMCA 201 as the method) before selecting a fan, to keep at least half an impeller diameter between the impeller and an outlet wall, and to center the inlet. Loren Cook's troubleshooting list puts poor inlet conditions among the causes of low capacity and advises a straight, clear duct at the inlet.

Identifying wheel type and rotation in the field

Wheel type. Count blades and read their lean. Per Loren Cook: 24 to 64 shallow curved blades is FC; 10 to 12 deeper blades is BI or airfoil; 6 to 10 flat blades is radial. Without a housing, in a plenum, you are looking at a plug or plenum fan.

Rotation. Loren Cook says to face the fan from the drive side and call the rotation clockwise or counterclockwise; for single-inlet fans the drive side is opposite the inlet. Twin City Fan's FanPedia uses the same drive-side view for centrifugal fans. A Twin City inline-fan manual says to apply power and check impeller rotation, that the correct direction is marked inside the motor housing, and that on a three-phase motor you can correct it by swapping any two power leads. Do that only with power off and locked out. After a rotation fix, see the backward phase rotation lesson for the same idea on compressors.

Why a backward fan still moves some air

Twin City Fan's inline manual warns that performance is heavily degraded in the wrong direction, and that tripped overloads or a burned motor are signs of a fan left running that way. Loren Cook lists wrong rotation under low capacity or pressure, and also under overheated motor. None of the sources we read gives a percentage. Our own reasoning: the wheel is still spinning inside a housing, so it still throws air outward by centrifugal action, but the blades meet the air at the wrong angle and do the job poorly. That is why a reversed fan can seem to work and still fail the airflow check. Always verify the arrow, not just the feel of air at the grille.

Worked example: FC wheel versus airfoil wheel

All inputs are made up for practice. A fan curve we invented says the system operates at 10,000 cfm and 2.0 in. w.g. total pressure. We use DOE's efficiency equation, in which total efficiency equals total pressure times airflow divided by bhp times 6,362; rearranged, air horsepower is cfm times total pressure divided by 6,362. (Twin City Fan's resource guide uses 6,343.3 with a compressibility factor; the difference is about a tenth of a percent.)

  1. Air horsepower (our own): 10,000 × 2.0 ÷ 6,362 = 3.14 hp.
  2. Efficiencies (assumed, from the ranges above): FC 60 percent, from DOE's 55 to 65 percent. Airfoil 85 percent, the low edge of DOE's above 85 percent. Real fans vary, so these are illustrative.
  3. Brake horsepower (our own): FC 3.14 ÷ 0.60 = 5.24 bhp. Airfoil 3.14 ÷ 0.85 = 3.70 bhp. The difference is about 1.54 bhp.
  4. Input kW at an assumed 90 percent motor efficiency (our own): FC 5.24 × 0.746 ÷ 0.90 = 4.34 kW. Airfoil 3.70 × 0.746 ÷ 0.90 = 3.07 kW. Here 0.746 kW per hp is the standard conversion.
  5. Annual energy at 6,000 hours (our own): FC 4.34 × 6,000 = 26,057 kWh. Airfoil 3.07 × 6,000 = 18,394 kWh. The difference is about 7,664 kWh a year.

At an assumed 15 cents per kWh that would be roughly $1,150 a year. The price is made up as well. Belt losses, drive and system effect are ignored, and a real selection also involves noise, cost and space.

Field card: Centrifugal Fan Types and Fan Curves

Count the blades, find the drive side, and never trust air at the grille alone.

  • FC: 24 to 64 shallow blades, low-pressure HVAC, power rises with flow, can overload.
  • BI and airfoil: 10 to 12 blades, non-overloading power curve, airfoil is most efficient and prone to stall.
  • Radial: 6 to 10 flat blades, dirty air, lower efficiency.
  • Plenum or plug fans: unhoused wheels in larger air handlers; arrays need even cells and clear inlets.
  • System effect: an elbow close to the outlet can cut flow by up to 30 percent (DOE).
  • Rotation: view from the drive side; three-phase fix is swapping two leads, power off.
  • Wrong rotation moves air but poorly and may overload the motor.
