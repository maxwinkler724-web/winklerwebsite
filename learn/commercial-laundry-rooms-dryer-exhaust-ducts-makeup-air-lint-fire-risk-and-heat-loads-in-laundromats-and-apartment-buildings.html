Commercial Laundry Rooms: Dryer Exhaust, Makeup Air and Lint Fire Risk

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A row of commercial dryers moves thousands of cubic feet of air per minute out of a building. Every cubic foot that leaves has to come back in from somewhere. This lesson covers the exhaust side, the makeup air side, and what goes wrong when either one is short. The home-sized version of this topic is in the residential dryer vent lesson linked below, so we do not repeat it here.

Check the label and the code

Check the label and the adopted code. Every number below comes from the maker manuals and government pages listed under Sources, and the manuals are for the specific models named. Another model, another year or another maker will differ. The installation manual for the exact dryer, and the mechanical code your jurisdiction has adopted, control the job. This page is general education.

Exhaust: each dryer is a blower with a limit

Commercial dryers pull room air through the drum, heat it, and push it out through an exhaust outlet of about 8 inches or larger. The makers print an airflow and a limit on how much back pressure the exhaust duct may add. Here is what the manuals we read say.

  • Dexter, 30 lb, 50 lb and 80 lb gas models (DCBD series, 60 Hz). Airflow of 830 cfm for the 30 lb and 50 lb models and 1,200 cfm for the 80 lb model, each with an 8 inch outlet. The manual sets a maximum static back pressure of 0.3 in. w.c. at the rear exhaust outlet.
  • Alliance Laundry tumble dryers, 120, 170 and 200 series. Maximum airflow of 1,600 cfm (120 series) and 2,450 cfm (170 and 200 series), with 10 inch and 12 inch outlets. The manual gives a maximum static back pressure of 0.8 in. w.c., measured 12 inches from the outlet with the dryer running.

Static pressure is the push-back the duct puts on the blower. A long run, tight elbows, a lint-clogged hood or a crowded collector all raise it. The Dexter T-30X2 manual says restricted airflow causes slow drying, excess heat and lint buildup. In plain terms, the dryer burns gas or draws power for longer cycles and runs hotter while it dries less. A CPSC lab report on electric home dryers saw the same direction: with the exhaust 75 percent blocked, the heater box reached about 150 degrees C, against just over 100 degrees C unblocked.

Duct length rules are tied to the model, not to one table. The Dexter DCBD manual says to use no more than 20 ft of straight 8 inch pipe with two right-angle elbows, subtract 2 ft for each elbow beyond two, and use no more than four elbows. The same manual also states 14 ft with two elbows for individual cylinders, so the text conflicts; use the figure for your model and ask the maker. The Alliance tumble dryer manual allows 14 ft of rigid duct plus two 90 degree elbows.

Individual or common exhaust: the maker decides

Some makers require a separate duct to the outside for every dryer. The Dexter T-30X2 manual says plainly that individual exhausting of the dryers is required. Others allow a common (manifold) collector if you follow their sizing and layout. The Dexter DCBD and Alliance manuals both allow it, with these conditions:

  • Each dryer's duct enters the collector at a 45 degree angle in the direction of airflow, never at a right angle.
  • The collector gets bigger as dryers are added. The Alliance manual publishes the diameter at each station of the collector, and the Dexter manual says to keep the main duct diameter through to the exhaust end.
  • Static pressure is checked with all the dryers on that collector running. The Alliance manual says so directly.
  • The collector needs cleanout access. The Alliance manual calls for one about every 6 ft on a manifold.

This is why manifolding is limited. Dryers share one outlet only as far as the maker's engineered layout goes. Add a dryer, swap in a bigger model, or use a different collector size, and the pressure at each dryer changes. The mechanical code agrees with the maker-first approach. The 2019 California Mechanical Code, as posted by the City of Carpinteria, says commercial dryer ducts must follow the dryer's listing and the maker's instructions. It also says dryer ducts must terminate outside with a backdraft damper and no screen, and that exhaust systems must be independent of other exhaust systems. The International Mechanical Code (IMC) has its own clothes dryer exhaust section; we read the California text, not the IMC itself, so check the edition your jurisdiction adopted.

Makeup air: what the room needs back

The maker manuals ask for a net free area opening to the outside for every dryer, sized to the airflow. The Dexter DCBD manual gives 1 sq ft for the 30 lb model, 1.25 sq ft for the 50 lb and 1.5 sq ft for the 80 lb. The Dexter T-30X2 manual asks for 1.5 sq ft per dryer. The Alliance tumble dryer manual asks for 360 sq in. per dryer (120 series) and 525 sq in. per dryer (170 and 200 series). These manuals say the opening is a free-area figure, so a screen or louver takes away part of it and the opening must be made bigger; ask the louver supplier for the free area. The Alliance manual adds that when makeup air is ducted, the duct area is increased by 25 percent.

The same Alliance manual says the openings must be enlarged to prevent downdrafts in gravity-vented appliances when all the dryers are running.

The California Mechanical Code handout gives a code check for the larger "Type 2" dryers: free area of at least 1 sq in. for each 1,000 Btu/h of total dryer input rating. The handout we read did not define Type 2, so confirm which of your dryers fall under it. Where a maker number and a code number differ, the larger one is the safer starting point.

When the room goes negative

If the openings are too small, blocked by snow or lint, or closed against the weather, the dryers still try to move their air. In our experience of how air behaves, they pull it from wherever it can leak in: doors, stairwells and any open flue. Two things follow.

  • Slow drying. Less air through the drum means longer cycles and a hotter machine. This is the same restricted-airflow symptom the Dexter manual describes for the exhaust side.
  • Backdrafting. A gas water heater or boiler in or near the laundry room can have its flue gases pulled back into the room instead of up the vent. That is why the Alliance manual ties the opening size to gravity-vented appliances. See the water heater backdrafting lesson for how to check the draft hood.

The combustion air lesson covers the openings an appliance needs; a laundry room with gas dryers and a gas water heater has to satisfy both. A direct makeup air unit is one way to supply the dryers; the makeup air unit lesson covers how those are built and controlled.

Lint: the fire side

The U.S. Fire Administration's report on clothes dryer fires in residential buildings (2008 to 2010 data) counts about 2,900 such fires a year, with about 5 deaths, 100 injuries and $35 million in property loss. It says failure to clean was the leading contributing factor, at 34 percent of fires, and dust, fiber and lint was the first item ignited in 28 percent. Its building mix includes multifamily buildings (17.4 percent) and hotels and motels (3.0 percent), which is the apartment-house side of this topic, but the report excludes commercial buildings, so it does not give a laundromat figure. We did not find a government page with a laundromat number, so we do not offer one.

The makers set the cleaning routine. The Dexter T-30X2 manual calls for cleaning the lint screen daily, checking motor ends and the control area monthly, cleaning burner air ports quarterly and checking the exhaust system annually. The Alliance tumble dryer manual says to clean the lint filter daily and examine and clean the exhaust duct annually after installation. It also wants duct air moving at least 1,200 ft/min while running; where that cannot be reached, it asks for monthly inspection and cleaning of the ductwork.

Booster fans

A booster fan in a dryer duct is a maker decision, not a field fix. Alliance Laundry's installation instructions for its 220 cfm dryers say its booster fan warning does not apply to a multiple-dryer system with an engineered exhaust duct installed to the maker's guidelines. Treat that as a pointer to the engineered layout, not permission to add a fan to an existing run. The California Mechanical Code handout covers power ventilators only for a single residential dryer (listed to UL 705). For commercial dryers, ask the dryer maker first.

Heat and the room load

A dryer rejects most of its input heat through the exhaust, but the cabinet and the warm room air it handles also warm the space. None of the manuals we read give a total Btu/h released to the room. The Alliance install sheet gives a surface figure of 60 Btu/ft² with no time unit in the text we read, so we do not turn it into a room load. The Dexter manuals list gas input only. Ask the maker for a heat release figure before sizing cooling for a laundry room.

What you can calculate is the makeup air itself. In winter, every cfm entering must be heated, and in summer it brings outdoor heat and humidity with it.

Worked example: six dryers

Assumptions (ours). Six Dexter 30 lb gas dryers, model DCBD30HC-10 at 60 Hz, in one room. We treat the maker's published airflow as the exhaust of each dryer and the maker's 1 sq ft as the minimum net free makeup opening for each. Louver free area is assumed at 50 percent of the gross opening (Our own assumption; ask the louver supplier). Winter design temperatures are assumed at 70 F room and 30 F outdoors, a 40 F difference (Our own assumption).

  1. Total exhaust. 6 × 830 cfm = 4,980 cfm (Dexter).
  2. Makeup air free area, maker basis. 6 × 1 sq ft = 6 sq ft of net free area, which is 6 × 144 = 864 sq in. (Dexter).
  3. Gross louver area at 50 percent free. 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 sq ft of gross louver face (Our own, from the assumption).
  4. Face velocity check. 4,980 cfm ÷ 6 sq ft = 830 ft/min through the free area. If we wanted only 400 ft/min (Our own assumption, a gentler target), the free area would be 4,980 ÷ 400 = 12.45 sq ft, or about 1,793 sq in. That is twice the maker's minimum, so the maker figure is a floor, and a quieter, less drafty room takes more.
  5. Code cross-check. Our own assumption: 90,000 Btu/h gas input per dryer (read the actual rating plate), so 6 × 90,000 = 540,000 Btu/h. At 1 sq in. per 1,000 Btu/h (California Mechanical Code, Type 2 dryers), the code check is 540 sq in. The maker's 864 sq in. is larger, so it governs.
  6. Winter makeup air heating (Our own arithmetic). Using the common sensible-heat rule of thumb of 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference: 1.08 × 4,980 × 40 = about 215,000 Btu/h. That is the cost of heating air the building just threw outside.

Room heat load is not computed here. Neither maker gave a total Btu/h released to the room, and gas input is not the same thing.

Field card: Commercial laundry rooms

Exhaust out, makeup air in, lint out of both.

  • Find the maker's airflow, static pressure limit and makeup air free area on the data plate or manual.
  • Examples we read: 830 cfm and 0.3 in. w.c. (Dexter 30 lb); 1,600 cfm and 0.8 in. w.c. (Alliance 120 series).
  • Individual or common duct is a maker decision. Dexter T-30X2 requires individual exhausting.
  • Common duct: 45 degree entries, no right-angle tees, cleanouts, checked with all dryers running.
  • Makeup opening is net free area. Louvers and screens take part of it.
  • Starved room: slow drying, hot machines, backdrafting of gas water heaters and boilers.
  • Clean the lint screen daily and check the exhaust duct at least annually.
  • No booster fan without the dryer maker's say-so.
