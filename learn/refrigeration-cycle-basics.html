Refrigeration Cycle Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every air conditioner, heat pump, refrigerator and chiller moves heat with the same loop. Learn the four stops on that loop and most of HVAC starts to make sense.

The big idea

Refrigeration does not make cold. It moves heat from a place you want cooler to a place where it can be thrown away. A refrigerant is the carrier: it picks up heat by boiling at a low temperature and drops it off by condensing at a higher one. The system is a sealed loop, so the same refrigerant goes around and around.

Stop 1: the compressor

The compressor takes in low-pressure refrigerant vapor and squeezes it. Squeezing raises both pressure and temperature, so the vapor leaving is hot and at high pressure. This is typically the part that uses the most energy, and it is what keeps the refrigerant moving around the loop. It is built to pump vapor, which is why liquid reaching it is a problem.

Stop 2: the condenser

The hot, high-pressure vapor flows into the condenser, which is a coil with air or water passing over it. The refrigerant gives up heat to that air or water, cools down, and changes from a vapor into a liquid. Many systems cool the liquid a little further below the point where it condensed. That extra cooling is called subcooling, and it helps make sure only liquid heads for the next stop.

Stop 3: the metering device

The liquid next passes through a metering device, such as a fixed orifice, a capillary tube or an expansion valve. It is a controlled restriction. Pressure drops sharply on the far side, and some of the liquid flashes into vapor, which chills the mixture to well below the temperature of the space being cooled.

Stop 4: the evaporator

The cold, low-pressure refrigerant now flows through the evaporator coil while warm room air (or water) passes over it. The refrigerant absorbs that heat and boils into a vapor. That absorbed heat is the cooling effect you feel. The vapor then returns to the compressor and the loop repeats. Usually the vapor is warmed a little beyond the boiling point before it leaves, which is called superheat, and it is another way of making sure the compressor sees vapor.

Why pressure and temperature go together

Inside a sealed system with both liquid and vapor present, a given refrigerant boils or condenses at a temperature set by its pressure. Higher pressure means a higher boiling and condensing temperature. This is why technicians read pressures to learn temperatures, and why the two sides of the system are called the low side and the high side. The dividing lines are the compressor and the metering device. See Pressure-Temperature Basics and Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Where a heat pump differs

A heat pump uses the same four parts plus a reversing valve that swaps which coil acts as the evaporator and which as the condenser. The cycle itself does not change. Only the direction of the heat movement does. More in Heat Pump Basics.

Symptoms that point back to the cycle

If a coil is not doing its job, the loop shows it somewhere. Poor airflow over the evaporator, a dirty condenser, too little or too much refrigerant, or a failing compressor will each shift pressures and temperatures in a recognizable way. Learning the cycle first means you can reason about the symptom instead of guessing.

Safety note. Refrigerant is under pressure and can cause frostbite, and handling it is regulated. Recovery, charging and certification rules apply where you work. See Refrigerant Safety.

Field card: Refrigeration Cycle Basics

Four stops, one loop.

  • Compressor: raises vapor pressure and temperature, moves the refrigerant.
  • Condenser: sheds heat, vapor becomes liquid.
  • Metering device: drops pressure, chills the refrigerant.
  • Evaporator: absorbs heat, liquid boils to vapor.
  • Pressure sets boiling and condensing temperature in a sealed system.
