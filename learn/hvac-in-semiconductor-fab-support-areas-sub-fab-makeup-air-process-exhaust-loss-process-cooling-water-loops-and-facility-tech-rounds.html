HVAC in Semiconductor Fab Support Areas: Makeup Air, Exhaust and Cooling Loops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most of what people picture in an electronics plant is the cleanroom. The facility tech spends the day in the rooms around it: the sub-fab below, the gray space and the mechanical rooms. This lesson covers the HVAC and process systems there: makeup air that holds temperature and humidity, large exhaust systems, process cooling water loops, and the heat that pumps and chillers add.

Check your site rules first

Check your site rules. This page is general education built from federal and national laboratory documents read in 2026. Every plant has its own permits, interlocks, lockout rules and emergency plan. Where this page and your site procedure differ, follow the site procedure and ask a supervisor. Cleanroom air classes, filters and pressure cascades are covered in Cleanroom HVAC: ISO Classes, HEPA Filters, Air Changes and Pressure Cascades, so we do not repeat them here.

Where the sub-fab and gray space fit

A federal environmental assessment of fab expansions describes the sub-fab as the basement area below the cleanroom where the electrical and mechanical systems that support the cleanroom are located. The same document says the cleanroom needs a consistent relative humidity in a band of 35 to 65 percent and that cleanrooms use energy for recirculation airflow and for temperature and humidity control. The gray space is the supporting area outside the cleanroom proper, such as corridors, chases and equipment rooms. Plants vary in how they use these terms, so learn the local names on your first walk.

HVAC is a big share of the energy bill. A national laboratory benchmarking paper found measured HVAC energy of 36 to 67 percent of total facility energy across the cleanrooms it studied. That is one reason these systems get close attention: they are both critical and expensive to run.

Makeup air units

A national laboratory paper on cleanroom environmental systems explains that makeup air supplies outdoor air to make up exhaust (general exhaust, heat exhaust, process exhaust and so on) and exfiltration, and to keep the cleanroom pressurized. A second paper adds that exhaust and makeup air needs are primarily governed by building and fire codes. In plain terms: whatever the plant throws out through exhaust has to be replaced by treated outdoor air, plus a little extra so the fab stays positive to its surroundings.

Treating that air is work. Outdoor air has to be filtered, heated or cooled, and humidified or dried to near room conditions before it joins the recirculating air. The benchmarked makeup air handlers in the Class 5 cleanrooms moved about 540 to 1,800 cfm per kW of fan power, and the authors found makeup systems generally much less efficient than recirculation systems, mainly because of greater pressure losses along the makeup air pathways. For a facility tech that means filters, coils, humidifiers and dampers in these units are where pressure drop and control problems show up first.

The exact temperature and humidity tolerances at the unit discharge are set by the plant. We have no sourced figures for them, so check the setpoints and alarm limits in your site documents rather than assuming a number.

Process exhaust and why losing it is an emergency

Fab tools use acids, solvents and reactive gases. The federal worker safety page on device fabrication notes that wet etching uses acid baths, that hazards include acid mixtures such as hydrofluoric, hydrochloric and sulfuric acid, and that solvents are used for cleaning, stripping and rinsing. It describes wet etch baths with a ring-type plenum exhaust or a slotted exhaust at the rear of the station. A national laboratory roadmap says wet benches and gas cabinets continuously exhaust large volumes of conditioned air.

Plants usually split this air into separate exhaust streams, commonly called acid, solvent and general exhaust, so that incompatible vapors are not mixed and each stream goes to the right treatment. The exact split is a site design choice.

The federal worker safety pages list ventilation to reduce gas concentration as a control and recommend gas monitoring with alarms and automatic shut-offs where appropriate. They also tell employers to evaluate exposure during startup, operations, maintenance, cleaning and emergencies. From those points we draw our own conclusion: exhaust is a life-safety control. When a fan trips, a damper sticks closed or a drive faults, vapors that were being captured at the tool can enter the work space. A loss of exhaust can also upset the pressure balance, because makeup air keeps arriving while the air that was leaving does not. Treat every exhaust alarm as urgent, report it through the site emergency channel, and do not silence or bypass an exhaust interlock to keep production running. Restart steps belong to the site procedure.

Process cooling water loops

Tools in the fab reject heat to a closed water loop, usually called process cooling water. The federal fab assessment says cooling water makes up about 23 percent of fab water demand, and abatement systems that treat hazardous gases from equipment account for about 20 percent of water demand. A national laboratory paper recommends separate high-temperature chillers for process cooling, since process loads do not need water as cold as comfort or dehumidification coils do.

The loop supply temperature is held tightly because the tools it serves depend on it. We have no sourced tolerance figure, so use the limits in your site documents. Two field habits follow from tight control: watch trends instead of single readings, and never change a loop setpoint or valve position without the owner's approval. For the pumping side of a chilled loop, see Chilled Water Pumps and Delta T.

Heat in the sub-fab

Pumps, chillers and abatement equipment all turn power into heat in the rooms where they sit. A national laboratory paper reports measured process load intensities of 4 to 26 W/ft² in six Class 5 cleanrooms and 36 to 49 W/ft² in two Class 4 rooms, against design loads of 75 to 125 W/ft², and concludes that process heat loads tend to be over-estimated in practice. The same paper says some process heat goes straight into the chilled water system and the rest is removed by air. A sub-fab with failed ventilation or a blocked heat exchanger will run warm, and motors and drives lose life when they run hot. The federal assessment also notes that fluorinated heat transfer fluids used for temperature control can escape to the atmosphere, and it lists recovery of those fluids at chiller service or retirement as a mitigation, so ask before opening any chiller circuit. Air pressure in these spaces matters as well; see Building Pressurization and Stack Effect in Tall Buildings for the general idea.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption, not data from a real plant.

Part 1: makeup air. Assume total exhaust is 120,000 cfm (acid, solvent and general exhaust added together) and the pressurization margin is 6,000 cfm of extra supply to cover leakage out of the fab.

  1. Makeup air = exhaust + margin = 120,000 + 6,000 = 126,000 cfm.
  2. Assume six equal makeup units share the load: 126,000 / 6 = 21,000 cfm each.
  3. Check: 6 x 21,000 = 126,000 cfm, which matches step 1.

If one acid exhaust fan carrying 30,000 cfm trips while makeup air stays at 126,000, the fab suddenly has about 30,000 cfm more coming in than going out. That is the pressure and vapor problem described above, and it is why controls usually tie makeup air to exhaust.

Part 2: heat removed by a cooling loop. Assume the process cooling loop flows 800 gpm and the water warms by 10°F across the tools. The standard water relation is Q (Btu/h) = 500 x gpm x temperature rise, where 500 comes from 8.33 lb/gal x 60 min/h x 1 Btu/lb°F.

  1. Q = 500 x 800 x 10 = 4,000,000 Btu/h.
  2. Tons = 4,000,000 / 12,000 = 333 tons.
  3. Kilowatts = 4,000,000 / 3,412 = about 1,172 kW.

Check with a different route: if the same 4,000,000 Btu/h were carried with only an 8°F rise, flow would be 4,000,000 / (500 x 8) = 1,000 gpm. A smaller rise needs more flow for the same heat, which is why a low delta T wastes pump power.

What a facility tech checks on rounds

The following is our own suggested checklist, built from the systems above. Your site rounds sheet takes priority.

  • Exhaust fans: running, no new vibration or noise, drive and damper positions normal, no active alarms.
  • Exhaust differential pressure and flow readings: note any drift from the usual range.
  • Makeup air units: filter pressure drop, coil and humidifier status, discharge temperature and humidity against the site setpoints.
  • Process cooling water: supply temperature, return temperature, flow and loop pressure; note the temperature rise and compare it to yesterday.
  • Pumps and chillers: seals, leaks, motor temperature, unusual sound, standby units ready.
  • Abatement equipment: alarms and water supply status; report faults to the owner rather than resetting them yourself.
  • Sub-fab room conditions: temperature, standing water, blocked ventilation openings.
  • Compressed air quality at the dryers: see Compressed Air Dryers for Facility Techs.

Field card: HVAC in Semiconductor Fab Support Areas

Exhaust out, makeup air in, cooling water steady.

  • Makeup air = total exhaust + pressurization margin.
  • Loss of exhaust is an emergency: report it at once and never bypass an interlock.
  • Acid, solvent and general exhaust are often kept as separate streams.
  • Process cooling water heat: Btu/h = 500 x gpm x temperature rise.
  • Our example: 800 gpm at 10°F rise = 4,000,000 Btu/h, about 333 tons.
  • Sub-fab pumps, chillers and abatement add heat to their rooms.
  • Use the site setpoints and alarm limits, not numbers from a lesson.
