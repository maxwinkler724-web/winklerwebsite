Cheese Aging Caves and Cure Rooms: High Humidity at Cool Temperatures

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cheese cave or cure room wants cool air that is also very wet, and an ordinary refrigeration coil is built to do the opposite. This lesson explains why the coil dries the room, how a small temperature difference helps, and what airflow, humidity and cleaning look like in a small aging space.

Check your own rules

Check your own rules. Food rules for aging cheese and curing meat come from your state or local food regulator and the product process, not from the HVAC side. Ask the regulator before you rely on any setpoint here.

What these rooms ask the equipment to do

The Center for Dairy Research at the University of Wisconsin-Madison (Dairy Pipeline, Summer 2022) says soft-type cheeses cure at about 55 to 60 F and hard types at about 40 to 55 F. It lists humidity by rind: 85 percent or higher for yeasty rinds, 90 to 95 percent for molded rinds, and 98 percent or higher for aggressive smear-ripened rinds. So a room at 50 to 55 F may be asked to hold 85 to 95 percent RH day and night.

Meat curing is different. The extension pages we read describe country-ham aging, which is warm and drier: Virginia Cooperative Extension lists 75 to 95 F and 55 to 65 percent RH, Mississippi State Extension lists 65 to 86 F and 60 to 80 percent, and the University of Kentucky says professional ham houses hold below 60 percent RH to control mold. Those are not cave conditions, and we found no public extension setpoint for a cool charcuterie room, so let your product process set the range.

A SARE grant report on cheese caves found underground caves swinging up to 15 F with the seasons, and many owners added conventional refrigeration. That is where HVAC work starts.

Why a normal coil dries the room

Air that touches a surface colder than its dew point drops water onto it. A coil running far below the room temperature is a very cold surface, so it keeps condensing water out of the room and the drain carries that water away. Trade writer Joe Marchese (ACHR News, 2012) puts numbers on the idea: a walk-in with a design coil TD of about 10 F, for example 35 F air over a 25 F coil, keeps humidity near 85 percent, while a reach-in with about 20 F TD runs near 65 percent. He adds that above 95 percent, meats get mold and slime.

The USDA Agriculture Handbook 66 chapter on storage facilities says the same thing in design terms: high RH comes from running the coil close to the room setpoint, with a large coil surface and refrigerant held at its highest possible temperature. For the basics of the equipment, see walk-in cooler basics and produce cold storage.

Worked example: coil temperature sets the humidity floor

These are our own assumptions, not data from a real cave: room at 10 C (50 F), sea-level pressure of 101.325 kPa, and a coil surface at the refrigerant temperature (a simplification; the real surface is a little warmer). Case A has a coil at 4 C, a TD of 6 K (about 11 F). Case B has a coil at 0.01 C, a TD of 10 K (about 18 F). These approximate the 10 F and 20 F figures above, using temperatures that appear in the steam table. Saturation pressures come from the Keenan, Keyes, Hill and Moore steam table (printed in bar; we converted to kPa): 10 C is 1.228 kPa, 4 C is 0.813 kPa, 0.01 C is 0.611 kPa.

  1. Air touching the coil is saturated at coil temperature, so its vapor pressure is the coil value.
  2. Case A: 0.813 / 1.228 = 0.662, about 66 percent RH.
  3. Case B: 0.611 / 1.228 = 0.498, about 50 percent RH.

That is the level the coil alone would drag a sealed, empty room toward. Real rooms sit higher, because only part of the air meets the coil, the surface is warmer than the refrigerant and the cheese gives off water. The direction is what matters: a colder coil pulls the room lower, and the humidifier must make up the difference.

Now the water. Take a target of 90 percent RH (an assumption within the molded-rind range) and 300 cfm over a continuously running coil (also an assumption). The room air's vapor pressure is 0.90 x 1.228 = 1.105 kPa. Humidity ratio uses W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw):

  • Room: W = 0.622 x 1.105 / (101.325 - 1.105) = 0.00686 lb/lb.
  • Case A leaving air: 0.622 x 0.813 / (101.325 - 0.813) = 0.00503. Delta W = 0.00183.
  • Case B leaving air: 0.622 x 0.611 / (101.325 - 0.611) = 0.00377. Delta W = 0.00309.

Using moisture (lb/h) = 4.5 x cfm x delta W: Case A is 4.5 x 300 x 0.00183 = 2.5 lb/h, and Case B is 4.5 x 300 x 0.00309 = 4.2 lb/h. With 4,840 x cfm x delta W, the latent loads are about 2,650 and 4,480 Btu/h. The larger TD pulls roughly 1.7 times as much water out of the room, and the humidifier must put it all back. So a smaller TD dries less. For the chart concepts see psychrometrics for technicians.

Choosing the coil

  • Ask for a low design TD. The handbook points to a large coil surface; a bigger coil lets the refrigerant run warmer for the same capacity.
  • Check frost. A coil near 0 C is borderline for ice. In Case A the coil is above freezing, so it should not frost in normal running; in Case B it can. See walk-in defrost, fan delay and icing. We did not find a public source for how to program defrost in a cave, so follow the equipment maker.
  • Know what people build. The CDR article describes one producer holding a cave at 53 to 54 F with a modified air conditioner and its fan off. We cite it as an example, not advice.

Gentle airflow across the shelves

The CDR article says typical velocities run from 0 to 1.3 ft/s even in low-velocity static systems, and that each cheese should get the same velocity, which is why rack layout, textile ducts and rotating wheels matter. The USDA handbook adds that slow air reduces moisture loss from product, and that once product reaches setpoint the airflow can drop to about 20 to 40 percent of design. The Virginia ham publication says circulation is needed mostly in the first 7 to 10 days, to dry the surface.

Humidifiers

Handbook 66 notes humidifiers may be needed because paper and wood packaging absorb water. The CDR article calls pouring water on the floor "far from ideal." The SARE report mentions agricultural misters placed around the room edge. For a steam unit, absorption distance and water quality matter, covered in commercial steam humidifiers. Put the RH sensor in the shelf air, not at the humidifier.

Mold, cleaning and separate rooms

Mississippi State Extension says mold grows best on ham with water activity above 0.9, that filtered, well-ventilated air helps control mold and mites, and that rooms should be fully cleaned at the end of each aging period. Its cleaning guidance includes hot water at 131 F or higher with an alkaline cleaner, followed by a sanitizer, and fans to dry the room before humidity is readjusted. For cheese, the CDR article recommends seamless walls and washable plastic panels, and says the air handler must be cleanable too. For HVAC, that means a sloped drain pan and coil and fan housings you can reach.

The CDR article also notes that stages may need different rooms: a room for smearing is often followed by a room that is cooler, drier and has fewer air renewals, and spare rooms let you rotate one out to clean. The AFDO fermented and dried sausage guidance says ready-to-eat items must be kept apart from products that need further processing. Separate rooms with separate coils keep spores and moisture from traveling.

Field card: Aging caves and cure rooms

Warm coil, slow air, wet room, washable surfaces.

  • Cheese: soft about 55 to 60 F, hard about 40 to 55 F; RH 85 to 98 percent by rind (CDR).
  • Smaller coil TD means less drying; about 10 F gives near 85 percent, 20 F near 65 percent (trade press).
  • Our example: 6 K TD removes about 2.5 lb/h at 300 cfm, 10 K TD about 4.2 lb/h.
  • Air: 0 to 1.3 ft/s; equal air for each cheese.
  • Keep coil above 32 F where you can; otherwise plan for frost and drainage.
  • Clean on a schedule; keep products in separate rooms.
