Thermal Imaging Cameras for HVAC: Emissivity and Limits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermal imaging camera is the one tool that lets you see a hot lug or a cold duct from across the room. It is also easy to fool. This lesson covers what the camera actually measures, why bare copper and aluminum give wrong numbers, and what the picture can never show you.

What the camera measures

An infrared camera collects the infrared energy leaving a surface and turns it into a picture. Fluke describes the result as the apparent surface temperature of what you point at. It is not air temperature. If you aim at a wall, you learn about the wall's skin, not the room air or the framing behind it. Testo makes the same point in its pocket guide: the imager reads surface temperature only, and for air you use an air thermometer.

Emissivity: why shiny metal reads wrong

Every surface both gives off radiation and reflects radiation from its surroundings. Emissivity is the share of the first kind, on a scale from 0 to 1. Fluke notes that reflection equals 1 minus emissivity, so a surface that emits poorly mostly shows you what is reflected in it. Fluke calls these misleading reflections "thermal lies" and says you cannot accurately measure the temperature of most bare metals.

Testo's guide lists example values and calls them only guidelines. Polished copper is 0.03, slightly tarnished copper 0.04, oxidized copper 0.76, unoxidized aluminum 0.02, and highly polished aluminum 0.09. Black matte paint is 0.97 and white paint 0.95. Cameras are often set near 0.95 by default, and the Bureau of Reclamation's thermal analysis manual says 0.9 to 0.95 is a reasonable starting guess for qualitative inspections only. That is fine for painted or dirty surfaces and wrong for clean bare metal.

The fix. Fluke describes sticking a small piece of electrician's tape on the metal, setting emissivity to 0.95, reading the taped spot, and claims roughly 2 degrees C or 2 percent of reading when done right. Testo describes a similar method with emission tape: read the taped spot, then adjust emissivity on the bare area until it matches. Follow your site safety rules before putting anything on equipment, and never tape energized parts you are not qualified and permitted to touch. Wait for the tape to match the surface temperature before you read it.

Reflected apparent temperature

The camera also needs the temperature of whatever is reflecting off the target. Testo says this reflected temperature equals the ambient temperature in many jobs. The Reclamation manual says background sources must be estimated and entered, and it advises moving around the target: a real hot spot stays put, while a reflection moves as you move. Fluke adds that the correction works worst when emissivity is below 0.6 or when the reflected temperature is far from the surface temperature.

Resolution and spot size

A camera has a grid of detector pixels, and Fluke says higher resolution gives clearer detail. Testo explains the limit in distance terms: one pixel covers a small area that grows with distance (3.5 mrad is 3.5 mm at 1 m), and the smallest spot you can measure accurately is about three times the smallest spot you can merely see. So a small lug from across a room may show as warm but give a wrong number. Get close enough that the target fills several pixels.

Uses in HVAC work

  • Air and insulation in buildings. The Department of Energy says auditors use infrared cameras to find heat loss and air leakage, to show where insulation is needed and to check that it was installed correctly. Fluke lists air leaks in HVAC systems among what imagers can detect. Pair this with the home energy audit lesson and the duct leakage testing lesson, which gives the numbers a camera cannot.
  • Electrical hot spots. The Reclamation manual treats thermal imaging as a way to compare similar components under the same load. See the worked example below and Electrical Basics for HVAC.
  • Radiant floor tubing. The Infrared Information resource at irinfo.org says start with the slab cool, turn the heat on, and scan after about 30 minutes or more. A leak shows as a blurry hot area spreading from the straight tube pattern, but closely spaced or shallow tubing can look like a leak, so a thermal spot is a suspicion until confirmed.
  • Steam traps. A trap's surface temperature is one of the clues; see Steam Trap Testing for the full method.
  • Refrigeration and installs. FLIR describes camera checks of refrigerated-room insulation and survey scans of new HVAC systems before handoff, and says air circulation problems have been located this way. FLIR's page is a vendor story, so treat it as an example, not a test.

What the camera cannot do

  • It needs a temperature difference. DOE says building scans work best with an indoor-outdoor difference of at least 20 degrees F. Testo recommends at least 10 degrees C for building thermography. With no difference, a missing patch of insulation looks like everything else.
  • It reads surfaces, not insides. DOE warns that heat seen on one part of a wall may come from a different spot inside. Testo says that on glass the imager measures only the glass surface, and glass also reflects, so it is not a window to see through.
  • Conditions matter. DOE says interior scans are generally more accurate than exterior ones because wind hides temperature differences. The Reclamation manual wants electrical gear near full load and at least 45 minutes after a load change.

Worked example: two breaker lugs

You scan a panel and find a lug at 140 F next to an identical lug at 95 F. Ambient is 80 F. All numbers below are Our own arithmetic.

  1. Delta-T between the lugs. Our own: 140 - 95 = 45 F.
  2. Rise above ambient, hot lug. Our own: 140 - 80 = 60 F.
  3. Rise above ambient, normal lug. Our own: 95 - 80 = 15 F.
  4. Compare. Our own: the hot lug's rise is 60 / 15 = 4 times the normal lug's rise.

Is 45 F serious? We did not find a severity table with set thresholds in the sources we read. The Reclamation manual says to apply severity criteria but does not give numbers in the part we read, and it notes that the same rise is more serious at 50 percent load than at 90 percent. So use your company's or the maker's guidance to decide urgency, and record the load when you scan.

Now the emissivity trap. Suppose the lug is bare, shiny copper (Testo lists polished copper at 0.03), but the camera is set to 0.95. The camera assumes the surface gives off nearly all the radiation it sends out, when this surface gives off very little and mostly reflects the room. The reading is then built mostly from the reflected background and a small share of the lug's own heat, and the camera corrects for the wrong mix. We will not invent a corrected number, because the error depends on the background and the real emissivity. What we can say is that both lugs may read wrong by different amounts, so the 45 F gap could be too big, too small or even hide a real problem. Tape each lug, set 0.95, and rescan under the same load.

Scan safety

The Reclamation manual says inspections should be noncontact, and that panels exposing the scanner to hazardous energy should not be opened under that method. If energy is exposed, the work follows arc-flash procedures under NFPA 70E. It also warns against using a metal tape measure near energized equipment. Ask your employer which maintenance and safe-work standards apply to your scans, such as NFPA 70B and 70E. Only qualified people open energized equipment.

Field card: Thermal imaging

The camera reads surface radiation, not air and not the inside of a wall.

  • Bare shiny metal has very low emissivity, so readings are unreliable.
  • Tape the spot, set 0.95, wait, then read.
  • Enter the reflected (background) temperature and move around to spot reflections.
  • Get close: small targets need several pixels.
  • Need a temperature difference: DOE says 20 F for building scans.
  • Glass shows its own surface only.
  • Compare like components under the same load; delta-T is not severity.
  • Stay noncontact; do not open energized panels unless qualified.
