Energy Recovery Wheels in Commercial Units: Effectiveness, Purge and Frost Control

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A rooftop energy recovery unit or a dedicated outdoor air system (DOAS) often has a slowly spinning wheel in the middle of it. This lesson explains what that wheel does, how to do the energy recovery wheel effectiveness calculation from a maker's number, and what the purge section, frost controls and drive parts mean when you service one.

What a wheel does

A wheel is a round, honeycomb-like media that turns between two airstreams: outdoor air coming in and exhaust air going out. Each half of the wheel picks up energy from the warmer or wetter stream and drops it into the other as it rotates. Greenheck's product FAQ and an Airxchange installation manual both describe it this way. Daikin's RoofPak manual says typical wheel speed is about 45 RPM, and the Airxchange manual gives roughly 45 to 50 RPM.

There are two families. A sensible wheel (often aluminum) moves heat only. An enthalpy wheel, also called a total energy wheel, has a desiccant coating, so it moves heat and water vapor. Greenheck describes polymer media with silica gel as the latent part of its wheel. If you read the ERV vs. HRV lesson, this is the same idea in a bigger, rotating form. Neither type is a filter. Greenheck says small particles pass through the wheel.

Effectiveness: what the number means

Effectiveness is the energy actually moved divided by the most that could move. For temperature (sensible effectiveness) the definition is:

Sensible effectiveness = (supply leaving wheel temp − outdoor air temp) ÷ (return air temp − outdoor air temp)

That form holds when the supply and exhaust airflows are equal, which is what the worked example assumes. Total effectiveness uses enthalpy instead of temperature. The performance standard for these devices is AHRI 1060 (Performance Rating of Air-to-Air Exchangers for Energy Recovery Ventilation Equipment). AHRI's public program page says certified ratings cover sensible effectiveness, latent effectiveness, supply and exhaust pressure drop, the Exhaust Air Transfer Ratio (EATR) and the Outdoor Air Correction Factor (OACF), for rotary, plate and heat pipe types rated from 50 to 5,000 scfm. Trane's engineering article defines effectiveness as actual transfer over maximum possible transfer, citing ASHRAE Standard 84. Makers publish different ranges. Greenheck lists about 30 percent total effectiveness for aluminum sensible plates, 50 to 60 percent for hygroscopic cores, and 70 to 80 percent for polymer and silica gel wheels. A Greenheck bulletin shows 75 percent sensible for an aluminum plate and up to 80 percent total for a total energy wheel. SEMCO's brochure says common selections at moderate face velocity give about 75 percent. Higher effectiveness generally costs more pressure drop, per Trane. Always use the AHRI directory or submittal value for the actual model and airflow.

Worked example: a winter morning

This is an invented case. A building has a balanced 2,000 CFM supply and exhaust. Outdoor air is 10°F, return air is 70°F, and the maker's sensible effectiveness is 75 percent, a value inside the ranges above.

  1. Maximum possible temperature change. 70 − 10 = 60°F.
  2. Actual change from the wheel. 0.75 × 60 = 45°F.
  3. Supply air leaving the wheel. 10 + 45 = 55°F.
  4. Recovered sensible heat. 1.08 × 2,000 CFM × 45°F = 97,200 Btu/h.

For comparison, heating 2,000 CFM from 10°F to 70°F with no wheel is 1.08 × 2,000 × 60 = 129,600 Btu/h. The wheel supplies 97,200 of that, so a heating coil only has to add the remaining 32,400 Btu/h (the 15°F from 55 to 70) if the space needs 70°F supply air. Our own note: the 1.08 factor is the common sea-level constant for standard air, so it will be a bit off at altitude or with very humid air. An enthalpy wheel recovers moisture on top of this sensible number, so total energy moved is larger.

Purge sections and cross-leakage

A wheel carries a small pocket of exhaust air in its passages as it turns from the exhaust side to the supply side. A purge sector uses a slice of supply air to flush that pocket back out. Daikin's manual says the purge captures the small amount of supply air held in the wheel, and that the purge angle is adjustable, trading purge against capacity. The Airxchange manual says its factory purge angle is 5 degrees on purge models and tells you to check seal contact and hand-turn the wheel after resetting.

Because a wheel has no airtight seal, it leaks toward the lower-pressure side, as Trane's article points out. AHRI rates this with EATR (how much exhaust ends up in the supply) and OACF (how much extra outdoor air the fan must handle to make up for leakage and purge). Greenheck says typical EATR is 3 to 5 percent, and notes that ASHRAE 62.1 allows up to 5 percent for Class 3 exhaust such as restrooms. For airflow targets, see Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics.

Frost control

Frost forms when the exhaust side of the wheel is cooled to its dew point at or below freezing, as AAON describes it. It blocks airflow and cuts effectiveness. Makers use these approaches:

  • Wheel speed control. Daikin's manual says wheel speed is controlled by exhaust temperature measurement, slowing or stopping the wheel as exhaust air nears saturation. AAON describes a cycle that spins the wheel, then pauses it so frost can clear.
  • Preheat. SEMCO's FV manual describes an electric preheat coil on the outdoor air intake, used to keep exhaust air above the dew point. Its ElitePro brochure lists electric preheat as an option.
  • Stop/jog and shutdown. The same FV manual describes stopping the wheel in moderate weather and jogging it about 30 seconds every 30 minutes, and a thermostat that shuts the whole unit off, which means no ventilation while off.

The bypass approach (damper around the wheel) comes up as an economizer feature in the Minnesota rule below, but I did not find a maker page describing it as frost control, so check your unit's sequence of operation. If a wheel stops or airflow drops in cold weather, look at the frost sequence before you blame the motor. For DOAS and rooftop context see Rooftop Unit Basics.

Belts, motors and cleaning

  • Belt. SEMCO's FV manual suggests checking belt tension at least every six months and after the first 24 hours at full load. Daikin says the same 24-hour recheck. Airxchange says to check that the belt tracks centered and is not twisted or worn.
  • Motor. Daikin says the drive motor should need no maintenance. Airxchange says to confirm the motor runs at or below nameplate full load amps and to check rotation direction. Trane notes these are fractional horsepower motors.
  • Rotation sensor. Some units have one that alarms when the wheel stops. A stopped wheel often means a failed belt or motor, and the unit will not warn you otherwise.
  • Cleaning. Daikin says to blow out with compressed air at no more than 20 psig and wash severely dirty wheels, drying fully before reinstalling. Greenheck says clean every two to three years, or every two to three months where smoke or oil is exhausted. The Airxchange manual lists cleaning intervals by air class, warns against acid cleaners, aromatic solvents and steam on polymer media, and says to remove and install segments in opposing pairs. Follow your unit's manual.

Lock out and tag out before opening the cabinet, as Daikin's manual says, and remember more than one disconnect may feed the unit.

When codes require energy recovery

ASHRAE 90.1 section 6.5.6.1 requires exhaust air energy recovery for many systems, depending on design supply airflow, the outdoor air percentage and climate zone, and the recovery must be at least 50 percent effective. An Oregon Building Codes Division training deck for the 2019 edition says the tables split by whether a system runs under or over 8,000 hours per year, and lists exceptions such as Class 4 exhaust, laboratory systems, and systems running under 20 hours per week at the covered outdoor air percentage. DOE and PNNL (energycodes.gov) material I read confirms that the thresholds have been revised across editions but does not print the cfm tables, so look at the adopted standard or your state's code. Two secondary examples of the table style: a Greenheck bulletin citing 90.1-2022 lists, for most zones, a minimum of 5,500 cfm at 30 to 40 percent outdoor air and 1,000 cfm at 70 to 80 percent, and Minnesota's amended rule lists 2,500 cfm at 30 to under 40 percent for climate zone 7. Your jurisdiction may differ. Also see Demand-Controlled Ventilation, since outdoor air that varies changes what a wheel recovers.

Field card: Energy Recovery Wheels in Commercial Units

Effectiveness is a ratio; the maker data and the AHRI listing give the number.

  • Sensible wheel moves heat; enthalpy wheel moves heat and moisture.
  • Sensible effectiveness = (supply − outdoor) ÷ (return − outdoor), balanced flow.
  • Example: 0.75 × (70 − 10) = 45°F rise, 55°F supply, 97,200 Btu/h at 2,000 CFM.
  • Purge flushes exhaust from the wheel; EATR and OACF measure leakage effects.
  • Frost control: wheel speed, preheat, stop/jog; know which your unit uses.
  • Check belt, rotation and filters; lock out first.
  • 90.1 thresholds depend on airflow, outdoor air percent and climate zone.
