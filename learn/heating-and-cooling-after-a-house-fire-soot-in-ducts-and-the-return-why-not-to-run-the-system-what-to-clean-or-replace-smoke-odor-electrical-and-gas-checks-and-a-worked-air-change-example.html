Your Heating and Cooling After a House Fire

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Even a small kitchen fire can leave a whole house smelling of smoke, and the heating and cooling system is a big reason why. The system moves the air of the entire house through one set of ducts, so smoke and soot that started in one room can end up in every room. This lesson explains what happens, why the system should stay off until it is checked, and what a technician looks at before people move back in.

Check the people who know your house

Check first. This page is general education from government and university sources, not a damage assessment. Your fire department, your local code office and a licensed technician decide what is safe for your house. Follow their word over ours.

Why soot and smoke travel through the ducts

A ducted system is a loop. The blower pulls house air in through the return grilles and return ducts, pushes it through the filter, coil or heat exchanger, and sends it back out through the supply ducts. If smoke is in the air when the blower runs, the return pulls it in. Fine particles that get past the filter coat the blower, the coil and the inside of the ducts, then get pushed back out. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency page on indoor air filtration notes that filters in the system can trap smoke particles, which is also why a used filter holds a lot of what the fire made. The same ducts also connect rooms far from the fire, including closed bedrooms.

For the dust version of this story, including how leaky returns pull in unfiltered air, see Is My HVAC Making My House Dusty?

Do not run the system until it is checked

There are two reasons. The first is air: running the blower in a smoky house recirculates residue and loads the filter fast. The second is equipment. Heat, smoke and firefighting water can damage controls, wiring and gas parts that still look fine. The federal fire agency says the fire department makes sure water, electric and gas service are safe, may have services turned off before they leave, and that you should not turn them back on by yourself. It also says not to enter the home until the fire department says it is safe.

If the system ran during the fire, or the thermostat was left calling for heat or cooling, tell the technician. We do not have a source that says how far smoke gets in a given case, so we leave that to the person who opens the ducts and looks.

What may need cleaning or replacing

Two university extension pages both say to have heating and cooling units and all ductwork professionally cleaned to remove soot, ash and smoke residue. Beyond that general advice, here is how we would walk the path of the air, using the federal duct-cleaning page for the insulation points:

  • Filters. The two university pages say to change filters when you first return and at least monthly for the first year. Use a filter the system can handle without choking its airflow; the federal environmental agency suggests asking a technician or the equipment maker which high-efficiency filter your system can use. See Air Handler Filter Racks and Filter Bypass Gaps for a snug fit.
  • Return ducts and grilles. These see the dirtiest air first. A technician checks them and the return plenum for soot.
  • Blower and coil. Soot on a blower wheel or an indoor coil is cleaned by a technician, not with a household hose. The federal environmental agency says steam and other moisture-based methods should not be used on duct work.
  • Metal ducts. Cleaning is the usual approach, done by a professional.
  • Flex duct and fiberglass-lined or fiberglass duct board. Porous insulation is the hard case. The federal environmental agency says insulation that gets wet or moldy cannot be effectively cleaned and should be replaced, and that damaged insulation or insulation with an unacceptable odor should be replaced by a qualified contractor. If firefighting water got into the ducts, treat this as a real possibility.
  • Combustion parts and the vent. Soot and heat can affect a furnace or boiler. Have the heat exchanger, burners and vent checked together. Cleaning Soot and Scale from Boiler and Furnace Heat Exchangers explains the shop side.

Smoke odor that comes back

A university extension page says the smell comes from tiny particles that cling to walls, furniture and floors, and that if you did not reach the tiny spaces, the smell may linger. A university extension page discourages odor-masking sprays, saying they cover the problem and do not last long term. Our own reasoning, not a finding from a source: if the smell returns on some days and not others, treat that as a sign that residue is still somewhere in the house or system, and tell the technician when it happens. Do not assume it is gone because the first week smelled fine. Wet insulation, a different problem, can also smell, and the federal environmental agency says moisture should not be present in ducts.

Electrical and gas checks on exposed equipment

For equipment that got hot or wet, a university extension flood page is a useful guide, even though it is about floods. It says to check whether water got into gas controls and the pressure regulator, to clean and recondition affected equipment including burner elements, electric controls and regulators, and to make sure the fan motor, ignition systems and wiring are completely clean and dry before power is turned on. It notes that many local codes require a licensed electrician to do that work, and that any heating system exposed to flooding should be professionally inspected, cleaned and reconditioned before reuse. A state environmental agency adds that appliances can be affected by smoke, heat or water and that the owner's manual is the place to start. A fire hose can do what a flood does to a furnace closet or an outdoor condenser, so use the same caution. Our companion page Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded: What to Do Before Turning It On covers that in full, and we do not repeat it here.

Document the damage

Before anything is cleaned or thrown out, take photos or video of the equipment, the ducts you can see, the filter and the nameplate, and keep every receipt. The federal fire agency says to save receipts for money you spend. We stop there on money matters; see HVAC Damage from Storms, Floods, Freezes and Power Surges: Documenting It for the photo and report habits, and talk to your own insurer and adviser about anything beyond that.

Worked example (our own): how often the return sees the house air

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any real house.

  • Assumed house air volume: 2,000 square feet times 8 feet of ceiling height is 16,000 cubic feet.
  • Assumed blower airflow: 1,200 cubic feet per minute (cfm).
  • Assumed run time: 1 hour, which is 60 minutes.

Air moved in the hour: 1,200 cfm times 60 minutes = 72,000 cubic feet.

Passes through the return: 72,000 divided by 16,000 = 4.5 house volumes in one hour.

Check: 4.5 times 16,000 = 72,000, which matches. In practice air does not mix perfectly, so this is a rough picture, not a count of how many times every particle passes the filter. The point is that a single hour of blower run moves the house air through the return several times, and every pass can pick up soot if the air has any.

Filter changes: assume the filter is changed on the day people return (day 0) and then monthly, as the university pages advise. Assume a 30-day month. In the first 8 weeks, which is 56 days, the changes fall on day 0 and day 30, so 2 filters. If the next monthly change on day 60 is added, the count is 3 by day 60. If a technician says to change sooner because the filter looks dark or smells, follow that. The federal environmental agency says to change a filter when it gets dirty or starts to smell.

Get it checked before people move back in

A sensible order is: the fire department clears the house, utilities are restored by the people who control them, a licensed technician inspects the heating and cooling equipment and the ducts, cleaning or replacement is done, a new filter goes in, and only then does the system run. Ask the technician in plain words what was checked: ducts, return, blower, coil, gas parts, wiring and the vent. Then ask what, if anything, cannot be cleaned and needs replacing.

Field card: Heating and Cooling After a House Fire

Leave it off, get it checked, then clean or replace.

  • The return and ducts carry smoke and soot to every room.
  • Do not turn the system or utilities back on until the fire department and a technician say so.
  • Ducts, blower and coil are cleaned by a professional.
  • Wet or smoky porous insulation and flex duct may need replacing, not cleaning.
  • Gas controls, wiring and burners exposed to heat or water need a licensed check.
  • Change filters when you return and at least monthly in the first year.
  • Photograph and keep receipts before cleaning.
