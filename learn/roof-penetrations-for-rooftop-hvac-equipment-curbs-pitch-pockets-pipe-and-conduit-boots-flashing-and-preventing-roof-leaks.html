Roof Penetrations for Rooftop HVAC Equipment: Curbs, Pitch Pockets, Boots and Flashing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every pipe, conduit and gas line that reaches a rooftop unit has to cross the roof membrane somewhere. Each crossing is a place water can get in. This lesson shows the common ways equipment gets through a low-slope commercial roof, which details are roofer work, and what a tech should and should not touch.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Roof details depend on the roof maker, the roofer, the local code and the warranty. This page is general education built from public documents read in 2026.

Curbs, rails and pipe supports

A curb is a raised frame fastened to the structure. The roofing membrane runs up its sides as base flashing, and the equipment sits on top. The Unified Facilities Criteria roofing guide says curbs on low-slope roofs should be at least 8 in. above the roof surface. It also asks for crickets, small sloped saddles, on the upslope side of penetrations wider than 24 in. so water drains around instead of ponding.

An equipment rail is a lighter support for small units, and a pipe support holds lines above the membrane on feet or sleepers. These are not penetrations, but they sit on the roof, and the roofer must protect the membrane under each foot.

Where the lines go through

  • Through the curb. On a utility curb, power, control and gas lines run down inside the curb, which is already flashed, so no extra membrane holes are cut.
  • Prefabricated pipe boots. For round pipes the UFC guide describes pre-made EPDM boots with a compression ring and stainless draw band on metal roofs. A boot is a flexible collar that seals around the pipe and is flashed into the membrane. The roofer chooses a type that matches the membrane.
  • Penetration housings. A metal or molded box flashed to the roof that holds several small lines through one opening, sealed inside. This groups many holes into one detail the roofer can inspect.
  • Pitch pockets. An open-bottom metal collar around a pipe or support, filled with pourable sealer. The UFC guide says to minimize pitch pans or pourable sealer pans. Our own reasoning for why: the sealer shrinks and cracks with sun and temperature swings and pulls away from the pipe, so a pitch pocket needs regular looking at and topping off, which makes it a maintenance item and not a permanent seal.
  • Gas and refrigerant lines. Gas piping runs on supports above the roof and enters the unit or curb, ideally without a new hole. Refrigerant or chilled-water lines on split or built-up systems usually go through a housing or boot. Sleeve and clearance rules come from the gas code and local authority; confirm locally.

Water, drains and condensate

A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building-science page says low-slope roofs should have a minimum slope of one-quarter inch per foot toward drains, scuppers or edges. Water that has nowhere to go sits at the curb walls and finds any weakness. Ask the roofer where condensate should discharge, and keep drains and scuppers clear when you service the unit.

Walking pads and service traffic

Foot traffic, dropped tools and sharp screws damage membrane. Roofers install walkway pads along the regular path from the roof hatch to each unit. We could not confirm a published size or spacing rule for walking pads in the sources we read, so ask the roofer where the pad route should run. Keep to it.

The International Mechanical Code summary from ICC says roofs under 3:12 are considered flat enough for safe walking and working, and it requires a receptacle outlet at or near equipment. It also says guards are needed where serviced components are within 10 ft of an edge with a drop of more than 30 in., and the guard must be 42 in. high and extend 30 in. past each end of the component. OSHA's general walking-working surface rule requires protection from falls at 4 ft or more above a lower level. See Ladder and Roof Fall Safety.

What a tech should and should not do

  • Do not cut, patch, caulk or reseal membrane, flashing, boots or pitch pockets yourself. A wrong patch can void a roof warranty and hide the leak path.
  • Do not drill new holes in a curb or roof. Call the roofer first.
  • Do note and photograph what you see: cracked pitch-pocket sealer, loose counterflashing, a torn boot, a bubbled membrane, standing water at a curb, a missing walk pad.
  • Do report it to the building owner or facility manager in writing and recommend a roofer visit. Many roof warranties limit who may repair the roof. The UFC guide calls for a roof information card kept at the roof access point.
  • Do tell the roofer before and after any job that moves a curb or lines. The PNNL page says to inspect every penetration and add missing flashing during reroofing or renovation, so a rooftop replacement is a good time to bring the roofer in.

Inspection after storms

After heavy wind, hail or a long rain, a quick walk along the pad route helps. Look at counterflashing edges, boot collars, pitch-pocket fill, loose unit panels and roof-edge debris. Check for pooled water that is still there a day or two later. See Seismic and Wind Restraints for HVAC Equipment for how wind affects equipment anchorage.

Worked example: grouping lines when a rooftop unit is replaced

These are assumptions, not data from a real roof. Our own assumptions: three rooftop units on one roof. Each unit has one gas line, one power conduit and one control conduit, all currently going through separate roof boots or pitch pockets next to its curb.

  1. Existing penetrations: 3 units x 3 lines = 9 (Our own count).
  2. Replacement plan, assumed: new curbs are utility curbs, so the three lines per unit run through the curb opening that is already flashed. New roof holes per unit: 0. Alternative if curbs cannot be changed: one penetration housing per unit holds all three lines, giving 3 penetrations total.
  3. Penetrations removed: 9 - 0 = 9 with utility curbs, or 9 - 3 = 6 with housings (Our own arithmetic).

Flashing height check, with assumed inputs. A curb is 48 in. wide, so a cricket is needed (the UFC guide calls for one on the upslope side of penetrations wider than 24 in.). Assume the cricket peak will be 3 in. above the roof surface (Our own assumption). The UFC guide asks for curbs at least 8 in. above the roof surface and at least 6 in. above the cricket high point.

  1. Rule 1: 8 in. above the roof surface.
  2. Rule 2: 3 in. + 6 in. = 9 in. above the roof surface.
  3. The larger governs: 9 in. minimum curb height at that side (Our own arithmetic using the UFC rules).

The roofer and roof maker decide the final details. For how mini-splits handle the same idea at a wall, see Mini-Split Wall Penetrations, and for the equipment itself Rooftop Unit Basics.

Field card: Roof penetrations for rooftop HVAC equipment

Every hole is a leak path. Group them, flash them, and let the roofer touch the membrane.

  • Low-slope curbs: at least 8 in. above the roof surface and 6 in. above any cricket high point (UFC roofing guide).
  • Cricket on the upslope side of penetrations wider than 24 in.
  • Pitch pockets are a maintenance item; the UFC guide says to minimize them.
  • Do not cut, patch or caulk membrane. Photograph, report, call the roofer.
  • Stay on the walk-pad route; protect the membrane from tools and fluids.
  • Guards when serviced equipment is within 10 ft of an edge with a drop over 30 in. (IMC summary).
  • After storms: check counterflashing, boots, pocket fill, ponding.
