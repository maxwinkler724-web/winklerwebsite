Natural Gas and Propane Leak Detectors at Home: Odorant Fade, Explosive Gas Alarm Setpoints, Mounting High vs Low, and Why They Do Not Replace CO Alarms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A carbon monoxide alarm and a gas leak alarm sound alike, but they watch for different things at very different strengths. This lesson explains why some homes add a gas alarm, why the rotten-egg smell is not a perfect warning, where the alarm goes, and why a gas alarm never takes the place of a CO alarm.

Check your own rules first

The alarm maker's instructions, your gas utility and your local code come first. This page is general education built from government and university pages we read in 2026. If you smell gas right now, leave and call from outside; the steps are in Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater. For CO alarms, see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds. We do not repeat those lessons here.

Two different jobs

A CO alarm warns about carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas made by incomplete burning. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says CO comes from burning fuels including natural gas and propane. A gas leak alarm, sometimes called an explosive gas alarm, watches for unburned fuel in the air. Unburned fuel is mostly a fire and explosion risk. A furnace can leak CO with no raw gas anywhere, and a cracked fitting can leak raw gas with no CO at all. Neither alarm sees the other problem.

Why the smell is not enough

Natural gas and pure propane have no smell. PHMSA says gas distribution systems are odorized with mercaptan or a similar chemical, and OSHA lists propane as odorless when pure. So the smell is added on purpose. It can still fail you in several ways.

  • New pipe. A New Jersey gas utility and a California gas utility both say odor fade happens mostly in new pipe, most of all in long runs of large new steel pipe. The odorant sticks to the pipe surface (adsorption), is soaked up by liquids (absorption) or is chemically changed by rust and air (oxidation). The same pages mention low or no gas flow for a long time as another cause.
  • Soil on underground leaks. When gas leaks from a buried pipe, the soil can strip out the odor before the gas reaches you. One utility page says clay holds odor better than sandy soil, and soils high in iron weaken it. Gas can enter a basement without much smell.
  • People. The utility pages say some people cannot detect the odorant because of a diminished sense of smell or because they have been around the odor so long that they stop noticing it, and other odors can mask it. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders reports measured smell dysfunction in about 4 percent of adults aged 40 to 49, 25 percent at 70 to 79 and 39 percent at 80 and older. A gas alarm does not depend on anyone's nose.

The utility pages are blunt: do not rely on smell alone. A gas alarm is a second line of defense for that gap. It does not replace the odorant, regular appliance service or the steps in the lesson linked above.

What an explosive gas alarm measures

Every fuel gas burns only inside a range of concentrations. The lower limit is the lowest mix that can ignite, the lower explosive limit (LEL). The federal chemical database CAMEO lists methane, the main part of natural gas, at an LEL of 5 percent by volume in air and an upper limit of 15 percent. OSHA lists propane at 2.1 percent and butane at 1.9 percent, with propane's upper limit at 9.5 percent.

An explosive gas alarm is designed to sound well before that lower limit, at a stated fraction of the LEL. The point is to warn while the room is still too lean to ignite. We found no government page that gives the exact setpoint for home alarms, so the number in our example below is an assumption. The setpoint for a given model is in its manual, and the gas alarm safety standard sets the test requirements for listed units.

Our own worked example: how different the scales are

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a maker specification.

  1. Methane LEL in ppm. The LEL is 5 percent by volume (CAMEO). One percent is 10,000 parts per million (ppm), because 1 / 100 = 0.01 and 0.01 x 1,000,000 = 10,000. So 5 x 10,000 = 50,000 ppm.
  2. Assumed alarm setpoint. Assume the alarm sounds at 10 percent of the LEL (our assumption). Then 0.10 x 50,000 = 5,000 ppm of methane, which is 0.5 percent by volume.
  3. CO comparison. A university safety page lists the response windows required of home CO alarms under the CO alarm safety standard: 70 ppm within 60 to 189 minutes, 150 ppm within 10 to 50 minutes and 400 ppm within 4 to 15 minutes. We treat 70, 150 and 400 ppm as our assumed CO alarm levels for this comparison.
  4. Ratio. 5,000 / 70 = about 71. 5,000 / 150 = about 33. 5,000 / 400 = 12.5. Even at its early warning point, the gas alarm is working at roughly 12 to 71 times the concentration at which a CO alarm is expected to react.
  5. Propane, same method. 2.1 x 10,000 = 21,000 ppm LEL. At the same assumed 10 percent, 0.10 x 21,000 = 2,100 ppm.

The takeaway: a gas alarm that waits for thousands of ppm will not notice a CO level of 70 ppm, which is already a concern. A CO sensor tuned to tens of ppm is not designed to watch for unburned gas. They are different instruments. Ppm here means parts per million by volume, and real alarms and rounding vary.

Mounting height: high or low

Gas rises or settles according to its weight compared with air. CAMEO lists methane's vapor density at 0.55 relative to air, lighter than air, so it rises. OSHA lists propane and butane mixes at 1.45 to 2.00, heavier than air, so they settle to low spots. A county health information sheet for food trucks puts it the same way: sensors for lighter-than-air gas go on or near the ceiling, and for heavier-than-air gas about 6 inches from the floor. The same sheet says a propane leak will settle to the lowest level possible.

So a natural gas alarm usually mounts high and a propane alarm low. The exact distance from ceiling or floor, and how far from the appliance, depends on the alarm. Follow the maker's directions. Some units plug into an outlet, so the maker's height rule decides which outlet you can use. Natural gas can also pool in a high spot such as a vaulted corner; a high placement on an inside wall away from drafts and windows is typical, but the manual decides.

Combination CO plus gas units

Some products put a CO sensor and a combustible gas sensor in one housing. That can save a wall outlet, but the two jobs still conflict on placement. CPSC says CO alarms go on every level and outside sleeping areas, and not in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances. A gas alarm goes where its maker says, near the appliance and high or low. A combination unit has to satisfy both, so read its manual before you pick the spot. If the maker's rules cannot both be met in one place, use two separate alarms.

Sensor life and testing

Sensors wear out. A county sheet says most RV propane detectors last five to seven years from the date of manufacture and recommends replacing them on a strict schedule. CPSC says CO alarms have a recommended replacement age in the product literature, and newer ones signal end of life. The CPSC page also says a CO alarm's test button checks the circuitry, not the accuracy of the sensor. We would read a gas alarm's test button the same way: it shows the horn and power work, but not that the sensor still responds. Check the date on the label, replace by the maker's schedule and follow the manual for any gas test. Do not spray cleaners, paint or aerosol near an alarm.

Many places now ask for them. One large city's building department requires natural gas alarms in dwelling units, installed near gas appliances, and lets owners use hard-wired, plug-in or battery units. Your area may differ, so ask your building department.

When a gas alarm sounds

  • Leave. Get everyone out right away, and take pets if it is quick. Do not search for the source.
  • No sparks inside. PHMSA says do not use a telephone or cell phone, switches, flames or start a vehicle near the leak. Do not flip lights or unplug anything on the way out.
  • Call from outside. From a safe distance, call 911 and your gas utility's emergency number. For propane, call the supplier's emergency number.
  • Stay out until the utility or fire department says it is safe. If you smell gas and the alarm is silent, treat it as real anyway.

A gas alarm never replaces a CO alarm

CPSC calls a CO alarm no substitute for proper appliance use and upkeep, and the same goes the other way around. A gas alarm is silent while a furnace with a cracked heat exchanger or blocked vent puts out CO, because there is no raw gas to see. Keep CO alarms on every level and outside bedrooms. See propane tanks and the gas meter and regulator for where fuel enters the home.

Field card: Gas Leak Detectors at Home

Nose, gas alarm and CO alarm each cover a different gap.

  • Gas has no smell until odorant is added. Odor can fade in new pipe or soil, and people can lose smell.
  • Gas alarms warn at a fraction of the LEL. Methane LEL is 5 percent, or 50,000 ppm.
  • CO alarms work at tens to hundreds of ppm. Different scale, different sensor.
  • Natural gas is lighter than air: high. Propane is heavier: low. Follow the maker's directions.
  • Replace by the date on the label. A test button does not prove the sensor works.
  • Alarm sounds: leave, no switches or phones inside, call from outside.
  • A gas alarm never replaces a CO alarm.
