Blast Chillers and Blast Freezers: Pull-Down Loads, Airflow and Defrost

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A blast chiller or blast freezer is a refrigeration system built for one job: pulling heat out of a batch of food fast. It is not a bigger reach-in. This lesson covers why the clock exists, how much heat a batch carries, what the air must do, and why the cabinet is a poor place to park product. For the Food Code basics and soft serve machines, see our earlier blast chiller lesson.

Why the time rules exist

FDA's employee fact sheet on the Food Code says cooked time/temperature control for safety food must go from 135 F to 70 F within 2 hours, then from 70 F to 41 F or less over the next 4 hours. The sheet lists shallow pans, thinner portions, rapid cooling equipment and ice baths as methods. A South Carolina agriculture department handout adds that food is checked with a calibrated probe at intervals, and discarded if it misses the 70 F or 41 F checkpoint.

Meat and poultry plants under USDA FSIS follow a different set of numbers. FSIS stabilization guidance lists a universal option of 130 F to 80 F in 1.5 hours, then 80 F to 40 F in 5 hours, and says cooling should be continuous and as fast as possible through the upper range, where spore-formers grow. These are rules for the food, not equipment ratings. Ask the local health department which rules and Food Code edition apply.

The three slices of pull-down load

Cooling food is a heat-removal problem with up to three parts. A university chapter on freezing from Texas A&M lays them out: remove sensible heat down to the freezing point, remove latent heat while the water turns to ice, then remove sensible heat again down to the storage temperature.

  • Sensible above freezing. Pounds times specific heat times temperature drop. The chapter's table shows specific heat above freezing from 3.75 to 4.34 kJ/kg·C for the foods it lists, close to water at 4.186. In our units that is roughly 0.90 to 1.04 Btu/lb·F.
  • Latent at the freezing point. Pure water gives up about 334 kJ/kg. Foods freeze a little below 32 F (the table runs from -0.6 C to -2.8 C), and latent heat scales with water content, from 220 kJ/kg for chicken to 306 for strawberries.
  • Sensible below freezing. Specific heat drops once the water is ice. The same table gives 1.84 to 3.62 kJ/kg·C below freezing, and ice itself is 2.108.

A chiller that stops above freezing only deals with the first slice. A blast freezer deals with all three, and the middle one is usually the biggest.

Worked example 1: chilling a batch (our own)

Assumptions, all Our own: 100 lb of cooked chili in 2-inch pans, specific heat 0.90 Btu/lb·F (the low end of the range converted from the table above), start at 135 F, finish at 38 F, and a chiller cycle of 90 minutes. We ignore pans, racks, fan heat and leakage, so this is a floor.

  1. Drop: 135 - 38 = 97 F.
  2. Heat to remove: 100 lb × 0.90 × 97 F = 8,730 Btu.
  3. Over a 90-minute (1.5 h) cycle: 8,730 ÷ 1.5 = 5,820 Btu/h.
  4. In tons: 5,820 ÷ 12,000 = about 0.49 ton.

Compare the Food Code pace. Stage 1 is 100 × 0.90 × 65 F = 5,850 Btu in 2 h, about 2,925 Btu/h. Stage 2 is 100 × 0.90 × 32 F = 2,880 Btu in 4 h, about 720 Btu/h. Stage 1 is where the machine works hardest.

Condenser heat. The condenser also rejects the compressor's work. Assuming (our own) an average coefficient of performance of 2.0, compressor input is 5,820 ÷ 2.0 = 2,910 Btu/h, so the condenser rejects 5,820 + 2,910 = 8,730 Btu/h, 1.5 times the food load. A self-contained cabinet puts that heat into the kitchen, so a greasy coil and tight clearances show up as slow cycles. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.

Worked example 2: freezing a batch (our own)

Assumptions, Our own except where a source is named: 50 lb of raw chicken pieces from 40 F to 0 F; initial freezing point -2.78 C = 27.0 F, latent heat 220 kJ/kg, and specific heat 4.34 kJ/kg·C above and 3.62 below freezing, all from the Texas A&M table. Unit conversions: 1 kJ/kg·C = 0.2388 Btu/lb·F and 1 kJ/kg = 0.4299 Btu/lb. That gives 1.037 Btu/lb·F above, 0.865 below and 94.6 Btu/lb latent. We treat all latent heat as removed at 27.0 F and ignore the bound water the chapter says stays unfrozen.

  1. 40 F to 27.0 F: 13.0 F × 1.037 = 13.5 Btu/lb.
  2. Freezing: 94.6 Btu/lb.
  3. 27.0 F to 0 F: 27.0 F × 0.865 = 23.3 Btu/lb.
  4. Total: 131.4 Btu/lb. Times 50 lb = about 6,570 Btu, of which 4,729 Btu (72%) is latent.
  5. Over a 4-hour cycle (assumed): 6,570 ÷ 4 = about 1,640 Btu/h, 0.14 ton.

The average is small, but it must be delivered with very cold cabinet air, with the compressor at low suction pressure. A review from the University of Auckland puts blast freezer air at -35 C to -45 C, with evaporating temperatures near -42 C to -52 C.

Airflow is the product

Heat moves from the food's center to its surface by conduction, then off the surface by convection. A fan only helps the second step. The Auckland review says batch tunnel freezers run 1.5 to 6 m/s, with about 4 m/s generally accepted as the best, and that going above 5 m/s barely raised the freezing rate because the inside of the product becomes the limit. An Oregon State University study of an industrial blast freezer says designers typically aim for about 1,000 fpm across the product, and that faster air adds fan heat with little gain. Cold air is denser: fan power ran almost 25% over rating at -30 F in that study.

In a kitchen cabinet this means the pans must leave the air a path. Loading mistakes these sources describe:

  • Stacked or tightly packed product. The Auckland review says most blast freezer operating problems come from poorly placed pallets or carts, and that stacking has to let air move over the whole product. It cites spacers up to 70 mm for cartons.
  • Lids and wrap. That review lists packaging as up to 59% of heat-transfer resistance for frozen fish and 38% for beef, and a sealed lid is a small version of the same thing. Mississippi State Extension says to cover food loosely while it cools; NC State says to cover once food reaches 70 F.
  • Deep pans. NC State recommends no more than 2 inches for thick foods like chili and stew and 3 inches for thinner soups, in pre-chilled pans.
  • Overloading. The review also says overloading lengthens freezing.
  • Probe in the wrong place. It should sit in the thickest part of the food.

Frost and defrost

Every pound of moisture that leaves the food or the room air ends up as frost on the coil, and frost cuts airflow and capacity. The Auckland review says blast freezer evaporators should be sized with a frost allowance and fin spacing of no more than 4 fins per inch, and that defrosting shortly after loading lets the coil run lightly frosted most of the time. An Oregon State study ran hot-gas defrosts of 30 to 40 minutes with fans off. Our own point: a defrost that lands in the middle of a pull-down stalls the cooling and can make a cycle miss its Food Code window. For defrost basics in storage boxes, see Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Defrost, and for plant-scale freezer rooms, Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings.

Why not use one as a storage box

This part is our own engineering judgment, not a source quote. Once food is at holding temperature, the load is a small fraction of the pull-down load, and the cabinet still pays for features it no longer needs: high-velocity fans, very cold coils and a defrost schedule built for heavy loading. It also cannot take the next batch, so the kitchen ends up cooling hot food in a walk-in again. Move product to a storage box once it is at target. The holding side is covered in Commercial Refrigeration Cold Holding at 41 F. Hot food in a walk-in is no substitute: NC State says never to use a refrigerator or freezer to cool large quantities of hot food, and the South Carolina handout warns a big hot batch can warm the food already inside.

Service checks

  • Check the probe in an ice bath against a trusted thermometer.
  • Look at the load: depth, spacing, lids, weight against the maker's rating.
  • Look at the coil for frost and the fan discharge for blockage.
  • Clean the condenser; check clearances and kitchen ambient per the manual.

Field card: Blast Chillers and Blast Freezers

Fast air, spaced pans, probe in the thick part, then move it to storage.

  • Food Code: 135 F to 70 F in 2 h, then to 41 F in 4 more h.
  • Load = lb x specific heat x drop; freezing adds latent heat, often the biggest slice.
  • Typical blast freezer air: about 4 m/s, -35 to -45 C; faster adds fan heat for little gain.
  • Lids, deep pans, stacking and overloading slow the cycle.
  • Frost on the coil cuts airflow; defrost should not land mid-pull-down.
  • Condenser heat is the food load plus compressor work.
  • A blast cabinet is not a storage box.
