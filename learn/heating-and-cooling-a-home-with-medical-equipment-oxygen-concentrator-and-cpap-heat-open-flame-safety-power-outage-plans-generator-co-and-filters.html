Heating and Cooling a Home With Medical Equipment: Oxygen, CPAP and Power Outages

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A bedroom that holds an oxygen concentrator, a CPAP machine and a person is warmer than the same room empty, and it has a few special safety rules. This lesson covers the heat the devices add, fire and carbon monoxide basics, outage planning, filters, and when to call for HVAC service. We are not medical professionals, and nothing here is medical advice.

Ask your care team first

Ask your care team. Room temperature, humidity, how long a device may be off, and what to do in a power outage are medical questions. Your doctor, nurse or equipment supplier can answer them for your situation, and the device manual comes first. We do not give a temperature target here, because the government sources we read do not give one for a home with medical equipment. What we can do is explain the building side: heat, airflow, flames and fumes.

Why small bedrooms get warm

Almost every watt a plug-in device draws turns into heat inside the room. A concentrator runs for hours and has a compressor and a fan, so it adds steady heat. A CPAP machine with a heated humidifier adds some more. Heat sources like these matter most in a small room with a closed door, where the supply air from the system may be small to begin with. If the room is warm at night but the rest of the house is fine, that is a clue. Our lesson on closed bedroom doors explains why shut doors can starve a room of air.

Worked example: the extra airflow (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Do not treat the wattages as facts about your equipment; read the label on yours.

  • Assumptions: a bedroom of about 10 ft by 11 ft; a concentrator drawing 300 W; a CPAP machine with a heated humidifier drawing 50 W; a lamp and chargers drawing 10 W; all of that power ends up as heat in the room; we want supply air that is 20 degrees F cooler than the room.
  • Step 1, total power: 300 + 50 + 10 = 360 W = 0.36 kW.
  • Step 2, convert to Btu/h: one kilowatt-hour equals 3,412 Btu (the federal energy statistics agency lists this conversion), so 0.36 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 1,228 Btu/h, about 1,230.
  • Step 3, airflow: the standard sensible heat relation for air is Q = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference. Solve for cfm: cfm = Q / (1.08 x temperature difference) = 1,228 / (1.08 x 20) = 1,228 / 21.6 = 56.9, about 57 cfm.

So in this example the equipment alone needs about 57 cfm of 20-degree-cooler supply air, on top of what the room needs for the sun, walls, the person and everything else. If the room has one small register that delivers less than that, it will drift warm. If you allowed only a 15 degree difference, the same math gives 1,228 / (1.08 x 15) = 75.8, about 76 cfm. Where the 1.08 comes from is covered in our heat transfer formulas lesson. This number is for understanding the problem; a technician measures real airflow.

Oxygen, open flames and heat

Oxygen does not burn, but it makes other things burn faster and hotter. A federal health library page on using oxygen at home says to keep oxygen far away from open fire, such as a gas stove, or any other heating source, and not to smoke while using oxygen. A federal fire administration safety graphic on oxygen tanks says to keep them at least 5 feet from a heat source and never to use a candle, match, lighter or other open flame near medical oxygen. That 5 foot figure is for tanks; for a concentrator, follow the distances in the maker's manual.

In practice that means the gas stove, a fireplace, a wood stove and a space heater all need real distance from the oxygen and its tubing, and the person should not cook on a gas burner with tubing trailing over it. Candles and lighters are out. For a space heater, a federal consumer product safety agency news release says to keep it at least 3 feet from drapes, furniture and bedding, to plug an electric one directly into a wall outlet, and never to leave it on while sleeping. Our space heater lesson goes into more detail. A federal health agency also warns that heating a home with a gas oven risks carbon monoxide poisoning, so do not use the oven for heat.

Heat, medicines and devices

Heat can affect people and the things they depend on. A federal health agency's guidance for clinicians says medicines and heat can interact, that some common drug types raise heat risk, and that insulin can lose effectiveness in heat. It also says inhalers can burst in extreme heat, so medicines should not be left in a hot car or other very hot place. It says patients should not stop medicines abruptly without a plan. Those are conversations for your care team. For older family members, see our older adults lesson.

Power outages and backup plans

  • A federal health agency's preparedness page says to contact your local fire department and power and water companies if you use electric-powered medical equipment, and to make an emergency power plan for your devices, including breathing machines and oxygen equipment.
  • A federal health agency's blog says the plan should list model and serial numbers, that you should check the manual or maker to see whether a device runs on batteries or a generator, and that you should fully charge battery devices before a storm.
  • The federal health library page on home oxygen says a person using a concentrator must have a backup tank of oxygen in case the power goes out. How much backup you need is a question for your care team and supplier.

Generators and carbon monoxide

The consumer product safety agency news release says to run a generator only outdoors, at least 20 feet from the home with the exhaust pointed away from buildings, never in a house, basement, shed or garage, and never to refuel it while it runs. A federal health agency says the same 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. The consumer product safety agency also says to put carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, use battery backup, and test them monthly. See our outage carbon monoxide lesson. Also check the device manual before connecting a medical device to a generator.

Filters and dust for breathing

A federal environmental agency says furnace or central filters filter air throughout a home, that upgrading the filter can help indoor air quality, that filters cannot remove every pollutant, and that filtration works best as a supplement to source control and ventilation. A federal energy department page on filters says a dirty or clogged filter can dramatically reduce airflow, which also hurts the cooling and heating that the bedroom depends on. In practice, check the filter on a schedule your system's manual gives, make sure it fits the slot, and ask your care team before choosing anything for a breathing condition. Our filter size and fit lesson helps with fit.

When to call for HVAC service

  • The bedroom stays hot or cold even with the door open and a clean filter.
  • Airflow from the register is weak, or the system short cycles.
  • A carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or you smell fuel or see soot. Leave, call emergency services, and do not go back in until cleared.
  • You plan to use a generator, or you want a technician to measure airflow and check whether the room needs more supply air.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Home With Medical Equipment

Ask the care team, keep flames far from oxygen, plan for outages.

  • Device watts x 3.412 = Btu/h of heat added to the room.
  • cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature difference).
  • Oxygen: no open flame, no smoking, and distance from any heat source.
  • Space heaters: 3 feet from combustibles, plugged into the wall, never on while sleeping.
  • Generators: outdoors only, 20 feet from the home.
  • Tell the power company about electric medical equipment and make a backup plan.
  • Dirty filters cut airflow; ask your care team about breathing needs.
