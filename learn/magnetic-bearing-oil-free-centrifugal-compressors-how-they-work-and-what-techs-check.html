Magnetic Bearing Oil-Free Centrifugal Compressors: How They Work and What Techs Check

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a conventional chiller compressor and you find an oil pump, an oil heater and an oil filter. Open a magnetic bearing machine and you find none of that, because the shaft floats on magnetic force and nothing needs lubricating. This lesson explains how that works, what happens when power drops, how the efficiency rating is built, and which service habits change.

We built this page from maker literature and a federal energy program page, listed at the bottom. Compressor lineups and manuals change, so the maker's current manual for your machine always wins over a general lesson.

What "magnetic bearing" means

A bearing holds a spinning shaft in place. Most compressors use oil-lubricated bearings. A magnetic bearing compressor instead holds the shaft by magnetic force. Danfoss describes its Turbocor compressor as having a motor shaft and impeller assembly that is "levitated" by a digitally controlled magnetic bearing system, with one thrust bearing and two radial bearings. It is the main moving part. Danfoss says sensors at the bearings check the shaft position 100,000 times per second and feed that to a bearing controller, which keeps adjusting the magnetic force so the shaft stays centered. In the maker's words, the bearings combine permanent magnets and electromagnets.

The U.S. DOE Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) describes the compressor class the same way: a two-stage centrifugal compressor on digital magnetic bearings, driven by a variable-speed drive. For the basic centrifugal design (impeller, volute), see Chiller Compressors: Centrifugal, Screw and Scroll Compared.

Why no oil system is needed

With no contact between shaft and bearing while running, there is nothing to lubricate. Danfoss says the design removes metal-to-metal contact and wear surfaces, and that it needs no oil management system. FEMP adds a second benefit: since no oil circulates, none ends up in the evaporator or condenser, and FEMP says that helps heat transfer. Daikin's catalog says its design eliminates gears, oil and hot gas bypass and uses an oil-free, refrigerant-cooled compressor. In the field that means no oil pump, oil heater, oil cooler or oil return to troubleshoot.

The variable-speed drive and part load

These compressors are paired with a variable-speed drive. Danfoss says the compressor slows down as condensing temperature or heat load falls, and that the drive converts DC to adjustable three-phase AC based on signals from the motor and bearing controller. It also says startup current is only about 2 amps, against 250 to 350 amps for conventional compressors of similar size. FEMP says the technology suits chillers that run at partial load most of the year and warns that savings shrink at or near full load.

Power loss and the backup bearings

Magnetic bearings need power to hold the shaft. So what happens when power drops? Two ideas from the makers:

  • Backup (touchdown) bearings. Danfoss says radial and axial bearings support the rotor when the compressor is not energized, preventing contact between the rotor and other metal surfaces.
  • The motor as a generator. Daikin's catalog says that during a power loss the spinning motor acts as a generator, "temporarily feeding the bearings and controls," and that its RideThrough feature supports operation for up to 17 seconds. Danfoss says its control scheme allows a normal de-levitation and shutdown after a power failure.

Daikin also says a longer outage can be followed by a quick restart, as little as 20 seconds after power returns with a UPS on the controls. These are maker claims for specific models. How many touchdown events a bearing tolerates, and when to inspect it, we could not confirm from the pages we read, so follow the maker's manual and ask for those limits by model.

IPLV versus full-load kW/ton

Full-load kW/ton tells you how the chiller performs on the hottest, busiest day. Integrated part load value (IPLV) blends four load points to better reflect a whole year. A Trane article describing the AHRI definition lists the four points as 100% load at 85°F entering condenser water, 75% at 75°F, 50% at 65°F and 25% at 65°F, with weights of 1%, 42%, 45% and 12%. The weights approximate the share of operating time at each point. The same authors argue that real plants often spend more time at high load than the 1% weight suggests, so treat IPLV as a rating tool, not a prediction of your building's bill.

Worked example

These are teaching values, not any real machine. A 300-ton chiller has a stated 0.60 kW/ton at full load, and stated values of 0.45 kW/ton at 75%, 0.38 at 50% and 0.36 at 25%. Our own arithmetic follows. Because each value is already kW per ton, the weighted average is a straightforward sum of weight times value.

  1. 100% point: 0.01 × 0.60 = 0.0060
  2. 75% point: 0.42 × 0.45 = 0.1890
  3. 50% point: 0.45 × 0.38 = 0.1710
  4. 25% point: 0.12 × 0.36 = 0.0432
  5. Our own IPLV = 0.0060 + 0.1890 + 0.1710 + 0.0432 = 0.4092, about 0.41 kW/ton.

Now the annual comparison at a stated 2,000 equivalent full-load hours. Our own arithmetic: 300 tons × 2,000 hours = 600,000 ton-hours of cooling.

  • At the full-load figure: 600,000 × 0.60 = 360,000 kWh.
  • At the IPLV figure: 600,000 × 0.4092 = 245,520 kWh.
  • Difference: 360,000 − 245,520 = 114,480 kWh, which is 114,480 ÷ 360,000 = about 32% less.

The gap shows why part-load behavior matters, and why a chiller that runs mostly near full load would see less benefit. Real results depend on condenser water temperature, actual hours at each load, and controls. (Some references write the IPLV formula with efficiency in COP, as one over the sum of weight divided by value; working in kW/ton gives the same result without the reciprocal.)

What techs check that is different

  • No oil work. No oil level, oil sample or oil filter on the compressor, since the makers describe it as oil-free. Other plant checks (condenser cleaning, filters, diagnostics) remain, and Daikin lists those among its planned services.
  • The drive. The drive and its DC capacitors are central parts. Danfoss lists capacitors for smooth DC voltage in the electronic package but gives no service interval on the page we read. Drive cooling varies: Daikin describes its drive as air cooled, which brings filters and airflow into the routine. Capacitors can hold a charge, so follow the maker's lockout and wait-time instructions before opening a drive. We did not confirm specific wait times.
  • Controls and trends. Danfoss mentions self-diagnostics and monitoring through ModBus or a building system. Trend condenser approach and lift as described in Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend.

Common alarms, as makers describe them

The maker pages we read stay general. Daikin lists ground fault protection and says its controller monitors status and provides fault protections. Danfoss mentions self-diagnostics. Neither page we read gave alarm codes. Treat code lists, reset steps and limits as maker-manual content, and read the alarm text and history before any restart. Our startup, shutdown and safeties lesson covers the general habit.

Where it fits

FEMP notes the technology works in both air-cooled and water-cooled chillers and says retrofits need a good existing evaporator and condenser. For the plant around the machine, see Chiller Plant Fundamentals. We do not recommend any brand here; compare makers on their current data.

Field card: Magnetic Bearing Oil-Free Centrifugal Compressors

Shaft floats, no oil, drive matters.

  • Position sensors feed a bearing controller that holds the shaft centered.
  • No oil pump, heater or sample; the maker's drive and controls need attention instead.
  • Touchdown bearings support the rotor when it is not energized.
  • Ride-through and restart times are maker claims by model; read the manual.
  • IPLV weights: 1%, 42%, 45%, 12% at 100, 75, 50, 25% load.
  • Teaching example: IPLV about 0.41 kW/ton; 245,520 vs 360,000 kWh.
  • Alarm codes and capacitor wait times: use the maker's manual.
