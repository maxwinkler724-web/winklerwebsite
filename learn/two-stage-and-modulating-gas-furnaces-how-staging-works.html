Two-Stage and Modulating Gas Furnaces: How Staging Works

Part of the Gas and Oil Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A single-stage furnace has one flame size: all on or all off. Two-stage and modulating furnaces can run smaller. This lesson shows how a two-stage furnace decides between low and high fire, what extra parts it uses, and how to check airflow for each stage.

Three kinds of gas valve

Single-stage burns at one rate, the nameplate input. Two-stage has two firing rates, called low fire and high fire. Modulating varies the rate in small steps. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America guideline on high-efficiency gas furnaces lists two-stage units at roughly 92 to 96 percent AFUE and step-modulating units with variable-speed blowers at roughly 96 to 98 percent. It says a modulating furnace can vary its capacity down to about 30 to 40 percent of maximum input and runs its fan longer each cycle, which gives more even air temperatures. The same guideline adds that these furnaces are less sensitive to undersized ducts than single-stage units. Notice what DOE does not claim: a fixed savings number. Real savings depend on the house, the ducts and the setup, so treat any bare percentage in a sales sheet with caution.

The low-fire size is a fraction of high fire, and it differs by maker. Trane's installation manual for its J952 two-stage condensing furnaces says low fire is 65 percent of high fire. A Goodman specification sheet for its GMVC80 furnaces gives low fire as 70 percent of high fire. Always read the nameplate on the furnace in front of you.

How the furnace picks low or high

It depends on the thermostat.

  • Two-stage thermostat. The thermostat decides. A troubleshooting guide for the 58CVA/58CVX two-stage furnaces says low heat is called by closing R to W1, and high heat by closing R to W1 and W2 together. Terminal letters are covered in Thermostat Terminal Letters: R, C, W, Y, G Explained.
  • Single-stage thermostat. There is only a W wire, so the furnace board has to guess when to step up. Trane's manual says to connect W to W1, which keeps the furnace in low fire, and it describes an optional timed autostage jumper. Goodman's GMVC96 manual describes a DIP switch (S1, positions 3 and 4): position 3 selects a two-stage or single-stage thermostat, and with a single-stage thermostat the furnace runs on low for a fixed period before stepping up. Position 4 picks a fixed 5 minutes or an automatic delay that averages the previous three cycles, between 1 and 12 minutes. The 58CVA/58CVX guide describes an adaptive mode instead: the board looks at the stored length of earlier heating periods to choose a low or high start, and low can run up to 16 minutes before it switches to high.
  • Low-heat-only setting. That same guide lists a switch (SW1-2) that forces low heat on a W1 call, while a W2 call always gives high heat.

Trane also warns not to jump W1 and W2 together while the autostage jumper is on. The lesson: switch labels and meanings differ by maker and model, so find the switch table in the manual for that exact furnace before you change anything.

Two pressure switches

Many two-stage furnaces have two pressure switches, one for each stage, because high fire needs more inducer draft than low fire. The 58CVA/58CVX guide has separate fault codes for them: code 31 for the high-heat pressure switch not closing or reopening, and code 32 for the low-heat one. It lists make and break points in inches of water column for those models, but those numbers belong to that furnace and change with altitude, so use the table in your manual. Trane's manual also says its upflow and horizontal furnaces have two pairs of switches, one pair on the inducer and one on the collector box. The test steps are in Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis. Never bypass a pressure switch to get a stage running.

Blower speed follows the stage

Low fire makes less heat, so the blower should move less air. The 58CVA/58CVX guide shows the blower turning on at low-heat airflow 45 seconds after the gas valve opens, and at high-heat airflow after 25 seconds when the furnace starts in high. Goodman's sheet states the idea as a formula: low heat CFM equals high heat CFM times 0.7. Trane's J952 manual uses DIP switch pins 1 to 4 to set the heating blower speed. Airflow is only right if the rise across the furnace is within the nameplate range. For how ECM motors hold airflow, see ECM Blower Motors, and for static pressure see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. If the rise is too high, High-Limit Trips and Airflow explains the trouble that follows.

Manifold pressure for each stage

A two-stage valve has a pressure setting for each stage, and the maker lists both. Goodman's GMVC96 manual lists natural gas manifold pressure from 0 to 7,000 feet as 3.5 inches water column for high and 1.9 for low. Do not copy those numbers to a different model. They belong to that manual, and other makers list other values. Trane says the manifold pressure must be set to the appropriate value for each installation, by a qualified person, and Goodman warns not to derate a furnace by lowering manifold pressure below the rating plate. Measure each stage at the valve's outlet tap with a manometer and compare with that furnace's table. To confirm the actual input, clock the meter at high fire as shown in Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input. The firing order itself is in Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation.

Worked example: airflow for each stage

The sensible-heat equation for air is Btu/h = 1.08 x CFM x temperature rise. A trade reference article and an HPAC Engineering article (both in Sources) show where 1.08 comes from: air density 0.075 lb per cubic foot, times 0.24 Btu per lb per degree for specific heat, times 60 minutes per hour. It is a rule of thumb for ordinary air near sea level. Rearranged, CFM = Btu/h output / (1.08 x temperature rise).

Use the Goodman GMVC80 0805C spec sheet figures: high fire input 80,000 Btu/h, output 64,000; low fire input 56,000, output 44,800; rating-plate temperature rise 20 to 50 degrees F. Aim for 35 degrees, the middle of the range.

  1. High fire: 1.08 x 35 = 37.8. Then 64,000 / 37.8 = about 1,693 CFM. (The same sheet lists 1,700 CFM at 35 degrees, a good match.)
  2. Low fire at the same rise: 44,800 / 37.8 = about 1,185 CFM.
  3. Check the ratio: 1,185 / 1,693 = 0.70, matching the sheet's "low heat CFM = high heat CFM x 0.7."
  4. Range check at high fire: 64,000 / (1.08 x 20) = about 2,963 CFM at 20 degrees, and 64,000 / (1.08 x 50) = about 1,185 CFM at 50 degrees. At low fire: 44,800 / (1.08 x 20) = about 2,074 CFM and 44,800 / (1.08 x 50) = about 830 CFM.

Real furnaces do not deliver the output exactly, so measure the supply and return air temperatures on each stage and compare with the rating plate. Place the supply thermometer out of direct sight of the heat exchanger, as Trane's manual advises.

Field card: Two-Stage and Modulating Gas Furnaces: How Staging Works

Stage by stage: call, draft, gas, air.

  • Single-stage: one rate. Two-stage: low and high fire. Modulating: many steps.
  • Two-stage thermostat: W1 is low, W1 plus W2 is high. Single-stage thermostat: the board times the step-up.
  • Staging switches differ by maker. Read the table for that model.
  • Many two-stage furnaces have a low and a high pressure switch. Never jump one.
  • Check manifold pressure for both stages against the manual.
  • CFM = output Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature rise). Do it for each stage.
  • Temperature rise must be inside the nameplate range on both stages.
