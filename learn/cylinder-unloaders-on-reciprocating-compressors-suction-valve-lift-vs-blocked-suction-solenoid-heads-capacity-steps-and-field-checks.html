Cylinder Unloaders on Reciprocating Compressors: Types, Steps and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A big semi-hermetic reciprocating compressor does not have to run flat out. Many have cylinder unloaders that stop some cylinders from pumping, so one machine can run at full, about two-thirds or about one-third capacity. This lesson covers how that works, the two main designs, how the heads are controlled, and the field checks that tell you whether an unloader is doing its job.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. Steps, limits and control settings differ by compressor family. The facts below come from compressor-maker technical literature, federal technical manuals and a patent, all cited as plain text in the Sources list. The worked example uses values we assumed, and says so. Use the data sheet for the machine in front of you.

Why unload at all

  • Capacity control. Cooling loads change with weather, product load and defrost. A machine that can drop to part capacity can follow the load instead of being on or off. A compressor maker's capacity-control bulletin also notes that control is needed when condensing pressure falls, since capacity rises at lower condensing pressure.
  • Fewer starts. The same bulletin says capacity control reduces starting frequency. Another maker guide recommends no more than 10 starts per hour and calls the minimum run time needed to return oil after start-up the most critical consideration. A unit that runs longer at part capacity cycles less. See Hot-Gas Bypass and Capacity Control for the other way to shed load.
  • Unloaded start. A maker's unloaded-start bulletin says a direct-on-line start draws a breakaway current that is a multiple of rated motor current, and that unloading during start-up avoids large voltage dips and helps starting at low supply voltage (about 85 percent of nameplate or less). A federal technical manual says the same in simpler words: starting under full load needs more torque and a larger motor.

Two ways to unload a cylinder

Suction-valve lift

A federal technical manual describes unloaders that lift the suction valves off their seats and hold them open, usually for cylinders in pairs. With the valve held open, the piston pushes gas back out into the suction manifold on the compression stroke instead of compressing it. That manual's compressor is unloaded when it is stopped, because a spring holds the lifting pins up; once oil pressure reaches roughly 30 psi, it pushes the unloader piston against the spring and the valves seat. It says the arrangement lets the compressor run at as little as 25 percent of rated capacity. A patent on this design describes the same action: the suction valve is kept from fully closing, so gas that enters the cylinder leaves through the intake port.

Blocked suction (suction cutoff)

The other design closes the suction port to the cylinder bank. A maker bulletin says a control piston closes the suction port of the valve plate. No gas gets in, so the cylinders pump almost nothing. Your maker's sheet will say which type a model uses; the head casting is different.

Who moves the piston: solenoid or pressure

  • Solenoid-actuated. A coil opens or closes a small valve that decides which pressure acts on the unloader piston. In the bulletin we read, a de-energized coil leaves the piston top vented to suction pressure, a spring holds the piston up, and the bank runs loaded. Energizing the coil lets discharge pressure push the piston down and block suction. So on that design, coil on means unloaded. A second maker's sheet agrees: with the solenoid de-energized, the compressor runs loaded. Coils come in 24 V, 120 V and 208-240 V versions, and must match the control circuit voltage. A thermostat, pressure switch or step controller energizes them.
  • Pressure-actuated. The valve is set by suction pressure and driven by discharge pressure, so no electrical control is needed. One maker's sheet says each valve controls 2 cylinders, the load point adjusts from 0 to 85 psig, and the load/unload differential adjusts from 6 to 22 psi. At start-up the controlled cylinders do not load until discharge pressure exceeds suction pressure by 19 psi. That sheet recommends against automatic pumpdown on a compressor with these unloaders.

The maker's application manual says pressure-actuated unloaders have a small internal high-to-low path, so continuous or automatic pump-down can short-cycle them. It also warns that a tight differential lets the pressure rise from unloading reload the bank at once, and recommends at least 6 to 10 psi.

Steps on a 6-cylinder machine

Banks usually hold two cylinders each. One maker's bulletin lists 66 and 33 percent remaining capacity for its 6-cylinder machines, 50 percent for 4-cylinder, and 75 and 50 percent for 8-cylinder. Another maker's application manual shows its 6-cylinder machines running at one-third load with two banks unloaded. Real capacity at a step moves with suction and discharge conditions, so these are nominal. With two unloader banks, we call the steps 100, 67 and 33 percent.

Minimum load: what the sources say and do not say

The capacity-control bulletin says unloaded operation changes the compressor's application range, and lists two cautions: the motor is less cooled because refrigerant flow is reduced, and oil circulation is more critical with capacity control. On a suction-gas-cooled machine, cool gas passing over the windings is the motor's main cooling, and an air-cooled or water-cooled motor needs its own airflow or coil; the maker's application guide says motors must always be cooled and gives a minimum airflow for air-cooled models. Less gas also means lower suction-line velocity, which makes it harder for oil to travel up risers (our reasoning). The bulletin does not give a numeric minimum load. Another maker's manual shows the limits as saturated suction and condensing temperature envelopes for its half-load and one-third-load operation, so we do not teach one number. Use the maker's envelope for the refrigerant, and do not park a machine on its lowest step with a high suction temperature, a long run and no oil return check.

Worked example (ours): amps at each step

Our own illustration, not a measured machine. Assumptions: a 6-cylinder machine with two unloader banks of two cylinders each; full-load running current 42.0 A; a nominal 30-ton full-load capacity at a fixed operating point; and fixed losses (friction, motor losses) of 15 percent of full-load power that do not shrink with cylinders. We also assume current moves with power, which is rough because power factor drops at part load. Check it with a meter.

  1. Capacity: 30 tons × 2/3 = 20 tons at the first step; 30 × 1/3 = 10 tons at the second.
  2. Power fraction = 0.15 + 0.85 × capacity fraction.
  3. First step: 0.15 + 0.85 × 0.667 = 0.717. Current: 42.0 × 0.717 = 30.1 A.
  4. Second step: 0.15 + 0.85 × 0.333 = 0.433. Current: 42.0 × 0.433 = 18.2 A.
  5. So the steps read about 42, 30 and 18 A: current falls to 72 and 43 percent, a smaller drop than the 67 and 33 percent capacity because of the fixed losses.

Cycling check. Take an 18-ton load. Full-load-only: 18 ÷ 30 = 60 percent on-time. On the 20-ton step: 18 ÷ 20 = 90 percent on-time. Fewer, longer runs is the point. Averaged over time (off counts as 0 A): 0.60 × 42.0 = 25.2 A against 0.90 × 30.1 = 27.1 A. Unloading mainly cuts starts and steadies suction pressure.

Field checks

  1. Amp draw at each step. Clamp each leg at full load, then at each step, once the machine settles. Compare with the nameplate and with the shape in the example: current should step down, not stay flat (see Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter). Legs should stay balanced.
  2. Coil checks. With power off and locked out, check the coil for an open or shorted winding with an ohmmeter and compare with a known-good coil of the same part. With power on, confirm the coil voltage matches its rating and that the control energizes it at the expected suction pressure. Confirm which state means unloaded on that design.
  3. Suction pressure response. After the bank unloads, suction pressure should rise and current should drop. If nothing changes, suspect a stuck unloader piston, wrong coil voltage, or a valve-plate gasket that does not fit the unloader; the capacity bulletin notes that some heads ship with an inactive gasket that leaves 100 percent capacity until the active one is fitted, and that a shipping plate left in place can cause erratic control-piston action.
  4. Looks unloaded, still pumps. Pressure rises but amps do not change: a leaking suction reed (see Testing Reciprocating Compressor Valves), or a bank that never unloads.
  5. Rack settings. On a rack, staging and unloader setpoints should overlap in the right order; see Supermarket Refrigeration Racks. The compressor-level background is in Compressor Basics and Failure Modes.

Field card: Cylinder Unloaders

Fewer cylinders pumping, fewer starts, less cooling for the motor.

  • Two designs: lift the suction valve, or block the suction port.
  • Solenoid heads: on one maker's design, coil on means unloaded. Check the data sheet.
  • Pressure heads: suction sets, discharge drives; maker lists a 6 to 22 psi differential range.
  • 6-cylinder steps are about 100, 67 and 33 percent.
  • Example (ours): assumed 42.0 A full load reads about 30 and 18 A on the steps.
  • Unloaded running cools the motor less and makes oil return harder.
  • Check amps, coil resistance and voltage, and the suction pressure response at each step.
