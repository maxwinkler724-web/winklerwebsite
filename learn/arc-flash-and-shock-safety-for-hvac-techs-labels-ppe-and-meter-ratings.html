Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs: Labels, PPE and Meter Ratings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most HVAC electrical injuries start with the same sentence: "It was only a quick check." This lesson separates three hazards that get lumped together (shock, arc flash and arc blast), shows how to read an arc flash label, and explains why your meter's rating matters before it ever touches a wire.

Check your own rules first

Check the rules that apply to you. This is general education built from federal pages and meter-maker notes. Your employer's electrical safety program, the label on the equipment and your training decide what you may do. If you are a student, do not work on energized equipment without an instructor.

Three hazards, three different injuries

Shock is current passing through your body. The NIOSH electrical safety booklet for the trades lists a faint tingle at about 1 mA and a painful "let-go" range, where muscles clamp and you cannot release the conductor, of about 6 to 25 mA for women and 9 to 30 mA for men. It says currents above 75 mA cause ventricular fibrillation, and 50 to 150 mA brings extreme pain and respiratory arrest.

Arc flash is different. No one has to touch anything. A fault makes current jump through the air and release heat and light. The same NIOSH booklet describes arc blasts as powerful, high-amperage currents arcing through air, with temperatures it gives as high as 35,000 degrees F. Arc blast is the pressure wave and flying material that come with it. NIOSH says a person 2 feet from a 25,000-amp arc feels a force of about 480 pounds on the front of the body, and molten copper or aluminum droplets can burn people 10 feet or more away.

The practical difference: shock protection is about not becoming part of the circuit (insulation, dead circuits, insulated tools). Arc flash protection is about distance, how fast the protective device clears the fault, and arc-rated clothing.

What an arc flash label shows

Equipment such as panels, disconnects and control cabinets in commercial and industrial buildings may carry an arc flash label; label makers base them on NFPA 70E (cited here in plain text). A label based on an incident energy study lists, according to one label maker's guide: the nominal system voltage, the arc flash boundary, the available incident energy in cal/cm2 at a stated working distance, the required PPE, and the date of the study. Incident energy is how much heat energy would land on a square centimeter of skin at that distance. Another safety training site defines the boundary as the distance within which a person could get a second-degree burn, where incident energy has dropped to 1.2 cal/cm2, and says 18 inches is a typical working distance for low-voltage equipment.

Two cautions. The label's date matters, because changes to the electrical system can change the numbers. And a label that is missing, faded or older than a system change cannot be trusted. Stop and ask who owns the equipment.

Worked example 1: reading a label (Our own arithmetic)

Suppose a label states 4.2 cal/cm2 at 18 in.

  1. Skin-burn reference: the boundary is where energy falls to 1.2 cal/cm2.
  2. Compare: 4.2 ÷ 1.2 = 3.5. At the stated 18 in, the energy is 3.5 times the second-degree burn onset level.
  3. So the arc flash boundary is farther out than 18 in. The label's boundary distance gives the exact number.
  4. PPE: one training site lists minimum arc ratings of 4 cal/cm2 for category 1 and 8 for category 2, and warns that incident energy and PPE category are two separate methods that should not be translated into each other. So we do not pick a category from 4.2. Use the label's stated PPE, and if it is unclear, ask.

Notice 4.2 sits just above 4. A guess based on a rule of thumb could leave you under-protected. That is why you read the label itself.

Why techs end up working live, and what OSHA says

Many HVAC faults only show up with power on: a contactor that will not pull in, a control board with no 24 V output, a motor drawing wrong amps. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.333(a)(1) says live parts must be deenergized before work on or near them, unless doing so would add hazards or is infeasible because of equipment design or operations. Parts under 50 volts to ground need not be deenergized if there is no increased exposure to electrical burns or arc explosion. Under 1910.333(c), only qualified persons may work on energized equipment, trained in energized-circuit techniques, PPE, insulating materials and insulated tools.

The 50 V line deserves care. It is a regulatory trigger, not a promise of safety. Our own advice: treat any exposed conductor as live until a tested meter shows it dead. Our lockout lesson covers the default: shut it off, lock it, verify dead. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

OSHA 1910.335 adds that workers near exposed energized parts must use insulated tools or handling equipment if they might touch those parts, use insulated fuse-handling equipment when fuse terminals are energized, use eye or face protection where arcs or flying debris are possible, and use shields, barriers or insulating materials for protection from shock, burns or arcing. Damaged insulation is a defect: do not use that screwdriver.

Worked example 2: shock current (Our own arithmetic)

Take 240 V across a stated body path of 1,000 ohms. NIOSH says wet skin may have a resistance of only 1,000 ohms, and dry skin 100,000 ohms or more. We use the wet figure.

  1. Ohm's law: I = V ÷ R.
  2. I = 240 V ÷ 1,000 ohms = 0.24 A.
  3. Convert: 0.24 A × 1,000 = 240 mA.
  4. Compare with NIOSH: let-go range tops out near 30 mA, and 240 ÷ 30 = 8, so about 8 times higher. It is above the 75 mA fibrillation figure: 240 ÷ 75 = 3.2 times.
  5. Dry skin for contrast, 240 ÷ 100,000 = 0.0024 A = 2.4 mA, around the faint-tingle range.

The message is not that wet hands are 100 times worse than anything else. A real body path varies. The lesson is that moisture, a tool in a wet hand, or a bare knuckle on a terminal can move you from a tingle to a life-threatening level. Treat 240 V as dangerous every time. See Electrical Basics for HVAC for Ohm's law.

Meter CAT ratings and test-before-touch

Meters carry a category (CAT) rating tied to where on the electrical system you may use them. Fluke's meter safety guide describes CAT II as receptacle-connected single-phase loads such as appliances and outlets; CAT III as fixed equipment, distribution panels, three-phase motors and short branch circuits fed from panels; and CAT IV as the service origin, meters, primary overcurrent protection and lines from the utility. Higher categories are built for higher available fault energy. The guide says to choose a meter rated for the highest category you might meet, with a voltage rating at or above the situation, and gives a 480 V distribution panel as needing at least CAT III-600 V.

The same guide says to inspect the meter, probes and accessories before each measurement and never use damaged leads, and to confirm the meter on a known source before relying on it. For a full routine, see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings. The sequence: meter on a known source, test the circuit, meter on the known source again.

Where this shows up on HVAC calls

Condenser disconnects and breakers are common test points. Read AC Condenser Disconnects, Fuses and Breakers and AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker for the equipment side. If a breaker keeps tripping, the fault is still there: do not bypass a protective device to keep going.

Field card: Arc Flash and Shock Safety

Shock is current through you. Arc flash is energy through the air.

  • Default: deenergize, lock out, verify dead.
  • Label: voltage, boundary, incident energy at a working distance, required PPE, study date.
  • Read the label's stated PPE. Do not convert cal/cm2 to a category in your head.
  • Shock figures (NIOSH): let-go about 6 to 30 mA, above 75 mA fibrillation.
  • Wet skin may be 1,000 ohms: 240 V gives 240 mA.
  • OSHA: energized work is for qualified persons; insulated tools near exposed parts.
  • Meter: right CAT and voltage, no damaged leads, check on a known source before and after.
