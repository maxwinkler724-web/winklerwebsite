Furnace Flame Rollout Switch Tripped: Causes and What Not to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas furnace that goes dead with a rollout fault is not broken in the usual sense. A small safety switch did its job because flame went somewhere it should not. This lesson explains what the switch is, what makes flame roll out, how a technician checks the circuit, and why the answer is never to bridge the switch.

What a rollout switch is

Inside a gas furnace, burners fire into the heat exchanger, and the inducer fan pulls the flame and exhaust gases through it. A flame rollout switch is a heat-sensitive switch placed near the burner opening. If flame comes back out of the heat exchanger toward the burner area, the switch heats up and opens, the control board sees an open circuit, and gas is shut off. Lennox describes it as a small heat-sensitive device near the burner assembly. Goodman's service instructions for one modulating furnace say the rollout limits are mounted on the burner and manifold assembly, are normally closed, and are manual reset. Their stated job is to guard against burner flames not being properly drawn into the heat exchanger.

Two kinds exist. Lennox says there are manual-reset and auto-reset versions and that most homes have the manual kind, with a small button that must be pressed before the burners can run again. Which one your furnace has, and where it sits, depends on the model, so use the manual for that exact furnace. Some furnaces have more than one; the wiring diagram shows how many and where.

Why flame rolls out

Flame follows the draft. Anything that stops the inducer and chimney or vent from pulling combustion gases through the heat exchanger can push flame back toward the burners. Lennox lists these causes: a blocked or cracked heat exchanger, dirty burners with soot that redirects flames, a blocked flue or vent (bird nests, debris, ice), gas pressure problems or a failing inducer motor, and airflow restrictions such as a clogged filter or failing blower. Each one deserves a short explanation.

  • Blocked heat exchanger or flue. If exhaust cannot leave, hot gas backs up and flame has nowhere to go but out. See Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis for how draft is proven.
  • Cracked heat exchanger. A new opening changes how air and gas move inside. Lennox names it as a cause, and the AHRI inspection guide covered in Cracked Furnace Heat Exchanger starts with watching for flame that rolls or distorts.
  • Inducer and venting trouble. A weak inducer motor or a damaged vent pulls less. Goodman's installation manual for a Category I furnace says the vent must meet its rules, including vertical height at least as great as the horizontal run. See vent categories.
  • Overfiring. Goodman warns that overfiring can cause premature heat exchanger failure and that input must never exceed the rating plate. Too much gas means more flame than the heat exchanger and draft were built to handle. Check it with meter clocking.
  • Combustion air. Goodman's manual says that furnace uses indoor air for combustion and that most homes need outside air supplied to the furnace area. A starved furnace room is one of the things a technician rules out.
  • Airflow. Heat that cannot leave through the house air side overheats the cabinet. Goodman links incorrect temperature rise and poor filters to heat exchanger damage. See High-Limit Trips and Airflow.

What not to do

Do not jumper, tape, or bypass a rollout switch to get heat. Goodman's service instructions carry the warning "DO NOT BYPASS SAFETY DEVICES" on the cover, and Lennox says plainly not to bypass the switch. A bypass does not fix flame in the wrong place. It only removes the one thing that would shut the gas off when it happens again.

Also do not treat the reset button as a repair. Lennox says one trip deserves attention and that multiple trips in a season mean you need a technician, not a reset button. If it trips again right after a reset, the cause is still there. Whether a homeowner should touch the reset at all is up to that furnace's manual. If a tripped switch comes back, switch the furnace off at the thermostat and call a qualified technician. If you smell gas, leave and call the gas utility from outside; see Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater.

Why this is a carbon monoxide issue

A U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission staff review of furnace and boiler carbon monoxide (CO) incidents says leakage into a home typically needs two failures: something that raises CO, and a path into the living space. Heat exchanger problems and operating conditions were each cited in 14 incidents, and vent problems in 7. The review linked the incidents to 13 deaths. CPSC advises a yearly professional inspection of fuel-burning heating systems and CO alarms near sleeping areas. A rollout trip is the same kind of warning, so respect it. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

How a technician checks the circuit

The control board will not start the sequence unless its limit and rollout switches are closed (see Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation). Many boards flash a code when one is open. One example is the White-Rodgers 50A66-743 control, whose diagnostic table lists red on with green slow flash as an open rollout switch and says to verify continuity through the rollout switch circuit. Other makers use other codes; see No-Heat Furnace: From LED Code to Part.

A technician then does two things, in order. First, find out why, before touching the switch: look at the burners and flame, check the vent and draft, confirm the inducer and pressure switch work, check the filter and blower, and measure gas input and manifold pressure against the nameplate. Second, test the circuit. A closed switch reads near zero ohms with power off and the wires removed. An open one reads open. The same circuit can be checked live: a closed switch drops almost no voltage, while an open one shows nearly the full control voltage across it.

Worked example: finding the open switch

This example uses made-up readings to show the method. A furnace shows a rollout fault. The board feeds a series string: high limit, then rollout switch A, then rollout switch B. With power off and one wire pulled from each switch, the technician reads:

  • High limit: 0.3 ohm (closed)
  • Rollout A: 0.2 ohm (closed)
  • Rollout B: OL, meaning open (tripped)

In a series string the open one stops everything. The closed parts add up to 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.5 ohm, which is negligible. Now the live check. Restore power with the call for heat on and put the meter across each switch, in volts AC. Say the transformer supplies 24.6 volts. Across the two closed switches the meter reads about 0.0 volts each, because a closed switch drops almost none. Across Rollout B it reads 24.6 volts, because all the control voltage appears across the one open point (24.6 - 0.0 - 0.0 = 24.6). That points to B. Pressing its reset button and seeing it hold is not the end. B tripped for a reason, and the burner box, vent and draft are the next stop. A jumper across B would make the 24.6 volts disappear and the board would light the burners, which is exactly the danger.

Field card: Furnace Flame Rollout Switch Tripped

A tripped rollout switch is a symptom. Find the cause.

  • Rollout switches sit near the burner opening and open if flame comes back out.
  • Many are manual reset; some are auto reset. Check the manual for that model.
  • Causes: blocked flue or heat exchanger, cracked heat exchanger, weak inducer, venting, overfiring, dirty burners, combustion air, airflow.
  • Never jumper or tape a rollout switch. Follow the model's manual on resets.
  • Repeat trips: shut the furnace off and call a technician.
  • Technician: read the code, find the cause, then check switch continuity and voltage.
  • Closed switch: near 0 ohms, near 0 volts across it. Open switch: OL, full control voltage across it.
