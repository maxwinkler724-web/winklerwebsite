Courthouse HVAC: Courtroom Noise, Jury Rooms, Holding Cells, Screening and After-Hours Zoning

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A courtroom is empty at 7 a.m., packed at 9:30, and silent while a witness speaks into a microphone. Next door a jury must deliberate without being heard in the hall or the courtroom. This lesson covers what that asks of the air system and what a facility tech can check, using one invented courtroom for the numbers.

Check the project documents

Check the project documents. Courthouse criteria change by owner and edition. This page was built from federal and state pages read in 2026. We could read the 2003 edition of the GSA court facilities standards in full on the points below, but we could not read the acoustics and mechanical chapters of the 2021 U.S. Courts Design Guide through our tools, so the numbers here come from the older edition. Confirm against the current documents for your building.

Empty to full, and what 62.1 says

ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (named in plain text only) sets the outdoor air for the breathing zone from people and from floor area. In the 2022 edition's Table 6-1, as shown in an ASHRAE addendum extract, the Courtrooms row lists 5 cfm per person, 0.06 cfm per square foot, and a default density of 70 people per 1,000 square feet. The people term is why the requirement swings: a courtroom at trial may need several times the outdoor air it needs at night. That is what demand control is for; see Demand-Controlled Ventilation. The state of Utah's judicial facility standards also call for CO2 sensors in multi-occupant spaces.

The older GSA court standards add comfort targets. For courtrooms they list 74°F and 50 percent relative humidity in summer and 74°F with 20 to 35 percent in winter, and they let the building automation system reset courtroom thermostats to 70°F before scheduled occupancy. That is pre-cooling for a room that fills in minutes.

Quiet air for speech and recording

The GSA standards give a background noise target of NC 25 to 30 for court facilities, and say courtrooms use constant-volume supply air. Utah's standards say courtroom background noise should be below NC-30 and that HVAC must not interfere with recordings. Constant volume matters to a tech: a VAV box that swings its damper can add noise that changes during testimony. For what NC means and how silencers work, read HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings, and for slow, wide outlets see Theater and Auditorium HVAC.

The GSA standards also list several design moves. Air handlers and VAV boxes sit at least 25 feet from courtrooms, jury rooms and chambers. They aim for no more than two courtrooms per air handler. Each courtroom's return is ducted directly back to the unit, so one courtroom does not hear another through the return. Chamber returns run at least 15 feet toward the return shaft. The U.S. Courts Best Practices Guide adds that courtrooms should not sit over or next to mechanical rooms, because fixing that later is costly, and that courtroom doors and glazing should be at least STC 50.

Jury rooms and sound through ducts

A deliberation room fails if you can hear it from outside, and a duct is an easy way out. The GSA standards line ducts serving trial and grand jury suites with 2 inches of absorption for at least 12 feet from the diffuser or return intake. The Best Practices Guide says to keep terminal units out of the space directly above deliberation rooms, put them in the corridor or vestibule, pick the lowest practical sound power, limit wall penetrations, and seal service penetrations airtight. It also says to avoid deliberation rooms adjacent to each other and to buffer them with chases, sound locks or storage.

For a tech, the failure is usually an open penetration: a ceiling cut for a new sensor, a duct that passes the wall without a sealed collar, or a lined section replaced with bare duct. Look at the sketch below, then read the example.

Chambers, zones and after-hours trials

The 2021 Design Guide says to plan isolated mechanical zones for the present and future off-hours needs of courtrooms and chambers. The GSA standards are more specific: the courtroom, judge's chamber suite and jury deliberation room form a court set that must be able to run after hours, on separate zones and thermostats, with chilled water piping that can switch to a secondary source and submetering so after-hours cost can be assigned. A separate thermostat for each trial jury room is described as desirable. Winter humidification is called for in designated special areas.

In practice, a night trial should not mean running the whole courthouse. Check that the court set can be scheduled on its own, that its air handler can run without the clerk's wing, and that the schedule overrides expire. This part is our own practical advice.

Holding cells

The GSA standards require detention-type grilles with tamper-proof fasteners in prisoner-accessible areas and refer to the U.S. Marshals Service requirements for detention. We do not repeat the details here. Read Correctional Facility HVAC. The same standards say critical areas such as the Marshals control room need separate, redundant HVAC on emergency power. Escort rules decide when you may enter, so plan access with court security first.

Entry screening queues

We found no cited HVAC numbers for screening lobbies. The Design Guide points to a GSA and Marshals lobby security notebook, and Utah says screening should be efficient with no extended delays. From that, our own field notes: a queue at the entry means a crowd in a small lobby and doors opening all morning. Check lobby temperature at the peak hour, door closers and vestibule doors, and whether the lobby is conditioned from a unit that also serves a quiet room. Do not move a sensor or damper in a screening area without the security lead.

Worked example: a 2,400 sq ft courtroom

Our own example. Assumptions, not measurements: 2,400 sq ft of floor, 120 people at the peak, a 16 foot ceiling, zone air distribution effectiveness of 1.0, a 20°F supply temperature difference, and 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent per person. The 250 and 200 match the "moderately active, office work" row of ASHRAE Handbook Fundamentals people-heat table (cited there as chapter 30, Table 1 of an older edition), as shown on a software maker's help page; the same table lists 225 sensible for a seated person in a theater, so 250 is slightly conservative for a seated room. The default density (70 per 1,000) would give 168 people, so 120 is below the default.

Step 1, outdoor air. Vbz = Rp × Pz + Ra × Az = 5 × 120 + 0.06 × 2,400 = 600 + 144 = 744 cfm, about 6.2 cfm per person. With the room empty, Pz is 0, so Vbz is 144 cfm: the area term alone.

Step 2, people heat. Sensible: 120 × 250 = 30,000 Btu/h. Latent: 120 × 200 = 24,000 Btu/h. We leave out lights, microphones, the envelope and the outdoor air load.

Step 3, supply for the people sensible load. cfm = 30,000 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 30,000 ÷ 21.6 = about 1,389 cfm (see HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas for 1.08).

Step 4, compare to the air change guideline. The GSA standards list 8 air changes per hour for courtrooms with ceilings above 15 feet. Volume is 2,400 × 16 = 38,400 cubic feet, so 38,400 × 8 ÷ 60 = 5,120 cfm. At that airflow the people load alone raises the supply-to-room difference by only 30,000 ÷ (1.08 × 5,120) = about 5.4°F. The older standard caps recirculation at 85 percent, which we read as at least 15 percent outdoor air in the supply. Our 62.1 outdoor air of 744 cfm is 744 ÷ 5,120 = 14.5 percent of that airflow, so the two criteria are close, and the 85 percent cap would call for about 768 cfm (0.15 × 5,120). That is our reading of the older standard, and the designer decides.

What it means. The people load needs only about 1,400 cfm, but the room is designed for far more air at a gentle temperature difference, which allows large, slow, quiet outlets. Cutting airflow to save fan energy would change both noise and mixing, so check the design intent first.

Field card: Courthouse HVAC: Courtroom Noise, Jury Rooms, Holding Cells, Screening and After-Hours Zoning

Empty to full, quiet, and sealed between rooms.

  • 62.1 courtrooms: 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per sq ft; default 70 people per 1,000 sq ft.
  • Vbz = Rp × Pz + Ra × Az. Example: 744 cfm at 120 people, 144 cfm empty.
  • People heat: 250 sensible and 200 latent Btu/h each (ASHRAE Fundamentals row). 1.08 × cfm × delta T.
  • GSA 2003 standards: NC 25 to 30; equipment 25 ft away; jury ducts lined 2 in for 12 ft; two courtrooms per air handler at most.
  • Check for open penetrations and unlined replacement duct first.
  • Court set (courtroom, chambers, jury room) needs its own after-hours zone.
  • Holding areas: see the correctional lesson; coordinate access with court security.
