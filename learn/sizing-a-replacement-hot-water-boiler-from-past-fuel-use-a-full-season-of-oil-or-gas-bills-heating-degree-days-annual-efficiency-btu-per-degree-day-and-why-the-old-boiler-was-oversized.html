Sizing a Replacement Boiler From Past Fuel Use

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The old boiler's nameplate tells you what it could burn, not what the house needed. A full season of fuel bills tells you what the house actually used. This lesson turns those bills into a design load in Btu/h, the number a replacement boiler should be sized against, and shows why that number is so often far below the old boiler's rating.

The idea in one paragraph

Every Btu the boiler put into the house came from fuel. If we know how much heating fuel was burned, how efficiently it was burned, and how cold the season was, we can work out how many Btu the house needs for each degree day. Multiply that by the temperature difference on a design day and we have a design load. It is a cross-check on a room-by-room heat loss calculation, and for a house with years of records it is often more honest than a guess, because it includes the real insulation, real leaks and real thermostat habits. For the heat loss and radiation side of the job, see Sizing a Replacement Hot Water Boiler: Heat Loss vs. Radiation and Oversizing. For the degree day basics, see Heating Degree Days: Estimating Seasonal Fuel Use. We go the other direction here: from fuel back to load.

Step 1: Collect a full heating season

Use at least one complete year, from the same date to the same date. Oil deliveries are lumpy, so a delivery total only matches use if the tank was filled at the start and the end of the period or the gauge was read on those dates. Gas bills are easier because each bill covers a meter-read period. Write down the gallons or therms and the dates of each bill. A New York State load-calculation guidance document (cited as plain text below) asks for two years of data and averages them, which is a good habit because one odd winter, a vacancy or a long power outage can distort a single year.

Step 2: Take out what is not space heating

If the boiler also makes domestic hot water, or the gas bill also feeds a range or dryer, part of the fuel never heated the rooms. The state guidance mentioned above handles this with a base load: take the lowest-use summer month, when the heating is off, divide by its days to get a daily figure, and multiply by 365. Subtract that from the annual total to get heating fuel. Our own note: this works best when summer hot water use is about the same all year. If the family is away in summer, or a clothes dryer runs more in winter, the base load will be a little off, so keep the guess modest and write the assumption down.

Step 3: Convert fuel to heat, then to heat delivered

The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists about 138,500 Btu in a gallon of home heating oil, and 100,000 Btu in a therm of natural gas, and says its factors are recalculated each year. The University of Maine's extension bulletin on biomass boiler sizing uses 138,000 Btu per gallon in its own oil example, so values in that range are normal. Multiply heating fuel by its heating value to get fuel energy in. Then multiply by the annual (seasonal) efficiency, as a decimal, to get the heat that actually reached the water. Seasonal efficiency is lower than a combustion test number because it includes cycling, standby and warm-up losses. If you only have a stack test, the result will read a little high, and the answer will be a little high too. See the oil burner combustion test lesson for what that test does and does not measure. A national laboratory reference manual for hydronic equipment describes its own method for annual fuel use and seasonal efficiency, with building design heat load, domestic hot water use and firing rate as inputs, which tells us this is a recognized way to think about the problem.

Step 4: Divide by degree days, then by 24

The Energy Information Administration defines a heating degree day as a measure of how much heating is needed, counted when a day's mean temperature falls below a 65 F base. A day with a 29 F mean is 36 heating degree days (65 minus 29). Use the total for the same dates as your bills. The Climate Prediction Center, part of the National Weather Service, publishes totals, and some utility bills print them. Heat delivered divided by degree days gives Btu per degree day. Since a degree day is one degree for 24 hours, divide by 24 to get Btu per degree day per hour, which is the same thing as the house's heat loss per degree, Btu/h per F.

Step 5: Multiply by the design temperature difference

Pick the indoor setpoint and an outdoor design temperature for the town. Our lesson on outdoor design temperatures explains where those values come from and why using the all-time record low oversizes. Subtract to get the design temperature difference, then multiply by Btu/h per F. The result is the design load in Btu/h. A different method in the Maine bulletin scales fuel per degree day up to the coldest single day in the record. That is a simpler shortcut, and it ignores efficiency and indoor setpoint, so we show the longer route here.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This house is invented for teaching. None of these inputs come from a real house or a published weather table. Every input below is an assumption.

  • Oil bought and burned in one full year: 1,100 gallons (assumed).
  • Summer use, heating off: 10 gallons per month, mostly hot water (assumed). Over 12 months, 120 gallons.
  • Heating degree days for the same dates, 65 F base: 5,200 (assumed).
  • Seasonal efficiency of the old boiler: 75 percent (assumed).
  • Indoor setpoint 70 F, outdoor design 10 F, so the design temperature difference is 60 F (assumed).
  • Old boiler nameplate: 140,000 Btu/h output (assumed).
  1. Heating oil. 1,100 minus 120 = 980 gallons.
  2. Fuel energy. 980 x 138,500 Btu/gal = 135,730,000 Btu.
  3. Heat delivered. 135,730,000 x 0.75 = 101,797,500 Btu.
  4. Btu per degree day. 101,797,500 / 5,200 = about 19,576 Btu per degree day.
  5. Per hour. 19,576 / 24 = about 816 Btu/h per F.
  6. Design load. 816 x 60 = about 48,900 Btu/h.
  7. Compare. 140,000 / 48,900 = about 2.9. The old boiler could put out nearly three times what the house needs on the design day.

With a gas boiler the steps are the same. A therm is 100,000 Btu, so 980 therms of heating gas would be 98,000,000 Btu of fuel energy before efficiency.

Why this so often shows the old boiler was oversized

Old boilers were commonly chosen by adding up radiation, adding a generous allowance and rounding up, or by copying the boiler before it. Since then many houses have gained attic insulation, new windows and tighter air sealing, so the load fell while the nameplate stayed put. A U.S. Department of Energy fact sheet on boiler cycling losses says boilers are often sized for peak loads that never show up, or for demand that later drops, and that an oversized boiler satisfies demand quickly, shuts down, and restarts, which lowers efficiency. It notes that fixed losses matter more at light load, with radiation loss rising from 1 percent of input at full load to 4 percent at one quarter load for the boiler type it describes. The fact sheet is about larger plant boilers, so treat those figures as an illustration of the trend, not a number for a house. Our lesson on hot water boiler short cycling covers what that looks like at the house scale.

Limits of the method

  • The 65 F base. A tight house with free heat may need no heat until it is well below 65 F. Counting those mild days as degree days spreads the fuel over too many degrees, so the load per degree reads slightly low. Our own reasoning, covered in the degree day lesson. A design load from this method is a check, not a replacement for a proper heat loss calculation.
  • Setbacks and comfort. If the family kept the house cool or closed off rooms, the bills show a smaller load than a fully heated house would have. The design load is what the house used, not what a hotter setting would need.
  • Radiation. The boiler can only deliver what the emitters release. Check the emitters against the load. For steam, use EDR instead.
  • Allowances. Makers add piping and pickup allowances to the design load, and some houses need extra for a big indirect water heater. Follow the instructions for the boiler you are installing.
  • Weather mismatch. Use degree days from a station near the house, over exactly the same dates as the bills.

The design load you get is a starting point. A licensed person who is responsible for the job makes the final sizing decision under the rules where the work is done.

Field card: Sizing a Replacement Boiler From Past Fuel Use

Fuel in, heat out, per degree day, times the design difference.

  • Use a full year of bills with matching dates.
  • Subtract summer base load: hot water, cooking, drying.
  • Fuel energy: gallons x about 138,500 Btu, or therms x 100,000 Btu.
  • Multiply by seasonal efficiency, then divide by heating degree days.
  • Divide by 24 for Btu/h per F, then multiply by indoor minus outdoor design.
  • Compare with the old output and with the installed radiation.
  • Treat the result as a cross-check on a heat loss calculation.
