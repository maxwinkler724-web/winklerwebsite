Floating Head Pressure Control in Commercial Refrigeration

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most refrigeration racks and condensing units sit outdoors, and the air around them is cold for much of the year. Floating head pressure means letting the condensing temperature drop with the outdoor air instead of holding a fixed high number. The compressor works against less pressure, so it uses less power. The catch is a floor, set by the liquid feed to the evaporators.

This lesson is about the savings side of the story: how far the setpoint can float, what has to be true of the valves, and where it conflicts with defrost and heat reclaim. For the opposite job, holding head pressure up with fan cycling and flooding valves, see Low Ambient Kit and Head Pressure Control. We do not repeat that here.

Fixed setpoint versus floating setpoint

An older control scheme picks one condensing pressure, often equal to a condensing temperature of 90 to 100 F, and cycles fans or floods the condenser to hold it all year. Sporlan's head pressure control bulletin gives that range for its flooding valve settings. Floating control replaces the single number with a target that follows outdoor air. A condenser always runs warmer than the air cooling it, so the target is the outdoor temperature plus an approach. A commercial refrigeration energy presentation hosted by Otter Tail Power lists design approaches of 15 F for medium temperature and 10 F for low temperature systems, with the reset limited by a system minimum. Why there is a floor

A thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) feeds liquid by pushing it through a port, and the push comes from the pressure difference between the liquid line and the evaporator. Sporlan's bulletin says low head pressure generally causes too little refrigerant to reach the evaporator. A starved evaporator means warm product and an unstable suction, which costs more than the compressor saved.

The floor therefore depends on the system. Two maker documents land on similar numbers for air-cooled units. The K-RP floating head bulletin describes a controller with a factory setpoint of 70 F condensing, floating below 90 F. the same presentation says to float racks down to 70 F minimum and standalone condensing units to 75 F minimum. California's nonresidential compliance reference, published by the Energy Code Ace program, goes further and says the minimum condensing setpoint must be 70 F or less for covered refrigeration systems, and that the setpoint must reset to outdoor dry bulb for air-cooled condensers. Treat these as examples. The floor for a real job comes from the equipment literature and the controller settings on that site.

Valves that cope with low pressure difference

A conventional TXV can change its capacity a lot when the pressure drop across it changes. Sporlan describes the balanced port design as one that prevents changes in pressure drop across the valve from influencing valve operation, and its TXV catalog, discussing walk-in efficiency (AWEF) applications, says lower head pressure settings greatly affect valve sizing and recommends balanced port valves there. The K-RP bulletin goes further and says the expansion valve must be balanced port and sized for the lower pressures and liquid temperatures. See Metering Devices: TXV and Piston for the basics.

Electronic expansion valves (EEVs) are the other route. A Danfoss technical paper says its electronic valves do not need a minimum static superheat to open and can open at zero pressure difference, and Otter Tail Power asks new facilities to make sure refrigerant reaches the evaporators at low head pressure, using EEVs or other strategies. Details of stepper valves and sensors are in Electronic Expansion Valves. An EEV is not a license to ignore the floor, because liquid still has to arrive at the valve without flashing. Subcooling and insulated liquid lines are program requirements for the Otter Tail Power rebate, and a 1988 ACEEE study named flash gas ahead of the valve as the main risk of cold-weather head pressure.

How much energy it saves

Numbers vary by climate, system and what else is changed, so use them as a range. The 1988 ACEEE study measured a compressor energy drop of 23 percent at one supermarket and said savings were about 1 percent for every degree the condensing temperature falls, with a stated range of 0.9 to 1.1 percent. Its text does not label the unit; the paper otherwise works in Fahrenheit. A Danfoss technical paper gives a rule of thumb of 2 to 3 percent for every 1 K drop in condensing temperature. Those two statements differ and come from different eras and systems, which is why we give a range and not a promise. Floating suction pressure works the same way on the other side of the compressor. Danfoss gives the same 2 to 3 percent per kelvin for a higher suction temperature, and the California reference lists floating suction control as its own requirement.

Defrost and heat reclaim

Floating is not free of conflict. Sporlan states that any system using hot gas for defrost or compressor capacity control must have a normal head pressure to operate properly, and warns that too little head can cause low suction pressure or iced coils. So a hot gas defrost system needs either a higher floor or a holdback valve during defrost. The same bulletin says head pressure controls help assure quality hot gas at a reclaim heat exchanger. The California reference requires a holdback valve on heat recovery coils to maintain condensing temperature during recovery.

The trade is energy against energy. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory report on supermarket waste heat says raising condensing pressure to get hotter waste heat can use more energy than it saves. Heat pump style recovery avoids this because head pressure does not have to stay high. For how racks and their defrost work, see Supermarket Refrigeration Racks.

Worked example: compression ratio at 105 F versus 75 F condensing

The compressor sees a pressure ratio, absolute discharge pressure over absolute suction pressure. Assumptions, all ours: the refrigerant is R-449A; evaporating temperature is 20 F; we use the saturated vapor (dew) pressure at both temperatures to keep the arithmetic simple. Pressures come from the Chemours Opteon XP40 thermodynamic properties sheet, which lists pressure in psia. The sheet does not label its two pressure columns, so we matched the lower column to the saturated vapor values on a distributor copy of the maker chart (rsd.net, Opteon XP40 pressure-temperature data), which agreed within about 1 psi.

  • Suction at 20 F evaporating: 59.15 psia.
  • Discharge at 105 F condensing: 244.62 psia.
  • Discharge at 75 F condensing: 156.35 psia.

Fixed 105 F: 244.62 / 59.15 = 4.14. Floating to 75 F: 156.35 / 59.15 = 2.64. The pressure rise across the compressor falls from 244.62 - 59.15 = 185.47 psi to 156.35 - 59.15 = 97.20 psi, which is about 48 percent less. The ratio falls by (4.14 - 2.64) / 4.14, or about 36 percent. Real power draw depends on the compressor map, so the ratio is a guide and not an energy figure. That same difference is roughly the push available to the TXV, and it is also why the floor matters: float too far and the push is gone.

Field checks

  • Find the floor in the controller and compare it to the equipment literature.
  • On a cold day, check the sight glass or subcooling for flash gas at the liquid line, and confirm the liquid line is insulated.
  • Watch superheat at each case for hunting. Unstable feed points to a valve that cannot handle the pressure difference.
  • Confirm the valves are balanced port or electronic where the design calls for them.
  • Run a defrost and watch suction pressure and coil clearing, because hot gas needs head pressure.
  • On reclaim systems, confirm the holdback or diverting valve works, and see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure before blaming the controls for a high number.

Field card: Floating Head Pressure Control in Commercial Refrigeration

Let the head float, but never below the floor the valves can feed.

  • Floating target = outdoor temperature + approach, limited by a minimum.
  • Examples of floors in the sources: 70 F for racks, 75 F for standalone units. Use the equipment literature.
  • Balanced port TXV or EEV needed at low pressure difference.
  • Roughly 1 to 3 percent compressor savings per degree of condensing drop (sources differ).
  • Hot gas defrost and heat reclaim need a normal head pressure or a holdback valve.
  • Check subcooling, liquid line insulation and superheat stability on cold days.
