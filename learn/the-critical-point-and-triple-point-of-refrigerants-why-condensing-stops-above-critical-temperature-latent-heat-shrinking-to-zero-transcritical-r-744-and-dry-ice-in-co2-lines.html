Refrigerant Critical Point and Triple Point: Why Condensing Stops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every refrigerant has a ceiling temperature above which it cannot be squeezed into a liquid, no matter the pressure. That ceiling is the critical temperature. It explains why a very hot condenser costs capacity and efficiency, and why carbon dioxide systems are built differently from the rest.

What the critical point is

On a pressure and temperature chart, the boiling line (saturation line) does not run forever. It ends at the critical point. A university thermodynamics reading describes it as the top of the vapor dome, where the difference between liquid and vapor can no longer be told apart, and the critical temperature as the highest temperature at which liquid and vapor can coexist (see Sources 2 and 3). Below that temperature, raising pressure turns hot gas into liquid. Above it, raising pressure just makes denser gas, with no sudden change and no liquid forming. So above the critical temperature there is no condensing.

Why latent heat shrinks to zero

Latent heat of vaporization is the enthalpy of saturated vapor minus the enthalpy of saturated liquid. On the dome those two lines move toward each other as temperature rises, and at the critical point they meet. Our reasoning from that: the gap, which is the latent heat, is zero there. The closer a condenser runs to the critical temperature, the less heat each pound of refrigerant can give up by condensing, so the compressor must move more refrigerant for the same heat rejection. The worked example below puts numbers on this. For the sensible and latent basics, see the related lesson on sensible versus latent heat.

Approximate critical temperatures

These values come from a federal measurement institute's published property data (Source 1). We converted kelvin to degrees F and rounded. Pressures were converted from bar or MPa to psia.

R-744 (carbon dioxide)about 88 F (304.2 K)about 1,070 psia (73.8 bar)
R-32about 173 F (351.3 K)about 839 psia (57.85 bar)
R-290 (propane)about 206 F (369.9 K)about 616 psia (42.5 bar)
R-134aabout 214 F (374.2 K)about 589 psia (4.06 MPa)

R-1234yf and R-454B are not in this table because we did not confirm their values from the same source. Blends like R-454B have a critical temperature of their own; use the maker's published property data for the exact refrigerant in the equipment you are servicing.

Why R-744 goes transcritical

Carbon dioxide's critical temperature is only about 88 F. On a warm day, outdoor air is hotter than that, so the high side cannot condense CO2 at all. The system then runs transcritical: the compressor pushes gas above the critical pressure, and a gas cooler removes heat while the fluid stays a single phase, cooling as a dense gas instead of condensing at one temperature. Pressure on that side is set by the controls, not by a boiling temperature. See the related lesson on CO2 heat pumps for pressures, gas coolers and safety. We do not repeat that here.

Why most others lose capacity on very hot condensers

R-32, R-290 and R-134a stay subcritical at normal condensing temperatures because their critical points are far higher. Still, a hot condenser (dirty coil, failed fan, extreme ambient) pushes condensing temperature up the dome. Three things follow from the shape of the dome, by our reasoning: latent heat per pound falls, the pressure ratio rises so the compressor works harder, and more of the liquid flashes to vapor in the expansion device, which leaves less liquid to absorb heat in the evaporator. The result is lower capacity and lower efficiency, and a hotter discharge. Condenser care and charge checks matter most on the hottest days.

Worked example (our own): R-134a latent heat at 40 F versus 160 F

This is our own example. Assumptions: saturated R-134a, values read from the federal property database (Source 1) at 277.59 K (40 F) and 344.26 K (160 F), in kJ/kg. We convert with 1 kJ/kg = 0.42992 Btu/lb. Real systems also have subcooling and superheat, which we ignore.

  • At 40 F: saturation pressure 0.34295 MPa (about 35 psig). Liquid enthalpy 206.00, vapor enthalpy 401.17. Latent heat = 401.17 - 206.00 = 195.17 kJ/kg, or about 83.9 Btu/lb.
  • At 160 F: saturation pressure 2.1700 MPa (about 300 psig). Liquid enthalpy 306.22, vapor enthalpy 428.78. Latent heat = 428.78 - 306.22 = 122.56 kJ/kg, or about 52.7 Btu/lb.
  • Change: 122.56 / 195.17 = 0.628, so latent heat falls by about 37 percent (about 31 Btu/lb less).

Even though 160 F is still about 54 F below the 214 F critical temperature, a large share of latent heat is gone. Condensing at 160 F is more extreme than ordinary operation; we chose it to show the trend clearly.

The triple point and dry ice in CO2 lines

The triple point is the single temperature and pressure where solid, liquid and vapor can exist together (Source 2). For carbon dioxide, the federal data give about 216.6 K, which is about -70 F, at about 5.185 bar, which is about 75 psia (Source 1). Below that pressure, CO2 cannot exist as a liquid. If liquid CO2 is let down to a pressure under about 75 psia, it can form solid CO2 (dry ice) and cold vapor together. In a line, dry ice can block a valve or orifice and trap pressure behind it. This is why CO2 systems keep pressure above the triple point during normal operation and why manufacturers give specific procedures for pump-down, venting and standby. Follow the maker's instructions and never vent CO2 into a closed space.

Field card: Critical point and triple point

Above critical temperature, no condensing.

  • Critical point: top of the dome, where liquid and vapor lines meet and latent heat is zero.
  • R-744 critical temperature is about 88 F, so hot weather means transcritical operation.
  • R-134a latent heat falls about 37 percent between 40 F and 160 F (our example).
  • Hot condensers cost capacity and efficiency even on subcritical refrigerants.
  • CO2 triple point is about 75 psia and -70 F: below that pressure, dry ice can form.
