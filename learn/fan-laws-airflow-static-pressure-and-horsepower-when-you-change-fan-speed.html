Fan Laws: Airflow, Static Pressure and Horsepower When You Change Fan Speed

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A customer wants more air, so someone says "just speed the blower up a little." The fan laws tell you what a little costs. Airflow goes up in step with speed, but pressure goes up with the square and motor power with the cube. That last one is what burns up motors.

The three fan laws

Let the speed ratio be new rpm divided by old rpm. Greenheck's fan fundamentals paper and Twin City Fan's engineering guides state the same three relationships:

  • Airflow (CFM) changes by the speed ratio: CFM2 = CFM1 × ratio.
  • Static pressure changes by the ratio squared: SP2 = SP1 × ratio².
  • Brake horsepower (bhp) changes by the ratio cubed: bhp2 = bhp1 × ratio³.

Brake horsepower is the power the fan shaft actually takes. It is not the motor nameplate rating, and it is not the electrical input. Greenheck gives a quick feel for the cube: a 25 percent rpm increase means a 95 percent horsepower increase (1.25³ is about 1.95).

What the laws assume

The laws only hold under conditions people forget:

  • Same fan, same system. Greenheck says the laws apply in a steady-state system where you stay on the same system resistance curve. If you also swap a filter, move a damper or fix a duct, the starting point changes and the math no longer predicts the result.
  • Same air density. Twin City Fan notes that pressure and horsepower change with density, so test data and design data must be compared at matching density. Fan tables are generally written for standard air of 0.075 lb/ft³. Hot air, cold air and altitude shift the real numbers.
  • Speed must stay within the fan's limit. Twin City Fan tells you to check the new rpm against the maximum allowed for that fan, and to ask the maker if the catalog does not list it.

For how pressure and airflow relate to your readings, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics; for measuring CFM, see Measuring Airflow (CFM).

Why a belt drive speed-up can overload the motor

On a belt-driven blower you change speed by changing sheave sizes. The motor does not know that. It only feels the bigger load. The Department of Energy's motor guidance explains that a motor's service factor is a multiplier on the nameplate rating, commonly 1.10 or 1.15, showing how far above full load it can run under certain conditions. It also warns that running above rated load raises winding temperature and shortens life, and that running into the service factor is less efficient. DOE's fan sourcebook says raising airflow by raising speed requires significantly more power and may call for a larger motor.

Worked example

The numbers are invented. An air handler runs at 4,000 CFM, 1.2 in. w.c., and 2.1 bhp at 900 rpm. The motor is 3 hp, service factor 1.15, 1,725 rpm, with a 10.0 in. fan sheave. We want 4,600 CFM.

  1. Speed ratio. 4,600 ÷ 4,000 = 1.15.
  2. New fan speed. 900 × 1.15 = 1,035 rpm.
  3. New static pressure. 1.15² = 1.3225. 1.2 × 1.3225 = 1.587, about 1.59 in. w.c.
  4. New brake horsepower. 1.15³ = 1.5209. 2.1 × 1.5209 = 3.19 bhp.
  5. Compare to the motor. The nameplate is 3.0 hp, so 3.19 bhp is already over it. Service-factor capacity is 3 × 1.15 = 3.45 hp. 3.19 ÷ 3.45 = 0.93, so the fan alone would use 93 percent of the motor's service-factor limit, before any belt loss. That is a motor living above its rating. DOE says this shortens life, and the belt drive adds its own losses. The honest answer is to size a larger motor, then confirm that the fan, shaft and bearings are rated for 1,035 rpm.
  6. Motor sheave for 1,035 rpm. Fan rpm = motor rpm × motor sheave ÷ fan sheave. So motor sheave = fan rpm × fan sheave ÷ motor rpm = 1,035 × 10.0 ÷ 1,725 = 6.0 in.
  7. Check the old setup. 900 rpm needs 900 × 10.0 ÷ 1,725 = 5.2 in., and 1,725 × 5.2 ÷ 10.0 = 897 rpm, close enough. The sheave grows from 5.2 in. to 6.0 in., a ratio of about 1.15, as it must.

Pressure also rose from 1.2 to about 1.59 in. w.c., which is an increase in fan work, not necessarily more pressure at the rooms. A bigger sheave also changes belt length and wrap, so use the sheave maker's data. A trade blog article on adjustable sheaves uses the same ratio math, warns against changing sheave position casually, and says a change must never cause an overload; measure blower amps before and after and compare them to the motor nameplate (see clamp-meter amp checks and Belt Drives and Sheave Basics).

Real-world limits

System effect. A fan can deliver less in the building than on the catalog test because of elbows, dampers or guards too close to the inlet or outlet. Greenheck gives an example: a system 20 percent short on air needs 20 percent more speed, which raises static pressure about 44 percent and brake horsepower about 73 percent. AMCA Publication 201 covers how fans are tested and how to apply system effect factors. Twin City Fan notes that fixing a near-fan outlet elbow by speeding up the fan costs power and efficiency. Fix the cause first.

Slowing down. The same cube law is why slowing a fan saves so much. DOE's tip sheet says a 20 percent flow cut can cut power about 50 percent for fans with no static lift. But variable-frequency drives (VFDs) have limits: efficiency falls at light loads, and makers set minimum speeds. See Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans, and follow the fan and motor makers for the lowest allowed speed.

The law is a prediction. After any change, measure. Check amps, rpm and airflow rather than trusting the calculation alone. For duct design context see Duct Sizing Basics.

Field card: Fan Laws

Air follows speed. Pressure follows speed squared. Power follows speed cubed.

  • ratio = new rpm ÷ old rpm. CFM × ratio; SP × ratio²; bhp × ratio³.
  • Holds for the same fan, same system, same air density.
  • Example: 15 percent more air means about 32 percent more pressure and 52 percent more power.
  • Compare new bhp to the motor nameplate and service factor; do not plan to live in the service factor.
  • Motor sheave = fan rpm × fan sheave ÷ motor rpm.
  • Check the fan's maximum rpm. Measure amps after any change.
  • Fix system effect and restrictions before adding speed.
