Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling: Oversizing, Zoning, Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot-water boiler that lights, runs a few minutes, shuts off and lights again is short cycling. It is not a part that failed so much as a mismatch: the boiler can make heat faster than the system can take it. This lesson is about water boilers only. For steam, see Steam Boiler Short-Cycling: Causes and Where to Look, where the trigger is usually a pressure control.

What it looks like

You hear the burner start, run for a few minutes, stop, wait a short time and start again, over and over, while the house is still calling for heat. A trade article by Steve Swanson in Contractor magazine describes it as an oversized boiler satisfying the demand quickly and shutting off repeatedly, for example firing for three minutes, stopping and repeating. He lists premature boiler failure, higher heating cost and poor system performance as results. A PHCP Pros article by Stephen Minnich adds that a modulating condensing boiler holds only about 2 to 5 gallons of water, against 12 to 20 gallons in older cast-iron boilers, so it heats up very quickly at similar firing rates and can cycle fast. He links frequent cycling to lower combustion efficiency and early wear of ignitors and blowers.

The cause is a heat balance. Every minute the burner runs, it adds heat. Every minute the emitters run, they remove it. When the first is much bigger than the second, the water reaches the shut-off temperature almost at once.

Cause 1: the boiler is bigger than the load, or than the smallest zone

Oversizing is the classic cause. A boiler sized for the coldest day will be far larger than the load on a mild day. The problem is worse with zoning. If one small zone calls alone, the boiler sees only that zone's load, however large the house is. Minnich calls the smallest zone the one that sets the minimum system load, and his example zone loses 5,000 Btu/h.

Cause 2: modulating boilers have a floor

A modulating boiler lowers its firing rate to match the load, but only down to a minimum. The ratio of maximum to minimum input is the turndown. If the load is below the minimum input, the boiler still cannot fire any lower, so it overshoots and shuts off. Published turndown figures vary a lot by maker and model:

  • Navien lists its NHB-150 at a 15:1 turndown with a 10,000 to 150,000 Btu/h input range.
  • Weil-McLain's suggested specification for its Ultra boilers describes modulating firing with a turndown of up to 5 to 1. A Weil-McLain manual addendum lists a factory low-fire rate of 31,000 Btu/h for the Ultra 155-CT.
  • Lochinvar describes its Knight as a fully modulating burner with 5:1 turndown, and its manual rating table shows ranges such as 16,000 to 80,000 Btu/h for the KBN080.
  • Viessmann's 2007 technical data manual for the Vitodens 100-W lists 29 to 80 MBH input for the WB1A 8-24 model. That works out to roughly 2.8 to 1, my own division, not a figure the manual states.

Read your own boiler's data. A higher turndown gives a lower floor, and a lower floor tolerates smaller zones.

Worked example: smallest zone vs. the boiler's floor

This example is partly hypothetical, and the numbers are labeled so you can swap in your own.

  1. Zone emitter output. Slant/Fin's 30 Series baseboard (part 101-401-5) is listed at 580 Btu/h per linear foot using 180°F water at 1 gpm. Hypothetical zone: 20 ft of that baseboard. 20 ft × 580 Btu/h per ft = 11,600 Btu/h. That is the most the zone can give off at 180°F water, so it is a ceiling on the load. The real load is whatever the rooms lose, and that is often lower.
  2. Boiler minimum input. Hypothetical boiler: 150,000 Btu/h at 10:1 turndown, the same figures Minnich uses. 150,000 ÷ 10 = 15,000 Btu/h minimum.
  3. Compare. 11,600 − 15,000 = −3,400 Btu/h. Even at its maximum, the zone takes less than the boiler's lowest fire. The extra heat has nowhere to go, the water temperature climbs and the boiler shuts off. With milder water from outdoor reset, the baseboard emits less than 580 per foot, so the gap gets bigger.
  4. Check a different boiler. With the Navien NHB-150 figures above (15:1), the minimum is 150,000 ÷ 15 = 10,000 Btu/h. The zone's 11,600 now sits just above the floor, which is workable on paper but with little margin.

Swanson's article shows the same pattern: a 65,000 Btu/h modulating boiler at 5:1 has a 13,000 Btu/h smallest flame, and a 5,000 Btu/h zone falls below it.

Cause 3: the control differential is too tight

The differential is how far the water must cool below the setpoint before the burner restarts. A narrow one means a restart after a small drop. Controls differ:

  • Honeywell's installation sheet for the L8148A, E and J aquastat relays says the differential is nonadjustable. A spec sheet for the L8148A1017 lists it as 8°F fixed.
  • Honeywell's L6006A instructions give an adjustable differential of 5°F to 30°F, to be set per the system maker's recommendation.
  • Hydrolevel's brochure for the HydroStat 3100 and 3150 lists a differential range of 10°F to 30°F.

On a fixed-differential relay, you cannot widen it. Widening an adjustable one can lengthen run times, but stay within the boiler maker's range, and never raise the high limit just to stop cycling. See Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs.

Cause 4: no buffer, and micro-zones

A buffer tank adds water volume so the boiler can run a full cycle and store the heat. Swanson describes it as storing heat for the small load, and in his example a 65,000 Btu/h boiler with a 5,000 Btu/h microload needs a 60-gallon tank, based on a 15-minute on-cycle (see his article for the full sizing method). Minnich's formula is run time × (minimum boiler output − minimum system load) ÷ (delta T × 8.33 × 60). With his inputs: 20 min × (15,000 − 5,000) = 200,000, and 20°F × 8.33 × 60 = 9,996, so about 20 gallons. He says tanks matter most for larger homes with many zones and suggests a 20-minute run time as a starting point, not less than 10. Follow the boiler maker's piping guidance when adding one.

Cause 5: outdoor reset set too high

If the reset curve makes the water hotter than mild weather needs, zones are satisfied fast and the boiler hits its target quickly. U.S. Boiler's ALTA instructions show defaults such as a low boiler water temperature of 130°F and a high outdoor temperature of 70°F. These are one maker's defaults. See Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation. Cooler water also lowers the boiler's return and matters for condensing; see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers, and Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature for how output falls with cooler water.

Cause 6: flow problems

Too little flow means heat cannot leave the boiler, so the supply temperature jumps and the limit opens. The Lochinvar Knight manual lists required flow by temperature rise: Model 80 needs 5.9 gpm at 25°F rise and 3.3 gpm at 45°F rise. Air locks, a stuck zone valve or a weak circulator all cut flow. Work through Hot-Water No-Heat Diagnosis first, then look at Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators.

Cause 7: anti-cycle settings on modern controls

Many modern boiler controls let you hold off a restart. U.S. Boiler's ALTA extended-features instructions list an Anti-Short Cycle Time with a 3-minute default and a 0 to 10 minute range, plus CH1 differentials with a 10°F default and a 2 to 30°F range. The same document lists a minimum modulation capacity setting. Settings and menus differ by maker, so use the manual for the exact boiler.

Order of checks

  1. Confirm the symptom: short runs while the call continues.
  2. Confirm flow and air first.
  3. Check the setpoint, differential and anti-cycle settings against the manual.
  4. Check the reset curve.
  5. Compare the smallest zone's load to the boiler's minimum input.
  6. If the load is below it, discuss a buffer tank, zone changes or a lower-input boiler with the designer.

Field card: Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling

Short cycling is the boiler making heat faster than the system can take it.

  • Check flow and air before touching settings.
  • Compare the smallest zone's load to the boiler's minimum input.
  • Read the control's differential and anti-cycle range in the manual.
  • Check the reset curve; lower water in mild weather.
  • A buffer tank adds volume; size it per the maker or designer.
  • Never raise the high limit to stop cycling.
