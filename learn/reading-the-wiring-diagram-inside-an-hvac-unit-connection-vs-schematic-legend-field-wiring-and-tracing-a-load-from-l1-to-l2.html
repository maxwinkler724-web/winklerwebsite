Reading the Wiring Diagram Inside an HVAC Unit: Connection vs Schematic, Legend and Tracing a Load

Last updated: 2026-11-03

The diagram glued inside a unit's panel is the most accurate document you will have on the job. It usually holds two drawings of the same wiring, plus a legend and notes. This lesson shows how to read all of it, and how to trace a single load from one line to the other.

Check the label on the unit

Always use the label on the unit in front of you. Line styles, symbols and colors differ between makers and even between models. This page teaches the method; the legend on your unit sets the meaning. This is general education, not a substitute for the maker's instructions.

Two drawings of one circuit

A maker's wiring sheet we read for this lesson (a residential condensing unit sheet from a manufacturer, cited as plain text below) shows two diagrams in each figure: one titled a connection diagram and one titled a schematic diagram in ladder form. They describe the same wires in different ways.

  • Connection (pictorial) diagram. It is drawn like the inside of the box. Parts sit roughly where they sit in the unit, and wires run terminal to terminal. Use it to find a part and see where a wire physically lands.
  • Schematic (ladder) diagram. It is drawn for logic, not location. Power rails run down both sides, and each horizontal rung holds a few devices and one load. Use it to see why something runs, and what must be true for it to run. The basics of rails and rungs are in Reading a Ladder Diagram.

A technical college's schematics class listing describes this same skill set: wiring diagram symbols, reading a legend, telling series from parallel circuits, and drawing both pictorial and ladder diagrams. A good habit is to find the problem on the schematic, then use the connection diagram to put your meter probe on the right terminal.

Start with the legend and notes

Before you trace anything, read the legend. On the maker's sheet we read, the legend defines separate line styles for factory power wiring, field power wiring, factory control wiring, field control wiring, component connections, field splices and junctions. It does not show a universal look for each one, so the sheet itself decides: on some labels field wiring is a dashed line, on others it is not. Wire colors are usually printed as short abbreviations next to each wire, such as BLK, RED, YEL, BLU and BRN on that sheet. Match each abbreviation to the real wire and to the terminal name.

Factory versus field wiring. Factory wiring was installed and tested at the plant. Field wiring is whatever the installer adds: the power supply from the disconnect and the thermostat or control cable. When a unit stops working after a new install, field wiring is the first place to look. When a unit that has run for years stops, factory wiring and parts that age are more likely, but the diagram tells you which wires are which.

The notes block matters too. The sheet we read says that if any original wire must be replaced, use the same or equivalent wire, and it calls for wiring that follows the National Electrical Code and local codes. Those notes are a quiet reminder that you are changing a built and tested product, so do it the way the label says.

Drawn at rest: normally open and normally closed

A university lecture note on control diagrams states the convention plainly: all contacts are drawn with the relay coil de-energized. A normally open (NO) contact is drawn open, and it closes when its coil or device is energized. A normally closed (NC) contact is drawn closed, and it opens when energized. The same note says heavy lines show power wiring and lighter lines show control wiring, which is a quick cue on a busy label. An extension-service tech note adds that an overload protection device appears as a normally closed switch in a control circuit, and that thermostat contacts must be identified as closing on a rise or on a fall in the sensed quantity.

That means a picture of a running unit is not what you are looking at. You are looking at everything off and waiting. To imagine the unit running, mentally energize a coil and flip all of its contacts. A contactor coil that is energized closes its NO power contacts and the load wires get voltage.

Tracing a load from L1 to L2

  1. Find the load. Locate the motor or device that is not working, by its label, such as COMP or CFM.
  2. Walk to L1. On the schematic, follow its rung one direction, listing every device in series: contacts, overloads, switches, capacitors in its branch.
  3. Walk to L2. Do the same in the other direction. A load needs a complete path through closed devices from L1 to L2 (or R to C in the control side).
  4. Find what controls each device. A contactor contact is closed only when its coil is energized, so move to the coil's rung and repeat.
  5. Put names on terminals. Use the connection diagram and wire colors to find each point on the real unit.

For the voltage-reading method on a series string, see Half-Splitting a Control Circuit. The same trace works for any rung.

Test safely

OSHA's lockout rule requires an authorized employee to verify that isolation and deenergization have been accomplished before work begins, and requires stored or residual energy to be relieved, restrained or otherwise made safe. It also states that push buttons, selector switches and other control circuit type devices are not energy isolating devices. In HVAC terms, a thermostat turned off is not an isolation point; the disconnect is. Run capacitors store energy, so the unit diagram and your meter help you confirm, and your employer's procedures set the details. Resistance, capacitance and winding checks are done with the power off and the capacitor discharged, as in Capacitors and Contactors.

Why trust the label over memory

Terminal names and wire colors look standard, but they are not guaranteed to be. Two units that look alike may use a different color on the same terminal, a different contactor arrangement, a board in place of a relay, or a different place for a safety. A replacement part may also change the wiring. Memory is a good start; the label shows what is in this unit. Replace a wire or board the way the legend and notes say, not the way the last unit was done.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed values, not a particular maker's unit. Assume a single-phase packaged AC. Line voltage is 240 V on L1 and L2; the transformer makes 24 V (R to C). The label's schematic is shown below: a 2-pole contactor K1, a compressor with an internal overload (OL) and a run capacitor, a condenser fan with its own run capacitor section, and a high-pressure switch (HPS) in series with the K1 coil.

Complaint: the thermostat calls for cooling, the condenser fan runs, the compressor does not.

  1. Read what the fan tells you. The fan runs only if K1 pulled in. K1 pulls in only if the 24 V rung is complete: transformer output present, Y closed, HPS closed and the coil good. So this single fact clears the transformer, the thermostat call, the HPS and the coil. A common mistake here is to suspect the high-pressure switch. In this drawing, an open HPS drops K1 and the fan would stop too. (Check your own label: some units run the fan on a separate control.)
  2. Check the shared path. Both loads sit behind the same K1 contacts, so the fan running also shows L1 and L2 are getting through K1 to the load side. The compressor shares that supply, so a failed contactor is unlikely. (If your label puts the compressor on different poles, check those poles.)
  3. List what only the compressor has. Compare the two rungs. What is on the compressor rung and not on the fan rung: the internal overload, the compressor windings, its own run capacitor section, and the wires and terminals to it. Those are the suspects.
  4. Measure at the compressor terminals. With the call on and the unit treated as live, measure 240 V across the terminals the diagram names, C and R in this example. If 240 V is there and nothing happens, the supply is fine and the fault is in the compressor or its capacitor. If voltage is missing, the fault is in the wiring between K1 and the compressor, such as a loose lead.
  5. Power off, then test the part. Isolate and verify, discharge the capacitor, then check the capacitor with a meter and the winding resistances. If both windings read open, the internal overload may be open from heat. See Compressor Internal Overload Protectors.

The diagram did not name the failed part, but it narrowed the search to the compressor leg before a probe touched anything.

Field card: Reading the Wiring Diagram in a Unit

Legend first, rung second, terminal third.

  • Two drawings: connection diagram for location, schematic (ladder) for logic.
  • Read the legend and notes before tracing; line styles and colors are set by the label.
  • Factory wiring versus field wiring: check which the legend says each line is.
  • Contacts are drawn with the coil de-energized: NO drawn open, NC drawn closed.
  • A load needs a closed path from L1 to L2 (or R to C).
  • A running load clears every device that its supply shares with it.
  • Isolate and verify before resistance or capacitor checks.
  • Trust the label on this unit over memory of another.
