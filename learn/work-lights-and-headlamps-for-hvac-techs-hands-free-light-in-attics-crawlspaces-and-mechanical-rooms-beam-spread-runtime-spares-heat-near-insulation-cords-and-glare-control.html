Work Lights and Headlamps for HVAC Techs: Hands-Free Light and Runtime Planning

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most HVAC equipment hides in the dark: attic corners, crawlspace runs, boiler rooms with one bare bulb. If one hand holds a flashlight, you have one hand for the work, and you will take shortcuts. This lesson covers how to choose and use hands-free light, plan battery runtime, and keep the light itself from becoming a hazard.

Why hands-free light matters

Attics, crawlspaces and mechanical rooms share three problems: little or no built-in light, tight spaces where you need both hands, and surfaces that hide hazards such as nails, wiring, open framing and wet floors. The federal construction rule on illumination sets minimums in foot-candles and lists, for example, 5 foot-candles for general construction area lighting, 3 for access ways, and 10 for electrical equipment rooms and shops (29 CFR 1926.56, Table D-3). Those figures are a useful yardstick for what a dim room is missing, though your employer's safety program decides what applies to a given job. Our takeaway: if you cannot clearly read a nameplate, a wire color or a floor edge, add light before you continue.

Headlamp, area light or magnetic light

  • Headlamp. Light goes where you look and both hands stay free. Best for crawling, reaching and reading labels. Weak point: it follows your head, so it casts flat light and shines into anyone you face.
  • Area light on a stand or hook. Lights the whole workspace from one side, which gives shadows that show depth. Best for long jobs in one spot, such as a coil swap. Weak point: it is one more thing to carry through a hatch, and it may need a cord.
  • Magnetic or clip light. Sticks to a furnace cabinet or clips to a rafter, so it can be placed low and to the side. Best for panel work and looking under a unit. Weak point: it falls if the surface is dusty, hot or not steel.

Many techs use two: a headlamp for moving and reading, plus a placed light for the main task. A second source also covers you if one dies mid-job.

Brightness and beam spread

More brightness is not always better. A tight spot beam reaches across a long attic but leaves you blind to your feet. A wide flood beam lights close work evenly but looks dim at distance. Choose a light with both modes, or carry one of each. In a white-insulated or foil-faced space, a bright flood can bounce back and cause glare, so drop to a lower setting for close work. Use the low mode whenever you can: it saves battery and eyes.

Battery runtime and spares

Runtime comes from two numbers: the battery's watt-hours (energy stored) and the light's watts (energy used). Hours equal watt-hours divided by watts. Real runtime is lower than the math because of conversion losses and temperature, so apply a derating factor. See cordless tool battery safety for how packs behave in heat and cold. One university safety office lists use above 113 F (45 C) as a possible sign of battery failure, so a light or pack that runs hot in an attic should be shut off and moved, not tolerated.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example, with assumed inputs. A tech has a 5-hour attic job. The headlamp and one area light are both on for the whole 5 hours, which is a conservative plan.

  • Assumptions: headlamp battery 7.4 Wh; headlamp 3 W on high, 0.5 W on low, used half the time on each; area light battery 40 Wh, light 12 W; usable fraction 80 percent (assumed); planning reserve 25 percent (assumed).

Headlamp. Average draw = (3 + 0.5) / 2 = 1.75 W. Energy needed = 1.75 W x 5 h = 8.75 Wh. With reserve: 8.75 x 1.25 = 10.94 Wh. Usable per battery = 7.4 x 0.80 = 5.92 Wh. Batteries needed = 10.94 / 5.92 = 1.85, so carry 2 in total (one in the lamp, one spare), and note that a single battery would last 5.92 / 1.75 = 3.4 h.

Area light. Energy needed = 12 W x 5 h = 60 Wh. With reserve: 60 x 1.25 = 75 Wh. Usable per battery = 40 x 0.80 = 32 Wh. Batteries needed = 75 / 32 = 2.34, so carry 3. One battery alone gives 32 / 12 = 2.7 h.

Plan: 2 headlamp batteries, 3 area light batteries, charged before you leave. If you cannot carry them all up, shorten the job plan or bring a corded light. Cold or hot attics can cut real runtime further, so treat the reserve as a minimum.

Heat from lights near insulation

Some lamps get very hot. A university safety sheet on halogen lamps says the bulb surface can approach 1,000 F and advises keeping combustibles at least 24 inches from the dome, warning that debris collected in the dome can contact the bulb and start a fire. Insulation, dust, paper facing and wood framing in an attic are exactly those materials. Our rules of thumb: prefer cool-running light sources, never lay a lit work light on or in insulation, never bury it, and shut it off when you leave the space. Let a light cool before you handle it, and check the cord and housing for damage before each use.

Cords and trip hazards

A cord across a hatch, a joist or a walkway is a trip hazard, and in a tight attic a fall can mean going through the ceiling. Federal electrical rules for construction say temporary lights shall not be suspended by their electric cords unless the cords and lights are designed for it, that lamps for general illumination must be protected from accidental contact or breakage, and that portable lighting in wet or conductive locations must run at 12 volts or less or on a ground-fault circuit interrupter (29 CFR 1926.405). Cordless lights remove most cord problems, so use them where you can. If you must run a cord, route it along the edge, tape or hook it up out of the walking line, and unplug it before moving the light.

Light near combustible dust

The federal workplace safety agency explains that any combustible material can burn rapidly when finely divided, and that dust suspended in air can become explosible; common materials include wood, paper, plastics and food products. Hot lamps and sparking switches are ignition sources, so do not use ordinary lights in a space with heavy visible dust clouds, and stop and ask a supervisor if the space is dusty from a process such as woodworking or grain handling. We cannot tell you which light is safe for such a room from these sources, so we leave that choice to site rules and the light maker's instructions.

Do not shine lights into eyes

A bright headlamp is dazzling at close range. When you talk to a customer or coworker, tilt the lamp down, switch to low or turn it off. In a crawlspace or attic with a helper, agree on a signal to dim lights. Do not aim a light at someone who is climbing a ladder or on a roof edge, since glare can cost them their footing. Avoid shining it into a customer's windows or toward neighbors.

Field card: Work Lights and Headlamps for HVAC Techs

Two light sources, spare batteries, nothing hot in the insulation.

  • Carry a headlamp plus a placed light; one source is a single point of failure.
  • Runtime hours = usable watt-hours / watts; derate and add a reserve.
  • Flood for close work, spot for distance; use low mode when you can.
  • Keep hot lamps away from insulation, paper facing and wood; shut off when leaving.
  • Keep cords out of walking lines; no hanging lights by their cords unless designed for it.
  • Dust clouds: stop and follow site rules.
  • Tilt the lamp down when facing people.
