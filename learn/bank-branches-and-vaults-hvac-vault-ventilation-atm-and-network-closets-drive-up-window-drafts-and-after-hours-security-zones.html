Bank Branch HVAC: Vault Vents, Network Closets, Drive-Up Drafts and After-Hours Access

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A bank branch looks like a small office, but it has a vault that must not be weakened, equipment rooms that never get a night off, a lobby that sets back at closing, and glass and windows where staff sit all day. This lesson walks a facility tech through each zone, with one worked example for a network closet.

Vault ventilation and the emergency vent

Burglary-resistant vault doors and modular panels are covered by a UL standard, UL 608. We name it so you can recognize it on a nameplate or drawing; we do not reproduce it. A federal specification for modular vault systems (GSA, 1990) is public, and it tells us how the vault envelope is treated. It says the vault includes a utility panel to carry heating, ventilation and air conditioning and to route cable into the vault, and that this panel must give the same resistance to entry as the other panels. It also says the vent design must comply with UL 680, titled Emergency Vault Ventilators and Vault-Ventilating Ports, and it limits vent penetrations to 64 square inches. Conduit entries are limited to 1 inch in diameter with an offset bend inside the panel.

What that means for us (our own practice, not a code quote): the vault is a security product with a rated envelope. The vent and the utility panel are part of that rating. We do not cut, drill, add a duct, or enlarge an opening. If a vault is warm, damp or stuffy, we report it to the branch manager and the vault service provider, who decide on any change. We also do not assume how a given vent works; the title tells us its purpose is emergency ventilation, and the maker’s documentation and the vault service provider explain operation and testing.

Keep the supply and return for the vault area outside the envelope. Treat the vault like a sealed room: if the branch wants the room conditioned, the design goes through the vault maker and the bank’s security staff, not through a quick field fix.

Is entering the vault a confined space issue?

OSHA describes a confined space as one that is large enough to enter, has limited entry and exit, and is not designed for continuous occupancy. A permit-required space adds a hazard such as a hazardous atmosphere or another recognized safety or health hazard. We do not decide whether a particular vault falls under that rule. That is a call for the employer’s written program. What we do: never enter and close a vault door behind us, tell someone where we are, and ask the branch what its entry rule is first.

Lobby, teller line and the ventilation table

ASHRAE 62.1 sets minimum outdoor air with Table 6-1. In Addendum w to the 2022 edition (approved Sept. 30, 2025), the row for banks or bank lobbies shows 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 15 people per 1,000 ft² (air class 1). Office space and main entry lobbies show 5 cfm per person and the same 0.06 cfm per ft². The design edition and any local amendment control a real building, so read the project documents.

Quick check, with an assumed 1,000 ft² bank lobby (our assumption): 15 people × 7.5 = 112.5 cfm, plus 0.06 × 1,000 = 60 cfm, for 172.5 cfm of outdoor air at default density.

Teller line comfort near glass

Staff sit still next to glass and a counter. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory paper on window comfort calculated, for a seated person 1 m from a large window in a design winter case (-17.8°C outside, 21.1°C inside), a radiant asymmetry of 9.4 K (16.9°F), close to the 10 K limit it cites from ASHRAE 55, and an occupant mean radiant temperature of 11.9°C (53.4°F). The authors say people often mistake that heat loss to cold glass for a draft. So a teller complaint of “a draft” may be cold glass, not air movement. Check with a smoke pencil or anemometer before you rebalance. The paper’s draft estimate near large glass was about 0.25 m/s (0.8 ft/s) in that case.

Drive-up window drafts and heat loss

A drive-up or transaction window is a hole in the envelope that opens every few minutes. Product catalog pages for transaction windows describe an optional air curtain that helps limit air conditioning or heat loss and keeps fumes, dust and insects out (we cite the page as plain text; we do not endorse a brand). Our checks: the window’s gaskets and drawer seals, the position of the nearest supply diffuser so it does not blow at the glass, and whether the lane exhaust is pulling air back through the opening. For how an air curtain works and is sized, see the lesson on air curtains below.

Steel ATMs, night drops and cash drawers in a wall also conduct heat and cold. A published patent application describes them as continuous losses, claiming a possible savings of about one third by covering them. That is the inventor’s unproven claim, so treat it as a reason to look at the thermal bridges with an infrared camera, not as a number to plan on.

ATM rooms and network closets run 24/7

The lobby sets back at night. The network closet and the ATM electronics do not. Every watt they draw becomes heat, and 1 W is 3.412 Btu/h (the inverse of the NIST factor of 0.2931 W per Btu/h). ENERGY STAR notes that ASHRAE’s recommended server inlet range is 64.4 to 80.6°F. If the closet depends on lobby air that stops at night, it overheats. Our full closet lesson covers equipment choices; here we cover the airflow check. A ductless unit with its own thermostat serves a closet that must be conditioned around the clock, with a condensate plan and a temperature alarm sent to someone who will see it on a weekend.

Worked example: 1.8 kW network closet

Inputs are measured draw: 1.8 kW of equipment. We use 1.08 as the rounded sensible factor; Trane’s engineers newsletter writes 1.085, from air density 0.075 lb/ft³, specific heat 0.241 Btu/lb·°F and 60 min/h.

  1. Watts: 1.8 kW = 1,800 W.
  2. Heat: 1,800 × 3.412 = 6,141.6, or about 6,142 Btu/h.
  3. Airflow for a 20°F rise: cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT) = 6,142 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 6,142 ÷ 21.6 = about 284 cfm.
  4. Only 100 cfm of transfer air: ΔT = 6,142 ÷ (1.08 × 100) = 6,142 ÷ 108 = about 56.9°F.
  5. If the transfer air is 72°F (our assumption), the closet air settles near 72 + 56.9 = about 129°F, far over the 80.6°F recommended inlet limit. The equipment would trip or fail first.
  6. Reverse check: 100 cfm can only carry off 1.08 × 100 × 20 = 2,160 Btu/h at a 20°F rise, about 633 W. Our closet makes nearly three times that.

The fix is a dedicated cooling unit sized from the 6,142 Btu/h load, not more transfer grille. See the closet lesson for margin and size steps.

After-hours work and security zones

General practice, not a standard: schedule after-hours work with the branch manager in advance and expect to be escorted. Do not share, write down or work around alarm codes; the branch arms and disarms. Expect to sign in, stay in the approved zones, and not enter the vault, teller area or cash rooms without authorization. If a task needs the roof, a ceiling above the vault, or a closet inside a secure area, ask first. Leave equipment, ladders and tools where you can account for each one, and report any alarm you trigger right away.

Field card: Bank Branch HVAC

The vault is a security product. Do not penetrate it.

  • Vault door standard is UL 608; emergency vault ventilators are UL 680. Vent and utility panel are part of the rated envelope.
  • Report vault comfort problems to the manager and the vault service provider. Do not cut or add ducts.
  • Network closet: watts × 3.412 = Btu/h. cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT).
  • 1.8 kW = 6,142 Btu/h = about 284 cfm at 20°F; with 100 cfm the rise is about 56.9°F.
  • Teller “drafts” may be cold glass. Check air speed before you rebalance.
  • After hours: scheduled, escorted, no shared codes, stay in approved zones.
