Refrigerant Temperature Glide: Bubble and Dew Points for Superheat, Subcooling and Liquid Charging

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pure refrigerant boils at one temperature for a given pressure. Many blends do not. They boil across a range, and that range changes which number on the pressure-temperature chart you should use. This lesson covers glide, the dew and bubble columns, and why blends go into a system as liquid.

Blends and glide

A zeotropic blend is a mixture whose parts boil at different temperatures. Freon technical literature from Chemours defines temperature glide as the difference between the saturated vapor (dew point) and saturated liquid (bubble point) temperatures at the same pressure, and says a single-component refrigerant has zero glide because it boils and condenses at one temperature. When a blend boils in an evaporator at steady pressure, the more volatile parts leave first, so the liquid left behind keeps getting a different mix and its temperature climbs until the last drop is gone. The same thing happens in reverse in a condenser.

ACHR News sorts blends in three groups. Azeotropic blends, such as R-500, R-502 and R-507, have negligible glide. Near-azeotropic blends, such as R-410A and R-404A, have a small amount. Zeotropic blends, the 400 series, glide more. Our own plain-language summary: azeotropic acts like a pure fluid, near-azeotropic acts almost like one, and zeotropic does not.

Typical glide values, as makers state them

Chemours' Freon literature lists under 11.5°F glide for R-407C, under 1.1°F for R-404A and under 0.5°F for R-410A. Its Opteon R-404A retrofit guide lists an average of 0.7°R for R-404A and about 7.6 to 7.7°R for R-449A (Opteon XP40), and a separate Opteon sheet gives about 7°F for XP40. The Opteon R-454B product sheet says about 2.0°R (1.1 K), and Honeywell's R-454B data sheet table works out to about 1.7°F at 0 psig and 2.2°F at 100 psig (our own subtraction of its dew and bubble columns). These are catalog numbers at stated conditions. Glide changes with pressure, so read your own chart.

Which column for which reading

The Chemours R-404A retrofit guide says to use the dew point (saturated vapor) when setting superheat and the bubble point (saturated liquid) for subcooling. ACHR News says the same: technicians must always use dew values for superheat and bubble values for subcooling. The reason is plain. Superheat starts after the last drop boils off, which is the dew point. Subcooling starts after the last bubble condenses, which is the bubble point. Honeywell's R-407C chart page gives the same arithmetic: actual temperature minus dew for superheat, bubble minus actual temperature for subcooling.

For the basics of the two readings, see Superheat and Subcooling Basics and Pressure-Temperature Basics.

The mid-point idea

The evaporator does not sit at the dew temperature. Its average temperature lies between bubble and dew. Chemours' Freon literature says that for R-407C you can take about 4°F off the saturated vapor temperature to estimate the average evaporator temperature, and that for R-404A and R-410A you can ignore the glide. ACHR News puts the average at the midpoint of an effective glide (about 75 percent of the total in its example). ACR Journal simply takes the halfway point between bubble and dew. These are for sizing and comparing coil temperatures, not for superheat. Superheat still uses dew.

Why blends are charged as liquid

If a cylinder is drawn from as vapor, the more volatile parts leave first and the mix going into the system changes. ACHR News calls this shift in composition fractionation. Chemours' Opteon bulletin says transfers should be liquid phase whenever possible to minimize composition changes, and says small cylinders should be inverted for charging unless they have a liquid valve.

Liquid straight into a running compressor is a different hazard. Chemours' Opteon XP40 guide says not to charge liquid into the suction line, and to flash it to vapor first through the manifold or a throttling valve. Its Opteon bulletin says a throttling valve should be used when charging liquid to the suction side. The XP40 guide also suggests adding in small steps of 2 to 3 percent by weight. Do the metering this way: draw liquid from the cylinder, throttle it at the manifold so it flashes in the hose, feed it slowly, and let the system settle before each reading. Follow the equipment maker's charging instructions for your unit.

What a leak does

We found little in the maker pages about leaks and glide. Chemours' Opteon bulletin says XP10, XP40 and XP44 can be topped off after leaks, and recommends having composition checked periodically when a blend is repeatedly recycled. The Opteon R-454B sheet lists its very low glide as the reason it can be topped off after leaks. For high-glide blends, follow the manufacturer's instructions on whether to top off or to recover and recharge.

Worked example: R-407C

We use Honeywell's Genetron 407C pressure-temperature chart. A system reads 70 psig on the suction line and the suction line temperature at the same spot is 55°F. The chart shows, at 70 psig, bubble 33.6°F and dew 44.5°F.

  1. Our own: glide at 70 psig = 44.5 − 33.6 = 10.9°F.
  2. Our own, correct superheat uses dew: 55 − 44.5 = 10.5°F.
  3. Our own, wrong answer using the bubble column: 55 − 33.6 = 21.4°F. That reads about 10.9°F too high, which looks like a starved evaporator when it may not be.

Now the liquid side. The liquid line pressure is 200 psig and the liquid line temperature is 85°F. The chart shows, at 200 psig, bubble 92.0°F and dew 101.2°F.

  1. Our own, correct subcooling uses bubble: 92.0 − 85 = 7.0°F.
  2. Our own, wrong answer using the dew column: 101.2 − 85 = 16.2°F. That reads 9.2°F too high and could suggest an overcharge or a restriction.

Honeywell's page shows the same method with its own numbers (40 psig with a 32°F reading, and 200 psig with 85°F). ACHR News shows the same kind of error at 70 and 180 psig.

Field card: Temperature Glide

Dew for superheat. Bubble for subcooling.

  • Glide is dew temperature minus bubble temperature at one pressure.
  • Azeotropic: negligible. Near-azeotropic (R-410A, R-404A): small. Zeotropic (R-407C): large.
  • Superheat = line temperature minus dew. Subcooling = bubble minus line temperature.
  • Charge blends as liquid, throttled so it flashes before the compressor.
  • Use the chart for the exact refrigerant and read its columns.
