HVAC Temperature Sensor Mounting: Wells, Strap-On and Averaging Sensors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A good sensor in a bad spot gives a confident wrong number. The controller trusts it, the trend log records it, and the unit chases a temperature that is not real. This lesson is about where and how a sensor is mounted, because mounting decides most of the error you will meet in the field. For how thermistors work and how to test one with a meter, see the thermistor lesson linked below.

Pipe sensors: wells first

An immersion sensor sits in a thermowell, a closed metal pocket in the pipe, so the sensor touches the metal and not the water. The weak point is the air gap between the sensor stem and the inside of the well. Air is a poor conductor, so a dry stem reads late and reads partly the room around the pipe fitting. A sensor maker's installation note says to fill that air space with heat transfer compound, to apply a generous amount to the tip, and to push the sensor in until the tip reaches the bottom of the bore. It adds that the compound reduces stem conduction error and speeds response. Penn State's facilities technical bulletin on this subject says the same thing in shop terms: fill the voids between the well and the sensor stem, cover the active length of the sensor, and renew the compound every time a sensor is pulled and reinstalled. It also gives a field diagnosis: if a building automation sensor disagrees with a reference instrument or responds slowly, pull it and look in the well for the off-white paste. If it is missing, add it.

Strap-on sensors: contact and insulation

When there is no well, a strap-on sensor is held against the outside of the pipe. It reads the pipe wall, which sits between the water temperature and the room temperature, so it is always a compromise. A rooftop-unit maker's installation sheet for its strap-on sensor lists the steps that keep the compromise small: clean and smooth the pipe for thermal contact, run the sensor along the pipe, place it on top of the pipe, press it down with the supplied thermal mastic strip and wire tie, and then cover it with insulation that extends 6 to 12 inches past each side of the sensor. The insulation is not optional decoration. It keeps room air from pulling the reading toward room temperature. Penn State's bulletin also covers surface probes: put a small dab of compound on the surface and press the probe into it.

Rule of thumb from these sources: use a well when you need the real fluid temperature, as with a heat meter. Use strap-on when you only need a trend or a limit, and insulate it. The BTU meter lesson explains why matched wells matter when the temperature difference is small.

Duct sensors: probe or averaging element

A rigid duct probe reads one spot. That is fine where air is well mixed, such as a long run downstream of a fan. It is a poor choice where the air is layered. An averaging sensor is a long flexible element, either a capillary or a serpentine of sensing wire, strung back and forth across the duct or coil face so the reading is the average along its length.

Two places need an averaging element. The first is the mixed-air section, where cold outdoor air and warm return air meet. A U.S. patent record for an averaging air sensor describes the problem: at low airflow the two streams do not mix before the supply duct, so one side carries warmer return air and the other cooler outdoor air, and the effect is worse in wide units and side-by-side designs. The second is just downstream of a coil, where one row or circuit can run hotter or colder than another. Duke University's facilities standard for sensors sizes averaging elements at one lineal foot of element for each three square feet of coil or duct face area, and requires a gasket at the wall penetration so the sensor does not leak air. Freezestat elements follow their own rule, covered in the freezestat lesson.

Worked example 1: how wrong can a single probe be?

Assumptions (ours, not measured): return air 72 F; outdoor air 20 F; 30% outdoor air by flow overall; the duct face splits into four equal horizontal bands that carry outdoor air fractions of 0.10, 0.20, 0.40 and 0.50.

  1. Check the average fraction: (0.10 + 0.20 + 0.40 + 0.50) / 4 = 1.20 / 4 = 0.30. It matches the 30% assumption.
  2. Each band temperature = 72 − f × (72 − 20) = 72 − 52f. Band 1: 72 − 5.2 = 66.8 F. Band 2: 72 − 10.4 = 61.6 F. Band 3: 72 − 20.8 = 51.2 F. Band 4: 72 − 26.0 = 46.0 F.
  3. True mixed-air average = (66.8 + 61.6 + 51.2 + 46.0) / 4 = 225.6 / 4 = 56.4 F. Check by the mixing formula: 72 − 0.30 × 52 = 56.4 F.
  4. A single probe in band 1 reads 66.8 F, which is 10.4 F high. In band 4 it reads 46.0 F, which is 10.4 F low.

An economizer or freeze-protection routine using that single reading would be making decisions off a number 10 degrees from the truth, even though the sensor itself is perfect. The economizer lesson shows what the controller does with mixed-air temperature. The serpentine reads 56.4 F in this example because it averages the whole face. A real averaging element weights by its length in each band, so it is an estimate of the average, not a guarantee.

Outdoor sensors: sun and shield

An outdoor sensor in the sun reads sun, not air. A controls distributor's technical article on outdoor sensor location gives these figures: a stacked-plate sun shield can still add about 5.4 F in full sun with wind above 5.6 mph, and 9 F or more at lower wind; a fan-aspirated housing adds under 1 F. Its placement advice: mount on the north side in the Northern Hemisphere, never over a sun-heated wall, roof or parking lot, at least 4 feet above the ground and 1 foot below the eave, not above windows, doors, vents or dampers, and with the probe pointing down or horizontal so water does not collect. The outdoor reset lesson shows why a few degrees of outdoor error moves a supply setpoint.

Room sensors

Duke's standard says to place zone sensors near return grilles and away from heat-producing equipment, and not on exterior walls. If no other location exists, use a thermally insulated sub-base. Also keep sensors out of direct sun, away from supply air streams and out of the dead pocket behind a door. The rule is simple: put the sensor where it sees the air the occupants breathe.

Response time lag

Every sensor lags. The time constant is the time to complete 63.2% of a step change, a definition used in university measurement labs. After one time constant a sensor is 63.2% of the way there; after two, 86.5%; after three, 95.0%; after five, 99.3% (our own arithmetic from 1 − e−n).

Worked example 2, assumed numbers: a bare sensor in a dry well has a time constant of 60 s, and with compound 20 s (assumptions, for illustration only). A water temperature steps from 100 F to 140 F. After 60 s the dry sensor has moved 40 × 0.632 = 25.3 F, so it reads 125.3 F. The compound-filled sensor after 60 s is 3 time constants along, 95.0%, so it moves 38.0 F and reads 138.0 F. Slow sensors make loops hunt and make quick checks misleading. Wait at least three time constants before comparing readings.

How to check a sensor against a reference

  1. Use a reference thermometer with known accuracy that is better than the sensor you are testing. Duke's standard asks for building sensors of ±0.5 F.
  2. Put both in the same place, ideally the same well, with compound. Do not compare a strap-on to a reference in air.
  3. Let both settle for several time constants and keep conditions steady.
  4. Read the controller and the reference together. A difference larger than the accuracy requirement means the sensor, the mounting or the input scaling needs attention.
  5. For a cold check, use an ice bath. Measurement Canada's procedure uses finely crushed ice from distilled water packed in an insulated flask with excess water drained, a 15 minute equilibration, the sensing portion at least 7 cm under the surface, and 3 to 4 cm off the bottom. Typical uncertainty is about 0.01 C. A shop version with tap water will be less exact. ASTM D7962 is a standard on ice-bath immersion depth and drift checks, not on accuracy.

If the difference stays after proper mounting and compound, then test the sensor with a meter using the thermistor lesson.

Field card: HVAC Temperature Sensor Mounting

Good contact, insulation, and a spot that sees the real air.

  • Well: fill the air gap with heat transfer compound, seat the tip at the bottom, renew compound after any removal.
  • Strap-on: clean pipe, top of pipe, mastic and tie, insulate 6 to 12 in. each side.
  • Layered air (mixed air, coil leaving): use an averaging element, not a single probe.
  • Averaging sizing in one university standard: 1 ft of element per 3 sq ft of face.
  • Outdoor: north side, shaded, 4 ft up, not over hot surfaces.
  • Room: near return, not on an exterior wall, away from heat sources.
  • One time constant gives 63.2%; wait three or more before reading.
  • Check against a reference in the same well, after settling.
