HVAC for Secure Rooms: Ducts, Bars, Sound and Tech Access

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some rooms in offices and government buildings are built so that what is said inside stays inside: high-security conference rooms, records vaults, server cages. The walls get most of the attention, but a duct or pipe is a hole in the wall. This lesson explains how HVAC is shaped around that fact, what a tech should expect on the way in, and how to do a simple duct sound check of your own.

Keep it general, and ask the owner

Check the site rules. Every secure room has its own written rules from the building owner and its security officer. This page is general, public-level education built from public design documents. It does not tell you what any one room requires, and it is not legal or security advice. If your job touches a secure room, the security officer's instructions win over anything here.

Why a duct is a weak spot

Sound travels through air, and a duct is a smooth, hard tube that carries it a long way with little loss. A public federal facilities design presentation for secure spaces says that all vents and ducts must be protected to meet the acoustic requirements. It names two common tools: sound baffles, also called duct silencers, and special duct penetrations. It also says fan sizing must be coordinated with the mechanical engineer, because a silencer adds resistance the fan must overcome. That presentation states the aim plainly: the acoustic measures are meant to keep occupants from being inadvertently overheard, not to defeat deliberate technical listening.

The same document says acoustical duct liner is not allowed and calls for double-wall acoustic duct instead, with a barrier layer so insulation fibers and contaminants stay out of the airstream. Techs who know ordinary lined duct should not assume it is acceptable here.

Penetrations: few, sealed, and barred

  • Keep them to a minimum. The presentation says penetrations of the secure perimeter must be kept to a minimum. Utilities should enter at a single point where they can, and utilities serving areas outside the room should not cross the perimeter without a mitigation measure.
  • Bars or baffles on big openings. It says vent or duct openings larger than 96 square inches that cross the perimeter get permanently fixed bars, grills, metal baffles or waveguides. If one dimension of the opening is under 6 inches, bars are not required. For reference, a 12 inch by 10 inch duct is 120 square inches, so it would be covered; a 12 by 8 is exactly 96, which is not over the limit.
  • A non-conductive break. Ducts and pipes get a short flexible, non-conductive section at the wall, using a material suited to the climate.
  • An inspection port. Where bars or baffles are used, the document calls for a 12 inch by 12 inch access panel in the bottom of the duct inside the perimeter. If the area outside is controlled, the port may sit outside the perimeter, secured with a high-security lock.
  • Sealing. All utility penetrations are sealed to limit sound and covert entry, and sound-rated walls are sealed on both sides at pipe and conduit penetrations. The document says to use non-hardening caulk and not to over-pack fibrous insulation; spray foam or hardening insulation can make the sound performance worse. A hole you enlarge or an escutcheon you leave off can undo a wall.

Fire is a separate issue. Many of these walls are also fire rated, so firestopping rules apply too. See the firestopping lesson and do not disturb a listed system.

Sound masking

The same presentation says sound masking may be used when normal construction and baffling are judged inadequate, and that masking devices can be provided at perimeter penetrations such as doors and duct penetrations. In plain terms, a quiet steady sound is added near the weak spot so faint speech is harder to pick out. For a tech this mostly means: do not remove, move or cover masking devices, and expect the security officer to want the room's sound tested again after you change a duct.

Dedicated cooling and why it helps

Our practical view, not a quoted rule: when the room has its own cooling (a small split system, a dedicated fan coil or a dedicated air handler), the equipment that needs routine service can often sit outside the secure space. The unit, filters, condensate drain and controls are then reachable without opening the room to outsiders. Only the supply, return and refrigerant or water lines cross the wall. Shared building ducts that run through a secure room and on to other spaces are the opposite case: they give sound and people two ways in. The source document says ducts, conduits or pipes serving other areas should not cross the perimeter without mitigation. Heat load still matters; for a server cage, see cooling a small server room.

Working in or near the room

  • Escort and badge. Expect to be badged, logged and escorted by the room's own staff. Plan the visit so parts, tools and ladders are counted in and out.
  • Phones and cameras. The public document says cell phones may be prohibited, that personal electronic devices are not allowed in the secure area, and that no cameras are allowed inside the perimeter. Leave your phone where you are told, and ask before you pull out a meter that records or connects wirelessly. If you need a record, write it by hand and ask what may be kept.
  • Alarmed access panels. Panels and ports may be locked or monitored. We did not find a public rule that says every access panel is alarmed, so treat it as a site question: ask whether opening a panel trips an alarm before you touch it.
  • Schedule with the security officer. Give the officer the work scope, the names of the techs, the tools and what will be opened, and agree who escorts, when the alarms are disabled and re-armed, and whether the room needs a sound re-test afterward.

Our own worked example: sound down a duct path

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not catalog data. Real silencer and elbow values come from tested submittals; one university silencer specification we read says silencers are rated for dynamic insertion loss under a standard duct-to-reverberant-room test at several airflows, so use the real data for any real job. For one band of sound, losses along a single path in decibels are subtracted one after another.

Assumptions. Speech level at the return grille inside the room: 65 dB. Target at the first outside tap: 30 dB or less. Path losses: first elbow 3 dB, a silencer, 20 ft of lined duct at 0.3 dB per foot, a second elbow 3 dB, and a branch takeoff or end reflection of 3 dB.

Try 1, silencer 18 dB. Total loss: 3 + 18 + (20 × 0.3 = 6) + 3 + 3 = 33 dB. Level outside: 65 − 33 = 32 dB. That is above 30, so it fails by 2 dB.

Try 2, silencer 24 dB. Total loss: 3 + 24 + 6 + 3 + 3 = 39 dB. Level outside: 65 − 39 = 26 dB. That meets the 30 dB target with 4 dB to spare.

Check. Running levels: 65 − 3 = 62; 62 − 24 = 38; 38 − 6 = 32; 32 − 3 = 29; 29 − 3 = 26. It matches the subtraction. The result is only as good as the assumptions: this ignores sound that leaks through duct walls, other paths such as ceilings and doors, and the extra resistance and self-noise the silencer adds. Note the lined duct in this example is for our arithmetic only; the source document says plain acoustical liner is not allowed in these spaces, so a real design would use a different duct type with different numbers.

For more on silencers and noise ratings, see HVAC noise in commercial buildings and recording studio HVAC.

Field card: HVAC for Secure Rooms: Ducts, Bars, Sound and Tech Access

A duct is a hole in the wall. Treat it like one.

  • Keep wall penetrations few, sealed on both sides, with a flexible non-conductive break.
  • Duct openings over 96 square inches get bars or baffles; under 6 inches in one dimension, none.
  • Silencers add resistance; the fan must be checked.
  • Path losses in dB subtract from the source level, one after another.
  • Do not remove masking devices or reseal with the wrong material.
  • Expect escorts, no phones or cameras, and locked or alarmed panels.
  • Plan every visit with the security officer first.
