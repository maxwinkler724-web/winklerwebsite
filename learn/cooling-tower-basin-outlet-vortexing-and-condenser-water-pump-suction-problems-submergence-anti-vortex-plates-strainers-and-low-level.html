Cooling Tower Basin Outlet Vortexing and Condenser Water Pump Suction Problems: Submergence, Anti-Vortex Plates, Strainers and Low Level

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condenser water pump can be perfectly sized and still sound like it is pumping gravel, because the trouble starts before the water reaches it. In an open tower the pump sucks from a basin, and if the water over the outlet is too shallow, the outlet makes a whirlpool that pulls air into the suction line. This lesson covers how that happens, what the fixes are, and what you can check with a tape measure and two gauges.

What a vortex at the basin outlet is

Water leaving a basin has to turn and speed up toward the outlet. Any sideways motion in the basin gets stretched into a spin as the flow converges, the same way a bathtub drain makes a whirlpool. A U.S. Bureau of Reclamation report on intake vortices says the main cause is uneven approach flow to the intake, and it points to the intake Froude number, a ratio of the water speed to the depth, as the main driver of how strong a vortex gets (source 1). The same report lists six vortex types. The mild ones are only a surface swirl or a small dimple. The two worst are a vortex that now and then pulls in air, and a fully developed air core (source 1).

Air is the problem for the pump. A trade article on open tower pump piping says pumps want water free of swirl and free of air, and it points out that tower water already carries a lot of air (source 6). A vortex that reaches the outlet sends slugs of that air down the suction pipe. A trade article on basin levels states the plain result: when the level is low, the pumps suck in air (source 7).

The picture

Submergence: how much water over the outlet

Submergence is the depth of water above the outlet. Researchers have proposed several rules for the minimum. One Bureau of Reclamation summary credits a conservative design relation to a 1987 paper: S = D(1 + 2.3 Fr), where D is the diameter and Fr is the Froude number at the opening (source 1). A university symposium paper gives the same relation as S/D = 1 + 2.3 FD and attributes it to the ANSI/HI 9.8 pump intake standard, with FD = V / √(gD), where V is the mean velocity and g is gravity (source 2). Use the current edition of that standard if a real design depends on it (source 8).

The takeaway is that required depth climbs with velocity. A trade article says the required level goes up as velocity rises, and that a tower pan rarely has the roughly 5 feet of water over the opening that it quotes as a rule of thumb (source 6). A university design standard for towers asks for at least 4 feet of suction head, or more if the pump needs it (source 5). The Reclamation report adds a caution: there is virtually no depth at which a designer can be certain of vortex-free operation (source 1). Treat the numbers as a screening check, not a guarantee.

Worked example (our own numbers)

We assume an 8 inch schedule 40 outlet with an inside diameter of 7.981 inches, a flow of 900 gpm, and the relation above used as a rough screen, even though a side outlet in a basin is not a bellmouth.

  1. Convert flow: 900 gpm ÷ 448.8 = 2.005 cfs.
  2. Pipe area: π/4 × (7.981 ÷ 12)2 = 0.3474 ft2. Diameter D = 0.665 ft.
  3. Velocity: V = 2.005 ÷ 0.3474 = 5.77 ft/s. The shortcut V = 0.4085 × gpm ÷ d2 gives the same 5.77.
  4. Froude number: FD = 5.77 ÷ √(32.17 × 0.665) = 5.77 ÷ 4.63 = 1.25.
  5. Submergence: S = 0.665 × (1 + 2.3 × 1.25) = 0.665 × 3.87 = 2.57 ft, about 31 inches.

Now compare with the basin. Assume the normal level is 30 inches above the outlet centerline. That is just under the 31 inches the screen asks for, so the outlet is marginal on a good day. Assume a stuck makeup float lets the level fall to 18 inches. That is far short, and a vortex is likely. Swap in a 12 inch outlet (inside diameter 11.938 inches) at the same flow and the same steps give V = 2.58 ft/s, FD = 0.46 and S = 2.04 ft, about 24.5 inches. The bigger opening needs 6 inches less water. The trade article makes the same point: an 8 inch outlet is more comfortable than a 6 inch one at the same flow, where its example runs at 7.55 ft/s (source 6). That article also notes a standard limit of 8 ft/s for suction from open tanks and suggests staying around 5 to 6 ft/s (source 6). Our 5.77 ft/s sits inside that window.

Anti-vortex plates and other fixes

The same trade article suggests asking the tower maker for an anti-vortex baffle, which it also calls a doghouse, and says the plate helps with the submergence problem (source 6). The idea is the one the Reclamation report gives for intakes in general: break up the spin, give it a longer path to the surface, or lower the velocity with a larger opening (source 1). A university paper tested a floating grid that splits one large vortex into many small ones and let a test pump run at a smaller depth before it failed the swirl limit (source 2). Those are laboratory results for intakes that are not tower basins, so we do not carry the numbers over. A Corps of Engineers hydraulics report is a reminder that a design tested at one geometry is only good for that geometry; changing the internal dimensions can spoil it (source 3). For a tower, the maker's plate for your model is the one to rely on.

Other fixes follow the arithmetic: keep the level up, use a larger outlet, or add a drop-down sump. A university tower standard asks for an outlet sump sized in depth to prevent cavitation and air entrapment in the pump (source 4).

Strainers and screens

Two university tower standards describe a removable or lift-out basin strainer with openings smaller than the nozzle holes (sources 4 and 5). It is there to protect the nozzles, but it also sits right at the pump's feed, so when leaves and scale pack it, the pump sees a restriction. The trade article on tower pump piping recommends strainers of 20 mesh or coarser, with finer straining kept in front of the condenser, and warns of a high pressure drop across a plugged one (source 6). That ties back to net positive suction head. See Pump NPSH Available vs Required for the numbers, and Pump Cavitation Basics for what the damage looks like.

Low basin level: makeup valve and float faults

A basin loses water to evaporation, drift and blowdown, and makeup water replaces it. The 2013 basin-level article says most towers use a float valve, that float valves can fail, and that the typical failure it names is sticking open, which overfills the basin (source 7). The reverse failure, a valve that sticks shut or a clogged inlet, is what starves the pump, and you can see it as a slow level drop. Two university standards pair a float valve or float switch with a solenoid makeup valve and tell the installer to adjust the level control to the operating level at startup (sources 4 and 5). With several cells, an equalizer line shares water between basins, and a cell that runs low can starve a shared pump; see Multi-Cell Cooling Tower Piping.

Signs at the pump

Air in the suction looks different from classic cavitation, though the two overlap. In our own field experience, which is not from a source, look for a rattling or gravel noise, motor amps that wander instead of holding steady, and a suction or discharge gauge needle that flutters. The pump-cavitation lesson lists the noise, vibration, lost performance and damage that follow (see Pump Cavitation Basics). We could not source a specific amp swing or gauge swing for air entrainment, so we give no number; compare against the readings you took when the system was healthy. Strainer trouble usually behaves differently, in our experience: the suction reading drops and stays low rather than flickering. Where the pump has a suction diffuser or a strainer ahead of it, the diffuser lesson shows how to read the strainer drop.

Field checks, in order

  1. Look at the basin level against the outlet. Measure the water over the outlet, with the pump running. The 2013 article says to check basin levels daily (source 7).
  2. Look at the surface above the outlet. A steady dimple or a spinning funnel means you are near the limit. A funnel that reaches the outlet is the air path.
  3. Check the makeup valve and float. Does it open as the level drops, and is the level control set where the maker or designer said? That same article suggests a meter on the makeup line to spot trends (source 7).
  4. Check the basin strainer. Look for debris, and compare with a known clean condition.
  5. Read the pump suction gauge at the same flow, and compare with your commissioning value. A steady drop points to a strainer or level problem; a swinging needle points to air.

Follow your site's lockout and confined space procedures before reaching into a basin or removing a strainer.

Field card: tower basin outlet and pump suction

Shallow water over the outlet makes a vortex, and the vortex makes air at the pump.

  • Required depth over the outlet rises with outlet velocity; a bigger outlet needs less water.
  • Screening relation: S = D(1 + 2.3 FD), FD = V / √(gD). Check the current standard before using it.
  • Our example: 8 inch outlet at 900 gpm gives 5.77 ft/s and about 31 inches; 12 inch gives 2.58 ft/s and about 24.5 inches.
  • Ask the tower maker for the anti-vortex plate for your model.
  • Check, in order: level over outlet, surface above outlet, makeup valve and float, basin strainer, pump suction gauge.
  • Steady low suction points to strainer or level; a swinging needle points to air.
