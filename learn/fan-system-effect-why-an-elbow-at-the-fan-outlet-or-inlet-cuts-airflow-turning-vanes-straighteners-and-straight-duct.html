Fan System Effect: Why a Fan Delivers Less Air Than Its Curve

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A fan's catalog curve was measured with smooth, straight airflow going in and coming out. Real installations rarely look like that. When an elbow sits right on the outlet, a plenum is cramped or the inlet air arrives spinning, the fan moves less air than the curve promises. That gap is called system effect, and it is one of the most common reasons a correctly selected fan fails its airflow check.

What system effect is

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) fan sourcebook defines system effect as the change in system performance that results from the interaction of system components. It explains the cause: non-uniform airflow profiles develop swirls and vortices, so components show losses higher than their published loss coefficients. The fan is not weaker. The system around it is worse than the one the fan was tested against, so the system curve moves up. The same sourcebook says an elbow placed close to a fan outlet can cut delivered flow by up to 30 percent, and that recovering it may take a higher fan speed, which raises power and lowers efficiency.

Where it comes from

  • Elbow at the outlet. A fan leaves a lopsided velocity profile at its discharge. Straight duct lets that profile even out. An elbow right at the outlet forces the uneven stream through a turn before it has settled, so the turn loses far more pressure than a catalog fitting loss suggests.
  • Elbow or box at the inlet. An inlet bend or a wall close to the inlet feeds the wheel unevenly. Air enters one side of the wheel faster than the other, or arrives with a spin. A spin in the same direction as the wheel lowers the pressure the fan can build, and a spin against it does the reverse and can raise power draw. This is our own plain-language summary of why inlet swirl matters; the DOE sourcebook describes swirl and non-uniform flow as the general cause.
  • Cramped plenum or cabinet. An unhoused fan needs room around the wheel so air can reach the inlet from every side. Walls, coils or partitions close to the wheel starve it the same way a bad bend does.

How it shows up as a lost-pressure allowance

Designers handle system effect as an extra pressure loss added to the system calculation before the fan is chosen. The DOE sourcebook describes the corrected result as a higher curve labeled "system curve (with system effect)." In practice that means picking a fan for the clean duct losses plus an allowance for each poor inlet or outlet condition. Skip the allowance and the fan, once installed, meets a higher system curve than the one it was selected for. The next section puts numbers on that.

The straight-duct rule of thumb

The usual advice is to keep several duct diameters of straight duct on the outlet before the first elbow or branch, and to put the first fitting well away from the inlet. The exact length depends on fan type and outlet velocity, and the sources we opened do not give a figure. Our own rule of thumb, not from a source: treat anything under about three equivalent duct diameters of straight run after a fan outlet as a likely system effect problem, and look up the fan maker's installation data for the real number. Faster outlet velocity needs more straight run, not less.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This example is our own, with invented inputs. We use q for airflow in thousands of cfm and pressure in inches of water gauge.

Assumptions. The fan curve is P = 3.2 − 0.012 q². The clean system curve is P = 0.02 q². The fan law for a fixed duct says pressure loss varies with flow squared, so each system curve is a constant times q². We assume a short elbow at the outlet adds a system effect loss of 0.50 in. at 10 kcfm, so it adds 0.50 / 10² = 0.005 q².

  1. Clean operating point (A). Set 3.2 − 0.012 q² = 0.02 q². Then 3.2 = 0.032 q², so q² = 100 and q = 10.0 (10,000 cfm). Pressure = 0.02 × 100 = 2.00 in.
  2. Penalized system curve. 0.02 q² + 0.005 q² = 0.025 q².
  3. New operating point (B). 3.2 = 0.012 q² + 0.025 q² = 0.037 q², so q² = 86.49 and q = 9.30 (9,300 cfm). Pressure = 0.025 × 86.49 = 2.16 in. Check on the fan curve: 3.2 − 0.012 × 86.49 = 2.16 in. It matches.
  4. Airflow lost. (10.0 − 9.30) / 10.0 = 0.07, so 7 percent, or 700 cfm.
  5. Check the allowance with the fan law. At 9.30 kcfm the system effect loss is 0.50 × (9.30 / 10)² = 0.43 in., and the clean duct loss is 0.02 × 86.49 = 1.73 in. Together: 2.16 in.

Notice that fan pressure went up slightly (2.00 to 2.16 in.) while airflow fell. A static-pressure reading alone would not warn you; the airflow is what is short. Now suppose we try to win the 10,000 cfm back by speeding the fan up. At 10 kcfm the penalized system needs 0.025 × 100 = 2.50 in. If speed ratio s scales the fan curve to P = 3.2 s² − 0.012 q², then 3.2 s² − 1.2 = 2.50, so s² = 1.156 and s = 1.075, about 7.5 percent more speed. Power rises roughly with the cube of speed (1.075³ = 1.24), so about 24 percent more input power, ignoring any change in efficiency. A 0.50 in. allowance costs 7 percent airflow, or 24 percent more power to get it back. Real fan curves are not this tidy; the point is the pattern.

Field symptoms

  • Measured airflow (a duct traverse) is short of design, yet the fan runs at its rated speed.
  • Static pressure across the fan looks normal or a little high, so the fan seems healthy.
  • Rooms at the end of the system are starved while the nearest ones are fine.
  • Turbulence noise or rumble near the fan fitting, or a duct that pulses at the elbow. These are our own field observations, not a measured rule.
  • The fan has to run faster than the schedule predicted to hit design flow, so power and noise are higher.

Fixes

  • Move the elbow. The cleanest fix is more straight duct between the fan and the first fitting. Where space allows, relocate the bend or lengthen the straight run (our own advice).
  • Turning vanes. The DOE sourcebook lists turning vanes as typically used on short-radius elbows. They guide the air around the turn so less of the profile is thrown to one side.
  • Flow straighteners. The sourcebook says that when the layout puts an elbow just upstream or downstream of a fan, some version of a flow straightener should be considered to improve operating efficiency. At an inlet, a straightener or a larger, gentler transition helps cancel swirl.
  • Give the wheel room. In a cramped plenum, open up clearance around the inlet or even out the cell the fan sits in.
  • Re-select the fan. If the layout cannot change, choose the fan for the higher system curve, as in the example above, rather than speeding up an undersized fan.

Field card: Fan System Effect

Catalog curves assume straight, even airflow. Bends at the fan break that assumption.

  • System effect moves the system curve up, so the operating point slides left on the fan curve.
  • DOE: an elbow close to the outlet can cut delivered flow by up to 30 percent.
  • Our own example: 0.50 in. allowance cost 7 percent airflow (10,000 to 9,300 cfm).
  • Winning it back by speed costs power roughly with speed cubed (about 24 percent in our example).
  • Fixes: more straight duct, turning vanes, straighteners, clearance at the inlet, or a fan selected for the real system.
