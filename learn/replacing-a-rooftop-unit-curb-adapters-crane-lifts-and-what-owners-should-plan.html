Replacing a Rooftop Unit: Curb Adapters, Crane Lifts, and What Owners Should Plan

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A rooftop unit swap looks simple from the parking lot: a crane lifts the old box off and sets a new one down. Most of the work happens before that morning, and most surprises come from the curb, the weight and the roof under it. This lesson walks through what an owner or a new tech should understand when a packaged rooftop unit is replaced.

Start with the curb

The roof curb is the steel frame that the unit sits on. It is flashed into the roof, and the supply and return ducts drop through it. If a new unit has the same footprint and duct openings as the old one, it may set right on the existing curb. Often it does not. Models change over the years, and a different brand rarely matches.

There are two usual answers. One is a new curb, which means opening the roof around it. The other is a curb adapter: a fabricated metal frame that sits on the old curb and presents the right shape to the new unit. A roofing trade article describes adapters as custom pieces, usually galvanized steel, made so the existing roof opening can be reused. Lennox describes its adapters as fully welded, insulated galvanized steel.

One parts seller of custom adapters says it needs the model and dimensions of both units, drafts drawings for approval, and lists 7 to 10 business days to ship, not counting the drawing step. Several points from maker and trade literature are worth knowing:

  • Adapters are designed from unit literature, not from the roof. MicroMetl's installation instructions say its adapters are built from the HVAC unit's literature, field changes may be needed, and the maker should be called if site dimensions differ. Someone still has to measure the old curb.
  • Overhang and mismatched openings happen. A distributor quote in one public project file warned of about 25 inches of overhang and supply and return openings that would not line up exactly, and asked the buyer to verify existing dimensions.
  • Adapters add air resistance. MicroMetl says adapters increase external static pressure, and that has to be counted in the unit's airflow figures.
  • Seal and fasten it. Trane's Quick Adapt curb instructions call for a new gasket between the old curb and the adapter, sealed duct transitions, and a second gasket under the unit. The adapter is fastened to the old curb through its perimeter flange. Trane warns that an unfastened adapter can collapse under the unit's weight and says not to modify the adapter in the field.

Weight and the roof

Maker documents list an operating weight for each model. Both the old and new numbers matter, and so does the adapter. MicroMetl's instructions say to verify that the existing curb and roof structure can carry the adapter and new unit, and to reinforce as required, because the new unit may be heavier than the old one. That is a job for a structural engineer. A tech or owner can ask for it and read the result, but should not guess.

The rules for who must check are local. Oregon's Building Codes Division, for example, says a structural permit is not needed for rooftop equipment replacement only when seven conditions are met. They include a weight increase of no more than 10 percent or 100 pounds, whichever is greater, the same location, and mounting on the existing curb. The same interpretation says a curb adapter or a different-size curb requires a structural permit. Your jurisdiction may differ, so ask the local building department early.

Worked example: old unit, new unit, adapter

A 2024 engineering calculation package posted in a city permit file covered replacing four identical rooftop units. Its table, built from the maker submittals for the units, lists these weights per unit. The adapter weight is the package's own figure; the file does not say where it came from.

ItemWeight (lb)
Old unit2,017
New unit2,622
Curb adapter394

The existing curb stays, so it is not added. Step by step:

  1. New unit alone against old unit: 2,622 − 2,017 = 605 lb. As a share of the old weight: 605 ÷ 2,017 = 0.30, or about 30 percent.
  2. New load on the old curb, adapter included: 2,622 + 394 = 3,016 lb.
  3. Change in roof load per unit: 3,016 − 2,017 = 999 lb. As a share: 999 ÷ 2,017 = 0.495, or about 49.5 percent.
  4. The adapter's part of the new load: 394 ÷ 3,016 = 0.131, about 13 percent.
  5. Four units: 4 × 999 = 3,996 lb added to the roof across the building.

Now compare with Oregon's rule from above. Ten percent of 2,017 is 202 lb, which is greater than 100, so the allowed gain would be about 202 lb. The actual gain of 999 lb is far over it, so that kind of job would call for structural review. In this project the engineer did that review. The package says the roof joists were within their allowable loading, with demand-to-allowable ratios of about 61 percent in bending and 83 percent in shear for the governing case. That result belongs to that building.

Rigging and the crane

Every maker prints its own lifting instructions, and they differ. Carrier's 50EJQ 20 and 25 ton manual calls for four lifting lugs on the base rails, four cables, and two 92 inch spreader bars, with the eyehook 74 inches above the unit top. It says all panels must be in place and that the unit is not meant for fork truck handling. Its corner weights show why balance matters: the corners are listed at 843, 859, 1,245 and 1,222 lb, so one end carries far more than the other. A smaller Carrier 48LC manual says spreader bars are not required, and it directs the rigger to hook shackles through the base rail holes. Always use the manual for the exact model and the rigging label on the unit.

OSHA's rigging rule for construction (29 CFR 1926.251) says rigging gets inspected before each shift, loads may not exceed the equipment's rated capacity, and damaged slings come out of service. A trade blog adds that crane work calls for rigging and signaling knowledge, and that load weight, reach, wind and a spreader bar all enter the choice of crane. Crane placement should avoid drains, soft ground and power lines.

Gas, power and refrigerant

  • Electrical. A trade blog says existing wires and breakers must meet the new unit's minimum circuit ampacity (MCA) and maximum overcurrent protection (MOCP), and wiring may need to reach a new entry point. Carrier's manual calls for a disconnect sized to MOCP and wire sized by MCA from the nameplate, so check the new nameplate against the old feed. See Electrical Basics for HVAC.
  • Gas. Carrier's 48LC manual points to the National Fuel Gas Code (NFPA 54) or local code. It calls for a drip leg, a manual shutoff within 6 feet of the unit, and a ground-joint union. For pressure tests above 0.5 psig, the supply pipe must be disconnected from the unit gas valve. Leak check with soapy water, never a flame. See RTU Gas Heat Section Basics.
  • Old refrigerant. The refrigerant in the old unit must be recovered before it leaves the roof. Trane's instructions remind technicians that Section 608 of the Clean Air Act applies in the U.S. The steps are in Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step.
  • Permits. Many places require a mechanical permit for a replacement, and, as shown, possibly a structural one. Ask your building department. The Oregon example asks for maker specifications for both units.

Economizer and controls

Do not assume the new unit's air side matches the old one. A trade blog says units must be sized for code-required outdoor air and that economizers, exhaust fans and make-up air can affect intake, mixing and pressure. Energy rules also change. York's article on California Title 24 says units of 4.5 tons or more need a system that can monitor and report economizer faults. That is one state's rule, but it shows why the order sheet should list the control options. For how economizers fail, see RTU Economizer Fault Diagnosis.

Scheduling

Plan the lead time of any custom adapter first, then the crane, then the hours. A trade blog lists wind as a factor in crane choice, so the lift date needs a weather backup. The 50EJQ manual adds 250 lb for power exhaust and 220 lb for a copper coil, so use the real option list for the lift plan. Ask the contractor how long the building will be without cooling or heat, and when the old unit's gas and power will be cut.

After the lift

A set unit is not a finished job. Gaskets, fasteners, drain, wiring and rotation must be checked before the unit is released. Use the maker's startup form and see RTU Startup and Checkout Basics. Afterward, put it on a schedule from the preventive maintenance checklist.

Field card: Replacing a Rooftop Unit

New weight = new unit + adapter. Compare it with the old unit.

  • Get old and new maker weights, plus options and the adapter.
  • Roof load change = (new unit + adapter) − old unit. Example: 3,016 − 2,017 = 999 lb.
  • A structural engineer checks the roof; ask the local building department about permits.
  • Follow the rigging label and manual for that model. Spreader bar needs differ by maker.
  • Match MCA and MOCP to the old feed; follow code for gas piping.
  • Recover the old refrigerant first.
  • Order custom adapters early, and plan for wind.
