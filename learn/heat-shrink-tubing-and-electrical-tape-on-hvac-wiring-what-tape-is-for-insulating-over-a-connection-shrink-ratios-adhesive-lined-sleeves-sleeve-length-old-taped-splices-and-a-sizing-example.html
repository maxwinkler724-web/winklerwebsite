Heat-Shrink Tubing and Electrical Tape on HVAC Wiring

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every service van has a roll of tape and a bag of shrink sleeves, and both get misused. Tape and sleeves are insulation. They are not a connection. This lesson covers what each one does, how to pick a shrink size that actually fits, how to heat it without cooking its neighbors, and when a splice should not be out in the open at all.

Insulation is not the connection

A splice has two jobs. First, the conductors must be joined mechanically and electrically with a proper device. Second, the joint must be covered so nothing can touch it. The federal workplace safety agency's general industry electrical rule separates these jobs: conductors are joined with splicing devices approved for the use (or by brazing, welding or soldering), and then all splices, joints and free conductor ends are covered with insulation equivalent to that of the conductors, or with an approved insulating device. Tape or a sleeve belongs in the second step. It never replaces the first one. If two bare wires are only twisted together and wrapped, the wrap is hiding a bad joint, not fixing it. For the first step, see our wire terminations lesson.

What tape is for, and what it is not

Electrical tape is good for covering a made connection that is going to live in a dry, protected place, for bundling, for marking, and for temporary protection. It is not a repair for damaged cords. A university electrical safety fact sheet we read states plainly that electrical tape may not be used to repair worn or frayed cords, because the outer jacket is what protects the inner conductors. The federal workplace safety agency has written in an interpretation letter that a damaged section of an extension cord can be cut out and the two ends fitted with a cord connector and an attachment plug instead of being spliced.

Tape also has limits we see in the field. This is our own field experience, not a measured number: tape on an outdoor unit that sits in sun and heat can get gummy, shrink back from its edges, or turn brittle and crack. A wrap that was neat on day one can be unwinding by the next cooling season. If a joint is going to see weather, tape alone is the wrong tool.

How heat-shrink works and what the ratio means

Shrink tubing is made, stretched while warm, and cooled so it holds the larger size. A university physics help page explains that the tubing keeps cross-links that pull it back toward its original narrow size when heated. It is commonly supplied expanded to about twice its recovered diameter, and over-expanded types reach 3:1, 4:1 or even 6:1. As the diameter drops, the wall gets thicker, while the length barely changes. A federal space agency workmanship standard for flight hardware lists a 2:1 ratio for common polyolefin sleeving and says other materials have different ratios, so the data sheet for the sleeve in your hand is the final word.

Read a ratio like this: a 3:1 sleeve supplied at 0.375 in inside diameter will shrink down to about one third of that, 0.125 in, if nothing is inside it. With a joint inside, it stops at the joint and the wall thickens around it.

Picking a size: two tests

The space agency standard gives the logic. The sleeve must be larger than the largest diameter it covers before shrinking, and after shrinking it must fit tightly over that largest area. Where a tight fit is needed at the smallest diameter too, it notes extra sleeving may be needed. We turn that into two tests:

  1. Slides on: supplied inside diameter is larger than the widest part of the joint, with a little clearance.
  2. Grips off: recovered inside diameter is smaller than the smallest thing the sleeve has to seal against, usually the single wire insulation at the thin end.

The ratio is what lets one sleeve pass both tests. A higher ratio gives a bigger gap between the two.

Worked example (our own): choosing a size and length

These are our own assumptions for practice, not data for any product. Treat every number below as an assumption.

  • A crimped butt splice has two insulated wires entering one end. Together they measure 0.30 in at the widest point.
  • The single wire at the other end has insulation 0.15 in across, the thinnest part the sleeve must seal against.
  • We want at least 0.05 in of sliding clearance over the widest part (our own working margin).
  • The sleeves on the bench are assumed to be a 2:1 type and a 3:1 type, each in a few supplied inside diameters.

Step 1, slide-on: supplied inside diameter must be at least 0.30 + 0.05 = 0.35 in. That rules out any sleeve supplied at 0.25 in or less.

Step 2, grip-off, 2:1 candidate supplied at 0.375 in: recovered diameter is 0.375 / 2 = 0.1875 in. That is larger than 0.15 in, so it would stay loose on the thin wire. It fails.

Step 3, grip-off, 3:1 candidate supplied at 0.50 in: recovered diameter is 0.50 / 3 = 0.167 in. Still larger than 0.15 in. It fails.

Step 4, grip-off, 3:1 candidate supplied at 0.375 in: recovered diameter is 0.375 / 3 = 0.125 in. That is smaller than 0.15 in, and 0.375 is at least 0.35 in, so it passes both tests. This is the sleeve to use. It slides over the 0.30 in joint with 0.075 in to spare, then shrinks down onto the 0.15 in wire, ending up a snug fit on the thin side and a firm fit over the barrel.

Step 5, length: the space agency standard asks that sleeving extend past the wire insulation by at least twice the larger wire diameter. Using the 0.30 in bundle, we take 2 × 0.30 = 0.60 in past each insulation end. If the barrel is assumed to be 1.0 in long, the length is 0.60 + 1.0 + 0.60 = 2.2 in. Because shrink tubing barely changes length, we would cut or buy about 2.25 in. That standard is for flight hardware, so we use it as a starting point, and the sleeve maker's data sheet and the equipment instructions come first.

Adhesive-lined sleeves and wet outdoor splices

Plain shrink tubing grips but does not seal. Water can still creep along the wire. The space agency standard describes a solder sleeve as a heat-shrinkable device with meltable sealing preforms at its ends, which is the same idea as an adhesive liner: a layer inside that softens and flows to close the gap. For a joint that has to live in rain, snow melt and condensation, we choose an adhesive-lined sleeve and follow the maker's data sheet for the right ratio, since the liner needs the sleeve to shrink down onto the wires to flow. Even then, an outdoor splice is a last resort, covered in the box section below.

Heating without scorching the neighbors

  • Use a heat gun or the heat source the sleeve calls for. The space agency standard lists a hot-air gun, oven or radiant heat, with heat level and time depending on sleeve size and type.
  • Shield what can be damaged. The same standard says nearby temperature-sensitive materials must be protected from direct heat and that a splice should not be made where adjacent parts cannot be shielded. In a condensing unit that means refrigerant line insulation, other conductors, plastic wire guides and capacitor labels. A folded piece of sheet metal or a heat shield works.
  • Work from the middle out. We start at the center of the sleeve and move toward each end so trapped air is pushed out. The space agency text calls for shrinking the gripping section first on molded boots.
  • Judge the result. The standard says there should be no partly shrunk areas, and no cracks, punctures, blisters or burned areas. Charred or blistered sleeving is a rework item.
  • Never use an open flame near a gas appliance or refrigerant. Heat the sleeve with a heat gun, away from anything that burns.

The federal workplace safety agency's rule on electrical equipment also says equipment must not have damaged parts such as broken, cut, corroded or overheated ones. A scorched wire jacket from careless heating is exactly that. Cut it back and redo it.

UV and heat ratings on a condensing unit

Look at the printing on the tape or sleeve package, or the maker's data sheet. Two ratings matter on an outdoor unit: the temperature range the material is rated for, and whether it is rated for sunlight exposure. Cabinet interiors and conductors near the compressor run hot, and anything near the top of the unit sees full sun. If a rating is not stated, we treat the product as indoor only. This is our own rule of thumb. Also see how UV-black ties are chosen for the same reason.

Inspecting old taped splices

Turn off and lock out the power and verify it is dead before touching any old splice. Then look for:

  • tape that is unwrapped at the ends, gummy, cracked or stiff;
  • discolored or brown insulation near the joint, which can mean the joint has been running hot (The federal workplace safety agency's workmanship rule counts overheated parts as damage);
  • a twisted-only connection hiding under the wrap, with no connector or crimp;
  • green or white crust on copper, a sign that water has been getting in;
  • a splice sitting loose in the open inside a unit, or in a junction that was never boxed.

Our practice: if the joint is a proper connector in good shape and only the cover is bad, recover it with a correctly sized sleeve. If the joint itself is questionable, cut back to clean copper and make it again with a proper connection. Wiring that is suspect belongs to a qualified electrician if it is power wiring beyond your own work scope.

When a splice belongs in a box

A state electrical regulator's wiring handout says all splices must be made in a junction box and that junction boxes must remain accessible, citing NEC sections 300.15 and 314.16 for fill. In practice, tape or shrink over a joint that is out in the open is a covered splice, not a boxed one. We put a power splice in a box with a cover, with proper fittings and room to spare, rather than wrapping it and leaving it hanging in a unit or an outdoor whip. Low-voltage control cable splices follow the rules covered in our control cable lesson; check the current NEC and your local adopted code for the exact wording. For outdoor whips and wet-location fittings see our condenser whip lesson, and note what you changed using the habits in our labeling lesson.

Field card: Heat-Shrink and Tape on HVAC Wiring

Cover a good connection. Never cover a bad one.

  • Tape and shrink are insulation. The connector or crimp is the connection.
  • Tape is not a repair for a damaged cord or jacket.
  • Slides on: supplied diameter greater than the widest part plus clearance. Grips off: recovered diameter less than the thinnest part.
  • Recovered diameter = supplied diameter divided by the ratio.
  • Sleeve length: joint plus extra past each end; length barely changes on shrink.
  • Wet outdoors: adhesive-lined sleeve, or better, a box.
  • Shield neighbors, no scorching, no charring.
  • Check temperature and sunlight ratings. No rating, treat as indoor only.
  • Lock out and verify dead before opening an old splice.
