Heating and Cooling Tips for Apartment Renters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Renting means you do not own the equipment, but you still live with it. This lesson covers who usually fixes what, how to report a problem in writing, how to log indoor temperatures with a cheap thermometer, and how to use window air conditioners, space heaters and carbon monoxide alarms more safely.

Not legal advice. Your lease and local rules decide who is responsible for what. This page is general education. For heat requirements, see our separate lesson, NJ Landlord Heat Requirements: Minimum Temperatures and Heating Season. For a dispute, contact your local housing or code office or a legal aid group.

Who usually fixes what

Start with your lease. Many leases split the work in a similar way, but yours may differ, so read it rather than assume.

  • Usually the building: the boiler, furnace, central air, radiators, in-wall or baseboard heaters, vents and ductwork, the water heater and the thermostat wiring.
  • Often the tenant: small things such as replacing a thermostat battery or keeping furniture off a radiator. Some leases also put a window air conditioner you bought, or a filter in your own unit, on you.
  • Unclear: filters, window air conditioners supplied by the landlord, and anything the lease does not mention. Ask in writing and keep the answer.

When the lease is silent or confusing, do not guess and do not repair building equipment yourself. Ask.

Report problems in writing, with dates

A phone call is quick, but it leaves no record. Follow it with a short message by email, text or the building's repair portal, whichever your lease names. A useful report has five parts:

  1. Your name, unit and a phone number.
  2. What is wrong, in plain words ("radiator in bedroom stays cold").
  3. When it started and every date you reported it.
  4. What you measured (see the log below).
  5. What you are asking for and a reasonable time to reply.

Keep copies. Stay polite and factual. A calm note with dates is easier for a manager to act on than a long complaint.

Log indoor temperatures with a simple thermometer

A basic room thermometer is enough. Our own method, which you can adjust:

  • Put it on an inside wall, about chest height, away from windows, radiators, sunlight and the kitchen.
  • Let it sit a few minutes before you read it.
  • Read it at the same two times each day, for example morning and evening, and write the date, time, room and reading.
  • Also note the outdoor weather in a few words, since a cold night explains a low morning number.

One week of readings shows a pattern better than a single bad night.

Filters you may be allowed to change

If your apartment has its own furnace, air handler or fan coil with an accessible filter, your lease may let you change it. Ask first. In a building with central equipment, the filters are the building's job. For cooling season, the U.S. Department of Energy says to check or service a central air conditioner's filters about every month or two, and more often if the system runs constantly, the home is dusty or pets shed. For sizes and direction, see How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV. Never open a gas appliance cabinet or a sealed panel.

Window air conditioner installation safety

Most apartment cooling problems are window-unit problems, and the safety issues are about falling units and blocked exits.

  • Use the support brackets, side panels and screws that come with the unit, and follow its instructions. A unit that is only resting on the sill can fall.
  • Never put an air conditioner in a window that is your way out. The New York City Fire Code (published by the city fire department) says an air conditioner shall not be installed in a window providing access to a fire escape, unless the same room has another unobstructed window to the fire escape that meets the required size, and units in windows next to fire escapes must not block the path of travel on the escape. Other cities have their own rules, so check with your local fire or code office.
  • Do not block a fire escape or a stairwell with equipment, boxes or plants anywhere.
  • Plug the unit straight into a wall outlet. Do not use an extension cord. Do not run the cord under rugs.
  • Ask your landlord before installing one if the lease says you need permission.

Sizing and sealing are covered in Window Air Conditioners: Sizing by Room, Installation, Tilt and Sealing.

Space heater safety

If the building heat is out, a portable electric heater may be a short-term bridge, not a replacement for repairs. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission advises keeping a space heater at least three feet from curtains, furniture, bedding and other things that can burn, plugging it directly into a wall outlet rather than an extension cord or power strip, and turning it off before you sleep. The Massachusetts state page adds: put it on a flat, stable surface, turn it off when you leave the room, keep it away from water, and buy a heater tested and labeled by a recognized testing company. More in Electric Space Heaters: Safety, Real Cost, and When They Make Sense.

Carbon monoxide alarms

CPSC says to install battery-operated alarms, or alarms with battery backup, on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and to keep them away from heating vents and from places furniture or drapes could cover them. It does not recommend putting them in kitchens or directly above fuel-burning appliances. The test button checks the alarm's circuitry, not the sensor's accuracy, and alarms have a replacement age listed by the maker. If a building should have alarms and yours is missing or chirping, put the request in writing. Details in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Radiator and baseboard clearance

Heat needs to leave the radiator or baseboard and circulate. Long curtains, a couch pushed against it, or a tight cover can trap heat and make the room feel colder. Leave open space around the unit and in front of the grille, and do not pile clothes on it. Radiators and baseboards can be hot enough to burn skin, so keep children and pets in mind. Our own rule of thumb: if you cannot slide a hand between the furniture and the unit, move it. For more on covers, see Radiator Covers, Enclosures, Paint and Reflectors. Do not bleed, valve or repaint building equipment without permission.

When to call emergency services

Some things are not repair requests.

  • Gas smell: leave right away with everyone in the home. Do not flip switches, light matches or touch appliances. Call 911 from a safe distance, and do not go back until responders or the utility say it is safe. This follows a state pipeline safety page.
  • CO alarm sounding: CPSC says never ignore it and do not try to find the source. Move outside to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, do a head count, and do not re-enter until responders say it is safe. Headache, dizziness, nausea and tiredness can be signs of exposure.
  • Fire, smoke or burning smell from equipment: leave and call 911.

After the emergency, notify the landlord in writing.

Our own worked example: a one-week temperature log

Our own example, with assumptions. A renter reads a thermometer on an inside living room wall at about 6:30 a.m. and 7:30 p.m. for seven days in cold weather. We assume the lease says the building supplies heat and the renter's comfort target is 68 F. That 68 F is a number we picked for this example only. It is not a legal standard. The readings are made up for teaching.

Sample living room log (degrees F, our own made-up numbers)
DayMorning (6:30)Evening (7:30)Outdoor note
Mon6466cold, clear
Tue6365cold
Wed6264windy
Thu6163very cold
Fri6365cold
Sat6264cold
Sun6062very cold

Arithmetic.

  • Morning total: 64 + 63 + 62 + 61 + 63 + 62 + 60 = 435. Divide by 7 = 62.1 F.
  • Evening total: 66 + 65 + 64 + 63 + 65 + 64 + 62 = 449. Divide by 7 = 64.1 F.
  • Lowest reading 60 F (Sunday morning), highest 66 F (Monday evening).
  • Readings at or above 68 F: 0 of 14.

Sample short repair request.

Hello, this is Unit 3B. Our living room has been cold since Monday. I called the office on Monday and Wednesday. I measured the room twice a day with a room thermometer on an inside wall. Over seven days the morning readings averaged about 62 F and the evening readings about 64 F. The lowest was 60 F on Sunday morning. None of the 14 readings reached 68 F. The radiator in that room feels only slightly warm. Could someone inspect the heat in our unit this week? The log is attached. Please reply with a date and time. Thank you.

Notice it states facts, dates and a request, and leaves out guesses about the cause. If the heat is completely out, say so in the first line.

Field card: Heating and Cooling Tips for Apartment Renters

Read the lease, write it down, stay safe.

  • Check the lease for who fixes what.
  • Report in writing, with dates, and keep copies.
  • Log two readings a day with a thermometer on an inside wall.
  • Window AC: use the brackets, do not block fire escapes.
  • Space heater: three feet clear, straight into the wall outlet, off at night.
  • CO alarms outside sleeping areas; test per the maker.
  • Gas smell or CO alarm: go outside and call 911.
