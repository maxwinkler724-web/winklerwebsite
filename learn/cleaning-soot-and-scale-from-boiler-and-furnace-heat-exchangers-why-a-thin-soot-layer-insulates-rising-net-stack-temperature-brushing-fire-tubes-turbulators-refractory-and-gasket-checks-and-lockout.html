Cleaning Soot and Scale From Boiler Heat Exchangers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The fire side of a boiler or furnace heat exchanger is where fuel heat is supposed to pass into water or air. Soot and scale get in the way. This lesson covers why a thin film matters, how to notice it from the stack temperature, and a safe order of work for cleaning and putting everything back.

Why a thin soot layer matters

Soot is a poor conductor of heat. A federal energy department tip sheet on checking heat transfer gives a table of how much heat transfer is lost for a given soot thickness: about 2.5 percent at 1/32 inch, about 4.5 percent at 1/16 inch and about 8.5 percent at 1/8 inch. The same sheet says that scale from hard water deposits on the water side cuts efficiency by roughly 1 to 7 percent. A university extension fact sheet on heating system maintenance says a 1/8 inch soot deposit can raise fuel use by as much as ten percent. Think of the soot as a sweater on the tube: the flame is just as hot, but less of its heat reaches the water, so more of it leaves through the flue.

Watching net stack temperature

Net stack temperature is the flue gas temperature minus the temperature of the air going into the burner. If you measure it at the same firing rate and the same excess oxygen each visit, a rising number points to fouling on the fire side or scale on the water side. A federal energy department steam tip sheet on boiler surfaces recommends trending stack temperature at constant firing rate and excess oxygen for just this purpose. The test method itself is in the combustion lessons linked below; here we only use the trend. Write the reading, firing rate and oxygen on the service record each time, so next season you have a clean baseline.

Our own worked example

This is our own example, with an assumed rule of thumb. Two federal energy department tip sheets state that boiler efficiency changes about 1 percent for each 40 F change in stack gas temperature; one of them limits that rule to firetube boilers without heat recovery equipment. We use it here as an assumption, not an exact law for any particular boiler.

  • Assumed: stack temperature was 400 F after the last cleaning and reads 480 F a season later, at the same firing rate and oxygen level.
  • Assumed: the boiler burns 10,000 therms a year.
  • Rise in stack temperature: 480 - 400 = 80 F.
  • Number of 40 F steps: 80 / 40 = 2.
  • Assumed efficiency loss: 2 x 1 percent = about 2 percent.
  • Extra fuel: 10,000 therms x 0.02 = about 200 therms a year.

The true figure depends on the boiler and the burner setup, and fuel use rises slightly more than the efficiency number alone suggests. The point is the size of the effect and the habit of trending the reading. Also remember that a high stack temperature can come from other causes, such as a burner firing too hard, so use it as a prompt to look, not as proof.

Lock out before you open anything

Before opening a boiler or furnace, the federal rule on controlling hazardous energy (29 CFR 1910.147) describes the sequence: notify affected people, shut the equipment down with its normal procedure, isolate every energy source, apply locks or tags, release or restrain stored energy and verify isolation before work starts. For a boiler that means electrical power to the burner and controls, the fuel supply, and any connected water, steam or return piping that could release hot contents. Let the boiler cool; a university safety office report on fire tube cleaning describes shutting down and cooling the boiler before the doors are opened. See the lockout lesson linked below for the verify-dead steps.

Cleaning by boiler type

  • Firetube boilers. Hot gas runs inside the tubes. Open the rear and front doors, then wire-brush each tube and vacuum the loosened soot. The university extension fact sheets describe wire-brushing and vacuuming the surfaces, or using special cleaning compounds. Brush from one end in the direction the maker recommends and collect the debris instead of pushing it into the room.
  • Watertube boilers. Gas passes around the outside of the tubes. Soot is cleaned from the outer surfaces through access doors, by brushing, vacuuming or the method in the maker's instructions. Do not enter the gas passes without the confined space procedure that applies at your site.
  • Cast-iron sections. Use brushes and a vacuum between and over the sections, and keep tools from hitting the casting. Follow the maker's instructions for access panels.
  • Furnace heat exchangers. Clean only what the maker allows you to reach. Never reach through a crack or open seam; see the heat exchanger inspection lessons.

The soot itself is a hazard. A university safety office exposure report on fire tube cleaning saw soot spread through the boiler room and into the hall, found that soot was pushed out of the tube ends, and recommended particulate respirators, eye protection, local exhaust and a heat-stress work and rest plan. Follow your site's respirator and eye protection rules.

Turbulators and baffles: take them out and put them back in order

The university extension fact sheets note that turbulators or baffles in boiler tubes slow and direct gas flow so more heat is absorbed. They need cleaning too, and they must go back the way they came out. A simple routine, based on the maker's instructions:

  1. Before pulling anything, photograph the installed arrangement and mark each tube position and each part's orientation.
  2. Remove the parts one at a time, keeping them in order on a clean surface.
  3. Clean the tubes and the parts, and look for warping, burn-through or cracks.
  4. Reinstall in the reverse order, matching your marks and photos, and confirm each is seated and cannot slide or rattle.
  5. If a part is damaged or you cannot tell where it goes, stop and use the maker's parts diagram.

Refractory and gasket checks

With the surfaces open, look at the refractory around the chamber and doors for cracks, loose pieces and missing sections, and at door and cover gaskets for hardening, gaps and burn marks. A door that does not seal can leak flue gas into the boiler room. Replace gaskets with the maker's parts, and treat repairs to refractory according to the maker's instructions. If you do not know what an old gasket or insulation material contains, stop and follow your site's procedure before disturbing it.

Close up and test

Reinstall doors and covers, remove locks only by the people who applied them, and restart following the boiler startup lesson. Then take a new set of combustion readings at the same firing rate. If net stack temperature has dropped back toward last season's baseline, the cleaning worked. If it has not, look at the water side, the burner setup and the draft.
