Fairgrounds and Livestock Show Barns: Ventilation, Heat and CO

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A county fairgrounds sits quiet for most of the year, then fills with crowds, animals, fryers and rented heaters for a few weeks. The buildings are big, open and lightly equipped, so the job is less about tonnage and more about moving air, keeping exhaust out of the room and getting equipment ready on a short clock.

What makes these buildings different

Exhibition halls and show barns are usually tall, with large doors, open sides and ridge openings. They are used a few weeks a year, so the owner may not run any mechanical cooling at all. A state university extension fact sheet on livestock comfort at county fairs says comfort for animals and people is compromised once temperatures pass 80°F with relative humidity above 65 percent, and that buildings need good air exchange so inside temperatures stay close to outside temperatures. The same fact sheet says tunnel ventilation is impractical at fairs and that negative-pressure systems work poorly because uncontrolled openings let air short-circuit. In plain terms, these buildings rely on open area and fans, and the techs who service them mostly deal with fans, power and heaters.

For openings, that fact sheet suggests 2 inches of ridge opening per 10 feet of building width (a 30-foot-wide building needs 6 inches) and at least 1 square foot of net cupola opening per 100 square feet of floor. It also notes that ridge openings in older buildings are often painted shut or boarded over, and that reopening them is a low-cost fix. Buildings under 40 feet wide with plenty of sidewall openings do better.

Fans: stirring versus exchanging

The same source says fans work best when they all push the same direction or run a racetrack pattern, and that floor-level aisle fans can be safety hazards. It describes 60-inch ceiling fans moving roughly 25,000 to 45,000 cfm and covering about 1,000 square feet. Be careful with that number. A ceiling fan mostly stirs the air already in the room and makes people and animals feel cooler by moving air across skin. It does not by itself bring in outdoor air. Heat only leaves the building when warm inside air is replaced by cooler outside air, which the worked example below measures. The fact sheet also warns that many fans can overload a fairground's electrical system. A university extension note on getting through hot fair weather likewise recommends rapid-movement fans in livestock barns and warns that stale, stagnant air becomes dangerous quickly.

Heat stress for animals and visitors

Cattle, pigs, rabbits and poultry shed heat poorly compared with people. The extension fact sheet says cattle rely on breathing and cooler evenings, poultry pant, and pigs cool best when their skin is wet. The Missouri note says cattle sweat at only 10 percent of the human rate and that pigs cannot sweat. Lower animal density makes less heat, and small birds should not be penned with cattle, since the fact sheet calls chickens the least heat-tolerant common fair species.

For people, a federal workplace-safety agency says heat illness can occur indoors or outdoors in any season, and lists thirst, irritability, rash, cramping, heat exhaustion and heat stroke. It says to give cool water, keep a cool rest place (shade, an air-conditioned space or an area with fans), and cool a suspected heat stroke victim right away while calling 911. That guidance is written for workers, but the same signs apply to fairgoers and to volunteers staffing the barns. Workers new to heat need a few days to build tolerance, with extra water, shorter shifts and more breaks.

Rented heaters, generators and carbon monoxide

Fall and spring fairs can be cold, and rented portable heaters and generators show up. The federal public-health agency is blunt: never run a generator inside a home or garage even with doors and windows open, keep it outdoors and more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, do not burn charcoal indoors, and do not use a portable gas camp stove indoors. Carbon monoxide builds up indoors and poisons people and animals, and symptoms start as headache, dizziness, weakness and upset stomach. Sleeping or impaired people may not notice symptoms before it is too late. Big open doors do not make an unvented fuel-burning heater safe. For the sleeping-quarters angle, see the lessons linked below on tents and converted barns, which cover heater types and alarms.

Food stands: exhaust and propane, at a general level

A state fire marshal vendor information sheet (plain text, not linked) gives a sense of what inspectors look for. It says propane containers and first-stage regulators must sit outside buildings, booths and tents, at least 60 inches from any tent. Containers must stay upright and strapped to something solid, and filling on the grounds is prohibited. Appliances rated only for outdoor use may not run in a building or tent. Grease-producing cooking, such as deep frying, needs shielding from tent fabric, and cooking concessions need extinguishers, with a kitchen-fire type for grease vapors. The sheet refers to hood and exhaust standards without spelling out hood sizes. For you as the HVAC tech, the useful point is that exhaust and makeup air at a food stand should be left to the vendor's approved equipment and the local fire official, and that cooking smoke must not be drawn into a livestock area or a heater intake.

Our own worked example: airflow to carry off crowd and animal heat

Our own example, with stated assumptions. We want the outdoor airflow needed to hold a hall at a set temperature rise above outdoors. The sensible heat equation is Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT. The 1.08 comes from our own arithmetic with standard-air assumptions: 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb°F = 1.08.

  • Hall (assumed): 120 ft × 60 ft = 7,200 ft², with a 16 ft average height = 115,200 ft³.
  • Crowd (assumed count): 400 people. A university lecture slide gives about 100 W of sensible heat per person, and 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h, so 100 W = 341 Btu/h. 400 × 341 = 136,400 Btu/h.
  • Animals (assumed heat): 60 show animals at 1,000 Btu/h each. This is our assumption, set from a Michigan State University tech note that gives roughly 1,000 Btu/h sensible for a 1,000 lb dairy cow at 80°F. It is not a measurement of any show animal, and heavier or hotter animals give more. 60 × 1,000 = 60,000 Btu/h.
  • Total sensible heat: 136,400 + 60,000 = 196,400 Btu/h.
  • Allowed rise (assumed): 8°F above outdoors.

Step 1: 1.08 × 8 = 8.64. Step 2: cfm = 196,400 / 8.64 = about 22,700 cfm. Step 3: air changes per hour = 22,731 × 60 / 115,200 = about 11.8. For a 5°F rise, the divisor is 1.08 × 5 = 5.4, giving about 36,400 cfm, so tightening the target from 8°F to 5°F costs 60 percent more air.

What this tells you: the hall needs about 12 full air changes an hour of real outdoor air to stay only 8°F warmer than outside. On a 90°F day that is a 98°F hall, well past the 80°F comfort point above, so ventilation alone cannot make it comfortable, and extra shade, fewer animals per pen, scheduling shows for cooler hours and fans for air movement all matter. The example counts only sensible heat from people and animals. It ignores sun on the roof, lighting, the moisture people and animals give off, and the fryers next door, so treat the answer as a minimum.

Seasonal startup and shutdown

Our own checklist, built from the sources listed below and general practice. Before the season: inspect any ridge openings and louvers for paint, nests and debris; check fan guards, blades and wiring; and check that the electrical supply can carry the fans you plan to run together. After the season: remove or drain what can freeze, shut down and tag units that will sit idle, and note what failed so repairs happen before next year. Always lock out and tag power before opening a unit.

Dust on coils

Barn dust, hay chaff and bedding collect on condensing units and air-handler coils. A federal laboratory measure guideline on air conditioner diagnostics (plain text, not linked) says a fouled evaporator reduces airflow, will eventually freeze the coil and may damage the compressor, and says to clean a clearly plugged condenser with a biodegradable coil cleaning solution and a hose after removing power. The same guideline says airflow below 200 cfm per ton can lead to coil icing and greatly shorten compressor life. One caution: a national-laboratory modeling study (plain text, not linked) found that at the fouling levels reported in the literature, coil and filter cleaning would save little energy in homes and none in small commercial buildings. So clean coils to protect airflow and the compressor, not because it will pay back in energy.

Freeze protection of water lines

A fairground's water lines to a stand or washing area often run through unheated buildings. Iowa State University Extension says to drain any line that can be emptied, using compressed air to clear low spots; to apply electric heat tape directly to pipe in open unheated areas, follow its instructions, never double-wrap it and avoid layering it with insulation unless the maker allows; to insulate enclosures to hold existing heat; and to keep heaters away from flammable material. It also notes that risk can last days or weeks after a cold snap. For a building that is empty all winter, draining is the simplest answer.

Field card: Fairgrounds and Livestock Show Barns

Open buildings still need real air exchange, and never an unvented heater.

  • Comfort drops past 80°F with relative humidity over 65 percent, for animals and people.
  • Sensible heat: Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT (standard air).
  • Our example: 196,400 Btu/h at an 8°F rise needs about 22,700 cfm, near 12 air changes an hour.
  • Ceiling fans stir air; only outdoor air replaces heat.
  • Generators outdoors, more than 20 feet from openings; no unvented fuel-burning heaters near animals or people.
  • Propane containers stay outside buildings and tents.
  • Clean coils for airflow; drain or heat-trace water lines before freezing weather.
