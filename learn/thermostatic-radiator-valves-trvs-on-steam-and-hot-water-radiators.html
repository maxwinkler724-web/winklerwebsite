Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) on Steam and Hot-Water Radiators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostatic radiator valve, or TRV, is a self-powered thermostat bolted onto a single radiator. It has no wires and no batteries. It does one job: stop that radiator from heating a room that is already warm enough. This lesson covers how it works on hot water and on one-pipe steam, how to read its numbers, and what it cannot do.

What a TRV is

A TRV has two parts: a valve body and a head. The head holds a sensor that expands when the room warms and shrinks when it cools. Caleffi describes a liquid-filled bellows that pushes the valve stem down as room air warms, which cuts flow, and lets a spring open the valve as the room cools. Heating-trade writer Dan Holohan says the sensing material in a head can be wax or liquid. No electricity is involved. Without a head, or with the head turned to its top setting, Caleffi says the valve is fully open.

Because a TRV only reduces heat, Holohan puts it bluntly: TRVs do not make heat, they only prevent overheating. The U.S. Department of Energy's steam balancing guide says the same thing: a TRV cannot draw more heat to a radiator, it can only stop one from overheating.

Hot water and two-pipe systems

On a hot-water radiator, the head throttles the flow of water through the radiator. Caleffi lists its valves for water or glycol mixtures up to 212 F, and notes that the control head can be swapped while the system is running because of the stem's double seal.

Caleffi limits the differential pressure across a valve with a head fitted to 15 psi, and Danfoss advises keeping the pressure drop low to avoid noise. See Hot-Water Balancing Basics for balancing.

One-pipe steam is different

A one-pipe steam radiator uses one pipe for both steam in and condensate out, and it only fills with steam as fast as the air can leave. (Read One-Pipe vs. Two-Pipe Steam Systems and Steam Radiator Air Vents first.) So you cannot throttle the supply valve; those pages explain why a half-open supply valve causes banging. Instead, the steam TRV controls the air.

Holohan describes the steam TRV as a normally open valve that screws into the radiator in place of the air vent, with the air vent then fitted to the valve. When the room is warm, the head closes the valve and traps the air in the radiator. With no place to go, the air blocks the steam, and the radiator stops heating. He notes that no TRVs are made for the inlet side of a one-pipe radiator, because throttling the inlet causes water hammer. The Building America field study of a New York one-pipe building says the same in plain terms: the TRVs sit on the vent side of the radiator, not the inlet.

Macon's instruction bulletin adds two details. A vacuum breaker goes between the steam vent and the radiator, and a radiator valve should not be applied in a one-pipe system unless each radiation unit has a steam air vent venting to atmosphere. Danfoss also says condensate must not be trapped in the valve or vent, and wants enough pitch for it to drain. The DOE guide recommends a straight-shank vent rather than an angled one for the same reason. A vacuum breaker matters because, as steam condenses in a closed radiator, a partial vacuum can pull more steam in; Holohan prefers TRVs that have one built in.

Reading the numbers on the dial

TRV dials usually show numbers, not degrees, and each maker prints its own table. Caleffi's 200 and 201 series heads list these approximate settings: 0 is 32 F, the snowflake frost setting is 45 F, 1 is 54 F, 2 is 61 F, 3 is 68 F, 4 is 75 F and 5 is 82 F. Stelrad, a UK radiator maker, shows a similar ladder in Celsius: 12, 16, 20 and 24 C for settings I to IIII, and 8 C for frost protection. Danfoss's steam TRV install sheet gives only the ends of its range: the lowest setting holds about 45 F, the highest about 85 F. Never assume one maker's number matches another's, and never use a hot-water table for a steam head.

Worked example: picking a dial number

A customer with a hot-water radiator and a Caleffi 200 series head wants about 70 F in the living room. Use the maker's table.

  1. Setting 3 is 68 F and setting 4 is 75 F. The gap between them is 75 - 68 = 7 F per step.
  2. 70 F is above 68 F by 70 - 68 = 2 F.
  3. 2 F divided by 7 F per step is about 0.3 of a step.
  4. So the dial belongs about a third of the way from 3 toward 4.

Check the other direction: a customer who turns it all the way to 4 is asking for about 75 F, which is 5 F warmer than wanted. Check the real room with a thermometer after several hours, since the table is approximate. Macon tells installers to let a room stabilize for 4 to 5 hours before judging a setting. This example reads one maker's hot-water table; it is a way to use a table, not a setting for your job.

Placement and remote sensors

A built-in sensor measures the air around the valve, and the valve and pipe are hot. Danfoss says to mount the head horizontally so air passes freely over it, and Caleffi says to install the head horizontally and keep it out of niches, behind curtains and out of direct sun. Stelrad warns that a vertical head can pick up heat from the pipework and shut off early. Where a radiator sits behind furniture, drapes or a cabinet, makers offer remote sensors on a capillary tube: about 78 inches (2 m) for Caleffi, and two 6-foot tubes for Danfoss's separate sensor and dial.

Why TRVs can help older NJ homes

Many older New Jersey homes and apartments have steam or hot water with uneven heat (see What Heats Older New Jersey Homes). The DOE Building America steam balancing guide says unbalanced steam systems can leave 10 to 15 F differences between units, with some apartments overheated and others barely heated, and owners often overheat the whole building to reach the far radiators. It cites research reporting up to 15 to 25 percent heating cost reductions from balancing, and 5 to 10 percent from larger main line vents.

The evidence for TRVs alone is mixed, and this lesson should say so. The same Building America study cites a 1995 NYSERDA-funded project that installed 224 TRVs in eight one-pipe steam buildings and reported about 15 percent heating savings. But its own test of TRVs in a Flushing, New York building found no measurable change in gas use per heating day, and it concluded that balancing, venting and boiler setpoint tuning should come first or alongside. Holohan agrees that master venting comes first, and says TRVs improve comfort mainly because people stop opening windows.

Common problems

  • Too much steam pressure. Danfoss says to keep steam pressure at or below 2 psig (maximum rating 15 psig), because higher pressure can push steam past a closed vent. Macon recommends 1.5 psi. A contractor quoted by Holohan says TRVs do not work well above about 1 to 2 psi. The DOE guide gives about one-half to 1.5 psig as typical for a one-pipe system. See Steam Boiler Pressure Controls and Gauges.
  • Stuck pin. Stelrad says that if a head has been turned off for a long time, the pin can stay pressed down, so the radiator stays cold even after the head is turned up. Its fix is to remove the head and free the pin. That is a hot-water panel radiator note from one maker; follow the maker's instructions for your valve, and let the system cool first.
  • Wrong orientation or flow direction. Heads belong horizontal. Macon's bulletin says the valve body arrow must point in the direction of flow, and Stelrad says its setting ring has to be turned to match the water direction.
  • Leaking supply-valve packing. Holohan says leaking packing can act as a vent and let steam in, so check it if a radiator still overheats.

The boiler still needs proper controls

A TRV does not run the boiler. Macon says the boiler must be cycled by its controls, and that if a space thermostat in one zone cycles the boiler, no radiator in that zone should get a valve. Holohan warns against downsizing a boiler because TRVs are wide open at startup and on very cold days. Limits, pressure controls and water-level devices stay as they are; see Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs. If a hot-water radiator is cold for other reasons, see Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold and Heat Emitters.

Field card: Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) on Steam and Hot-Water Radiators

A TRV only prevents overheating; it makes no heat.

  • Non-electric head: wax or liquid expands with room air and closes the valve.
  • Hot water: valve throttles flow. One-pipe steam: valve goes on the vent side and traps air.
  • Never throttle the supply valve of a one-pipe radiator.
  • Dial numbers are each maker's table (Caleffi 3 = 68 F, 4 = 75 F). Not interchangeable.
  • Head horizontal, clear of drapes and sun; use a remote sensor when blocked.
  • Steam pressure low: Danfoss says 2 psig or less.
  • Cold radiator with a TRV: check the pin and head before blaming the boiler.
  • Boiler controls and venting still come first.
