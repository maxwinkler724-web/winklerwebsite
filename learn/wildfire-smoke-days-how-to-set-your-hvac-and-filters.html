Wildfire Smoke Days: How to Set Your HVAC and Filters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When the sky turns hazy and the air smells like a campfire, your HVAC system can either pull smoke into the house or help keep it out. The settings are simple. This lesson walks through what EPA, AirNow and CDC say to do, in the order a homeowner would do it.

The short version

EPA's wildfire guidance for homes boils down to five moves: keep windows and doors shut, set the system to recirculate (or close the outdoor-air intake), run the fan, use the best filter the system can take (MERV 13 if it fits), and add a portable air cleaner in the room where you spend the most time. CDC's 2023 health advisory gives the same big picture: stay indoors, keep smoke outside, use your HVAC system well, and use a portable air cleaner or set up a cleaner-air room.

Step 1: Know the number (AQI basics)

AirNow reports the Air Quality Index, or AQI, in six color-coded categories. Good is 0 to 50 (green). Moderate is 51 to 100 (yellow). Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups is 101 to 150 (orange). Unhealthy is 151 to 200 (red). Very Unhealthy is 201 to 300 (purple). Hazardous is 301 and up (maroon). AirNow's fire and smoke map and app, which CDC points people to, show local readings. Check them before you decide how hard to seal up the house.

Washington State's health department puts a line on the useful trigger: when outdoor air reaches the Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups category for fine particles, close outside-air intakes unless you have a MERV 13 or better filter, and open up again when the air improves.

Step 2: Set the central system

  • Recirculate, not fresh air. EPA says that if your system has a fresh-air intake, set it to recirculate or close the outdoor-air damper. Many homes have no such damper, and the system already recirculates. If you are unsure, look at the thermostat or ask a technician.
  • Fan on. EPA advises running the fan as much as possible with the setting on "On" instead of "Auto" when you have a high-efficiency filter, so the filter keeps cleaning the room air even when nothing is heating or cooling. A longer fan run costs more electricity.
  • Best filter that fits. EPA says to choose a filter rated MERV 13 or as high as the system and filter slot can handle, that MERV 13 and up must remove at least 50 percent of the smallest particles tested, and that most furnaces can take MERV 13 if the filter is changed often. It also says HVAC systems are rarely labeled with a filter limit, so a technician can confirm what yours allows. The details on fit, ratings and change intervals are in How Often to Change Your Air Filter, and What MERV Means.
  • Seat it snugly and swap early. Gaps let air go around the filter. In heavy smoke, EPA says to replace it sooner than the usual schedule, especially if it looks soiled. Washington's health department suggests keeping a spare on hand, since good filters can sell out during an event.

Step 3: Window units, portable ACs and mini-splits

EPA's wildfire page says to close the outdoor-air damper on a window air conditioner and, if you cannot, to skip using it during smoke. Seal the unit to the window as tightly as you can. Single-hose portable ACs can draw smoke in from elsewhere in the house, so EPA says to use them sparingly; with a two-hose unit, seal the window kit tightly. Washington's health department makes the same point: learn how to switch off "fresh air mode" on a window unit. For sizing and common problems, see Window and Portable Air Conditioners.

None of the sources I read says anything specific about ductless mini-splits. Check your unit's manual for any fresh-air or ventilation feature, and see Mini-Split Basics for how these systems work.

Step 4: ERV and HRV units

An ERV or HRV brings outdoor air in on purpose, so it is the part of a tight house that can bring smoke in. EPA's wildfire page, as I read it, does not mention them. Washington's health department says to close outside-air intakes at the Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups level unless you have MERV 13 or better filtration. A Green Builder Media article (trade press, not an agency) tells owners of tight homes to shut these units off while the outdoor air is smoky and turn them back on afterward. Look in your unit's manual for a smoke or shutoff instruction, and ask your installer if it says nothing. How these units work is in ERV vs. HRV.

Step 5: Portable HEPA and DIY box-fan cleaners

EPA says to pick a portable cleaner sized for the room, with a known clean air delivery rate (CADR) and no ozone output, run on high as often as possible, and placed where you spend the most time. EPA's clean-room page says California Air Resources Board keeps a list of tested devices. EPA's page on home air cleaners warns against devices that intentionally make ozone, a lung irritant.

If you cannot buy a unit, EPA lab research tested box-fan cleaners: a 20-by-20-inch fan with a 20-by-20-inch filter (1 or 4 inches thick), MERV 13 suggested, taped or strapped to the back with the airflow arrow pointing into the fan. In simulated wildfire smoke they cut fine particles about as well as a small commercial cleaner, and a cardboard shroud and extra filters helped. The caveats matter. Clogged filters did almost nothing, the DIY units were louder and drew more power, and EPA says it is still studying smoke gases and real-world performance. EPA recommends DIY units only when products of known performance are unavailable or unaffordable. Use a fan made in 2012 or later with a UL or ETL mark, never use extension cords, and do not leave an older fan running unattended or while you sleep.

Worked example: sizing a cleaner and reading the AQI

Reading the number. AirNow shows an AQI of 162. That falls between 151 and 200, so it is Unhealthy (red) for everyone, and sensitive groups may feel worse effects. Step 2 settings apply, and CDC says to wear an N95 or P100 respirator if you must go out in visible smoke.

Sizing for a bedroom. The room is 14 feet by 20 feet with an 8-foot ceiling.

  1. Area = 14 × 20 = 280 square feet.
  2. EPA's chart lists minimum CADR for 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 and 600 square feet as 65, 130, 195, 260, 325 and 390 cfm. Round up to the 300 square foot row.
  3. Minimum CADR is about 195 cfm for particle removal. The chart is an estimate and assumes 8-foot ceilings, so taller rooms need more.
  4. Check: 195 ÷ 300 = 0.65, the same ratio as the 65-cfm-per-100-square-feet chart start. A cleaner labeled 200 or higher works. A 150 cfm unit is too small for this room.

Short tech section

If a customer asks about MERV 13, do not just drop one in. EPA says a technician should confirm what the system accepts. Check the filter slot depth, the blower's rated static pressure and the manufacturer's stated maximum, and look for gaps at the rack. A dense filter that is not changed becomes a restriction, so use the symptoms in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics as your cue to measure. Do not stack filters. For the bigger picture of source control, ventilation and filtration, see Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics.

What to avoid indoors

EPA's clean-room page says to run exhaust fans or range hoods only briefly and to avoid adding indoor smoke sources, such as candles, incense, gas or wood stoves, frying and aerosol sprays. It also says to vacuum only with a HEPA filter. CDC lists the same habits. If you cannot keep cool, the power fails or smoke still gets in, EPA suggests going somewhere else, such as a clean-air shelter or a large air-conditioned building.

Field card: Wildfire Smoke Days: How to Set Your HVAC and Filters

Seal up, recirculate, fan on, best filter that fits.

  • Check AirNow. AQI 101 to 150 is orange, 151 to 200 is red.
  • Close windows. Set the system to recirculate or close the outdoor-air damper.
  • Fan "On" with a high-efficiency filter; MERV 13 if the system allows.
  • Window AC: close the fresh-air damper or skip it. Single-hose portables pull in smoke.
  • ERV/HRV: follow the unit manual; some agencies say close outside-air intakes.
  • Portable cleaner CADR is at least 65 cfm per 100 sq ft (EPA chart, 8 ft ceiling). No ozone makers.
  • DIY box fan: 20 by 20 filter, MERV 13, fresh filters, newer UL/ETL fan, never unattended if old.
