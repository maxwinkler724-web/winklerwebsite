Cool Roofs and Roof Surface Temperature: What Techs and Owners Should Expect

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A roof is the first thing the summer sun hits, and its color and finish decide how much of that energy ends up in the attic, the ducts and the rooms below. This lesson explains solar reflectance and thermal emittance in plain words, how hot dark and light roofs get, and what that means when you service the equipment under or on top of a roof.

Two surface properties, in plain words

Solar reflectance is the fraction of incoming sunlight a surface bounces back, from 0 (black, absorbs everything) to 1 (a perfect mirror). Whatever is not reflected is absorbed as heat. So absorbed sun equals (1 minus reflectance) times the sunlight arriving. A federal environmental agency page calls reflectance the most important property for how well a roof reflects sunlight.

Thermal emittance is how well a surface sheds heat by giving off infrared radiation, also from 0 to 1. Most roofing materials other than bare metal have high emittance. The same agency page says emittance matters most in warm, sunny climates, because a surface with high emittance cools itself better once the sun has heated it. A bright, bare metal roof can reflect well but shed heat poorly, which is why the two properties are listed separately. See our night-sky cooling lesson for the same emittance idea working in the other direction after dark.

How hot a roof gets

A federal energy department cool roofs page says a conventional roof can reach 150°F or more on a sunny summer afternoon, and that a reflective roof in the same conditions could be more than 50°F cooler. That is a large swing. Our worked example below lands in the same range for the dark roof, using assumed numbers we state openly.

From a hot roof to the heat in the building

The energy department page says lower roof temperatures reduce the heat flowing into the building, which in an air-conditioned building can cut cooling energy, and in an unconditioned building can improve comfort. A federal environmental agency page reports that in non-air-conditioned homes, cool roofs can lower maximum indoor temperatures by 2.2 to 5.9°F, and that in air-conditioned homes they can cut peak cooling demand by 11 to 27 percent. Those are ranges from studies, not a promise for your building.

In a house with an attic, the roof deck heats the attic air and the attic floor, so heat reaches the ceiling below and any ducts or air handler in the attic. A cooler deck means a cooler attic, and that lowers duct heat gain and the load on the ceiling. A reflective roof is one tool and a radiant barrier is another, with its own limits. We cover that in attic radiant barriers and reflective insulation. For ducts in hot attics, see air handlers and ducts in unconditioned attics.

Ducts on a roof are the most direct case. Laboratory researchers at a national lab measured bare metal rooftop ducts on small commercial buildings at daytime surface temperatures roughly 35 to 40 K above the outside air. A white coating on three test systems cut conduction heat gain, by about 0.64 MJ per day per square meter of duct on the first system and 0.34 to 0.46 on the other two, and improved delivery effectiveness by about 2 percent on the first and 5 to 10 percent on the others. If you service roof ducts, insulation and a light finish both matter.

Rooftop units and condensers: a small effect

It is tempting to blame a hot roof for a rooftop unit or roof-mounted condenser running hot. The same national lab studied this directly on a rooftop condensing unit. With the compressor and condenser fan running on a hot roof, air entering the condenser averaged about 0.3°C warmer than a reference spot far from the unit during peak sun. A reflective coating around the unit removed that rise. The authors estimated, from published performance curves, about 0.6 to 0.7 percent better efficiency from that cooler intake air. So expect a real but small gain. A clogged coil, a dirty filter or a failed fan will hurt far more than the roof color, so do the normal checks first. See rooftop unit basics.

Aging and dirt

A cool roof does not stay at its day-one reflectance. National lab researchers state that soiling and weathering lower the reflectance of most roofing materials, which weakens the benefit. A lab method from the same group reproduces three years of natural exposure in three days, using dust minerals, black carbon, humic acid and salts. Another lab study found that most single-ply membrane samples could be washed back to within 90 percent of their unweathered reflectance, and some needed an algaecide. A third lab paper found that most of the darkening in roof deposits comes from carbon soot, and that how long it lasts depends on how well it sticks and whether rain washes it off. The federal environmental agency lists periodic cleaning as an ongoing cost of keeping a roof reflective. The practical point: dirty roofs perform between the new and the black case, and cleaning helps.

The winter heating penalty

A reflective roof also reflects the weak winter sun, so less free heat enters the building. The energy department page says this can raise energy costs in colder climates if the yearly heating penalty exceeds the yearly cooling savings. The environmental agency page says the penalty is typically offset by summer cooling savings, is smaller because winter sun is lower and days are shorter, shrinks with good insulation, and is smallest where snow stays on the roof. In a cold or mixed climate, expect a mixed result, and look at attic insulation first. See attic insulation and air sealing.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measurement. We treat a flat roof section with no attic, one lumped outside heat transfer coefficient, and a steady afternoon.

Assumptions: sunlight on the roof G = 800 W/m²; outdoor air Ta = 35°C; space under the roof Ti = 27°C; roof U-value = 0.5 W/m²·K (about 0.088 Btu/h·ft²·°F); combined convection and radiation coefficient to the air h = 20 W/m²·K; dark reflectance 0.10; light reflectance 0.60; our own made-up values, chosen to be reasonable.

Step 1, absorbed sun. Dark: (1 − 0.10) × 800 = 720 W/m². Light: (1 − 0.60) × 800 = 320 W/m². The dark roof absorbs 400 W/m² more.

Step 2, surface balance. Absorbed sun leaves two ways: to the air, h(Ts − Ta), and down through the roof, U(Ts − Ti). So absorbed = h(Ts − Ta) + U(Ts − Ti), which gives Ts = (absorbed + h·Ta + U·Ti) / (h + U). The bottom is 20 + 0.5 = 20.5, and h·Ta + U·Ti = 700 + 13.5 = 713.5.

Dark: Ts = (720 + 713.5) / 20.5 = 1433.5 / 20.5 = 69.9°C (about 158°F). Light: Ts = (320 + 713.5) / 20.5 = 1033.5 / 20.5 = 50.4°C (about 123°F). The light roof is about 19.5 K (35°F) cooler.

Step 3, heat down through the roof. Dark: 0.5 × (69.9 − 27) = 0.5 × 42.9 = 21.5 W/m². Light: 0.5 × (50.4 − 27) = 0.5 × 23.4 = 11.7 W/m². The difference is 9.8 W/m².

Check. Dark: 20 × 34.9 = 698 W to the air, plus 21.5 down, is 719.5 W, matching 720. Light: 20 × 15.4 = 308 plus 11.7 is 319.7, matching 320.

Scale up. Over 100 m² (about 1,076 ft²), 9.8 W/m² is about 980 W, or roughly 3,300 Btu/h (980 × 3.412 = 3,344). An aged light roof at reflectance 0.50 would absorb 400 W/m² and settle near 54.3°C, giving back some of the gain. Real roofs differ: wind, attic ventilation, roof insulation and cloud all shift these numbers.

What techs and owners should expect

  • A roof that is a cool color will have a much lower surface temperature, but the building will not feel drastically different if the ceiling is well insulated.
  • Gains are largest on uninsulated or poorly insulated roofs and on ducts in or on the roof.
  • Gains at a rooftop unit intake are real but small. Do not use roof color as a fix for a coil or airflow problem.
  • Reflectance drops with dirt. Ask when the roof was last cleaned before judging it.
  • In cold climates, the winter penalty may cancel some summer savings. Do not promise savings to an owner.
  • On a hot day, any roof can be dangerously hot to work on. See heat stress in attics and on roofs.

Field card: Cool roofs and roof surface temperature

Reflectance sets how much sun is absorbed; emittance sets how well the roof sheds it.

  • Absorbed sun = (1 minus reflectance) × sun on the roof.
  • A conventional roof can reach 150°F or more; a reflective roof can be over 50°F cooler (federal energy department).
  • Rooftop ducts and attic ducts gain the most from cooler roofs.
  • Condenser or rooftop unit intake air: about 0.3°C cooler in one lab test, a small gain.
  • Dirt and weathering lower reflectance; washing restores most of it.
  • Cold climates have a winter heating penalty, smaller with insulation and snow cover.
  • Our own example: 0.10 vs 0.60 reflectance, 69.9°C vs 50.4°C, 21.5 vs 11.7 W/m² through the roof.
