Holiday Lights and Your Heating and Cooling Equipment

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every winter, strings of lights and inflatable figures end up on, around and under the equipment that heats and cools the house. Most of it is harmless until a cord gets pinched, a blower blocks airflow or a snow pile covers a vent. This lesson shows where decorations should and should not go, and does the watts and kWh math on old and new light strings.

The outdoor unit: look, but do not decorate

The outdoor heat pump or air conditioner moves a lot of air through its coil and out the top through a fan. Anything draped over it, tied to its cage or leaned against its sides gets in the way of that air. A heat pump also runs a defrost cycle in cold weather, and it needs open space around it to shed frost and meltwater. Our linked lesson on outdoor unit clearances covers the numbers that makers print; this page is about the holiday version of the same problem.

  • No light strings on the unit. Do not wrap the cabinet, hang strings from the fan guard or tie them to refrigerant lines or the electrical whip. Wire and plastic bulbs can be pulled into the fan guard, and a string can block the coil face.
  • No inflatables on or against it. A big inflatable that sags in the wind can fall across the top and cover the fan discharge. Anchor it well away from the unit.
  • Keep blowers and cords out of the clearance zone. An inflatable's blower and its cord should sit outside the open space the unit's manual asks for, with the cord routed around the unit instead of across its base or service side.
  • Do not use the unit as a support. Tying a string to the cabinet means someone may reach into the unit to fix it, which is a job for a technician with the power off.

If a fan or cabinet is damaged by a decoration, do not poke at it with the power on. Turn the unit off at the thermostat and its disconnect, then call a technician.

Cords and outlets outdoors

Outdoor lighting needs outdoor-rated parts. State fire-safety guidance says to make sure outside cords and decorations are rated for outdoor use, to plug outdoor lights and inflatables into circuits protected by a ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI), and to put lights on a timer or switch them off at bedtime. It also says to avoid nails and staples that can damage a cord, to keep outdoor connections lifted away from snow and water, and not to daisy-chain extension cords. A federal consumer safety fact sheet says a GFCI cuts power quickly when it detects a ground fault, that the national electrical model code has required GFCI protection on outdoor receptacles since 1973, and that you should test each GFCI at least once a month. Press the test button, confirm the power drops, then reset it.

A consumer safety flyer also says to use no more than three standard-size sets of lights per single extension cord, and a fire administration page says to read the maker's instructions for how many strings can be joined. Follow the package. If a string has frayed or pinched wire, cracked sockets or loose connections, throw it away.

Indoors: registers, rugs and returns

  • Floor registers. Do not lay cords across a floor register or let a string sit in the airflow. Blocked or crowded registers can upset how air moves through the house, and a cord can be pulled into the opening.
  • Rugs. Consumer safety guidance says not to run cords under rugs, through doorways or under furniture, because that can damage them. A hidden cord can also overheat without anyone seeing it.
  • Returns and space heaters. Keep paper decorations, fabric, artificial snow and candles away from return grilles and from space heaters. A fire administration page says to keep candles at least 12 inches from anything that burns, in sturdy holders, where they cannot be knocked over. Flameless battery candles are a calmer choice near vents. See our lessons on space heater safety and holiday trees and heat sources.
  • Overheated cords. Consumer safety guidance says extension cords can overheat and cause fires when used improperly. If a cord or plug feels hot, unplug it.

Vent terminations, snow and decorations

Furnaces and water heaters push exhaust outdoors through a vent termination, and high-efficiency models also pull combustion air in through a pipe outside. These openings must stay clear. Do not wrap them in lights, hang wreaths or inflatables in front of them, set decorations against the pipes or stack snow around them. A blocked vent can shut a furnace down or, in the worst case, let combustion gas build up indoors, so keep your carbon monoxide alarms working. Our lessons on snow and ice on furnace vent pipes and carbon monoxide alarms explain what to look for and what to do. If you smell gas or an alarm sounds, go outside and call the utility or emergency services.

When shoveling, do not pile snow against the unit, the vent pipes or the meter area. A pile slides, melts and refreezes, and may bury a termination you thought was clear.

Worked example (our own): what do light strings cost to run?

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any product. Light strings turn nearly all the power they draw into heat, so the heat they add to a room or the wall they hang on matches their power draw. We use the conversion 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h, and kWh = kW x hours.

Assumptions (ours):

  • Ten 100-bulb incandescent mini-light strings at about 40 W each.
  • Ten LED strings at about 5 W each.
  • Run 6 hours a day for 30 days, which is 6 x 30 = 180 hours.
StepIncandescentLED
Total watts10 x 40 W = 400 W10 x 5 W = 50 W
Btu/h400 x 3.412 = 1,364.8, about 1,365 Btu/h50 x 3.412 = 170.6, about 171 Btu/h
kW400 / 1,000 = 0.4 kW50 / 1,000 = 0.05 kW
kWh over 180 h0.4 x 180 = 72 kWh0.05 x 180 = 9 kWh

The difference is 400 - 50 = 350 W, and 72 - 9 = 63 kWh over the month. The LED set uses one eighth of the energy (9 / 72). To turn kWh into dollars, multiply by your own utility rate. The load is also useful for cord checks: 400 W at 120 V is about 3.3 A (400 / 120), well within a typical cord, but the maker's rating on the cord tag and the string's package limit still decide how many strings you may join.

Field card: Holiday lights around heating and cooling equipment

Air in, air out, vents open, cords rated and protected.

  • Nothing draped on, tied to or leaned on the outdoor unit; keep blowers and cords out of its clearance.
  • Outdoor-rated cords and decorations on GFCI-protected outlets; test the GFCI monthly.
  • Use timers; follow the package limit on strings per cord.
  • No cords across floor registers or under rugs.
  • Candles, paper and fabric away from returns and space heaters.
  • Furnace and water heater vent terminations stay clear of decorations and snow piles.
  • Our own example: 10 incandescent strings at 40 W is 400 W, about 1,365 Btu/h and 72 kWh; 10 LED strings at 5 W is 50 W, about 171 Btu/h and 9 kWh.
