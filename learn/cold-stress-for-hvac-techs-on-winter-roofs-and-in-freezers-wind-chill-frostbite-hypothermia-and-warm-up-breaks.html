Cold Stress for HVAC Techs on Winter Roofs and in Freezers: Wind Chill, Frostbite, Hypothermia and Warm-Up Breaks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Winter is when the no-heat calls stack up, and many of them are on a rooftop unit in the wind. Cold stress is what happens when your body loses heat faster than it can make it. This lesson covers how wind chill is calculated, the four cold illnesses federal agencies list, what to do first, and how to set up a roof or a walk-in freezer job so your hands and judgment stay working.

Why cold is a work hazard, not just discomfort

NIOSH describes cold stress as a risk for outdoor workers and for people in poorly insulated or unheated spaces, and says it can matter even where temperatures are only near freezing if people are not used to winter. A federal fast-facts sheet on indoor work adds that workers around freezers and refrigerators are at risk too, and that cold stress can occur at temperatures as high as 60 degrees. OSHA has no specific cold-environment standard, but it says employers must protect workers from recognized hazards, including cold stress. So a no-heat call is a cold-exposure job for you, even if the customer is the one shivering at the start of it.

How wind chill works

The National Weather Service wind chill index estimates how cold exposed skin feels with the wind moving over it. The formula, as the NWS publishes it, is:

Wind chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75(V0.16) + 0.4275T(V0.16)

where T is the air temperature in °F and V is the wind speed in mph. Four limits matter for your decisions, all from the NWS page:

  • The index is defined only for air temperatures at or below 50°F and winds above 3 mph.
  • Frostbite requires the actual air temperature, not the wind chill, to be below freezing.
  • Wind chill does not cool an inanimate object below the air temperature. It describes skin, not your pipes or your gauges.
  • The NWS brochure says the index uses wind speed adjusted to about 5 feet, adult face height, and no sun. A rooftop in open air may see more wind than a ground-level report, so treat a forecast number as a floor, not a ceiling. That last part is our own caution, not an NWS statement.

Worked example: a 0°F morning, 15 mph wind

Assumed inputs (ours, picked for the example): air temperature T = 0°F and wind V = 15 mph. These match the example the NWS itself uses, so you can check our answer against their page.

  1. Raise the wind to the 0.16 power: 150.16 = 1.5423 (rounded).
  2. Multiply by 35.75: 35.75 × 1.5423 = 55.14.
  3. The two temperature terms are 0.6215 × 0 = 0 and 0.4275 × 0 × 1.5423 = 0, so they drop out at T = 0.
  4. Add it up: 35.74 + 0 − 55.14 + 0 = −19.4, which rounds to −19°F.

Reading the chart (NWS statement, not our calculation): the NWS says that at 0°F with a 15 mph wind, the wind chill is −19°F and exposed skin can freeze in 30 minutes. The chart has shaded zones for 30, 10 and 5 minutes. NOAA's chart page also warns that you cannot rely on the wind chill number alone to judge frostbite risk, because timing depends on wind speed as well. Since the air is below freezing here, frostbite is possible. If the same wind came with 40°F air, the skin would not freeze, but you could still chill.

The four cold illnesses: signs and first steps

Federal sources agree on the names and most signs. First-aid wording differs a little between them, and we note where.

  • Hypothermia. OSHA defines it as body temperature below 95°F (normal is 98.6°F) and says it can happen above 40°F if you get chilled by rain, sweat or cold water. Early signs: shivering, fatigue, loss of coordination, confusion. Late signs: shivering stops, blue skin, slowed pulse and breathing, unconsciousness. First steps: call for medical help, get the person into a warm room or vehicle, remove wet clothing, warm the chest, neck, head and groin first, give warm sweet drinks without alcohol only if alert, and start CPR if there is no pulse.
  • Frostbite. Most often hits fingers, toes, nose, ears and cheeks. Signs: numbness, tingling or aching, and skin that looks pale, waxy, white, gray or bluish and feels firm. First steps: get to a warm room, do not walk on frostbitten feet unless needed, do not rub or massage. On rewarming the sources differ: NIOSH says warm the area in warm (not hot) water or with body heat, while OSHA says do not rewarm it yourself because refreezing does more damage, and to wrap it loosely in dry cloth until medical help arrives. Follow your employer's first-aid training, and keep heating pads, radiators and fires away from numb skin (NIOSH).
  • Trench foot. Caused by long exposure to wet and cold, and it can happen at up to 60°F if feet stay wet. OSHA says wet feet lose heat 25 times faster than dry feet. Signs: redness, numbness, cramps, swelling, tingling pain, blisters. First steps: take off boots and wet socks, dry the feet, and stay off them.
  • Chilblains. Skin damage from repeated exposure to temperatures from just above freezing up to 60°F. Signs: redness, itching, possible blisters and swelling. First steps: do not scratch, warm the skin slowly, and keep any blisters clean and covered.

Layers, dry gloves and cold hands

NIOSH and OSHA both say to wear several layers of loose clothing, because tight clothing cuts circulation to the extremities. Add waterproof insulated boots and a hat, and protect ears, face, hands and feet. OSHA says dampness, including sweat, speeds heat loss, so a layer you can peel off before you sweat is part of the plan. Carry spare socks, gloves and a hat; NIOSH lists those plus a change of clothes, blankets and a thermos of hot liquid. Both agencies say to avoid touching cold metal with bare skin, which on a roof means panels, screws and tool steel.

A federal fast-facts sheet notes that cold reduces dexterity and also impairs complex mental tasks. A NIOSH study of 40°F cold rooms found workers skipping gloves because they thought gloves hurt dexterity, and suggested thin liner gloves for fine work and hand warmers kept outside the cold room. In our own words, the tool-handling takeaway is to keep a dry spare pair of gloves in the truck, keep a lanyard or tool pouch on you when you are on a roof, and set screws and small parts down where they cannot roll off an edge. Numb fingers drop things, and a dropped nut driver is a falling object to anyone below.

Icy roofs, ladders and working alone

OSHA says snow-covered roofs can hide hazards like skylights, that ice makes surfaces slippery, and that a snow-loaded surface must be inspected by a competent person. It also calls for insulated, water-resistant boots with good rubber treads, short steps and a slower pace, and fall arrest equipment where it applies. Our rule of thumb, not an OSHA number: if you cannot see the roof surface or the ladder rungs, the call is not ready. Setup, ladder angles and edge protection are in Ladder and Roof Fall Safety for HVAC Work, and the gas-heat side of a rooftop no-heat is in RTU Gas Heat Lockouts and Rooftop Safety.

OSHA recommends working in pairs, and NIOSH says to watch your own condition and your coworkers'. Neither page addresses lone workers directly. If your company sends techs out alone, that is a gap to raise with your employer: agree on a check-in time, tell someone which roof you are on, and set a rule that shivering you cannot control means you stop and warm up. OSHA also says not to ignore uncontrollable shivering.

Warm-up breaks and walk-in freezers

NIOSH says to use warm locations on breaks, and OSHA lists frequent warm-up breaks, warm sweet drinks and high-carbohydrate snacks, and scheduling outdoor work for the warmest part of the day. On a no-heat call you do not pick the hour, so the break is the lever: take it in the truck with the heater on, change damp gloves, and do not wait until the hands stop working.

For walk-in freezers, the most solid figure we found is for a 40°F cold room, where a NIOSH evaluation recommended rotating employees every 2 hours and giving them dry clothes to change into. We did not find a cited time limit for sub-zero freezer work that fits service calls, so we are not giving one. Ask your employer for their written cold-room procedure. An ACGIH-based warm-up table reproduced in a federal fact sheet begins only at about −15°F, and it was written for outdoor work, so it should not be treated as a freezer rule. Also note the NIOSH authors said wind chill calculations do not apply at 40°F in a cold room. For door heaters and gaskets on the equipment itself, see Walk-In Freezer Door Heaters, Gaskets and Pressure Relief Ports, and for the opposite hazard see Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs.

Field card: Cold stress for HVAC techs

Dry, layered, paired up, and off the ice.

  • Wind chill = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75(V0.16) + 0.4275T(V0.16); valid at 50°F or colder and winds above 3 mph.
  • Example: 0°F and 15 mph is about −19°F; NWS says skin can freeze in 30 minutes.
  • Hypothermia: body temperature below 95°F; shivering, clumsiness, confusion. Get warm, dry and call for help.
  • Frostbite: pale or waxy, numb, firm skin. Do not rub it. Get warm and get medical help.
  • Trench foot and chilblains happen above freezing, up to 60°F. Keep feet dry.
  • Layer loosely, keep spare dry gloves and socks, avoid bare skin on cold metal.
  • Work in pairs, take warm-up breaks, and do not climb onto an iced roof you cannot inspect.
