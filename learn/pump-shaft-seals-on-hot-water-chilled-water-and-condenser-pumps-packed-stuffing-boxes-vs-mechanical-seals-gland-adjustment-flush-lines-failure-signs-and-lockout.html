Pump Shaft Seals: Packed Stuffing Boxes vs Mechanical Seals

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every pump has to let a spinning shaft pass through a wall full of pressurized water. Plant pumps do it one of two ways: soft packing squeezed around the shaft, or a mechanical seal made of two flat faces. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what a drip means and what to do about it.

Safety comes first

Treat every seal job as a hazardous-energy job. A pump can start from a remote command, a drive or a control signal, and hot-water and condenser systems hold scalding or pressurized water. Follow your site procedure and the manufacturer's instructions. The steps are covered further down.

Two ways to seal a shaft

On a packed stuffing box, rings of braided packing sit in a chamber around the shaft. A gland, held by two nuts, pushes the rings inward. A federal energy department pumping-systems sourcebook says packing "typically requires some leakage in order to remain lubricated and cooled," and that because packing touches the shaft it wears over time and leaks more.

A mechanical seal has no soft rings against the shaft. Instead, one hard face turns with the shaft and a second hard face is fixed to the pump. A university turbomachinery-conference paper describes the arrangement: one ring is held stationary while the other rotates with the shaft, a spring acts as a closing force, and the thin film between the faces lubricates, cools and keeps the faces from touching. The same sourcebook calls mechanical seals more effective at sealing than packing but more costly and more maintenance-intensive.

Where you will see each type

Our own field experience is that older hot-water, chilled-water and condenser-water base-mounted pumps are often packed, while newer ones and most small in-line pumps use mechanical seals. Check the pump rather than assuming: a packed box has a visible gland with two nuts, while a mechanical seal sits behind a gland plate with small connections for flush or vent lines.

Packing: why it must drip

Packing is not meant to be bone dry. The water that escapes carries away friction heat and lubricates the shaft or its replaceable sleeve. Squeeze it dry and it overheats, hardens and scores the sleeve. The sourcebook's maintenance checklist puts acceptable packing leakage at usually 2 to 60 drops per minute, and an energy-tips sheet from the same department adds that if the gland must be tightened excessively to control leakage, the packing should be replaced, because overtightening wears the shaft or sleeve and raises power use.

Adjusting gland nuts evenly

This is our own practice note; follow the pump maker's instructions first. With the pump running only if your procedure allows hands near the box, or better, stopped and locked out, snug the two gland nuts in small, equal steps, such as a flat or less at a time, alternating sides. Wait several minutes between steps, because packing needs time to settle and the drip rate changes slowly. A cocked gland pinches one side of the shaft, runs hot and leaks on the other side. Stop when the drip is steady and modest, and when the packing is used up or the gland bottoms out, repack rather than crank harder. For the packing-ring technique used on valves, see the related lesson below.

Mechanical seals: the thin film

A mechanical seal is supposed to leak almost nothing you can see, because the film between the faces is only a few micrometers thick; the paper above reports about 2.5 micrometers for one design. The flat faces are pressed together by a spring, and the pumped water itself provides the film. That is the weak point. If the pump runs without water, there is no film, the faces rub, and heat builds fast. The same paper describes a seal whose faces overheated and a mating ring that was destroyed by heavy friction under dry running. The sourcebook likewise warns that centrifugal pumps are not designed to run without fluid and that seals, packing and wearing rings are damaged if not lubricated. Our own rule of thumb is that dry running is measured in seconds, so never bump-test a pump that has not been filled and vented.

Flush and vent lines

Many seals have small tubing from the pump discharge, or from a clean outside source, into the gland. The sourcebook notes that seals are often served by special flushing lines with filters to catch debris. The flush keeps the faces cool and clean, so a plugged flush line is a seal killer. The paper above also stresses that the cooling fluid should be clean and stable to form a good film. Our own checks: confirm the flush line is open and the strainer or filter is clear, and open any seal-chamber vent while filling so trapped air does not leave the faces dry.

Signs of seal trouble

  • Steady drip or spray from a mechanical seal. The energy tip sheet says mechanical seals that leak excessively usually must be replaced.
  • Packing leaking far above the 2 to 60 drops per minute range even after careful adjustment.
  • Crust or mineral stain at the seal, which shows slow leakage has been drying there. Our own note.
  • A wet bearing frame or rusty bearing housing, because water that reaches the bearings ruins them; see the lesson on vibration below for the later symptoms.
  • Shaft sleeve wear. A groove under the packing means the sleeve needs replacing; the energy-tips sheet links excess tightening to wear on the shaft or sleeve.

Before any seal work: isolate and lock out

The federal workplace safety agency's lockout/tagout standard lays out the order. Prepare by knowing the energy types and hazards, shut the equipment down in an orderly way, locate and operate every energy isolating device, apply a lock or tag to each, make hazardous stored or residual energy safe, and verify isolation before starting work. On a pump that means the electrical disconnect and the isolation valves on suction and discharge, plus relieving trapped pressure and letting hot water cool. The standard also says the written procedure must include specific steps for shutting down, isolating, blocking and securing equipment, and for testing that the controls work. See the lockout lesson linked below for the electrical side.

Worked example: what a packing leak costs in water

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Assumptions: a packed condenser-water pump drips 60 drops per minute (the top of the sourcebook range), and we assume about 20 drops per milliliter.

  1. 60 drops per minute divided by 20 drops per mL = 3 mL per minute.
  2. There are 24 x 60 = 1,440 minutes in a day, so 3 mL x 1,440 = 4,320 mL per day.
  3. 4,320 mL = 4.32 liters per day, about 4.3 L.
  4. One gallon is 3.785 liters, so 4.32 / 3.785 = about 1.14 gallons per day.

So even the top of the normal range is a bit over a gallon a day. It is small, which is why packing is allowed to drip, but a drip that doubles is a sign to look closer. The drop size is an assumption; measure with a graduated cup for a stronger number.

Field card: Pump Shaft Seals: Packed Stuffing Boxes vs Mechanical Seals

Packing drips to live; a mechanical seal should barely show.

  • Packing: expect a modest steady drip, roughly 2 to 60 drops per minute per the sourcebook checklist.
  • Tighten gland nuts in small equal steps, alternating, with waits between.
  • If the gland needs heavy tightening, repack; do not crank.
  • Mechanical seal: a spring holds a rotating face on a stationary face with a thin fluid film.
  • Dry running destroys the faces in seconds. Fill and vent first.
  • Check flush and vent lines are open and clean.
  • Failure signs: steady drip or spray, crust, wet bearing frame, sleeve wear.
  • Before work: shut down, isolate, lock out, relieve pressure, verify.
