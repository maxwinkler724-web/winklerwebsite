Spiders, Wasps and Nests in Gas Burners: Venturis, Pilots and Air Shutters

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas burner is a clean, simple machine until something small moves in. Spiders, mud-dauber wasps and other insects like the narrow, dry, sheltered tubes in a burner, and a plugged tube changes how the flame burns. This lesson covers why it happens, what it looks like, what a safe look-over involves and when to stop and call a pro.

Why burners attract nesters

Seasonal gas appliances sit idle for months: furnaces after summer, outdoor grills and patio heaters over winter, pool heaters in the off season, camper water heaters in storage. A burner tube is a dark, dry, protected opening with a screen or shutter on one end. That is what a spider wants for a web, and it is close to what a wasp wants for a nest.

The federal consumer product safety agency has said for decades that the venturi tube on a gas grill is often a nesting place for spiders and other insects while the grill is stored for the winter, and that if anything blocks gas flow to the main burner, a fire hazard exists. Its grill fact sheet tells owners to check that tube for blockage by insects, spiders or food drippings at least once a year. A university extension page on mud daubers adds that these wasps stock their mud nests with paralyzed spiders, and that they build on the sides of equipment and inside the housings of unused machinery. An idle appliance fits that description. The same page says mud daubers are not aggressive, but we still treat any nest as a sting risk until we see it is empty.

The same idea applies on a furnace, a patio heater or a water heater, even though the federal grill papers we read are about grills. Where a burner has an air opening and a small gas passage, small things can plug it. That part is our own reasoning, so check the maker's service instructions for your exact unit.

The parts that clog

  • Venturi (burner tube). Gas leaves a small orifice and pulls air in behind it. The tube mixes the two before the mixture reaches the burner ports. A web or nest here chokes the mixture.
  • Air shutter. The adjustable opening at the start of the tube. It sets how much primary air gets in. Lint, webs and mud narrow it.
  • Pilot tube and pilot orifice. A very small passage. Our explanation: a tiny opening is easy to plug with a web, so a pilot flame can shrink or wander.
  • Burner ports. Rows of small holes where the flame forms. Debris here makes uneven flames.

For how atmospheric, inshot and premix burners differ, see the related lesson on burner types linked below. This page stays on the nests.

Primary air in one paragraph

Burning a gas needs air. A federal laboratory circular on domestic burner design gives the rule that about nine cubic feet of air are needed per thousand Btu of gas burned. The part of that air mixed in with the gas as it enters the burner is called primary air. The rest comes from around the flame and is called secondary air. Less primary air means the gas has to find more of its oxygen from the room, and the flame changes.

Signs to look for

  • Yellow or lazy flames instead of mostly blue ones with a steady shape. See the flame color lesson for what each color means.
  • Soot on the burner, heat exchanger, grill or vent. The federal safety publication we read lists sooting, especially on appliances and vents, as a warning sign, along with unfamiliar or burning odors and a furnace that cannot heat the house or runs continuously.
  • Flame rollout: flame reaching out of the burner compartment, or a tripped rollout switch.
  • Flame coming back out of the air shutter, or a fire behind the controls on a grill. The grill release warns that a poorly seated venturi can cause a fire behind the control panel.
  • A pilot that will not stay lit, or a burner that lights only in part. A blocked path is one possible cause among several, so a tech checks the rest of the sequence too.

Our own worked example: a partly blocked air shutter

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not measured data. It shows the size of the effect, not what any real appliance will do.

Assumptions (all ours):

  • Natural gas carries 1,000 Btu per cubic foot.
  • The burner input is 40,000 Btu per hour.
  • The burner is designed to pull in primary air equal to 50 percent of the air needed for complete burning.
  • A web and lint block 40 percent of the shutter opening.
  • For simplicity, primary air drawn in is proportional to the open area. Real burners are more complicated, so treat this as a rough guide.

Step 1, air needed. Using the rule of nine cubic feet of air per thousand Btu (federal circular), a cubic foot of gas at 1,000 Btu needs 9 cubic feet of air.

Step 2, gas flow. 40,000 Btu/h divided by 1,000 Btu/ft³ is 40 cubic feet of gas per hour.

Step 3, total air needed. 40 × 9 = 360 cubic feet of air per hour.

Step 4, primary air when clean. 50 percent of 9 = 4.5 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of gas, so 40 × 4.5 = 180 cubic feet per hour.

Step 5, primary air when 40 percent blocked. 60 percent of the opening remains, so 4.5 × 0.6 = 2.7 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of gas, or 40 × 2.7 = 108 cubic feet per hour. That is 108 / 360 = 30 percent of the air needed, down from 50 percent.

Step 6, what changes. Primary air fell by 180 − 108 = 72 cubic feet per hour, a 40 percent cut. Secondary air now has to supply 360 − 108 = 252 cubic feet per hour instead of 180. The flame has to reach farther for oxygen.

Why that matters (our explanation). With less air mixed in ahead of time, the flame burns more slowly and grows taller, and gas in the middle of the flame does not meet enough oxygen. Unburned carbon glows yellow and can deposit as soot. If the flame touches a cool surface or the room air supply is also short, carbon monoxide can form. A federal safety page notes that carbon monoxide comes from incomplete burning of fuels such as natural gas and propane. The same weak mixture can also push the flame back toward the air opening, which is why flame at the shutter is a stop sign.

Safe checks with gas and power off

  1. Turn off the gas at the appliance or cylinder valve and switch off electric power, including ignition and fan power. Let everything cool.
  2. If you smell gas, stop, leave the area and call the gas utility or emergency number from outside.
  3. Follow the maker's instructions for removing panels or the venturi. On a grill, the federal release says to find the tube using the owner's manual, remove it, inspect and clear it, and make sure it is seated properly on the valve outlet when it goes back.
  4. Look for webs, mud, leaves and lint. Wear eye protection and gloves. Treat any mud nest as possibly occupied.
  5. Clear the tube with a venturi brush or a pipe cleaner, as the federal grill sheet suggests, or blow it out with air outdoors. Push debris toward the main burner section and clean it out, do not push it into the gas orifice.
  6. After reassembly on a grill, the federal fact sheet says to check for leaks with a soapy water solution, and that if bubbles appear there is a leak and the grill must not be lit until it is fixed.
  7. Light it as the maker directs and watch the flame. It should be steady and mostly blue. See the flame color lesson if it is not.

Screens and parts the maker supplies

Some makers supply insect screens or covers for burner air openings. Use only those parts, fitted as directed. A homemade screen of cloth or fine mesh can clog with lint and cut primary air just like a web. That is our caution, so follow the maker's guidance for your model. For a seasonal unit, a cleaning at the start of the season is the habit the federal grill sheet supports.

When to call a pro

  • Any flame rollout, scorched wiring or a tripped rollout switch. See the rollout switch lesson.
  • Soot that comes back after cleaning, or soot on a heat exchanger or vent.
  • Flame at the air shutter, or a pilot that will not stay lit after the pilot area is clear.
  • A carbon monoxide alarm sounding. Get to fresh air first. The federal publication says to leave, get medical attention if poisoning is suspected and call the fire department. See the alarm lesson.
  • A gas smell, or any leak that does not stop after tightening.

The federal safety publications we read also say heating systems should be professionally inspected every year, and the agency has said consumers should not service their own appliances. As a trainee, work under your employer's procedures and the maker's instructions.

Field card: Nests in gas burners

Gas off, power off, then look.

  • Idle months invite spiders and wasps into tubes and openings.
  • Check the venturi or burner tube for blockage at least yearly on grills.
  • Yellow, lazy flames, soot and flame at the shutter are warnings.
  • Our example: 40 percent shutter block cuts primary air 40 percent (50 to 30 percent of needed air).
  • Less air means soot and a carbon monoxide risk.
  • Use maker-supplied screens only.
  • Rollout, returning soot or a CO alarm means stop and call a pro.
