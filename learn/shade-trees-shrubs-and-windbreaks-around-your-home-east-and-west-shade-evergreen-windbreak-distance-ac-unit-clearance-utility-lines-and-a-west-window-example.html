Shade Trees, Shrubs and Windbreaks Around Your Home: Where to Plant to Cut Heating and Cooling Loads

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Plants are the cheapest "equipment" a house can have, but where you put them matters. The right tree on the east or west side can cut summer sun on the walls, an evergreen row on the windy side can slow winter wind, and the wrong plant in the wrong spot can choke the outdoor unit, touch a power line or clog a drain. This lesson covers the placement rules that extension and federal guides agree on, and where they differ.

Why plants change heating and cooling loads

Sun through glass and sun on walls and roofs add heat in summer. Wind pushes outdoor air through small gaps in winter. Leaves and branches reduce both. A federal landscaping guide says properly placed deciduous trees on the south side can screen 70 to 90 percent of summer sun, and one extension fact sheet says well-placed deciduous shade trees cut summer heat gain on east and west windows and walls by about 50 percent. These are claims for good placement on a mature planting, not promises for every house. Our own caution: a young tree does very little for years (see the growth section).

East and west: deciduous trees

Deciduous trees drop their leaves, so they block summer sun and let winter sun through. One extension fact sheet says east walls and windows need shade roughly from 7 to 11 a.m. and west surfaces from 3 to 7 p.m. in June through August. It suggests trees that reach 25 feet or more at maturity go 10 to 20 feet east and west of the house, and another extension fact sheet gives the same 10 to 20 feet in parallel lines. A third, from a different region, says shade trees are best at least 25 feet from houses and buildings, so check the mature size and crown width of the species before you choose a spot.

The afternoon sun is low, and that changes the pick. A federal guide advises trees with crowns lower to the ground on the west to shade the lower afternoon angles. A tree that only shades the roof at midday does little for a west window at 5 p.m. For the roof itself, one extension fact sheet notes that trees which do not overhang the roof cast little shade on it at midday.

Do not shade away the winter sun you want. One extension fact sheet says winter sun in its region is less than 45 degrees above the horizon, and another lists roughly 30 degrees in December against about 77 degrees in June at a mid-latitude example. That fact sheet keeps trees on the south side at least 2.5 times their mature height from the building, and southeast and southwest trees about four times, so winter light still reaches south glass. See the window U-factor and SHGC lesson for why sunny glass drives cooling, and the window shades and awnings lesson for fixes that work the day you install them.

Evergreen windbreaks: which side and how far

Plant a windbreak upwind. One extension fact sheet says that usually means the west, northwest and north sides, but local terrain can change it, so watch where winter wind actually comes from. Dense evergreen trees and shrubs planted together work best, according to a federal consumer guide. Dense rows of juniper, spruce or fir are typical choices in the extension sheets.

Distance is where the sources vary, so we give each as stated:

  • A federal landscaping guide and one extension fact sheet both put the windbreak 2 to 5 times the mature tree height from the house. A 30-foot tree means 60 to 150 feet away.
  • One extension fact sheet says the best wind reduction is about 1 to 3 times tree height, with useful protection out to about 6 times.
  • Another says protection extends downwind 10 to 20 times the windbreak height, and that snow drifts are worst at 2 to 3 times that height. Keep that in mind if a drive or walk sits right behind the row.

The same fact sheet estimates that a cedar-type windbreak can cut wind from 12 mph to 3 mph up to two times the tree height. In wildfire-prone areas, one extension fact sheet advises keeping evergreens out of the close zone around the house and not planting them under larger trees where they act as ladder fuels, which fits the 2 to 5 times spacing.

Shading the outdoor AC or heat pump unit

A federal consumer guide says shading an air conditioner can raise its efficiency by as much as 10 percent. An extension fact sheet says to shade the unit from mid-morning through evening, but also to prune branches to leave at least several feet of clearance around it, and to keep shrubs away because they cut airflow.

The airflow rule always wins over shade. One extension fact sheet says manufacturers' clearance distances should be followed "to the letter." It gives no number, because the number is in the installation manual for your unit. Our outdoor unit clearances lesson shows how to read those tables. Our own practical points: no shrub hugging the coil, keep a tree's canopy above and beside the unit rather than its trunk close by, and never wrap or fence the unit in. A federal cooling guide says to clean debris and leaves from the fan, compressor and condenser, and our own experience is that tree leaves, seed pods and fluff collect on and against coils, so a unit under a messy tree needs more frequent checks. See the condenser coil cleaning lesson for safe methods.

Keep trees away from lines, roofs and vents

  • Overhead power lines. One extension fact sheet says tall-growing trees should be at least 20 feet from power lines, and 50 feet away to avoid future pruning; trees under lines should stay under 15 feet at maturity. Another says to avoid large shade trees within 25 feet of wires. If branches already touch a line, call the electric utility and do not prune them yourself.
  • Buried utilities. Call 811 before you dig, and plant away from the marked lines. Plan the tree position on paper before the hole is dug.
  • Roots near lines and drains. An extension fact sheet says roots can grow to interfere with underground pipes, cables and wires. Another says roots dislike being confined by paving and foundations. Our own advice: pick a spot well away from the sewer or drain line, and ask a licensed plumber or tree professional when unsure.
  • Roofs and gutters. A federal guide says to plant far enough from the house that mature roots will not damage the foundation, and that leaves and branches should not clog gutters or damage the roof. It also advises at least 1 foot between full-grown plants and the exterior wall.
  • Chimneys and vent terminations. Our own rule, not from the sources above: keep branches and shrubs well clear of chimney tops and of furnace, water heater and dryer vent terminations so exhaust can leave and intake air can enter. Check the appliance manual for its clearance and have a technician check any vent you cannot keep clear.

Years to maturity

A federal guide says a 6 to 8 foot deciduous tree planted near a house starts shading windows the first year and the roof in 5 to 10 years, and that slow growers may take up to 10 years to shade a roof but tend to live longer. One extension fact sheet calls shade planting a long-term job and suggests mixing fast and slow growers, removing the fast ones as the slow ones mature. Another suggests planning for what the yard looks like in 10 or 20 years. We do not have a source for a single "years to full size" number, so we give none.

Worked example (our own): a west window with and without tree shade

These inputs are our own assumptions for a hot summer afternoon, not measured data, except that the shade fraction is based on the 50 percent figure above.

  • Sun intensity on the west glass: 200 Btu/h per square foot (assumed)
  • Window area: 20 square feet
  • Solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC): 0.40 (assumed, the fraction of sun that becomes heat inside)
  • Shade fraction from a mature tree: 0.50 (assumed; half the sun is blocked)

Step 1, no shade: 200 x 20 x 0.40 = 1,600 Btu/h.
Step 2, with shade: 1,600 x (1 - 0.50) = 800 Btu/h.
Step 3, saved: 1,600 - 800 = 800 Btu/h. For a 4-hour afternoon peak: 800 x 4 = 3,200 Btu of cooling load avoided.
Step 4, in electricity, if the air conditioner delivers 14 Btu per watt-hour (assumed): 3,200 / 14 = about 229 watt-hours, or 0.23 kWh.

So 800 Btu/h is about 7 percent of one ton (12,000 Btu/h) of cooling, from one window. A house with three or four west and east windows, a shaded wall and a shaded roof adds up, but a single window saves modestly. The point of the arithmetic is the method, so rerun it with your own window area and its SHGC from the label.

Field card: Trees, shrubs and windbreaks

Shade the glass, slow the wind, never crowd the unit.

  • Deciduous trees east and west; low crowns for the low west sun.
  • Evergreen windbreak upwind, about 2 to 5 times mature height away (sources vary).
  • Follow the maker's clearances at the outdoor unit; no shrubs against the coil.
  • Stay well clear of overhead lines; call 811 before digging.
  • Keep branches off roofs, chimneys and vent terminations.
  • Expect years, not weeks, before a new tree helps.
