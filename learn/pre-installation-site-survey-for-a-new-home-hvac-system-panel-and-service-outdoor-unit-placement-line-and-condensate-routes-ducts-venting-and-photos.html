Pre-Installation Site Survey for a New Home HVAC System

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A good quote starts with a walk around the house, not a catalog. The survey is how a tech or estimator learns what the existing panel, walls, attic, drains and ducts will allow, and it is where most surprises (a full panel, a 60-foot line run, a return that is too small) get found while they are still cheap. This lesson lays out what to check, what to photograph, and one filled-in sheet for an assumed house.

Safety first. A survey is mostly looking, measuring and photographing. Opening an electrical panel cover, testing gas appliances and entering a hot attic belong to a trained person following the rules where they work. Homeowners: photograph the outside of the panel and the labels, and leave the cover on.

Why a survey comes before the price

The ENERGY STAR quality installation fact sheet we read names four things that decide how well a new air conditioner or heat pump performs: proper sizing and component matching, the correct refrigerant charge, adequate airflow, and sealed ducts. Its chart lists duct leakage, sizing, refrigerant charge and airflow as the main installation shortfalls. Every one of those depends on facts you can only get on site. For the full list of what a quality install includes, see New AC or Heat Pump Installation Checklist, and for questions a homeowner can put to a contractor, see What to Ask an HVAC Contractor Before Hiring. This page is the step in between: what gets looked at before a number is written down.

Electrical: panel, service size and the disconnect

Start at the meter and panel. Note the main breaker rating, the busbar rating (the label inside, which a qualified person reads), the number of open spaces, and whether they are single-pole or 2-pole. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory presentation on avoiding panel upgrades says new homes almost always have enough capacity (200 A is typical), that air conditioning is what typically required a 100 A panel in older California homes, and that it is worth checking the heat pump nameplate draw: it lists 2 to 4 ton split units at roughly 4,300 to 7,000 W (18 to 29 A). It also describes two National Electrical Code methods for existing homes: Section 220.87 (15-minute metered data, multiplied by 1.25, plus the new load) and Section 220.83 (a bottom-up sum of connected loads). Which one applies, and what the result is, is the electrician's or inspector's call. Our job at the survey is to collect the inputs. The full load discussion is in Will My Electrical Panel Handle a Heat Pump?

Then find where the disconnect for the outdoor unit will live and where the circuit will run. Ask: is there an existing disconnect, is it in sight of the unit, and is there working space in front of the panel? Panel and disconnect clearances are a code matter, covered in Working Space and Service Clearance Around HVAC Electrical Panels; disconnect and breaker basics are in AC Condenser Disconnect Switches.

Outdoor unit: placement and clearances

The Building America resource guide on split heat pumps tells installers to place and install the unit per the maker's instructions and the national residential installation standard rather than giving its own dimensions, so write down the real clearances you measure and compare them to the maker's manual for the model being quoted. Look for walls, fences, shrubs, windows, dryer vents, roof drip lines and the route a service tech would use to reach the unit. In cold climates the same guide notes that ice can build up during severe weather and that heat trace on the outdoor unit is an option, so note snow drift and roof-shed risk too. Photograph the site from two angles with a tape measure in the shot.

Refrigerant line route and length

Trace the path from the indoor unit to the outdoor site: wall penetration, attic or crawlspace run, elbows, vertical rises, and total length. A Department of Energy air conditioner measure guideline says long line sets are a common cause of undercharge and that charge for a new install can be estimated by weighing in refrigerant based on line length and diameter, and that a long line through a hot attic changes where to measure suction temperature. The Building America guide adds that line sets are sealed during construction, evacuated to 500 microns, and charged by the maker's method by an EPA-certified technician. The maker's limit on length and rise belongs in the survey notes as a question to answer before quoting. See Refrigerant Line Set Sizing for why length matters to oil return.

Condensate route

A cooling coil produces water, and the survey decides where it goes. A national laboratory building specification we read calls for condensate piping sloped at not less than 1/8 inch per foot, drains carried to the building exterior (with a pump where gravity will not work), a secondary pan with its own visible drain under equipment in or above conditioned space where leaks could do damage, float switches that cut power when drainage fails, and at least R-4 insulation where freezing or sweating is possible. For a condensing furnace it adds a neutralizer if the drain goes to waste lines. Check the slope you can actually get, the termination point, and whether a pump is needed. Trap depth for draw-through units is covered in AC Coil Condensate Traps.

Ducts: condition, leakage and static pressure

Look at what you can reach: disconnected or crushed flex, taped joints, undersized returns, a filter slot that is too small. The DOE air conditioner guideline says ducts should be tested and sealed before diagnostics because return leakage distorts readings, gives 15 percent of system airflow at 25 Pa as an acceptable leakage level for existing homes, and says flex duct is often constricted by hangers and tight bends. It reports that manufacturers' maximum recommended static pressure is about 0.8 in. w.c., that a clean evaporator coil should show no more than about 0.25 in. w.c., that filters in a California field sample averaged 0.28 in. w.c. compared with about 0.10 for a standard clean furnace filter, and that typical design airflow is 350 to 400 cfm per ton. Measuring total external static pressure on the existing fan, with the fan speed noted, is how you find out whether the ducts can carry the new equipment. The Building America guide also warns that heat pump supply air is about 80 to 115 F compared with 130 to 140 F from a furnace, so more air volume is needed and ducts may need to be larger. All of that is worked through in Replacing a Gas Furnace With a Ducted Heat Pump. At the survey, record grille and filter sizes, duct materials, and where static pressure can be probed.

Gas: combustion air and venting

If a gas appliance stays or goes, the survey includes it. The Building America pre-retrofit combustion assessment says to inspect the appliances and vent first, check the vent connector size using the National Fuel Gas Code tables, look for changes in the number or type of appliances, added exhaust fans or appliance location, calculate the combustion air needed, test draft, and test for CO, with listed CO alarms (Standard 2034) near the appliances and outside sleeping areas. The furnace guide says sealed-combustion condensing furnaces are preferred, their exhaust is under 140 F so PVC vent pipe is acceptable, intake and exhaust belong above the expected snow line, and natural-draft Category I units can backdraft when exhaust fans or a dryer depressurize the space. When a furnace comes out and a water heater stays on the old chimney, that vent needs a fresh look. Attic and horizontal furnaces have their own access and drain issues in Gas Furnaces in Attics and Horizontal Installs.

Access and permits, in general terms

Check how equipment and people get to the install: attic hatch size, pull-down stair, crawlspace opening, door and stair widths, and a safe working platform. Permits depend on where you live. The Department of Energy heat pump page says the installer can provide information on regulations or permitting that might be required, and model codes such as the International Residential Code and International Mechanical Code (both named, not quoted) are adopted and changed locally. Put "permit needed? who pulls it? inspection steps?" on the sheet and get the answer from the local building department.

Photos and notes to take

Photos save a second trip and let the estimator, the installer and the customer agree on what was there. Number them so the notes match.

  1. Meter and service entrance.
  2. Panel exterior, then panel label and directory (cover off only by a qualified person).
  3. Existing or proposed disconnect location.
  4. Outdoor unit site with a tape measure for clearances.
  5. Where the line set will pass through the wall.
  6. Indoor unit location with its nameplate.
  7. Each return grille and filter slot, with size noted.
  8. Condensate termination point.
  9. Attic hatch or crawlspace access.

Worked example: a survey sheet for an assumed house (our own)

Our own example. All values are assumptions for teaching, not measurements and not from a source, except the cited reference limits. Assumed house: one story, closet air handler, attic hatch in the hall, 100 A panel with two open spaces, a 3-ton heat pump proposed to replace an old split system. The sketch below shows the layout and photo points.

Field card: Pre-Installation Site Survey

Look, measure, photograph, then quote.

  • Panel: main and busbar rating, open spaces, load calculation inputs.
  • Disconnect: location, type, in sight of the unit, working space.
  • Outdoor unit: measured clearances against the maker's manual, snow and runoff.
  • Line set: route, total length, rises, wall entry; check the maker's limit.
  • Condensate: 1/8 in per ft slope, termination, secondary pan, float switch.
  • Ducts: leakage, returns, filter size, total external static pressure (about 0.8 in. w.c. is a typical maker maximum).
  • Gas: vent size, combustion air, draft and CO check.
  • Number every photo and match it to the notes.
