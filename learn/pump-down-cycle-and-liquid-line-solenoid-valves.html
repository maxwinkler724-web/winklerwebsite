Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many walk-in coolers do not stop the compressor with the thermostat. The thermostat closes a valve, the compressor pumps the evaporator empty, and a pressure switch shuts the compressor off. This lesson explains that sequence, why it is used, and how to check the valve that makes it work.

The parts of a walk-in are in Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics, and the basic cycle is in Refrigeration Cycle Basics. This page covers only the pump-down control.

How the sequence works

A normally closed solenoid valve sits in the liquid line ahead of the TXV. A low-pressure (LP) control senses suction pressure and sits in the compressor's control circuit. An ACHR News piece supplied by Emerson's flow controls group (formerly Alco) lays out the order of events:

  1. Call for cooling. The box thermostat closes, the solenoid coil is energized and the valve opens. Liquid flows to the TXV and evaporator, and suction pressure rises. When it passes the LP control's cut-in setting, the control closes and the compressor starts.
  2. Box satisfied. The thermostat opens and the solenoid closes. The compressor keeps running and draws the refrigerant out of the evaporator and suction line.
  3. Pump-down ends. Suction pressure falls to the LP control's cut-out setting and the compressor stops. The refrigerant is stored in the condenser and receiver.

Why a pump-down is used

Refrigerant moves toward the coldest part of the system while the compressor is off. If that is the crankcase, liquid mixes into the oil. At startup the oil can foam and wash off the bearings, and liquid in the cylinders can break parts. Copeland's application guidelines for its refrigeration scrolls say a pump-down system should be used to control migration. Its A/C scroll guidelines say off-cycle migration must be held down with heaters, pump-down or solenoid valves, and both documents still call for a crankcase heater above set charge limits. Ferris State professor John Tomczyk, writing in ACHR News, says a heater helps keep refrigerant out of the oil but does not stop migration the way a pump-down does. For the failure side, see Compressor Basics and Failure Modes; for heaters on package units, see RTU Refrigerant Circuit and Compressor Protection.

One limit: Bryan Orr's trade-blog explainer says a pump-down does not suit heat pumps, because in heating mode it would try to pump into the indoor coil. This is a refrigeration technique.

Single pump-down versus recycling pump-down

Tomczyk describes two families. In a recycling (continuous) arrangement the solenoid is wired in series with the box thermostat, so the system pumps down before every off cycle. If anything lets the low side creep back up, the LP control closes again and the compressor restarts. In a single (one-time, non-recycling) arrangement a latching relay keeps the compressor off after the first pump-down until the thermostat calls again. A leaking solenoid, discharge valve or unloader then cannot trigger endless restarts.

Emerson's A/C scroll guidelines call recycling pump-down "not preferred" because of short-cycling risk and prefer a one-time pump-down at the end of the cycle. Emerson's refrigeration scroll guidelines give a limit of 10 starts per hour and warn that cycling shorter than the minimum run time causes progressive oil loss. Walk-ins also tie pump-down to defrost: on electric defrost, Tomczyk notes the pump-down empties the evaporator before the heaters come on. The timing is in Walk-In Defrost: Termination, Fan Delay and Icing.

The normally closed solenoid

Sporlan's solenoid bulletin 30-10 says the normally closed valve is the most common type: it opens when the coil is energized and closes when the coil is de-energized. It ties the maximum opening pressure difference (MOPD) rating to 85 percent of rated voltage with a hot coil, so low coil voltage can leave a valve unable to open even though the coil looks energized. Sporlan also advises a filter or strainer ahead of solenoid valves, because dirt can hold the valve open or stop its plunger.

Diagnosing the valve

Joe Marchese's ACHR News column on liquid line solenoids gives a simple order of checks. Follow your site's electrical safety rules; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

  • Valve will not open. Hold a small screwdriver near the top of the coil. A pull means the coil is energized. No pull: measure voltage at the coil. No or wrong voltage points to the control circuit. Correct voltage with no magnetism means a bad coil. A coil that pulls but leaves the valve closed points to a stuck valve body.
  • Valve leaks by. Refrigerant keeps entering the evaporator after the thermostat opens. On a single-evaporator pump-down the compressor runs continuously or short cycles on the LP control: pressure drops and trips the switch, then creeps back above cut-in and restarts it.
  • Isolating the cause. Marchese's pump-down article says to front-seat the king valve and watch the low side. If the compressor then cycles off and stays off, the solenoid is leaking. If nothing changes, the compressor's internal valves may be bleeding back.

The column adds that a coil removed for a bench test needs a metal rod through its center, since an unloaded coil overheats, and that valves are directional, so confirm the arrow on the body after a replacement.

Worked example: setting the LP control for a walk-in cooler

Marchese's ACHR News articles give the starting points: a pump-down cut-out in the range of 5 to 10 psig, and a cut-in the switch will reach when the solenoid opens. He also says to confirm with the equipment maker, and cites one maker's pair of 15 psig cut-in and 4 psig cut-out as an example only.

Here is the arithmetic for an R-448A cooler held at 35°F, using Honeywell's printed pressure-temperature chart for Solstice N40 (R-448A, document TCS-24-11-EN). R-448A is a blend, so the chart lists a bubble and a dew column, and Marchese's 2006 blend article says to base cut-in on dew point. This is a teaching example, not a setting for a real unit. The pressure-to-temperature idea is in Pressure-Temperature Basics.

  1. Cut-out. Pick 10 psig, inside the 5 to 10 range. The chart gives bubble -30.6°F and dew -19.7°F at 10 psig. Copeland's refrigeration guidelines give 0.3 bar(g) as one minimum for an R-404A scroll, about 4.4 psig (0.3 x 14.5), so 10 psig clears that example. Your compressor's own operating envelope sets the real limit.
  2. What the cut-in must reach. When the solenoid opens, the coil is near box temperature, 35°F. On the chart 63 psig is dew 34.0°F and 67 psig is dew 36.7°F. Interpolate: 63 + 4 x (35.0 - 34.0) / (36.7 - 34.0) = 63 + 4 x 0.37 = about 64.5 psig. The cut-in must sit below that or the compressor never starts.
  3. Choose a cut-in with margin. A coil running about 10°F colder than the box would sit near 25°F. The chart gives dew 23.7°F at 49 psig and 26.0°F at 52 psig, so 50 psig is about 23.7 + (1/3) x 2.3 = 24.5°F. Set the cut-in at 50 psig.
  4. Differential and margin. Cut-in minus cut-out is 50 - 10 = 40 psig. After pump-down, leak-by must lift the low side 40 psig before the compressor restarts on its own. The margin when the valve opens is 64.5 - 50 = 14.5 psig.

Then verify on the system. Marchese says the compressor should cycle off at cut-out, possibly after one or two extra short runs, and should not cycle continuously. The unit's literature has the final word on the numbers.

Field card: Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves

Thermostat closes the valve, compressor empties the coil, LP switch stops it.

  • Normally closed solenoid: energized opens, de-energized closes.
  • Pump-down keeps liquid out of the crankcase during the off cycle.
  • Single pump-down stays off until the next call; recycling pump-down restarts if the low side creeps up.
  • Short cycling on the LP switch suggests the solenoid (or compressor valves) is leaking by.
  • No pull at the coil: check voltage at the coil before replacing anything.
  • Cut-out is commonly 5 to 10 psig; the cut-in must be reachable when the valve opens. Use the maker's values and the refrigerant's chart (dew point for blends).
  • Never jumper the LP switch.
