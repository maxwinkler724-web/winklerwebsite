HVAC for Small Observatories and Telescope Domes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most buildings are conditioned for people. A telescope dome is conditioned for the air the telescope looks through. The goal is nearly the opposite of comfort: keep the dome interior as close as possible to the outdoor air temperature at night, and keep every warm thing away from the light path.

Why a dome should match the night air

Turbulence blurs a star image. Part of that turbulence is made inside the dome when warm surfaces or warm air mix with cooler air in the beam. Astronomers call this dome seeing. A university lecture page on observing sites says an enclosure must not add turbulence, and that a mirror just 1 degree C warmer than the surrounding air can degrade the seeing to about 1 arcsecond, while a cooler mirror is acceptable. A university handout on atmospheric seeing makes a related point: a telescope that has not reached outdoor temperature shows a "boiling" effect, and it suggests letting it cool for at least an hour.

A peer-reviewed analysis of dome seeing at a large national-observatory telescope found the worst dome seeing when the mirror was warmer than the dome air and the dome air warmer than outdoors, and little degradation when the mirror was colder. Its authors suggested a lower air-conditioning set point, weighed against condensation risk, and linked summer degradation partly to heat leaking into the dome from other parts of the building. Those findings come from a very large dome, so use them as direction, not as a design number for a small one.

For an apprentice, the takeaway is simple: our job is to remove stored daytime heat before dusk and to stop new heat from entering at night.

Daytime cooling and venting

A dome sits in the sun all day. The metal skin, the floor and the telescope mount soak up heat and give it back slowly at night. A national observatory's thermal control procedures describe actively cooling the main mirror during the day toward the temperature expected for the coming night, and running the dome air handlers and floor cooling to hold the building cool. A university observatory FAQ describes the opposite, low-cost approach for a small dome: no temperature control, with fans circulating outside air so the optics settle at ambient temperature.

Both ideas fit a small dome in practice, and the choice is ours to weigh with the owner:

  • Passive and vented. Light exterior color, an insulated shell, low inlet vents near the floor and an exhaust fan or high vent near the top. Opening the slit and vents before dusk flushes the warm air out.
  • Cooled by day. A small split or packaged unit pre-cools the dome interior through the afternoon, then is shut off before observing so its supply air does not stir the beam. A lecture page on observing sites mentions reflective paint and refrigerating the dome interior as ways to fight daytime solar heating.

The national observatory page also notes that the dome opens about an hour before sunset with the slit facing away from the sun, so no direct sunlight enters. We borrow that habit: open early, but keep the slit off the sun.

One lesson from the large-dome paper applies at any size. Air-conditioning supply and return vents were linked to a peak in dome seeing at the matching compass direction, and the authors recommended deflectors. Keep supply air from blowing across the telescope, and turn cooling equipment off or shield it during observing.

Keep the warm room out of the telescope space

Observers need a warm place to sit, and computers, cameras and power supplies give off heat. If that room shares air with the dome, warm air leaks in. Our approach:

  • Build the control room as its own insulated, air-sealed space with a closing door, not an open corner of the dome.
  • Condition it with its own equipment, and discharge its exhaust and equipment heat outdoors, away from the dome vents and slit.
  • Put heat-producing electronics in the warm room where possible and run cables through sealed penetrations.
  • Keep the door closed. A door opening lets a pulse of warm air into the telescope space.

The large-dome paper blamed summer degradation partly on "excess heat leaking into the dome from other parts of the building," which is exactly this path.

Dehumidifying without creating condensation

Optics are cold at dusk and can fall below the dew point of the dome air. The same national observatory page closes the glycol cooling valve when the mirror gets within about 7 degrees of the dew point, to prevent condensation. The large-dome paper noted average relative humidity near 60 percent and warned that colder dome air raises condensation risk.

The practical rules are these. Dry the dome air while it is closed, with a dehumidifier sized from the humidity lessons linked below. Keep the telescope covered and the dome closed in damp weather. Then open the dome when outdoor dew point is lower than the optics temperature, because opening lets outdoor air in. A rising outdoor humidity is the cue to close up. The theory is in our psychrometrics-based lessons, and the museum lesson covers dew point on cold surfaces.

Planetarium dome theaters: the contrast

A planetarium dome is an audience room. The needs are quiet, even air and comfort for seated people in the dark. A university planetarium page describes insulating and sealing the walls behind the dome with foam that both insulates and suppresses echoes, and placing air returns low at the back corners to improve airflow. Quiet low-velocity supply and crowd loads are covered in our theater lessons. Projection equipment and the audience add heat that we remove with ordinary comfort cooling. A telescope dome has no such needs: we want little air movement near the optics at night, and no people heat at all.

Worked example: flushing a small dome (our own example)

These are our own assumptions, chosen to show the arithmetic. They are not measurements from any site.

  • Dome interior air volume: 4,000 cubic feet.
  • At dusk the dome air is 12 degrees F warmer than outdoors.
  • Air density 0.075 lb per cubic foot and specific heat 0.24 BTU per lb per degree F, standard values for room-temperature air.
  • Steel dome skin and mount in contact with the air: 3,000 lb, specific heat 0.12 BTU per lb per degree F, averaging 8 degrees F warmer than outdoors.

Step 1, heat in the air. Air mass = 4,000 x 0.075 = 300 lb. Heat = 300 x 0.24 x 12 = 864 BTU.

Step 2, heat in the steel. 3,000 x 0.12 x 8 = 2,880 BTU. The steel holds more than three times as much heat as the air, which is why it keeps warming the air after a flush.

Step 3, vent airflow. With good mixing, each full air change removes about 63 percent of the remaining temperature difference. Three air changes leave about 5 percent: 12 x 0.05 = 0.6 degrees F. To get three changes in 20 minutes we need 4,000 x 3 = 12,000 cubic feet in 20 minutes, which is 600 cfm.

Step 4, starting heat rate. The standard sensible relation is heat in BTU per hour = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference. At the start: 1.08 x 600 x 12 = 7,776 BTU per hour. It falls as the difference shrinks.

Step 5, daytime cooling of the steel. To remove the 2,880 BTU over a 4-hour afternoon, we need 2,880 / 4 = 720 BTU per hour of net cooling, a very small load. In real life the shell gains sun heat too, so this is a floor, not a unit size.

Step 6, dew point check. Suppose the closed dome sits at 75 degrees F and 60 percent humidity, which has a dew point of about 60 degrees F by a standard psychrometric chart. If the mirror cools to 55 degrees F in the night, it is below that dew point and will fog when the dome opens to humid air. A dehumidifier that holds the dome closer to 45 percent humidity lowers the dew point to roughly 52 degrees F, keeping the mirror above it. These dew points are chart readings under our assumed conditions.

Field card: HVAC for Small Observatories and Telescope Domes

Match the night air, keep warm things out, keep optics above dew point.

  • Dome seeing comes from warm surfaces and warm air in the beam.
  • Pre-cool or flush the dome before dusk, then stop equipment that stirs air during observing.
  • Seal and insulate the control room, and exhaust its heat outdoors.
  • Dry the dome while closed, and open only when outdoor dew point is below optics temperature.
  • A planetarium wants quiet comfort air for people; a telescope dome wants still, cool air and no people heat.
