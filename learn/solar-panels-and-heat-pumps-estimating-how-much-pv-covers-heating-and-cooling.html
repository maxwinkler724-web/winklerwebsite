Solar Panels and Heat Pumps: Estimating How Much PV Covers Heating and Cooling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

"How many solar panels do I need to run a heat pump?" has a real answer, but it takes two steps: estimate how much electricity the heat pump uses in a year, then estimate how much a kilowatt of panels makes in a year. This lesson shows both steps with the arithmetic, and it is honest about what the math leaves out.

Step 1: The heat pump's yearly electricity

You need two loads and two ratings. The loads are the heat your house needs in a heating season and the heat it must shed in a cooling season, both in Btu. A load calculation or a past-bills analysis gives these; see Heat Load Calculation: An Intro to Manual J. The ratings are on the equipment data sheet; see What Does a SEER2 Rating Mean? for what each one means.

Federal test rules (10 CFR 430, Appendix M, read here in the Cornell LII copy) define the older SEER and HSPF as Btu divided by watt-hours. SEER is the heat removed in the cooling season divided by the electricity used. HSPF is the space heating delivered in the heating season divided by the electricity used. The text we read covers the original SEER and HSPF, and says the newer Appendix M1 applies to SEER2 and HSPF2 claims made from January 1, 2023. We did not read M1 itself, so we treat SEER2 and HSPF2 as the same kind of ratio, scored with the newer test method. Rearranged, that gives two formulas:

  • Heating kWh = heating load in Btu ÷ HSPF2 ÷ 1,000
  • Cooling kWh = cooling load in Btu ÷ SEER2 ÷ 1,000

The divide by 1,000 turns watt-hours into kilowatt-hours. Some data sheets give a COP (coefficient of performance) instead of HSPF2. COP compares heat out to electricity in, both in the same units. The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatt-hour as 3,412 Btu, so COP is roughly HSPF2 ÷ 3.412, and heating kWh = heating Btu ÷ (3,412 × COP). An HSPF2 of 8.0 is a COP of about 2.3.

These ratings are scored across a standard test season, not your house on your coldest week. The U.S. Department of Energy describes a heat pump as moving heat from a cool space to a warm one using electricity, rather than making heat from fuel. Your real figure moves with the weather, the ducts and the install. Treat the result as an estimate, not a bill.

Step 2: What a kilowatt of panels makes

NREL (the National Renewable Energy Laboratory) publishes a free tool called PVWatts. Its page says it estimates the energy production of grid-connected PV systems anywhere in the world, and that homeowners, installers and others can use it. You enter an address, system size, roof tilt and direction, and losses, and it returns production estimates. Version 8 uses NREL's weather data set. Its manual (Version 5) sets default system losses at 14 percent, covering things like soiling, shading, wiring and downtime, and the default array is 4 kW.

The number you want from PVWatts is annual kWh for each kW of panels, for your address, roof tilt and roof direction. We did not run PVWatts for this page, and we could not find a New Jersey kWh-per-kW figure from NREL or a state agency to quote. So the example below uses an illustrative 1,100 kWh per kW per year. That is the production ratio of 1.1 shown for New Jersey in a state table on the EnergySage solar marketplace site, which does not name a source for it. Think of it as a placeholder. Replace it with your own PVWatts result; a shaded or north-facing roof will be lower than a clear south-facing one.

Worked example

These inputs are assumptions chosen for the example, not a typical house: a heating load of 50 million Btu per season, a cooling load of 18 million Btu per season, an HSPF2 of 8.0, a SEER2 of 16, a PV yield of 1,100 kWh per kW per year (the placeholder above), and 400 watt panels.

  1. Heating electricity. 50,000,000 Btu ÷ 8.0 = 6,250,000 Wh = 6,250 kWh.
  2. Cooling electricity. 18,000,000 Btu ÷ 16 = 1,125,000 Wh = 1,125 kWh.
  3. Heat pump total. 6,250 + 1,125 = 7,375 kWh per year.
  4. PV size. 7,375 kWh ÷ 1,100 kWh per kW = 6.70 kW of panels.
  5. Panel count. 400 W is 0.4 kW. 6.70 ÷ 0.4 = 16.8, so round up to 17 panels (17 × 0.4 = 6.8 kW).

Check the sensitivity. If your PVWatts result were 1,300 kWh per kW, the same load needs 7,375 ÷ 1,300 = 5.67 kW, or 14.2 panels, so 15. A swing of 200 kWh per kW moved the answer by two panels. That is why the PV figure should be yours, not a placeholder.

Also note what this answer covers: only the heat pump. Lights, appliances, hot water and an electric car are extra. And the heat pump replaces a fuel use: if you switch from gas or oil, the old fuel use goes away, but you still need to compare costs. See Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Jersey Home. Cold weather also lowers a heat pump's real efficiency; see Cold-Climate Heat Pumps, and note that backup electric heat strips use more kWh than the ratings assume.

The seasonal mismatch

By definition, HSPF2 measures the heating season, so most heating electricity is used in winter, when days are short. Panels make most of their energy in the longer, sunnier months. That is our own reasoning from how the ratings are defined, and PVWatts shows the monthly output so you can see the shape for your roof. The yearly totals can match while the months do not.

Net metering, as New Jersey sources describe it

Net metering is how the grid can act as storage on paper: summer surplus becomes credits that offset winter use. A New Jersey Board of Public Utilities (BPU) staff notice dated November 8, 2024 describes it as crediting customers at the full retail rate for each kilowatt-hour a Class I renewable system makes, up to their total use over an annualized period, with leftover credits paid at the supplier's avoided cost of wholesale power. The same notice says staff found the state passed the 5.8 percent net metering threshold in Energy Year 2024 and started a proceeding to recommend an updated mechanism for new customers. One utility's net metering page describes a 12-month contract year with a true-up at the end, after which remaining credits are valued and the balance resets to zero.

We read those pages, not the current rules. Terms for new systems may differ from what we read, so check the BPU and NJ Clean Energy pages and your utility before you rely on net metering in your math.

Do the house first

Every kWh of load you remove is a panel you do not need. Air leaks, thin attic insulation and leaky ducts raise the heating load in Step 1. An audit measures them; see What Happens in a Home Energy Audit. Fix what it finds, re-run the load, and size the heat pump and panels after that.

About incentives

Incentives, rebates and credits for heat pumps and solar change often, and rules differ by program and date. This page gives none. Check current federal, state and utility program pages before you decide, and get written quotes from licensed contractors.

Field card: Solar Panels and Heat Pumps

Loads over ratings gives kWh. kWh over yield gives kW.

  • Heating kWh = heating Btu ÷ HSPF2 ÷ 1,000.
  • Cooling kWh = cooling Btu ÷ SEER2 ÷ 1,000.
  • COP is about HSPF2 ÷ 3.412 (1 kWh = 3,412 Btu).
  • PV kW = yearly kWh ÷ your PVWatts kWh per kW-year; panels = kW ÷ panel kW, rounded up.
  • Use your own PVWatts result, not a placeholder.
  • Winter heating and summer sun do not line up; net metering rules can change.
  • Audit and seal the house first; this page quotes no incentives.
