Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach

A plate heat exchanger packs a lot of surface into a small frame, which is why it is so efficient and why a thin layer of deposit hurts it so much. You can usually tell it is fouling without opening it.

What you are measuring

Four readings tell most of the story, and they are only meaningful together:

  • Approach temperature. How close the leaving temperature of one fluid gets to the entering temperature of the other. A clean unit gets close; as deposits build, the same flows deliver a wider gap.
  • Delta-T on each side. With the same fluids, the heat one side gives up should roughly match what the other gains. If the two do not agree, suspect a bad sensor, a wrong flow assumption, heat loss or an internal leak.
  • Pressure drop across each side at the same flow. Rising drop at constant flow means narrower passages.
  • Flow. Without it the other numbers are ambiguous, and low flow makes a clean unit look dirty.

Reading the combination

  • Approach widening and pressure drop rising at the same flow: deposits or plugging.
  • Approach widening but pressure drop normal: a thin film, an air pocket, low flow on one side, or a control issue.
  • Pressure drop high but approach normal: debris at the inlet, a partly closed valve, or a strainer.
  • Hot-side and cold-side delta-T disagree: instrument, flow or leakage question.

Baselines

You can only tell something changed if you know what clean looked like. Record approach, delta-Ts, flows and pressure drops at commissioning or right after cleaning, and compare at similar flow and load. Two published examples show how others set triggers: one manufacturer (SWEP) says a pressure-drop increase of 30 percent or more over the expected clean value indicates cleaning is needed, and a trade article says fouling can cut heat transfer by as much as 30 percent in narrow-channel gasketed units. Those are labeled examples. For scale, Alfa Laval says plate exchangers can reach approaches as close as 1 °F, so a very small clean approach is possible; what matters is your own unit’s baseline. Your unit’s data sheet and your site’s own rules decide your trigger.

What causes fouling

  • Scale from hard or poorly treated water, especially on hotter surfaces. The same manufacturer notes high temperature, hard water and high pH raise the risk.
  • Particles such as rust, pipe scale and weld debris. A missing or clogged strainer is a classic cause.
  • Biological growth in open or poorly treated loops, such as those fed by a cooling tower.
  • Corrosion products from treatment problems.
  • Trapped air, which is not a deposit but blanks off surface area.

Cleaning, in general terms

Clean-in-place circulates a cleaning solution through the unit without taking the plate pack apart. A trade article describes the solution entering at the bottom and leaving at the top so foam and gas rise out and air is not trapped, with periodic flow reversal to move chemical into dead zones. The chemistry depends on the deposit and on the plate and gasket materials, so the manufacturer’s instructions and the cleaning-fluid supplier govern. Cleaners can burn skin and eyes and mixing products can release dangerous gas, so use the safety data sheet and PPE. Opening a gasketed unit, rebuilding it and pressure testing it are jobs for trained people following the manufacturer’s sequence.

Before blaming the exchanger

A capacity shortfall can come from controls, flow, air or a valve that is not moving. Rule those out first. Keep strainers clean, keep treatment current, vent air at high points after any drain-down, and never exceed the nameplate pressure or temperature. Isolate both sides and verify zero energy before opening anything, because each side is a separate circuit. See Heat Exchanger Basics.

Printable checklist

  • Record inlet and outlet temperature on both sides at similar flow and load
  • Record flow and inlet/outlet pressure on each side
  • Calculate approach and compare with your baseline
  • Check hot-side and cold-side delta-T for agreement
  • Strainers clean; pressure drop noted
  • Air vents working; no air pockets
  • No gasket weeps or signs of cross-leakage
  • Relief valves unobstructed
  • Decide: monitor, schedule cleaning, or isolate and escalate

Field card: Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach

You can usually see fouling in the numbers.

  • Approach, delta-T, pressure drop and flow work as a set.
  • Rising pressure drop at the same flow means narrowing passages.
  • Low flow can imitate fouling; check it first.
  • You need a clean baseline to see change.
  • Cleaning chemistry must match plates and gaskets; follow the maker.
