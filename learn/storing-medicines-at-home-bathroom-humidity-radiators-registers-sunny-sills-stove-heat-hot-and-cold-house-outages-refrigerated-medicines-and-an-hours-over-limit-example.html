Storing Medicines at Home: Heat, Humidity and Your Heating and Cooling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Medicine labels carry storage directions, and your heating and cooling system shapes the temperature and humidity where those bottles sit. This lesson is general home safety from an HVAC point of view. It is not medical advice, and it gives no dosing guidance. For any question about a specific medicine, ask a pharmacist or doctor.

Why the steamy bathroom cabinet is a poor spot

A federal health library page on storing medicines says to keep them in a cool, dry place and gives a dresser drawer or a kitchen cabinet away from heat sources as examples. It advises against the bathroom, because humidity from showers, baths and sinks can reduce a medicine's strength or cause spoilage before the expiration date. It also says heat, air, light and moisture may damage medicines [1].

From the HVAC side, a bathroom is often the wettest room in the house. A hot shower puts a lot of water vapor into a small space, and a small exhaust fan may not clear it quickly. Our own rule of thumb: if the mirror fogs, the medicine cabinet is getting that same damp air. See condensation and indoor humidity for how moisture moves around a home.

Heat sources to keep medicines away from

The same federal page says to keep medicines away from heat sources. At home, our own list of common trouble spots is:

  • Radiators and baseboard heaters. Surfaces run hot all winter, and a shelf above one catches rising warm air.
  • Supply registers and grilles. A bottle on a floor register or a shelf in the airflow gets blown with warm air in heating season.
  • Sunny windowsills. Sun through glass heats whatever sits there, even on a cold day.
  • The top of the refrigerator. Warm air from the condenser side rises there, and it is near the kitchen heat.
  • The stove and oven, and the cabinet beside or above them. Cooking heat builds up in closed cabinets.

A federal drug safety agency page on drug safety after a disaster says temperature-sensitive medicines may lose potency when they are not stored at the recommended temperature, and that you should follow the labeled storage instructions [2]. Our own simple fix: pick one cool, dry, dark spot away from all of the above, such as a bedroom drawer on an interior wall, and keep medicines in their original containers, which the library page also advises [1].

Follow the label, and ask when unsure

The label or package insert tells you the storage temperature for that product. Read it, follow it, and ask a pharmacist about any specific storage instructions [1]. If a medicine may have been too hot, too cold or too damp, do not guess. A pharmacist or doctor can say whether it is still usable, and the federal drug safety agency page above says the same: check storage instructions, or contact a pharmacist, health care provider or the maker [2].

Worked example (our own): hours over a label limit

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement. Your cabinet, oven and label will differ.

Assumptions (all our own):

  • A closed kitchen cabinet next to the oven sits at 74 F before baking.
  • The label says to store at or below 77 F. (Read your own label; this limit is only for the example.)
  • While the oven runs, the cabinet air warms 3 F per hour. The oven runs 3 hours.
  • After the oven is off, the cabinet cools 2 F per hour until it is back at 74 F.

Step 1, warming. After 3 hours the cabinet is at 74 + (3 x 3) = 74 + 9 = 83 F. It crosses 77 F after (77 - 74) / 3 = 1 hour. So it is above the limit from hour 1 to hour 3: 3 - 1 = 2 hours.

Step 2, cooling. From 83 F down to 77 F is 83 - 77 = 6 F, at 2 F per hour: 6 / 2 = 3 hours above the limit while cooling. It reaches 74 F about (83 - 74) / 2 = 4.5 hours after the oven goes off.

Step 3, total. 2 + 3 = 5 hours above the limit for one baking session, with a peak of 83 F, which is 6 F over the limit. If that happens three times in a week, 3 x 5 = 15 hours over the limit. The fix costs nothing: move the medicine to a bedroom drawer. The check is a thermometer, covered below.

When the air conditioning fails or the heat goes out

A closed house heats up in a summer outage and cools down in a winter one. Our own point: indoor temperature drifts toward the outdoor temperature, and the drift is slow in a tight, insulated home and faster in a leaky one. Our lesson on how fast a house cools when the heat stops shows the math. The upstairs of a hot house is usually the worst place for a medicine shelf, and a room on the cold north side is the coldest in winter.

The drug safety page above notes that high temperatures can reduce or destroy the effectiveness of medicines and suggests considering replacement of medicines exposed to excessive heat [2]. Whether a particular medicine is still fine after a hot or cold stretch is a question for a pharmacist or doctor, not for a thermostat reading. For people, a federal disaster guide says that with no air conditioning you should contact the local health department or find an air-conditioned place, and that in the cold you should have a safe heat source and follow the maker's instructions for any heater [3]. Never run a generator or fuel heater indoors; see carbon monoxide during winter outages.

Refrigerated medicines in a power outage

Keep the refrigerator door closed as much as possible. A federal food safety page says a refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours if the door stays unopened, and that you should keep an appliance thermometer in it [4]. That figure is about food, not medicine, so do not treat it as a safe window for any medicine. A federal disaster guide says that if the power is out for a day or more, refrigerated medicine should be thrown away unless the label says otherwise, and that if a life depends on a refrigerated drug, use it only until a new supply is available [3][5]. That is a decision to make with your pharmacist or doctor. Call before the outage if you can: the federal drug safety agency page suggests asking your pharmacist or health care provider about a plan for refrigerated medicines [2].

Mail-order packages left in heat or cold

We found no federal page with a number for how long a parcel may sit outdoors, so we give none. Our own common sense: a box on a porch or in a mailbox can get far hotter in summer sun, or colder in winter, than the room where you store the medicine. Bring packages inside promptly, read the label and package directions, and if a parcel sat out in extreme weather, or it says to keep refrigerated and arrived warm, call the pharmacy before using it.

A simple min-max thermometer in the storage spot

Our own tip: put a small min-max thermometer, the kind that remembers the highest and lowest reading since you last reset it, where the medicines are kept. Check it weekly for a month, then after any heat wave, cold snap or outage. Compare the highest and lowest numbers with the label, not with a general rule. If the spot swings outside the label range, move the medicines to a better spot and ask a pharmacist about anything that may have been affected. A refrigerator thermometer does the same job for a fridge, and the food safety page above recommends one [4].

Field card: Storing medicines at home

Cool, dry, away from heat, and follow the label.

  • Skip the steamy bathroom cabinet; humidity can damage medicines [1].
  • Keep medicines away from radiators, registers, sunny sills, the top of the fridge and the stove.
  • Keep them in the original container and read the label temperature.
  • Not sure after heat, cold or an outage? Ask a pharmacist or doctor.
  • Outage: keep the fridge closed and call a pharmacist or doctor about refrigerated medicines.
  • Our own tip: a min-max thermometer in the storage spot shows what really happened.
  • Our own example: 3 h of oven heat put a cabinet over a 77 F limit for 5 hours.
