Backflow Prevention on HVAC Makeup Water: RPZ vs Double Check and NJ Testing Rules

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A boiler, chiller or cooling tower makeup line is a pipe that joins the building's drinking water to a system full of treated, stagnant or warm water. If the pressure ever flips, that water can run backward into the tap. Backflow preventers exist to stop that, and the type you need depends on how dangerous the water on the other side is.

Check the local authority

Check the local authority. Backflow rules differ by water supplier and inspector and change over time. This page is general education from sources read in 2026, not legal advice. Choosing, installing, replacing and testing these assemblies is licensed plumbing work or work by a certified tester. A technician, apprentice or building owner should not install, adjust, bypass or test one without the proper New Jersey license or certification. Confirm every requirement with your water supplier, local plumbing subcode official and the license issuer.

Why makeup lines are cross-connections

The EPA Cross-Connection Control Manual defines a cross-connection as a link connecting a source of pollution with a drinking water supply. A makeup or fill line fits that exactly. One side is the city main. The other is a boiler, chiller loop or tower basin that may hold inhibitors, glycol, biocides and rust. The manual says reversed flow comes from two causes. Backsiphonage is a vacuum pulling water backward, for example when a main breaks. Backpressure is the downstream system holding more pressure than the supply,. The manual reports real cases: boiler water and chemicals reached drinking water through leaking check valves on boiler feed lines, and glycol from an air conditioning tank reached a medical center's supply.

The New Jersey Department of Community Affairs (DCA) Bulletin 99-2 lists boilers among the places where cross-connections are likely. The fill valve just downstream is covered in our boiler fill valve lesson.

Hazard level picks the device

The EPA manual ties device choice to hazard. It describes reduced pressure principle devices on high-hazard sites and double check valves on low-to-medium hazard sites. Ohio's backflow manual says a double check may be used only at low hazard, and lists chemically treated boilers and chillers under the reduced pressure row. It says boilers with no additives can use an intermediate atmospheric vent device, but where conditioning chemicals are added the connection needs an air gap or a reduced pressure preventer. That is Ohio's code, not New Jersey's, so use it for the logic only.

Once chemicals, glycol or biocide go into the loop, treat it as high hazard. See our closed-loop treatment lesson for why those chemicals are there.

Three ways to protect the supply

  • Air gap. The manual says an air gap should be twice the supply pipe diameter and never less than one inch. It has no moving parts, but the manual says it still has to be inspected as often as mechanical devices.
  • Double check assembly. Two spring-loaded checks in one body, with test cocks and shutoff valves so each check can be tested (EPA manual). New Jersey's rule for approved physical connections says each check must hold at least a one psi pressure differential in the direction of flow.
  • Reduced pressure zone (RPZ) assembly. Two checks with a relief valve between them. The space between is the zone.

How an RPZ works

The EPA manual and a Febco sheet on the maker's site say the relief valve keeps the zone at least two psi below supply. Febco adds that during normal flow the zone sits at least 5 psi below inlet while the relief valve stays closed. New Jersey's rule on approved physical connections (N.J.A.C. 7:10-10.3) uses that 5 psi figure too.

If supply pressure collapses or reverses, the second check closes against the back pressure. If the first check fouls and leaks, zone pressure creeps up toward supply pressure. When the gap falls to 2 psi, the relief valve opens and dumps the zone to atmosphere, so even a failed check cannot push downstream water into the supply. The device protects by spilling water on purpose.

Why the relief valve discharges, and why it needs a drain

EPA says a discharge from the relief port signals a malfunction and that plugging the port must never be allowed. Ohio's manual is more specific: constant discharge points to a fault, while occasional discharge is usually normal. Watts Series 009 literature (a distributor-hosted PDF, cited as text) says a dead-end service such as boiler feed or cooling tower makeup can spit now and then, and that adding a spring-loaded, rubber-seated check upstream can reduce it.

Because water will come out, it needs somewhere to go. New Jersey's rule requires an air gap at the relief drain at least as large as the relief port. The Watts literature says to pipe a drain with an air gap so discharge is visible, to keep the drain full line size, never to pipe it directly into a ditch, sewer or sump, and never to install it where it could be submerged. A Zurn Wilkins sheet shows the assembly mounted 12 to 30 inches above the floor drain, and New Jersey bars an approved physical connection from a pit or vault.

Annual testing and who may do it

DCA Bulletin 99-2 says testable backflow preventers are tested before final inspection and then annually, except at one- and two-family dwellings. It says inspectors cannot do the tests themselves; the owner hires a qualified tester, who must be certified by an agency recognized by the NJDEP Bureau of Safe Drinking Water. Ohio and Watts also give a 12-month interval, and Watts adds more often if conditions warrant.

For approved physical connections, N.J.A.C. 7:10-10.6 puts testing on the facility owner and calls for a tester holding a certification from an agency NJDEP has approved. An RPZ test checks check-valve tightness, shutoff valve tightness and the relief valve opening pressure. EPA says the relief valve should open no lower than 2 psid below inlet pressure. A double check is tested with a differential gauge. The rule gives owners 30 days to repair and retest a failed device. NJDEP form BWSE-CITR-IQ records the tester's certifying agency, ID number and expiration date.

N.J.A.C. 7:10-10.8 sets the rules for the certifying agencies: at least 26 hours of lecture and six hours of practice testing, a written exam passed at 70 percent or higher, a practical exam passed at 80 percent or higher, and recertification at least every three years.

How the New Jersey pieces fit together

N.J.A.C. 7:10-10 is NJDEP's rule on backflow prevention devices. Its first section says it covers permits, design and testing for connections to unapproved or doubtful water sources, and sets a framework for water suppliers to run cross-connection control programs that require containment devices at hazard facilities. It works alongside the Plumbing Subcode of the Uniform Construction Code. The subcode text we read (N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15) adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code and points its backflow sections back to N.J.A.C. 7:10-10. Which device your job needs, and whether your water supplier wants a containment device at the meter, is decided locally. Ask the plumbing subcode official.

Worked example: pressure left at the fill valve

Our own example. All supply pressures and the loss choice below are assumptions to show the arithmetic, not data for a real building.

Cited inputs: EPA says pressure loss across an RPZ typically averages 10 to 20 psi in normal operation, depending on size and flow. A Febco specification sheet plots its headloss charts from 0 to 20 psi, over flow ranges of 15 to 30 gpm for the 3/4 inch size and 30 to 60 gpm for the 1 inch size. Neither source gives one number for one flow, so the loss values below are our own picks inside the EPA range. Read the maker's curve for the actual assembly and flow.

Assumptions: the unit runs at a flow near the top of its chart, so we take 20 psi for the loss in the weak case. Ignore pipe friction and elevation. The fill valve is set to 15 psi, and the boiler needs about 16 psi cold fill for a 30 ft building (from our boiler fill valve lesson, using the Taco and Watts rule).

  1. Good supply, 60 psi at the meter. Take the middle of EPA's range, 15 psi. Remaining: 60 − 15 = 45 psi at the fill valve inlet. That is far above its 15 psi setting, so the fill valve has plenty to work with.
  2. Weak supply, 40 psi at the meter, loss of 20 psi. Remaining: 40 − 20 = 20 psi.
  3. Check against the setting: 20 − 15 = 5 psi of margin over a 15 psi valve, but only 20 − 16 = 4 psi over the 16 psi needed for the 30 ft building. A small dip in street pressure or a clogged strainer could leave the system unable to fill.

The lesson: an RPZ costs real pressure, so check supply pressure and the maker's chart before one goes on a tall building.

Field card: Backflow Prevention on HVAC Makeup Water

Treated loop means high hazard. High hazard means RPZ or air gap.

  • A makeup line is a cross-connection between drinking water and a system with water of unknown quality.
  • EPA: RPZ for high hazard, double check for low-to-medium hazard.
  • RPZ zone stays at least 2 psi below supply; relief valve opens to dump the zone if a check fails.
  • RPZ pressure loss is typically 10 to 20 psi (EPA). Read the maker's curve.
  • Relief discharge needs a visible air gap and a full-size drain. Never plug the port.
  • Air gap: twice the pipe diameter, at least one inch (EPA).
  • NJ: test before final inspection and annually (DCA Bulletin 99-2) by a tester certified through an NJDEP-approved agency.
  • Installing and testing need the proper license or certification.
