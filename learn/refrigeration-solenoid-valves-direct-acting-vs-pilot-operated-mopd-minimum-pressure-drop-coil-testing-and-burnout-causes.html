Refrigeration Solenoid Valves: Direct-Acting vs Pilot-Operated, MOPD, Coil Testing and Burnout

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A solenoid valve is a coil and a plug: power the coil, the plug moves. But the choice between a direct-acting and a pilot-operated design decides whether the valve opens at all on a given job, and the coil is the part that fails first. This lesson is about the valve itself. The pump-down sequence is in Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves, so we will not repeat it.

Direct-acting versus pilot-operated

In a direct-acting valve the coil's magnetic pull lifts the plug straight off the seat. The force needed grows with port area and with the pressure difference across the valve, so a trade-press article on solenoid selection explains that direct-acting valves are used for small ports or limited pressure differences. A maker's application document puts the limit at ports below 1/4 inch. The same article says refrigeration service usually wants a rating of at least 300 psi, which holds a direct-acting port to roughly 1/8 inch.

A pilot-operated valve lets the line pressure do the heavy lifting. The coil only opens a small pilot port. That relieves the pressure on top of a diaphragm or piston, and the higher inlet pressure underneath pushes the main element off the large port. That is how a coil of a few watts can open a big liquid, suction or hot gas line. The price is a requirement: something has to push the piston open.

Minimum pressure drop and MOPD

Two ratings sit on the valve and they limit it from opposite sides.

  • Minimum pressure drop. A pilot-operated valve needs some difference between inlet and outlet to hold the main port open; a return spring is trying to close it. A trade-press article says an oversized piloted valve at too small a drop may chatter, chug or open only partway, giving very low flow. A maker's datasheet for one family of valves lists no minimum for its direct-acting models, 0.03 bar (about 0.4 psi, our conversion) for its smaller pilot-operated models and 0.2 bar (about 2.9 psi, our conversion) for its piston-type pilot-operated models. Always read the number for the valve in your hand.
  • MOPD, maximum operating pressure differential. This is the largest difference between inlet and outlet that the coil can still open the valve against. A maker's application document gives an example: 250 psig at the inlet and 50 psig at the outlet is a 200 psi difference, so the valve must carry an MOPD of 200 psi or more. Ratings are set with the coil at 85 percent of its rated voltage, and the document says MOPD falls as the coil gets hotter or the voltage drops.

Suction line service is the tricky case, because the allowable drop is tiny. The maker's document says suction valves should have a drop under 2 psid and that piston-type pilot valves often need more than that, so it recommends diaphragm types there. The trade-press article gives 0.25 psid as the most one low-temperature system might allow across a suction solenoid.

Normally closed, normally open and the flow arrow

Normally closed (NC) is the common type: energize the coil and the valve opens, lose power and it closes. A normally open valve does the opposite. Normally open models exist but are less common, and the datasheet we read offers them only in the pilot-operated style.

The body is marked with an arrow or the word IN on the inlet, according to a maker's installation bulletin. Install it so flow goes the marked way. The pilot circuit depends on which side is the high-pressure side, so a valve piped backward is a suspect when it will not open or close. A few bi-flow models are built to pass flow either way, and their datasheet gives flow figures for both directions. Some valves also have a manual lift stem. The bulletin warns that a stem left in the open position keeps the valve from closing.

The coil: voltage, inrush and holding

An AC coil is an inductor. With the plunger out, the air gap is large and the coil draws a surge; once the plunger seats, the current drops. The trade-press article says AC inrush is often about twice the holding current. A maker's datasheet for AC coils, offered in 24 V, 120 V and 208-240 V versions, lists 32 VA inrush and 16 VA holding, with 8 W at holding. We assume those figures apply to a 24 V coil. DC coils draw the same current throughout, according to the article, which is one reason a DC coil usually has a lower MOPD.

The maker's bulletin gives a voltage window of -15 percent to +10 percent on 24 V, 120 V and 220-240 V coils. For a 24 V coil that is 20.4 V to 26.4 V (our arithmetic).

Where we find these valves

  • Liquid line: pump-down and per-unit temperature control, as in the pump-down lesson.
  • Hot gas defrost: the solenoid sends discharge gas into the evaporator. The trade-press article sizes such a valve for a drop on the order of 150 psid. See Hot Gas Defrost in Commercial Refrigeration.
  • Suction stop: a maker document says hot gas defrost also needs a suction solenoid to isolate the compressor side.
  • Capacity control: the same document lists compressor unloading and hot gas bypass as solenoid uses.
  • Heat reclaim: reclaim circuits also use valves to route refrigerant; see Heat Recovery Chillers for the system view.

How to test a solenoid valve

These steps are our own field routine, not a maker procedure, so follow the maker's instructions where they differ. Work in order from the supply toward the valve.

  1. Voltage at the coil. With the call for cooling present, measure across the coil leads. Compare it with the coil label and with the window above. Meter basics are in Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays With a Meter.
  2. Coil resistance. Power off and locked out, disconnect one lead and read ohms. An open reading means a burned-open winding. A near-zero reading suggests a shorted winding. Compare with a known good coil of the same part number.
  3. Magnetic pull. With the coil energized and on the valve, hold a steel screwdriver tip near the coil housing. A pull means current is flowing and a field exists. No pull with proper voltage points to a dead coil. Keep fingers and the screwdriver clear of line-voltage terminals.
  4. Click. A good coil usually clicks as the plunger moves. A hum with no click can mean low voltage, a stuck plunger, or too little pressure difference for a pilot valve.
  5. Temperature drop. If the valve is stuck partly shut, a liquid line may show a noticeably cooler outlet side than inlet side, from refrigerant flashing as it is throttled, and the sight glass may show bubbles. A fully open valve has almost no temperature change across it.

Why coils burn out

  • Wrong voltage. The bulletin names continuous over-voltage above 10 percent and under-voltage beyond 15 percent.
  • Too much pressure difference. The trade-press article lists a difference too high to open against, which keeps current at inrush levels.
  • Energizing a coil off its valve. The article says an AC coil with no iron core in it can draw about four times normal current. The bulletin says never to separate the coil from the valve while energized and to take the coil off before brazing. A missing or damaged plunger tube is the same problem: the magnetic path is broken.
  • Heat. A maker's application document says each 10 degrees C above the coil's limit cuts expected life by half.
  • Wrong coil. Voltage, frequency and valve type must match. The document notes that dual-frequency coils draw more power and run hotter at 50 Hz.

Worked example (ours): the 24 V transformer budget

Assumptions, not field measurements: a 40 VA control transformer. A liquid line solenoid with the AC coil figures above (assumed to apply at 24 V), 32 VA inrush and 16 VA holding. A contactor coil at 10 VA and a relay at 5 VA, both assumed holding values.

Amps. Amps = VA / volts. Inrush: 32 / 24 = 1.33 A. Holding: 16 / 24 = 0.67 A. The transformer is rated 40 / 24 = 1.67 A.

One valve. Holding load = 16 + 10 + 5 = 31 VA, which is 31 / 40 = 78 percent of the rating. It fits. Peak while the valve pulls in = 32 + 10 + 5 = 47 VA, above 40 VA, but only for a moment. A little sag is likely, so the coil must still see at least 20.4 V (85 percent of 24 V).

Add a hot gas solenoid of the same type. Holding load = 16 + 16 + 10 + 5 = 47 VA, over the 40 VA rating continuously. The transformer runs hot and its voltage sags, and the sagging voltage can burn a coil. The fix is a larger transformer, for example 75 VA (75 / 24 = 3.1 A, assuming that size is available), not a bigger fuse; see Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

Worked example (ours): the pilot valve that will not open

Assumption: a piston-type pilot valve requires a minimum drop of 0.2 bar, about 2.9 psi (our conversion), and sits in a line where the two sides measure only 1.0 psi apart. The coil is energized, the voltage is correct, the screwdriver is pulled to the housing, and the plunger clicks. Yet there is no flow and the valve may buzz.

The coil is fine. The pilot port opens, but there is not enough difference to lift the piston: 1.0 psi is less than 2.9 psi. Options: find out why the difference is so small, choose a valve rated for the lower drop (the datasheet we read lists 0.03 bar types and direct-acting types with no minimum), or use a diaphragm or direct-acting valve. Do not replace the coil.

Field card: Refrigeration solenoid valves

Pilot valves need a push, coils need the right volts.

  • Direct-acting: coil lifts the plug. Small ports only.
  • Pilot-operated: coil opens a small port, line pressure lifts the piston. Needs a minimum drop.
  • MOPD is the most difference the valve can open against, rated at 85 percent voltage.
  • Follow the arrow or IN mark. NC valves open when energized.
  • AC inrush is often about twice holding. Size the transformer for both.
  • Check volts, coil ohms, magnetic pull, click, then pressure difference.
  • Never energize a coil off its valve.
