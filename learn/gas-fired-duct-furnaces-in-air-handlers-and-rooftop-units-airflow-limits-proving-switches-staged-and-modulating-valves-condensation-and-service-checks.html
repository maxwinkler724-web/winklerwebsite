Gas-Fired Duct Furnaces in Air Handlers and Rooftop Units

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A duct furnace is a gas burner and heat exchanger built to sit inside an airstream instead of having its own blower. Commercial air handlers and rooftop units use them for the heating section. The air does the cooling work, so the airflow across the exchanger decides whether the heater is safe.

Check your own equipment first

Check the nameplate and manual. Every limit, rise range and clearance below varies by unit. This page teaches the ideas. The nameplate and the installation manual for the unit in front of you are the authority.

How a duct furnace sits in the airstream

In a rooftop unit the supply fan pulls or pushes air across the heat exchanger. Combustion happens inside the exchanger tubes, and the hot combustion gases are kept apart from the air. Our rooftop unit lesson covers the whole box, and our make-up air lesson covers the direct-fired and indirect-fired designs. Here we stay on the indirect gas heating section, the part that looks like a furnace without a blower.

Minimum and maximum airflow

The unit nameplate gives a range of allowed temperature rise, which is outlet air temperature minus inlet air temperature. Rise is set by airflow: for the same burner input, less air means a bigger rise. That gives two limits.

  • Minimum airflow. Too little air and the exchanger metal runs hot, the rise climbs past the maximum, and the high-limit switch opens. Treat every limit trip as a warning, not a nuisance.
  • Maximum airflow. Too much air pulls the rise below the minimum. The air is cooler, the exchanger walls run cool, and flue gas can cool enough to condense.

So duct static pressure, dirty filters, closed dampers and a slow fan all change heating behavior, not just comfort. See our airflow and static pressure lesson for how to measure those.

Airflow proving and high-limit switches

Two different switches protect the section. An airflow proving switch confirms that air is actually moving before the burner is allowed to fire, so a failed fan or closed damper keeps the heat off. A high-limit switch opens if the air leaving the exchanger gets too hot and shuts the gas valve. Do not jumper either one to see if the heat works. A jumpered switch removes the only protection against an overheated exchanger.

Staged and modulating gas valves

Larger sections fire in steps. A staged valve gives fixed fractions of full input, such as a low stage and a high stage. A modulating valve varies the gas flow over a range, so the burner follows the load more closely. The control board sets how much gas is released, and the airflow must still stay above the minimum at every firing rate. Valve construction and lightoff behavior are in our combination gas valve lesson. Gas pressure at the manifold sets the firing rate, so the manifold pressure check below matters at each stage.

Discharge air temperature control

The controller reads a sensor in the supply duct and compares it to a setpoint. With staged heat it adds or drops stages. With modulating heat it adjusts the valve. Cold outdoor air makes the controller work harder, which is why a mixed-air unit with an economizer stuck open can overload the heater. If the discharge reading hunts up and down, look at sensor location and airflow before blaming the valve.

Condensation when cold air hits the exchanger

Burning natural gas makes water vapor. A university combustion text gives a flue gas dew point for natural gas of about 120 to 140 degrees F, depending on excess air. Hot exchanger surfaces stay above that. But when very cold outdoor air crosses the exchanger, metal surfaces can drop below the dew point and the vapor condenses on them. The same text notes that condensing designs are built for it, with products cooled well below the dew point, while non-condensing furnaces deliver warmer air. Whether a given exchanger tolerates condensation is a design question, so read the unit data. A federal energy department guide describes condensing furnaces as sealed-combustion units that vent through a dedicated pipe and capture latent heat. Common ways to reduce the problem include keeping the airflow in range, keeping mixed air from running far colder than the design allows, and following the manufacturer rules for minimum entering air temperature, which are on the unit data.

Venting and combustion air

The university text describes how newer units use a fan to meter combustion air, which keeps excess air in a controlled range. It says at least 5 percent excess air is needed for complete combustion so carbon monoxide does not form. Combustion air openings must stay clear of snow, leaves and bird nests, and flue outlets must be clear and sealed. For openings and sizing rules, see the analyzer lesson linked below. Roof-mounted flue outlets must not sit near fresh air intakes. That is a manufacturer and code item, so check the install manual.

Carbon monoxide matters at work. A federal worker safety fact sheet says CO forms when carbon-based fuels burn incompletely, that early symptoms include headache, dizziness and nausea, and that large amounts can overcome a person in minutes without warning. It tells employers to maintain fuel-burning equipment and ventilate work areas. A federal public health report on a food plant found CO came from gas burners replaced days earlier where ventilation could not clear combustion products. It lists ventilation, routine maintenance and detectors as prevention.

Worked example: temperature rise and minimum airflow

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a duct furnace section with a gas input of 400,000 Btu/h and a thermal efficiency of 80 percent. Assume the nameplate allows a maximum rise of 60 degrees F. Assume the supply fan moves 8,000 cfm. We use the standard-air sensible heat relation, heat (Btu/h) = 1.08 x cfm x rise. The 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb per cubic foot x 60 minutes x 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F.

  1. Heat to the air = 400,000 x 0.80 = 320,000 Btu/h.
  2. Rise at 8,000 cfm = 320,000 / (1.08 x 8,000) = 320,000 / 8,640 = 37.0 degrees F.
  3. If the air entering is 10 degrees F, the discharge air is about 47 degrees F.
  4. Minimum airflow for a 60 degree F maximum rise = 320,000 / (1.08 x 60) = 320,000 / 64.8 = 4,938, so about 4,940 cfm.
  5. Check: at 4,940 cfm, 1.08 x 4,940 x 60 = 320,111, which matches 320,000 within rounding.

So in this example, if the fan drops under about 4,940 cfm, from loaded filters or a closed damper, the rise exceeds 60 degrees F and the high limit should trip. At 4,000 cfm the rise would be 320,000 / 4,320 = 74 degrees F. These are not real nameplate values. Use the nameplate for yours.

Service checks

  • Temperature rise. Measure supply and return (or mixed) air temperatures with the burner steady at full fire. Compare to the nameplate range. If it is high, check airflow first. If it is low, check input and gas pressure.
  • Gas pressure. Read the manifold pressure at each stage with a manometer and compare to the manual. Do not adjust it to fix a rise problem without finding out why.
  • Flue gas. Use a combustion analyzer to read oxygen and carbon monoxide at the flue outlet. See the combustion analyzer lesson.
  • Heat exchanger inspection. A federal energy department guide lists checking the heat exchanger for cracks or leaks in a furnace tune-up. Look for rust streaks, warped tubes, soot and flame rollout, and clean the burners.
  • Safeties. Test the proving switch by blocking or stopping air with the unit in a safe state, and confirm the high-limit wiring is intact and not jumpered.

Field card: Gas-Fired Duct Furnaces in Air Handlers and Rooftop Units

Rise is set by airflow. Protect the exchanger.

  • Heat to air (Btu/h) = 1.08 x cfm x rise (our own standard-air relation).
  • Low airflow gives a high rise and tripped limits. High airflow gives a low rise and condensation risk.
  • Never jumper the proving switch or the high limit.
  • Check nameplate rise range, manifold pressure at each stage, and flue gas CO.
  • Inspect the exchanger for cracks, rust and soot, and keep vent and air openings clear.
