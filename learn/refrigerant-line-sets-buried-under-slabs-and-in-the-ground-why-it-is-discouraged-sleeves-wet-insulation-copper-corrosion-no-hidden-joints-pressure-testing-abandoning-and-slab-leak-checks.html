Refrigerant Line Sets Buried Under Slabs and in the Ground: Why It Is Discouraged

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Sometimes the outdoor unit is on one side of a building and the air handler is on the other, with a slab in between. Burying the copper under the slab or in the yard looks like the easy answer. It is also the choice you cannot undo cheaply, so many installation manuals and local rules push back on it. This lesson explains the reasons and what a tech does when a buried line leaks.

Check the rules that apply to the job

Check the rules that apply to the job. We did not find a federal page that sets one rule for buried refrigerant lines. The limits come from the equipment maker's installation manual, the mechanical code your local inspector enforces, and the project specification. Read all three before you plan a buried run. This page is general education, not a substitute for them.

Why people do it

The usual reasons are a slab-on-grade house with no attic or basement path, or a detached building across a walkway. Some jobs have no other route, so the question becomes how to limit the risk.

Sleeves or conduit: the line is never bare in the ground

Our own rule of thumb: a refrigerant line that must go below grade or through a slab rides inside a continuous sleeve or conduit that is larger than the insulated line, sloped so water drains out rather than collects, and sealed or capped at the ends so soil, water and rodents stay out. The sleeve is there for two jobs: it keeps wet concrete and soil off the insulation, and it gives you a path to pull a replacement line later. A sleeve with no way to drain only turns into a long pipe that holds water against the line. Follow the maker's manual and your local code for the sleeve material and sizing.

Wet insulation is the quiet problem

Cut seams, split slits and open ends can let moisture into line insulation over the years. A federal measurement study on roofing systems found that moisture raised heat flow through insulation, with glass fiber at about 20 percent moisture by volume passing roughly 3 times the heat of its dry value and other materials about 1.5 to 2.4 times (Federal standards bureau building science report). Those were roof boards, not pipe foam, so we use the 3 times figure only as a rough illustration below. The takeaway is that wet insulation stops insulating. On a suction line that means extra heat picked up from the ground, and under a slab you cannot see it, dry it or replace it. For above-ground insulation basics, see the line set insulation lesson.

Copper in soil and concrete

Copper tube is a good choice indoors, but the ground is a harder place. A federal standards bureau underground corrosion circular reports that poorly aerated, wet soils tend to be more corrosive, that corrosive soils contained large amounts of soluble salts and so had low electrical resistivity, and that one earlier study found pipeline corrosion rose with higher total acidity (page numbers 8 to 14 of that report; its findings were mostly for steel, so we apply them to copper with care). A separate federal standards bureau paper on cementing materials notes that fresh concrete and plaster are good electrolytes, that they never fully dry, and that dissimilar metals in building construction are nearly always in electrical contact. In that paper's one case with copper tubing, the copper was the protected metal and the steel tie wires were the ones that corroded. So in a slab, the practical concern is copper touching reinforcing steel or other metal, plus chlorides and wet fill around the pipe. Keep the line inside a sleeve, away from steel, and do not rely on the soil being gentle. Treat the soil conditions at your site as unknown until someone checks.

Leaks you cannot reach

A leak above ground gets found with a leak detector and fixed in an hour. A slab leak means breaking concrete. That is why hidden runs are held to a stricter standard than visible ones.

No joints in hidden runs

Every brazed joint or fitting is a place a leak can start. Our own practice: use one continuous length of tube, factory-coiled and soft, through any hidden section, with all joints at accessible ends, in sight, before the line goes into the sleeve. Use long-radius bends, not elbows, and avoid kinks (see the tubing bending lesson). Do not rely on supports you cannot inspect; for supported runs see the supports and rub-through lesson.

Oil return, length and lift

Buried runs tend to be long, and long runs raise the oil return question. Oil leaves the compressor with the refrigerant and must travel back along the suction line. Line diameter sets the vapor velocity that carries it. Lift (a vertical rise where the line comes up out of a slab or trench) adds a trap where oil can collect, and the liquid line also loses pressure over long runs and rises. Do not guess: use the equipment maker's stated maximum line length and lift and its sizing tables. Our lesson on line set sizing, suction velocity and oil return covers the idea. If your buried route needs more length or lift than the manual allows, the route is wrong, not the manual.

Pressure test before you cover

Pressure test the finished line with dry nitrogen and hold it, with gauges you trust, before anything is poured or backfilled, using the pressure and hold time in the maker's instructions. Temperature changes move the reading, so log ambient temperature; see the nitrogen pressure testing lesson. Photograph the run with a tape measure and record the route on the drawings, so nobody drills into it later.

Worked example (our own): heat gain into a buried suction line

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not measured data. Assume a 3-ton system (36,000 Btu/h) with a 40 ft buried suction line, a suction line temperature of 45 F, and soil at 55 F. Heat gain is the heat-loss-per-foot value (U per foot, in Btu/h per foot per degree F) times length times temperature difference. We assume these U per foot values: dry insulated line 0.10; the same insulation wet, at 3 times that, 0.30 (using the roof-board result above only as a rough guide); bare tube in moist soil, 1.3.

  1. Temperature difference: 55 F minus 45 F = 10 F.
  2. Dry insulated: 0.10 × 40 ft × 10 F = 40 Btu/h, which is 40 / 36,000 = about 0.1 percent of capacity.
  3. Wet insulation: 0.30 × 40 × 10 = 120 Btu/h, about 0.3 percent.
  4. Bare tube: 1.3 × 40 × 10 = 520 Btu/h, about 1.4 percent, which is 13 times the dry value.

The capacity numbers are small, and we want you to see that. The heat gain alone is not why buried lines are discouraged. The bigger problems are the wet insulation you cannot dry, the corrosion you cannot inspect and the leak you cannot reach. In our own reading, the same heat also raises the vapor temperature returning to the compressor, which matters more on long runs. Soil temperature and U values change with the site, so rerun the arithmetic with your own assumptions.

Abandoning an old buried line and running a new one

If a buried line has failed, the usual choice is to abandon it and run a new one on an accessible route, such as along a wall or in a chase, or through the old sleeve if the sleeve is sound. Before you walk away from the old line, the refrigerant has to be recovered, not vented; see the reusing a line set lesson for why old lines are risky to reuse. Cap or seal the old line ends so it is not mistaken for a live line, mark the drawings, and tell the owner. Do not reuse a buried line you suspect is wet, corroded or contaminated, and do not tie a new section into it.

What a tech checks when a slab line leaks

  1. Confirm it is the line: check coils, fittings and the service valves first, since those are cheap to reach.
  2. Isolate the line: recover the charge, disconnect both ends and cap them.
  3. Pressure test the isolated line with dry nitrogen and watch for a drop; correct for temperature.
  4. If it holds, the leak is elsewhere. If it falls, decide: locate and open the slab, or abandon the line and run a new one.
  5. Look at the sleeve ends for water, soil or damaged insulation, which hints at the failure.
  6. Report the result to the owner honestly, with the trade-offs.

For a larger system, the federal environmental agency's leak repair page says that for appliances holding 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant, leaks over the trigger rate (10 percent for comfort cooling) must be repaired within 30 days of discovery or covered by a retrofit or retirement plan finished within 1 year, and that owners must verify repairs with initial and follow-up tests. Check that page for the current wording before you rely on it. A buried line that cannot be reached and repaired is a hard case under those timelines.

Field card: Buried Refrigerant Line Sets

If you cannot reach it, you cannot fix it.

  • Read the maker's manual and local code first; they set the limits.
  • Use a continuous sleeve that drains and is sealed at the ends.
  • No joints in hidden sections; keep all joints where you can see them.
  • Stay within the maker's line length and lift limits.
  • Pressure test with dry nitrogen before covering and record the route.
  • Wet insulation passes more heat; soil and concrete are harder on copper than air.
  • Abandon a failed buried line after recovery, cap it, and run a new accessible one.
