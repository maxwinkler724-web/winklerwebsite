Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors: Types and Search Technique

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An electronic leak detector is only as good as the way you hold it. The sensors can notice a leak of about one gram a year, but only if the probe is close, slow and in the right place. This lesson covers the common sensor types, what the makers' manuals say about technique, and how to keep the tool honest.

What the detector actually senses

These handheld tools pull a little air across a sensor and watch for a change. They find refrigerant in the air, so you need some refrigerant in the system and you need to get the probe tip into the gas that is escaping. For the other half of the job, pressurizing with nitrogen and holding a test, see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods. For why a low system means a leak in the first place, see Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.

Heated diode vs infrared

INFICON's refrigerant detector page sorts its line into two families. Its TEK-Mate is a heated diode tool, and its D-TEK 3 is infrared. Fieldpiece does the same: the SRL8 is heated diode and the DR82 is infrared. Bacharach's H-10G manual describes a heated element that operates around 1,000°F.

The tradeoffs show up in the numbers the makers publish:

  • Heated diode sensors wear out. The TEK-Mate manual says its sensor runs about 100 hours before replacement. Testo gives 80 to 100 hours, about a year of normal use. The SRL8 manual says 5 years typical, and an H-10 PRO user manual says 1 year typical.
  • Infrared sensors last much longer. The D-TEK 3 manual lists up to 2,000 hours and the DR82 manual says 10 years typical.
  • Both can false alarm. The D-TEK 3 manual warns that high radio-frequency environments may cause a false alarm. The DR82 manual says its turbo mode may false trigger from abrupt motion. Heated diode tools react to bumping and moisture, covered below.
  • Refrigerant coverage differs. The D-TEK 3 manual lists sensitivities for R134a, R1234yf, R290, CO2 and ammonia in its specification table, with different values for each gas. A sensitivity figure is always for a named refrigerant under test conditions, not a promise for every gas.

Ultrasonic tools listen for the sound of gas escaping instead of sensing the gas itself. An ACHR News piece quoting a Fieldpiece trainer names ultrasonic with infrared and heated diode as common types.

Reading sensitivity in oz/yr

Makers rate sensitivity as the smallest leak the tool should find, in ounces or grams per year. The D-TEK 3 manual gives 0.04 oz/yr (1 g/yr) for R134a, tested to the European standard EN 14624. The SRL8 manual gives 0.03 oz/yr stationary and 0.1 oz/yr moving. An H-10 PRO user manual gives 0.1 oz/yr moving, per SAE J2791. A Tracer Products announcement says its infrared detector is certified to SAE J2791 and J2913, and says the revisions set new certification procedures and leak detection requirements; it does not list the requirements, and neither do we. Fieldpiece's SRL8 page cites SAE J1627 instead. Treat the standard names as the maker's claim, and note that stationary readings (probe held still) are more sensitive than moving ones.

Search technique

  1. Warm up and zero in clean air. The D-TEK 3 manual footnote recommends running the unit 15 minutes before use. The SRL8 self-calibrates for about 30 seconds at power-up and then re-zeros automatically. Do this away from the equipment, because the tool zeroes to whatever is in the air.
  2. Stay close. The TEK-Mate and SRL8 manuals both say to keep the tip within 1/4 inch (about 5 to 6 mm) of the possible leak. Testo says a maximum of 6 mm. The Bacharach H-10G manual says no more than 0.25 inch. The D-TEK 3 manual gives no number but says to place the tip as close as possible without blocking the airflow.
  3. Go slow. Manuals disagree a little, so use the one for your tool. SRL8: 1 to 3 inches per second. TEK-Mate: 1 to 2 inches per second. Testo: 2.5 to 5 cm per second (about 1 to 2 inches). H-10G: about one inch per second, not over two. DR82: about 3 inches per second. A Fieldpiece trainer quoted in ACHR News says about three inches per second and explains that moving slowly avoids stirring a breeze that can mask the leak.
  4. Do not park the probe. The TEK-Mate manual says holding the tip on a spot lets the auto-zero feature gradually zero out the signal. Keep moving. Do not whip the probe either, because the TEK-Mate manual says sudden motion changes the airflow and sets off the alarm.
  5. Mind the air. The H-10G manual says a contaminated area can be improved by ventilating or isolating it. ACHR News says drafts and temperature swings disturb the gas flow. Block a fan or open door if you can, but do not stop ventilation that safety requires.
  6. Think about gravity. Bryan Orr, in a 2018 HVAC trade blog article, says to start at the top of the component and work down, because refrigerant is heavier than air and sinks. The Fieldpiece trainer in ACHR News says the gas collects in low spots, so when you get an alarm low down, work upward until the alarm stops to find the source. Our own reading of both: the highest spot that alarms is closer to the leak than the lowest one.
  7. Back off and recheck. If the tool alarms, pull it into clean air, let it clear, and sweep the spot again. The D-TEK 3 and SRL8 manuals describe this. If the leak is large, drop to a lower sensitivity setting, as the SRL8, Testo and D-TEK 3 manuals suggest, then go back to high to pinpoint.

Worked example: scanning a coil and sizing a leak

Part 1, time to scan. Suppose an evaporator coil has 60 feet of tubing and joints you want to cover, and you follow the SRL8 range.

  1. Convert to inches: 60 ft × 12 = 720 inches.
  2. At 1 inch per second: 720 ÷ 1 = 720 seconds, which is 12 minutes.
  3. At 3 inches per second: 720 ÷ 3 = 240 seconds, which is 4 minutes.

The slow pass takes three times as long. If your detector's manual says 1 to 2 inches per second, plan for 6 to 12 minutes, and a second pass over any alarm.

Part 2, what a leak rate means. Take a system with a 100 oz charge (6 lb 4 oz).

  1. A leak of 0.5 oz/yr (about the size the H-10 PRO manual associates with its reference vial): 100 ÷ 0.5 = 200 years to lose it all.
  2. A leak of 4 oz/yr: losing 20 percent of the charge is 100 × 0.20 = 20 oz, and 20 ÷ 4 = 5 years.
  3. A leak of 16 oz/yr (one pound a year): 20 ÷ 16 = 1.25 years.

This assumes a steady rate. Real leaks change with pressure, temperature and vibration, so use it only to compare sizes. A system that is low after one season has a bigger leak than the table suggests.

Keeping the tool honest

  • Check it on a known leak. The H-10G manual has you hold the probe over a reference bottle for 2 to 3 seconds before use and replace the sensor when it no longer responds. The same article suggests a reference bottle check before each use. Fieldpiece describes a capped tank port test for the DR82. The D-TEK 3, TEK-Mate and Testo manuals each say to check annually with a calibrated leak standard.
  • Protect the sensor. The H-10G manual says holding the probe in a concentrated stream of halogen drastically shortens sensor life. Testo says oils and other desorbing substances can destroy the sensor. Water and oil plug the probe filter on several models, so replace a wet or dirty filter.
  • Expect nuisance alarms. TEK-Mate and Testo both say a detector that alarms when moved may have bent sensor leads or moisture, and give 20 minutes of running time to dry out. The H-10G manual says it cannot tell background contamination from a leak.
  • Confirm before you condemn. Orr advises returning to the spot several times and, when possible, confirming with bubbles. Mark the spot, apply bubble solution if the system is pressurized, and look for growth. Do not condemn a part on one alarm.

Flammable refrigerants

Do not assume a detector is safe around flammable refrigerants. The DR82 manual prohibits use with A3 refrigerants such as R-290 and in combustible gas atmospheres, and Fieldpiece lists the DR82 and DR58 on its A2L compatible tools page. Read your own tool's manual for what it allows. For fixed sensors built into A2L equipment, see A2L Leak Detection Sensors. If you find a leak you must recover the refrigerant, not vent it; see Recover, Recycle and Reclaim. EPA leak repair and record rules are summarized in the leaks lesson.

Field card: Electronic Leak Detector Technique

Close, slow, keep moving, and prove it twice.

  • Zero in clean air, warm up as the manual says.
  • Tip within about 1/4 inch (5 to 6 mm).
  • Speed: 1 to 3 inches per second, per your manual.
  • Gas sinks: start high on the component, work down; if it alarms low, work up.
  • Cut drafts. Do not hold the tip still or whip it.
  • Back off, clear, sweep again. Confirm with bubbles if you can.
  • Heated diode sensors last hours to a few years; infrared lasts longer. Check with a reference leak and replace on schedule.
  • Check the maker's A2L and flammable-atmosphere rules before using the tool.
