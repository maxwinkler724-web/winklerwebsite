Produce Cold Storage, Ripening Rooms and Controlled Atmosphere: Ethylene, Humidity, Forced-Air Cooling and Low-Oxygen Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Fruit and vegetables are alive. They breathe, give off heat and lose water, so a produce room is not just a cold box. This lesson covers what a refrigeration tech needs to know about holding temperatures and humidity, forced-air pre-cooling, banana ripening rooms that use ethylene, and apple storage rooms where the air is kept low in oxygen on purpose, which can kill a person who walks in.

Temperature and humidity depend on the crop

There is no single produce setting. The University of Maine bulletin on storage conditions lists apples at 30 to 40°F and 90 to 95 percent relative humidity (RH), lettuce at 32°F and 98 to 100 percent, and early-crop potatoes at 40°F and 90 to 95 percent. Mature-green tomatoes are listed at 55 to 70°F. A cold box that is perfect for lettuce would hurt the tomatoes.

The reason is chilling injury: damage from cold that is above freezing. The same bulletin gives a lowest safe temperature of 55°F for mature-green tomatoes, which then color poorly, and 36 to 38°F for certain apple varieties, which can brown inside. The Global Cold Chain Alliance banana storage guide says to avoid pulp temperatures below 56°F, and that injury can show up within 2 to 24 hours below that. A thermostat set a few degrees low is a real crop loss, not just a comfort problem.

Humidity matters because produce loses water to dry air. A 1949 USDA circular explains the refrigeration side in plain terms: the spread between refrigerant and room temperature matters more for humidity than for holding the room temperature steady. A wide spread means a cold coil that condenses water out of the air and dries the crop. A larger coil running closer to room temperature keeps humidity up. That circular is old, so treat it as the principle, not a design number. The Ontario forced-air cooling factsheet asks for at least 80 percent RH and preferably over 90 percent, and warns that air blowing straight off the coil can cause cold injury. For the basics of coils, defrost and walk-in boxes, see our walk-in cooler lesson; for large freezer buildings, see our refrigerated warehouse lesson.

Forced-air pre-cooling and the seven-eighths cooling time

Field heat is removed fast because quality drops while produce is warm. Room cooling is slow, so many packers use forced-air cooling: rows of pallets are set facing each other with a gap between them, a tarp closes the top and ends, and a fan pulls room air through the vent holes in the boxes instead of letting it go around them. The pressure difference makes air move through the load, so it touches the produce directly.

The standard way to compare cooling methods is the seven-eighths cooling time: how long it takes to remove 87.5 percent of the difference between the starting pulp temperature and the room air temperature. The NC State Extension page gives an example with a 95°F start and a 41°F room, where the pulp target is 95 − (54 × 0.875) = 47.75°F. At about 5 cfm per pound the seven-eighths time is about 30 minutes; at 2 cfm per pound it is about 90 minutes, three times as long. Its rule of thumb is roughly 2 to 3 cfm per pound at 0.10 inch of water static pressure. The Ontario factsheet puts very perishable crops such as broccoli and leaf lettuce at 2 to 6 cfm per pound with seven-eighths times of 0.75 to 1.5 hours, and moderate crops such as peaches at 0.5 to 1.5 cfm per pound over 2 to 6 hours. It also notes that air leaving the pallets can start 20 to 30 F° above room temperature (NC State), and that oversized fans just add heat because their electrical input becomes load.

Worked example: our own forced-air pre-cooling load

This is a sizing exercise, not a design. Everything below is an assumption unless a source is named.

  • Produce weight (assumption): 20 pallets at 1,000 lb each = 20,000 lb. NC State says full pallets range from about 350 lb to over 1,000 lb.
  • Specific heat (cited): 0.9 Btu/lb·°F, the value the Ontario factsheet uses for its load formula.
  • Temperatures (assumption, matching the NC State example): 95°F start, 41°F room, so the seven-eighths drop is 0.875 × 54 = 47.25°F.
  • Time (assumption): 1.5 hours, the NC State seven-eighths time at 2 cfm per pound.

Step 1, heat to remove: Btu = lb × cp × ΔT = 20,000 × 0.9 × 47.25 = 850,500 Btu.

Step 2, average rate: 850,500 ÷ 1.5 h = 567,000 Btu/h.

Step 3, tons: 567,000 ÷ 12,000 = 47.25 tons on average.

That average hides the peak. Cooling slows as the produce nears room temperature, so the load is highest at the start. The Ontario formula multiplies the full temperature difference, mass, specific heat and 2.08 and divides by the seven-eighths time: 2.08 × 54 × 18,000 ÷ 1.5 = 1,347,840 Btu/h, about 112 tons at the start (our arithmetic on their formula). Ontario suggests designing for two-thirds of that momentary rate, about 75 tons here. The same pallets cooled in 30 minutes would average 141.75 tons by the same arithmetic. Left out of all these numbers: the heat the produce gives off by respiration, fan heat, box and pallet mass, and infiltration. The point for a tech: pre-cooling load is large, short and depends on how fast the owner wants it done.

Ripening rooms and ethylene

Ethylene is a gas that triggers ripening. Banana ripening rooms use it on purpose. The Global Cold Chain Alliance banana guide describes gassing at 100 ppm (0.01 percent) for 24 to 48 hours with pulp at 58 to 65°F, keeping RH at 90 to 95 percent on green fruit, and holding carbon dioxide below 1 percent. Fans should run continuously, boxes need a 4-inch air channel between rows, and the guide recommends forced-air ripening for new facilities. In a pressurized ripening room, pallets are stacked two or three tiers high, tarps cover the top and outside ends, and fans mounted high on the back wall pull air through the boxes, just like the cooling tunnel above. Fresh air exchange matters because the fruit puts out CO₂ as it ripens, and the room is vented between gassings.

Ethylene is also flammable. The International Chemical Safety Card lists explosive limits of 2.7 to 36 percent in air and calls it extremely flammable. A 1993 Fire Engineering article reports the fire-code limit for ripening rooms as 0.1 percent (1 part per 1,000), with gas introduced under positive control and measured, and classified electrical equipment where an explosive atmosphere could exist. That article is old, so check the fire code your jurisdiction uses now. On our arithmetic, 100 ppm (0.01 percent) is 270 times below the 2.7 percent lower limit, and even the 0.1 percent code limit is 27 times below it. The danger is a leaking cylinder, a wrong valve or a sealed room with a spark source, not the working concentration. Do not wire or service a ripening room's controls without learning how the gas is metered and what electrical classification applies.

Controlled atmosphere storage and the asphyxiation hazard

Controlled atmosphere (CA) storage slows ripening by cutting oxygen and raising CO₂. The University of Maine fruit program says oxygen is generally kept at 1 to 3 percent for apples and lists 2.5 percent for the varieties in its table, with CO₂ mostly 2 to 2.5 percent. The Global Cold Chain Alliance apple guide gives general ranges of 1 to 3 percent O₂ and 1 to 5 percent CO₂, with temperatures from 30 to 32°F for chilling-insensitive types and 36°F for McIntosh. The room must be gas-tight. CO₂ is removed by reaction with lime, adsorption on activated carbon, membranes or nitrogen flushing; Maine describes bags of hydrated lime at 0.6 lb per bushel in smaller rooms. A leaky room lets oxygen rise above target.

The same guide states plainly that CA atmospheres will not support human life. Normal air is about 21 percent oxygen. OSHA defines an oxygen-deficient atmosphere as under 19.5 percent in 29 CFR 1910.146, the permit-required confined space rule, and a space that has or can have one is a hazardous atmosphere. Two real cases show the hazard:

  • NIOSH reviewed a 2008 Michigan death where a worker removed a viewing window on a room under 3 percent oxygen, climbed in and collapsed. The owner who pulled him out also leaned into the room and risked his own life. The report says never enter to rescue unless trained and equipped, call emergency rescue, treat a sealed room as hazardous even without a drawdown (oxygen fell from 21 to 18 percent over three days in a sealed room), and ventilate with fans for extended periods and recheck oxygen at several points before opening.
  • In October 2023, a worker died in a Washington apple CA room reading about 2 percent oxygen. Washington Labor and Industries said an entry alarm had sounded, no one warned him and no attendant was posted. It said the state minimum is 19.5 percent while the contractor's handbook allowed 17.5 percent.

For an HVAC or refrigeration tech the rule is simple: a CA room is a confined space. Do not step in, lean in or reach in to fix a coil, a door seal or a sensor. Get the facility's entry procedure, test the air, and ask who is watching. Our confined space lesson covers testing order. Rooms using propane forklifts also raise carbon monoxide; NIOSH recommends electric forklifts and ventilation.

Field card: Produce Storage, Ripening and CA

Right temperature for the crop, high humidity, fresh air for the fruit, and never enter low oxygen.

  • Each crop has its own temperature and RH; chilling injury happens above freezing.
  • Banana pulp below 56°F risks chilling injury.
  • High RH needs a coil that does not run far below room temperature.
  • Seven-eighths cooling time: time to remove 87.5 percent of the temperature difference.
  • Load: Btu = lb × cp × ΔT; divide by hours, then by 12,000 for tons. Peak load is higher than average.
  • Ethylene: flammable 2.7 to 36 percent; ripening uses about 100 ppm. Check fire code.
  • CA rooms are low oxygen (about 1 to 3 percent). Below 19.5 percent is oxygen-deficient. Do not enter or rescue.
