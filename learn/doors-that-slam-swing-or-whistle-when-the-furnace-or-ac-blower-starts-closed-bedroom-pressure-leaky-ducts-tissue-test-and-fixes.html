Doors That Slam or Whistle When the Blower Starts: Room Pressure Fixes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You start the furnace or the AC and a bedroom door drifts shut, slams, or sings a thin whistle along the floor. That is air pressure at work. The blower is pushing more air into a closed room than can leave it, and the rest of the house is paying for it. This lesson explains the cause, two quick checks you can do yourself, and the usual fixes.

Why a closed bedroom gets pressurized

A forced-air system moves air in a loop. Supply registers push conditioned air into rooms, and the return grille pulls the same amount back to the blower. In many homes there is one big return in a hall or a few common spaces. With a bedroom door open, the supply air finds its way out of the room and back to that return. With the door closed, the room still gets supply air but has almost no way to send it back.

The room pressure rises a little. The air that cannot leave the room escapes any way it can: through the gap under the door, through the walls and ceiling, around windows and outlets. Meanwhile the return grille is still pulling the full amount from the main hall, so the hall pressure drops below the rest of the house. A national laboratory guide on pressure balancing bedrooms describes this plainly: stale air needs a path back to the central return, a closed door blocks it, and the closed room is overpressurized.

Why the door slams, swings or whistles

Pressure difference pushes on the door. A door pushed toward the hall by a pressurized room can swing or pop open when the blower starts, and a door pulled shut by a depressurized hall can slam when the blower turns on or off. The whistle is air squeezing through a narrow gap at speed. If you hear it under or beside a door, that door is carrying a pressure difference. Treat it as a sign, not as the problem: the fix is to give the air somewhere easier to go.

How big can the difference get? A federal research report on home air flow cites an older field study of 70 homes in which closed doors produced an average pressure drop of 9.2 Pa across them, with the worst closed rooms reaching 57 Pa. It also notes that closing one master bedroom door pulled the rest of one house to about -7 Pa. The same report says one state's mechanical code later limited the pressure across a closed door to 2.5 Pa. Pa (pascals) are small units; the point is that a closed door can create a pressure difference you can hear and feel.

Leaky ducts make it worse

Duct leaks add their own imbalance. A national laboratory guide on air flow says supply leaks to the outdoors can depressurize the building, and return leaks to the outdoors can pressurize it. Leaks to the inside of the house mainly affect comfort, health and safety: supply leaks pressurize a small area and depressurize the rest, and return leaks do the reverse. So a house with a closed bedroom and leaky supply ducts in an attic can end up with the main hall pulled even more negative.

The federal report gives an example of the scale: a supply leak of 400 cfm drew about 400 cfm of air into a house from outdoors and the attic, leaving the house at about -4.9 Pa. It adds that many homes leak mostly through the ceiling, so much of that replacement air comes from the attic. Return leaks matter too, because they pull air from wherever the return duct runs: an attic, a crawl space, or a garage. The report notes that air handlers in garages can carry garage air, including vehicle exhaust, into the house. For the garage case see attached garages and your home heating and cooling.

Why a safety issue can hide inside a comfort problem

A depressurized area pulls air in from every opening it can find, and that includes the vent pipes and flues of gas or oil appliances. The national laboratory air flow guide says return leakage where combustion appliances sit, such as a basement, equipment room or closet, can cause spillage, backdrafting, carbon monoxide production and flame rollout. The federal report likewise says a return leak that depressurizes a mechanical closet can pull air down a vent from outdoors. Exhaust fans and dryers can add to the same effect. If a door slams or whistles and you also notice a burning smell, soot, or a carbon monoxide alarm, shut the appliance off, get fresh air, and call a qualified technician. See combustion appliance zone worst-case testing for how technicians check this.

The same guide also warns that a pressurized room can push warm, moist air into wall and ceiling cavities, which can lead to mold and rot over time. That is one more reason not to ignore a bedroom that is always pressurized.

Two simple checks

  1. Door test. With the blower running (set the thermostat fan to On), open and close the bedroom door slowly. If the door wants to push open, resists closing, or you feel and hear air hissing at the gap right at the end of the swing, the room is under pressure. Try it again with the door open to compare.
  2. Tissue test. Hold a single tissue or a strip of light paper at the gap under or beside the closed door. If it flutters away from the room, air is leaving the room. If it is pulled toward the room, the room is depressurized instead.

These checks tell you direction and rough strength, not a number. A technician with a pressure gauge can measure the actual difference across the door.

Fixes, from simple to bigger

  • Door undercut. Trimming the bottom of the door gives a free-area path. A national laboratory report on room return path sensitivity treats the undercut as the return path for a closed door. It also notes that a restricted undercut roughly doubled the airflow imbalance in its models, so a rug or a door bottom that touches the floor will undo the gain.
  • Transfer grille. A louvered grille through the wall above the door, or in the door, gives a larger, quieter path than a gap. Sizing is covered in transfer grilles and jump ducts for closed bedroom doors.
  • Jump duct. A short duct through the ceiling space connects the bedroom to the hall, with grilles on each end, so the air has a path without a hole in the wall.
  • More return. The national laboratory guide on pressure balancing lists individual ducted returns in each room, or central ducted returns, as ways to fix the problem. The room return path report found a distributed return, with a return duct to each room, was least sensitive to closed doors.
  • Seal duct leaks. Sealing leaky ducts reduces the extra imbalance and keeps attic and garage air out. See duct leakage testing for how leakage is measured, and aerosol duct sealing for one sealing method.

Pick the fix by how bad the problem is. A door that whistles slightly may need only a small path; a bedroom that gets 120 cfm or more and stays shut at night may need a grille or a return. Do not cut a hole in a fire-rated or insulated wall without checking what is inside, and ask a licensed contractor to make duct changes.

Worked example (our own): sizing a door undercut

Our own example with assumed numbers. The bedroom gets 120 cfm of supply air (assumption). We assume the air can pass through the undercut at 300 feet per minute (assumption, chosen to keep the gap quiet). The door is 30 inches wide (assumption).

  1. Free area in square feet = cfm / fpm = 120 / 300 = 0.4 sq ft.
  2. Convert to square inches: 0.4 x 144 = 57.6 sq in.
  3. Undercut height = area / door width = 57.6 / 30 = 1.92 inches.

Check the math. Work it backward: 30 in x 1.92 in = 57.6 sq in; 57.6 / 144 = 0.4 sq ft; 0.4 sq ft x 300 fpm = 120 cfm. It matches.

What it tells us. A gap of about 1.9 inches under the whole door is tall, more than most homeowners want, and it only counts if the gap is truly open. That is why a bedroom with a lot of supply air usually needs a transfer grille, a jump duct or a return instead of an undercut alone. As a second check, the national laboratory report mentioned above modeled a 0.75 inch by 32 inch undercut as 50 cfm at 2.5 Pa. Our arithmetic: 0.75 x 32 = 24 sq in, which is 0.167 sq ft, and 50 / 0.167 = 300 fpm. So that modeled undercut is about 24 sq in for about 50 cfm, well short of 120 cfm.

Field card: Doors That Slam or Whistle When the Blower Starts

Supply with no way back means a pressurized room and a depressurized hall.

  • Closed bedroom, supply air, no return: the room goes up in pressure and the hall goes down.
  • A whistle at the door gap means air is squeezing through a small path.
  • Check: open and close the door with the blower running, and hold a tissue at the gap.
  • Fixes: undercut, transfer grille, jump duct, more return, sealed ducts.
  • Our own example: area = cfm / fpm x 144, so 120 / 300 x 144 = 57.6 sq in, which is 1.92 in on a 30 in door.
  • Negative pressure can pull air from attics, garages and vent pipes. Stop and get help if you smell combustion products or a CO alarm sounds.
