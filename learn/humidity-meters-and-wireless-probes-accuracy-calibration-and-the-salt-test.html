Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes: Accuracy, Calibration and the Salt Test

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A humidity reading is only useful if you can trust it. A probe that reads three points high will send you chasing a moisture problem that is not there, or miss one that is. This lesson covers how the common meters work, what their makers publish for accuracy, why they drift, and a simple salt test that checks a probe against a known value.

Two ways to measure humidity

Capacitive sensors. Most digital hygrometers and wireless probes use a thin polymer film. Vaisala, a sensor maker, describes the film as taking up water vapor as humidity rises and releasing it as humidity falls. As the film's water content changes, its dielectric properties change, which changes the sensor's capacitance, and the electronics turn that into a percent RH number. A temperature sensor sits next to the film, because RH only means something at a known temperature. The instrument can then calculate dew point from the two readings.

Sling psychrometers. This is the older method and needs no electronics. Two thermometers are mounted on a handle. One bulb is covered with a wet wick, the other is dry. You whirl the device so air moves over both, then compare the wet-bulb and dry-bulb readings with a table. An Iowa DOT test procedure says to swing it two to three times per second and keep going until the readings settle, which it says should take one to two minutes. Read the wet bulb first, then the dry bulb, without delay. The wick has to be saturated, and the dry bulb has to stay dry. Our psychrometrics lesson explains why the gap between the two readings tells you how dry the air is.

What accuracy do the makers publish?

Accuracy numbers vary by instrument, so read the data sheet for the one in your hand. Two published examples show the typical range:

  • A Fieldpiece wireless psychrometer probe manual lists RH accuracy of plus or minus 2.5 percent from 10 to 90 percent RH, stated at 73.4 F (23 C), with about 1 percent RH hysteresis. Temperature accuracy is plus or minus 1 F from 32 to 113 F. It lists a response time of 60 seconds typical for 90 percent of the total range.
  • A Testo catalog page for a duct-mount thermo-hygrometer lists plus or minus 3 percent RH and plus or minus 0.5 C for temperature, using a capacitive humidity sensor.

Notice what "plus or minus 2.5 percent RH" means. A probe reading 55 percent could be sitting anywhere from 52.5 to 57.5 percent in the real air, even when it is working perfectly. That is a normal tolerance, not a defect. Accuracy specs also hold only near the stated reference temperature. The Fieldpiece manual gives a temperature coefficient outside that range, so a probe in a 40 F attic or a hot truck is less certain than the headline number.

Why probes drift

The Fieldpiece manual warns that extreme conditions or exposure to solvent vapor may offset the RH sensor. Think of cleaning sprays, adhesives, refrigerant-system cleaners and long stays in very wet or hot air. Its manual also says to keep the slip cover on the sensor when the probe is not in use and to store it between -4 and 140 F and 0 to 80 percent RH with the batteries removed. Vaisala says an instrument's performance must be checked against a reference "from time to time." Its technology note recommends a 2 year calibration interval for one of its own sensor families, which shows that makers set intervals by product and use, not by one universal rule. A Japan Meteorological Agency calibration handout lists yearly calibration plus periodic checks for weather instruments, which is a much harsher duty than a truck-bound probe.

The saturated salt test

Here is the idea. If you keep a pile of table salt (sodium chloride) wet, in a sealed space, the air above it settles at a very steady humidity. Vaisala's published Greenspan-based table gives sodium chloride at 75.3 percent RH at 25 C (77 F), with an uncertainty of plus or minus 0.1 percent, and it changes only slowly with temperature: 75.5 at 20 C and 75.1 at 30 C. So the sealed space is a cheap, known reference.

The Fieldpiece manual describes its own version: put wet salt in an open container, keep the solution away from the probe, seal both in a large bag at room temperature, and hold the sensor at 75 percent RH between 68 and 86 F for 24 hours. Vaisala's calibrator guide adds good habits that apply to any salt check:

  • Let the probe and the setup sit in the same room for at least 30 minutes first.
  • After you seal the probe in, allow 10 to 20 minutes or more for a reliable reading. A homemade bag is slower than a metal chamber, which is why the maker above asks for 24 hours.
  • Keep the temperature steady. Vaisala says a 1 C difference between chamber air and sensor at 20 C causes about 3 percent RH error at 50 percent RH. Do not hold the container or the probe in your hand.
  • Keep the salt clean and keep liquid off the sensor. A small water droplet near it can distort readings.

Worked example: a probe fails the salt test

The room is 77 F (25 C), so the reference is 75.3 percent RH. After 24 hours the probe reads 78.0 percent.

  1. Offset. 78.0 - 75.3 = +2.7. The probe reads 2.7 points high.
  2. Compare to spec. A plus or minus 2.5 percent spec plus the salt's 0.1 uncertainty allows about 2.6. A 2.7 error is just outside it. Treat this probe as suspect.
  3. Apply the offset. A field reading of 55.0 percent becomes 55.0 - 2.7 = 52.3 percent. A single-point offset like this is only a rough field fix near the test point. Vaisala says a proper adjustment uses two points at least 50 percent RH apart, one for offset and one for slope. Do not assume the error at 55 percent is the same size as at 75 percent. Use the corrected number for judging, not as a certified value.
  4. What the error does to dew point. Take the air at 75 F (23.9 C). Columbia University's IRI help page gives the relation from the Clausius-Clapeyron equation: E0 = 0.611 kPa, L/Rv = 5423 K, T0 = 273 K, with Es = E0 x exp[5423 x (1/273 - 1/T)] and E = RH x Es.
    Es at 296.9 K: 5423 x (0.0036630 - 0.0033681) = 1.599, and exp(1.599) = 4.949, so Es = 0.611 x 4.949 = 3.02 kPa.
    At 55.0 percent: E = 0.550 x 3.02 = 1.66 kPa. Solve E = E0 x exp[5423 x (1/273 - 1/Td)] for Td and you get about 287.5 K, which is 14.5 C or 58.1 F.
    At 52.3 percent: E = 0.523 x 3.02 = 1.58 kPa, giving Td of about 13.7 C or 56.7 F.
  5. Result. The 2.7 point error moved the dew point by about 1.4 F. A full 3 point error (55 versus 52 percent) works out to 58.1 versus 56.5 F, about 1.6 F. Near 55 percent RH at 75 F, each point of RH is worth roughly half a degree of dew point.

The same page gives a quick shortcut, Td = T - (100 - RH)/5 in degrees C, and says it is fairly accurate above 50 percent RH. It gives 14.9 C at 55 percent and 14.4 C at 52.3, a smaller gap of about 1 F, which is why the full relation is better for a close call.

Placement and response time

A fast, steady reading needs steady air. The Fieldpiece manual says to select supply or return in its app and place the probe on the matching side of the system, using a 3/8 inch (10 mm) hole in the duct, and warns against drilling blindly into a plenum that houses the evaporator or heat exchanger. A probe rated at 60 seconds to reach 90 percent of a change still needs a few minutes in a duct whose temperature is moving. Let the readings stop trending before you write anything down. With a sling psychrometer, whirl again and re-read until two readings in a row agree. Our humid house lesson shows where those numbers get used, and our airflow measuring lesson covers other duct-side readings. Temperature accuracy matters too, since RH is tied to it, and the same care applies to the thermometers used in superheat and subcooling work.

When to send it for calibration

  • The salt check is off by more than the published spec, and a repeat gives the same result.
  • The probe sat in solvent vapor, condensation, or extreme heat or cold.
  • The readings look wrong against a second instrument you trust.
  • A job requires documented accuracy. The Testo catalog lists traceable humidity calibration documents at 11.3 and 75.3 percent RH at 25 C, a two-point check.
  • The maker's stated interval has passed. Check your own instrument's manual.

Only the maker or a calibration lab can adjust many probes. Do not open one and bend anything.

Field card: Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes: Accuracy, Calibration and the Salt Test

Check the probe against a known humidity before you trust the number.

  • Capacitive probes read a polymer film's capacitance; sling psychrometers compare wet and dry bulbs.
  • Typical published RH accuracy: 2.5 to 3 percent RH. Check your data sheet.
  • Wet salt in a sealed space gives 75.3 percent RH at 77 F (Greenspan table).
  • Let it stabilize. Do not hold it. Keep the temperature steady.
  • Offset = probe reading - 75.3. Subtract it as a rough field correction only.
  • 3 points of RH at 75 F and 55 percent is about 1.5 F of dew point.
  • Send it for calibration if it fails twice or got solvent, water or heat.
