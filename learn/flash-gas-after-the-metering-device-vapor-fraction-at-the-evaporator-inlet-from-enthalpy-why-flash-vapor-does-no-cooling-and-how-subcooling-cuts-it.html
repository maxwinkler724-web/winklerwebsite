Flash Gas After the Metering Device: Inlet Vapor Fraction and Why It Does No Cooling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Liquid refrigerant leaves the condenser at high pressure and hits the metering device, where pressure falls to evaporator pressure. The liquid cannot stay all liquid at that lower pressure, so part of it boils on the spot. That vapor is flash gas, and it arrives at the evaporator having done no cooling. This lesson shows how to calculate how much there is, using enthalpies.

Why part of the liquid boils immediately

A metering device such as a thermostatic expansion valve, a piston or an electronic valve is a restriction. Refrigerant passes through it quickly, with no meaningful heat exchange with the surroundings and no work done. Under those conditions the enthalpy of the refrigerant is the same on both sides of the restriction. Thermodynamics notes from a university engineering department set the throttling outlet enthalpy equal to the inlet and describe the state after the valve as a saturated liquid-vapor mixture at the lower pressure. A university open lecture on the physics of energy makes the same point: the throttle is a constant-enthalpy step, some liquid flashes to vapor, and the result is a cold, low-pressure, liquid-rich mixture.

The reason is simple. Liquid at condenser temperature holds more heat per pound than saturated liquid at evaporator temperature. When the pressure drops, the liquid must shed the extra heat to reach the new, colder saturation temperature. The only place that heat can go is into boiling some of the liquid. The boiling cools the rest. The entering liquid does not stay at its warm temperature; it drops to the evaporator saturation temperature, and part of it is now vapor.

Computing the vapor fraction (quality)

Quality, written x, is the mass fraction that is vapor. In the evaporator, at the evaporator pressure, there are three enthalpies to read from a property table or chart:

  • h: the enthalpy of the liquid entering the metering device. This is also the enthalpy leaving it.
  • hf: saturated liquid enthalpy at evaporator pressure.
  • hg: saturated vapor enthalpy at evaporator pressure. The difference hg minus hf is the latent heat at that pressure, hfg.

A mixture's enthalpy is the liquid part plus the vapor part, so h = hf + x × hfg. Solve for x:

x = (h − hf) / (hg − hf)

The same enthalpy balance gives the refrigeration effect, which is the heat each pound can absorb in the evaporator: hg − h (assuming we ignore superheat and count only the evaporating process). The university notes do this exact thing in a worked solution: it takes the heat added in the evaporator as the enthalpy leaving minus the enthalpy leaving the valve, and divides the load by it to get mass flow. Property values for real refrigerants come from a federal property database or the maker's tables; the numbers below do not.

Why flash vapor does no cooling

The evaporator absorbs heat by turning liquid into vapor. Vapor that arrives already vapor has no more boiling left to do. It occupies tube volume, adds to the pressure drop, and rides through to the suction line without having removed heat from the air or water. Every pound of flash gas is a pound that did not do latent cooling. A federal research presentation on a transcritical refrigeration system lists the benefit of subcooling as "less flash gas produced" and "more refrigerant participates in the cooling process."

The flash is not a leak or a fault. It is required to bring the liquid down to evaporator temperature. What matters to a technician is the fraction: the less of it, the more of every pound of circulating refrigerant is still available to absorb heat.

Our own worked example

Illustrative numbers, not table values. We chose round numbers for a common medium-pressure refrigerant so the arithmetic is easy to follow. Do not use them for real work; look up values for your refrigerant, pressure and temperature.

Assumptions (ours): at the evaporator pressure, hf = 20 Btu/lb and hg = 107 Btu/lb, so hfg = 87 Btu/lb. Case A: liquid entering the metering device has an enthalpy of 46 Btu/lb (low subcooling, about 5°F). Case B: the same system with more subcooling, about 15°F, gives 43 Btu/lb (we assumed liquid enthalpy falls about 0.3 Btu/lb for each degree F of added subcooling, a round figure).

Case A. x = (46 − 20) / 87 = 26 / 87 = 0.299, or about 30 percent vapor. Refrigeration effect = 107 − 46 = 61 Btu/lb.

Case B. x = (43 − 20) / 87 = 23 / 87 = 0.264, or about 26 percent vapor. Refrigeration effect = 107 − 43 = 64 Btu/lb.

Check. The liquid part must produce the same effect through boiling alone: Case A, (1 − 0.299) × 87 = 0.701 × 87 = 61.0 Btu/lb, which matches. Case B, 0.736 × 87 = 64.0 Btu/lb, which matches.

Result: 10°F more subcooling cut the inlet vapor fraction from about 30 to about 26 percent, about 3.5 points, and raised the refrigeration effect from 61 to 64 Btu/lb, about 5 percent. The same load now needs about 5 percent less refrigerant flow. The conversion from effect to pounds per minute per ton is in the mass flow lesson linked below.

How to cut the flash fraction

  • More subcooling. Cooler liquid has lower enthalpy h, so the numerator in x falls, as the example shows. Subcooling is measured at the condenser outlet or liquid line; see the subcooling basics lesson.
  • Lower condensing pressure. A lower condensing pressure means lower liquid temperature and lower liquid enthalpy for the same degrees of subcooling, so x falls. A clean condenser and good airflow help for this reason. A smaller pressure ratio also eases the compressor.
  • Higher evaporator pressure. It moves hf up and hfg down, so the liquid has less excess heat to shed, although the effect is smaller and you rarely pick it for this purpose.

Not the same as flash gas in the liquid line

Two different things get the same name. Flash at the metering device is expected and happens by design after the pressure drop. Flash gas in the liquid line is bubbles forming before the metering device, because pressure drop from friction, a vertical lift or a restriction pushed the liquid below its saturation pressure, or because the liquid had little subcooling to begin with. Line flash starves the metering device and shows as bubbles in a sight glass; see the lesson on liquid line pressure drop and flash gas. The calculation here assumes the liquid reaches the metering device as a true liquid and measures what happens in the valve.

Field card: Flash Gas After the Metering Device: Inlet Vapor Fraction and Why It Does No Cooling

Same enthalpy in, same enthalpy out; the extra heat boils some liquid.

  • Enthalpy before and after the metering device is equal.
  • Inlet quality x = (h − hf) / (hg − hf), with hf and hg at evaporator pressure.
  • Refrigeration effect = hg − h.
  • Flash vapor does no cooling; it only takes up tube volume.
  • More subcooling or lower condensing pressure lowers h and so lowers x.
  • Flash at the valve is normal; bubbles ahead of the valve are a problem.
