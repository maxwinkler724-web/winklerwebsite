Heating and Cooling an Adobe or Cob Home: Thermal Mass and Dry Walls

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An adobe or cob house has earth walls a foot or more thick. Those walls hold a lot of heat, but they do not resist heat flow as well as their thickness suggests. They work best where days are hot and nights are cool. They need much more help where cold or humid weather lasts for weeks. The other big job is keeping the earth dry.

Mass is not the same as insulation

Two different things matter in a wall. R-value measures how hard it is for heat to pass through. Thermal mass measures how much heat the wall can soak up and hold. Earth is heavy, so it is very good at the second and only modest at the first, per inch. In our own judgment, a 16 inch earth wall will not match the R-value of a modern insulated frame wall, so on a long cold spell it will lose heat steadily. See our heat-loss lesson for how R-values add up.

A federal housing research text on adobe recommends at least 16 inches of wall thickness for the mass to do its job, and says thinner walls pass heat too fast to help. It lists adobe at roughly 125 to 140 pounds per cubic foot. It also says adobe takes in heat slowly by day and releases it at night, and that this only pays off where there is a large daily temperature swing, as in dry desert climates. The same text does not give an R-value or a time-lag figure, so we do not quote one.

Time lag: how the wall smooths the day

Heat takes hours to cross a thick earth wall. By the time an afternoon peak on the outside surface reaches the inside, it is evening and the outdoor air is already cooling. The wall acts like a slow battery. It flattens the swing, so the room stays cooler in the afternoon and gives warmth back after sunset. In our own judgment the effect is real but limited: it depends on a swing of 20 F or more between day and night, and it fades when the outdoor temperature stays high or low for days.

Worked example (our own): how much heat can the wall hold?

Our own example. The density, specific heat and night heat loss are assumed round numbers for teaching, not measured values. Assumptions: wall thickness 16 in = 16 / 12 = 1.33 ft; density 100 lb/ft3 (assumed; the federal text above lists higher values, so this is on the low side); specific heat 0.2 Btu/lb-F (assumed); wall swings 10 F over the day; 800 ft2 of wall; house night heat loss of 4,000 Btu/h (assumed).

  • Heat stored per square foot = thickness x density x specific heat x swing = 1.33 x 100 x 0.2 x 10 = 266, about 267 Btu per ft2 (using 1.333 ft gives 266.7).
  • Over 800 ft2: 267 x 800 = 213,600; using the unrounded 266.7 gives 213,333. We call it about 213,000 Btu.
  • Carryover in theory = 213,000 / 4,000 = 53 hours.

Do not trust that 53 hours. It is an upper limit, for two reasons. First, a 10 F swing through the full 16 inches is generous. In practice the inner layers of the wall move only a few degrees, so far less heat is usable. Second, much of the stored heat leaves toward the cold outdoors, not the room, as the figure shows. Real carryover is far less, often a few hours of smoothing rather than days. The example is useful for scale: the wall can store a lot, but it is a buffer, not a heat source. It does nothing to heat the house through a week of cold gray weather.

Where it works, and where it struggles

  • Hot days, cool nights. The best case. The wall absorbs afternoon heat and the house stays comfortable into the evening.
  • Long cold spells. The wall keeps losing heat and has nothing to recharge it, so a heat source is needed.
  • Humid climates. Nights stay warm, so there is little to flush out, and damp air raises the moisture risk for the walls.

Night flushing and winter sun

In summer, open windows or run a fan at night when outdoor air is cooler than the wall, then close up in the morning. This cools the mass so it can absorb the next day's heat. Our night-flush lesson covers dew-point limits and fan energy. In winter, our own advice is to let low sun in through south-facing glass so it lands on a floor or wall that can hold it, and to shade the glass in summer with overhangs. Without a way to keep that heat in, extra glass can lose more than it gains at night.

Keep the earth dry

Moisture is the main threat to an earthen wall. A federal parks preservation brief on adobe notes that strength drops as water content rises, and that groundwater can climb into the wall by capillary action and cause erosion and bulging. It names a watertight roof with good drainage as the best protection, and recommends directing water away from the foundation. The housing text asks for at least 6 inches between finished grade and the base of earthen walls, with the top of the foundation waterproofed to stop wicking.

  • Breathable finishes. Both sources warn about cement stucco on adobe. The housing text says it can trap moisture on unstabilized adobe, and the preservation brief says it cracks because it expands and contracts differently, which lets water in and keeps the wall from drying. Plastic and latex coatings have also caused damage. Mud or lime plaster lets vapor out. Before adding paint or a sealer, ask who built the wall what finish it was designed for.
  • Roof overhangs and drainage. Neither source gives an overhang size for adobe. In our own judgment, wide eaves, working gutters or drains, and ground sloped away keep rain and splash off the base.
  • Indoor humidity. A federal environmental agency guide says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, to dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, and to vent moisture-producing appliances outdoors. Fix leaks quickly.

Equipment that fits

  • Small ductless heads. Thick earth walls have no cavities for ducts. One or two small heat pump heads can heat and cool without them. Size from a real room-by-room load; an oversized unit short-cycles.
  • Radiant floors. Warm water in the slab or floor works well with mass. In our own judgment it responds slowly, so set schedules with that in mind.
  • Vented heaters only. Use sealed or vented units that exhaust outside. A federal consumer product safety agency says never to run unvented fuel-burning appliances where people sleep.

Carbon monoxide and wood stoves

The same safety agency says to put CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, away from heating vents and not directly above fuel-burning appliances. The test button checks the circuit, not the sensor, and alarms have a replacement age. If one sounds, go outside to fresh air and call 911; do not search for the source. Have the heating system and flues inspected every year.

A state extension guide on wood stoves says to keep combustibles such as furniture, rugs and draperies away from the stove, follow the maker's instructions, have a qualified person install and inspect it at least yearly, check the stovepipe for creosote through the season, and burn well-dried wood. It also says to install CO alarms throughout the home but not directly above or next to the stove. Exact clearance distances come from the stove's label and the installation manual, so we do not give a number here.

Field card: Heating and Cooling an Adobe or Cob Home

Mass smooths the day. It does not replace heat or dry walls.

  • Earth walls hold lots of heat but have modest R-value per inch.
  • Best with hot days and cool nights; weak in long cold or humid spells.
  • Time lag: heat arrives hours late, which smooths the daily swing.
  • Flush with cool night air in summer; use south glass for winter sun.
  • Our example: about 267 Btu/ft2, about 213,000 Btu on 800 ft2; real carryover is far less than 53 hours.
  • No vapor-tight paint or cement plaster; keep roof, drainage and footings dry.
  • Vented heaters only, CO alarms, and stove clearances from the label.
