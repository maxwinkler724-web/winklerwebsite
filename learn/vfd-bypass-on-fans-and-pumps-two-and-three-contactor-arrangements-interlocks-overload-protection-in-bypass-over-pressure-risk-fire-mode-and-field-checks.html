VFD Bypass on Fans and Pumps: Contactors, Interlocks and Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A variable frequency drive is a single point of failure for the fan or pump it runs. A bypass is the second path that lets the motor run straight across the line when the drive is faulted or out for service. It keeps the building going, but it also removes every speed limit the drive was providing. This lesson covers how the bypass is built, how it stays safe, and what changes at full speed.

What a bypass does

In drive mode, the drive makes the variable-frequency power the motor sees. In bypass mode, a contactor connects the motor directly to the line, so it runs at its fixed full speed with no soft start and no speed control. Design specifications from public universities describe the bypass as an output contactor and a bypass contactor, plus an isolation (service) switch and input fuses, all packaged with the drive. One of those specifications also expects the controller to watch both contactors and flag a welded contact or an open coil.

Two-contactor and three-contactor arrangements

Here is our own plain-language summary of the common layouts. Both are drawn in the figure below.

  • Two contactors. The drive output contactor sits between the drive and the motor, and the bypass contactor sits between the line and the motor. The drive input stays connected to the line through its disconnect or isolation switch, so the drive can stay powered while the motor is in bypass.
  • Three contactors. An input contactor is added ahead of the drive. With the input and output contactors open and the bypass closed, the drive is cut off from the line and from the motor, which is what lets it be serviced while the motor keeps running. Lockout and verification are still required, because the bypass path and the line side stay live.

Interlocks: the drive output must never back-feed the line

If the output contactor and bypass contactor were closed at the same time, line power would be tied to the drive output terminals, which can destroy the drive and create a hazard for anyone working on it. The two contactors are therefore interlocked, so closing one prevents closing the other. Most designs use both a mechanical interlock between the two contactors and electrical interlocks in the control circuit. The mechanical part is the one that still works when a control wire is wrong. The design specifications we read also interlock the enclosure doors so that input power is removed before an enclosure can be opened, and they require that outside safety interlocks (freeze, fire, smoke contacts) stay active in both drive mode and bypass mode.

Motor overload protection in bypass

In drive mode the drive protects the motor electronically. In bypass mode the drive is out of the circuit, so protection has to come from an overload device in the bypass path. One of the university specifications requires motor overload protection in both drive and bypass modes, and another asks for selectable trip class on the electronic overload and for detection of single-phase input while in bypass. The overload setting is set from the motor nameplate full-load amps (FLA), not from the drive setting and not from what the motor happens to draw. See the lesson on phase loss and overload settings for the trip details; we do not repeat them here.

Why full speed can hurt: over-pressure and overflow

Drive mode usually holds a pressure or flow setpoint, and the drive often runs the motor well below line speed. Bypass takes away that control. A fan at full speed can push duct static past what the ductwork, VAV boxes, dampers or access doors were built to see. A pump at full speed can overflow a loop, over-pressure coils or valves, or run off its curve. Before you move a selector to bypass on a live system, check these first:

  • High-static limit switch or safety: confirm it is wired, in the circuit, and not jumpered. It should trip the motor, not just alarm.
  • Damper positions: supply, return and relief paths open so the fan is not deadheaded, with fire and smoke dampers in their normal state.
  • Valve positions: for pumps, enough open control valves or a bypass leg so the pump is not running against a closed system, and not so open that it runs out on its curve.
  • Motor current: a bypass fan can pull far more power than the drive ever allowed it to. The power rises with the cube of speed, and the DOE motor guide notes that doubling speed takes eight times the power.

Fire or smoke override on drives

General practice, as shown in the university specifications we read, is that a drive or bypass has a dedicated input for smoke control and another programmable override input for fire-fighter or authority-required behavior. One of those documents says the smoke control input transfers the motor from drive to bypass and ignores keypad and serial commands. The same text says its override does not defeat the customer-defined safety run interlock. How an override should behave in a given building comes from the approved smoke control design and the local authority. Test it with the building owner, do not improvise it.

Worked example: duct static in bypass (our own example)

Our own example, with assumptions. A supply fan normally runs at 45 Hz on a 60 Hz motor and holds 1.2 in. w.c. at the fan. We assume the duct system and damper positions stay the same, so the fan laws apply, and we assume the fan draws 5 kW at 45 Hz and moves 10,000 cfm. These inputs are assumptions for the example, not measured data. The drive fails and the fan is moved to bypass at 60 Hz.

  1. Speed ratio = 60 / 45 = 1.333 (the fan will run 33% faster).
  2. Airflow scales with speed: 10,000 x 1.333 = about 13,333 cfm.
  3. Pressure scales with speed squared: 1.333 squared = 1.778. Static = 1.2 x 1.778 = about 2.13 in. w.c.
  4. Power scales with speed cubed: 1.333 cubed = 2.370. Power = 5 x 2.370 = about 11.9 kW.

Static goes from 1.2 to about 2.13 in. w.c., an increase of 78%, and the power more than doubles. If this duct class or the high-static limit was 2.0 in. w.c. (also an assumption), bypass would exceed it, and a motor sized near the 45 Hz load could trip its overload or overheat. Real fans deviate somewhat from the ideal laws, so treat the result as a screening estimate. The fan laws lesson covers the method.

Field checks

  • Selector switch positions. Know what Drive, Bypass and Off positions do on this panel and what the Hand-Off-Auto selector does. One specification describes a bypass Hand-Off-Auto selector plus a drive/bypass mode selector with indicator lights. Leave the switches where the owner wants them.
  • Contactor coils and contacts. With power off and locked out, look for burned or pitted contacts and a welded contact. Check coil voltage against the nameplate on the coil. Confirm the interlock moves freely and blocks the other contactor.
  • Overload setting. Compare the overload dial or electronic setting to the motor nameplate FLA, and check trip class.
  • Safety circuits. Verify high-static, freeze and smoke contacts work in bypass too.
  • Lockout. A failed drive does not mean a dead panel. The bypass path and line side can stay energized, and the drive can hold stored energy after power is removed. Federal workplace safety rules require that stored or residual energy be relieved or restrained and that isolation be verified before work (29 CFR 1910.147). See Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

Field card: VFD bypass

Bypass is full speed with no drive protecting the motor.

  • Bypass runs the motor across the line at full speed; the drive is out of the circuit.
  • Output and bypass contactors are interlocked, mechanically and electrically, so line power never reaches the drive output.
  • Overload protection must exist in bypass; set it from nameplate FLA.
  • Static pressure rises with speed squared; power with speed cubed. Example: 1.2 to about 2.13 in. w.c. going 45 to 60 Hz.
  • Check high-static limits, damper and valve positions before selecting bypass.
  • Lock out and verify; stored drive energy and a live bypass path remain.
