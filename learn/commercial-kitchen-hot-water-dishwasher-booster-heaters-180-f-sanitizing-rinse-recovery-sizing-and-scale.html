Dishwasher Booster Heaters: 180 F Rinse, Sizing and Scale

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A restaurant's building water heater is usually set well below what a high-temperature dish machine wants for its last rinse. A booster heater closes that gap, a small heater that sits near the machine and lifts 140 F water to about 180 F only for the rinse. This lesson explains why that number exists, how to size a booster from the rinse flow and the temperature rise, and what scale, pressure and safety have to do with it.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Health codes, plumbing codes and electrical codes differ by place and change. This page is general education built from sources read in 2026. Installing a booster is plumbing and electrical work (or gas work on a gas model), and it needs the proper license in your state, plus any permit and health department plan review. Follow the maker's installation manual and the local inspector.

Why the final rinse has a number

A high-temperature machine sanitizes with heat, not chemicals. The wash and rinse clean the dishes, and the last rinse has to be hot enough to kill germs on the surface. The model rule is the FDA Food Code, and states adopt their own version. New Jersey's rule for mechanical warewashing, N.J.A.C. 8:24-4.9, says the fresh hot water sanitizing rinse, measured as it enters the manifold, may not be less than 180 F for most machines or 165 F for a stationary rack, single-temperature machine, and may not be more than 194 F. The same rule sets the rinse flow pressure at 15 to 25 psi and requires a pressure gauge or similar device. Separately, NJ's sanitizing rule asks for a dish surface temperature of 160 F, checked with an irreversible registering indicator. The maker sizing guides we read, and the Department of Energy, all use 180 F as the working rinse figure. Confirm the exact numbers with your local health department, because they can differ from state to state and by machine type.

The common design is a machine that makes its own wash tank heat but gets the rinse water from the building at 140 F. Hot water that is plenty for a sink is too cool for the rinse, and setting the whole building to 180 F would waste energy and raise scald risk at every fixture. A Hatco article notes that building water heaters typically run 110 to 145 F, and that going higher for the whole building is impractical.

How a booster is sized

Sizing starts from two numbers: how much rinse water the machine uses, and how many degrees the booster must add. Hatco's sizing guide defines the rise as the goal temperature minus the hot water temperature you measure at the tap, and the goal is 180 F for a heat-sanitizing machine. The rinse use, in gallons per hour, comes from the machine's NSF listing or the maker. Hatco's formula is gallons per hour times the temperature rise, divided by 400, which gives kilowatts. Allied Engineering's booster manual writes the same relation the other way: gallons per hour equals 415 times kW divided by the rise. The small difference is the constant, so use the maker's own number for that product.

Where do those constants come from? A gallon of water weighs about 8.33 pounds and takes one Btu per pound per degree F. One kilowatt-hour is 3,412 Btu. So kW = gph x 8.33 x rise / 3412, and 3412 / 8.33 is about 410. The maker constants of 400 and 415 bracket that, which is why they agree closely. Shasta County, California, uses the same Btu logic in its health department guide, with an assumed electric efficiency of 98 percent.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assumptions: the machine's listing shows a rinse use of 70 gallons per hour (about 1.17 gpm while rinsing); the building water heater delivers 140 F at the booster; the target is 180 F; we ignore tank losses and treat the heater as 100 percent efficient.

  1. Temperature rise = 180 - 140 = 40 F.
  2. Heat needed = 70 gph x 8.33 lb/gal x 40 F = 23,324 Btu per hour.
  3. kW = 23,324 / 3412 = 6.84 kW.
  4. Cross-check with the Hatco constant: 70 x 40 / 400 = 7.0 kW. With the Allied constant: 70 x 40 / 415 = 6.75 kW.

So we would look for a booster rated at or above about 7 kW for a 40 F rise, then check the maker's chart and the voltage and phase available. Now change one input. If the building heater has drifted to 120 F, the rise is 60 F and the need becomes 70 x 8.33 x 60 / 3412 = 10.25 kW. A booster chosen for a 40 F rise would fall short. That is why the incoming temperature should be measured at the tap, not assumed. Allied's manual wants inlet water around 140 F and not above 170 F.

Recovery and storage

The kW rating sets recovery, the gallons per hour the heater can keep making at the stated rise. Allied's check example: a 4 kW unit at a 40 F rise gives about 41 gph. Storage matters because a rack cycle pulls the rinse in short bursts. Hatco lists a 6-gallon compact electric booster and a 16-gallon larger electric model rated up to 573 gph. A bigger tank can ride through the burst on stored hot water, while a small tank leans on the elements. Rinse draw can also include fill water for the wash tank, so the sizing chart may add that demand.

Scale and water treatment

Minerals come out of hard water when it is heated, and they coat elements, tanks, spray nozzles and rinse arms. Hatco says mineral buildup cuts efficiency and life, and that softening and treatment help. Allied's manual gives no numeric hardness limit but wants hardness, pH and chlorides at normal levels (pH 6.0 to 8.0, chlorine and chlorides below 100 ppm) and suggests a softener or a water treatment expert when unsure. For a gas-fired or tankless unit, see Tankless Gas Water Heater Problems: Flow, Scale and Flushing for the same scale pattern.

Pressure and piping

The rule above asks for 15 to 25 psi at the rinse. Allied's booster uses a pressure reducing valve preset at 20 psi, required if inlet pressure is higher, and says to readjust it with water running. It also asks for a reducing valve with a bypass, or thermal expansion can open the relief valve. Both Hatco and Allied say not to put a check valve on the supply line to the booster, and to use gate or ball valves rather than globe valves. Allied calls for copper tubing and an expansion tank close to the machine.

Low-temperature machines as the alternative

A low-temperature machine rinses with a chemical sanitizer in about 140 F water, per the Department of Energy buying guide, and N.J.A.C. 8:24-4.9 sets the chemical wash at no less than 120 F. Hatco says these machines can also use a booster, sized for a smaller rise. The trade-offs, per DOE, are chemical cost and handling against energy. ENERGY STAR certifies both machine types, with limits set by type and sanitizing approach, and the DOE page lists an ENERGY STAR door-type high-temperature model at 0.89 gallons per rack.

Safety

  • Scald. The San Diego Fire Department says 160 F water can burn skin in about one second. Rinse water at 180 F is far worse. See Water Heater Temperature: Scald Risk, Legionella and Mixing Valves and Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics.
  • Relief valve. Allied's manual says the factory relief valve opens above 210 F or 125 psi, must mount directly on the heater with no valve or restriction between, and must discharge downward, not directly to a sewer, ending within 6 inches of the floor where it is visible.
  • Dry firing. Allied warns to fill the tank with water before power is on, because dry elements burn out, and to purge air for at least five minutes.
  • Electrical. Hatco suggests confirming voltage and phase with a licensed electrician. Allied calls for copper conductors sized from the nameplate and wiring that follows the NEC and local code.
  • Controls. Hatco describes three probes: low water level, high temperature limit and thermostatic control.

Where it fits in the building

If the booster is fed through a long run, measure the inlet at the booster; the recirculation lesson Hot Water Recirculation Pumps: Timers, On-Demand and Crossover Valves covers how building loops work. Field card: Dishwasher Booster Heaters

Measure the inlet, size the rise, then check the rinse at the manifold.

  • High-temperature machines need a 180 F final rinse in most cases (165 F for a stationary rack single-temperature machine in NJ rule); max 194 F.
  • Rinse flow pressure: 15 to 25 psi with a gauge (NJ rule).
  • Rise = 180 minus measured inlet temperature.
  • kW = gph x 8.33 x rise / 3412, or maker formula (Hatco /400, Allied 415 x kW / rise).
  • Our example: 70 gph, 40 F rise is about 6.84 kW. 60 F rise needs about 10.25 kW.
  • No check valve on supply; PRV with bypass; relief valve piped down, visible.
  • Soften or treat hard water; scale kills elements.
  • Plumbing, gas and electrical work need the proper license.
