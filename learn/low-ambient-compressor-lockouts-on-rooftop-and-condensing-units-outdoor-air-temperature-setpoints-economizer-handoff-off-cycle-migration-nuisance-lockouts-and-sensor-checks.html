Low-Ambient Compressor Lockouts on Rooftop and Condensing Units

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Cooling compressors are built to run against warm outdoor air. When the air outside turns cold, the controls on a rooftop unit or condensing unit are supposed to stop the compressor and let something else do the cooling. This lesson covers that lockout: what it protects, how it hands off to free cooling, and why it sometimes locks out a server room that really does need cooling.

Why a cold-air compressor is a problem

An air-cooled condenser rejects heat to outdoor air. The colder that air is, the lower the condensing pressure falls, and a cooling circuit needs a minimum pressure difference to push refrigerant through its metering device. With too little head pressure the evaporator can starve, the coil can freeze, and oil may not return to the compressor well. A maker's applications manual we read says its standard split cooling systems are rated for operation down to 55 F outdoor air, and that below that the system needs field additions such as a fan speed controller for the condenser and a crankcase heater. That is the design basis behind most lockouts: either you add head pressure control, or you stop the compressor. For how head pressure control works, see Low-Ambient Cooling: Head Pressure Control, Fan Cycling and Flooding Valves. We will not repeat it here.

The lockout setting

Most commercial unit controllers include an outdoor air temperature lockout for mechanical cooling. The unit reads a small outdoor sensor, and when the reading is below the setpoint the compressors are not allowed to start, whatever thermostat or zone sensor asks for. The setpoint is a maker setting and it varies, so find the right number in the unit's controls manual and the installation sheet, not in a rule of thumb. If the unit is a condensing unit paired with an air handler, the lockout may sit in a field-installed low-ambient control or in the building controller instead of the unit itself.

A lockout is not the same as a low pressure safety. The low pressure switch reacts after pressure has already dropped. The lockout acts earlier, from air temperature, so the compressor never runs in the conditions that cause the problem.

Handing off to free cooling

If a unit has an air economizer, the cold outdoor air becomes the cooling source. Economizer control compares outdoor conditions with a high limit, above which outdoor air stops being useful for cooling. A national-lab training deck on economizer operation suggests setting the operating range as wide as possible: between 30 F and 75 F for dry climates, between 30 F and 68 F for normal climates, and between 30 F and 65 F for humid climates. A separate national-lab field report says a fixed dry-bulb high limit is usually set between 55 F and 75 F. Those are general ranges, not settings for your unit.

The handoff works in order. Warm air above the high limit: compressors cool, the damper sits at minimum. Between the lockout and the high limit: the damper opens and, if it cannot meet the load alone, the compressor is allowed to add capacity. Below the lockout: the damper does the work and the compressor is blocked. Our economizer settings lesson covers changeover, minimum position and the low limit; economizer fault diagnosis covers damper checks. If the damper is stuck closed, a locked-out unit has no cooling at all, which is why the two systems have to be checked together.

The 2018 IECC, as summarized by a state university energy-code outreach group, requires economizers on some systems once cooling capacity passes 300,000 Btu/h for most occupancies, with different values for other cases, and requires fault detection on air-cooled unitary units with economizers. It also says the system must monitor supply, return and outside air temperatures and report sensor faults and improper economizing. That second point matters for lockouts: a bad outdoor sensor can make both the lockout and the economizer misbehave.

Off-cycle migration and the crankcase heater

A locked-out compressor sits idle in cold air for hours. Refrigerant vapor tends to move to the coldest part of the system and can condense in the compressor crankcase, mixing with the oil. When the compressor finally starts, that liquid can flash, foam the oil and starve the bearings. The maker's manual we read lists a crankcase heater as required for its cooling systems with low-ambient head pressure control, and shows a liquid line solenoid valve that closes during the off cycle. When a lockout releases after a long cold spell, the heater should have been energized long enough to boil off the refrigerant first. Read Crankcase Heaters: Why Compressors Need Them and How to Test One for the test, and follow the maker's wait time before restart.

Nuisance lockouts on server and kitchen loads

Some spaces need cooling when it is cold outside. A server room or network closet produces heat all winter. A commercial kitchen can push a dining room to call for cooling on a cold day. If the economizer is working, it handles those loads, and a lockout causes no trouble. If it is not working, the room overheats and someone calls the tech.

Before raising or bypassing a lockout, check these in order:

  1. Is the economizer damper open and moving? Command it and watch it.
  2. Is the outdoor sensor reading true? See the next section.
  3. Is the damper held at minimum by a ventilation or freeze setting?
  4. Does this space need a dedicated system designed for year-round cooling, with head pressure control built in? See Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet.

Do not lower a lockout to make a complaint go away unless the maker's data says the unit is rated for that air temperature with the head pressure control that is installed.

Check the sensor against a thermometer

The lockout is only as good as its input. A sensor in direct sun, or one that has drifted, can read many degrees off. To check it, let the sensor settle in shade, hold a trusted thermometer beside it without touching the unit's metal, wait for the reading to stop moving, and compare it with what the controller shows. Record both. If they differ, check the sensor's mounting, wiring and any offset entered in the controls before replacing it. Sensor type and expected value tables are in the controls manual. We do not give pass or fail numbers here because they depend on the sensor and the controller.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a field record. The assumptions are ours: a rooftop unit serving a server room has a call for cooling all day. Mechanical cooling lockout is 50 F, the economizer high limit is a fixed 65 F dry-bulb, the low limit is 30 F, and there is no differential. Each reading below holds for its 3-hour block. Compressor stage 2 is allowed in the 50 to 65 F band only if the damper alone cannot meet the load.

12 AM38 FAbove 30 and below 50Free cooling only
3 AM31 FAbove 30 and below 50Free cooling only
6 AM28 FBelow 30 low limitMinimum air, compressor locked out, watch room temperature
9 AM41 FAbove 30 and below 50Free cooling only
12 PM57 FAbove 50 and below 65Free cooling, compressor may assist
3 PM68 FAbove 65 high limitCompressor only, minimum air
6 PM62 FAbove 50 and below 65Free cooling, compressor may assist
9 PM49 FBelow 50 by 1 FFree cooling only

Arithmetic: compressor allowed in 3 blocks (12 PM, 3 PM, 6 PM), so 3 × 3 = 9 hours; locked out in the other 5 blocks, 5 × 3 = 15 hours. 9 + 15 = 24. The 6 AM block at 28 F is the weak spot: the economizer is at minimum and the compressor is blocked, so the room only stays cool if internal heat is low or the unit has another cold-weather cooling method. The 9 PM block at 49 F shows how a 1 F difference decides the mode, which is why the sensor check matters.

Field card: Low-Ambient Compressor Lockouts on Rooftop and Condensing Units

Cold outdoor air: the damper cools, the compressor waits.

  • A lockout blocks the compressor by outdoor air temperature, before pressure drops.
  • The setpoint is a maker setting. Read the controls manual.
  • Economizer handoff: damper first in cool air, compressor only above the lockout if needed.
  • After a long lockout, let the crankcase heater run the maker's wait time before restart.
  • Server and kitchen complaints in winter: check the damper and sensor first.
  • Check the outdoor sensor against a thermometer in shade.
  • Do not lower a lockout without maker data and head pressure control.
