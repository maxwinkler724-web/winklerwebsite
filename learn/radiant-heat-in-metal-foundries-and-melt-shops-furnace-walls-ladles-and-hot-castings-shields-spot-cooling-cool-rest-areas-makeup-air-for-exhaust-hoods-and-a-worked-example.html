Radiant Heat in Foundries and Melt Shops: Shields, Spot Cooling and Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Stand near an open melt or a freshly poured casting and your face and chest burn long before the air around you feels unusual. That is radiation. For a tech asked to cool or ventilate a melt shop, the first lesson is that moving more room air does little for a worker facing a glowing surface. This page explains why, what actually helps, and how to put numbers on it.

Why radiation dominates near hot metal

Every surface sends out infrared energy, and the amount climbs with the fourth power of its absolute temperature. A furnace wall, an open bath, a ladle of molten metal and a casting just out of the mold are all very hot surfaces, so they throw heat across the room in straight lines. A worker standing in that line absorbs it directly, whatever the air temperature. A federal workplace safety technical manual names foundries, along with glass plants and boiler areas, as places with a significant radiant heat source. The federal occupational health institute's criteria document on heat gives a simple body-side picture: radiant exchange depends on the difference between the temperature of the surrounding surfaces and the worker's skin temperature, not on the air temperature. When those surfaces are hotter than skin, the worker gains heat.

A federal health hazard evaluation of an aluminum casting plant shows the scale. Investigators measured black globe temperatures of about 180°F in front of an open furnace door, and recommended keeping furnace doors closed when not needed. A globe thermometer responds to radiation as well as air, which is why it reads so high there.

Why general ventilation alone does not cool the worker

General ventilation changes the air in the room. It does little to the radiant stream landing on a worker's skin, because that energy never travels through the air as heat. Moving air can help sweat evaporate, but the same hazard evaluation warns that fan air above about 95°F can add heat by convection, and in that plant fan air exceeded 100°F. Hot shop air blown on a worker is not relief. Ventilation still matters for fumes and for removing the convective heat that rises off the process, but it cannot be the only control for radiant load.

Controls that work on radiation

  • Insulate or line the hot surface. The federal technical manual lists insulating hot surfaces such as furnace walls as an engineering control. The hazard evaluation recommended refractory linings on equipment that carries hot material.
  • Put a shield between the source and the worker. The criteria document says to place radiant-reflecting or heat-absorbing shielding or barriers between the heat source and the worker when surroundings exceed skin temperature. The hazard evaluation recommended extra heat shields along furnace sides and near transfer troughs where workers stand. It also found that a lift truck cab with a clear plastic window worked as a radiant shield, cutting globe temperature from about 181°F to 107°F.
  • Add distance or limit time. The criteria document lists increasing distance and isolating the source, and it lists more recovery time in a cool place as a way to limit time in the heat.
  • Reflective clothing. The hazard evaluation suggests reflective aprons where workers face radiant sources, paired with cooling when convective load is also high. The criteria document cautions that heat-reflective aprons, leggings and gauntlets are among ensembles that call for lowering exposure limits, so the worker carries more strain than the thermometer suggests.

Spot cooling with directed air (man coolers)

Where workers stay in fixed spots, such as at a tapping station or a pour deck, supplying cool air right at the person is far more effective than cooling the whole building. The hazard evaluation recommended spot cooling, meaning cool air supplied near frequently occupied spots, and it named ceiling vents and air-conditioned rest or monitoring areas as options. For a tech, the design questions are supply temperature (cooler than the worker's skin by a useful margin, never hot shop air), throw so the stream reaches the person, and noise and drafts. The federal technical manual also lists cooling fans and air conditioning as engineering controls. See Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers in Warehouses and Server Emergencies for the equipment side.

Cool rest areas

The criteria document says employers should provide a cool area, for example air-conditioned or shaded, for workers to rest and recover. In a melt shop that means a room with its own cooling, away from the radiant sources, close enough that people actually use it. Door swings and leaks matter: a rest room fed with hot shop air defeats its purpose. For general heat-illness signs and water and rest habits, see Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs for HVAC Techs.

Makeup air for big exhaust hoods

Melt shops exhaust a lot of air through canopy and capture hoods for fume and heat. Every cubic foot taken out must come back in. The federal technical manual's ventilation appendix says exhaust must be balanced by replacement air, and that open doors, windows and louvers are acceptable sources, while leakage through cracks and roof vents is not. A dedicated makeup air system, usually interlocked with the exhaust, avoids strong drafts through gaps and lets the incoming air be tempered. Without it, the room goes negative and exhaust airflow drops. The same manual says that when a hood has adequate flow but poor control, check for cross-drafts from sources including worker-cooling fans, supply air and open doors, and that cross-drafts can cut hood effectiveness by as much as 75 percent. So spot-cooling streams and makeup air registers must be aimed so they do not blow across the hood face. Tempering makeup air in winter is worth the cost, but air supplied to workers is a separate stream from air supplied to replace the hood. A similar canopy-hood situation on a small scale appears in Glassblowing Studio and Blacksmith Forge Ventilation.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not measured data. It estimates the radiant heat flux from a hot surface to a worker, using the net exchange form shown in university heat transfer lecture notes: q = ε × σ × (T14 − T24), with σ about 0.1714 × 10−8 Btu/h per square foot per R4. That gives the form used here: q = ε × 0.1714 × ((T1/100)4 − (T2/100)4), with temperatures in degrees Rankine (°F + 460).

Assumptions (ours): hot casting surface 1,200°F; worker clothing and skin surface 100°F; one effective emissivity of 0.8 for the pair; the surface fills the worker's view and the worker is directly facing it. The last assumption makes this an upper bound; a real worker at a distance sees only part of the source.

  1. T1 = 1,200 + 460 = 1,660 R. T2 = 100 + 460 = 560 R.
  2. (1,660/100)4 = 16.64 = 75,933. (560/100)4 = 5.64 = 983.
  3. Difference = 75,933 − 983 = 74,950.
  4. q = 0.8 × 0.1714 × 74,950 = about 10,300 Btu/h per square foot.

With a shield. Now assume a shield sits in between and its worker-facing surface runs at 300°F (760 R), for example because it is water-cooled or well ventilated on its back side. Same emissivity.

  1. (760/100)4 = 7.64 = 3,336.
  2. Difference = 3,336 − 983 = 2,353.
  3. q = 0.8 × 0.1714 × 2,353 = about 323 Btu/h per square foot.

The flux falls from about 10,300 to about 323, a drop of roughly 97 percent. If the worker only sees 10 percent of the source (an assumed view factor of 0.1), those become about 1,030 and 32. Even the smaller numbers show why distance and shielding matter. One caution from the university notes: an ordinary passive shield that is not cooled settles at a temperature between the two sides, and for two plates with equal emissivity the notes show that one shield cuts the flux only about in half. Large reductions need a cooled or ventilated shield, a low-emissivity face, or several layers. Treat this as arithmetic for understanding, not a design value.

Field card: Radiant Heat in Foundries and Melt Shops

Radiation ignores room air. Block it, cool the person, and replace what the hoods exhaust.

  • Radiant exchange depends on surface temperature versus skin temperature, not air temperature.
  • Radiation scales with absolute temperature to the fourth power, so a hotter surface matters a great deal.
  • Shields, insulation, distance and closed furnace doors act on the source.
  • Spot cooling must be cool air, aimed at the worker and away from hood faces.
  • Cool rest areas need their own cooling and no hot-shop leakage.
  • Big exhaust hoods need balanced makeup air; cross-drafts can wreck capture.
