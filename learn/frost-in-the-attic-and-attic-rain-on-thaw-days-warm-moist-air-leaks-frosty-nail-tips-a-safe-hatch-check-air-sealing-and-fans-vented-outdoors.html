Frost in the Attic and Attic Rain on Thaw Days

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You climb up for a flashlight look during a cold snap and the underside of the roof is white, with every nail tip furry with frost. A few days later it warms up and the ceiling gets a drip. Nothing is wrong with the shingles. The attic is catching moisture from your living space, and this lesson shows where it comes from, how to look safely, and what to fix first.

What is happening

A cold attic roof deck sits near the outdoor temperature. Warm indoor air holds far more water vapor than cold air can. When that air leaks upward and meets the cold wood and nail tips, the vapor turns directly to frost, and it keeps stacking up as long as the cold lasts. When the weather warms, the frost melts and runs or drips, which people call attic rain. A university extension housing fact sheet names humid indoor air moving into the attic during the heating season as the main source of attic moisture, and says that vapor condenses on the cold attic surfaces. A federal building-science report adds that moving air carries roughly an order of magnitude more water vapor through a building shell than diffusion through the materials does. Leaks matter more than the ceiling material.

Where the moist air gets in

  • Attic hatch or pull-down stairs. The housing fact sheet lists attic access stairs among the penetrations that need air sealing. A thin, unweatherstripped hatch is a common big hole.
  • Recessed lights. A university extension page on ice dams names recessed lights as a feature that raises ice dam risk because they let warm air out of the house.
  • Plumbing, wire and chimney-chase gaps. Holes drilled for pipes, cables and vent pipes through the top-floor ceiling or wall top plates. The housing fact sheet lists vent pipes and fans among the unsealed penetrations.
  • Bath and kitchen fans that end in the attic. The housing fact sheet says fans vented into attics cause severe moisture problems and that they should always vent outdoors. The federal environmental agency moisture page also says to push bath and kitchen air out of the building, not into the attic.
  • Leaky or uninsulated attic ducts. The ice dam page lists inadequately insulated or leaky attic ductwork as a heat source. Leaks in a return or supply duct also move house air into the attic. See our ice dam and attic HVAC lesson for the duct side.

How to check safely from the hatch

This checklist is our own common-sense guidance, not a quote from a source. Treat it as a minimum, and skip the check if you are unsure of your footing.

  1. Pick a cold, dry morning during a cold snap, when frost is at its heaviest. Wear eye protection and a dust mask, and take a bright flashlight.
  2. Open the hatch and look from the ladder. Do not step onto the ceiling below; drywall will not hold a person, and you can fall through. Never put a foot between the joists on the drywall.
  3. Sweep the light across the underside of the roof deck. Look for frost or damp on nail tips, near the eaves, and for dark staining around the bath fan duct.
  4. Look at the bath fan duct. A hose that is loose, disconnected or simply ends in the insulation is dumping humid air into the attic.
  5. Look for flattened or dirty insulation near lights, pipes and the hatch. Dark, dusty streaks in insulation often mark the path of leaking air.
  6. Do not touch wiring, and do not pull insulation away from recessed light housings. Take photos from the hatch and stop there.

What to fix first

The ice dam page says to seal the ceiling first so warm, moist air cannot move from the house into the attic, and to consider more insulation only after that. The housing fact sheet recommends the same order and notes that a typical homeowner may not have the skill to seal an attic well, so hiring a professional is reasonable. Our order of work:

  1. Vent every fan outdoors. A fan that ends in the attic is the easiest large source to remove. Our bathroom exhaust fan lesson covers ducting. The housing fact sheet suggests running bath fans during a shower and for at least 15 minutes afterward.
  2. Seal the ceiling holes. Hatch, lights, pipe and wire holes, and duct boots, in that order of size. The housing fact sheet says air sealing between living space and attic comes before extra insulation. Our attic insulation and air sealing lesson goes deeper.
  3. Seal and insulate attic ducts. Leaks add to the load.
  4. Add insulation last. It keeps the attic colder, so it works best after the leaks are closed.

Lower the indoor humidity during deep cold

The federal environmental agency says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. Its moisture-control page also lists maximum indoor humidity levels that go down as the outdoor temperature falls: 35 percent at 20 F outdoors, 30 percent at 10 F, and 25 percent at 0 F. It says a humidistat is set too high if moisture collects on windows. In a cold snap, turn the humidifier down, run the bath and kitchen fans, and vent the clothes dryer outdoors. Our humidistat lesson shows how outdoor reset does this automatically. Lowering humidity helps, but it does not replace sealing the leaks.

Worked example (our own): how much water can one leak carry up?

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measurement of a real house. Assume 50 cfm of 70 F indoor air at 35 percent relative humidity leaks into the attic. The humidity ratio of that air is about 0.0055 lb of water per lb of dry air (an assumed psychrometric value). Assume the outdoor air on a 10 F day has a humidity ratio of about 0.0013. Air density is 0.075 lb per cubic foot, and one gallon of water is 8.34 lb.

  1. Air flow per hour: 50 cfm x 60 min = 3,000 cubic feet per hour.
  2. Dry air per hour: 3,000 x 0.075 = 225 lb per hour.
  3. Humidity ratio difference: 0.0055 - 0.0013 = 0.0042 lb water per lb dry air. We use the difference because outdoor air coming in to replace it carries a little water.
  4. Net water carried up: 225 x 0.0042 = 0.945 lb per hour.
  5. Per day: 0.945 x 24 = 22.7 lb.
  6. In gallons: 22.7 / 8.34 = about 2.7 gallons per day.

Not all of that stays in the attic. Some leaves through attic vents, and the frost only holds what freezes out. Still, a few gallons per day at this leak rate shows why a cold snap can coat the roof deck. If the indoor air were at 30 percent instead, our own estimate of the humidity ratio is about 0.0047. The difference is 0.0034, so the net water drops to about 0.77 lb per hour, or roughly 2.2 gallons per day. A drier house helps, but the air leak itself is still the larger lever, because the water scales directly with the cfm.

When it is mold or a roof leak

The housing fact sheet gives a useful test: roof-leak stains are small and local, while condensation stains cover a large area, and heavy mold on the roof sheathing signals condensation. A leak usually follows rain or melting snow at one spot, such as near a chimney, vent pipe or valley. Frost-and-thaw staining follows cold snaps, shows up across a wide area, and often tracks the leak paths above. If you find a roof leak, tell a roofer. The federal environmental agency mold guide says to dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, because in most cases mold will not grow if that is done. It says homeowners can usually handle a moldy area smaller than about 10 square feet, and that larger areas or significant water damage call for a professional with mold cleanup experience. It also says mold can grow unnoticed above ceiling tiles from roof leaks or too little insulation. Wet insulation that stays wet, black or fuzzy growth on the deck, or a ceiling stain that keeps growing are signs to call a professional.

Questions about permits or rules for the work depend on your local building department, so ask them.

Field card: Frost in the Attic and Attic Rain on Thaw Days

Frost up top means leaks from below.

  • Frost on nail tips is moisture from the house freezing on a cold roof deck.
  • Look from the hatch: stay on the ladder and never step on ceiling drywall.
  • Fix first: fans vented outdoors, then ceiling air sealing, then insulation.
  • Keep indoor humidity lower as it gets colder outside (federal environmental agency: about 30 percent at 10 F).
  • Our example: 50 cfm leak at 70 F, 35 percent RH carries about 0.95 lb per hour, 2.7 gal per day.
  • Large, spread-out stains: condensation. One small spot after rain: roof leak.
  • Dry wet material within 24 to 48 hours. Mold over about 10 square feet: call a professional.
