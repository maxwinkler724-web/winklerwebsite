Compressed Air Systems for Facility Techs: Leaks, Pressure, Dryers and Drains

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Plant air is easy to treat as free because the tool just works when you pull the trigger. It is not free. The U.S. Department of Energy calls compressed air probably the most expensive form of energy in a plant. This lesson covers the parts of a system, the maintenance items that waste the most, and one worked leak-cost calculation.

Check your site first

Check your site first. This page is general education built from federal and maker documents. Your site procedures, the equipment manual and your supervisor come first, and only qualified people work on compressors and their electrical supply.

Why air is expensive

The DOE and Compressed Air Challenge sourcebook says compressed air systems usually have a wire-to-work efficiency of around 10 percent, and that more than 80 percent of the electrical energy going to a compressor becomes available heat. A DOE tip sheet gives a similar picture: overall efficiency as low as 10 to 15 percent, and a 1-hp air motor at 100 psig drawing roughly 7 to 8 hp of electrical power at the compressor. The same tip sheet says about 10 percent of electricity at typical facilities goes to making compressed air, and 30 percent or more at some. So every unit of air that escapes or is used for a job that did not need it carries a large electric bill behind it.

The parts, in plain terms

The sourcebook lists compressor families as positive displacement (reciprocating and rotary) and dynamic (centrifugal and axial). The receiver stores air to meet peak demand and helps control system pressure. For dryers the sourcebook names refrigerant, regenerative-desiccant, deliquescent, heat-of-compression and membrane types, and notes that refrigerant dryers are the most commonly used. Filters, separators and aftercoolers sit in the same train, and the sourcebook lists them among the parts that cause pressure drop. The Compressed Air Challenge adds that air drier or cleaner than the end uses need wastes money and energy, so ask what the tools actually require before changing dryer settings.

Drains: timed versus no-loss

Condensate has to leave the system somewhere. The sourcebook lists four drain styles: manual, level-operated mechanical, electrically operated solenoid (timed), and zero air-loss traps with reservoirs. It criticizes the timed solenoid because it operates even when little or no condensate is present, and each open cycle blows air to the drain. Kaeser says its level-controlled electronic drains discharge condensate with zero compressed air loss and work independently of condensate volume. A drain that is wedged open is a leak you can hear at the receiver.

Finding leaks

The sourcebook says the best way to find leaks is an ultrasonic acoustic detector, and that soapy water on suspect fittings is a simpler alternative. It also describes a shutdown style test: with no demand on the system, run the compressor and time its loaded period (T) and unloaded period (t). Leakage in percent is T times 100 divided by T plus t. A pressure-decay version uses system volume and how fast pressure falls. The sourcebook says a poorly maintained plant will likely lose about 20 percent of capacity to leaks, and that a proactive program can bring that under 10 percent. It names two program styles, tag and repair later, or seek and repair at once, tells you to fix the biggest leaks first, and says the program must be ongoing because leaks keep appearing.

Pressure setpoints

The sourcebook says that for each 2 psi increase in discharge pressure at around 100 psig, energy use rises approximately 1 percent at full output flow. It also says stabilizing the header pressure at its lowest practical range minimizes leakage. Higher pressure also raises air use at unregulated end uses. The sourcebook calls that excess artificial demand. The Compressed Air Challenge likewise says to deliver air at the lowest practical pressure. As a tech, do not change a setpoint on your own. A drop that starves one machine at the far end of the plant can be misread as a compressor problem, so take pressure readings along the system first and talk to the person who owns the controls.

Inappropriate uses

The sourcebook's list of potentially inappropriate uses includes open blowing, personnel cooling, open hand-held blowguns, cabinet cooling and vacuum generation, which it describes as under 4 percent total efficiency. When a blower or electric device can do the job, the air line is the costly way to do it.

Worked example: one 1/8 inch leak (Our own arithmetic)

Inputs and where they come from: a 1/8 in orifice at 100 psig leaks 26 cfm in the ENERGY STAR table as reproduced by the Arkansas state environment agency and an NV Energy fact sheet (the DOE sourcebook table gives dollars, not cfm). For power we use 18 kW per 100 cfm, the average a DOE plant-audit case study reports for generating air. Our own choices: 8,760 hours a year and $0.15 per kWh. A sharp-edged hole would leak less, since the table's footnote multiplies by 0.61, but we use the base value.

  1. Power per cfm: 18 kW / 100 cfm = 0.18 kW per cfm.
  2. Leak power: 26 cfm x 0.18 = 4.68 kW.
  3. Annual energy: 4.68 kW x 8,760 h = 40,996.8 kWh, about 41,000 kWh.
  4. Annual cost: 40,996.8 kWh x $0.15 = $6,149.52, about $6,150.

Cross-check, Our own: the DOE sourcebook says a 1/8 in leak costs $2,095 a year at $0.05 per kWh with constant operation. Dividing, $2,095 / $0.05 = 41,900 kWh, which is close to our 41,000 kWh. Tripling the rate to $0.15 would give about $6,285. The two agree within about 2 percent on energy, which is reassuring for a rule-of-thumb figure. Your own plant's kW per 100 cfm will differ, so use a measured value when you have one.

Condensate handling

The sourcebook says a separator is generally installed after each intercooler or aftercooler to remove condensed moisture, and that contaminated condensate needs its lubricant removed before it goes to a sewer, with limits set by the local sewage authority. Kaeser states that all compressor condensate contains some contaminants and that regulations commonly limit oil content before discharge. It describes oil-water separators that use replaceable filter cartridges, and warns that an unmonitored or poorly maintained separator can release oil-laden condensate. We did not find specific rules named in the sources we read, so ask your site environmental contact what applies.

Safety

  • Stored energy. OSHA's lockout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, lists pneumatic energy among energy sources and requires hazardous stored or residual energy to be relieved, disconnected, restrained or otherwise made safe, with isolation verified. Receivers and piping hold pressure after the compressor stops. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.
  • Cleaning with air. 29 CFR 1910.242(b) says compressed air shall not be used for cleaning except where reduced to less than 30 psi, and then only with effective chip guarding and personal protective equipment. An OSHA letter of interpretation says employers should not allow employees to use compressed air to clean themselves or their clothing, and warns that eyes and the respiratory system may be damaged. Never point air at skin.

Where this connects

Older buildings use a small compressor, dryer and tubing for control air; that is covered in Pneumatic HVAC Controls. Compressors are also rotating equipment, so see Vibration Analysis Basics, Infrared Thermography and Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings.

Field card: Compressed Air Systems

Air is costly: find leaks, fix drains, keep pressure only as high as needed.

  • Wire-to-work efficiency is roughly 10 percent, per DOE and Compressed Air Challenge.
  • Find leaks with an ultrasonic detector; soapy water works on fittings.
  • Timed drains blow air when no condensate is present; level-controlled drains do not.
  • About 2 psi of discharge pressure is about 1 percent of energy near 100 psig.
  • Our own: one 1/8 in leak at 100 psig, 26 cfm, about 41,000 kWh and $6,150 a year at $0.15.
  • Relieve and verify stored air before opening a line.
  • Under 30 psi, with guarding and PPE, for cleaning. Never on skin or clothing.
