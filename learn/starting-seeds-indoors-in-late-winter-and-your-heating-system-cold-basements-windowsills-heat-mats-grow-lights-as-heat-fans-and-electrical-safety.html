Starting Seeds Indoors and Your Heating System

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Late winter is when many households start vegetable and flower seeds indoors. The seedlings want warm roots, bright light and moving air. The house offers cold basements, drafty windowsills and heating equipment that is a bad neighbor for a tray of wet soil. This lesson shows where the heat in a seed-starting setup really comes from, how it compares with a space heater, and how to keep the electrical side safe.

Why the place you pick matters

A university extension guide says the potting mix in a container can run up to 5°F cooler than the room air, and that cool soil can lead to seedling loss from disease. It also says to keep the air above 60°F and to avoid windowsills and basements that run too cold. Another extension guide says most seeds germinate at soil temperatures of 65 to 75°F, while cool-season crops such as broccoli, cabbage and spinach prefer 55 to 65°F. So the question is not only "is it bright" but "how warm is the root zone, day and night."

A windowsill is the classic trap. Glass is the coldest surface in a heated room and drafts slip around the frame at night. See our lesson on window condensation for why glass runs cold. An unheated basement is steady but cool, which is why a heat mat or a shelf in a warmer room often works better. Our houseplant lesson covers the same window and register problems for grown plants.

Heat mats and thermostats

An extension guide says bottom heat helps seeds sprout sooner and helps prevent damping off, and that a heating mat is useful for warmth-loving seeds such as tomatoes and peppers in a cool room. Another extension guide recommends pairing a mat with a thermostat, which it says prevents containers from getting too hot. The first guide also says not to plug a heat mat into a light timer. We read that as a reminder that a mat should follow its own thermostat, not a clock. Mats we see are small loads, often in the tens of watts, so what you read on the nameplate is the number to use. Follow the maker's instructions for the mat you own.

Grow lights are a small heater too

Every watt a light uses ends up as heat in the room, whatever the lamp type. The extension guide advises keeping lights no more than 4 inches above the seedling tops, with 2 inches ideal, and giving 12 to 16 hours of light a day with a dark period at night. Another guide notes that lights close to the seeds give off some warmth. That warmth is a bonus for the tray but small for the house, as the example below shows. Lights on a timer are fine. Heat mats are the exception noted above.

Humidity domes and damping-off

An extension guide says a clear dome or bag keeps moisture in, and that covered trays should not sit in direct sun because the greenhouse effect can overheat them. Another guide says to take the dome off once seedlings are tall enough to touch it. Damping-off is seedling rot at the soil line caused by soilborne fungi and water molds. A university plant-health guide says it is worst in saturated soil and humid conditions, and lists prevention: avoid overwatering, use sterile mix and cleaned trays, do not overseed, and space plants so air moves between them. That guide discusses air movement only through spacing. A small fan on low speed is a common way to keep the air from going stale, but we did not find a source here that sets a fan speed or schedule, so we keep that advice general: gentle, not blowing straight on the soil.

Keep trays off registers and radiators

A floor register or a radiator is a strong local heat source. Soil that sits on one can dry out in hours and then get overwatered to compensate, which sets up the conditions for damping-off. Warm air from a register is also dry, and a tray in that stream may swing hot and cold as the furnace cycles. Place the shelf where the room is steadily warm and use a mat with a thermostat for root warmth. Do not block the register either: see our lesson on blocked vents and returns.

Worked example: what does one shelf add to the room? (our own example)

Our own example. These are assumptions, not measured values: one shelf with four 40 W LED grow lights on 16 hours a day, plus two 20 W heat mats that we assume draw full power 24 hours a day (a thermostat will cycle them off part of the time, so this is an upper bound). The conversion is 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h. We compare the shelf with a 1,500 W space heater.

  1. Lights: 4 × 40 W = 160 W. Heat: 160 × 3.412 = 546 Btu/h while on.
  2. Mats: 2 × 20 W = 40 W. Heat: 40 × 3.412 = 136 Btu/h while on.
  3. Whole shelf, everything on: 160 + 40 = 200 W, or 200 × 3.412 = 682 Btu/h.
  4. Daily energy, lights: 0.160 kW × 16 h = 2.56 kWh.
  5. Daily energy, mats: 0.040 kW × 24 h = 0.96 kWh.
  6. Total: 2.56 + 0.96 = 3.52 kWh a day. That is 3.52 × 3,412 = about 12,010 Btu a day, or an average of about 500 Btu/h over 24 hours.
  7. Space heater: 1,500 W × 3.412 = 5,118 Btu/h. The shelf at full load is 200 ÷ 1,500, about 13 percent of that.
  8. The heater would use the shelf's daily 3.52 kWh in 3.52 ÷ 1.5, about 2.35 hours of running.

So the shelf is a small fraction of a space heater's output. It helps a cold corner a little, but it is not a heating plan. Do not use a space heater to warm a seed shelf; our space heater lesson covers why those need their own cord and clearances. Electric heat is also 100 percent converted: every watt-hour you put in becomes heat in the house, which is why the Btu/h figure equals the nameplate watts times 3.412.

Electrical safety for mats and lights

  • Water and power meet here. A state fire services fact sheet says a qualified electrician should install GFCI receptacles near water, such as kitchens, bathrooms and other moisture-prone areas. Seed trays get watered, spilled and misted, so plug into a GFCI-protected outlet and keep cords and plugs above the drip line. Learn more in our GFCI lesson.
  • Do not overload a power strip. The same fact sheet says power strips and extension cords are for temporary use only, that you should not overload strips, and that they suit a few low-current devices. The shelf above draws 200 W, which is about 1.7 amps at 120 V (200 ÷ 120, our arithmetic). That is modest, but do not add a heater, a dehumidifier or a second shelf to the same strip.
  • Keep cords visible. The fact sheet says not to run cords under rugs, carpet or furniture or pinch them behind furniture.
  • Check the listing label. A federal safety fact sheet says to look for a label from an independent testing lab on the product and the package, and to replace cracked or worn cords.
  • Mats and lights follow the maker's directions. Do not cover a mat in ways the instructions forbid, and unplug anything with a damaged cord.

If a breaker trips, a plug runs warm or you smell hot plastic, unplug the load and have a qualified electrician look at the circuit.

Field card: Starting Seeds Indoors and Your Heating System

Warm roots, steady room, no overloaded strip.

  • Avoid cold windowsills and cold basements; keep the air above 60°F and use a thermostat-controlled mat for the roots.
  • Do not put a heat mat on a light timer.
  • Lights as heat: 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h. Our shelf: 200 W is about 682 Btu/h, 3.52 kWh a day.
  • A 1,500 W space heater is 5,118 Btu/h, about 7.5 times that shelf.
  • Prevent damping-off: sterile mix, no overwatering, no overseeding, space for air.
  • Keep trays off registers and radiators, and do not block them.
  • GFCI outlet near water; no overloaded power strip.
