Stack Effect and Building Pressure in Tall Buildings: Neutral Plane and Door Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tall building acts like a chimney. In winter, warm indoor air wants to rise and leave through the upper floors while cold outdoor air is drawn in low. That one idea explains lobby doors that will not close, whistling around elevator doors, cold ground floors and odors that seem to travel upward. This lesson covers the physics, a worked pressure calculation, and how controls and building design deal with it.

What stack effect is

Building Science Corporation (BSC) defines stack effect as the pressure difference between inside and outside that changes with height, driven by heating: heated air is less dense, so air tends to flow out of the top of the building and pulls air in at the bottom. BSC adds that taller buildings and colder weather increase it. A Trane engineers newsletter on building pressure states it from the pressure side: with warm indoor air, pressure is negative at the bottom and positive at the top, and with cold indoor air the pattern reverses. A trade article in Specifying Engineer describes the same thing in terms of load: in winter, cold air enters low and exits high, the lowest floors carry the biggest infiltration load, and in summer the process reverses, so the upper floors carry the biggest load.

The neutral pressure plane

BSC calls the height where inside and outside pressure are equal the neutral pressure plane (or zone). Openings above it leak air out, and openings below it let air in, in the no-wind, no-fan idealization. Where it sits depends on how much leakage area is above and below it. BSC says exhaust fans and chimneys raise it, and that sealing large holes at the top of a building lowers it. The exercise below assumes it sits at mid-height.

Wind and fans add to it

The Trane newsletter lists three causes of building pressure: stack effect, wind and mechanical ventilation. Wind pushes on the windward side and pulls on the leeward side. For fans, the net depends on intake versus relief and exhaust airflow: more intake than relief pressurizes the building, and more relief than intake depressurizes it. BSC makes the same point about fans: supply fans, exhaust fans and ducted systems all break the no-flow idealization. So a tech who finds a door problem should ask three questions, not one: how cold is it, how windy is it, and what are the fans doing right now. Our make-up air lesson and kitchen hood lesson cover big exhaust loads that can pull a building negative.

Symptoms you can recognize

  • Doors. Trane lists outward-swinging doors that are hard to open and inward-swinging doors that fail to close fully, plus noisy high-velocity air around doors and windows.
  • Elevators. A published case study of a 245 m, 60-story tower (Sustainability, 2017) reports elevator doors that malfunctioned under large pressure differences and a whistling noise in elevator halls. Its highest first-floor reading was 121 Pa across a high-rise elevator door, where the doors would not close.
  • Cold lobbies. Specifying Engineer names cold lobbies as a common stack effect problem.
  • Odors and drafts. Trane says drafts and stratification can spread odors. BSC notes that in tall buildings the air in lower units ends up in the upper units.
  • Hot top floors in summer. The reversed pattern puts the largest infiltration load on the upper floors, per Specifying Engineer.
  • Moisture. BSC and Trane both note moisture carried by leaking air can condense in walls and cause damage.

Worked example: stack pressure in a 20-story building

An ICC case study on smoke spread in high-rise buildings gives the stack pressure difference as ΔP = Ks (1/To − 1/Ti) h, with Ks = 3460, To the outdoor and Ti the indoor temperature, and h the height. The paper does not print units for Ks, but its own example (about 128 Pa top to bottom for −15 °C outside, 20 °C inside, 80 m) works only with temperatures in kelvin, height in meters and the answer in pascals. We checked: 3460 × (1/258.15 − 1/293.15) × 80 = 128 Pa. So we treat Ks as 3460 Pa·K per meter. Here h is the height from the neutral plane to the point you care about.

Our own arithmetic. Hypothetical building: 20 stories, 200 ft tall (60.96 m), neutral plane at mid-height, so h = 100 ft = 30.48 m to the top and to the bottom. Indoor 70 °F, outdoor 10 °F.

  1. Convert to kelvin: 70 °F = 21.11 °C = 294.26 K. 10 °F = −12.22 °C = 260.93 K.
  2. Reciprocals: 1/260.93 = 0.0038325 and 1/294.26 = 0.0033984. Difference = 0.000434 per K.
  3. Multiply by Ks: 3460 × 0.000434 = 1.502 Pa per meter of height.
  4. Multiply by h: 1.502 × 30.48 = 45.8 Pa.
  5. Convert (1 in. w.c. = 249.09 Pa): 45.8 / 249.09 = 0.184 in. w.c.

So at 10 °F the theoretical difference is about 46 Pa (0.18 in. w.c.) at the top, pushing out, and the same size at the bottom, pulling in. Across the full height that is 91.6 Pa (0.368 in. w.c.).

At 0 °F: 255.37 K, 1/255.37 = 0.0039160, difference = 0.0005175 per K, so 3460 × 0.0005175 = 1.791 Pa per meter, times 30.48 = 54.6 Pa = 0.219 in. w.c. at each end, 109.2 Pa (0.438 in. w.c.) top to bottom. These are ideal figures for a building with no interior floors or shaft resistance and no wind. BSC notes real flow modifies pressures, so measured values run lower. For scale, the case-study tower above measured 121 Pa at an elevator door, and the authors judged doors stopped malfunctioning near 100 Pa, while the paper says ASHRAE's recommended maximum is 25 Pa.

Measuring building pressure

Pressure here is always a difference between two points. Specifying Engineer describes a building pressure sensor with an indoor port and an outdoor reference; for the outdoor port it suggests a static probe about 15 ft above the roof to cancel wind effects. Trane says to avoid perimeter indoor locations affected by wind, to filter the signal, and to put outdoor sensors well above the roof, averaging several for large buildings. Specifying Engineer says to keep indoor ports out of pressurized spaces, ceiling plenums, entrances and diffuser jets. Because stack pressure changes with height, the reading depends on the floor: its example shows a 0.104 in. w.c. difference between the first and ninth floors. When you chase a door complaint, note the floor, the outdoor temperature, wind and which fans were running. Low-range sensors matter; Setra's application page lists typical ranges of 0.25 in. w.c. or less for room static and stairwell pressurization. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics for reading small pressures.

What target?

Sources do not agree on one number, and the answer changes with season. Trane gives about 0.00 to +0.05 in. w.c. or more in summer and about 0.00 to −0.02 in. w.c. or less in winter, and says the right setpoint depends on climate, shelter, sensor location and tightness. Specifying Engineer advises providing only enough pressurization to offset stack effect on the worst-case floor, and gives a typical differential setpoint range of 0.001 to 0.05 in. w.c. It also warns that overpressurization can drive exfiltration up to 50 percent of design ventilation airflow. Follow the design documents for the building.

How controls handle it

Specifying Engineer lists four common methods: manually balanced, fan speed tracking, differential-pressure control, and airflow offset control, with the last two most common in new buildings. In differential-pressure control the relief airflow varies to hold a pressure setpoint. Offset control keeps a fixed airflow difference between outdoor air intake and relief or exhaust, which it says generally keeps the building positive but needs more airflow measuring stations. Trane describes return fan or relief fan control driven by a differential pressure sensor, calls signal and flow tracking less effective, and says a passive gravity relief damper suits only small, single-story buildings because wind and stack effect disturb it. For tall buildings, Specifying Engineer suggests one indoor port low for winter control and one on the top floor for summer control.

Fixes at the building

Trane recommends vestibules and revolving doors, which tolerate larger pressure differences without affecting door operation, plus vertical compartmentalization, a good air barrier and low-leak relief dampers. Specifying Engineer calls compartmentalization the designer's best weapon and recommends sealed shafts, floor openings and stairwell doors. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) guide says to isolate elevator shafts from corridors with air-sealed vestibules and weather-stripped doors, and to seal the walls and ducts of vertical service shafts. In the case-study tower, the authors pressurized the upper zone with outdoor air rather than depressurizing the lower zone, which cut the field pressure difference at an elevator door from 135 Pa to 95 Pa. Sealing work on ducts is covered in duct leakage testing.

Field card: Stack Effect and Building Pressure

Warm air rises; the building leaks in low and out high in winter.

  • Winter: negative at the bottom, positive at the top. Summer reverses.
  • The neutral pressure plane is where inside equals outside.
  • Stack, wind and fan imbalance add together.
  • Door trouble, elevator whistling, cold lobbies and traveling odors point to pressure.
  • Formula in one source: ΔP = 3460 × (1/To − 1/Ti) × h, kelvin, meters, pascals.
  • Our example: 200 ft, 70 °F inside, 10 °F outside, about 46 Pa (0.18 in. w.c.) each end.
  • Fixes in sources: vestibules, revolving doors, sealed shafts, compartmentalization.
