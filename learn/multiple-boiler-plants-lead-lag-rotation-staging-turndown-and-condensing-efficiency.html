Multiple Boiler Plants: Lead-Lag, Staging and Turndown

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A large building rarely has one huge boiler. It usually has two to eight smaller ones that a controller turns on and off in order. This lesson explains why, how the controller decides who runs, and the one question that decides whether three boilers at low fire beat one boiler at higher fire.

Why several smaller boilers

PNNL's modular boiler O&M guide says several smaller units can follow a changing heating load through the year, while one large boiler often runs inefficiently at part load. It adds that units not in use can be serviced during the heating season, and that losing one unit does not shut the building down. The DOE steam tip sheet on cycling losses makes the same point from the other side: replacing one oversized plant with several smaller boilers lets operators follow load swings without short cycling. It also warns that efficiency drops "when multiple boilers are operated at low firing rates," because fixed losses become a bigger share of a small load. Keep both ideas in mind. Several boilers help, but only if the controller runs them sensibly.

Turndown, in plain terms

Turndown is the ratio of a burner's maximum firing rate to its minimum. Weil-McLain's white paper says makers offer burners from 2:1 up to 35:1 and notes a DOE-recommended minimum of 5:1. DOE's Building America condensing boiler guide gives the conversion: a 20 percent modulation rate equals 5:1. Maker literature we read shows the spread: Lochinvar's Knight XL brochure states 5:1, while AERCO and a Lochinvar Crest product page state 20:1 for their models (the AERCO brochure lists 15:1 on another). A higher ratio lets one boiler stay lit at a smaller load instead of cycling off. Weil-McLain also cautions that high turndown does not always raise efficiency.

See Sizing a Replacement Hot Water Boiler for turndown on a single boiler and Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling for what happens when the load falls below the minimum fire.

Staging strategies

PNNL describes two approaches. Cascade brings boilers on one at a time, modulating each to its maximum before starting the next, and PNNL says this suits non-condensing boilers. Unison control starts all boilers at low fire and modulates them together, which PNNL calls well suited to condensing boilers in variable-temperature systems. Lochinvar's brochure shows the same split in its own terms: a lead-lag cascade where additional boilers fire as demand rises, and an efficiency optimized cascade.

Why the difference? A condensing boiler recovers more heat when return water is cool, and AERCO's brochure says condensing operation improves efficiency when return water falls below 135°F and that efficiency is highest at the lowest firing rates. So spreading a load over more boilers at low fire can pay. A distributor-hosted AERCO-related article (Deppmann, Part 4 of 4) says part-load firing can raise efficiency 1 to 2 percent depending on temperature, and gives an example where three boilers sharing a load beat one boiler by about a point. DOE, however, tells operators to bring the most efficient boiler on first and take the least efficient off first, and to check each boiler's efficiency and cost. The right rule depends on the boiler type and the return temperature, so read the maker's sequence. We did not find a source on series versus parallel firing, so we do not cover it. See Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers and Outdoor Reset for what keeps returns cool.

Lead-lag rotation

One boiler is the lead and the rest lag. PNNL says the controller rotates lead status based on operating hours, which gives equal wear and less cycling, and it suggests checking that accumulated firing hours stay reasonably consistent across units. Lochinvar's SMART SYSTEM shifts the lead role to the next boiler every 24 hours in both of its cascade modes. The tekmar 275 control lists an "Equal run time rotation" feature and a pump sequencer with Rotate, Fixed and Last settings. A trade blog (RAS Mechanical) describes rotation as either time-based or runtime-based. If the lead never changes, it wears out first.

Minimum on and off times, and short cycling

Each firing cycle includes pre-purge, firing, post-purge and idle, and DOE notes that purge air carries heat out of the boiler. The Deppmann article says hourly efficiency can drop 10 to 15 percent from cycling many times an hour. Lochinvar lists an "Anti-Cycling" feature but its brochure gives no settings, so the minimum on and off times you set must come from the control manual for your boiler. Short cycling in a plant usually means the last running boiler is asked for less than its minimum fire.

Where the controller reads temperature

RAS Mechanical says demand is usually read from the common header rather than from individual boilers, so the reading reflects the whole system. The tekmar 275 data sheet lists a boiler supply sensor and an optional boiler return sensor, but does not describe how it uses them. We found no source comparing header and return control, so confirm which sensor your control uses and where it is wired.

Piping and isolation

Maker literature shows multi-boiler plants piped primary-secondary: Lochinvar lists a "Primary Secondary - 4 Boilers, 1 Indirect - Priority" drawing, and Deppmann's example uses primary-secondary piping with racked boilers. AERCO lists "no primary boiler pumps" and "no three-way valves" as design features of its boilers, so check the piping diagram for your model. See Primary-Secondary Piping Basics. PNNL says idle units can be taken offline for service, but their pumps, valves and piping still need maintenance, and piping gets more complex with many small boilers. We did not find a maker statement on isolation valves for idle boilers, so follow the manual and the engineer's drawing.

Worked example: one boiler or three?

Plant: three condensing boilers of 1,000 MBH each (3,000 MBH design load). For the arithmetic we assume 10:1 turndown. That is our assumption, inside the 5:1 to 20:1 spread above; use your boiler's published number. To keep it simple we treat the firing rate as a percent of 1,000 MBH and ignore combustion losses in the percentages. The efficiency values are illustrative only: we did not find a maker curve for this boiler, and we chose a one-point gap to match the Deppmann example.

  1. Minimum fire per boiler (our own): 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100 MBH.
  2. (a) One boiler (our own): 600 ÷ 1,000 = 0.60, so 60 percent firing. Above the 100 MBH minimum, so it can modulate.
  3. (b) Three boilers (our own): 600 ÷ 3 = 200 MBH each; 200 ÷ 1,000 = 0.20, so 20 percent firing. Above the 10 percent minimum (100 MBH), so all three can modulate together.
  4. Illustrative fuel comparison (our own): assume 94 percent for (a) and 95 percent for (b). Fuel in: 600 ÷ 0.94 = 638.3 MBH and 600 ÷ 0.95 = 631.6 MBH. Difference: 638.3 − 631.6 = 6.7 MBH, about 1 percent (6.7 ÷ 638.3 = 0.0105). Real results depend on return temperature and the boiler. If returns are warm and the boilers do not condense, the gain can vanish and three running boilers add pump and standby cost.
  5. Minimum load before the last boiler short-cycles (our own): one boiler at minimum fire is 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100 MBH. Below 100 MBH the last boiler must cycle off. As a share of design: 100 ÷ 3,000 = 0.033, about 3.3 percent. In a mild shoulder season, loads that low are common, so size one boiler or add a small one if this is the typical load.

What to trend

PNNL lists controller firing rates and accumulated hours per unit, with controller readings checked against gauges. It also lists fuel input metered for the whole plant and ideally for each unit, make-up water, and water temperatures. We add (our own) supply and return temperature at the header and each boiler, and the number of starts per day per boiler, because starts expose short cycling. PNNL also says to verify that the controller shuts off unneeded boilers and follows the intended sequence.

Field card: Multiple Boiler Plants

Run only what the load needs, and rotate who leads.

  • Turndown = max firing rate ÷ min firing rate; makers state it per model.
  • PNNL: cascade suits non-condensing; unison (all at low fire) suits condensing.
  • Rotation: PNNL says by operating hours; compare accumulated hours across boilers.
  • Last boiler cycles when load < its minimum fire (max ÷ turndown).
  • Trend: firing rate, hours, starts, supply/return temps, fuel, make-up water.
  • Always read the maker's sequence and the engineer's drawing.
