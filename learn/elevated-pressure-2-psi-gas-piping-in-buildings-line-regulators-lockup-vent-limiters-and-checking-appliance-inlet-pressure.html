Elevated-Pressure (2 psi) Gas Piping: Line Regulators, Lockup, Vent Limiters and Appliance Inlet Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most homes get gas at a few inches of water column. Some buildings carry it at 2 psi through small pipe and drop it near the appliance. This lesson covers why, what the line regulator does, and how to check what the appliance receives.

Check the code and the maker

Check the code and the maker. The fuel gas code text is paywalled, so we used a public code interpretation, a federal letter and a regulator maker's handbook. The adopted code, your inspector and the regulator's own instructions decide any real job.

Why run 2 psi inside a building

A pipe carries more gas at higher pressure, so elevated pressure lets installers use smaller tubing, often corrugated stainless steel tubing (CSST), on long runs. The tradeoff is a second pressure drop. A regulator maker's handbook says its line regulators sit between the building service regulator and the appliance, are listed for 2 psi inlet, and are typically placed at or near the appliance. For ordinary low-pressure sizing, see Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method.

Units: psi to inches of water column

Gas techs read low pressure in inches of water column (in. w.c.). NIST lists 1 psi as 6,894.757 pascals and 1 inch of water at 60 °F as 248.84 pascals. Dividing gives about 27.7 in. w.c. per psi (our own arithmetic). So 2 psi is about 55.4 in. w.c., and the roughly 7 in. w.c. a line regulator is often set near is only about 0.25 psi (7 ÷ 27.7, our own arithmetic). The regulator handbook gives a spring range of 7 to 11 in. w.c., with a factory setting of 8 in. w.c. on natural gas and 11 in. w.c. on propane.

How a line regulator works

A diaphragm senses outlet pressure on one side and a spring pushes on the other. When outlet pressure falls below the spring setting, the valve opens; as it rises, the diaphragm throttles the valve toward closed. Only a few inches of water column separate open from closed, so use a manometer, not a dial gauge.

Lockup and why outlet pressure creeps

When the appliance shuts off, flow stops and the valve must seal. The handbook says a regulator is built for this dead-end lockup, with outlet pressure settling slightly above the set point. It sets limits: for its Class I units, no more than 150 percent of the initial outlet pressure or the initial pressure plus 5 in. w.c., whichever is greater. It also says excessive lockup can keep automatic valves from opening, and that debris or excess thread sealant can prevent lockup.

If the seat is dirty or damaged, gas leaks past it and outlet pressure creeps toward inlet pressure. The handbook says its regulator has no internal relief, so a regulator that will not lock up has failed. It tests lockup by cycling an automatic valve five times and recording each reading. Treat a rising static reading as a reason to stop and call for repair.

Vent limiter or vent line

The diaphragm needs a vent, and that vent is where gas escapes if the diaphragm ruptures. A code interpretation (IFGC 2006, section 410.3, as quoted by the publisher) says regulators needing a vent must vent directly outdoors, with the opening protected against insects, water and foreign objects. The exception is a regulator labeled for use with an approved vent-limiting device installed per the maker's instructions.

The handbook says its vent limiter caps escape at 2.5 cubic feet per hour of natural gas (1.0 for propane), screws directly into the vent opening, and does not keep out water or insects. It wants the regulator mounted upright, since a limiter on its side can close and cause high lockup. Where a vent line is required, it cites NFPA 54 section 5.8.6.1: run it outdoors, end at least 3 feet from an ignition source, and never tie into a flue.

Location and access

The handbook cites NFPA 54 section 5.8.4 (accessible for service) and 5.8.5 (protected from physical damage). The interpretation says a 2 psi line regulator with a vent limiter is permitted in a mechanical room that serves as a plenum, when installed per the maker's instructions. A regulator buried behind a finished wall with no access does not fit that intent; ask the inspector.

Over-pressure protection

A failed regulator passes full inlet pressure downstream. The handbook says an over-pressure protection device (OPD) is required when the inlet exceeds 2 psi, limiting downstream pressure to 2 psi if the regulator fails (it cites NFPA 54 section 5.8.3), and that the OPD must arrive factory-assembled with the regulator.

Why 2 psi must never feed an appliance directly

A federal PHMSA interpretation letter on 49 CFR 192.197 describes a maximum residential inlet of about 1/2 psi (14 in. w.c.) and says pressures above 1 psi (28 in. w.c.) are likely to damage an appliance regulator. That is the letter's working assumption, not your appliance's rating. A 2 psi line at 55.4 in. w.c. is about 4 times 14 (our own arithmetic). So a 2 psi line must always drop through a line regulator first. See Gas Meter and Regulator Basics for the utility regulator upstream.

Who may work on gas piping

Rules differ by state and town. A New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs rule proposal said gas piping on the load side of a meter falls under HVACR practice and needs a master HVACR contractor license or another state license whose scope covers it. That page is a proposal, so confirm the current rule and local permit requirements before touching house piping.

Checking inlet pressure at the appliance

A manometer at the appliance inlet tap shows what the appliance actually gets. Read it twice. Static means the appliance is off and the regulator is locked up. Running means burners are firing. A line can look fine static and sag when running if the pipe or regulator is too small. Tap locations and technique are in Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer.

Worked example: static and running readings

Assumptions (ours, for practice): natural gas; a furnace rating plate that lists inlet pressure 4.5 to 10.0 in. w.c.; a line regulator set near 7 in. w.c.; a 2 psi supply line.

  1. Convert the supply line: 2 psi × 27.7 = 55.4 in. w.c.
  2. Static reading at the furnace inlet (assumed): 9.0 in. w.c. Inside 4.5 to 10.0, so static passes; being above the 7 in. w.c. setting is normal lockup.
  3. Running reading, burners firing (assumed): 6.5 in. w.c. Inside the range, so it passes. The drop is 9.0 − 6.5 = 2.5 in. w.c.
  4. Decision: pass on both; record the 2.5 in. w.c. drop.

Change the running reading to 4.0 in. w.c. and the system fails, below the 4.5 minimum: look for undersized pipe, a restricted regulator or a low setting. A static reading above 10.0 suggests a creeping regulator. Read the real rating plate.

Field card: 2 psi gas piping and line regulators

Two pressures, one regulator, never skip it.

  • 1 psi is about 27.7 in. w.c.; 2 psi is about 55.4.
  • A line regulator drops the pressure near the appliance.
  • A good regulator locks up; a rising static reading means it is not sealing.
  • Vent limiter or vent line outdoors, per the maker and inspector.
  • Read inlet static and running; compare both to the rating plate.
  • Gas piping work needs the right license.
