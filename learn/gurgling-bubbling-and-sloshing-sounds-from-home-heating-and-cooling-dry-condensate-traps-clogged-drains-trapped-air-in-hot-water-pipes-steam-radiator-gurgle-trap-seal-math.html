Gurgling and Sloshing Sounds from Home Heating and Cooling: What to Check

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gurgle, bubble or slosh from your heating or cooling equipment is usually water and air trading places somewhere they should not. Most causes are minor, and a few are not. This lesson helps you work out which sound comes from which part, what you can safely look at, and when to stop and call a professional.

Start by listening for when it happens

The single best clue is timing. Write down what is running when you hear the sound. Our own field rule of thumb, not a published figure: a sound that follows the blower points to a drain or duct, a sound that follows the burner or a pump points to the water side, and a sound after a recent refill points to trapped air.

  • Only while the blower runs, in cooling or in a condensing furnace: suspect the condensate trap or drain line.
  • Only while the circulator pump runs on a hot-water system: suspect air in the pipes or baseboard.
  • Only while a steam boiler is making steam: suspect water pooled in a radiator or pipe.
  • A small pump in a closet or basement humming then sloshing: suspect the condensate pump or its discharge line.

Cause 1: a dry or shallow condensate trap

A cooling coil and a high-efficiency furnace both make water. That water leaves through a drain, and most drains include a trap, a bend that holds a plug of water. The plug stops air from being pulled into or pushed out of the cabinet through the drain. On a draw-through air handler the blower sits after the coil, so it pulls a small suction on the coil pan. If the plug is too shallow, or has dried out after a long shutdown, the blower pulls air through the drain instead of letting water out. You hear gurgling while the blower runs, and water can back up into the pan. The related lesson on coil condensate traps and how deep they need to be covers the sizing in detail.

Worked example (our own): why 0.5 inch fails and 1.5 inch holds

These are our own assumptions for a teaching example, not a measured house. Assume a draw-through air handler pulls about 0.8 inch of water column (in. w.c.) of negative pressure at the coil drain. Assume the water seal depth is the plug of water the trap holds against that suction.

  1. Convert to psi so the size is clear. A federal measurement agency lists 1 inch of water as 249.0889 pascals and 1 psi as 6894.757 pascals.
  2. 1 in. w.c. = 249.0889 / 6894.757 = 0.03613 psi, about 0.036 psi.
  3. Suction: 0.8 in. w.c. x 0.03613 = 0.0289 psi, about 0.029 psi. That is tiny, yet it is enough to move a shallow plug of water.
  4. Trap A, 0.5 in. of seal: 0.5 is less than 0.8, so the suction exceeds the seal by 0.8 - 0.5 = 0.3 in. The water is pulled out of the way, air passes through the drain, and you hear gurgling. Trap A fails.
  5. Trap B, about 1.5 in. of seal: 1.5 is more than 0.8. The margin is 1.5 - 0.8 = 0.7 in. (seal equals suction plus a margin: 0.8 + 0.7 = 1.5). The water holds and drains normally. Trap B holds.

The 0.7 inch margin is our own choice to allow for a dirtier filter or a faster blower speed raising the suction. It is a teaching assumption, so follow the equipment maker's instructions for the real trap depth on your unit.

Cause 2: a partly clogged drain line or condensate pump

Algae and slime build up in drain lines. A line that is partly blocked passes some water and some air, which sounds like a sink draining slowly. A condensate pump adds a tank, a float and a small discharge pipe; if the float sticks or the discharge line is blocked, you can hear sloshing and the tank may overflow. See condensate pump not working for how those pumps behave. In a condensing furnace, a blocked internal trap can also make the furnace lock out, covered in the lesson on collector boxes and pressure switch trips.

A federal environmental agency's brief guide on mold and moisture says to keep air conditioning drip pans clean and the drain lines unobstructed and flowing properly. That is the maintenance goal behind everything above.

Cause 3: air trapped in hot-water baseboard or radiators

In a hot-water system, air is lighter than water and collects at high points. After a refill or repair you may hear a rushing-water sound, and the tops of radiators can feel cooler than the bottoms because air sits where hot water should be. Air also pushes against flow, so a baseboard loop may heat poorly. Our own rule: the noise tells you air is present, but not why. Our lesson on dissolved air in hot-water heating explains where it comes from.

Cause 4: steam radiator gurgle and banging

A steam system should drain condensed water back to the boiler. If a pipe or radiator holds a pool of water, steam rushing through pushes it around and you get gurgling or banging, often called water hammer. Common reasons are a radiator that no longer tilts toward its supply valve, a stuck air vent, or a pipe that sags. See steam radiator air vents. A steam system is hot and under pressure, so leave anything inside the boiler room to a professional.

What a homeowner can safely check

  • Turn the system off first at the thermostat for any hands-on check, and let hot parts cool.
  • Look at the drain line and trap without cutting or taking anything apart. Is the pan wet? Is water standing in the drain stub? Is the pump tank full?
  • Check the outlet where the drain ends. Is anything blocking it or has it frozen in winter?
  • Bleed one radiator air vent only if your system has a bleed valve, the system has cooled, and you have a rag and a small container. Open slowly until water spits, then close. Check the pressure gauge afterward if your system has one.
  • Replace a dirty filter. A clogged filter raises the suction on a draw-through unit, which makes a weak trap fail sooner (our own explanation).
  • Keep notes of when the sound happens. A technician can use them.

When to call a professional

  • Water on the floor, ceiling or around the unit.
  • A furnace that keeps shutting down or locks out. Do not bypass safety switches.
  • A pump that runs constantly or will not stop.
  • Air that needs bleeding again and again. That can mean a leak, a failed automatic air vent, or an expansion problem (our own caution).
  • Any smell of gas, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounding. A federal agency advises fuel-burning furnaces be inspected by a trained professional at the beginning of every heating season, and says that if a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, ventilate, turn off potential sources including the furnace, and have a qualified technician inspect. A consumer safety agency advises carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.

Field card: Gurgling and Sloshing Sounds

Time the sound first, then match it to the part.

  • Blower on, gurgle: dry or shallow condensate trap, or a blocked drain.
  • Pump on, rushing water: air in hot-water baseboard or radiators.
  • Steam on, banging: trapped water in a radiator or pipe.
  • Our own example: 1 in. w.c. is about 0.036 psi; 0.8 in. suction beats a 0.5 in. seal, not a 1.5 in. seal.
  • Water on the floor, lockouts, repeated air or a CO alarm: call a pro.
