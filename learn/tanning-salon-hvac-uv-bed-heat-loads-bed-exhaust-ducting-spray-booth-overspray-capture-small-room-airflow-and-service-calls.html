Tanning Salon HVAC: UV Bed Heat Loads, Bed Exhaust Ducting, Spray Booth Overspray Capture, Small Room Airflow and Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tanning salon is a row of small closed rooms, each with a lamp bed that behaves like a space heater with a fan. Add showers and a spray booth and you get a building that runs too hot, too humid and too lint-filled for a plain residential split. This lesson shows where the heat goes, how to size for it, what ducting part of it outdoors buys, and what the service calls usually turn out to be.

Check the rules that apply to your site

Check the rules that apply to your site. Mechanical, electrical, health and tanning-facility rules differ by state and town, and this page is general education. Confirm requirements with the local authority and the equipment instructions before you design or change anything.

Where the heat comes from

A UV bed or booth is an electronic product built around ultraviolet lamps, plus ballasts and cooling fans. Nearly every watt it draws ends up as heat in the building unless the air carrying it is ducted outside. The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatthour as 3,412 Btu, so each kilowatt of electrical input is 3,412 Btu per hour of load. The Oklahoma State University Extension fact sheet defines one ton of cooling as 12,000 Btu per hour. Nameplate input, not lamp wattage alone, is the number to use, because ballasts, fans and any facial lamps draw power too.

Beds do not run all hour. Sessions are short and rooms are cleaned between clients, so we use a duty cycle: the fraction of the hour the bed is actually powered. The salon below is the busy-hour case, and that is the case that sets the cooling size.

A short note on the federal rule

For context only: FDA's performance standard for sunlamp products is 21 CFR 1040.20, published on the eCFR. It covers sunlamp products and ultraviolet lamps made on or after September 8, 1986. Among other items it requires a timer, a control that lets the person being exposed stop the emission at any time, a warning label that includes protective eyewear language, and protective eyewear supplied with the product. It is a manufacturer standard, not an HVAC rule, and nothing in it tells us how to size cooling. On health, the CDC advises avoiding indoor tanning and says tanning beds and booths expose users to high levels of UV rays, which over time can cause skin cancer and eye cancer. We say nothing beyond that here.

Bed cooling fans and ducting the exhaust outside

Most beds move room air across the lamps to keep them in their working temperature range, then blow it back out warm. In a small closed room that warm stream is the whole problem: the room climbs, the client is uncomfortable, and the thermostat in the hallway never sees it. Where the bed maker allows it, a duct from the bed's exhaust opening to the outdoors takes a share of that heat out before it mixes with the room. Use only the connection and duct size the bed instructions describe, because restricting a bed's airflow can overheat the lamps. Hook up a duct to a bed that was not built for one only with the maker's approval.

Ducting has two costs. The air that leaves must be replaced, so outdoor air comes in somewhere, and that makeup air is a load of its own. The worked example below puts a number on both sides. Sealed duct, a backdraft damper or a screened wall cap, and a clear path to the outdoors matter more than the fan.

Small closed rooms: supply, return and transfer

A room with a door that closes and one supply diffuser but no return path will pressurize, starve the diffuser and still run hot. Each room needs its supply air to have somewhere to go. The choices are a ducted return, a transfer grille or a door undercut, sized so the airflow can pass without a loud whistle. We usually put the transfer grille high or low on the corridor wall, away from the bed's exhaust stream, with a central or corridor return picking the air up. Privacy matters in these rooms, so a sight-proof or lined transfer duct is better than a bare opening.

Humidity from showers

Salons with showers add steam to a building that is already tight. The EPA recommends keeping indoor relative humidity at or below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and suggests running a bathroom fan or opening a window when showering. In a salon that means a shower exhaust fan ducted outdoors, an undercut or grille so makeup air can find the shower room, and a thermostat set so the unit runs long enough to dry the air. Short cycling on a mild day wets the building instead of drying it.

Spray-tan booths and overspray capture

A spray-tan booth atomizes a liquid solution into a fine mist. Whatever the client does not absorb settles on walls, floor, fans and, if the mist reaches it, the return grille and filter. We did not find a government page that gives an air rate for spray-tan booths, so what follows is our own reasoning, not a sourced requirement: capture the mist near the booth, pull the air through a filter and out of the building, and give the booth a separate exhaust path so it does not share a return with the bed rooms. Check the booth maker's instructions for its exhaust connection and filter, and your local authority for any rule that applies. Keep the booth's exhaust away from the outdoor condenser and the fresh-air intake. Do not guess an air rate; use the maker's number.

Dedicated zones: ductless or rooftop

Because the bed load is steady and the hallway thermostat misses it, we prefer to treat the bed rooms as their own zone. A ductless mini-split head in each room (or one per pair of rooms) gives each room its own control, and a rooftop unit with a zoned or dedicated duct run suits a larger strip-mall salon. Either way, keep the spray booth, showers and bed rooms on separate exhaust, and do not serve them from one return that carries spray residue and lint to the coil.

Electrical loads

Beds are large, steady loads, often on dedicated circuits. Read the bed nameplate, count what runs at the same time, and let the electrical code and the equipment instructions decide circuit and breaker size. For scale only: an assumed 3.0 kW bed on a 240 V circuit draws 3,000 / 240 = 12.5 A (our arithmetic). Several beds on one panel plus the cooling equipment can exceed the service the building was wired for, so tally the connected load before adding mini-splits.

Worked example (our own)

Every input here is an assumption for illustration, not a typical figure from a source. Use your own nameplates and the bed maker's duct data.

  1. Beds (assumed): 8 beds, each 3.0 kW input (lamps, ballast and fans).
  2. Duty cycle (assumed): 60 percent of the busy hour powered. Average per bed = 3.0 x 0.60 = 1.8 kW. Eight beds = 14.4 kW.
  3. Heat: 14.4 x 3,412 (EIA) = 49,133 Btu per hour.
  4. Tons: 49,133 / 12,000 (OSU Extension) = about 4.1 tons for the beds alone. Per room, 1.8 kW is about 6,142 Btu per hour, roughly half a ton in a closet-sized space.
  5. Ducting outdoors (assumed 40 percent of bed heat captured): 49,133 x 0.40 = 19,653 Btu per hour leaves with the exhaust. Heat left in the rooms = 49,133 - 19,653 = 29,480 Btu per hour, about 2.5 tons.
  6. Makeup air (assumed 400 cfm total bed exhaust, outdoor air 15 F hotter): 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb per cubic foot x 60 minutes x 0.24 Btu per pound per F (standard air, our arithmetic). 1.08 x 400 x 15 = 6,480 Btu per hour, about 0.5 ton. Latent load of the makeup air is not included.
  7. Net cooling load from the beds: 29,480 + 6,480 = 35,960 Btu per hour, about 3.0 tons, down from 4.1 tons. The assumed ducting saves about 1.1 ton.

Not counted: clients, lights, showers, the spray booth, the building envelope and the outdoor air needed for ventilation. A real design adds them. If the capture fraction turns out to be 20 percent instead of 40, the saving halves, so measure before you trust it.

Common service calls

  • Rooms too hot. Check for a missing return or transfer path, a supply diffuser that is blocked or throwing the wrong way, a bed exhaust duct that has come off or crushed, and a hallway thermostat that does not see the rooms.
  • Clogged filters. The U.S. Department of Energy says clogged filters restrict airflow, and that bypassing air deposits dirt on the evaporator coil. It advises cleaning or replacing filters every one to two months in cooling season and checking more often when conditions are dusty. A salon with towels and bed fans is dusty, so we inspect monthly and set the interval by what we see.
  • Lint on grilles and coils. Towels and robes shed lint onto return grilles. Clear them, check the coil, and keep the laundry exhaust apart from the condenser.
  • Spray residue. A sticky film on the booth fan, duct and filter means the capture is not keeping up. Clean it, change the filter and check the exhaust path.
  • Musty humidity after showers. Confirm the shower fan runs and ducts outdoors.

Field card: Tanning salon HVAC

Every watt is heat, every closed room needs a way out for its air.

  • Bed kW x duty cycle x 3,412 = Btu per hour (EIA); 12,000 Btu per hour is one ton (OSU Extension).
  • Duct bed exhaust outside only as the bed maker allows, and count the makeup air.
  • Every closed room: supply plus a return or transfer path.
  • Keep indoor humidity at or below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 (EPA); vent showers outdoors.
  • Spray booth: its own exhaust and filter, per the booth maker.
  • Inspect filters at least monthly (DOE says every 1 to 2 months, more often when dusty).
  • Federal sunlamp standard 21 CFR 1040.20 is a product rule, context only.
