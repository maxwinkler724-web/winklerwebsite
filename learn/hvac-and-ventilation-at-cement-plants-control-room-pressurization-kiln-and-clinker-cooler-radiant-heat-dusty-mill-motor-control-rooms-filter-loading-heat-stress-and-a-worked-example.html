HVAC and Ventilation at Cement Plants: Control Room Pressurization, Kiln Heat and Filter Loading

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A cement plant turns crushed rock into powder, and a lot of that powder ends up in the air. The control room, the electrical rooms and the people near the kiln all depend on HVAC that can live with heat, fine dust and alkaline material. This lesson walks through each space and ends with our own worked example for pressurization airflow and filter loading.

How the plant makes dust and heat

A federal air-emissions reference describes the process in four steps: raw materials are acquired, the kiln feed is prepared, the material is heated in the kiln system, and the resulting clinker is finish-ground. Raw materials are ground and dried, often with hot kiln exit gases, and the finish step grinds clinker with a small amount of gypsum, almost entirely in ball or tube mills. That reference lists the dust sources as quarrying and crushing, raw material storage, dry grinding and blending, clinker production, finish grinding, and packaging and loading, and it says the largest particulate source in a plant is the kiln system, including the kiln and clinker cooler stacks.

The same source gives temperatures for the burning zone, about 1510°C (2750°F), and says clinker is cooled from about 1100°C to 93°C (2000°F to 200°F) using ambient air that is then reused as kiln combustion air. So there are two very different environments: hot, radiant areas around the kiln and cooler, and dusty areas around the mills, conveyors and bag filters. HVAC lives in both.

The central control room: cool, clean and pressurized

The control room holds operator consoles, servers and communication gear that must run around the clock. Three jobs fall to the HVAC system.

  • Cool. The load is people, electronics, lighting and any heat that leaks through walls and windows facing the kiln. Our plant control room lesson covers spare cooling capacity and siting, so we do not repeat it here.
  • Clean. Outdoor air passes through a coarse prefilter and a finer final filter before it reaches the room, and the return air is filtered as well.
  • Pressurized. A small positive pressure pushes air out through every crack so dust is not drawn in. The outdoor air fan supplies more air than leaks out, and a differential pressure gauge tells operators whether it is working.

Fresh air needs a source. Put the intake where the air is cleanest and away from bag filter discharges, truck traffic and the clinker cooler exhaust. Hot air from the cooler stack will raise intake temperature, and a high intake can overload the cooling coil.

Radiant heat at the kiln and clinker cooler

Hot surfaces warm people and rooms by radiation, which does not care what the air temperature is. A university heat transfer lecture gives the radiation constant as 5.67 x 10-8 W/m2-K4 and notes that the surface temperature must be absolute (kelvin or Rankine), so a surface twice as hot in absolute terms radiates sixteen times as much. A federal energy report on the cement industry reports kiln shell losses of 575,000 Btu per tonne of clinker for a dry kiln and 166,000 for a preheater kiln, and notes that a kiln surface at 300°F to 400°F accounts for about a 5 percent energy loss. That heat is large and it goes somewhere: into the building and onto people.

What HVAC can do about it:

  • Keep control room windows off the kiln side, or use shaded, reflective glazing and shields. The federal heat stress recommendations list reflective or heat-absorbing shielding as an engineering control.
  • Raise air velocity at work stations that cannot be moved, which is another engineering control on the same list.
  • Provide a cool, air-conditioned break room close to the work. The same recommendations tell employers to allow rest and water breaks and to give new workers lighter work and more rest.

Heat stress for workers near the kiln

The federal worker-health agency defines heat stress as the combination of metabolic heat, environmental heat, and clothing and protective equipment. It lists indoor radiant heat sources, limited air movement, dehydration and exertion as risk factors. Its recommendations call for acclimatizing over 7 to 14 days: new workers should start at no more than 20 percent of the usual time in the heat and add no more than 20 percent each day, while workers with prior experience can start at no more than 50 percent on day 1, 60 percent on day 2, 80 percent on day 3 and 100 percent on day 4. It also recommends a buddy system and water that is cool and close to the job. HVAC techs who service rooftop or kiln-area equipment on a hot day should follow the same plan. Our steel mill pulpit lesson and hot-mix asphalt plant lesson treat the same hazards in other industries.

Motor control and electrical rooms near the mills

Rooms with motor starters, drives and switchgear near the raw mill and finish mill see the worst fine dust. Mills run large motors, and the drives that feed them make heat that the room or enclosure must reject. Dust on electronics can bridge insulation and clog heat sinks; our drive cooling lesson explains the enclosure side. The room side follows the same pattern as the control room: filtered, pressurized outdoor air, recirculating cooling units inside, and a gauge. If the building shares a wall with a dusty process area, seal the cable and pipe penetrations, because leakage area is what sets the airflow you need. Our quarry and aggregate plant lesson has a worked heat load for a similar room.

Alkaline dust on coils, fans and filters

The federal emissions reference says cement kiln systems have highly alkaline internal environments. The finished powder is also an irritant: the federal chemical hazard pocket guide for portland cement lists irritation of the eyes, skin and nose, cough, wheezing and dermatitis, and advises preventing skin and eye contact, washing contaminated skin and removing wet or contaminated clothing. Treat dust on a coil or fan the same way.

  • Wear eye protection and gloves, and a respirator that your site has fit-tested you for.
  • Clean coils and fan blades with wet methods or a fine-dust vacuum, whichever your site procedure approves, since dry dust is what becomes airborne.
  • The federal construction silica standard states that employers shall not allow dry sweeping, dry brushing or compressed air for cleaning where it could add to silica exposure, with narrow exceptions. A federal worker-health page lists cement among the materials where crystalline silica is typically found, and says silicosis is not reversible but is preventable. A federal mine safety rule page gives the same 50 micrograms per cubic meter limit and 25 microgram action level, both as 8-hour averages; check that page for the current status of the mine rule at your site, since compliance dates have moved.

Practical rules: use a vacuum rated for fine dust or wet methods, not a blower; bag the loaded filters before moving them; and put the dust load on the schedule, not on the wish list.

Worked example (our own): pressurization airflow and filter loading

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for teaching, not a measured plant value or a requirement from any source.

Assumptions. A control room 40 ft x 25 ft x 10 ft (10,000 ft3). Total leakage area through doors, penetrations and walls: 0.5 ft2. Target pressure: 0.05 in. w.c. (about 12.5 Pa) above the plant. Orifice discharge coefficient 0.65 and air density 1.2 kg/m3. Outdoor dust at the intake: 0.5 mg/m3. Filter efficiency by weight: 95 percent. A filter bank that holds 300 g of dust before it reaches the changeout pressure drop.

Step 1, leakage airflow. 0.5 ft2 x 0.0929 = 0.0465 m2. Velocity through the leaks: v = sqrt(2 x 12.5 / 1.2) = sqrt(20.83) = 4.56 m/s. Flow Q = 0.65 x 0.0465 x 4.56 = 0.138 m3/s, or 0.138 x 2119 = 292 cfm.

Step 2, design flow. Add 25 percent for door openings and filter loading: 292 x 1.25 = 365 cfm. That is about 2.2 air changes per hour (365 x 60 / 10,000 = 2.19).

Step 3, dust arriving per week. 365 cfm = 0.1723 m3/s. A week is 604,800 s, so the room draws 0.1723 x 604,800 = 104,200 m3. At 0.5 mg/m3 that is 52,100 mg, or about 52 g of dust at the intake.

Step 4, what the filters hold. At 95 percent: 52.1 x 0.95 = 49.5 g captured per week. About 2.6 g gets through. With a 300 g capacity, the bank reaches the changeout point in 300 / 49.5 = 6.1 weeks.

What changes the answer. Loading scales directly with dust level. In an upset that raises outdoor dust to ten times our assumed value, the same bank would load in about 0.6 weeks. And as filters load, airflow falls unless the fan or drive compensates, so watch the room differential pressure gauge and the filter gauge, not only the calendar. The leakage-area input is the least certain: doubling it to 1.0 ft2 doubles the required flow.

A second check, radiant surface (our own). A kiln shell at 350°F (450 K) facing surroundings at 90°F (305 K) with an assumed emissivity of 0.8 emits 0.8 x 5.67 x 10-8 x (4504 - 3054) = 0.8 x 5.67 x 10-8 x 3.235 x 1010 = about 1,470 W/m2 at the surface, or roughly 465 Btu/h per ft2. What reaches a person is less, because of distance and view factor, but it shows why shields and shade matter.

Field card: HVAC at Cement Plants

Cool, clean, pressurized, and watch the gauge.

  • Intake goes where the air is cleanest, away from bag filter discharges and cooler exhaust.
  • Leakage area sets the airflow you need; seal penetrations before you add fan.
  • Dust loading scales with outdoor dust and airflow; read both gauges, not just the calendar.
  • Shield and shade radiant surfaces, and give crews cool rest areas and water.
  • No compressed air or dry sweeping on silica dust where it adds to exposure.
  • Cement dust irritates eyes and skin: protect them and wash up.
