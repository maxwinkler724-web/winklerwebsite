Atrium and Glass Lobby HVAC: Stratification, Solar Gain, Skylight Condensation and Smoke Exhaust Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tall glass space looks simple on the plans and acts strangely in service. Sun heats the top, cold glass drops air onto the floor in winter, skylights drip, and in a fire the whole volume becomes a smoke reservoir that fans must manage. This lesson covers atrium hvac design from a technician's side: what the air does, what the glass adds, and where the smoke exhaust system fits.

Condition the people, not the volume

Warm air rises, so a tall space sorts itself into layers. The aim in most atriums is to hold comfort only in the occupied zone near the floor and let the upper volume float. Supply air is usually thrown or displaced into the lower band, and the return takes from low or mid height. For the air-pattern side of that idea, see Underfloor Air Distribution and Displacement Ventilation. For pushing warm air back down in a big open room, see HVLS Fans and Destratification. Those lessons cover fans and layers in warehouses and gyms; here the extra problems are glass and smoke.

Solar gain through the glass

The U.S. Department of Energy's window glossary describes the solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) as the fraction of solar radiation admitted through a window, door or skylight, either transmitted directly or absorbed and then released as heat inside. A lower SHGC means less solar heat gets in. DOE's Energy Saver page on window technologies adds that low-e coatings come in high, moderate and low solar gain versions, and that for cooling-dominated sites you pick glazing that passes as little infrared as possible while keeping daylight. It says to look for an NFRC label to compare products.

How much sun is "incident" depends on orientation and season. A National Research Council of Canada building digest gives about 75 W/ft² as the annual peak on east and west walls in Ottawa, and 93 W/ft² on a horizontal surface at noon on the summer solstice. Roofs and skylights see more sun than walls, which is why a glass roof dominates the cooling load. The same digest notes that ordinary glass passes roughly 70 to 87 percent of the sun that hits it, depending on angle, and that its example of 100 sq ft of single glazing on a west wall gains about 6.5 kW but peaks as a cooling load at about 60 percent of that, because the building mass soaks up part of it.

Worked example: gain through our own glazing

These numbers are ours, so every input is an assumption. Use the formula Q = area × SHGC × incident solar.

  • Assumed glazing area: 1,200 sq ft (our own).
  • Assumed incident solar: 250 Btu/h per sq ft (our own round number, near the digest's 75 W/ft² east and west peak, which converts to about 256 Btu/h per sq ft at 3.412 Btu/h per watt).
  • Glass A: SHGC 0.40 (assumed). Glass B: SHGC 0.25 (assumed).

Glass A: 1,200 × 0.40 × 250 = 120,000 Btu/h. At 12,000 Btu/h per ton, that is 120,000 ÷ 12,000 = 10.0 tons.

Glass B: 1,200 × 0.25 × 250 = 75,000 Btu/h, or 75,000 ÷ 12,000 = 6.25 tons.

Difference: 120,000 − 75,000 = 45,000 Btu/h, or 3.75 tons. This is the instantaneous solar gain, not the final cooling load. Load calculations also count conduction, people, lights and the building's storage effect, and they use design-day sun values for the actual site. Treat this as a feel for scale, not a sizing tool. The point is that a glass choice moved the load by almost four tons without touching the air handler.

Winter: cold glass, downdraft and perimeter heat

Cold glass chills the air next to it, and that air falls. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory window comfort paper estimates a peak downdraft of about 0.25 m/s (0.8 ft/s) near a large window with 0°F outdoors, and shows how much colder the glass surface is: its example gives about 17°F on the inside of single clear glass, versus about 45°F for clear double glazing. The cold surface also pulls heat out of people by radiation. The paper notes that convectors placed near the facade reduce downdraft. That is why perimeter heat sits at the glass base in a lobby: it breaks the falling layer before it reaches the floor and feet. If a lobby feels drafty in January, check that perimeter heat is actually running and not blocked by furniture or planters before blaming the air handler.

Skylight condensation and dew point

Condensation forms when a surface drops below the dew point of the air touching it. A glass maker's technical document puts the dew point at 44.6°F for 70°F air at 40 percent relative humidity, and shows simulated inside glass temperatures for a sealed double unit with plain clear glass of 44.1°F at 0°F outdoors and 37.0°F at −20°F. Those temperatures sit at or below the dew point, so even a modest indoor humidity can wet that glass. Warm, humid air also collects at the top of a tall room, right where the skylight and its frame are coldest. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for finding dew point from a chart.

The fixes are the usual ones: lower the humidity at the source (check humidifiers, pool or fountain areas and outdoor air control), keep air moving across the glass, and confirm the frames and seals are not leaking humid air into the cold edge. A leaking building is often as much of a cause as the glass temperature.

Smoke control in an atrium

An atrium is a vertical opening, and fire codes treat it as a way for smoke to travel between floors. The International Building Code (IBC) section 404.5 requires a smoke control system built under section 909 in an atrium, with exceptions. According to the ICC's public summaries, one exception is an atrium that connects only two stories (not in Group I-2 or Group I-1 Condition 2), and the 2021 edition added another for atriums where only the two lowest stories open to the space and higher stories are separated by rated shaft enclosures. Section 404.7 requires standby power for smoke control equipment. Your jurisdiction adopts its own edition, so ask the local fire code official which one applies. The design standard behind these systems is NFPA 92, which we name here but do not reproduce.

In a typical design, exhaust fans at the top pull smoke out, and makeup air openings low in the space supply replacement air. A trade article in HPAC magazine reports these design rules of thumb: makeup air near 85 to 95 percent of exhaust flow so the space is not pressurized, makeup air velocity kept under about 200 fpm where it could touch the smoke plume unless analysis supports more, and a smoke layer depth of at least about 20 percent of the floor-to-ceiling height unless analysis shows otherwise. The same article warns that sun on a glass roof can build a hot layer above 120°F, and that smoke from a small fire can be cooler than that layer and stall beneath it. That is why the comfort side and the smoke side are linked.

Periodic testing and the tech's role

Smoke control is not install-and-forget. A fire marshal bulletin from a county fire authority lists weekly automatic self-tests under IBC 909.12.1, semiannual operation of each control sequence in dedicated systems and annual operation in non-dedicated systems, with testing under standby power at each cycle. It allows a cumulative approach under an approved test plan (for example, at least 25 percent of smoke control each year and all of it every four years). A guideline from another region's fire code committee gives similar intervals and says testing is coordinated with the fire alarm contractor and verified at the panel. Both say a special inspector (qualified engineer or agency approved by the fire code official) writes the plan and runs the tests, and that results and records are kept on site, with life safety impairments reported to the fire authority right away.

The bulletin also says a fire alarm system with troubles, faults or supervisory alarms is not a clean baseline, and that retesting may be needed when fire alarm panels are replaced, programming changes substantially, or control moves between the building automation system and the fire alarm system. That matters to us: a quiet change to a control point or a damper can affect a tested system.

Where does a tech fit? Our own advice, not a code rule: we do not sign off on smoke control. We keep the parts healthy. That means fans, belts, motors, dampers and actuators that move freely, makeup air louvers that open, and no overrides or hand-switch positions left behind. If a smoke control fan or damper is down, tell the owner and the fire alarm contractor in writing, because the bulletin treats an impairment as reportable. For the related dampers, see Fire Dampers and Smoke Dampers, and for pressure-based systems in stairs see Stairwell Pressurization and Smoke Control Systems.

Field card: Atrium and Glass Lobby HVAC

Sun on top, cold at the glass, smoke fans overhead.

  • Condition the occupied zone; the upper volume stratifies.
  • Solar gain = area × SHGC × incident sun. Lower SHGC cuts the gain in proportion.
  • Our example: 1,200 sq ft at SHGC 0.40 vs 0.25 and 250 Btu/h per sq ft gives 10.0 vs 6.25 tons (assumed inputs).
  • Cold glass causes downdraft; perimeter heat at the glass base counters it.
  • Condensation appears when glass is below the dew point of the room air.
  • IBC 404.5 sends atrium smoke control to section 909; NFPA 92 is the design standard.
  • Expect weekly self-tests plus semiannual or annual operation tests; report impairments.
