Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step: A How-To

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the EPA 608 Guide

Recovery means pulling refrigerant out of a system into an approved cylinder instead of letting it escape. The steps are simple; the safety habits around cylinders and pressure are what matter.

Why recovery is not optional

EPA's page on the Clean Air Act says it is against the law to intentionally vent or otherwise knowingly release refrigerant, and that covers ozone-depleting refrigerants and many substitutes such as HFCs. It also says anyone removing refrigerant from an air-conditioning or refrigeration appliance must evacuate it to a set level using certified recovery equipment before servicing or disposing of the appliance. The exact levels depend on the equipment and refrigerant, so use the current EPA tables and your certification training. For the vocabulary (recover, recycle, reclaim) see Refrigerant Recovery, Recycling and Reclaiming.

Before you connect anything

  • Know what is in the system. Check the nameplate and confirm the machine and cylinder are made for that refrigerant. Never mix refrigerants in one cylinder.
  • Inspect the gear. One distributor guide says to check the machine, hoses and gauges for wear or damage first. Look at the cylinder label and its test date too.
  • Protect yourself. Gloves and splash-proof goggles guard against frostbite and sudden high-pressure releases. See Refrigerant Safety.
  • Look at the machine itself. HVAC School suggests cleaning the inlet screens, using a flare line drier on the inlet, and not storing refrigerant in the machine. Follow your machine maker's manual for oil and purge steps.

Hoses and connections

HVAC School and a distributor article both say short, larger-diameter hoses reduce restriction and speed recovery. HVAC School adds that removing Schrader cores (where your procedures allow it) can speed recovery and keep the tank cooler, and that hoses should be purged before you start. Tight, clean connections matter, because a loose fitting is a leak. Close valves before you disconnect anything.

The basic recovery run

  1. Connect gauges, machine and cylinder as the machine manual shows, then open the valves in the order it gives.
  2. Start the machine and watch system pressure, cylinder pressure and cylinder temperature.
  3. If the cylinder warms up, cool it. Both sources above suggest a water bath or running water, or a fan.
  4. Continue until the system reaches the required recovery level or the machine signals completion, then close valves before disconnecting.
  5. Label the cylinder with refrigerant type, date and system details, and store it upright and secured.

Never overfill the cylinder

Several sources agree on one limit: do not fill a recovery cylinder past 80 percent. This is industry guidance, not a quoted federal percentage. The federal text we read (49 CFR 173.304) says the liquid in a liquefied-gas cylinder may not completely fill it at 131 °F, and 80 percent is a common way to stay well under that. HVAC School says to weigh the refrigerant and base the limit on refrigerant capacity, not water capacity. A distributor guide warns that an overfilled cylinder can rupture as temperature rises. Use an accurate scale, and check the cylinder's own markings and test date.

Liquid, vapor and push-pull, as awareness

One distributor guide describes liquid recovery as much faster but able to carry oil out with the refrigerant, and vapor recovery as slower but gentler on oil and suited to small systems. It describes technicians often starting on liquid and finishing on vapor. It also describes push-pull, which uses the machine to push vapor and drive liquid into the cylinder on larger systems, with a multi-hose setup and a sight glass. Treat push-pull as something to learn hands-on from a trained mentor and the equipment instructions, not from an article.

If something looks wrong

Stop if pressures climb unexpectedly, the cylinder feels very hot, the machine trips or you smell refrigerant. Close the valves, let things settle and sort out the cause before you continue. Never heat a cylinder with a flame to speed things up, and never use a cylinder that is out of test date or damaged. If the system might hold a flammable refrigerant, follow the extra ventilation and equipment rules in A2L Service Safety and Tools and the machine maker's instructions.

Paperwork

Label every cylinder and keep your own records of what you recovered and where it went. One guide says to keep accurate recovery records for compliance audits. EPA's page notes that some recordkeeping rules differ by refrigerant and appliance size, so check the current EPA rules. Related habits are in 608 Records: What Technicians Keep.

Field card: Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step: A How-To

Recovery means pulling refrigerant out of a system into an approved cylinder instead of letting it escape.

  • Never vent refrigerant. Recover it with certified equipment into the right cylinder.
  • Check the label and refrigerant type first; never mix refrigerants in one cylinder.
  • Short, large-diameter hoses and a clean inlet screen speed recovery; purge hoses first.
  • Cap the cylinder at 80 percent by weight of its refrigerant capacity (industry guidance; never exceed the cylinder's marked limits); use a scale.
  • Watch cylinder pressure and temperature; cool a hot cylinder, never overfill.
  • Liquid is fast but moves oil; vapor is slower and gentler; push-pull is for trained, supervised use.
