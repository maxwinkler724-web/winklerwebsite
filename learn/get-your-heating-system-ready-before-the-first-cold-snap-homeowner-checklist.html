Get Your Heating System Ready Before the First Cold Snap: Homeowner Checklist

A little time in October saves a no-heat call in January. This checklist covers what a homeowner can safely check and what to hand to a licensed tech.

Safety first. Never use a gas oven to heat a home. Never bypass or disable a safety control. If a CO alarm sounds and anyone feels sick, leave the building and get medical help. Anything inside a furnace or boiler cabinet, burner, flue or gas line belongs to a licensed tech.

Why do this in the fall

Most heating failures show up on the coldest day, when every company is busy. A short walk-through of what you can safely check, plus a booked professional visit, puts you ahead. The U.S. Department of Energy says to have a heating specialist service equipment before each heating season, and a state extension publication adds that this can help avoid winter breakdowns.

What you can do yourself

1. Filter (forced-air systems)

DOE's Energy Saver guide says to clean or replace furnace filters once a month, or as recommended, while a University of Missouri Extension publication says to check them every two months during the heating season. The two differ, so follow your filter and furnace instructions, and check at least once before winter starts. Details are in How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV.

2. Thermostat

Replace dead batteries if your model uses them, and confirm it calls for heat. A utility checklist notes that an improperly calibrated thermostat can make a furnace over- or under-heat a home, which is one item a tech checks during a tune-up. DOE gives a labeled example: setting a thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees F for 8 hours a day can save up to 10 percent a year on heating and cooling. If you have a heat pump, DOE cautions against manual setbacks that trigger costly backup electric heat. See Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

3. Vents, registers and radiators

DOE advises keeping registers, baseboards and radiators clear of furniture, carpeting and drapes, and vacuuming radiators, convectors and baseboard units because dust wastes heat. Keep the area around the furnace free of dust, lint and clutter, as the Missouri publication advises.

4. Air in hot-water radiators

DOE and Missouri Extension both say trapped air in hot-water radiators can be bled, with Missouri describing opening the valve over a cup and removing only air, not draining water. DOE suggests calling a pro if unsure. Steam radiators are different: one steam-heating guide says not to bleed them because they purge air automatically, and not to adjust or paint vents. If you do not know which you have, read What Heats Older New Jersey Homes first.

5. Carbon monoxide alarms

The Consumer Product Safety Commission says to put CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and to check batteries regularly. The EPA tells readers to follow the manufacturer's instructions for placement, use and maintenance, to choose a UL-certified unit, and says alarms are a backup, not a replacement for maintaining fuel-burning appliances. EPA also advises a professional inspection of fuel-burning appliances before each heating season, and checking that flues and chimneys are connected, in good condition and unobstructed. More in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What To Do.

What to leave to a professional

A utility tune-up checklist from the City of Palo Alto lists items such as inspecting the heat exchanger for cracks, checking high-limit and other safety controls, checking for flue obstructions, checking burners, and checking the gas line for leaks. It connects heat exchanger cracks with carbon monoxide leakage. Missouri Extension lists odors, soot at the burner, black smoke from the chimney, or surging water in a boiler as warning signs needing professional help.

Do a test run early

On a mild day, turn the thermostat up and confirm the system starts and the rooms warm. If nothing happens, run through No-Heat First Checks, then call. Booking early beats waiting for a cold snap.

Field card: Get Your Heating System Ready Before the First Cold Snap: Homeowner Checklist

Do the easy checks now; book the pro early.

  • Book the professional tune-up before the season; do not wait for the first freeze.
  • Filter: check before winter; follow the furnace and filter instructions for how often.
  • Thermostat (general practice, not from a cited source): fresh batteries if needed, confirm it calls for heat.
  • Clear registers, baseboards and radiators; vacuum dust.
  • Bleed air only on hot-water radiators; never on steam.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; follow the maker's instructions.
  • Smell, soot, black smoke or surging boiler water: stop and call a pro.
