Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer: How the Pressure-Reducing Valve Works

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A closed hot-water boiler is filled from the building's drinking-water line, which runs at a higher pressure than the boiler wants. The boiler pressure reducing valve, usually called the fill valve, steps that pressure down and tops up the system on its own. A backflow preventer ahead of it keeps boiler water from ever travelling back toward the tap.

What the fill valve does

The fill valve is a spring-loaded pressure regulator. Taco's installation sheet for its 329 feed valve describes the system-side outlet as adjustable and set at the factory. If the pressure on the boiler side falls below the setting, the valve opens and lets in water. When the boiler side reaches the setting, it closes. That is why a small leak in a heating loop can go unnoticed for a long time: the valve quietly replaces what is lost. Watts describes its regulator as providing make-up water to the boiler, and Caleffi states its valve is for closed hydronic systems only, not for plumbing uses. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure for how the tank shares the work.

Factory settings differ by maker

Do not assume every valve ships at the same number. These are the figures the makers print:

  • Taco 329: factory set to 12 psi, adjustable from 10 to 25 psi (Taco install sheet and Taco catalog).
  • Watts 1156F: set at about 15 psi at the factory (Watts installation manual). Watts' product page lists an adjustable range of 10 to 25 psi.
  • Caleffi automatic fill valve: pre-set at 15 psi, adjustable from 6 to 90 psi, about 7 psi per turn of the knob (Caleffi manual).
  • Resideo (Honeywell) FM911 / VF06 fill valve: factory setting 15 psi, adjustment range 8 to 50 psi (Resideo product sheet).

The spread, 12 to 15 psi, is the reason to read the label on the valve in front of you and the boiler manual, and not to rely on a number remembered from another job.

The fast-fill lever

The fill valves we read have a lever or push button that holds the valve open so a new system fills quickly and air can be purged. Taco's sheet says the lever has an automatic position and an override position, and that it should not be moved while the system is operating. Watts says to always watch the boiler pressure gauge while using it. Both makers warn about the same mistake: fast fill with the purge valve closed can over-pressurize the system and make the relief valve discharge very hot water. Taco's sheet says that can cause serious injury. Treat the lever as a tool for the pro doing the filling, not a quick fix for a low gauge. The Caleffi valve fills quickly to its set pressure and then shuts the feed off by itself, according to its manual, so check the maker's description for your model.

Setting cold fill pressure by building height

The fill pressure has one job at the top of the system: it must be high enough to push water up to the highest radiator or baseboard and leave some pressure there, so vents work and air does not get pulled in. Water gains about 0.433 psi for each foot of height. Two published rules turn that into a setting:

  • Taco's install sheet and the Watts 1156F manual both say: measure the height in feet from the valve to the top of the highest radiator, multiply by 0.43, and add 3 psi.
  • An article in Plumbing & HVAC trade press gives the same idea with a bigger margin: height times 0.433, plus 5 psi as a safety factor. It notes that a typical two-story home needs only about 7 psi at the bottom to reach the top, so the extra margin is what makes 12 psi the usual default.

Worked example: a 3-story building

Example inputs, chosen for the arithmetic (not from a source): the fill valve sits in the basement and the top of the highest radiator is 30 feet above it.

  1. Taco and Watts rule: 30 ft × 0.43 = 12.9 psi. Add 3 psi: 12.9 + 3 = 15.9 psi, so about 16 psi cold.
  2. Trade-article rule: 30 ft × 0.433 = 12.99 psi. Add 5 psi: 12.99 + 5 = 17.99 psi, so about 18 psi cold.
  3. Check against a 12 psi factory setting: 12 psi is less than the 12.99 psi needed just to hold water up 30 feet, so the top floor would be at zero or negative pressure.

Both results sit inside the 10 to 25 psi range Taco and Watts list for their valves. The two rules disagree by about 2 psi, which is why the boiler manual's number is the one to use. Remember the expansion tank precharge should be set to match the fill pressure, per the tank maker; see the linked lesson above. Then confirm the boiler's relief valve rating on its data plate. Taco and Watts describe built-in relief valves that open at 30 psi, but do not assume that is the rating on your boiler.

Why the backflow preventer is there

The fill valve connects a closed heating loop, which holds treated or stale water, to the drinking-water supply. Watts describes its Series 9D as a dual check valve with an intermediate atmospheric vent that prevents reverse flow of hot or cold polluted water into the potable supply, and lists boiler feed lines as a use. The Watts 1156F manual says local codes typically require a backflow preventer, such as the 9D, installed upstream of the regulator, and tells the installer to check with the local authority for type and testing rules. Resideo's FM911 pairs its fill valve with a double check backflow preventer with an intermediate vacuum breaker, and its sheet says a shutoff valve and backflow preventer belong on the city-water side. Caleffi's manual says its fill valve has no internal check valve and recommends an upstream backflow preventer that meets applicable codes.

The built-in check in a Taco feed valve is a different thing. Taco's catalog says it keeps the system from emptying if the incoming pressure fails. Taco's materials we read do not describe it as a code backflow device, so do not count it as one. The Watts combination units (such as the 911 series) put the 9D and the 1156F in one assembly so both are present.

Signs the fill valve is failing

Watts' manual says a dripping relief valve can mean the regulator needs service, or that the expansion tank is waterlogged or undersized. Because the relief valve is the symptom and not the cause, a pro has to sort out which part is at fault. Things an owner can notice:

  • Gauge pressure that is higher than the manual's number when the system is cold.
  • Pressure that climbs on its own over days, not just when the burner runs.
  • The relief valve discharge pipe is wet or dripping.
  • The opposite: pressure that drops and a feed valve that keeps adding water, which can point to a leak. See Boiler Pressure Too High or Too Low: A Homeowner Guide and Boiler Safety Valves and Overpressure Protection.

Watts also says the supply pipe should be flushed before the regulator is installed to clear chips, scale and dirt, and that the strainer screen should be serviced at least twice a year. Caleffi's manual likewise has a cleanable stainless filter in the valve cartridge.

Owner tasks and pro tasks

Owner: read the gauge cold and hot and compare it with the manual; look for drips and stains; look at the relief discharge pipe from a distance; write down or photograph what you see. Professional: setting or replacing the fill valve, using the fast-fill lever, cleaning the strainer, replacing the backflow preventer, testing it where required, and anything on the relief valve. Taco says its valve should only be installed by qualified heating professionals, Caleffi says installation and servicing must be done by qualified personnel, and Watts calls for annual inspection of the safety and control valves in the system and a qualified plumbing and heating technician.

Field card: Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer

Backflow preventer, then fill valve, then boiler.

  • Factory settings: Taco 12 psi; Watts, Caleffi and Resideo 15 psi. Read your label.
  • Height rule from Taco and Watts: feet × 0.43, plus 3 psi.
  • 30 ft example: 30 × 0.43 = 12.9, plus 3 = 15.9, about 16 psi.
  • Fast fill with the purge valve closed can over-pressurize and discharge hot water.
  • A dripping relief valve can mean a fill valve problem or a bad expansion tank.
  • Setting, servicing and relief valve work belong to a pro.
