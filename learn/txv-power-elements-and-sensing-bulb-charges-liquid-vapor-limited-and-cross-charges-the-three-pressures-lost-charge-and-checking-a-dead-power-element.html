TXV Power Elements and Sensing Bulb Charges

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The power head on a thermostatic expansion valve is a small pressure machine. A sealed bulb, a thin capillary tube and a diaphragm turn a pipe temperature into a force. What is sealed inside the bulb decides how steady the valve holds superheat. This lesson covers the three pressures, the common charge types, and what happens when the charge is lost.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. This page is general education. Charge type, spring setting and replacement parts come from the valve maker's literature for the exact valve and refrigerant. We do not repeat valve basics, bulb placement or bi-flow valves here. For those, see TXV and piston metering devices, TXV sensing bulb placement, equalizers and adjusting superheat and heat pump metering in both directions.

What the power head does

The power head has three working parts. The bulb clamps to the suction line near the evaporator outlet. The capillary tube connects the bulb to the space above the diaphragm. The diaphragm is a thin flexible disc that moves a pin to open or close the valve seat. A doctoral thesis from a European technical university, which studied valve charges in detail, describes the bulb as a temperature to pressure converter. Because the capillary joins bulb and capsule, the pressure above the diaphragm follows the pressure in the bulb.

The three pressures

A set of lecture notes from a university mechanical engineering program shows the diaphragm responding to the difference between bulb pressure and evaporator pressure, balanced against a spring. The doctoral thesis gives the same balance: bulb pressure times diaphragm area equals evaporator pressure times area plus spring force, with a flow-force term in some designs. In plain terms:

  1. Bulb pressure pushes the diaphragm toward opening the valve.
  2. Evaporator pressure pushes back toward closing. On a valve with an external equalizer, this is the pressure taken near the evaporator outlet.
  3. Spring force also pushes toward closing. The spring is what sets the superheat the valve needs before it opens.

Bulb pressure above evaporator pressure by more than the spring equivalent opens the valve. When the evaporator outlet warms, bulb pressure rises and the valve feeds more refrigerant. The added refrigerant cools the outlet and the bulb, and the valve starts to close again. That feedback loop is how the valve holds superheat roughly constant. A federal energy office guideline on air conditioner diagnostics says the purpose of a TXV is to hold superheat roughly constant, so measured superheat shows whether the valve is working.

Bulb charge types

The doctoral thesis sorts charges by what is sealed in the bulb and how much.

  • Liquid (universal) charge. The bulb always holds some liquid, so its pressure equals the saturation pressure at the bulb temperature. The thesis notes that a parallel charge uses the same refrigerant as the system.
  • Limited charge with a maximum operating pressure. The charge is small enough that all the liquid boils away at a set temperature. Past that point bulb pressure barely climbs, so the valve stops opening further. That caps suction pressure, which protects a compressor at start-up after an off cycle. The thesis also notes that these charges are affected by charge migration and respond quickly. People also call these vapor or gas charges. Use the maker's wording for the exact valve.
  • Cross charge. The bulb holds a different substance than the system, sometimes with a trace of nitrogen to shift the pressure level. The thesis says a parallel charge lets superheat rise as temperature falls, so a flatter curve is preferred. A cross charge is chosen to give that flatter response, so superheat stays steadier as evaporating temperature changes. The nitrogen raises the pressure level without greatly changing the slope.

The thesis also describes a thermal ballast, a porous brick in the bulb that slows the response, especially to rising temperature. That is one reason bulb contact and insulation matter, covered in the placement lesson.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Our own example. Every value below is an assumption for teaching, not a catalog or chart value.

  1. Assume bulb pressure 75 psig, evaporator pressure 68 psig and spring force equal to 7 psi on the diaphragm.
  2. Net opening pressure is bulb minus evaporator: 75 - 68 = 7 psi. That equals the 7 psi spring, so the valve sits steady and the flow matches the load.
  3. Now the outlet warms and bulb pressure rises 2 psi to 77 psig. Evaporator pressure stays 68 psig.
  4. Net opening pressure is 77 - 68 = 9 psi, which is 2 psi more than the spring. The diaphragm moves toward open.
  5. Using the lecture-notes relation, valve movement equals pressure difference times diaphragm area divided by the spring rate. Assume 0.5 square inch of area and a 40 lbf per inch spring rate. The extra 2 psi gives 2 x 0.5 = 1 lbf of extra force, and 1 / 40 = 0.025 inch of added opening.
  6. To turn pressure into temperature, you would use a pressure-temperature relationship for the refrigerant. Assume a simple 1 psi per degree F slope near these pressures. Then the 2 psi rise means the bulb is about 2 degrees F warmer than before. Real refrigerants differ, so read your own chart.
  7. More flow cools the outlet, bulb pressure falls back and the valve settles near its balance point again.

When the bulb loses its charge

This part is our own reasoning from the force balance. If the sealed system leaks, bulb pressure falls toward nothing. Evaporator pressure plus the spring now win, so the valve closes. The evaporator is starved, suction pressure drops, and superheat climbs. A federal national laboratory technical note on installation faults describes a TXV hitting its limits at low load conditions, and the federal energy guideline says a fouled or throttling valve can show normal superheat while capacity still drops, so a dead element should be confirmed rather than assumed.

Checking for a dead power element

  • Look for the pattern above: low suction pressure, high superheat and a liquid line that is full and warm to the touch. This is our own reasoning, and the same pattern also fits a clogged valve or an undercharge.
  • Confirm the bulb is clamped tight on a clean line and the equalizer line is open, so the pattern is not a placement fault.
  • Gently warm the bulb with your hand while watching suction pressure. A live element lets the pressure rise as the valve opens. A dead one shows no response. Follow the maker's test procedure and do not overheat the bulb.
  • If the element is dead, replace the valve or power head as the maker allows, then recheck superheat. Recovery and charging rules apply as for any open system.

Field card: TXV Power Elements and Sensing Bulb Charges

Bulb pushes open; evaporator and spring push closed.

  • Bulb pressure opens the valve; evaporator pressure and spring close it.
  • Liquid charge: pressure follows saturation at the bulb temperature.
  • Limited charge: caps bulb pressure, so the valve stops opening past a set point.
  • Cross charge: different substance, flatter response across temperatures.
  • Dead element: valve closes, evaporator starved, suction low, superheat high.
  • Rule out placement, clogging and undercharge first.
