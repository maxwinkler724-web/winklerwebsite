HVAC Thermistors: Resistance Tables and How to Test

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermistor is a tiny part that tells a control board how hot or cold something is. When one goes bad, the board may shut a system down, run defrost wrong or show an error code. Testing one takes a meter, a thermometer and the maker's chart. This lesson shows how to do that, and why you should never guess.

What an NTC thermistor does

Almost every sensor in this lesson is an NTC thermistor. NTC means negative temperature coefficient: resistance goes down as temperature goes up. Vishay's application note describes these parts as sintered metal oxide, with a steep resistance-versus-temperature curve, which is why a small temperature change makes a big resistance change. The Fujitsu General service support page on thermistor testing makes the same practical point: if a reading moves the wrong way as the sensor warms, suspect the wrong chart or a bad sensor.

You find them in many places: the outdoor air, outdoor coil (defrost), indoor coil, room and discharge sensors on heat pumps and mini-splits, and the outdoor and supply or return water sensors on furnaces and boilers. For what the defrost board does with the coil and air readings, see Heat Pump Defrost Control Boards and Sensors. For what an outdoor sensor does on a boiler, see Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation.

The curve is not a straight line

Vishay's note approximates the curve with a Beta value, written B. The idea: pick two temperatures, compare the two resistances, and B is a single number that describes how steeply that part's resistance falls. A higher B means a steeper curve. Vishay also says Beta changes a little with temperature, so it is only a first approximation, and it offers a longer polynomial for better accuracy. A related fit you may hear called Steinhart-Hart is the same family of idea: a formula that fits real material curves. Vishay's note does not use that name, so treat it as background.

What matters in the field: two sensors can both be called "10K" and still have different curves. Navien's universal temperature sensor data sheet lists its probe as a 10K NTC at 25 degrees C (77 degrees F), 10,000 ohms plus or minus 1 percent, with a nominal Beta of 3,977 K. Fujitsu's procedure likewise says to pick the chart for that specific thermistor type. The same trap in building controls is covered in BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits. So do not swap in a sensor with a different curve just because the label says 10K. The board converts resistance to temperature using one curve, and a different curve will make every reading wrong except near the match point.

Reading a maker's resistance table

The maker's table is the only thing to trust. Trane's installation sheet for its outdoor temperature sensor (BAYSEN01ATEMPA, described as a 10K ohm thermistor) lists resistance in thousands of ohms, so 10.5 means 10,500 ohms. A few rows from Table 1:

  • 50 F: 19.9 thousand ohms
  • 60 F: 15.3
  • 65 F: 13.5
  • 70 F: 11.9
  • 75 F: 10.5
  • 100 F: 5.8

Notice the sensor is called 10K, yet this table has no 77 F row, and the value near 75 F is 10.5 thousand ohms. A "10K" label tells you about one point only. That is why the full table matters. Also notice the rows are 5 F apart, so most real readings land between two rows.

How to test one

These steps follow the Fujitsu General service support pages and Daikin's service manual wording. Use your own model's service manual for exact connector and wait-time details.

  1. Get the true temperature first. Fujitsu's procedure says to measure the actual temperature at the sensor with a reliable contact thermometer and let both settle together. A coil sensor on a cold pipe is not at room temperature, so room air is not a valid reference for it.
  2. Power off. Isolate power and wait the time the service manual gives. Resistance tests are done with power isolated and the thermistor unplugged from the board. Do not measure a sensor that is still wired to a live board, because the circuit will skew the reading.
  3. Inspect. Look at the tip, cable, connector, and for corrosion or water. Fujitsu's list includes the connector and terminal tension, since a poor contact can look like a bad sensor.
  4. Measure. Set the meter to ohms, pick a range that suits the expected value, and touch the two terminals. Fujitsu suggests thin leads or a straightened paper clip if probes are too big, taking care not to short the terminals.
  5. Compare to the table for that exact sensor at the temperature you measured, using the maker's tolerance.

Moving the cable while watching the meter helps too. Fujitsu notes that a reading that changes when the cable moves points to a bad lead, connector or solder joint.

Worked example: outdoor air sensor on a heat pump

Say an outdoor sensor is the Trane BAYSEN01ATEMPA type. You unplug it, power is off, and a contact thermometer next to it reads 66 F. Your meter reads 13.0 thousand ohms (13,000 ohms). Is it good?

  1. Find the two rows around the reading. Resistance 13.0k sits between the 65 F row (13.5k) and the 70 F row (11.9k).
  2. Interpolate. The rows drop 13.5 - 11.9 = 1.6k over 5 F. Your reading is 13.5 - 13.0 = 0.5k below the 65 F row. 0.5 / 1.6 = 0.3125, and 0.3125 x 5 F = 1.56 F. So the sensor is reporting about 65 + 1.56 = 66.6 F.
  3. Compare to the thermometer. 66.6 F against 66 F is about 0.6 F apart.
  4. Check against the tolerance. Trane's sheet says the sensor is good if resistance is within plus or minus 5 percent of Table 1. At 66 F the table value is about 13.5 - (1 / 5 x 1.6) = 13.18k. Five percent of that is about 0.66k, so the good window is about 12.52k to 13.84k. Your 13.0k is 0.18k low, about 1.4 percent. It passes.

Straight-line interpolation is a small approximation over a 5 F gap. As a cross-check, Trane's own 50 F and 100 F rows give a Beta of about 3,900 K by the Vishay formula, and applying it from the 65 F row gives about 13.16k at 66 F, essentially the same as the 13.18k from the straight line. Keep in mind that this B value is computed from one table for illustration, not a published spec. Also note this sensor test is different from a pass for the whole circuit. A good sensor with a corroded connector or a weak board input can still give a wrong reading.

Open, short and error codes

An open sensor reads infinite resistance (OL on most meters). A shorted one reads near zero. Control boards watch for both. Daikin's service manual for one split indoor unit says its thermistor error is decided when the input voltage is above 4.96 V or below 0.04 V while the compressor runs, and its procedure is to unplug the thermistor from the board, read resistance, and compare with the resistance table. If it matches, check the connectors, and only then suspect the board. Trane's sheet covers its outdoor sensor: its troubleshooting text ties a very high reading to wires touching or a damaged sensor and a very low reading to a wiring connection problem or a damaged sensor, and two dashes mean not connected properly, shorted or open. Brands name the codes differently, so see Mini-Split Blinking Lights and Error Codes for how to find your own maker's table.

Placement, clips and insulation

A perfect sensor in the wrong spot lies. Trane's outdoor sensor sheet says to mount it on the north side of the building, out of direct sun, clear of dryer vents and the outdoor unit's fan discharge, at least 12 inches above expected snow, and to seal the wire hole so attic heat or drafts do not affect it. A coil sensor needs real contact. A Trane installation document says to relocate and secure the coil sensor with a new 3/8 clip on the coldest circuit when frost banding shows uneven feeding, after checking charge and airflow first. A Lennox replacement guide for rooftop units shows coil thermistors clamped to bare copper. Where a maker calls for insulation over a sensor, follow it. A loose sensor reading air instead of the pipe is one of the easy faults to miss.

Habits that save a call

  • Match the curve to the board. Same part number from the maker is best.
  • Test the sensor before replacing a board.
  • If several sensors read wrong at once, think of the board's reference supply before the sensors.
  • Write down the temperature, the reading and the table value on the work order.

Field card: HVAC Thermistors: Resistance Tables and How to Test

Real temperature in, maker's table out.

  • NTC thermistor: resistance falls as temperature rises.
  • "10K" is one point on a curve. Different makers have different curves.
  • Power off, unplug the sensor, measure ohms, compare to the table for that sensor at the measured temperature.
  • Interpolate between rows, then compare to the maker's tolerance (Trane's outdoor sensor sheet: plus or minus 5 percent).
  • Open reads infinite, short reads near zero. Read your maker's code table.
  • Check mounting, clip contact and wiring before replacing parts.
