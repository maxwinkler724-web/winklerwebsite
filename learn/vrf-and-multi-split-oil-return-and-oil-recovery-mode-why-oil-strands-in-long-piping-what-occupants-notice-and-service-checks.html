VRF and Multi-Split Oil Return Mode: Why Oil Strands in Long Piping, What Occupants Notice and Service Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every compressor sends a little oil out with the discharge gas, and in a VRF or multi-split system that oil has to find its way home through long, branched piping, often while most of the indoor units are idle. Outdoor units handle it with a scheduled oil recovery mode. This lesson explains why oil strands, what the mode does, what occupants notice, and what to check before anyone touches the oil charge.

Check the maker's document for your system

Check the maker's document for your system. Timers, valve positions, piping limits and fault codes differ by maker and series. We show the spread on purpose. The installation and service manuals for the exact outdoor unit govern, and this page is general education only.

Why oil strands in a branched system

A US patent on a heating-mode oil return method for variable refrigerant flow systems (number 11,536,495) describes the basic problem: some oil leaves the compressor and travels into every indoor unit. In heating, the expansion valves of idle indoor units are held nearly closed so the running units get the refrigerant. The patent says that small opening lets refrigerant return but leaves the oil behind, so oil builds up in the idle branches and the compressor can run short.

Gas speed makes it worse. In the suction line, oil depends on moving gas to carry it. A VRF system unloads far below full capacity, and a branch that serves one small room at part load carries only a trickle of gas. Our line set sizing lesson and our suction riser lesson cover the published minimum velocities. Here we only need one idea: slower gas moves less oil.

Worked example (ours): the same branch at 100 and 25 percent load

Everything below is an assumption for practice, not a field measurement. A suction branch has an inside diameter of 1.00 in. It carries 8.0 lb/min of R-410A at full load and 2.0 lb/min at 25 percent load. We hold the vapor density the same at both loads to keep the arithmetic simple. For the density we use a refrigerant maker's thermodynamic property table, which lists R-410A saturated vapor at 40 F as 2.1999 lb/ft³ (133.1 psia).

Flow area. Area = π/4 × 1.00² = 0.7854 in². Divide by 144 to get 0.005454 ft².

Full load. Volume flow = 8.0 / 2.1999 = 3.637 cfm. Velocity = 3.637 / 0.005454 = about 667 fpm.

25 percent load. Volume flow = 2.0 / 2.1999 = 0.909 cfm. Velocity = 0.909 / 0.005454 = about 167 fpm.

Result. Velocity drops to one quarter, because the area and density did not change. Our line set lesson lists published horizontal minimums from different makers in roughly the 500 to 800 fpm range. At full load our branch is near that band. At 25 percent it is far below it, and the same pipe that returned oil at full load can let it creep and pool. Multiply that by many branches, many joints and a long main pipe and you see why a VRF controller cannot count on part-load gas to bring oil home.

What the oil recovery cycle does

The fix is to schedule a short burst of high flow. The makers' documents we read agree on the shape of it, with different details:

  • It is timed. One maker's technical reference starts a cooling recovery after 3 hours of accumulated compressor run time (the first after 1 hour) and a heating recovery after 8 hours. Heating recovery lasts 4 minutes there. A second maker's service manual starts a heating oil return 2 hours after power-up and then every 8 hours, ending after 6 minutes or when the suction superheat measure falls below its threshold. A maker's feature sheet says its timing also uses an oil discharge amount, not run time alone.
  • Compressor speed rises. The patent raises the compressor to its upper frequency limit and checks the oil level after about 5 minutes. The technical reference runs all inverter compressors together.
  • Indoor valves move. In the patent's cooling-mode recovery, the valves of idle units are opened fully and the valves of running units are narrowed to about half of their heating position. The technical reference pulse-controls the indoor and outdoor valves.
  • Heating may be interrupted. The patent says a conventional method switches to cooling so liquid refrigerant carries oil back, and that this lowers room temperature sharply. Its method tries to stay in heating when discharge pressure allows.

What occupants notice

Expect a brief change, then normal service. Three maker documents describe what the controls show. A wired controller or central remote displays an oil-recovery indication, and the indoor unit lamps flash slowly in one technical reference. A user page from another maker says the operation lamp flashes and operation stops during the process. A third service manual shows a one-minute compressor pause between phases to reduce reversing-valve noise. The patent confirms the comfort cost: a heating room can cool briefly if the system swaps to cooling. We found no maker text describing fan behavior on idle units, so we do not state it. If a customer reports a fan or noise from an unused room during a cycle, check that maker's sequence for the specific model.

A good explanation for a customer: the system is returning oil to the compressor, it happens every few hours, and it ends by itself within minutes. A cycle that is frequent, long, or ends in a stop is not routine.

Separators and oil level sensing

The first defense is the oil separator in the outdoor unit. One maker's table says it recovers refrigerant oil that escapes through the compressor discharge, and a solenoid valve on its return line also balances oil between parallel outdoor units. A feature sheet claims separation efficiency up to 99 percent; we treat that as a maker claim. Oil level sensing is often indirect. One maker's service manual infers level from a temperature sensor on the compressor oil circuit. If the level reads empty for 20 minutes the outdoor unit stops, and the oil depletion code is logged after 8 such stops. That is a protection against dry running, not a fix for the piping.

Piping rules that help

The best oil control is piping that stays inside the maker's limits. One maker's selection guide for a large heat recovery series lists, for example, 3,280 ft total piping, 656 ft equivalent and 574 ft actual for the longest run, 132 ft from the first branch to the farthest indoor unit, 360 ft with the outdoor unit above, 230 ft with it below, and 98 ft between indoor units. A second maker's table lists a 40 m (about 131 ft) longest pipe after the first branch, extendable to 90 m if listed conditions are met. The first guide also recommends an oil return bend in the main gas pipe every 32.8 ft (10 m) when the outdoor unit sits more than 164 ft (50 m) above the indoor units. Different series, different limits. Never carry a number from one model to another, and never add a trap the manual does not call for. For trap theory see the suction riser lesson linked above.

Service checks

  1. Read the data first. One maker's controller menu shows a status of defrosting, oil return, standby or running. Read the status, the run history and any codes before opening anything.
  2. Check the piping against the manual. Compare actual and equivalent length, rise and drop, and first-branch distance with the maker's limits for that model. A line that is too long, an extra trap or a missing oil bend explains repeat oil trouble better than any setting.
  3. Follow the maker's code flow. The oil depletion flow we read checks the shut-off valve, the oil-level temperature sensor and its resistance, the regular oil return circuit, a blocked dry core and the suction strainer, in that order.
  4. Do not add oil on a guess. The same flow reaches compressor oil only at the end and gives a system-wide amount for that model. Our rule: add oil only on the maker's instruction, because extra oil raises the stranded volume and can flood the compressor later.

Field card: VRF oil recovery

Slow gas strands oil, so the unit schedules fast gas.

  • Part-load branch velocity can fall to a quarter of full load.
  • Oil recovery is timed by run time, in hours, and lasts minutes.
  • The compressor speeds up and idle-unit valves open.
  • Occupants may see a flashing lamp or a brief comfort dip.
  • Read the status and codes before touching anything.
  • Check pipe length, rise and bends against the maker's limits.
  • Add oil only on the maker's instruction.
