Fountain Beverage Dispenser Cooling: Cold Plates, Ice Banks, Remote Chillers and CO2 Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fountain drink is cold water, cold syrup and CO2 gas, all pushed through a nozzle. Refrigeration techs get the call when the drink comes out warm or foamy, and the cause is nearly always the cooling method behind the counter. This lesson covers the three common ways to chill the product, the carbonator, CO2 gas safety, and the water-line food safety basics that go with the job.

Three ways to chill the product

Cold plate. A cold plate is a metal block (usually aluminum) with stainless tubing cast inside. It sits on the floor of the dispenser bin and ice is dumped on top of it. Water and syrup travel through the tubing, and the ice pulls heat out through the metal. There is no refrigeration circuit in it at all; the ice supply is the cooling. That is why a troubleshooting guide from a water-treatment supplier lists low ice, ice bridged over the plate, and standing water in the bin as causes of foamy drinks (see Sources). Water standing in the bin is not ice, and an air gap under a bridged ice pile means the plate cannot reach the ice. When we get this call, we look at the bin before we look at anything with refrigerant in it. The ice usually comes from a separate machine, covered in Ice Machine Freeze and Harvest Cycle Basics.

Ice-bank dispenser. Here a small refrigeration system freezes a block of ice on an evaporator coil inside a water tank. The product tubing runs through the same tank. An agitator keeps the water moving past the ice and the product coils, so the water in the bath stays near 32°F as long as some ice remains. A probe in the tank sees ice thickness and tells the compressor when to start and stop. A maker's service manual for an ice-bank beer dispenser says the unit should stay powered at all times so the control can cycle the compressor, that the compressor and condenser fan wait five minutes after power returns, and that the agitator keeps running after the bank is built. The same manual lists a seized agitator motor and a bad ice-bank control among the causes of product that will not get cold, and tells the tech to look for a low charge. The ice bank works like a battery: it is rebuilt slowly, then spent quickly at rush time. That lets a small compressor serve a large peak.

Remote recirculating chiller. When the dispenser is far from the equipment room, a chiller sits remotely and pumps cold bath water or glycol solution through insulated, bundled lines (often called a trunk line) up to the counter. The product lines travel in the same bundle, so they stay cold all the way to the valve. A maker manual for an ice-bank system describes riser lines banded to and insulated with the recirculation lines for exactly this reason. The trunk line, power pack and foam problems are covered at more length in Draft Beer Systems for Refrigeration Techs, and glycol strength and freeze protection in Brewery and Winery Glycol Chillers. Wet insulation, a damaged jacket or a bundle lying against a hot surface lets the line warm up, and the first drinks of a pour come out warm.

Carbonator and pump

A carbonator is a tank, a water pump and a pump motor. It mixes CO2 gas with tap water to make carbonated water, which then goes to the cooling coil. One maker handbook gives CO2 settings that depend on the system: 75 psi for a cold-carbonation system and 90 to 105 psi for an ambient system. Use the nameplate and the manual for the unit in front of you, not a number from this page. Gas dissolves better in cold water, so warm water in the carbonator means flat or spitting drinks even when the gas pressure is correct. The same handbook says lowering pressure may reduce foaming and that spitting usually calls for a slight increase. If the carbonator motor will not run, the water-treatment supplier's guide starts with power: the plug, a tripped GFCI or breaker, and the probe connection.

CO2 gas safety

CO2 is not poisonous like carbon monoxide, but it displaces air. It is also heavier than air: the OSHA chemical data page gives a vapor density of 1.53, so a leak settles low and can collect in a basement, a walk-in or a small storeroom where cylinders are kept. The NIOSH pocket guide lists a recommended exposure limit of 5,000 ppm as a time-weighted average, a short-term limit of 30,000 ppm, and an IDLH value of 40,000 ppm; OSHA's permissible exposure limit is 5,000 ppm over eight hours. Symptoms listed there run from headache, dizziness and shortness of breath up to coma, asphyxia and convulsions. Liquid or dry ice can also cause frostbite.

  • Chain or strap cylinders upright. Close the cylinder valve before moving a cylinder.
  • If you feel dizzy, get a headache or become short of breath in a cylinder room, leave first and report it. Do not rely on smell to detect CO2.
  • Do not enter a cellar or closed room with a suspected CO2 leak. Ventilate it from outside, and treat it as a confined space under your site procedure.
  • Ask whether the room has a fixed CO2 monitor with an alarm. Our own view is that any bulk tank or multi-cylinder room should have one; check your local code and your site rules.

Common complaints and where to look

  • Warm drinks, cold plate: low ice, ice bridging, water standing in the bin. Break up ice, drain the bin and wait. The supplier's guide says to allow 15 to 20 minutes for drinks to chill after correcting it.
  • Warm drinks, ice bank: the bank is gone or never built. The maker manual we read lists airflow clearance, a leak and low charge, a dead condenser fan and a dirty condenser, and, when the load is above capacity, adding a pre-chiller.
  • Ice bank not building: check power and fuses first, then the probe and its connection, the control, the agitator, and the refrigerant charge. A shorted or unplugged probe is a listed fault. A warm refill of the tank, or hot water feeding the dispenser, will also keep the bank from forming.
  • Foamy drinks: product temperature, then CO2 and water pressures, then ice or bank condition. The supplier's guide also lists an empty or loose syrup container connector.
  • Condenser dirt: the same manual says to clean the condenser weekly, warns that a dirty condenser can trip the compressor on overload, and calls for at least 15 inches of clearance above the unit and six inches around it. Dirt on the coil also raises head pressure; see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Agitator and tank: the manual says to inspect the agitator blade yearly, descale limestone, and keep the overflow clear of dirt and ice. If product is freezing in the coils, shut the unit off and look for the cause before restarting.

Water-line food safety

The dispenser is connected to drinking water, so the connection must protect the building supply. A state health department fact sheet explains the hazard: acidic carbonated liquid that flows backward into a copper water line can dissolve copper, and copper-contaminated drinks cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and stomach cramps. It quotes that state's plumbing code, which requires an air gap or a vented backflow preventer (a vented dual check type made for beverage dispensers) and bars copper pipe downstream of the preventer for carbonated beverage dispensers. A 1958 U.S. Public Health Service article reached the same conclusion from a series of illnesses linked to faulty check valves. We do not state your local rule here. Plumbing codes differ by place, so check the one that applies. Never plug the vent on a backflow preventer, and treat a leaking vent as a failed device. See Refrigerated Drinking Fountains and Bottle Fillers and Ice Machine Water Quality, Scale and Sanitizing for related water-side checks.

The FDA Food Code is the model food-safety code that state and local regulators use as a base for their own rules; its contents include parts on the plumbing system and on how often equipment must be cleaned. Which edition applies, and what it says about dispenser nozzles and water lines, depends on the adopting jurisdiction, so ask the health department or the site manager rather than assuming. Clean and sanitize only to the maker's instructions and the site's procedures.

Worked example: sizing the refrigeration (our own example)

These inputs are our assumptions, not data from a particular machine. Suppose an ice-bank tank holds a 60 lb ice bank. We want it rebuilt in 5 hours of low demand, and the dispenser serves 10 gallons per hour of water cooled from 75°F to 38°F.

  • Constants (standard physical values, our own use): latent heat of fusion of ice about 144 Btu/lb (a 1939 National Bureau of Standards measurement of 333.5 J/g converts to about 143.4 Btu/lb); water weighs about 8.34 lb/gal; specific heat of liquid water is 1 Btu/lb°F; one ton of refrigeration is 12,000 Btu/h.
  • Latent load to rebuild the bank: 60 lb × 144 Btu/lb = 8,640 Btu. Divided by 5 h = 1,728 Btu/h.
  • Sensible load of the water: 10 gal/h × 8.34 lb/gal = 83.4 lb/h. Temperature drop is 75 − 38 = 37°F. So 83.4 × 1 × 37 = about 3,086 Btu/h.
  • Total if both happen together: 1,728 + 3,086 = 4,814 Btu/h, about 0.40 ton. We ignore heat gain through the tank walls, the agitator motor heat and compressor derating in a hot room, so a real unit needs margin.
  • What it tells us: with the compressor off, the water load alone melts 3,086 ÷ 144 = about 21.4 lb of ice per hour, so the 60 lb bank lasts about 60 ÷ 21.4 = 2.8 hours of that continuous draw. A busy rush that outlasts the bank leads to warm product.

Field card: Fountain Beverage Dispenser Cooling

Warm or foamy? Check ice, agitator and condenser before the charge.

  • Cold plate: ice is the cooling. Look for low ice, bridging and standing water.
  • Ice bank: probe, control, agitator, charge and condenser. Keep the unit powered.
  • Remote chiller: insulated trunk line carries cold bath water or glycol with the product lines.
  • CO2 is heavier than air (vapor density 1.53) and has no warning odor. NIOSH: 5,000 ppm average, IDLH 40,000 ppm.
  • Backflow: air gap or vented dual check, no copper downstream of it where your code says so. Never plug the vent.
  • Our example: 60 lb × 144 ÷ 5 h = 1,728 Btu/h, plus 3,086 Btu/h for 10 gal/h of water, about 0.4 ton.
