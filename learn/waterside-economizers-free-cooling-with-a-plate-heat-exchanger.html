Waterside Economizers: Free Cooling with a Plate Heat Exchanger

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On a cold, damp day a chiller can sit idle while the building is still cooled. A waterside economizer does it by passing cooling tower water through a plate heat exchanger. This lesson explains how that works, how to predict the water temperature you will get, and when the system uses all, some or none of the free cooling.

The idea in one paragraph

A waterside economizer, also called free cooling, uses the cooling tower to make chilled water instead of running the chiller's compressor. MEP Academy describes it as depending on the outdoor wet-bulb temperature, not dry bulb, because the tower cools by evaporation. When the wet bulb is low enough, tower water is colder than the building's return water and can take heat out of it. The tower water never mixes with the chilled water. A plate-and-frame heat exchanger sits between the two loops. If you need a refresher on the tower itself, see Cooling Tower Basics, and for the two loops see Chiller Plant Fundamentals.

Why a plate heat exchanger

The exchanger has to deliver chilled water only a few degrees warmer than the tower water. Alfa Laval explains that in a plate unit the two fluids flow in opposite directions, which allows a very close temperature approach. Tranter lists a close approach of 1 C (about 2 F) for one of its plate-type designs. Tube-type exchangers can only do this to a limited extent. The exchanger also keeps dirty open tower water out of the closed chilled-water loop. That is why fouling matters; read Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach and Heat Exchanger Basics.

Two approaches add up

Approach means how close one temperature gets to another. A free-cooling plant has two of them stacked in a row:

  • Tower approach: tower leaving water minus outdoor wet bulb. The standard tower rating is 3 gpm per ton, 95 F to 85 F, with 78 F wet bulb, a 7 F approach, as R.L. Deppmann notes. Deppmann cites one manufacturer's data of about 50.5 F water at 45 F wet bulb, a 5.5 F approach, and a Consulting-Specifying Engineer article says tower approach ideally should be limited to about 5 F.
  • Heat exchanger approach: tower water entering the exchanger minus chilled water leaving it. Deppmann advises never designing below a 2 F approach and suggests considering 3 F for selection margin and first cost.

Chilled water you can make = wet bulb + tower approach + exchanger approach. That one line is the whole decision. Everything else is about what to do with the answer.

Worked example

Suppose the outdoor wet bulb is 40 F, the chilled-water setpoint is 44 F, and the building return water is 56 F. The return temperature is an assumption for teaching; real plants vary, and Chiller Plant Fundamentals notes that many are designed for a 10 to 16 F spread.

  1. Tower water: 40 F wet bulb + 7 F tower approach = 47 F.
  2. Chilled water leaving the exchanger: 47 F + 2 F exchanger approach = 49 F.
  3. Compare with the setpoint: 49 F is 49 - 44 = 5 F too warm. The economizer alone cannot meet 44 F, so full free cooling is out.

What happens next depends on the arrangement:

  • Non-integrated: the exchanger can carry the load only if the setpoint can rise to about 49 F or more. Otherwise the chiller runs and the exchanger is off.
  • Integrated (series): return water goes through the exchanger first. Total temperature drop needed is 56 - 44 = 12 F. The exchanger delivers 56 - 49 = 7 F of it, which is 7 / 12, about 58 percent. The chiller finishes the other 49 - 44 = 5 F, about 42 percent. That is partial free cooling.

The split works as a fraction of the temperature drop only because the same water flows through both devices. Check the real plant's flows. If the wet bulb falls to, say, 34 F, the same arithmetic gives 34 + 7 + 2 = 43 F, which is below 44 F, so full free cooling becomes possible, subject to the tower's own limits in the winter section below.

Integrated vs non-integrated

MEP Academy describes the two layouts. In a non-integrated system the chiller and the economizer take turns. When free cooling runs, the chiller is fully off and the tower water meets the whole load. The exchanger is piped around the chiller. In an integrated system the economizer runs at the same time as the chiller and reduces its load. The exchanger sits in series ahead of the chiller on the chilled-water side, so it precools the return water, while the tower water is piped to the exchanger and the chiller in parallel. Motorized valves change the flow paths between modes.

Integrated is the more flexible choice because it still helps on days when the numbers land at 49 F instead of 44 F. Consulting-Specifying Engineer notes that series piping with integrated control can begin free cooling at around 47 to 50 F wet bulb, higher than non-integrated. Deppmann notes the exchanger belongs on the chilled-water return to the chillers, often with its own pump. A parallel arrangement, where the exchanger and chiller outputs blend, can confuse a supply sensor placed downstream of both, so sensor location matters.

Changeover: who decides

The controller compares what the economizer can make with the setpoint. MEP Academy says the startup logic checks whether the two approaches can meet the chilled-water supply setpoint. Many plants also use a wet-bulb threshold as a first filter. The 45 F wet bulb with 50 F dry bulb figure shows up in code summaries, discussed next. Know your plant's own enable and disable points and the sensors behind them, because a drifting sensor moves the changeover point. See BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits.

The chiller has limits too. Consulting-Specifying Engineer says 55 F condenser water or higher has been a baseline for chillers, with older machines needing perhaps 60 F and newer ones working down to about 45 F. When integrated mode sends cold tower water toward the chiller, the maker's minimum entering condenser water temperature must be respected. Condenser-water control is covered in Cooling Tower Fan VFDs, Staging and Condenser Water Reset.

Warmer chilled water is not free

If the plant accepts 49 F supply, coils behave differently. Deppmann, drawing on coil data, says warmer supply reduces sensible capacity modestly but removes latent (dehumidifying) capacity sharply, nearly eliminating it at 52 to 53 F.

Winter and freeze protection

Free cooling is a cold-weather job, so the tower must run in freezing conditions. Do not guess here; follow the tower maker's cold-weather instructions. The general ideas from the sources:

  • Keep water flowing over the fill. A tower guide from Cummins-Wagner says to hold flow at or above the design minimum for each active cell, and to reduce the number of active cells when total flow drops. Consulting-Specifying Engineer suggests keeping at least half of the peak summer flow at the nozzles, or using weir dams.
  • Basin heaters, if fitted, are used to hold basin water above about 40 F, but only when they are covered with water. Exposed water-filled lines need heat trace.
  • At light load, send all flow to the basin rather than modulating a bypass over the fill, because low-flow areas of the fill can freeze.
  • Some ice at the air inlet is normal in subfreezing weather; excess ice damages towers. A smaller range and a warmer tower leaving temperature lower the risk. The same guide gives an example where 40 F cold water with a 20 F range could reach 32 F at the bottom of the louver face, while a 10 F range stays near 36 F.

Deppmann also notes that towers have a practical minimum leaving temperature, around 42 F in one example. Follow lockout rules and the maker's procedure for cold-weather work; see Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks.

Where energy codes come in

You will often hear that code requires a waterside economizer. Trade summaries of ASHRAE 90.1-based state codes, such as Deppmann's review of Michigan's commercial code, say the economizer must be able to carry up to 100 percent of the expected load at 50 F dry bulb and 45 F wet bulb or below, and must work together with the chillers to give partial cooling. Exceptions exist and depend on the building and the code edition adopted where you work, so check the local code. The air-side version is in Economizers Basics.

  • Plate HX between tower water and chilled water; loops never mix.
