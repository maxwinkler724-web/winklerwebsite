Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The nameplate on a gas furnace tells you how much fuel it is built to burn. The gas meter tells you how much it is really burning. Timing the small test dial on the meter, called clocking the meter, lets you compare the two with a stopwatch and some arithmetic.

Why check the input at all

Flame quality, heat exchanger life and temperature rise all assume the furnace burns the right amount of gas. Goodman's installation manual for its 80% furnaces warns that "overfiring of the furnace can result in premature heat exchanger failure" and says the gas input to the burners must not exceed the rated input on the rating plate. Under-firing is not harmful in the same way, but it can leave a house cold and the equipment running long. If a furnace is shutting down on its limit, start with High-Limit Trips and Airflow; this lesson is the gas side of that same question. The order of events inside the furnace is in Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation, and the same ideas apply to the gas heat section of a rooftop unit (see RTU Gas Heat Section Basics).

Reading the meter

A residential gas meter has a row of dials that add up to the usage you are billed for, plus a small test dial that sweeps around quickly. The test dial is marked for a fraction of a cubic foot, one cubic foot or two cubic feet per full turn. One trade reference (listed in Sources) advises finding the smallest dial and says it is often one-half cubic foot. Read the marking on your meter instead of assuming.

The procedure

  1. Isolate the appliance. Turn off every other gas appliance. The same reference adds that even pilot lights should be shut off, because they can throw off the reading. Goodman's manual has the same first step.
  2. Run the furnace at full rate. Let the burners light and settle. For a two-stage furnace, compare the high-fire reading with the nameplate input.
  3. Time one full turn of the test dial. Plumber magazine suggests penciling a mark on the glass at the pointer so you know where to start and stop. If the pointer jerks instead of sweeping, time several turns and divide by the number of turns.
  4. Do the math (next sections).
  5. Compare to the rating plate. Goodman's text says the measured input must not be greater than the input on the rating plate.

The arithmetic

Cubic feet per hour (cfh) equals 3,600 times the dial size in cubic feet, divided by the seconds for one turn. Btu per hour equals cfh times the heating value of the gas in Btu per cubic foot. Goodman's manual writes the same thing another way: heating value times 3,600, divided by seconds per cubic foot. For a one-half cubic foot dial, seconds per cubic foot is the turn time doubled.

The heating value is not a fixed number

Natural gas is a mix of gases, and its energy content depends on the mix. The U.S. Energy Information Administration says the heat content of natural gas may vary by location and by type of consumer, and tells people to check with their gas distributor. Its national average for gas delivered to consumers was 1,036 Btu per cubic foot in 2025. A bill explanation from New Jersey Natural Gas multiplies cubic feet used by a "BTU content" of 1.071 to get therms, which is 1,071 Btu per cubic foot on that sample bill. So a local value can sit a few percent above the national average. Ask your utility for the figure for your area and write down the one you used. A round 1,000 is common in teaching examples, including that reference's, but it can misstate your result.

Worked example

A furnace nameplate says 80,000 Btu/h input. The meter has a one-half cubic foot test dial. With everything else off and the furnace on high fire, one turn takes 24 seconds. Assume the utility says 1,036 Btu per cubic foot (the EIA 2025 national average; use your local value).

  1. Cubic feet per hour: 3,600 x 0.5 = 1,800, then 1,800 / 24 = 75 cfh.
  2. Btu per hour: 75 x 1,036 = 77,700 Btu/h.
  3. Compare: 77,700 / 80,000 = 0.971, so the furnace is at about 97% of nameplate input.

Check with the other form: 24 seconds for half a cubic foot is 48 seconds per cubic foot. Then 1,036 x 3,600 / 48 = 77,700. Same answer.

Now suppose the turn took only 20 seconds. That is 3,600 x 0.5 / 20 = 90 cfh, and 90 x 1,036 = 93,240 Btu/h, about 117% of nameplate. That furnace is over-firing and should not be left that way.

One caution about output. Multiplying input by an efficiency rating gives only an estimate of output, because AFUE is a seasonal figure, not a measurement of what the furnace does on a given day.

If the number is off: what to look at

The measured input is a symptom. The next step is to find the cause, using the manufacturer's manual for that exact model.

  • Manifold pressure. This is the gas pressure at the burner manifold with the burners firing, read with a manometer at the gas valve's outlet tap. Targets are model-specific. Goodman's MES80/CES80 manual lists 3.2 to 3.8 inches of water column for natural gas and 9.7 to 10.3 for propane. A Lennox kit instruction lists different values for different valves, such as 3.5 plus or minus 0.3 for some natural gas valves, and a high-fire and low-fire value for two-stage models. Do not borrow numbers from one model for another.
  • Orifices. The orifice size sets how much gas passes at a given pressure, so a wrong size changes the firing rate. Goodman's manual says not to try to raise the firing rate by changing orifices or increasing manifold pressure below 5,500 feet, and says installations above 5,500 feet may need both a pressure switch and an orifice change from the manufacturer's kits.
  • Supply pressure. Goodman gives an allowed range at the furnace of 4.5 to 10.0 inches water column for natural gas and 11.0 to 13.0 for propane, and warns that pressure above 13 inches can permanently damage the gas valve. Supply outside the range will make the readings hard to trust.
  • Fuel type. A furnace set up for one gas and fed the other is a serious mismatch. Goodman says conversions must be done by a qualified installer or service agency.

Plumber magazine adds that the fix depends on the unit: over-firing may call for a regulator sized to the overage, while under-firing may call for orifice or burner adjustments.

Who adjusts what

Changing gas pressure is not a casual adjustment. Goodman's manual says only personnel trained to install, adjust, service or repair the equipment should work on it, and that only minor adjustments should be made at the valve regulator, following the manual's pressure table. A Lennox instruction for one of its modulating models says not to attempt adjustments to the gas valve at all. So any adjustment follows that model's instructions, is done by a qualified person, and ends with a leak check of every fitting you disturbed. If you are an apprentice, practice the clocking and the arithmetic and leave regulator changes to your supervisor.

Field card: Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input

Time the dial, do the math, compare to the nameplate.

  • Turn off every other gas appliance first.
  • Run the furnace at full rate. Time one turn of the smallest test dial.
  • cfh = 3,600 x dial size / seconds. Btu/h = cfh x heating value.
  • Heating value varies. Get it from the utility and note it.
  • Measured input must not exceed the rating plate input.
  • Check manifold pressure, orifices and supply pressure against that model's manual.
  • Gas pressure changes: manufacturer's steps, qualified people only, then leak check.
