Car Wash HVAC and Heating: Freeze Protection, Radiant Slabs, Humid Bays and Hot Water Loads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A car wash bay is wet, cold in winter, full of chemical mist and often full of idling engines. That mix creates four jobs for the tech: keep water from freezing, keep humid air moving without rotting the equipment, size the hot water, and keep exhaust gas out of the breathing zone. This lesson walks through each one.

Freeze protection: bays, pipes and doors

Water is sprayed on purpose, then left on the floor and on the doors. Trade press on winterizing washes describes a few common defenses. Self-serve bays often use hydronic radiant heat built into the slab. Tunnels whose doors open constantly may do better with gas-fired infrared tube heaters, and some run them overnight so the equipment soaks up warmth. Overhead hoses and lines can be protected with electric heat trace or with hydronic tubing bundled alongside. Before cold weather, the same article suggests checking equipment-room thermostats and fans and the pressure and antifreeze level in floor-heat loops (Professional Carwashing & Detailing, "How to prevent a carwash freeze").

Many sites also use a weep system, which keeps a small flow moving through high-pressure lines so they cannot freeze. That article quotes one operator who treats 38 to 40 F as the safe zone for thermostatic weep valves. Treat that as one operator's practice, not a code value, and use the equipment maker's instructions for settings.

Doors are part of the heating system. A second trade article says continuous side and top seals keep cold air out and keep sprayed water from freezing on the door, that worn bottom seals are the first fix when a door freezes to the floor, and that plastic tracks and rollers conduct less cold than steel while resisting corrosion (Professional Carwashing & Detailing, "Carwash Door Winterization Tips").

Radiant slab and snow melt

Penn State's EGEE 102 energy material describes a "wet" radiant installation as tubing embedded in a concrete slab, which uses the slab's thermal mass to store and release heat. It also says older copper and steel tubing in concrete could corrode and leak, while cross-linked polyethylene tubing with an oxygen barrier has proven more reliable, and that a boiler or other water heater circulates the warm water through zoned loops. The same page lists slow response to thermostat changes as a drawback (Penn State EGEE 102).

That slow response matters in a wash. A slab holds heat well between cars, but it will not recover quickly after a door has been open for ten minutes in January. Pair it with air heat or good door seals. We do not repeat snow-melt design here: heat output per square foot, glycol, idling and sensors are covered in our hydronic snow melt lesson and the basics of tubing and manifolds in radiant floor heating basics. The apron outside a bay is a snow-melt problem; the floor inside is a freeze-and-comfort problem.

Humid bays and corrosion

A bay air stream is warm, wet and carries soap and, in winter, road salt. The trade article on freeze prevention notes that winter brine residue is harder to remove and suggests a desalting product. Salt plus constant moisture is hard on anything unprotected. Our general guidance, from the same ideas covered in our natatorium lesson, is to ask the equipment maker whether the unit offers coated coils, sealed or washdown-rated motors, and corrosion-resistant cabinets or fasteners for wet service. The door article above notes plastic tracks and rollers resist corrosion. Keep electrical controls out of the spray path, and ask the maker how the unit should be rinsed and inspected. We do not recommend a brand, and a coating does not replace keeping the equipment out of direct spray.

Ventilation and exhaust of humid air

Exhaust fans pull moisture and vehicle exhaust out, so they need makeup air. Without it the bay goes negative, doors are hard to open and cold air whistles in around seals and through the drain. In winter, the makeup air itself must be tempered, which is where the heating load comes from. Our own field habits: run exhaust only when the bay needs it, put the intake away from the exhaust discharge, and keep the fan and its motor on the clean side where possible. Exhaust ducts that carry wet air should slope to a drain point so condensate does not run back into the fan.

Water heating loads

Federal and industry data give the water side. The EPA WaterSense at Work guide reports an average of 60.0 gallons per vehicle for in-bay automatic washes without reclaim, 65.8 gallons for friction conveyor washes and 15.0 gallons for self-service bays, and says reclaim can cut in-bay use to as low as 8.0 gallons. The International Carwash Association study, Water Use, Evaporation and Carryout in Professional Car Washes (Brown, 2018, data from 2017), found average freshwater use of 30.0 gallons per vehicle for conveyor washes and 44.8 for in-bay automatics. The two sources differ because they measure different samples, so use them as a range, and ask the site for its meter readings. Neither source gives how much of that water is heated, so the fraction below is our assumption.

Worked example (our own, assumptions stated)

Assumptions (ours): 20 cars per hour at peak; 30 gallons per car, matching the ICA conveyor average; 25 percent of that water is heated, so 7.5 gallons of hot water per car; cold water arrives at 55 F and is heated to 120 F; a heater thermal efficiency of 80 percent.

  1. Hot water per hour: 20 cars x 7.5 gal = 150 gal/h.
  2. Flow in gallons per minute: 150 / 60 = 2.5 gpm.
  3. Temperature rise: 120 - 55 = 65 F.
  4. Output: 8.33 lb/gal x 2.5 gpm x 60 min/h x 65 F = 8.33 x 2.5 = 20.825; x 60 = 1,249.5; x 65 = 81,217.5, or about 81,000 Btu/h.
  5. Input at 80 percent: 81,217 / 0.80 = 101,522, or about 102,000 Btu/h.

Real demand arrives in surges, and the heater also needs capacity to fill any storage and recover after a rush. The example is an average, not a sizing result. The method for storage and peak-hour recovery is in our commercial hot water sizing lesson, so we do not repeat it. Colder inlet water in winter raises delta T and the load; recompute with the real temperatures.

Vehicle exhaust and carbon monoxide

An enclosed bay with idling engines is a carbon monoxide risk. OSHA's carbon monoxide fact sheet calls internal combustion engines one of the most common workplace sources, lists garage mechanics among higher-risk workers, and gives a permissible exposure limit of 50 ppm averaged over eight hours. It tells employers to install ventilation that removes CO, keep CO-producing equipment such as water heaters maintained, and use CO monitors with audible alarms. NIOSH lists a recommended exposure limit of 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling, and an immediately dangerous level of 1,200 ppm (NIOSH Pocket Guide; NIOSH notes OSHA status should be verified). A gas water heater in the bay needs its own combustion air and venting, as in the parking garage lesson, which covers detection and fan staging so we do not repeat it here. Follow site procedures and the rules where you work, and do not let engines idle in a closed bay.

Field card: Car wash HVAC and heating

Keep it warm, keep it moving, keep it hot, keep CO out.

  • Seals, doors and floor or tube heat all work together against freezing.
  • Slab heat is slow; do not expect it to recover quickly from open doors.
  • Ask for coated, sealed or washdown-rated equipment for wet service.
  • Exhaust needs makeup air, and winter makeup air needs heat.
  • Btu/h = 8.33 x gpm x 60 x delta T. Our example: about 81,000 Btu/h output.
  • OSHA PEL for CO: 50 ppm (8-hour). NIOSH REL: 35 ppm, 200 ppm ceiling.
