Duct Insulation R-Values for Attics, Crawl Spaces and Basements: What Codes and DOE Say

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "duct insulation r value attic," the short answer from the code summaries we read is R-8 for ducts 3 inches or larger outside the conditioned space, and R-6 for smaller ducts. This lesson explains why the number exists, what the common duct insulation products are rated at, why you seal before you insulate, and when moving the duct is smarter than wrapping it.

Why ducts outside the envelope lose and gain heat

A supply duct carries air that is much colder than a summer attic or much warmer than a winter attic. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Building America program says vented attics can be very hot in summer and very cold in winter, and that estimated thermal losses through ducts installed in unconditioned attics range from 10 to 45 percent. A second DOE Building America sheet says conduction through duct walls adds heat gain or loss even when the ducts are sealed, and that conduction alone can raise heating and cooling energy use by 10 percent or more. So there are two separate losses: air that leaks out (covered in Sealing Duct Leaks) and heat that soaks through the metal and wrap. Insulation only fights the second one.

What the codes say

The International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) is the model code most states adopt, often with changes. We could not open the ICC's own text for free, so we read two public compliance guides for the 2021 edition. Baltimore County's guide says supply and return ducts outside conditioned space must be insulated to R-8 minimum, with R-6 when the duct is less than 3 inches. The State of Maine's 2021 IECC guide says R-8 for ducts 3 inches in diameter and greater, and R-6 for smaller ones. Both say ducts must be sealed. A Washington State code page we read carries the same 3-inch split, and adds that a duct may count as inside the conditioned space only if it is completely within the air barrier and the thermal envelope.

You may hear "R-8 in attics and R-6 everywhere else" as a rule of thumb. The summaries we read for the 2021 edition split by duct size instead, so editions and state changes clearly differ. The number that binds you is the one your local code official enforces. ENERGY STAR's duct insulation page (via DOE's Building Science Education Solution Center) also calls for a minimum of R-8 on ducts in unconditioned spaces. An older ENERGY STAR fact sheet gave ranges instead: roughly R-4 to R-8 in attics in warm and mixed climates, and R-6 to R-11 in cold-climate attics.

Types of duct insulation and their R-values

Every maker prints R-values a little differently, so read which column you are looking at.

  • Fiberglass duct wrap on metal duct. Owens Corning's SOFTR Duct Wrap FRK data sheet lists a nominal R-value for the thickness out of the package and a lower installed R-value, because the wrap is squeezed when stretched around the duct. Its 1.5 inch wrap is R-4.2 installed, its 2.2 inch wrap R-6.0 and its 3 inch wrap R-8.3. Johns Manville's Microlite FSK data sheet lists the same installed values (R-4.2, R-6.0, R-8.3 for those thicknesses) and says installed values assume no more than 25 percent compression. The lesson: a "3 inch" wrap is the one that meets R-8, and crushing it with tight straps or tape pulls the real number down.
  • Duct board. CertainTeed's ToughGard duct board is a rigid fiberglass board that is cut and folded into the duct itself. The 1 inch board is listed at R-4.3, and its outer foil laminate acts as an air barrier and vapor retarder.
  • Insulated flex duct. ATCO's flexible air duct data sheet lists R-4.2, R-6.0 and R-8.0 versions. It says those are measured on flat insulation at installed thickness, so a sagging or compressed run can do worse. See Flex Duct Installation.

Matching the rating to the code matters. Buying R-6 flex for an attic where your inspector wants R-8 is a failed inspection, not a savings.

Vapor retarder facing and sweating ducts

In summer a supply duct runs colder than the dew point of humid attic or basement air. DOE's Building Science Education Solution Center says insulation with a vapor barrier limits heat gain or loss and helps prevent condensation, and that the insulation must be fully in contact with the duct walls. The facings in the data sheets above are that barrier: Johns Manville lists its foil facing at 0.02 perms before lamination, and ATCO lists its flex duct jacket at 0.05 perms. A torn facing or an unsealed seam lets humid air reach the cold metal, and the duct sweats inside the wrap. Seal seams with tape that matches the facing, as the Owens Corning sheet recommends. The step-by-step dew point check is in Sweating Ducts and Vents, and the damp-air picture under a house is in Crawl Space Ducts and HVAC.

Seal first, then insulate

Insulation is not an air barrier. The Building America Solution Center's guidance on encapsulating ducts says to fasten and mastic-seal every connection before the insulation goes on, and to test leakage before foaming because leaks are hard to fix afterward. DOE's solution center says ducts should be sealed with mastic or metal tape meeting UL 181, 181A or 181B. The 2021 code summaries above also require ducts, air handlers and filter boxes to be sealed. Mastic versus foil tape is covered in Sealing Duct Leaks.

Worked example: what does R-4.2 versus R-8 cost you?

Heat flow through a layer can be estimated with Q = A × ΔT ÷ R, where Q is Btu per hour, A is surface area in square feet, ΔT is the temperature difference in degrees F, and R is the R-value. A plumbing textbook from BCcampus writes the same idea as area times a K value (which is 1 ÷ R) times the design temperature difference. Say a summer supply trunk in an attic has these assumed numbers:

  • Surface area A: 60 square feet (about 30 feet of 8 inch round duct, rounded).
  • Attic air: 120°F. Air inside the duct: 58°F. These are the temperatures used in a Green Building Advisor example of an attic duct.
  • ΔT = 120 − 58 = 62°F.

With R-4.2: Q = 60 × 62 ÷ 4.2 = 3,720 ÷ 4.2 = about 886 Btu/h.

With R-8: Q = 60 × 62 ÷ 8 = 3,720 ÷ 8 = 465 Btu/h.

Difference: 886 − 465 = about 421 Btu/h of heat the air conditioner no longer has to remove from that one run, roughly 0.035 ton (421 ÷ 12,000). This is a simplified estimate. It ignores air films, the larger outside surface of the wrap, compression at supports, and radiant attic heat, so real numbers differ. Whole systems have more duct than one trunk: Green Building Advisor's example of a house with 560 square feet of attic supply duct put the added cooling load at about 5,227 Btu/h at R-8 and 6,305 at R-6.

When moving the duct beats adding R-value

Doubling the R-value cuts the loss roughly in half, but it never gets to zero while the duct sits in a 120°F attic. DOE Building America reports that properly installed buried ducts (laid on the attic floor under loose-fill insulation, with closed-cell spray foam first in humid climates) can reduce thermal losses to 3 percent or less. Its ducts-in-conditioned-space sheet reports an 8 to 15 percent air-conditioning cost saving in a hot-dry study and sealed attics near 80°F instead of 140°F. The Building America Solution Center states that the best location for ducts is inside conditioned space, and gives an 8 inch round duct with 1.5 inches of closed-cell foam an effective R-12.7. Practical ways to do it include dropped ceiling chases, runs through open-web floor joists, and an air-sealed, insulated attic. DOE adds that in humid and mixed climates the ducts are wrapped in closed-cell spray foam before the loose-fill goes on, and Green Building Advisor calls plain burial risky in humid climates, so follow the local guidance. Field card: Duct Insulation R-Values

Seal it, wrap it to the right R, keep the facing intact.

  • 2021 IECC summaries: R-8 for ducts 3 inches or larger outside conditioned space, R-6 for smaller. Your local code rules.
  • Heat flow: Q = A × ΔT ÷ R, in Btu/h.
  • Example: 60 sq ft, 62°F difference: R-4.2 loses about 886 Btu/h, R-8 about 465.
  • Installed R-value is lower than package R-value; do not crush the wrap.
  • Foil or metallized facing is the vapor retarder. Tape every seam to match.
  • Seal with mastic or UL 181 tape before insulating.
  • Best fix: ducts inside the envelope, or buried or encapsulated per DOE guidance.
