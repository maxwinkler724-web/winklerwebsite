Gas Clothes Dryers for Heating Techs: Burner, Vent and CO

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas heating tech walks into a basement to look at a furnace and finds a gas dryer in the corner, or in a closet next to the water heater. The dryer is a small gas-fired appliance with its own burner, its own gas connector and its own vent. This lesson looks at it the way a heating tech would: what burns, where the products of combustion go, and what can turn a laundry closet into a carbon monoxide or fire problem.

Follow the maker and the local rules

This page is general education. The dryer's installation instructions and rating plate, and the rules where you work, control the job. If something here differs from the maker's instructions, follow the instructions.

What is inside: burner, igniter and gas valve

The federal energy agency describes a gas clothes dryer as the same tumble-drum, forced-air appliance as an electric dryer, except that the heat source is gas. In practice that means a small burner sits in the cabinet, an igniter lights it, and a gas valve opens and closes the gas to it while a fan pulls room air across the flame, through the tumbling clothes and out the vent. A heating tech will recognize the same basic parts found in a furnace: a fuel supply, a way to light it, a way to prove and control it, and a path for the products of combustion.

The burner's input is printed on the dryer's rating plate. Read it there for each job rather than assuming a number. The federal dryer test procedure tells labs to hold the burner within plus or minus 5 percent of the maker's hourly Btu rating, which shows how much the rating plate number matters to how the dryer is meant to run.

Natural gas versus propane

The same federal test procedure uses natural gas of about 1,025 Btu per standard cubic foot and propane of about 2,500 Btu per standard cubic foot. It tests natural gas dryers with 7 to 10 inches of water column at the inlet and propane dryers with 11 to 13 inches. Propane carries roughly 2.4 times the heat in each cubic foot, so a burner set up for natural gas would fire far too hard on propane, and one set up for propane would starve on natural gas. That is why the burner orifice and any built-in regulator must match the gas on site. Any change between gases is done with the maker's conversion parts and instructions, by a qualified person. Our propane conversion lesson, linked below, covers the same idea on furnaces and boilers.

Gas connector and shutoff valve

The dryer usually connects to the house piping with a flexible connector and a shutoff valve. The federal consumer product safety agency has warned that older uncoated brass connectors, used mostly on ranges, ovens and clothes dryers, can fail where the tube meets its end fittings and leak gas, which can lead to fire or explosion. In a 1996 release it reported about 200 failure reports tied to 35 deaths and 59 injuries. The safety agency says any uncoated brass connector should be replaced right away with a plastic-coated brass or stainless steel one, that connectors should be replaced whenever the appliance is replaced or moved, and that homeowners should not slide the appliance out to check, since even small movement can break an old connector. On a service call, that is a good reason to look at the connector before you pull the dryer away from the wall.

Why the dryer vent is also a flue

On an electric dryer the vent carries warm, wet air and lint. On a gas dryer the same duct also carries the products of combustion from the burner. That makes the dryer vent the appliance's flue. A university extension housing specialist put it plainly: a gas clothes dryer should never be vented indoors under any circumstances, because the chance of carbon monoxide poisoning is too great. The same release notes that a typical load holds roughly 1 to 2 gallons of water, which is why indoor venting also brings mold and condensation problems.

Lint is the other half. The safety agency says lint built up in the dryer or exhaust duct can block airflow and cause overheating and fires. Its warning signs are clothes still damp after a normal cycle, drying that takes longer than usual, and no air coming out of the outside vent while the dryer runs. A university extension fact sheet on gas appliances lists lint-clogged dryer vents among the things that can change how gas appliances perform. For a gas dryer, a blocked vent is both a fire risk and a path for combustion products to spill back into the room.

Short, rigid metal vent runs

The safety agency recommends replacing plastic or foil accordion duct with rigid or corrugated semi-rigid metal duct, because flexible plastic and foil trap lint more easily and kink or crush, which cuts airflow. The federal fire agency adds: have the dryer installed and serviced by a professional, never run it without the lint filter, clean the filter before and after each cycle, check that the duct behind the dryer is not crushed or restricted, and confirm the outdoor flap opens when the dryer runs. Keep the run as short and straight as the layout allows. Length limits, elbows and termination details are in our dryer vent lesson, linked below, so we do not repeat them here.

Makeup air and combustion air in small closets

Every cubic foot the dryer blows outdoors has to be replaced by air leaking or flowing in from somewhere else in the house. Only a small part of that air is burned; most of it is drying air. The extension fact sheet says not to block fresh air openings and to make sure enclosed appliances get enough combustion air. In a tight laundry closet that also holds a water heater or furnace, a running dryer can lower the closet pressure and pull on the other appliances' vents. That is the condition tested in a worst case depressurization test, and closet sizing for combustion air is covered in the combustion air lesson, both linked below.

Carbon monoxide alarms near laundry areas

The safety agency recommends carbon monoxide alarms, battery-operated or with battery backup, on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and says interconnected alarms are best. It does not recommend mounting them in kitchens or right above fuel-burning appliances, so an alarm meant to cover a gas laundry area belongs nearby, not on the wall over the dryer. The extension fact sheet suggests at least two detectors, one in the furnace room and one near sleeping areas. The safety agency says that nationally more than 200 people a year die on average from carbon monoxide made by non-automotive consumer products, and that early symptoms look like the flu without a fever. If an alarm sounds, the safety agency says to get outside to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, and not operate the suspect appliance until trained personnel have serviced it. The safety agency also warns never to use a gas clothes dryer to heat the home.

When to call the gas utility

If you smell gas inside, a state consumer gas safety page says to leave the building quickly, not switch lights or appliances on or off, not try to shut off the gas, and call 911 once outside. If the smell is outdoors, call the gas company from outside. The safety agency gives the same advice: leave, do not use a phone in the house, and call the gas supplier or 911 from a neighbor's home. A smell of gas at a dryer is a call to the utility or 911 first, not a reason to start pulling the dryer out.

Worked example (our own): combustion air versus house air exhausted

This is our own example with assumed numbers, to show the size of the two airflows. It is not a sizing method. Use the rating plate and the maker's figures on a real job.

  • Assumed dryer input: 22,000 Btu per hour (our assumption; read the actual rating plate).
  • Assumed air burned: 10 cubic feet of air per 1,000 Btu of gas (our assumption for the example).
  • Assumed vent airflow: 120 cubic feet per minute (our assumption).
  • Assumed house volume: 12,000 cubic feet (our assumption).
  1. Combustion air: 22,000 ÷ 1,000 = 22 thousand Btu per hour. 22 × 10 = 220 cubic feet of air per hour.
  2. Air the dryer exhausts: 120 cubic feet per minute × 60 minutes = 7,200 cubic feet per hour.
  3. Compare: 7,200 ÷ 220 = about 33. The dryer moves about 33 times more air than it burns.
  4. House air pulled out: all 7,200 cubic feet per hour leave the house. Of that, 220 is combustion air and 7,200 − 220 = 6,980 cubic feet is drying air. Over one hour of running, 7,200 ÷ 12,000 = 0.6, so in this example the dryer exhausts air equal to about six tenths of the house volume.
  5. Gas used, for interest: with the federal test value of about 1,025 Btu per cubic foot for natural gas, 22,000 ÷ 1,025 = about 21.5 cubic feet of gas per hour. On propane at about 2,500 Btu per cubic foot, 22,000 ÷ 2,500 = 8.8 cubic feet per hour.

Check the math: 220 × 33 = 7,260, close to 7,200 because we rounded 32.7 to 33. 220 + 6,980 = 7,200. 0.6 × 12,000 = 7,200. 21.5 × 1,025 = about 22,040, and 8.8 × 2,500 = 22,000. The takeaway: the burner needs only a little air, but the dryer as a whole pulls a lot of house air outdoors, and that replacement air has to come from somewhere.

Field card: Gas Clothes Dryers for Heating Techs

The dryer vent is the dryer's flue.

  • Read burner input from the rating plate; do not assume it.
  • Natural gas about 1,025 Btu per cubic foot, propane about 2,500: the orifice must match the gas.
  • Replace old uncoated brass connectors; replace connectors when the dryer is replaced or moved.
  • Gas dryers never vent indoors. Vent carries moisture, lint and combustion products.
  • Rigid or semi-rigid metal duct, short run, outdoor flap opens when running.
  • A dryer exhausts far more house air than it burns; watch tight closets shared with other appliances.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, not right above the appliance.
  • Smell gas: leave, no switches, call 911 or the gas utility from outside.
