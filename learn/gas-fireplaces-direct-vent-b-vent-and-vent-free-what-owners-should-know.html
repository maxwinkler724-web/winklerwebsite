Gas Fireplaces: Direct Vent, B-Vent and Vent-Free

Last updated: 2026-10-16

"Is a vent-free gas fireplace safe?" depends on which kind you have, how it is sized to the room, and whether it is kept clean and alarmed. This lesson helps a homeowner tell the three types apart, then explains what the manuals say about the safety features, moisture, carbon monoxide and hot glass.

Check your own manual first

Your manual rules. Everything below comes from the manuals and safety pages listed under Sources. Your fireplace may differ. Use the manual for your exact model, and ask your local code office what is allowed where you live. This is general education, not an inspection.

The three types and how to tell them apart

Direct vent. Heat & Glo's Passage manual describes a unit built so that all combustion air comes from outside the building and all exhaust goes outdoors. The fire sits behind fixed glass, with a barrier in front of it. The vent is a pipe that runs through a wall or roof.

B-vent (natural vent). The Heatilator Novus B-Vent manual says the exhaust goes outdoors but combustion air is pulled from the room. It calls for double-wall B-vent pipe, 5 inches across for those models, and warns that negative pressure in the house can cause spillage of combustion fumes and soot. A B-vent unit also has hot glass, so glass alone does not tell you the type.

Vent-free (unvented). These have no flue at all, so the room itself has to supply the air and absorb the water vapor. The Empire, Desa and Grand Canyon vent-free manuals therefore carry strict size, ventilation and shutoff rules, covered below.

To identify yours: read the model name and the manual title (they usually say direct vent, B-vent or vent-free), look at the rating plate for the model number, and look at the vent. A sealed unit with an air-and-exhaust terminal on an outside wall or roof suggests direct vent. A double-wall flue running up through the ceiling suggests B-vent. No pipe at all suggests vent-free. Confirm with the model number and manual. The manuals read here refer to the rating plate for gas type and model, but do not say where it is mounted, so check your manual.

What the oxygen depletion sensor does

Vent-free units use an oxygen depletion sensor (ODS) pilot. The Empire manual says the ODS will shut the heater down if enough fresh air is not available, and that you should not relight until you provide fresh air. The Desa manual says the same. The Grand Canyon manual adds that the pilot can be relit only when fresh air is available. If your unit keeps shutting off, treat it as a warning about the room or the pilot, not a nuisance. The ODS pilot also needs cleaning: Grand Canyon requires at least yearly cleaning of the burner and ODS pilot. For how pilots and flame sensing work in general, see Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit.

Moisture and combustion products indoors

Burning gas makes water vapor and, when something is wrong, carbon monoxide. The Empire manual says an unvented heater produces about one ounce of water for every 1,000 Btu of input per hour, and suggests a dehumidifier in high humidity. Desa warns that a room without enough ventilation air can grow mildew from too much moisture. Grand Canyon says tight construction or high altitude can cause excess heat or moisture, and that it may be necessary to open a window slightly, 1 to 2 inches. Empire's manual also says not to use the heater as a primary heat source.

Room size and run time limits

The Empire, Desa and Grand Canyon manuals all define a confined space as less than 50 cubic feet of room volume per 1,000 Btu per hour of input. Empire and Desa divide the room volume by 50 to find the largest Btu/h input the space can support. Grand Canyon writes the same rule as length times width times height times 20. They also restrict bedrooms and bathrooms: Desa says its heater may not be installed in a bedroom or bathroom unless installed as a vented appliance, and Empire says an unvented heater above 10,000 Btu/h may not go in a bedroom and one above 6,000 Btu/h may not go in a bathroom. Empire also notes that Massachusetts bans these heaters in bedrooms and bathrooms, so local law can be stricter than the manual.

None of the manuals read here gave a maximum run time in hours. Grand Canyon says only that the unit is for attended use. Desa asks for cleaning every 2,500 hours of operation or every three months. If your manual gives a run-time limit, follow it.

Worked example: is this room big enough?

Say the manual rates a vent-free fireplace at 30,000 Btu/h. The room is 14 ft by 12 ft with an 8 ft ceiling, and the manual's rule is 50 cubic feet per 1,000 Btu/h.

  1. Room volume: 14 x 12 x 8 = 1,344 cubic feet.
  2. Divide by 50: 1,344 / 50 = 26.88.
  3. Multiply by 1,000 Btu/h: about 26,880 Btu/h is the most the room supports.
  4. Cross-check with Grand Canyon's form: 1,344 x 20 = 26,880 Btu/h. Same answer.
  5. Compare: 30,000 is more than 26,880, so by the manuals' rule this room is a confined space for that unit. It needs added ventilation air as the manual describes, a smaller input, or a different location. (Empire's own sample is a 16 x 10 x 8 ft room, 1,280 cubic feet, which supports 25,600 Btu/h.)
  6. Moisture check, using Empire's figure: 30,000 / 1,000 = 30 ounces of water per hour, about a quart of water every hour the unit runs.

This uses a single room, as the manuals' worksheets do. Open doorways to other spaces and air leakage are handled in the manuals' worksheets, so use yours.

CO alarms and annual service

The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) recommends CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, tested monthly, and not directly above fuel-burning appliances. It also recommends professional inspection of heating systems every year. When an alarm sounds, CPSC says to get to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, and stay out until responders say it is safe; do not run the suspect appliance until it has been serviced. Our CO alarm lesson walks through those steps. Manufacturers add their own: Empire, Heat & Glo and Heatilator want inspection at least annually by a professional.

Pilot and glass-front burn hazards

CPSC's 2013 statement on vented gas fireplaces says hot glass fronts have seriously burned children, and notes the vented-fireplace ANSI standard now requires a protective barrier when the glass exceeds 172°F. It also said the standard for unvented fireplaces had not yet been updated for contact burns. The Heat & Glo manual says hot glass will cause burns, to supervise children, and never to run the unit with the barrier removed. The Heatilator manual says the glass stays hot while the unit cools. Desa and Empire tell owners to keep children and adults away from hot surfaces and to supervise young children.

Field card: Gas fireplaces, three types

Know the type, size the room, alarm the house.

  • Direct vent: outside air in, exhaust out, sealed glass.
  • B-vent: room air in, pipe out the top; negative pressure can spill fumes.
  • Vent-free: no flue; room volume at least 50 cubic feet per 1,000 Btu/h (volume x 20 = max Btu/h).
  • ODS shutoff means fresh air is needed; do not relight until you provide it.
  • Vent-free makes about 1 oz of water per 1,000 Btu per hour.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; test monthly.
  • Professional inspection at least yearly; keep the barrier on; supervise children.

For the technician: quick service notes

Verify the rating plate model and gas type before you service. On vent-free units, clean the burner and ODS pilot (Grand Canyon requires at least yearly) and check log placement, since Grand Canyon warns misplaced logs cause sooting and improper CO output. On direct vent, inspect the termination for debris; Heat & Glo says it must not be recessed into a wall or siding. On B-vent, confirm the pipe size and draft.
