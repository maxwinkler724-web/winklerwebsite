R-600a Isobutane in Household Refrigerators and Freezers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Open the back of a newer household refrigerator and the refrigerant may be isobutane, R-600a, a flammable gas charged in grams rather than pounds. This lesson covers why makers use it, what A3 flammability means in a kitchen, how to spot these units, and how to handle leak checks, recovery, brazing and disposal without creating an ignition problem.

Why makers moved to R-600a

The federal environmental agency lists isobutane as an acceptable refrigerant for new household refrigerators and freezers. Its global warming potential is 3, it does not deplete the ozone layer, and the federal environmental agency says hydrocarbon refrigerants can improve a refrigerator's energy efficiency compared with R-134a. Hydrocarbons already dominate this market in several other regions. The trade-off is flammability, which is why the rules around these units are strict.

Very small charges, measured in grams

The federal environmental agency's 2011 final rule limited each sealed system to 57 grams (2.0 ounces). A later federal environmental agency rule removed that cap and now relies on the appliance safety standard, which allows up to 150 grams (about 5.3 ounces) per separate circuit in equipment made after March 12, 2018. Many units hold less than the limit. For scale, the federal environmental agency says domestic refrigerators and freezers overall typically contain 0.1 to 0.6 pounds of refrigerant, roughly 45 to 272 grams (our own conversion). Because the charge is so small, a scale that reads in tenths of a gram is the right tool, and the data plate charge is the number to trust.

A3 flammability and what it means in a kitchen

The 2011 rule classes isobutane as A3: low toxicity, higher flammability. Its lower flammability limit (LFL) is 18,000 ppm, which is 1.8 percent by volume in air. Gas that leaks from the cabinet is about twice as heavy as air (our own comparison: 58 versus about 29 grams per mole), so it tends to settle low. The federal environmental agency's risk screening in the 2011 rule assumed 95 percent of a release mixed into the bottom 0.2 meters (9 inches) of the room. A 57-gram leak in a 13.8 cubic meter kitchen would reach the LFL in that low layer, but the federal environmental agency reported the smallest kitchen in its survey at 31 cubic meters and found 99 percent were at least 53 cubic meters. A sealed system with no ignition source nearby is a low risk. A cut line, a pilot light or a spark from a switch is what changes that.

Recognizing an R-600a unit

  • Data plate. Read the refrigerant name and charge in grams. Do not assume from the age or size of the unit.
  • Warning markings. The federal environmental agency's rules called for permanent danger and caution markings on the exterior, near the machine compartment, and near exposed tubing, in letters at least 1/4 inch (6.4 mm) high. They warn against puncturing tubing, mechanical defrosting and untrained service.
  • Red at puncture points. Refrigerant-carrying tubing and devices are to show red at service ports and other places where puncturing could occur, extending at least 1 inch each way.
  • Process tubes. The federal pages we read do not describe process tube markings, so we leave that to the maker's service information. When the data plate and the tubing disagree, stop and find out why before cutting anything.

The federal environmental agency also says isobutane is allowed only in new equipment designed for it and clearly labeled. It may not be used to retrofit a unit built for another refrigerant.

Leak checking without ignition sources

The federal environmental agency's further-information notes for these units call for adequate ventilation, spark-proof tools, a Class B dry powder extinguisher nearby, chemical goggles and gloves (the gas can cause freeze burns on skin), and training in flammable refrigerants. They also say that if the air concentration reaches one fourth of the LFL, which is 4,500 ppm for isobutane (our own arithmetic from 18,000 ppm), people should leave and return only after ventilating. In practice that means unplugging the unit, opening doors and windows, and keeping lighters, pilots, space heaters and anything that arcs away from the work. Which leak detector or pressure method is acceptable is a question for the maker's service instructions; the federal pages we read do not list one. For general detector behavior, see Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors.

Recovery and venting rules for household appliances

The federal environmental agency states that isobutane is exempt from the federal venting prohibition in household refrigerators, freezers and combination units. That exemption is about the environment, not about safety. The federal environmental agency's notes still say to use recovery equipment designed for flammable refrigerants and to release refrigerant only in a well-ventilated area, such as outdoors. The federal environmental agency noted in 2011 that such equipment was not yet widely available. Separately, the federal environmental agency says people recovering refrigerant from small appliances of five pounds or less do not need the federal technician credential, and that the final disposer of an appliance is responsible for recovering any refrigerant in it. Check your employer's procedures and the maker's instructions before you recover or release anything. For the wider rule, see the EPA 608 venting prohibition lesson.

Why sealed-system repair is often replaced

This part is our own view, not a federal finding. Opening a sealed system with a flammable charge means flammable-rated recovery gear, a controlled work area, a trained person and a brazed repair, all for a charge worth very little. Add the age of many failed units and a replacement often makes more sense than a repair, and a trained technician can say honestly when that is the case. When a repair is done, do it only per the maker's instructions.

Brazing cautions

A torch is an open flame, the opposite of the spark-proof tools the federal environmental agency calls for, so no brazing should start until the system has been recovered per the maker's instructions and the area ventilated (our own reading of those notes). Do not puncture tubing to release pressure. For the general hot-work habits that apply, see A2L and A3 Refrigerants.

Safe disposal

The federal environmental agency says hazardous parts such as compressor oil must be removed before an appliance is scrapped, and that it encourages scheduling pickup through a recycler, retailer or public works department, because curbside appliances may be landfilled or vented. Do not puncture tubing at any point.

Our own worked example

These are our own numbers, built from stated assumptions. They are not measurements of any real unit.

Assumptions. A refrigerator with an R-600a charge of 50 g, an older R-134a unit of similar size with 120 g, a kitchen of 30 cubic meters (12.5 square meters of floor, 2.4 m ceiling), and the 18,000 ppm LFL from the federal environmental agency. Isobutane molar mass 58.12 g/mol and an ideal-gas molar volume of 24.06 L/mol at 20 °C (our own constants).

  1. Ounces. 50 g ÷ 28.35 g/oz = 1.76 oz. 120 g ÷ 28.35 g/oz = 4.23 oz. The isobutane charge is 50 ÷ 120 = 42 percent of the R-134a charge, so 58 percent less by weight.
  2. Vapor volume. 50 g ÷ 58.12 g/mol = 0.860 mol. 0.860 × 24.06 L/mol = 20.7 L of vapor, or 0.0207 m³.
  3. Evenly mixed. 0.0207 ÷ 30 = 0.00069, which is 690 ppm. 690 ÷ 18,000 = 3.8 percent of the LFL.
  4. Settled low. Suppose all of it sat in the bottom 0.2 m: 12.5 m² × 0.2 m = 2.5 m³. 0.0207 ÷ 2.5 = 0.0083, or 0.83 percent, which is 0.83 ÷ 1.8 = 46 percent of the LFL.

Check. Both answers stay under 100 percent, and the low-layer case is about 12 times the evenly mixed case, matching the ratio 30 ÷ 2.5. Real leaks mix unevenly: a slow leak disperses and may never reach these values, while a pocket behind a toe kick or in a closed cabinet can run much richer than the room average. Treat the room-average figure as a floor, not a safety result.

Field card: R-600a in household refrigerators

Small charge, real flammability. No flame, no sparks, no punctures.

  • R-600a is A3, with an LFL of 18,000 ppm (1.8 percent).
  • Read the data plate for refrigerant and grams before touching tubing.
  • Look for danger and caution markings and red at service ports.
  • Ventilate, use spark-proof tools, keep a Class B extinguisher close.
  • Use recovery equipment designed for flammable refrigerant.
  • Brazing waits until the system is recovered per maker instructions.
  • Our example: 50 g is 1.76 oz; 120 g of R-134a is 4.23 oz.
