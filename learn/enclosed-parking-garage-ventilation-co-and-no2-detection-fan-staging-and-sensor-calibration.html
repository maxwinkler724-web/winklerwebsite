Enclosed Parking Garage Ventilation: CO and NO2 Detection, Fan Staging and Sensor Calibration

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A garage full of idling and creeping cars fills with exhaust faster than most people expect, and you cannot smell the dangerous part. This lesson covers why enclosed garages need mechanical exhaust, how CO and NO2 sensors tell the fans when to stage up, the exposure limits behind the setpoints, how sensors get checked with test gas, and what the fans can save you.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Garage ventilation rules differ by state, city and code edition. This page is general education built from federal exposure data, maker application guides and one state energy code guide. Your local amendments and the engineer's drawings decide the actual setpoints.

Why a garage needs exhaust

Engines burn fuel, and burning fuel puts out gases. EPA lists automobile exhaust from garages and parking areas as a source of carbon monoxide (CO), and says nitrogen dioxide (NO2) comes mainly from burning fuel, including cars, trucks, buses and off-road equipment. Belimo's parking garage article describes CO as colorless, odorless and tasteless, and NO2 as highly poisonous. Neither gas gives a warning by smell, so the building depends on sensors and fans. For the household side of CO, see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds.

Belimo also notes that the airflow a garage needs changes with how often cars come and go. A garage that is busy at 8 a.m. and empty at 2 a.m. does not need the same airflow at both times. That is the case for demand control.

Continuous or demand-controlled

Kele's garage gas detection guide says the International Mechanical Code lets an enclosed garage either run its ventilation continuously or use automatic controls driven by gas detection, and that under the 2021 and later editions the automatic option calls for both CO and NO2 detectors that are UL 2075 listed. Another maker guide, from Macurco, cites a different section of the IMC for a CO trigger of 25 ppm. The two guides number the sections differently, so read the edition your jurisdiction adopted instead of trusting a section number from a brochure. ASHRAE 62.1 is the other standard the guides point to for ventilation rates and demand-controlled strategies. We name both in plain text only. Standards are sold, so we do not link copies.

The idea matches demand-controlled ventilation with CO2 sensors in offices: measure the contaminant, move air only when needed. The contaminant here is toxic instead of merely stuffy, so the controls are less forgiving.

Where the sensors go

  • Height. Kele says sensors are typically mounted 4 to 6 feet above the floor, at breathing level. Macurco's guide says about 5 feet. Belimo says some jurisdictions require CO at 4 feet and NO2 at half the ceiling height, which in an 8 foot garage puts both at the same height.
  • Coverage. Kele and Belimo both describe a coverage radius of about 50 feet per sensor. Macurco lists up to 7,500 square feet per detector for its CO and NO2 models, and up to 3,750 square feet when wall-mounted instead of column-mounted. Always use the coverage figure from the installation sheet of the sensor you are installing.
  • Layout. Belimo says coverage is drawn as circles on the mechanical plans, and overlapping circles improve coverage. It also says pillars are an excellent mounting location.
  • State energy code example. The California Energy Commission's guide to its enclosed garage rules requires at least one CO sensor per 5,000 square feet, placed where the highest concentrations are expected, and at least two per proximity zone.

Alarm and fan staging setpoints

Neither Kele nor Belimo publishes ppm numbers, and Kele says local jurisdictions may add their own trigger thresholds. Macurco's guide lists typical trip points that show how staging works:

Notice the gap between rising and falling points. That gap is a deadband, and it keeps a fan from short cycling when the reading hovers at one number. These are the maker's typical values, not a code requirement. The California guide, for its part, requires CO to be held at 25 ppm or less at every sensor, and requires fans to stage or modulate down to 50 percent or less of design capacity when contaminant levels allow. Those two documents disagree on the start point, which is exactly why you use the project's approved sequence.

The California guide also describes a sensor-failure rule: during occupied hours a sensor is judged failed if its 30-minute average differs from the other sensors in its zone by more than 15 ppm, and the system then goes to design ventilation and sends an alarm. Wire a failed-sensor alarm into the building system; see BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them.

Exposure limits behind the numbers

  • CO. The OSHA permissible exposure limit is 50 ppm as an 8-hour average. NIOSH recommends 35 ppm as a time-weighted average and a 200 ppm ceiling. NIOSH lists 1,200 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health (OSHA chemical data and the NIOSH Pocket Guide).
  • NO2. OSHA's PEL is a 5 ppm ceiling. NIOSH's recommended limit is 1 ppm as a 15-minute short-term limit, and its immediately dangerous level is 13 ppm (NIOSH Pocket Guide).

Compare the NO2 numbers with the table above: the maker's alarm of 5 ppm sits right at the OSHA ceiling, and the fan start of 2.5 ppm is above NIOSH's 1 ppm short-term recommendation. Sensor setpoints are control and alarm points. They are not a statement that anything below them is safe for every person.

Calibration and bump testing

A bump test exposes the sensor to a known gas to see that it responds. A calibration adjusts the instrument to match a known gas. The Minnesota Department of Health defines them that way. A Honeywell gas detection bulletin separates a function check (does gas reach the sensor and do alarms trip) from an accuracy check, and separates zero calibration in clean air from span calibration with a known gas. For fixed monitors that bulletin suggests an accuracy check every 4 to 6 months as a minimum, and zero and span calibration at least annually, with typical acceptance of plus or minus 10 to 20 percent of the applied gas value unless the maker or regulator says otherwise. The bulletin is a distributor-hosted PDF, so we cite it as text only. Macurco says to follow the manufacturer's intervals, and lists sensor life of about 7 years for its CO sensor and 2 years for its NO2 sensor. NO2 sensors age faster, so that is the one to watch.

In practice: use the maker's gas concentration and flow rate, record the as-found reading before adjusting anything, and log it. Fresh air for zeroing must really be fresh; a garage is the wrong place to assume it.

Makeup air paths

Our own plain-language point: exhaust fans only work if replacement air has a way in, so a blocked or closed opening starves them. Open ramps, louvers and dedicated makeup openings are the usual paths. Position the openings far from the exhaust so air crosses the sensed area, as in the sketch. If heated makeup air is used, see Make-Up Air Units. Belimo adds that position-based damper control saves heating or cooling energy as well as fan power.

Worked example: continuous vs demand control

Our own example, all inputs are assumptions. One exhaust fan draws 7.5 kW at full speed on a variable frequency drive. Hours per year: 8,760. Demand control profile: 6,000 hours at 50 percent speed, 500 hours at full speed, and 2,260 hours off. Electric rate: $0.15 per kWh.

  1. Continuous at full speed: 7.5 kW x 8,760 h = 65,700 kWh.
  2. Fan law cube relation (see fan laws): at 50 percent speed, power is 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.125 of full, so 7.5 x 0.125 = 0.9375 kW.
  3. Low speed: 0.9375 kW x 6,000 h = 5,625 kWh.
  4. Full speed: 7.5 kW x 500 h = 3,750 kWh.
  5. Demand control total: 5,625 + 3,750 = 9,375 kWh.
  6. Savings: 65,700 - 9,375 = 56,325 kWh, which is 56,325 / 65,700 = 85.7 percent. At $0.15 that is about $8,449 per year.

The cube relation is ideal. Real drives, motors and system curves lose some of it. As a cautious check, the California guide limits fan power to 30 percent or less of design wattage at half airflow. Using 30 percent: 7.5 x 0.30 = 2.25 kW x 6,000 h = 13,500 kWh, plus 3,750 = 17,250 kWh. Savings are 48,450 kWh, or 73.7 percent, about $7,268. Either way the savings are large because most hours need little or no exhaust. Your garage's real profile will differ, so trend the fan runtime in the BAS before promising a number.

Field card: Enclosed Parking Garage Ventilation: CO and NO2 Detection, Fan Staging and Sensor Calibration

Sensors at breathing height, fans staged with a deadband, test gas on a schedule.

  • CO and NO2 both matter; neither can be smelled.
  • Sensors about 4 to 6 ft above the floor; check the sheet for coverage area.
  • OSHA CO PEL 50 ppm (8 h); NIOSH REL 35 ppm and 200 ppm ceiling.
  • OSHA NO2 ceiling 5 ppm; NIOSH short-term 1 ppm.
  • Rising and falling setpoints differ so fans do not short cycle.
  • Bump test to prove response, calibrate to adjust; follow the maker's intervals.
  • Keep makeup openings clear and far from the exhaust.
  • Never bypass a failed-sensor alarm; fix the sensor.
