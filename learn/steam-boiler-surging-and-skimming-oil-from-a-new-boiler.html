Steam Boiler Surging and Skimming Oil From a New Boiler

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new steam boiler can fire up and then bounce its water line like a bobber. Oil left from manufacturing and from new pipe is a common reason, and the usual cure is skimming: letting the oily layer run off the top of the water. This lesson shows what surging looks like, what three makers print about skimming, and how to size up a skim run.

Check your own manual first

The maker's manual wins. The procedures below come from the Weil-McLain, Peerless and U.S. Boiler manuals we read, plus a published article by a U.S. Boiler trainer. They disagree on details such as pipe size and firing. Skimming also involves hot water and, on some boilers, caustic cleaners. Treat this page as study material, and have a qualified technician do the work on a real boiler.

What surging and priming look like

Surging means the water level in the gauge glass swings up and down instead of holding near its mark. A U.S. Boiler trainer, Ron Beck, describes surging as a level that bounces more than roughly half an inch to an inch. Weil-McLain's troubleshooting table lists violent waterline fluctuations and water passing into the steam mains as one problem, called priming. Water thrown into the mains is the part you hear and see downstream:

  • Bouncing or unreadable water line in the sight glass.
  • Wet steam: drops of water riding with the steam, so radiators heat poorly. See Wet Steam and Carryover Basics.
  • Banging in the pipes, covered in Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.
  • A low-water cutoff that shuts the burner off when the swinging level dips below it; see Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance.
  • Burner starting and stopping often, which can have other causes; see Steam Boiler Short-Cycling: Causes and Where to Look.

Why a new boiler carries oil

Oil is a film on the water surface. Bubbles of steam rise to it, cannot break through, and pile up as froth. The froth lifts water toward the steam outlet. U.S. Boiler's installation manual says oil from new piping connections and sediment in existing piping must be removed from the system. Peerless says contaminants cause foaming, surging and an unstable water line. A new boiler and new near-boiler piping bring pipe-thread oil and similar residue, and an old system returns its own dirt. Weil-McLain's manual also warns against petroleum-based cleaning or sealing compounds in a steam boiler, so even the pipe dope matters.

How makers say to skim

Skimming means filling the boiler to the level of a tapping near the waterline, then letting water and its oily film run out of that tapping while fresh water comes in below. The makers agree on the idea and differ on the details.

Weil-McLain SGO steam manual (page 35)

  1. Run 1-1/2 inch skim piping from the skim tapping to a floor drain, with a tee to watch the skim level, and raise the waterline to the middle of the tapping.
  2. Fire the burner just enough to keep the water below steaming temperature.
  3. Feed water to hold the level, cycle the burner to keep pressure from rising, and keep skimming until the discharge runs clear.
  4. When the boiler is warm but not hot, drain it, remove the skim piping, refill to the normal line, and steam for 15 minutes to drive off dissolved gases.
  5. Check traps and air vents, and repeat after several weeks of operation if needed.

The manual adds a warning: skim the boiler only. Do not try to clean old piping this way or leaks can appear.

Peerless Series 63/64 manual (page 33)

Peerless says to clean the boiler no later than one week after first start-up, after running it 24 to 48 hours to loosen sediment. It specifies a 1-1/4 inch nipple, ball valve and drain line on the skim tapping, heating to 180 to 200 degrees F while cycling the burners so the boiler does not steam, and continuous make-up water. Skim until the water runs clear, which it says can take several hours. Peerless uses washing soda in this procedure, at one pound per 800 square feet of net EDR rating, poured in through the relief valve tapping, and then calls for draining and refilling once or twice more to flush the soda out. Do not use that amount on another maker's boiler. It also warns not to leave the boiler unattended and not to let the water drop below the bottom of the glass.

U.S. Boiler Independence manual (pages 43 and 44)

This one starts away from the boiler: fill to the normal line, fire, and run steam through the system for several days so oil from new piping returns to the boiler, draining condensate at the wet return until it runs clean. A boil-out of at least five hours with the maker's recommended compound follows. For stubborn cases, a second boil-out uses the 1-1/4 inch surface blow-off tapping with 1 inch pipe to an open drain and no shut-off valve. The water boils about five hours without making steam, with a steady trickle running out until the discharge is clear.

A trainer's version

Beck's article has the boiler make steam, then shut off for the whole skim, because firing stirs the water and drags oil back down. He uses a side tapping of at least 3/4 inch with a horizontal nipple and no upturned elbow, so water leaves from the surface. He sets the stream at about the thickness of a pencil and skims until the water is comfortable to hold a hand under, about an hour for a residential boiler. He says one skim is usually enough. That is the opposite of the makers above on firing, so the install manual for your model decides.

Worked example: how many boiler volumes get flushed

None of the manuals we read gives a skim flow in gallons per minute, so the flow here is an assumed round number for practice, not a maker figure. Use the U.S. Boiler Independence IN7I, whose manual lists a steamable water volume of 8.3 gallons. That figure counts only the water between the normal waterline and the low-water cutoff level, so the true water content is larger.

  1. Assume the stream is 0.5 gallon per minute.
  2. In one hour: 0.5 x 60 = 30 gallons.
  3. Volumes passed: 30 / 8.3 = 3.6. Because real content is bigger than 8.3, the true number is lower than 3.6.
  4. Time to pass three of those 8.3 gallon volumes: 3 x 8.3 = 24.9 gallons, and 24.9 / 0.5 = about 50 minutes.

The arithmetic shows why makers say "until it runs clear" instead of giving a fixed time: the run depends on the real water content, your flow, and how much oil was there. Measure the stream with a bucket and a timer if you want a real number for your own job.

How long, and when to repeat

Times in the sources run from about an hour for a small boiler (Beck) to several hours for dirty systems (Peerless, U.S. Boiler). Weil-McLain says to repeat after several weeks of operation if needed. Beck says that if two skims do not improve the surging, oil is probably not the cause.

When skimming is not the fix

Weil-McLain's table also lists a waterline that is too high, incorrect piping, and a sudden release of steam from zone valves. Piping at the boiler is a frequent culprit, since wrong near-boiler piping sends wet steam out. Check it against Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping instead of skimming again. Beck also lists flame impingement and water pH. For reading the level before you decide, see Steam Boiler Sight Glass: Water Level and When to Add Water. Only use chemical cleaners if your maker's manual calls for them, in the amount it names.

Field card: Steam Boiler Surging and Skimming

Oil makes froth, froth makes surging, skimming lets the film run off.

  • Surging: water line bouncing, wet steam, banging pipes, low-water cutoff trips.
  • New boilers and new pipe carry oil; makers say remove it, often within the first week.
  • Skim from the tapping at the waterline, with the pipe running straight out.
  • Weil-McLain and Peerless: keep water warm, below steaming, until it runs clear. Beck: boiler off.
  • Never leave it unattended, and never let water fall below the glass.
  • No improvement after two skims: look at piping, level, zone valves.
  • Chemicals only as your maker's manual says.
