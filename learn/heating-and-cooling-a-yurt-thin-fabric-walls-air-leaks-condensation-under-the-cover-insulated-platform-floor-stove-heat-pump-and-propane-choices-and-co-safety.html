Heating and Cooling a Yurt: Fabric Walls, Leaks, Condensation and Safe Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A yurt is a round dwelling with fabric walls and roof on a raised platform. It is lovely to live in and hard to heat, because a thin fabric shell holds very little insulation and leaks air in many places. This lesson explains where the heat goes, what to fix first, and which heat sources are safe to use inside a fabric building.

Why a yurt behaves differently

A frame house has wall cavities that hold thick insulation. A yurt has a layer of fabric, often a quilted or reflective liner inside it, and little else. A liner slows heat flow somewhat, but it cannot match a framed wall, and fabric panels leave gaps at the door frame, at the ring where the roof meets the crown, and at the base of the wall where fabric meets the platform. Those gaps matter as much as the fabric itself. Warm air that leaks out is replaced by cold air coming in, and the heater must warm that new air all night.

Air leakage also drives where heat collects. Warm air is lighter and rises, so it gathers under the crown and the dome skylight, where the fabric is thin and the glazing is cold. One university extension page on ice dams describes the same pattern in ordinary houses: warm air leaking toward the top of a building carries heat with it, and sealing the leaks comes first, with insulation added after. The same order works in a yurt, as our own advice: seal first, then insulate.

Where the heat goes

  • Walls and roof: conduction through fabric and liner. This is the part we can calculate below.
  • Door, roof ring and wall base: air leakage. It is hard to calculate and easy to feel on a windy night. Weatherstrip the door, add a flexible seal at the wall base, and close the gap at the ring.
  • Dome skylight: a small area, but it is often single glazed and it sits where the warm air collects. A fitted insulated cover for cold nights helps, and it can be removed on mild days.
  • Floor: a platform sits in the open air, so the floor loses heat to cold air moving underneath. Insulate between the joists and skirt the sides of the platform so wind cannot blow through the space below. Keep the insulation and skirting dry.

Condensation inside the cover

On a cold night the inside surface of the fabric is cold. Indoor air carries moisture from breathing, cooking, showers, drying laundry and stored firewood, and when that air touches the cold surface the moisture turns to water. A university extension page on moisture lists those sources, along with humidifiers and unvented appliances, and says humidity has to be kept low in winter to prevent condensation. It suggests a winter target of about 25 percent relative humidity and warns that levels above 50 percent can cause problems.

In a yurt we handle this three ways, all our own practical advice. First, cut the moisture you add: vent the cooking and shower steam outdoors, keep firewood outside, and do not run a humidifier. Second, crack a vent or the crown briefly to let damp air out. Third, keep the liner snug and the fabric from touching the liner in a way that traps wet spots, and wipe or air out any damp areas so mold does not start. Our site has a related lesson on winter condensation and indoor humidity.

Worked example: how much heat does a 20-foot yurt lose? (our own example)

This is our own example, with assumed inputs. It is not from a measured building.

Assumptions (ours): 20-foot diameter, 7-foot walls, a roof cone rising about 5 feet to the crown, an insulated liner giving about R-4 overall for the walls and roof, and a 40 degree F difference between inside and outside.

  1. Wall area. Circumference times height: 3.14 x 20 ft x 7 ft = about 440 square feet.
  2. Roof slant length. The cone has a radius of 10 ft and a rise of 5 ft, so the slant is the square root of (10 squared + 5 squared) = the square root of 125 = about 11.2 ft.
  3. Roof area. 3.14 x 10 ft x 11.2 ft = about 351 square feet.
  4. Total shell. 440 + 351 = about 791 square feet.
  5. Conduction loss. Area divided by R, times the temperature difference: 791 / 4 x 40 = about 7,900 Btu per hour.

That figure is before air leakage and before the floor. To see how leakage adds up, assume (our own number) that the yurt leaks one full air change per hour. The volume is a cylinder, 3.14 x 10 x 10 x 7 = about 2,200 cubic feet, plus a cone, one third of 3.14 x 10 x 10 x 5 = about 520 cubic feet, for about 2,720 cubic feet. One change per hour is 2,720 / 60 = about 45 cubic feet per minute. Using the usual sensible-heat factor of 1.08, the loss is 1.08 x 45 x 40 = about 1,950 Btu per hour. A leakier yurt on a windy night could lose far more, which is why sealing the door, ring and base is the cheapest way to cut the load. The takeaway is that a small heater with a good liner and tight seals can work, while a leaky yurt with a bare cover needs a much larger one.

Choosing a heat source

Whatever you choose, follow the maker's installation instructions and local rules, and treat fabric as a fire hazard. The federal fire agency's heating page says to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from every heat source, including wood stoves and space heaters. Fabric walls and a vinyl or canvas cover are exactly that kind of material. Our own rule of thumb: when a maker's clearance is larger, use the larger one.

  • Wood stove. Use a stove that has been tested by a qualified testing laboratory, set on a proper non-combustible pad, with a listed chimney kit made for this use and a roof thimble that passes through the fabric safely. Keep the clearance to fabric that the stove and kit instructions require. The fire agency advises having the chimney inspected and cleaned each year, and putting ashes in a lidded metal container outdoors at least 10 feet from the building. The federal environmental agency indoor air page lists wood stoves as a possible source of CO through back-drafting and says doors should fit tightly. Our own addition: small yurts overheat quickly, so a small stove and dry fuel work better than a big one.
  • Direct-vent propane wall heater. A sealed, direct-vent heater takes combustion air from outside and sends exhaust outside, so it does not use room air. The federal environmental agency page advises choosing a vented model when replacing an unvented heater. It must be installed to the maker's instructions, with the vent terminal sleeved safely through the wall and clear of the fabric. Have a qualified installer handle the gas connection.
  • Small ductless heat pump. A small mini-split can both heat and cool, which suits a yurt. Mount the outdoor unit on a pad or a wall bracket, clear of snow and not hanging off the fabric. Because the loads are small, choose a small unit, and see our lesson on tiny houses and very small homes for the oversizing problem.
  • Electric heater. Use one on a proper circuit sized for it, plugged into a wall receptacle and not an extension cord. The fire agency's heating pictographs warn against extension cords with heating appliances and say to turn space heaters off when you leave the room or go to bed. It also reports that portable heaters made up 3 percent of home heating fires in 2017 to 2019 but 41 percent of the deadly ones. Our own advice: pair a portable heater with a thermostat and a tip-over shutoff, and keep it well clear of fabric.

Summer heat and the crown

In summer the sun heats the cover and the hot air rises into the crown. If the dome skylight can open, opening it lets that hot air escape while cooler air enters low through screened doors or windows. That is a natural chimney effect. Our own tips: shade the south side, use a reflective or light-colored cover, open the crown early in the day, and use a small ductless heat pump or a fan for the hottest afternoons. The condenser-side clearances for any ductless unit are in its manual.

Safety: CO, smoke and fire

  • Alarms. The consumer product safety agency says to install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of a home and outside sleeping areas, to keep them out of kitchens and away from directly above fuel-burning appliances, and to leave the building and call 911 if one sounds. A yurt is small, so place a CO alarm near the sleeping area and a smoke alarm in the main space, and test them. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.
  • No charcoal. The agency says never to burn charcoal inside a home, garage, vehicle or tent. A yurt is a tent-like fabric building, so we treat that as a firm rule.
  • No generators indoors. Never run a portable generator in or near an enclosed space. Open doors and windows do not stop CO from building up. The agency links generators to 92 CO deaths in 2020.
  • No unvented fuel heaters. The agency says never to use unvented fuel-burning appliances in any room where people sleep, and the federal environmental agency page lists unvented kerosene and gas space heaters as CO sources. In a small, tight yurt we say never to use them at all, as our own conservative rule. Never heat with a gas range or oven either.
  • Fabric and flame. Keep curtains, bedding and the liner away from every heat source, and never leave a stove or heater running unattended.

For similar small, leaky buildings, see our lessons on container homes and cabins and event tents.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Yurt

Seal first, line second, heat small, keep fabric clear.

  • Seal the door, roof ring and wall base before buying a bigger heater.
  • Warm air collects under the crown; cold air leaks in low.
  • Insulate and skirt the platform floor, and keep it dry.
  • Cut moisture sources to limit condensation on the cold cover.
  • Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heat sources, more if the maker says so.
  • Our own example: about 791 sq ft of shell at R-4 and 40 F difference loses about 7,900 Btu/h by conduction, plus leakage and floor.
  • CO alarm near sleeping, smoke alarm in the main space. No charcoal, generators or unvented fuel heaters inside.
