Comparing Heating Fuel Costs per Million BTU: Gas, Oil, Propane, Electric and Heat Pump

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Gas is billed in therms, oil and propane in gallons, electricity in kilowatt-hours. You cannot compare those prices directly. This lesson shows the one-line method that puts every fuel on the same scale: dollars per million BTU of heat that actually reaches the house.

Why raw prices mislead

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) says plainly that comparing fuel prices in different units is not meaningful. Its method is to divide the price per unit by the heat content per unit and multiply by 1,000,000, which gives dollars per million BTU (written MMBtu). EIA also lists the efficiency of the appliance that turns the fuel into heat as a factor to weigh next to cost. So the full recipe has two parts: fuel cost per MMBtu, then divide by efficiency to get cost per delivered MMBtu. The Heat Pump or Gas Furnace lesson (linked below) uses this idea for two fuels. Here we extend it to all five common choices.

Step 1: Energy content of each fuel

EIA's Btu page gives these standard values:

  • Natural gas: 1 therm = 100,000 Btu (10 therms make 1 MMBtu)
  • Heating oil: 1 gallon = 138,500 Btu
  • Propane: 1 gallon = 91,452 Btu
  • Electricity: 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu

EIA says these are sample factors that are recalculated each year. Its separate conversion-calculator page uses a slightly different 2026 estimate for heating oil (137,381 Btu per gallon), so expect small differences between sources. For comparing fuels, a difference that small does not change the ranking. To turn a gas bill in ccf into therms first, see the gas bill lesson linked below.

Step 2: Efficiency inputs

Burners lose some heat up the flue, so the efficiency of a furnace or boiler is a fraction below 1. The example below assumes 95% for a gas furnace, 85% for an oil boiler and 95% for a propane furnace. These are assumptions for the example; use the rating on the nameplate or the contractor's quote for a real house.

Electric resistance heat has a COP of 1.0: one unit of heat for every unit of electricity. A heat pump moves heat instead of making it, so its COP is above 1. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Energy Saver says a properly installed air-source heat pump can deliver up to about three times as much heat as the electricity it uses, and that modern heat pumps can cut heating electricity use by as much as 75% compared with resistance heat. DOE describes the seasonal rating, HSPF, as the heat delivered over an average heating season divided by the electricity consumed. The example uses a seasonal COP of 2.5.

Why heat pump COP is not one number

A single COP is an average. Efficiency changes with the outdoor temperature, and rating labels only capture part of that. NEEP, the Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships, notes that the HSPF metric has no test points below 17°F. Its cold-climate heat pump specification therefore reports performance at several outdoor temperatures (47, 17 and 5°F and the lowest listed temperature) and requires a COP above 1.75 at 5°F with the unit at maximum capacity. Compare that with the 2.5 seasonal average: the same machine does better on a mild day and worse on a very cold one. DOE adds that staged or inverter-driven compressors adjust output to the outdoor temperature. This is also why dual-fuel systems exist; see the changeover lesson below.

Step 3: Worked example

All prices here are invented examples for practice, not current New Jersey prices. Replace them with your own bills.

Invented prices: gas $1.60 per therm; oil $4.20 per gallon; propane $3.50 per gallon; electricity $0.20 per kWh.

Gas furnace, 95%

  1. 1,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = 10 therms per MMBtu.
  2. 10 × $1.60 = $16.00 fuel cost.
  3. $16.00 ÷ 0.95 = $16.84 delivered.

Oil boiler, 85%

  1. 1,000,000 ÷ 138,500 = 7.220 gallons.
  2. 7.220 × $4.20 = $30.32 fuel cost.
  3. $30.32 ÷ 0.85 = $35.68 delivered (carrying the unrounded numbers).

Propane furnace, 95%

  1. 1,000,000 ÷ 91,452 = 10.935 gallons.
  2. 10.935 × $3.50 = $38.27 fuel cost.
  3. $38.27 ÷ 0.95 = $40.29 delivered.

Electric resistance, COP 1.0

  1. 1,000,000 ÷ 3,412 = 293.1 kWh.
  2. 293.1 × $0.20 = $58.61.
  3. $58.61 ÷ 1.0 = $58.61 delivered.

Heat pump, seasonal COP 2.5

  1. The electricity cost per MMBtu is the same $58.61.
  2. $58.61 ÷ 2.5 = $23.45 delivered.

Ranking, cheapest to most expensive: gas $16.84, heat pump $23.45, oil $35.68, propane $40.29, resistance $58.61.

One more useful number: the break-even COP against a fuel is the electric resistance cost divided by that fuel's delivered cost. Against gas it is 58.61 ÷ 16.84 = about 3.5, so with these prices a heat pump only matches gas if its COP averages 3.5. Against oil it is 58.61 ÷ 35.68 = about 1.6. With a different gas price or electric rate the answer changes, which is why you rerun the math with your own bills.

Step 4: Find current New Jersey prices

  • Oil and propane: EIA publishes a weekly Heating Oil and Propane Update with residential prices by state, including New Jersey. EIA collects these prices during the heating season, October through March.
  • Gas and electricity: Use the rates on your own utility bill, or the utility information pages of the NJ Board of Public Utilities (BPU). Our read of the BPU home page did not confirm a rate-comparison tool there, so ask your utility if in doubt.
  • Use the all-in price: Bills often split supply from delivery charges; add them to get one price per unit.

What the number leaves out

EIA also lists installation, operation and maintenance costs, tax credits and rebates, and fuel availability as factors besides price per MMBtu. The delivered cost compares fuel only. Equipment price, duct work and electrical service belong in a separate quote.

Field card: Comparing Heating Fuel Costs per Million BTU

Price ÷ Btu per unit × 1,000,000, then ÷ efficiency.

  • Energy content (EIA): therm 100,000; oil gal 138,500; propane gal 91,452; kWh 3,412 Btu.
  • 1 MMBtu = 10 therms = 7.22 gal oil = 10.9 gal propane = 293 kWh.
