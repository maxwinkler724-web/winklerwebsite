Packaged Ice Plants and Bagged Ice Storage: Freezer Loads and a Worked Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Bagged ice looks simple, but it is a food made in a plant, packed in a cold room and stored in a freezer. For the refrigeration tech that means three jobs in one building: ice making, a bagging room that fights condensation, and a storage freezer that has to stay cold all day. This lesson walks through the equipment, the loads, the sanitation rules and one worked freezing-load example.

The chain from water to pallet

This is a general overview of how packaged ice plants are laid out. Plants vary a lot. Ice makers drop or push ice into a bin or onto a conveyor. A bagging machine fills and closes the bags, and the filled bags move into a cold storage room or into freezer merchandisers at stores, where they are held until they are sold or loaded onto a truck. Each stage has its own refrigeration, and each can fail in its own way. How the ice machines themselves freeze and harvest is covered in Ice Machine Freeze and Harvest Cycle Basics and the flake and nugget machine lesson, so we do not repeat that here.

Why bagged ice is held well below 32 F

This is general practice, backed by one source. A cold-storage commodity manual from a refrigerated-warehouse trade association says packaged ice is usually stored at 10 to 25 F, with 20 F as a typical room setpoint for stacked ice. The same manual ties sticking to ice that leaves the maker wet: refreezing it in a cold room right after bagging dries the surface so pieces do not freeze together. Ice held near 32 F tends to melt a little on the surface and then refreeze into a block, so keeping the room well below freezing is how plants keep bags loose. The manual also warns that very cold storage (below 0 F) increases sublimation, which is ice turning to vapor, and that slight frost can form inside bags after about three months. Cold is good, but colder is not always better.

The same manual notes that stacked ice can lean toward the room entrance after several weeks, which it blames on warm air coming in at the door. Door habits matter for the load too, as the next section shows.

What loads a storage freezer

A freezer room load is a sum of parts. The walk-in load lesson, Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Box Load Calculation, shows the full method. For packaged ice the parts are:

  • Product. Ice entering the room is usually near freezing or slightly colder than the setpoint. A university extension chapter on refrigeration load writes product heat as mass times specific heat times temperature change. For ice, that sensible heat is the only product term, because it is already frozen.
  • Walls, ceiling and floor. Heat leaks through insulation in proportion to the temperature difference across it.
  • Doors. The same extension chapter adds an allowance for door openings that grows with how often and how long doors stay open. A forklift in and out of a freezer all day brings in warm, moist air.
  • Lights, fans and equipment. The extension chapter notes that nearly all electrical power ends up as heat in the room. Light and fan motor watts count as load.
  • Defrost. Moisture that comes in with warm air freezes on the evaporator as frost. Defrost removes it but adds heat that the system then has to pull back out. The refrigerated warehouse lesson covers defrost and dock doors in more depth.

Floor heave at a general level

A freezer that runs below freezing for years can freeze the soil under its floor. A national building research digest explains that frost heave needs freezing temperatures, fine-grained soil and a water supply; water moves toward the freezing zone and forms ice lenses that lift the floor and structure. For continuously operated cold stores the digest describes heat under the floor, from warm-air ducts or electric cables, as probably the most satisfactory remedy, with below-grade insulation or replacement of the soil as other measures. As a tech you should know why floor heaters exist: do not shut them off or ignore alarms on them without a plan.

The bagging room and condensation

Bagging rooms sit between warm, humid outdoor or plant air and cold ice. A state university extension fact sheet on food facilities explains the basics: water vapor condenses on any surface at or below the air's dew point, and air leaving a chilled space can already be above 90 percent relative humidity, so it condenses on slightly cooler surfaces such as freezer doors. The same fact sheet says condensation near food or packaging can support bacterial growth and lead to drips onto product, and lists control measures: seal openings, keep the space under positive pressure with dry air, dehumidify, keep floors and drains dry, and insulate or warm cold surfaces. Warming surfaces raises energy use. See also the water side of this in Ice Machine Water Quality, Scale and Sanitizing.

Water treatment and sanitation, in general

A federal food agency's page on packaged ice says ice is a food no matter its shape or source, and that packaged ice in interstate commerce is regulated like other foods under its current good manufacturing practice rules. Inspectors look at whether plumbing prevents contamination of the water supply or stored ice, and the page says the water should meet federal drinking-water standards. Small intrastate makers and food service ice are usually handled by state and local authorities, often using a federal model food code. In practice, general water treatment, such as filtration or scale control, protects both the equipment and the product, but the exact program for a plant belongs to the plant owner and its regulators. For a tech the sanitation takeaways are simple: clean hands, clean tools, no tools or parts left in bins, and close up any opening you make in a water line, drain or bin.

Refrigerant handling under EPA 608

Ice plants use commercial refrigeration equipment, so Section 608 of the Clean Air Act applies. The EPA's Section 608 page says it bars intentionally venting ozone-depleting refrigerants and their substitutes, including HFCs, while servicing or disposing of refrigeration equipment. Its venting page allows only narrow cases such as de minimis releases while making good-faith efforts to recapture refrigerant, normal operation emissions, and substitutes EPA has found pose no environmental threat. Recover refrigerant, keep records, and use the right technician certification. See EPA 608 Certification Basics. The EPA page also notes a final rule from October 11, 2024 managing certain HFCs and their substitutes; read the current rule text for your equipment. Always read the nameplate: new equipment may use refrigerants other than the ones you saw last year, and each has its own service and safety rules.

Worked example (our own): freezing load for 20,000 lb of ice per day

This is our own example with assumed inputs. It is not a sizing method and not a real plant.

Assumptions (ours): the plant makes 20,000 lb of ice per day; incoming water is 60 F; ice is cooled to a 10 F storage temperature; the plant refrigeration runs 24 hours a day. Properties: specific heat of liquid water 1.0 Btu/lb-F; latent heat of fusion of ice about 144 Btu/lb; specific heat of ice about 0.5 Btu/lb-F (sources below).

  1. Cool water from 60 F to 32 F: 20,000 lb × 1.0 × (60 − 32) = 20,000 × 28 = 560,000 Btu/day.
  2. Freeze at 32 F: 20,000 lb × 144 Btu/lb = 2,880,000 Btu/day.
  3. Cool ice from 32 F to 10 F: 20,000 lb × 0.5 × (32 − 10) = 20,000 × 11 = 220,000 Btu/day.
  4. Total: 560,000 + 2,880,000 + 220,000 = 3,660,000 Btu/day.
  5. Per hour over 24 hours: 3,660,000 ÷ 24 = 152,500 Btu/h.
  6. In tons: a ton of refrigeration is 12,000 Btu/h, so 152,500 ÷ 12,000 = about 12.7 tons.

If the plant only runs 20 hours a day, the hourly figure goes up: 3,660,000 ÷ 20 = 183,000 Btu/h, or 15.25 tons. The latent step is about 79 percent of the total (2,880,000 ÷ 3,660,000), which is why freezing, not cooling, drives the size. This number leaves out compressor and pump heat, heat from the bagging room, storage freezer wall, door and light loads, and any water that is not frozen. Real sizing adds those.

Field card: Packaged Ice Plants and Bagged Ice Storage

Freezing is the big load; doors and wet air are the daily problems.

  • Stored bagged ice is held well below 32 F; one storage manual gives 10 to 25 F, typically 20 F.
  • Below 0 F increases sublimation; check stock periodically.
  • Freezing 1 lb of water takes about 144 Btu; cooling ice takes about 0.5 Btu/lb-F.
  • One ton of refrigeration is 12,000 Btu/h.
  • Doors, lights and fans add heat to the storage freezer; watch door habits.
  • Condensation forms when a surface is at or below dew point; keep the bagging room air dry.
  • Floor heaters stop frost heave; do not disable them.
  • Ice is a food: clean tools, clean hands, potable water.
  • No venting of refrigerant; recover it and read the nameplate.
