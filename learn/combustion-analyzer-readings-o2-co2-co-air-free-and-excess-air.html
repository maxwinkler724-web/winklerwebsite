Combustion Analyzer Readings: O2, CO2, CO Air-Free and Excess Air

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A combustion analyzer prints a screen full of numbers. Only a few are measured; the rest are math done on those few. This lesson explains what each number tells you, why too little and too much air both cost you, and why the target numbers always come from the appliance maker.

Start with the basics, then the numbers

This page picks up where Combustion and CO Safety Basics stops. That lesson covers fuel, air, venting and what to do when a CO alarm sounds. For the alarm side, see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. For how the burner proves flame, see Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics. Here we only read the analyzer.

What the analyzer actually measures

Most residential analyzers measure oxygen (O2), carbon monoxide (CO) and temperature. Some add a CO2 sensor; many do not. A training deck from TruTech Tools lists O2, CO and temperature as the three basic sensors and says CO2 is calculated unless an extra sensor is added. The UEi C163 manual, by contrast, says its CO2 is measured directly. So check your own meter's manual before you assume a number is measured or calculated.

The readings, one at a time

  • O2. The percent of oxygen left in the flue gas after combustion. Fresh air is about 20.9 percent, and the UEi manual uses that figure as its fresh-air check. An ACHR News article calls O2 by far the most important combustion reading, because it tells you how much extra air went through the appliance.
  • Excess air. The air supplied beyond what the fuel needs to burn completely. Burners need some, but the ACHR article says the goal is the lowest excess air that still keeps CO safe. The UEi manual gives a reference point: natural gas at 3 percent O2 is about 16.7 percent excess air.
  • CO2. Usually calculated from O2, so it mirrors it. The ACHR article gives a theoretical maximum of about 11.8 percent CO2 for natural gas, never reached in practice. Real appliances read lower.
  • CO as measured. The parts per million (ppm) of carbon monoxide in the sample, diluted by whatever extra air is in it.
  • CO air-free (also called undiluted CO, or CO at 0 percent O2). The same CO reading mathematically corrected so the dilution is removed. It is always higher than the as-measured value, and it lets you compare one appliance with another.
  • Stack (flue) temperature. The temperature of the exhaust where the probe sits. Net stack temperature is stack temperature minus the temperature of the combustion air entering the appliance. The UEi manual says that for a sealed appliance with ducted intake you compare against the ducted air, and for room-air appliances against room air.
  • Efficiency. A calculated combustion efficiency from flue gas composition and temperature. UEi says it is not the same as AFUE, and ACHR News says it is not representative of the published AFUE. Net versus gross settings differ in whether the energy in water vapor is counted as recovered, which matters for condensing equipment.

Why O2 and CO2 move opposite ways

Air is the diluent. As you add more excess air, the same amount of CO2 from the fuel is spread into more total gas, so the CO2 percentage falls while the leftover oxygen percentage climbs. That is why a drop in CO2 and a rise in O2 are the same event told two ways. Bacharach also points out a trap: CO2 can read the same number on the air-short side and the air-heavy side of the best setting, so O2 and CO are what tell you which side you are on.

Too little air, too much air

Too little air means the fuel cannot burn completely, so the flame makes CO (see the CO curve rising on the left of the diagram). That is the safety problem. Too much air does not make CO, but it carries heat out of the vent. The Mechanical Hub condensing boiler article puts it this way: efficiency improves as excess air is reduced, but only to the point where CO does not start to form. Tuning a burner is working toward that edge, then staying a safe distance from it.

Where to sample

The sample has to come after the heat exchanger and before any dilution air gets in. TruTech Tools says so directly. The UEi manual says to put the probe tip in the center of the flue, to use the depth-stop cone, and with balanced flues to position it so air cannot back-flush into the probe. An ACHR News article lists the typical spots: the vent connector or stack on draft-induced appliances, the PVC flue pipe on high-efficiency units (with intake air temperature taken at the inlet), and in the stack before the barometric damper on a power burner. For atmospheric appliances, it says to measure in each cell of the heat exchanger. A draft hood or diverter adds room air, so reading downstream of it gives a falsely high O2 and low CO. Where the appliance has a factory test port, the manufacturer's instructions name the location, for example the IBC condensing boiler sheet that refers to the "flue gas test port". When the maker names a spot, use that spot.

Worked example: CO air-free and excess air

A furnace's analyzer shows 60 ppm CO and 8.0 percent O2. The formula in the UEi manual is CO air-free = CO × 20.9 ÷ (20.9 − O2).

  1. Subtract: 20.9 − 8.0 = 12.9.
  2. Divide: 20.9 ÷ 12.9 = 1.62.
  3. Multiply: 60 × 1.62 = 97 ppm CO air-free.

The as-measured reading looked low, but the corrected value is about 97 ppm. Excess air is approximately O2 ÷ (20.9 − O2) × 100:

  1. 8.0 ÷ 12.9 = 0.62.
  2. 0.62 × 100 = about 62 percent excess air.

Now see how dilution hides CO. The same 60 ppm at 12.0 percent O2 gives 20.9 ÷ 8.9 = 2.35, and 60 × 2.35 = 141 ppm air-free. The more extra air in the sample, the bigger the correction. The UEi manual checks the formula the same way: 325 ppm at 5 percent O2 gives 427 ppm air-free.

Where the targets come from

This page gives no pass or fail numbers for your equipment, because the target belongs to the manufacturer. Two examples show why. Rinnai technical bulletin TB-152 gives O2 settings for its M-series condensing boilers, around 4.2 to 4.9 percent on natural gas at maximum input depending on the model, with minimum-input readings higher. It requires the setting be made with a calibrated combustion analyzer. An IBC condensing boiler sheet instead gives CO2 percentages for high and low fire, and forbids adjusting the gas valve without a calibrated analyzer. Two makers, two different measures, two different ranges.

CO limits are separate again. An ACHR News article says the allowable stack limit is 400 ppm air-free and that flue CO should be kept below 100 ppm air-free, and that any CO rising or unstable calls for immediate testing and repair. A SERVICE trade article attributes the 400 ppm figure to ANSI Z21.47 as a certification ceiling, not a target. We did not read the standard itself, so treat that as a pointer. Local rules and the manufacturer set what you must do.

Using temperature and efficiency sensibly

Stack temperature is a clue about heat leaving unused. The UEi manual lists typical stack ranges but says to follow the equipment maker. On a condensing appliance, a flue that runs hot suggests it is not condensing; see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers for why return water decides that. Remember ACHR News also says cutting stack temperature by 50 degrees Fahrenheit gains less than 1 percent in efficiency, so do not chase it. For input checks before you analyze, see Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.

Field card: Combustion Analyzer Readings

Measure O2 and CO, calculate the rest, and read the maker's targets.

  • O2 is the excess-air gauge. As excess air rises, O2 rises and CO2 falls.
  • CO2 is often calculated from O2. Check whether your meter measures it.
  • CO air-free = CO × 20.9 ÷ (20.9 − O2). It is always higher than as-measured CO.
  • Excess air is about O2 ÷ (20.9 − O2) × 100.
  • Net stack temperature = stack temperature minus combustion air temperature.
  • Too little air makes CO. Too much air carries heat up the vent.
  • Sample after the heat exchanger and before dilution air. Use the maker's test port.
  • Targets come from the appliance manual. Rising or unstable CO means stop and find the cause.
