Yard Engines and Gas Cans Near a Furnace or Water Heater: Safe Garage and Basement Habits

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A snowblower or mower leaves the garage dirty and cold, and it goes back next to the furnace or water heater. That small habit mixes two hazards: a running or hot engine makes carbon monoxide and heat, and the fuel in the tank and the can gives off vapor that can reach a gas flame. This lesson covers where engines should run, how to park and fuel them, and how to store them.

Never run the engine in the garage, even with the door open

It is tempting to start a snowblower inside to warm it up, then roll it out. Don't. A federal consumer product safety agency says never to leave a car running in an attached garage, even with the garage door open, and that in such spaces carbon monoxide (CO) can build to lethal levels even with open doors and windows. The same page lists lawn mowers among the gasoline engines that produce CO and says never to operate a gasoline engine-powered tool in or near an enclosed space. A federal emergency management agency fact sheet makes the same point about generators: keep the engine outside, and do not run it in homes, garages or crawl spaces, even in areas with partial ventilation.

The habit: start it, warm it and use it outdoors, away from doors, windows and vents, as the fire administration flyer says for generators.

Let a hot engine cool before you park it

A just-used engine has a hot muffler and exhaust. Parking it beside a gas can, a water heater or stored paint puts a hot surface next to vapor and a flame. The consumer product safety agency says never to refuel power equipment such as mowers while the engine is running or still hot, and the emergency management fact sheet says to turn a generator off and let it cool before refueling. The sources give no cool-down time, so follow the owner manual. Our own habit: leave the machine outdoors until the muffler is no longer too hot to touch, then roll it in.

Why gasoline vapor and gas flames do not mix

A gas water heater or furnace has a burner and, in many older units, a pilot light. The consumer product safety agency says gasoline vapor can be ignited by appliances, static electricity and pilot lights, and it says to keep gasoline outdoors, far from living quarters and ignition sources. A federal workplace safety agency page describes flammable liquid vapors as often heavier than air and able to concentrate in enclosed spaces. Burners and pilots sit low, where heavy vapor tends to collect. The related lesson on flammable vapors and gas appliances covers raised heaters; this lesson focuses on engines and cans.

Fuel cans: approved, small and closed

  • Approved containers only. A national park service fire-prevention article says to use only approved, properly labeled gasoline containers, and to replace cans that are damaged, missing parts or leaking. Glass, food or milk containers and rusted cans are especially hazardous.
  • Small amounts. That agency describes private gasoline storage as usually limited to five gallons or less and says storage is for emergencies only. Local rules may be stricter, and that release is from 1979, so treat it as older guidance.
  • Tightly closed. The agency says all storage cans should be secured and tightly sealed.
  • Outdoors, not in the house. It says never to store gasoline in the house or automobile and to keep cans outdoors, far from living quarters and ignition sources, giving a well-ventilated shed as an example. The emergency management fact sheet adds not to store fuel near a fuel-burning appliance such as a gas stove.
  • Leave room in the can. The park service article says to fill a container only 95 percent full so the fuel can expand as it warms, and to set the can on the ground when filling it.

Why not store fueled equipment in a basement with gas appliances

A basement has little air movement, low floors where heavy vapor can settle, and a burner close by. The advice above is to keep gasoline out of the house and away from fuel-burning appliances, and a fueled mower is a can of gasoline with an engine attached. Our own judgment: keep fueled equipment out of a basement with gas appliances, and if it must stay in an attached garage, keep it and any cans well away from the appliance.

Refuel outdoors on a cold engine

Do it in the open air, with the engine off and cool. The consumer product safety agency warns against refueling mowers and other power equipment when running or still hot. The fire administration flyer warns that spilling gas on a hot engine can cause a fire. If you spill, wipe it up and let the vapor clear before bringing the machine in. The consumer product safety agency also says to change clothes at once if gasoline gets on them.

CO alarms near the door into the house

The consumer product safety agency says to put battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and to avoid spots near heating vents or where furniture or drapes can cover them. The fact sheet says to follow the alarm maker's placement instructions. Our own suggestion: if the garage door opens into a hallway, an alarm there (per the maker's rules) warns sooner if exhaust gets in. Our own note: a CO alarm is not a gasoline vapor detector. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do, and attached garages and your heating and cooling for how fumes reach living space.

End-of-season storage basics

Start with the owner manual. A university extension article says to refer to it first. It offers two options: drain the fuel into an approved container, or add a fuel stabilizer so the fuel does not degrade over several months. A second extension article calls draining the tank and running the carburetor empty the best way to avoid restart problems. Which your machine wants depends on its manual. Keep leftover fuel in an approved container in a well-ventilated place away from heat and sparks.

Our own worked example: how much air does one cup of spill make flammable?

This is our own illustration with stated assumptions, not a measurement or a safety limit. Real vapor mixes unevenly.

Assumptions (our own): one cup (8 fluid ounces) of gasoline spills and fully evaporates; gasoline weighs 0.74 times as much as water (the federal public health agency's chemical hazards guide lists a specific gravity of 0.72 to 0.76); vapor averages about 100 grams per mole (our own round number; real gasoline varies); one mole of vapor takes up 24.1 liters at about 70°F; the lower flammable limit is 1.4 percent vapor in air (same guide); the garage floor is 20 ft by 22 ft with an 8 ft ceiling.

  1. Volume of liquid: 8 fl oz × 29.57 mL per fl oz = 236.6 mL.
  2. Mass: 236.6 mL × 0.74 g/mL = 175 g.
  3. Moles of vapor: 175 g ÷ 100 g/mol = 1.75 mol.
  4. Vapor volume: 1.75 mol × 24.1 L/mol = 42.2 L, and 42.2 ÷ 28.32 L per cubic foot = 1.49 ft³.
  5. Air-vapor mix at the lower flammable limit: 1.49 ft³ ÷ 0.014 = 106 ft³.
  6. Floor area: 20 × 22 = 440 ft². A 106 ft³ blanket spread over it is 106 ÷ 440 = 0.24 ft, about 2.9 inches deep.

Check: 0.24 ft × 440 ft² = 105.6 ft³, matching 106. For the whole room, 20 × 22 × 8 = 3,520 ft³; at 1.4 percent that needs 3,520 × 0.014 = 49.3 ft³ of vapor, or 49.3 ÷ 1.49 = 33 cups, about 2 gallons. So by our math one cup does not fill a whole garage with flammable mix. It can make a flammable blanket a few inches deep, though, and that low zone is where a water heater burner or pilot may sit. The 1979 agency release says less than half a pint (one cup) can fill a garage with explosive vapors and shows no math; ours measures a flammable layer, not a whole room. Treat both as reasons for caution, not as limits.

Field card: Yard Engines and Gas Cans Near Gas Appliances

Run it outside, cool it down, fuel it outside, keep cans away from flames.

  • Never start or warm up an engine in a garage, even with the door open.
  • Let a hot engine cool before parking it; follow the owner manual.
  • Keep equipment and gas cans away from burners and pilot lights; vapors are heavier than air.
  • Do not store fueled equipment in a basement with gas appliances.
  • Refuel outdoors with the engine off and cold; use approved, labeled, tightly closed cans in small amounts.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; consider the hall by the garage door.
  • End of season: drain, run dry or stabilize as the owner manual says.
