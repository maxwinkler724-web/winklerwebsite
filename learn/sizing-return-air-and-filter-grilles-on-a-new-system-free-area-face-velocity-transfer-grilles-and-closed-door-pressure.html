Sizing Return Air and Filter Grilles on a New System

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The supply side gets the attention, but the return side decides whether a new system is quiet, whether the filter does its job and whether the bedrooms feel stuffy with the doors shut. This lesson shows how to size a return grille and a filter grille from free area, why one small central return causes trouble, and how to confirm the result with a static pressure reading.

Free area is not the grille size

A grille is mostly bars and blades. The number on the box, such as 24 by 36, is the nominal face size. Air only passes through the open gaps, and the open part is called the free area. A national-lab retrofit guide for building scientists says it directly: the grille free area "is not the grille size." Free area as a share of the face varies by model, so we use the number printed in the maker's data and never a guess. In the worked example below, the ratio is an assumption we state out loud.

The same guide gives a rule of thumb for transfer openings: at least 1 square inch of grille free area for each cubic foot per minute (CFM) of supply air to the room. It also asks for a clear return path from every room with a supply outlet, except bathrooms and kitchens, because of odor spread.

Face velocity: what the sources give

Face velocity is airflow divided by free area, in feet per minute (fpm). Here are the numbers we could source:

  • A national-lab residential air distribution guideline sets a target for return duct velocity of under 500 fpm, return capacity of twice the total supply volume, and a return grille whose net free area is 1.5 times the cross-sectional area of the return duct (section 4.3). It also calls a low-resistance return path one with less than 0.05 inches of water column (in. w.c.) pressure loss.
  • A building science guide on higher-efficiency filters calls 500 fpm the maximum desired velocity across a residential filter, with 200 to 400 fpm preferred and 300 fpm used in its examples.

The guidelines do not set a separate grille face velocity for quiet operation or for filter grilles. For noise targets and why speed makes sound, see Noisy Ductwork at Home.

Filter area and pressure drop

The filter is part of the return path. The building science guide says the filter typically accounts for 20% to 50% of the total system pressure drop. It lists example readings, each for one product: a 4-inch MERV 12 filter at 0.2 in. w.c. at 300 fpm and 0.35 in. w.c. at 500 fpm, and 1-inch, 2-inch and 4-inch MERV 7 filters at 0.20, 0.13 and 0.12 in. w.c. at 300 fpm. It adds that a 4-inch-deep pleat has twice the surface area of a 2-inch pleat, and deeper or more numerous pleats tend to give lower pressure drop at the same MERV rating. Read those as examples, not as a rule for every filter. Use the pressure-drop data for the actual filter at your airflow. Filter fit and gaps are covered in Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption for teaching, not a design rule.

  • A 3-ton system moves 1,200 CFM (we assume 400 CFM per ton).
  • Target return duct velocity: 500 fpm, the national-lab upper limit.
  • Grille free area: 1.5 times the duct area, per the same guideline.
  • Assumed free-area ratio: 60% of the nominal face (check the real number in the maker's data).
  • Assumed filter face velocity: 300 fpm.

Step 1: duct area. 1,200 CFM / 500 fpm = 2.4 square feet = 2.4 x 144 = 346 square inches.

Step 2: free area needed. 346 x 1.5 = 518 square inches = 3.6 square feet. Check: 1,200 / 3.6 = 333 fpm through the open gaps, our assumed target face velocity.

Step 3: nominal size. 518 / 0.60 = 864 square inches of face. A 36 by 24 grille is exactly 864. Two 24 by 18 grilles (432 each) also work.

Step 4: the undersized case. A single 20 by 20 grille has 400 x 0.60 = 240 square inches = 1.67 square feet of free area. Then 1,200 / 1.67 = 720 fpm, well above the 500 fpm guideline, and a likely source of hiss and extra pressure drop.

Step 5: filter grille. At 300 fpm, filter area = 1,200 / 300 = 4.0 square feet = 576 square inches. Actual filters run a bit smaller than nominal, so we want margin. Two 20 by 25 nominal filter grilles give 1,000 square inches nominal, or about 173 fpm on nominal area. Check the bars too: 1,000 x 0.60 = 600 square inches of free area = 4.17 square feet, so 1,200 / 4.17 = 288 fpm, and 600 is above the 518 needed. Both checks pass. A pair of 16 by 25 grilles (800 nominal) would pass the filter test but fail the bar test (480 free, under 518). Whichever needs more area wins.

Why one small central return pressurizes bedrooms

When the door is closed, the supply air entering a bedroom has to leave by cracks or a return opening. If it cannot, the room pressure rises until air squeezes out. A university solar-energy center field study found closed rooms averaged +6.2 pascals (Pa) against outdoors, with the central zone averaging -2.9 Pa and 64 of 70 homes negative in the central zone. Some rooms reached +37 Pa. It also reported that higher room pressure raises supply duct pressure, so duct leakage can increase, and that house air leakage rose when interior doors were closed with the air handler running. The effect is also on the supply side: the pressurized room resists supply air, and the room gets less than designed. That is the reason for the point in Register Booster Fans and Smart Vents: weak rooms are usually a duct problem. The pressure target in the national-lab guides, drawn from a federal program for new homes, is within plus or minus 3 Pa (0.012 in. w.c.) of the main house with bedroom doors closed and the blower on its highest design speed, with plus or minus 5 Pa (0.020 in. w.c.) acceptable for rooms of 150 CFM or more.

Transfer grilles, jump ducts and door undercuts

The national-lab guides say typical door undercuts of 1/2 to 3/4 inch do not give adequate return volume, especially over carpet, and that a widely used residential duct design procedure does not approve them. Better paths:

  • Transfer grille: a grille through the wall between the room and the hall. The guide suggests placing the room-side grille high on the wall and the hall-side grille low, or offset or baffled openings to cut sound and light transfer.
  • Jump duct: a short piece of insulated flex duct, typically 10-inch, run above the ceiling from the room to the hall, with a grille at each end.
  • Ducted return from the room: best when the room has a high supply flow.

Our own sizing: a bedroom getting 150 CFM needs at least 150 square inches of free area at each opening by the 1-square-inch-per-CFM rule. At an assumed 60% free-area ratio, that is 150 / 0.60 = 250 square inches of face, and a 14 by 18 grille (252) fits. The field study measured transfer openings at 2.5 Pa and found an open hole needed 54 square inches per 100 CFM, grilles on both sides needed 79 to 103 per 100 CFM, which for 150 CFM means about 119 to 155 square inches. That agrees with the rule of thumb.

Check total external static after install

  1. Run the blower at the speed it will use for cooling, with a clean filter and every grille open.
  2. Measure supply plenum static and return static (before the blower) with a manometer, each against the room. The building science guide describes adding the supply and return readings.
  3. Add the two readings, ignoring the sign. Our illustration: +0.25 and -0.20 in. w.c. total 0.45 in. w.c.
  4. Compare the total with the external static rating on the equipment data plate. The building science guide says total system static should be no higher than about 0.4 to 0.5 in. w.c. for the common motor types it discusses, with the more efficient type running up to about 1 in. w.c.
  5. Then close the bedroom doors with the blower on high and measure pressure across each door with a manometer, aiming for the plus or minus 3 Pa target above.

If the number is high, look at the return first: grille free area, filter pressure drop and any kinked or crushed duct. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics and Duct Sizing Basics. Sizing the equipment itself starts with the load; see Outdoor Design Temperatures for New HVAC Systems.

Field card: Sizing Return Air and Filter Grilles

Size by free area, not by the number on the box.

  • Face velocity = CFM / free area in square feet.
  • Return duct under 500 fpm; grille free area about 1.5 times duct area (national-lab guideline).
  • Filter face velocity: 500 fpm max, 200 to 400 preferred (building science guide).
  • Filter is typically 20% to 50% of system pressure drop.
  • Transfer opening: at least 1 square inch free area per CFM supplied to the room.
  • Door undercuts alone are not enough.
  • Closed-door target: within plus or minus 3 Pa (0.012 in. w.c.).
  • Total static: add supply and return readings and compare with the data plate.
