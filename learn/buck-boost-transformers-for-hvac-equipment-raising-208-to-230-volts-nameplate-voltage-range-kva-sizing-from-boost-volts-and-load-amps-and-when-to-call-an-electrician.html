Buck-Boost Transformers for HVAC Equipment: Raising 208 V Toward 230 V

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A condenser or blower motor that is rated for 230 volts but sits on a 208 volt supply can run hot, draw extra current and trip on overload. One fix people reach for is a buck-boost transformer, a small transformer that adds a few volts to the supply or takes a few off. This lesson explains how it works, how to size it, and the point where the job belongs to the utility or a licensed electrician.

Check first: this is general education

Read this before touching anything. A licensed electrician and your local code govern any install that adds or changes a power circuit. This page explains the idea so you can read a nameplate, do the arithmetic and ask good questions. It is not an installation guide. A federal worker safety agency page on electrical work practices says live parts should be turned off before work, each disconnect locked out, and the circuit tested with proper test equipment to confirm it is dead, including checks for leftover or backfed voltage. Follow those habits every time.

What a buck-boost transformer is

It is usually a small two-winding isolation-type transformer with a primary rated for the line voltage and a low-voltage secondary, often a few tens of volts. Instead of using it to feed a separate circuit, you wire the two windings in series so the secondary adds to the line voltage (boost) or subtracts from it (buck). Wired this way it is an autotransformer: the windings share the current path, so the output is no longer electrically isolated from the input. A university lecture example on autotransformers shows that the load voltage is the source voltage times one plus the turns ratio, and that the winding power can be a small fraction of the load power. That is why a small unit can correct a large load.

The wiring diagrams are printed on the transformer, and the connection depends on whether you want to boost or buck and on the input and output voltages. Our own advice: copy the printed diagram, never one from memory.

Read the nameplate range first

Motor and equipment nameplates list a rated voltage, and some list a range or two ratings. A federal energy agency tip sheet on motors says motors are designed to operate within plus or minus 10 percent of the nameplate rated voltage. For a 230 volt rating, our own arithmetic puts the bottom of that band at 230 x 0.90 = 207 volts. A 208 volt supply is right at the edge before you add any voltage drop in the wiring or a sagging utility feed. Some condensing units carry a dual nameplate that already covers 208 and 230 volts. If yours does, you may not need a transformer at all. Measure the voltage at the unit, with it running, and compare it with the nameplate range before you decide anything.

Why a few percent low voltage costs you

The same federal tip sheet gives typical numbers for a general-purpose induction motor at 90 percent of nameplate voltage: full-load current rises about 5 to 10 percent, full-load efficiency falls about 1 to 3 percent, and starting and maximum running torque fall about 19 percent because torque follows the square of the voltage. The extra current raises winding heating, which can cause overheating at rated load. The sheet also notes that at 110 percent of nameplate, full-load current falls but starting current rises, so too high is not a free fix either. Our reading: a motor that has less voltage pulls harder on the wiring, runs hotter and stalls more easily, which is why compressors and fan motors on weak supplies fail early or trip their protection.

Bucking high voltage down

The same transformer, wired the other way, subtracts its secondary from the line. If measured voltage is high at the equipment and above the nameplate range, bucking it down can bring it back. The first move is still to ask why the voltage is high. Measure at different times of day, and if the reading stays outside the nameplate range, call the utility, because it can adjust the service side and a transformer on your side only hides the problem.

Sizing: kVA from boost volts and load amps

Because the windings carry only part of the power, the transformer is rated on the boost or buck voltage times the load current, not on the full load. Our own working formula for single-phase:

  • Buck-boost kVA needed = boost volts x load amps / 1000.
  • Load kVA = output volts x load amps / 1000, for comparison.
  • Input amps = load kVA x 1000 / input volts. This is the current the supply, the wiring and the overcurrent device must carry.

Pick a unit whose kVA rating is at or above your result, and confirm that its listed voltages and current ratings match the diagram you plan to use. Rating tables for these units vary, so use the one printed on the transformer or in the maker instructions.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Assumptions, all our own and not from any product: a single-phase 230 volt rated condensing unit; supply measures 208 volts; we boost by 24 volts, so output = 208 + 24 = 232 volts; the unit draws 22 amps at that voltage.

  1. Boost voltage x load amps = 24 x 22 = 528 volt-amps. Divide by 1000: 0.528 kVA. That is what the buck-boost transformer must handle.
  2. Load kVA = 232 x 22 = 5,104 volt-amps, or 5.104 kVA.
  3. Ratio: 0.528 / 5.104 = 0.103, about 10 percent. The transformer handles roughly one-tenth of the load power. Check: 24 / 232 = 0.103, the same answer.
  4. Input amps = 5,104 / 208 = 24.5 amps. The supply side carries more current than the 22 amp load, because the same power now comes in at a lower voltage. Wiring and overcurrent protection on the input side must be checked against that higher figure, which is a licensed electrician's call.
  5. Voltage check: 232 is about 0.9 percent above 230, and well inside the plus or minus 10 percent band. Without the boost, 208 is 9.6 percent below 230: (230 - 208) / 230 = 0.096.

In this example the unit is a stated assumption, so your numbers will differ. Redo the arithmetic with the real nameplate, the measured voltage and a measured running current.

Overcurrent protection, in general terms

A transformer needs overcurrent protection on the input side, and the conductors have to carry the higher input current. Which device, what rating and where it goes are set by the transformer maker's instructions and by local code, and they must also stay compatible with the equipment's own nameplate maximum overcurrent rating. Do not size a breaker from this page. Have the electrician decide, and do not exceed the equipment nameplate.

When to call the utility or an electrician instead

  • Voltage is out of range at the meter or the service entrance, or swings by time of day: call the utility.
  • One leg reads high and another low, or lights flicker when a load starts: suspect a service problem, not an equipment problem. See the lost-neutral lesson linked below.
  • The equipment is three-phase: buck-boost connections for three phase differ and need an electrician to engineer.
  • The fix needs new conductors, a new disconnect or a new breaker: that is electrical work for a licensed electrician under local code.
  • Long wire runs are the real cause. Fixing voltage drop with larger wire is often better than adding a transformer.

Control-circuit transformers are a different job: see the related lesson on 208 versus 240 volt primary taps for 24 volt control power.

Field card: Buck-Boost Transformers for HVAC Equipment

Measure first, size on the boost volts, and let an electrician decide the wiring.

  • Motors are designed for plus or minus 10 percent of nameplate voltage (federal tip sheet).
  • At 90 percent voltage, full-load current rises about 5 to 10 percent and heating goes up.
  • Buck-boost kVA = boost volts x load amps / 1000.
  • Our example: 24 V x 22 A = 0.528 kVA, about 10 percent of the 5.104 kVA load.
  • Input amps are higher than load amps when boosting.
  • Lock out, test, then work. Local code and a licensed electrician govern the install.
