Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Approach temperature is a simple subtraction that tells you how well heat is crossing from one fluid to another. On a chiller, it is one of the best early-warning numbers on the log sheet.

What approach means

Approach is the gap between two temperatures that would be equal if heat transfer were perfect. If the gap is small, heat is moving easily. If the gap grows at the same load, something is getting in the way: a film on the tubes, too little water flow, or something in the refrigerant circuit that should not be there.

The two chiller approaches

  • Evaporator approach is the leaving chilled-water temperature minus the refrigerant saturation temperature in the evaporator.
  • Condenser approach is the refrigerant saturation (condensing) temperature in the condenser minus the leaving condenser-water temperature, the definition used across the chiller lessons on this site. Some maintenance guides subtract the entering water temperature instead, so check your machine’s documentation and use the same one every day.

The saturation temperatures come from the refrigerant pressure readings, converted with the right pressure-temperature chart for that refrigerant. If that conversion is new to you, see Pressure-Temperature Basics.

Why the trend beats the number

One maintenance reference calls the evaporator approach the most predictive reading on the daily log and suggests that a sustained rise above baseline may point to tube fouling, low refrigerant charge or non-condensables. For the condenser, a rising approach suggests condenser fouling or non-condensables. The same page gives example numbers, including a normal evaporator approach under about 1.5 F and a flag above 3 F, and an HVAC School article says approach values of 0 to 3 F with correct flow indicate good heat transfer. These are labeled examples from those pages. Your machine, refrigerant and design decide what is normal for you, so the best baseline is your own record from a clean, properly running machine.

Compare like with like

Approach changes with load. A machine at light load will often show a smaller approach than the same machine at full load. So record load or percent capacity beside every approach, and compare against days with similar load and similar outdoor conditions. A reading that looks bad at one load can look fine at another.

Reading the pattern

  • Condenser approach rising slowly over weeks. Suspect tube fouling from scale or biofilm, a water treatment problem, or air and other non-condensables in the machine. Check treatment records and tube cleaning history.
  • Evaporator approach rising. Commonly suspect tube fouling, low refrigerant charge, oil in the evaporator, or poor water flow. Check flow and water treatment before blaming the machine.
  • Both normal but the plant still runs poorly. Look at the system side: see Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained.
  • Sudden jump. Check for a bad sensor or a gauge error first, then for a real change such as a stopped pump or a closed valve.

Do not skip flow

Low water flow makes a clean machine look fouled. Before you decide the tubes are dirty, confirm the pumps and valves are doing their job. Water-side fouling is a service decision: cleaning, refrigerant work and tube inspection belong to qualified people following the manufacturer’s procedure and the rules where you work.

Good logging habits

Use a calibrated gauge or the machine’s own display consistently. Write the units. If two instruments for the same measurement disagree, check them against a handheld meter before trusting either. Keep the log somewhere you can see two weeks at once.

Printable checklist

  • Note load and outdoor conditions beside every approach reading
  • Convert refrigerant pressure to saturation temperature with the correct chart
  • Evaporator approach: leaving chilled water minus evaporator saturation temperature
  • Condenser approach: condenser saturation temperature minus leaving condenser-water temperature (confirm against your machine’s documentation)
  • Compare only with days of similar load
  • Confirm water flow before blaming fouling
  • Check suspect instruments against a handheld meter
  • Report a multi-day upward trend to your lead
