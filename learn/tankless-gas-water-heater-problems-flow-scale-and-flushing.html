Tankless Gas Water Heater Problems: Flow, Scale and Flushing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a tankless water heater is not working, the cause is often one of four things: not enough water flow to start it, mineral scale in the heat exchanger, a clogged screen, or a gas or vent problem. This lesson starts with what a homeowner can safely check, then covers the parts that belong to a licensed technician.

Start here: safe homeowner checks

Your unit's own manual rules over this page. Brands differ, and the makers say so. If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility from outside. Do not troubleshoot a gas smell yourself. See Water Heater No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks for the same safety steps on tank units, and Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom if you see water at the unit.

  • Is anything else wrong at the fixture? Try another faucet. If only one fixture is cold, the problem may be that fixture, not the heater.
  • Is the display showing a code? Write it down. Codes are specific to the maker and often the model, so look it up in your own manual and do not trust a code list from a different brand.
  • Are the hot and cold lines reversed on a new install? Rinnai's user guide lists this as a check when a new unit will not fire.

The minimum flow rate: why a trickle stays cold

A tankless heater only fires when enough water moves through it. The flow has to pass a minimum before the burner starts. Navien lists a minimum flow rate of 0.5 gallons per minute (GPM) for its NPE-2 series. A Rinnai spec sheet for its RUR98i model lists a minimum activation flow of 0.4 GPM in the copy we read, so confirm the figure on your own model's sheet. Those two numbers are for those models only, so find yours in your own spec sheet. Rinnai's user guide for another series gives no number, but tells you to verify you have at least the minimum flow rate to fire the unit.

In practice this means a faucet opened just a crack, a low-flow aerator clogged with grit, or a dribbling shower head may never start the burner. The water you feel stays cold.

The inlet filter screen

Most tankless units have a small screen where cold water enters. Debris after plumbing work or a water main event can clog it, and flow drops below the starting point. Rinnai's guide says to clean the inlet water supply filter when the unit does not respond. Navien's quick installation guide tells installers to stop using the heater after the first ten minutes and clean the cold water filter and the recirculation filter of trapped debris. Navien's maintenance page describes the cleaning as removing the filter, rinsing it under cold running water and brushing it if needed, after draining the unit. It also warns that damage during do-it-yourself maintenance may void the warranty, so follow your manual and ask your installer if unsure.

The cold-water sandwich

The "cold-water sandwich" is a short burst of cold water between two hot ones. You run hot water, shut it off, and run it again shortly after, and a cold stretch shows up between the two hot ones. Navien's trade press release uses the term and says its ComfortFlow recirculation feature helps eliminate it. If your unit has a recirculation option, ask the installer whether it fits your home.

Scale: the slow problem

Hard water leaves mineral scale inside the heat exchanger. The result is reduced efficiency and eventually an unhappy unit. Rinnai's user guide says hard water must be treated to prevent scale build-up or damage to the heat exchanger. Noritz says the rate of build-up depends on local water hardness. The makers do not agree on one schedule:

  • Noritz publishes a hardness table. The harder-water rows we could read list flushing once a year, and a footnote says flushing is required if no water treatment device is installed. It also calls for Noritz isolation valves so the unit can be flushed.
  • Rinnai lets the installer set a time-based "service soon" reminder at half a year, one year or two years, and points to its flushing section. One Rinnai service manual says to flush the heat exchanger when hot spots suggest scale.
  • Navien recommends annual maintenance that includes flushing the heat exchanger with a pump and cleaning solution, and says the frequency varies with the installation and use. It suggests an anti-scale or softener system to limit buildup.

Noritz describes its flush as a pump circulating 3 to 4 gallons of distilled white vinegar through the heat exchanger for at least an hour, then a cold-water rinse of at least five minutes or about 20 gallons. It says the gas valve stays off during the flush, the unit stays powered, and it expects error code 11 to appear during the procedure. Codes shown during a procedure are the maker's own. The takeaway is that a flush is a maker-specific procedure. Noritz itself says to bring in a plumbing contractor if you hesitate.

Error codes belong to the maker

There is no universal tankless code list. For example, Rinnai's guide lists codes for an air supply or exhaust blockage and for no ignition, while Navien tells you to press the Back button to reset after an error and refer to its manual if the message returns. When a code keeps coming back, the code table in your manual is the right place to start.

Worked example: temperature rise versus GPM

A tankless heater has a fixed amount of heat to add to water. The more the water has to be heated, the less water per minute it can handle. The "temperature rise" is the outlet setting minus the incoming water temperature. Manufacturers publish a chart of the maximum flow at each rise. Here is the Navien NPE-150S2 row from the NPE-2 quick facts sheet.

Step 1: find the rise. The Department of Energy's sizing page assumes 50°F incoming water if you do not know yours, and a 120°F output. Rise = 120 − 50 = 70°F.

Step 2: add up the demand. DOE's example is a 0.75 GPM faucet plus a 2.5 GPM shower head. 0.75 + 2.5 = 3.25 GPM.

Step 3: read the chart. Navien's NPE-150S2 shows 3.4 GPM at a 70°F rise. 3.4 is more than 3.25, so it just meets the demand, with 0.15 GPM to spare.

Step 4: try a cold winter. If the incoming water is 40°F, the rise is 120 − 40 = 80°F. The chart gives 3.0 GPM. 3.0 is less than 3.25, a shortfall of 0.25 GPM, or about 8 percent. In real life that shows up as water that is a bit cooler than you set, when the shower and faucet run together. The same chart gives 4.2 GPM at 70°F rise for the larger NPE-180A2 and 3.7 GPM at 80°F, so that unit would cover both cases. Every model and brand has its own chart, so use the one for your unit.

Tech section: gas, venting and combustion air

These items are for licensed technicians and installers. A homeowner should not adjust them.

Gas supply. A tankless burner needs a large volume of gas when it fires. Navien's NPE-2 spec sheet lists natural gas supply pressure of 3.5 to 10.5 inches water column and says the design is compatible with 1/2 inch gas pipe up to 24 feet equivalent length. Rinnai's service manual for a REU-V3237 model gives an inlet test reading of 6 to 10.5 inches on natural gas with all gas appliances running at maximum rate, and says low pressure means inadequate supply. That manual also says to confirm the gas line, meter and regulator are sized properly. Navien's quick installation guide says the water heater is recommended to be the first appliance on the supply line and that it cannot operate properly without enough inlet gas pressure and volume. For the sizing method itself, see Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method.

Venting and combustion air. The Navien NPE-2 sheet lists a forced-draft direct-vent design that can use 2 or 3 inch PVC, CPVC, approved polypropylene or stainless steel. Rinnai's spec sheet for the RUR98i lists indoor direct vent with maker-approved systems, and says allowed lengths are in the installation manual. Rinnai's guide ties its blockage code to checking vent length, blocked combustion air or exhaust, approved materials and a full condensate trap. Use only the vent materials the manual approves, and always follow the vent chapter of the unit's manual. The vent categories behind these rules are explained in Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained. A blocked vent can also be a carbon monoxide risk, so a technician checks the vent and the combustion air before cleaning anything else.

Field card: Tankless Gas Water Heater Problems

No hot water? Flow, filter, scale, then gas and vent.

  • Gas smell: leave and call the utility. Do not troubleshoot it.
  • Check flow first: the unit needs a minimum flow to fire (Navien NPE-2 lists 0.5 GPM).
  • Clean the inlet screen as the maker's manual describes.
  • Rise = outlet setting − incoming temperature. More rise means less flow. Use your unit's own chart.
  • Flush interval varies by maker and water hardness. Follow your manual.
  • Error codes are maker-specific. Look yours up.
  • Gas pipe size and vent work are technician items.
