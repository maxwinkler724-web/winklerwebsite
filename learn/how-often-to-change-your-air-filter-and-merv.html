How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A clean air filter is the cheapest thing you can do for your heating and cooling. The hard part is knowing how often, and which one to buy.

The short answer

Check your filter once a month, as ENERGY STAR recommends, and replace it when it looks gray or clogged or when the filter maker says to. Heavy use, dust, or pets mean sooner. MERV is a rating of how well a filter catches particles. A higher number catches smaller particles but can make the system work harder to pull air through it.

How often to change it

There is no single schedule for every house, so the habit that works is the monthly check. ENERGY STAR says to check the filter in your furnace, air conditioner, or heat pump once a month and clean or replace it as needed. The Department of Energy says to clean or replace it every month or two during the cooling season, and more often if the system runs constantly, the air is dusty, or you have fur-bearing pets. A Lennox page on frozen heat pumps suggests replacing the filter every 1 to 3 months, and the PNNL Building America guide says to replace the filter every 3 to 6 months. The EPA does not give an interval and says to follow the filter maker's replacement guidance. Check the filter monthly, since DOE and ENERGY STAR advise it. Replacing every 1 to 3 months is a common baseline, and a clean home with a thick filter may stretch toward 3 to 6 months. Thicker media filters generally last longer between changes, so follow the label or instructions that came with yours. The simplest test is to hold the filter up to a light. If it looks gray, dusty, or you cannot see light through it, change it.

MERV in plain English

MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. It is a scale that shows how well a filter removes particles from the air. Higher numbers remove more of the smaller particles. The EPA says that if you upgrade, choose at least MERV 13 or the highest rating your system can handle, and to ask an HVAC professional. By its numbers a MERV 13 filter averages at least 50% capture for particles of 0.3 to 1.0 micron, 85% for 1 to 3 microns and 90% for 3 to 10 microns. PNNL groups the ratings roughly this way: MERV 1 to 4 catch lint, 5 to 8 dust and pet dander, 9 to 12 auto emissions, and 13 to 16 smoke and fine bacteria. The trade-off is airflow: a very dense filter clogs faster and can restrict airflow. PNNL says to check with an HVAC company before moving from a MERV 1 filter up to MERV 13 or higher. The EPA adds that most furnaces can handle MERV 13 if the filter is changed often, but systems rarely say which filters they accept.

A clogged filter restricts airflow and can cause weak airflow at the vents, short cycling, overheating, or a frozen coil. If a filter swap does not help, ask a technician what rating and thickness your system allows. Do not stack two filters.

Things you can safely check

  • Find the filter. It is usually in a slot near the furnace or air handler, or behind a return grille. Turn the thermostat off first.
  • Read the size printed on the old filter frame and write it down. If there is none, measure the slot. The printed nominal size can differ from the real dimensions, and PNNL says to use the thickest filter the slot takes.
  • Check the arrow. It should point toward the blower, in the direction air flows.
  • Make sure it fits snugly with no gaps or bending, since air will take the easy path around it.
  • Set a reminder on your phone or write the date on the frame.
  • Look at the manual for the maker's maximum filter rating.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. Filters are one of the few parts that are meant for homeowners to handle.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out.
  • You change the filter and the system still has weak airflow, short cycles, or ices up.
  • You want a higher rating for allergies or smoke and are not sure the system can handle it.
  • You cannot find a filter slot, or the slot has gaps that let air bypass the filter.
  • You smell gas, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, at any time.

Field card: Air Filters and MERV

Change it before it is clogged, and match it to the system.

  • Check monthly. Replace when gray or clogged; sources range from every 1 to 3 months to every 3 to 6 months for a clean home, and sooner if dusty.
  • Upgrading? EPA says MERV 13 if your system can handle it; ask a pro first.
  • Pets, dust, and heavy use mean sooner. Follow the label on your filter.
  • Higher MERV catches smaller particles but resists airflow.
  • Weak flow, short cycling, or ice after a filter swap means ask a pro.
  • Never stack filters or leave the slot empty.
