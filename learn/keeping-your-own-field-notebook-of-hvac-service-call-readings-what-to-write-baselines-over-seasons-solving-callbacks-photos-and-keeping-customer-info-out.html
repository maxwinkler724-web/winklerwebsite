Your Field Notebook: What to Record on Every HVAC Service Call

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The best diagnostic tool in many service vans is a pocket notebook. A reading only means something when you can compare it with another reading from the same machine. This lesson covers what to write down on every call, why old numbers speed up new diagnoses, and what stays out of the book.

Why keep your own notebook

A single set of readings tells you how the system is behaving today. Two sets from the same unit tell you what changed. That change is often the whole diagnosis. Techs who write readings down on every call, not only the problem calls, end up with a baseline for the machines they see again: the amps a compressor normally pulls, the static pressure a duct system normally shows, the temperature drop across a coil in July. When that machine acts up, you are no longer guessing what normal looks like.

Your own notebook is different from your company's service ticket and different from required refrigerant records. The ticket goes to the customer and the office. The notebook is for you, and it can hold the small stuff nobody would put on an invoice. See Writing a Useful HVAC Service Ticket for the customer-facing side.

What to write each time

  • Date and outdoor conditions. Outdoor air temperature, and indoor return temperature. Add humidity if you have a meter. Readings only compare fairly when conditions are close.
  • Model and serial number of each piece of equipment, copied from the data plate. This ties your numbers to one machine.
  • Volts and amps. Line voltage at the unit, and running amps on the compressor, fan motors and blower. Compare the amps with the data plate, and note the voltage you measured them at.
  • Pressures and temperatures. Refrigerant pressures with the matching line temperatures, the supply and return air temperatures, and any water temperatures on hydronic equipment. Note where each probe or gauge was placed.
  • Static pressure. Total external static pressure and the supply and return parts of it, with the blower speed setting. Our airflow and static pressure basics lesson explains the readings.
  • Before and after any repair. Write the readings you took before you touched anything, then take the same set after the repair. The pair shows whether the repair did what you thought it would.
  • Filter condition, thermostat settings and anything odd. A short note such as "filter loaded, return grille blocked by furniture" can explain a number later.

Use the same order and the same measuring spots every time. For a new installation, the startup readings lesson already lists the full sheet, so we do not repeat it here. Your notebook is the same habit applied to every later visit.

Baselines over seasons

One year of notes gives you a cooling-season picture and a heating-season picture for a machine. Several years show slow drift: static creeping up as ducts load with dust, amps rising as a motor or bearing wears, a temperature drop that gets a little smaller each summer. Slow changes are easy to miss when you only see the unit on bad days. They stand out against a written baseline.

Baselines also tell you when not to worry. If a unit has always run at the high end of its amp range, a reading near that end is not news. Without the note you might chase a problem that does not exist.

Using notes to solve a callback

A callback means the first fix did not hold, or there was a second problem. Before you drive out, open your notes from the last visit. Look for what you measured, what you changed and what you did not check. Then take the same readings in the same order and compare. If everything you measured last time matches, the cause is probably something you did not measure, and the notebook shows you what that was. If one value moved, start there. Writing down what you ruled out is as useful as writing down what you found, because it keeps you from repeating the same checks.

Photos

Photos fill gaps that numbers cannot: the data plate, wiring as you found it before you disconnect anything, the condition of a coil, a burner flame, a piping layout. Take the wiring photo before work begins, not after. Frame photos on the equipment and keep faces, mail, house numbers and family pictures out of them. Store photos by equipment and date, not by customer name.

Keeping customer personal information out

The notebook can get lost, left on a roof or photographed over your shoulder. Treat it as something a stranger might read. Identify each machine with a job code or equipment tag that only you and your employer's records can decode, not a name, phone number, door code, alarm code or full street address. If you want to know which house a note belongs to, keep that link in your company's own system. Follow your employer's policy on notes and photos, since the work records belong to the business you work for.

How this differs from required refrigerant records

Federal rules require certain refrigerant records, and they are narrow. We read 40 CFR 82.166, which covers records for larger appliances. For appliances with 50 or more pounds of refrigerant, a technician or contractor must give the owner an invoice or other documentation of how much refrigerant was added. The owner must keep records of the date and type of each service and the amount of refrigerant added, and must keep leak-repair and verification test records on site. Records are kept for a minimum of three years unless the rule says otherwise. The same section covers a few other cases, such as purge destruction and full-charge range records.

Those rules say nothing about your amps, static pressure or air temperatures. A notebook is not a substitute for the required records, and the required records are not a substitute for a notebook. The notebook is voluntary and personal. The refrigerant records are mandatory and belong to the owner and the company. For what to keep, read EPA 608 Records: What Technicians Keep, and check the current rule text and your employer's procedures for your own situation.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions: this is an invented example, not a real customer. A 3-ton ducted air conditioner is visited twice, one year apart, on mild summer days. Same tech, same gauges, same test spots, same blower speed. The numbers are our own, chosen to show the method, and are not typical values for any model.

ReadingVisit 1 (baseline)Visit 2 (a year later)
Outdoor air82 °F83 °F
Return air76 °F77 °F
Supply air58 °F66 °F
Temperature drop (return minus supply)76 - 58 = 18 °F77 - 66 = 11 °F
Total external static0.50 in. w.c.0.52 in. w.c.
Line voltage238 V239 V
Compressor amps13.8 A12.6 A
Suction pressure125 psig108 psig
Discharge pressure290 psig270 psig

Step 1, conditions. Outdoor air differs by 1 °F and return air by 1 °F, so the visits compare fairly.

Step 2, airflow. Static went from 0.50 to 0.52, a change of 0.02 in. w.c. That is small, so the airflow side looks the same as last year. A dirty coil or restricted duct would normally push static up noticeably.

Step 3, electrical. Voltage is the same within 1 V, so the amp change is not a supply issue. Amps fell by 13.8 - 12.6 = 1.2 A, about 9 percent (1.2 / 13.8 = 0.087).

Step 4, refrigerant side and air. Both pressures dropped, the compressor is working less, and the temperature drop shrank from 18 to 11 °F (7 °F less) with the same airflow.

Conclusion. Without last year's page, the 11 °F drop looks like it could be airflow, a failing capacitor or a weak compressor. With the page, airflow and voltage are ruled out, and the pattern of lower pressures, lower amps and a smaller drop points to less refrigerant in the system, so the next step is a leak search. Our example shows a pattern, not proof. The leak search and any repair still have to confirm it, and if a leak is found, the refrigerant rules above apply to what you record officially.

Field card: Your Field Notebook

Same machine, same spots, same order, every call.

  • Write date, outdoor and return conditions, model and serial.
  • Record volts and amps, pressures and temperatures, and static pressure.
  • Take readings before and after any repair.
  • Compare against last visit before you decide on a cause.
  • Photograph data plates and original wiring before you start.
  • Use job codes, not names, phone numbers, addresses or door codes.
  • The notebook is personal. Required refrigerant records are separate and belong to the owner and company.
