HVAC for Cigar Shops: Walk-In Humidor Rooms and Smoking Lounges

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A cigar shop asks its HVAC for two opposite things. The walk-in humidor room wants air that is mild and quite humid, held steady all day. The smoking lounge, where one exists, wants lots of air moved out. This lesson shows how a tech thinks about each room, and why the two must never share air.

Check local rules first

Check local rules. Whether indoor smoking is allowed in a shop, and under what conditions, varies by place and changes over time. This page is general education, not legal advice. We say nothing here about what is permitted where you work. Ask the local authority and the building owner.

The humidor room: a small, humid, steady space

A walk-in humidor is a closet-sized to bedroom-sized room kept at a steady temperature and a high relative humidity so stored tobacco does not dry out. The exact temperature and humidity are the shop's chosen setpoints, set by the owner and the product. We did not find a government or university source that states target numbers for this room, so we give none. Your job is to hold whatever the owner picked, with little swing.

Two things make this hard. First, the target is humid, so the room is always close to the point where water condenses on cold surfaces. A federal environmental agency guide says mold needs moisture, and that condensation on windows, walls or pipes is a sign of high humidity that should be dried and fixed quickly. Second, every time the door opens, drier air from the sales floor pours in and the humidifier must make up the loss.

Humidifier types and water quality

Several kinds of humidifier can serve a small room: an electric steam unit, a wetted-media unit that evaporates water into passing air, an ultrasonic unit that makes a fine mist, and a high-pressure fogging unit. Our steam humidifier lesson covers steam dispersion and water quality in more depth, and our air-handler humidifier lesson covers wetted media, fogging and ultrasonic units.

Water matters more than the type. A federal environmental agency page on home humidifiers says ultrasonic and impeller units disperse the minerals in tap water efficiently, and that minerals also form scale that can harbor microorganisms. It says distillation is the most effective way to remove minerals, that deionization and reverse osmosis are less effective, and that bottled water labeled spring or mineral has not been demineralized. It also says white dust is a sign that a filter or cartridge is not removing minerals. Those statements are about home units, but the minerals-in-the-air idea is the same in a shop: white dust on cigar boxes and shelves points to the water supply.

The same page says not to humidify above 50 percent relative humidity in a home, because higher levels can encourage biological growth. A humidor is held higher than a home by design, which is exactly why the mold section below matters. Do not copy the home number to a humidor. Follow the shop's setpoint, and keep surfaces clean and dry.

Vapor barriers and sealed doors

Water vapor moves through walls, ceilings and gaps toward drier air. A university extension bulletin on wall moisture explains that indoor vapor, including vapor from humidifiers, can pass into walls, condense inside them in cold weather and damage the structure. It recommends a continuous polyethylene vapor barrier on the warm side of exterior walls and ceilings, sealed tightly around outlets, doors, windows and other openings. In a humidor the humid side is the room inside, so the same idea applies: the room needs a continuous, sealed barrier on its humid side, and any pipe, wire or duct that goes through should be sealed. See our wine cellar lesson for how barriers and insulation fit together in a cool room.

The door is the largest leak. Our own view as techs: a tight, gasketed door with a sweep, and a small vestibule or a habit of closing it behind customers, cuts the moisture the humidifier must replace. The worked example below shows how much that load can be.

Small cooling that does not dry the room

Lights, people and warm walls add heat to the room, so it may need a little cooling. Our own reasoning: a coil colder than the room dew point pulls water out of the air, which is the opposite of what the humidor needs. So choose a small unit sized for the real heat load, with a coil that runs warm and long cycles, and let the humidifier work against it only as a last resort. If the cooler and humidifier fight each other, the shop pays to make water vapor and then pay to remove it. Our cheese cave lesson shows the same trade-off at cool temperatures. A humidistat on the humidifier and a room sensor logged over several days show whether the two are cycling against each other.

Mold risk

High humidity and mold go together. The federal guide says none of the many types of mold grow without water or moisture. It also says that if wet or damp materials are dried within 24 to 48 hours after a leak or spill, mold will in most cases not grow. For a humidor the lesson is steady control and no wet spots: a dripping humidifier, a leaking drain pan, a wet carpet or a condensing cold pipe all invite growth. Insulate cold pipes, fix leaks at once, and avoid shelving tight against outside walls where air stands still. Mold on stored product or in the room is a job for the owner and a qualified remediation firm, not a wipe-down.

Keep humidor air away from the sales floor and the lounge

Humidor air should not be ducted to or from the sales floor or the lounge. Our own design rule: give the humidor its own humidifier and its own small cooling, tie it to no shared return, and use a sealed door as the only connection. That protects the product from lounge smoke and keeps the humid air from condensing in a cold sales-floor duct.

Lounge smoke ventilation, and its limits

A lounge that allows smoking is designed around exhaust. The usual approach is a high outdoor air or exhaust rate, set by the local code and the design engineer (we give no number), with the lounge held under negative pressure so air flows into it from the sales floor and not out. The exhaust must discharge outdoors, away from intakes and from neighbors. Check how much air leaves through the exhaust and how much makeup air is supplied, so the room stays slightly negative and the front door does not whistle or stick.

Ventilation does not make smoke safe. A federal public health agency page summarizes the U.S. Surgeon General's 2006 report this way: separating smokers, cleaning the air and ventilating a building cannot eliminate exposure to secondhand smoke. The page gives three reasons: air cleaners remove larger particles but not small particles or gases, standard HVAC systems can spread smoke through a building, and negative-pressure smoking rooms still leak into nearby areas. It also says no level of exposure is risk-free. So filters on a lounge help with odor and some particles, but they are not a guarantee, and a tech should not promise a customer that a system makes the air healthy. Our casino floor lesson covers the same limits at larger scale, and our bar lesson covers crowds, doors and drafts.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This is our own example. Every input is an assumption or a reading from our own psychrometric chart, not a source figure. We are sizing the humidifier for a winter day, when make-up air is driest.

  • Assumed room: 10 ft by 12 ft by 9 ft high. Volume = 10 x 12 x 9 = 1,080 cubic feet.
  • Assumed air change from door openings and small leaks: 0.5 air changes per hour. Air moved = 1,080 x 0.5 = 540 cubic feet per hour.
  • Assumed air density: 0.075 pounds per cubic foot. Dry air mass = 540 x 0.075 = 40.5 pounds per hour.
  • Our chart readings of humidity ratio, in pounds of water per pound of dry air: the humidor room at an assumed 70 F and 70 percent reads about 0.0110. Assumed winter outdoor air at 20 F and 50 percent reads about 0.0011. We treat all make-up air as outdoor-condition air, which is a conservative simplification.
  • Difference = 0.0110 - 0.0011 = 0.0099 pounds of water per pound of dry air.
  • Moisture lost = 40.5 x 0.0099 = about 0.40 pounds of water per hour.
  • Humidifier output needed: add an assumed 25 percent margin, 0.40 x 1.25 = 0.50 pounds per hour. At 8.34 pounds per gallon that is about 0.06 gallons per hour, or about 1.4 gallons in a day.

What the numbers show: tightening the door and cutting leakage in half would cut the load in half, and a humidifier far larger than 0.5 pounds per hour would mostly cycle on and off and overshoot. Real shops differ, so measure the room, and read the humidifier's output rating in the same units.

Field card: Cigar shop HVAC

Humidor humid and steady, lounge exhausted and separate.

  • Humidor targets are the shop's chosen setpoints; hold them steady.
  • Water quality decides white dust and scale: low-mineral water, especially with ultrasonic units.
  • Seal the humid side of the room and gasket the door.
  • Small cooling should not strip moisture; log the room for several days.
  • Dry wet spots within 24 to 48 hours; fix condensation and leaks.
  • No shared air between humidor, sales floor and lounge.
  • Lounge: negative pressure, exhaust outdoors, and ventilation does not make smoke safe.
  • Smoking rules vary locally; ask the local authority.
