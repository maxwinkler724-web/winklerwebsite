Crematory Equipment Room HVAC: Combustion Air, Heat Rejection, Stack Draft and Air Permit Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cremation unit is a gas-fired furnace that runs hot for hours in a room where people work. The room needs air for the burners, a way to carry off the heat the unit gives up, and enough balance that the stack keeps drawing. This lesson covers those three jobs in plain terms, with one worked example, and ends with how air permits are handled.

Check the maker and the agency

The unit maker's installation manual and the owner's air permit govern. This page is general education built from agency and trade pages read in 2026. It is not legal advice and not a design. Anything labeled "Our own" is a teaching assumption.

The three jobs of the room

First, supply air for the burners and the draft. Second, remove the heat that escapes the unit into the room. Third, keep the room pressure close enough to outdoors that the stack and burners behave. One louver and one fan can serve all three, but only if they are sized together.

Combustion air

Gas burners need air. An ACHR News article puts it at about 10 cubic feet of air for each cubic foot of natural gas for perfect combustion, and 15 cubic feet when about 50 percent excess air is allowed for. A clean-boiler training page gives about 9.5. The maker's number for the whole unit is the one to use. For the general rules on louvers, free area and confined rooms, see combustion air for furnaces, boilers and water heaters; we do not repeat them here.

Agencies care about the combustion itself. The New York DEC operator manual says combustion improves when temperature, air input and burn rate are kept at proper levels, and it lists "combustion air and draft fan" on its inspection list. A Florida DEP handout adds that the burner air-to-fuel ratio must be maintained and the flame checked at least once per shift. A Canadian public-health review adds that high CO can signal poor combustion.

Heat rejected to the room

The unit is insulated, but a Fire Engineering article says this equipment gives off a great deal of radiant heat, and that a stainless stack can run roughly 110 to 125 degrees F on its surface during continual use, so combustibles stay clear and a stack needs 10 to 12 inches of free air around it. That heat ends up in the room unless ventilation removes it. For the same heat-load approach in a kiln room, see pottery kiln rooms.

The sensible heat equation links airflow, heat and temperature rise: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. An ACCA article explains that 1.08 comes from air density 0.075 times specific heat 0.24 times 60 minutes, and that it fits sea level and near room temperature. Rearranged: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T).

Why the room must not go strongly negative

An exhaust fan removes air, and that air has to come back from somewhere. If the louver is too small, closed, or iced over, the room pressure drops below outdoors. Natural Resources Canada explains that depressurization can reverse airflow in a chimney or vent, so air flows down instead of up, and that start-up while this is happening can cause spillage. Some unit designs use a draft inducer or fan to hold stack flow, and those tolerate more than a natural-draft chimney; one West Virginia permit evaluation for an animal crematory lists two induced-draft fans rated 2,000 cubic feet per minute each at 6 inches water column. We found no public, sourced limit for room pressure at a crematory, so the unit maker's installation manual sets the allowed value. The test idea in worst-case depressurization testing still applies: fire the exhaust equipment together and see what the combustion area does.

This is the opposite problem from the one in funeral home preparation rooms, where mild negative pressure is wanted to hold fumes in the room. Keep the two systems from fighting each other through shared doors and ducts.

Worked example (Our own)

Question: how many cfm of room ventilation carry off the heat, how does that compare with combustion air, and how big must the louver be?

Our own assumptions, not from any maker: burner input of 2,000,000 Btu/h at peak; natural gas at 1,000 Btu per cubic foot; 15 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of gas (the ACHR News figure with excess air); heat released to the room of 75,000 Btu/h (a stand-in for the maker's published surface and stack loss); allowed room rise of 15 F above outdoor supply air; sea-level air; combustion air taken from the room; a louver face velocity of 500 feet per minute; a louver that is 50 percent free area.

  • Combustion air. Gas = 2,000,000 / 1,000 = 2,000 cubic feet per hour. Air = 2,000 x 15 = 30,000 cubic feet per hour. In cfm: 30,000 / 60 = 500 cfm.
  • Ventilation for heat. cfm = 75,000 / (1.08 x 15) = 75,000 / 16.2 = about 4,630 cfm.
  • Comparison. 4,630 / 500 = about 9.3. The heat-removal airflow is roughly nine times the combustion air in this example.
  • Total through the low louver. The louver must replace what the fan exhausts and what the unit draws: 4,630 + 500 = 5,130 cfm.
  • Free area. 5,130 / 500 = about 10.3 square feet. Gross opening at 50 percent free area = 10.3 / 0.50 = about 20.5 square feet.
  • What goes wrong if it is undersized. A louver sized only for combustion air needs 500 / 500 = 1.0 square foot free. With the fan on, the missing 4,630 cfm has to leak in elsewhere, and the room goes negative.

Treat this as a screening number. Real heat gain, outdoor design temperature, winter freeze protection and louver data must come from the maker and a design engineer. Consider an interlock so the fan cannot run with the louver shut.

Worker heat stress

Staff may work near a hot unit for hours, including when handling the equipment during cooling. NIOSH recommends phasing in heat exposure over 7 to 14 days, cool drinking water near the work, rest breaks that lengthen as heat rises, and engineering controls such as more air movement and reflective shields. Its illness page lists heat stroke warning signs such as confusion, fainting, seizures and a very high body temperature, and says to call 911 and cool the person quickly. An Oregon OSHA death care guide also flags thermal exposure from the furnace and recommends evaluating protective equipment against burns. Ventilation helps, but it does not replace water and breaks.

Air permit basics

Cremation units are regulated as air pollution sources, and states run the permitting. EPA's New Source Review page says Congress created the preconstruction permitting program in the 1977 Clean Air Act Amendments, and its Title V page says state or local agencies issue most operating permits. The New York manual says every crematory there needs either an air registration or a state facility permit. A West Virginia evaluation says its rule requires every incinerator, regardless of size, to get a construction or modification permit. Florida lets human crematories register under an air general permit. Ask the owner which permit applies; it may limit fuel, rate, temperature and records.

Typical conditions, as one group of mid-Atlantic and southeastern state air agencies recommends them, include allowed fuels of natural gas, propane or distillate oil, no charging until the secondary chamber is at least 1,600 F, a residence time of at least one second, an opacity limit of 5 percent, a trained attending operator, and records kept at least five years. Individual permits differ; New York, for example, uses a 10 percent opacity trigger.

In New Jersey, NJDEP's Bureau of Stationary Sources permits stationary air sources. An NJDEP advisory says modifications to permitted equipment must be pre-approved, that a permit covers only the equipment listed, and that the permit copy should be kept on site. We could not open NJDEP's own crematory permit pages, so confirm New Jersey requirements with NJDEP before any work. For boilers, see NJ air permit basics for boilers.

Mercury and emissions

We report only what agencies and reviews state. A Canadian public-health review says mercury can be released from dental amalgam fillings, that Canada had no federal mercury limit, and that abatement equipment has cut emissions where required. The mid-Atlantic agency document lists a recommended mercury emission factor and suggests EPA Method 29 for testing.

Field card: Crematory equipment room

Louver low, fan high, sized together, and the permit governs.

  • Burners need roughly 10 to 15 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of gas, per ACHR News.
  • Room cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T).
  • Our example: 75,000 Btu/h at 15 F rise is about 4,630 cfm; combustion air 500 cfm.
  • The louver must pass fan cfm plus unit air.
  • A strongly negative room can reverse stack flow; the maker sets the limit.
  • NIOSH: acclimatize, cool water, rest, shields.
  • States issue the air permits; ask which one applies.
