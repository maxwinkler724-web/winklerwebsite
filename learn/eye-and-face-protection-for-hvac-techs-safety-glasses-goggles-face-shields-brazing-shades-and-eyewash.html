Eye and Face Protection for HVAC Techs: Safety Glasses, Goggles, Face Shields, Brazing Shades and Eyewash

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An HVAC tech can get hurt in the eyes three different ways before lunch: a flying chip from a drill, a splash from a coil cleaner, and bright light from a brazing torch. No single pair of glasses is the right answer for all three. This lesson sorts out which protector fits which hazard.

Check your rules

Check your rules. This page is general education built from federal regulation text and a federal safety-agency article read in 2026. Your employer, the product label and the safety data sheet for each chemical have the final word on what to wear.

What the rules require

OSHA's general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.133, and its construction rule, 29 CFR 1926.102, say the same core things. The employer must make sure each affected worker uses suitable eye or face protection when exposed to flying particles, molten metal, liquid chemicals, acids or caustic liquids, chemical gases or vapors, or potentially harmful light radiation. Where flying objects are a hazard, the protection must have side protection, and detachable clip-on or slide-on side shields are acceptable. Eye and face equipment must also be marked so the maker can be identified. HVAC work happens in both settings, so a tech can be under either rule depending on the site: the same equipment satisfies both.

Under 1910.133(b), the equipment has to meet a consensus standard. The rule lists ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2010, ANSI Z87.1-2003 and ANSI Z87.1-1989 (R-1998), and treats equipment shown to be at least as effective as compliant. A NIOSH article adds that later versions, Z87.1-2015 and Z87.1-2020, are also accepted and that some products do not print the year.

Reading the Z87 mark

The NIOSH article says industrial safety eyewear and face shields should be marked "Z87" for normal safety use and "Z87+" for high impact. Prescription safety glasses carry "Z87-2" or "Z87-2+". The mark should appear on both the frame and the lens. Extra letters add other features: D for dust, U for an ultraviolet tint, and R for an infrared tint. Plain dress-eyewear marks such as Z80 cover optical quality, not industrial protection, so they do not count. The same article says the standard requires wrap-around coverage or side shields on safety glasses for the high-impact rating, and that nearly all safety glasses are therefore Z87+. If you cannot find a mark, do not assume the pair is rated.

Glasses, goggles and face shields

NIOSH lists goggles, face shields, spectacles and full-facepiece respirators as the main kinds of eye and face protection, and says the right choice depends on the nature and extent of the hazard, how the exposure happens, other protective equipment in use and the wearer's vision needs. Neither NIOSH nor OSHA publishes a one-line chart ranking them, so the comparison below is our own plain-language summary. Always confirm against the maker's rating on the item.

  • Safety glasses with side protection are the everyday choice for flying chips, wire ends and debris from the front and sides. They sit open at the top and bottom, so they are a poor pick for liquid splash.
  • Goggles seal around the eyes. They suit overhead debris, dust, and splash, and they take a filter lens for torch work. A tech working under or above equipment is a case where debris falls from odd angles, which NIOSH uses as a reason for better-fitting glasses or goggles.
  • Face shields cover the whole face. We treat them as added protection worn over glasses or goggles, not as a replacement, because a shield can leave gaps at the eyes.

Brazing and the shade table

Torch light is a hazard the table in 29 CFR 1910.133 addresses directly with a filter lens, a tinted lens rated by shade number. A higher number is darker. We read the table on the federal eCFR site, which lists these minimum shades: torch soldering, shade 2; torch brazing, shade 3; gas welding by plate thickness, shade 4 for light work under 1/8 in., shade 5 for medium work from 1/8 in. to 1/2 in., and shade 6 for heavy work over 1/2 in. The construction table in 1926.102 (its Table E-1) lists torch brazing as shade 3 or 4, soldering as shade 2, and the same gas welding bands as 4 or 5, 5 or 6 and 6 or 8. Brazing copper line sets falls in the torch brazing row, which does not change with pipe size. For a method refresher, see the brazing lesson linked below.

Chemicals, refrigerant and eyewash

Coil cleaners can be acidic or caustic, and both the general industry and construction rules name acids and caustic liquids as reasons to wear eye or face protection. Liquid refrigerant is a different hazard: it boils off fast and can freeze tissue, so eye protection matters whenever you open a charged line (see our refrigerant safety lesson). Read the product label and the safety data sheet for the exact eyewear called for.

The eyewash rule is one sentence. Under 29 CFR 1910.151(c), where the eyes or body of any person may be exposed to injurious corrosive materials, suitable facilities for quick drenching or flushing of the eyes and body must be provided within the work area for immediate emergency use. The NIOSH article points to ANSI/ISEA Z358.1 for eyewash equipment details. On a rooftop, find out what the site provides before you open the cleaner, because a tech working alone may have only the bottle on the truck.

Prescription wearers

Under 1910.133(a)(3), a worker who wears prescription lenses must use eye protection that incorporates the prescription in its design, or protection that fits over the regular lenses without disturbing their position or the protector's. Everyday glasses are not safety glasses. Prescription safety glasses carry the Z87-2 mark, and over-glasses should show a Z87 mark too. Fit-over goggles with a filter lens are an easy answer for brazing.

Worked example (ours): three jobs, three setups

These are our own examples. The recommendations are our judgment applied to the rules above, and the one assumed input is flagged.

  1. Brazing a 7/8 in. line with oxyacetylene. Hazard: infrared and bright light, plus sparks and flux splatter. Row in the table: torch brazing. Shade: 3 under 1910.133, or 3 or 4 under 1926.102. Assumption: we pick shade 4, the darker end of the construction table, because an oxyacetylene flame is bright and shade 4 also satisfies the general industry minimum of 3. Gear: Z87 goggles or fit-over goggles with a shade 4 filter lens, and side protection. A pipe size does not change the shade.
  2. Spraying acid coil cleaner on a condenser. Hazard: liquid acid mist and splash, with spray drifting and dripping back. No shade number applies. Gear: sealed goggles, with a face shield over them for face cover. Confirm the exact eyewear on the product label and safety data sheet, and know where the eyewash is. Because the cleaner is a corrosive, 1910.151(c) calls for drenching or flushing facilities in the work area.
  3. Drilling overhead into concrete. Hazard: falling chips and dust, with debris dropping at odd angles. No shade number applies. Gear: Z87+ safety glasses with side protection at a minimum, or goggles for a better seal around the eyes, with a face shield over them if chips are heavy. Dust control and respirators are covered in the silica lesson linked below.

Arithmetic check on the brazing pick: table minimum 3, construction table range 3 to 4, our choice 4, which is inside both rules.

Habits that keep eyes safe

  • Check the Z87 or Z87+ mark on frame and lens before you start.
  • Replace scratched, cracked or fogged protectors; the visibility loss is a hazard of its own.
  • Wear protection before you open a line or start the drill, not after the first spark.
  • Pair eye protection with the rest of your gear: respirator, hearing protection and gloves.

Field card: Eye and Face Protection

Match the protector to the hazard, then check the mark.

  • Look for Z87 (basic) or Z87+ (high impact) on frame and lens.
  • Flying chips: safety glasses with side protection. Splash: sealed goggles, face shield over them.
  • Torch brazing: filter lens, minimum shade 3 (1910.133); 3 or 4 (1926.102).
  • Corrosive materials: drenching or flushing facilities in the work area (1910.151(c)).
  • Prescription: build it in, or wear over-glasses that do not shift your lenses.
  • Follow the label and the safety data sheet for each chemical.
