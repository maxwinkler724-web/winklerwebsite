Cold-Climate Heat Pumps

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Not long ago people assumed a heat pump quit working below freezing. Cold-climate models are built and tested to prove otherwise.

The old problem

In general, a heat pump gets less capacity as outdoor temperature falls, just when the building needs more. At the same time the compressor has to work harder. An ordinary heat pump may need a lot of backup heat in a cold snap, which costs more to run. Cold-climate models aim to keep delivering useful heat much further down the thermometer.

How the design helps, in general

  • Variable-speed (inverter) compressors that can run faster in cold weather and slowly in mild weather. See Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.
  • Larger coils and refined refrigerant circuits tuned for low outdoor temperatures. This is a manufacturer design topic, so details are model specific.
  • Better defrost control, since cold, damp air frosts coils often. See Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat.

What ENERGY STAR checks

ENERGY STAR publishes criteria for cold-climate air-source heat pumps. As listed on its key product criteria page, these are some of the requirements (labeled here as examples from that page, so check the page for the current version):

  • Non-ducted split systems: heating efficiency of at least 8.5 HSPF2, with at least 15.2 SEER2.
  • Ducted split systems and single-package equipment: at least 8.1 HSPF2, with at least 15.2 SEER2.
  • A coefficient of performance (COP) of at least 1.75 at 5 degrees F.
  • Heating capacity at 5 degrees F of at least 70 percent of the capacity at 47 degrees F.
  • A controls verification procedure showing the 5 degree F results are achieved with the unit's own controls, working as they would in a home.

How to read that

COP is heat delivered divided by electricity used. A COP of 1.75 means 1.75 units of heat for each unit of electricity. HSPF2 is a seasonal heating efficiency rating. The capacity ratio tells you how much heat output survives when it gets cold. Those figures are test conditions and minimums for a label, not a promise for any particular house. Real results depend on installation, ductwork, load and weather.

Thinking about backup heat

Even a cold-climate heat pump may be paired with backup heat for the coldest days or for equipment protection. How and when that heat is allowed to run is set by the controls and the installer. A well-set system keeps backup heat from running when the heat pump could do the job alone.

For students

The takeaways for an exam or a job are the ideas: capacity drops as it gets colder, inverter compressors and good controls help, and ratings tell you how a model was tested. The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. Always use the manufacturer's published capacity tables for real sizing.
