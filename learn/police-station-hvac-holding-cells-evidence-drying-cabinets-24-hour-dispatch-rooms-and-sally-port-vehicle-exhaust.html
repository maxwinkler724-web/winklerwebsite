Police Station HVAC: Holding Cells, Evidence Drying, Dispatch Rooms and Sally Ports

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A police station is four buildings under one roof: a lockup, an evidence room, a communications room that never closes, and a vehicle garage. Each has a different air problem, and all of them restrict who can reach the equipment. This lesson walks through them from the service tech's side.

The station at a glance

Holding cells

The grilles, fasteners, thermostats and smoke control in a lockup follow the same logic as a jail, so we do not repeat it. Read Correctional Facility HVAC first, and see Courthouse HVAC for holding areas next to courtrooms.

What a station adds is churn. People arrive, wait and leave within hours, and some arrive sick. The CDC's tuberculosis page for correctional settings lists poor air flow, shared airspaces and overcrowding as conditions that help TB spread, and says facility health staff screen people when they enter and apply airborne precautions, which include environmental controls, to people with signs or symptoms. It gives no air change rate for a holding cell, so we do not either. The practical rule for a tech: do not rebalance, add transfer openings or change recirculation in a cell block on your own. Ask the facility what its infection-control plan assumes about cell exhaust, and write down what you changed.

Evidence rooms and drying cabinets

Wet or bloody clothing has to dry before it is packaged, because bacteria can ruin the biological material. The NIST handbook on biological evidence describes two ways to dry it. A low-tech drying room should be under negative pressure with 12 to 15 air changes per hour, vented to the outside of the building, kept at 60 to 75 °F and no more than 60 percent relative humidity. A high-tech setup is a commercial drying cabinet that passes air through a HEPA filter, usually in larger agencies with daily drying. The handbook notes the filter itself may become evidence, so ask the property officer before you change one.

Worked example (our own, with assumed room size). Assume a drying room 10 ft by 12 ft with a 9 ft ceiling.

  1. Volume: 10 × 12 × 9 = 1,080 ft³.
  2. At 12 air changes per hour: 1,080 × 12 ÷ 60 = 216 cfm.
  3. At 15 air changes per hour: 1,080 × 15 ÷ 60 = 270 cfm.

So the exhaust fan should move roughly 216 to 270 cfm, with the room measured negative to the corridor. The make-up air is a heating or cooling load, so ask where it comes from.

The handbook lists dry storage for most evidence at 60 to 75 °F with humidity under 60 percent, and refrigerated storage at 2 to 8 °C. It says refrigerators and freezers should alarm on a temperature rise and be monitored around the clock, for example by a call to a communications center. That is a good reason to know which circuits those units share with your HVAC.

Odor and drug evidence

NIOSH visited a police department's criminal investigation section and found the evidence room slightly negative but with no dedicated exhaust, so the odor of stored marijuana was pungent inside and outside the room. It recommended a dedicated exhaust system. In the same report, the crime lab and photo lab were positive to their surroundings, which can carry odors and contaminants out. In another NIOSH evaluation of a crime lab complex, a vault was not served by building ventilation, and surface samples at a lab door bottom had detectable methamphetamine and cocaine, which NIOSH said supports the importance of pressurization. NIOSH recommended dedicated ventilation for the vault and airflow from lower-hazard to higher-hazard areas. A third NIOSH report on an evidence processing area found supply airflow below design and humidity below ASHRAE guidelines.

The FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin adds a planning point: one agency kept narcotics out of its new property building at first because the fire department treats large amounts as hazardous material. Ask who classifies the room before you assume ventilation alone is the answer.

The 24-hour dispatch room

A dispatch room has no off hours, so there is no maintenance window. One state's 911 standards require the HVAC to hold temperature and humidity within the equipment makers' limits, a standby power source with documented tests at least monthly, and a ready backup site. The FCC's planning guide warns that batteries are good for short outages but often do not power the air conditioning, and that computers may be damaged or shut off when overheated. For us that means the cooling must sit on the generator, not only on the UPS.

The WBDG security pages recommend redundancy and physical separation of critical HVAC for assets that cannot be disrupted. In practice that is two units, each able to carry the room, on separate circuits, with an alarm that reaches someone. See Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet for the equipment side.

Worked example (our own, all inputs assumed). Assume eight dispatch positions plus a rack, 6,000 W of electronics in all, 500 W of lighting, six people at 250 Btu/h sensible each, and a 20 °F rise between supply air and room air.

  1. Electronics: 6,000 W × 3.412 = 20,472 Btu/h.
  2. Lighting: 500 W × 3.412 = 1,706 Btu/h.
  3. People: 6 × 250 = 1,500 Btu/h.
  4. Total sensible heat: 20,472 + 1,706 + 1,500 = 23,678 Btu/h.
  5. Airflow: cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT) = 23,678 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 23,678 ÷ 21.6, about 1,096, call it 1,100 cfm.
  6. Cooling: 23,678 ÷ 12,000 = about 1.97 tons of sensible cooling.

The ACHR News article on the Btu/h formula explains where 1.08 comes from and that it fits air near 70 °F. For N+1, unit A and unit B should each deliver about 1,100 cfm and about 2 tons of sensible cooling, and a real design would add latent load and margin. If one unit fails, the other must pick up the whole room without anyone touching a switch.

Sally port exhaust

A sally port is a garage with a door at each end, so only one opens at a time. Cars idle there while prisoners are processed. The CO limits, tailpipe capture and NO2 are covered in Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC, and diesel handling in Fire Station Apparatus Bay HVAC. For orientation, OSHA lists 50 ppm for carbon monoxide as an 8-hour average and NIOSH lists 35 ppm.

One NIOSH visit to a city garage found the exhaust fans on one wall switched off because neighbors complained about noise and exhaust odor. NIOSH recommended replacing them with louvers and adding fans on the other side, plus keeping vehicle idling short. Look for fans that someone turned off, and find out why before you turn them back on. If a sally port opens into the booking area, check which way the air moves when the inner door opens. It should not carry exhaust toward the cells.

Getting in to do the work

WBDG recommends access control to mechanical rooms, emergency HVAC shutoff and control, and barriers over openings larger than 96 square inches. All of that affects you.

  • Arrange an escort and a time with the station before the visit. Dispatch and the lockup may not be entered on short notice.
  • Our own habit: count tools in and out, carry no loose fasteners into cells, and never prop a secure door.
  • Do not shut down dispatch cooling, evidence refrigeration or sally port exhaust without a named person agreeing.
  • If the control system is network-connected, treat it as part of the building's security. See the correctional lesson for escort rules.

Field card: Police Station HVAC

Four rooms, four air paths, one escort.

  • Holding cells: follow the correctional lesson; do not change cell airflow without the facility's infection-control plan.
  • Drying room: negative, 12 to 15 air changes per hour, exhausted outdoors; 60 to 75 °F, 60 percent RH or less (NIST).
  • Evidence room odor needs its own exhaust; keep labs and vaults at the right pressure.
  • Dispatch: two units, each carrying the room; cooling on generator power.
  • Heat: watts × 3.412 = Btu/h; cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT).
  • Sally port: do not leave exhaust fans off; check air direction at the inner door.
  • Escort, tool count, no outages without a named approval.
