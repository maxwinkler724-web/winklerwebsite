UV Lights in HVAC Systems: Coil Cleaning vs Air Disinfection Claims

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A UV lamp bolted into your furnace or air handler sounds like a simple upgrade. But "UV light" is sold for two very different jobs, and the marketing often blurs them. This lesson separates the jobs, shows what EPA's technical summary says about each, and covers the safety and maintenance details that decide whether a lamp is worth owning. It does not recommend any brand.

Two jobs, one name

EPA's technical summary on residential air cleaners describes two ways UV lamps are used in HVAC. One is surface irradiation: a lamp aimed at the cooling (evaporator) coil and drain pan, where a damp surface can grow mold or slime. The other is airstream disinfection: a lamp mounted in a duct to treat the air as it flows past. Resideo's installation sheet for its UV systems describes both, a duct-mounted type for the return air and a surface type placed near the coil, which it says reduces growth on duct surfaces, coils and drip pans.

What EPA says about each

EPA's summary says UV lamps can inactivate bacteria, viruses and fungal growth, but how well depends on dose, which is lamp intensity times the time microbes spend in the light. In a duct, air passes the lamp in a few seconds. EPA says the effective destruction of airborne viruses and of fungal and bacterial spores requires much higher exposures than typical residential units deliver, and that UV appears not to be effective as the only control device. It also says airborne bacteria dropped by only about 25 to 30 percent in the study it cites for surface units, and it found no research showing UV reduces dust mite or mold allergenicity. Adding UV to a high-grade filter offers only minimal extra infection control, according to the same summary.

The coil is a better fit. EPA says UV can still help on cooling coils and drain pans because exposure there lasts much longer than in moving air. It cites a study where surface irradiation cut microbial contamination on HVAC surfaces by 99 percent and says UV-C lamps reduced growth on duct lining and drain pans. EPA adds that moisture control and good HVAC design remain the primary approach, and that UV does not reach microbes inside filter media, crevices, or porous insulation. EPA also notes that a cleaner coil can improve heat transfer. It says there is no specific standard test method to rate and compare UV cleaners in HVAC, though ASHRAE standards 185.1 and 185.2 cover in-duct airstream and surface irradiation testing.

In plain terms: surface irradiation has a plausible mechanism and some supporting data; "air disinfection" claims for a typical home duct lamp are the weakest part of the pitch. UV also kills or deactivates microbes but does not remove them, so a filter is still doing the capturing. See Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics for what moves the needle more.

Ozone: read the label

EPA says uncoated UV-C lamps at 254 nm and below can make ozone from oxygen, and that some makers use special lamp coatings to prevent this. Ozone is a lung irritant. California's Air Resources Board (CARB) says even low concentrations are harmful to biological tissue, and that electronic air cleaners sold there, including in-duct electronic devices, must meet an ozone limit of 0.050 parts per million. Not every lamp does this: Resideo's product data sheet for its UV100E lamps states that the UV lamp does not produce ozone; confirm the statement for the exact model you buy. Ask for the manufacturer's statement, and avoid any lamp sold to produce ozone on purpose.

Eye and skin safety

Manufacturer manuals are blunt. Resideo's sheet says the light is harmful to bare skin and eyes, can cause temporary or permanent vision loss, and tells you never to look at a lit bulb or mount the device where its light can be seen after installation. Sanuvox's manuals say never to expose eyes or skin to ultraviolet light from any source, and to disconnect power before service. One Sanuvox model has a cut-off switch that kills power when the lamp access door opens. Fresh-Aire's APCO manual says to protect eyes from UV light and to turn off or disconnect the unit before servicing. A viewing window or door interlock is a feature of some designs, not all, so never assume one exists. EPA lists potential eye injury among UV's disadvantages. UV can also yellow or crack plastics; Resideo warns of color shift or structural degradation of plastic HVAC parts near the lamp.

Lamp life: the hidden running cost

A lamp keeps glowing long after it stops working. Fresh-Aire's manual says its lamps keep emitting visible light but lose germicidal effectiveness over time, and it specifies replacement every year for one lamp model and every two years for another. Sanuvox manuals we read list three years for the units cited below. Resideo's surface system runs its lamp three hours on and three hours off for a two-year life cycle and says to replace the bulb when its status LED stays solidly lit, with quarterly cleaning of the bulb recommended. EPA also says lamps need regular cleaning and replacement.

Worked example: what a replacement interval means in lamp hours

Resideo's surface system cycles three hours on, three hours off for a two-year life cycle.

  1. One full cycle is 3 + 3 = 6 hours, with the lamp lit for 3 of them, so it is lit half the time.
  2. Lit hours per day: 24 hours × 0.5 = 12 hours.
  3. Two years is about 2 × 365 = 730 days.
  4. Lit hours over the cycle: 730 × 12 = 8,760 hours.

Compare a lamp that burns nonstop with a one-year replacement interval, as Fresh-Aire specifies for one of its lamp models: 365 × 24 = 8,760 hours. Different designs, same total lit hours. Our arithmetic assumes the lamp runs as described and that the manufacturer's interval is the lamp's germicidal life. Always use the interval in your own unit's manual, since it differs by model.

Questions worth asking before you buy

  • Is it aimed at the coil or in the duct, and which problem does it solve?
  • What is the replacement interval, and what does a replacement lamp cost per year?
  • Does the manufacturer state it produces no ozone?
  • How does it protect the person servicing it?
  • Is the real problem moisture, a clogged drain, or a dirty coil? A dirty-sock smell is often a coil or drain issue first; see Furnace and AC Smells: What Each Odor Means. For dust and duct claims, see Should You Get Your Air Ducts Cleaned?

Tech section

UV-C at 254 nm is the typical wavelength of low-pressure mercury lamps, per EPA. Techs should power a UV circuit from constant supply as the Fresh-Aire APCO sheet says (not the blower relay), lock out power before touching a lamp, and mount the lamp so its light cannot be seen after installation, as Resideo instructs.

Field card: UV Lights in HVAC Systems

Coil lamp: plausible. Duct air lamp: weak claims. Always check ozone, eyes, lamp date.

  • Two uses: surface lamp at coil and pan, or lamp in duct for air.
  • EPA: typical residential duct units give too little dose; UV is not a sole control.
  • Surface use has the stronger evidence because the exposure is long.
  • Uncoated UV-C lamps can make ozone; ask for the maker's ozone statement.
  • Never look at a lit lamp; lock out power before service.
  • Replace lamps on the manual's schedule; a glowing lamp may not be killing anything.
  • Moisture control and filtration still come first.
