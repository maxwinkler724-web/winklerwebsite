Mag and Insertion Turbine Flow Meters on Plant Water

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Electromagnetic ("mag") meters and insertion turbine meters both show up on chilled, condenser and hot water lines. They measure flow in very different ways and fail in very different ways. This lesson explains what each reads, what makes each one lie, and how to cross-check a suspicious number in the field.

How a mag meter reads flow

A mag meter works from Faraday's law of induction: a conductor moving through a magnetic field has a voltage induced across it. A university thesis on magnetic meters writes it as e = B l v, where B is field strength, l is the distance between electrodes and v is velocity. Here the moving conductor is the water itself. Two electrodes in the pipe wall pick up the voltage, and since the field strength and electrode spacing are fixed, the voltage is proportional to velocity. The electronics then multiply by the pipe cross-section to report gpm.

Why the pipe must be full and the liquid conductive

The meter only works if the liquid carries a little electrical current, so it reads conductive liquids and will not read most oils or clean air. A university fluid-dynamics text says the fluid needs "some minimal electrical conductivity." Ordinary plant water is fine. A maker write-up we read says the older designs needed a small minimum conductivity and newer ones tolerate less; check the maker's data for very pure water.

The pipe must also be full. The meter multiplies velocity by a fixed area, so air in the bore breaks the math. The same thesis notes that many meters carry extra electrodes at the top and bottom of the pipe to provide grounding and to report full-pipe status. A national laboratory metering guide also says to avoid mag meters where water carries iron particles or air bubbles, because they affect accuracy. On a long horizontal run, place the meter where the pipe stays flooded, such as a rising section, and keep the electrodes off the top of the pipe.

Grounding and electrical noise

The signal is only millivolt-sized, so stray current in the pipe can swamp it. A maker's flow-measurement write-up says the meter must be electrically grounded to the process liquid and that poor grounding can cause a zero shift. In practice that means grounding rings or grounding electrodes tied to the pipe flanges, and the meter body bonded the way the maker shows, especially on plastic or lined pipe. A university facility detail we read calls for a grounding ring at each end on its in-line mag meters. If a reading drifts or jumps when a nearby motor or drive starts, suspect grounding first. Check zero with the pipe full and flow stopped.

Straight run: cite the maker, not a rule of thumb

There is no single number. A thesis that tested several meters listed published recommendations of 3 to 5, 5, 8 to 10 and "at least 10" diameters upstream. It then found maker requirements from 1 to 5 diameters, and that shorter upstream pipe increased error. In one test setup one meter averaged about 3.3 percent low when close-coupled to an elbow, compared with 0.2 percent low in a long straight run. A national laboratory guide says electronic meters can be installed with minimal to zero straight pipe depending on the maker. A university facility standard detail asks for 4 diameters up and down on in-line mag meters. A maker write-up we read says five diameters upstream and two downstream. Use the maker's table for the exact model, and never place the meter just after a pump discharge, a partly open valve or an elbow.

Insertion meters: depth and location

An insertion meter is a probe pushed through a tapped fitting, so it reads velocity near the tip and not the pipe average. That makes insertion depth part of the calibration. A university energy report lists errors from improper insertion depth as a source of error and says alignment matters, but gives no depth, so set depth from the maker's pipe-size chart and re-check it after any reinstallation. The same report found that insertion mag-type meters deviated 10 percent or more below about 3 ft/s. Another study of an insertion paddle-wheel meter downstream of an elbow found the largest error at 2.1 diameters, and its abstract says finding 10 to 15 diameters of straight pipe is a key retrofit problem. A university facility detail asks for 5 diameters downstream and at least 10 upstream of an insertion mag meter in its first listed case.

Turbine meters: bearings and debris

A turbine meter has a rotor the water spins; speed relates to velocity. Because it has moving parts, a national laboratory guide notes that mechanical meters wear over time, reducing accuracy and reliability. The same guide says to put a strainer upstream to remove debris, that sediment can damage internals, and that accuracy tends to be poor at frequent low flows. It gives turbine straight-run figures of at least 5 diameters upstream and downstream in one section and typically 10 upstream and 5 downstream in another, so read the maker's figure. Signs of trouble are a reading that creeps low over the years, a rotor that stops at low flow, or noise. Closed loops with iron oxide or fresh pipe scale are hard on a rotor. Mag meters have no moving parts to wear.

Glycol and the reading

With glycol in the loop, the fluid is thicker and holds less heat per pound. A velocity reading from a mag meter is the physical flow rate, but any BTU or capacity figure built on it needs the glycol fluid properties, and a turbine rotor may need a viscosity correction from the maker. We could not confirm specific percentage corrections from public sources, so we give none. See Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops and confirm with a fluid test and the maker.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumptions: clean water, 4 inch schedule 40 pipe with inside diameter about 4.026 in, velocity 6 ft/s, and a conversion of 7.48 gallons per cubic foot. We also assume the water velocity stays 6 ft/s when the pipe is partly empty, which is simplified.

  1. Area = 0.7854 x 4.026 x 4.026 = 12.73 square inches, which is 12.73 / 144 = 0.0884 square feet.
  2. Flow = 0.0884 x 6 = 0.530 cubic feet per second.
  3. Gallons per minute = 0.530 x 7.48 x 60 = 238 gpm (full pipe).
  4. If the pipe is partly empty and water covers only 80 percent of the area, the true flow is 0.80 x 238 = 190 gpm.
  5. The meter assumes the full area, so it still reports 238 gpm, which is 48 gpm too high. That is 25 percent over the true flow, or the reading is 1.25 times the truth.

The lesson: a 25 percent error from one flooded-or-not question is bigger than any accuracy spec on the meter. Real partial-fill velocity changes too, so treat this as an illustration, not a correction.

Field cross-checks

  • Pump curve: read pump differential pressure, convert to head, and find that head on the curve. Compare the gpm to the meter. See Reading Pump Curves and System Curves.
  • Clamp-on meter: put a clamp-on ultrasonic meter on the same line, away from the mag meter's disturbances. See Clamp-On Ultrasonic Flow Meters.
  • Other flow methods: see Differential Pressure Flow Meters and BTU Meters and HVAC Submetering.
  • Look first: is the pipe full, is there a strainer, and is the zero stable at no flow?

Field card: Mag and Insertion Turbine Flow Meters

Full pipe, clean ground, maker's straight run.

  • Mag meter voltage is proportional to velocity; gpm = velocity x full area.
  • It needs conductive liquid and a full pipe; air or a half-empty pipe reads high.
  • Grounding rings or electrodes and bonding stop noise and zero shift.
  • Straight run varies by model; use the maker's diameters.
  • Insertion meters read near the tip; depth must follow the maker.
  • Turbine meters wear and foul; fit a strainer.
  • Cross-check with a pump curve or a clamp-on meter.
