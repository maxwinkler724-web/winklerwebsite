Industrial Laundry Plant HVAC: Heat, Humidity, Lint and Soiled-to-Clean Pressure

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A plant that washes linens for hospitals and hotels is one of the hardest rooms to ventilate. Gas-fired dryers and flatwork ironers pour heat into the room, wet linen puts moisture in the air, and lint is everywhere. On top of that, the building has to keep dirty linen air away from clean linen. This lesson walks through the loads, the exhaust, the makeup air and the pressure plan, with a worked example of our own.

Check your own plant

Check the site documents. This page is general education built from government pages read in 2026. A real plant has its own design drawings, fire plan and infection-control rules. Follow those, and the equipment makers' instructions, before anything you read here.

What is in the building

A big linen plant usually has a receiving area for soiled bags, a tunnel washer that moves loads through many compartments, presses or extractors that squeeze out water, large dryers, flatwork ironers for sheets and tablecloths, and folding and packing areas. Hot water and steam or gas heat run all of it. A federal energy-cost handbook for laundry and dry cleaning plants, assembled from detailed energy-use analyses of plants, lists boiler and steam system repairs, insulation, water temperature and dryers among its savings measures. For an HVAC tech, the same list is a map of where heat comes from.

Wash temperatures matter too. The federal infection-control guidance says a wash of at least 160°F for a minimum of 25 minutes is commonly recommended for hot-water washing, and that chlorine bleach becomes active at roughly 135°F to 145°F water. Those hot wash baths, plus the steam or hot water piping that feeds them, warm the room even before the dryers start.

Where the heat and humidity come from

  • Ironers. A flatwork ironer is a long heated surface. Some are steam heated and some are gas heated. Whatever is not carried away by the linen or the ironer's own exhaust ends up in the room as radiant and convective heat.
  • Dryers. Most of a dryer's heat leaves in its exhaust, but the shell, the doors and the loading area give off some. The exhaust is also loaded with moisture and lint.
  • Washers, tanks and piping. Open or loosely covered hot water, wet floors and uninsulated pipes add heat and humidity. Our existing lesson on open hot water tanks covers that evaporation load in detail.
  • Steam leaks. The federal workplace-safety page on heat controls lists stopping steam leaks entirely and insulating hot surfaces. Both cut load and are cheaper than cooling the heat afterward.

Why workers feel it

The federal laundry heat-stress page warns that laundry work can bring heat exhaustion and heat stroke. It lists general and local exhaust ventilation at high-heat points, spot cooling fans, and evaporative cooling or air conditioning as engineering controls. It also calls for hourly checks of temperature and humidity and says acclimatization usually takes up to two weeks. The national occupational-health criteria document adds the physics: when the air is warmer than skin, reduce air temperature or air speed to cut heat gain, and when the air is cooler than skin, more air speed helps the body shed heat. It also names bringing in cooler outside air, shielding workers from radiant sources and air conditioning as controls.

That gives a practical split. Hot air at the ironer delivery end calls for exhaust and shielding. A work station with cooler air blowing on the operator is spot cooling. The room itself gets general ventilation. Our lesson on heat stress for HVAC techs covers the warning signs, which apply to plant staff and to you when you service this equipment.

Dryer exhaust and lint

The federal hospital-laundry fire page ties lint buildup on ceilings and other surfaces to a higher fire risk and names lint collecting in dryer traps as a hazard. Its remedy is routine cleaning of lint from surfaces and emptying lint traps, plus a fire prevention plan and regular maintenance of safeguards on heat-producing equipment. For an HVAC tech, that means:

  • Each dryer needs its own exhaust path to the outdoors, kept clean and unobstructed. Our lesson on commercial laundry rooms covers duct and makeup-air basics, and our dryer vent lesson covers lint fire risk and length limits.
  • Lint collection (filters, screens or collectors) must be cleaned on a schedule the plant sets. Do not leave a collector access panel open or a filter bypassed after service.
  • A federal patent record describes a heat exchanger in the dryer exhaust path that preheats wash water and dries the air. Our own caution: any exchanger in a lint-laden stream clogs unless it is cleanable, so ask who cleans it and how often.
  • Keep lint out of the HVAC too. Our own advice is to check that outdoor air intakes, return grilles and cooling coils near the dryers are not catching lint, because a loaded coil or filter cuts airflow and the room gets hotter.

Makeup air

Every cubic foot of air that dryers, hoods and exhaust fans send outside has to come back in. If it does not, the room goes negative, dryers burn poorly or run longer, doors are hard to open and air is pulled through cracks from places you do not want. Makeup air in a plant like this is also the main way to remove heat: the building brings in outdoor air cooler than the room and exhausts the warm air. The next section shows how much outdoor air that takes.

Place makeup openings so the air passes workers before it reaches the heat sources, and keep them away from dryer exhaust outlets and from the soiled side. In winter, the makeup air needs tempering so it does not chill wet floors and staff. Match the controls so makeup fans and exhaust fans start and stop together.

Soiled side and clean side

The federal infection-control guidance says that areas receiving contaminated textiles should be at negative air pressure relative to the clean areas. The same page says laundry chutes should be kept under negative pressure to stop microorganisms from moving floor to floor. The page gives no pressure difference number. So the design intent is simple to state: air should move from clean to soiled, never the other way. The specific pressure target and air change rate come from the plant's design documents or the health care customer's requirements, not from this page.

In practice, supply and makeup air go mostly to the clean side. Exhaust is pulled mostly from the soiled side and from the dryers. Doors and openings between the zones stay small, and the air flows through them toward soiled. A tech who changes a fan speed, opens a wall panel or props a door can break that flow without anyone noticing. Our building pressure and stack effect lesson explains why doors make this hard, and our cleanroom lesson shows the cascade idea in a stricter setting.

Worked example (our own): outdoor air to carry away the heat

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement of any plant. Gas input is a heat rate in Btu per hour. The rule of thumb for sensible heat in air at standard conditions is Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T, where Q is in Btu per hour, cfm is airflow and delta T is the rise in air temperature in degrees F. Our heat transfer formulas lesson shows where 1.08 comes from.

Assumptions.

  • Flatwork ironer gas input: 800,000 Btu/h (assumed).
  • Share of ironer input released to the room: 15 percent (assumed).
  • Dryer gas input, all dryers running: 1,200,000 Btu/h (assumed).
  • Share of dryer input released to the room through shells, doors and loading: 5 percent (assumed).
  • Allowed air temperature rise between outdoor makeup air and exhaust air leaving the room: 12°F (assumed).

Step 1, heat to the room.

  • Ironer: 800,000 x 0.15 = 120,000 Btu/h
  • Dryers: 1,200,000 x 0.05 = 60,000 Btu/h
  • Total: 120,000 + 60,000 = 180,000 Btu/h

Step 2, solve for airflow. Rearrange to cfm = Q / (1.08 x delta T).

  • 1.08 x 12 = 12.96
  • 180,000 / 12.96 = 13,889 cfm, about 13,900 cfm of outdoor air

Check by working backward. 1.08 x 13,889 x 12 = 180,000 Btu/h. The math closes.

What it tells us. If the allowed rise is cut to 8°F, the same load needs 180,000 / (1.08 x 8) = 180,000 / 8.64 = 20,833 cfm, about 20,800 cfm. A smaller temperature rise means much more air, which is why spot cooling and shielding the hot sources can be cheaper than trying to hold the whole room cool. Remember three limits. The 1.08 factor covers sensible heat only, so it ignores the moisture from wet linen and steam. The result is airflow for heat removal alone, and the dryers, hoods and pressure plan decide how that air must be arranged. And the share-to-room percentages are guesses that a real plant would measure or have estimated by its design engineer.

Field card: Industrial Laundry Plant HVAC

Remove heat at the source, move air clean to soiled, keep lint out of everything.

  • Heat sources: ironers, dryers, hot wash baths, steam and hot water piping, steam leaks.
  • Fix steam leaks and insulate hot surfaces first.
  • Soiled areas stay at negative pressure relative to clean areas; the target comes from the design documents.
  • Each dryer has a clean, unobstructed exhaust path; lint collection stays in service.
  • Makeup air must match exhaust and start with it.
  • Heat balance: Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T (sensible heat only).
  • Our example: 180,000 Btu/h at a 12°F rise needs about 13,900 cfm.
  • Use spot cooling and exhaust at high-heat points; watch for heat illness.
