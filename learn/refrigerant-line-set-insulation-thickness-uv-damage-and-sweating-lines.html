Refrigerant Line Insulation: Thickness, UV Damage and Sweating Lines

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The foam on the copper lines between the indoor and outdoor units looks like a minor accessory until it crumbles in the sun or drips on a ceiling. This lesson covers which lines get insulated, what the equipment makers and a few codes say about thickness, why outdoor foam fails, and how to replace it so the seams stay closed.

Check the maker's document for your model

Check the maker's document for your model. Thickness and material rules differ by brand, model and climate, and codes differ by city and edition. This page was built from the documents listed under Sources. They are examples, not a rule for every job. Follow the installation manual that came with the equipment and your local code.

Why the lines are insulated at all

The suction (vapor) line carries cool, low-pressure gas back to the compressor, so its surface is often colder than the air around it. Cold copper in humid air gets wet, the same way a glass of ice water does. The sweating ducts lesson explains dew point; the same idea applies here. Foam does two jobs: it keeps the surface above the dew point so water does not form, and it slows heat from warm air soaking into the cold gas. Trane's installer guide for its 4TWX6 heat pumps puts it plainly: the vapor line must always be insulated.

Which lines

On a conventional split system, the maker's text I read names the vapor line. Trane's guide asks for it, and the residential code language below is written for vapor (suction) lines only. Mini-split makers go further. Lennox's mini-split manual says to insulate the gas side and the liquid side pipe, with thickness that depends on pipe size. Daikin's ducted split manual says to insulate both the liquid and gas sides. Fujitsu's branch kit manual says to insulate the liquid and gas pipes completely and warns that skipping it can cause condensation or reduced performance. Seattle's code guidance says liquid lines are insulated as the system maker requires, and that mini-split and split systems typically need it. So the rule of thumb is: suction line always, liquid line when the maker says so, and on mini-splits plan on both. For sizing the lines themselves, see the line set sizing lesson.

How thick

There is no single number. These are the figures I could read in primary documents:

  • Daikin (ducted split manual, models FDMFQ): at least 15 mm where conditions reach 30°C and 75% relative humidity, and at least 20 mm at 30°C and 80%.
  • Mitsubishi Electric (optional parts sheet for a distributing pipe kit): at least 12 mm of heat-resistant insulation over the field-supplied pipe.
  • Lennox (M22D mini-split manual): a table that sets thickness by pipe size, one grade thicker in hot, humid conditions, and 0.39 in (10 mm) or more in warm, humid areas. It also says the insulation must tolerate at least 248°F (120°C), and lists EPDM or NBR as materials.
  • Fujitsu (branch kit manual): no thickness on the page I read, but heavier insulation where it is 95°F or warmer with 85% humidity, or 77°F or warmer with 90% humidity.
  • Codes: Seattle's residential rule treats vapor lines as needing R-4 with an outer surface permeance of no more than 0.05 perm. Its commercial amendment lists 1 inch outdoors and 1/2 inch indoors for suction lines. A Florida building official interpretation of the 2010 residential code gives the same R-4 and 0.05 perm for vapor lines. A South Carolina adoption of the model residential section lists R-2.5 instead, so editions and states differ. Check your local adopted code.

The takeaway for an apprentice: humid climates and hot attics push the number up, and the manual for the unit you are working on wins.

What the foam is made of

Most line set foam is closed-cell elastomeric rubber. Armacell's Armaflex data sheet lists thermal conductivity of 0.25 Btu·in/(h·ft²·°F) at a 75°F mean temperature, and water vapor permeability of 0.05 perm-inch. Closed cells are why it works as both an insulator and a moisture retarder.

Why outdoor foam fails

Sunlight is the usual killer. The Armaflex data sheet says that for outdoor use the product must be covered with at least two coats of the maker's water-based finish, or with a suitable cladding, and that the maker gives no guarantee of actual UV performance. Armacell's application manual adds that most of its products need protection within 3 days, that finish paint takes two coats, and that glued seams should face away from the sun. It also says two of its lines (HT/ArmaFlex and NH/ArmaFlex Smart) are UV resistant and do not need extra protection. Lennox's mini-split manual says to wrap with insulating tape where the pipe is exposed to sun. Plain black foam with no coating or cover will chalk, crack and split along its seam, and then the copper sweats in the gap.

Signs the insulation has failed

  • Crumbling, cracked or flaking foam, especially on the sunny side of the run.
  • Open seams or butt joints, and foam pulled back from the service valves or the wall.
  • Water on the line, drips below it, or stained drywall under a line in a wall or ceiling. If the line is iced instead of wet, that is a different problem; see the frozen coil lesson.

Worked example: heat gain through the suction line

Setup (example values, not from a maker's table): a 3/4 inch outside diameter copper suction line, 50 feet long, in 95°F air, with the line surface near 45°F, so the temperature difference is 50°F. Use the Armaflex conductivity of 0.25 Btu·in/(h·ft²·°F) from the data sheet. I treat the copper wall as having no resistance and ignore the air film and sun, so these are a floor, not a total.

  1. Convert to feet: k = 0.25 / 12 = 0.02083 Btu/(h·ft·°F).
  2. Radii: inner r1 = 0.375 in. With a 1/2 inch foam wall, outer r2 = 0.875 in. Ratio r2/r1 = 2.333, and ln(2.333) = 0.847.
  3. Conductance per foot of pipe = 2π × 0.02083 / 0.847 = 0.1545 Btu/(h·ft·°F).
  4. Heat gain per foot = 0.1545 × 50 = 7.7 Btu/h per foot.
  5. For 50 feet: 7.7 × 50 = 386 Btu/h.

The same arithmetic for other wall thicknesses on the same pipe:

  • 3/8 inch wall: 9.4 Btu/h per foot, 472 Btu/h for 50 feet.
  • 1/2 inch wall: 7.7 Btu/h per foot, 386 Btu/h for 50 feet.
  • 3/4 inch wall: 6.0 Btu/h per foot, 298 Btu/h for 50 feet.
  • 1 inch wall: 5.0 Btu/h per foot, 252 Btu/h for 50 feet.

Against a 36,000 Btu/h (3 ton) system, 386 Btu/h is about 1.1%. Going from 1/2 inch to 1 inch saves about 134 Btu/h on this run. Doubling the wall does not halve the loss, because the pipe curves. The sweating risk matters more than the lost capacity: a bare line has no foam resistance, so its surface sits near the gas temperature and below the dew point, and water forms. Foam that is cracked, split or pulled back gives you the same wet spot in a short stretch.

Replacing it

  1. Fix the leak or fault first if there is one. Replacing foam does not require opening the refrigerant circuit. Do not cut or bend the copper; if lines are damaged, that is a different job.
  2. Pull off the old foam and any tape. Wipe the copper dry and clean. Pipelagging's Armaflex guide says the pipe must be dry and in the adhesive's range of about 5°C to 40°C.
  3. Choose the material and thickness from the unit manual and the local code. Use slit tube if the line is already in place, and fit it so the seam is not stretched.
  4. Seal seams. For glued slit tube, apply Armaflex 520 adhesive to both faces, wait until it is touch-dry (about 2 to 5 minutes at 20°C), then press the seam closed. Seal each butt joint with adhesive on both faces and push the ends together. Armacell's application manual says to push glued joints together, not pull them, and not to rely on self-adhesive tape alone for butt or longitudinal joints.
  5. Daikin and Lennox both say to place the seam facing up on the pipe fitting; Lennox also says the insulation must not get thinner at bends or hangers.
  6. Outdoors, protect from sun with two coats of the maker's finish or a cladding as in the data sheet, with seams turned away from the sun.

Wall penetrations and supports

Trane's installer guide says lines passing through a wall or sill should be insulated and isolated, and its checkout list says to seal passages through masonry. A Carrier commercial heat pump manual says to seal the opening so vibration does not pass into the structure and to hang tubes so they do not damage the vapor tube insulation. Daikin says to caulk the gap around insulation and tubes where the outdoor unit sits higher than the indoor unit, so moisture does not run indoors. Keep foam continuous through the hole and do not let a clamp crush it. For line set sizing and run basics, see the mini-split installation lesson.

Field card: Refrigerant Line Insulation

Suction line always, liquid line when the maker says so, seams tight, sun covered.

  • Foam keeps the cold line above the dew point and slows heat gain.
  • Trane says the vapor line must always be insulated; mini-split makers (Lennox, Daikin, Fujitsu) ask for both lines.
  • Thickness is set by the unit manual and humidity. Examples: Daikin 15 to 20 mm, Mitsubishi 12 mm minimum.
  • Outdoors: finish coat or cladding, or a UV-rated foam; seams away from the sun.
  • Seal seams and butt joints with adhesive; keep the same thickness at bends and hangers.
  • Example: 3/4 in line, 1/2 in foam, 50 ft, 50°F difference gains about 386 Btu/h.
  • Sweating, crumbling or open seams mean it is time to replace.
