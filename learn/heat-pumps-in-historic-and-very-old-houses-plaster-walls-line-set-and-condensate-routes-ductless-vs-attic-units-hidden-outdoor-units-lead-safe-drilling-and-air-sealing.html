Heat Pumps in Historic and Very Old Houses: Plaster Walls, Hidden Routes, Lead-Safe Cutting

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An old house is built to be looked at as much as lived in. Plaster walls, wide trim, painted woodwork and tight closets all stand between you and a clean heat pump install. This lesson covers how techs and homeowners route refrigerant lines, pick between ductless heads and a small ducted unit, hide the outdoor unit, cut into painted surfaces safely, and seal air leaks without trapping moisture in the walls.

Start with the house, not the equipment

Historic and very old houses were usually built to leak and to dry out. The federal historic preservation guidance on energy efficiency in historic buildings says cutting air leakage should be the first priority of a preservation retrofit plan, and it also says a tightened, uninsulated wood building can lose the drying that used to protect it. So the order of work matters: fix water problems, then seal air, then size the heat pump to the smaller load. If your house is in a historic district or has a preservation easement, rules may limit what you can change on the outside or cut into inside. We cannot tell you what those rules are. Ask your local historic preservation office before anyone drills, and ask what they want to see on paper.

Plaster and lath walls

Plaster over wood lath is brittle. Vibration from a hammer drill or an oversize core bit can crack keys (the plaster pushed between the lath strips) and leave a hairline crack across the room. That damage is slow and costly to mend, so the habits that matter are practical ones (our own field advice, not a cited standard): we use and teach: find studs and chases with a scanner before marking, drill a small pilot hole first, use a sharp bit at low speed, tape the surface so it does not spall, and cut the smallest hole that clears the line set and the drain. Wall-mounted heads also help here, because a single 3 inch or smaller hole is often all a head needs. Plan to patch with plaster-compatible material rather than rock patches wherever a hole will show.

Lead-safe practices when you cut painted surfaces

This is the part that cannot be skipped. A federal environmental agency web page for homeowners says roughly three quarters of U.S. homes built before 1978 still contain some lead-based paint, and that disturbing painted surfaces can release lead dust. The same page says paid contractors working in these homes must be certified in lead-safe work practices, and that homeowners doing their own work on their own home are not covered by the rule but are told to follow lead-safe methods anyway. Rules differ if you rent or run a child-care setting there.

The agency's booklet on lead-safe renovation lists what to do before and during a cut or drill:

  • Cover the floor with plastic sheeting that reaches at least 6 feet out from the work, keep people and pets out, and close the windows and doors.
  • Seal ducts in the work area with taped plastic so dust does not travel through the system.
  • Mist the surface before drilling or cutting, except within 1 foot of a live electrical outlet.
  • Score the paint with a utility knife before separating trim, and pry parts apart instead of pounding.
  • Clean daily with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes, and do a final cleaning check at the end of the job.
  • Never burn or torch paint, never run high-speed sanding or grinding tools without a shroud and HEPA vacuum, and never use a heat gun above 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit on paint.

The booklet also describes a small-job exception for disturbing 6 square feet or less of interior paint per room, but it notes that window replacement and demolition are not exempt. Whether a given job qualifies is a call for a certified renovator, not for this page. For asbestos on old pipe and duct wrap, which you may meet in the same basement, read Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems.

Routing line sets and condensate

In a house without a clear wall cavity, the route is the whole job. These are the usual paths, from least to most invasive:

  • Closets. A stacked closet, one above the other, is a natural chase. Run the line set, drain and control cable up the back corner and hide it behind a removable panel.
  • Basements and crawl spaces. Run the lines horizontally between joists, then up through an existing chase, such as an old chimney flue that is no longer used. Check the flue is truly abandoned and not shared with a boiler or water heater.
  • Attics. Attic runs are easy to hide but need insulation on the cold suction line and a plan for water if a drain pan overflows.

Condensate is the quiet problem. A head or air handler makes water in cooling mode and a drain must slope down with no dips. Where gravity cannot do it, a small condensate pump or a drain that ties to an existing floor drain are the usual fixes, and the pump needs a safety switch. A drain that freezes or clogs in an attic can stain old plaster ceilings, so add a secondary pan or float switch above finished rooms. Always follow the maker's manual for slope, trap and line rules.

Ductless heads or a small ducted unit in the attic

A federal energy department HVAC guide says ductless heat pumps can provide zone heating and cooling without ducts, that up to eight indoor units can connect to one outdoor unit, and that installing ducts in an existing home may not be worth the cost. In an old house the choice usually comes down to this:

  • Ductless heads need only a small hole and a wall surface. The trade-off is that a box sits on the wall of a room people care about, and rooms without a head get less conditioning. A government case study of ductless homes noted that systems usually do not have heads in every room and that bathrooms, with no head, ran more humid.
  • A small ducted unit in the attic keeps the room look clean, with only small ceiling or wall grilles. The cost is attic space, duct sealing and insulation. The federal historic preservation guidance says ducts in unconditioned space should be sealed and insulated, and that air leaks can waste as much as 35 percent of conditioned air. It also recommends treating an attic that holds equipment as conditioned space where possible, which helps stop condensation on the equipment.

For how cassettes and ducted mini-splits compare with wall heads, see Ceiling Cassette and Ducted Mini-Splits vs Wall-Mounted Heads. For houses with no ducts at all, see Adding AC to a House Without Ducts, and for a house that already has a steam boiler, see Adding Heat Pumps to a House with Steam Heat.

Outdoor unit placement out of view

The federal energy guide says outdoor units are mounted on a wall or on a concrete or stone pad outside the house. For a historic exterior, the usual goals are the rear or a side yard, behind a fence or planting, and not on a front elevation. Keep the line set run short, because long runs cost capacity and charge adjustments (see the example below). Leave clearance for airflow and service as the maker states, keep the unit above the snow line and out from under roof drip, and do not box it in so tightly that it recirculates its own air. Ask the local preservation office whether any screening or location approval is needed.

Air sealing without trapping moisture

A federal historic preservation brief on controlling unwanted moisture lists the sources: roof and wall leaks, ground water, plumbing leaks, indoor activity and climate control, and construction water. It warns that sealing a building so completely that it cannot ventilate is the worst approach, and that vapor retarders are debated by experts. A university extension air sealing guide says to seal larger gaps with foam rather than caulk when the gap is 1/4 inch or more, to run a blower door test before major sealing, and that sealing around gas appliances can starve them of combustion air and make carbon monoxide.

The federal historic preservation energy guidance adds specific cautions for old buildings: do not caulk the underside of clapboards, so trapped water can escape; spray foam bonds tightly to old materials and its expansion can damage plaster; and mechanical ventilation may be needed after sealing. It also says to fix existing moisture problems first. A heat pump that dehumidifies in summer can help, but the moisture brief warns to manage existing moisture before adding dehumidifying systems, and to check that a dehumidifier is not pulling water through foundation walls. For attic sealing details in a typical house, read Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for New Jersey Homes.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own example, not a real house. Every input below is an assumption chosen to show the method. We are using a simple heat loss method: loss in Btu/h equals the sum of (U times area) plus air leakage, all multiplied by the temperature difference.

Assumptions: inside 70 F, outside design 10 F, so a difference of 60 F. Uninsulated plaster-and-lath wall U = 0.25. Windows with storm panels U = 0.50. Attic ceiling over the bedroom U = 0.30. Ceiling heights 9 ft. Air leakage 0.8 air changes per hour (ACH) in the parlor and study, and 1.0 in the bedroom. The air factor is 1.08, which is 0.075 lb/ft³ times 0.24 Btu/lb-F times 60 minutes.

Parlor. Wall 96 x 0.25 = 24.0. Windows 30 x 0.50 = 15.0. Air: 2,268 x 0.8 / 60 = 30.2 cfm; 1.08 x 30.2 = 32.7. Sum 71.7 Btu/h-F, times 60 = 4,302, about 4,300 Btu/h.

Bedroom. Wall 88 x 0.25 = 22.0. Windows 20 x 0.50 = 10.0. Ceiling 168 x 0.30 = 50.4. Air: 1,512 x 1.0 / 60 = 25.2 cfm; 1.08 x 25.2 = 27.2. Sum 109.6, times 60 = 6,576, about 6,600 Btu/h.

Study. Wall 162 x 0.25 = 40.5. Windows 36 x 0.50 = 18.0. Air: 1,080 x 0.8 / 60 = 14.4 cfm; 1.08 x 14.4 = 15.6. Sum 74.1, times 60 = 4,446, about 4,400 Btu/h.

Total load is about 4,300 + 6,600 + 4,400 = 15,300 Btu/h.

Matching heads. Assume heads come in 6,000, 9,000 and 12,000 Btu/h nominal sizes, and assume each delivers 80 percent of nominal at the design outdoor temperature (read the maker's real table for your model). That gives 4,800, 7,200 and 9,600 Btu/h. Parlor 4,300 fits the 6,000 head (4,800 is 112 percent of the load). Bedroom 6,600 fits the 9,000 head (7,200 is 109 percent). Study 4,400 fits a 6,000 head (4,800 is 109 percent). Nominal total is 6,000 + 9,000 + 6,000 = 21,000 Btu/h. With an assumed 24,000 Btu/h outdoor unit delivering 80 percent, 19,200 Btu/h is more than the 15,300 load, so it passes. Do not jump to a bigger head for safety. Oversized heads cycle and dehumidify poorly.

Line set check. Assume the longest run is 18 ft horizontal in the basement, 12 ft up the closet chase and 8 ft across the attic, so 18 + 12 + 8 = 38 ft. Assume the maker allows 50 ft of line and 30 ft of height difference. 38 ft is under 50 (12 ft to spare) and 12 ft of lift is under 30. Assume the unit is pre-charged for 25 ft and the maker adds 0.3 oz per extra foot: 38 - 25 = 13 ft, and 13 x 0.3 = 3.9 oz of refrigerant to add by weight. See Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments. Real numbers come from the installation manual of the actual model.

Field card: Heat pumps in old houses

Ask the preservation office, cut lead-safe, seal air without trapping water.

  • Ask the local historic preservation office about outside changes before work starts.
  • Pre-1978 paint may hold lead: mist, contain, HEPA vacuum, no torches or hot heat guns.
  • Small pilot hole, sharp bit, low speed in plaster and lath.
  • Closets, basements and attics are the usual routes; slope every drain down.
  • Wall heads need small holes; attic ducted units hide better but need sealed, insulated ducts.
  • Size heads to each room load, not to the biggest size available.
  • Check line length and lift against the maker's table, and weigh in extra charge.
  • Fix water problems first, then seal air; ventilate and watch for moisture and combustion air.
