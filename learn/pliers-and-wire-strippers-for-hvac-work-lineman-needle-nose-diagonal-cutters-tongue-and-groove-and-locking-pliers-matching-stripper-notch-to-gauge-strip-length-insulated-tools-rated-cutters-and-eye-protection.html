Pliers and Wire Strippers for HVAC Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A new tech can wire a whole control circuit with six hand tools that fit in a pouch. Most of the damage we see comes from using one of them for another one's job: pliers on a fitting, a dull stripper on a thin conductor, or bare-handled cutters near live parts. This lesson sorts out which plier does what and how to strip wire cleanly.

What each plier is for

  • Lineman's pliers. A flat-nosed, heavy-duty pair with a cutter at the pivot. They grip, twist and cut copper conductors. They are the general electrical plier for panels and disconnects.
  • Needle-nose pliers. Long, narrow jaws for forming a small hook or loop on a conductor and for placing a wire in a tight spot. They are for light work, not for heavy twisting.
  • Diagonal cutters. Side cutters whose angled jaws cut conductors flush, such as trimming a lead or a zip tie.
  • Tongue-and-groove pliers. Adjustable-jaw pliers for gripping round or odd-shaped parts. Useful for holding, not for tightening.
  • Locking pliers. Pliers that clamp and stay clamped, handy as a third hand or to hold a damaged part while you work.
  • Wire strippers. A jaw with notches sized by wire gauge, which removes insulation without cutting the copper.

Strippers: match the notch to the gauge

Each notch on a stripper is marked with a gauge number. Pick the notch that matches the conductor, close the jaws and pull the insulation off. A notch that is too small cuts into the copper. A notch that is too large leaves a film of insulation on the strands. Nicked strands weaken the conductor and can break later from vibration, so after stripping we look at the bare copper: it should be bright, round and without cuts. If we see a nick, we cut the end off and strip again. Our own habit: for thermostat cable, we check the gauge printed on the jacket or the conductor first, instead of guessing by eye.

We then strip to the terminal's length. Terminal blocks and equipment labels often show a strip-length mark or give the length in the installation instructions. Too short and the clamp bites insulation; too long and bare copper is left exposed beside the next terminal. See the wire terminations lesson for crimp terminals, connectors and tug tests, which we do not repeat here.

Insulated tools for electrical work

Federal electrical safety rules say that workers near exposed energized conductors or circuit parts must use insulated tools or handling equipment when the tools might contact those parts, and that the insulation must be protected if it can be damaged (federal workplace safety rule, 29 CFR 1910.335). A university safety fact sheet adds two practical points: use pliers with insulated grips for electrical wiring, and check the coverings for cracks or holes before using them. Our rule: a cracked or chewed grip is not insulation any more, so we tag the tool out of electrical use. Insulated grips are a backup. We still de-energize and test first, as taught in Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Pliers are not wrenches

Two university fact sheets say the same thing: do not use pliers in place of a wrench, because they chew up bolt heads and do not grip nuts and bolts securely, so they slip. One of them adds not to use a pipe wrench on nuts or bolts and not to add a pipe extension to a wrench for leverage. A slipping tool is how knuckles get cut and how brass fittings get rounded off. Federal rules put the responsibility on the employer for the safe condition of tools, including those workers bring themselves (federal workplace safety rule, 29 CFR 1910.242). The federal hand-tool booklet states a general rule that fits: use the right tool for the job, keep tools in good condition and check them for damage before use. For flare nuts, packing nuts and hex fittings, we use the correct wrench. Screws and nut drivers belong to the screwdrivers lesson.

Cutting: use the cutter that is rated for it

Ordinary lineman's and diagonal cutters are made for copper conductors. Hardened steel, such as a bolt, a spring or a screw, and steel wire rope, as on a hanger cable, can chip or spring the cutting edges. We only cut those with a cutter whose own marking or instructions say it is rated for the material. If it is not marked for it, we do not use it. Sheet metal cuts use snips, covered in the hand sheet metal lesson, and general tool hazards are in the hand and power tool safety lesson.

Eye protection when cutting wire

A university fact sheet says to always wear safety glasses while cutting wire, to hold the far end so the cut piece does not fly off, and to clamp the wire in a vise when cutting it with pliers. Federal rules require suitable eye protection when workers are exposed to eye or face hazards from flying particles (federal workplace safety rule, 29 CFR 1910.133). A snipped copper end is a small, fast particle, and it is usually aimed at your face when you cut overhead in a ceiling or air handler. Wear the glasses before the first cut.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions (ours, not from a source). A new thermostat and a condenser are wired with 18-gauge solid conductors. The thermostat cable has five conductors. The thermostat terminals call for 1/4 inch of bare copper. The air handler control board calls for 3/8 inch. The condenser control cable has two conductors. Both ends call for 1/4 inch. Check the real strip lengths on the labels of your equipment.

  1. Thermostat cable, thermostat end: 5 conductors x 1/4 in = 1.25 in.
  2. Thermostat cable, board end: 5 conductors x 3/8 in = 1.875 in.
  3. Condenser cable, both ends: 2 conductors x 2 ends = 4 strips; 4 x 1/4 in = 1.0 in.
  4. Count of strips: 5 + 5 + 4 = 14 strips.
  5. Total stripped length: 1.25 + 1.875 + 1.0 = 4.125 in of bare copper.

That is 14 strips on this job, and each one is a chance to nick a strand. With the stripper notch set for 18 gauge, we strip, look, and re-strip any end that shows a nick. Bare copper beyond the stated lengths would mean a strip that is too long.

Field card: Pliers and Wire Strippers for HVAC Work

Right tool, right notch, right length, glasses on.

  • Lineman's for grip and cut; needle-nose for small loops; diagonals for flush cuts.
  • Tongue-and-groove and locking pliers hold; they do not tighten fittings.
  • Match the stripper notch to the gauge; re-strip any nicked end.
  • Strip to the terminal's stated length.
  • Insulated grips: inspect for cracks or holes; they do not replace testing dead.
  • Hardened steel or wire rope only with a cutter rated for it.
  • Safety glasses on before cutting wire; hold the far end.
