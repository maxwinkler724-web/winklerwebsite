Chilled Beams: Active vs Passive and Condensation Control

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chilled beam cools a room with water-filled coils hung at the ceiling, with little or no fan. The trick that makes it work is also the thing that can ruin it: the water must stay warmer than the room dew point, or the beam sweats.

Check the manual first

Check the manual first. This page is general education built from maker literature and engineering articles read in 2026. Beams differ by model. Follow the installation manual for the exact unit and the engineer's design for the building, and have only qualified people work on the system.

The basic idea

A beam is a finned coil in a housing, fed with chilled water that is much warmer than the water a typical air handler coil sees. It gives sensible cooling only, which means it lowers air temperature but removes almost no moisture. A facilities-engineering article says the supply water must be raised several degrees above the design space dew point, and that a separate dedicated outdoor air unit must handle dehumidification. If the terms are fuzzy, read Psychrometrics for Technicians first.

Passive beams: natural convection

A passive beam has no air supply. Room air touching the cold coil gets denser and sinks out of the beam, and warmer air rises to take its place. That slow loop is natural convection. A beam design article lists passive output around 300 to 500 watts per linear meter, which is roughly 310 to 520 Btu/h per foot. It places passive beams in offices, libraries, lobbies and conference spaces. An HPAC article calls them limited in hot, humid climates and mostly useful as a supplement where an active beam falls short.

Active beams: induction with primary air

An active beam is connected to a duct. Treated primary air, the ventilation air from the dedicated outdoor air system (DOAS), is forced through small nozzles at high velocity. That jet pulls room air across the coil, a process called induction. HPAC describes mixing at roughly one part primary air to two to four parts induced room air. The design article gives active output of about 500 to 800 W per meter, about 520 to 830 Btu/h per foot, and lists classrooms, hospital rooms, dormitories and hotels. The tradeoffs are more noise, and drafts if primary airflow is too high. Because primary air carries the ventilation, the room needs less ductwork than an all-air system such as VAV.

Why dew point controls everything

Condensation forms on any surface colder than the dew point of the air touching it. Price Industries points out that beam coils are only about two rows deep, so fins can run well below dew point before drops appear, and that uninsulated coil connections, piping and the control valve often sweat before the fins do. Air handler coils are commonly six to eight rows deep, so they behave differently. Price notes that room air is typically 50 percent relative humidity or less, that supply water is often held around 58 °F, and that most air systems deliver primary air at a dew point of 55 °F or lower.

Three jobs keep a beam dry:

  • Dry primary air. The DOAS removes ventilation moisture and helps carry the room's own latent load. Price says ventilation is usually the largest moisture load. The TROX manual says primary air must be dry enough, and supplied in enough quantity, that the room dew point stays below the chilled water temperature, and that prolonged operation below dew point must be avoided.
  • Watch the room. Price lists room humidity sensors and condensation sensors on exposed supply piping near the beam as common strategies. HPAC adds that a pipe sensor should sit at the coldest pipe location and, if it detects moisture, zone water should shut off until it dries.
  • Raise the water if needed. Resetting supply temperature from outdoor conditions can help where infiltration is poorly controlled, but Price says it should not be the only control, and HPAC advises against relying on outdoor conditions alone in hot, humid climates.

Open windows, a missed night humidity setback and a DOAS that trips off are the usual ways a dry building turns wet. Facilitiesnet and Price both flag unoccupied hours and building startup as risky times.

Worked example: margin and capacity

Part 1, the dew point margin. A room is held at 75 °F and 50 percent relative humidity, and the chilled water supply is 58 °F. The Magnus formula (using one common constant set, b = 17.67 and c = 243.5 °C; Lawrence's 2005 paper in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society explains the formula) gives the dew point.

  1. 75 °F = (75 − 32) × 5/9 = 23.89 °C.
  2. γ = ln(0.50) + (17.67 × 23.89) / (243.5 + 23.89) = −0.693 + 1.579 = 0.886.
  3. Dew point = 243.5 × 0.886 / (17.67 − 0.886) = 12.9 °C, about 55.1 °F.
  4. Margin = 58 − 55.1 = 2.9 °F.

Now let the room drift to 55 percent. γ = −0.598 + 1.579 = 0.981, dew point = 243.5 × 0.981 / (17.67 − 0.981) = 14.3 °C, about 57.7 °F, and the margin shrinks to 0.3 °F. Five points of humidity ate almost all of it, and pipes and valves can run colder than the beam itself. That is why designers watch humidity so closely.

Part 2, passive capacity. Titus lists a passive beam, 8 ft long and 18 in. wide, at 2,327 Btu/h sensible with 1.0 gpm of water, based on an 18 °F difference between entering air and entering water. Titus says entering air is typically 2 to 3 °F above room temperature, so take 75 + 3 = 78 °F air against 58 °F water, a 20 °F difference. Titus's multiplier for 20 °F is 1.11.

  1. Capacity = 2,327 × 1.11 = 2,583 Btu/h.
  2. Per foot = 2,583 / 8 = 323 Btu/h per foot, inside the 310 to 520 range above.
  3. Water temperature rise = 2,583 / (500 × 1.0 gpm) = 5.2 °F, using the usual 500 factor for water.

Leaving water is about 63 °F. Note the small water-side delta-T: the facilities article says beam systems run a significantly lower delta-T than fan coil or VAV systems, which raises pumping energy. Compare Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T and Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained. This is a learning example at the maker's test conditions; a real job uses the engineer's selection.

Where beams fit and where they do not

Beams suit rooms with high sensible loads and modest moisture, such as offices, labs, classrooms and hospital rooms. The facilities article warns that they cannot dehumidify, need at least one beam per room, and are less effective at heating. HPAC says the envelope must be tight and occupant and process moisture moderate. Spaces with big latent loads or leaky older buildings are poor fits. Compare fan coil units, which have a drain pan and filter; the facilities article notes beams have neither, so there is less to maintain, but also no pan to catch a condensation problem.

Maintenance

TROX describes its beams as having no moving or consumable parts: inspect for leaks and clean the coil, grille and any drip trays as needed, annually at first until a pattern is clear. To reach the coil, release the sliding pins, lower the grille on its safety wires and vacuum toward the coil with a soft brush attachment. TROX says to use two people on beams longer than 4 ft and not to lift a beam by its water pipes. For nozzles, TROX gives primary airflow as CFM = C × √ΔP, with ΔP read at a pressure tap on the nozzle plate, so primary air can be checked without opening the nozzles. A University of Michigan guide specification calls for coils removable from the room side and grilles that open without special tools.

Field card: Chilled Beams

Sensible cooling only: keep the water above the room dew point.

  • Passive: natural convection, no primary air, about 300 to 500 W/m per one design article.
  • Active: primary air through nozzles induces room air, about 500 to 800 W/m.
  • Supply water is often around 58 °F per Price; the margin over dew point can be only a few degrees.
  • The DOAS must supply dry air; use room humidity and pipe condensation sensors.
  • Water-side delta-T is small, so expect higher flow.
  • Not for high latent loads or leaky buildings.
  • Coil cleaning: lower the grille on its safety wires, vacuum gently.
