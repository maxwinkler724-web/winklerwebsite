What an HVAC Tune-Up Should Include: A Homeowner Checklist

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tune-up is only worth booking if you know what it is supposed to contain. This lesson lays out what ENERGY STAR and the ACCA maintenance standard say a visit should cover, what you can do between visits, and how to read the paperwork you are handed afterward.

Safety first

Anything inside the furnace or boiler cabinet, the gas line, the flue or the refrigerant circuit belongs to a licensed technician. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside to fresh air and call 911 from outside. The homeowner tasks below stay on the outside of the equipment.

How often: fall for heat, spring for cooling

ENERGY STAR's maintenance checklist says to check the heating system in the fall and the cooling system in the spring, and its heating and cooling page recommends a yearly tune-up. If you have a single heat pump that does both jobs, the same logic suggests a check before each hard season. ACCA's standard, as summarized in trade press and ACCA's own blog, lists a recommended minimum frequency for each task and allows seasonal systems to be serviced twice a year. Usage can change the schedule, so a home where the equipment runs hard year round may want visits more often than a seldom-used vacation home.

What the visit should include

ENERGY STAR splits its typical check-up into general items, cooling items and heating items.

  • General: check thermostat settings; tighten electrical connections and measure motor voltage and current; lubricate moving parts; inspect the condensate drain; check system controls, including startup and shutdown.
  • Cooling (spring): clean the evaporator and condenser coils; check the refrigerant level and adjust if needed; clean and adjust blower components.
  • Heating (fall): check gas or oil connections, gas pressure, burner combustion and the heat exchanger. ENERGY STAR calls improper gas or oil connections a fire hazard.

The Department of Energy's air conditioner page adds that a technician should check for the correct amount of refrigerant, test for leaks with a leak detector and measure airflow through the evaporator coil.

What ACCA Standard 4 adds

ACCA Standard 4 (ANSI/ACCA 4 QM, Maintenance of Residential HVAC Systems) is the industry's own minimum task list. ACHR News reported that ANSI approved its reaffirmation as 2019 (R2024) in August 2024, and that the task list lets consumers compare any extra corrective work a contractor proposes against a common baseline. The same article describes the tasks as recommended minimums. The Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center, run by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, hosts a page describing the standard.

The detailed text we read was the 2013 edition, so newer editions may differ; treat this as a picture of the idea, not the current wording. In that edition the gas furnace checklist asks for measured values, not just a tick: line voltage, motor amperage, airflow across the heat exchanger, inlet gas pressure, manifold pressure, flame signal, combustion analysis results and the temperature difference across the heat exchanger. The cooling checklists ask for the temperature split across the evaporator coil, static pressure, line voltage, compressor and condenser fan amperage compared with nameplate values, and, when airflow is right but the split is not, a refrigerant charge check by the manufacturer's method. That edition lists superheat or subcooling as good practice for the charge check, not as a mandatory item.

It also has a documentation section. The contractor records measurements, observations and recommended corrective actions, including the equipment inventory, the checklist tasks done and any deviations, code violations, external conditions that affect the system, components that could not be reached, and corrective actions authorized. The customer is to be told what was found, what was done, what is recommended and what it costs to complete it, and if an unsafe condition is found the equipment is shut off and the owner notified in writing.

What to ask for in writing

  1. The list of tasks that will be done, matched to the checklist above.
  2. Recorded readings, with units, for the items that are measurements: voltage, amps, temperature split, gas pressures, combustion results.
  3. The manufacturer or nameplate value next to each reading, so you can see whether it is in range.
  4. Anything not done or not reachable, and why.
  5. Recommended repairs listed separately from the tune-up, each with a reason.

For background on what the combustion and charge readings mean, see Combustion Analyzer Readings and Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Gaps worth noticing in a cheap offer

We could not load consumer-protection guidance from the FTC, so this section does not rely on it. Instead use the checklists above as a yardstick. If an offer cannot tell you which tasks are included, if the visit produces no readings you can see, or if the paperwork is one line such as "system checked, OK," you cannot tell whether the ENERGY STAR items were covered. Ask the questions in the previous section before booking, and compare repair recommendations against the task list. Your state attorney general or consumer affairs office is the place to check local rules.

What you can do yourself

  • Filter. ENERGY STAR lists the air filter as the homeowner task and says to inspect, clean or change it once a month. The Department of Energy says every month or two during the cooling season, more often with heavy use, dust or pets. Follow your equipment's instructions; see How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV.
  • Outdoor unit. The Department of Energy says condenser coils get very dirty where it is dusty or foliage is nearby, and advises keeping debris away and trimming plants back at least 2 feet. Turn the unit off at the disconnect before pulling debris, and leave coil washing to the technician.

Worked example: reading a sample service record

This record is made up for teaching. Suppose a spring cooling visit leaves you this:

  • Return air 76 F, supply air 58 F
  • Compressor current 15.8 A; nameplate rated load amps 17.6 A
  • Condenser coil: cleaned
  • Refrigerant: "checked OK"
  • Condensate drain: no entry

Step 1, temperature split: 76 - 58 = 18 F. The record gives a number, but you still need the manufacturer's target to judge it, and the ACCA text compares the split with the manufacturer's value, so ask for that target to be written beside it.

Step 2, current against nameplate: 15.8 / 17.6 = 0.898, about 90 percent of the rated load. Reading below nameplate is the kind of comparison the standard describes; it only means something because both numbers are on the page.

Step 3, compare the rest with the checklist. ENERGY STAR's cooling items include coil cleaning (done), refrigerant level (the entry has no readings, so you cannot tell how it was checked), the condensate drain (no entry), and electrical connections and controls (no entry). The honest summary is: two items documented with numbers, one cleaned, three missing or vague. The next call is not to accuse anyone but to ask: "Can you add the drain and connection checks, and the pressures or temperatures behind the refrigerant check?"

Want to see how a cooling charge check is actually done? Read Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Keep the paperwork. It also helps when you decide between repair and replacement; see How Long Do HVAC Systems Last? Repair or Replace, and for heat-season prep see Heating System Checklist Before the First Cold Snap.

Field card: What an HVAC Tune-Up Should Include

If it is not written down with a number, you cannot check it.

  • Heat check in the fall, cooling check in the spring; ENERGY STAR recommends a yearly tune-up.
  • General: thermostat, electrical connections, motor volts and amps, lubrication, condensate drain, controls.
  • Cooling: clean coils, check refrigerant, clean and adjust blower.
  • Heating: gas or oil connections, gas pressure, burner combustion, heat exchanger.
  • Ask for readings with units and the manufacturer value beside each.
  • You: filter monthly or as the manual says; keep 2 feet of clearance around the outdoor unit.
