Cascade Refrigeration Systems for Very Low Temperatures: Stages, Start Order and Service

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A walk-in freezer holds around zero. An environmental test chamber or an ultra-low lab freezer has to hold far colder than that, and one compressor and one refrigerant cannot do it well. The answer is a cascade: two separate refrigeration circuits that share a heat exchanger. This lesson explains why one stage runs out of room, how the two circuits hand heat to each other, the start order, the expansion tank, refrigerant rules, oil at low temperature, and the service problems we see on paper most often.

Why one stage runs out of room

A compressor squeezes vapor from the suction (evaporator) pressure up to the discharge (condensing) pressure. The compression ratio is the absolute discharge pressure divided by the absolute suction pressure. As the evaporator gets colder, the suction pressure falls, so the same discharge pressure gives a bigger ratio. A trade-press article on very low temperature systems says a single stage is limited by the compressor's compression ratio and the ambient temperature, and reaches only about -40 to -50 F, so anything colder needs a cascade.

A big ratio hurts in two ways. The compressor pumps less vapor per stroke, so capacity drops. And it works the gas harder, so discharge temperature rises. Our lesson on discharge line temperature shows how to read that, and the lesson on liquid injection on low-temperature compressors covers one way to hold it down. A maker design guide for ultra-low freezers (plain-text citation) gives a sense of scale: evaporating near -90 C while condensing near +40 C is beyond standard compressor design, and the guide aims for a low-stage ratio below 14 with an evaporating pressure of about 1 to 2 bar absolute. It also notes that compressors with high shell temperatures, above 80 C, should run at a lower ratio.

Two circuits, one shared heat exchanger

A cascade splits the temperature lift. The high stage looks like an ordinary refrigeration system: compressor, condenser, expansion device and an evaporator. But its evaporator is not in the cold space. It is the cascade heat exchanger. The low stage uses a different refrigerant that can still make useful pressure at very low temperatures. Its compressor discharges into the other side of the cascade heat exchanger, where it condenses, so that side is the low-stage condenser. The liquid then goes through its own expansion device to the real evaporator in the cold space. The same article describes the low side as able to condense at roughly -20 to -30 F and evaporate as low as -120 F.

In short: low-stage condenser = high-stage evaporator. The high stage carries away the low stage's heat plus the heat of its compressor work and rejects it all to ambient. Because it does the final rejection, the high-stage compressor is usually the larger of the two in the load it carries. The maker guide says the temperature held in this exchanger (it calls it the intercooler) strongly affects capacity and thermal balance, and that setting it too low can starve the low stage of refrigerant.

Start order: high stage first

The low-stage condenser is the high-stage evaporator, so the low stage can only reject its heat once the high stage is pulling that exchanger down. If the low stage starts first, its discharge pressure climbs with nothing to condense it. The maker guide says to start the high-temperature compressor first and bring the low-stage compressor on after a delay, which also avoids a very high starting current from both at once. Controls usually do this with a time delay or a temperature sensor on the cascade exchanger. We say "usually" because the exact scheme differs by maker; follow the sequence on the unit's wiring diagram.

The expansion tank and standstill pressure

When the system is off and warms to room temperature, all the low-stage refrigerant boils off and the pressure rises sharply. The trade-press article says low-stage refrigerants can exceed 700 psig when warmed to room temperature, and says the gas pressure is usually kept to no more than 250 psig. To get there, an expansion tank (or other system volume) gives the charge room to vaporize. That tank connects to the low-stage vapor side, as drawn above. Charging is often done by bringing the empty system up to a static pressure first. The 250 psig figure is a design target from that article, not a limit we can promise for any unit; the nameplate and manual rule.

Refrigerant choices under the federal rules

We describe refrigerants by type, not by product. EPA's Significant New Alternatives Policy (SNAP) program keeps a list of substitutes for the end-use called very low temperature refrigeration. In the 2015 final rule EPA described that equipment as intended to hold temperatures considerably lower than food refrigeration, for example -80 C (-170 F) or lower. Entries on that list that matter to cascades include:

  • High stage: common blends and pure fluids of the R-404A, R-507 and R-407C types, and propane (R-290) with use conditions.
  • Low stage: R-23 and the R-508A and R-508B blends, carbon dioxide (R-744), ethane (R-170) with use conditions, and ethylene (R-1150) with use conditions and narrowed use limits, listed in 2023.

The SNAP page says Federal Register notices take precedence over the table. For ethane, the 2015 rule says it is acceptable in new equipment only, subject to use conditions that include compliance with Standard 471 for commercial refrigerators and freezers, a charge-size limit, and marked pipes and hoses with warning labels. Ethane, propane and ethylene are flammable, so treat them under the flammable-refrigerant rules in that notice. Also check whether a given refrigerant is still available to buy; SNAP says what is acceptable to use, not what is sold. Our lesson on compressor oils covers matching oil to refrigerant.

Oil return at low temperature

Standard oil becomes very thick when cold. The trade-press article says hydrocarbon additives of 5 to 10 percent by weight help keep oil fluid, that oil return works down to about -100 F evaporator temperature, and that long runs or complex piping call for an oil separator after the low-stage compressor. The maker guide agrees on the separator and adds that oil can freeze and block a capillary tube. See also oil separators and oil management. Moisture is worse here too: the article says excess moisture separates from the refrigerant and clogs capillary tubes, so drier care matters most on the low stage.

Worked example (our own): one stage versus two

This is our own example. Every pressure below is an assumption chosen to show the arithmetic, not a table value for any refrigerant. All pressures are absolute (psia).

Assumptions:

  • Single stage: suction 10 psia, discharge 250 psia (assumed).
  • Cascade low stage: suction 10 psia, discharge 70 psia (assumed).
  • Cascade high stage: suction 20 psia, discharge 250 psia (assumed).
  • The two stages use different refrigerants, so the pressures do not come from one chart.

Step 1, single stage. 250 / 10 = 25.

Step 2, low stage. 70 / 10 = 7.

Step 3, high stage. 250 / 20 = 12.5.

Step 4, compare. The worst ratio falls from 25 to 12.5, about half. Each compressor now works with a ratio inside a range ordinary machines are built for, and neither stage heats its gas as much as the single stage would. (A rule of thumb that the maker guide aims for, a low-stage ratio under 14, is met by both stages here.) Note the ratios do not multiply to 25, because the stages use different fluids.

Common service problems

These are our own general observations, framed from how the system works; always follow the maker's manual.

  • Low-stage high head because the high stage is weak. If the high stage cannot pull the cascade exchanger down (dirty condenser, low charge, failed fan, worn compressor), the low stage cannot condense and its discharge pressure climbs. Fix the high stage first.
  • Frosting. Frost on the low-stage liquid line or suction line beyond where it should be, or on the cascade exchanger insulation, points to a restriction, a wet drier, or wrong superheat. Check the drier and moisture before adjusting valves.
  • Low-stage leaks. Low-side pressures can sit below atmospheric when running, so leaks can pull in air and moisture. Test with the unit off and warm, when pressure is highest, and use only the recovery and handling rules for that refrigerant.
  • Overcharge at standstill. A charge that fits the expansion tank at the nameplate pressure can overpressure the system if over-added. Charge by the nameplate quantity and static pressure, not by running pressures alone.

Field card: Cascade Refrigeration Systems for Very Low Temperatures: Stages, Start Order and Service

Two circuits, one shared exchanger; the high stage must win first.

  • Single stage tops out near -40 to -50 F because of compression ratio.
  • Ratio = absolute discharge pressure / absolute suction pressure.
  • Low-stage condenser = high-stage evaporator.
  • High stage starts first; low stage follows after a delay.
  • Expansion tank holds low-stage charge at standstill.
  • Low-stage high head: check the high stage first.
  • Moisture and oil are worse at low temperature; keep the low-stage drier fresh.
  • Flammable low-stage fluids follow the SNAP use conditions.
