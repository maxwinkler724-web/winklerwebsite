Gas Floor Furnaces in Older Homes: How They Heat Without Ducts

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In some older homes the only heater is a square grate in the floor. Under it hangs a gas burner box in the crawlspace or basement, and the grate is the whole delivery system: no ducts, no blower, no thermostat wiring beyond a few low-voltage wires. They are simple, but they carry a burn hazard few other heaters do and hang where combustion problems are easy to miss.

What it is and how it heats

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a vented floor furnace as a unit suspended from the floor of the space being heated. It belongs to the federal family called direct heating equipment, which delivers warmed air straight to the room.

The physics is the same as in a gravity warm-air furnace, just with no ducts at all. Air around the hot heat exchanger gets lighter and rises out through the middle of the grate. Cooler room air is heavier, so it sinks back through the outer part of the grate and down the sides of the casing to be heated again. The loop is slow, so the room around the grate warms first and a far bedroom may not.

The same DOE test-procedure notice makes one point that explains a lot: a floor furnace is the only vented heater type considered to have part of its jacket outside the heated space. Some heat leaks into the crawlspace, so DOE requires a jacket loss measurement for floor furnaces that other vented heaters do not need.

The hot grate: burns to children and pets

The grate is the hazard that sets this heater apart. A 1993 paper in a medical journal indexed by the National Library of Medicine described two boys, aged 11 and 12 months, who each had a "classic grid-like pattern" of burns from contact with the registers of gas-fired floor furnaces. The burns were partial thickness and healed without skin grafts. The authors concluded that infants and toddlers are at particular risk and that manufacturers should design safer registers.

Federal sources show the wider pattern. A CDC report on steam radiator burns in Chicago, which involved ten children aged 7 months to 5 years, noted that floor furnace burns to children had been reported before. A Consumer Product Safety Commission news release on space heaters (not floor furnaces specifically) said more than two-thirds of those injured needed treatment for burns and about half were under five. Small children put hands and knees on whatever is on the floor.

We found no government burn count for pets on floor furnaces, so we give no number, but a cat that likes a warm spot is in the same position. Practical steps:

  • Treat the grate as a hot surface whenever the burner can fire, and supervise young children closely, as the CPSC advises for space heaters.
  • Keep anything that can burn at least three feet from furnaces and space heaters, as the Washington State Patrol (State Fire Marshal) advises. A floor furnace manufacturer's label may call for more, and it controls.
  • Do not set anything on the grate: not a rug, a basket, a toy, a box or a laundry pile. Anything on it can scorch, and it can block the cool-air return around the edges, which disturbs the air loop.
  • If a child is burned, get medical care; this page cannot give first-aid steps.

Venting, and the space it hangs in

A floor furnace burns gas, so its exhaust has to leave the building. The DOE combustion appliance safety tech brief lists floor furnaces in its vent table, and a figure caption says CO is measured by placing the probe in each combustion port of the floor furnace. The vent pipe from the unit runs up and out; if it is rusted, loose, blocked or not rising as it should, exhaust can spill into the crawlspace, where it may seep into the house through the same floor.

Many floor furnaces take their burner air from the space they hang in. The DOE Building America guide to ventilated crawlspaces warns that sealing foundation vents without dealing with natural-draft equipment in the crawlspace "could make the situation worse" and could create a deadly situation. It says to confirm combustion air and makeup air strategies before sealing, to put a working CO detector in the crawlspace area, and to have a qualified mechanical contractor analyze the equipment when naturally vented appliances are present. Read our lesson on vented and sealed crawl spaces and the one on combustion air before anyone seals or insulates under a house with this heater.

Standing pilot and thermocouple

Most older floor furnaces use a standing pilot: a small flame that burns all the time and heats a thermocouple. The thermocouple's tiny voltage holds the gas valve open, so if the pilot goes out, the main gas shuts off. Our lesson on pilots that will not stay lit covers testing. For a floor furnace, relight only by the label's steps, and if the pilot keeps going out, stop: repeated relighting can hide a blocked vent or failing part. If you smell gas at any point, leave, then call the gas utility from outside.

CO risk and alarms

The CPSC reports that heating equipment is among the top contributors to CO deaths. A floor furnace hangs in a crawlspace or basement and its grate opens into the living space, so spilled exhaust has a short path upward. Alarm guidance from the CPSC: install CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, avoid placing them near heating vents or where furniture or draperies can cover them, and note that the test button checks the circuit, not the sensor's accuracy. If one sounds, go outside to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, and do not use the appliance until trained personnel have serviced it. See CO alarms and what to do for the full steps. An alarm is a backup, not a substitute for yearly service.

The yearly checkup

The CPSC urges a yearly professional inspection of all fuel-burning heating systems, naming flues and vents in its list. For a floor furnace, a visit commonly includes these items, though the manufacturer's service instructions rule:

  • Looking at the vent pipe, its joints and its path for rust, gaps and blockage.
  • Checking the burner flame, the pilot flame and the thermocouple.
  • Measuring CO in the exhaust ports, as the DOE tech brief shows, and reading ambient CO nearby.
  • Inspecting the burner box and grate for rust and loose parts.
  • Checking the crawlspace or basement for water, debris and blocked air openings.

Why many are replaced, and the options

Age, rust, hard-to-find parts and the grate burn hazard are common reasons. Efficiency is another: DOE minimum efficiency for new gas floor furnaces made after April 16, 2013 is 57 percent AFUE for units up to 37,000 Btu/h input and 58 percent above that. Three common replacement routes:

  1. Gas wall furnace. Sealed-combustion wall units draw air from outside and vent outside,. See gas wall furnaces. The DOE Building America guide names sealed combustion and direct-vent units, or all-electric heat pumps, as the preferred replacement for natural-draft equipment in a crawlspace.
  2. Ductless heat pump. One or more wall or floor heads heat and cool without ducts and have no open flame in the room. See mini-split basics. The PNNL cold-climate heat pump guide says to size to the design heating load at the design heating temperature, to use extended performance data and not nameplate capacity, and to avoid rules of thumb.
  3. A ducted system. A furnace or heat pump with ducts serves every room but needs room for equipment and duct runs.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example, not a sizing method; a real job needs a load calculation.

Assumptions. We assume a floor furnace with a 35,000 Btu/h input rating and an efficiency of 57 percent, the DOE minimum for new units up to 37,000 Btu/h; an older unit may be lower. We also assume a 900 square foot house with a design-day heat loss of 30 Btu/h per square foot. Both loss numbers are assumptions.

Heat delivered. 35,000 Btu/h × 0.57 = 19,950 Btu/h.

House heat loss. 900 sq ft × 30 Btu/h per sq ft = 27,000 Btu/h.

Compare. 19,950 ÷ 27,000 = 0.74. On the design day the heater covers about 74 percent of the loss, leaving a gap of 27,000 − 19,950 = 7,050 Btu/h. Expect a cool house, and far rooms fare worst.

Tighter house. If the same house were sealed and insulated so the loss fell to an assumed 18,000 Btu/h, the heater would produce 19,950 − 18,000 = 1,950 Btu/h more than needed (111 percent). It would cycle in shorter bursts.

What it tells us. A replacement should be sized from the calculated load, not the old nameplate. For a ductless heat pump in the 27,000 Btu/h case, check the maker's data for capacity at the local design temperature, not the 47°F rating on the box.

Check before you act. Do not move, cover, repair or replace a gas heater yourself. Gas, venting and CO work call for a trained, properly licensed pro where you live.

Field card: Gas Floor Furnaces in Older Homes

Hot grate, hidden vent, hungry crawlspace.

  • Gravity heat: warm air rises at the grate center, cool air returns at the edges.
  • The grate burns toddlers; nothing goes on it, and combustibles stay back at least three feet.
  • Vent pipe rising and tight; check the space it hangs in.
  • Do not seal crawlspace vents without a combustion air plan.
  • Standing pilot: relight per the label only; if it keeps going out, stop.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.
  • Yearly pro inspection, including flue and CO readings.
  • Replace by calculated load, not old nameplate.
