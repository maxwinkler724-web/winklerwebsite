Leak Repair When a Fix Cannot Finish in Time: Retrofit or Retirement Plans, Extensions, Mothballing and Records

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most leaks get fixed inside the repair window. This lesson is about the other cases: the part is on back order, the compressor is worn out, or the owner would rather replace the machine than keep chasing leaks. The federal refrigerant management rule gives those cases a defined path with clocks, a written plan and records, and it shows up on both the Type II and Type III parts of the exam.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. Federal rules change. This page was built from the federal regulation text and federal agency web pages read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. Confirm every deadline against the current regulation text and your site procedures before you rely on it.

Who this applies to

These options are for appliances with a full charge of 50 pounds or more of an ozone-depleting refrigerant (CFC or HCFC). The federal agency page on leak repair says the owner of such an appliance, once it leaks above its trigger rate, has two choices: repair the leak within 30 days, or develop a retrofit or retirement plan within 30 days and finish it within one year. The trigger rates are 30 percent a year for industrial process refrigeration, 20 percent for commercial refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and everything else. We cover how to calculate the rate in our leak rate lesson. Systems that use only HFCs are handled under a separate rule, which our leaks and leak repair basics lesson explains.

The first clock: 30 days to repair

The regulation text ties the 30 days to the refrigerant addition that shows the appliance is over its trigger rate. The agency web page words the same thing as 30 days from discovery. In the ordinary case the two are the same day, because the leak rate is calculated when you add refrigerant. If the job needs an industrial process shutdown, the period is 120 days. A certified technician must do a leak inspection, and the repair has to bring the leak rate below the trigger rate. Initial and follow-up verification tests then document the repair; see our verification tests lesson for those.

The retrofit or retirement plan

The plan is the owner's way out of an unfinished repair. The regulation requires one within 30 days of any of these: the owner decides to retrofit or retire instead of repairing, the owner does not act on the leak, or the appliance still leaks above the trigger rate after the required repairs. What goes in it, according to the regulation text, are seven items:

  1. The identity and location of the appliance.
  2. The type and full charge of the refrigerant it uses now.
  3. For a retrofit, the type and full charge of the refrigerant it will use.
  4. For a retrofit, an itemized conversion procedure, including changes needed for the new refrigerant to work.
  5. A plan for disposing of the recovered refrigerant.
  6. For a retirement, a plan for disposing of the appliance.
  7. A completion schedule that does not run past one year.

An authorized company official signs and dates the plan. A copy stays at the site, and the original has to be available to the federal agency on request. All identified leaks must be repaired as part of any retrofit under the plan.

How long you get. The work must be finished within one year of the plan's date. There is also a cap: no more than 13 months from when the plan was first required. A late plan does not buy extra time, because the cap is measured from when the plan was required, not from when it was signed.

Retrofit or retirement: what counts

The definitions in the rule are short. A retrofit converts an appliance from one refrigerant to another, including making it compatible with the new refrigerant, which may mean changing lubricant, gaskets, filters, driers, valves, o-rings or other parts. To retire an appliance means removing the refrigerant and then disassembling or impairing the refrigerant circuit so the appliance cannot be used again by anyone. Our own plain reading: a retrofit keeps the machine running on a different refrigerant, and a retirement ends the machine. Replacing a chiller with a new one is a retirement of the old one. If the replacement uses an exempt substitute refrigerant, owners are automatically allowed 18 months to retire, instead of the one-year schedule. That allowance is for retirements, not retrofits.

Extensions of the repair deadline

  • Parts unavailable. If a needed component cannot be had in the first period, the extra time runs up to 30 days after delivery, but the total cannot pass 180 days (270 days with an industrial process shutdown) from the date the rate was exceeded.
  • Other rules. If other federal, state or local requirements make repair within the period impossible, extra time is allowed only as much as needed to comply with those rules.
  • Industrial process shutdown. The base period is 120 days instead of 30, as above.

Leaks the technician identifies as significant must still be repaired and verified inside the initial period. The owner documents the repair efforts and why they could not be finished, and asks the federal agency for the extension within the first period (30 days, or 120 with a shutdown). The text says the request is approved unless the agency says otherwise. Separate requests, tied to industrial process and federally owned equipment, can extend the retrofit or retirement schedule. The study point is that an extension is requested and documented, never assumed.

Mothballing

The rule defines to mothball as removing the refrigerant from the appliance, or from an isolated section or component, down to at least atmospheric pressure, and temporarily shutting it down. While an appliance is mothballed, the repair and the retrofit or retirement time frames are suspended. The clock restarts on the day refrigerant is added back (or added back to the isolated component). The owner keeps a record of when it was mothballed and when refrigerant was added again. Atmospheric pressure or below is the exam hook; the recovery levels themselves are in our required evacuation levels lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume a 300 lb comfort cooling chiller holding a CFC or HCFC refrigerant. Topping it off on Monday, March 2, 2026 shows an annualized leak rate above the 10 percent trigger. We assume the discovery date and the addition date are the same day, so Day 0 is March 2. Days are counted by simple calendar addition.

  1. Day 0, Mar 2, 2026. Trigger exceeded. Repair deadline is Day 30.
  2. Day 10, Thu Mar 12. We assume the owner decides to retrofit instead of repairing.
  3. Day 21, Mon Mar 23. We assume the plan is signed and dated by an authorized official. It lists the seven items and a completion schedule of one year.
  4. Day 30, Wed Apr 1. Last day to repair, or to have the plan written. A plan dated Mar 23 is on time, 9 days to spare.
  5. One year from the plan date: Mar 23, 2027 (Day 386: 365 days after Mar 23, 2026 plus 21 days). That is our planned finish.
  6. Outer cap: 13 months from when the plan was required, which was Apr 1, 2026, gives May 1, 2027 (Day 425). The plan date limit of Mar 23, 2027 comes first.

Compare a plan dated on Apr 1: the one-year date is Apr 1, 2027, still inside the cap. If the owner had instead hit a parts delay on a repair, the extension ceiling would have been Day 180, which is Aug 29, 2026.

Records to keep

The regulation's record list covers these items related to this topic: the service records (date, work done, technician, refrigerant added or removed, full charge and the leak rate with its method), the leak inspection records, verification test records, the retrofit or retirement plan, extension requests with the dated copy, and mothballing dates. Records are kept at least three years; full charge records are kept until three years after the appliance is retired. See also our inspections and chronic leaker lesson.

How this shows up on the exam

For study, be ready to answer these in both Type II and Type III settings (Type III covers low-pressure chillers, where the same 50 lb threshold and rules apply when the refrigerant is covered):

  • Which two choices does the owner have once the trigger rate is exceeded, and how many days for each?
  • How long does a plan run, and who signs it?
  • Is the finish date counted from the discovery date or the plan date?
  • What pressure ends a mothball, and what restarts the clock?
  • Which of these is a retrofit and which is a retirement?

Question wording varies, so learn the logic, not a memorized phrase.

Field card: Leak Repair When a Fix Cannot Finish in Time: Retrofit or Retirement Plans, Extensions, Mothballing and Records

Fix it, or write the plan. Either way, the clock is 30 days.

  • Applies to 50 lb or more of an ozone-depleting refrigerant above the trigger rate: 30, 20 or 10 percent.
  • Plan due within 30 days; finish within one year of the plan date, never past 13 months from when it was required.
  • Plan: seven items, signed and dated, kept on site.
  • Retrofit changes the refrigerant. Retire disables the appliance for good.
  • Parts delay: up to 30 days after delivery, 180 days total (270 with a process shutdown).
  • Mothball: refrigerant out to atmospheric pressure or below; clocks pause until refrigerant is added.
  • Keep plan, extension, mothball and service records three years.
