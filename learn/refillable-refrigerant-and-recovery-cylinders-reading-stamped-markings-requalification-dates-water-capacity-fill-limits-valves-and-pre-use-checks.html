Refillable Refrigerant and Recovery Cylinders: Reading Stamped Markings, Requalification Dates, Water Capacity, Fill Limits, Valves and Pre-Use Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A refillable cylinder is a pressure vessel that gets reused for years, so it carries its history stamped into the metal. Before you hang a recovery cylinder on a job or fill one with new refrigerant, you read that history, weigh the cylinder, and look it over. This lesson shows how to read the stamps, what an out-of-date cylinder means, how the fill limit works, and what to check on the valves and body.

Reading the stamps on the collar

Federal cylinder specifications require permanent markings on the upper part of the cylinder. For a typical specification cylinder, 49 CFR 178.35 says they are stamped on the shoulder, top head or neck, not the side wall. The sequence starts with the DOT specification marking followed right away by the service pressure, such as DOT-3A1800 in the rule's own example. Next come the serial number, then the manufacturer's registered symbol letters, then the inspector's mark with the date of the original test written as a month and two-digit year with a hyphen, so 5-95 means May 1995.

Refillable refrigerant and recovery cylinders are commonly in the 4B family (4B, 4BA, 4BW), which is the family the requalification table covers below. The exact spec and pressure on your cylinder is whatever is stamped on it.

  • Specification and service pressure: the maximum pressure the cylinder is built to hold at normal temperature. Match it to the refrigerant you plan to put in. The valve, hoses and gauges must be rated for that pressure too.
  • Serial number and symbol: together they identify this one cylinder.
  • Original test date: the month and year the cylinder was first tested, which is effectively its manufacture date.
  • Requalification marks: 49 CFR 180.213 says a cylinder that passes periodic requalification is marked with the month and year of the test, and the approver's identification number (the RIN) sits between them in a square pattern. The example in the rule is September 2006, shown as a 9 and a 06 with the RIN between. Those marks go on the upper end, not the side wall.
  • Tare weight and water capacity: many cylinders also show a tare (empty) weight and a water capacity, on the collar or on a tag or label. Those are not part of the federal list above, so treat the maker's marking as the source. If you cannot find the water capacity, do not guess it. Ask the supplier or retire the cylinder from recovery use.

Requalification: the federal interval and an out-of-date cylinder

Table 1 in 49 CFR 180.209 lists the periods for 4B, 4BA and 4BW cylinders as 5, 7, 10 or 12 years. The longer periods apply only under the conditions in the paragraphs the table points to. For example, paragraph (e) allows a 12-year volumetric expansion test for these types in non-corrosive service. Five years is the base interval, and you cannot tell which period applies to your cylinder from the table alone. The practical rule: find the latest requalification date on the collar and count forward from it. If there is no requalification mark, count from the original test date.

What if the date has passed? Under 49 CFR 173.301(a), no one may fill a cylinder that is overdue for periodic requalification and offer it for transportation. The same paragraph says a cylinder with a crack, leak, bulge, defective valve or pressure relief device, fire damage, or detrimental rust or corrosion may not be filled and offered for transport. A damaged cylinder can be repaired and requalified only as subpart C of part 180 prescribes. So an overdue cylinder is out of service until an approved requalifier tests and marks it. Our own advice: use the same standard for recovery work, because a recovery cylinder usually travels in a van and then to a reclaimer. Tag it, set it aside, and do not use it.

The regulation also asks for earlier requalification for cylinders with leaks, corrosion, denting, bulging or rough usage, or a tare weight loss of 5 percent or more (180.209(c)). That is a reason to check the tare each time you weigh the cylinder.

Cylinder color: a convention, not a rule

Recovery cylinders are commonly gray with a yellow top. Treat that as a common convention, not a federal requirement. The sources we opened do not state a color rule, colors fade or get painted over, and the label and the stamps are what count. Never identify the refrigerant inside by color.

Water capacity and the fill limit

Water capacity is how much water the cylinder would hold, in pounds. It is a measure of the inside volume. Because refrigerant liquid is heavier or lighter than water by a known ratio, you can convert volume to weight.

The federal rule in 49 CFR 173.304(b) says the liquid portion of a liquefied gas may not completely fill the container at any temperature up to and including 131°F (55°C). It gives no percentage for refrigerants in that paragraph. Section 173.304a has a filling-density table that lists some gases by percent of water capacity (R-22 appears at 105 percent, for example), and paragraph (c) says liquefied gas is filled by weight. For recovery work, the figure most technicians use is 80 percent of the cylinder's capacity, which is trade practice rather than a number we found in the federal text. We use it as a working limit because it leaves vapor space as the liquid expands when it warms.

Temperature matters. A cylinder filled to its limit in a cool basement then sits in a hot van or an attic. Liquid expands as it warms, and a liquid-full cylinder can build pressure quickly. Weigh while you fill, stop well short of the limit on a hot day, and keep full cylinders out of direct sun.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These numbers are assumptions for practice, not data for any particular cylinder or refrigerant. Assume the stamp or tag gives a water capacity of 30 lb and a tare of 14 lb. Assume the refrigerant is a common HFC whose liquid is 1.20 times as heavy as water at 77°F and 1.07 times as heavy at 131°F (assumed ratios; your safety data sheet has the real ones).

  1. Volume limit: 30 lb × 0.80 = 24 lb of water-equivalent volume.
  2. Convert to refrigerant at 77°F: 24 × 1.20 = 28.8 lb. That is the most liquid refrigerant to put in.
  3. Maximum gross weight on the scale: 14 + 28.8 = 42.8 lb.
  4. Check against the 131°F rule: at 131°F the same 28.8 lb of liquid takes up 28.8 ÷ 1.07 = 26.9 lb of water-equivalent volume. That is 26.9 ÷ 30 = about 90 percent of the cylinder. It is below 100 percent, so the liquid does not completely fill the cylinder at 131°F.

If the cylinder already holds 4 lb, there is room for 28.8 − 4 = 24.8 lb more. Remember that the refrigerant ratio changes with the refrigerant, so redo the math for each one. Our push-pull recovery lesson and the recovery machine lesson cover how the fill limit shows up while you recover.

Dual-port valves and relief devices

Many refillable and recovery cylinders have two ports so you can reach the liquid or the vapor space. In general (our own description, check your valve's label or manual), one port draws from or fills the liquid, and the other connects to the vapor space. Knowing which is which matters, especially in a liquid transfer. Keep the hand valves closed when not connected and the protective cap on while storing or moving it.

49 CFR 173.301(f) requires pressure relief devices on cylinders filled with gas, sized and tested under the industry pressure-relief standards that the rule names, with listed exceptions. A relief device is the cylinder's last protection against over-pressure. Never plug it, adjust it, or remove it. If it is leaking, corroded or damaged, take the cylinder out of service. Section 173.301(a) lists a defective valve or relief device among the reasons a cylinder may not be filled and offered for transport.

Pre-use checks

Section 173.301(a) requires the person filling a cylinder to visually inspect the outside before each filling. Make it a habit before each use on a job as well:

  • Read the stamps. Is the requalification date current? Does the service pressure fit the refrigerant?
  • Look for dents, bulges, cuts, deep rust or pitting (especially near the base), a damaged foot ring or collar, and heat or fire marks. PHMSA's pre-fill guide for propane lists the same conditions.
  • Check the valve: stem not bent, no leaks at the threads or packing, caps and plugs in place, ports clean.
  • Weigh it. Compare the empty weight to the tare, and note any gain that suggests a heel of old refrigerant. Check that it holds the same refrigerant type your label says.
  • Confirm the label. One refrigerant per cylinder, and no mixing. Our mixed and contaminated refrigerant lesson covers what to do when you are not sure.

Field card: Refillable and Recovery Cylinders

Read the stamps, weigh it, check the valves, then use it.

  • Read the collar: spec and service pressure, serial, maker symbol, original test date, requalification month, ID and year.
  • Table 1 in 49 CFR 180.209 gives 5, 7, 10 or 12 years for 4B, 4BA and 4BW; read your marks and count forward.
  • Overdue or damaged: do not fill it or offer it for transport.
  • Gray with a yellow top is a common convention only. Trust the label.
  • Fill limit: trade practice is 80 percent of water capacity; the federal rule says liquid must not completely fill the cylinder at 131°F. Weigh as you fill.
  • Our example: 30 lb × 0.80 × 1.20 = 28.8 lb refrigerant, 42.8 lb gross with a 14 lb tare.
  • Never tamper with the relief device. Caps on, valves closed, cylinder secured.
