Adding a Gas Appliance to an Existing House: Load, Meter, Pipe and Utility Checks

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A standby generator, a gas range, a pool heater, a tankless water heater or a second furnace all feel like one more pipe and one more shutoff. But the gas meter, the regulator and the pipe were sized for the appliances that were there before. Adding one means checking the whole chain, from the utility's meter to the new burner, before anyone cuts a pipe.

Check your local rules

Check your local rules. Codes, editions and utility practices differ by place and change over time. This page is general education built from government and national-lab pages read in 2026. The adopted code, the local code official and your gas utility have the last word.

Step 1: Total the connected load

Start with the nameplate input of every gas appliance, in Btu per hour, and add the new one. The fuel gas code text we read, Section 402.2 of the 2018 Seattle Fuel Gas Code (which is based on the International Fuel Gas Code), says the flow to be provided is the sum of the maximum input of the appliances served. It lets pipe sizing be based on a smaller load only where a diversity of load can be established.

Diversity versus all on at once. Diversity means the appliances will not all burn at full rate together, so the pipe can be sized for less than the sum. It is a claim you have to be able to support, and it is the code official's call, not a guess. A furnace and a tankless unit can easily both fire on a cold morning, so for this lesson we use the plain sum, the all-on case. The sizing lesson linked below takes the same approach.

How big is the new load? A national-lab building-science page says tank-type burners are often 30,000 to 50,000 Btu/h, while tankless units typically have burner capacities of 120,000 to 200,000 Btu/h. That jump is why a tankless unit so often triggers a gas-supply review. Generators, pool heaters and second furnaces get the same treatment: read the nameplate.

Step 2: Convert to cfh and compare with the meter

A meter is rated in cubic feet per hour, so convert each input to cfh by dividing by the heating value of the gas. The Seattle code appendix says to divide the total Btu/h input by the average heating value per cubic foot, and its own example uses 1,000 Btu per cubic foot. The U.S. Energy Information Administration says heat content varies by location and over time, gives about 1,037 Btu per cubic foot as the 2025 U.S. average, and tells you to ask the gas utility for local figures. Its annual table shows 1,036 to 1,037 for 2020 through 2025. Anything from about 1,000 to 1,050 is a reasonable working range, so the choice moves the answer only a few percent.

The meter's rated capacity is a number the utility knows. You cannot read it from the pipe, and you should not assume it. Compare your all-on total against it, and if the total is close or over, that is the question to put to the utility.

Step 3: Check the existing piping

Section 401.4 of the same Seattle code is blunt: where an additional appliance is to be served, the existing piping shall be checked to determine if it has adequate capacity. If it does not, the existing system must be enlarged or separate piping of adequate capacity provided.

The method is the longest length method in Section 402.4.1: size every section using the length from the point of delivery to the most remote outlet, then read the matching row of the table. Full steps are in Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method. Remember that the old trunk line now carries the new appliance's gas too.

Step 4: Call the utility about the meter and regulator

The meter and service regulator are normally the utility's equipment, so changing them is the utility's job and not yours. The ownership split is covered in Gas Meter and Regulator Basics. One state commission filing we read, a utility's general gas rules, says the company locates the point where its meter installation attaches to the customer's piping, and a city handout for tankless installs says simply that a larger gas meter may be required. Neither gives meter capacity numbers, which is the point: the utility has to tell you.

The Seattle appendix also notes that a line regulator must have adequate capacity. On a higher-pressure system, see Elevated-Pressure (2 psi) Gas Piping in Buildings. A practical call covers:

  • The make, model and input in Btu/h of each appliance you are adding.
  • Your current appliances and what you have been running.
  • A request that the utility confirm the existing meter and regulator can serve the new total, or upsize them. What each utility asks for, how long it takes and what it charges differ, so check its own page.

Do not remove, reset or swap a utility meter or regulator yourself.

Worked example (our own example, assumed numbers)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption for teaching, not a real house. The only cited numbers are the heating value and the pipe table row.

  1. Assumed appliances. Furnace 80,000 Btu/h, tank water heater 40,000, range 65,000. New: tankless water heater 199,000 Btu/h (inside the 120,000 to 200,000 range above). Assumed meter rating: 250 cfh. Assumed longest run from meter outlet to the farthest outlet, the tankless unit: 50 ft. Assumed existing trunk pipe: 1 in.
  2. Heating value. Use 1,037 Btu per cubic foot (EIA 2025 average).
  3. Convert and round up. Furnace 80,000 / 1,037 = 77.2, use 78 cfh. Water heater 40,000 / 1,037 = 38.6, use 39 cfh. Range 65,000 / 1,037 = 62.7, use 63 cfh. Tankless 199,000 / 1,037 = 191.9, use 192 cfh.
  4. Totals. Before the addition: 78 + 39 + 63 = 180 cfh. After: 180 + 192 = 372 cfh (384,000 Btu/h in all).
  5. Meter. Before: 250 - 180 = 70 cfh of room. After: 372 - 250 = 122 cfh over the assumed rating. With 1,000 Btu per cubic foot the total would be 384 cfh, about 3 percent higher, so the conclusion does not change. Even leaving the range out as a diversity argument, 78 + 39 + 192 = 309, still over 250. In this example the utility must be called before the work is planned further.
  6. Pipe run. From Table 402.4(2) in the Seattle code (Schedule 40 metallic pipe, natural gas, inlet pressure under 2 psi, 0.5 in. w.c. drop), the 50 ft row reads 72 cfh for 1/2 in., 151 for 3/4 in., 284 for 1 in. and 583 for 1-1/4 in. The trunk carries 372 cfh: the 1 in. pipe holds only 284, so it is 88 cfh short (it passed before at 180 cfh, with 104 to spare). 1-1/4 in. at 583 passes. The tankless branch carries 192 cfh: 3/4 in. at 151 fails, 1 in. at 284 passes.

The answer for this made-up house is a utility call about the meter and a larger trunk and branch, not just a short run of new pipe off the nearest tee.

Low inlet pressure: what undersized piping looks like

The code requires gas to reach each appliance at not less than the minimum supply pressure that appliance needs (Section 402.1). The Seattle appendix gives a rule of thumb for a regulator-equipped appliance: about 5 in. w.c. at the inlet so the appliance regulator can deliver about 3.5 in. w.c. to the burner. The furnace manual quoted in the clocking lesson lists 4.5 in. w.c. as its minimum natural gas inlet pressure, so always use the number on the data plate or in the manual.

When the pipe or meter is too small, pressure at the appliance sags when other appliances are running, even if it looked fine with one appliance alone. Our own explanation of what that can look like, to be confirmed by measurement:

  • Weak or unsteady flames, or yellow flames. A city safety page we read says a yellow flame means the appliance is not working properly and should be checked right away. Low supply pressure is only one possible cause, so measure before adjusting anything.
  • A new tankless heater that lights, then shuts down or throws a fault. These units prove flame and watch ignition. If they are starved, they may lock out, and often only when a second appliance is also running. The fault code list in the maker's manual tells you what it means.
  • Other appliances change. The furnace or range starts to behave differently only after the new load was added.

The test is a manometer at the appliance inlet with everything firing, not a guess. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer, and Gas Furnace and Stove Flame Color for what the flame tells you. If you smell gas, leave and call from outside. Do not turn the manifold pressure up to compensate; that hides a supply problem and can overfire the appliance.

Permits and inspection, in general terms

Local building departments publish short handouts that show the pattern. One city code we read says a permit shall be obtained from the code official prior to each installation, alteration, repair, replacement or remodel, that work is inspected and must stay exposed until approved, and that gas or fuel may not be connected to equipment that needs a permit until the code official approves. Another city handout says permits are required for installing or extending any gas piping, with a pressure test before pipe is covered and appliances connected, and a final inspection. Two more ask for sizing calculations, one before a tankless permit is issued and one at the first inspection.

So expect to hand over your load total and pipe sizing. Who is allowed to do gas piping, and what the local office wants, is set where the work is done. Ask the local building department before you start.

Field card: Adding a Gas Appliance

Total it, compare it, then call the utility.

  • Add the new appliance's Btu/h to every existing gas appliance. Use the all-on total unless diversity can be shown.
  • cfh = Btu/h divided by heating value (about 1,000 to 1,050; national average about 1,037).
  • Meter and regulator rating comes from the utility. Close or over means call it first.
  • Recheck existing pipe with the longest length method. The trunk now carries the new load.
  • Tankless units commonly run 120,000 to 200,000 Btu/h, far above a tank heater.
  • Low inlet pressure shows up with all appliances on. Measure at the appliance inlet.
  • Expect a permit and inspection. Ask your local building department.
