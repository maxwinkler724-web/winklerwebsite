Setpoint Scheduling and Optimal Start Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Half the comfort complaints in a commercial building are not broken equipment. They are a schedule that does not match how people actually use the space.

Occupied, unoccupied, and the setback

Most commercial thermostats and building automation systems keep at least two modes. In occupied mode the space holds its normal setpoints and brings in ventilation. In unoccupied mode the setpoints move out, or fans shut off, so the equipment does less work. A federal lab's building tune-up guide calls unoccupied mode and night setback a major cost saver and recommends covering nights, weekends and holidays.

What optimal start adds

A plain time clock starts the system at a fixed time, usually early enough for the worst day of the year. A Trane controls newsletter explains that optimal start instead calculates how long each zone needs to reach its occupied setpoint, using the current temperature gap and past warm-up or cool-down performance, and starts as late as it can while still meeting occupancy. A Trane support article on one controller describes the same idea: it learns each zone's heating and cooling rates, can use outdoor temperature to improve the estimate, and will not start more than two hours early. Other products differ in inputs and limits.

Related ideas

  • Optimal stop: ending heating or cooling a little early, so the space drifts a few degrees by the time people leave. Ventilation can keep running.
  • Warm-up and cool-down without fresh air: the tune-up guide suggests avoiding outdoor air during warm-up except for a flush before occupancy.
  • Overrides: push-button or timed overrides handle after-hours users without running the whole system.

Schedule mistakes to look for

  • Equipment running around the clock because nobody set weekend or holiday schedules.
  • Pumps, boilers or chillers running at night after the air side has shut down.
  • Overrides left on, or added again and again in response to complaints instead of fixing the schedule.
  • Optimal start available in the controller but never turned on.
  • Outdoor-temperature interlocks overriding the schedule for far longer than their design intent.
  • Setpoints that fight each other, such as a heating setpoint close to the cooling setpoint in the same zone.

Caveats worth knowing

The Trane newsletter points out savings depend on climate, building use and utility rates, and that some strategies bring tradeoffs: for example, longer unoccupied runs can affect humidity. If humidity matters in the building, watch it before and after changing a schedule. Optimal stop also assumes people tolerate a small drift.

Check at the controller

  • Read the schedule the equipment actually follows, not just the one on the front screen.
  • Compare scheduled times with when the fan and compressor really run.
  • Check trend logs for zone temperatures drifting at night and recovering in the morning.
  • Write down any change you make, and tell the building owner.

Field card: Setpoint Scheduling and Optimal Start Basics

Fix the schedule before you blame the equipment.

  • Occupied versus unoccupied modes save energy when the schedule matches use.
  • Optimal start calculates how early to start; a plain clock guesses.
  • Optimal start is a controller feature that must be enabled and set up.
  • Look for round-the-clock running, stale overrides and plant equipment left on.
  • Savings and humidity effects depend on the building. Write down changes.
