Sizing an Air Handler Filter Bank: Face Velocity and Pressure Drop

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A filter bank is a wall of filter frames, and the number of frames decides how fast air moves through each one. Too few frames and the air is pushed hard through every filter, the pressure drop climbs, and the fan burns extra energy for the life of the building. This lesson shows how to size a bank from face velocity and pressure drop, using a worked example of our own.

Face velocity is just cfm over area

Face velocity is the airflow divided by the face area of the filters, in feet per minute. A design slide deck on efficient fan systems defines it the same way for coils and filters: unit cfm divided by face area. Use the area the air actually passes through, not the nominal catalog size. A frame sold as 24 by 24 inches is a bit smaller in real life, and gaskets and holding frames hide more of it, so a real bank has slightly less area than the nominal count suggests. For fast field math we count nominal area and say so. Gaps and bypass around the frames are covered in Air-Handler Filter Racks and Bypass: Gaps, Gaskets, Holding Frames and Gauges, so we do not repeat them here.

Typical design face velocities

A university facilities specification for air filtration rates its pleated panel, V-bank and rigid-cell filter types at 500 feet per minute and says to estimate pressure drop at that velocity. That is a rating point, not a law: the same specification uses 12-inch deep V-bank and rigid-cell filters as well as 2-inch pleated panels. A 1965 federal laboratory test report on deep-bed panel filters ran them at a rated 2,000 cfm, which it describes as a net face velocity of about 600 ft/min. A federal workplace-health conference abstract from 2002 reported air velocities between 205 and 1,090 ft/min, depending on filter type, for bag and pleated V-panel alternatives. So 500 fpm is a common reference, and the right value for your bank is whatever the maker publishes for that filter at the airflow you have. Lower velocity always means lower drop for the same filter, but it needs more frames.

How pressure drop rises with velocity

Here we have to be careful about what the sources support. The slide deck states the general duct-and-coil rule: pressure drop varies with velocity squared, which is the usual result for turbulent flow. We found no source that states a squared law for filter media. A national laboratory conference abstract on fibrous filters says measured drop followed a viscous-inertial theory at higher velocities rather than the straight-line (linear) Darcy relation. That tells us a straight line is the low-velocity starting point for fibrous media and that the curve bends upward as velocity rises. The same sources do not give one exponent for HVAC filters. For planning, treat the true answer as somewhere between linear and squared, and use the maker's drop-versus-airflow curve for the actual filter. In the worked example below we show both bounds so the uncertainty is visible.

Clean versus recommended final resistance

Every filter has an initial (clean) resistance and a recommended final resistance, which is the drop at which the maker says to replace it. The university specification lists both. At 500 fpm it shows a 2-inch pleated panel at 0.28 in. w.g. clean and 1.0 final, a 12-inch V-bank at 0.27 clean and 0.60 final, and a 12-inch rigid cell at 0.53 clean and 1.0 final. It requires the schedule to list both values, and it says the fan static pressure should be estimated from the drop at the recommended final condition, not the clean one. We agree with that logic: a fan sized for a clean filter will fall short of design airflow as the filters load. The 1965 laboratory report ended its loading tests at about 1.0 in. w.g. across the filter, a similar end point.

Choosing a change-out point from a gauge

The same specification calls for a filter gauge at each bank, with separate static taps upstream and downstream, mounted where it can be read. A gauge is only useful if you know what to compare it with. Our own practice suggestion:

  • Record the clean reading at start-up, with the fan at its normal speed, and write it on the gauge or the filter log.
  • Look up the recommended final resistance for that exact filter in the submittal or maker data.
  • Change at or before that final value. If the system can no longer hold design airflow, change earlier.
  • Always compare readings at the same fan speed, because a gauge reads higher when airflow is higher and lower when a variable-speed fan slows down.

A rising reading means the filter is loading. A sudden fall can mean a torn or blown-out filter or a bypass opening, so check before you celebrate low numbers.

Deeper media or more face area

There are two ways to cut the drop: spread the air over more frame area, or give each frame more media. A federal energy-office article on filter selection says deeper pleats lower pressure drop and gives the example that a 4-inch pleated filter has twice the surface area of a 2-inch one. It adds that putting two filters side by side can achieve the same effect. Deeper media needs a rack and housing deep enough to hold it. More frames need more wall area and framing. Neither is free of limits, so ask which one the cabinet can take. Deep media also tends to hold more dust before reaching its final resistance, though we did not find a source with a figure, so we leave that as a general point to confirm with the maker.

What high drop does to the fan

The slide deck gives fan power as cfm times pressure drop in inches, divided by 6,345 times fan efficiency times motor efficiency, and lists typical fan efficiency as 60 to 70 percent. The filter drop is a straight multiplier in that formula, so doubling the drop doubles the filter's share of fan power at the same airflow. The federal energy-office article also reports that clean filters with higher resistance cut airflow by about 7 percent with one motor type and raised fan power by about 9 percent on average with another, and that improperly sized filters and ducting could push some motors to over 50 percent more energy. Those results come from small residential systems, so use them as a direction, not a prediction for a built-up bank.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a source. Assumptions: the unit moves 12,000 cfm; the bank uses nominal 24 by 24 inch frames of the 2-inch pleated type in the university table (0.28 in. w.g. clean at 500 fpm, 1.0 final); we count nominal face area of 4 sq ft per frame; fan efficiency is 65 percent and motor efficiency 90 percent; the fan runs 8,760 hours a year.

  1. Starting layout: 3 frames wide by 2 high is 6 frames. Area is 6 × 4 = 24 sq ft.
  2. Face velocity: 12,000 ÷ 24 = 500 fpm. Clean drop is 0.28 in. w.g.
  3. Add a third row: 3 wide by 3 high is 9 frames, or 36 sq ft. Face velocity is 12,000 ÷ 36 = 333 fpm.
  4. New clean drop, bounded. If drop were linear in velocity: 0.28 × (333 ÷ 500) = 0.28 × 0.667 = 0.19. If it were squared: 0.28 × 0.6672 = 0.28 × 0.444 = 0.12. So we expect roughly 0.12 to 0.19 in. w.g., and the maker's curve would settle it.
  5. Fan power for the filter alone at 6 frames: 12,000 × 0.28 ÷ (6,345 × 0.65 × 0.90) = 3,360 ÷ 3,712 = 0.91 hp. At 9 frames: 12,000 × 0.19 ÷ 3,712 = 0.61 hp (linear bound) or 12,000 × 0.12 ÷ 3,712 = 0.40 hp (squared bound).
  6. Saving: 0.91 minus 0.61 is 0.30 hp, and 0.91 minus 0.40 is 0.50 hp. At 0.746 kW per hp and 8,760 hours, that is about 0.22 to 0.37 kW, or roughly 2,000 to 3,300 kWh per year of fan input, using the 0.91, 0.61 and 0.40 hp results above.
  7. At the final resistance of 1.0 in. w.g. the 6-frame bank at the same airflow would cost 12,000 × 1.0 ÷ 3,712 = 3.2 hp for the filter alone. We did not scale the final value for the 9-frame bank, because that needs the maker's data.

Adding frames also changes the fan operating point and may fit only if the cabinet has room, so treat this as a sizing check and not a final design.

Field card: Sizing a Filter Bank

More frames, lower velocity, lower drop, lower fan energy.

  • Face velocity (fpm) = cfm ÷ face area (sq ft).
  • 500 fpm is a common rating point for pleated, V-bank and rigid-cell filters, not a limit.
  • Drop falls with velocity: squared for turbulent flow, closer to linear for media at low velocity. Use the maker's curve.
  • List clean and recommended final resistance, and size the fan at final.
  • Log the clean gauge reading at the same fan speed; change at or before final.
  • Deeper media or more frames: pick what the cabinet can hold.
