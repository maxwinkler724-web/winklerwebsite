Linear Interpolation for HVAC Techs: Reading Between Table Rows

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Pressure-temperature charts, steam tables and maker performance tables list values at set steps. Your reading rarely lands exactly on a row. Linear interpolation is the quick way to estimate a value between two rows, and knowing when it is good enough is part of reading data like a pro.

The one formula

Suppose a table gives output y1 at input x1 and output y2 at input x2, and your input x sits between them. Assume the data runs along a straight line between the two rows. Then:

y = y1 + (x - x1)(y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1)

Read it in three pieces. The fraction (x - x1)/(x2 - x1) says how far you are from the first row to the second, as a number from 0 to 1. Multiply that fraction by the full change in output, (y2 - y1), and add it to the first row's output. A university numerical-analysis note describes this same line as the straight line through two table points, and a mechanical-engineering numerical-computation note uses it for piecewise-linear estimates between tabulated values.

Sanity checks before you trust the answer

  • Your answer must land between y1 and y2. If it does not, a sign or a row is wrong.
  • If x is halfway between x1 and x2, y should be halfway between y1 and y2.
  • Keep units consistent. Do not mix gauge and absolute pressure, or mix rows from different tables.
  • Keep the rows that bracket x. Do not use rows from farther away. Going outside the table (extrapolating) is a different job and a risky one.

Our own example 1: a pressure-temperature chart

This is our own example with made-up numbers, not data for any real refrigerant. Assumptions: a chart for a fictional refrigerant lists 60 psig at 38.0 degrees F and 80 psig at 50.0 degrees F. Your gauge reads 65 psig and you want the saturation temperature.

  1. Fraction of the way: (65 - 60)/(80 - 60) = 5/20 = 0.25.
  2. Change in output: 50.0 - 38.0 = 12.0 degrees F.
  3. Estimate: 38.0 + 0.25 x 12.0 = 41.0 degrees F.

Sanity check: 41.0 is between 38.0 and 50.0, and 65 is a quarter of the way up, so the answer should be a quarter of the way up too. With rows only 20 psig apart on a gently curved chart, this is typically fine for field work.

Our own example 2: a steam table and a wide gap

Again our own made-up numbers, not real steam data. Assumptions: a table lists saturation temperature at 10 psig as 240 degrees F, at 30 psig as 274 degrees F and at 50 psig as 298 degrees F. We pretend a finer table also gives 259 degrees F at 20 psig, so we can see how far off the straight line gets.

  • Narrow gap, 10 to 30 psig, find 20 psig: 240 + (20 - 10)(274 - 240)/(30 - 10) = 240 + 10 x 34/20 = 257 degrees F. The finer table says 259, so we are 2 degrees F low.
  • Wide gap, 10 to 50 psig, find 30 psig: 240 + (30 - 10)(298 - 240)/(50 - 10) = 240 + 20 x 58/40 = 269 degrees F. The table row says 274, so we are 5 degrees F low.

The wider the gap, the bigger the miss, and here the miss grew by more than double. Steam saturation temperature rises quickly at low pressure and flattens as pressure climbs, so the data bows above the straight line. A straight-line estimate on a bowed curve always lands on the same side of the truth, which is why the error here is low both times. For help reading the real thing, see the steam-table lesson linked below, including its gauge versus absolute pressure warning.

When straight-line is good enough, and when it is not

A university numerical-analysis overview gives the error of a straight-line estimate as the product (x - x1)(x2 - x), times the curve's second derivative, divided by 2. For evenly spaced rows that works out to no more than h squared over 8, times the largest second derivative on the interval, where h is the gap between rows. Plain meaning: the error scales with the square of the gap and with how sharply the curve bends. The mechanical-engineering note agrees that piecewise-linear estimates are second-order accurate, so halving the row spacing cuts the error to about a quarter. It also warns that the bound needs a smooth curve.

  • Usually fine: narrow row spacing, gently bending data, and a result you only need to a degree or a few percent, such as an estimated saturation temperature from a gauge reading.
  • Be careful: wide row spacing, data that bends hard (steam at low pressure in our example), and results feeding another calculation that magnifies error, like superheat or subcooling.
  • Do not interpolate across a break: if the table switches regimes between rows, for example a maker table that lists a different mode on each side, the straight line means nothing.

Two habits help. First, always use the two nearest rows. Second, when the rows are far apart, look for a finer table, a maker calculator or a published formula instead. And if a result matters, say whether it is read from a table or interpolated when you record it.

Double interpolation for two-way tables

Maker capacity tables usually depend on two things, such as outdoor temperature across the rows and indoor wet-bulb temperature across the columns. Your conditions may fall between both. A university lecture note on bilinear interpolation describes the idea: interpolate along one axis twice, then interpolate between those two answers along the other axis.

Our own example 3, made-up numbers. Assumptions: a table for a fictional cooling unit lists total capacity in thousand Btu per hour (MBH) as 30.0 at 85 degrees F outdoor and 62 degrees F wet-bulb, 33.0 at 85 and 67, 28.0 at 95 and 62, and 31.0 at 95 and 67. We want 90 degrees F outdoor and 64 degrees F wet-bulb.

  1. At 85 degrees F outdoor, interpolate across wet-bulb: 30.0 + (64 - 62)(33.0 - 30.0)/(67 - 62) = 30.0 + 2 x 3.0/5 = 31.2.
  2. At 95 degrees F outdoor, the same way: 28.0 + 2 x 3.0/5 = 29.2.
  3. Now interpolate between those at 90 degrees F outdoor: 31.2 + (90 - 85)(29.2 - 31.2)/(95 - 85) = 31.2 + 5 x (-2.0)/10 = 30.2 MBH.

Check by doing it the other way round: at 62 wet-bulb, the 90 degree value is 29.0, at 67 it is 32.0, and at 64 it is 29.0 + 2 x 3.0/5 = 30.2. Same answer, as it should be for straight-line steps. Write down the four corner values before you start so you do not pick the wrong cell. For tables with more than two inputs, or with total and sensible capacity side by side, work one value column at a time. See the lessons on expanded cooling and heat pump tables below.

Field card: Linear interpolation

Field card: Linear interpolation

  • y = y1 + (x - x1)(y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1)
  • Use the two rows that bracket your value, in the same units and basis.
  • The answer must fall between y1 and y2.
  • Error grows with the square of the row gap and with how sharply the data bends.
  • Two inputs: interpolate twice along one axis, then once along the other.
  • Do not interpolate across a change of regime, and do not extrapolate off the table.
