Natural, Forced and Induced Draft on Gas Furnaces, Boilers and Burners

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every gas appliance needs something to move combustion products out of the building. What does the moving decides whether the heat exchanger and vent run below room pressure or above it, and that decides how a leak behaves, how the vent is built and which safety switch watches it. This lesson compares the three draft types side by side.

Three ways to move flue gas

Natural draft uses no fan. Hot flue gas is lighter than the cooler air outside, so the column of gas in the vent rises, the same stack effect that pulls smoke up a chimney. A federal energy department national laboratory guide describes older natural draft (also called atmospheric) boilers as relying entirely on high flue temperatures, and says the flue runs at negative pressure compared with the room around the appliance.

Forced draft puts the fan on the supply side. A blower pushes combustion air into the burner and firebox. Everything downstream of that fan, including the heat exchanger, runs at a pressure above the room unless something else relieves it. Packaged commercial burners and many boilers work this way, and the same laboratory guide notes that forced draft is also called power vented.

Induced draft puts the fan on the exhaust side. An inducer pulls combustion air and flue gas through the heat exchanger, so the heat exchanger runs below room pressure. What happens after the inducer depends on the design. On a fan-assisted Category I appliance the vent stays negative and still depends on flue temperature to lift the gas. On a Category IV furnace the inducer pushes gas out through a vent that is positive. The laboratory guide puts the national fuel gas code categories this way: Category I is negative vent pressure, and Categories III and IV are positive.

The picture

Why pressure decides what a leak does

This part is our own reasoning from basic pressure behavior, not a quote. A crack in a heat exchanger or a loose vent joint lets gas move toward lower pressure. If that part of the gas path is below room pressure, room air leaks in and the flame or the gas flow gets disturbed. If it is above room pressure, flue gas leaks out into the space. That is why positive-pressure vents (forced draft and Category III and IV) must be sealed and listed for the job, and why a single failed joint can matter so much.

A federal consumer product safety agency test report on a condensing, induced-draft Category IV furnace shows the point. It had a pressure switch at the inducer inlet that shut the unit down. In the test the furnace kept running with the vent up to 86 percent blocked, and shut off within about 20 to 40 seconds at 88 percent or more. A vent pipe that was fully separated gave no shutoff at all, and carbon monoxide in the test chamber climbed with firing rate. The same report says the product standard of that era had no requirement for disconnected vents. A switch that proves the fan is moving gas does not prove the pipe is still connected.

Natural draft: breach, barometric damper and draft hood

Natural draft is weak and changes with weather, so the vent system has to control it. A draft hood or a barometric damper does two jobs: it lets room air dilute the vent gas, and it holds draft near a set value. A state rule for large gas equipment says equipment that needs controlled chimney draft must be able to regulate it automatically, by a barometric damper, a mechanically operated damper or a draft hood, and that a double-acting barometric regulator needs a manual-reset device that shuts off fuel if spillage lasts too long. The same rule says a draft hood or barometric control may not sit where it can see a different pressure from the combustion air supply. For the oil side of the damper, see the lesson on barometric dampers linked below.

Because the draft is small, a depressurized house can overpower it. A federal energy department field study found that depressurization above about 5 pascals reversed most vents in the homes it examined, and that the draft hood is the typical spillage site on atmospheric appliances. Power-vented and direct-vent appliances were named as one way to remove most spillage problems.

Forced and induced draft: proving the fan

When a fan is part of the draft, the control system must prove the fan before gas flows. The state rule above says equipment with forced or induced draft fans must prevent gas flow if those devices fail, and that mechanical draft sequences prove air flow and then run a purge before ignition. A federal veterans health agency boiler safety device testing manual describes a combustion air pressure switch as a differential pressure measurement across the fan. For a constant speed fan it sets the trip point at about 80 percent or more of the minimum differential, found by measuring it, and it explains that without enough air, combustion is incomplete. The same manual lists a furnace pressure interlock that guards against furnace pressure rising from blocked flow, and a stack damper position interlock that confirms the outlet damper is open during purge. On residential induced-draft furnaces the pressure switch at the inducer plays the same role. The lesson on pressure switches and inducers covers its diagnosis.

Our own worked example: theoretical natural draft

Our own example, with assumed inputs. We use a common stack draft relation: draft in inches of water column equals 0.52 x H x P x (1/To - 1/Tf), with H the vent height in feet, P the barometric pressure in psia and temperatures in degrees Rankine (degrees F plus 459.67). Assumptions: a straight 20 ft vent, 350 F flue gas, 60 F outdoor air, P = 14.7 psia, and flue gas treated as having the density of air. This gives a theoretical figure with no friction or heat loss.

  1. To = 60 + 459.67 = 519.67 R. Tf = 350 + 459.67 = 809.67 R.
  2. 1/To = 0.0019243 and 1/Tf = 0.0012351, so the difference is 0.0006892.
  3. 0.52 x 20 x 14.7 = 152.88.
  4. Draft = 152.88 x 0.0006892 = 0.1054 in. w.c., about 0.105.

Converting at about 249 pascals per inch gives roughly 26 pascals. Real draft is lower, because gas cools as it rises, and the vent has friction and elbows. Still, compare it with the roughly 5 pascals (about 0.02 in. w.c.) of house depressurization that the field study linked to vent reversal: a tenth of an inch of theoretical draft is not a large margin. Doubling the height to 40 ft roughly doubles the result, and a cooler flue gas shrinks it, which is why a cold start spills more easily.

Field checks

  • Draft reading. Use a manometer with inches of water column resolution at the draft hood outlet, the breach or the vent connector, as the maker's instructions say, with the appliance warmed up. Compare to the maker's required range. We did not find a single sourced target to quote, so use the appliance label and manual.
  • Worst case. The state rule requires a final vent check at maximum input with all building exhaust fans running and outside openings closed. The federal energy department findings show why: the rest of the building can pull a natural draft vent backward.
  • Spillage. Check at the draft hood relief opening after the appliance has run for the time the code and maker specify. The fuel gas code tests spillage after five minutes of operation according to the federal energy department report.
  • Fan proving. Confirm the inducer or combustion fan starts, the switch closes only with proven flow, and the burner will not light with the switch held open. Never jump a proving switch.
  • Vent condition. Look for separated joints, rust at seams and sagging runs, especially on positive-pressure vents. Test combustion gas levels in the room.

Combustion safety is about carbon monoxide. Follow the maker's instructions and your site procedures, and shut down and report any appliance that spills or runs without a proven draft.

Field card: Natural, forced and induced draft

Find the fan, then find the pressure.

  • Natural draft: stack effect only; vent runs below room pressure; draft hood or barometric damper.
  • Forced draft: fan pushes air in; firebox and heat exchanger run above room pressure.
  • Induced draft: fan pulls gas through; heat exchanger runs below room pressure.
  • Category I vent is negative; Categories III and IV are positive and must be sealed.
  • Theoretical draft in our example: 0.105 in. w.c. (20 ft, 350 F, 60 F).
  • Prove the fan before gas flows; never jump a pressure switch.
  • Check draft warmed up, with exhaust fans on, and look for spillage.
