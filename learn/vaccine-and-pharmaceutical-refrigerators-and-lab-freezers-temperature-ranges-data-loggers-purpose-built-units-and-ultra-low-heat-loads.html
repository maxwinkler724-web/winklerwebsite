Vaccine Refrigerators and Lab Freezers: Temperature Ranges, Data Loggers and Heat Loads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you search for the vaccine refrigerator temperature range, the answer is 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F). A refrigeration tech gets involved when a unit will not hold that range, when it has to be moved, or when a room full of ultra-low freezers overloads the air conditioning. This lesson covers what the federal guidance says about the equipment, and how to work around it without putting product at risk.

Check the current guidance

Check the current guidance. This page was built from CDC, ENERGY STAR, DOE and EIA pages read in 2026. Each vaccine has its own storage limits in its package insert, and a state immunization program may add rules. Product decisions belong to the facility's vaccine coordinator, not to us as the service tech.

The temperature ranges

The CDC Vaccine Storage and Handling Toolkit says storage units must hold their range at all times. Refrigerators: 2 to 8 degrees C (36 to 46 degrees F). Freezers: minus 50 to minus 15 degrees C (minus 58 to plus 5 degrees F). Ultra-cold freezers: minus 90 to minus 60 degrees C (minus 130 to minus 76 degrees F). The CDC also notes a narrower band for one frozen product, minus 40 to minus 15 degrees C, so a unit shared with it must not drop below minus 40. The thermostat should be left at the factory setting or midpoint, such as 5 degrees C (41 degrees F) for a refrigerator. Thermostat markings show relative coldness, not temperature, which is why a separate monitoring device is needed.

Refrigerated food is a different job with a different limit; see Commercial Refrigeration Cold Holding, 41 F and Food Safety Checks. Medicine storage has a band that protects product at the warm end but also has a floor, because freezing can damage a refrigerated vaccine. That makes overcooling as much a fault as overheating.

Purpose-built units, and why dormitory units are out

The CDC equipment page ranks storage units. A unit that conforms to NSF/ANSI Standard 456 is preferred. Next come purpose-built units made for vaccines, then pharmaceutical-grade units, often with a microprocessor control, a digital sensor and fan-forced air. Household-grade units are a last option and for refrigeration only: the CDC says never to store vaccines in the freezer compartment of a household combination unit, so frozen product needs a separate stand-alone freezer.

The CDC is blunt about one type: never use dormitory- or bar-style combined refrigerator/freezer units. They have one door and an evaporator plate that can freeze product. If you are asked to supply a replacement, that is the type to refuse, even as a stopgap. The CDC also says not to store food or drinks in a vaccine unit, and not to crowd it, since crowding blocks air circulation and causes temperature swings.

Data loggers and the probe

A thermostat dial cannot prove temperature, so the toolkit calls for digital data loggers. Required features include a buffered, detachable probe, a low-battery indicator, and a display of current, minimum and maximum temperature. The toolkit recommends an uncertainty of plus or minus 0.5 degrees C (1 degree F), a programmable logging interval and an out-of-range alarm, with calibration testing every 2 to 3 years or on the maker's schedule. In our words, a buffered probe is built to follow the temperature of the product rather than every swing of the air when the door opens. The CDC page also says to have at least one backup logger, and the toolkit says the monitoring device should be centered among the vaccines.

For us, the lesson is simple: do not move or unplug the probe without telling the vaccine coordinator, and put it back where it was.

Room conditions and clearances

The CDC says to place units in a well-ventilated room, typically 20 to 25 degrees C (68 to 77 degrees F), with good air circulation around the outside of the unit. Keep at least 2 inches (5 cm) from the ceiling and 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) from adjacent walls. Keep the bottom 1 to 2 inches off the floor, the unit level, and the motor compartment cover unblocked. Follow the maker's own clearance numbers if they are larger. Our own habit: on a unit that drifts warm, a dirty condenser coil or a blocked grille is a first check. After a repair or a new unit, the CDC says to allow 2 to 7 days for a refrigerator and 2 to 3 days for a freezer to stabilize; it counts as stable after two consecutive days in range.

Before the service call, and during an excursion

The CDC excursion steps tell staff to notify the vaccine coordinator, label affected product "DO NOT USE", move it to correct conditions and keep it separate, not discard it, document the event, and call the immunization program or the maker for a decision. For a planned move, the toolkit tells staff to prepare packing before opening doors and to confirm that the backup storage location can accept the product before packing. It also says not to transport vaccines in the original shipping materials from the distributor.

Our suggested routine as the tech: ask who the coordinator is, ask whether the product has been moved before you open anything, write down the readings you see, and do not give opinions on whether product is still good. Record what you changed and the time the unit came back into range.

Power and backup

The toolkit says to plug in only one storage unit per outlet, use a safety-lock plug or cover with a "DO NOT UNPLUG" sign, and avoid outlets on wall switches or power strips. Any UPS or surge protection has to be rated for the unit's maximum current. For generators it says to keep fuel for at least 72 hours, test quarterly and service annually. If you work on the generator side, see Emergency Generator Room Ventilation.

Ultra-low freezers and the room they heat

ENERGY STAR says a conventional ultra-low temperature freezer can use about 20 kWh a day, as much as an average U.S. household, and DOE's Federal Energy Management Program says the same. ENERGY STAR's Version 2 lab-grade specification (a final draft we read, effective June 30, 2025) describes these freezers as holding set points between minus 70 and minus 80 degrees C, and caps daily energy at 0.46 kWh per cubic foot below 20 cubic feet and 0.35 kWh per cubic foot at 20 cubic feet and above, measured at minus 75 degrees C. A university green-labs page measured a newer upright at 12.2 kWh a day at minus 80 and 9.2 at minus 70 degrees C. DOE suggests a set point between minus 80 and minus 70 degrees C to save energy. That applies to research freezers; a vaccine unit has to stay inside the CDC range, so it should never be adjusted without the coordinator.

All that electricity ends up as heat in the room (our own reasoning: energy goes in and none of it leaves as stored product). A case study in the I2SL Smart Labs Toolkit adds that each ultra-low freezer causes an additional 4.5 kWh a day in building cooling requirements. The room AC has to be sized for that load; the same logic appears in Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet. Lab rooms with hoods are covered in Laboratory Fume Hoods, and sterile compounding rooms in Compounding Pharmacy HVAC.

Worked example: six freezers in one room

Assumptions (ours): six conventional ultra-low freezers; each uses 20 kWh/day (the ENERGY STAR and DOE figure); we ignore lighting, people and the building envelope. Conversion factor: 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu (EIA). Our own: one ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h.

  1. Daily energy: 6 x 20 = 120 kWh/day.
  2. Average power: 120 / 24 = 5.0 kW.
  3. Heat: 5.0 x 3,412 = 17,060 Btu/h.
  4. Tons: 17,060 / 12,000 = about 1.42 tons.

So the freezers alone ask the room AC for about 1.4 tons, around the clock, before people and lights. Now compare six newer units. Assumption (ours): each is 28 cubic feet and each just meets the ENERGY STAR cap of 0.35 kWh/day per cubic foot. That is 28 x 0.35 = 9.8 kWh/day each, 6 x 9.8 = 58.8 kWh/day, 58.8 / 24 = 2.45 kW, 2.45 x 3,412 = about 8,360 Btu/h, or about 0.70 ton. That is less than half the load. The ENERGY STAR figure is a limit at minus 75 degrees C, so a unit run at minus 80 will use more. Our own advice: because this load runs every hour of every season, ask whether the AC can run in cold weather, and think about a second unit so one failure does not warm the room.

Field card: Vaccine Refrigerators and Lab Freezers

Hold the range, protect the product, count the heat.

  • CDC ranges: refrigerator 2 to 8 C (36 to 46 F); freezer minus 50 to minus 15 C; ultra-cold minus 90 to minus 60 C.
  • Never a dormitory- or bar-style unit. Household units are refrigeration only, a last option.
  • Logger: buffered probe, min/max display, alarm, calibration every 2 to 3 years.
  • Clearance: 2 in. above, 1 to 2 in. at the sides, unblocked motor compartment, room 20 to 25 C.
  • Before service: the coordinator moves product first; write down readings; do not judge the product.
  • Heat: kWh/day / 24 x 3,412 = Btu/h; divide by 12,000 for tons.
