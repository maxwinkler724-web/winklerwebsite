Chiller Compressors: Centrifugal, Screw and Scroll Compared

Open the door of a chiller room and the compressor often tells you the machine's class. Three designs dominate: centrifugal, screw and scroll. Here is how each squeezes refrigerant, and where each usually fits.

Same job, three methods

Every water chiller runs the same refrigeration cycle: compress, condense, expand, evaporate. What differs is how the compressor does the first step. If the cycle itself is new to you, start with Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

Centrifugal

An engineering explainer describes refrigerant vapor entering at the center of a spinning impeller. The blades fling it outward at high speed, and the pressure builds as the vapor collects in the outer curl of the housing, called the volute, on its way to the condenser. A smaller variant often called a magnetic-bearing or oil-free machine uses two compression stages and magnetic bearings, and the same source credits that design for high efficiency.

A selection guide places centrifugal chillers at the large end of the range, in places like office towers and malls where load stays high. Maker catalogs agree: Trane lists its centrifugal lines from about 175 up past 4,000 tons, and Carrier lists one centrifugal family up to nearly 3,000 tons. Lineups change, so check the current catalog. The selection guide also says efficiency is good near full load and drops off at low load, and that surge, a flow instability inside the compressor, is a concern at low load on some machines, though newer designs have features that widen the stable range.

Screw

Two interlocking helical rotors trap refrigerant in the gap between them and squeeze it into a smaller space as they turn, raising its pressure and temperature. A manufacturer-side explainer describes the continuous motion as reducing vibration and holding performance steady as loads change.

The same selection guide describes screw chillers as suited to medium loads and buildings whose load swings, such as hotels and hospitals, and says they hold up better than centrifugal machines at part load but give up some efficiency at full load. In maker catalogs, Trane's screw lines run from about 80 up to 400 tons, while Carrier lists a water-cooled screw family from about 70 up to about 985 tons. So the screw range starts near 80 tons and reaches several hundred, and some makers' lines approach 1,000 tons. The ranges of the three types overlap across a wide middle band, where the load profile, space and total cost decide. Treat all of these as catalog examples, not rules.

On the machine, the explainer notes that water-cooled screw chillers usually carry the compressor on top, while air-cooled ones have it underneath, and air-cooled units often use one or two smaller screw compressors in parallel.

Scroll

Two spiral discs, one fixed and one orbiting, trap refrigerant and move it inward as the orbit continues. The explainer says scroll compressors are used mostly on air-cooled chillers, grouped in banks because one compressor rarely carries the whole load. Catalog examples are small to mid-size: Trane lists scroll chillers from 20 to 75 tons, and Carrier lists an air-cooled scroll family up to about 270 tons. A manufacturer overview lists offices, hotels, schools and clinics as typical homes. Staging compressors on and off is how such a chiller follows the load.

What reciprocating compressors add

Piston compressors, with their many moving parts, are described in the same explainer as strong and reliable when maintained. They are less common in current commercial chiller lineups, and the Trane catalog read for this lesson lists none. You may still meet an older one, so ask what the maker supports.

What the type means for service

  • **Centrifugal:** watch for approach and lift trends, because operation at low load and high lift is where trouble shows. See Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend.
  • **Screw:** follow the maker's instructions for the compressor. Details differ by model.
  • **Scroll banks:** with several compressors, one failed circuit can hide behind the others, so read each compressor's amps and status, not only the total.

Pulling it together

Size and load profile steer the choice. Big, steady loads lean centrifugal, medium and variable loads lean screw, and smaller air-cooled packages lean scroll. Real selection depends on a designer's analysis, and your site's equipment may break these patterns. When you meet an unfamiliar machine, find the compressor first, then the nameplate, then the maker's manual. Those three tell you what you are looking at and what the maker expects you to do, and they keep you from applying habits learned on a different type of machine. Ask the plant's operators how the machine behaves at low load too, because they know its quirks.

Field card: Chiller Compressors: Centrifugal, Screw and Scroll Compared

Same cycle, three squeezes.

  • Centrifugal: spinning impeller and volute; large plants.
  • Screw: two meshing rotors; medium and variable loads.
  • Scroll: orbiting spiral; banked on smaller air-cooled chillers.
  • Capacity ranges are catalog examples and change. Read the nameplate and manual.
  • Service habits differ by type, so learn the maker's instructions first.
