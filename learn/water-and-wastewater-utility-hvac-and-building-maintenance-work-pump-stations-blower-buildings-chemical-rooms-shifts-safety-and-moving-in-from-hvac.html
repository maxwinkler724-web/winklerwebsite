Water and Wastewater Utility HVAC and Maintenance Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every city and district that treats drinking water or wastewater also runs buildings: pump stations, blower buildings, a lab, a control building, chemical rooms and small remote stations. Those buildings need heating, ventilation and cooling, and someone has to keep them running. This lesson describes that work for HVAC techs thinking about it, and why it is a different kind of job from a service van.

What the job looks like

The federal labor statistics handbook describes treatment plant operators as people who manage machines that move or treat water or wastewater. They add chemicals, watch gauges, collect samples and keep equipment and tanks clean. At small plants one person may look after nearly everything; at large plants staff specialize. Separate from operators, the same handbook describes general maintenance and repair workers, whose duties include heating and air-conditioning systems, electrical repairs and preventive maintenance, and who coordinate with HVAC mechanics when a job is beyond their skill. A utility may have either type of person, or a dedicated HVAC tech on a maintenance crew.

Typical HVAC items at a utility, by building type:

  • Pump stations. Wet-well and dry-well ventilation fans, unit heaters, and a small cooling unit for the motor control room. Heat from motors and drives is the main cooling load.
  • Blower buildings. Large blowers make a lot of heat and noise. Expect intake louvers, relief dampers, exhaust fans and filters that load up.
  • Lab and control building. This is closest to ordinary commercial work: rooftop or split equipment, a computer and control room that must stay cool, lab exhaust and a clean, comfortable office area.
  • Chemical rooms. Dedicated exhaust, makeup air, corrosion-resistant duct and fans, and alarms. The ventilation protects people, so a failed fan is a safety problem, not a comfort call. See our battery room ventilation lesson for a related room that depends on exhaust.
  • Remote stations. Small buildings with a unit heater or electric heat to keep pipes, instruments and controls above freezing, often a long drive apart.

Shifts and on-call patterns

The handbook says plants run around the clock, so rotating shifts that include days, nights and weekends are common for operators, who may also be on call for emergencies. It says maintenance workers may be on call for emergency repairs outside business hours, including evenings and weekends. HVAC and building maintenance staff at a utility more often work a day schedule with an on-call rotation than a fixed night shift, but that varies by employer, so ask. A failed heater at a remote station in January, or a failed fan in a chemical room, is the kind of call that comes after hours.

Safety culture

Utility work puts several hazards together, and crews take them seriously.

  • Confined spaces. The federal worker safety agency describes confined spaces as large enough to enter but not designed for continuous occupancy, with limited ways in or out: tanks, vaults, pits, manholes. A space is permit-required if it has or could have a hazardous atmosphere, material that could engulf an entrant, a trapping shape, or another recognized hazard. Wet wells and vaults at stations often qualify. A tech who is not trained and on the entry permit stays out. Our confined spaces lesson covers the basics.
  • Hydrogen sulfide. The same agency says this gas occurs naturally in sewers, is heavier than air so it collects in low, enclosed places such as manholes and vaults, and smells like rotten eggs at low levels. Your sense of smell can fade, so you may stop noticing it. Effects range from headaches and eye irritation to unconsciousness and death, and it can overcome rescuers. That is why crews use gas monitors and ventilation and never rely on the nose.
  • Chemical handling. Under the federal hazard communication rule, employers must keep safety data sheets (a 16-section format) available, make sure labels are in place, and train workers on them. Read the sheet before working near a chemical room, and follow the site's rules for lockout and entry.
  • Electrical. Pump and blower motors, drives and panels are everywhere. See our arc flash and shock lesson.

The handbook also lists slippery walkways, dangerous gases and malfunctioning equipment among operator hazards and says operators use gloves, safety glasses and respirators. Follow your employer's written procedures; this page is not a procedure.

Skills that carry over from HVAC

  • Reading a nameplate, a wiring diagram and a control sequence.
  • Troubleshooting motors, contactors, belts, bearings and fans, which describes much of the work at a station.
  • Airflow, pressure and temperature measurement, including proving a ventilation rate or a room pressure.
  • Hot-water and small-boiler basics, if the lab or control building uses them.
  • Controls: thermostats, interlocks, alarms that report to a central control room.
  • Record keeping. Utilities log inspections; the handbook notes maintenance workers keep detailed records.

New skills to expect: pumps, seals and basic water or sewage process vocabulary; gas monitor use; confined-space and chemical training; and working beside operators who know the process.

How techs move into this work

The handbook says maintenance experience is useful for operators, and that prior experience or a related-trade program can shorten on-the-job training for maintenance workers. It describes operator training as long-term and on the job. A federal water-sector workforce page says systems face a shortage of qualified workers because of expected retirements, and mentions internships and apprenticeships for skilled water utility trades. Practical steps: apply for maintenance or facilities postings at a city, county or district; ask what training and entry rules the employer sets; and build your confined-space, electrical and chemical safety knowledge. We make no claims here about credentials, since they vary by place.

Worked example (our own): a sample week of planned hours

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not data from any utility. Assumptions: a district with 6 pump stations, 2 blower buildings, 1 lab and control building, 2 chemical rooms and 4 remote heater stations. Planned hours per unit per week are assumed: pump station 3.0 h of which 0.5 h is HVAC; blower building 6.0 h of which 1.0 h is HVAC; lab and control building 12.0 h of which 4.0 h is HVAC; chemical room 5.0 h of which 1.5 h is ventilation and HVAC; remote station 1.0 h of which 0.5 h is heater work.

  1. Pump stations: 6 × 3.0 = 18.0 h total; 6 × 0.5 = 3.0 h HVAC.
  2. Blower buildings: 2 × 6.0 = 12.0 h; 2 × 1.0 = 2.0 h HVAC.
  3. Lab and control: 1 × 12.0 = 12.0 h; 4.0 h HVAC.
  4. Chemical rooms: 2 × 5.0 = 10.0 h; 2 × 1.5 = 3.0 h HVAC.
  5. Remote stations: 4 × 1.0 = 4.0 h; 4 × 0.5 = 2.0 h HVAC.
  6. Total planned: 18.0 + 12.0 + 12.0 + 10.0 + 4.0 = 56.0 h. HVAC: 3.0 + 2.0 + 4.0 + 3.0 + 2.0 = 14.0 h.
  7. HVAC share: 14.0 ÷ 56.0 = 0.25, or 25.0%.

Check: the other hours are 56.0 − 14.0 = 42.0 h, and 42.0 ÷ 56.0 = 75.0%, so the two shares add to 100%. If two people work 40 hours each (80 h), planned work fills 56.0 ÷ 80 = 70.0% of the crew week, leaving 24.0 h for breakdowns, travel and paperwork. The lesson: in this assumed district, about a quarter of planned hours are HVAC, so a tech there also does non-HVAC tasks.

Field card: Water and Wastewater Utility HVAC and Maintenance Work

Same fans, heaters and controls, more hazards and more process.

  • Buildings: pump stations, blower buildings, lab and control, chemical rooms, remote heaters.
  • Shifts: operators rotate; maintenance staff may be on call for after-hours repairs.
  • Hazards: confined spaces, hydrogen sulfide (do not trust your nose), chemicals, electrical.
  • Carry-over: motors, airflow, controls, records.
  • Our example: 14.0 of 56.0 planned hours is HVAC, 25.0%.
