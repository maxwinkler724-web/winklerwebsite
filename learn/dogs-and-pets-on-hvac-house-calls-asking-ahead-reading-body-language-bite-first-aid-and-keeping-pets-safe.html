Dogs and Pets on HVAC House Calls: Asking Ahead, Body Language, Bite First Aid

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most homes you service have a dog, a cat, or both, and the basement, attic or utility closet is often where they hide, sleep or eat. A calm pet is part of the job. A frightened one can bite, bolt out the door you propped open, or get into your tools and chemicals. This lesson covers how to ask ahead, read a dog, respond to a bite, and leave the home safe for the animals in it.

Ask before you arrive

The Postal Service tells dog owners to secure the dog before the carrier reaches the property, and reminds them that any dog can bite, including a friendly one (USPS local release, 2024). The same idea works for us. The easiest bite to survive is the one that never has a chance to happen, so make pets part of scheduling. Our own suggested wording when you book or confirm: "Do you have any pets? For everyone's safety, please have dogs put in a closed room or crate before we arrive, and tell us which room. We will also keep doors closed so nobody slips out." Put the answer in the job notes so the tech who arrives knows. See writing a useful service ticket for where such notes belong.

Do not trust "he's friendly"

The CDC says to ask the owner before petting a dog, even one that seems friendly, and not to approach an unfamiliar dog. It also lists times when dogs are more likely to bite: when scared or nervous, eating, playing or guarding toys or puppies, not feeling well, or wanting to be left alone. A basement furnace is often the dog's den, bed or food spot, so you are working in its territory. Owners are usually sincere. Treat the word "friendly" as information about the owner's experience, not a guarantee.

Reading body language

A college canine welfare center at Purdue University publishes a guide that groups dog signals into relaxed, stressed or fearful, and threat behaviors. We turned its lists into the chart below, in our own words. Look at the whole dog, not one signal. The guide notes that calming signals such as lip licking or yawning do not mean a dog will not bite, and that you should stop the interaction when they show up alongside other fearful behavior.

If you see stress or warning signs, do not reach out, do not stare, and do not corner the dog. Ask the owner to secure it, and wait.

If a dog approaches

The CDC steps for an unfamiliar dog that approaches: stay still and calm, avoid direct eye contact, and stand with the side of your body toward the dog. Firmly say "no" or "go home" in a deep voice. Slowly raise your hands to your neck with elbows tucked in, then wait for the dog to pass or slowly back away. Do not panic, shout, or run, and do not try to break up a dog fight. If you are knocked down, the CDC says to curl into a ball, tuck your head, and cover your ears and neck. The Postal Service adds that you can put something such as a satchel between you and the dog; a clipboard or toolbag can do the same job. It also tells carriers never to try to pet or feed a dog.

If you are bitten

  1. Minor wound. The CDC says to wash thoroughly with soap and water, apply antibiotic cream and cover with a clean bandage. Germs can spread even from a wound that looks small.
  2. Deep or bleeding wound. Press with a clean, dry cloth and get medical help right away. The CDC says to call 911 if bleeding will not stop or you feel faint or weak. NIH MedlinePlus also says to raise the bitten area, and to call a doctor right away for bites on the head, face, neck, hand or fingers.
  3. See a provider if the wound is deep or serious, shows infection (redness, pain, swelling, fever), you do not know whether the dog has a rabies vaccination, or it has been more than 5 years since your last tetanus shot (CDC). MedlinePlus says to check for increasing redness, swelling and pain over the next 24 to 48 hours.
  4. Report it. The CDC says to report bites to animal control or police, especially if the rabies vaccine status is unknown. If possible, get the owner's name, address and phone, the vet's name and the rabies vaccine license number.

Tell your employer, and record what happened on the ticket. For truck kit contents see first aid for HVAC jobsite injuries. Torn skin and bleeding hands are also a reason to read the glove lesson.

Doors, gates and escape

A pet that slips out while you carry equipment through is a real risk to the animal. Keep doors and gates closed behind you, even for a quick trip to the truck. The Postal Service tip for carriers is to brace a foot against an outward-swinging door to keep a dog from getting out. Before you prop anything open, find where the pets are and ask the owner to confine them. Close garage doors, side gates and bulkhead doors once you pass through. If a pet does escape, tell the owner at once instead of chasing it, since a loose, frightened dog may run from a stranger.

Open cabinets, refrigerant, chemicals and bait

Pets investigate open panels, warm furnace cabinets, dropped parts and spilled liquids. Our own habits: put the panel back or close the cabinet when you step away; keep tools, cleaners, coil cleaner, drain-pan tablets and refrigerant cylinders on the truck or on a closed cart; wipe up spills at once; and keep your own food and drinks out of reach.

Some household products are real dangers. A Michigan State University veterinary diagnostic page lists cleaners, antifreeze and rodent poisons among common pet hazards, and says that if a pet may have swallowed something, call a poison hotline immediately and keep the container. A University of Illinois veterinary college page says antifreeze containing ethylene glycol tastes sweet to pets and can be fatal, that any exposure should be treated as serious, and that rodent bait often contains bromethalin, cholecalciferol or anticoagulants that dogs and cats frequently eat. That page says two teaspoons of bromethalin can be toxic to a 25-pound dog. If you see bait out in a basement or utility space, do not move it; tell the owner it is a risk. For refrigerant handling see refrigerant safety. A gas furnace problem can affect pets too, since carbon monoxide hurts animals as well as people; see combustion and CO safety.

Worked example: a basement furnace call with a loose "friendly" dog

This is our own scenario. The dog, house and conversation are assumptions we made up to show the steps.

The call

Assumption: the customer calls about a furnace that will not start, and the furnace is in the basement.

Scheduler: "Do you have pets at home?"
Customer: "One dog, Max. He is friendly, he'll just follow you around."
Scheduler: "Thanks. Our tech will still need the dog in another room or a crate while working, since a basement is tight and parts and tools will be out. Can someone do that before the tech arrives? Which room should the tech go to, and is anything in the basement the dog eats or sleeps near?"
Customer: "His bed is next to the furnace."
Scheduler: "OK. I will note the bed, and that the tech will ask you to confine Max."

On site, step by step

  1. Stay at the door. Knock or ring and wait. Keep the storm door and any gate closed between you and the dog until someone has control of it.
  2. Ask again. "Is Max put away?" If he is loose, say pleasantly: "I need him in another room while I work. Can you do that now?" Do not pet him, offer food or make eye contact.
  3. Read the dog from where you stand. Relaxed signs from the chart: loose body, soft eyes. Stressed or warning signs: stiff body, hard stare, growl, hiding under stairs. If any warning sign appears, stop, wait outside, and ask for a closed room. If the owner cannot control the dog, reschedule. That is a safety decision, not a failure.
  4. Walk the route. With the dog gone, check the basement for the dog's bed, food bowl, bait, antifreeze or chemicals. Ask the owner to move the bed.
  5. Close doors behind you at the top and bottom of the stairs.
  6. Work. Keep panels, parts and cleaners off the floor within reach of a returning dog. Cover the cabinet when you step away.
  7. Clean up. Pick up screws, wire clippings, filter scraps, tape and rags, wipe any drips, and put the cabinet door back. Tell the owner when the room is safe and the dog may return.
  8. Ticket. Note that the dog was confined, and anything unsafe you saw.

If the dog comes at you before it is secured, follow the approach steps above, put your toolbag between you and the dog, and back toward the stairs or door without turning and running.

Cleaning up for pets

Pets will sniff and chew what you leave. Collect small parts, wire ends, zip ties, cut insulation, and tape. Take used rags and old filters with you or bag them. Wipe up drips, and keep cleaner and coolant containers closed. Tell the owner if you used anything a pet should not lick or eat.

Field card: Pets on service calls

Ask ahead, confine early, close doors, leave it clean.

  • Ask about pets when booking. Request that dogs be confined before arrival.
  • "Friendly" is not a guarantee; do not pet or feed.
  • Read the whole dog: relaxed, stressed or warning signs.
  • If approached: stay still, side-on, no eye contact, firm "no," back away slowly.
  • Bite: wash with soap and water, press if bleeding, 911 for uncontrolled bleeding, seek care for deep wounds or unknown rabies status, report it.
  • Close doors and gates behind you.
  • Keep cabinets, cleaners and refrigerant out of reach; call a poison hotline if a pet swallows something.
  • Leave no small parts, rags or spills.
