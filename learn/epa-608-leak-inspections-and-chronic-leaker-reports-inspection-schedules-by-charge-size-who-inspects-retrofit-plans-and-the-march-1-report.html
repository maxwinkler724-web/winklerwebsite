EPA 608 Leak Inspections and Chronic Leaker Reports: Schedules by Charge Size, Who Inspects, Retrofit Plans and the March 1 Report

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Once a big system leaks more than its allowed rate, the federal rules stop treating it as a one-time repair and start treating it as a watched appliance. This lesson covers the inspection schedule, who may do the inspecting, the retrofit or retirement plan, and the report EPA wants when an appliance loses a whole charge or more in a year.

Check current EPA rules

Check current EPA rules. This is general education, not legal advice. We read the eCFR text and EPA pages in November 2026. The eCFR is unofficial and rules change, so confirm against EPA and the published regulation before a compliance decision. We do not repeat the leak-rate math, the verification tests or the full records list here; those have their own lessons, linked below.

Which rule covers which refrigerant

Two sets of rules now sit side by side, and the refrigerant decides which one applies.

  • 40 CFR 82.157 (the Section 608 rule). The text says that since April 10, 2020, the section applies only to appliances with a full charge of 50 or more pounds, and that it does not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants. In plain terms: ozone-depleting refrigerants, meaning CFCs and HCFCs, in appliances of 50 lb or more.
  • 40 CFR 84.106 (part 84, subpart C). The eCFR text applies it from January 1, 2026 to appliances with a full charge of 15 or more pounds of a regulated substance or a substitute with a global warming potential above 53. It excludes appliances containing solely an ozone-depleting substance, and it excludes residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump appliances. EPA's January 2026 fact sheet says the same: HFC and certain HFC-substitute appliances at 15 lb or more.

So the same 80 lb rack could be under 82.157 if it holds an HCFC, or under 84.106 if it holds an HFC blend above that GWP line. The structure of the two rules is the same, which is why one lesson can teach both. The difference to remember is the size floor: 50 lb for ozone-depleting refrigerant, 15 lb for the newer HFC-type coverage. EPA has also released a prepublication proposal that would exempt road and intermodal container transport refrigeration units from 84.106; it is a proposal only, and EPA says it is not proposing other changes to that rule. Definitions for terms like full charge, commercial refrigeration and comfort cooling are in our definitions lesson.

The inspection duty is triggered by the leak rate

The regulation does not ask every appliance for a routine inspection. 82.157(g)(1) says the owner or operator must conduct a leak inspection on any appliance exceeding the applicable leak rate. The trigger rates in 82.157(c) and 84.106(c)(2) are 20 percent for commercial refrigeration, 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other appliances. How the owner gets that percentage is in our leak-rate lesson.

Under 84.106(g)(1) the inspections run "after the date of a successful follow-up verification test." That is the cue to read the verification test lesson: the inspection clock does not start until that test passes. The schedule, from 82.157(g)(1) and 84.106(g)(1):

  • Commercial refrigeration or industrial process refrigeration, 500 lb or more: every three months, until the appliance has not leaked above its rate for four quarters in a row.
  • Commercial refrigeration or industrial process refrigeration, under 500 lb: once a year, until it has not leaked above its rate for one year. The lower edge is 50 lb under 82.157 and 15 lb under 84.106.
  • Comfort cooling and other appliances: once a year, until one year without exceeding the rate.

Direction matters: bigger commercial and industrial systems get more frequent checks (every three months), and the stop condition is a clean stretch, not a fixed number of visits. An automatic leak detection system that is audited or calibrated annually can stand in for the scheduled inspections of what it monitors, subject to its own requirements in 84.108 and the matching 82.157 paragraphs.

Who may inspect and what counts

82.157(g)(2) says leak inspections must be conducted by a certified technician, using methods the technician finds appropriate for that appliance. That could be an electronic detector, bubble solution, dye or a pressure check; the rule leaves the method to the technician. (Our detector lesson covers search technique.)

The scope is set by 82.157(g)(3): all visible and accessible components of the appliance. The listed exceptions are components that are insulated, under ice on the outside of the equipment, underground, behind walls or otherwise inaccessible; locations that would put personnel more than two meters above a support surface; and components that site personnel determine are unsafe to inspect. The eCFR summary of 84.106(g) lists the same kind of limits. So an inspection is a walk-through of what can be reached and seen with a proper method, not a teardown.

Retrofit or retirement plans

An owner can choose to retrofit or retire an appliance instead of repairing it, and must do so if repairs do not hold. Per the eCFR text for 84.106(h) and 82.157(h), the written plan is due within 30 days of choosing that route, of failing to act on a leak, or of continued leaking after repairs and verification tests. It identifies the appliance and location, refrigerant and charge, and what will be done, with a schedule of no more than one year. It is signed and dated by an authorized official and kept on site; EPA's leak repair page says it is provided to EPA on request. Work generally must finish within one year. A request for more time under (i) must be submitted within seven months of discovering the excess leak, and it is approved unless EPA objects within 60 days.

Chronic leaker reports

The chronic leaker rule is separate from the trigger rates. Under 82.157(j), owners of appliances with 50 lb or more that leak 125 percent or more of the full charge in a calendar year must submit a report to EPA by March 1 of the subsequent year, describing efforts to identify leaks and repair the appliance. Under 84.106(j) the same test applies from 15 lb. The 84.106 report content is listed in the regulation as the owner, facility and appliance identity, refrigerant type, full charge, annual percent loss, addition dates and amounts, the last successful follow-up test date, the cause of the losses, repairs, any retrofit or retirement plan and expected date, and a signed statement from an authorized company official. It goes in electronically through EPA's reporting platform. Notice the wording "125 percent or more": exactly 125 percent counts.

Records

Inspection results, verification tests, retrofit plans and extension requests are kept for at least three years under the regulation text, and 84.106 adds full charge records for each appliance of 15 lb or more. The details are in our records lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a commercial refrigeration system with a full charge of 80 lb that has been in service for years. Assume each time refrigerant is added, the technician brings the system back to full charge, and that no refrigerant is removed or purged for any other reason. Assume four additions in calendar year 2026, each 73 days after the one before. We use the annualizing method: pounds added divided by full charge, divided by (days since last addition divided by 365), times 100. Because 73 divided by 365 is exactly 0.2, each rate is the pounds-added fraction times 5.

  • Which rule? If the refrigerant is an HCFC, 82.157 applies because 80 lb is at least 50 lb. If it is an HFC blend above a GWP of 53, 84.106 applies because 80 lb is at least 15 lb and this is commercial refrigeration, not residential or light commercial air conditioning. The numbers below are the same either way.
  • Trigger: commercial refrigeration is 20 percent.
  • Addition 1, 20 lb: 20 / 80 = 0.25; 0.25 / 0.2 = 1.25; times 100 = 125 percent. Above 20 percent.
  • Addition 2, 30 lb: 30 / 80 = 0.375; / 0.2 = 1.875; 187.5 percent. Above 20 percent.
  • Addition 3, 35 lb: 35 / 80 = 0.4375; / 0.2 = 2.1875; about 218.8 percent. Above 20 percent.
  • Addition 4, 15 lb: 15 / 80 = 0.1875; / 0.2 = 0.9375; about 93.8 percent. Above 20 percent.

Inspection schedule. The system is commercial refrigeration under 500 lb, so the schedule is once a year after a successful follow-up verification test, until a full year passes without exceeding 20 percent. It is not the every-three-months schedule, which starts at 500 lb. If the same system held 500 lb or more, it would be quarterly.

Chronic leaker check. Total added in the year: 20 + 30 + 35 + 15 = 100 lb. 100 / 80 = 1.25, which is 125 percent of the full charge. The rule says "125 percent or more," so the report is triggered, due by March 1, 2027. Our reading is that the amount leaked is tallied from the refrigerant needed to restore the charge, which is why we assumed additions equal losses. As a check on direction: had the fourth addition been 14 lb, the total would be 99 lb, or 123.75 percent, and this test would not be met, though the annual inspections and repair duties would still apply.

Field card: leak inspections and chronic leakers

Over the rate means inspect on a schedule. A full charge or more lost in a year means report.

  • 82.157: ozone-depleting refrigerant, 50 lb or more. Not appliances with only substitutes.
  • 84.106: HFC-type, 15 lb or more, from January 1, 2026. Excludes residential and light commercial AC and heat pumps.
  • Triggers: 20 percent commercial, 30 percent industrial, 10 percent comfort cooling and other.
  • Inspect every 3 months at 500 lb or more (commercial and industrial) until 4 quarters pass.
  • Inspect yearly below 500 lb and for comfort cooling until 1 year passes.
  • A certified technician inspects all visible and accessible components.
  • Retrofit or retirement plan: within 30 days, finish in about one year.
  • Chronic leaker: 125 percent or more of full charge in a calendar year, report by March 1.
