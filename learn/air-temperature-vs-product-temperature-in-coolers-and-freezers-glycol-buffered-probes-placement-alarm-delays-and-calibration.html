Air vs Product Temperature in Coolers and Freezers: Buffered Probes, Alarm Delays and Calibration

Last updated: 2026-10-28

A walk-in controller shows 46 F for twenty minutes every time it defrosts, and the alarm goes off. Nobody opened the door, nothing is spoiling, and the owner has learned to silence the horn. The reading was real, but it was the temperature of the air, not of the product. This lesson explains the difference and how a buffered probe, sensible placement and an alarm delay turn a noisy alarm into a useful one.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page was built from a federal vaccine-storage toolkit, university extension material and state guidance read in 2026. Food rules are adopted locally and each vaccine has its own package insert, so the operator, the vaccine coordinator and the local health department decide what applies. We are describing equipment and sensors, not judging whether product is safe.

Why air and product move differently

Air has very little mass. When a door opens or an evaporator heats up during defrost, the air around the sensor changes in minutes. Product, boxes, liquid and the walls are heavy, and they change slowly because heat has to soak in. In our words: the air is the quick, noisy signal, and the product is the slow, steady one. A bare thermometer follows the air. The CDC Vaccine Storage and Handling Toolkit says the same thing plainly: standard thermometers typically measure air temperature, while a buffered probe better reflects vaccine temperature. Warm air that lasts only a short time does not warm a full load by the same amount. That is why the defrost cycle can push box air well past the limit and leave product only a degree or two warmer.

What a buffered probe is

The CDC toolkit glossary describes a buffered probe as a sensor immersed in a liquid such as glycol, ethanol or glycerin, in loose media such as sand or glass beads, or set in a solid block such as plastic or aluminum. The sensor sits in a bottle or block with real thermal mass, so it lags the air the way product does. The toolkit also says a probe permanently embedded in its buffer is acceptable if the whole assembly can be calibration-tested. Our own note: a product simulator only mimics product. It is a stand-in, so it does not replace a real product check where the food rules call for one.

Where to put the probe

For vaccine storage, the toolkit says to place the buffered probe in the center of the unit with the vaccines surrounding it, and to keep the display outside so staff do not open the door to read it. It warns that a probe near a wall, the floor, a vent, the ceiling or the door can read colder or warmer than the product. The same ideas carry over to a walk-in, in our words: keep the probe away from the evaporator discharge, the door and the floor, and put it where product actually sits. A box has warm spots and cold spots, so a second probe at the warmest shelf, usually near the door, is a common choice. Our own advice for a service visit: write down where the probe was before you touch it, and put it back there. See Portable Data Loggers for HVAC Diagnostics for placement and sample intervals on a diagnostic logger.

Alarm delays

The toolkit lists an out-of-range alarm and a programmable logging interval as required logger features, and it does not give a delay time, so any delay is a site decision. Our own rule of thumb: an alarm on a bare air probe either needs a long delay (to ride through defrost and door traffic) or it will cry wolf. An alarm on a buffered probe can use a shorter, more meaningful delay because the probe has already filtered out the brief spikes. The cost of any delay is time: the alarm sounds later, so pick the delay with the operator and write it down.

Worked example (ours)

All numbers here are our own assumptions for illustration, not measurements or maker data. A walk-in cooler normally holds air near 37 F. Its evaporator defrosts four times a day (assumed). During each defrost the air probe reads 46 F for 20 minutes. A glycol-buffered probe in the same box starts at 37 F. We model it as a simple first-order lag: it moves toward the air temperature by the fraction 1 minus e to the power of (minus time divided by a time constant).

  1. We pick the time constant so the buffered probe rises 1.5 F in 20 minutes: 37 + 1.5 = 38.5 F. The air step is 46 minus 37 = 9 F.
  2. Fraction of the step covered: 1.5 divided by 9 = 0.1667.
  3. So 1 minus e^(-20/tau) = 0.1667, which gives e^(-20/tau) = 0.8333. Take the natural log: 20/tau = 0.1823, and tau = 20 / 0.1823 = about 110 minutes.
  4. When the air drops back to 37 F, the probe drifts back down. After another 40 minutes it is near 37 + 1.5 x e^(-40/110) = 37 + 1.5 x 0.695 = about 38.0 F.

Now the alarms. Alarm A is on the air probe at 41 F with no delay. It trips about 1 to 2 minutes into every defrost and stays on for about 20 minutes: four false alarms a day, a horn that nobody trusts. Alarm B is on the buffered probe at 41 F with a 30-minute delay. The probe never gets above 38.5 F, so it stays quiet through every defrost. A 20-minute air spike also could not trip a 30-minute delay by itself.

The delay does cost time on a real fault. Suppose a door is left open and the air sits at 50 F. The buffered probe reaches 41 F when 37 + 13 x (1 minus e^(-t/110)) = 41. That gives 1 minus e^(-t/110) = 4/13 = 0.3077, so t = 110 x 0.368 = about 40 minutes. Add the 30-minute delay and Alarm B sounds at about 70 minutes, while the air probe would have shown the problem in minutes. That is why we pair them: a buffered-probe alarm for product, and a separate, slower or higher air alarm that warns of a door left open or a failed fan. How long a real product can sit warm is a decision for the operator and the health department, not for us.

Calibration with an ice-point bath

An ice-point bath checks a sensor at one known temperature. A Penn State Extension video describes the method: fill a glass to the top with crushed ice, add water, stir, wait about a minute, then put the stem in the center, away from the sides and bottom. After 15 to 30 seconds it should read 32 F (0 C). The same page says thermometers for food use should be accurate to within plus or minus 2 F. The CDC toolkit asks more of vaccine monitors: a recommended uncertainty of plus or minus 0.5 C (1 F), calibration testing every 2 to 3 years or on the maker's schedule, and replacement if the device tests outside plus or minus 0.5 C (1 F). It says acceptable calibration reports show traceability to national standards, such as ISO/IEC 17025 conformity. Two limits of our own: the bath is a field check at one point, not a formal calibration, and a buffered probe is slow, so give it extra time to settle before you read it. Do not trust a reading taken while the number is still moving. Our meter and gauge lesson shows the ice bath for tool checks.

What the food and vaccine guidance says

The FDA Food Code is a model code that local regulators adopt in their own versions. A state inspection guidance document we read cites 41 F or below for cold holding of foods that need temperature control for safety, under section 3-501.16(A)(2). The FDA page itself did not state the number, so confirm it in your local code. That is a limit for the food, which is why an air probe alone is only a clue. See Cold Holding at 41 F. For vaccines, the CDC toolkit gives a refrigerator range of 2 to 8 C (36 to 46 F), tells staff to check and record minimum and maximum temperatures at the start of each workday, and to keep logs for 3 years. It also says unit doors opened often for other items can harm the internal temperature, and it lists chart recorders, alcohol or mercury thermometers, infrared devices and food-use devices as unacceptable monitors. Our vaccine refrigerator lesson covers the equipment side.

Field card: Air vs Product Temperature

Air is the fast signal. Product is the slow one.

  • Standard thermometers read air; a buffered probe (glycol, glass beads, sand or a solid block) better reflects product (CDC).
  • Probe goes in the center of the load, away from walls, floor, vents, ceiling and door; display outside.
  • Air alarms need a delay or they fire at every defrost; buffered alarms can use a shorter delay.
  • Delays add time to a real alarm. Our example: buffer reaches 41 F in about 40 min plus a 30-min delay.
  • Ice-point check: crushed ice and water, 32 F; food thermometers within 2 F; vaccine monitors within 1 F (0.5 C).
  • Cold holding 41 F or below applies to food; local code governs. Vaccine refrigerators: 36 to 46 F.
  • You report readings and locations. The operator decides about the product.
