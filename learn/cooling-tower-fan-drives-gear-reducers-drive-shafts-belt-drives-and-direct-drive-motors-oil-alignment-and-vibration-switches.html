Cooling Tower Fan Drives: Gear Reducers, Belts, Direct Drive and Vibration Switches

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The motor on a cooling tower turns about 1,780 rpm, but the fan wants to turn a few hundred. Something has to slow it down. This lesson compares the three ways towers do that, what to check on each, and how the vibration switch protects the whole arrangement.

Check your own manual

Check your own manual. Oil grades, intervals and switch settings differ by tower maker and gear maker. The numbers below come from the maker manuals we list and are examples, not rules for your tower. We do not recommend any brand.

Lock it out before you go on the fan deck

A tower fan can start from a remote command, and a stopped fan can windmill in the breeze. One maker manual says a lockable disconnect must be within sight of the unit and that service starts only after power is off and the unit is locked and tagged out. Another says to lock out the electrical disconnect until the period of exposure to injury is over. The federal rule, 29 CFR 1910.147, describes the order: prepare, shut down, isolate, apply locks or tags, release or restrain stored energy, then verify the equipment is isolated before work begins. A VFD maker note adds that you should confirm the drive is off before entering the unit. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC for the full habit.

Three ways to turn the fan

  • Gear drive. The motor sits outside or beside the fan stack and drives a right-angle gear reducer through a drive shaft with a coupling at each end. The reducer cuts the speed and turns the shaft 90 degrees up to the fan.
  • Belt drive. Sheaves and belts cut the speed. One maker applies belts on some induced-draft units, with the motor mounted outside or inside the unit. Our belt drive lesson covers the basics.
  • Direct drive. The fan hub mounts on a slow-speed motor. One motor maker describes a permanent magnet synchronous design that takes the place of the gearbox, jack shaft, pillow block bearings and couplings, and says a drive is required to run it. A CTI paper by a motor maker gave an example of a 50 hp motor at 208 rpm in place of an induction motor and gearbox. This lesson does not cover EC motors in detail because we did not find a maker manual we could cite for them.

Gear reducer oil: type, level and change

Always follow the gear maker's manual, because the oil is part of the design. One tower maker's reducer manual lists a synthetic oil in ISO 150 for air temperature at the reducer below 110 F (43 C) and ISO 220 above that, and warns that a substitute oil must not carry additives that moisture harms. Its checks are timed in months and years, not run hours: shut down, wait 5 minutes for the level to settle, then read the level monthly. It says that oil from the maker can run five years between changes, but must be inspected every six months for contamination, while turbine-type mineral oil must be changed every six months. A different maker's schedule is simpler: check the level with the unit stopped 24 hours after start-up and then monthly, look for leaks monthly, and replace the oil semiannually. Its manual says to follow the gear maker for oil type. A third maker's schedule, for its own reducer, calls for checking the level daily, checking for water and sludge monthly, and changing oil semiannually.

Three makers, three schedules. That is why we say read your tower's manual first. Milky oil or oil with a sludge layer points to water getting in. Report it. For long idle periods, one maker says to run the reducer 5 minutes each week during a few idle weeks, and to fill it completely with oil for longer idle periods, then drain to the normal level before running.

Drive shaft and coupling checks

One maker lists a monthly inspection of the coupling flex elements and hardware for tightness, proper torque, cracks and deterioration, and an alignment check between reducer and coupling 24 hours after start-up and then monthly. Another puts the coupling bolt and set screw check at annual, and sends you to the separate driveshaft or coupling manual for alignment. The two differ, so use the interval in the manual for your drive. A cracked flex element or a loose hub gives you a wobble you can feel in the whole stack.

Belt tension

For belt-driven towers, one maker says to check tension at start-up, again after the first 24 hours to take out the first stretch, and monthly after that. The target is about 1/2 inch of deflection with moderate pressure at the midpoint between the sheaves. A belt that is right will not chirp or squeal when the motor starts. The same manual notes that very tight belts can overamp the motor. On the externally mounted motor models, both adjusting bolts should show the same amount of thread, which keeps the sheaves aligned.

Vibration cutout switch

The switch stops the fan if the drive starts shaking hard, for example from ice on the blades or a failing part. One maker's manual says it shuts the fan off to avoid damage or failure of the drive system. A mechanical switch has a magnet-held latch. A shock or vibration beyond the setting moves the magnet and releases the latch, which flips its contacts. Typical wiring is one set of contacts in the motor starter or VFD run circuit, and the other in the building alarm circuit.

  • Routine checks. One tower maker lists checking the enclosure for loose wiring and moisture one month after start-up and monthly after that, and adjusting the sensitivity at start-up and every year.
  • Function test. A switch maker's manual hosted on a tower maker's site tells you to confirm the switch is reset, then strike the switch lid or support beam with a dead-blow hammer and watch for the red plunger to pop out. Do this only with the fan locked out, because the manual also says not to adjust the set point with live wires present. With the circuit locked out you can also check the contacts with an ohmmeter: another tower maker's procedure gives 0 ohms on the closed contact when reset, and open on the other, with the readings flipping when tripped.
  • Setting. One tower maker's procedure finds the trip point first, then backs off in quarter turns. The switch maker says the factory range is 0 to 7 g and that a change of 1/16 inch can move the trip point a lot. Do not force the screw. Use the maker's steps, not guesses.
  • Never bypass it, and do not just reset a tripped switch. The switch maker says to inspect the tower, fan and drive train first.

VFDs and resonance lockout bands

A VFD can run the fan through a speed where the structure shakes. One tower maker says to step through the range in 2 Hz steps, wait at each step until the fan settles, then step back down, and note any rise in vibration. Frequencies that shake the tower are then locked out in the VFD settings during commissioning. That maker lists 25 percent speed (15 Hz) as the lowest recommended fan speed unless the submittal says otherwise. The switch maker adds that if the switch trips on a slow ramp up, the tower may need a vibration analysis, with a ramp of about 5 seconds per 2 Hz as its suggested pace. See our VFD staging lesson and vibration basics.

Blade pitch and tip clearance

One maker says every blade must be set to the same pitch, and that pitch is set so the fan draws the contract horsepower at design flow, checked by measuring motor load after about 30 minutes of running. The manual gives no pitch angle, so you read it from the fan manual. Another maker's startup list says blade tip clearance should be at least 1/4 inch to the fan cowl, with blades tight in the hub. A rubbing blade is fixed by adjusting the cylinder. The same maker lists a quarterly look at blades for cracks and missing balance weights, and a third lists tip clearance semiannually. Before start-up, turn the fan by hand to be sure it spins freely.

Worked example: fan speed and tip speed

Our own, with assumed inputs. We assume a 1,780 rpm motor, a 7.6:1 gear reducer, and a 12 ft diameter fan. These are not from any maker.

  1. Gear drive fan speed = 1,780 ÷ 7.6 = 234.2 rpm.
  2. Tip speed = π × diameter × rpm = 3.1416 × 12 ft × 234.2 = 8,830 fpm.
  3. The 1x vibration frequency is 234.2 ÷ 60 = 3.9 Hz, while the motor turns at 1,780 ÷ 60 = 29.7 Hz. A 2 Hz VFD step is 2 ÷ 60 of full speed, or about 7.8 fan rpm.

Belt version, also assumed. Take a 4.0 in motor sheave and a 28.0 in fan sheave. Fan rpm = 1,780 × 4.0 ÷ 28.0 = 254.3 rpm. Tip speed = 3.1416 × 12 × 254.3 = 9,590 fpm. The ratio is 7:1, close to the gear case, so the fan runs a bit faster. Fan power rises with the cube of speed, one CTI paper notes, so even a small speed change matters: half speed is one eighth of the power.

Field card: Cooling Tower Fan Drives: Gear Reducers, Belts, Direct Drive and Vibration Switches

Lock out, then look, listen, check oil, never bypass the switch.

  • Lock out and verify before reaching into the fan stack.
  • Gear oil: follow the gear maker; check level with the fan stopped; intervals vary by maker.
  • Milky or sludgy oil: report it.
  • Coupling: flex elements, bolts and alignment per the manual.
  • Belts: about 1/2 in deflection at midpoint in one maker's manual; equal thread on both adjusting bolts.
  • Vibration switch: check wiring and moisture, test by the maker's method, never bypass.
  • VFD: sweep in 2 Hz steps and lock out resonant bands.
  • Tip clearance: at least 1/4 in in one maker's list.
