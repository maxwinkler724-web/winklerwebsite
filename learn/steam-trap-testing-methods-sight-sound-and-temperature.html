Steam Trap Testing: Sight, Sound and Temperature

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

A steam trap can fail open and waste steam, or fail closed and flood a coil. Finding out which takes more than a quick touch. Each test method answers a different question and misses another.

What you are trying to find

A trap that fails open lets live steam blow through, wasting energy. A trap that fails closed backs condensate up, which cuts capacity and can lead to water hammer. A federal technology review of steam traps describes both and ranks several test methods by reliability. For the basic idea of what a trap does, see Steam Traps Explained.

Sight

Watching the discharge can show the difference between flash steam, which is a soft cloud from hot condensate dropping in pressure, and live steam, which is a clear fast jet. The federal review lists sight first for reliability, but it needs a sight glass or a test valve. A trap manufacturer says to start with a visual check because it sometimes gives a clear answer, and that it also shows external leaks at joints and gaskets. In closed piping, you often cannot see the discharge.

Temperature

A cold trap on the inlet side suggests it is blocked or flooded. The manufacturer adds two cautions:

  • Temperature cannot tell leaking live steam from flash steam, since they are at the same temperature. Relying on it alone can lead to replacing good traps.
  • Measure on the inlet side. Outlet readings depend on the downstream pressure.

It also says a cold trap may be stalled for a reason such as undersizing, and a replacement will not fix that. Another source says temperature checks need the expected steam and condensate pressures known to judge whether a system is misbehaving.

Sound and ultrasound

Ultrasonic testers pick up the turbulence from steam and condensate passing through. The manufacturer calls ultrasound the preferred way to detect steam leaks. Its table describes a working trap as making almost no sound to a soft swoosh, a leaking one like a compressed air leak and a cold trap as silent. Blocked traps make little noise, so temperature is needed to confirm them.

An ultrasound supplier describes comparing a trap's current output with a benchmark for good operation, ideally at several points such as the upstream pipe, downstream pipe and body, to spot early deviations. Sound interpretation takes experience. Simple mechanic's stethoscopes are portable but less accurate than electronic instruments.

Conductivity

The federal review also describes a probe in a sensing chamber that tells steam from condensate and can be wired for continuous remote monitoring. It suggests pairing it with temperature to avoid misreading a flooded trap. It says such monitoring suits large industrial traps, with the highest initial cost.

Use at least two methods

The strongest message from these sources is to combine methods. One recommends at least two. Another says the most reliable diagnosis uses instruments that measure both temperature and ultrasound. For example, ultrasound says there is a leak, temperature confirms a cold, blocked one.

Survey habits

  • The federal review says the most important decision is to have a regular assessment program. Its cost estimates use orifice size, steam pressure and operating hours.
  • DOE Steam Tip Sheet #1 suggests more frequent checks at higher pressures: weekly to monthly at 150 psig and above, monthly to quarterly from 30 to 150 psig, and yearly under 30 psig. It also says a system left unmaintained for three to five years may have roughly 15 to 30 percent of its traps failed, while a maintained one should keep leakers under about 5 percent. These are the tip sheet's figures, so use your site's policy. TLV's guidance adds that stall under a modulating control valve can look like a bad trap, and a new trap will not cure it.
  • Record each trap's location, type, service and result, so a repair has a history.

Before you swap a trap

Confirm the failure first, then check the equipment around it, including strainers, check valves and bypass valves. The trap may be doing what the system lets it do.

Steam and hot condensate burn badly. Do not stand in the path of a discharge, use appropriate gloves and eye protection, and follow your site's lockout and permit rules.

Field card: Steam Trap Testing: Sight, Sound and Temperature

One test gives a clue. Two tests give a verdict.

  • Sight shows flash versus live steam if you can see the discharge.
  • Temperature finds cold, blocked traps but cannot tell a leak from flash steam.
  • Ultrasound finds leaks; silence plus a cold inlet points to blockage.
  • Combine two methods, then check the equipment around the trap.
  • Test only with steam-safe habits: hot surfaces, hot discharge, site permits.
