Aerosol Duct Sealing From the Inside: How It Works, When It Beats Mastic, and What It Cannot Fix

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Mastic and tape work when you can reach the leak. Many leaks sit inside walls, chases and floor cavities where nobody can. Aerosol duct sealing attacks those leaks from the inside of the duct. It is a real tool with real limits, and this lesson shows both sides so a homeowner or new tech can judge a quote.

How it works

The crew blocks every register and grille, isolates the air handler, and connects a fan and a sealant injector to the duct system. The fan pressurizes the ducts. Fine polymer particles ride the air stream, and where air escapes through a leak the particles stick first to the edges of the hole and then to each other until the gap closes. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) developed the process and describes it this way. A 1996 conference paper on field results in 36 houses, written by LBNL-affiliated researchers, describes the sealant as a vinyl acetate polymer in water, with the air handler and heat exchanger isolated. A UC Davis case study adds that the aerosol deposits at the leaks and does not coat the inside of the duct.

Because the system is pressurized and metered the whole time, the equipment can read the leakage as it falls and stop at a target. Dentz and co-authors, who tested the method in apartments for the Building America program, describe exactly that: airflow and leakage measured throughout, with the run ending when the target is reached.

How leakage is measured before and after

The built-in readings are useful during the job, but a homeowner should want an independent number before and after. A standard duct leakage test uses a calibrated fan and a pressure of 25 pascals and reports cubic feet per minute (CFM25), either total or to outside. See the duct leakage testing lesson for the difference. This matters because Dentz and co-authors point out that the sealing equipment does not measure leakage to outside. Their results show why a separate test is wise. In their apartment study, leakage to outside averaged 15.8 CFM25 per 100 square feet of floor area before work and 1.5 after aerosol sealing, but about 70 percent of the total reduction came from hand sealing at the air handler and the register-to-ceiling and floor junctions, so the aerosol alone cannot take all the credit.

What sizes of leak it can and cannot close

  • Sweet spot. LBNL says leaks up to an inch wide can be sealed, but cracks up to a quarter inch wide are the main target.
  • Larger gaps. Dentz and co-authors recommend that gaps wider than 5/8 inch be sealed by hand with fiberglass mesh and mastic. We would treat anything near that size as a hand repair.
  • Different duct types seal differently. In the 36-house study about 78 percent of leakage was sealed on average: 87 percent for sheet metal, 77 percent for ductboard and 67 percent for plastic flex duct. Larger leaks sealed less efficiently.
  • Very tight systems. Dentz and co-authors found the process poorly suited to systems already below roughly 40 to 60 CFM of leakage, because the airflow gets too low to keep the sealant suspended and the equipment may shut down.

When it beats mastic and tape

Sealing by hand is still the baseline. The NREL duct brochure says to use UL Listed mastic, with mesh embedded for gaps over a quarter inch, and the ENERGY STAR duct sealing page says mastic or metal tape, never duct tape. See our mastic and foil tape lesson. Aerosol earns its place when the leaks cannot be reached:

  • Ducts buried in finished walls, soffits and chases.
  • Trunks and branches between floors.
  • Many small joint and seam leaks spread through a long run, where finding each one would take longer than the job is worth.
  • Leaks the tech never finds, since the particles go to the leak whether anyone has located it.

In the 36-house study the researchers still sealed large leaks and platform returns conventionally. In practice the two methods are partners: hand repair first for the big, reachable problems, aerosol for the many small hidden ones.

What it cannot fix

  • Disconnected runs. The 1996 paper assumed catastrophic leaks such as disconnected ducts were repaired before sealing. The NREL brochure says the same: reconnect first, then seal the smaller leaks.
  • Crushed or kinked flex. Sealant does not restore a squashed duct. See the flex duct lesson.
  • Big holes. See the size limits above.
  • The air handler cabinet and register boots. The air handler is isolated during the process, and Dentz and co-authors say leaks there and at the boot-to-finished-surface gaps need hand sealing. They also warn that flex duct without an inner liner, and unlined ductboard, may be unsuitable because the duct needs an interior air barrier.
  • Poor design. Undersized or badly routed ducts stay that way. That is a separate problem, covered in the attic ducts lesson.

Occupant prep and ventilation during application

The sources we read do not give health or exposure guidance, so we will not invent any. What they do say is that registers and the air handler are blocked so the sealant does not foul the equipment or escape into the living space. Our own practical advice, not from those sources: ask the contractor before the visit for the product's safety data sheet and written instructions, ask what to do about people and pets, and ask whether the home needs to be vented afterward. Clear access around registers, equipment and furniture will help the crew. Dentz and co-authors also report that high humidity slowed drying, so scheduling matters. The UC Davis case study raises a ventilation point for buildings with outside air: sealing raised ventilation flow by about 25 percent in the exhaust systems studied, so fans were slowed to keep the intended rate.

Worked example: what a cut in leakage to outside means

These inputs are assumptions of ours, picked to be plausible and to show the arithmetic. They are not measurements of any house.

  • Assumption: system fan flow 1,200 CFM.
  • Assumption: leakage to outside before sealing is 240 CFM25.
  • Assumption: after sealing it is 60 CFM25, a 75 percent cut, a little below the 78 percent average sealed in the 36-house study.
  • Assumption: supply air about 20°F colder than the room, 500 full-load-equivalent cooling hours, and an equipment efficiency of 11 Btu per watt-hour.

Step 1, leakage as a share of fan flow: 240 ÷ 1,200 = 20 percent before; 60 ÷ 1,200 = 5 percent after. The NREL brochure gives 5 percent or less on each side as good practice, so the after value just meets it.

Step 2, airflow no longer lost: 240 − 60 = 180 CFM25.

Step 3, heat content of that air: 1.08 × 180 CFM × 20°F = 3,888 Btu per hour. (The 1.08 factor is the standard air constant: 0.075 lb per cubic foot × 60 minutes × 0.24 Btu per lb°F.)

Step 4, rough electricity: 3,888 ÷ 11 = about 353 watts, and 353 W × 500 h = about 177 kWh per cooling season.

Caveat: CFM25 is measured at 25 pascals, and the pressure during normal running is usually lower, so the real loss is probably smaller than this simple count. Treat 177 kWh as a ceiling for this made-up house. LBNL says savings can reach several hundred dollars a year and that repairs ran $300 to $600 per home when the work was done in the 1990s, so ask for a current written price and compare it with your own utility rate.

How to judge the results

  1. Get a duct leakage test before and after, preferably leakage to outside, from a tester who is not paid by the sealing result.
  2. Compare the numbers to the target in your contract or local code (see the testing lesson above).
  3. Check that the big problems were repaired by hand first.
  4. Walk the registers: airflow should be steady, and some houses see modest gains. LBNL reports register flows up by up to 7 percent.
  5. A home energy audit adds a house-level view; see the home energy audit lesson.

Field card: Aerosol duct sealing

Block the registers, pressurize, let the leaks collect the sealant.

  • Best for hidden leaks in walls, chases and between floors.
  • Aim: cracks up to 1/4 inch; hand-seal gaps over about 5/8 inch.
  • Reconnect and repair first: disconnected runs, crushed flex, big holes.
  • Air handler cabinet and register boots need hand sealing.
  • Test before and after, with leakage to outside if possible.
  • Ask for the safety data sheet and instructions before the visit.
  • Our worked example (assumed numbers): 240 to 60 CFM25 is 180 CFM25 less, about 3,888 Btu/h of air.
