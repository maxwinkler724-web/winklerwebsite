Contactor Coil Pickup and Dropout Voltage: Why 24 V Coils Chatter

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A condenser contactor that buzzes, clicks on and off, or only pulls in on some calls is often not a bad contactor. The coil is not getting enough voltage at the moment it needs it. This lesson shows how pickup and dropout work, where the volts go between the transformer and the coil, and how to find the weak spot with a meter.

Pickup and dropout, in plain terms

A relay or contactor coil is an electromagnet. It must pull the armature across an air gap, which takes the most force, so it has a pickup (pull-in) voltage. Once the armature is closed the gap is gone and much less force holds it, so the dropout (release) voltage is lower than pickup. That gap between the two is why a contactor that is closed can ride through a small dip, while one that is open needs a clean, full-strength start.

Published relay guidance from federal and military sources gives the idea in percentages. One government equipment specification says to energize a relay at at least 110% of its minimum rated energizing voltage, and says dropout should happen at less than 90% of the maximum rated dropout voltage, so you have margin on both ends. A military engineering document on relays adds that running below nominal ratings can cause switching failures and longer switching times, and that longer switching times add contact damage. We do not give one pickup number for every 24 V coil here. Your contactor's maker data lists the real limits, and we use an assumed value only in the example below.

Why low voltage makes a coil chatter

An alternating-current coil draws much more current while the armature is open than after it closes, because the open magnetic path has lower impedance. That means the pull-in moment is also the moment the control voltage sags the most. Here is the loop that causes buzzing:

  1. The call starts and the open coil draws its high current.
  2. That current drops volts in the transformer and in the thermostat wire, so the coil sees less than the transformer's open-circuit reading.
  3. If the coil voltage sits right at pickup, the armature moves partway, or closes and falls open again.
  4. Each time it falls open, current jumps back up, the sag returns, and the cycle repeats as a buzz or rapid clatter.

Many AC contactors also carry a small shading coil that keeps the magnet from humming on the AC zero crossings. A federal nuclear-plant event report we read describes contactor chatter traced to loose shading coils, so a mechanical cause can look like a voltage problem. Check both. See the lesson on coil impedance and inrush for the open-versus-sealed current difference.

Where the volts go

Three things take voltage away between the transformer secondary and the coil:

  • Transformer sag. A transformer's windings have impedance, so the secondary voltage falls as load current rises. A university power text defines regulation as the change in secondary voltage from no load to full load. A small control transformer that is already near its VA limit with a blower relay, a gas valve and a contactor can sag noticeably when the contactor inrush lands on top of everything else. Our transformer sizing lesson covers adding up the load.
  • Thermostat wire resistance. The current goes out on one conductor and returns on another, so the wire resistance that counts is the round-trip length. Thin conductors and long runs add up. See the voltage drop lesson for the formula.
  • Bad connections. A loose wire nut, a corroded terminal or a weak safety switch contact adds resistance right where the current is highest. A bad splice in a thermostat cable drops volts exactly like extra wire does.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a field measurement. We assume the following and ask you to treat each as an assumption: 18 AWG two-conductor cable, 200 ft one way from the air handler to the condenser, so a 400 ft round trip; coil inrush current of 0.60 A while the armature is open; a transformer that reads 20.8 V at the secondary during that inrush because the system is heavily loaded; and a contactor whose pickup is 80% of 24 V. Real contactors differ, so read the maker's data.

Step 1, wire resistance. A wire table from a state university lists annealed copper at 0.000209 ohm per centimeter for 18 AWG, and notes that copper resistance changes a lot with temperature and that values are approximate. Converting with 1000 ft = 30,480 cm gives 0.000209 × 30,480 = 6.37 ohms per 1000 ft. Round trip: 6.37 × 0.400 = 2.55 ohms.

Step 2, drop at inrush. 0.60 A × 2.55 ohms = 1.53 V.

Step 3, voltage at the coil. 20.8 V − 1.53 V = 19.27 V.

Step 4, compare with pickup. 80% of 24 V = 19.2 V. The margin is about 0.07 V, so the contactor may or may not close, and any small extra drop can tip it into chatter. The wire alone took 1.53 V of the available 20.8 V.

Step 5, try a fix. Use a spare pair in the same cable in parallel with each working conductor. That halves the resistance to 1.27 ohms. The drop becomes 0.60 × 1.27 = 0.76 V, so the coil sees 20.8 − 0.76 = 20.04 V, a margin of 0.84 V. If the transformer is also corrected so it holds 22.5 V during inrush, the coil sees 22.5 − 0.76 = 21.74 V. Once the contactor is sealed the current falls (we assume 0.20 A), so the wire drop shrinks to 0.20 × 2.55 = 0.51 V, which is why the unit may run fine after a rough start.

Measuring voltage at the coil during a call

  1. Set the meter to AC volts. Read the transformer secondary with the system idle and note it.
  2. Put the probes across the coil terminals on the contactor (the two low-voltage terminals), not at the thermostat or the board.
  3. Have someone raise the call, and watch the reading in the first second, then after it settles. A min/max or peak-hold setting helps catch the dip.
  4. Compare the first-second reading with the maker's pickup limit, and compare the settled value with the idle value.
  5. To find where the drop is, measure the same call across the transformer secondary, then across each wire run, then across each connection and switch contact. A reading across a connection that should be near zero points straight at it.

Always read low voltage with power on only after you have checked that it is safe to do so, keep line-voltage terminals away from your probes, and follow the equipment instructions. Our meter testing lesson covers the rest of the contactor checks.

Buzzing, pitting and what it does to contacts

When the armature chatters, the main contacts touch and part again under load. Each make or break can draw a small arc, which melts a tiny spot of contact material. The military relay document we read notes that contact life is further degraded by contamination or pitting, and that high inrush currents hurt contacts. Over many cycles the contacts get pitted, carbon builds up, the resistance across them rises, and a compressor or fan motor that sees a weak or noisy connection can run hot or fail. A pitted contactor should be replaced, and the low voltage cause fixed first, or the new one will chatter too.

Fixes, in order of effort

  • Clean and tighten every low-voltage connection, and replace any bad splice.
  • Use a spare pair in parallel on long runs, or pull heavier cable (a fatter conductor has lower resistance per foot).
  • Check the transformer load. If the secondary sags below its nameplate rating during inrush, reduce load or use a correctly sized transformer.
  • Make sure the transformer primary tap matches the supply voltage.
  • Replace a contactor that is pitted, a coil that is open or burned, and any loose shading coil.
  • Use the correct coil voltage. A coil rated for a different voltage will not behave the same way.

Field card: Coil pickup and dropout voltage

Measure at the coil, during the call, in the first second.

  • Pickup voltage is higher than dropout voltage; an open coil needs the most.
  • Open-coil inrush is the moment of the biggest sag.
  • Round trip wire length counts, not one way.
  • Check the transformer, the wire and every connection in turn.
  • Buzzing contacts pit and heat; fix the voltage, then the contactor.
