MRI Suite HVAC: Quench Vents, Chillers, RF Penetrations and Zone IV Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An MRI suite looks like a quiet room with a big machine in it. For an HVAC tech it is a magnetic hazard, a helium relief path, a shielded box with rules about every duct and pipe that crosses its wall, and a dedicated water-cooling loop that the scanner cannot run without. This lesson covers what to know before you walk in with a toolbag. It is general education, not a substitute for the site's MR safety staff.

Check the site rules first

Check with the MR safety staff. Every suite has its own screening rules and a named person who controls access. Nothing on this page lets you enter a controlled area. Get a briefing and an escort first, and follow the maker's documents for the actual scanner.

The magnet is always on

The American College of Radiology (ACR) Manual on MR Safety says to assume the static magnetic field is always on, and it calls for a red illuminated sign at the scanner room entrance reading that the magnet is always on. The ASRT white paper on MR safety says the field stays on continuously, even when the machine is not scanning. The FDA's MRI risk page says the strong static field attracts magnetic objects and that they can become projectiles, with injuries from projectile events listed among reported adverse events. A ladder, gauge manifold, steel toolbox or cordless drill can be pulled toward the bore. Heavy items like oxygen tanks, stretchers and wheelchairs are called out as the most harmful.

The four zones

The ACR manual splits an MR site into four zones. Zone I is open to the public. Zone II is the interface between the public and the controlled areas, where screening often starts. Zone III is the controlled access area with direct doorway access to the magnet room. Zone IV is the magnet room itself. The manual advises Zone IV entry doors stay closed and preferably locked when nobody is monitoring them, and it says only trained MR personnel at the right level may admit non-MR people into Zones III and IV. Most HVAC work on the suite is done in Zone I and II spaces, the equipment room, and on the roof. Anything in Zone III or IV needs an escort and a screening.

Helium, quench and the vent pipe

A superconducting magnet is cooled with liquid helium. A peer-reviewed safety review on the NIH PMC site says these machines typically hold 1,500 to 2,000 L of cold liquid helium. When the magnet loses superconductivity, which is called a quench, the helium boils off and leaves through a quench pipe. The same review warns that if the pipe fails, helium can vent into the room, and that the room should have an oxygen monitor because helium can push oxygen out. It also says the depletion can raise room pressure, which can make the door hard to open. The oxygen-depletion hazard is the same kind of problem covered in Confined Spaces in HVAC Work, just with helium instead of a gas leak.

The VA's 2008 MRI Design Guide (a reposted copy; the VA replaced it in 2020 with the Imaging Services design guide) asks for a dedicated cryogen vent pipe run as directly as possible to the outdoors, with the discharge 25 feet from any air intake or operable window and protected from wind-driven rain. It also asks for a dedicated emergency exhaust for the scan room at a minimum of 12 air changes per hour on emergency power, with return air shut off when that fan runs. It says sites with a superconducting magnet should inspect the vent, the active exhaust and the pressure equalization at least annually. A 1986 report from the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM Report 20) lists cryogen venting and oxygen monitoring in the magnet room among the safety items, and a Siemens planning document says the quench pipe is a thermally insulated non-magnetic metal tube to outside the building with a fenced exclusion zone below the vent. For you the rules are simple. Do not cover, cap, reroute or hang anything on a quench pipe, and do not put an air intake near its discharge. Do not touch emergency exhaust controls or dampers without the MR staff.

The magnet chiller and why uptime matters

The scanner has its own cooling loop. Siemens basic planning information for a 1.5 T model lists cooling water supplied at 19 to 22 °C and 120 L/min for the gradient system, with 35 to 40 percent ethylene glycol antifreeze, and it lists a separate minimum flow of 15 to 20 L/min for the helium compressor. The VA guide says the MRI magnet chiller is provided by the MRI manufacturer and installed per its requirements. A service company's page on cold head compressor shutdowns lists chiller malfunction as a common cause and says a cooling failure can cause helium loss and possibly a quench. That is why this loop is treated as critical: a nuisance trip on a chiller can cost the owner a scanner day, or a lot more. Do not shut it off, valve it off or change its setpoints without the maker's service contact. For glycol handling see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops, and for flow and temperature difference see Chilled Water Pumps and Delta T.

RF-shielded room penetrations

The magnet room is a metal-lined box that keeps radio signals out. Every duct and pipe that goes through it is a hole in that box. The VA guide requires waveguides at ductwork and piping penetrations of the RF shield, furnished and installed by the shielding vendor. It asks that piping, ductwork, hangers and dampers inside the shield be non-ferrous, and that domestic water and drain lines pass through waveguides with dielectric breaks in the piping. AAPM Report 20 describes a honeycomb waveguide panel in a ventilation pipe, notes that it collects dust because all the room air passes through it, and says non-ferrous pipe is advised within 25 feet of the magnet center. Treat a waveguide like a fragile part of the shield. Do not patch it, drill it or swap the duct. AAPM also advises isolating nonmagnetic vent pipes electrically at penetration points.

Temperature, humidity and pressure

The scanner maker sets the numbers. The VA guide lists 70 to 75 °F and 40 to 60 percent relative humidity for the scan room and says to coordinate with the manufacturer. The 1.5 T Siemens document lists 18 to 22 °C and 40 to 60 percent for the magnet room, and a minimum of 6 air changes per hour (10 recommended). Those two sets do not match exactly, which is the point: use the document for the machine on site. The VA guide also says positive pressure applies unless noted, which is different from isolation rooms; see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare.

CT and X-ray rooms

CT rooms have no magnet, but they have heat and limits. A maker's CT quick reference (Philips) says scan room temperature should stay within 18 to 24 °C and not change by more than 5 °C per hour, with 40 to 70 percent relative humidity, non-condensing, and warns against scanning outside range. A PET/CT planning document from another maker states 4.4 kW of heat into the scan room for one model (its own Btu figure for that kW does not match, so we use only the kW). Ask for the sheet for the machine in the room.

Worked example: scanner room cooling and chiller flow

These are our own planning-style numbers, for study only. They are not a design.

  • Assumptions: room area 590 sq ft (the VA's 590 NSF for a scanning room); equipment heat to room air 3 kW (the Siemens 1.5 T example lists up to 3 kW for the exam room and the 2008 VA guide uses 3,000 W); lighting 2.0 W per sq ft (VA guide); 2 people at 250 Btu/h sensible each (our assumption); envelope, outdoor air and latent load ignored (our assumption, so a real load is higher).
  • Equipment: 3 kW x 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 10,236 Btu/h.
  • Lighting: 2.0 x 590 = 1,180 W; 1,180 x 3.412 = 4,026 Btu/h.
  • People: 2 x 250 = 500 Btu/h.
  • Total: 10,236 + 4,026 + 500 = 14,762 Btu/h; 14,762 / 12,000 = 1.23 tons.
  • Chiller loop (assumptions): heat to water 45 kW (the Siemens listing for one gradient option, taken as the whole loop); delta T 10 °F; plain water factor 500. 45 x 3,412 = 153,540 Btu/h. gpm = 153,540 / (500 x 10) = 30.7 gpm.
  • Cross-check: the maker's 120 L/min is 31.7 gpm (120 / 3.785). Our flow is close, which tells us the delta T is about 9.7 °F at that flow.
  • Caution: the maker's loop uses glycol, which carries less heat per gallon than water, so the 500 factor overstates it and real flow would be a bit higher. Use the maker's value.

The point: the room air load is small, about a ton, while the water loop carries around ten times the heat. Notice what is easy to forget: the chiller, not the room unit, makes the scanner go.

Field card: MRI Suite HVAC

Magnet always on. Vent stays clear. Chiller stays on.

  • Assume the field is on all the time; no steel tools, ladders or carts in the magnet room.
  • Zone IV is the magnet room; Zone III and IV entry needs MR staff approval.
  • Quench pipe goes to the outdoors; never block, cap or reroute it.
  • Keep air intakes away from the vent discharge (the VA 2008 guide says 25 feet).
  • Oxygen monitor in the scan room; if it alarms, leave and call the MR staff.
  • Do not shut off the magnet chiller or change setpoints without the maker's service contact.
  • Waveguides stay intact; non-ferrous ducts and pipes inside the shield; dielectric breaks in piping.
  • Use the maker's temperature and humidity sheet for the machine on site.
