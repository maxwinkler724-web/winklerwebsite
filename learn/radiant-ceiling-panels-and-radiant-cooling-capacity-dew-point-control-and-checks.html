Radiant Ceiling Panels: Capacity, Dew Point Control and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A radiant ceiling panel is a water-filled surface that trades heat with the room by radiation. Run warm water through it in winter and it heats. Run cool water through it in summer and it cools. The cooling side comes with one hard rule: the panel must stay warmer than the room dew point, or it drips.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. This page is general education built from maker literature and engineering articles read in 2026. Panels, controls and limits differ by product. Follow the installation manual and the engineer's design for the actual building, and have only qualified people work on the system.

How a panel moves heat

A panel does not blow air. Its surface takes heat from people, walls and furniture by radiation, and from room air by a smaller amount of natural convection. A trade article in ACHR News gives the heating rule of thumb for ceiling panels: about 0.71 Btu/h per sq ft for each degree F that the average water temperature is above room temperature. At 110 F water in a 70 F room, that is about 28 Btu/h per sq ft. For cooling the same article uses a curve rather than a straight line: q = 1.48 × (TR − TC)1.1, where TR is the room operative temperature and TC is the average ceiling surface temperature. It lists about 8.7 Btu/h per sq ft with a 5 F difference and about 18.6 with a 10 F difference. Capacity rises faster than the temperature difference, but a colder surface is exactly what puts you closer to the dew point.

How much can a real panel carry? A 2006 scoping study from the UC Berkeley Center for the Built Environment (CBE) puts ceiling panels at about 30 Btu/h per sq ft of active surface for cooling, and radiant slabs at about 24 Btu/h per sq ft, both leaving out ventilation. It adds that installed panels often cover only about half the ceiling. Price Industries' brochure agrees on the order of magnitude: radiant panels cool up to 30 Btu/sq ft, but the page does not state the temperature difference behind that figure. Zehnder's ZIP submittal rates its panels per linear foot at a stated 18 F surface-to-room difference, about 37 Btu/h per ft with insulation, and gives no per-square-foot number. Always read the difference a rating assumes before you compare two makers.

Why the water must stay above dew point

Radiant panels remove sensible heat only. They do not dry the air. Water vapor turns to liquid on any surface colder than the dew point of the air touching it, so a panel colder than the room dew point collects moisture and can drip onto people and ceiling tile. The ACHR News article gives the standard example: 75 F air at 50 percent relative humidity has a dew point of about 55 F. It advises running panels a few degrees above the current dew point, with supply water about 3 to 4 F above it. The Plumbing & Mechanical article adds that a margin of about 3 F suits current sensor accuracy. If dew point is new to you, read Psychrometrics for Technicians first.

The CBE study notes that lab tests held a chilled surface 14 F below dew point for 8.5 hours without dripping, but warns that real panel geometry can differ. We would not plan around that. Treat dew point as a line you stay above.

Dew point sensors and supply temperature reset

The dew point is not fixed. It moves with outdoor weather, door openings, and people. So the controller measures it and sets the chilled water supply from it. The ACHR News article describes one three-way motorized mixing valve that follows the room dew point in cooling and follows outdoor reset in heating. See Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics and Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation for the heating side. Both articles also warn that an entry vestibule or other damp zone can have a higher dew point than the main space, so it may need its own sensor and mixing control.

The air side: pair panels with a DOAS

Someone has to remove the moisture, and it is not the panel. Price says radiant systems are typically paired with a dedicated outdoor air system (DOAS), and that supply air volume drops by about 60 to 80 percent because the panels carry the sensible load. An HPAC article on hybrid radiant-DOAS systems says DOAS supply air must be drier than the room, with a dew point typically around 48 to 50 F. A humidistat in a critical space tells the controller to lower the DOAS discharge dew point when humidity climbs, then to relax it again. That is how the system "decouples" sensible and latent loads. Read Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems for the unit itself, and Chilled Beams for a close cousin with the same rule.

Condensation alarms and valve shutoff

Sensors are a second line of defense. Price says condensate sensors on its panels shut down or modulate cooling to prevent condensation on the element surface, and Price's condensation article suggests a sensor on exposed chilled water piping near the product. Piping and valves can sweat before the panel does. The CBE study describes an older, simple design: a swelling-element water sensor at the first panel inlet, where condensation shows up first, trips a switch that closes a spring-return valve and stops chilled water flow. Note what these sources do not give: a numeric alarm setpoint. Use the maker's.

Slabs, too

The same dew point rule applies to radiant slabs, which respond more slowly. The HPAC article quotes radiant cooling at about 14 Btu/h per sq ft, up to 25 with high direct sun, and says slab cooling surfaces should stay at or above 66 F for comfort under ASHRAE 55. For the heating side see Radiant Floor Heating Basics.

Worked example: dew point, supply setpoint and panel capacity

The room is held at 75 F and 50 percent relative humidity. This is a learning example, not a design.

Step 1, dew point. ACHR News states that 75 F air at 50 percent RH has a dew point of about 55 F. Our own Magnus-formula check in the Chilled Beams lesson gave 55.1 F. We use 55 F.

Step 2, chilled water supply. Our own choice: 2 F above dew point. 55 + 2 = 57 F. (The sources above recommend 3 to 4 F, so 2 F is tighter than they advise. We used it because the lesson asked for it, and a margin this small leaves little room for sensor error.)

Step 3, panel surface. ACHR News says average circuit water runs about 4 F below the average ceiling surface. Our own simplification: treat 57 F as the average water temperature, so the surface is 57 + 4 = 61 F. That is 6 F above the 55 F dew point, so the surface stays dry.

Step 4, capacity per sq ft. Using the ACHR News curve, with the room at 75 F: TR − TC = 75 − 61 = 14 F. 141.1 = 18.23. q = 1.48 × 18.23 = 27.0 Btu/h per sq ft. Our own check: the same curve gives 8.7 at 5 F and 18.6 at 10 F, matching the article, and 27 is under the 30 Btu/h per sq ft ceiling that CBE and Price give for panels.

Step 5, total. Our own arithmetic: 27.0 × 200 sq ft = 5,396, about 5,400 Btu/h of sensible cooling, or about 0.45 ton (5,400 / 12,000). A maker rating could differ, so a real job uses the maker's table at the actual water temperature. Latent load is still the DOAS's job.

Service checks

  • Panel water temperature. Compare supply and return at the panel or manifold against the controller's setpoint and against the room dew point now, not the design dew point.
  • Dew point sensor accuracy. The 3 F margin the articles describe is tied to sensor accuracy, so a drifting sensor eats the margin. Check it against a trusted reference meter and follow the maker's calibration steps. Our own suggestion; no source we read gives a test method.
  • Air in panels. Zehnder's manual says air must be removed at start-up and during operation, that some systems need repeated venting, and that noise or odd valve behavior after balancing means you should recheck for unvented air. See Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.
  • Valve actuators. Confirm each valve moves fully and that the shutoff closes when the condensation input trips. The sources do not give a test procedure, so use the maker's.
  • Condensation sensors and pipe sweating. Look at exposed piping and valves for moisture, especially at start-up and after humid weather.

Field card: Radiant Ceiling Panels

Sensible only: keep the water above the room dew point.

  • Heating rule: about 0.71 Btu/h per sq ft per F of water above room (ACHR News).
  • Cooling curve: q = 1.48 x (room - surface)^1.1 Btu/h per sq ft.
  • Panels: about 30 Btu/h per sq ft cooling ceiling (CBE, Price). Slabs: about 24 (CBE).
  • 75 F at 50 percent RH: dew point about 55 F. Supply 3 to 4 F above it.
  • DOAS dew point about 48 to 50 F handles moisture.
  • Condensation sensor closes valve; watch exposed piping.
  • Vent air at start-up and during operation.
  • Use the maker's rating and its stated temperature difference.
