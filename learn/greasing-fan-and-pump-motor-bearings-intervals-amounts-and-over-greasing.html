Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings: Intervals, Amounts and Over-Greasing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

"How often should I grease a motor?" has no single answer, and "until it looks full" is the wrong one. Makers publish interval tables and grease amounts, and several warn that too much grease hurts. This lesson shows how to read them and do the arithmetic.

Check the nameplate and the maker first

Check the maker's data. Numbers below come from maker manuals read in 2026. Your motor's nameplate or own manual overrides them; WEG's manual, for one, says the interval is on the nameplate.

First question: does this bearing take grease at all?

Many small motors have sealed or shielded bearings that are packed at the factory and never regreased. An EC&M magazine article says sealed bearings need no relubrication, and a Mobil technical guide says bearings sealed on both sides are typically lubricated for life. LEESON says motors of 10 hp and smaller are usually factory-lubricated for long service, so read the nameplate or terminal box label first. Daikin's air handler manual says its prelubricated fan and motor ball bearings should not be greased before startup, and its EC fan arrays have permanently sealed bearings.

Why over-greasing does damage

  • Heat. Timken's guide says an overfilled bearing can churn the grease and run hot, and grease oxidizes faster as it warms. Nidec (US Motors) says over-greasing can cause excess bearing temperature, early lubricant breakdown and failure.
  • Grease in the windings. A Mobil guide says excess grease can migrate into the windings or rupture seals, and the EC&M article says it can contaminate winding insulation.
  • Speed. Baldor says injecting grease too quickly can also cause early bearing failure.

Reading a maker's interval table

Baldor's manual lists hours between relubrication by frame size and speed. For the row "over 210 to 280" frames, which includes a NEMA 254T, it gives these figures:

SpeedHours between greasing
3,600 rpm3,600
1,800 rpm9,500
1,200 rpm15,000
900 rpm18,000

These are for ball bearings. Divide by 2 for roller bearings (Baldor) or for a horizontal motor run vertically (WEG). Baldor-Reliance's manual defines service classes: Standard is 8 hours a day in clean air at up to 40 degrees C, multiplier 1.0; Severe is listed at 16 hours a day with moderate dirt, multiplier 0.5. Toshiba defines severe duty as 24 hours a day or dirty conditions.

The grease amount formula

Baldor's manual gives a rule for the grease to add: weight in grams = 0.005 times the bearing outside diameter (D, in mm) times the bearing width (B, in mm). A Mobil guide prints the same formula, and a trade article gives an imperial version: fluid ounces = 0.11 times OD in inches times width in inches. Use the motor's own table when it has one.

Worked example: 15 hp, 1,800 rpm, frame 254T

Invented motor: 15 hp, 4-pole (1,800 rpm), NEMA frame 254T, ball bearings, grease fitting and relief plug. For this example we assume its drive-end bearing is a 6309; check the nameplate for the real bearing. A 6309 measures 45 mm bore, 100 mm outside diameter and 25 mm wide.

  1. Standard-duty interval. Frame 254T falls in Baldor's "over 210 to 280" row. At 1,800 rpm the table says 9,500 hours.
  2. 24/7 interval. Our own reading: running around the clock is beyond the 8 hours a day that defines Standard, so we use the Severe multiplier of 0.5: 9,500 x 0.5 = 4,750 hours. (The manual implies this multiplication but does not print it.)
  3. Turn hours into calendar time. Running 24 hours a day is 24 x 365 = 8,760 hours a year. Then 9,500 / 8,760 = about 1.08 years, or 13 months, for standard. And 4,750 / 8,760 = 0.54 years, about 6.5 months, for 24/7 duty.
  4. Grease amount. G = 0.005 x 100 x 25 = 12.5 grams per bearing. Baldor's own bearing-size table lists 6309 at 12.5 g, so the formula and the table agree. (A separate Baldor frame-class table shows 17.4 g for the whole group, which covers larger bearings.) In ounces: 12.5 / 28.35 = about 0.44 oz, which matches the Mobil guide's 0.114 x 3.94 x 0.98 = 0.44 oz.
  5. Convert to gun strokes. Toshiba's procedure says a typical low-pressure hand gun delivers about 1.25 grams per pump. So 12.5 / 1.25 = 10 strokes.
  6. Check your own gun. A Noria motor lubrication guide reports an audit of 33 hand guns that ranged from 0.56 to 3.10 grams per pump. On that range the same 12.5 g is 12.5 / 3.10 = 4 strokes to 12.5 / 0.56 = 22 strokes. Weigh a few strokes from your gun on a small scale before trusting a stroke count.

The result: about every 6.5 months of 24/7 running, add roughly 12.5 grams to each bearing of this motor, slowly, then follow the relief-plug steps below. The non-drive end has its own, usually smaller, bearing, so look up its size too.

The relief-plug procedure

Makers differ on details, so use the one for your motor. The common steps:

  1. Clean the fitting, the gun nozzle and the area around the relief plug, and confirm the gun holds the right grease.
  2. Remove the relief plug and clear any hardened grease from the hole. Nidec (US Motors) says to clear a blocked drain with a probe without damaging the bearing.
  3. Add the measured amount slowly. Baldor says to take about a minute.
  4. Run the motor with the plug out so extra grease can leave. Times vary: 10 to 15 minutes (Hyosung), 15 to 20 (Baldor manuals), 15 to 30 (Nidec), 30 (EC&M). Then reinstall the plug and wipe off the excess.

Whether to grease while stopped or running depends on the maker. Baldor allows either, LEESON says only at standstill, and Hyosung says stop the motor. Follow your site lockout procedure.

Compatibility: polyurea and lithium complex

Grease is oil held in a thickener, and different thickeners can fight each other. A Hyosung motor manual says its standard grease is polyurea-thickened and not to mix it with a different thickener such as lithium. LEESON suggests polyurea for Class B, F or H motors. Nidec (US Motors) says bases such as lithium and polyurea may not be compatible when mixed, and Timken says a bad mix can soften and leak out, or turn lumpy and hard. No manual we read rules on one specific pair, so check the tag.

On an air handler the drive can have two greases. A Dodge manual for large-bore pillow blocks says they ship with a lithium or lithium-complex grease, while the motor may carry polyurea. Do not share one gun between them without reading both labels.

Pillow blocks on a fan shaft

Pillow blocks follow the same logic, with the interval set by hours of use. A Twin City Fan manual suggests 12 to 18 months for light duty in clean air, 6 to 12 months at 8 to 16 hours a day, 3 to 6 months at 12 to 24 hours a day or with moisture, and 1 to 3 months in dirty, hot or vibrating spots. It says to use only 1 or 2 hand-gun shots in most cases and not to over-grease. A Dodge manual shows speed matters too: at 501 to 750 rpm it suggests 3 weeks at 24 hours a day and 5 weeks at 16. Daikin says to purge old grease from belt-driven fan bearings while rotating the shaft. For belts and sheaves, see Belt Drives and Sheave Basics.

Habit to carry

Log date, amount and bearing temperature (see Preventive Maintenance Checklist Habits). A Mobil guide says to check ventilation, load and alignment before greasing a hot bearing; for pumps, see Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits.

Field card: Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings

Right grease, right amount, slowly, and let the old grease out.

  • Sealed or shielded bearings are not regreased; check before adding.
  • Nameplate and maker's table beat any general rule.
  • Baldor 254T-class frame at 1,800 rpm: 9,500 hours standard; x0.5 for severe, so 4,750 hours.
  • Quantity: grams = 0.005 x OD (mm) x width (mm). A 6309 is 12.5 g.
  • Gun output varies; weigh your gun (about 1.25 g per pump is one maker's typical figure).
  • Open the relief plug, add slowly, run 15 to 30 minutes, then close it.
  • Do not mix thickeners without checking; motors and pillow blocks may use different greases.
