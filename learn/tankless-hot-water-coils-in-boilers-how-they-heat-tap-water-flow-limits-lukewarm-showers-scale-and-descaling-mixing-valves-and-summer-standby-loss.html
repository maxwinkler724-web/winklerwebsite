Tankless Hot-Water Coils in Boilers: Flow Limits, Scale and Summer Standby Loss

Last updated: 2026-10-31

Some older boilers make tap hot water with no tank at all: a coil of copper tube sits inside the boiler, and water heats as it flows through. It works, but it has built-in limits. This lesson explains the limits so you can tell a service problem from a design problem. A boiler with a separate storage tank is a different setup, covered in our lesson on indirect water heaters on a boiler and in our lesson on DHW priority.

How the coil heats tap water

A tankless coil (also called an internal or "domestic" coil) is a bundle of tubing mounted in the boiler water jacket. Boiler water surrounds the outside of the tube. Cold city water enters one end, picks up heat through the tube wall as it travels, and leaves the other end as hot tap water. Nothing is stored. When a faucet opens, flow starts, and heat moves in only as fast as the coil, the flow and the boiler water temperature allow. The Vermont state energy office describes the same arrangement: water is heated as it passes through a coil or heat exchanger inside the boiler, and it calls this one of the least efficient ways to heat domestic hot water.

Why the boiler stays hot all year

The coil can only make hot water if the water around it is hot. So the boiler must hold a minimum temperature even when no house heat is needed. That is the job of the low-limit setting on the boiler aquastat: when boiler water falls below the low setting, the burner fires to bring it back, with or without a call for heat. The high-limit setting stops the burner at the top of the range. Our lesson on boiler aquastats and cutoffs covers how these controls work. The practical effect is that the boiler cycles in July just to keep the coil ready.

That is the standby loss. Heat leaks from the hot jacket, flue and piping into the room all day and night, and the burner replaces it. The U.S. Department of Energy defines standby loss as heat escaping from a water heater into the surrounding area and notes that holding water hotter wastes more. A boiler is much bigger than a water heater, so a year-round idle burn is expensive. Raising the low setting makes the coil stronger but increases that waste; lowering it saves fuel but weakens hot water. Owners often hear about warm-weather shutdown controls; those are a poor match here because shutting the boiler down also shuts off the coil. See warm-weather shutdown for how that works with indirect tanks.

Flow limits and lukewarm showers

A coil has a heat-transfer limit measured in Btu/h, often written as a gallons-per-minute rating at a stated temperature rise. Beyond that flow, outlet temperature falls. Typical complaints follow directly:

  • Two things at once. A shower plus a running dishwasher or washing machine asks for more gpm than the coil can heat, so everything goes lukewarm.
  • Long showers fade. Boiler water near the coil cools as it gives up heat faster than the burner can replace it.
  • Winter versus summer. Colder inlet water means a bigger temperature rise, so the same coil delivers fewer gpm in January.
  • Low boiler setpoint. If the aquastat low limit is set low, the coil starts with less heat available.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These are assumptions, not data from any maker. Assume a shower and sink draw 2.5 gpm. Assume inlet water is 50 F and we want 115 F at the outlet. Assume the coil is rated 75,000 Btu/h at that rise. The common rule of thumb is Btu/h = 500 x gpm x temperature rise, where 500 comes from about 8.33 pounds per gallon times 60 minutes per hour times 1 Btu per pound per degree F.

  1. Temperature rise = 115 - 50 = 65 F.
  2. Heat needed = 500 x 2.5 x 65 = 81,250 Btu/h.
  3. Assumed coil rating = 75,000 Btu/h, so the coil is short by 81,250 - 75,000 = 6,250 Btu/h.
  4. Maximum flow at this rise = 75,000 / (500 x 65) = 75,000 / 32,500 = about 2.3 gpm.
  5. At the full 2.5 gpm the coil can only raise the water 75,000 / (500 x 2.5) = 60 F, so the outlet is about 110 F, noticeably cooler.

Now a cold-winter case with the same coil: inlet 40 F, target 115 F, rise 75 F. Maximum flow = 75,000 / (500 x 75) = 2.0 gpm. The same shower plus a second tap tips it into lukewarm. If a faucet or showerhead also passes more than the coil can handle, adding a flow restrictor is a legitimate fix, but confirm the actual rating on the coil nameplate or maker data for your unit.

Scale and descaling

Hard water leaves mineral scale inside the coil tubes. Scale insulates the tube, so less heat reaches the water, and it narrows the passage, so flow drops. Symptoms are a slowly worsening shower, lower pressure at hot taps only, and a coil that "used to keep up." The U.S. Department of Energy notes that lower water temperature settings slow mineral buildup, which is another reason to keep the outlet reasonable. Descaling means isolating the coil, circulating a descaling solution through it with a small pump, then flushing until the water runs clear. Follow the solution maker's directions, use eye and skin protection, and neutralize and dispose of the used solution as local rules require. A coil that is badly plugged may need replacement. Our lesson on tankless gas water heaters covers the same scale and flushing ideas on a different appliance.

Anti-scald mixing valve on the coil outlet

To get decent flow, the boiler water is often held well above what is safe at a tap. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says water at 140 F can cause a third-degree burn in about 6 seconds and water at 150 F in about 2 seconds. A thermostatic mixing valve on the coil outlet blends hot water with cold and delivers a set, lower temperature. This also stretches the coil: hotter coil output mixed down gives more usable gallons. Check it by measuring the mixed temperature at a tap. A mixing valve that fails open or sticks can pass full coil temperature, so test and replace it as the maker directs. A Department of Energy combination-systems report says a scald-prevention mixing valve is a necessary safety component in any hot water system controlled above 120 F. Learn more in our lesson on scald risk and mixing valves.

Leaking coil gasket

The coil mounts on a flange with a gasket. When that gasket weeps, water drips from the boiler jacket flange area, often showing as rust streaks or a wet floor below. Before blaming the gasket, find where the water comes from. Replacing the gasket means shutting off and draining the boiler, pulling the coil, fitting a new gasket and torquing the bolts evenly. A hot pressurized boiler must never be opened. If the coil tubes themselves are pitted, replace the coil.

Coil versus indirect tank

PointTankless coilIndirect tank
Hot water storageNone, heats on demandStored, buffers peaks
Boiler must stay hotYes, all yearNo, boiler fires on a tank call
Long or simultaneous drawsFade to lukewarmHold up until the tank is used
Summer fuel useHigh standbyLower
Mixing valveRecommendedRecommended
Scale in the heat exchangerYes, within the coilYes, in the tank coil, but the boiler water side is closed

Many owners move from a coil to an indirect tank during a boiler replacement for the reasons above. Make that choice based on the household draw pattern and the cost of a new boiler.

Field card: Tankless Hot-Water Coils in Boilers: Flow Limits, Scale and Summer Standby Loss

Coil hot water is only as good as the boiler water around it.

  • Lukewarm: check the aquastat low setting, simultaneous draws and inlet temperature first.
  • Fading flow: suspect scale; descale and flush the coil.
  • Check mixed temperature at a tap, not just the dial.
  • Wet flange below the coil: suspect the gasket, then confirm the source.
  • Heat needed (Btu/h) = 500 x gpm x temperature rise.
