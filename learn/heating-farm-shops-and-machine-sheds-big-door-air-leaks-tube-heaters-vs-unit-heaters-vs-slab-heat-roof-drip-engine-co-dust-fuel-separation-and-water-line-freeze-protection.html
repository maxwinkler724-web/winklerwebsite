Heating Farm Shops and Machine Sheds

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A farm shop is a big, tall box with a door the size of a barn wall, a metal roof, a slab on the ground, and engines that sometimes run inside it. Heating it well is less about picking a heater and more about knowing where the heat goes, what the heater leaves in the air, and what else is stored under the same roof.

Start with where the heat goes

A shop loses heat two ways. Heat conducts through the walls, roof, doors and slab edge, and heat leaves with the air that leaks out while cold air leaks in. In a house the leaks are small. In a machine shed, overhead doors with worn seals, a man door that gets propped open, and wall-to-slab gaps can matter as much as the insulation. An extension guide on insulating for lower heating cost makes the same point for houses: work on air leaks first, then add insulation. The same order makes sense in a shop. Fix door seals and gaps before you size up a heater.

Big doors are the hard part. A door that opens to let a tractor in dumps a large share of the building's warm air in minutes. Plan for that with a heater that recovers quickly, or accept that floor and object temperature, not air temperature, is what keeps the shop usable. That leads to the heater choice.

Three ways to heat the space

Gas-fired infrared tube heaters hang overhead and warm the floor, machines and people directly, much as the sun does. Because they heat surfaces more than air, a door opening hurts comfort less. Clearances, venting and upkeep are covered in Infrared Tube Heaters for Warehouses and Garages, so we do not repeat them here.

Gas unit heaters are a burner, heat exchanger and fan that blow warm air into the room. They are simple and recover air temperature fast, but the warm air rises to the roof peak, and a door opening lets a lot of it out. See Garage and Workshop Heaters for details on these.

Radiant floor loops in the shop slab are the most comfortable option for people working at floor level, and they leave stored heat in the concrete. They respond slowly, so they suit a shop that is used every day better than a shed that is heated for a few hours. The basics are in Radiant Floor Heating Basics. A hangar example of the same ideas is in Aircraft Hangar Heating and Ventilation. This comparison is our own general summary, not a measured ranking.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a design for your building. We assume a shed 40 ft by 60 ft with a 14 ft ceiling height, so the volume is 40 x 60 x 14 = 33,600 cubic feet. We assume the insulated shell (walls, roof, doors and slab edge together) has a combined UA of 900 Btu/h per degree F. That UA is an assumption, not a measurement. We assume the shop is held at 55 F inside on a 5 F day, so the temperature difference is 50 F. We assume one air change per hour from leaks and door use, and we use 0.018 Btu per cubic foot per degree F for the heat needed to warm air.

  1. Shell loss: 900 x 50 = 45,000 Btu/h.
  2. Air leakage: 33,600 cubic feet per hour x 0.018 x 50 = 30,240 Btu/h.
  3. Total load: 45,000 + 30,240 = 75,240 Btu/h.
  4. Heater input at an assumed 80 percent efficiency: 75,240 / 0.80 = 94,050 Btu/h, so a heater near 94,000 Btu/h input would just meet the load.

Now test the leak assumption. At two air changes per hour the leakage term doubles to 60,480 Btu/h, the load becomes 105,480 Btu/h, and the input becomes 131,850 Btu/h. Air leakage moved the answer by about 40 percent, which is why sealing the doors is worth checking first. A real design also considers warm-up from a cold start and the heater's rated output, which we left out.

Condensation and dripping on a metal roof

Heating a shed with an uninsulated metal roof raises the moisture in the air, and the roof stays cold. When the metal is colder than the dew point of the room air, water forms on it. An extension article on metal-walled buildings states the cause as the metal being below the dew-point temperature of the interior air. It also says ventilation is the primary way to reduce condensation. That article covers walls, not roofs, but the physics is the same on a roof, and the drip comes from the roof side because warm moist air rises there. Unvented fuel-burning heaters add water as well, because burning gas makes water vapor.

The same article says insulating the inside of the metal can prevent surface condensation, but that it does not stop condensation inside the assembly. That takes a properly used vapor barrier or closed-cell insulation, and the barrier needs protection from punctures. Extension guidance for houses puts a vapor retarder on the warm side of insulation in northern climates. At a general level: insulate, seal air paths, keep the vapor control on the warm side, and ventilate. Details depend on climate, so confirm with local guidance and the insulation maker's instructions.

Venting and carbon monoxide

Fuel-fired heaters need the vent system and combustion air their installation manual and the adopted fuel gas code require. We do not give clearances here. What matters for the shop is that engines are another source. The federal workplace safety agency's carbon monoxide fact sheet names internal combustion engines as one of the most common workplace sources, and lists indoor fuel-burning appliances too. It advises ventilation that removes carbon monoxide from work areas, keeping gasoline-powered engines and tools out of poorly ventilated spaces, and monitors with audible alarms. Its generator guidance says never to run a generator in an enclosed space such as a garage, and that open windows and doors may not prevent carbon monoxide from building up. Early symptoms include headache, dizziness, nausea and fatigue, and the agency lists a permissible exposure limit of 50 ppm averaged over eight hours. A tractor warming up inside a closed shop breaks these rules even if the heater is perfect. Pull engines outside, or use exhaust capture, and add alarms. For home-side background see Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

Dust, hay and fuel

A heater is an ignition source, and a shop collects fuel for a fire. The federal agency's combustible dust guidance says dust that can burn becomes an explosion hazard when five things come together: fuel, ignition, oxygen, dispersion of the dust in the air, and confinement. It lists wood dust, organic dusts and plastic or rubber dust among examples and recommends routine removal of settled dust. It also warns against clearing dust with compressed air because that puts it in the air. For wood shops, see Woodshop Dust Collection and Ventilation.

A university extension fire guide for barns says to store hay in a separate structure from animal housing if possible, to cure baled hay outdoors before storing it, to keep fuel and flammable materials in a secure area away from heat sources, to fuel equipment in a designated area away from structures, and to clean cobwebs and dust off lights, wiring and heating sources. It also reports that heating equipment was the leading cause of barn fires in a 2006 to 2010 period. Our general takeaway: keep hay, fuel containers and oily rags well away from any heater, and do not hang a heater where hay or dust can pile up on it.

Freeze protection for water lines

An extension guide on protecting water from freezing notes that new trench soil can leave a buried line at risk, that surface insulation helps where cover is thin, and that a small continuous flow can keep a line open in extreme cases. Lines that can be emptied for winter should be drained, and hoses should come off hydrants and outdoor faucets because connected hoses can trap water. If you use heat tape, follow its instructions, never wrap it over itself, and do not run it over or under pipe insulation unless the maker allows that. A heated shop helps only if the heat reaches the pipe. Run supply lines inside the heated envelope, not along the cold roof edge or beside a door.

Field card: Heating Farm Shops and Machine Sheds

Seal the doors, then size the heater.

  • Load = UA x temperature difference + 0.018 x air changes x volume x temperature difference.
  • Input = load / efficiency. Our example: 75,240 Btu/h load, about 94,000 Btu/h input at 80 percent.
  • Tube heaters warm surfaces, unit heaters warm air, slab loops are comfortable but slow.
  • Cold metal below the room dew point drips. Insulate, seal, ventilate.
  • Engines indoors make carbon monoxide. Use exhaust capture, ventilation and alarms.
  • Keep hay, fuel, rags and dust away from heaters.
  • Keep water lines inside the heated envelope and drain what you can.
