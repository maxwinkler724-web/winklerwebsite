Wobbe Index and Gas Interchangeability: Why Propane Needs Smaller Orifices

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A gas burner does not care how many Btu are in a cubic foot of gas as such. It cares how many Btu per hour come through a fixed hole at a fixed pressure. That quantity is tracked by one number, the Wobbe index. It explains why propane needs a much smaller orifice than natural gas, and why a small shift in the utility's gas can change a burner's input and flame.

The definition

The Wobbe index is the higher heating value of the gas divided by the square root of its specific gravity:

Wobbe = HHV ÷ √SG

HHV is in Btu per cubic foot. Specific gravity (SG) is the gas density compared with air at the same conditions, so air is 1.00. A 2005 interchangeability white paper hosted by the University of Texas Bureau of Economic Geology says the Wobbe number is based on the heating value and specific gravity of a gas and is related to the thermal input to a burner in Btu per hour. A U.S. Department of Energy National Energy Technology Laboratory paper on fuel interchangeability gives the same form and calls it a measure of the heat input of a fuel to a given burner design. For what HHV means and how it differs from the lower value, see higher vs lower heating value of natural gas and propane.

Why a fixed orifice follows Wobbe

The same national-laboratory paper says specific gravity allows a range of fuels to be generalized to a given burner for a constant pressure drop and fixed orifice size. Here is the reasoning in our own words. Gas leaves an orifice at a speed set by the pressure pushing it. For the same pressure drop, a lighter gas moves faster and a heavier gas moves slower. The speed varies with one over the square root of the density, which is where the square root of specific gravity comes from. So for a fixed opening area A and manifold pressure P:

  • Volume flow of gas is proportional to A × √P ÷ √SG.
  • Heat input is volume flow times HHV, so input is proportional to A × √P × (HHV ÷ √SG).
  • The last bracket is the Wobbe index. Keep the orifice and pressure fixed and input moves in direct proportion to Wobbe.

The same paper cautions that Wobbe is a good measure of heat input for appliances designed for a fixed thermal input but is not intended as a measure of overall interchangeability. Flame stability, air supply and combustion quality also matter.

Worked example (our own numbers)

This example is our own. Inputs: natural gas HHV 1,036 Btu per cubic foot (the U.S. Energy Information Administration sample conversion factor). Propane vapor HHV 2,516 Btu per cubic foot (the same agency's figure for pure propane). Specific gravity is where we have to be careful. We assume 0.60 for pipeline natural gas, a typical round figure and not a measured one. For propane we compute it: molar mass 44.0956 (NIST Chemistry WebBook) divided by an assumed dry-air molar mass of 28.96, which gives 1.523, rounded to 1.52. Treating both as ideal gases is our own simplification.

  1. Natural gas. √0.60 = 0.7746. Wobbe = 1,036 ÷ 0.7746 = 1,337.
  2. Propane. √1.52 = 1.2329. Wobbe = 2,516 ÷ 1.2329 = 2,041.
  3. Ratio. 2,041 ÷ 1,337 = 1.53. Propane delivers about 53 percent more heat through the same hole at the same pressure.
  4. Orifice area at the same pressure. Input is proportional to area times Wobbe, so the area ratio is 1,337 ÷ 2,041 = 0.655. Propane needs about two thirds of the area.
  5. Orifice area at a higher pressure. Suppose natural gas runs at 3.5 and propane at 10.0 inches of water column, the example manifold figures used in the LP conversion kit lesson. Input is also proportional to √P, so the area ratio is 0.655 × √(3.5 ÷ 10.0) = 0.655 × 0.5916 = 0.388. That is about 39 percent of the area, or 62 percent of the diameter (√0.388 = 0.623).
  6. Check. 0.388 × 1.526 × √(10.0 ÷ 3.5) = 0.388 × 1.526 × 1.690 = 1.00. Same input.
  7. What if nothing is changed? Propane through the natural gas orifice at the same pressure runs at about 1.53 times input. At 10.0 inches it would be about 1.526 × 1.690 = 2.58 times. That is why the burner must not be run on the wrong gas.

These are idealized ratios. Real orifices have discharge coefficients, and makers publish drill sizes and pressures from testing. They are not a recipe.

Why propane needs smaller orifices and more pressure

The smaller orifice follows directly from the example: one cubic foot of propane vapor carries much more energy, so far less volume must pass. The higher manifold pressure is a design choice by each maker. Our own explanation, not a sourced figure: propane needs a lot more combustion air per cubic foot of gas, and an atmospheric burner draws that air in with the momentum of the gas jet, so a smaller hole at a higher pressure gives a stiffer jet. The maker's manual gives the actual numbers for each model. A qualified technician follows the maker's conversion instructions; a reader should not change orifices or pressures at home.

When the utility gas changes

Natural gas is not one fixed mixture. The EIA notes methane is about 1,010 Btu per cubic foot and that U.S. natural gas averaged around 1,030 in July 2014, because ethane, propane, carbon dioxide and nitrogen change the energy content. The University of Texas paper reports a 1992 average U.S. gas with a Wobbe number of 1,345 at 1,035 Btu per cubic foot, and an observed range in its data from 1,201 to 1,418, with the middle 80 percent between 1,331 and 1,357. An interim guideline it describes allows about 4 percent either side of the average (about 1,291 to 1,400).

Our own example. Assume an 80,000 Btu per hour burner set at a Wobbe of 1,345, with orifice and manifold pressure unchanged and the regulator holding pressure steady. Input follows Wobbe:

  • At 1,400: 80,000 × 1,400 ÷ 1,345 = about 83,300 Btu per hour, 4.1 percent high.
  • At 1,291: 80,000 × 1,291 ÷ 1,345 = about 76,800 Btu per hour, 4.0 percent low.
  • A 1 percent addition of nitrogen or air lowers Wobbe about 1.3 percent, according to the paper, so 80,000 would drift to about 78,960.

Swings of a few percent are small, which is why gas quality limits exist. A large change is another matter, and the burner is not just a metering hole.

What higher or lower Wobbe means for burners and CO

  • Higher Wobbe. More heat through the same orifice and a richer mix for the same air opening. The paper ties a maximum Wobbe limit to yellow tipping, incomplete combustion and higher NOx and CO, and ties soot to high Wobbe together with heating value.
  • Lower Wobbe. Less input, and the paper says a minimum Wobbe limit addresses lifting, blowout and CO. Dilution with inert gas can lower it.
  • Gas with more heavy hydrocarbons. The paper links high heating value to flashback concerns above about 1,100 Btu per cubic foot.

In the field, the checks are the same ones used for any suspect burner: measured inlet and manifold pressure, a combustion analyzer reading, and a look at flame color. A shift in utility gas is a question for the utility and a licensed technician, not a reason to adjust a burner alone. Suspected CO or gas odor means leave and call for help.

Field card: Wobbe Index and Interchangeability

At a fixed orifice and pressure, input follows Wobbe.

  • Wobbe = HHV ÷ √SG. Input is proportional to orifice area × √pressure × Wobbe.
  • Our own example: natural gas 1,337, propane 2,041, ratio 1.53.
  • Equal input at 3.5 vs 10.0 in. w.c. needs about 39 percent of the area (our own example).
  • Higher Wobbe: richer, yellow tipping, soot, CO. Lower: lifting, blowout, CO.
  • A qualified tech follows the maker's conversion instructions; do not change orifices or pressures yourself.
