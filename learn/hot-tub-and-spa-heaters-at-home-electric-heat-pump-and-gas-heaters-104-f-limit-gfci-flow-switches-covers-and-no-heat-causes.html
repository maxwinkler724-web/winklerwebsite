Hot Tub and Spa Heaters at Home: Electric, Heat Pump and Gas, the 104 F Limit, GFCI and No-Heat Causes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot tub is a small insulated tank with a pump, a filter and a heater, running on a 240 volt circuit next to wet people. That mix is why the heater has built-in safeties and why the electrical rules are strict. This lesson covers how a home spa heats, how electric, heat pump and gas options compare, what the cover is worth, the safe water temperature, and what a homeowner can check when the tub stops heating. For pool-size gas and heat pump heaters, read Pool Heaters, Gas vs Heat Pump; we do not repeat it here.

How an electric spa heater works

Most home hot tubs heat with electric resistance. The U.S. DOE says a portable electric spa has equipment for heating and circulating water, and that its 120 volt models plug into an ordinary outlet while 240 volt models are typically hard wired to a 240 volt circuit. A spa maker's service page we read describes the safety layout. The heater sits in a metal tube, and a pressure (flow) switch confirms water is moving through it, because the heater should not run without flow. A separate high-limit sensor shuts the heater off if the water in the heater gets too hot. The pack's control board reads those switches plus the water temperature sensor and decides when to energize the element.

How big is the heater? DOE's pool heater rule identifies 11 kW as a typical output capacity below which electric resistance heaters are built into spas. We found no single sourced "typical" figure inside that limit, so read the nameplate on your pack. At 240 volts, amps equal watts divided by volts. Our own example uses 5.5 kW, which draws 5,500 / 240 = 22.9 A while the element is on, before pumps are counted.

Electric, heat pump and gas compared

  • Electric resistance. Simple and nearly all the electricity ends up in the water. DOE's pool heat pump page puts resistance heating at about $1,000 a year against about $200 for a heat pump at a COP of 5.0 in its pool example, so the running cost is the weak spot on a big pool. A spa is small, so the heater matters less than the cover.
  • Heat pump. DOE says pool heat pumps run at COPs of 3.0 to 7.0 and work best when outdoor air is above about 50 F. In its 2023 pool heater rule, DOE also found heat pump technology is not a viable option for electric spa heaters in self-contained portable spas, so it did not set standards for them. If a heat pump is part of a larger pool and spa system, that is a pool-heater question.
  • Gas. DOE says gas pool heaters are ideal for heating quickly and can hold temperature in any weather, with some models at 89 to 95 percent efficiency and sizes from 75,000 to 450,000 Btu. Gas needs a gas line, combustion air and venting, so installation and service are for a licensed professional.

The cover matters most

A Washington State University Extension Energy Program factsheet says tubs are covered and unused over 95 percent of the time, yet that is when 75 percent of their energy use occurs. It calls the cover's insulation and seal the most important factors in overall energy use, and says heat losses rise six-fold or more with the cover off. DOE's standby estimate agrees: standby operation is 75 percent of a portable electric spa's energy, and the average standby power of a standard spa in its database was 194 W. For pools, DOE says covering a pool when it is not in use can cut heating costs 50 to 70 percent. A cover also matters for safety: CPSC says to use a locked safety cover when the spa is not in use.

Safe water temperature

CPSC's current spa safety page says to keep spa water at 104 F or below. An archived 1979 CPSC release gives more detail, and its age means it may not reflect current guidance. It says water should never exceed 104 F, calls 100 F safe for a healthy adult, suggests special caution for young children, and says water above 102 F in the first three months of pregnancy can cause fetal damage, so pregnant women should follow the 100 F maximum. It also warns that thermostats can be off by as much as four degrees, so check with an accurate thermometer. For a child or anyone pregnant, we would keep soaks cool and short and ask a doctor. A stuck heater that overheats the tub is a reason to turn the power off, not to wait.

GFCI and the disconnect

A 2003 CPSC release says the National Electrical Code calls for GFCIs on pumps and electrical equipment used with pools, spas and hot tubs, including heaters on 240 volt circuits, and says to test GFCIs monthly. CPSC also says to know where the pump's cut-off switch is, so you can shut it off in an emergency. The rules for the disconnect, its location and the GFCI sit in NEC Article 680, which we name in plain text only. The electrician and inspector decide the placement for your yard. Our practical rule: the tub should have a labeled disconnect you can find and reach, and the GFCI test button should trip it and reset.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Every input here is an assumption for practice, not data from a real tub.

  • Spa volume: 400 gallons. DOE's market table has its most common standard spa bin at 181 to 300 gallons, so this is a large tub.
  • Start 60 F, target 102 F, so the rise is 42 F.
  • Heater: 5.5 kW at 240 V.
  • Electric rate: $0.20 per kWh.

Step 1, energy to heat the water. Pounds of water: 400 x 8.33 = 3,332 lb. Btu: 3,332 x 42 = 139,944 Btu. One kWh is 3,412 Btu, so 139,944 / 3,412 = 41.0 kWh.

Step 2, hours at 5.5 kW. 41.0 / 5.5 = 7.5 hours. That is the ideal figure with no heat loss; a real tub needs longer because it loses heat while it warms, which is why people leave the spa hot.

Step 3, monthly standby, covered vs uncovered. Assume a covered daily heat loss of 5 kWh, close to DOE's 194 W average (about 4.7 kWh a day). Assume that with the cover left off the loss is six times higher, 30 kWh a day, using the six-fold figure from the WSU factsheet as a rough guide.

  • Covered: 5 x 30 days = 150 kWh x $0.20 = $30 a month, about 0.9 heater hours a day (5 / 5.5).
  • Uncovered: 30 x 30 = 900 kWh x $0.20 = $180 a month, about 5.5 heater hours a day.
  • Difference: 750 kWh, or $150 a month. The cover is worth more than any heater upgrade.

When it will not heat: what you can check

The maker page we read lists causes of a flow fault: clogged filters, debris in the pump, a partly closed valve and low water. A supplier troubleshooting article on the high limit adds trapped air after a refill, low flow from scale, a bad thermostat or sensor, and a failed switch, and says the red reset button on the pack resets it. Here is how we would sort the owner's work from a professional's.

  • Safe for an owner: read the panel message; confirm the water level is well above the skimmer; clean or swap the filter; open valves; clear leaves from the pump basket if the pack is off; check the breaker or GFCI (power off, press test, then reset); and wait for trapped air to clear after a fill.
  • Call a pro: anything inside the control box; a high limit that trips again after one reset; a flow switch you think is bad; a tub that has flow and power but will not warm (this points to the element, a relay or a sensor, our own reasoning); and a GFCI that trips repeatedly or will not reset. The maker page says only trained electricians should work inside the control box, with power off.

Never bypass a flow switch or high limit. Those parts exist so the element cannot run dry or cook the water. A GFCI that keeps tripping is telling you about leakage current, not nuisance. See Equipment Grounding and GFCI.

Field card: Hot tub and spa heating

Cover on, 104 F max, GFCI tested, never bypass the switches.

  • Home spas use a resistance element in a flow-through tube; 240 volt models are hard wired.
  • Flow switch: no flow, no heat. High limit: too hot, heater off.
  • Energy to heat = gallons x 8.33 x rise in F; divide by 3,412 for kWh.
  • Standby is about 75 percent of spa energy; the cover is the biggest saver.
  • CPSC: 104 F maximum; the archived release says 100 F for pregnancy and caution for young children.
  • Test the GFCI monthly; know where the disconnect is (NEC Article 680 covers the rules).
  • Owner checks: water level, filter, valves, GFCI. Pro: control box, repeat trips, element.
