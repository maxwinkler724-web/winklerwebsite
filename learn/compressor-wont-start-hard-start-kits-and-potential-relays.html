Compressor Hums and Won't Start: Hard Start Kits and Potential Relays

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You walk up to a condensing unit. The fan spins, the contactor is pulled in, and the compressor hums, then goes quiet. A minute or two later it tries again. Before you reach for a hard start kit, you need to know why it will not start, because the kit helps some causes and hides others.

Why a single-phase compressor hums and trips

A hum with no rotation means the motor is drawing locked-rotor current and not turning. The internal overload eventually opens from the heat, the compressor cools, and the cycle repeats. A Tecumseh troubleshooting chart for its single-phase compressors lists the usual suspects for "hums but trips the overload": wrong wiring, low voltage, a defective start capacitor, a relay that does not close, an open or shorted winding, internal mechanical trouble, and liquid refrigerant in the compressor. Sort them into five groups:

  • Low voltage. Starting current drops the supply voltage, and lower voltage means less torque. Copeland notes that a weak supply can sag for roughly 300 to 400 milliseconds on start, which is when lights dim. Measure voltage at the unit during the start attempt.
  • Failed run capacitor. The capacitor gives the start winding its phase shift, and a dead one leaves the motor with a weak push. See Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures and Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter for the tests. Do them first.
  • High head pressure. The Tecumseh chart lists high discharge pressure among causes of overload cycling. Check the condenser first; Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure covers it.
  • Unequalized pressures. A quick restart against pressure is a heavy start for a piston compressor, and Tecumseh suggests a pump-down arrangement when the starting load is too high. Copeland says its single-phase scroll compressors start unloaded even when system pressures are not balanced, so for scrolls this cause is less likely.
  • Mechanical trouble. In one ACHR News case a compressor climbed from about 20 amps to about 30 amps and tripped its overload with normal pressures and a good capacitor. The diagnosis was internal bearing damage. The overload tripped well below the 35 amp breaker.

What a start capacitor and potential relay do

A permanent split capacitor (PSC) compressor has run and start windings, and the run capacitor shifts the start winding's current. A start capacitor adds more capacitance in parallel with the run capacitor, in series with the start winding, for extra starting torque. ACHR News columnist John Tomczyk explains that the extra capacitance is only wanted until the motor is moving, so a relay takes it out.

The potential relay has a very high resistance coil and normally closed contacts. The coil is wired across the start winding, and the contacts are in series with the start capacitor. As the rotor speeds up it generates voltage in the start winding, called back EMF. It opposes the line voltage, and Tomczyk says it can run in the hundreds of volts. The coil sees that same voltage. At its pickup voltage, usually when the motor is near three-quarter speed, the coil opens the contacts and drops the start capacitor out of the circuit. The motor then runs as an ordinary PSC motor. When power is removed, back EMF fades, the coil lets go and a spring closes the contacts again for the next start. The relay needs more voltage to pick up than to hold.

That is why the relay must match the compressor. If its pickup is too high for the motor's back EMF, the contacts never open, the start capacitor stays in circuit, amps stay high and the protector trips. If a weakened compressor slows down, its back EMF can fall below the drop-out value and the relay recloses and cycles. Copeland's start-assist bulletin shows the relay sensing the C to S voltage and lists different relay part numbers for different compressor sizes.

A PTC start device is a different approach. It is a heat-sensitive resistor wired in parallel with the run capacitor. It is cheap, but it does not sense the motor. Copeland lists a 25 ohm PTC for less severe voltage drops and a start capacitor plus relay for severe drops or high starting torque. The HVAC Training Shop notes a kit shortens the time spent at start amps but does not reduce the start amps, and that kits are for single-stage compressors, not inverter, variable speed or two-stage units.

Check the windings first: a worked example

With power locked out and proven off, and the wires off the compressor terminals, measure resistance between each pair of terminals. A compressor motor has three terminals and two windings that share the common. Craig Migliaccio of AC Service Tech and Joe Marchese in ACHR News give the same pattern: the lowest reading is common to run, the middle reading is common to start, and the highest is run to start, because that path goes through both windings. The highest should equal the sum of the other two.

Suppose you write down three readings:

  • Pair A: 0.8 ohm (lowest)
  • Pair B: 2.4 ohms (middle)
  • Pair C: 3.2 ohms (highest)

Step 1: the lowest pair is common to run, so C-R = 0.8. Step 2: the middle pair is common to start, so C-S = 2.4. Step 3: the highest pair is run to start, so R-S = 3.2. Step 4: add the two smaller readings: 0.8 + 2.4 = 3.2, which matches the highest reading. The windings agree with each other. If you had read 0.8, 2.4 and 4.1 instead, the sum would not match, and a bad connection, a bad meter lead or a damaged winding is likely. Clean, re-test, then compare with the manufacturer's published values, as Copeland advises before condemning a compressor. Also test each terminal to clean bare metal on the shell. A reading of OL means it is not grounded. These tests find a dead or shorted winding, not one that fails under load.

When a hard start kit helps, and when it hides the problem

It helps when the compressor and motor are good but the start is marginal: poor supply voltage, undersized wiring, a heavy starting load, an older unit, or a high-efficiency system with a lower-torque motor. Those are the conditions Riley Archer of RectorSeal lists in ACHR News. His company sells hard start devices, so read his numbers as vendor claims.

A kit hides a problem when you install it before finding the cause:

  • Weak run capacitor. Archer calls skipping the run capacitor check the most common mistake and says a bad run capacitor can damage the kit. His field check: measure the amps on the start terminal, multiply by 2,654, and divide by the volts across the run capacitor. A result within 10 percent of the rating is a pass. For 7 amps and 370 volts: 7 x 2,654 = 18,578, and 18,578 / 370 = about 50.2 microfarads.
  • Mechanical wear. A kit may get a tired compressor to turn, but the rising amps from bearing damage come back. For repeat relay problems on an aging compressor, Tomczyk recommends replacing the compressor, the relay and both capacitors, not adding more parts.
  • Wrong kit. Archer says some compressor makers blame a large share of warranty returns on burned start windings caused by wrong hard start devices. A wrong relay can keep the start capacitor in the circuit too long. Use the compressor maker's part, or the kit maker's selection chart.

Copeland says start assist devices are usually not required on its scroll compressors, so a kit on a healthy scroll is a sign to keep looking. If you add one, record start amps before and after.

Safety before you touch the terminals

  • Lock out and tag out, and prove the unit dead with a meter you have checked. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC and Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.
  • Capacitors can hold charge after power is off. Discharge them by an approved method before you touch them. The capacitor lessons linked above cover it.
  • The glass and pins in the terminal cluster hold refrigerant in. Copeland says a compressor terminal that is damaged, weakened or loses its seal can spray pressurized oil, refrigerant and debris, and the spray can ignite, even with A1 refrigerants. So never use the terminals as a handle or lever, and do not pull or twist on terminal fittings. Remove connectors gently.
  • Stand to one side when you put a meter lead on a compressor terminal, as Marchese advises.
  • Never energize a compressor with its terminal cover or plug off. Copeland adds that neither the cover nor the molded plug removes the risk. If you hear sizzling, sputtering or popping, get away immediately.

Field card: Compressor Hums and Won't Start

Find why it will not start before adding anything to help it start.

  • Hum then overload trip: check voltage during the start attempt, the run capacitor, head pressure and compressor condition.
  • The start capacitor adds torque. The potential relay drops it out using back EMF across the start winding (coil across C-S, contacts in series with the start capacitor).
  • Windings (power off, wires off): C-R lowest, C-S middle, R-S highest, and C-R + C-S = R-S. Example: 0.8 + 2.4 = 3.2.
  • Test each terminal to bare shell metal: OL is good.
  • Kit: the right part for the compressor, run capacitor tested first, start amps recorded.
  • Terminals are not a handle. Cover on. Stand to the side. Leave if it sizzles.
