Natural-Convection Convectors on Hot Water: Chimney Effect and Lint

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Older buildings often heat with a finned pipe element standing inside a tall metal cabinet or recessed into a wall. There is no fan. The cabinet itself is the engine: hot fins warm the air, the warm air rises, and room air is pulled in below. This lesson explains that draft, what blocks it, and how to size up the loss with simple numbers.

What a convector is

A university facilities glossary describes a convector as a tube with metal fins inside a cabinet, much like baseboard, that moves heat from hot water or steam into room air. It adds that radiators are now usually replaced by convectors. A federal rehabilitation guide for homes notes that in finned-pipe baseboard, more heat is delivered by convection than by radiation. Two common shapes exist: a recessed wall unit set between studs, and a freestanding cabinet that stands on the floor. Both hide a copper or steel tube with closely spaced fins behind a front panel, with an inlet grille low and an outlet grille high.

How the cabinet makes its own draft

Air touching the fins heats up and becomes lighter than room air. In a tall cabinet, that warm column pushes up and out the top grille while heavier room air enters at the bottom. This is the same chimney (stack) effect that a Canadian national building research digest describes for tall buildings: the pressure difference depends on height and on the temperature difference between inside and outside. Our explanation for a convector is the same idea in miniature, using the cabinet column in place of a building and room air in place of outdoor air. More cabinet height above the element, or hotter fins, gives more draft.

The air side is the weak link. A university heat exchanger text says the thermal resistance on the air side is large because the air-side heat transfer coefficient is low, which is why fins exist at all. Anything that slows the air or coats the fins makes that weak link weaker.

Grilles, dampers and air vents

The inlet grille at the bottom and the outlet grille at the top are the two ends of the draft path. Many cabinets also have a damper door or flap that the occupant can close to cut airflow when a room is too warm. A closed damper works by shutting off the draft, so a damper that is stuck nearly shut, or one that someone closed years ago, can look like a bad boiler. A university conversion guideline for a large campus says that when an old steam convector or radiator is reused on hot water, its old air vent is removed and a new manual vent made for hot water goes at the high point. Our reading for cabinets: the vent sits inside the element at its highest point, so air trapped there leaves part of the finned tube cold. Bleed it only with the system cool or the pump off as the building procedure says, and keep clear of the hot outlet.

Lint, dust and blocked grilles

Lint packs between the fins like a filter and adds resistance to the draft. It also insulates the fin surface. A federal guide says damaged fins or fin guards should be repaired or replaced because they affect baseboard performance; a bent or matted fin does the same job on a cabinet convector. The remedy is a cool, shut-down element and a vacuum with a brush tool, working along the fins, not across them. Fins are thin and sharp, so wear gloves and avoid bending them. The sister lesson on baseboard cleaning covers combing and damper checks in more detail.

Closing or covering the top grille takes away the outlet. A shelf, a folded blanket, a box of books or a heavy drape over the top stops the warm column from leaving. Our reasoning: with no way out, air in the cabinet stays hot, the draft collapses, and the room gets little of the heat. The cover and enclosure lesson linked below goes deeper on grille openings; here the point is that a convector depends on its own height as a chimney.

Output versus water temperature

A university text gives the heat rate of a finned heat exchanger as effectiveness times the smaller heat capacity rate times the temperature difference between entering water and entering air, so output goes down as the water cools. A campus conversion guideline states that a steam radiator or convector gives off less heat on hot water because of the lower temperature, and that existing hot water terminals must be checked at design conditions. It also says that on a large conversion project, the capacity loss may force extra units. Lowering supply water is therefore not free in a room with a convector. See the baseboard output lesson linked below for the curves.

Compared with fan-forced cabinet heaters

A fan-forced cabinet unit pushes air across a coil, so airflow no longer depends on height or on how warm the coil is. That makes it better for short, low spaces and for stairwells, and it responds faster. The price is a motor, a filter or screen, and noise, and the campus facilities glossary notes that classroom fan-coil cabinets need maintenance once or twice a year. A natural-convection cabinet has nothing to wear out, but it only works as well as its draft allows. The cabinet unit heater lesson linked below covers the fan-forced type, including freeze protection.

Worked example (our own example, assumed numbers)

All of these are our assumptions, not measurements from a real unit. Room air 21 C (294.15 K). Average air in the warm column 36 C (309.15 K). Warm column height H of 0.6 m (about 2 ft) above the element. Air pressure 101,325 Pa and gas constant 287.05 J/kg-K. Flow area through the fins 0.04 m2, with a total loss coefficient of 12 for the grilles and fins together. Average water temperature 70 C (158 F). Element conductance UA of 10 W/K at full airflow.

Step 1, air densities. Density = 101,325 / (287.05 x T). Room: 101,325 / (287.05 x 294.15) = 1.200 kg/m3. Warm column: 101,325 / (287.05 x 309.15) = 1.142 kg/m3. The difference is 0.058 kg/m3.

Step 2, draft pressure. Draft = density difference x g x H = 0.0582 x 9.81 x 0.6 = 0.343 Pa. That is about 0.0014 inch of water, a very small pressure, which is why a few millimeters of lint matters.

Step 3, airflow. Velocity = square root of (2 x 0.343 / (1.142 x 12)) = 0.224 m/s. Volume flow = 0.224 x 0.04 = 0.0089 m3/s, about 19 cfm. Mass flow = 0.0089 x 1.142 = 0.0102 kg/s. Heat capacity rate C = 0.0102 x 1006 J/kg-K = 10.3 W/K.

Step 4, full-flow output. Effectiveness = 1 - exp(-UA/C) = 1 - exp(-10/10.3) = 0.62. Output = 0.62 x 10.3 x (70 - 21) = about 313 W (roughly 1,070 Btu/h). Air leaves at 21 + 0.62 x 49 = 51.5 C, so the 36 C column average we assumed checks out (21 plus 51.5, divided by 2, is 36.2).

Step 5, lint cuts airflow 50 percent. C falls to 5.14 W/K. If UA stayed at 10, effectiveness = 1 - exp(-1.95) = 0.86 and output = 0.86 x 5.14 x 49 = about 216 W, a 31 percent loss. Slower air also lowers the air-side coefficient, and a simple assumption that conductance goes with the square root of airflow gives UA = 7.07 W/K. Then effectiveness = 1 - exp(-1.376) = 0.75 and output = 0.75 x 5.14 x 49 = about 188 W, a 40 percent loss. Lint itself insulates the fins as well, so the real loss could be larger.

Lesson from the math. Halving the airflow does not halve the heat, because the leaving air just gets hotter, but it still takes off roughly a third to two fifths in this case. We ignored the small extra draft from the hotter outlet air, so treat the result as a range. The Canadian digest formula is the same physics in a tall building. We did not use its constant, and the density-difference method above is our own derivation.

Water temperature check. Dropping average water from 70 C to 60 C at the same airflow takes the temperature difference from 49 K to 39 K, so output goes down by about 20 percent (39 / 49 = 0.80), ignoring small changes in draft.

Field card: natural-convection convectors

Height makes the draft, and fins and grilles let it through.

  • Fins warm air; the warm column rises and pulls room air in at the bottom.
  • Check inlet grille, outlet grille and damper position first.
  • Lint on fins adds resistance and insulates; vacuum with the element cool, along the fins.
  • Never put a shelf, cloth or box over the top grille.
  • Air at the high point of the element leaves part of it cold; use the hot-water vent only as the building procedure says.
  • Lower water temperature means lower output.
  • Our example: halving airflow cost about 31 to 40 percent of output.
