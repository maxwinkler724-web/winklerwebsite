Type B Gas Vents for Furnaces and Water Heaters: Double-Wall Pipe, Clearances, Connector Slope, Roof Termination Height, Caps, and Why Not for Condensing Appliances

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Type B vent is the double-wall metal pipe that carries hot, draft-driven flue gas from a standard-efficiency furnace, boiler or water heater up through the house and out the roof. It looks simple, but the clearances, slope, height and cap all have reasons. This lesson walks through them, and explains why the same pipe is the wrong choice for a condensing or fan-pushed appliance.

What Type B vent is and where it fits

Seattle's fuel gas code, which is built on the model fuel gas code and is hosted by the city government, requires Type B and BW gas vents to be listed to a product safety standard. Its venting table lists a Type B gas vent as one of the options for a listed Category I appliance. Category I means the appliance makes a negative pressure in the vent and runs hot enough that the gases stay above the temperature where they would condense in the pipe. Appliances with a draft hood fit here. Other chimney types are also allowed for Category I; see the lesson on chimney liners when a masonry chimney is involved.

Two parts are easy to confuse. The vent connector is the short run from the appliance flue collar or draft hood to the vent. The vent is the vertical run through the structure to the roof. The rules for each differ, as the sections below show.

How the air space keeps the inner pipe warm

This part is our own plain-language explanation, not a quote. Type B vent is two metal tubes, one inside the other, with a gap of still air between them. Air in a closed gap is a poor conductor, so the gap slows heat moving from the inner pipe to the outside wall. That does two jobs. The inner pipe stays warmer, which keeps the flue gas buoyant so it keeps rising, and it stays above the temperature where water vapor in the exhaust turns to liquid. And the outer wall stays cooler than a bare pipe would, which is why a listed Type B vent can sit close to framing. Nothing about that works if the pipe is dented, crushed, or its joints are not fully locked, so gently handle and inspect the lengths.

Clearance to combustibles

For listed Type B vent material used with listed draft-hood appliances, the Seattle code table says the clearance is "as listed." Practically, you follow the number on the pipe's label and the maker's instructions, not a rule of thumb. The same table gives bigger numbers for the connector, because single-wall pipe is much hotter on the outside. For a single-wall metal connector on a listed appliance with a draft hood the table shows 6 inches. For an unlisted residential appliance with a draft hood it shows 9 inches, and for other residential and low-heat appliances 18 inches. Those distances can be reduced where the combustible surface is protected as the code allows. The code also says single-wall connectors may not pass through a wall, floor or ceiling, with limited exceptions. Where the vent itself passes through a floor or ceiling, the code calls for noncombustible fireblocking under the building code, and the vent maker's firestop parts are how that is usually met.

Connector slope, length and support

  • Slope: the connector goes in without dips or sags and slopes up toward the vent at not less than 1/4 inch per foot.
  • Support: it has to be supported for the weight of the material used, so it cannot sag or pull out of the draft hood.
  • Horizontal length: for a single-wall connector the maximum is 75 percent of the vent height. For a Type B double-wall connector it is 100 percent of the vent height. Engineered systems are the exception.

An upward slope matters because hot gas wants to rise and a long flat run slows it. A low spot can also trap condensed moisture and let it sit on the pipe seams.

Roof termination: height, rules, and caps

The code treats the vent top in two cases. A vent 12 inches or less across that is at least 8 feet from a vertical wall ends at a height taken from a roof-pitch table. A vent over 12 inches across, or one within 8 feet of a vertical wall, ends at least 2 feet above the point where it passes through the roof and 2 feet above any part of the building within 10 feet horizontally. Separately, a Type B vent must end at least 5 feet above the highest connected draft hood or flue collar. The pitch table in the Seattle code gives 1.0 foot for a flat roof to 6/12, 2.0 feet for over 8/12 up to 9/12, and 2.5 feet for over 9/12 up to 10/12. Other rows exist; read the table in your adopted code.

The vent must pass through the roof flashing and finish with a listed cap or listed roof assembly. A cap keeps rain and debris out and is part of the tested system, so a homemade hat or a bare cut pipe does not qualify. Local codes can differ from Seattle's, so check the adopted edition where you work.

Our own worked example: a vent on a 9/12 roof

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Assumptions: a 4-inch Type B vent, so 12 inches or less; the vent is at least 8 feet from any vertical wall; nothing taller is within 10 feet; the highest draft hood is 10 feet below the roof penetration point; the roof is 9/12; and we use the Seattle table above.

  1. Roof pitch 9/12 falls in the row "over 8/12 to 9/12," which gives a minimum height H of 2.0 feet above the roof.
  2. Convert: 2.0 ft × 12 = 24 inches of vent above the roof at the penetration.
  3. Check the 5-foot rule: the vent must reach 5 ft above the draft hood. It rises about 10 ft + 2 ft = 12 ft above it, so 12 − 5 = 7 ft to spare.
  4. Check the connector. Assume the connector is single-wall and the vent height is 20 ft. The horizontal limit is 0.75 × 20 = 15 ft. A 6 ft horizontal run is under that. At 1/4 in. per foot it needs at least 6 × 0.25 = 1.5 inches of rise.

If the same vent were on a 10/12 roof (over 9/12 to 10/12), the table value would be 2.5 ft, or 30 inches: a 6-inch increase just from a steeper roof.

Why not for condensing or positive-pressure appliances

A condensing appliance pulls so much heat out of the exhaust that the gas leaves cool and wet. It is meant to condense, so the vent must handle liquid and be made of corrosion-resistant material with sealed joints. Type B vent is built for hot gas. In Seattle's code, a forced-draft appliance that puts positive pressure in the vent needs a venting system designed for positive pressure, and the code requires a way to collect and dispose of condensate for the condensing categories. Its Category I table row is the one that lists Type B. Putting condensing or pressurized exhaust into B-vent can leak flue gas out of the seams and rot the pipe. See the lesson on special gas vent for the correct material.

The reverse trouble also occurs. When an old furnace is replaced with a smaller or condensing unit and the water heater is left alone on the old vent, the vent may be oversized and cool. See orphaned water heaters. The code lets you resize a vent specifically to control condensation. We do not give a dew point temperature here because we have no sourced number; the idea is simply that exhaust cooler than its dew point drops water.

Corrosion and condensation signs

From the CPSC, vents on furnaces, water heaters and boilers should be checked to make sure they are not loose or disconnected, and flues should be checked for blockage. In a CPSC furnace test, a disconnected vent let indoor carbon monoxide climb to about 159 ppm at the highest firing rate tested, and the test standard has no requirement covering a disconnected vent. Our own list of things to look for on B-vent:

  • Rust streaks or flaking on the draft hood and connector seams.
  • White or brown stains and drips under joints, or water near the base of the vent.
  • Dents, crushed sections, or a loose twist-lock joint.
  • Soot around the draft hood, a sign gas is spilling out. Our lesson on backdrafting covers the test.
  • A cap that is missing, bent, or full of debris.

What an inspector or tech looks at

  1. Is the vent type right for the appliance category and label?
  2. Is the pipe listed, undamaged, and joined as the maker says?
  3. Does the connector slope, stay supported, and stay within the length limit?
  4. Are clearances and firestops in place at every floor, ceiling and attic crossing?
  5. Does the vent top clear the roof by the code height and carry a listed cap?
  6. Does the draft test show gases leaving with the appliance running?

Field card: Type B gas vent

Hot gas up, sloped connector, listed cap, wrong for condensing.

  • Type B vent is for Category I appliances only.
  • Air space between the walls keeps the inner pipe warm and the outside cooler.
  • Vent clearance: as listed on the label. Single-wall connector: 6 in. (listed draft-hood appliance, Seattle table).
  • Connector rises at least 1/4 in. per foot; no sags; supported.
  • Connector horizontal limit: 75 percent of vent height single-wall, 100 percent double-wall.
  • Roof: pitch-table height (2.0 ft for over 8/12 to 9/12), 5 ft above the highest draft hood, listed cap.
  • Condensing or positive-pressure exhaust needs a vent made for it.
  • Check for rust, stains, loose joints and spillage.
