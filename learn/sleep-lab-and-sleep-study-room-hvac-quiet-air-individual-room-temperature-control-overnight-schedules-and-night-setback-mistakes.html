Sleep Lab and Sleep Study Room HVAC: Quiet Air, Room Control and Night Schedules

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most buildings relax at night. A sleep lab is the opposite: the rooms are full from evening to morning, and the people in them are trying to sleep while wired to monitors. A facility tech gets called when a patient is too warm, a draft hits the bed, or a fan hum shows up on the recording. This lesson covers what the published standards say and the mistakes behind most after-hours calls.

Check the source documents

Check the source documents. This page was built from public pages read in 2026 and is general education. Your facility's accreditation file, design documents and building codes control. Confirm every number with them.

What the accreditation standards say, and what they leave out

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine publishes standards for accrediting sleep testing facilities. We read the 2025 edition (named in plain text in the sources). Standard L-6 says each testing bedroom must be single occupancy, private, comfortable and quiet, with hard floor-to-ceiling walls and a privacy door onto a corridor or common area. Standard L-7 requires at least 24 inches of clear space on three sides of the bed, and L-10 sets a control room of at least 40 square feet or 20 square feet per bedroom, whichever is larger. The AASM FAQ page repeats the bedroom wording. In what we read, we found no temperature setpoint, no sound level and no ventilation rate. "Comfortable and quiet" is the whole HVAC requirement, so the numbers come from other documents, and from the facility's own design brief. Ask for it.

Why temperature control matters here

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute tells the public to keep the bedroom quiet, cool and dark but gives no temperature number. A 2024 systematic review in Sleep Medicine Reviews (Chevance and colleagues) found that warmer outdoor or indoor temperatures are generally linked to worse sleep, while rating study quality low to moderate. Individual studies it summarizes include 5.10 fewer minutes of sleep per degree C of bedroom night temperature in older adults in Shanghai, and a 3.40% drop in sleep efficiency when summer bedroom nights were above 25 C compared with below 21 C. These are home studies, not lab rules, but they show why a warm, unmoving room costs a sleep study its data. A room that is too cold causes the opposite complaint. Treat the owner's setpoint range as the target and hold it steady, because a swinging bedroom is what wakes people.

The classic mistake: the building's night setback

A building automation schedule usually calls the whole building occupied from early morning to evening. After that it sets back temperatures, closes or minimizes outdoor air, and may shut terminal units to minimum. See thermostat setback schedules and building automation basics for the mechanics. In a sleep lab that is exactly backward. Our own field experience of what goes wrong:

  • The lab zone sits on the same schedule as the offices, so the bedrooms drift at 10 p.m. when the patients are asleep. Create a separate lab schedule: occupied from before lights-out until after wake-up, with a morning recovery.
  • Nights with no study leave the rooms unoccupied, which is fine, but the schedule must be easy to override from the control room so a late booking is not conditioned by hand at midnight.
  • A one-hour "override" button expires mid-study. Check the override time and who owns it.
  • Staff forget to return an override to normal. The U.S. Department of Energy's healthcare setback discussion lists this as the main concern for setbacks in general, and suggests tying the mode to other signals. Use an automatic return, not memory.

A health-care ventilation setback talk from the same DOE program reads ASHRAE Standard 170 (plain text only) as having no air-change requirement for an unoccupied patient room, but cautions against going below code rates. For an occupied room, the rate does not drop.

One control per bedroom

Six bedrooms on one thermostat means one patient decides for six, and the sensor in the corridor or the sunniest room runs everyone. Put a sensor in each bedroom, away from the diffuser and from the headboard wall, or fit a terminal unit per room (a VAV box with reheat, or a fan coil). Each person's cooling need differs, and a body in a room shifts the load. Our own practice: lock the thermostat cover and show no bright display, since lights bother sleepers, and give the technologist a setpoint adjustment at the control room.

Drafts, equipment heat and humidifiers

These are our own field notes, not from a standard. Aim supply air away from the bed so cool air never lands on a sleeping face; ceiling diffusers over the foot of the bed work better than over the pillow. The control room holds screens, recorders and staff for the whole night, so it often needs its own cooling and a setpoint different from the bedrooms. As an assumed example, 600 W of electronics converts to 600 x 3.412 = about 2,047 Btu/h, which is a real load on a small room. A heated humidifier on a CPAP unit works against a cold room: the American Sleep Apnea Association explains that water condenses in the hose when the humidifier and the tubing are at different temperatures, and that a chilly bedroom makes it worse.

Worked example: six 160 sq ft bedrooms on one zone

These inputs are our own assumptions: one patient and one overnight caregiver per room (the standards allow a caregiver bed), a 9 ft ceiling, and a zone air distribution effectiveness of 1.0. Check yours against the design.

  1. Rates. ASHRAE 62.1-2022 Table 6-1, bedroom/living room row in the hotel, motel, resort and dormitory group: 5 cfm per person and 0.06 cfm per square foot, air class 1. A barracks sleeping area row uses the same two rates.
  2. Per room. Breathing zone outdoor air = 5 x 2 people + 0.06 x 160 = 10 + 9.6 = 19.6 cfm. With the table's default density of 10 people per 1,000 sq ft, 1.6 people gives 8 + 9.6 = 17.6 cfm. We use 19.6 because the caregiver is real.
  3. Six rooms. 6 x 19.6 = 117.6 cfm of outdoor air for the zone group, at the assumed effectiveness of 1.0. That is the floor, even at 3 a.m.
  4. Share of supply. At an example 200 cfm per bedroom, 6 x 200 = 1,200 cfm, so outdoor air is 117.6 / 1,200 = 9.8%. Each room has 200 x 60 / (160 x 9 = 1,440 cu ft) = 8.3 air changes per hour.

The point: ventilation is a small slice, and it is required only while occupied. Setting back at night does not save a sleep lab much, and it turns the lab into the first call at 11 p.m.

The noise idea: why less airflow is quieter

For the standard NC concepts, see our commercial HVAC noise lesson; we do not repeat them. A terminal-unit maker's acoustics guide (2018) lists NC 25 to 30 for hospital private rooms and NC 30 to 35 for hotel guest rooms, and says diffuser NC ratings subtract 10 dB from measured sound power to account for a typical room. A reference article gives an empirical duct equation, sound power = 10 + 50 log(velocity) + 10 log(area). In that equation, at a fixed duct size, halving velocity changes sound power by 50 x log(0.5) = about -15 dB.

Example: suppose a diffuser is rated NC 30 at 200 cfm. Our own estimate: dropping to 100 cfm at the same neck size cuts its self-noise by roughly 15 dB, so the diffuser should no longer be the loudest thing in the room, and fan, damper and ambient noise take over. That is a rule of thumb for flow-generated noise only. Catalog sound data is the real answer, and a damper closed down to throttle air can add noise instead of removing it. The same maker guide says to slow the fan, not choke the air. Also, with 100 cfm each, the outdoor air share rises to 117.6 / 600 = 19.6%, and air changes fall to about 4.2 per hour, so check that cooling still meets the load before you call it done.

Field card: Sleep Lab and Sleep Study Room HVAC

Occupied at night, one sensor per bedroom, quiet air.

  • AASM standard L-6: bedrooms single occupancy, private, comfortable and quiet. No temperature or sound number in what we read.
  • Lab schedule must read occupied from lights-out to wake-up; do not leave it on the office schedule.
  • Override timers: check the length and the automatic return.
  • ASHRAE 62.1-2022 bedroom row: 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per sq ft. Example room: 19.6 cfm.
  • Maker table targets: NC 25 to 30 hospital private rooms; NC 30 to 35 hotel rooms.
  • Rule of thumb: halving duct velocity lowers flow noise about 15 dB (50 log). Fan speed, not dampers.
  • Keep air off the pillow. Control room electronics: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Cold bedroom plus CPAP humidifier equals hose condensation.
