Gas Fireplace Pilot Between Seasons: Off for Summer or Leave It On?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many older gas fireplaces and gas log sets keep a small flame burning all year, even when nobody wants heat. Every fall the same question comes up: shut it off for summer, or leave it alone? This lesson walks through the trade-offs, the safe way to think about relighting, and a short checklist before the first cold night.

Check the label first

Your own manual rules. Models differ a great deal. This page is general education. The rating plate and the owner manual for your exact fireplace decide what is allowed, and a licensed gas technician or your gas utility can answer questions about your unit.

What a standing pilot is

A standing pilot is a tiny gas flame that stays lit so the main burner can ignite when you flip a switch or press a remote button. It also heats a sensor that tells the gas valve the flame is present. If you want the parts in detail, see our lesson on millivolt gas valves on fireplaces and wall heaters.

How much gas does it use? A federal energy rulemaking document quotes a state energy agency analysis that assumed a standing pilot runs about 4,612 hours a year and uses about 4,161,569 Btu per year per unit. Dividing one by the other gives about 900 Btu per hour (our own arithmetic). That is the same neighborhood as the 800 Btu per hour we use in the example below. Your unit may be higher or lower, so read the rating plate.

Turn it off for summer, or leave it on?

Reasons people shut the pilot off:

  • Gas used. The pilot burns whether or not you use the fireplace. The worked example below shows the size of that amount.
  • Extra heat in summer. The pilot adds a little heat to the room and the flue. In a house that is being cooled, that heat is unwanted. How much stays in the room depends on the unit, so we call this our own observation, not a measured figure.

Reasons people leave it on, all of them our own field observations and not sourced figures:

  • Pests. A burning pilot keeps its tube and orifice warm and moving with air. When the pilot is off, spiders and insects can build nests in the tiny passages, and a blocked pilot is a common reason one will not light in the fall. Our lesson on nests in gas burners and pilot tubes covers what to look for.
  • Moisture. Burning gas makes water vapor, and a cold, shut-off firebox in a humid summer can feel damp. A small pilot may help keep it drier, though we have no measurement to put on that. If you see rust or dampness, mention it at the yearly service.
  • Convenience. A pilot that stays lit is one less relight to do.

No one answer fits every home. The first thing to read is your owner manual: some makers say what to do for long shutdowns and some do not. Whatever you choose, do not improvise your own steps.

Relighting: the maker steps only

Relighting steps differ between models, so we do not give a universal sequence here. Use the lighting instructions on the label or in the owner manual, in the exact order printed. Do not use a tool, match or lighter in a way the manual does not show. If the pilot will not stay lit after the printed number of tries, stop and call a technician.

If you smell gas, leave. A federal pipeline safety page says to leave the area on foot right away, avoid anything that could spark (including switches and starting a vehicle), and call for help from a safe location away from the smell. Do that from outside: call 911 and the gas utility from your yard or the neighbor's. Do not look for the leak yourself.

Newer units light only on demand

Many newer fireplaces use an intermittent pilot or electronic ignition. A spark lights a small flame only when the unit is called, and the pilot goes out when the call ends (our own plain-language description; the maker defines how yours works). The same federal document cites the same state analysis for yearly pilot energy per unit: about 4.16 million Btu for standing pilots, about 1.75 million for on-demand pilots, and about 0.19 million for intermittent pilots. The diagram shows the gap. For troubleshooting, see intermittent pilot ignition and flame proving.

Electronic ignition systems use batteries or house power, which brings the next topic.

Hot glass, children and pets

A sealed glass front gets hot enough to burn skin. A federal consumer product safety staff briefing described a draft voluntary standard that would require a protective barrier on gas fireplaces whose glass front is above 172 degrees F, with the barrier itself not a burn hazard if touched. For homes with toddlers or pets, a screen or guard that fits your model is a sensible step, and the maker can tell you which ones are approved. Our own advice: keep children and pets back from the glass while the fireplace runs and for a good while after it shuts off, because glass cools slowly. See also our lesson on childproofing home heating and cooling.

Never remove or leave off a maker's glass front or screen to get more heat or a better look.

Remote and wall switch batteries

Handheld remotes and receiver boxes run on batteries, and so do many on-demand ignition systems. A weak battery is a common reason a unit will not light, and a leaking battery can damage a receiver. These are our own field observations. Replace batteries in the fall, using the type the manual names, and keep spares nearby. Some millivolt systems need no batteries at all, so check before you buy any.

Worked example: pilot gas over a summer (our own example)

Assumptions (ours): pilot input 800 Btu per hour, burning 24 hours a day for 5 months (June through October, 153 days). Actual pilot input varies by model, so read the rating plate for your number. A federal energy information page gives 1 therm = 100,000 Btu and about 1,036 Btu per cubic foot of natural gas as sample conversion factors.

  1. Per day: 800 Btu/h x 24 h = 19,200 Btu.
  2. Over 153 days: 19,200 x 153 = 2,937,600 Btu.
  3. In therms: 2,937,600 / 100,000 = 29.4 therms.
  4. In cubic feet at 1,036 Btu per cubic foot: 2,937,600 / 1,036 = about 2,836 cubic feet.

So an 800 Btu per hour pilot would use roughly 29 therms across that summer. A 900 Btu per hour pilot would use 33 therms (900 x 24 x 153 / 100,000 = 33.0). Your gas bill shows your own therm rate if you want to turn this into a number.

Fall checklist

  • Test the CO alarm. A federal consumer product safety fact sheet says the test button checks the circuitry but not the sensor accuracy, and tells owners to follow the maker's instructions and replace alarms that reach their replacement age or sound an end-of-life signal. It advises alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.
  • Look at the vent cap. Check from outside that the vent cap or termination is clear of nests, leaves and snow, and not cracked or loose. A federal consumer product safety release advises having flues and chimneys inspected before each heating season for blockage. Our own advice: do not climb on a roof to look; use binoculars or ask a pro.
  • Check glass and gasket. With the unit cold and off, look for cracked glass and a gasket that is torn, hanging or missing. Our own advice: do not run it with damaged glass or a gasket in poor shape; call for service.
  • Book the yearly professional check. The same CO fact sheet advises a professional inspection of heating systems once a year, including chimneys and flues for blockage, corrosion and loose connections. A university extension fact sheet likewise says a technician should check that exhaust draft is correct and test combustion appliances for CO.

Field card: Gas Fireplace Pilot Between Seasons: Off for Summer or Leave It On?

Maker steps only. Smell gas: leave and call from outside.

  • A standing pilot burns all year; on-demand and intermittent pilots light only when called.
  • Summer off saves gas, about 29 therms in our 800 Btu per hour example; pests and moisture are the other side.
  • Relight only by the label or owner manual. If it will not stay lit, call a technician.
  • Glass fronts get hot: use a guard and keep children and pets back.
  • Fall: test CO alarm, clear vent cap, check glass and gasket, book a yearly professional check.
