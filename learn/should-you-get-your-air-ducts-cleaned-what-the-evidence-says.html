Should You Get Your Air Ducts Cleaned? What the Evidence Says

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Few home services come with bigger promises than duct cleaning. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency takes a more cautious view: it does not recommend routine cleaning, and it lists a short set of situations where cleaning does make sense. This lesson walks through that guidance for homeowners, then adds a short section for new techs.

What the evidence says

EPA's page "Should You Have the Air Ducts in Your Home Cleaned?" says knowledge about duct cleaning is still in its early stages, so the agency gives no blanket recommendation. It states that duct cleaning has never been shown to actually prevent health problems. Studies have not conclusively shown that dirty ducts raise the amount of dust in a home, or that cleaning lowers it. EPA notes that much of the dirt in a duct sticks to the surface instead of entering the rooms, and that cooking, cleaning, smoking, outdoor pollution and ordinary activity can expose people to more particles than the ducts do. It sees no evidence that a light layer of dust in ducts is a health risk. Its bottom line on health is blunt: no one knows.

That is not the same as saying cleaning is harmful. EPA says properly done cleaning is not considered harmful. The point is that the benefit is unproven, so you should have a reason before you pay for it.

When cleaning does make sense

EPA lists three situations where cleaning may be warranted:

  • Visible mold. Substantial mold growth inside hard-surface ducts or on other parts of the system. EPA says only an expert can confirm that a growth is mold, and a lab may be needed. Wet or moldy duct insulation cannot be cleaned effectively and should be replaced.
  • Vermin. Rodents or insects living in the ducts.
  • Excessive debris. The ducts are clogged with dust and debris, or particles are actually coming out of the supply registers.

EPA adds that if the cause of a mold problem, usually moisture, is not fixed, the problem will likely come back. It calls moisture control the most effective way to prevent biological growth in ducts. If nobody in the home has allergies or unexplained symptoms, and there is no visible mold, musty odor or heavy dust, EPA calls cleaning probably unnecessary. Dusty return registers are normal and can simply be vacuumed.

Two related points from the same page. First, EPA says some research suggests that cleaning the cooling coil, fan and heat exchanger can improve efficiency and equipment life, while there is little evidence that cleaning only the ducts does. Second, fuel-burning appliances should be inspected and serviced before each heating season because of carbon monoxide, which is a separate matter from duct cleanliness.

Cautions on biocides and sealants

Some providers offer to spray a chemical or a coating. EPA's page is cautious on both. On biocides (chemicals meant to kill microbes), it says research has not shown them to be effective in duct cleaning or free of health risks, and that no EPA-registered biocides are approved for internally insulated duct systems or for fiberglass duct board or liner. Spraying can move material through the system, and some people react badly. If a biocide is ever used, EPA says the provider should first show visible microbial growth, and that settling-plate tests are inappropriate for that decision. Ozone, also sometimes proposed, is regulated as a lung irritant, and EPA calls its use in ducts controversial.

On sealants (coatings sprayed inside ducts), EPA says lab tests suggest they do not fully coat the surfaces, and that their safety, toxicity and long-term durability are unknown. They may affect fire-retardant properties and noise, and could void a manufacturer's warranty. Acceptable uses are narrow, such as repairing damaged fiberglass insulation or fire damage. EPA also says never to apply sealant over wet liner, actively growing mold or debris. It adds that steam cleaning or other moisture-based methods should never be used on ductwork.

Red flags in a cleaning offer

EPA's provider tips double as a red-flag list. Be wary of a provider who:

  • makes sweeping health claims, such as promising cleaning will prevent illness;
  • recommends routine cleaning on a schedule, which EPA does not;
  • claims to be "EPA certified." EPA says it does not certify or endorse duct cleaners;
  • cannot show you the contamination that supposedly justifies the work;
  • will not put the scope and total price in writing before starting.

EPA suggests getting written estimates from at least three providers, checking references and complaints with your local consumer affairs office or the Better Business Bureau, and asking about state licensing where it exists. For hourly pricing, ask for an estimated time. EPA reports typical costs of $450 to $1,000 per heating and cooling system, depending on size, access, climate and how dirty the system is. Treat that as EPA's published range, not a current price list.

Worked example: comparing three quotes

A homeowner collects three written quotes for one system (these numbers are made up for practice): A is $390, B is $720, C is $980. EPA's range is $450 to $1,000.

  1. Quote A: 450 − 390 = $60 below the bottom of the range. A low number is not proof of a problem, but it is the one to question: ask what is excluded, such as the coil or the return side, and whether extras get added on site.
  2. Quote B: 720 is inside the range. The midpoint of the range is (450 + 1,000) ÷ 2 = $725, so B sits almost exactly in the middle.
  3. Quote C: 1,000 − 980 = $20 under the top. Ask what justifies a price near the ceiling; a very dirty or hard-to-reach system might.
  4. Spread between highest and lowest: 980 − 390 = $590. A spread that large means the quotes probably cover different work. Compare scope line by line before comparing price.

The decision comes first, though. If none of the three EPA situations applies, the cheapest correct answer may be no cleaning at all.

NADCA standards

The National Air Duct Cleaners Association (NADCA) publishes the industry standard that EPA tells homeowners to look for. NADCA's current standard is called ACR, The NADCA Standard (2025 Edition). NADCA describes it as covering the assessment, cleaning and restoration of HVAC systems, including checking how clean components are, reducing job hazards and guiding work to a set cleanliness level. NADCA also offers a certification for individuals, Air Systems Cleaning Specialist (ASCS), and a code of ethics for its members. A provider who says they follow the standard should be able to explain how they verify cleanliness, which is a fair question to ask.

For new techs: what you may be asked on a call

Customers will ask you about duct cleaning, so it helps to know the neutral answer. Use these points, all from EPA's page:

  • Moisture is the root of most growth. Look for coil condensation problems, drain pans that do not slope to the drain, standing water, wet insulation, and uninsulated ducts in attics or crawl spaces. Oversized AC units cycle often and dehumidify poorly. See House Feels Humid With the AC Running.
  • A qualified cleaner opens access ports, checks for asbestos before disturbing material, uses vacuums that exhaust outside or have HEPA filtration, protects furnishings, uses soft brushes on fiberglass duct, and reseals the access holes.
  • Cleaning coils and drain pans belongs in routine maintenance, per EPA.
  • Leaky ducts and poor filtration let dirt in. Fixing leaks and using the right filter is often a better first step. See Ductwork Basics and Common Problems and How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics.

Do not give a mold diagnosis yourself. EPA says only an expert can confirm mold. Suggest the homeowner have it assessed.

Field card: Duct Cleaning, What the Evidence Says

No signs, no cleaning. Fix the moisture first.

  • EPA: duct cleaning has never been shown to prevent health problems; no routine cleaning recommended.
  • Clean when there is visible mold, vermin, or excessive debris or particles at registers.
  • Fix the cause (usually moisture) or the problem returns.
  • Wet or moldy duct insulation: replace, do not clean.
  • Biocides and sealants: cautions from EPA; no registered biocides for insulated or fiberglass ducts.
  • Red flags: health claims, "EPA certified," routine schedules, no proof, no written scope.
  • Get three written quotes. Ask about NADCA ACR standard.
