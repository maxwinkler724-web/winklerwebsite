Reach-In and Display Case Defrost, Gaskets, Drains and Coil Care

A reach-in cooler or display case looks simple from the front, but behind the glass it is a small refrigeration system with a clock, heaters, a drain and two coils that both want to clog. Most service calls trace back to ice, water or dirt.

Why frost forms in the first place

An ACHR News white paper explains that refrigerant in an evaporator runs a few degrees to roughly sixteen degrees colder than the air passing over it. Because of that, the coil surface can sit below freezing even when the product itself is stored above freezing. The article gives meat held at 34 to 36 degrees F as an example, which can put the coil surface somewhere around 24 to 31 degrees F. Frost is insulation. It chokes airflow and cuts heat transfer, so every low-temperature box needs a way to melt it. The same article notes that low-temperature coils are built with wide fin spacing, about four fins per inch, to leave room for frost to build between defrosts.

The three common defrost types

  • Off-cycle. Refrigeration stops and the evaporator fans keep moving room air across the coil. The white paper says this only works when the air is above freezing, so it suits beverage and produce coolers, not freezers. It is cheap and simple.
  • Electric. Resistance heaters in or near the coil melt the frost. The white paper notes that heater surfaces get very hot and that meltwater can steam and refreeze on nearby surfaces, which is one reason controls often limit how long the heaters run.
  • Hot gas. Hot discharge gas is sent through the coil tubing. The same article describes it as the primary method in supermarkets and the most costly to install, though generally very effective.

One manufacturer manual for a glass-top merchandiser lists both electric and gas defrost options, with different run times and termination temperatures by model, which shows how much this varies.

Start, end and drip time

Most small equipment starts a defrost by time and ends it by temperature, with a time limit as a backstop. A manufacturer manual for a reach-in unit describes defrost as time initiated and temperature terminated, with a failsafe time, a thermostat that ends the cycle at a stated coil temperature, and evaporator fans that wait until the coil is cold again. That delay matters: the ACHR article says fans should stay off after defrost so that meltwater drains rather than blowing onto product. Treat any manual's numbers as that model's example only, and reset the clock after a power loss.

Doors and gaskets

Warm room air is the enemy. A tired gasket lets humid air in, and that moisture becomes frost on the coil. One maintenance guide suggests testing a gasket by closing a sheet of paper in the door and looking for a spot where it slides out easily. Others advise cleaning gaskets with mild detergent, drying them completely, and replacing torn or warped sections. Also check that the cabinet is level front to side, because one manufacturer ties even door closing to leveling, and that door switches stop the evaporator fans when a door is held open. Heated door frames and glass heaters are there to stop sweating, so a cold or dead door heater is a clue when you see fog or water at the frame.

Condensate, drains and pans

Defrost water has to go somewhere. Some cases use a heated pan to evaporate it, and a manufacturer manual describes a vinyl drain tube feeding that pan, a trap to stop cold air traveling up the drain, and a warning that ice in the pan can point to a failed heater, low voltage, an unlevel cabinet or a bad drain tube. Another manual says the pan is not made for wash water, so lift the drain hose into a bucket when you wash the case interior, then reconnect it firmly. A blocked defrost drain leaves standing water, which is a sanitation problem as well as an ice problem.

Evaporator and condenser care

Keep product away from the evaporator cover and out of the return air path, because overloaded or blocked shelves make a case run warm. On the condensing side, sources give cleaning intervals that range from quarterly to every six months and say to clean sooner in dusty or greasy rooms. Brush or vacuum the fins, or use low-pressure air, and keep soap and water away from electrical parts. A dirty condenser raises head pressure and makes the compressor work harder; see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure. Always lock out power before opening a panel.

Order of authority

Field card: Reach-In and Display Case Defrost, Gaskets, Drains and Coil Care

Ice, water and dirt cause most reach-in problems.

  • Frost forms because the coil runs colder than the air. Defrost removes it.
  • Off-cycle only suits boxes above freezing. Electric and hot gas suit freezers.
  • Typical small-unit defrost: time initiated, temperature terminated, fan delay afterward.
  • Check gaskets, levelness, door switches and door heaters for moisture sources.
  • Keep drains, traps and pan heaters clear, and clean the condenser on a schedule.
