Running a Furnace or Boiler on a Generator: Sizing and Safe Hookup

Last updated: 2026-10-16

After a storm takes the power out in New Jersey, a furnace or boiler that burns fuel still needs a little electricity to run its fan or pump. The question is how big a generator that takes, and how to connect it without hurting anyone. Sizing is arithmetic you can do from a nameplate. The hookup is a job for a licensed electrician.

Safety first. Run a generator only outdoors, never in a garage, basement or shed, and never plug it into a wall outlet to feed the house. The sizing math below is for learning and for talking with your electrician. It is not a design.

Why a gas, oil or boiler system needs power at all

A fuel-burning furnace makes heat with fuel, but the blower that moves air, the burner motor on an oil unit, and the control board all run on household electricity. A hot-water boiler needs a circulator pump and controls. That is why a house with a perfectly good furnace goes cold in an outage. The electrical load is small next to an air conditioner or an electric heater, which is why a modest generator can often cover it. For what to check when the grid returns, see Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start.

Step 1: Find the running load on the nameplate

Look for the data label on the furnace or boiler and on its motors. Manufacturers print amps and volts, and watts follow from a simple formula: watts = volts × amps. Treat the answer as a planning number, not a meter reading.

Bard's installation manual 2100-392 for its oil furnaces (115 volt, 60 hertz) shows how a maker lists it. For model D84D011HF it gives a 1/2 horsepower blower motor at 10.5 amps, a burner motor at 1.7 amps, and 12.2 total amps. The two motors add up to the total, which is a good check that you are reading the right columns. Goodman's spec sheet SS-312D for its GMNT gas furnaces lists only minimum circuit ampacity (for example 12.5 amps for the 1/2 horsepower models). That figure is for choosing wire and breaker size and includes a safety margin, so it overstates the running load. Use a running-amps or total-amps line when the label has one, and ask a technician when it does not.

Boilers are lighter. Taco's submittal for its Model 007 circulator lists a 1/25 horsepower, 115 volt motor at 0.74 amps. The burner and controls are extra. Generac's owner's manual wattage guide for its GP generators lists an oil burner on a furnace at 300 running watts, so a small boiler load is often a few hundred watts in total. Read your own labels rather than trusting a typical figure.

Step 2: Allow for starting

Motors draw more when they start than when they run. Generac's sizing guide calls this starting or surge wattage and says it is often two to three times the running figure. The owner's manual for its GP series says motors may need about three times their running watts to start. Generac's guide also gives the method for a whole list of loads: add all the running watts, then add the single largest starting burst, on the assumption that the motors will not all start at the same moment. In the example below, we swap the blower's running watts for its starting watts, which is the same idea with less double counting.

Step 3: Add the other things you want to run

In this example the household also wants a refrigerator and a few lights. Honda's generator wattage guide lists an Energy Star refrigerator or freezer at 132 to 192 running watts and 1,200 starting watts. For incandescent lamps it says to use the bulb's printed wattage. Honda warns not to simply add up everything you own, because the point is to pick what you will really run.

Worked example: one oil furnace, one refrigerator, four lamps

These numbers are hypothetical, built on the Bard D84D011HF figures above, a refrigerator at the top of Honda's running range, and four 60 watt lamps.

  1. Furnace running load: 115 V × 12.2 A = 1,403 W. The blower part is 115 × 10.5 = 1,208 W (rounded) and the burner part is 115 × 1.7 = 196 W (rounded).
  2. Refrigerator running: 192 W.
  3. Lamps: 4 × 60 W = 240 W.
  4. Total running: 1,403 + 192 + 240 = 1,835 W.
  5. Blower start: 3 × 1,208 = 3,624 W, using Generac's roughly-three-times figure.
  6. Peak at the moment the blower starts: the other loads are already running, so 1,835 − 1,208 = 627 W, and 627 + 3,624 = 4,251 W.

Now compare with two Generac ratings from owner's manuals. The GP3300i lists 2,500 W rated power and 3,300 VA surge. The GP5500E lists 5.5 kW rated and 6,875 VA surge.

  • GP3300i running: 1,835 W is under 2,500 W, so it passes (1,835 ÷ 2,500 is about 73 percent).
  • GP3300i starting: 4,251 W is over 3,300, so it fails on the start.
  • GP5500E running: 1,835 W is under 5,500 W. Starting: 4,251 W is under 6,875. Both pass.

The lesson is that the start, not the running load, usually decides the size. Also read the outlet limits. The GP manual rates the 120 V, 30 A receptacle at 3,600 W at 120 V, so have your electrician check the inlet, the circuit and the manual against the start load. Generac also says maximum output drops with altitude and heat, so treat a close pass as a fail.

Inverter or conventional: does the control board care?

Modern furnaces and boilers have electronic control boards. Generac explains the difference in power quality with total harmonic distortion (THD), a measure of how far the output waveform strays from a clean curve. Its article says power under 6 percent THD counts as clean, that standard portable generators typically run 9 to 15 percent and some reach 30, and that its inverter generators produce 3 percent or less. It warns that units over 6 percent can make sensitive electronics such as computers, televisions and phones fail early. The article does not mention furnace boards, and we did not find a furnace maker's published statement on generator power, so ask the furnace maker or your installer. Many people choose an inverter model for that reason, but check the THD number on the spec sheet, not just the word inverter.

Safe connection: transfer switch, installed by an electrician

Do not run the furnace from an extension cord strung to the generator, and do not plug the generator into a wall outlet. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission calls plugging into a wall outlet "backfeeding." It says it is extremely dangerous, can electrocute utility workers and neighbors on the same transformer, and bypasses household circuit protection.

The Missouri Public Service Commission says the only safe way to connect a portable generator to existing wiring is a transfer switch installed by a licensed electrical contractor. The New Jersey Department of Community Affairs said the same in its 2012 generator advisory, calling for a qualified, licensed electrician to install a transfer switch. Generac's GP owner's manual requires that the transfer switch isolate the generator from utility power at all times and that a qualified electrician do the installation. PSE&G adds that backfeed can start a fire or explosion, that you are legally responsible for preventing it, and that a self-installed generator may need an electrical inspection and a permit. We found no published source here that blesses a plug-in cord for a furnace, so treat it as off the table and ask your electrician.

Where the generator goes

The CDC says to use generators outdoors at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, and PSE&G gives the same 20 feet. CPSC says never run one in a garage, crawlspace or shed, and that open doors or fans do not make it safe. Generac's manual also asks for 5 feet of clearance on every side for cooling and to point the exhaust away from buildings. Put a battery-powered CO alarm in the home. The full story is in Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

Field card: Furnace or Boiler on a Generator

Size for the start, connect through a transfer switch, run it outside.

  • Watts = volts × amps from the nameplate; use running or total amps, not circuit ampacity.
  • Motors can need about three times their running watts to start (Generac).
  • Total = other running watts + the motor's starting watts. Compare both to the generator's rated and surge numbers.
  • Conventional portable units often run 9 to 15 percent THD; clean is under 6 percent (Generac).
  • Never backfeed through an outlet. Use a transfer switch installed by an electrician.
  • Outdoors, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents (CDC, PSE&G).
