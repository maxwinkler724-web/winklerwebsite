Heat Exchanger Effectiveness and the NTU Method for HVAC

Last updated: 2026-11-02

The log-mean method needs all four temperatures. Often you only know the two inlet temperatures, the flows and what the exchanger is capable of. The effectiveness and NTU method starts from exactly those and hands you both outlet temperatures. This lesson shows how it works with a plate exchanger example worked step by step.

Effectiveness: actual over maximum

Effectiveness, written with the Greek letter epsilon, is the heat an exchanger actually moves divided by the most it could possibly move. The Purdue ME 418 lecture notes and the CSUN ME 375 notes both define it this way. The most any exchanger could move is set by the stream that changes temperature the most easily, so it is C_min times the difference between the hot inlet and the cold inlet temperatures. An effectiveness of 0.54 means the unit delivers 54 percent of that ceiling.

C_min, C_max and the ratio Cr

C is the capacity rate of a stream: mass flow times specific heat. For water in gpm, the familiar shortcut is C = 500 x gpm in Btu/h-F (see the HVAC heat transfer formulas lesson). The smaller of the two streams is C_min and the larger is C_max. The ratio Cr = C_min / C_max always falls between 0 and 1. The stream with the smaller C is the one whose temperature swings more, which is why it sets the ceiling.

NTU: how big the exchanger is for the flow

NTU, the number of transfer units, is UA / C_min, as both university notes state. UA is the overall coefficient times the surface area, which is covered in the U and fouling lesson. NTU is dimensionless. A larger NTU means more surface, or higher U, relative to how much heat the weaker stream can carry. More NTU raises effectiveness, but with diminishing returns, as the chart below shows.

The counterflow formula

For a counterflow exchanger the CSUN notes give

effectiveness = (1 - e^(-NTU(1 - Cr))) / (1 - Cr x e^(-NTU(1 - Cr)))

When Cr equals 1 that expression cannot be evaluated directly. Taking the limit (our own algebra) gives effectiveness = NTU / (1 + NTU). Both forms are plotted in the figure. For the other layouts, such as cross-flow in an air coil, the equations differ, so check the source for your geometry. The air-to-air case is in the fixed-plate exchanger lesson.

Why use it: outlet temperatures unknown

Both the log-mean method and this one come from the same energy balance. The log-mean lesson works well when you know the outlets and want the heat or the area. The CSUN notes point out that the NTU method works from the inlet temperatures and UA to find the outlets, and the Purdue notes say it is the better choice when the exchanger size and performance, meaning UA, are given. That is the rating situation: a plate exchanger or coil of known UA is asked to run at new flows, and you want to know what leaves it without trial and error.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a maker or test. Assumptions: a counterflow water-to-water plate exchanger; hot side 20 gpm entering at 140 F; cold side 30 gpm entering at 60 F; UA = 10,000 Btu/h-F; C = 500 x gpm for both sides (water-like fluids, our own assumption); UA held constant.

  1. Hot side: C = 500 x 20 = 10,000 Btu/h-F. Cold side: C = 500 x 30 = 15,000 Btu/h-F.
  2. C_min = 10,000 (hot side). C_max = 15,000 (cold side).
  3. Cr = 10,000 / 15,000 = 0.667.
  4. NTU = 10,000 / 10,000 = 1.0.
  5. The exponent: NTU x (1 - Cr) = 1.0 x 0.333 = 0.333, and e^(-0.333) = 0.7165.
  6. Effectiveness = (1 - 0.7165) / (1 - 0.667 x 0.7165) = 0.2835 / 0.5223 = 0.543.
  7. Q_max = C_min x (140 - 60) = 10,000 x 80 = 800,000 Btu/h.
  8. Q = 0.543 x 800,000 = about 434,000 Btu/h.
  9. Hot outlet = 140 - 434,000 / 10,000 = 140 - 43.4 = about 96.6 F.
  10. Cold outlet = 60 + 434,000 / 15,000 = 60 + 28.9 = about 88.9 F.

Energy balance check: hot side gives up 10,000 x (140 - 96.6) = 434,000 Btu/h; the cold side gains 15,000 x (88.9 - 60) = 433,500 Btu/h from the rounded outlet, which matches within rounding. Direction check: the hot stream cools, the cold stream warms, and the hot outlet (96.6 F) is above the cold inlet (60 F) and the cold outlet (88.9 F) stays below the hot inlet (140 F), so no temperature cross is violated. In a counterflow unit the cold outlet can pass the hot outlet temperature; here it does not, since effectiveness is only 54 percent.

As a trend check, the plate fouling and approach lesson explains why UA drops over time. If fouling cut UA to 5,000, NTU would fall to 0.5 and the effectiveness would drop, so the outlets would move toward the inlets.

When one side changes phase

If steam or a refrigerant condenses (or boils) on one side, its temperature stays nearly constant while it gives up heat, so its capacity rate behaves as infinite. Then C_min is the other stream and Cr = 0. The CSUN notes give effectiveness = 1 - e^(-NTU) for this case, for any exchanger arrangement. At NTU = 1.0 that is 1 - e^(-1) = 1 - 0.3679 = 0.632, or 63.2 percent.

Our own example: assume saturated steam condensing at 212 F heats 30 gpm of water entering at 60 F, UA = 15,000 Btu/h-F. C_min = 500 x 30 = 15,000, so NTU = 1.0 and effectiveness = 63.2 percent. Q_max = 15,000 x (212 - 60) = 2,280,000 Btu/h; Q = 0.632 x 2,280,000 = about 1,441,000 Btu/h. The water leaves at 60 + 0.632 x 152 = about 156.1 F. Check: 15,000 x 96.1 = about 1,441,000 Btu/h. The steam side has no outlet temperature change, so there is only one outlet to find. See the steam converter lesson for the hardware side.

Reading the curves

At every NTU, a lower Cr gives higher effectiveness, and the Cr = 0 curve is the top one. With NTU of 5 a Cr = 1 counterflow unit reaches only 0.83 while a Cr = 0 unit reaches 0.99. At Cr = 0 the gain from NTU 3 to NTU 5 is only 0.95 to 0.99, so past roughly NTU 3 extra surface buys little when one side changes phase. Because the curves flatten, a dirty exchanger that loses some UA may lose less capacity than you expect when NTU is already high, and more when NTU is low.

Field card: Effectiveness and NTU

Know the inlets and UA? Find the outlets with NTU.

  • Effectiveness = actual heat / (C_min x (hot in - cold in)).
  • C = flow x specific heat; water: 500 x gpm in Btu/h-F.
  • NTU = UA / C_min. Cr = C_min / C_max.
  • Counterflow: (1 - e^(-NTU(1-Cr))) / (1 - Cr x e^(-NTU(1-Cr))); Cr = 1 gives NTU / (1 + NTU).
  • Phase change on one side: Cr = 0, effectiveness = 1 - e^(-NTU); NTU 1.0 gives 63.2 percent.
  • Our example: C_min 10,000, C_max 15,000, NTU 1.0, effectiveness 0.543, Q about 434,000 Btu/h, outlets 96.6 F hot and 88.9 F cold.
  • Always run the energy balance on both sides as a check.
