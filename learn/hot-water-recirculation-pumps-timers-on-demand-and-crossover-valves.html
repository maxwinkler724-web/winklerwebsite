Hot Water Recirculation Pumps: Timers, On-Demand and Crossover Valves

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You turn on the kitchen tap and wait while good water goes down the drain. A recirculation pump is the usual fix for a long wait, but there are two very different kinds, four ways to control them, and some limits you should know before you sell or install one. This lesson explains each in plain terms and does the water math for a real pipe run.

Why the wait happens

After a tap is shut, the hot water left in the pipe cools to room temperature. The next time someone opens that tap, the cooled water in the pipe has to be pushed out before warm water from the heater reaches the fixture. So the wait depends on how much water the pipe holds, which means pipe length and diameter, and on how fast the fixture flows. ENERGY STAR describes the same thing: in a conventional system, the cold water sitting in the pipes between the heater and the tap is sent down the drain before hot water arrives.

A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory pilot study measured five showers in three California homes. On average about 30 percent of the shower water (2.9 of 12.3 gallons) and about 41 percent of the hot water energy went to the wait and adjustment period before the shower was usable. It was a small sample, and the author notes some data had to be filled in, so treat it as a picture of the problem, not a national figure. The study did not look at recirculation systems.

Two kinds of system

Dedicated return line. A second pipe carries water from the far end of the hot line back to the water heater, and a small pump keeps it moving. This is the layout most often built into new homes and larger buildings. A well-designed system keeps water volume low: the EPA WaterSense new-home guidance suggests a target of 0.5 gallon or less between the heater and each end use.

Under-sink crossover (comfort) valve. Many existing homes have no return pipe. The kits sold for those homes put a pump at the water heater and a temperature-sensing valve under the fixture farthest away. When the hot line cools, the valve lets cooled hot water cross into the cold line, and the cold line carries it back to the heater. ENERGY STAR describes this as an integrated loop that briefly warms the cold line. Watts says its version needs no return line and no electricity under the sink because the pump sits at the heater.

Four ways to control the pump

  • Continuous. The pump never stops. Water is always hot at the tap, but the pump uses power and the circulating pipe loses heat all day. ENERGY STAR warns that a continuously running system can use more energy than it saves. A Fine Homebuilding article calls continuous pumping wasteful for the same reasons.
  • Timer. The pump runs on a set schedule, such as before the morning showers. Grundfos's UP15 instructions suggest running from about 30 minutes before the first shower until 15 minutes after the last. The weak spot is unpredictable use: outside the timer window you wait as before, and inside it you pay for heat loss whether anyone draws water or not. The Grundfos timer also loses its settings in a power outage and must be reset.
  • Temperature (aquastat) control. A sensor starts the pump when the loop cools and stops it when the water is warm again. Bell & Gossett's kit uses a temperature sensor in the under-sink valve and a dial on the pump for the target.
  • On-demand. A push button, motion sensor or flow sensor starts the pump, and a temperature sensor stops it. ENERGY STAR names push button, timer or motion sensor as ways to trigger a demand system. The Fine Homebuilding article recommends this type and says the pump typically runs 20 to 45 seconds per draw.

The DOE building science site recommends demand-initiated control as the best way to run a recirculation pump. A national-lab report on multifamily buildings found that demand control cut pump runtime to about 14 minutes a day, though that is a large apartment building, not a house.

What the makers say

  • Grundfos. The UP15 instructions have you mount the thermal bypass valve at the fixture with the longest run and say plainly: "this is not an anti-scald device." They note that warm water may remain in the cold line under the sink and that it clears quickly once the cold tap is opened. They also state a maximum water temperature of 150°F for the pump. The sheet I read addresses a standard storage heater only.
  • Watts Premier. The product page says the system "is not designed for tankless water heaters" and offers a 24-hour timer that can run the pump only when needed or continuously.
  • Taco. The SmartPlus 006 instructions show a flow check valve on the return line, installed with its arrow pointing away from the hot water source. Its Pulse mode runs the pump 1 to 10 minutes and then holds it off for 10 minutes, and Smart mode learns your use over a week.
  • Bell & Gossett. The Ecocirc wireless kit blends cold water at the sink to deliver warm water, with the target set between 86 and 95°F. It warns that water above 125°F can scald and says to install water hammer arrestors to cut noise.

Rinnai's guide for tankless units shows how a maker handles it: RUR models have an integrated pump and crossover valve, but that internal pump runs on time intervals only, not water temperature. A dedicated return line allows temperature control. Follow the heater maker's manual before adding any pump to a tankless unit, and see Tankless Gas Water Heater Problems.

The lukewarm cold tap

The main customer complaint with a crossover valve is that the cold tap runs warm at first. That is how the design works: it moves cooled hot water into the cold line. Grundfos says it dissipates quickly, and ENERGY STAR says the cold line returns to its usual temperature soon after. A customer who wants ice-cold water on the first draw may not like it, so say so before installing, and consider a return line in new work.

Safety and Legionella

A warm cold line is also worth thinking about. CDC's Legionella toolkit for building water systems says Legionella can grow in water between 77 and 113°F, can grow as low as 68°F, and recommends keeping stored hot water above 140°F and circulating hot water at or above 120°F. That toolkit is written for building water management programs, not houses with crossover valves, and I did not find a source that rates the risk of home crossover systems. For what a safe water heater setting looks like, read Water Heater Temperature Setting.

Worked example: water wasted waiting

The Copper Development Association table lists the water volume of Type L copper tube: 0.0121 gallon per foot for 1/2 inch and 0.0251 gallon per foot for 3/4 inch. Suppose a kitchen sits at the end of 50 feet of 3/4 inch Type L copper.

  1. Water in the pipe: 50 ft × 0.0251 gal/ft = 1.255 gallons, about 1.26.
  2. Use an assumed four waits a day (my number, not a measured one): 4 × 1.255 = 5.02 gallons per day.
  3. Per year: 5.02 × 365 = 1,832 gallons.
  4. Time to clear it at an assumed flow of 1.5 gallons per minute: 1.255 ÷ 1.5 = 0.84 minute, about 50 seconds.

The same 50 feet in 1/2 inch tube holds 50 × 0.0121 = 0.605 gallon, so smaller pipe wastes less. These are upper-end figures, because the water is not all cold and some of it is used anyway, for example when you fill a pot after it warms. A recirculation pump does not erase the pipe heat loss; it trades wasted water for pump power and standing loss. That is why demand control matters.
