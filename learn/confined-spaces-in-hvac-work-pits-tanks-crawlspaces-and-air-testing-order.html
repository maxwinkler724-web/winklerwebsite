Confined Spaces in HVAC Work: Pits, Tanks, Crawlspaces and Air Testing Order

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some HVAC jobs put you in a space you can climb into but cannot easily climb out of: a boiler firebox, a chiller pit, a vault, a big plenum. This lesson covers how OSHA sorts those spaces, why the air inside can kill without warning, and the order in which the air gets tested.

Check the rules where you work

This is general education. Your employer's written program, your site rules and your state's OSHA plan decide what you must do. Never enter a space on the strength of a web page.

Confined space versus permit space

OSHA's general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.146, says a space is a confined space when it meets three tests at once: a person can bodily enter and do work, entry or exit is limited or restricted, and the space was not designed for people to occupy continuously. OSHA's confined spaces page lists tanks, vessels, silos, vaults, pits, manholes, tunnels and pipelines as examples.

A permit-required confined space (permit space) is a confined space with at least one more problem: it has or could have a hazardous atmosphere, it holds material that could engulf an entrant, its shape could trap or smother a person (walls that slope inward, a floor that tapers), or it has any other recognized serious hazard. The OSHA page adds unguarded machinery, exposed live wires and heat stress as examples of other hazards.

Construction work uses a separate rule, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart AA. Section 1926.1201 says it covers construction activities at a worksite with one or more confined spaces, and its note lists tanks, sewers, silos, boilers and manholes as examples. Where another OSHA standard also addresses a hazard, the employer must meet both. In construction, 1926.1203(a) says a competent person has to identify the confined spaces at a site and decide which are permit spaces, with testing as needed. Which of the two rules applies to your job depends on the work, so ask your employer.

Where HVAC techs meet them

Whether a given space qualifies is decided by those tests, not by its name. Our reading of the tests: a boiler fireside or firebox, a tank or vessel, a below-grade vault or sump, and a pit holding a chiller usually look like confined spaces, and a large duct or plenum can qualify if a person can enter and the opening is tight. A crawlspace is a judgment call. A tall, open, well-lit one with two exits may fail the "limited entry" test, while a low one with a single hatch may not. Have a competent person classify it, and do not guess. For general crawlspace conditions see Crawl Space Ducts and HVAC.

What counts as a hazardous atmosphere

Under 1910.146, an atmosphere is hazardous if it may cause death, incapacitation, impaired self-rescue, injury or acute illness because of any of these:

  • flammable gas, vapor or mist above 10 percent of its lower flammable limit (LFL);
  • oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent by volume;
  • a toxic substance above its permissible exposure limit or dose; or
  • any other condition immediately dangerous to life or health.

So the working range for oxygen is 19.5 to 23.5 percent, and flammables must stay at or below 10 percent of the LFL. Normal air is about 20.9 percent oxygen. Those two figures are the ones to remember.

The testing order

OSHA's rule says to test for oxygen first, then combustible gases and vapors, then toxic gases and vapors (1910.146(d)(5)(iii); the construction rule says the same in 1926.1204(e)(3)). Follow that order every time, even when you expect a different hazard. OSHA also says testing happens before entry and the space is monitored during entry, and its 1926.1203(e)(2) alternate-procedure text calls for a calibrated direct-reading instrument. OSHA's 2013 bulletin on portable gas monitors covers calibration and function checks, so confirm your meter is checked before use.

Why gas leaks starve the air of oxygen

Air does not "get used up" by a leak. Another gas moves in and pushes some air out. The U.S. Chemical Safety Board's nitrogen bulletin says nitrogen is not a poison in the usual sense; it is a hazard when it displaces oxygen. It also says people cannot smell it and get no warning that oxygen is low, and it lists effects: impaired judgment and coordination near 16 percent, nausea and very poor judgment near 12.5 percent, and unconsciousness and death below 10 percent. For HVAC work this matters for nitrogen used for pressure testing and purging, and the same displacement logic applies to a leaking refrigerant in a closed pit. The CSB page we read covers nitrogen; for refrigerants see Refrigerant Safety.

Worked example: a pit and a blower

Our own arithmetic for a pit that measures 10 ft long, 8 ft wide and 6 ft deep, with a 500 cfm blower. Cfm means cubic feet per minute.

  1. Volume. Our own: 10 ft x 8 ft x 6 ft = 480 cubic feet.
  2. Air changes per hour. Our own: 500 cu ft/min x 60 min/hr = 30,000 cu ft/hr. Then 30,000 / 480 = 62.5 air changes per hour.
  3. One air change takes. Our own: 480 / 500 = 0.96 minute, about 58 seconds.
  4. How much air was displaced? Suppose the meter reads 20.9 percent and then falls to 19.0 percent. Our own: (20.9 - 19.0) / 20.9 = 1.9 / 20.9 = 0.091, about 9 percent. Roughly 9 percent of the air has been replaced by gas with no oxygen in it.
  5. Compare to the limit. 19.0 is below 19.5, so OSHA's definition makes this a hazardous atmosphere. Our own: the drop to 19.5 needs only (20.9 - 19.5) / 20.9 = 1.4 / 20.9, about 6.7 percent displacement.

Two cautions. The blower figure is a stated number, not a promise; real air does not mix perfectly, and dead corners and low spots can stay stale. A blower is never a reason to skip the test. The displacement figure also assumes the intruding gas holds no oxygen, as nitrogen does.

Attendant, entrants and rescue

For a permit space, 1910.146 describes an attendant who stays outside until relieved, keeps an accurate count of entrants, communicates with them, monitors conditions, and orders evacuation if something changes. The attendant calls rescue as soon as entrants may need help and does no other job that interferes. Rescue is planned in advance: employers evaluate whether a rescue team can respond in time, and entrants generally wear a harness with a retrieval line, with a mechanical retrieval device for vertical spaces deeper than 5 feet. The CSB bulletin reports that about 10 percent of the fatalities in its data came from attempts to rescue someone, which is why untrained rescuers must stay out.

Ventilation

OSHA's publication on confined spaces says that where the only hazard is an atmosphere that continuous forced air ventilation can make safe, an employer may be exempt from some requirements such as permits and attendants, but must still test before entry, ventilate continuously and verify the readings. Construction's 1926.1203(e) sets conditions, including a clean air source and a way for entrants to exit if ventilation stops. Ventilation is a control, not a replacement for testing.

Related practice: before working on equipment inside, isolate energy as in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead; a fuel-fired space also brings the hazards in Combustion and CO Safety Basics; and hot spaces add heat stress.

Field card: Confined Spaces in HVAC Work

Classify first, test oxygen first, never enter to rescue.

  • Confined: can enter, restricted entry or exit, not made for continuous occupancy.
  • Permit space: confined plus an atmosphere, engulfment, trapping shape or other serious hazard.
  • Test order: oxygen, then flammables, then toxics.
  • Oxygen 19.5 to 23.5 percent; flammables at or under 10 percent of LFL.
  • Displacing gas (nitrogen, a leaked refrigerant) gives no smell and no warning.
  • Attendant stays outside; rescue is planned before entry.
  • Ventilation helps; it does not replace testing.
