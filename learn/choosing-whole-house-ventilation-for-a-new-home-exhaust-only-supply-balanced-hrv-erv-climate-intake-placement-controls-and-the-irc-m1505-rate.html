Choosing Whole-House Ventilation for a New Home: Exhaust-Only, Supply, Balanced (HRV/ERV), Intake Placement, Controls and the IRC M1505 Rate

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A tight new house needs a fan to bring in outdoor air, and the first design choice is which kind of fan system. This lesson compares exhaust-only, supply-only and balanced (HRV or ERV) systems, where the intake and exhaust go, how controls and run time work, and how the model residential code sets the minimum rate.

Where this fits

Our lesson on infiltration and ventilation in a new-home load calculation explains why tight houses need mechanical air and what that air adds to the load, so we do not repeat it. Our ERV vs. HRV lesson covers what each recovers and how to maintain them. This page is about choosing between system types and the code minimum.

The three strategies

The U.S. Department of Energy describes whole-house systems as exhaust-only (fresh air leaks in), supply-only (stale air leaks out) or balanced (both an exhaust and a fresh-air intake). The Building America Solution Center, run by a national lab for DOE, lists the pros and cons of each.

Exhaust-only

The Building America Solution Center says exhaust-only has low equipment and operating cost, uses very little energy (it lists 5 to 12 watts for 50 to 80 cfm), is easy to test and works with ductless heating and cooling. The downsides: it may depressurize the home, offers no energy recovery, is easy for an occupant to switch off, and draws outdoor air through unfiltered leaks. It works well in cold, dry climates and should be used with caution in humid ones. The same source warns that depressurizing a house can interfere with natural-draft combustion appliances, pull in garage air and draw in radon. Our combustion appliance zone lesson covers the testing. It also advises picking fans by their rating at about 0.25 inch of water column, since many ratings are given at 0.1 inch and real duct runs add resistance.

Supply-only, including a fresh-air duct to the air handler

The common form is central fan integrated supply: a duct carries outdoor air to the return side of the air handler, which filters, tempers and distributes it. The Solution Center lists these pros: air is filtered, comes from a known location and maintenance is low. The cons: it is hard to commission, the intake airflow is low, and a leaky duct system raises heating and cooling penalties. It pressurizes the house, which the source says can help keep wall cavities drier in humid climates and is a poor choice in cold, dry climates where indoor air is more humid. Supplemental dehumidification is often necessary in humid zones (IECC 1A, 2A and 3A). Its suggested duct leakage target is under 10 cfm at 50 pascals. A county summary of the 2024 International Residential Code adds that outdoor air intakes need dampers that close when the air handler is not running. Duct leakage matters here, so see our new-home rough-in lesson for pre-drywall tests.

Balanced (HRV or ERV)

A balanced system moves supply and exhaust air at about equal rates, so the house is neither pressurized nor depressurized, and it recovers heat (and with an ERV, moisture) from the outgoing air. The Solution Center says most ERVs recover about 70% to 80% of the sensible energy in the exiting air. The cons it lists are higher cost, more maintenance (regular filter changes), noise, and a harder install and commissioning. It also describes an intermittently balanced setup: a supply fan and an exhaust fan run together at similar flows, without a heat exchanger.

Choosing by climate and tightness

  • Cold, dry climate: exhaust-only is workable; supply-only is a poor fit; balanced is the choice when recovery matters.
  • Hot, humid climate: supply can keep wall cavities drier but usually needs dehumidification; use exhaust-only with caution.
  • Very tight house: with fewer leaks to make up the air, a given exhaust flow depressurizes it more (see the example below). Balanced avoids that.
  • Natural-draft appliances: avoid depressurizing. Best practice per the Solution Center is to replace them with sealed-combustion or non-combustion units.

The Solution Center notes the IRC requires an air leakage of 5 ACH50 or less together with mechanical ventilation, and that homes with natural air change rates under 0.35 ACH benefit from added ventilation. EPA likewise says tight enclosures may need supplemental ventilation supply, particularly with fuel-burning appliances. Confirm the edition and local amendments your jurisdiction adopted.

Where the intake and exhaust go

The Solution Center says outdoor air intakes should be at least 10 feet from the nearest exhaust or sanitary vent termination, and suggests an intake screen with mesh no larger than 0.5 inch, accessible for cleaning. The code pages we read do not give separation distances, so check the adopted code and the equipment instructions. DOE says kitchen and bathroom vents should lead directly outside and never vent into the attic. Our bathroom exhaust fan lesson covers ducting.

Controls and run time

The Oregon residential code bulletin calls for controls that allow manual override, and says a motorized damper can control run time. The code summaries we read say the system runs continuously unless its controls run it at least 25% of each 4-hour period, and the net result must still meet the table. The Solution Center says cycling can save energy and gives an example: a dwelling needing 50 cfm continuous could use 150 cfm for 20 minutes each hour. For air-handler systems it says controls should account for cooling operation rather than run only during heating or cooling. Humidity or air quality sensors can close intakes in poor outdoor air, such as wildfire smoke.

The code requirement

Section M1505.4.3 of the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC) sets the continuous rate from Table M1505.4.3(1), using floor area and bedrooms, or from an equation that county and state code offices reproduce: cfm = 0.01 x floor area (sq ft) + 7.5 x (bedrooms + 1). For part-time operation the rate is multiplied by the Table M1505.4.3(2) factor for the run-time share of each 4-hour segment: 25% is 4, 33% is 3, 50% is 2, 66% is 1.5, 75% is 1.3 and 100% is 1.0. Interpolation is allowed, extrapolation is not. We read the table values in an Oregon bulletin on its 2021 residential code and a county summary of the 2024 IRC; the values match. Oregon's own rule requires a balanced system within about 10%, while the county summaries say supply, exhaust or a combination are allowed, with exhaust-only needing care around natural-draft appliances. We did not read the IRC text itself, so check your adopted edition.

Our own worked example: 2,400 sq ft, 4-bedroom new home

This is a made-up home. Our assumptions: 2,400 sq ft of floor area, 4 bedrooms, 8 ft ceilings (19,200 cu ft), 3 ACH50 airtightness, a leakage exponent of 0.65, and a single-leak model. Real houses leak unevenly.

Equation: 0.01 x 2,400 = 24; 7.5 x (4 + 1) = 37.5; 24 + 37.5 = 61.5 cfm.
Table M1505.4.3(1): row 1,501 to 3,000 sq ft, column 4 to 5 bedrooms = 75 cfm. We design to 75 cfm, the higher value.

Part-time operation: 75% run time: 75 x 1.3 = 97.5 cfm. 66%: 75 x 1.5 = 112.5 cfm. 50% (30 minutes each hour): 75 x 2 = 150 cfm. 33%: 75 x 3 = 225 cfm. 25%, the minimum: 75 x 4 = 300 cfm. Check at 50%: 150 cfm x 30 min = 4,500 cu ft, the same as 75 cfm x 60 min.

House pressure. CFM50 = 3 x 19,200 / 60 = 960 cfm. Pressure for a net flow Q through the leaks is 50 x (Q / 960)^(1/0.65) pascals.

Use the trend, not the digits: the same 75 cfm requirement can leave the house nearly neutral or well below outdoor pressure, depending on the system and how long the fan runs.

Field card: Choosing whole-house ventilation

Pick the strategy first, then size the fan to the code.

  • Exhaust-only: simple and cheap; depressurizes; watch combustion appliances.
  • Supply or air-handler fresh-air duct: filtered; pressurizes; needs a closing damper and tight ducts.
  • Balanced: near-neutral pressure with recovery; more cost and upkeep.
  • Intake at least 10 ft from exhaust and sanitary vents (Building America).
  • IRC M1505.4.3: 0.01 x area + 7.5 x (bedrooms + 1), or the table. Our example: 75 cfm continuous, 150 cfm at 50% run time.
