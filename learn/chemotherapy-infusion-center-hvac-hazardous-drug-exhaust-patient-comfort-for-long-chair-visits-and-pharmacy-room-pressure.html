Chemotherapy Infusion Center HVAC: Hazardous Drug Exhaust, Patient Comfort and Pharmacy Room Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An outpatient infusion center has two jobs for the air. Up front, people sit in recliners for hours, often chilled and tired, and they will tell you about every draft. In back, a small pharmacy room and a soiled utility room handle drugs and waste that must not drift into the chairs. This lesson sorts out what a facility tech can check and what belongs to the designer, the pharmacy and the infection-control team.

Check the rules that apply to your building

Check the rules that apply to your building. This page is general education from public pages read in 2026. The adopted ventilation standard, the health-facility guidelines and the pharmacy rules are set by your state and the authority having jurisdiction. Never change pressure, exhaust or filters in a drug room without the facility's written procedure and its pharmacy and safety staff.

The chair area: a long, still visit

A published addendum to ASHRAE Standard 170-2021 (addendum h) lists a "cancer treatment area" in its outpatient table. As we read it, that row shows no pressure requirement, 2 air changes per hour (ACH) of outdoor air, 6 ACH total, a design temperature of 70 to 75 F and MERV-8 filtration. We read an addendum, not the adopted edition, so confirm the numbers your state enforces. The same table has no row for the nurse station.

Why do chairs run complaints? Our own shop-floor view, not a sourced fact: patients barely move, many are older, and some feel cold during treatment, so a diffuser that drops cool air onto a recliner is felt right away. There is also a clinical reason to take cold complaints seriously. A nursing-society page on one chemotherapy drug, oxaliplatin, advises patients to avoid cold drinks even during the infusion and not to breathe deeply in cold air, because cold can trigger throat symptoms quickly. That is why staff may ask for room-temperature drinks and an extra blanket, and why a cold bay is not just a comfort issue.

Rules of thumb for the tech, all our own: ask which chairs draw complaints; check that diffusers are not aimed at a chair and that nothing blocks them; confirm the room sensor is not sitting in a warm spot by the nurse station; and fix the two cold chairs with diffuser patterns and blankets before you warm the whole bay. Older patients are often the coldest, and our adult day center lesson covers draft and setpoint habits that carry over.

The hazardous drug room

Chemotherapy drugs are mixed in a pharmacy room built to contain them. We do not repeat the room layout, ISO classes and air change numbers here; see the compounding pharmacy lesson. The short version from OSHA's hazardous drug guidance, which cites USP General Chapter 800 (named in plain text, not linked): the containment room should hold a negative pressure of 0.01 to 0.03 inch water column relative to all adjacent areas, and the hood for sterile hazardous drugs should be vented to the outside. Our own conversion: 0.01 inch is about 2.5 pascals, so a propped door erases it.

The NIOSH guide for healthcare settings (publication 2023-130) adds the plumbing-and-duct points that matter to us. It prefers a HEPA filter on the exhaust with all of the filtered air sent outside, and says to make sure air exhausted outside is not pulled back into the building. It also calls for uninterrupted power for the ventilation that holds negative pressure, and a continuous airflow monitor on each ventilated cabinet. OSHA's guidance notes that some drugs can pass through HEPA filters, which is one reason outdoor exhaust matters.

Keeping the exhaust away from intakes

CDC's 2003 environmental infection control guideline notes in a table footnote that exhaust outlets should be more than 25 feet from air intakes, and that the bottom of an outdoor air intake should be 6 feet above ground or 3 feet above a roof. The guideline says AIA guidance was superseded by FGI, so the number in force is the one your code and the design documents set. Our outdoor air intake lesson shows how plume and wind decide the real answer.

Soiled utility and spill areas

The same ASHRAE addendum lists a "soiled workroom or soiled holding" row as negative, 2 ACH outdoor air, 6 ACH total, with all air exhausted to the outdoors, 72 to 78 F and MERV-8. Treatment-area patients may leave drug-contaminated urine, vomit and other excreta behind. OSHA's hospital eTool says staff who handle urine from patients treated with hazardous drugs in the previous 48 hours need PPE, and NIOSH suggests closing the toilet lid or using a plastic-backed absorbent pad when flushing. For the tech: toilet and soiled utility exhaust must keep running, a belt or damper problem is a pressure problem, and a spill kit and procedure belong at the site. NIOSH says facilities should write spill procedures and hold drills, so report a spill to staff and wait for their cleanup before you enter.

Worked example: bay air changes and a pressure check

All inputs are our own assumptions, not from a standard or a real building.

  1. Assumption: the infusion bay is 1,800 sq ft with a 9 ft ceiling. Volume = 1,800 x 9 = 16,200 cu ft.
  2. Using the 6 ACH total from the addendum row: cfm = ACH x volume / 60 = 6 x 16,200 / 60 = 1,620 cfm. Outdoor air at 2 ACH = 2 x 16,200 / 60 = 540 cfm.
  3. Assumption: the soiled utility room is 10 x 10 x 9 ft = 900 cu ft. At 6 ACH, exhaust = 6 x 900 / 60 = 90 cfm.
  4. Pressure check. Assumptions: leakage is only the door undercut, 36 in wide by 0.75 in high = 27 sq in = 0.1875 sq ft, and the target is 0.02 inch water column (our pick; the addendum gives no number). We use the orifice relation Q = 4,005 x Cd x A x sqrt(dP) with a discharge coefficient Cd of 0.65 (our assumption), or about 2,600 x A x sqrt(dP).
  5. Leak flow = 2,600 x 0.1875 x sqrt(0.02) = 2,600 x 0.1875 x 0.1414 = about 69 cfm.
  6. So the room must exhaust about 69 cfm more than it receives. With 90 cfm of exhaust, supply would need to be about 90 - 69 = 21 cfm. At 0.01 inch the leak is about 49 cfm.

Lesson: a small undercut takes most of a small exhaust flow, so closing the door gap or adding a door sweep changes the pressure much more than people expect, and a stalled exhaust fan drops it to zero. Confirm real numbers with the balancing report.

Filter and fan work on drug exhaust

Treat anything on the drug room exhaust or its cabinet as contaminated until staff say otherwise. NIOSH says work on a ventilated cabinet should be done by trained, qualified people following procedures, that the cabinet should be cleared of drugs and decontaminated before maintenance starts, and that used filter media should not be broken, crushed, torn or distorted. OSHA's guidance says technicians who service cabinets or change HEPA filters should wear the same PPE recommended for large spills, that ventilation and exhaust systems should be certified at least every six months, and that cabinets be re-certified after a move or repair. NIOSH adds that cabinets be field-certified after installation, relocation, internal repairs and HEPA filter replacement, and every six months after.

Plan it this way: get the written procedure; confirm lockout and that the exhaust path is isolated; wear the gloves, gown, eye and face protection and respirator the procedure names; bag the used filter in the facility's hazardous waste method without bending it.

What to leave to others

You do not set the pressure target, change a cabinet, bypass a monitor or approve a new exhaust location. You do keep the fans, belts, dampers, controls and filters in the schedule, report alarms and write down what you find. For the pressure basics, see our negative and positive pressure rooms lesson.

Field card: infusion center HVAC

Warm, still patients in front; contained drugs in back.

  • Chair area addendum row: 6 ACH total, 2 ACH outdoor air, 70 to 75 F, MERV-8.
  • Cold chairs: check diffusers and sensor location first, blankets second, setpoint last.
  • Drug room: negative 0.01 to 0.03 in. wc, hood vented outdoors.
  • Soiled utility: negative, all air exhausted outdoors, 6 ACH total.
  • Keep exhaust more than 25 ft from intakes (CDC footnote); confirm the code value.
  • Filter work: written procedure, spill-level PPE, bag without bending.
