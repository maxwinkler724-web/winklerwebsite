Evaporative Condensers in Refrigeration: How They Work, Wet Bulb Capacity, Water Treatment and Winter Operation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk onto the roof of a cold-storage building or a large food plant and you will often see a big steel box with a fan on top and a mist of water inside. That box is usually an evaporative condenser. It does the job of a condenser, a cooling tower and a pump in one casing. This lesson explains how it works, why the outdoor wet bulb temperature sets its capacity, how to turn a maker's table into a number, and what water treatment and winter operation look like when the maker's manual is the guide.

Check the maker's literature

Check the maker's literature. Numbers here come from published maker documents and public pages we read in 2026. Every model has its own ratings, limits and instructions. Use the manual for the unit in front of you and your site's written procedures.

What is inside the box

An evaporative condenser puts the refrigerant coil inside the same casing as the water system. Hot refrigerant gas from the compressors flows through the coil. Pumped water is sprayed over the outside of the coil, and a fan moves air through the casing. An ACHR News article by an EVAPCO product manager calls the coil the heart of the evaporative condenser and describes the other parts: spray headers that distribute the water, a fan (forced draft or induced draft), air inlet louvers, and a sump or basin that collects the water for recirculation. EVAPCO's operation and maintenance manual states the idea simply: units reject heat by evaporating a portion of the recirculated water into the airstream. The CDC adds that high-efficiency drift eliminators should be used. Drift eliminators sit in the air path above the spray and catch droplets so they do not leave with the air.

How it differs from a tower plus a water-cooled condenser

Our own plain-language comparison: with a cooling tower and a water-cooled condenser, refrigerant gives up heat to a loop of water inside a shell-and-tube or similar condenser, and a separate tower then rejects that heat to air. That is two heat exchangers and a condenser water loop with its own pump. In an evaporative condenser the refrigerant coil sits directly in the spray, so there is one casing and one set of heat transfer steps. EVAPCO's manual draws a related line between its two product types: closed circuit coolers cool a process fluid in a coil, while evaporative condensers condense refrigerant. For where this fits in a plant, see our ammonia refrigeration lesson and the supermarket rack lesson.

Why wet bulb sets the capacity

The coil is wet, so the cooling comes from evaporation, and evaporation is limited by how much moisture the air can still take. That is why the entering wet bulb matters more than the dry bulb. A U.S. DOE Building America guideline on residential evaporative condensers says refrigerant temperatures more closely follow outdoor wet bulb than dry bulb, and that air-cooled units typically run 15°F to 25°F above outdoor dry bulb. The ACHR News article puts two rules in plain words: the condensing temperature can never be lower than the wet bulb temperature, and the closer the two are, the larger the condenser has to be. It also says that a 2°F rise in wet bulb can reduce performance by 16 percent, and that a rise of 5°F to 6°F can cut capacity by 50 percent.

Reading a maker's capacity factor

BAC's evaporative condenser engineering data (publication E101) rates its models at a base condition. For ammonia (R-717) the model number denotes capacity in evaporator tons at a 96.3°F condensing temperature, 20°F suction and 78°F entering wet bulb. The selection steps are: find total heat rejection (evaporator capacity in MBH plus compressor BHP times 2.545), look up a capacity factor for the design condensing temperature and entering wet bulb, multiply, then choose a unit whose base heat rejection equals or exceeds the result. Here is the 95°F condensing row of BAC's Table 6 for R-717 at a few wet bulbs:

The factor rises with wet bulb. A higher wet bulb means the same load needs a bigger unit, so the required rating is multiplied up. A different maker, refrigerant or product line has its own table.

Worked example

We use BAC's own selection example (8,127 MBH, 95°F condensing, 76°F wet bulb, factor 1.35, selected model with a base heat rejection of 11,266 MBH) and then ask what that same unit can carry at a lower wet bulb. Steps marked Our own are our arithmetic.

  1. Required heat rejection, from BAC's example. 550 TR × 12 = 6,600 MBH, plus 600 BHP × 2.545 = 1,527 MBH, total 8,127 MBH.
  2. Selection check, from BAC. 8,127 × 1.35 = 10,972 MBH, and the 11,266 MBH base rating covers it.
  3. Our own: heat the unit can carry at 76°F wet bulb. 11,266 ÷ 1.35 = 8,345 MBH.
  4. Our own: heat it can carry at 70°F wet bulb. The Table 6 factor at 95°F and 70°F is 1.08, so 11,266 ÷ 1.08 = 10,431 MBH. That is 1.35 ÷ 1.08 = 1.25 times as much, or 25 percent more, because the air is drier.
  5. Evaporation rate. A Colorado water conservation toolkit page (The Brendle Group) gives a rule of thumb of 3 gpm of evaporation per 100 tons of cooling load, one ton being 12,000 Btu/h. It is written for cooling towers, so applying it to a condenser is Our own estimate.
  6. Our own: convert the 8,345 MBH. 8,345 ÷ 12 = 695 tons of heat rejection. Evaporation = 695 ÷ 100 × 3 = 20.9 gpm. The toolkit rule is stated per ton of cooling load, and heat rejection tons run higher, so our own view is that this figure leans high.
  7. Our own: bleed at 4 cycles. Bleed = evaporation ÷ (cycles − 1) = 20.9 ÷ 3 = 7.0 gpm, about 417 gallons per hour (7.0 × 60).
  8. Our own: makeup. Evaporation plus bleed = 20.9 + 7.0 = 27.9 gpm, ignoring drift and assuming full load.

Real loads, wet bulbs and drift change these numbers hourly. The point is the method. See our cycles of concentration lesson for the formula in more detail.

Water treatment, bleed and scale

Evaporation leaves minerals behind, so the sump water gets harder and saltier unless some is bled off. EVAPCO's manual says units need a bleed line, recommends an automated conductivity controller, and says a qualified water treatment company should design a site-specific program. It lists target ranges by coil material. For galvanized steel, for example, it gives pH of 7.0 to 8.8 and conductivity under 2,400 micromhos/cm. It does not recommend ongoing acid use. It warns about white rust on new galvanized units and says soft water is not appropriate for galvanized parts. An IIAR tech tip by an EVAPCO author calls scale perhaps the number one reason condensers lose efficiency over time, says pH is the most important water chemistry check, and says water quality is managed mainly through the bleed line. EVAPCO also says scale on inlet louvers can come from poor treatment, too little bleed, excessive fan or pump cycling, or high solids. Our condenser water treatment lesson covers the chemistry basics.

Legionella and drift

The CDC says its cooling tower guidance covers all types of cooling towers and evaporative condensers, and that an effective water management program is the primary strategy to control Legionella. It recommends high-efficiency drift eliminators, siting towers at least 25 feet from building air intakes, an offline cleaning and disinfection at least annually, and flushing dead legs at least weekly. EVAPCO's manual points to ANSI/ASHRAE 188 as the basis for a site water management program. See Cooling Tower Water Management and Legionella Awareness. Do not change chemical feeds unless that is your job.

Winter operation

Makers describe a few approaches. A remote sump in a heated space is called the simplest and most effective freeze protection in EVAPCO's manual because water drains back when the pump stops. BAC's remote sump page says it may preclude the need to winterize the equipment. Basin heaters are the other option; EVAPCO says its heaters are designed to hold 40°F water at 0°F ambient, run only when the pump is off, and do not protect the pump or external water lines, which need heat tracing and insulation. For dry operation (pump off, basin drained, fans cycling) EVAPCO says to keep the basin drain open, cycle discharge dampers open and closed once a day, limit fan starts to six per hour, and keep VFD minimum speed at 50 percent in cold weather. A 2003 ACHR News article with BAC tips adds frequent visual checks for icing. Dry operation depends on the product line, so read your manual.

Field checks

EVAPCO's schedule lists the bleed valve, basin level and float, spray pattern and makeup valve monthly, and drift eliminators and fan blades quarterly. The CDC wants site log sheets kept. Lock out power before reaching into the casing and keep the access doors and fan screens in place.

Field card: Evaporative Condensers

Wet coil, wet bulb, bleed.

  • Refrigerant coil, spray water, fan, sump and drift eliminators share one casing.
  • Capacity follows entering wet bulb; condensing temperature cannot go below it.
  • Maker method: required heat rejection × factor, then pick a base rating at or above it.
  • BAC example: 11,266 ÷ 1.35 = 8,345 MBH at 76°F; ÷ 1.08 = 10,431 MBH at 70°F (Our own).
  • Bleed = evaporation ÷ (cycles − 1): 20.9 ÷ 3 = 7.0 gpm at 4 cycles (Our own).
  • Winter: remote sump or basin heater; dry operation rules come from the manual.
  • Check bleed valve, spray, float and drift eliminators on the maker's schedule.
