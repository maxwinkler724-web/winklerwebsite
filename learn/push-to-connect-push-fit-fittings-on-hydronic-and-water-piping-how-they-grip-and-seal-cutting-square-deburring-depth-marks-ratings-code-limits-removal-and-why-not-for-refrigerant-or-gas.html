Push-to-Connect Fittings on Hydronic and Water Piping: Grip, Depth Marks, Ratings, Code Limits and Removal

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A push-to-connect (push-fit) fitting joins a tube with no flame, no flux and no press tool. That makes it the fast answer when you must fix a leaking line near a boiler. It is also easy to misuse. This lesson covers how the joint works, how to prepare the tube, where the ratings and codes limit it, and why it never belongs on refrigerant or gas.

Check the listing, the maker sheet and the local code

Check first. This page is general education, not legal advice and not a substitute for the maker sheet. Whether a fitting is allowed on your job depends on its listing, its printed ratings and the code your local inspector enforces. Read all three before you open a line.

How the joint grips and seals

In general terms, a push-fit fitting has two jobs done by two parts. A rubber seal ring, usually an O-ring, presses against the outside of the tube and holds the water in. A toothed or collet-style grip ring bites the tube wall and holds the tube from backing out under pressure. Designs differ by maker, so treat this as the simple picture in the diagram, not a spec. The tube must reach the internal stop. If it stops short, the seal may sit on the wrong part of the tube or on a scratch, and the grip ring has less to hold.

Prepare the tube: square, clean, marked

  • Cut square. A rotary tube cutter gives a square end. A hacksaw cut on an angle leaves one side of the seal ring with less tube under it.
  • Deburr inside and out. A sharp edge or burr can slice the seal ring as the tube is pushed in. Ream the inside and file or chamfer the outside edge, then wipe the tube clean. Look along the last two inches for scratches, dents, scale or old solder; a seal cannot cover those, so cut back to clean tube.
  • Mark the insertion depth. Measure the depth from the maker sheet and mark the tube with a marker or tape, as in the diagram. After the push, the mark should sit at the fitting mouth. If you can still see a long bare stretch of mark, the tube is not fully in. Do not guess the depth from a similar fitting; sizes and makers differ.
  • Push straight, then pull-test by hand. Push with a slight twist if the maker allows it, keep the tube in line with the fitting, and confirm the mark. Then pressure test the repaired line before it goes back in service, as the soldering lesson describes for solder work.

Support the pipe

A push-fit joint is not an anchor. The tube can still move inside the fitting if the line swings, sags or gets pushed by thermal growth. Put a hanger or clip on each side of the fitting, keep the run straight so side load does not pry the seal, and let long runs expand through an offset or loop. Our expansion and hanger lessons below cover the details. For scale, one university design standard for plant hot-water piping specifies adjustable clevis hangers on horizontal runs shorter than 20 feet and copper-clad hangers where a hanger touches copper pipe.

Ratings: read the fitting, not the box front

Temperature and pressure limits are printed on the fitting body, listed in its third-party listing and spelled out on the maker sheet. They are not the same for every fitting, and they often go down as the water gets hotter. We do not put a number on this page for your job because only your fitting can answer it. Compare the rating at your operating temperature, not at room temperature, against the boiler relief valve setting and the highest temperature the loop can reach. For a sense of what designers plan for, one state university design standard sets hot-water heating piping at a minimum of 150 psig at 200°F. That is a design target for large plants, not a rule for your boiler. Also check that the fitting is listed for the pipe material you have, whether copper, PEX or another tube, and that any insert or support sleeve the maker requires is installed.

For drinking-water lines, the federal lead-free definition is a weighted average of 0.25 percent lead across wetted surfaces for pipes, fittings and fixtures. A fitting that touches potable water should be marked and listed for that use. A heating loop is not potable, but a fitting on a combined or domestic line must meet that definition.

What codes say, and where they stay silent

Model codes such as the Uniform Plumbing Code and the International Plumbing Code are adopted by states and cities with amendments, so the same fitting can be fine in one town and refused in the next. Three points from the state pages we read:

  • One Midwestern state's plumbing rules, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code, say pipe, fittings and devices must be listed or labeled by a third party, and that field markings are not acceptable. They also say an alternate material or method needs technical documentation given to the authority having jurisdiction before installation.
  • The same state text allows unions in drainage piping if accessibly located, but in the part we read it had no joint-specific rule on push-fit being concealed or accessible. Other editions and local amendments may add one.
  • One Northwestern state plumbing code requires a transition connection for plastic water service piping that ends inside a building to be accessible. This is a rule for that connection, not a general push-fit rule. The same chapter says relief discharge piping must be rated for at least the system operating temperature.

So the answer to "can I bury a push-fit joint in a wall" is not on this page. Many inspectors want any mechanical joint reachable, and some makers print their own limits on concealed use. Ask the inspector and read the maker sheet before closing a wall. We make no statement about licensing rules in any state.

Temporary repair or permanent joint?

Push-fit joints are widely used for quick repairs because they go in wet-line conditions where solder will not take, and because there is no fire risk near wood framing or insulation. Whether a given joint is allowed to stay permanently comes back to the listing, the maker sheet, the code edition and the inspector. If any one of those says no, treat the fitting as temporary and mark it on the job record so someone replaces it with a soldered, brazed or pressed joint later. If all four say yes, it is a permanent joint, and it needs the same support and testing as any other joint. Because a seal ring is rubber, high heat, oil in the water or years of cycling can shorten its life; the maker sheet is the place to see what the seal material tolerates.

Removal tools

Most push-fit fittings are released by holding the grip ring in with a small plastic tool or clip while pulling the tube, so the fitting can be reused. Use the release tool the maker lists for that fitting, depressurize and drain first, and check the tube end for scoring before you reinsert it. If the end is scratched, cut it back and redo the prep. Never hammer or pry a fitting off, and never reuse one that has been overheated or whose seal looks nicked.

Why not for refrigerant or gas

The listing on a push-fit water fitting says water. Refrigerant systems and fuel gas systems have their own joint methods, pressure and leak-test requirements, and their own listing paths. A gas leak creates a fire and explosion hazard. Use the methods in the press-fit lesson for refrigerant lines and the threaded gas pipe lesson for gas, and never trust a water rating to cover either.

Worked example (our own): replacing a section near a boiler

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Suppose a 3/4 inch copper return line beside a boiler has a pinhole leak. We cut out a short section and install a new piece with 2 couplings, 1 tee for a drain, and 1 elbow. Count joints, not fittings: a coupling has 2 joints, a tee has 3, an elbow has 2.

  • Joints: 2 couplings x 2 = 4, plus 1 tee x 3 = 3, plus 1 elbow x 2 = 2. Total: 4 + 3 + 2 = 9 joints.
  • Assumed time per soldered joint, including clean, flux, shield, heat, solder and wipe: 10 minutes.
  • Assumed time per push-fit joint, including cut, deburr, mark, push and check: 3 minutes.
  • Assumed shared work for either method (drain, dry the line, refill, purge air, pressure check): 30 minutes.

Solder: 9 x 10 = 90 minutes, plus 30 = 120 minutes. Push-fit: 9 x 3 = 27 minutes, plus 30 = 57 minutes. The difference is 120 − 57 = 63 minutes, about 52 percent less (63 / 120 = 0.525). Your times will vary, and the push-fit saving only counts if the fitting is rated for the loop temperature, supported, and allowed by the code. A cheaper job that fails inspection is not faster.

Field card: Push-to-Connect Fittings on Hydronic and Water Piping: Grip, Depth Marks, Ratings, Code Limits and Removal

Square, deburred, marked, supported, rated.

  • Cut square, deburr inside and out, wipe clean, check for scratches.
  • Mark the insertion depth from the maker sheet and confirm the mark at the mouth.
  • Support both sides of the fitting; do not let it carry the pipe.
  • Read the rating at your operating temperature and pressure.
  • Check the listing, the maker sheet and local code, including concealed use.
  • Use the release tool to remove; never pry.
  • Water only. Not for refrigerant or fuel gas.
