Refrigerated Rail Cars and Reefer Containers: Bottom-Air Delivery, Set Points, Pre-Trip Checks and a Heat-Load Example

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A refrigerated intermodal container is an insulated steel box with its own refrigeration unit, built to ride on a ship, a rail car or a chassis. A refrigerated rail car works on the same ideas at a larger scale. If you can read a walk-in cooler, you already know most of the physics. What changes is the power source, the airflow path and the fact that the box moves for days with nobody watching it.

Check your own paperwork first

Check the unit manual. This page is general education built from federal and university pages read in 2026. Your unit's operator and service manual, your employer's procedures and current federal rules come first. Nothing here replaces them.

Two ways to run the unit

Most container units run from electric power. Out on the road or at a yard, that power comes from a diesel-driven generator set clipped to the chassis or rail car. At a terminal, port or powered yard slot, the unit plugs into a supply. Rail cars and some trailers can carry a diesel-driven refrigeration unit instead. For a tech, the practical point is that the box has two lives: a plug-in life, where the supply must match the unit's nameplate, and an on-the-move life, where fuel, battery and generator condition decide whether the cargo arrives cold. Always read the unit's own nameplate and manual for its electrical and fuel details; we do not give numbers for them here. The truck and trailer version of this story is in Transport Refrigeration: Truck and Trailer Reefer Units, so we focus here on the container and rail side.

Bottom-air delivery

Many containers push cold air along the floor. The floor is a grid of channels, and the fan sends supply air under the cargo and up through the load, then it returns across the top to the unit. The federal handbook on protecting perishable food in transport describes the same idea for bottom-air trailers: air is forced under and up through the load at 3,000 to 5,000 cfm, at a static pressure of 1.5 to 3 inches of water. Those figures are for trailers in that handbook, not for every container, so use them to understand scale only.

The handbook's loading advice carries over as a principle. Load tightly over the floor so air has to go up through the cargo, cover open floor space, and leave at least 4 inches between the top of the load and the ceiling for return air. It also says to leave at least 4 inches between the load and the rear doors. A load stacked to the ceiling or a pallet laid over the channels can starve the cargo of airflow even when the unit is healthy. A tech called for "warm cargo" often finds a loading problem, not a refrigeration problem.

Set points and return air

The set point depends on the cargo. The federal handbook lists typical ranges of 0°F or below for frozen food, about 35°F for chilled food and about 55°F for chill-sensitive products. It also warns that for near-freezing products the air leaving the unit can be several degrees colder than the set point; its example says a 32°F product may need a setting of 34 to 36°F, because a set point that is too low can freeze the top layer. Different units control to different sensors, so read the manual to learn which one your unit follows.

Pre-trip checks

The handbook's pre-loading checklist reads like a good pre-trip list: unit operating properly, thermostat calibrated, door seals in good condition, floor drains open, and the box precooled or prewarmed to the transit temperature. It says precooling can take two or more hours in hot weather, and that a box whose estimated heat load exceeds the unit's rating should not be used. It also notes that newer microprocessor units can run a pre-trip mode with diagnostic tests. On a container, add the things we can see: a clean floor with open channels, undamaged wall liners, working drain plugs and a sound power cord and plug.

Defrost and fresh-air vents

Defrost keeps ice from blocking the evaporator. The handbook warns that open doors let moist air in, which can ice the coil and block airflow, and it advises shutting the unit off while loading from an open dock. For the defrost cycle itself, see Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings; the ideas are the same, only smaller.

Produce breathes. The handbook gives the example of bananas, which need a fresh-air vent so ethylene does not build up and trigger early ripening, and it says opening vent doors periodically helps. Cargo that does not need air exchange is shipped with the vent closed. Whoever loads the box should know which setting the shipper asked for, and the tech should know that a stuck-open vent adds warm outside air to the load. More on produce behavior is in Produce Cold Storage, Ripening Rooms and Controlled Atmosphere.

Records and data loggers

The handbook says to record product temperatures during loading, and mentions that some units can be monitored remotely. A temperature record is the only proof of what happened in transit, so never clear or reset a logger without permission, and write down what you changed on a service call. Check the logger's date and time, and keep it away from the unit's discharge air.

Refrigerant rules that apply

The federal environmental agency's venting rule under the Clean Air Act bars intentionally venting ozone-depleting refrigerants and their substitutes when servicing or repairing air conditioning and refrigeration equipment. Small releases that occur while making good-faith efforts to recapture, such as when connecting or disconnecting hoses, are treated as de minimis. The federal environmental agency's questions and answers for certified technicians explains that leak inspections apply to appliances with 50 pounds or more of ozone-depleting refrigerant that exceeds the threshold leak rate, and that records kept for recovery work must be held for three years. The federal environmental agency's separate leak repair fact sheet for HFC systems, effective January 1, 2026, covers appliances with 15 pounds or more and lists "refrigerated transport" in a 10 percent leak-rate group, with repair due within 30 days after refrigerant is added. That fact sheet is informational and says not to rely on it for compliance, so read the actual rule and your employer's procedure. We did not find a federal page that settles how every container unit is classified, so confirm with your supervisor.

Worked example (our own): box size the heat leak and the produce

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not data for any real unit. We want the refrigeration capacity needed to hold a 40-foot box of oranges at 41°F on a hot day.

  • Assumption 1: overall wall UA (area times U-value, all walls added) = 60 Btu/h per °F. Our own assumption.
  • Assumption 2: outside air 100°F, inside 41°F, so the difference is 100 - 41 = 59°F. Our own assumption.
  • Assumption 3: load = 20 short tons of oranges. Our own assumption.
  • Assumption 4: safety margin = 25 percent. Our own assumption.
  • Source data: a federal agriculture storage handbook table lists orange respiration at 6 mg per kg per hour at 5°C (41°F), and says to multiply mg per kg per hour by 220 to get Btu per ton per day. A university extension postharvest engineering chapter gives 1 ton of refrigeration as 12,000 Btu/h.

Step 1, wall heat leak: 60 × 59 = 3,540 Btu/h.

Step 2, respiration heat rate: 6 × 220 = 1,320 Btu per ton per day.

Step 3, produce heat: 20 × 1,320 = 26,400 Btu/day; 26,400 / 24 = 1,100 Btu/h.

Step 4, add: 3,540 + 1,100 = 4,640 Btu/h.

Step 5, margin: 4,640 × 1.25 = 5,800 Btu/h, which is 5,800 / 12,000 = 0.48 ton of refrigeration.

What it shows: on a hot day the walls leak more than the oranges generate. Swap in head lettuce at the same temperature (17 mg per kg per hour in the same table) and produce heat becomes 20 × 17 × 220 / 24 = 3,117 Btu/h, almost as large as the wall leak, so total load would be 6,657 Btu/h before margin. We left out sun on the roof, outside air through the vent, door openings and pulldown from a warm start. Real capacity must be compared with the maker's published capacity at the actual outside and box temperatures, which differ from rated conditions.

Field card: Refrigerated Rail Cars and Reefer Containers

Air must move up through the load; the unit only holds what it is handed.

  • Power: plug-in at yards and ports, generator set or diesel unit on the move. Read the nameplate.
  • Bottom air: keep floor channels open, load tight to the floor, 4 in of return gap at top and rear.
  • Typical set points: 0°F or below frozen, about 35°F chilled, about 55°F chill-sensitive.
  • Precool the box first; do not use a box whose heat load is above the unit's rating.
  • Fresh-air vent: open only when the shipper's instructions say so.
  • Do not clear a logger without permission; record what you changed.
  • Venting refrigerant is prohibited; confirm leak repair rules for the unit's refrigerant.
  • Our example: 3,540 + 1,100 = 4,640 Btu/h, times 1.25 = 5,800 Btu/h.
