EPA 608 Type II Required Evacuation Levels: The 82.156 Table, Leaks and Exam Wording

Last updated: 2026-11-03

One table in the federal refrigerant rules decides how deep a vacuum you must pull before opening a large appliance. It looks simple until a question changes the charge, the pressure class or the age of the recovery machine. This lesson reads the table row by row, shows how to read a vacuum gauge against it, and covers the leak rule and the major-repair exception.

Check the current rule text

Check the current text. We read the eCFR version of 40 CFR 82.156 and 82.152 and an EPA service-practice page in November 2026. Rules change. This is study help, not legal advice, so confirm against the live eCFR before you rely on it.

What the table is, and when it applies

Paragraph (a) of 40 CFR 82.156 says that before opening or disposing of an appliance, technicians must evacuate the refrigerant, "including all the liquid refrigerant," to the levels in Table 1. It covers every appliance except small appliances, motor vehicle air conditioners and similar equipment, which have their own rules. The text also says you may evacuate the whole appliance or only the part you will service, if that part can be isolated to a system receiver.

The table, exactly as printed

The eCFR labels the numbers "Inches of Hg vacuum (relative to standard atmospheric pressure of 29.9 inches Hg)." The columns are headed by the date of the recovery and/or recycling equipment.

EPA's stationary refrigeration service-practice page lists the same values. The pressure classes come from 40 CFR 82.152: medium-pressure means a liquid saturation pressure between 45 psia and 170 psia at 104 °F; high-pressure means between 170 psia and 355 psia at 104 °F; very high-pressure means a critical temperature below 104 °F or a pressure above 355 psia at 104 °F; low-pressure means below 45 psia at 104 °F. Our own note: a class is set by the refrigerant, so look up its pressure data rather than guessing. See Key EPA 608 Definitions.

Three patterns that make the table easy

  • Older machines are the easy case. Every non-zero row in the older column is 4, so a machine made before the cutoff date never needs more than 4 inches (low-pressure aside).
  • Newer machines pull deeper as the pressure class drops. On the newer column, 10 and 15 apply only to medium-pressure and large high-pressure appliances. Medium-pressure over 200 pounds gets the deepest inches: 15.
  • High-pressure under 200 pounds is a zero in both columns. Questions like to use it as a trap because the row looks like it should ask for more.

Reading inches of mercury vacuum

Atmospheric pressure is the starting point: 0 inches of vacuum, 0 psig. A compound gauge reads vacuum on the other side of zero, in inches of mercury, so a bigger number means a deeper vacuum and more work for the machine. Using the 29.9 inches in the table's unit note, a reading of 10 inches of vacuum is about 19.9 inches of mercury absolute (29.9 minus 10), and 15 inches is about 14.9 absolute. That arithmetic is our own. A "0" row means you only need to reach atmospheric pressure on the gauge, which matches the 0 psig wording elsewhere in the section.

The low-pressure row is a different kind of reading. It is 25 mm Hg absolute, which is about 0.98 inch absolute (25 divided by 25.4), or roughly 28.9 inches of vacuum by the same arithmetic. That is a much deeper level and is read with an absolute-pressure gauge. Our own practice tip, not rule text: after you reach the level, stop the machine and watch the gauge. A fast climb back suggests liquid still boiling off or a leak. See Type III recovery from low-pressure chillers.

When a leak keeps you from reaching the level

Paragraph (a)(2) begins "If leaks in the appliance make evacuation to the levels in Table 1 unattainable," and then sets the steps. First isolate leaking components from non-leaking ones where possible. Evacuate the non-leaking components to the Table 1 levels. Evacuate the leaking components to the lowest level attainable without substantially contaminating the refrigerant, and in no case above 0 psig. The EPA page says the same. The takeaway: a leak does not excuse you from the table; it changes what you do with the part that will not hold a vacuum.

The major-repair exception

Paragraph (a)(1) offers a lighter option when the appliance will not be evacuated to the atmosphere after the work and the repair is not major as defined in 82.152. Medium-, high- and very high-pressure appliances must be evacuated to a pressure no higher than 0 psig before opening. Low-pressure appliances must be pressurized to no higher than 0 psig. For an oil change, the appliance must be evacuated or pressurized to no higher than 5 psig. Section 82.152 defines major maintenance, service or repair as work that involves removing a compressor, condenser, evaporator or auxiliary heat exchange coil, or uncovering an opening of more than four square inches of flow area for more than 15 minutes. So the shortcut is for small jobs; a compressor swap goes back to Table 1. Our full treatment is in Major vs other repairs, and machine habits that help you hit the level are in Recovery machine care.

Worked example: our own three jobs

Our own example. These jobs are invented. We assume the stated pressure class is correct for each refrigerant; confirm real classes from pressure data. In every job the work is major (a compressor or coil comes out), so the exception does not apply, and no leak blocks the vacuum.

  1. Job 1. A high-pressure rooftop unit with an 18 pound full charge, recovered with a machine made in 2015. Row: high-pressure, under 200 pounds. Newer column: 0.
  2. Job 2. A medium-pressure chiller with an 85 pound full charge, machine made in 2015. Row: medium-pressure, under 200 pounds. Newer column: 10 inches of vacuum, which is about 19.9 inches absolute.
  3. Job 3. A high-pressure condensing system with a 240 pound full charge. With a machine made in 1992 the row is high-pressure, 200 pounds or more, older column: 4. If the crew borrows a machine made in 2015, the same row gives 10. Same appliance, different answer, because only the machine date changed.

How exam questions are phrased

This section is our own advice, not text from any exam. Questions usually hand you more facts than you need. Sort them in this order: (1) what is the pressure class, (2) is the charge under or over 200 pounds, (3) when was the recovery machine made, (4) is the repair major, (5) is there a leak. Watch for distractors such as the age of the appliance, the model year of the compressor or the refrigerant's brand name. Watch the units too: the question may offer "10 inches" and "10 psig" as different choices, and "inches of vacuum" is not the same as "inches absolute."

Six practice questions (our own)

  1. A high-pressure appliance has a 150 pound full charge. The recovery machine was made in 2012. What level is required?
    Answer: 0 inches of vacuum (row: high-pressure, under 200 pounds).
  2. A medium-pressure appliance holds 350 pounds. The machine was made after 1993. What level is required?
    Answer: 15 inches of mercury vacuum.
  3. The same 350 pound medium-pressure appliance, but the machine was made in 1992.
    Answer: 4 inches.
  4. A high-pressure appliance with a 300 pound charge, machine made in 2010. The gauge stops at 8 inches of vacuum. May you open it under the table?
    Answer: No. The newer column requires 10 inches, and 8 is a shallower vacuum.
  5. A medium-pressure appliance has a leak that keeps the whole system above the Table 1 level. What do you do?
    Answer: Isolate the leaking part if you can, evacuate the non-leaking part to Table 1, and take the leaking part to the lowest attainable level without substantially contaminating the refrigerant, no higher than 0 psig.
  6. A 90 pound medium-pressure appliance needs a new compressor, and the machine was made in 2015. Does the 0 psig shortcut apply?
    Answer: No. Removing the compressor is major repair, so evacuate to the Table 1 level of 10 inches.

Field card: Required evacuation levels

Class, charge, machine date, then major or not.

  • Very high-pressure: 0. High-pressure under 200 lb: 0.
  • High-pressure 200 lb or more: 4 (older machine), 10 (newer).
  • Medium-pressure under 200 lb: 4 or 10. Over 200 lb: 4 or 15.
  • Low-pressure: 25 mm Hg absolute.
  • Cutoff date November 15, 1993 applies to the recovery machine.
  • Leak: isolate, Table 1 on the good part, lowest attainable (max 0 psig) on the leaking part.
  • Not major and not venting after: 0 psig (5 psig for an oil change).
