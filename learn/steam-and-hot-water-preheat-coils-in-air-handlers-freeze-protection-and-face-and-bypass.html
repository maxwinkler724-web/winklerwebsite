Steam and Hot Water Preheat Coils: Freeze Protection and Face-and-Bypass

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The preheat coil is the first thing outdoor air meets in a building air handler, so it is the first thing to freeze. A split coil floods the unit, and the repair is expensive. This lesson explains why steam and hot water preheat coils fail in cold weather and the layouts that make them harder to freeze. It adds a worked steam-load example you can redo on paper.

Check the maker and the drawings

Check the maker and the drawings. Pitch, trap drop, valve sequence and setpoints differ by coil model and by job. The numbers here are examples from the sources at the bottom. The coil manufacturer's installation manual and the control drawings win. This page names no brand to buy.

Why preheat coils freeze

A steam coil is only safe while steam keeps pushing condensate out. Marlo Coil's article on frozen coils says freezing happens when condensate stays in the coil and meets sub-freezing air. Water expands as it turns to ice, the tubes swell, and they can rupture. Marlo lists poor drainage as the most frequent cause: wrong coil pitch, an undersized or wrongly installed trap, or a plugged or failed trap. An ACHR News article on frozen steam coils adds a quieter cause. If the steam supply shuts off, a slight vacuum can form and hold a pocket of condensate in the coil, where it freezes.

Three conditions stack up in practice:

  • Condensate held in the tubes. Anything that stops drainage, such as a coil pitched the wrong way, a bad trap or a return that cannot take the flow, leaves liquid sitting in the coldest air.
  • Stratified outdoor air. Cold air does not arrive evenly. A NIH technical bulletin on freeze protection for 100 percent outside air units says steam must reach every circuit, especially at low load, to avoid cold spots, and that freezestats must cover the full coil face. See Freezestats on Air Handlers.
  • A modulated steam valve at low load. Marlo says low pressure or an undersized or poorly controlled valve can keep steam from filling the coil, leaving parts cold enough to freeze at partial load. The NIH bulletin warns that a single modulating steam valve can create sub-atmospheric pressure in the coil, which holds condensate in. The ACHR article makes the same point about modulating control and lift: it can drop the steam pressure below what the trap needs.

Coil types built for cold air

Standard steam coils. The ACHR article says standard coils are meant for entering air above 35°F with constant steam pressure, and it does not recommend them for freeze-prone service. Greenheck's coil manual says entering air should not be below 40°F with its standard steam coils, and that even coils marked non-freeze (steam distributing) will freeze below the freezing point, so freeze safeties are needed where freezing is possible.

Distributing (tube-in-tube) coils. Marlo describes an inner tube with nozzles that spray steam along the outer tube wall. That cuts cold spots, and condensate drains from the steam-inlet end. The NIH bulletin says distributing coils are typical for 100 percent outside air units because steam in the inner tube keeps condensate in the outer tube from freezing when air is below 32°F. The ACHR article says the same kind of inner tube acts as a steam tracer.

Integral face-and-bypass (IFB) coils. A Wing page on IFB coils says the coil controls air temperature while keeping full steam pressure or water flow inside the coil. Dampers split the air between the heating face and a bypass channel. Wing's vertical version is aimed at preheating outdoor air for makeup systems. The NIH bulletin describes a vertical IFB coil with a bypass area between the tubes and clamshell dampers on both sides. When heat is needed, the dampers open to the fins. Near freezing, the steam valve goes to a minimum position or fully open to keep flow continuous, and the dampers handle temperature control. The bulletin also notes IFB coils need room downstream for the air to mix before the next coil, so they do not always fit. Marlo makes a similar dampered face-and-bypass coil and says maximum steam pressure is held at all times rather than modulated.

The important idea in all of these: the steam side stays wide open and the air side does the controlling. The claims that a coil "prevents freeze-up" come from the makers and are not independent test data. Treat them as design intent.

Trap, drop and vacuum breaker

Greenheck's manual says to locate the trap at least 12 inches below the coil return connection. The NIH bulletin calls out a 14 inch fill leg as typical and asks for redundant trap assemblies on a preheat coil. Greenheck also says each individually controlled coil needs its own trap and that coils in series need a trap per coil or bank. It says float-and-thermostatic traps suit high or low pressure and are the choice where air must be vented. The ACHR article puts the trap at the coil's low point and gives a rule of thumb that lifting condensate takes about 1 psi per 2 feet, so a trap fed by low steam pressure cannot lift it far. Armstrong's steam conservation guide gives the same 2 feet per psi figure for syphon lift and points out that at zero or vacuum pressure only gravity pushes condensate through the trap.

Greenheck says vacuum breakers and air vents must be installed so the coil does not hold condensate or air, with the vacuum breaker generally connected between the coil inlet and the trap. The NIH bulletin says vacuum breakers belong on the steam headers. When the steam valve closes and the coil steam cools, the coil pulls a vacuum. The breaker lets air in so the condensate can run out. For more on traps, see Steam Traps Explained and Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits.

Greenheck also says not to reduce pipe size at the coil return connection, to pitch a coil without a pitched casing 1/4 inch per foot toward the return, and not to drip steam mains through coils. Marlo adds that the coil must be pitched toward the condensate header, and that small installation errors can stop drainage.

Keep the steam valve open in freezing air

Greenheck prefers a two-position steam valve with face-and-bypass dampers when modulating control is needed on high-pressure installations. The NIH bulletin says that near freezing the IFB coil's valve goes to a minimum position or fully open. It also suggests avoiding a single modulating steam valve and using a one-third and two-thirds valve arrangement for better control. The idea is the same in each: when the outdoor air is cold, give the coil full steam and let dampers or downstream coils trim the temperature.

Pump-around hot water or glycol preheat

A hydronic preheat coil with its own circulating pump can be freeze-resistant for a different reason: flow stays turbulent and the fluid is protected by glycol. The NIH bulletin says glycol lowers the freezing point of the loop, so concentration must be inspected and topped up. It calls for N+1 circulating pumps on a preheat coil, in the bypass position, running whenever heat is called for. They keep flow up at low load and keep circulating if the preheat system fails, which delays freezing. The pumps are on emergency power in that design. Coils should be drainable at the coil level. See Hydro-Air Systems: Hot Water Coils in an Air Handler.

The freezestat is the last line

None of this removes the freezestat. The NIH bulletin puts freezestats after the preheat coil and ahead of the cooling coil, with a typical setpoint of 37°F. On a trip the unit shuts down, the pump starts, and a manual reset is needed. Several are spread across the face because a capillary only senses near the cold part of its length. Greenheck says to install freeze safeties where freezing is possible and notes that a coil damaged by freezing is not covered by the standard warranty. A trip means find the cause; do not just raise the setpoint.

Worked example: 10,000 cfm of outdoor air, 0°F to 50°F

This is an invented unit. All arithmetic is ours.

  1. Temperature rise. Our own: 50°F − 0°F = 50°F.
  2. Heating load. Our own: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × rise. The 1.08 is the usual standard-air constant (about 0.075 lb per cubic foot × 60 minutes per hour × 0.24 Btu per lb per °F). 1.08 × 10,000 × 50 = 540,000 Btu/h.
  3. Latent heat of the steam. Armstrong's steam table lists 960.1 Btu/lb at 20.0 psia (about 5.3 psig), steam at 227.96°F. We use about 960 Btu/lb. Check the table for your own steam pressure.
  4. Steam flow. Our own: 540,000 Btu/h ÷ 960.1 Btu/lb = about 562 lb/h of steam, which condenses to about 562 lb/h of condensate.
  5. Trap sizing with a safety factor. Armstrong's guide says users generally need a safety factor, ranging from 1.5:1 up to 10:1. It uses 3:1 for a coil condensing 500 lb/h, and 2:1 in its figure for a continuous coil with a very small steam space. With a stated 2:1: our own, 562 lb/h × 2 = about 1,125 lb/h trap capacity at the working differential. Use the trap maker's chart for the real selection, and keep in mind that a 3:1 factor would give about 1,687 lb/h.

Note that this is a full-load number at 0°F air. A modulating valve at 25°F outdoor air would pass less steam. That lower pressure is exactly when the trap loses lift and the coil is most at risk.

Field card: Steam and Hot Water Preheat Coils

Keep the steam on, drain by gravity, let the air side control.

  • Freezing needs condensate left in the tubes plus cold air.
  • Cold spots and modulated steam at low load are the usual set-up.
  • Distributing and face-and-bypass coils hold full steam and let dampers trim temperature.
  • Trap at least 12 in. below the return connection per Greenheck; NIH cites a 14 in. leg. Follow the coil maker.
  • Vacuum breaker between coil inlet and trap; air vent where needed.
  • Steam load: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × rise; steam lb/h = Btu/h ÷ latent heat.
  • Pump-around loops need glycol, running pumps and drainable coils.
  • The freezestat is the last line, not the plan. Find the cause of a trip.
