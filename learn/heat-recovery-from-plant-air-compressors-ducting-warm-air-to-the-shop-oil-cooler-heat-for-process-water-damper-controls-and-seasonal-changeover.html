Heat Recovery From Plant Air Compressors: Warm Air, Oil-Cooler Water Heat, Dampers and Seasonal Changeover

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A plant air compressor does not make much compressed air energy in the end. Nearly everything the motor draws leaves the machine as heat, and a facility tech is often the person asked whether that heat can warm the shop. This lesson covers the common ways to use it, the controls that keep it safe, and a worked example of how much fuel it can displace.

Where the heat comes from

Federal energy guidance for industrial compressed air says more than 80 percent of the electrical energy going to a compressor becomes available heat, and it notes that most air-cooled packaged rotary screw units use radiator-style coolers that give an opportunity to recover that heat for facility heating (DOE compressed air sourcebook, listed below). A compressed air industry group fact sheet gives a wider range, up to 80 to 93 percent, and says a well-designed system can often capture 50 to 90 percent of that heat. The heat is low grade. That same fact sheet says it is generally not hot enough to make steam. Think of it as a very large, free, low-temperature heater that only runs when the compressor runs.

The compressor is not an equipment room heater by design. Its job is to make air, and its cooling system is sized to stay inside the maker's temperature limits. Any heat recovery you add must never make that job harder.

Option 1: ducting warm air from an air-cooled packaged unit

An air-cooled packaged compressor pulls room or outdoor air through its coolers and blows it out warm. The industry fact sheet says recovery from such units is about 80 to 90 percent, with discharge air typically 30 to 40 degrees F above the cooling air inlet, and gives a rule of thumb of about 50,000 Btu/h per 100 cfm of compressor capacity at full load. A duct then carries that warm air into the shop.

  • Winter: a damper sends the warm air into the space it serves, so the unit heater, rooftop or boiler runs less.
  • Summer: a second damper sends the same air outdoors so the compressor does not heat the shop. A thermostat can drive a hinged vent or motorized damper between the two paths. The fact sheet describes a thermostatically controlled vent that modulates airflow and says hot air can be ducted outside in summer.
  • Fan help: the fact sheet says a duct run usually needs an added fan to handle the duct load and avoid backpressure on the compressor's cooling fan. Do not assume the built-in fan can push through new ductwork.
  • Air source: it warns that if the compressor takes its air from outside and heat goes to another space, or if intake air is not drawn from outside, the cabinet static pressure and efficiency can change. With outside air, some return air may be needed so below-freezing air does not harm the unit.

Option 2: heat for water from oil-cooled and water-cooled units

On oil-flooded units, a heat exchanger can be placed on the lubricant circuit. Hot lubricant passes through it and warms water on the other side. The fact sheet says that, depending on design, the water can be non-potable or potable, and that when hot water is not needed the lubricant goes to the standard cooler. It gives about 50 to 60 percent recovery for water-cooled units and says space heating from them is less common because it takes an extra heat exchange step at lower temperature. Typical uses are process water, wash water or preheating boiler makeup water. See the boiler stack economizer lesson for how feedwater preheat fits into a boiler plant.

Our own caution: where the water side could ever touch drinking water, ask the maker and the code authority about the heat exchanger type and cross-connection protection before anyone connects anything.

Controls and backpressure limits

  • Follow the maker's limits. Compressor makers publish limits for cooling air flow, discharge temperature and allowable external static pressure. Those numbers belong to the specific model. This lesson cannot give them, so get them from the maker's manual and keep the system inside them. This is our own caution.
  • Keep the compressor's own cooling in charge. Heat recovery should be a branch that can be bypassed. If the recovery side is blocked, shut or starved, the compressor must still be able to reject its heat.
  • Thermostatic bypass. A thermostat that sends lubricant to the standard cooler when no hot water is needed, or air to outdoors in summer, is the common pattern.
  • Check after install. Compare discharge temperature, high-temperature trips and fan amperage before and after. A new trip is a sign of too much backpressure.

Safety

  • Never block compressor cooling. A closed damper with no relief path can overheat the unit. Make the failed or unpowered damper position the one that exhausts the heat. Our own recommendation.
  • Keep intake air clean. Do not draw the compressor's intake from a spot with solvent vapor, welding fume, dust or engine exhaust. Warm air that has been in a work area is not clean air to feed a compressor. Intake contaminants can enter the compressed air system. Our own caution.
  • Lock out before opening ducts. Treat the compressor and any motorized damper as hazardous energy.
  • Do not send exhaust air into occupied air supply without the engineer of record, because compressor cabinet air can carry lubricant mist.

Seasonal changeover

At the start of the heating season, set the dampers or vent to the shop path, check that the added duct fan runs and that the damper actuators move through full travel. At the start of cooling season, set them to exhaust. Many sites mark the compressor room with a changeover tag and a date. Where outside air is the compressor intake, watch freezing air in winter, as the fact sheet notes. Also see our lesson on compressed air systems for facility techs, because fixing leaks and lowering pressure can reduce the load and so the recoverable heat.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measured site.

  • Assumed: a 100 hp air-cooled rotary screw compressor averaging 75 percent load, so about 75 hp of average input.
  • Assumed: 80 percent of that heat is captured as warm air. The industry fact sheet gives 80 to 90 percent for air-cooled units; the federal sourcebook says more than 80 percent of input becomes available heat but gives no recovery fraction we could confirm, so 80 percent is our assumption.
  • Assumed: 2,000 heating-season hours at that load.
  • Assumed: the unit heater it displaces is 80 percent efficient on natural gas (100,000 Btu per therm).
  1. Heat in: 75 hp × 2,545 Btu/h per hp = 190,875 Btu/h. (2,545 Btu/h per hp is the standard conversion.)
  2. Recoverable: 190,875 × 0.80 = 152,700 Btu/h.
  3. Season heat delivered: 152,700 × 2,000 h = 305,400,000 Btu = 3,054 therms.
  4. Fuel displaced: 3,054 ÷ 0.80 = about 3,818 therms per season.
  5. Airflow check: at a 35 degree F rise, 152,700 ÷ (1.08 × 35) = about 4,040 cfm. This is our own sensible-heat estimate, and it shows why a duct fan is needed.

Fan power and heat that the shop does not need in shoulder months would reduce the real savings.

Field card: Heat Recovery From Plant Air Compressors: Warm Air, Oil-Cooler Water Heat, Dampers and Seasonal Changeover

Most compressor input becomes heat. Use it, but never at the cost of the compressor's cooling.

  • Over 80 percent of input power becomes available heat (federal sourcebook).
  • Air-cooled: duct warm air to the shop in winter and outdoors in summer, with dampers.
  • Oil or water cooled: heat exchanger to process or makeup water, with a bypass.
  • Add a duct fan; avoid backpressure; stay inside the maker's limits.
  • Failed damper position should exhaust the heat.
  • Keep intake air clean. Change over each season.
