Lumber Dry Kilns: Heating, Venting and Wet-Bulb Control

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A lumber kiln is a big insulated box with one job: remove water from wood slowly enough that the boards do not crack. For an HVAC tech it is a plant system with coils, traps, fans, dampers and humidity control. This lesson covers how conventional and dehumidification kilns work, how a schedule is set, and how to size the heat needed to drive off the water.

Two ways to dry lumber

A conventional kiln uses heating coils fed by steam or hot water. A federal wood-drying manual lists steam as historically the most common heat source, with hot water, hot oil, electricity and direct firing as other options. Moisture leaves as vapor in air discharged through vents, and fresh air is warmed to replace it. A state university extension fact sheet puts it this way: the kiln uses steam flowing through pipes that radiate heat into the kiln air.

A dehumidification kiln uses a refrigeration cycle instead. The same extension fact sheet says water is removed as liquid rather than vented outside, and that heat is recycled instead of discharged. Warm, wet kiln air crosses a cold coil, the water condenses and drains, and the compressor heat warms the air again. The federal manual says these kilns usually operate up to 160 °F, need very tight vapor barriers and insulation of R-20 or more, and use electric strip heaters to bring the kiln up to a minimum temperature so the compressor can work efficiently. If you know a heat pump dehumidifier, you know the idea. See the related lesson on sensible and latent heat below.

What is inside a conventional kiln

  • Heating coils. The federal manual says finned pipe has four to eight times the radiating capacity of plain black iron pipe, and that aluminum fins move heat at twice the rate of steel fins. It also says low-pressure steam needs more radiating surface than high-pressure steam for the same heat.
  • Fans that reverse. The manual says all modern kilns use forced air circulation. Fans run one way for a while and then the other, so the stacks dry evenly. Its figure caption notes that the vent on the high-pressure side of the fans becomes the fresh air inlet when circulation reverses.
  • Vents. A state university extension chapter on kiln operation warns that excessive venting adds steam use and favors drying defects, while too little venting at high wet-bulb temperatures slows drying. Its prestart check is to see that all vents open and close completely.
  • Humidity spray. The same chapter says to keep the hand valve on the steam spray line closed during warmup, because spray then can make lumber pick up moisture and cause condensation, water stain and corrosion of kiln parts. Spray is mainly used later, for equalizing and conditioning.

Moisture content and equilibrium moisture content

Moisture content compares the weight of water in a board with the weight of the wood when fully dry. The extension fact sheet gives the fiber saturation point as 28 to 30 percent moisture content. Equilibrium moisture content (EMC) is the balance point between the wood and the air around it: wood gains or loses water until it matches the air. The federal manual's example is air at 150 °F dry bulb and 130 °F wet bulb, a 20 °F wet-bulb depression, which it ties to an EMC of 8 percent at 57 percent relative humidity. That is why a kiln can aim for a final moisture content just by controlling the wet-bulb depression. If you want to measure it, read psychrometrics for technicians.

Schedules: temperature and wet-bulb depression

A drying schedule is a list of dry-bulb and wet-bulb settings, stepped as the lumber dries. The extension fact sheet gives an example for a softwood or hardwood schedule that begins near 110 °F with a tiny depression (110 °F dry bulb and 107 °F wet bulb, a 3 °F depression, while moisture content is above 50 percent). It ends at 160 °F dry bulb and 110 °F wet bulb, a 50 °F depression, near the final target. The depression gets larger because the air has to get drier for the wood to keep giving up water. The extension chapter on operation adds that a mixed charge follows the milder schedule, and that a green black cherry charge starts at 130 °F dry bulb with a 7 °F depression to avoid damaging the wood. Those are examples, not settings for your kiln: the species, thickness and the kiln maker's controller manual decide.

Corrosion from acidic wood vapors

Kilns are hot, wet and sometimes chemically harsh. The federal manual says steel structures need paint or sealer, especially near doors and in the bottom 12 to 18 inches where condensation forms, and that aluminum is extremely resistant to corrosion. It also says heavy-gauge steel fins are subject to corrosion. A university abstract on dehumidification drying of red oak reports that cooling coils in a laboratory kiln corroded, that the condensate had a blue tinge and left blue crystals identified as copper acetate, and that coils in industrial kilns with anti-corrosion finishes showed no corrosion. Our reading, which is our own inference and not the abstract's statement, is that acid vapors from some woods attack bare copper, so coil finish and material matter when you replace parts.

Coil and trap service

The extension chapter on operation connects dry-bulb swings to faulty design or poor trap maintenance, and says too much active coil area can cause large temperature cycles and waterlogging of the coils. It recommends the smallest radiation and lowest steam pressure that holds the required dry bulb, and says to blow all steam traps to the atmosphere briefly at startup to clear scale and dirt. For trap types and testing, see Steam Traps Explained. Check coil fins for corrosion damage and confirm that vents and dampers move fully, because poor wet-bulb control is usually linked to poor kiln maintenance.

Worked example (our own): how much water and how much heat

This is our own example with assumptions we chose. It is not a design.

  • Assumed charge: 8,000 board feet.
  • Assumed starting moisture content: 70 percent. Assumed final moisture content: 8 percent.
  • Assumed oven-dry weight: 1.6 pounds per board foot.
  • Assumed drying time: 14 days, which is 14 x 24 = 336 hours.
  • Latent heat: about 1,000 Btu per pound evaporated. The federal manual states the heat of evaporation of water as approximately 1,000 Btu/lb, and a 1939 federal standards laboratory paper on saturated water and steam lists 560.2 calories per gram at 65 °C (about 149 °F). Times 1.8, that is about 1,008 Btu per pound, so 1,000 is a fair round value near kiln temperatures.

Step 1, dry wood weight: 8,000 x 1.6 = 12,800 lb.

Step 2, water removed: 12,800 x (0.70 − 0.08) = 12,800 x 0.62 = 7,936 lb of water.

Step 3, latent heat: 7,936 lb x 1,000 Btu/lb = 7,936,000 Btu.

Step 4, average latent load: 7,936,000 Btu / 336 h = about 23,600 Btu/h.

That is only the evaporation load. Real kilns also need heat to warm the wood and its water, cover wall and floor losses, and warm incoming vent air; the federal manual lists all of those. So the coil or compressor must be larger than 23,600 Btu/h. As a check on our assumption, the same manual says drying green southern pine to 15 percent takes out 1.92 lb of water per board foot, much more than our 0.99 lb per board foot (7,936 / 8,000), because our example uses a drier starting condition. In a dehumidification kiln most of that latent heat is recovered when the water condenses on the cold coil, which is why those kilns use less outside heat.

Safety at high temperature and inside the kiln

  • Shut off heat, spray and fans before entering, and cool the kiln by opening doors and vents if it ran hot (extension operating chapter).
  • Never enter a running kiln alone. Have a coworker who knows you are there, stationed outside.
  • Surfaces can be dangerously hot. When you open a door, stand to the side because hot, humid air rushes out.
  • The same chapter says do not enter when the wet-bulb temperature is 120 °F or more without head and body covering, and that people with heart or breathing problems should avoid 110 °F wet bulb or more.
  • If a fan switch cannot be locked off, post a sign that says Do Not Start Fans.

A kiln interior is also a confined space for many sites: follow your site procedure and see Confined Spaces in HVAC Work.

Field card: Lumber Dry Kilns: Heating, Venting and Wet-Bulb Control

Coil adds heat, vents remove water, the wet-bulb depression sets the drying rate.

  • Conventional kiln: steam or hot-water coils, reversing fans, vents, spray line.
  • Dehumidification kiln: a refrigeration cycle condenses the water and reuses the heat.
  • Fiber saturation is 28 to 30 percent; EMC follows dry bulb and wet-bulb depression.
  • Latent heat is about 1,000 Btu per pound of water evaporated.
  • Our example: 7,936 lb of water, 7.9 million Btu, about 23,600 Btu/h over 14 days.
  • Keep the spray closed during warmup; blow down traps at startup.
  • Never enter a running kiln alone; wet bulb of 120 °F or more needs head and body covering.
