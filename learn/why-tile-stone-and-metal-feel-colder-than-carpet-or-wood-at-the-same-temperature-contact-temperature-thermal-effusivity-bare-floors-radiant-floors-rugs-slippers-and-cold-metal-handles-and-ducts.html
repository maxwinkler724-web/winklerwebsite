Why Tile and Metal Feel Colder Than Carpet at the Same Temperature

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Step barefoot onto a tile floor and a rug in the same room. Both have been sitting at the room temperature, yet the tile feels cold and the rug feels fine. A thermometer would read the same number on both. The difference is how fast each one pulls heat out of your skin, and that is a heat-transfer idea you will meet again on cold ducts, metal handles and radiant floors.

Your skin feels heat flow, not temperature

Skin has no thermometer. Its nerve endings respond to how fast the skin is cooling and to the temperature the skin surface settles at. A federal space-agency technical brief on touch temperature says the sensation of an object's temperature depends on the type of material touched, and that there is a time lag between the object's temperature and the skin's temperature. When you touch something, the skin surface does not take the object's temperature. It lands somewhere between the skin temperature and the object temperature. How close it lands to each side depends on the two materials.

For a broader look at why a room can feel cold at 70 degrees, see the six comfort factors. This lesson covers a narrower question: the surface you touch.

Three properties, one plain-language number

  • Thermal conductivity (k) is how easily heat moves through a material. A university physics reference table lists copper near 385 W/m·K, aluminum near 205, steel near 50, concrete and ordinary glass near 0.8, wood roughly 0.04 to 0.12, and still air near 0.024.
  • Density (ρ) is how much material is packed into a cubic meter.
  • Heat capacity (c) is how much heat it takes to warm one kilogram by one degree.

Heat that leaves your skin has to be stored somewhere. A dense, conductive material carries the heat away from the surface quickly and has a lot of material to absorb it, so the surface stays near the floor temperature. A light, air-filled material cannot carry heat away fast, so its top surface warms toward your skin temperature almost at once. Engineers wrap this into one number called thermal effusivity, e, which is the square root of k times ρ times c. In plain words, effusivity is how hard a material pulls heat out of whatever touches it. Higher effusivity means it feels colder (or hotter, if the material is warm). The same square-root product, called thermal inertia in the federal brief, is how that brief ranks materials for touch limits: aluminum and stainless steel sit at the low end of its inverse-inertia list, meaning they take heat from skin the fastest, while plastics and fabrics sit at the high end.

The weighted-average rule

For two thick bodies that touch with good contact, the surface temperature at the instant of contact is a weighted average of their starting temperatures, weighted by effusivity:

T contact = (e1 × T1 + e2 × T2) / (e1 + e2)

Whichever body has the larger effusivity wins the tug of war, and the contact temperature ends up close to its starting temperature. Skin has a large effusivity, so skin pulls hard on a carpet and the carpet's surface quickly warms to nearly skin temperature. Against steel, the steel pulls harder than skin does, and the skin surface drops toward the metal's temperature.

This is an idealized first-moment value. Real contact also depends on touch time, how rough the surface is, moisture and pressure, and the skin cools further as touching continues. Treat the numbers below as a way to compare materials, not as a prediction of what a thermometer pressed on a foot would read.

Worked example (our own): skin on 70 F floors

Our own example with stated assumptions. We compare bare skin at 91 F touching four surfaces that have all sat at 70 F. The 91 F skin figure is our rounding of the 32.5 C (90.5 F) initial skin temperature used in the federal technical paper listed in the sources. Because the weighted average is linear, we can work directly in degrees Fahrenheit.

Step 1: skin effusivity. The same federal paper gives skin conductivity 0.54 W/m·K and thermal diffusivity 1.3 × 10-7 m²/s. Diffusivity equals k divided by (ρ times c), so ρc = 0.54 / (1.3 × 10-7) = 4.15 × 106 J/m³·K. Then kρc = 0.54 × 4.15 × 106 = 2.24 × 106, and its square root is about 1,500 W·s1/2/m²·K. This is our derived value; the source does not print it.

Step 2: floor effusivities.

  • Ceramic tile: a published measurement of the layers of one commercial ceramic tile reports an effusivity of 585 for the tile body (substrate). We use 585.
  • Oak: a property table from a thermoelectric-parts maker lists oak at 0.15 W/m·K, 0.61 g/cm³ and 0.57 cal/g·C. Converting (1 cal = 4.184 J), density is 610 kg/m³ and c is 2,385 J/kg·K. So kρc = 0.15 × 610 × 2,385 = 218,000, and e = about 467.
  • Low-carbon steel: the same table gives 48 W/m·K, 7.85 g/cm³ and 0.11 cal/g·C, which is 7,850 kg/m³ and 460 J/kg·K. So kρc = 48 × 7,850 × 460 = 173 million, and e = about 13,200.
  • Carpet: we found no published effusivity for a carpet surface we could cite, so this is an assumption, not a measured value. We assume 150 for a thick, air-filled pile, and show how the answer moves for 300.

Step 3: contact temperature. Each result is T1 = 91 F skin, T2 = 70 F floor.

  • Steel: (1,500 × 91 + 13,200 × 70) / 14,700. The top is 136,500 + 924,000 = 1,060,500. Divide by 14,700 to get 72.1 F.
  • Tile: (1,500 × 91 + 585 × 70) / 2,085. The top is 136,500 + 40,950 = 177,450. Divide by 2,085 to get 85.1 F.
  • Oak: (1,500 × 91 + 467 × 70) / 1,967. The top is 136,500 + 32,690 = 169,190. Divide by 1,967 to get 86.0 F.
  • Carpet, assumed 150: (1,500 × 91 + 150 × 70) / 1,650. The top is 136,500 + 10,500 = 147,000. Divide by 1,650 to get 89.1 F. With an assumed 300 it is 87.5 F.

What the numbers say. Steel drops the skin surface to about 72 F, nearly the floor temperature, so it feels cold at once. Tile lands about 4 degrees below carpet in this model, and about 1 degree below oak. Oak and tile are closer than your feet would suggest, partly because a real wood floor has a finish and a real tile floor has grout, mass below it and a slab that keeps drawing heat. Carpet's air-filled pile gives the warmest contact. The rank order is the point: steel, then tile, then oak, then carpet.

Why a bare floor feels cold in a 70 degree house

The air may be at 70 F while the floor you stand on is at or below that, which can happen over a basement, crawl space or slab. Even a floor at exactly 70 F feels cold, as the example shows. Your feet touch the floor directly, so the heat loss is by conduction, and a floor with high effusivity pulls hard at that contact. A slab below tile can also act as a large cold reservoir; for that side of the story see thermal mass in homes.

What this means for radiant floors, rugs and slippers

  • Radiant floors. Warming the floor changes the second temperature in the formula. As an extra step of our own, an 85 F tile surface against 91 F skin gives (1,500 × 91 + 585 × 85) / 2,085 = 89.3 F. The floor still has high effusivity, but a warmer starting point removes most of the chill. The floor-covering choice then affects how warm the surface gets; see radiant floor heating under tile, wood and carpet, and radiant floor heating basics.
  • Rugs. A rug is a thin layer with low effusivity placed on top of a high-effusivity floor. At first touch, your foot contacts the rug, not the tile, so it feels warmer. The floor underneath is not any warmer, and over time the rug can also slow a radiant floor's output into the room.
  • Slippers. Same idea: a soft, air-filled layer between the skin and the floor. It lowers the heat flow and keeps the skin surface warm.

Cold metal handles and ducts

A steel handle or sheet-metal duct at the same temperature as a wood door feels colder for the same reason. Another assumption of our own: a bare sheet-metal duct carrying cooled air has a surface at 50 F, and a hand at 91 F touches it. Using the steel effusivity above, (1,500 × 91 + 13,200 × 50) / 14,700 = (136,500 + 660,000) / 14,700 = 54.2 F. The skin surface is pulled almost all the way down to the duct temperature. That is why a technician's bare hand on a cold coil cabinet or refrigerant line feels so cold, and why metal that is well below freezing is a real hazard: the federal touch-temperature brief lists skin at or below 0 C (32 F) as the tissue-damage threshold, and pain-level limits near 15 C (59 F) for incidental contact. Wear gloves on cold metal. Insulation on a duct works the same way as a rug: it puts a low-effusivity layer where the hand lands.

Field card: Why Tile and Metal Feel Colder Than Carpet

Same temperature does not mean same feel. Feel follows effusivity.

  • Skin senses heat flow and its own surface temperature, not the material's temperature.
  • Effusivity = square root of (conductivity × density × heat capacity).
  • Contact temperature = (e1 × T1 + e2 × T2) / (e1 + e2).
  • Metal and tile pull heat out fastest; carpet, rugs and wood feel warmer.
  • Warming a floor surface or adding a rug or slippers raises the contact temperature.
  • Cold bare metal can hurt skin. Use gloves and follow site procedures.
