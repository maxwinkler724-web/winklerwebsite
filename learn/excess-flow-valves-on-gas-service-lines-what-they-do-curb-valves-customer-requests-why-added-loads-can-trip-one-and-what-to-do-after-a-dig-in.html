Excess Flow Valves on Gas Service Lines: EFVs, Curb Valves and Added Loads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Between the street main and the meter, most newer homes have a small spring-loaded safety valve that most owners never hear about. It is built for one job: shutting off the gas fast when a service line is broken, usually by a digger. This lesson covers what that valve does, what it does not do, and why a big new appliance can matter to it.

Check the local rules

Check with your gas utility. The valve belongs to the utility side of the system. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026. Rules, costs and procedures vary by utility and state, so ask your utility about your own service line.

What an excess flow valve does

An excess flow valve (EFV) sits inside the service line between the street and the meter. A federal rulemaking document from the pipeline safety agency describes it this way: a spring holds the valve open in normal use, and if flow surges, as it would from a damaged line, the force of the gas overcomes the spring and the valve closes. It stays closed until pressure is restored and then resets itself. Turning appliances on is designed not to close it. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building-science guide adds that EFVs sit on the utility side of the meter, and that the utility owns and maintains them.

What the federal rule says

The rule is 49 CFR 192.383. After April 14, 2017, a gas operator must install an EFV before activating a new or replaced service line that serves a single-family home, or a branch line to one. Multifamily buildings and a single small commercial customer are covered if the known load is 1,000 standard cubic feet per hour (SCFH) or less, based on installed meter capacity. There are exceptions, including lines that do not run at 10 psig or higher through the year, known gas-stream contaminants, interference with maintenance such as blowing liquids from the line, and no compliant valve being commercially available.

The rule also gives existing customers a right to ask. If your service line is 1,000 SCFH or less and no exception applies, you may request an EFV, and the operator must install it at a mutually agreeable date. The operator's rate-setter decides how the cost is divided, so ask about cost first. Operators must tell customers about this right in writing or electronically. The pipeline safety agency's EFV page also says that for new or replaced services with meters above 1,000 SCFH, operators use either a manual service line shut-off valve (such as a curb valve) or an EFV, where appropriate.

Curb valves

A curb valve is a different device. The agency's 2016 rulemaking describes it as a manual shut-off valve buried in the service line at or near the property line, in a protective box, and turned with a removable key or wrench. It cannot close as instantly as an EFV, but it can limit damage, and it has to stay accessible and be operated from time to time to prove it still works. Treat the curb valve as the utility's device. Our own advice: do not dig around it or try to turn it yourself.

What an EFV does not do

An EFV reacts to a large, sudden flow. The national lab guide says EFVs activate only if the service line is severed, and do not activate for a small puncture or small leak. They also do not stop flow inside the house from a small leak or an appliance malfunction, they do not prevent leaks, and they may let a small amount of gas through after closing. They also do not operate on every service line, for example very low-pressure or high-volume lines. So a leak at a union, a flex connector or a pilot is still found by the nose and by a leak check. See Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do.

Why a big new load can matter

An EFV is chosen for a flow range. The 2016 rulemaking says operators should size them on the maximum expected load, including future anticipated loads, and notes that many operators size by meter capacity. The same document records the older worry that EFVs could close by accident and interfere with normal service. A valve sized for a small house may be sitting close to its closing flow once a large appliance is added. Our own plain reading: the closing point is a number printed on the valve, the utility knows it, and the homeowner usually does not. That is why adding a tankless water heater is a call to the utility, not just a plumbing job. The meter and regulator limits are in Gas Meter and Service Regulator Basics, and the full checklist is in Adding a Gas Appliance to an Existing House.

Worked example: sizing against connected load

This is our own example. Every input is an assumption for teaching, not a real house or a real valve. The only cited number is the heating value.

  1. Heating value. The U.S. Energy Information Administration table shows 1,036 Btu per cubic foot as the U.S. average for gas delivered to consumers in 2025. Local values differ, so a utility figure is better when you have it.
  2. Assumed loads. Existing appliances total 180,000 Btu/h. A new tankless water heater adds 199,000 Btu/h. All-on total: 180,000 + 199,000 = 379,000 Btu/h.
  3. Convert to cfh (Btu/h divided by Btu per cubic foot, rounded up). Before: 180,000 / 1,036 = 173.7, so 174 cfh. The tankless alone: 199,000 / 1,036 = 192.1, so 193 cfh. After: 379,000 / 1,036 = 365.8, so 366 cfh.
  4. Assumed EFV closing rating: 300 cfh. This is a made-up number to show the method. Before: 174 is 126 cfh under it. After: 366 is 66 cfh over it. In this example the valve could close the first time the new heater and the other appliances run together, or at a start-up surge.
  5. Check the other limits. Both totals are far below the 1,000 SCFH level in the rule, so the rule still covers this house. But the meter, regulator and pipe also need checks, as in Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method. The sibling lesson uses 1,037 Btu per cubic foot, which is within a rounding step of 1,036.

The point of the example is the comparison, not the numbers: the utility must confirm the actual valve size (or whether the line even has one), because only the utility knows the rating and can replace it. A clocked meter, as in Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input, shows actual use but not the valve rating.

Before and after digging

  • Before. The pipeline safety agency's damage prevention page says to call 811 before every digging project, including small jobs like planting a tree. The call is free, utilities mark their lines, and you wait the required time, respect the marks and dig with care.
  • If you smell gas or hit a line. Our own advice, consistent with the lesson linked above: leave the area, keep ignition sources away, and call 911 and the gas utility from a safe distance. The agency page we read does not list these steps, so follow your utility and local emergency services.
  • If gas simply stops after digging nearby. A closed EFV is one possibility, and a damaged line is another. Our own advice: shut off nothing extra, do not relight appliances, and call the utility to inspect. Per the agency, a closed EFV resets itself once pressure returns, but that follows the utility's repair, not a homeowner fix.

Field card: Excess Flow Valves

An EFV stops a big break, not a small leak.

  • EFV: spring valve in the service line, closes on a sudden surge, resets itself after pressure returns.
  • 49 CFR 192.383: required on new or replaced single-family services (and small loads up to 1,000 SCFH); customers may request one.
  • Curb valve: manual, buried near the property line, utility-operated.
  • It will not catch small leaks or appliance leaks. Leak check and nose still matter.
  • cfh = Btu/h divided by about 1,036. Compare the all-on total to the utility's numbers.
  • New large load: call the utility to confirm EFV, meter and regulator.
  • Call 811 before digging. Smell gas: leave, then call 911 and the utility.
