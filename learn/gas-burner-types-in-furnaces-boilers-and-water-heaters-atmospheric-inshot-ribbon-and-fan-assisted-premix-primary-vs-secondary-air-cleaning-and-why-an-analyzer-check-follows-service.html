Gas Burner Types: Atmospheric, Inshot, Ribbon and Premix

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas burner has one job: mix gas with the right amount of air and hold a steady flame. Furnaces, boilers and water heaters do that in a few different ways, and each way fails in its own pattern. Knowing which type you are looking at tells you where to look first when a flame goes wrong.

Primary air and secondary air

Burner people split combustion air into two streams. Primary air is mixed with the gas before the flame, inside or just ahead of the burner. Secondary air is drawn in around the flame to finish burning what primary air could not. A federal home-performance tech brief tells technicians that when carbon monoxide runs high, the fix may be adjusting the primary and secondary air at the gas burners, and that fuel pressure and draft may need attention first. A federal safety-inspection checklist for combustion appliances also lists adjusting input and primary air as part of the burner check.

Atmospheric (natural draft) burners

An atmospheric burner uses no fan to mix. Gas leaves a small hole, the orifice, as a fast jet. That jet drags room-pressure air into an open end of a mixing tube (a venturi shape). The mix of gas and primary air then flows to the ports, where it burns and pulls in secondary air from around the flame. A national laboratory report on a natural draft burner describes the same idea: a low fuel supply pressure, about 8 inches of water column, with a venturi that induces air without any forced air, and an orifice placed upstream of the throat. The same report notes that the air delivered this way does not rise in a straight line with gas flow.

Many atmospheric burners have an adjustable air shutter at the tube inlet that sets how much primary air gets in. Because nothing pushes the air, these burners are sensitive to anything that blocks the inlet or the tube.

Inshot burners

An inshot burner is a small tube-shaped burner fed by its own orifice. A federal consumer safety report on furnace carbon monoxide lists the test furnace burner as an inshot type with a No. 45 orifice (0.082 inch) at a 3.5 inch water column manifold pressure for a 100,000 Btu/h rating. It is still a natural-draft style burner: the gas jet entrains primary air at the tube mouth, and the flame burns in an open combustion chamber.

Ribbon and slotted-port burners

These are atmospheric-style burners with a different head: instead of round ports, a slotted or ribbon-style flame surface. The mixing principle is the same, so the same dust, lint and tube-blockage problems apply, and the same federal tip applies: dirt or rust that keeps the flame from burning evenly can produce CO. For the flame pattern itself, see the flame color lesson linked below.

Fan-assisted premix burners

Condensing equipment often uses a blower to do the mixing work. In a premix burner, a combustion air blower brings in air, gas is added to it ahead of the burner surface, and the mixture burns at the burner surface. A federal furnace guideline describes high-efficiency furnaces as having a draft inducer or combustion air blower, and says they condense water vapor from the flue gases. The same guideline says that, unlike conventional furnaces, there is no adjustment for CO2 level in these furnaces; you set the gas input first, then verify. A national laboratory report on industrial burners warns that premixed firing can be prone to plugging and flashback, which is a reason these burners and their air paths need to stay clean and tight.

What each type is sensitive to

  • Dust and lint at air openings. Shutters, tube mouths and blower inlets collect lint. Less primary air makes a lazy, yellow-tipped flame and more carbon monoxide. A federal tech brief says dirt and rust on burners can prevent an even flame and cause CO.
  • Spiders and debris in tubes. Tubes and orifices are small enough to be blocked by webs and insects, which starve one burner of mix and change its flame.
  • Carryover. The flame must pass between burners through the crossover. Blocked ports slow that and are covered in the delayed ignition lesson linked below.
  • Flame lifting. Too much air or gas velocity can push a flame off its ports. A national laboratory report defines lean blowoff as the flame lifting above the burner, and a federal inspection checklist says the main burner should not float, lift or flash back. See the lifting-flame lesson linked below.

Cleaning and inspection

With the gas off, power locked out and the appliance cool, follow the maker's service instructions for removing burners. Typical steps are to clear lint and dust from air openings, brush and vacuum the burners and the heat exchanger inlet area, check the tubes for debris, and look at each orifice for damage or blockage without enlarging it. A federal tech brief says to clean gas burners of dirt and rust and to clean the heat exchanger with a brush and vacuum. Reinstall everything in the original position and check for gas leaks as the maker directs.

Why you test with an analyzer afterward

A burner that lights and looks blue can still make carbon monoxide or run with poor air. Flame appearance is a clue, not a measurement. The federal tech brief gives action levels for gas furnaces, boilers and water heaters of 100 ppm CO as measured (200 ppm air-free) and tells technicians to run the unit to steady state before reading. A federal process heating tip sheet likewise says air-to-fuel ratios can be set by flue gas analysis and should be checked regularly. Use the analyzer lesson linked below for reading the numbers, and always follow the maker's limits.

Worked example: how much air does a burner need? (our own example)

A federal furnace guideline says furnaces use roughly 10 cubic feet of air for every cubic foot of gas burned. A federal process heating tip sheet gives the same idea from the heat side: about one cubic foot of air per 100 Btu released. We will use these as the stoichiometric figure (the chemically exact amount, with none left over).

Our assumptions: burner input 60,000 Btu/h; gas heating value 1,000 Btu per cubic foot (a typical round number; the tip sheet's chart uses 1,002); 10 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of gas; 50 percent excess air.

  1. Gas burned: 60,000 Btu/h divided by 1,000 Btu/cf = 60 cubic feet of gas per hour.
  2. Stoichiometric air: 60 cf/h times 10 = 600 cubic feet of air per hour.
  3. Excess air: 600 times 0.50 = 300 cubic feet per hour.
  4. Total air: 600 + 300 = 900 cubic feet per hour, or 900 / 60 = 15 cubic feet per minute.

Check with the other relation: 60,000 Btu/h divided by 100 Btu per cubic foot of air is 600 cf/h, which matches step 2. Check against the guideline: its 13 cfm for an 80,000 Btu/h furnace equals 80 cf/h of gas times 10, divided by 60 minutes, which is about 13.3. Note that the figure is air volume at room conditions; the burner's own mix is only a part of it, and the rest is secondary air.

Field card: Gas Burner Types: Atmospheric, Inshot, Ribbon and Premix

Gas jet pulls the air. Keep the air path clean, then measure.

  • Primary air mixes before the flame; secondary air finishes it around the flame.
  • Atmospheric and inshot burners use the gas jet and a mixing tube; premix burners use a blower.
  • Lint, spiders, dirt and blocked ports upset the mix and the flame.
  • About 10 cubic feet of air per cubic foot of natural gas is the chemically exact amount.
  • Our example: 60,000 Btu/h at 1,000 Btu/cf is 60 cf/h gas, 600 cf/h exact air, 900 cf/h with 50 percent excess.
  • After any burner service, verify combustion with an analyzer.
