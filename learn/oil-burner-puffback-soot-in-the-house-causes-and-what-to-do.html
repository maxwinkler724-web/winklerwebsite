Oil Burner Puffback: Soot in the House, Causes and What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You hear a thud from the basement, and then there is greasy black soot on the ceiling, the curtains and the kitchen counter. That is an oil burner puffback. This lesson explains what happened inside the burner, what usually causes it, what to do in the first minutes, and how annual service lowers the odds of a repeat.

Safety first. Oil burner repair belongs to a qualified technician. If you see flame outside the burner, heavy smoke, or you feel sick, get everyone outside and call 911. Nothing on this page is a repair procedure.

What a puffback is

A puffback is a delayed ignition. Fire Engineering describes it as atomized oil delivered to the combustion chamber but not lit right away. When the oil finally ignites, the result runs from a small thud to a blast strong enough to blow open the firebox door or knock down the flue pipe. Beckett's service bulletins treat delayed ignition as the same family of problem: the flame is slow to establish after oil starts spraying, and a weak ignition arc can cause delayed ignition or puff-backs.

The sooty mess comes from the burst. Montagna's Fire Engineering article calls a puffback usually an emergency and not a fire, but burning oil or a jet of flame can still ignite nearby combustibles.

Why it happens

All the causes below come down to one thing: oil sprays but does not light promptly, so unburned oil and vapor collect before the flame arrives. Sources describe these common reasons.

  • Nozzle trouble. Beckett says a partly plugged nozzle can give an unbalanced spray that misses the ignition arc. EDT Engineers lists a dirty or clogged nozzle as a leading cause.
  • Weak ignition or electrode problems. Beckett's electrical bulletin says the transformer should put out about 10,000 volts open circuit, and that eroded electrode tips widen the gap while electrodes set too far forward can ground to the head. Its CF375 manual lists a 5/32 inch electrode gap for that burner; for other burners, Beckett says to set electrodes to the manufacturer's specifications.
  • Air in the oil line or nozzle adapter. Beckett's CF375 manual warns that failing to bleed the pump properly could cause unstable combustion, hot gas puff-back and heavy smoke. InspectApedia adds that dirt on the fuel unit's shutoff valve seat can let oil leak into the chamber after shutdown.
  • Poor draft. Beckett's bulletin says over-fire draft that is too extreme or too weak upsets combustion air. InspectApedia says a chimney that is too short or clogged can cause inadequate draft and sooty operation.
  • Blocked chimney connector or cracked heat exchanger. EDT lists poor venting from a clogged chimney or connector, or from a cracked heat exchanger, as a cause.
  • Repeated restarts. EDT notes that each failed restart after the primary control shuts the unit down adds more unburned oil. See the next section.

Water, sludge or old oil in the tank can add to nozzle and ignition trouble; see Home Heating Oil Tanks in New Jersey.

Immediate steps

  1. Get safe. If there is fire, heavy smoke, or anyone feels dizzy or ill, go outside and call 911.
  2. Shut the burner off. Turn off the thermostat and the burner switch. Fire Engineering advises shutting down both the burner and the fuel supply, and warns that the red emergency switch at the top of the cellar stairs may be defective or not connected to the burner, so do not rely on it alone. Stay out of the area directly in front of the burner.
  3. Do not keep pressing reset. InspectApedia says to press the reset button only once, because repeated presses when the burner does not run risk a puffback. Beckett's CF375 manual says that if the control locks out three times in a row it goes to restricted lockout, and warns against starting the burner when excess oil has built up in the appliance. The recycle limit exists to prevent excessive oil accumulating in the firing chamber. Details are in Residential Oil Burner Sequence: Cad Cell and Primary Control.
  4. Do not touch the soot yet. Take photos first.
  5. Call a licensed oil burner service company. Fire Engineering says the goal of responders is to mitigate the hazard, with repairs left to a licensed oil burner service.

If the burner locked out after the tank ran dry, air is the likely cause; see Ran Out of Heating Oil: How to Get the Heat Back Safely.

Carbon monoxide

A puffback can also be a venting event. Fire Engineering says that when smoke is diverted from a blocked or dislodged flue into the building, responders should check for carbon monoxide and check occupants for poisoning, evacuating and ventilating if levels are dangerous. InspectApedia likewise warns that a puffback that blows apart the flue connector can release combustion gases into the building. If a CO alarm sounds, or anyone has headaches or nausea, follow Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. Do not wait for the soot to be cleaned first.

Soot cleanup and insurance

Puffback soot is oily, so it smears. ServiceMaster Restoration warns against scrubbing, which can damage drywall and carpet, and against dry cleaning sponges, which it says can push oily soot deeper into surfaces. It also says not to run the heat before the ducts are cleaned out, and to wear protective gear including a respirator. PuroClean, another restoration company, says water and soap can spread oil-based soot and cause permanent staining, and that the process runs from assessment and containment to odor removal. These are restoration companies describing their own trade, so treat their advice as a starting point and ask your insurer.

On insurance, both companies advise documenting damage with photos before cleanup. PuroClean says most homeowner policies treat puffback cleanup as sudden and accidental smoke damage, but that furnace repairs and losses made worse by do-it-yourself cleaning are typically not covered. Your own policy governs. Call your insurer and read the policy language before you sign for any work.

Prevention: annual service

EDT Engineers present yearly service as the main way to reduce puffback risk. The Massachusetts Department of Energy Resources tip sheet says an oil burner should have an annual tune-up and cleaning before the heating season begins. A good tune-up touches the causes above. Beckett's annual checklist calls for a new nozzle of equal size, electrodes set to the maker's specification, an inspection of insulators for cracks, a draft check, and a smoke test. See Oil Burner Nozzles, Pump Pressure and Firing Rate for the nozzle side, and Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat for the venting side. Also treat sooty, smelly or noisy running as a warning, as InspectApedia advises, and call before the burner fails.

Worked example: reading draft against Beckett's range

Hypothetical numbers for teaching, not from a real house. Beckett's tune-up checklist says to check draft over the fire and set it to specification, usually 0.02 inch W.C., and its delayed-ignition bulletin gives over-fire draft as -0.01 to -0.02 inch W.C. unless the appliance maker says otherwise. It says the breech (stack) draft is generally slightly higher than the over-fire reading.

Suppose a technician measures over-fire draft of -0.05 inch W.C. and breech draft of -0.08 inch W.C. on a burner with a history of rumbling at startup.

  • Beckett's upper limit is -0.02. The reading is -0.05. The amount outside the range is 0.05 - 0.02 = 0.03 inch W.C.
  • As a ratio, 0.05 ÷ 0.02 = 2.5 times the strongest draft in the range.
  • Breech minus over-fire: 0.08 - 0.05 = 0.03 inch W.C. The breech is stronger, which matches Beckett's general statement.

Beckett's bulletin only says that draft which is too extreme or too weak disturbs combustion air; it does not say what to change, and neither do we. The technician would follow the appliance maker's draft value, adjust the draft regulator as Beckett's checklist describes, then recheck. A reading far from the range is a clue to look at the chimney, regulator and air setting, not proof of the cause.

Field card: Oil Burner Puffback: Soot in the House, Causes and What to Do

Oil sprays, no flame, then a bang. Reset once, then call.

  • Puffback = delayed ignition: oil sprays but lights late.
  • Causes: dirty nozzle, electrode or ignition faults, air in the oil line, poor draft, blocked chimney or cracked heat exchanger.
  • First steps: get safe, shut off burner and fuel, do not repeat resets.
  • Beckett: over-fire draft typically -0.01 to -0.02 inch W.C. unless the maker says otherwise.
  • Check for CO. Photograph soot before cleanup. Call your insurer.
  • Prevent with annual service before the heating season.
