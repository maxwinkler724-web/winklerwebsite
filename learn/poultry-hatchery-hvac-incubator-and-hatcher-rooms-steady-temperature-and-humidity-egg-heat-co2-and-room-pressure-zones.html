Poultry Hatchery HVAC: Incubator and Hatcher Rooms, Egg Heat, CO2 and Pressure Zones

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hatchery looks like a row of big cabinets, but the building around them is an HVAC job. The machines make their own warmth and humidity, so the room has to hand them steady air, carry off the heat that late-stage eggs give back, and keep dusty, downy hatcher air from drifting toward clean eggs.

Check the plan and the owner

Check the plan. Hatchery settings come from the owner's poultry company and the machine instructions. This page is general education from extension and journal sources. Do not change setpoints or pressures on a running hatchery without the hatchery manager.

What the room has to give the machines

The machines do the fine control of egg temperature. The room's job is to give them a calm, steady supply. A University of Georgia poultry tip lists 75 to 80°F and 50 to 60 percent relative humidity for setter and hatcher rooms. A trade magazine piece by a hatchery specialist describes air-handling units that condition incoming air to 24 to 26°C (our conversion: about 75 to 79°F) and 60 to 70 percent relative humidity, and says swings of about 0.3°C can change embryo metabolism. The takeaway for a tech is that a hunting thermostat, a cycling humidifier or a coil that ices up is a hatchability problem, not just a comfort problem.

For scale, Mississippi State Extension says large commercial units run about 99°F and small units about 100°F, that high temperature does more harm than low, and that a typical chicken setting uses a wet bulb near 85 to 87°F, which they put at about 57 to 60 percent relative humidity. Their extension also says the exhaust from a hatcher or incubator should be ducted outside the building. Humidity added to the room air is a supply-side job: see commercial steam humidifiers for dispersion and water quality.

Egg heat late in incubation

Eggs are not just a heat load on a shelf. They are small heaters that get stronger as the chick grows. A Brazilian Journal of Poultry Science review (Molenaar and others, 2010) puts embryo heat production at about 140 mW for a 62 g egg at day 18, and says it almost doubles in broiler embryos after internal pipping. The University of Alberta calorimeter paper (Segura and others, 2006) reports a peak of 137 mW per egg at day 18. Both are close, so we use 0.14 W per egg below. The same Mississippi State publication that sets the machine temperatures notes that embryo heat is greatest in the second half of incubation.

Worked example: one hatchery room, late-stage eggs

These are our own assumptions, not a design. We assume 20,000 eggs at day 18 are in the machines and that all of their heat reaches the room air as sensible heat. We assume the room air can warm 10°F between supply and exhaust.

  1. Heat from eggs: 20,000 eggs × 0.14 W = 2,800 W.
  2. Convert to Btu/h: 2,800 W × 3.412 = 9,554 Btu/h (about 9,550).
  3. Air to carry it: cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT) = 9,554 ÷ (1.08 × 10) = 9,554 ÷ 10.8 = 885 cfm.
  4. If heat nearly doubles after pipping (Molenaar and others), the same math gives about 19,100 Btu/h and 1,770 cfm at the same 10°F rise. Machine cooling and exhaust paths change the real split.

Compare that with the fresh-air minimum for breathing. The University of Georgia poultry tip says 1,000 eggs need about 143 ft³ of fresh air per day on day 18. For 20,000 eggs that is 2,860 ft³ per day, which is 2,860 ÷ 1,440 min = about 2 cfm (our arithmetic). Heat removal needs hundreds of cfm while breathing needs a couple, so the cooling air, not the oxygen, sizes the supply. The 1.08 factor is explained in the HVAC heat transfer formulas lesson.

CO2 limits

The Georgia tip says hatchability starts to fall above 0.5 percent CO2 in the setter, with large losses near 1.0 percent, and that hatchers tolerate about 0.75 percent. It says the best place to measure is the exhaust duct of the setter or hatcher. The magazine piece agrees with the 0.5 percent setter ceiling and adds at least 20.6 percent oxygen in the fresh air. Outdoor air in a clean location is far below those numbers, so CO2 trouble usually means recirculated or blocked air. Mississippi State warns against small rooms where the windows and doors are shut and exhaust air is recycled, and says a machine near full should not have its dampers set more than half closed. A barn comparison is in poultry and livestock barn ventilation.

Hatcher exhaust: down and dust

A trade magazine article on cross-contamination says chick down is easily airborne, should not build up in ducts, and that hatcher exhaust should go directly to plenums that are easy to clean and disinfect. It also says ducts should carry clean, unused air only. For an HVAC tech that means: do not tie hatcher exhaust into a return that feeds other rooms, build exhaust paths with access doors, and plan on frequent cleaning of screens, filters and ducts. Our own note: a plugged exhaust filter raises machine room pressure and can starve the hatcher of air, so check it at each visit.

Clean-to-dirty pressure zones

The same article names the zones: egg arrival, setter room, transfer, hatcher room, and the chick handling and dispatch area, with the setter room the cleanest and chick processing relatively dirty. It says the setter room should be at overpressure relative to the hatcher rooms and that clean should never meet dirty. A University of California 4-H poultry sheet says the incubator room should be under positive pressure and can be checked with a manometer that reads inches of water column. The magazine piece quotes +5 to +15 Pa in clean areas, which is about 0.02 to 0.06 in. w.c. (our conversion). These are small differences, so the doors, transfer openings and balancing dampers matter. For the pressure-monitor habit in another setting, see negative and positive pressure rooms in healthcare.

Egg storage room cooling

The University of Georgia egg holding tip lists hatching egg holding temperatures of 65 to 69°F for under 4 days, 60 to 65°F for 4 to 7 days and 55 to 60°F for 7 days or longer, and says embryo development stops near 68 to 70°F. It warns that eggs sweat when moved into warmer air, because moisture condenses on the cooler shell, and that sweating may help contaminants enter the pores. So the egg room needs a steady setpoint and a way to warm eggs before they move: see also generator room ventilation for the backup plant that keeps the cooler running.

Backup power and alarms

A trade magazine article by a hatchery specialist calls an emergency generator with automatic start the only structural fix for power loss, with regular testing and fuel checks. It says that in a closed machine eggs past 9 to 10 days tend to overheat, and in a stopped closed hatcher most embryos and chicks die within 30 minutes. It ranks hatchers first, then setters holding embryos at 15 days or more. Mississippi State Extension suggests an alarm with two sensors, one for low temperature (below 97 to 98°F) and one for high temperature (above 102°F). For the room, our own checklist is: backup power on the room air handler and exhaust fans, not just on the machines; an alarm for room temperature, humidity, pressure and power; and a written test.

Field card: Poultry Hatchery HVAC

Steady supply, strong exhaust, clean to dirty.

  • Room air for machines: 75 to 80°F, 50 to 60 percent RH (University of Georgia).
  • Late-stage egg heat: about 0.14 W per egg at day 18. Multiply by 3.412 for Btu/h.
  • Air to carry heat: Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT).
  • CO2 targets: under 0.5 percent in setters, about 0.75 percent in hatchers; test at the exhaust duct.
  • Setter room positive to hatcher room; hatcher exhaust ducted outside.
  • Egg room: 55 to 69°F by holding time; avoid sweating.
  • Backup power covers hatchers first.
