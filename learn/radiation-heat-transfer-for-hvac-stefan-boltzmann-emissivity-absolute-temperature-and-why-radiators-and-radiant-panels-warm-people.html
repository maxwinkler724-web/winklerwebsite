Radiation Heat Transfer for HVAC: Stefan-Boltzmann, Emissivity and Why Radiators Warm People

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A cast-iron radiator, a warm floor and a ceiling panel all send heat across a room without touching you and without moving the air. That is thermal radiation, and it follows a rule with a fourth power in it. This lesson shows the rule, the temperature scale that makes it work, why a painted surface and a shiny one behave so differently, and how to turn the rule into a simple coefficient you can add to the convection number.

The Stefan-Boltzmann law

Every surface gives off thermal radiation, and the amount rises with the fourth power of its absolute temperature. For an ideal emitter (a black body) the emissive power is sigma times T to the fourth. Lecture notes from Virginia Tech and from MIT's thermal-energy notes both give sigma as 5.67 x 10-8 W/m2K4 with temperature in kelvin, and the national-lab page we read lists it as 5.670373 x 10-8. In the English units used in U.S. heating work, a textbook value is 0.1714 x 10-8 Btu/h-ft2-R4. A real surface emits only a fraction of the ideal amount, called its emissivity, a number from 0 to 1 (the Virginia Tech notes define it as the ratio of the surface's emissive power to that of a black body).

Because of the fourth power, a hotter surface gains output much faster than its temperature rises. Double the absolute temperature and the radiation goes up sixteen times; the conduction, convection and radiation lesson shows that comparison with metric numbers. Here we stay in Fahrenheit-based units and focus on radiators.

Absolute temperature: add 459.67

The law only works on an absolute scale. Kelvin is Celsius plus 273.15. Rankine is the Fahrenheit-sized absolute scale: Rankine equals Fahrenheit plus 459.67 (our own conversion; people often round to 460). So 180 F is 639.67 R, 120 F is 579.67 R and 68 F is 527.67 R. Using Fahrenheit numbers directly is the classic mistake. In the example below it would give about 1.6 Btu/h instead of 138.7, roughly 87 times too small, so always convert first.

Emissivity: painted versus bare polished metal

MIT's thermal-energy lecture notes (Part 3, Section 9, Table 9.1) show the spread. Emittance is near unity for rough surfaces such as ceramics or oxidized metals and about 0.02 for polished metals or silvered reflectors. Paint is high whatever the color: oil paints at 40 C run about 0.92 to 0.96, white paint 0.89 to 0.97 and black gloss 0.9. Bare metals are low when polished and climb when they oxidize: polished steel 0.07 to 0.1, highly polished copper about 0.02 at 90 C, but black oxidized copper 0.76, heavily oxidized aluminum 0.2 to 0.33 versus 0.04 to 0.06 for polished aluminum, and rough oxidized sheet steel 0.8. Treat these as guideline values; the table gives them at stated temperatures and conditions only. The practical lesson: a dull, painted or dirty surface radiates nearly like an ideal emitter, and a clean polished one radiates very little. The same property is why bare shiny metal fools a thermal camera; see infrared thermography for HVAC and electrical gear.

Why radiant surfaces warm people and objects directly

The Department of Energy's radiant-heating page (an archived copy we read) describes radiant heating as delivery of heat directly from the hot surface to the people and objects in the room by radiation. It lists radiant floors (air, electric or hydronic) as the main type and notes that they also rely heavily on convection. Its wall and ceiling panels work on a line-of-sight basis, so you feel the most warmth close to the panel. Radiation crosses the room without needing the air to carry it, so a warm panel can make a person comfortable while the air itself is not especially warm. The other half of the picture is that you also radiate to cold walls and windows, covered in why a room feels cold at 70 degrees. For floor systems, see radiant floor heating basics.

Why shiny pipe loses less by radiation

Emission scales with emissivity, so at the same temperature a polished pipe or duct radiates a small fraction of what a painted one does. That helps when you want to keep heat in, and it hurts when you want a radiator to give heat out. It is also why a surface with a metallic finish gives up less to the room: the 1925 federal letter described in the radiator covers and paint lesson reports an aluminum-painted radiator giving off about 80 percent as much heat as the same radiator in ordinary paint. The loss is only partial because the rest of the output is convection, which paint does not change much. Remember that a bare metal surface that dulls with oxidation, or that gets painted, loses its advantage as a heat saver.

The linearized radiation coefficient

Convection is usually written as h x area x temperature difference. The ORNL MEASUR tools page gives a radiation coefficient with the same form: hrad = sigma x emissivity x (Ts4 - Tsurroundings4) / (Ts - Tsurroundings), in absolute temperatures, so it can be added directly to the convective coefficient. It also notes this is valid when the temperature difference is small relative to the absolute temperatures. Factoring the difference of fourth powers (our own algebra) gives hrad = emissivity x sigma x (Ts + Tr) x (Ts2 + Tr2), with every temperature in Rankine. For a surface at 180 F and room surfaces at 68 F, a perfect emitter would have h of about 1.38 Btu/h-ft2-F. Multiply by the emissivity to get the real coefficient, then by the temperature difference in F.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions we chose: 1 sq ft of painted radiator surface with emissivity 0.9; surface temperature 180 F; all room surfaces at 68 F; the radiator is small compared with the room, so the net exchange is q = e x sigma x (Ts4 - Tr4); sigma = 0.1714 x 10-8 Btu/h-ft2-R4. These are illustrative inputs, not measurements of any product.

  1. Convert: Ts = 180 + 459.67 = 639.67 R. Tr = 68 + 459.67 = 527.67 R.
  2. Fourth powers: 639.674 = 1.6743 x 1011. 527.674 = 7.7526 x 1010. Difference = 8.990 x 1010 R4.
  3. q = 0.9 x 0.1714 x 10-8 x 8.990 x 1010 = about 138.7 Btu/h per sq ft.
  4. Cooler water, 120 F: Ts = 579.67 R, Ts4 = 1.1291 x 1011, difference = 3.538 x 1010, so q = 0.9 x 0.1714 x 10-8 x 3.538 x 1010 = about 54.6 Btu/h.
  5. Polished surface, emissivity 0.1, back at 180 F: q = 0.1 x 0.1714 x 10-8 x 8.990 x 1010 = about 15.4 Btu/h.

Compare. Dropping from 180 F to 120 F cuts the temperature difference to the room by 54 percent (112 F down to 52 F) but cuts radiation by about 61 percent, because the fourth power makes radiation fall faster than the temperature difference. The polished surface gives 15.4 / 138.7 = 11 percent of the painted output, about one ninth. Check with the coefficient: 0.9 x 1.376 x 112 = 138.7. The numbers are radiation only; a real radiator also loses heat by convection, so total output falls by less than these ratios when you cool or polish the surface. This helps explain why low-temperature water makes some radiators feel weak, which the steam-to-hot-water conversion lesson takes up for output.

Field habits

  • Convert to Rankine or kelvin before using any fourth-power formula.
  • Paint, dirt and oxidation raise emissivity; polishing lowers it.
  • A hotter surface radiates more than in proportion to its temperature difference.
  • Low emissivity cuts radiation only; convection and conduction are unchanged.

Field card: Radiation Heat Transfer for HVAC

Fourth power, absolute scale, emissivity decides.

  • Radiation = emissivity x sigma x (Ts^4 - Tr^4), with temperatures in R (F + 459.67) or K.
  • Sigma = 5.67e-8 W/m2K4, or about 0.1714e-8 Btu/h-ft2-R4.
  • Our own example: painted surface (0.9) at 180 F to 68 F room surfaces: about 138.7 Btu/h per sq ft; at 120 F: about 54.6; polished (0.1) at 180 F: about 15.4.
  • Linearized: h_rad = e x sigma x (Ts + Tr)(Ts^2 + Tr^2); add to the convection coefficient.
  • Radiant panels and floors warm people and objects directly; shiny pipe radiates less.
