Reading Pump Curves and System Curves: Finding the Operating Point

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pump does not deliver the flow on its nameplate or the flow you wish for. It delivers whatever flow makes its own head equal the head the piping needs at that flow. This lesson shows how to read the pump's curve, draw the piping's curve, find where they cross, and check the answer on a running pump with two gauges.

What a pump curve shows

The Hydraulic Institute describes a performance curve as the head a specific pump model makes at flows from zero up to its maximum, usually plotted together with efficiency, input power and NPSHR. Head is on the vertical axis in feet, flow is on the horizontal axis in gpm. Taco's pump-curve article says hydronic circulator curves always show head energy added on the vertical axis, starting at the shut-off head (no flow) and sloping down as flow rises. Bell & Gossett (Xylem) points out why head in feet is used: unlike pressure, it does not depend on water temperature or density.

Look for these items on a maker's curve sheet:

  • Head-flow lines, one per impeller size. Armstrong notes that a curve is valid only for one impeller diameter and speed. A smaller impeller gives a lower curve. Always match the line to the impeller actually installed.
  • Efficiency islands. Armstrong describes these as U-shaped contours joining points of equal efficiency, such as 60, 70 and 80 percent. The center is the best efficiency point, or BEP.
  • Motor horsepower lines. Bell & Gossett shows brake horsepower in standard motor sizes, so you can read the motor size needed at your operating point. Grundfos shows the same idea: if the pump needs a little over 2.5 brake horsepower at the duty point, a 3 hp motor is suggested.
  • NPSHr. Net positive suction head required rises with flow. Grundfos describes it as the suction head needed to avoid cavitation at that point. Bell & Gossett says it matters mostly for open systems and hot or volatile liquids, and seldom limits a closed hydronic loop. For the failure itself, see Pump Cavitation Basics.

What a system curve is

The system curve is the piping's side of the story: how much head the circuit consumes at each flow. In a closed hydronic loop there is no static lift to add, so Bell & Gossett says the head changes as the square of the change in flow: h2 = h1 × (Q2 / Q1)2. Double the flow and the head needed goes up four times. (Tube friction alone follows a slightly lower power; see Hot Water Heating Pipe Sizing. The square law is the standard planning shortcut for a whole system.) Start from one known point, such as the design flow and its head loss, and you can draw the entire curve.

Where they cross

Plot both on the same axes. Taco says it plainly: where the curves cross is the operating point, and a vertical line down to the flow axis gives the flow. Bell & Gossett adds why: the intersection is the only point that satisfies both laws at once. At any other flow, the pump would make more or less head than the pipe uses.

Best efficiency point and why far left or right hurts

The Hydraulic Institute defines BEP as the flow and head where efficiency is highest for a given speed and impeller diameter. It adds that a pump is normally specified to run near BEP, where vibration and noise are lower. The Institute names a preferred operating region around BEP, roughly 90 to 110 percent of BEP flow at its narrowest and 70 to 120 percent at its widest, depending on the pump. Wilo's training page gives 70 to 120 percent as typical.

  • Too far left (low flow). Grundfos warns about running near shut-off: low efficiency and heat building in the water inside the pump, with damage that may show within one to six months of continuous minimum flow. Wilo adds higher radial thrust on the shaft, more vibration and more seal and bearing wear.
  • Too far right (high flow). Efficiency falls, and Grundfos and Wilo both flag a higher cavitation risk and possible motor overload. Bell & Gossett advises picking a pump at or left of the middle of its published curve so there is room before the end of the curve.

What throttling or adding a zone does

Armstrong says the system curve gets steeper as a discharge valve closes. Throttling therefore does not change the pump; it moves the crossing point up and to the left along the same pump curve. Opening more zones does the reverse: the system curve gets flatter and the operating point slides right. Taco notes that adjusting a valve or other part changes the system curve and so shifts the operating point along the pump curve. That is why Taco says flat-curve circulators suit zone-valve systems: flow through a zone changes less as other zone valves open and close.

Worked example

The system. An invented hydronic loop needs 40 gpm and loses 30 ft of head at that flow. It is a closed loop, so there is no static lift to add.

Step 1: system curve. Use h = 30 × (Q / 40)2.

  • 20 gpm: 30 × (0.5)2 = 30 × 0.25 = 7.5 ft
  • 30 gpm: 30 × (0.75)2 = 30 × 0.5625 = 16.9 ft
  • 40 gpm: 30 × 1 = 30.0 ft
  • 50 gpm: 30 × (1.25)2 = 30 × 1.5625 = 46.9 ft

Step 2: the pump. The table below is an invented pump with its BEP set at 40 gpm. It is not a maker's data, so the numbers only teach the method.

Step 3: find the crossing. At 40 gpm the pump makes 33 ft but the pipe uses 30 ft, so the pump has head to spare and flow rises. At 50 gpm the pump makes 22 ft and the pipe wants 46.9 ft, so flow cannot go that high. The crossing lies between. Between 40 and 50 the pump drops 1.1 ft per gpm. At 41 gpm the pump gives 33 − 1.1 = 31.9 ft and the pipe needs 30 × (41/40)2 = 31.5 ft, still slightly short of equal. At 41.2 gpm they match near 31.7 ft. Operating point: about 41 gpm and 32 ft, which is 103 percent of BEP flow, comfortably inside the preferred region. Pump efficiency there is about 65 percent.

Step 4: motor check. Bell & Gossett gives water horsepower as gpm × head × specific gravity ÷ 3960. For us, 41 × 31.7 × 1.0 ÷ 3960 = 0.33 hp. Brake horsepower is 0.33 ÷ 0.65 = about 0.5 hp, so a small motor is fine.

Step 5: throttle it. Suppose a balancing valve is closed until the loop needs 60 ft at 40 gpm, double the original. The new curve is 60 × (Q / 40)2. Working the same crossing gives about 32 gpm and 39 ft, which is 80 percent of BEP flow and an efficiency near 61 percent. The pump moved left along its curve, as Armstrong describes. Adding zones instead (say the loop falls to 21 ft at 40 gpm) gives about 45 gpm and 27 ft, 113 percent of BEP, still in the region but heading toward the right-hand limits.

Step 6: gauge check on a running pump. You read 12 psi at the suction and 25 psi at the discharge, water. The Pumps & Systems article gives the formula: head (ft) = differential pressure (psi) × 2.31 ÷ specific gravity.

  • Differential: 25 − 12 = 13 psi
  • Head: 13 × 2.31 ÷ 1.0 = 30.0 ft
  • On our pump table, 30 ft falls between 40 gpm (33 ft) and 50 gpm (22 ft). Distance from 33 ft: 33 − 30.0 = 3.0 ft, and 3.0 ÷ 1.1 ft per gpm = 2.7 gpm.
  • Flow: 40 + 2.7 = about 43 gpm.

The gauges say about 43 gpm and the calculated crossing says 41 gpm. That is close. One psi is 2.3 ft, which is about 2 gpm on this pump, so a gauge error of one psi would explain the gap. In our own experience, a mismatch much larger than that usually means the real piping differs from the drawing, or the pump has the wrong impeller, a worn impeller, or a clogged strainer. Taco's example for hot water uses a density of 61 lb/ft³ at 160°F and its factor of 144 ÷ 61 = 2.36 in place of 2.31, so hot loops read about 2 percent higher.

Cautions for field readings

  • Pumps & Systems says you need the correct curve for the pump, matched to the installed impeller, and gauges that are intact and calibrated.
  • For speed changes by drive, see Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans. For chilled-water loops, see Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T.

Field card: Reading Pump and System Curves

The pump runs where the two curves cross.

  • System curve in a closed loop: h2 = h1 × (Q2 / Q1)2.
  • Operating point = pump curve meets system curve; drop straight down for gpm.
  • Stay near BEP; 70 to 120 percent of BEP flow is a typical preferred region.
  • Throttling steepens the system curve and moves the point left; more zones move it right.
  • Gauge check: head (ft) = psi difference × 2.31 ÷ SG, then find that head on the curve.
  • Use the curve for the installed impeller, and good gauges.
