Hacksaws and Reciprocating Saws on HVAC Installs: Choosing Blades and Cutting Safely

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most install cutting is done with a hand hacksaw or a reciprocating saw: threaded rod for hangers, strut, pipe stubs, sheet-metal openings. A blade with the wrong tooth spacing grabs, chatters and breaks, and a saw that is not clamped or aimed carefully can hurt you. This lesson gives a simple rule for choosing teeth per inch, then the habits that keep the cut clean and your hands, eyes and ears safe.

Teeth per inch and the three-teeth rule

Teeth per inch (TPI) is the number of teeth along one inch of blade. Fine blades have many small teeth; coarse blades have few big ones. Shop guidance for band saws from a university machine-shop manual says there should be at least two teeth in the material when cutting aluminum and three when cutting steel. A band saw is a different machine, but the idea carries to a hand hacksaw and a reciprocating saw: if fewer teeth are in the cut, each tooth takes a bigger bite, the tooth can snag on the edge of the wall and the blade can chatter or strip teeth. For steel on installs we use three as our working minimum. The rule gives a quick check: minimum TPI = 3 divided by the wall thickness in inches, for a blade cutting straight through a flat wall.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not from a source. Assumptions: we cut straight across a flat wall; the wall thicknesses below are made-up round numbers; and the blades on the shelf are 18, 24 and 32 TPI, the values a university shop guide lists for a power hacksaw blade. Rule: minimum TPI = 3 divided by thickness.

  • 0.250 in solid rod: 3 / 0.250 = 12 TPI. Next blade up on the shelf is 18 TPI. Choose 18.
  • 0.188 in wall: 3 / 0.188 = 16 TPI. Choose 18.
  • 0.120 in wall: 3 / 0.120 = 25 TPI. Choose 32.
  • 0.060 in sheet: 3 / 0.060 = 50 TPI. No blade on our shelf gets there, even 32 TPI puts only 32 x 0.060 = 1.9 teeth in the cut.

The last line is the lesson for thin sheet and thin-wall tubing. When the rule cannot be met, do not just push a coarse blade harder. Use the finest blade you have, tilt the saw so the blade crosses the wall at a shallow angle (more teeth touch at once), support the sheet close to the cut so it does not vibrate, or use snips for sheet. Round tube is a bit kinder than a flat wall because the blade meets the wall at an angle, but start with the flat-wall number as a safe check.

Blade type, length and stroke

  • Bi-metal blades. A bi-metal blade pairs hardened tooth edges with a more flexible body, so it can cut steel pipe, strut and rod and bend a little without snapping. A plain carbon blade is stiffer and more brittle. For mixed metal work on a ladder or in an attic we keep bi-metal blades in the bag. (Our own field habit.)
  • Blade length. For a reciprocating saw the blade should be long enough to pass fully through the work with room to spare; a short blade in a thick pipe leaves no clearance for the shoe. A hand hacksaw is used with long, smooth strokes that use most of the blade so wear is spread over all the teeth. (Our own habit.)
  • Stroke and speed. A university shop text notes a portable power hacksaw with a 10 inch blade and a 4 inch stroke at 115 strokes per minute. A university safety sheet for reciprocating saws says to start metal cuts at a slow speed and increase gradually.
  • Install direction. Hand hacksaw teeth cut on the push stroke, so install the blade with teeth pointing away from the handle and tension it until it is straight. (Our own habit.)

Threaded rod, strut and pipe

For hanger rod, thread two nuts onto the rod past the cut line, cut, then back the nuts off over the end. That cleans up the first threads. Thin steel strut has long, thin legs, so use a fine tooth blade and cut with the shoe held firmly against the strut. For pipe, mark a line around it so you can see whether the blade is wandering. For copper refrigerant tubing, see the related tubing lesson; a tube cutter is the better tool there.

Clamp the work

A federal workplace safety booklet on hand and power tools says to secure work with clamps or a vise, freeing both hands to operate the tool. Several university sheets say the same. On an install that can mean a pipe vise on the tailgate, a locking clamp on strut, or a helper steadying the piece only when it cannot be clamped and their hands are clear of the blade path. Keep a light grip on the saw, let the teeth do the work and do not lean on a blade that is bending.

Check behind the surface before you cut

A university safety sheet for reciprocating saws lists injury from unseen hazards and binding among the hazards of the tool. Plunge cuts into drywall, a wall cavity or a floor can hit a wire, a water or gas pipe or a duct. Before you cut, look from the other side or through an access hole, trace where cables enter the box, shut off circuits you can identify and keep the blade shallow. Do not plunge blindly into a closed wall. For the approach to finding wires and joists, see the register opening lesson linked below, and the drilling and coring lesson for hidden wires and tendons.

Kickback and pinching

A bound blade can snap the saw sideways or back toward you. A university safety document describes an anti-kickback feature on power saws as one meant to reduce the chance of the workpiece being thrown back at the worker. The best controls on a reciprocating saw are our own habits: hold the shoe flat against the work, start with the blade not touching, never start a cut with the blade pushing on the surface, support the offcut so it does not pinch the blade as it drops, and let go of the trigger as soon as the blade binds. The same university sheet adds that if metal cannot be cut from an edge, drill a hole larger than the widest part of the blade first.

Protect eyes, ears and hands

  • Eyes. Wear safety glasses for every cut; metal chips and broken blade pieces fly. See the related eye protection lesson.
  • Ears. A federal workplace safety page says its permissible noise limit is 90 dBA for an 8 hour day and that hearing conservation is required at an 85 dBA average. The same page offers a rule of thumb: if you need to raise your voice to speak to someone 3 feet away, the noise might be over 85 dBA. A federal health institute recommends a limit of 85 dBA averaged over eight hours. A saw in a small room or against sheet metal can be loud, so wear hearing protection.
  • Hands and clothing. A university sheet says to keep hair, clothing and gloves away from moving parts and not to wear loose clothing or jewelry. It also says to unplug the saw before changing the blade, to check the cord and plug, and to tag out a damaged tool. A cordless saw needs the battery removed for the same step.

Deburr the cut end

A saw leaves sharp burrs and thin slivers on the cut edge. Our own habit is to file or ream the end, knock the burr off the inside of tubing and pipe, and wipe the chips away before the piece goes into the system. Burrs cut hands, snag insulation and, inside a pipe, can shed into the system. Dull and dirty blades are a hazard too: a university sheet says to replace dull blades and clean dirty ones, and another says dull tools can be more hazardous than sharp ones.

Field card: Hacksaws and Reciprocating Saws on HVAC Installs: Choosing Blades and Cutting Safely

Three teeth in the cut, clamp it, look behind it.

  • Minimum TPI = 3 divided by wall thickness in inches (our own check for steel).
  • When the rule cannot be met, angle the cut, support the sheet, or use snips.
  • Clamp or vise the work so both hands run the tool.
  • Check behind walls and floors before any plunge cut.
  • Hold the shoe flat, support the offcut, release the trigger if the blade binds.
  • Wear eye and hearing protection; unplug or remove the battery to change blades.
  • Deburr every cut end.
