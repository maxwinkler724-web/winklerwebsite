Pests in Attics, Crawlspaces and Rooftop Units: What HVAC Techs Should Know

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The equipment is not the only thing living in the attic. Condenser cabinets and rooftop unit panels shelter wasps, crawlspaces shelter spiders and rodents, and old bird or bat droppings can sit under insulation. This lesson covers what the CDC, NIOSH and university extension pages say about each hazard, and where the line is between handling it yourself and stopping to call pest control.

Check your own situation first

This is general education, not medical advice. Your employer's procedures, local health department and a doctor come first. If you have a known sting allergy, or a weak immune system, ask your doctor before doing any of the cleanup work described here.

Where each hazard turns up

Stinging insects

NIOSH says nests of bees, wasps and hornets may be in trees, under roof eaves or on equipment, and that they are most active in warmer months. An Oklahoma State University extension fact sheet adds that mud daubers build mud nests in attics, and that yellowjackets nest mostly underground or in enclosed spaces such as wall voids and attics. Open the access panel on a rooftop unit or a condenser top on a summer day and that is the kind of place you should expect to find one.

  • Before you open it: NIOSH advises light-colored, smooth-finished clothing that covers as much skin as possible, no perfumed products, and a clean work area, since discarded food attracts insects.
  • If one insect is flying near you: stay calm and still. NIOSH says swatting may cause a sting.
  • If several attack: run to get away, preferably indoors or into shade. Do not jump in water, because some stinging insects hover above it.
  • After a sting: have someone stay with you, wash the site with soap and water, and remove the stinger by wiping it with gauze or scraping it with a fingernail. Never squeeze it or use tweezers. Ice reduces swelling, and scratching makes it worse.

If you have a sting allergy

NIOSH says stings can cause severe allergic reactions that need immediate emergency care and can be fatal. It advises workers with a history of severe reactions to carry an epinephrine autoinjector and wear medical identification. The Oklahoma State fact sheet says serious reactions can begin within 10 to 30 minutes and that most reported deaths occur within one hour. It lists hives, nausea, vomiting, asthma, dizziness, confusion, a blood pressure drop or unconsciousness as warning signs, and says epinephrine is the first step for anaphylaxis. It also recommends seeing an allergist if you suspect you are allergic. In practice that means: tell your partner where your injector is, never work a nest-prone panel alone, and treat a sting as an emergency, not a nuisance.

Spiders and ticks

A University of Kentucky entomology fact sheet says brown recluse spiders hide in basements, attics and crawl spaces, around ducts and registers, and in boxes of stored items. Black widows are mostly outdoors under boards and debris, and indoors in places like garages and sheds. Bites usually happen when you lift an object or reach into a hidden spot. It advises heavy gloves when moving materials and a bright flashlight to inspect hidden areas first. Seek prompt medical care after a black widow bite. A recluse bite is often painless at first, with redness and swelling appearing three to eight hours later, and prompt care can reduce ulceration. See our work gloves lesson for glove choice.

For ticks, which matter most at the condenser pad, in brush and at open crawlspace edges, NIOSH advises long sleeves and pants tucked into boots or socks, an EPA-registered repellent used as the label directs, and a daily check of skin and clothes, especially hair, underarms and groin. It says to treat clothing and gear (not skin) with a product containing 0.5% permethrin, and to wash and dry work clothes in a hot dryer. To remove a tick: use fine-tipped tweezers, grasp it as close to the skin as possible, pull the body away with a steady motion and clean the area with soap and water. If you get symptoms, see a doctor and mention that you work where ticks live.

Mouse droppings and hantavirus

The CDC names the deer mouse as the U.S. carrier of the hantavirus that causes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome. People are infected through contact with rodents, especially their urine, droppings or saliva, and stirring up fresh droppings or nest material can release virus into the air. The CDC says symptoms start 1 to 8 weeks after contact and that 38% of people who develop respiratory symptoms may die. The CDC's cleanup page is direct: do not vacuum or sweep rodent urine, droppings or nesting materials. If you already did, follow the cleanup steps anyway.

Bird and bat droppings (histoplasmosis)

NIOSH's histoplasmosis publication (since replaced by two 2022 NIOSH documents we could not open) lists HVAC installers and service people among workers at risk. The fungus grows in soil enriched with bird or bat droppings, and the publication names pigeon habitats and bat habitats as risk sites. Symptoms usually start 3 to 17 days after exposure and can look like the flu. Its dust-control advice is to wet dusty material with a water spray before handling it and never to shovel or sweep it dry. For extremely dusty work such as removing manure from an attic, it recommends a powered air-purifying respirator with a full facepiece and notes half-facepiece respirators give no eye protection. All respirators need fit testing and a respiratory protection program; see our respirator lesson. Treat a heavy accumulation as a job for specialists, not part of a service call.

Raccoons

A CDC fact sheet on raccoon roundworm says infected raccoons leave eggs in feces at latrine sites, including in attics, and people can be exposed by swallowing eggs from contaminated hands or objects, or by inhaling eggs that become airborne once dry. It says most chemicals do not kill the eggs, extreme heat such as boiling water does, and that treatment works best within 3 days of exposure. A raccoon in the attic also means an animal that may defend itself. We would not clean a latrine or move a raccoon ourselves.

Worked example (ours): mouse droppings on the blower and duct

This is our own example, built from the CDC cleanup page. The attic setting and the decision to stop are our judgment; the times and ratios are the CDC's.

You pull the panel on an attic air handler and see droppings on the blower housing and the duct below it. Assume it is a small amount, not a heavy infestation.

  1. Stop and look. Do not touch, blow or vacuum anything. Our rule is to shut the unit off first so the blower does not stir the dust. The CDC does not say this; it is a common-sense precaution.
  2. Ventilate. The CDC says to open doors and windows and leave the area for 30 minutes before cleaning a home or outbuilding. Use that as the starting point in an attic, with access open as far as possible.
  3. Get your gear. The CDC says rubber or plastic gloves for all cleanup. For heavy infestations add coveralls, boots or shoe covers, goggles and a half mask air-purifying respirator with a HEPA filter.
  4. Mix or choose the disinfectant. The CDC says an EPA-registered household disinfectant, or 1.5 cups of household bleach in 1 gallon of water (1 part bleach to 9 parts water), mixed fresh each time.
  5. Soak, do not scrub. Spray the droppings and urine until very wet. Let it soak 5 minutes, or as long as the disinfectant label says.
  6. Wipe up. Use paper towels and put them in a covered garbage can that is regularly emptied. Wipe hard surfaces with disinfectant.
  7. Bag any nest or dead rodent. Spray it, soak 5 minutes, then put it in a plastic bag, knot it, put that in a second bag and knot it.
  8. Wash. Wash gloved hands, remove the gloves, then wash again with soap and warm water.
  9. Check the ducts. The CDC says if there is evidence rodents have reached heating or cooling ducts, contact a professional rodent exterminating service. A duct cleaning company can then address contamination in the system. We would stop here and report it to the customer and your supervisor, not open the duct and keep going.

Exposure and symptoms: if you feel ill in the following weeks, remember the CDC window of 1 to 8 weeks and tell your doctor about the exposure.

When to stop and call pest control

  • A wasp nest in a wall void, in an attic, or a large yellowjacket nest. The Oklahoma State fact sheet says to seek professional help for those.
  • Rodent evidence in the ducts (CDC), or a heavy infestation. For heavy infestations the CDC also points to your local or state health department or state occupational safety authority.
  • Large accumulations of bird or bat droppings, anything needing a powered air-purifying respirator, or animals you cannot safely exclude.
  • Raccoon latrines or a raccoon in the space.
  • Anyone on site, including you, with a known sting allergy and no injector at hand.

Heat adds to every one of these; see our heat stress lesson. For the spaces themselves, see attic air handlers and ducts and crawl space equipment.

Field card: Pests in attics, crawlspaces and rooftop units

Look first, wet it down, never sweep it dry.

  • Check panels and cabinet tops for wasps before you reach in.
  • Sting: wipe or scrape the stinger, wash, ice, and have someone watch you. Allergy means injector and emergency care.
  • Ticks: check daily; fine tweezers, close to skin, steady pull.
  • Droppings: do not sweep or vacuum. Ventilate 30 minutes, spray very wet, soak 5 minutes, wipe up.
  • Bleach mix per CDC: 1.5 cups per 1 gallon of water, fresh each time.
  • Bird and bat droppings: wet first; heavy dust needs specialists and a proper respirator program.
  • Rodents in ducts, nests in walls or attics, raccoons: stop and call pest control.
