Window and Door Contact Switches for HVAC Lockout

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Open a patio door in a hotel room and the heat pump or PTAC under the window quits. Close it and the unit comes back. A small magnet and a sealed switch do that job. This lesson covers how those contacts work, how they wire to the equipment, what to set, and how to find a bad one with a meter.

Why buildings interlock HVAC with openings

A unit that keeps heating or cooling while a window or patio door stands open is moving conditioned air outdoors. Hotels, dorms, offices and some homes with sliding doors add a contact switch so the equipment stops, or goes to a wide setback, while the opening is open. A federal-energy-office demonstration report on guest room controls found that the controls it tested paired an occupancy sensor with a door switch. It measured average HVAC savings of 18.4 percent across its hotel sites, with a range of 10.8 to 26.5 percent. That figure comes from the whole control package, not from the window contact alone. A university hotel-controls report describes a simpler case: every room had a patio door switch that disables the air conditioning and heating while the door is open.

Newer model energy codes also list an open-door lockout among their commercial controls measures. A national-lab study of 24 new commercial buildings named it in its list of 51 control measures, though it did not describe how it works or field test it. For rules in your area, check the local code and the building owner's design documents.

How a magnetic reed contact works

A reed switch is two thin metal blades sealed in a glass tube. A nearby magnet pulls the blades together or lets them spring apart. The magnet rides on the moving part (the sash or the door), and the switch body sits on the frame. Two things to know:

  • Normally open (NO) reed: the contacts are open with no magnet nearby. With the door shut, the magnet is beside the switch and the contacts are closed. Open the door and the circuit opens.
  • Normally closed (NC) reed: the contacts are closed with no magnet nearby. With the door shut, the magnet holds them open. Open the door and the circuit closes.

The word "normally" means magnet away, which is the door open. That trips up many people, so do not trust the label alone. Put the meter on it with the door shut and see what the circuit does. The controller input decides which type you need: some inputs run the unit when the loop is closed, others run it when the loop is open. Read the equipment's wiring diagram for which one it wants.

Where the switch wires

  • PTAC or fan coil enable input. Many units have a pair of low-voltage terminals or a jumper plug meant for a field switch, such as a remote on/off or occupancy input. The contact opens or closes that loop and nothing else. Read the unit's wiring label for the terminal names and for whether it expects a dry contact.
  • Thermostat or building automation input. The contact lands on a digital input, and the control program decides what to do: stop the fan and valves, set back the setpoint, or send an alarm. This is the better choice where you want a time delay and a record of how long the window stays open.
  • Dry or powered? A plain reed contact is dry: it has no voltage of its own. Some inputs supply a small voltage to the loop and others expect the contact to switch a voltage you supply. See Dry Contacts vs Powered Contacts in HVAC Controls before you land wires.

One wiring point works in your favor. If the loop is wired so the unit runs only when the contact is closed, a broken wire or a lost magnet shows up as a lockout and a "no heat" or "no cool" call. That wastes no energy, but it does cost the guest comfort. Keep that in mind when you troubleshoot.

Time delays, and what occupants notice

A person stepping onto a balcony for a moment should not make the unit stop and start. A time delay on the lockout fixes that: the controller waits before it acts. The same demonstration report on guest room controls says each manufacturer used a different delay before changing HVAC operation, and that this may affect energy savings. A university hotel report shows the same idea for occupancy: setback began only after 10 unoccupied minutes by day and 45 by night. Delays are set to match the site, so ask the building's operators what they want before changing one.

The unit's own compressor protection timer is separate. Even after the door closes, a PTAC or heat pump may wait a few minutes before the compressor restarts. The guest sees that as the fan running with no cooling for a bit. Explaining this saves a service call.

What guests and occupants notice: the fan stops, then the room drifts toward outdoor temperature. In heating season, the room gets cold if a door is left open overnight. Many complaints are really a door that is not latched all the way, so the magnet is a quarter inch too far from the switch.

False trips from misaligned magnets

A reed switch only closes when the magnet is close enough and lined up. Common causes of a false lockout are a sash that sags or binds, a loose switch or magnet, a screw that backed out, a new weatherstrip that holds the door off its stop, or paint and tape over the pair. Sliding doors drift as the rollers wear. The demonstration report also lists broken or missing door switches among problems it saw in the field, and it recommends checking that switches are present, attached and working.

Troubleshooting with a meter

  1. Look first. Is the door fully closed and latched? Is the magnet on the moving part and the switch on the frame, within the gap the maker states?
  2. Remove power from the unit or controller before you touch the input terminals. The loop is low voltage, but the equipment around it is not.
  3. Lift one wire off the input, or measure at the input with the controller input unpowered. Set the meter to ohms across the two wires. With the door shut a normally open reed should read near zero ohms. Open the door, and it should read open.
  4. No change when you move the door? Move the magnet by hand next to the switch. If it still does not change, the switch has failed or a wire is broken. If the switch works only when the magnet is right against it, the alignment is the problem.
  5. If the input supplies voltage to the loop, power the controller back up and read the DC voltage across the input with the door shut and again with it open. Compare with the controller's input description.
  6. Return to the building automation display, if there is one, and watch the point change as you open and close the door.

Why jumpering the switch out is a bad fix

A jumper across the loop makes the complaint stop, because the unit never locks out. It also gives up the savings the switch was installed to get. The same demonstration report found that missing or broken door switches were among the things that undercut savings. Also, a jumper hides the fault from the next tech and may violate the building's own controls requirements. Fix the alignment or replace the switch, and note the repair for the building's operators. If a jumper is a temporary test, remove it before you leave and write that down.

Worked example: heat lost through an open window

Our own example, with assumed numbers. We assume a heat pump room unit is heating a room with one window left open. Assumptions: air moving through the opening is 60 cubic feet per minute (cfm); indoor air is 70 F; outdoor air is 30 F; the window stays open for 6 hours; the heat pump averages a coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.5. These are all assumed, not measured.

The sensible heat relation for air is Q = 1.08 × cfm × delta-T, in Btu per hour. A university reference derives the 1.08 from the specific heat of air, 0.24 Btu per pound per F, times about 4.5 pounds of air moved per hour for each cfm: 0.24 × 4.5 = 1.08.

  1. Delta-T = 70 - 30 = 40 F.
  2. Q = 1.08 × 60 × 40 = 2,592 Btu per hour.
  3. Over 6 hours: 2,592 × 6 = 15,552 Btu.
  4. Convert to kWh of heat: 15,552 / 3,412 = 4.56 kWh (using 3,412 Btu per kWh).
  5. Electricity at COP 2.5: 4.56 / 2.5 = 1.82 kWh.

So under these assumptions the unit uses about 1.8 kWh extra just to replace what the open window lets out, if it can even keep up. A real opening depends on wind, the size of the opening and the stack effect, so treat this as a size check, not a prediction. The relation covers sensible heat only. It leaves out moisture.

Field card: Window and Door Contact Switches

Door shut, loop in the state the unit wants. Check with a meter, not the label.

  • A magnet on the moving part and a reed switch on the frame signal door open or shut.
  • NO reed: closed with the door shut. NC reed: open with the door shut.
  • Wire to the unit enable input or to a thermostat or building automation input; check dry or powered first.
  • A time delay keeps a quick door opening from cycling the unit.
  • False trips: sag, loose parts, a magnet too far from the switch.
  • Meter: power off, ohms across the loop, door shut then open.
  • Do not jumper the switch out. Repair it.
  • Heat lost by air flow: Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta-T (Btu per hour).
