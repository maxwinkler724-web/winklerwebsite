Motor Control Centers in Plants and Large Buildings: Buckets, Bus and Starters

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a plant or a large building, the big pumps and fans are rarely wired to a lone starter on the wall. They run from a motor control center, a lineup of cabinets in an electrical room. HVAC techs work on the motors and the equipment those starters feed, so you should know what is behind each door, how to read it, and where your own job stops.

Check your site rules

Check your site rules. This lesson is general education built from federal and university pages we read in 2026. It is not a procedure for working in electrical gear. Your employer's electrical safety program, the site owner's rules and the equipment instructions decide what you may do.

What an MCC is

A motor control center (MCC) is a floor-standing assembly of vertical sections bolted side by side. Power comes in to a horizontal bus that runs through the top of the lineup. In each section a vertical bus drops from it, and plug-in units, usually called buckets, connect to the vertical bus with stabs. One university design guide we read requires safety shutters over every plug-in location so the vertical bus is covered when a bucket is out, and it requires the units to be drawout types with an interlock that stops a bucket from being withdrawn while its contactor is closed. That is one owner's requirement, but it shows what the design is trying to do: keep the bus covered and keep the load from being broken by pulling hardware.

What is inside a bucket

A typical bucket is a small self-contained starter. Reading from the supply side to the motor:

  • Disconnect. A breaker or fused switch with a handle on the door. The same university guide calls for combination units with a motor circuit protector as the disconnect.
  • Contactor. The switch that the control circuit opens and closes to start and stop the motor.
  • Overload relay. Protects the motor from running too long above its rated current. See Motor Starters and Overload Relays for trip class and the dial.
  • Control power transformer. Steps the line voltage down for the control circuit. The same guide asks for a 120 volt control circuit with a transformer of at least 150 VA and 100 VA spare.
  • Door devices. Hand-off-auto selector, run and fault lights, and terminals for the remote start signal from the building automation system.

What HVAC loads hang on an MCC

Large chilled-water and hot-water pumps, condenser water pumps, cooling tower fans, and the supply and return fans of big air handlers are the usual HVAC loads. Some buckets feed a variable-speed drive instead of a plain starter; the drive is then the thing to read about. Smaller loads, such as unit heaters and exhaust fans, may share a bucket feeder or sit on a separate panel. When a pump will not start, the MCC bucket is one of the places the fault can live, along with the motor, the wiring and the control signal.

Labels and one-line diagrams

Each bucket should carry a nameplate naming the load, and the same design guide asks for nameplates on the whole MCC, every starter, every spare and every space, plus drawings that show unit locations, ratings and power and control wiring. Use that pair together. The bucket label tells you which load it serves. The one-line diagram, a single-line drawing of how power flows from the utility down to the bus and out to each feeder, tells you what is upstream of that bucket and what else could lose power. If a label and the drawing disagree, stop and ask who owns the drawings. Do not guess which bucket feeds a pump.

The equipment will also carry an arc-flash label. NFPA 70E is the electrical safety standard that sets up the flash hazard analysis behind these labels; the DOE electrical safety handbook we read says protective clothing is selected based on a flash hazard analysis required by NFPA 70E. Our own advice: the label tells you the hazard exists and who has to plan for it. For a more complete walk through label fields, read Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs.

Why qualified electrical workers rack buckets

Removing or inserting a bucket moves hardware close to energized bus. NIOSH describes an arc as reaching about 35,000 degrees Fahrenheit, with a pressure wave and molten metal that can throw a worker or ignite clothing. OSHA's rule 29 CFR 1910.332 says that employees who face this kind of risk must be trained, and a qualified person is at a minimum trained to tell exposed live parts from other parts, to find the nominal voltage of exposed live parts, and to know the clearance distances. OSHA 1910.333 then says live parts must be de-energized before an employee works on or near them, with narrow exceptions, and that work on exposed live parts is for qualified persons. Racking, bucket removal, bus work and opening the live compartments belong to qualified electrical workers following the site's program. For an HVAC tech the correct action is to ask for them, not to help.

Lockout at the bucket disconnect

When you need a pump or fan off for service, the bucket disconnect is usually the energy isolating point your lock goes on, under the site's written procedure. OSHA 1910.147 requires a documented procedure covering shutdown, isolation, applying locks and tags, relieving stored energy and verifying isolation before work starts. OSHA 1910.333(b)(2) adds, for electrical work, that control devices such as a start button cannot be the only means of de-energizing, and that a qualified person must verify the circuit is dead, including checking for induced voltage or backfeed. The DOE handbook adds that test equipment is checked right before and right after the test. The full routine is in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Two bucket-specific cautions, our own: a pump may have a second source such as a local disconnect or a control circuit powered from elsewhere, and a motor can be a running generator if the load is turning. Check the one-line diagram and the equipment before you trust one lock.

Infrared scans of MCCs

The Federal Energy Management Program O&M guide lists motor control centers among the electrical systems to survey and says the main value of electrical thermography is locating problems, with exact temperature usually far less important. A loose or corroded connection runs hotter because of added resistance. Bureau of Reclamation guidance says electrical equipment should be surveyed annually, or more often if conditions change, and that inspections should be done at or near full load when possible because partial load can hide a problem. It also says thermal inspections should be noncontact on easily accessible equipment, and panels that would expose the camera operator to hazardous energy should not be opened; infrared windows or other approaches are used instead, and any exposure calls for arc-flash procedures. The practical rule for HVAC techs is to hand scan requests to the qualified people who do them, and to read the report. See Infrared Thermography for HVAC and Electrical Gear.

Common faults and what they usually mean

  • Tripped overload. The motor ran above its setting for too long. The cause is usually in the load or the supply: a seized bearing, a closed valve, a bad belt, a low voltage, or a lost phase. Resetting without finding the cause repeats the trip. See Phase Loss and Single-Phasing.
  • Failed control transformer fuse. The bucket looks dead and the motor never gets a start signal. The fuse protects the transformer and control circuit; a shorted coil, a bad auxiliary device or a wiring fault can blow it. Replacing a fuse inside the bucket is the qualified electrical worker's task.
  • Contactor wear. Pitted or worn contacts can chatter, overheat or weld. Signs are heat discoloration, noise and a motor that runs rough on one phase.

These are our own summaries of common patterns, not figures from a source. The tech's job is to describe the symptom clearly, such as which pump, which hour, what the building system showed and what the motor did, and hand it over.

Worked example: is the overload dial set right? (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed numbers. The general rule we use is the one in NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code, Article 430, Section 430.32, which we name in plain text and have not reproduced: for a motor with a service factor of 1.15 or more, the overload is generally set to trip no higher than 125 percent of the nameplate full-load current; for others, 115 percent. The Michigan State University extension note we read agrees that the service factor and the nameplate full-load current drive the overload element selection and points to 430.32 for the percentages. The code has further allowances when a lower setting will not let the motor start; those are for the qualified electrical worker and the designer.

Assumed inputs: a chilled-water pump motor on a 460 volt MCC bucket, nameplate full-load current 14.0 A, service factor 1.15. The bucket's label and the as-built drawing show the dial set to 18.0 A.

  1. Service factor is 1.15, so the 125 percent line applies.
  2. Limit = 14.0 A x 1.25 = 17.5 A.
  3. Dial = 18.0 A. As a fraction of nameplate: 18.0 / 14.0 = 1.286, or about 128.6 percent.
  4. 18.0 A is greater than 17.5 A, so the setting is above the general rule and should be reported.

If the motor had a service factor of 1.0, the line would be 14.0 x 1.15 = 16.1 A, and a dial at 16.0 A would sit just inside it. Notice two things. First, the rule is a ceiling; many sites set the dial nearer the nameplate value, per the relay maker's instructions, which is a different question. Second, the HVAC tech does not change the dial in a live bucket. Write down the nameplate, the label and the dial reading you were able to see without opening anything energized, and send them to the qualified electrical worker who owns the setting.

What an HVAC tech may and may not do

  • May: read labels and the one-line diagram, ask for a bucket to be locked out, apply your own lock under the site's procedure if you are an authorized employee, check that the driven equipment is stopped, describe symptoms and record nameplate data from the motor.
  • May not, unless qualified and assigned: open energized compartments, rack or remove buckets, adjust settings inside live gear, replace control transformer fuses or contactors, or take readings inside the bucket.
  • Never: bypass an interlock, defeat a shutter, or reset a tripped overload over and over to keep a pump running.

Field card: Motor Control Centers in Plants and Large Buildings: Buckets, Bus and Starters

Read it, ask for it, lock it out, and leave the live work to qualified electrical workers.

  • MCC: vertical sections, horizontal bus, vertical bus, plug-in buckets.
  • Bucket: disconnect, contactor, overload relay, control transformer.
  • Match the bucket label to the one-line diagram before trusting it.
  • Racking and bucket work: qualified electrical workers only.
  • Lock at the bucket disconnect; a qualified person verifies dead.
  • Our own example: 14.0 A x 1.25 = 17.5 A limit; an 18.0 A dial is high.
  • Infrared scans: noncontact, at or near full load, by qualified people.
