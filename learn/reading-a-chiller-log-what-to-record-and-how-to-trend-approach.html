Reading a Chiller Log: What to Record and How to Trend Approach

A chiller log is a diary the machine cannot write for itself. Kept the same way every time, it shows trouble weeks before an alarm does. This lesson covers what to write down and how to read it.

Premium lesson. Planned for the premium tier; free to read while the site is in preview.

Why keep a log at all

One reading tells you where the machine is today. A log tells you where it is heading. Fouling, low charge and air in a low-pressure machine all creep in slowly, and slow changes are easy to miss when you only look at today's number. The daily walk-through is covered in Chiller Plant Daily Rounds Walkthrough; this lesson is about what the numbers mean once they are on paper.

Safety first

Take readings from the controller display or installed gauges where you can. Follow your site rules for entering the machine room, respect lockout/tagout before touching anything, and never reach into or bypass a safety device to get a reading. If a refrigerant leak alarm or a safety trip is active, report it instead of logging around it. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

What to record

Published checklists describe logs like this. Your maker's log sheet comes first if you have one.

  • Water temperatures: entering and leaving chilled water, and entering and leaving condenser water.
  • Refrigerant side: evaporator and condenser pressures and their saturation temperatures.
  • Compressor: running amps or percent load.
  • Oil: level, temperature and filter pressure drop where shown.
  • Refrigerant level and, on low-pressure machines, any purge or non-condensables indication.
  • Flow status and pressure drop across each heat exchanger.
  • Run hours, the date and time, and what else was running.

What approach means

Approach is the gap between a water temperature and the refrigerant's saturation temperature in the same heat exchanger. HVAC School defines two. Condenser approach is the condensing saturation temperature minus the leaving condenser water temperature. Evaporator approach is the leaving chilled water temperature minus the evaporating saturation temperature. Some log sheets word the condenser version slightly differently, so use the definition on your machine's sheet and keep it the same all year. The deeper explanation is in Chiller Approach Temperatures: Reading the Trend, so it is not repeated here.

Judge it against this machine's own baseline

The best comparison for a chiller is itself. Record a full set of readings at startup or after a cleaning, when the tubes are known to be clean, and note the load. Later readings are compared with that set at a similar load and similar condenser water temperature. Published guidance on logs stresses the same idea: judge each reading against its own baseline, and trend it over time, because slow drift hides on a page of daily numbers but shows on a month-long trend.

This lesson gives no number at which an approach becomes an alarm. Published rules of thumb vary, and your maker's manual and your engineer set the limits for your machine.

Reading the pattern

  • Approach rising at the same load points toward fouling in that heat exchanger. A rising condenser approach with higher leaving condenser water temperature and more tower fan effort points at the condenser tubes or the tower side.
  • Rising evaporator approach can also come from fouled tubes, and published checklists add low charge or an expansion device problem as other candidates.
  • A change in flow can mimic any of this. Confirm flow first, since the approach idea assumes the water flow is right.

Fouling is a lead, not a verdict. Confirm with inspection, water treatment records and the maker's guidance. Water-side scale and slime link back to Condenser Water Treatment, and tube cleaning is a lockout job for qualified people.

Good logging habits

Log at about the same time and under similar load. Write the actual number, not "OK." Note anything unusual: a recent tower cleaning, a cold snap, a changed setpoint. Use the same instruments, and flag any reading that looks odd for a second look rather than rounding it away. A trend line on a chart makes a drift obvious to people who never read the raw sheet.

Field card: Reading a Chiller Log: What to Record and How to Trend Approach

Same readings, same way, judged against the machine's own clean baseline.

  • Log entering and leaving water temperatures on both barrels.
  • Log evaporator and condenser pressures and saturation temperatures, plus amps or percent load.
  • Log oil readings, refrigerant level, purge or non-condensables indication, flow status and run hours.
  • Approach = water temperature versus refrigerant saturation temperature in that heat exchanger; keep one definition.
  • Rising approach at the same load points toward fouling; confirm flow first.
  • No numeric alarm limit here: use the maker's manual and your engineer. Never bypass a safety to get a reading.
