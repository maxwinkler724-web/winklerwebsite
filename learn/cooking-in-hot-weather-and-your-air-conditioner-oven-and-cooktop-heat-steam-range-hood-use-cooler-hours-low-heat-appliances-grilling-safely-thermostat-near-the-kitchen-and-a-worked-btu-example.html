Cooking in Hot Weather and Your Air Conditioner: Oven Heat, Steam and Smart Habits

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a hot afternoon your air conditioner is already working hard. Then someone turns on the oven. This lesson explains where cooking heat and steam go, why the AC has to remove them, and the simple habits that keep the kitchen from adding to the load. It ends with our own worked example in Btu.

Check your own house

Check your own house. This page is general education built from federal and university pages read in 2026. Follow the instruction manuals for your appliances, grill and air conditioner, and ask a licensed pro about anything involving gas, wiring or refrigerant.

Why cooking is part of the cooling load

An air conditioner does not make cold. It moves heat out of the house. Every watt a heating appliance uses ends up as heat in the building unless something carries it outside. An oven is a heater inside a box with a door and a vent, so when it runs, the kitchen gets warmer and the AC has more to remove. A state extension article on keeping the kitchen cool lists heavy cooking and baking as the activities to move away from the hottest hours, and a federal energy page tells homeowners to avoid the oven in summer and grill outside, which keeps heat out of the house so the AC works less.

There are two kinds of load. Sensible heat raises the air temperature. Latent heat is the energy carried by water vapor, and the AC must condense that moisture on its coil before the air feels dry. A boiling pot of pasta or a steaming pan puts a lot of vapor in the room. See AC Can't Keep Up on the Hottest Days for why a system has little spare capacity at peak. Our own point: a kitchen in use can eat into that margin.

The main heat sources

  • Oven. The biggest single heat source in most kitchens. Preheating, long bakes and opening the door all add heat. The extension and federal pages above both point toward other ways of cooking.
  • Cooktop. Burners and elements put heat into the room around the pan, and boiling adds steam. Covering pots and using a burner close to the pan size wastes less.
  • Dishwasher drying. One extension article suggests running the dishwasher overnight and skipping the heated drying cycle to cut heat and humidity in the kitchen.
  • Toaster oven. It uses a smaller box than the range oven, so it is the better choice for small jobs. It still adds heat, just less of it.

Use the range hood, and use it vented outside

A range hood that vents outdoors removes heat and steam right where they start, before they spread through the house. An extension article suggests starting it when cooking begins and keeping it on during cooking so heat and humidity do not spread through the home. A recirculating hood only filters air and sends it back into the room, so it does little for heat or moisture. Our lesson Kitchen Range Hoods at Home: Ducted vs Recirculating and Sizing covers the difference and how a hood is sized. The hood also helps with cooking fumes, as described in Cooking and Indoor Air. Our own note on a tradeoff: a hood moving a lot of air also pulls conditioned air out of the house, which new air leaks back in to replace. Run it while you cook and turn it off when the pan is done.

Cook when the house is cooler

A university extension article advises scheduling heavy cooking, baking and kitchen chores for early morning or late evening, when the kitchen is cooler. Our own point: outdoor air is also usually cooler then, so the AC has an easier job. If you do bake, cook several things in one oven load, and consider cooking once and serving twice, an idea another extension article also gives for leftovers. Cold meals such as main-dish salads need no heat at all.

Smaller appliances add less heat

That extension article lists microwaves, slow cookers, toaster ovens, air fryers, induction cooking and programmable electric cooking pots as options that generate less ambient heat than a full-size oven and cooktop. The second extension article suggests using the microwave for more than reheating, with baked potatoes in about 4 to 6 minutes and corn on the cob in about 2 to 3 minutes per ear, and mentions electric skillets and toaster ovens. We are also adding two items from our own reasoning, not from those pages: a pressure cooker finishes food faster, so it spends less time putting heat in the room, and an electric kettle heats only the water you need. Neither is a cure, and each still uses energy that ends up as heat, but the heat is smaller and shorter than from a full oven.

Grilling outdoors, safely

Grilling moves the heat outside, and the second extension article notes that meats cook quickly and add little heat indoors. It brings its own risks. Federal fire safety guidance says to keep the grill at least 3 feet from siding, deck rails and eaves, and another federal fire page says to keep it away from the house and out from under eaves and overhanging branches, to keep a 3-foot clear zone around it for children and pets, and never to leave a lit grill unattended. A federal product safety page says grills belong outside only, never in a garage, breezeway, carport or porch, and warns that gas and charcoal grills can cause fire or carbon monoxide poisoning. It also says to check hoses for cracks, holes and leaks and to look up the grill model for recalls.

Those pages do not mention air conditioners, so here is our own advice: treat the outdoor AC unit, windows and doors like part of the house. Place the grill so smoke and hot air do not blow into open windows or a door, and keep it clear of the outdoor unit, its wiring and its airflow. Our own point, not from a source: a grill upwind of an open window can fill the room with smoke, so check the wind before you light it.

Do not let cooking heat fool the thermostat

A federal energy page says heat from lamps and TVs near a thermostat can make it read the room as warmer than it is, so the AC runs longer and more often. The same logic applies to a thermostat on a wall right beside or across from the kitchen. Hot air and steam from the oven can make it call for cooling the rest of the house does not need. Our lesson Where to Put a Thermostat: Placement, Height and False Readings explains the placement rules. If yours is near the kitchen, run the hood, cook at cooler hours, and consider asking a pro about moving it or adding a remote sensor.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with our own assumptions, not a measurement from a real house. Assumptions: an electric oven averages 2,000 watts over one hour of baking (an oven cycles on and off, so the average is below its peak rating), and the central air conditioner is a 2 ton unit, which we treat as removing 24,000 Btu per hour. The conversion 1 watt = 3.412 Btu per hour is a standard unit conversion.

  1. Energy used: 2,000 W x 1 hour = 2,000 Wh = 2 kWh.
  2. Heat in Btu: 2,000 W x 3.412 Btu per hour per watt = 6,824 Btu in that hour.
  3. Share of the AC hour: 6,824 / 24,000 = 0.284, about 28 percent.

Check: 24,000 x 0.28 = 6,720, close to 6,824, so 28 percent is right. In plain words, one hour of baking uses up roughly a quarter of what that AC could remove in an hour. This is a simplified comparison: it ignores steam, the oven vent, hood use and the rest of the house's load, and the AC does not run flat out all the time. It also says most of that oven energy ends up as heat in the kitchen unless a hood vents it outside. Our reasoning is simple: electric energy used by the oven turns into heat, and a vented hood can carry some of it out but not all of it, so assume the kitchen gets the majority.

Field card: Cooking in Hot Weather and Your AC

Every watt the oven uses ends up as heat in the house unless it is vented.

  • Our example: a 2,000 W oven for 1 hour is 2 kWh, 6,824 Btu, about 28 percent of a 24,000 Btu per hour AC hour.
  • Run a hood that vents outdoors from the start of cooking.
  • Cook early or late, in one batch.
  • Microwave, slow cooker, toaster oven and similar appliances add less heat.
  • Skip the dishwasher heated dry, and run it overnight.
  • Grill 3 feet or more from siding, rails and eaves, never indoors or in a garage.
  • Keep cooking heat away from the thermostat.
