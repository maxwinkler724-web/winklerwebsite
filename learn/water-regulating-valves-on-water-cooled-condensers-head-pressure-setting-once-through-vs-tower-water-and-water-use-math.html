Water-Regulating Valves on Water-Cooled Condensers: Head Pressure Setting, Once-Through vs Tower Water, and Water-Use Math

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A water-cooled condenser has no fan to slow down, so something has to decide how much water it gets. On many small and mid-size units that job belongs to a water-regulating valve: a mechanical valve that reads refrigerant pressure and opens only as far as it needs to. This lesson covers how that valve works, how to set it, and what it means for water use.

How the valve senses head pressure

A refrigerant-pressure-actuated water valve has a small sensing line, a capillary tube, that connects the valve's power element to the high side of the system. A federal laboratory report that tested nine such valves describes a bellows-type power element that responds to condensing pressure and a capillary of roughly 30 inches. Refrigerant pressure pushes on the bellows. An adjustable spring pushes back. When refrigerant pressure rises above the spring setting, the valve opens and lets water through. When it falls, the spring closes the valve.

That balance has three practical results. First, a hot, loaded condenser raises head pressure, which opens the valve wider and sends more water, which pulls head pressure back down. Second, the valve holds head pressure fairly steady instead of holding water flow steady. The same report found that flow was roughly proportional to condensing pressure through the middle of the valve's range, then flattened near full open. Third, when the compressor stops and head pressure drops, the valve should shut the water off.

Setting the opening point

The spring adjustment sets the opening point, the pressure at which water first starts to flow. Maker installation instructions for a two-way valve say to run the system at normal load, put a refrigerant gauge on the high side, and turn the range adjustment screw until the valve opens at the head pressure you want. In the instructions we read, turning the screw counterclockwise raised the opening point and clockwise lowered it. Always check the arrows and markings on your own valve.

Pick the target the way you pick any condensing target: choose the saturated condensing temperature you want, read the matching pressure for that refrigerant on a pressure-temperature chart, and set the opening point near it. Setting it lower saves compressor energy but uses more water. Setting it higher saves water but raises head pressure and compressor work. The same report notes that a valve with a sloppy shutoff pushes you to set the spring higher, and that makes the refrigeration unit run less efficiently.

Valves come in pressure ranges. The two-way instructions we read list a direct-acting range of 200 to 400 psi (factory set at 200 psi) and a reverse-acting range of 135 to 300 psi. Pick a valve whose range covers your refrigerant and your target pressure.

Two-way versus three-way valves

A two-way valve sits on the water inlet or outlet and simply throttles flow. It suits city water, well water or any supply you can shut off. A three-way valve has a third port for a bypass. The maker instructions we read for a three-way valve describe port 1 as the inlet from the cooling tower, port 2 as the outlet to the condenser, and port 3 as the bypass back toward the tower. As the condenser flow is throttled, the rest of the water goes through the bypass, so the tower pump still sees a flow path. A balancing valve in the bypass line sets the minimum flow. Use three-way valves where the pump and piping serve a loop, such as a tower loop with several condensers, and two-way valves where water is simply drawn off and discarded.

Once-through water versus tower water

With once-through (single-pass) cooling, water goes through the condenser one time and then to the drain. With tower water, a pump circulates water between the condenser and a cooling tower, and only makeup water is added. A federal energy-program page on single-pass cooling says these systems need about 40 times more water than a cooling tower running at five cycles of concentration to remove the same heat. It lists condensers, ice machines and air conditioners among the equipment involved, and it suggests converting to a closed loop, using a tower, or replacing the equipment with air-cooled models.

Some places restrict once-through cooling. As one example, New York City's Local Law 54 of 2010 amended its plumbing code so that potable water may not be used for once-through cooling. Ice makers, walk-in coolers and air conditioning equipment must use air-cooled condensers or recirculating condenser water, with exceptions for small ice machines and for older piping installed before 2011. We cite it only as an example of a local rule. Water and plumbing rules differ by city and state, so check the code and the water utility where the equipment is installed before you design or replace a once-through system. Symptoms and what they point to

  • Water keeps running when the unit is off. The valve is not closing. In hot weather, head pressure can stay high enough during the off cycle to hold the valve open. The two-way instructions say to raise the opening point just enough to stop flow at standby. Debris on the seat is the other cause: those instructions warn that debris embedded in the seats can cause seat leakage that flushing will not fix. The laboratory report also notes that a trickle of water at shutoff could be caused by foreign matter.
  • High head pressure. Check for a scaled or fouled condenser, a valve that is stuck or set too high, a plugged strainer, and low supply water pressure. See the related lesson on dirty condensers.
  • Valve chatters or leaks around the stem. The laboratory report recorded chattering in some valves at high supply pressure, and it forced water past diaphragm seals. Check inlet pressure.

Checks to make

  1. Water temperature rise. Measure water in and water out. Compare the rise with the equipment data. A rise far above normal means too little flow; a tiny rise at high head may mean a fouled tube surface instead.
  2. Approach. In this lesson, approach means the saturated condensing temperature minus the leaving water temperature. A growing approach at the same load is a classic sign of scale or fouling on the water side.
  3. Valve response. Watch the gauge and the water outlet. Water should start near the opening point and stop shortly after the compressor does.
  4. Sensing line. The maker instructions say to tap the top of the high-side line, avoid sharp capillary bends, and mount the valve so the sensing element is above the tap point if possible.
  5. Flushing. The instructions we read say to flush piping after installation, and that the valve itself can be flushed by lifting the spring guide with screwdrivers on both sides. Shut off the supply and relieve pressure first, and follow the maker's steps for your valve.

Worked example (ours): 5-ton unit, how much water?

This is our own example with assumed numbers. We assume a 5-ton water-cooled unit and use 15,000 Btu/h of heat rejected per ton. That figure comes from the 12,000 Btu/h in one ton of refrigeration times the ratio of about 1.25 that a federal water-use guide gives for cooling tower tons versus chiller tons (the compressor's heat adds to the load).

Step 1, heat rejected. 5 tons × 15,000 Btu/h = 75,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, the water formula. One gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds (assumed), and one Btu warms one pound of water by one degree F. So gpm × 8.34 × 60 = gpm × 500 Btu/h per degree F. Then gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × temperature rise).

Step 3, flow for each rise.

  • 10 F rise: 75,000 ÷ (500 × 10) = 75,000 ÷ 5,000 = 15 gpm.
  • 20 F rise: 75,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 75,000 ÷ 10,000 = 7.5 gpm.

A bigger rise means less water, and a regulating valve moves toward a bigger rise when head pressure is allowed to be higher.

Step 4, yearly water if once-through. A federal guide says a piece of equipment using 1 gpm of cooling water uses 525,000 gallons a year; that is 60 minutes × 8,760 hours. We assume the unit runs nonstop all year, which is a worst case.

  • 15 gpm × 525,000 = 7,875,000 gallons per year.
  • 7.5 gpm × 525,000 = 3,937,500 gallons per year.

If the compressor really runs only 3,000 hours a year (our assumption), scale by 3,000 ÷ 8,760, about 0.34: roughly 2.7 million gallons at 15 gpm and 1.35 million at 7.5 gpm. Either way the numbers are large, which is why many owners convert to a tower or air-cooled equipment, and why some cities restrict once-through water.

Field card: Water-regulating valves

The valve follows head pressure, not water temperature.

  • Capillary brings condensing pressure to a bellows; a spring sets the opening point.
  • Set it at normal load with a gauge; lower opening point = lower head, more water.
  • Two-way throttles supply; three-way bypasses the rest back to the tower loop.
  • Water on when off: valve not closing (high standby head or debris on the seat).
  • Check water rise, approach, strainer, supply pressure; flush per maker steps.
  • Water in gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × rise). 5 tons: 15 gpm at 10 F, 7.5 gpm at 20 F.
  • 1 gpm nonstop = 525,000 gallons a year.
