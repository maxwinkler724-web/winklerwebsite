Food Truck HVAC and Refrigeration: Rooftop AC Loads, Hood Exhaust, Propane and Generator CO

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A food truck packs a fryer, a griddle, two or three coolers, a generator and a few people into a box about the size of a parking space. The rooftop AC is usually the smallest piece of the puzzle. This lesson walks through the heat, the fire and gas rules that shape the layout, and the power limits that decide whether the coolers keep up.

Check your local authority

Check the local rules. Mobile food rules come from the local fire marshal and health department, and they differ. The examples below are from public pages we read in 2026. They are education, not a permit checklist. Ask the agency that will inspect the unit.

Hood and fire suppression, as the public pages state it

Two fire marshal pages we read name NFPA 96 (the standard for ventilation control and fire protection of commercial cooking operations) in plain text. One city fire marshal says any vendor making grease-laden vapor needs a Type 1 hood, that appliances must sit fully under the hood, and that the suppression system must be serviced every 6 months. It also asks for a steel baffle 8 inches high between a deep fryer and adjacent open flames. A county fire marshal guide says a suppression system is required over fryers, griddles and enclosed exhaust, that it must disable fuel and power to the appliances when it discharges, and that the manual pull station must be labeled and near the exit path. It says roof exhaust must end 10 ft above the roof or 3 ft above structures within 10 ft.

Both pages require extinguishers. The city page adds an extra 3A40BC extinguisher for vendors using a portable generator. A health department page we read adds only that "adequate ventilation" must keep grease and condensation off walls and ceilings, and sends readers to the fire and building departments for hood details. For how a hood and make-up air work in a full kitchen, see Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics. We do not repeat it here.

Why this matters to the AC. A hood pulls room air out, and the truck has to let replacement air in through windows, doors or a make-up path. If the serving window is the only opening, that air comes from outside, hot and unfiltered, and the rooftop AC does not cool it. Keep the AC intake and condenser air away from the hood discharge and from grease. We found no published clearance between roof exhaust and AC, so we give none.

Propane: outside, upright, tested

The county guide, citing NFPA 58 (the LP-gas code), says cylinders must be upright, secured and 10 ft from ignition sources, with LP-rated hoses inspected daily. The city page says keep containers outside the unit, point relief valves away from any canopy, and use hoses listed for LP gas. The propane industry council's portable cylinder guide describes a soapy-water leak check: brush solution on the valve and connection, open the valve slowly, and watch for bubbles. If bubbles persist, close the valve and call the retailer. It says to check before first use each season and whenever a cylinder is connected.

The same guide says propane can settle near the ground and that you may not smell a leak, so odor alone is not a safe test. If you smell gas, remove ignition sources, close the cylinder valve if there is no flame or visible cloud, leave, and call 911. It also says to keep the valve closed when the appliance is off and to keep cylinders upright and tied down so they cannot tip or roll, which matters on a moving truck.

Generator exhaust and the service window

The CDC says to run generators and other gas-powered equipment outside, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, and to install battery-operated CO detectors. The Consumer Product Safety Commission gives the same 20 foot figure, says never to run a generator in an enclosed space even with doors open, notes that CO can linger for hours after the engine stops, and says to choose CO alarms certified to UL 2034, IAS 6-96 or CSA 6.19.01.

A truck is awkward because it is small. We cannot find a published rule in the pages we read that sets a generator distance from a truck, and the county guide gives only 5 ft from combustibles and proper grounding. So treat the 20 foot guidance as the goal: exhaust pointing away from the service window, the customer line, the AC intake and the make-up air path. If the generator is built into the truck, it needs its own exhaust routing and a CO alarm inside. For the general lesson see Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages, and for generator heat and air see Emergency Generator Room Ventilation.

Worked example: does the rooftop AC keep up?

All inputs below are assumptions chosen to show the method. They are not measurements from a real truck. Unit conversions (3.412 Btu/h per watt, 12,000 Btu/h per ton) are standard factors. All figures are Our own.

  • Assumed fryer input 80,000 Btu/h and griddle input 60,000 Btu/h, so 140,000 Btu/h total gas input.
  • Assumed fraction reaching the room air with the hood running: 20 percent. Heat radiates from the appliance bodies even when the hood captures most flue gas and hot air. 140,000 x 0.20 = 28,000 Btu/h.
  • Assumed 3 people at 500 Btu/h each: 1,500 Btu/h.
  • Assumed solar and roof heat gain: 3,000 Btu/h.
  • Assumed two undercounter coolers averaging 400 W each as heat released: 800 x 3.412 = 2,730, about 2,700 Btu/h.
  • Assumed lights and small devices, 500 W: 500 x 3.412 = 1,706, about 1,700 Btu/h.

Total: 28,000 + 1,500 + 3,000 + 2,700 + 1,700 = 36,900 Btu/h, or about 3.1 tons (36,900 / 12,000).

Assumed rooftop AC capacity: 13,500 Btu/h (1.1 tons). The gap is 36,900 - 13,500 = 23,400 Btu/h, so the load is about 2.7 times the capacity (36,900 / 13,500). Result: the AC will not hold a comfortable temperature while both appliances run. It can help the crew, but it cannot be sized to cancel cooking heat.

The same numbers show the electrical side. At an assumed 9 Btu/h per watt of efficiency, the AC draws about 13,500 / 9 = 1,500 W, or 13 A at 115 V (1,500 / 115). Add the coolers at 800 W and lights at 500 W: 2,800 W running, before compressor start surges. A generator rated near that figure has no margin. This is why cutting load (a shade, a better hood, reduced idle appliance time) usually beats a larger AC.

Reach-ins and undercounter units on generator or shore power

The health department page we read says refrigeration must hold potentially hazardous foods at 41 F and that each unit needs a thermometer accurate to plus or minus 2 F, reading air in the warmest part. It says the power source (a generator or a plug-in) must keep cold holding going at all hours of operation. A generator that stalls overnight, or a shore cord that trips, becomes a food safety problem. Size the shore cord and breaker for the total, not for one cooler.

Practical points (Our own): start the coolers before the AC and the fryer so surges do not stack. Coolers that run on 12 V battery systems need the battery, charger and wire size matched to their running amps; we have no source with numbers, so follow the maker's manual. Keep condenser coils clear of grease and give the cooler's vent the airflow its maker's manual asks for. A closed, hot truck raises condenser air temperature and lengthens run time. Defrost and care steps are in Reach-In and Display Case Defrost and Care. Cooking-heat ideas from another trade appear in Bakery HVAC: Oven Heat Loads.

Vibration and road service

We found no source with numbers, so this is general shop experience (Our own). Road travel loosens fittings, rubs line sets and wiring, and shakes cylinders. Check these after a trip: coolers latched and gaskets sealed; condenser fan and mounting screws tight; line sets and wires not chafing; the AC roof gasket and cover sealed; propane hoses and fittings re-tested with soapy water; generator mounting and exhaust joints tight.

Field card: Food Truck HVAC and Refrigeration

Cooking heat beats the AC. Gas outside. Exhaust away.

  • Fire marshals name NFPA 96, Type 1 hood, and suppression service every 6 months in the pages we read. Ask your local office.
  • Propane: outside, upright, secured, soapy-water test at each connection.
  • CDC and CPSC: generator 20 ft from doors, windows and vents; CO alarm inside.
  • Our own example: 36,900 Btu/h load vs 13,500 Btu/h AC (assumed numbers).
  • Cold holding 41 F in the health page we read; power must stay on at all hours.
  • After a drive, recheck fittings, line sets, mounts and leaks.
