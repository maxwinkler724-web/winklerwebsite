Half-Splitting a Control Circuit: Finding the Open Safety in a Series String with a Voltmeter

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace limit string or a rooftop unit safety chain can have eight or ten switches in a row, and the unit just sits there. You do not have to test them one by one. With a voltmeter and a fixed reference point you can cut the search in half with every reading.

Why a series string is easy to test

In a series string every device has to be closed for current to reach the load at the end, usually a relay coil, gas valve or contactor. Reading a Ladder Diagram covers why contacts in series act like AND, and Relays, Transformers and 24V Control covers the 24 V side. Because the string is one path, a single open device stops everything, and the source voltage has to show up somewhere: across that open device.

Why open reads full voltage and closed reads near zero

An Ontario open-education multimeter textbook states it plainly: when a switch is open, the full source voltage appears across its contacts. In a series path the same current flows through every part, so each part's voltage drop is proportional to its resistance. The same textbook works an example where 2 A through a 40 ohm resistor drops 80 V and through a 20 ohm resistor drops 40 V. A closed switch has almost no resistance, so almost no voltage drops across it. An open contact acts like a huge resistance, so it takes essentially all of the source voltage and nothing is left for the devices after it.

A Canadian technical institute's open textbook gives a rule worth memorizing: if you measure voltage across a switch, the switch is open. It also warns that zero volts across a switch does not prove the switch is closed, because a dead supply reads zero too. Prove the supply is there first. It adds that if a load shows correct voltage but does not run, the load has failed. Both texts say voltage testing is practical because you do not move wires or change the circuit, so you do not disturb a fault you are hunting.

Half-splitting: the method

A trade magazine article on the method says each step eliminates half the circuit as the possible cause. Its example of 10 series safety switches goes from ten suspects to five, then two, then one, using a voltmeter at the midpoint of the chain.

  1. Pick one reference and keep the black lead there. On a 24 V system a common choice is the transformer secondary terminal marked C. Leave the black lead on it so you only move one probe.
  2. Prove the meter and the supply. Check the meter on a known live source, then confirm control voltage is present at the start of the string.
  3. Measure at the midpoint of the suspect group. Touch the red probe to the terminal between two devices near the middle.
  4. Read the result. Full source voltage means power is getting past everything before that point, so the open is downstream. Near zero means the open is upstream.
  5. Repeat in the half that still holds the fault until one device remains.
  6. Confirm by reading across that one device. It should show full source voltage while the string is open.

Worked example: our made-up 8-device string

This is our own example, not from a real unit. Our assumptions: a 24 V control circuit, eight normally closed safeties S1 through S8 in series from R to a gas valve coil, test points TP0 through TP8 between them, and one hidden open device. The black lead stays on C for every reading.

TPn sits just after device Sn. A reading there is 24 V if S1 through Sn are all closed, and about 0 V if any of them is open.

  • Reading 1, TP4 to C: 24 V. S1 to S4 pass power, so the open is in S5 to S8. Suspects: 8 down to 4.
  • Reading 2, TP6 to C: 0 V. Power is lost between TP4 and TP6, so the open is S5 or S6. Suspects: 4 down to 2.
  • Reading 3, TP5 to C: 24 V. S5 passes power and TP6 is dead, so S6 is open. Suspects: 2 down to 1.

The math: after k readings, 8 ÷ 2k suspects remain. We need 8 ÷ 2k = 1, so 2k = 8 and k = log2 8 = 3. Walking the string from the start, one point at a time, could take as many as 7 readings for the same string (the eighth device is then the only one left). For 16 devices the half-split takes log2 16 = 4 readings. For 10 devices it takes 4, because log2 10 is about 3.3 and you round up. Doubling the string adds only one reading.

Confirm: red probe on TP5, black on TP6, directly across S6. We expect 24 V, which fits the rule that voltage across a switch means the switch is open.

When the real string is not in neat order

Real equipment rarely numbers its terminals this conveniently. Use the wiring diagram to find the order of the devices and which terminals you can reach. If the true midpoint is not reachable, use the nearest reachable point. Parallel branches change what you should expect, so redraw the string on paper before you start.

Intermittent faults

An intermittent open reads full voltage one minute and zero the next. The sources we read assume a steady fault, so this section is our own practice, not a cited procedure. Leave the meter connected at the point where the reading drops out and watch it rather than measuring once. Many meters have a minimum and maximum capture mode, so check yours. Once the drop is repeatable, half-split as usual. Keep hands and probes away from bare line-voltage terminals while you watch, and run several full cycles before you call it fixed.

Safe meter practice on live circuits

These are live tests. OSHA's general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.333, says live parts must be deenergized before work on or near them unless deenergizing adds hazards or is infeasible, and its notes give testing that can only be done on an energized circuit as an example. It also says live parts under 50 volts to ground need not be deenergized if there is no increased exposure to burns or arc explosion, that only qualified persons may work on energized parts, and that workers may not reach blindly into spaces that may hold energized parts. NFPA 70E and your employer's electrical safety program set the details for line-voltage work.

  • A 24 V string is lower risk, but the unit's line-voltage wiring is in the same cabinet. Keep probes and hands away from it.
  • Use a meter with the right rating and good leads; see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.
  • Take the reading, then power down, lock out and verify dead before you replace any part; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

Confirm the fix

After replacing or resetting the device, restore power and read the load: it should now show control voltage. Run a full call for heat or cooling and watch for the fault to come back. If a safety opened because it did its job (a limit tripped by low airflow, for example), swapping it only hides the cause, so find out why it opened. If a short or blown fuse is involved, see Low-Voltage Short, Blown Fuse and Transformer Troubleshooting.

Field card: Half-splitting a series string

Black lead on common. Red lead to the middle. Full voltage means look downstream, zero means look upstream.

  • Open switch: full source voltage across it. Closed switch: about zero.
  • Prove the meter and supply first. Zero across a switch does not prove it is closed.
  • Our example: 8 devices, 3 readings. Readings needed = log2 N, rounded up.
  • Confirm across the suspect, fix, then run a full cycle.
  • Find out why a safety opened. Lock out before replacing parts.
