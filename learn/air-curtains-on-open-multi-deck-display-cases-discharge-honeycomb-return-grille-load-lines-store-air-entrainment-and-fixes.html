Air Curtains on Open Multi-Deck Display Cases: Discharge Honeycomb, Return Grille, Load Lines, Store Air Entrainment and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-10-28

An open multi-deck case has no glass between the product and the shopper. The only thing holding the cold in is a moving sheet of air. When that sheet is whole, the case holds temperature. When it is torn, the case runs warm, the coil frosts and the rack works harder. This lesson is about how the sheet is built, how it gets torn and how a tech can tell.

Check the case and the store

Check the equipment. This page is general education from public documents read in 2026. Case makers set their own discharge temperatures, load lines and placement distances. The data sheet and manual for the exact case in front of you come first, then your employer's procedures.

How the curtain is built

A fan and coil in the base push cold air up a duct behind the back panel. It leaves through a discharge grille at the top front of the case, falls down the open front and is drawn back in through a return grille at the bottom front. A 2002 fluid-mechanics study of a display case describes the same loop: cold air supplied at the top through a discharge grille and recirculated through a return grille at the bottom, with a honeycomb at the discharge grille to shape the flow. A refrigeration service company's technical article puts it plainly: the honeycomb straightens the air into a smooth sheet. The same study lists what the curtain's performance depends on: discharge angle, throw, discharge velocity, grille width, honeycomb geometry, the return air system, discharge air temperature and the number of air bands.

Two things in the drawing matter for service. The honeycomb and the return grille are the curtain's two ends, so anything that clogs either one changes the whole sheet. And the product sits right next to the sheet, so how it is stocked is part of the airflow design.

Why most of the load is store air

A U.S. Department of Energy project page says open vertical medium-temperature cases are nearly half of the case lineups in a typical supermarket and that more than 80 percent of their cooling load is attributed to infiltration of air from the surrounding space. A 2016 university conference paper on case air curtains cites earlier work putting infiltration at roughly 67 to 81 percent of the total thermal load of vertical open cabinets. The service article above, quoting a utility paper, says about 80 percent. Read those as a range for typical cases, not a number for your case. Even so, the message is the same: the curtain is not a wall. Some store air always mixes into it. The 2002 study measured the entrained air as roughly 46 to 48 percent of the discharged air, and found the jet mostly held its shape but spilled near the return grille, drawing in room air there.

That store air arrives warm and carrying moisture. The warm part is sensible load. The moisture is latent load, and it freezes on the coil, which means more defrost heat and more fan and airflow trouble. For the defrost side, see Reach-In and Display Case Defrost, Gaskets, Drains and Coil Care.

What breaks the curtain

  • Product past the load line. A case maker's guidance, quoted in the service article, says product stocked past the load line will not be properly refrigerated. The same article quotes advice to leave 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) below the shelf above. Boxes that reach into the sheet bend it, as in the drawing.
  • A blocked return grille. Makers quoted in the article say the outlet and return must stay open, with no product, packages or signs on the grille. If the sheet cannot land in the grille, it spills into the aisle and brings room air with it.
  • A dirty or poorly seated honeycomb. Lint and film narrow the cells, so the air leaves uneven. One maker's manual quoted in the article warns that a badly installed honeycomb causes poor airflow. The article suggests weekly cleaning with detergent and a soft brush.
  • Store HVAC blowing at the case. The article quotes one maker keeping cases at least 10 feet (3 m) from ceiling vents, 15 feet (4.5 m) from outside doors and 5 feet (1.5 m) from windows. Treat those as that maker's example. A diffuser aimed at the case pushes the sheet into the product.
  • Failed or slow fans. Less air in the sheet means it falls short of the return. Weak airflow along the whole case is a call for a tech the same day, especially if ice is showing.
  • An iced coil. A frosted coil starves the sheet the same way. Fix the cause, not just the ice.

Store humidity

A University of South Florida conference paper modeled a Tampa supermarket and reported that a 5 percent drop in store relative humidity cut total display case energy by about 10 percent: refrigeration load about 7 percent, defrost about 14 percent and anti-sweat heaters about 15 percent. Store air conditioning energy rose about 2 percent. The paper has a few internal inconsistencies in its tables, so treat the figures as a model, not a measurement of your store. The idea is solid: wetter store air means more moisture reaches the coil. A standard design point for supermarkets is 75 F and 55 percent relative humidity, according to the service article. For the heater side, see Anti-Sweat Heaters on Glass-Door Cases.

Diagnostic checks: discharge and return air

Put a probe in the discharge air and one in the return air, and read both with the case in refrigeration, not in defrost. The service article lists maker setpoints for discharge air: dairy and deli 29 to 32 F, meat 25 to 30 F, produce about 31 F. Use them only as a sanity check against the case data sheet. Our own reasoning from there:

  • Discharge air on target but return air climbing means the air is coming back with more heat in it. Look for a broken curtain or too much product before you touch the refrigeration side.
  • Discharge air itself too warm points to the coil, defrost termination, an evaporator pressure regulator or a suction-side problem. See Supermarket Rack and Case Controllers.
  • Log the discharge-to-return spread when the case is clean and stocked right, and compare against that later. We found no published fixed limit for the spread, so a baseline from the same case is the best yardstick.
  • The service article also describes a quick test: hold a light plastic bag at the front of the case. If it is drawn in, store air is getting in.

Worked example (our own)

A 12-ft medium-temperature case. Before cleanup, discharge air is 30 F and return air is 38 F. After someone stacks boxes on the return grille, return air reads 41 F. Estimate the change in heat the curtain air is carrying using the sensible-heat relation, Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T.

  1. Assumption: curtain airflow of 100 cfm per foot of case. That is our own assumed number, not a measured one. 12 ft x 100 = 1,200 cfm. Measure your case and replace it.
  2. Before: delta T = 38 - 30 = 8 F. Q = 1.08 x 1,200 x 8 = 1,296 x 8 = 10,368 Btu/h.
  3. After: delta T = 41 - 30 = 11 F. Q = 1.08 x 1,200 x 11 = 1,296 x 11 = 14,256 Btu/h.
  4. Change: 14,256 - 10,368 = 3,888 Btu/h, about 0.32 ton at 12,000 Btu/h per ton. That is 3 / 8 = 37.5 percent more heat in the air stream.

If the true airflow is 75 cfm per foot (900 cfm), the change is 1.08 x 900 x 3 = 2,916 Btu/h. At 125 cfm per foot (1,500 cfm) it is 1.08 x 1,500 x 3 = 4,860 Btu/h. This is a sensible-only estimate. It leaves out the latent load from the moisture, which would add to it. It also treats all of the return air rise as case load, but some of that heat comes from product, lights and radiation, not only infiltration. It assumes the airflow and discharge air stay constant. Use it to see the size of the problem, not as a measurement.

Fixes

  • Load-line discipline. Stock to the line and keep clear space under each shelf above. Show the stocking crew where the line is.
  • Keep the return grille clear. No product, signs or baskets on it. Look at it every visit.
  • Clean the honeycomb and the case, then check it seats properly.
  • Redirect or move diffusers and fans that blow at the case. Pass it to the store's HVAC contractor if the fix is on the air side.
  • Night covers. A cover over the opening after hours cuts the exchange. See Strip Curtains and Night Curtains for sourced savings figures.
  • Doors on the case. This takes the curtain out of the job. For rack background, see Supermarket Refrigeration Racks. A 2011 university news release on a retrofit that changed the discharge and return grille angle reported about a 10 percent cut in warm-air infiltration on a test case.

Field card: Air Curtains on Open Multi-Deck Cases

The curtain is a moving sheet of air. Keep both ends clear and product inside the line.

  • Honeycomb at the top front shapes the sheet; the return grille at the bottom front catches it.
  • DOE page: more than 80 percent of an open medium-temp case's cooling load is attributed to infiltration. Papers cite about 67 to 81 percent.
  • Curtain breakers: product past the load line, blocked return, dirty honeycomb, diffusers, dead fans, iced coil.
  • Discharge air on target but return air high: look at the curtain first.
  • Sensible-only: Q = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. Our example: 12 ft at an assumed 1,200 cfm, return 38 F to 41 F with 30 F discharge adds about 3,900 Btu/h.
  • Fixes: load-line discipline, clear grille, clean honeycomb, redirect diffusers, night covers, doors.
