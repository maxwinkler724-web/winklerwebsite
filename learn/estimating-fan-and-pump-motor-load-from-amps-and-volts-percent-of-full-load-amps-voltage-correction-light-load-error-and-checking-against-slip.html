Estimating Motor Load From Amps and Volts

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You are on a rooftop unit or a pump skid and want to know whether a motor is working hard or loafing. A clamp meter and a voltmeter give you a quick answer in percent of rated load. This lesson shows how to turn amps and volts into that percentage, where the answer stops being trustworthy, and what a high or low reading suggests about the fan or pump behind it.

The basic estimate: amps against nameplate

A motor draws more current as its shaft load rises. The U.S. Department of Energy fact sheet on determining motor load says the current of an induction motor varies roughly in a straight line with load, down to about half of full load. So the simplest estimate is measured current divided by the nameplate full-load amps (FLA), turned into a percent. Measure all three phases, add them and divide by three to get the average current, because the legs are never exactly equal. See Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter for meter technique.

Why it is only approximate at light load

Part of a motor's current does no useful work. It magnetizes the iron so the rotor can turn, and it stays nearly the same whether the shaft is loaded or not. At full load the working part dominates and current follows load closely. At light load the magnetizing part dominates, power factor falls, and current stops dropping in step with the load. The DOE fact sheet puts it plainly: below about 50% load the current curve becomes increasingly nonlinear, and in the low load region current measurements are not a useful indicator of load. A second DOE tip sheet repeats that below 50% load the amperage curve is not a good indicator. For a reading in that range, report "lightly loaded" instead of quoting a precise percent.

Correct for the voltage you measured

Nameplate amps apply at nameplate voltage. The DOE fact sheet explains that if supply voltage is below the nameplate value the motor draws more current for the same load, so the reading must be adjusted downward, and the reverse is true above nameplate. Its current-based formula multiplies two ratios:

Load (%) = (measured amps ÷ nameplate FLA) × (measured volts ÷ nameplate volts) × 100

Use the average of the three line-to-line voltages. The correction is usually a few percent, but on a site with a high or low supply it is worth doing. If the legs are far apart, chase that first; see Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance before trusting any single estimate.

Comparing with the slip method

Slip compares measured shaft speed with synchronous speed and with the nameplate full-load speed. Our slip lesson covers the method and the tachometer work, so we only compare the results here. The DOE fact sheet gives the formula as (synchronous rpm minus measured rpm) divided by (synchronous rpm minus full-load rpm), with a voltage-compensated version that multiplies the full-load slip by (rated volts / measured volts) squared. The same sheet says the biggest uncertainty is the tolerance allowed on nameplate full-load speed, that nameplate speeds are typically rounded to multiples of 5 rpm, and that a 5 rpm error on a 40 rpm slip changes the calculated load by about 12%. It also notes that slip varies inversely with terminal voltage squared, while current varies with voltage to the first power, and it calls the slip method generally not recommended for field load determination. We treat slip as a cross-check on the amps estimate, never the other way around. Amps work well in the upper range; slip needs a good tachometer and a trustworthy nameplate speed.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These numbers are ours, made up for teaching. They are not from a real motor.

Assumptions: a 5 hp, 460 V, three-phase, four-pole motor on a belt-driven supply fan. Nameplate FLA is 6.9 A (assumed). Nameplate full-load speed is 1750 rpm (assumed). Synchronous speed for four poles at 60 Hz is 1800 rpm.

Measured: currents 3.9 A, 4.1 A and 4.0 A. Line-to-line voltages 474 V, 478 V and 472 V. Shaft speed 1770 rpm.

  1. Average current: (3.9 + 4.1 + 4.0) ÷ 3 = 12.0 ÷ 3 = 4.0 A.
  2. Average voltage: (474 + 478 + 472) ÷ 3 = 1424 ÷ 3 = 474.7 V.
  3. Current ratio: 4.0 ÷ 6.9 = 0.580, so 58% before voltage correction.
  4. Voltage ratio: 474.7 ÷ 460 = 1.032.
  5. Corrected load: 0.580 × 1.032 = 0.598, about 60%. That is roughly 0.60 × 5 hp = 3.0 hp of shaft output.
  6. Slip, uncorrected: (1800 − 1770) ÷ (1800 − 1750) = 30 ÷ 50 = 60%.
  7. Slip, voltage compensated: (460 ÷ 474.7) squared = 0.939, so full-load slip becomes 50 × 0.939 = 47.0 rpm. Then 30 ÷ 47.0 = 64%.

Reading the result: the amps estimate says about 60% and the slip estimate says 60% to 64%. They agree within a few points, which is a good sign. Notice how touchy slip is: if the tach had read 1765 rpm instead of 1770, the uncorrected slip answer would jump to 70%, while a 0.1 A change in current moves the amps answer by only about 1.5 points. Because the result sits above 50%, we trust the amps number more. If it had come in at 25%, we would simply write "lightly loaded."

What a high or low reading suggests

The DOE tip sheet reports that field tests of 60 motors found they averaged about 60% of rated load, and that motors driving supply or return fans generally ran at 70% to 75%. So a fan motor near 60% to 75% is unremarkable. Beyond that, the following are our own field reasoning, so confirm each one with airflow or pressure measurements instead of assuming:

  • Low load on a centrifugal fan: airflow may be restricted by a closed or stuck damper, a plugged filter bank or a collapsed duct. On many centrifugal fans power falls as airflow is choked off, so low amps with low airflow points to a restriction, not a healthy motor.
  • High load or amps above FLA: the fan may be moving more air than designed, for example after a sheave change, an open bypass or missing access panels. Fan power rises steeply with speed; see Fan Laws. A wrong sheave is the usual cause after a swap.
  • Persistently low load on a pump: check for a throttled valve, an oversized pump, or air in the system.
  • High amps with low voltage, or one leg far from the others: look at the supply, not the load.

A motor running far below half load is not automatically a problem. The second DOE tip sheet calls the idea that lightly loaded motors must be replaced right away a persistent myth, and notes that larger motors can run with reasonably high efficiency down to about 25% load, while efficiency drops faster in small motors. For the cost side, see the motor efficiency and replacement lesson, and for kW math see three-phase power math.

Safety

Clamping a conductor with the panel energized is live work. Use the right PPE, keep guards on rotating drives, and lock out the unit before you touch any belt or sheave. Tachometer readings on a belt-driven fan need a clear line to the shaft and no loose clothing near the drive.

Field card: Estimating Motor Load From Amps and Volts

Average the legs, correct for volts, trust it above half load.

  • Load % = (avg amps ÷ FLA) × (avg volts ÷ nameplate volts) × 100.
  • Best above about 50% load; below that, say "light" and stop.
  • Cross-check with slip, but slip is touchy about 5 rpm and nameplate speed.
  • Low load: look for restrictions. High load: look for speed, sheave or supply problems.
  • Our worked example: 4.0 A on 6.9 A FLA, 474.7 V on 460 V, about 60%.
