Heat Pump Base Pan Heaters and Defrost Ice Under the Outdoor Unit

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump in heating mode melts frost off its outdoor coil all winter, and that water has to leave the unit before it freezes again. Where it goes, and what keeps it from turning into a block of ice, is the subject here.

Where the meltwater goes

In a defrost cycle the unit warms the outdoor coil until the frost lets go (see Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter and Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat). The water runs down the coil fins into the base pan, the shallow tray in the bottom of the cabinet, and out through drain holes or slots in that pan to the ground below. The ground side is why mounting height matters; the pads and stands lesson covers how high and on what, so we will not repeat it.

The trouble comes when the outdoor air is below freezing. Water that reaches the pan can freeze there before it drains, especially if the drain holes are blocked by snow, leaves or a unit sitting flat on a slab. Each defrost then adds a layer. A northeast efficiency group's 2017 installation assessment lists a unit set on a pad or short risers as a source of "defrost ice build-up" trouble. Ice that rises from the pan can reach the bottom of the coil and, in a worst case, the fan blade. Our own advice: treat any ice higher than the pan edge as a problem, not a quirk.

What a base pan heater is

A base pan heater is a small electric resistance element in or under the pan. Its job is to keep meltwater liquid long enough to drain. A trade writer describes it as a small element that keeps meltwater from refreezing, notes that some makers sell the same model with or without it, and says it is not generally needed in milder climates if daily highs stay above freezing, while calling it necessary in cold ones.

Do you need one?

The federal energy lab guide for installing cold-climate heat pumps says pan heaters are not generally needed when meltwater clearance and protection are in place, and may be of limited value except in extreme (for example marine) environments. The NEEP 2017 assessment says the same thing in a table: needed only where clearance is inadequate, of limited value with a drip cap and adequate ground clearance. Both documents also say to keep the unit out from under roof drip lines, away from walkways where meltwater could freeze into a slip hazard, and above the snow line (the NEEP table gives more than 24 inches). So the order of defenses is: raise the unit, keep it off drip lines, keep drain holes open, then add a heater where the site still ices up.

When the heater runs

Control varies by model, so read the unit's own documents. One maker's submittal for a base pan heater kit (cited as plain text, no link) says the heater is powered and controlled by the outdoor unit board. In heating mode below 30°F (-1°C) it turns on. A separate snow-prevention setting can also be enabled: after the unit has been idle 30 minutes with outdoor air at 41°F (5°C) or lower, the heater runs 5 minutes out of every 30. The kit also requires an outdoor option setting to be turned on. NEEP's cold-climate specification says that where a pan heater is built in or sold as an accessory, the maker must report its standalone input power in kW and what determines when it runs, and it keeps pan heater power out of the efficiency test table. So pan heater energy is real but is not in the rated efficiency number. Defrost boards and sensors are covered in their own lesson.

Worked example: yearly kWh in a NJ-like climate

Cited inputs. The kit submittal above lists 123 W at 208 V and 150 W at 230 V. Our own check: resistance heaters scale with voltage squared, and 150 × (208 / 230)2 = 150 × 0.818 = 122.7 W, which matches 123, so the pairing holds. We use 150 W = 0.150 kW (a 230 V home). The NWS Newark January 1991 to 2020 normals show an average high of 40°F, low of 25.5°F and mean of 32.8°F; February is 43°F, 27.2°F and 35.1°F.

Assumptions (Our own, not measured data). Hours below 30°F by month, chosen to fit those normals: November 40, December 110, January 240, February 190, March 60.

  1. Total hours below 30°F: 40 + 110 + 240 + 190 + 60 = 640 h.
  2. Worst case, heater on every one of those hours: 0.150 kW × 640 h = 96 kWh per year.
  3. If the unit is heating only half of those hours (assumed 50 percent duty): 96 × 0.5 = 48 kWh.
  4. Snow-prevention mode instead (assumed 1,400 hours per year at or below 41°F with the unit idle; duty 5 / 30 = 0.167): 1,400 × 0.167 = 233 h on, and 233 × 0.150 = 35 kWh.
  5. Cost at an assumed $0.20 per kWh: 96 kWh = $19.20; 48 kWh = $9.60; 35 kWh = $7.00.

Reality check. Our own caution: wattage alone does not set the bill. The control logic and the hours below the trigger temperature do, so read the maker's description of when the heater runs before you estimate cost.

Drain holes: keep them open

  • Look at the pan drain holes after snow and ice storms; the unit should sit high enough that snow does not reach them.
  • Do not stack leaves, mulch, a cover or stored items against the base.
  • Route any piped condensate downhill, away from crawl spaces, walkways and outdoor equipment, as the federal lab guide says. Check the maker's vertical lift limit if the drain is piped.
  • Do not seal or plug a pan drain hole to keep water in.

Homeowner do's and don'ts

A national maker's homeowner page (plain text citation) says: do not chip or break the ice, because tools can damage coils, fins and fan blades; never pour hot water on the unit, because the sudden temperature change can crack components; turn the heat pump off at the thermostat first; and call a professional if it refreezes quickly or will not restart. We add, as our own judgment, that you should keep people and pets away from a unit with ice hanging above the fan guard.

When to call a tech

  • Ice that stays through several defrost cycles or climbs above the pan edge.
  • Ice touching the fan blade, or a fan that will not turn.
  • A pan heater that never seems to run, or one that runs all day in mild weather (a tech checks the sensor, option setting and wiring; owners should not open the panel).
  • Meltwater freezing on a walkway or steps.

Those same symptoms are also covered in the Cold-Climate Heat Pumps lesson from the system-sizing side.

Field card: Base pan heaters and defrost ice

Raise it, drain it, then heat it.

  • Meltwater leaves through pan drain holes; frozen holes mean ice builds in the pan.
  • Federal lab guide: pan heaters are often not needed with good clearance and a drip cap.
  • Check the unit's own control: one kit runs below 30°F in heating mode.
  • Worked example (assumptions labeled): 0.150 kW × 640 h = 96 kWh a year.
  • Never chip ice or pour hot water. Turn off at the thermostat and call a pro.
