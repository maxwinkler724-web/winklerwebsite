Where to Put a Thermostat: Placement, Height and False Readings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostat does not know how warm your house is. It knows how warm the small patch of air around it is. If that patch is sunny, drafty or heated by a vent, the whole system follows a bad number. This lesson covers the best place to put a thermostat, using the makers' own install rules.

The short answer

Several makers agree on the basics: mount it on an inside wall, about 5 feet (1.5 m) above the floor, in a spot with good air circulation at a typical temperature for the home. Keep it out of direct sun, away from supply air, and out of drafts and dead-air corners. Then plug the hole behind it, because air moving inside the wall can fool it too.

What the makers say

  • Resideo (Honeywell Home). The instructions for the T8775 Digital Round say to install the thermostat about 5 ft above the floor in an area with good air circulation at average temperature, and to avoid drafts, dead spots behind doors and in corners, and hot or cold air from ducts. The T10 product data adds unheated or uncooled areas such as an outside wall behind the thermostat to the avoid list.
  • White-Rodgers (Copeland). Its instruction sheet says about 5 ft up, on a section of wall free of pipes or air ducts. It lists windows and exterior walls, direct sun and other heat sources, the front of air outlets, concealed ducts and chimneys, spots with poor airflow such as behind a door, and frequent drafts such as the head of a stairway.
  • ENERGY STAR. Its heating and cooling guide says to keep a thermostat away from registers, appliances, lighting, doorways, fireplaces, skylights, windows, and areas with direct sun or drafts. It says interior walls are best. That is where lamps and other heat-giving lights come in: lighting is on its list.
  • Google Nest, ecobee and Sensi. The Nest thermostat install page and the ecobee and Sensi install pages we opened did not give location rules for the thermostat itself. Their sensor pages do. Nest says chest height, and Sensi says 5 ft off the floor facing a room with good airflow. Both say to avoid vents, stoves, windows, uninsulated walls, direct sun and closed-in spots.

The hidden problem: air inside the wall

Even a perfectly placed thermostat can read wrong if the hole its wires come through is open. Air can move inside the wall cavity, from an attic, a crawl space or another room, and flow across the back of the thermostat. ecobee calls this "wall draft," says it is the most common outside factor that affects a reading, and says it can make the furnace or AC run longer than needed. Its fix is to plug the hole behind the thermostat with plumber's putty. Lutron's instructions say the back of a wall-mounted thermostat should not be exposed to airflow or drafts and, for its metal wallbox, to seal the gaps between the wires. Switch off power to the heating and cooling system before you touch wiring.

Remote sensors and averaging

If you cannot move the thermostat, many models can use a separate sensor in a better spot. The rules differ by brand, so read the one for your model.

  • ecobee says that once a SmartSensor is in use, the temperature shown is the average of the thermostat's own sensor and the SmartSensor. If the thermostat itself sits in direct sun, it says you can turn its sensor off and rely on a SmartSensor in a better spot.
  • Resideo's T10 product data says its indoor sensors can be used together for temperature averaging or on their own, and that when more than one is used the readings are averaged.
  • Google says that while a Nest sensor is active, the thermostat uses the sensor's temperature instead of its own internal sensors.
  • Sensi says its temperature offset applies only to the thermostat, not to its sensors.

Sensors need good spots too: Resideo's sensor page says about 5 ft up on an inside wall, away from furniture, doors, sun and vents.

Symptoms of a badly placed thermostat

  • Some rooms are too hot while others are too cold. Google says a sensor placed too high or too low can cause over- or under-cooling elsewhere in the home.
  • The system runs longer than it should. ecobee ties this to wall draft.
  • The display disagrees with another thermometer. Sensi says that in nearly all cases where a digital thermostat reads inaccurately, the cause is a poor thermostat location.

If the equipment starts and stops quickly, a misplaced thermostat is one thing to rule out, but not the only one. See Why Is My Furnace Short Cycling? for the common equipment causes, and Thermostat Blank or Showing the Wrong Temperature if the screen itself is the problem.

Check it with a separate thermometer

Do not guess. Put a second thermometer right beside the thermostat, at the same height and not touching the wall or the thermostat, and let both settle. Then compare. Next, put the second thermometer in the middle of the room you care about and compare again. The two comparisons tell you different things: the first tells you whether the thermostat is reading correctly where it hangs, and the second tells you whether where it hangs is a good spot.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

These numbers are made up to show the method. They are not measurements from a real house.

  1. Three readings with a thermometer beside the thermostat: thermostat shows 74.0, 73.5 and 74.5 °F. The thermometer shows 71.5, 71.0 and 72.0 °F.
  2. Average the thermostat: (74.0 + 73.5 + 74.5) ÷ 3 = 222.0 ÷ 3 = 74.0 °F.
  3. Average the thermometer: (71.5 + 71.0 + 72.0) ÷ 3 = 214.5 ÷ 3 = 71.5 °F.
  4. Difference: 74.0 − 71.5 = 2.5 °F high. The thermostat reads warm.
  5. What that means in winter: with a setpoint of 70 °F, the thermostat thinks it is 70 when the air at that spot is about 67.5 °F. It shuts the heat off early and the room runs cool.
  6. Look for a cause before correcting it. Say the hole behind the thermostat was open. Plug it, wait, and repeat the readings. Suppose they now give 72.5 and 71.5, a difference of 1.0 °F. The plug removed 2.5 − 1.0 = 1.5 °F of error.
  7. If a 1.0 °F error remains, some thermostats let you enter an offset. Sensi's offset page allows adjusting by plus or minus 5 °F in total, so a 1.0 °F correction fits. Check your model's manual for its range.
  8. Now the averaging case. If the thermostat reads 74 °F in a hall and a sensor in the bedroom reads 70 °F, an averaging setup shows (74 + 70) ÷ 2 = 72 °F. The 72 is a blend. It does not match either place, so judge comfort by the room you sit in.

Be careful with offsets. A fixed offset fixes a steady error. It does not fix sun that hits the wall for two hours, or a draft that comes and goes. Sensi says that if a plus or minus 5 °F offset will not give you comfort, the next step is to check equipment sizing with a load calculation. Moving the thermostat, or adding a sensor, fixes the cause.

Moving a thermostat: the wiring side

Relocating a thermostat means working with the control wires. They are low voltage, but guides still say to turn off power to the system first. Photograph the wiring and label each wire by its terminal letter, not by color, before you disconnect anything. Thermostat Terminal Letters: R, C, W, Y, G Explained walks through what each letter asks of the equipment, and Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats covers smart models. If the new spot needs new cable runs, ask a licensed pro. In New Jersey, who may do control wiring depends on the license.

Does height have a legal rule?

For homes, the makers' 5 ft figure is a performance rule, so the reading is accurate. Accessibility is separate. The U.S. Access Board lists thermostats among operable parts. Its guide gives an unobstructed forward or side reach range of 15 to 48 inches, applying to the operable portions only. It does not give a thermostat-specific height. Whether those rules apply to a given building is a question for the local building authority.

Field card: Where to Put a Thermostat

The thermostat controls the house by reading one small patch of air. Make it a fair patch.

  • Inside wall, about 5 ft (1.5 m) up, with good airflow, in a typical-temperature area.
  • Not in sun, not by supply air, not in drafts or behind a door.
  • Plug the wire hole behind it; wall draft can skew the reading.
  • Sensors: averaging, or sensor replaces the internal reading, depending on brand.
  • Check with a second thermometer beside it, then in the room.
  • An offset hides a steady error; moving it fixes the cause.
  • Label wires by terminal letter, with power off, before moving.
