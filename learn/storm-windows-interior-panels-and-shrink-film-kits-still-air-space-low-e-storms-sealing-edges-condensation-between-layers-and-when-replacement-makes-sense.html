Storm Windows, Interior Panels and Shrink Film Kits: Adding a Still Air Space

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A single pane of glass is a poor barrier to heat. Adding a second layer, whether a storm window outside, a panel inside, or a sheet of shrink film, traps a pocket of air that slows heat flow. This lesson explains how that works, how the options differ, why the edges matter so much, and how to estimate what one extra layer saves over a heating season.

Why a second layer helps

One university extension storm window page says the air trapped between the storm layer and the existing window acts as insulation, slowing heat loss and cutting drafts. Both that page and a second university extension storm window guide recommend an air space of about 3/4 inch to 4 inches between the layers. The key word is still. Air only insulates well when it cannot move. If outdoor air or room air can stream through gaps at the edges, the pocket turns into a drafty channel and most of the benefit is lost.

A second layer also helps with air leakage. The federal energy agency's consumer storm windows page calls storm windows an air sealing measure and says they can cut a home's overall air leakage by 10% or more.

Exterior storms, interior panels and film

The federal energy agency page says storm windows can go on either the inside or the outside of the existing window. The extension guides describe three common kinds:

  • Exterior combination storms. The second extension guide describes these as aluminum-framed units mounted outside. The first extension page adds that they are permanent units with glass and insect screens that let you open the window for ventilation.
  • Interior panels. The first extension page says interior options are easier to install and maintain, leave the outside of the house unchanged, and suit city, multi-level, historic and rental homes. The second extension guide says rigid plastic sheets should be mounted inside, and that interior mounting avoids wind damage.
  • Shrink film kits. The second extension guide calls plastic film the simplest option, made at home or bought as a kit. It suggests clear kits that tighten when warmed with a hair dryer, and warns that tacking or stapling film straight to the window frame damages it. It suggests a simple 1-by-2-inch wood frame instead, or permanent mounting tracks so only the film is replaced each winter.

For renters, interior panels and film are the usual choice because they come off without changing the building. Ask the landlord before using tape or fasteners on frames. The second extension guide says the three types are about equally effective at saving energy; the more permanent ones mainly win on looks, convenience and the ability to open them.

Low-e storm windows

Older storm windows were plain clear glass. The federal energy agency page says newer models are available with a low-emissivity (low-e) coating and that low-e storm windows reflect radiant heat 35% better than clear glass storms. The same page says low-e interior or exterior storms can save 10% to 30% on heating and cooling, depending on the window already in place. A national lab report on low-e storm windows across climate zones modeled savings separately for the coating, for the lower U-factor and for reduced air leakage, which is a reminder that the edges and the glass both count.

Sealing the edges

The federal energy agency page recommends weatherstripping at all movable joints and interlocking or overlapping joints on storm windows. Both extension guides say to caulk and weatherstrip the primary window sash and frame first, then weatherstrip the storm window so the air space is effective. For exterior mounting, the first extension page advises against silicone caulk and suggests butyl or elastomeric caulk, which is easier to deal with when the unit is removed or repaired later. With film, run the tape on a clean, dry surface and shrink the film until it is smooth and tight.

Condensation between the layers

The first extension page says a well-sealed storm window can reduce condensation and frost, and the second extension guide says the same about a good seal. Both tie moisture control to caulking and weatherstripping the primary window. The first extension page also notes that storm windows with weep holes help keep moisture from building up. If you see fog or frost between the layers, look first for gaps where warm, damp room air is getting into the space, and check indoor humidity. Our lesson on condensation on windows in winter covers the humidity side.

Comfort near cold windows

A national lab study of interior storm window inserts in one older home reported a 22% drop in the HVAC energy bill. Its comfort result is worth reading carefully: the measured indoor temperatures showed no measurable change, but the occupant reported the house felt warmer in winter, cooler in summer and more even from room to room. So expect a change in feel, and do not count on a thermometer to prove it.

When replacement windows make more sense

The federal energy agency page says low-e storm windows can deliver savings similar to full window replacement. An added layer only works if the window behind it is sound, though. If the sash or frame is rotted, glass is broken, or the window will not close and latch, those problems need repair or replacement, and a storm layer will not fix them. For the numbers behind window ratings and how replacement units compare, see our lesson on window U-factor and solar heat gain. Get a written assessment from a qualified contractor before deciding.

Worked example: one window, one season (our own example)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen to show the method, not a measured or published value for any real window.

  • Assumed window area A = 15 square feet.
  • Assumed single-pane U-factor = 1.0 Btu per hour per square foot per °F.
  • Assumed U-factor with a storm panel added = 0.5 Btu per hour per square foot per °F.
  • Assumed indoor temperature 70°F and outdoor temperature 20°F, so delta-T = 50°F.
  • Assumed season of 5,000 heating degree days (65°F base).

Step 1, heat loss rate at that moment (Q = U × A × delta-T).

  • Single pane: 1.0 × 15 × 50 = 750 Btu per hour.
  • With storm panel: 0.5 × 15 × 50 = 375 Btu per hour.
  • Reduction: 750 − 375 = 375 Btu per hour, half the conduction loss.

Step 2, over the season (Btu = U × A × HDD × 24 hours per day).

  • Single pane: 1.0 × 15 × 5,000 × 24 = 1,800,000 Btu.
  • With storm panel: 0.5 × 15 × 5,000 × 24 = 900,000 Btu.
  • Saved: 1,800,000 − 900,000 = 900,000 Btu per season for this one window.

Check. Cutting U in half should cut the loss in half at any temperature, and both steps do: 375 is half of 750, and 900,000 is half of 1,800,000. Units also check: Btu/h·ft²·°F × ft² × °F-days × h/day leaves Btu.

Limits of this estimate: it counts conduction only. It leaves out air leakage (which a sealed panel also reduces), sun through the glass, and the efficiency of the heating equipment. Step 1 uses a 70°F room while the degree days use a 65°F base, so the two steps are separate illustrations, not one calculation. The heating degree days lesson explains the base temperature and how to turn Btu into fuel.

Field card: Storm Windows, Interior Panels and Shrink Film

Still air insulates. Moving air does not.

  • An extra layer traps air; extension guides suggest a 3/4 to 4 inch gap.
  • Exterior combination storms, interior panels and shrink film all add that layer.
  • Low-e storms reflect radiant heat 35% better than clear storms (DOE).
  • DOE: low-e storms can save 10% to 30% on heating and cooling, depending on the existing window.
  • Caulk and weatherstrip the primary window first, then weatherstrip the storm.
  • Fog between layers: look for leaks of damp room air and check indoor humidity.
  • Heat loss: Q = U × A × delta-T; season: U × A × HDD × 24.
  • Rotted or broken windows need repair or replacement; a storm layer will not fix them.
