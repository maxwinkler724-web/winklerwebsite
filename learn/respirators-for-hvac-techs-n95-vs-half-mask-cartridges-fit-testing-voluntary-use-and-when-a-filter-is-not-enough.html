Respirators for HVAC Techs: N95 vs Half-Mask Cartridges, Fit Testing, Voluntary Use and When a Filter Is Not Enough

Last updated: 2026-10-27

A paper mask from a box and a fitted rubber half-mask are not the same tool, and neither one helps if the air has no oxygen in it. This lesson shows how to match a respirator to the job, what the markings mean, and what the federal respirator rule asks for.

When HVAC work calls for one

Here are common jobs where we stop and think about breathing protection: mold on a coil, drain pan or duct liner; dusty attics and fiberglass insulation; cutting or coring masonry and concrete (see Silica Dust for HVAC Techs); spray-on coil cleaners and other solvent products; and the acidic residue after a burned-out hermetic compressor (see Compressor Burnout Cleanup). Which respirator fits depends on what is in the air, not on the trade. Read the product label and safety data sheet for any chemical, and treat old insulation or paint as possibly asbestos or lead until tested (see Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems). On mold, the EPA remediation guide lays out three tiers: a minimum tier with an N-95, a limited tier with a half-face or full-face air-purifying respirator and a HEPA cartridge, and a full tier with a full-face powered air-purifying respirator. It says limited containment is generally recommended for roughly 10 to 100 square feet of contamination, and notes that area alone is not decisive. Our companion lesson on mold on the coil or in ducts covers the moisture side.

When a filter is not enough

An air-purifying respirator only cleans the air that passes through it. NIOSH says these respirators are not designed for gases, vapors or very small particles that their filters will not capture. A particulate filter, such as a dust mask, will not stop a gas or vapor, and OSHA's Appendix D makes the same point: a dust filter will not protect you from gases, vapors or very fine fumes and smoke. Gas and vapor protection needs cartridges chosen for the specific chemical.

Low oxygen is a different problem entirely. Under 29 CFR 1910.134(d)(2), an atmosphere below 19.5 percent oxygen by volume is treated as immediately dangerous to life or health, and in that case the rule calls for a full facepiece pressure-demand self-contained breathing apparatus rated for at least 30 minutes, or a combination full facepiece pressure-demand supplied-air respirator with an auxiliary air supply. No filter or cartridge makes oxygen. Pits, tanks and tight crawlspaces get tested first and entered under a permit program; see Confined Spaces in HVAC Work.

N95 vs elastomeric half-mask

A filtering facepiece (the N95 type) is the whole mask: it is thrown away when it is dirty, damaged or hard to breathe through. An elastomeric half-mask is a reusable rubber or silicone facepiece with replaceable filters or cartridges. NIOSH notes that filtering facepieces are comfortable and need no maintenance but are harder to fit-test, and cites one study with an average dust reduction of only 67 percent; it also lists skin irritation and trouble with glasses as downsides of reusable half-masks. Half-masks take cartridges for vapors as well as particulate filters, which a paper mask cannot do.

Filter labels have a letter and a number. NIOSH says N means not oil resistant, R is oil resistant for one shift, and P is oil proof. The number is the minimum share of test particles captured: 95 means 95 percent, 99 means 99 percent, and 100 means 99.97 percent. Oil mist from a percussion drill is a reason to move past N.

Reading the NIOSH markings

NIOSH says an approved filtering facepiece shows the maker name, a part number, the filter series and efficiency (such as N95 or P100), a TC approval number (written TC 84A followed by digits), the word NIOSH in capital letters, and a lot number. It states that a respirator missing a required label item is not NIOSH approved, even if it is sold as an "N95." You can look up the TC number on the NIOSH Certified Equipment List. Appendix D likewise tells users to pick a respirator certified for the contaminant they face.

Voluntary or required use

If a respirator is only a personal choice and the air is within limits, use is voluntary. Under 1910.134(c)(2) the employer may allow it if it does not itself create a hazard, and must give the employee the Appendix D information. NIOSH adds that when only filtering facepieces are used voluntarily, a full program is not required. Appendix D tells voluntary wearers to follow the maker's instructions for use, care and storage, to keep their own respirator identifiable so they do not pick up someone else's, and reminds them that a poorly kept respirator can be a hazard. If exposure is above a limit or the employer requires the respirator, it is required use, and the full written program applies. The EPA mold guide says anyone wearing a half-face or full-face respirator should be trained, medically cleared and fit tested under an OSHA program.

Medical evaluation, fit testing and seal checks

  • Medical evaluation. A physician or other licensed health care professional must decide that the worker can use a respirator before fit testing or work use (1910.134(e)(1)).
  • Fit testing. Required before first use, whenever the make, model, style or size changes, and at least once a year. A change such as scarring, dental work or a major weight change calls for a new test (1910.134(f)).
  • Seal check. Every time a tight-fitting respirator is put on, the wearer does a user seal check (1910.134(g)(1)(iii)). It does not replace a fit test.
  • Facial hair. The employer must not allow a tight-fitting facepiece if facial hair lies between the sealing surface and the face or interferes with valve function, and a fit test may not be done over facial hair ((g)(1)(i)(A) and Appendix A). NIOSH says loose-fitting powered units can fit over facial hair.

Cartridge change schedules and storage

For gases and vapors, 1910.134(d)(3)(i)(B) allows an air-purifying respirator if it has a NIOSH-certified end-of-service-life indicator for that contaminant; otherwise the employer sets a change schedule from objective data and records the basis. NIOSH guidance says odor should not decide when to change cartridges, and anyone who smells the contaminant should leave the area and the schedule should be reviewed. For storage, 1910.134(h)(2) says to protect respirators from damage, contamination, dust, sunlight, temperature extremes, moisture and damaging chemicals, and to store them so the facepiece and exhalation valve do not deform. A sealed bag in a clean case does that job better than a loose spot in the truck bed.

Worked example (ours)

The OSHA table of assigned protection factors (APF), in 1910.134(d)(3)(i)(A), gives these air-purifying values: half mask 10, full facepiece 50. The maximum use concentration (MUC) is the APF multiplied by the exposure limit, and the employer must keep exposure at or below it.

Assumptions (ours, not real measurements): a particulate with an assumed limit of 2 mg/m3. Job A, an assumed measured level of 12 mg/m3. Job B, an assumed level of 50 mg/m3. No gas, no oil mist, normal oxygen.

  1. Required reduction, Job A: 12 / 2 = 6. A half mask (APF 10) has an MUC of 10 x 2 = 20 mg/m3. Since 12 is under 20, a fitted half mask with a particulate filter is enough, assuming a fit test, medical clearance and a clean-shaven seal.
  2. Job B: 50 / 2 = 25. The half-mask MUC of 20 is below 50, so it fails. A full facepiece (APF 50) has an MUC of 50 x 2 = 100 mg/m3, and 50 is under 100, so it works.
  3. Job C, if oxygen were found at 17 percent: below 19.5 percent, so no air-purifying choice applies and we go to supplied air or SCBA under the rule above.

This does not decide a filter class: a measured level and a real limit for the actual substance must be known first, and for mold there is rarely a numeric limit, so we follow the EPA tiers above.

Field card: Respirators for HVAC Techs

Pick the respirator for the air, then fit it, check it, store it.

  • Know the contaminant first; read the label and safety data sheet.
  • A particulate filter does not stop gases or vapors. Vapors need the right cartridge.
  • Oxygen below 19.5 percent is IDLH: supplied air or SCBA, never a filter.
  • Look for NIOSH, the TC number and the filter class on the respirator; verify the TC number on the NIOSH list.
  • Required use means medical evaluation, fit test at least yearly, and a seal check each time.
  • Facial hair under the seal means no tight-fitting mask.
  • Change cartridges by schedule or indicator, not by smell.
  • Store clean and sealed, away from heat, sun and chemicals.
