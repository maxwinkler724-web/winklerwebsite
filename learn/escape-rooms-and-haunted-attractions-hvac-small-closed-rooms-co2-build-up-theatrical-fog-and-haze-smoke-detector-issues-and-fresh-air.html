Escape Rooms and Haunted Attractions HVAC: CO2, Fog and Smoke Detectors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An escape room business often takes one tenant space and cuts it into five or six small rooms, then asks the one or two rooftop units that served the open floor to keep every room fresh. Each room holds a team for about an hour with the door closed. This lesson covers the CO2 arithmetic, how to get air into and out of each room, and why theatrical fog is a fire-alarm question before it is an HVAC one.

Check the authority having jurisdiction

Check the authority having jurisdiction. Fire and building rules for game rooms and temporary attractions are set locally. This page was built from public government pages read in 2026 and is general education, not code advice. Your fire marshal and building official decide what applies.

Why a closed room fills with CO2

People breathe out CO2 and the room does not know the door is shut. NIST Technical Note 2213 gives the steady-state relation as concentration rise equals generation divided by outdoor airflow, and lists about 0.0051 L/s of CO2 per adult in an office at 1.4 met (a measure of activity). Teams solving puzzles move more than office workers, so treat that as a floor. The EPA-hosted Lawrence Berkeley analysis of its BASE data treats the indoor-minus-outdoor CO2 difference as a stand-in for ventilation per person, not as a cause of symptoms. Outdoor air is often taken as about 400 ppm (NIST default).

For limits, NIOSH lists carbon dioxide at 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average and 30,000 ppm short-term, and OSHA's limit is 5,000 ppm average. Those are exposure limits for workers, not comfort targets. They are far above where a sensor-based control would act; see the sensor lesson linked below.

Worked example: one sealed room

These inputs are our assumptions for a teaching example, not measurements of any real room. Room 10 ft by 12 ft by 9 ft. Six players. CO2 generation of 0.0051 L/s per person (NIST office adult value), which is 0.0051 x 2.119 = 0.0108 cfm per person. Well-mixed air, no leaks, outdoor CO2 400 ppm.

  1. Volume: 10 x 12 x 9 = 1,080 ft3.
  2. Room generation: 6 x 0.0108 = 0.0648 cfm of CO2.
  3. No fresh air at all. Rise per minute = 0.0648 / 1,080 = 0.00006, or 60 ppm per minute. After 60 minutes that is about 3,600 ppm above the start, roughly 4,000 ppm absolute from a 400 ppm start.
  4. Steady state with air. Rise = 0.0648 / Q x 1,000,000 ppm. At 15 cfm the rise is 4,323 ppm; at 30 cfm, 2,161; at 60 cfm, 1,081; at 93 cfm, 697.
  5. Airflow for a 700 ppm rise. Q = 0.0648 / 0.0007 = 93 cfm of outdoor air, about 15 cfm per player, or 93 x 60 / 1,080 = 5.2 air changes per hour.
  6. Time constant = 1,080 / 93 = 11.6 minutes, so the room is near steady state (95 percent) after about 3 x 11.6 = 35 minutes (our arithmetic from the standard exponential).

Four such rooms at 93 cfm each need 372 cfm of outdoor air together. Active players (above 1.4 met) generate more, so the 93 cfm is a floor for this assumed team. The 700 ppm target is our choice for the example; the engineer of record sets the real one.

Ventilation rates for these uses sit in ASHRAE Standard 62.1 and local mechanical code. We did not find a public table for escape rooms, so ask the engineer of record which occupancy row applies. We name the standard only; read it from a licensed copy.

Getting air into and out of each room

  • Supply to each room. A single return in a hallway starves the rooms. Give every room its own supply branch with a balancing damper, then set the damper so the room gets its share of outdoor air, not just its share of total air.
  • A way out. Air pushed into a shut room needs an exit or the room pressurizes and the door sticks or whistles. See Transfer Grilles and Jump Ducts for sizing. In a game room the grille also carries sound and light, so an acoustically lined path or offset opening is the usual idea; ask the owner what the story needs.
  • The RTU sees all rooms mixed. One unit serving many rooms cannot give each its own outdoor air from a single damper. Rooms with sensors can drive a shared damper (see Demand-Controlled Ventilation) but the worst room should set the minimum, or add a room-level boost.
  • Dense occupancy and heat. Compare with Esports and Gaming Lounge HVAC, which has the same room-by-room problem with more electronics.

Theatrical fog, haze and detectors

A NIOSH science blog on theater safety says NIOSH found no evidence that theatrical smoke, at the levels measured in four Broadway shows, causes occupational asthma, but that aerosolized glycols and mineral oil may irritate or dry mucous membranes. It suggests moving machines away from performers, using less fluid, using only the fluid the maker approves, and heating the fluid only as much as needed. OSHA and NIOSH both list mineral oil mist at 5 mg/m3 as an 8-hour average (NIOSH also gives 10 mg/m3 short-term). In a NIOSH evaluation of fire-training simulants, mineral oil mist and diethylene glycol both exceeded short-term limits in a closed training room, and concentrations just outside the door were much lower with the door mostly closed than with it open. That is heavy fog in training, not a haze machine in a game room, but it supports the same habit: keep doors closed while fog runs, then exhaust.

The detector question is a fire-code question. A university fire marshal page says smoke and haze can trip detectors and the building alarm, asks for requests at least two weeks ahead, weighs how near detectors and exits are, and may require staff to monitor the alarm. A Wisconsin state guideline for haunted houses says artificial fog needs approval from the authority having jurisdiction. A Texas Department of Insurance page suggests low-lying or fast-dissipating fog and an emergency stop that shuts off fog and effects. A Massachusetts state document says the fire official can order removal of fog or haze machines that could obstruct egress.

HVAC consequences, from our own reasoning. The return carries fog particles to the filters, so expect faster loading and an oily film on filters and coil surfaces if glycol or oil fog runs often. Check pressure drop across filters and look at the coil face more often. If the unit has a duct smoke detector, the unit may shut down on fog even when no room detector alarms. Never bypass a detector yourself. Tell the fire alarm contractor which unit serves which rooms. For the stage version of this problem see Theater and Auditorium HVAC.

Locked rooms and the fire marshal

Whether a game room door may be locked is not an HVAC call. A Charlotte Fire Department interpretation, issued after talking with the North Carolina State Fire Marshal's office, says a main exit door may be locked only if a second exit is clearly marked and fitted with approved panic hardware. Other places may differ. Smoke control, duct detectors and room detectors in a space split up with new walls are worth a call to the fire marshal before the walls go up.

Seasonal haunted attractions in temporary buildings

Several state fire marshal documents set limits that touch HVAC. Delaware's guidelines allow temporary use only up to 15 days in a calendar year, prohibit portable heating units and open flame, and require a permit. Massachusetts notes that an unheated building can expose permanently mounted smoke detectors to temperature extremes that may violate their listing, which may call for replacement or another approved detection type. Wisconsin's guideline lets an official allow temporary public use for up to 180 days (extensions possible) and may require extra safety measures. The Texas page recommends against tents, membrane structures, trailers and shipping containers, and says special amusement buildings are assembly occupancies regardless of occupant load. So, for a seasonal build: no portable heaters unless the fire official approves them, plan fresh air and heat through the permitted equipment, and confirm detector temperature ratings on cold nights.

Field card: Escape Rooms and Haunted Attractions HVAC

Count people, count cfm, ask before the fog.

  • Sealed room rise is generation divided by outdoor air: ΔC = N x G / Q. (Our example: 6 people, 0.0108 cfm each, 93 cfm gives about 700 ppm.)
  • With no outdoor air, our example room gains about 60 ppm per minute.
  • Every room needs supply, a path for air to leave, and a sensor.
  • Fog and haze need fire marshal approval; tell the fire alarm contractor and do not bypass detectors.
  • Expect fog residue on filters and coils.
  • Temporary haunted buildings: check heater rules and detector temperature ratings.
