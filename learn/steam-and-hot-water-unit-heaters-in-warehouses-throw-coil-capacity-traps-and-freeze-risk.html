Steam and Hot-Water Unit Heaters in Warehouses: Throw, Coil Capacity, Traps and Freeze Risk

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hydronic unit heater is a finned coil and a fan in a box, hung from the ceiling of a warehouse, shop or loading dock. Steam or hot water goes through the coil, the fan blows room air across it. Simple, but the piping around a steam unit heater decides whether it lasts or hammers itself to pieces. This lesson covers placement, capacity corrections, trap piping and freeze risk. For gas-fired and electric heaters, see the garage and workshop heater lesson.

Check the maker's literature

Check the maker's literature. Tables, factors and piping rules differ by brand and model. This page was built from maker manuals and public design standards read in 2026. It is general education. Your heater's own manual and the engineer's drawings win.

Horizontal or vertical

Sterling's application manual describes the two shapes this way. A horizontal unit blows sideways. The maker says to mount it at the lowest practical level and aim the air at the areas of greatest heat loss, because a higher unit needs more airflow to push warm air down to the people. It also says several smaller units spread heat better than a few large ones. A vertical unit hangs near the ceiling and blows down. Sterling says that placement cuts stratification (hot air layering at the roof) without using floor space. With an optional diffuser the discharge spreads in a pattern. Without one, the air leaves fast and gives spot heating near a dock door or other high-loss spot. Tilting the diffuser toward horizontal lowers the allowed mounting height.

Mounting height and throw come from the maker's table for your size. Modine's manual bases its height table on 2 lb steam or 220°F water and 60°F entering air, and says the maximum height drops as entering air gets warmer. We do not quote distances here, because they change with every model. Read the table for the unit on the tag.

Capacity changes with steam pressure and entering air

Catalog ratings are quoted at one condition. Sterling's horizontal ratings are at 2 psig steam and 60°F entering air, where the correction factor is 1.00. Hotter steam raises the temperature difference between coil and air, so capacity rises. Colder entering air raises it too. Sterling's Table 2 gives a multiplier for each pair. A few rows from that table, for horizontal units:

  • 2 psig steam: 1.24 at 30°F air, 1.16 at 40°F, 1.00 at 60°F.
  • 10 psig steam: 1.38 at 30°F, 1.29 at 40°F, 1.13 at 60°F.

Sterling's vertical table (Table 16) has slightly different numbers, so use the table that matches the unit. Sterling also says to count about 1°F of warming for each foot of mounting height when finding the real inlet air temperature. A different maker has its own table, so do not borrow factors across brands.

Steam piping that works

  • Takeoff. Michigan State University's construction standard says the branch connects to the top of the main, so water in the main does not run into the heater. Modine words it as taking the branch off and back above the centerline of the lines.
  • Strainer and control. MSU puts strainers ahead of control valves and traps. Armstrong says to put a full pipe-size strainer ahead of each control valve. Modine adds a strainer between the heater and the trap, with a screen finer than the trap orifice.
  • Trap. Modine says to use a float and thermostatic trap, or an inverted bucket trap with an air bypass, placed below the unit outlet. Armstrong recommends one trap per unit. MSU wants the trap at least 14 inches below the coil outlet on temperature-regulated equipment, draining by gravity with no lifts. For how traps work, see Steam Traps Explained and Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits.
  • Drip leg and scale pocket. Armstrong says to add drip legs even where no natural drain point exists. MSU puts one at a coil outlet and a dirt pocket of at least 8 inches ahead of each trap. Modine calls for a scale pocket at the bottom of the heater, about 6 inches long.
  • Vacuum breaker. Modine says to fit one between the unit outlet and the trap when the steam is modulated by a motorized valve. MSU places it downstream of the control valve, near the coil inlet.
  • Freeze drain. Modine says to put a drain valve below each unit that sits where it can freeze.

Why modulating valves cause trouble

Spirax Sarco calls the problem stall. When a control valve throttles steam to match a lighter load, the pressure in the coil can fall to the pressure the trap discharges into. Then nothing pushes condensate out, the coil floods, the temperature drops, the valve opens, pressure rises, and the cycle repeats. Spirax Sarco says the result can be crackling, banging and thumping noises, sometimes severe water hammer, and in a frost coil with cold air a flooded section can freeze and be damaged. That is why makers ask for a vacuum breaker with modulated steam. Read Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water for the hammer mechanism, and Steam and Hot-Water Preheat Coils in Air Handlers for coil freeze protection in air handlers.

Hot water versions and glycol

Hot water units need air vents at high points and the ends of the mains, plus a balancing valve in the return, per Modine. Where freezing is possible, glycol is common. Sterling's manual gives heat transfer penalties (a 50 percent glycol mix carries less heat per gallon than water) and says to allow for higher flow and pump head. Confirm the percentage against the freeze point listed for that mix.

Fan controls

Modine describes an aquastat strapped to the return pipe so the fan cannot run when the coil is not hot, with a room thermostat starting and stopping the fan. On continuous-fan units the thermostat controls the steam or water valve instead. Thermostats go about 5 feet above the floor, out of drafts.

Worked example

Our own numbers, with an assumed heater, not a real model. Suppose a horizontal heater is rated 100,000 Btu/h at 2 psig steam and 60°F entering air.

  1. Factor from Sterling Table 2, 10 psig row and 40°F column: 1.29.
  2. Our own: 100,000 x 1.29 = 129,000 Btu/h at 10 psig and 40°F air.
  3. Latent heat: TLV's steam table lists 952.1 Btu/lb at 240°F, 10.272 psig. That is the nearest row to 10 psig, so we use it.
  4. Our own: condensate = 129,000 / 952.1 = 135.5 lb/h.
  5. Trap: Modine says the trap should pass at least two times the normal condensate at the minimum differential pressure. Our own: 135.5 x 2 = about 271 lb/h.

This ignores any cooling of the condensate, and the factor belongs to Sterling's table, so a different brand needs its own.

Routine checks

Modine says to service before each heating season, clean the coil at least once a year, and check traps for sticking and strainers for dirt, because a failed trap lets condensate build up and cause water hammer. Motors with no oiling label get oil about every 2,000 hours. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that systems left alone for three to five years can have 15 to 30 percent of traps failed, and lists temperature, sound, visual and electronic tests. Its low-pressure (under 30 psig) test interval is yearly. Shut off steam or water and electrical power before service.

Field card: Steam and Hot-Water Unit Heaters

Drain by gravity, trap below, no stall.

  • Horizontal: low as practical, aim at heat loss. Vertical: near ceiling, diffuser for pattern.
  • Ratings are at one condition (Sterling: 2 psig, 60°F air). Use that maker's correction table.
  • Condensate lb/h = Btu/h / latent heat. Trap at least 2x at minimum differential (Modine).
  • Strainer, trap below outlet, scale pocket, vacuum breaker on modulated steam.
  • Modulated steam can stall the coil: hammer and freeze damage.
  • Drain valve in freezing areas; glycol on hot water.
  • Yearly: clean coil, test traps, clean strainers, check motor.
