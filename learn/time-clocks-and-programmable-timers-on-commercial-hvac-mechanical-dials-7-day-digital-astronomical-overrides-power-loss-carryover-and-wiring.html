Time Clocks and Programmable Timers on Commercial HVAC: Mechanical Dials, 7-Day Digital, Astronomical, Overrides, Power-Loss Carryover and Wiring

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A time clock is the cheapest energy saver in a building, and the source of some of the most annoying service calls. The unit runs all weekend, or the tenants walk into a cold office on Monday. This lesson covers the common kinds of time switch, how they connect to a rooftop unit, what the overrides do, and what an energy code asks of them.

Three kinds of time switch

Mechanical dial timers. A motor turns a dial once every 24 hours, or a wheel once a week. Small pins or movable tabs called trippers are clamped around the rim. When a tripper reaches the pointer it flips a switch on or off. They have no memory to lose, but they stop when the power is out, so the dial reads wrong afterward. Someone has to turn it to the true time of day. They also do not change for daylight saving time on their own, and each tripper sets one on or off time for every day the dial covers.

7-day digital time switches. A small processor keeps the clock and a list of on and off events for each day of the week. Most also hold holiday dates. Programming is by buttons and a display, so a Saturday half day or a closed Friday afternoon is easy to enter.

Astronomical clocks. These are digital time switches that also calculate sunrise and sunset from the location and date. They are mostly used for exterior lighting, where the on time follows the sun instead of the clock. For fans and compressors the building schedule follows people, not daylight, so a plain 7-day schedule is the usual choice. Treat this as our own summary of how the types are used.

How the schedule reaches the equipment

There are two basic ways to use the switch contact, and they are different jobs.

  • Signal to the unit. Many rooftop units and controllers have an occupied/unoccupied input, or a remote shutdown input. The time switch contact is wired as a dry contact, a simple switch with no voltage of its own, across those terminals. The unit then decides what to do: run the fan, cycle it, set back the temperature. This is the cleaner method because the unit keeps its own safeties and delays. Follow the unit wiring diagram for which terminals and which contact state means occupied.
  • Power to a coil or load. The contact can open a control circuit or energize a contactor coil that starts a fan or pump. A coil is a small load, so a modest contact is fine. A contactor with its own heavy contacts is the part that carries the motor current.

The third, and most risky, way is to run the unit's line-voltage load through the time switch contacts themselves. The contact must be rated for that load, and motor loads are rated differently from resistive loads. Read the contact ratings on the nameplate, compare them with the motor nameplate, and use a contactor when in doubt. Our contact ratings lesson explains pilot duty, resistive and horsepower ratings. Never switch the compressor circuit with a timer alone; shutting the whole unit off with a timed disconnect also bypasses the unit's own delays. A timer that cuts power and restores it at 6:00 can start everything at once.

Overrides and timed bypass

An override button lets someone work late without reprogramming. A good one closes the contact for a set time and then hands control back to the schedule. A bad one, or a setting left on "hold", keeps the unit on forever, which is the usual cause of the unit running all weekend. One state's adopted commercial energy code (Washington, edition effective July 1, 2020) says a manual override must allow temporary operation for up to 2 hours (see sources).

Power loss, batteries and capacitors

Digital switches keep time during an outage on a battery or a capacitor. A capacitor gives short carryover and a battery gives longer carryover, but both have limits and a battery wears out. After that the clock restarts at a default time and the schedule is off by the time of the outage. The same state code requires the programming and time settings to survive at least 10 hours without power. In the field: after any outage, check the displayed time against a phone before leaving. A dead battery shows up as the clock being wrong after every outage, or the schedule vanishing.

Daylight saving time and drift

NIST states that U.S. daylight saving time begins at 2:00 a.m. on the second Sunday in March and ends at 2:00 a.m. on the first Sunday in November, which makes the 2026 dates March 8 and November 1. NIST also notes that some clocks still follow the older, pre-2007 date rule and may change on the wrong date. A mechanical dial never changes by itself, so it needs a hand adjustment twice a year. A digital switch with the automatic option turned on should follow the rule, but check the setting. Switch clocks also drift, running a little fast or slow over a year. This is a common reason a "6:00 start" is really 6:20 by midsummer; set the clock to a trusted time source when you visit.

What energy codes ask for

The model commercial energy codes (the IECC commercial provisions and Standard 90.1) require HVAC to turn off or set back when a space is unoccupied. A U.S. Department of Energy program webinar summarizing a field study of 90.1-2013 and the 2015 IECC lists setback (the example given is 55 degrees F heating and 85 degrees F cooling), automatic shutoff and programmable start/stop controls. The Washington code text we read adds details: setback by an automatic time clock or programmable control, seven different daily schedules per week, 10 hours of power-loss retention and a 2-hour override. Section numbers vary by edition; DOE's code reference table lists setback at 6.4.3.3.2 in 90.1-2013 and C403.2.4.2.1 in the 2015 IECC. Your local jurisdiction decides which edition applies.

Researchers analyzing data from 843 buildings (5,706 air handlers) found only 23 percent of air handlers fully complied with the 90.1 unoccupied shutdown requirement (a national laboratory summary). Schedules are worth checking.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. A supply fan on a small rooftop unit, assumed to draw 5 kW whenever it runs. Assumed electricity price: $0.14 per kWh. Assumed schedule: Monday to Friday, 6:00 to 18:00, with the fan off at all other times.

  1. Hours in a week at 24/7: 24 x 7 = 168 hours.
  2. Scheduled hours: 12 x 5 = 60 hours.
  3. Hours saved: 168 - 60 = 108 hours per week.
  4. Energy saved: 108 x 5 kW = 540 kWh per week.
  5. Money saved: 540 x $0.14 = $75.60 per week.
  6. Per year (52 weeks): 540 x 52 = 28,080 kWh, and $75.60 x 52 = $3,931.20.

This ignores holidays, fan cycling to hold setback temperatures, heating and cooling energy, and the fan's actual load. It counts the fan alone. Use it as a scale, not a prediction.

Common service calls

  • Unit running all weekend. Check for an override on hold, wrong clock time (AM/PM), a dead 7-day program after an outage, a Saturday/Sunday entry left on, or a contact stuck closed. Make the clock correct first, then see what the display says the contact is doing.
  • Building cold on Monday morning. The start time may be too late for the weather, the clock may be an hour off after a daylight saving change, or an outage cleared the program. A building that sat in setback needs time to recover, so move the start earlier. See our setback lesson and the electrical basics lesson for meter checks.
  • Unit never shuts off after hours. Check whether the time switch drives the unit's input or only a lighting circuit, and whether the thermostat fan is set to "on".

Always lock out and tag power before opening a control panel. See Capacitors and Contactors and Economizers Basics, since outdoor air dampers should close in unoccupied mode.

Field card: Time clocks and programmable timers

Right time, right days, override not stuck.

  • Mechanical dial: set to true time after every outage and every time change.
  • 7-day digital: check time, AM/PM, day, DST setting and holidays.
  • Astronomical: sun-based; good for exterior lighting more than fans.
  • Prefer the unit's occupied input over cutting its power.
  • Check contact ratings before switching motor loads; use a contactor if unsure.
  • Our own example: 108 hours saved a week x 5 kW x $0.14 = $75.60 a week (assumed values).
