HVAC Damage From Storms, Floods, Freezes and Power Surges: Documenting It for an Insurance Claim

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a storm, flood, hard freeze or power surge damages a furnace, boiler, water heater or air conditioner, the first job is safety and the second is a clear record. This lesson covers what to photograph, which numbers to capture, what to keep, what a technician's written report should say, and how flood insurance differs from a standard homeowners policy. It is general education, not legal advice, and it makes no promise that any insurer will cover anything.

Safety first

Do not run equipment that water reached, and do not stand in water to get to the panel or the unit. FEMA's FloodSmart recovery page says to turn off electricity and gas before entering a flooded space and to have a qualified expert inspect before power is restored. Our separate lesson explains why: Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded: What to Do Before Turning It On. The same caution applies after wind damage to an outdoor unit (see the hail and storm lesson). If you smell gas, leave and call your gas utility from outside. Photos are never worth a risk; take them only from where it is safe to stand.

Photos and video before cleanup

New Jersey's insurance department says to photograph damaged property before you dispose of it. FEMA's FloodSmart page says to take clear pictures and videos of damaged items before cleaning, fixing or throwing them out, and to include wide shots and close-ups. Its NFIP claims checklist adds that you should shoot from multiple angles and distances and capture floodwater lines. A routine we suggest for a basement mechanical room:

  1. Wide shot from the doorway or stairs, showing the whole room and the water line on walls and equipment.
  2. A tape measure held against the wall at the water line, then a photo of the reading.
  3. Each appliance from the front, both sides if reachable, then close-ups of the controls, gas valve area, wiring and the lowest parts.
  4. A short video walking the room slowly, speaking the date and what you see.
  5. Keep the original files. Back them up to a second place that is not in the damaged building.

Freezes and surges look different. For a freeze, photograph split pipes, ice, wet insulation and any stain or drip path before anything is mopped or repaired. For a surge, there may be nothing to see, so photograph any scorched board, burned smell source, tripped breaker, dead display or blown surge device, and note what stopped working and when.

Nameplate photos: model, serial, age

A claim file should say exactly which equipment was damaged. Photograph the data plate on each unit, close enough to read the model number and serial number, with a second shot a little farther back showing the plate on the cabinet. FEMA's basement flooding fact sheet recommends recording manufacturer, model, serial number and capacity for basement equipment such as furnaces, central air conditioners and appliances, and says the adjuster must provide this information during the NFIP claims process. Our lessons on reading an HVAC nameplate and finding the age from the serial number explain what the numbers mean. If the plate is washed out or unreadable, photograph it anyway and also look for the same numbers on the installation paperwork or on a label inside the door.

Keep damaged parts, and ask before throwing anything away

New Jersey's storm-claims guidance says to keep damaged property until the insurance company has inspected it or says you may dispose of it, and that most companies will want it available for inspection. FloodSmart's documentation page says to save small samples of damaged materials, and the NFIP checklist says not to throw anything away before the adjuster sees it unless it is a health hazard or local law requires disposal. For HVAC, that means if a technician removes a gas valve, control board, motor or contactor, ask them to bag and label it with the date and the unit it came from, and keep it. Do not remove parts yourself from gas or electrical equipment.

The technician's written report

A photo shows what it looks like. A written report says what a trained person found. Ask the technician for a short dated report on their letterhead or invoice that states, in plain words:

  • Date and time of the visit, address and which equipment was inspected, with model and serial numbers.
  • The cause as the technician observed it (for example, water reached the burner compartment, a pipe split at a frozen elbow, or a board shows signs of an electrical overload), and how sure they are. They should write what they saw, not guess at things they could not check.
  • What is damaged, what was tested and how, and which parts were removed and kept.
  • Whether repair is practical or replacement is recommended, and the manufacturer instruction behind that if there is one.

The insurer, not the technician and not us, decides what a policy covers. A good report simply gives them facts.

Prevent further damage

New Jersey's guidance says to make necessary temporary repairs to protect the property from further loss, and to wait for the adjuster to review the damage before permanent repairs. FloodSmart notes that mold can start forming within 24 to 48 hours of flooding and that standard NFIP policies do not cover mold damage, so drying matters and delay can cost you. Our own practical examples: pump out standing water once the power is safely off, shut off a leaking water supply, and keep a space heated to protect plumbing (see how to keep pipes from freezing). Photograph before each of these steps and keep the receipts.

Flood insurance vs a standard homeowners policy

New Jersey's insurance department states that flood insurance is not part of standard homeowners, condominium or renter policies, and that flood coverage can be bought from the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer. FloodSmart says homeowners insurance generally does not cover flood damage and that standard NFIP coverage starts 30 days after purchase, with a few exceptions such as buying alongside a mortgage. FEMA's basement fact sheet lists furnaces, water heaters and central air conditioners among covered building items in a basement under the NFIP policy, if they are installed in their functioning location, and lists generators and non-integral dehumidifiers as not covered. Those are NFIP rules; a private flood policy may differ, and a homeowners policy for wind or a burst pipe is a separate document. Read the declarations page and ask your agent which policy you actually hold.

Sudden damage vs wear and tear

Many policies draw a line between sudden, accidental damage and gradual deterioration or maintenance problems, and this is a common reason claims are questioned. We did not find a government page that defines the line for every policy, and the wording varies, so check your own policy and ask your insurer or agent. A dated record of the equipment's condition and any service history helps show what the unit was like before the event, though we cannot say how an insurer will treat it.

Keep every receipt

New Jersey's guidance says to save all receipts and bills to submit with a claim, and says temporary repairs are usually covered under a policy but receipts are still needed. FloodSmart says to keep receipts for repair costs and newly purchased items, and to keep warranty or manufacturer details for replaced appliances. Also record your claim number and the adjuster's contact details, and keep written notes of every conversation. FEMA's checklist says to submit NFIP documentation within 60 days of the flood or within any timeframe the adjuster gives you, so ask for your deadline in writing.

Field card: Documenting HVAC Damage for a Claim

Safe first, photos second, cleanup third.

  • Power and gas off. Do not run anything that got wet.
  • Photos and video of everything before cleanup or disposal.
  • Nameplate photo: model, serial, age for each unit.
  • Keep damaged parts, bagged and labeled, until the insurer says otherwise.
  • Get a dated technician report: cause, damage, repair or replace.
  • Temporary repairs to prevent more damage; permanent repairs after the adjuster.
  • Flood is a separate policy from standard homeowners. Check your own policy.
  • Keep every receipt and note every call.
