Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics

Pulling a vacuum is not just waiting for a number. The micron gauge is a window into what is still inside the system, and reading it correctly tells you whether you are fighting moisture, a leak or just a slow setup.

Why evacuate

The goal is to remove air, other non-condensable gases and water vapor so the refrigerant circuit is clean and dry before it is charged. A vacuum pump lowers the pressure, and liquid water left inside boils off at that low pressure and is pulled out as vapor. Evacuation does not replace a pressure test for leaks. It comes after the system is believed to be tight. See Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.

What a micron gauge measures

A micron gauge reads very low pressure. Atmospheric pressure is a bit over 760,000 microns, and deep vacuum work happens at a few hundred. A regular manifold gauge cannot read this range, so a dedicated electronic gauge is needed. Targets such as the level to reach and how long to hold it come from the equipment manufacturer or job specification. One technician source describes a common target as below 500 microns, and says some new systems call for lower. Another trade video page describes pulling down to 300 microns, which the presenter gives as a personal recommendation, not a maker requirement. Treat all of these as examples only, and follow the equipment label or installation sheet for the required level. We did not find a maker-published limit for how far the reading may rise during a decay test, so the numbers below are rules of thumb from trade sources.

The decay test

Once the system is down at depth, the pump is valved off from the system and the gauge is watched. The way the reading rises tells a story:

  • Climbs steadily toward atmosphere: this points to a leak.
  • Rises, then levels off: this points to moisture, since water vapor builds up until its vapor pressure stabilizes. A source gives about 20,000 to 25,000 microns at room temperature as an example of where that can level off.
  • Stalls low in the thousands of microns: moisture may have frozen, and a source says outside heat may be needed to release it.
  • Holds: the system looks clean, dry and tight for the test.

One source's rule of thumb for a small job is to get below 500 microns, isolate, and see that it does not rise above 1000 microns in 10 minutes, but acceptable numbers and times vary by application. Large commercial jobs may hold for much longer. Use the manufacturer's value.

Where the gauge goes

A gauge mounted at the pump shows the pump's vacuum, not the system's. A published example shows the pump at 250 microns while the system was still above 2,000. The vacuum is weakest at the point farthest from the pump. Technician guidance is to place the gauge as far from the pump as practical, at the system, so a decay test reads the system itself.

Setup habits that help

  • Use hoses larger than 1/4 inch. Narrow hoses and Schrader cores choke the flow.
  • Use vacuum-rated core removal tools and valves, so the system can be isolated without leaking.
  • Check the pump oil before each job and change it if it is low or milky.
  • Check that the pump can reach a deep vacuum on its own with the gauge attached to it.
  • Refrigerant can upset a micron gauge sensor, so keep it clean and read it with a clear head.

Safety and limits

Do not vent refrigerant to the atmosphere, and keep nitrogen pressure testing separate from evacuation, with a regulator on the cylinder. See Refrigerant Recovery, Recycle, Reclaim. This page is concepts only. The equipment manufacturer's instructions give the required vacuum level, hold time and any limits for that system, and local rules apply.

Field card: Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics

A micron gauge tells you what is left in the system, if it is placed well.

  • Evacuation removes air and water vapor. It does not find leaks by itself.
  • Use a micron gauge, not a manifold gauge.
  • Decay test: steady climb suggests a leak, a rise that levels off suggests moisture.
  • Gauge goes at the system, away from the pump. Use big hoses and core removal tools.
  • Target level and hold time come from the manufacturer.
