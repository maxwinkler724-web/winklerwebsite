How Fast a House Cools When the Heat Goes Out in Winter

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When the furnace quits or the power goes out on a cold night, the house does not drop to freezing in an hour. It also does not stay cozy until morning. How fast it cools depends on two things: how quickly heat leaks out, and how much heat the building has stored. This lesson shows a simple way to estimate it, then covers safe ways to slow the loss.

Safety first. Never use a gas oven, a grill, a camp stove or a camp heater inside the house, and run any generator outside, well away from the building. These can fill a home with carbon monoxide. See Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages. If anyone feels dizzy, sick or confused, get outside and call 911.

Heat loss versus stored heat

Think of a bathtub with the drain open. The drain size is the heat loss. The water in the tub is the stored heat. A wide drain empties the tub fast. A big tub takes longer.

  • Heat loss is the rate heat escapes through walls, roof, windows, floors and air leaks. It is measured in Btu per hour (Btu/h). The bigger the gap between inside and outside temperature, the faster the loss. A leaky, poorly insulated house has a big drain.
  • Stored heat sits in the framing, drywall, floors, furniture and contents. Heavy materials hold more. It is measured in Btu per degree F. A house with a lot of mass cools more slowly than a light one with the same leaks.

Heat loss shrinks as the house cools, because the gap to the outdoors shrinks. That is why the temperature falls fast at first and then slows, as the curve below shows.

Worked example (our own, a rough model)

This is our own example, not a measured house. The assumptions are ours: the house loses 30,000 Btu/h when it is 68 F inside and 20 F outside, the outdoor temperature stays at 20 F, and the house holds 10,000 Btu of stored heat for each degree F of temperature change. We ignore sunshine, people, appliances and wind changes, and we treat the whole house as one temperature.

Step 1: heat loss per degree. The gap is 68 - 20 = 48 F. Heat loss per degree = 30,000 Btu/h / 48 F = 625 Btu/h per F.

Step 2: time constant. The time constant is stored heat divided by heat loss per degree: tau = 10,000 Btu/F / 625 Btu/h per F = 16 hours. It is roughly the time for the gap to shrink to about 37 percent of what it was.

Step 3: use the cooling relation. T = Tout + (Tin - Tout) x e^(-t/tau).

  • After 8 hours: e^(-8/16) = e^(-0.5) = 0.607. T = 20 + 48 x 0.607 = 20 + 29.1 = about 49 F.
  • After 24 hours: e^(-24/16) = e^(-1.5) = 0.223. T = 20 + 48 x 0.223 = 20 + 10.7 = about 31 F.

The same relation says the house reaches 32 F in about 16 x ln(48/12) = 16 x 1.386 = about 22 hours.

Read this carefully. This is a rough model. A real house has a warm core and cold edges. A leaky or lightly built house may have a tau of only a few hours, and a heavy, tight one much longer. Wind raises air leakage and speeds the drop. Treat the answer as a feel for the shape of the curve, not a promise. The main lesson: small changes in leakiness or mass change the clock a lot. The house average is also misleading for pipes, because the coldest spots (outside walls, crawl spaces, garages) can freeze while the living room still feels fine.

Slow the loss: first hours

  1. Pick one warm room (our own general practice). A small interior room with few or no outside walls and few windows is best. Fewer square feet and fewer cold surfaces means the stored heat and body heat go further.
  2. Close doors (our own general practice) to the rooms you are not using, and close fireplace dampers on cold fireplaces you are not burning in.
  3. Curtains and shades (our own general practice). Close them at night to cut window loss. Open south-facing ones on sunny days to let in sunshine.
  4. Seal drafts. Federal public health storm-preparation guidance lists sealing gaps around doors and windows with caulk and weather-stripping [3]. A rolled towel at a door bottom is our own general practice. Do not block a vent or flue.
  5. Layer up. Federal public health cold-weather guidance says to wear several layers of loose-fitting clothing, a hat, a scarf or face cover, and mittens or gloves, and to keep skin covered in warm, dry clothing [2]. Blankets and sleeping bags are our own general practice.
  6. Check on people at risk. The federal public health agency says older adults without enough heat, and babies sleeping in cold rooms, are at risk of hypothermia, and suggests older adults keep an easy-to-read thermometer where they will see it [2][3]. Signs in adults include shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands and slurred speech, and a body temperature below 95 F needs medical attention right away [2].

Safe heat options and the ones to avoid

  • Never heat the house with a gas oven, burn charcoal indoors, or use a portable gas camp stove or flameless chemical heater indoors, and do not burn anything in an unvented stove or fireplace [1].
  • Generators go outside. The federal public health agency says to run a generator only outdoors, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, and never inside the home or garage even with doors and windows open [1].
  • A battery-powered or battery backup CO alarm near every sleeping area, and use one when running a generator [1].
  • Electric space heaters need power, so they only help with a generator, and they are a fire risk. See Electric Space Heaters.

Watch the pipes

Pipes on outside walls are the weak spot. The federal public health agency advises insulating water lines that run along exterior walls so the supply is less likely to freeze [3]. Water pipes in crawl spaces, attics, garages and cabinets on exterior walls are also exposed. Our hot-water heating lesson maps the same spots for baseboard and boiler piping: Frozen Hot-Water Heating Pipes and Baseboard.

  • The tips below are our own general practice, not taken from the sources we could open. Opening the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls lets room air reach the pipes.
  • When the indoor temperature is heading toward freezing, a thin steady drip from a faucet on an exposed line keeps water moving. Choose faucets on the pipes that run through the coldest areas.
  • If you know where the main shutoff is, find it now. If a pipe does freeze or split, shut the water off and call a plumber.
  • Do not use an open flame to thaw a pipe (our own general practice).

When to leave

This section is our own general practice. Go to a friend, family member, a warming shelter or a hotel if the indoor temperature keeps dropping toward freezing, if there is anyone vulnerable in the home, if the outage is forecast to last, or if you have no safe way to stay warm. Our model above puts a typical case around 49 F after 8 hours, so deciding early is easier than deciding at 3 a.m. Shutting off the water and draining the system is a job for a plumber or qualified tech, not a guess.

When power returns

  • Wait a few minutes and let the system start on its own. Many thermostats and compressors hold off for several minutes after power returns. See Power Came Back but HVAC Won't Start.
  • Check the thermostat is on and set to heat, and replace its batteries if it uses them.
  • Check for tripped breakers. If one trips again, stop and call a licensed tech.
  • Walk the house for leaks, wet ceilings and dripping pipes before you turn the water back on or turn faucets off.
  • Turn the faucets you left dripping back off only when the house is warm again.
  • Reheating a cold house takes hours.

For long-term improvement, see Attic Insulation and Air Sealing, which lowers heat loss per degree and so lengthens the clock.

Field card: House Cooling in an Outage

Slow the drain, use one warm room, never burn fuel indoors.

  • Heat loss per degree = Btu/h / (inside - outside). Time constant = stored heat / heat loss per degree.
  • T = Tout + (Tin - Tout) x e^(-t/tau). Our example: tau 16 h, 49 F at 8 h, 31 F at 24 h.
  • One small interior room, doors closed, curtains closed at night, layers.
  • No gas oven, grill or camp heater indoors. Generators outside, away from the house.
  • Open cabinet doors on outside walls. Drip exposed lines when near freezing.
  • Leave early if it keeps falling or someone is vulnerable.
  • On return: wait, check thermostat and breakers, then call a tech if it will not start.
