Aerial Lifts, Scissor Lifts and Rolling Scaffolds for HVAC Work at Height: OSHA Rules, Inspection and Power Line Clearance

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A unit heater hung at 18 feet, a duct run over a gym floor, an atrium diffuser: the ladder stops being the right tool somewhere around there. This lesson compares a boom lift, a scissor lift and a rolling scaffold, and walks through the OSHA rules that decide how you protect yourself on each one.

Check the rules where you work

Check your employer, your site and your jurisdiction. Federal OSHA has construction rules (29 CFR 1926) and general industry rules (29 CFR 1910), and some states run their own plans. Which set applies depends on the work. This page is general education built from OSHA pages read in 2026, not legal advice. The maker's operating manual and the equipment's load plate always come first. For ladders and roof edges, see Ladder and Roof Fall Safety for HVAC Work.

Three machines, two rulebooks

OSHA treats these machines differently, and that changes your fall protection.

  • Boom lift (aerial lift). A basket on an extensible or articulating boom, usually self-propelled. In construction, 1926.453 covers it; in general industry, 1910.67 covers vehicle-mounted platforms. Both say a worker stands on the basket floor and wears fall protection attached to the boom or basket, and OSHA's 2000 letters say workers in boom-type platforms must be tied off.
  • Scissor lift. A platform that rises straight up on crossed beams. OSHA's letters say scissor lifts are not aerial lifts under 1926.453. They meet the definition of a scaffold, so the scaffold rules in 1926.451 and the mobile scaffold rules in 1926.452(w) apply. OSHA's scissor lift page also lists 1910.27, 1910.28 and 1910.29 for general industry.
  • Baker-style rolling scaffold. A frame scaffold on casters, pushed by hand. It is a mobile scaffold under the same Subpart L rules.

OSHA's letter says a worker on a scissor lift with guardrails that meet 1926.451(g)(4) does not have to be tied off, because neither 1926.451 nor 1926.452(w) requires a harness in that case. The same letter says workers on a boom-type platform must be tied off. That is the main difference to remember: the boom swings and can throw you, so you wear a harness and lanyard; the scissor platform stays put, so the guardrail is the protection. Guardrails must stay in good condition, and the scissor lift's own maker may require a harness anyway. Follow whichever is stricter.

Fall protection details

Boom lift. The regulation text says to wear a body belt with a lanyard to the boom or basket. A note in that text says body belts have not been acceptable as part of a personal fall arrest system since January 1, 1998; OSHA's letter says arrest requires a full body harness. So wear a harness. Do not tie off to an adjacent pole or structure, do not sit or climb on the basket edge, and do not stand on planks or ladders in the basket.

Scissor lift and rolling scaffold. 1926.451(g)(1) requires protection for anyone on a scaffold more than 10 feet above a lower level. For supported scaffolds placed in service after January 1, 2000, the guardrail top edge sits between 38 and 45 inches above the platform, and a midrail or crossbracing fills the gap. A rolling scaffold needs these rails too once you are above 10 feet. Our own reading: close the entry gate or chain, stay on the floor of the platform, and never climb the rails to get that extra foot. On a rolling scaffold, ask for the maker's instructions on mounting and casters before the first climb.

Picking the right machine for the job

  • Warehouse unit heater at a fixed spot: a scissor lift on a flat slab is usually enough. The platform is big enough for a heater and a hanger kit. See Garage and Workshop Heaters and Infrared Tube Heaters for Warehouses and Garages for what you are hanging.
  • High ductwork or equipment over racks, pools or fixed seating: a boom lift reaches over and around obstacles, but you need the harness and the floor loading checked.
  • Light, short jobs in a clear room: a rolling scaffold works if the floor is smooth and you can build it to the maker's instructions.
  • For rooftop air-turnover units and large-volume mixing, see Warehouse Air Turnover Units.

Inspect before you raise it

OSHA's aerial lift eTool says to do a pre-start inspection before each shift, following the manufacturer's recommendations, and tag out any lift with a defective part until a qualified person repairs it. It lists vehicle checks (fluid levels and leaks, wheels and tires, battery, lower controls, horn and alarms, steering, brakes) and lift checks (controls, personal protective devices, hydraulic and electrical systems, insulating components, placards, fasteners and pins, cables, outriggers, guardrails, loose parts). The regulation itself requires lift controls to be tested each day before use. OSHA's scissor lift page adds that the brakes must hold the lift in position. For scaffolds, 1926.451(f)(3) calls for a competent person to check for visible defects before each shift and after any event that could affect structural integrity.

Ground, slope, wind and moving the lift

Choose firm, level ground free of holes, debris and drop-offs. OSHA's scissor lift page says to follow the manufacturer's rules on moving while raised (usually not allowed), keep the lift away from traffic, and use outdoors only in good weather; it notes outdoor-rated lifts are generally limited to winds below 28 mph. Check the nameplate for your own wind limit. For a boom lift, 1926.453 requires brakes set, outriggers (if used) on pads or a solid surface, and wheel chocks before using on an incline when they can be installed safely. The truck may not be driven with the boom raised and workers in the basket unless it is designed for it. For rolling scaffolds, 1926.452(w) requires the height-to-base-width ratio to be two to one or less while moving, unless the scaffold meets recognized stability tests. Our own advice: lock the brakes or casters before anyone steps on, and never move the scaffold with a person or a heater on it.

Power lines

OSHA's aerial lift eTool says to treat all overhead lines as energized, stay at least 10 feet away, and have the utility de-energize nearby lines. Its scissor lift page also says to stay 10 feet from power lines and notes that arcing can electrocute without direct contact. The general industry electrical rule, 1910.333(c)(3), sets the same 10 feet for unqualified people up to 50 kV, and adds 4 inches for every 10 kV over 50 kV. That distance applies to the worker and the longest conductive object the person may touch, which includes a raised mast or boom and the metal you are carrying.

The scaffold rule 1926.451(f)(6) has its own table: 3 feet for insulated lines under 300 volts, 10 feet for insulated lines from 300 volts to 50 kV and for uninsulated lines under 50 kV, and 10 feet plus 0.4 inch per kV over 50 kV above that. In construction, OSHA's 2001 letter says the controlling rule for aerial lifts is 1926.416(a)(1), which asks whether the circuit is de-energized and grounded or guarded, not a fixed distance. So treat 10 feet as the working minimum and ask the utility for more where the rule or the site says so. See Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs for the electrical side.

Platform load and training

Every lift has a rated platform capacity on its nameplate. OSHA's eTool says not to exceed it and to count the workers, tools and materials together; do not use the lift as a crane; do not carry objects larger than the platform. For a boom lift, 1926.453 says the manufacturer's boom and basket load limits must not be exceeded. Training: only trained and authorized people operate an aerial lift. OSHA's list says training covers electrical, fall, struck-by and falling object hazards, correct operation, maximum load, inspection, and the maker's requirements. Workers should show they can do it, and retraining follows an incident, a new hazard or a different lift type. The same letter says Subpart L training under 1926.454 applies to scissor lifts and scaffolds.

Worked example: load and power line check

Everything in this example is assumed. The numbers are invented for practice; on a job, use your lift's nameplate and the heater's data plate.

  • Assumed platform rating: 500 lb (our assumption; real units vary).
  • Tech in work clothes with harness and boots: 210 lb (assumed).
  • Tool bag: 40 lb (assumed).
  • Unit heater: 95 lb (assumed).
  • Hanger rods, brackets and fasteners: 15 lb (assumed).

One tech: 210 + 40 + 95 + 15 = 360 lb. 360 ÷ 500 = 0.72, or 72 percent of the rating, leaving 140 lb. The load is within the rating.

Two techs: add a second 190 lb person (assumed): 360 + 190 = 550 lb. 550 ÷ 500 = 1.10, 110 percent. That is over the rating by 50 lb, so it fails. Options: lift the heater up first on a hoist rated for it (not the lift), use a larger rated lift, or send one tech up at a time.

Power line clearance (assumed voltage). Suppose a utility line outside the loading dock is 13.2 kV (assumed). That is under 50 kV, so the 10 ft minimum applies. If the platform is raised and the longest metal object is a 6 ft pipe, measure from the pipe tip, not from your body. If the line were 69 kV (also assumed), it is 19 kV over 50 kV, so 19 × 0.4 in = 7.6 in on top of 10 ft, about 10 ft 8 in with rounding up (our own arithmetic from the 0.4 in per kV figure in 1926.451(f)(6); 1910.333 expresses it as 4 in per 10 kV, which gives the same result).

Field card: Lifts and Rolling Scaffolds at Height

Right machine, harness or rails, load counted, 10 feet from lines.

  • Boom lift: harness and lanyard to the basket; no tie-off to nearby structures.
  • Scissor lift and rolling scaffold: guardrails in place, no tie-off required with proper rails, per OSHA letter; follow the maker.
  • Scaffold fall protection starts above 10 ft; guardrail top edge 38 to 45 in.
  • Inspect before each shift, test controls daily, tag out defects.
  • Firm level ground, brakes set, outriggers on pads, chock on a slope when safe.
  • Moving a rolling scaffold: height to base width two to one or less.
  • Stay 10 ft from lines up to 50 kV; add 4 in per 10 kV above that.
  • Load = people + tools + heater + hardware, under the nameplate rating.
