Hardwood Floors and Indoor Humidity: Winter Gaps, Summer Cupping and Year-Round Targets

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A hardwood floor is a lot of wood that quietly breathes with your house. In a dry January the seams between boards open up. In a muggy August the same boards can swell, lift at the edges and cup. None of that is a flooring defect by itself. It is wood following the air, and the air is something your heating and cooling system helps control.

Wood trades moisture with the air

Wood holds water, and the amount is called its moisture content, written as a percent of the wood's dry weight. A federal forest products lab paper on moisture and wood explains that the moisture content of interior wood depends mainly on the relative humidity of the room air. It lists values for air at about 70 F: roughly 6.2 percent moisture content at 30 percent relative humidity, 9.2 percent at 50 percent, and 11.0 percent at 60 percent. A state university extension fact sheet gives a rough rule of thumb: the settled moisture content is about the relative humidity divided by five. Both agree the wood is always drifting toward whatever the air is doing, though fast swings mostly affect only the surface.

Wood also does not shrink evenly. The same extension fact sheet notes that wood shrinks more around the growth rings than across them, and very little along its length. For a floor, that means the width of each board is what changes, not the length. Wide, flat-sawn boards move the most.

Winter: gaps between boards

Cold outdoor air holds little water. When it leaks in and is heated to 70 F, the relative humidity of the house can fall far below 30 percent. The boards lose moisture, shrink in width, and the seams open. The extension fact sheet says that wood installed too wet, or wood that keeps drying after installation, can open gaps in flooring. The federal lab paper adds a subtle point: when boards swell tightly against each other in a wet season, they can be squeezed and take a small permanent set, so the gaps in the next dry season can be wider than before. Dirt that falls into a gap can make that worse.

Summer: cupping, crowning and buckling

When the air or the space under the floor is damp, the boards take on moisture and swell. Cupping means the board edges rise higher than the center. The federal lab paper describes cup as a difference in dimensional change across the width of a board, and notes that a wood floor installed over a wet concrete slab will warp and much of that warp may be permanent. Crowning is the opposite shape, with the center higher than the edges. The extension fact sheet warns that dry wood taking on moisture can swell and buckle, which is when boards lift off the subfloor. If you see cupping, the first job is to find the moisture source, not to sand or refinish.

Why some boards move more

Look at where gaps appear first. Boards close to a floor register, a radiator, baseboard heat or a sunny window get warmer air and so drier local air, and they tend to dry and shrink sooner than boards in the middle of a room. A floor register blowing hot, dry air across the boards is a common spot for a long thin gap. Boards near a bathroom, a kitchen sink, a doorway that gets rained on, or above a damp part of the basement or crawl space tend to swell first. Do not aim a space heater at one patch of floor.

Moderate humidity year round

A federal environmental agency guide to mold and moisture advises keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent relative humidity. A different state extension energy series says people are most comfortable between 35 and 55 percent, that dust mites do well above 50 percent, and that mold grows when the air is above 60 percent. Put those together and a simple target is the middle of the range, around 35 to 50 percent, with the upper part used in cold weather only if windows do not fog. The federal lab paper also suggests installing interior wood near the moisture content it will have in service, with 8 percent as the average for most of the country. That matches the 50 percent figure above, which gives about 9 percent.

Use a hygrometer

Guessing does not work, because 25 percent and 45 percent feel about the same. The federal environmental guide says relative humidity can be read with a humidity meter, which is a small instrument sold at many hardware stores. Put one in the room with the floor you care about, away from a register, an outside wall and direct sun, and read it often during the first season.

Winter: add moisture carefully

If the meter shows a long stretch below 30 percent, a humidifier can raise it. A built-in unit tied to the air handler is covered in the humidistat lesson, and portable types in the room humidifier lesson. Raise it slowly and watch the windows. The federal environmental guide says condensation on windows, walls or pipes can signal high humidity, so if windows fog, lower the setting. See Condensation on Windows in Winter.

Summer: air conditioning and dehumidifiers

The federal environmental guide says to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. A working cooling system removes moisture as it cools. If a house stays clammy at 60 percent or higher, especially a basement, a dehumidifier sized for the space helps; see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage. Keep the condensate drain and drip pan clear, as that guide also says.

Crawl spaces and basements under the floor

A floor over a damp crawl space or basement can swell from below even when the room air is fine. The state extension energy series lists crawl spaces as moisture sources: moist crawl space air can move into the house by vapor diffusion and through air leaks. The federal environmental guide also says the ground should slope away from the foundation so water does not collect there. Fixing the source, with drainage, ground cover and a sealed or properly vented crawl space, is a job for the right trade. Our crawl space lesson compares vented and sealed designs.

New flooring: let it acclimate

New boards should be at about the moisture content they will have in service. The federal lab paper says to install wood as close as possible to that value. The common practice is to bring the boards into the finished, heated and cooled house, with the HVAC system running, and let them settle for the time the flooring maker's instructions give. Follow those instructions for the wait, the moisture readings they require, and the subfloor conditions. We give no number of days here, because it varies with product and climate.

Worked example (our own): a winter shrinking board

Our own example with assumed inputs. We assume a 5 inch wide oak board, and that its width changes by about 0.2 percent for each 1 percent change in moisture content. That coefficient is our own assumption for a board cut between flat-sawn and quarter-sawn. The federal lab paper lists commercial red oak at 0.37 percent per percent across the growth rings (flat-sawn) and 0.16 percent per percent in the other direction, so 0.2 sits inside that range. We also assume the board falls from 9 percent moisture content in summer to 6 percent in winter, which fits the 50 and 30 percent humidity values above.

  1. Moisture change: 9 minus 6 = 3 percentage points.
  2. Fractional change in width: 3 x 0.2 percent = 0.6 percent, or 0.006.
  3. Change per board: 5 inches x 0.006 = 0.030 inch, about 1/32 inch.
  4. Across 10 boards: 10 x 0.030 = 0.30 inch, a bit under 5/16 inch, which shows up as gaps spread through the floor.

Check with the handbook range: at 0.16 percent per percent, 5 x 0.0016 x 3 = 0.024 inch per board and 0.24 inch across 10. At 0.37 percent, 5 x 0.0037 x 3 = 0.0555 inch and 0.555 inch across 10. So our 0.030 inch is plausible but could be nearly double for wide flat-sawn boards. The lab paper notes this simple equation is only approximate. The point for a homeowner is that a drop of three points, an ordinary seasonal swing, adds up to a visible gap over a whole room.

Field card: Hardwood Floors and Indoor Humidity: Winter Gaps, Summer Cupping and Year-Round Targets

Wood follows the air. Keep the air steady.

  • Dry winter air: boards shrink in width and gaps open.
  • Humid summer air or a damp crawl space or basement: boards swell and may cup.
  • Registers, radiators and sunny windows dry nearby boards faster.
  • Aim for moderate humidity, about 30 to 50 percent, and read it on a hygrometer.
  • Winter: humidify slowly and watch windows. Summer: run the AC or a dehumidifier.
  • Find and fix floor-level moisture before refinishing.
  • Acclimate new flooring and follow the flooring maker's instructions.
  • Our example: 5 inch board, 9 to 6 percent moisture content, about 0.030 inch per board and 0.30 inch over 10 boards (assumed 0.2 percent per percent).
