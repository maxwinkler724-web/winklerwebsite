Bar, Tavern and Nightclub HVAC: Crowd Heat, Fresh Air and Beverage Gas

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A bar at midnight is one of the hardest rooms to keep comfortable. The crowd is dense and moving, the door keeps opening, the effects machines are running, and the beer and soda gas is sitting in a cellar below. This lesson walks through the loads and the hazards in the order a service tech meets them.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Ventilation requirements, fire alarm rules and gas-handling rules differ by jurisdiction and change with each code cycle. This page is general education built from public pages we read in 2026. Confirm what applies with the local building and fire officials and follow the equipment instructions.

Dense crowds are mostly a heat and moisture problem

People give off heat in two forms. Sensible heat warms the air. Latent heat is moisture that the cooling coil must condense out. The harder someone works, the more of both. A published occupant heat-gain table (1997 edition, reproduced in a university-hosted professional-development PDF) lists moderate dancing at about 1,360 Btu/h of total heat per person, for a room at 78°F dry bulb. The same table lists seated at rest at about 400 Btu/h, so a person on a dance floor adds roughly three times the heat of a person in a seat. That table also notes that in a warmer room the sensible part gets smaller and the latent part gets larger while the total stays the same. A packed bar is therefore a latent-load room as well, which is why a coil that holds temperature may still leave the air clammy. We could not confirm a sensible/latent split for each activity, so we give the total only. Our companion lesson on the human body and people loads covers how the body sheds heat.

Outdoor air and CO2 build-up

People breathe out carbon dioxide, so indoor CO2 sits above the outdoor level. A national laboratory report on demand-controlled ventilation says the indoor-to-outdoor difference falls as ventilation per person rises, and it describes a control setpoint of 600 ppm above outdoor.

For the rate itself, Washington State's adopted mechanical code table (Table 403.3.1.1) lists bars and cocktail lounges at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot, and disco and dance floors at 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot. It also lists a default occupant density of 100 people per 1,000 square feet for both. Your jurisdiction may have adopted a different edition. The dance-floor rate is almost three times the bar rate per person.

A CO2 sensor in the return or the room can ramp the outdoor air damper up when the crowd arrives. See Demand-Controlled Ventilation for placement and calibration. Treat that sensor as a comfort and ventilation control. It is not a leak alarm for the cellar, which is a different job (see below).

Late-night schedules and setback

This section is our own practice note, not a cited number. A bar may be empty at noon and full at 1 a.m., so a fixed daytime schedule fails. Start pre-cooling before staff and deliveries arrive, hold ventilation on as soon as the doors open to the public, and let setback begin only after the last patron and the cleaning crew leave. If the space cannot pull down before open, start earlier. Keep ventilation running until the crowd is gone.

Beverage gas in cellars and coolers

Beer and soda systems commonly use CO2 from cylinders or a bulk tank. The CDC pocket guide lists a vapor density of 1.53 for CO2, meaning heavier than air. An OSHA hazard bulletin on filling low-pressure CO2 supply systems says the gas is colorless and odorless, treats it as a material with poor warning properties, and says it can persist in pits and below-grade areas. The bulletin describes a delivery driver who died at a restaurant fill station in a below-grade stairwell. It recommends putting new fill receptacles at ground level in the open, ventilating below-grade areas by exhausting from the lowest level and bringing make-up air in higher, and monitoring for CO2. It also says not to rely on oxygen readings alone, since CO2 can be toxic even when oxygen is normal. The New Jersey Division of Fire Safety issued an alert saying leaked CO2 can pool at floor level in poorly ventilated rooms, that the danger is not necessarily limited to the cylinder location, and that CO2 detectors are not required by the state Uniform Fire Code but fire inspectors should suggest them where CO2 systems exist.

Exposure numbers: the CDC pocket guide lists an exposure limit of 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average, 30,000 ppm as a short-term limit and 40,000 ppm as the level immediately dangerous to life and health. OSHA's chemical database repeats the 5,000 ppm average. The OSHA bulletin says 10% (100,000 ppm) or more can cause unconsciousness or death.

What this means for the HVAC tech: a cellar or cooler that holds CO2 cylinders needs an exhaust that pulls from near the floor and a make-up path from above, and any detector should sit low. We do not size the fan here; use the local code and the system maker. If you smell nothing, that does not mean the room is safe. Do not enter a room that you suspect holds leaked CO2. Ventilate from outside and call the right people. See our lessons on draft beer systems for the cooler and line side.

Beer coolers and ice machines are heaters too

A walk-in cooler and an ice machine remove heat from product and water, but the refrigeration cycle moves that heat to the condenser. A federal appliance-standards engineering document on commercial ice makers says the air-cooled condenser transfers heat from the refrigerant to the ambient air, and that a fan moves air over the coil. In a bar that ambient air is often the back room, a cellar or the service corridor. If that air is already warm and the louvers or coil are dirty, the machines run longer, the head pressure rises and the room gets hotter. The document does not give a number for how much heat goes to the room, and we do not either. Keep condenser airflow clear and tell the owner not to stack cases against the unit.

Fog, haze and detectors

Many city and university fire offices treat theatrical fog and haze as a fire alarm issue. One state university policy says smoke and haze is capable of tripping smoke detectors and activating the building's fire alarm, and that approval considers how close the effect is to detection devices. A city fire department summary allows machines only if there is no impact on the fire alarm and protection systems and the output does not fill a room so that exits are blocked. From the HVAC side, an air handler can carry haze to a duct smoke detector, so the effects schedule and the fan schedule should be talked through with the fire alarm contractor. Do not bag or disable a detector yourself. Our lesson on escape rooms and haunted attractions goes deeper on fog and filters. Expect filters to load faster in a room with haze or smoke, so check them on a shorter interval (our own advice).

Doors, kitchens and drafts

Doors are a big part of why a bar has cold or hot spots. A national laboratory report on entrance vestibules says the number of people using a door per hour is a key input for estimating airflow through it, and that the pressure difference across the door comes from stack effect and wind. In its tables, vestibules cut peak infiltration by roughly 28% to 41% for the buildings it modeled. It modeled other building types, not bars. Where a vestibule does not fit, see air curtains over commercial doors.

Our own reasoning on kitchens: a kitchen hood takes air out of the building, and that air has to come back in somewhere. If the makeup air is short, the door and the cellar stairs become the intake, and patrons feel it as a draft. Check which way the front door pushes before blaming the thermostat.

Worked example: our own numbers

This is our own example with stated assumptions. It is not from a real venue.

  • Assumption: a 2,400 square foot room (40 ft by 60 ft) with a peak of 300 people.
  • Assumption: for a short peak, we treat every person as moderate dancing, at the cited 1,360 Btu/h each. This is conservative, since at any moment many people stand or sit.
  • Assumption: we apply the cited dance-floor outdoor air rates of 20 cfm per person and 0.06 cfm per square foot to the whole room.

People heat. 300 people x 1,360 Btu/h = 408,000 Btu/h. Dividing by 12,000 Btu/h per ton gives 408,000 / 12,000 = 34 tons of cooling just to offset the people at that peak, before lights, effects, equipment, the envelope and outdoor air.

Outdoor air. People part: 300 x 20 cfm = 6,000 cfm. Area part: 2,400 ft² x 0.06 cfm/ft² = 144 cfm. Total = 6,000 + 144 = 6,144 cfm, or about 20.5 cfm per person (6,144 / 300).

Comparison. At the bar rates of 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm/ft²: 300 x 7.5 = 2,250 cfm, plus 2,400 x 0.18 = 432 cfm, so 2,682 cfm. The dance-floor rate needs more than twice as much outdoor air.

Note: 125 people per 1,000 ft² is above the table's default of 100. A real design needs a full load calculation.

Field card: Bar, Tavern and Nightclub HVAC

Crowd heat, low-lying CO2, and doors.

  • Moderate dancing is about 1,360 Btu/h total per person in a 78°F room, about three times seated at rest.
  • Dance floors use about 20 cfm per person in the cited table; bars 7.5 cfm per person.
  • Our own example: 300 people x 1,360 = 408,000 Btu/h (34 tons); outdoor air 6,144 cfm.
  • CO2 is about 1.5 times as heavy as air: exhaust low, make-up high, detector low.
  • CO2 exposure limit: 5,000 ppm average; 40,000 ppm is immediately dangerous.
  • Fog and haze can trip detectors; coordinate with the fire alarm contractor.
  • Vestibules and balanced makeup air cut door drafts.
