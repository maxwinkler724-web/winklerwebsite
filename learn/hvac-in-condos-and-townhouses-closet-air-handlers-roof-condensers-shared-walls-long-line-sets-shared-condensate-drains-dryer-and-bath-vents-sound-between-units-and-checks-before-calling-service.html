HVAC in Condos and Townhouses: Layouts, Shared Walls and Checks Before You Call

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A house has its own roof, its own walls and its own equipment. A condo or townhouse shares some of that with neighbors, and the HVAC shows it: equipment is squeezed into closets, condensers end up on roofs or balconies, and drains, vents and sound travel in ways a detached house never has to deal with. This lesson walks through the common layouts, what the shared walls do to heat flow, and what you can check before you call anyone.

Who owns and repairs what

Read your documents. Who owns a given part, and who must repair it, depends on the governing documents and any lease or service agreement for your building. A closet air handler may be yours while the roof it sits under is not, and a shared drain stack may belong to the association. This page is general education, gives no legal advice, and cannot tell you which side of that line your equipment falls on. Ask the manager or board, in writing, before you spend money.

Common layouts

  • Closet air handler with a remote condenser. A small indoor unit sits in a hall or utility closet, and its outdoor condenser is on a roof, a balcony or a pad at grade. Refrigerant lines run between them, sometimes through a shared chase.
  • Through-wall units. A single cabinet sits in the exterior wall and handles one room. See PTAC Units: How They Work, Cleaning and Common Problems.
  • Stacked units sharing chases. Units on several floors line up vertically, so their pipes, drains and exhaust ducts run through the same vertical shaft.
  • Older buildings with a central boiler. Hot water or steam comes from a shared plant to radiators or fan coils in each home, and cooling may be a window or through-wall unit.
  • Water-loop heat pumps. Each home has a small heat pump tied to a building loop that a shared plant keeps in a set temperature range. See Water-Source Heat Pumps in Apartments and Offices.

Heat through shared walls and floors

Heat flows from warm to cool, and how fast depends on the area, the wall's U-factor (how easily heat passes through it) and the temperature difference. Our own shorthand for it is heat flow in Btu per hour equals U times area times temperature difference. See Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings for how U-factors are built up.

A wall between two homes kept at similar temperatures has almost no temperature difference across it, so it loses very little heat. A wall to the outdoors has a large one. That is why a middle townhouse is usually easier to heat than an end unit. The catch is that the party wall only helps while the neighbor is heated: a home left cold for a winter vacation, or a vacant unit, turns that wall into an exterior-like surface. Floors and ceilings work the same way, so a top-floor unit under a roof and a unit over an unheated space see the largest swings.

Air moves too, not only heat. A national laboratory presentation on multifamily compartmentalization reported that, in its modeling, flow from other units was significant in leaky buildings and negligible in tight ones, with worst-case flows typically 1 to 2 cubic feet per minute at its reference tightness level. A companion laboratory paper found that transfer between units was mostly vertical and that colder climates raised transport by more than a factor of two. Those are modeled results, not a measurement of your building. The practical lesson is that stacked units with shared chases can trade air and odors, and that sealing around pipe and duct openings helps.

Long refrigerant runs to a roof condenser

When a condenser sits on a roof or a distant pad, the line set can be long and may have a large height change. Longer lines mean more pressure drop, more oil-return risk and sometimes an adjusted charge. Our general advice, not a number: the installation instructions for your specific equipment give a maximum line length and height difference, and a technician should check your run against them. See Refrigerant Line Set Sizing, Suction Velocity, Oil Return and Long Runs. Also look for sun-baked, uninsulated or damaged line insulation where the lines cross a roof, and for lines that sweat and drip inside the chase.

Condensate lines tied into shared drains

A cooling coil removes water, and that water has to go somewhere. In a condo it often ties into a shared drain stack or a laundry standpipe instead of a yard. A university extension article on summer home checks says the condensate line must drain freely and that, where it discharges outdoors, water should drip from the end after the system has run and land at least one foot from the foundation. In a shared-drain building you cannot watch the end of the pipe, so look for a small visible tailpiece or cleanout, a clear pan, and signs of staining under the air handler. A clogged drain is a common reason a closet unit leaks into the unit below, and that unit's owner may be your first complaint. A float switch that shuts the unit off is a feature, not a fault. Do not modify the drain tie-in yourself: it is usually shared plumbing, and your documents may say who maintains it.

Dryer and bath vents in tight buildings

Tight buildings have little accidental leakage to supply the air that fans exhaust. In our own reasoning, a bath fan or dryer in a closed-up unit can struggle to move air when doors are shut and the unit has no other makeup air, so weak airflow is not always a blocked duct. Dryer lint is a separate matter. A federal fire administration report on residential dryer fires found failure to clean was the leading factor, at 34 percent of those fires, and that dust, fiber and lint were the first material ignited in 27.6 percent. The federal consumer product safety agency advises cleaning the lint screen every load, periodically cleaning the vent and duct, checking that air exits outdoors while the dryer runs, and replacing plastic or foil accordion duct with rigid or semi-rigid metal duct. Long vent runs are common in condos. See Clothes Dryer Vents: Lint, Fire Risk, Length Limits and Booster Fans.

Sound between units

Closet air handlers sit against bedrooms, and roof or balcony condensers sit above or beside someone else. Common sources are fan whoosh, a rattling duct, a hum that travels through a rigid mount, and shared pipes that tick as they heat. General practice is vibration isolation on mounts, flexible connections to ducts and pipes, and sound-absorbing closet liners where the building allows it. Do not drill into or alter a shared wall or fire-rated enclosure without checking first. See Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Noise for outdoor placement.

Worked example: middle unit versus end unit

Our own example, with our own assumed inputs. These are not measured values. Two townhouses in a row, each 24 ft wide by 40 ft deep, two stories at 8 ft each (16 ft tall). The middle unit has two exterior walls (front and back); the end unit has those two plus one side wall. Assumptions: whole-wall U-factor 0.060 Btu/h·ft²·°F, window U-factor 0.30, indoor 70°F, outdoor 30°F (a 40°F difference), neighbors also at 70°F so party walls lose nothing, and roof, floor and air leakage left out to keep the arithmetic short. Windows: 100 ft² on the front and back of each home, plus 40 ft² on the end unit's side wall.

Step 1, exposed areas. Front plus back wall gross area: 2 × (24 × 16) = 768 ft². End unit adds the side: 40 × 16 = 640 ft², so 1,408 ft² gross.

Step 2, net wall and window areas. Middle: windows 100 ft², wall 768 − 100 = 668 ft². End: windows 100 + 40 = 140 ft², wall 1,408 − 140 = 1,268 ft².

Step 3, heat loss at a 40°F difference. Middle: walls 668 × 0.060 × 40 = 1,603 Btu/h; windows 100 × 0.30 × 40 = 1,200 Btu/h; total 2,803 Btu/h. End: walls 1,268 × 0.060 × 40 = 3,043 Btu/h; windows 140 × 0.30 × 40 = 1,680 Btu/h; total 4,723 Btu/h.

Step 4, compare. The difference is 4,723 − 2,803 = 1,920 Btu/h, so the middle unit loses 1,920 ÷ 4,723 = about 41 percent less than the end unit in this example. Real homes differ in windows, air leakage, roof and floor exposure, so treat this as an illustration of the reason, not a prediction. If a neighbor drops to 60°F, the party wall starts to lose heat too, and the gap narrows.

Checks before you call for service

  1. Check the thermostat mode, setpoint and batteries.
  2. Check and replace the air filter. A closet filter behind a grille is easy to forget.
  3. Look at the condensate tailpiece, pan or cleanout for water, and check whether a float switch has shut the unit off.
  4. Listen and feel for airflow at registers. Weak air with the door closed may be a return problem, not a failed unit.
  5. If the condenser is accessible, look for blocked airflow or debris, and do not climb onto roofs or reach over balcony rails to do it.
  6. Boiler or loop homes: note whether other units are also cold, since a shared plant problem is for the building, not you.
  7. Write down what you saw and when, and report it in writing to whoever your documents say to call. If you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave and call the emergency number.
