Sizing a 24-Volt Control Transformer: VA Loads, Coil Inrush and Sag

Last updated: 2026-11-02

A 24 volt transformer that is "big enough" on paper can still leave a contactor humming, a zone valve slow to open or a fuse blowing on the first cold morning of the year. The reason is almost always that the load was added up wrongly, or only added up once. This lesson shows how to total the load, why the first instant is harder than the steady state, and when one transformer should become two.

Check the equipment drawing

Check the equipment drawing. Terminal names, VA labels, fuse ratings and wiring rules differ by equipment. This page is general education. Follow the wiring diagram on the unit and the maker's instructions before you change anything, and treat the transformer primary as live line voltage. The basics of what a transformer and a relay do are in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control, and the primary tap and Class 2 marking checks are in HVAC Control Transformers: 208 vs 240 Volt Taps, VA Sizing and Class 2 Limits.

VA is the unit that matters

A University of Utah transformer note defines the VA rating as rated voltage times rated current, says not to run steady current above it, and says short startup currents above the rating are acceptable if the transformer does not overheat. So the steady total must fit under the rating, and the short peak must not drag the voltage down too far. On a 24 volt secondary, amps equal VA divided by 24. A 40 VA transformer is rated for 40 / 24 = 1.67 amps (our own arithmetic).

Step 1: total the sealed (holding) VA

Sealed VA is what each device draws once it is pulled in and sitting there. One distributor control-transformer guide defines it as the steady VA of everything energized at the same time. List every device that can be on together, not just the ones on at the bench.

That means contactor and relay coils (including isolation relays), the gas valve, zone valves, damper actuators, any humidifier solenoid, and thermostats and boards that draw from the 24 volt side.

Use the label value in VA. If the label shows amps, multiply by 24. An HVAC maker's support article on sizing a transformer for unit controllers says to add every device's draw in VA, converting other units first, and to size 30 to 50 percent above the total for growth. Running a transformer flat out shortens its life through heat. We use a margin of our own in the example below: keep the sealed total at 80 percent of the rating or less. The 80 percent figure is our choice, not a code number.

Contact ratings such as pilot duty or resistive amps are different numbers from the coil draw. See Reading Contact Ratings on HVAC Relays so you do not mix the two.

Step 2: why inrush is several times the holding VA

This is our plain-language explanation. An AC coil pulls an iron armature in. While the armature is open there is a big air gap in the magnetic path, so the coil presents little opposition to the AC and draws a large current. When the armature seats, the gap closes, the coil opposes the AC much more, and the current falls to the holding value.

One maker's control transformer data sheet gives the size of the effect: electromagnetic devices draw roughly 3 to 10 times their normal current for about 30 to 50 milliseconds. Its method adds the inrush VA of everything that can start together, plus lights and timers on at that moment. A distributor chart for industrial contactors lists, for one small size, 80 VA of inrush against 7.5 VA sealed. Those are industrial starters, not HVAC coils, so read your own device's label, and treat the 3 to 10 range as a warning, not a number to plug in.

Step 3: sag, chatter and the 85 percent line

A transformer is not a perfect voltage source. A Michigan Technological University lecture defines voltage regulation as the change in secondary voltage from no load to full load, and explains that the windings have impedance, so current through them causes a drop that changes with the current. The bigger the inrush, the deeper the dip.

The maker data sheet and the distributor guide both size control transformers so that the secondary stays at or above 85, 90 or 95 percent of its rated voltage during inrush, with 85 percent the lowest column. Take 85 percent of 24 volts: 20.4 volts (our own arithmetic). Below that, the pull of a coil falls faster than the voltage. As a rule of our own, force goes roughly as the square of voltage, so 85 percent voltage is about 72 percent of the pull.

That is where chatter comes from. The coil starts to close, the inrush collapses the voltage, the armature falls back, the voltage recovers, and the cycle repeats. You hear a buzz or rattle and the contacts arc and pit. A transformer that carries the sealed total but not the pull-in peak causes exactly this symptom. If the voltage is fine at the transformer but low at the coil, it is a wire problem; see Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits, and use Half-Splitting a Control Circuit to find where it disappears.

Worked example (our own example): a 4-zone hydronic system on a 40 VA transformer

All VA values below are assumptions of our own for teaching. They are not data for any product. Read your devices' labels for real numbers. The load is four zone valves at an assumed 7 VA each holding, four thermostats at an assumed 1 VA each, and one relay coil at an assumed 4 VA. The transformer is rated 40 VA.

  1. Zone valves: 4 x 7 = 28 VA.
  2. Thermostats: 4 x 1 = 4 VA.
  3. Relay coil: 4 VA.
  4. Sealed total: 28 + 4 + 4 = 36 VA.
  5. Share of the rating: 36 / 40 = 90 percent. Amps at 24 V: 36 / 24 = 1.5 A, against the 40 / 24 = 1.67 A rating.
  6. Margin check: 36 / 0.80 = 45 VA needed, so 40 VA fails our 80 percent rule before any inrush is considered. The next common size, 50 VA, passes.

Startup inrush. Assume a cold morning when all four zones call at once. For this example we treat every coil-type load as drawing 3 times its holding VA at the start, the low end of the 3 to 10 range in the maker sheet. That is an assumption, since real valve operators vary. Valves: 4 x 21 = 84 VA. Relay: 3 x 4 = 12 VA. Thermostats stay at 4 VA. Peak total: 84 + 12 + 4 = 100 VA, which is 100 / 40 = 2.5 times the rating.

To see the sag we need a model. Assume the transformer reads 28 V with no load and 24 V at its rated 1.67 A, so its internal impedance is (28 - 24) / 1.667 = 2.4 ohms, and treat the loads as fixed resistances. At peak the load resistance is 24 x 24 / 100 = 5.76 ohms. The secondary voltage is 28 x 5.76 / (5.76 + 2.4) = 19.8 V, which is 82 percent of 24 V and below the 85 percent line (20.4 V). By the square rule, pull is about 0.82 x 0.82 = 68 percent of normal. The relay coil chatters and the valves open slowly, and a board may reset. So the 40 VA unit fails at startup even though 36 VA looks close to 40 VA.

The fix. Redo the same arithmetic with a 50 VA unit on the same assumed 28 V open-circuit and 24 V rated behavior: rated current 50 / 24 = 2.08 A, impedance 4 / 2.083 = 1.92 ohms, peak voltage 28 x 5.76 / (5.76 + 1.92) = 21.0 V, or 87.5 percent. That clears 85 percent, with thin room. A 75 VA unit gives 22.9 V (95.5 percent). Before you pick 75 VA, check that the unit is marked Class 2 and that the equipment allows it, and see the section on splitting loads below.

Secondary fuse sizing

The secondary fuse or breaker protects the thin control wire and the transformer from a short. Our approach is simple: use the fuse value printed on the control board or equipment label, and never go up a size to stop a nuisance blow. The maker's support article above says a transformer up to 50 VA is inherently limited, so an overload makes it fail safe without a fuse, while one from 60 to 100 VA must have a resettable breaker or secondary fuse to meet Class 2 requirements. OSHA's construction electrical rule (29 CFR 1926.408) says an overcurrent-protected Class 2 source is limited to 30 volts AC and that the supply must be durably marked with its class and rating.

The National Electrical Code (NEC) covers Class 2 circuits and transformer protection. Its rules depend on the edition adopted where you work, so we do not quote numbers from it. One maker of industrial control transformers says the control-circuit fuse is sized per the NEC and lists maximum secondary fuse ratings by VA and voltage; its 63 VA, 24 V entry is 4 A. That is about 1.5 times the unit's 2.6 A rated current (our arithmetic), so that maker's fuse sits modestly above rated amps, not several times above. A blown fuse after an added accessory is a VA problem first; see Low-Voltage Short, Blown Fuse and Transformer Troubleshooting.

Phasing two transformers, or keeping them separate

Every transformer has a polarity. A Southern Illinois University lecture says transformers with matching polarity give no phase shift, while reversed secondary leads give a 180 degree shift. If you tie the secondaries of two transformers together and they are out of phase, the two sources fight through almost no resistance, and you get a blown fuse, a hot transformer or a burned board.

Our default is to keep them separate. Each transformer feeds its own set of loads, with its own R and its own C, and the two circuits pass signals through dry contacts such as an isolation relay. Fan Center Relay Boards and Isolation Relays shows that layout.

If a diagram does call for joining two secondaries, here is our own check, done with the loads disconnected and both transformers energized from the same supply. Measure AC volts between one secondary lead of each. About 0 V means in phase, so those two leads may be joined. About 48 V (double) means out of phase, so swap one transformer's leads and measure again. A reading between those two, around 42 V (24 x 2 x sin 60 degrees, our arithmetic), points to primaries fed from different legs of a three-phase supply, and the secondaries should not be joined at all.

When to add a second transformer

  • The total will not fit in one Class 2 unit. The support article says 75 VA is the largest commonly offered 24 volt size, with Class II units under 100 VA. Our rule: if the sealed total divided by 0.8 is above 75 VA, split the load.
  • Zone systems that grow. Example (our own assumption set): eight zones at 7 VA is 56 VA, plus 8 VA of thermostats and a 4 VA relay, 68 VA sealed. 68 / 0.8 = 85 VA, above 75. Put zones 1 to 4 and zones 5 to 8 on two 50 VA units, each with its own thermostats and a relay, so each loop stays on one transformer: 28 + 4 + 4 = 36 VA each, which is 72 percent of rating. Peak per unit falls to the 100 VA case computed above, which the 50 VA example cleared at 87.5 percent.
  • Different systems. A boiler and an air handler, or a motorized damper bank, are usually best on their own transformers. See Zone Valve End Switches and Zone Control Panels for how the end switch hands the call to the boiler without sharing power.

Whatever you do, keep each coil and the thermostat switch that drives it on the same transformer. Mixing the two sides of a loop between transformers is how the out-of-phase problem appears.

Field card: Sizing a 24 V Control Transformer

Add the holding VA, then test the peak.

  • VA = volts x amps. Amps = VA / 24 on a 24 V secondary.
  • Total sealed VA of everything that can be on together; our margin is 80 percent of rating.
  • Inrush is roughly 3 to 10 times normal for tens of milliseconds (one maker data sheet). Read your device label.
  • Keep the secondary at 85 percent of 24 V or better during inrush: 20.4 V.
  • Our example: 36 VA sealed on 40 VA fails; 100 VA peak sags to 19.8 V. 50 VA gives 21.0 V.
  • Fuse to the label. Never upsize to stop a blow.
  • Keep secondaries separate. Join only if the diagram says so, after a phase check.
  • Over 75 VA needed? Split the loads onto a second transformer.
