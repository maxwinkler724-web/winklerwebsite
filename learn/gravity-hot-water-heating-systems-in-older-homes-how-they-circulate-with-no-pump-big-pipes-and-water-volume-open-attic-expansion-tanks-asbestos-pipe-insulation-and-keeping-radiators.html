Gravity Hot-Water Heating in Older Homes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some older houses have a boiler, cast-iron radiators and no pump at all. These gravity hot-water systems move heat with nothing but the weight difference between hot and cooler water. They are slow, full of water, and often tied to an open tank in the attic. This lesson explains how they work, how to spot one, and what changes when you add a pump.

How a gravity system moves water

A federal housing rehabilitation guide on heating and plumbing says old hot-water systems relied on gravity flow: the hot water rises to the radiators and pushes the cooler water back to the boiler, while today's systems use a pump and forced flow. Hot water is lighter than cooler water, so the column of water in the supply pipe weighs a little less than the column in the return. That small difference is the only push in the loop. Our own reasoning from that: the push grows with the height between the boiler and the radiators and with the temperature difference, which is why gravity systems put the boiler low, in the cellar, and run hot.

Why the pipes are so big

The same guide notes that in a pumped system hot water moves faster, with better heat transfer, through smaller pipes. A gravity system has no pump to overcome friction, so the designers used large mains and gentle slopes to keep friction low. The result is a lot of water in the building. A state home energy guide says hot-water radiator systems are typically found in older homes, and that cast-iron radiators absorb heat from the water and release it into the room over time. The federal guide adds that cast iron holds more water and gives off heat for a longer time. Put the big pipes, the big boiler and the heavy radiators together and the whole system has a lot of thermal mass, which leads to the slow warm-up described below.

The open expansion tank in the attic

Water takes up more room as it heats. The federal guide says an expansion tank with an overflow pipe discharges any extra water in the system, and that all hydronic systems need an expansion tank to make room for the increase in volume. In many gravity systems that tank is open to the air and sits at the highest point, often in the attic, with a pipe running down to it from the supply main and an overflow pipe leading to a drain or outdoors. Two risks follow from our own reasoning, not from a cited source:

  • Freezing. An uninsulated tank and pipe in a cold attic can freeze if the house is unheated for a stretch. Ice can split the tank or block the pipe that gives the water somewhere to go.
  • Overflow and leaks. Because the tank is open, water can slosh or boil out, and an old tank can rust through. A stain on the attic floor or ceiling below is a warning. Water lost from the tank also has to be replaced, which brings in fresh oxygen and can speed rust inside the system.

Signs you have one

  • A tank in the attic or at the top of a closet, with a small pipe going down and an overflow pipe.
  • Radiators, a boiler, and no circulator pump on the pipes near the boiler.
  • Very large steel or iron mains in the cellar, often much bigger than the pipes you see on a modern system.
  • A boiler with a gauge showing only temperature, or little pressure to read, since the system is open to the air.

A system can look like this and still have a pump added later, so look for a small motor on the pipes before you decide. If radiators run on a single loop with special tees, see One-Pipe Diverter-Tee Hot-Water Heating Systems. Houses heated by a central warm-air unit with big sloping ducts are a different old design, covered in Gravity Warm-Air Furnaces.

Slow warm-up

Gravity systems are known for coming on slowly. In our own explanation: the push is tiny at first, because the supply and return water start at nearly the same temperature, so flow builds only as the boiler water heats. All that water and cast iron then has to be heated too. The upside is that the radiators keep giving off heat after the burner shuts off, which the state guide and the federal guide both describe in terms of cast iron releasing heat over time. Thermostat setbacks work poorly on such a system, because recovery takes long.

What a circulator and closed tank change

Adding a circulator lets the water move faster than gravity alone. That makes the warm-up faster and lets the system serve rooms that were hard to reach, and the federal guide says forced flow through smaller pipes is the modern approach. A closed, diaphragm-type expansion tank can then replace the open attic tank, which takes away the attic freeze and overflow problems. The tradeoff is that the system now runs under pressure, so it also needs a fill valve, a relief valve, an air removal method and a pressure gauge, all covered in Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure. The pipes, valves and radiators of a gravity system were built for a low-pressure open loop, so a pressure test and a check by a qualified hydronic tech are the sensible first step. Air in a converted system can leave some radiators cold; see Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold.

Our own worked example: water volume and expansion

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measurement of any real house. The pipe volumes use standard inside diameters of steel pipe, which we assume: 2.067 in, 1.610 in and 1.049 in for nominal 2 in, 1.5 in and 1 in. Volume per length is the circle area times the length, divided by 231 cubic inches per gallon.

Assumed gravity system: 120 ft of 2 in main, 80 ft of 1.5 in branch, 60 ft of 1 in runouts, ten radiators holding about 1.0 gallon each (assumed), and a boiler holding about 8 gallons (assumed).

  • 2 in: 0.7854 × 2.067² = 3.356 sq in; × 120 ft × 12 in = 4,832 cu in; ÷ 231 = 20.9 gal
  • 1.5 in: 0.7854 × 1.610² = 2.036 sq in; × 960 in = 1,954 cu in; ÷ 231 = 8.5 gal
  • 1 in: 0.7854 × 1.049² = 0.864 sq in; × 720 in = 622 cu in; ÷ 231 = 2.7 gal
  • Pipes: 20.9 + 8.5 + 2.7 = 32.1 gal. Add 10 gal of radiators and 8 gal of boiler: about 50.1 gal.

Assumed modern system for the same ten radiators: 200 ft of 0.785 in inside-diameter copper tube, the same ten radiators, and a small boiler holding 1.5 gal (assumed). Tube: 0.7854 × 0.785² = 0.484 sq in; × 2,400 in = 1,162 cu in; ÷ 231 = 5.0 gal. Total: 5.0 + 10 + 1.5 = about 16.5 gal, roughly a third of the gravity system.

Expansion from an assumed cold fill of 60 °F to an assumed hot 180 °F. The federal geological agency water density table lists 62.366 lb/ft³ at 60 °F and 60.586 lb/ft³ at 180 °F. The same weight of water takes up more room when hot, so the volume ratio is 62.366 ÷ 60.586 = 1.0294, an increase of 2.94 percent.

  • Gravity system: 50.1 gal × 0.0294 = about 1.47 gal that the open tank must take up.
  • Modern system: 16.5 gal × 0.0294 = about 0.49 gal for the closed tank to take up.

Check: 1.47 ÷ 0.49 is 3.0, matching the 50.1 ÷ 16.5 ratio of 3.0. The point is the scale: a gravity system needs three times the cushion for its expansion, which is one reason the old tanks were large. A real closed tank also needs a pressure rating and size set by its maker, so use this only to understand the idea.

Asbestos pipe insulation cautions

Many old boiler rooms have pipe and boiler wrap that may contain asbestos. The federal environmental agency page on exposures to asbestos says pipe, furnace and boiler insulation can contain it, that you generally cannot tell by looking, and that usually the best thing is to leave asbestos-containing material alone if it is in good condition. It warns against sawing, sanding or drilling it, and against dusting, sweeping or vacuuming debris that may contain it. Damaged material may release fibers, so the page says to limit access and not touch slightly damaged areas, and that repair or removal needs a trained and accredited professional. The federal homeowner booklet adds that firm, intact pipe and boiler insulation is generally safe to leave alone, and that removal should be left to professionals. For a technician the rule is simple: before you cut, drill or strip old wrap to add a circulator or tank, stop and have it tested by a professional. See also Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems.

Keep or replace the radiators?

The federal rehabilitation guide says replacing radiators is probably more an issue of lifestyle and aesthetics, and that cast iron holds more water and gives off heat longer than finned copper, which works faster and has a higher output. In plain terms, sound cast-iron radiators are often worth keeping when they do not leak and heat the room, while bent-fin or damaged baseboard units are better replaced. Our own checklist: pressure test before you decide, bleed or fix air problems first, and size any new boiler to the actual radiators.

Field card: Gravity Hot-Water Heating in Older Homes

No pump, big pipes, open tank up high.

  • Hot water rises, cooler water sinks back: flow comes from weight difference alone.
  • Large pipes and radiators mean lots of water and a slow warm-up.
  • The open attic tank can freeze, overflow or rust through.
  • A circulator and closed tank speed things up but make the loop pressurized.
  • Our example: about 50 gal gravity vs 16.5 gal modern; expansion 1.47 vs 0.49 gal.
  • Leave good asbestos wrap alone; have a professional test before cutting or stripping.
  • Keep sound cast-iron radiators; replace damaged ones.
