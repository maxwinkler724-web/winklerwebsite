Pumping Down a Ductless Mini-Split Before Moving It: Service Valve Order, Forced Cooling Mode, Safety and When Recovery Is Required

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before a ductless mini-split is moved, the refrigerant in the indoor unit and line set is usually pulled back into the outdoor unit and sealed in with the two service valves. Done right, it keeps the charge out of the air and out of a recovery cylinder. Done wrong, it can wreck the compressor or send air through it. This lesson covers the order of the valves, the forced cooling mode, the gauge reading, the dangers of air, and when pump-down is not enough.

Check the maker's document for your unit

Check the maker's document for your unit. Forced cooling, gauge targets and timing differ by maker, and we show the spread on purpose. This page is general education from manuals and federal rules read in 2026, not a substitute for the manual of the exact outdoor unit.

When pump-down is allowed and when recovery is required

Federal rules say refrigerant may not be knowingly vented. EPA's page on the revised Section 608 regulations says the venting prohibition covers ozone-depleting refrigerants and substitutes such as HFCs, and that anyone removing refrigerant from an air-conditioning appliance must evacuate it to a set level, using certified recovery equipment, before servicing or disposing of it. The regulation itself (40 CFR 82.156(a)) adds two details that matter here. A technician may evacuate either the whole appliance or only the part to be serviced, if the refrigerant in that part can be isolated. And when the appliance will not be vented afterward and the work is not "major" as defined in 40 CFR 82.152, a medium-, high- or very high-pressure appliance may instead be evacuated to no higher than 0 psig before it is opened.

Our reading, not a legal opinion: when a mini-split is relocated, the pump-down is how the technician isolates the charge in the outdoor unit so the indoor unit and line set can be opened. The 82.152 definition of "major" work covers removing a compressor, condenser or evaporator, or leaving an opening larger than 4 square inches for over 15 minutes. Replacing a compressor or coil would be major; moving the pipes alone is not listed. Confirm how your EPA 608 certification training treats your job. Pump-down is the wrong tool, and recovery with certified equipment is the answer, in these cases:

  • The circuit leaks. One maker's service manual says not to pump down with the compressor when the refrigeration circuit has a leak, and tells you to recover into a separate cylinder.
  • The charge will not fit. A service manual for a multi-split outdoor unit says the unit can collect only its rated amount, shown on the nameplate, and that if the system holds more, you should not pump down and should use another recovery method.
  • The compressor cannot run, or the unit is being scrapped or opened for major repair.
  • The maker says so. If the manual for the unit tells you to recover, recover.

The pump-down sequence

Several single-zone and multi-zone installation manuals give the same shape: take the caps off both service valves, run the unit in forced cooling, close the liquid valve, then close the gas valve and stop. Two of them (R-410A and R-32 units from the same maker) say to close the liquid valve with a hex wrench after 5 to 10 minutes of forced cooling, and then close the gas valve and stop forced cooling after 2 to 3 minutes. One of them also warns not to open or close the stop valves at an outdoor temperature of −5 °F (−21 °C) or lower.

  1. Start forced cooling. It is a service mode that runs the unit in cooling, started by a switch on the board or indoor unit, a remote controller sequence, or a service tool. One manual sets switches on the outdoor board; another cycles the power and presses an emergency switch once, and runs up to 30 minutes. Use your unit's method.
  2. Close the liquid service valve. The liquid valve is the small line, usually a hex-key stem. With it closed, the compressor keeps pulling refrigerant out of the indoor coil and line set.
  3. Watch the compound gauge. Hang the gauge on the gas valve service port. A mini-split manual from one maker sends you to close the gas valve when the gauge falls into 14.2 to 7.1 psig, and says that running too long damages the compressor. A second maker's manual closes the gas valve when the gauge reads between 0.05 and 0 MPa, which is about 7.3 psig down to 0 (our conversion), and has you start with the gas valve nearly closed so it can be shut fast. A service manual for another unit says to check the manifold for vacuum before closing the gas valve. Two other installation manuals give no gauge value at all, only times. Our rule: the gauge is the stop signal and the clock is the backup.
  4. Close the gas service valve and stop the unit. One manual says to close the gas valve and immediately turn the unit off. Then cap the valve ports and, if the manual says so, cover the open flare ends.

Some outdoor units have a low-pressure switch to protect the compressor. A service manual warns in plain words never to short it out during pump-down.

Why a vacuum and air are dangerous

A compressor is built to compress refrigerant vapor, not air. If it keeps running after the circuit is empty, or with a connection open, the suction side can fall below atmospheric pressure and pull air in through any opening. The manuals are blunt about it, in their own words:

  • A manual for a multi-zone unit says to stop the compressor before removing the refrigerant piping; if the compressor runs with a stop valve open, air will be sucked in when the piping is removed and the pressure can rise abnormally.
  • A service manual for another multi-system unit warns of an explosive hazard when executing pump-down, and says that running the compressor can cause self-combustion because air can enter.
  • A single-zone manual says to turn the power off before removing the hose after pump-down, because running with the hose disconnected lets air into the compressor and the pressure rise can cause an explosion and injury.
  • A third maker says air can cause an abnormal pressure rise that may result in explosion or injury.

Our explanation, not quoted from a manual: air carries oxygen, and the compressor holds oil. Squeezing oxygen and oil mist together makes heat, which is why makers talk about explosion. The practical rule is the same in every manual: stop the compressor at the right moment, with both valves closed, before any pipe is loosened.

How much charge the outdoor unit can hold

The outdoor unit can only hold so much, so there is a limit. The multi-system manual above says the nameplate shows the rated amount. Maker documents also record how much charge was added in the field: one tells you to write the added refrigerant on the nameplate behind the stop valve cover, and another has a charge label where you record the factory charge, the amount added and the total. With a long line set, read those numbers before you plan on a pump-down.

Worked example (ours): 12,000 Btu/h unit, 20 extra feet

All inputs here are assumptions for practice, except the rate, which comes from a maker. We assume a 12,000 Btu/h R-410A outdoor unit with a factory charge of 41.6 oz for 25 ft of line, and an assumed maximum outdoor unit charge of 48 oz (3.0 lb) from a nameplate. The installer used 45 ft of line, so 20 ft more than the factory length. One maker prints an additional charge of 20 g per meter (shown as 0.21 oz/ft in another place on the same maker's manuals). We use 20 g/m.

Step 1. 20 ft × 0.3048 = 6.096 m.

Step 2. 6.096 m × 20 g/m = 121.9 g.

Step 3. 121.9 g ÷ 28.35 g/oz = 4.30 oz. (At the printed 0.21 oz/ft, 20 × 0.21 = 4.2 oz, so the two agree to a tenth of an ounce.)

Step 4. Total charge now in the system = 41.6 + 4.3 = 45.9 oz.

Step 5. Compare with the assumed limit: 48 − 45.9 = 2.1 oz of room. So it still fits, with a narrow margin.

If the charge were over the limit, the second line of that manual applies: recover instead of pumping down.

Reinstalling at the new location

Treat the new location like a new install. See Mini-Split Installation Basics and Flare Fittings on Mini-Splits.

  1. Make the connections with the maker's torque values.
  2. Pressure test with nitrogen. One manual says to hold for at least 1 hour and recommends 24 hours; if the pressure drops, find the leak.
  3. Evacuate the indoor unit and lines. Two manuals say to pull below 500 microns for at least 1 hour, then close the gauge valve and watch that the pointer does not swing back.
  4. Open the valves. One manual opens the liquid valve first and then the gas valve, both fully with a hex wrench, never past the stop, then checks the flares and valve stems with soapy water.
  5. Add any extra charge the longer line needs, record it, and torque the caps. See Refrigerant Service Valves and Schrader Cores.

For pump-down as a walk-in control cycle, see Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves. When recovery is the answer, see Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step.

Field card: mini-split pump-down

Liquid valve first, gas valve at about 0 psig, then stop.

  • Venting is prohibited. Pump-down is allowed only if it fits the rule and the maker's manual.
  • Do not pump down a leaking circuit or one holding more than the nameplate charge.
  • Forced cooling, then the liquid valve after 5 to 10 minutes, per one maker.
  • Gauge targets differ: 14.2 to 7.1 psig for one, 0.05 to 0 MPa for another.
  • Close the gas valve, stop the unit, then cap. Never loosen pipe while the compressor runs.
  • Air plus a running compressor is an explosion warning in several manuals.
  • Reinstall: leak test, evacuate below 500 microns, open the liquid valve then gas valve.
