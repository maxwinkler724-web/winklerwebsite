Fixed-Plate Air-to-Air Heat Exchangers in ERVs and Air Handlers: Cross-Flow vs Counterflow, Frost and Cross-Leakage

Last updated: 2026-10-26

Some energy recovery devices have no moving parts at all: just stacked plates with the exhaust air on one side and the outdoor air on the other. This lesson covers the fixed-plate core: how cross-flow and counterflow differ, why frost is the big winter problem, why leakage between airstreams is so low, and what the core costs you in fan energy.

Where this fits

A fixed-plate exchanger is one of several ways to move energy between exhaust and outdoor air. Rotating media are covered in Energy Recovery Wheels in Commercial Units, and coil-based options in Run-Around Coil Loops and Heat Pipes. Plate cores also sit in packaged ERVs, air handlers with a recovery section, and the units in Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems. The home-sized version is in ERV vs. HRV.

How the core works

The core is a stack of thin plates that form alternating channels. Exhaust air flows through every other channel and outdoor air through the rest. Heat passes through the plate wall from the warmer stream to the cooler one. Because the plates are fixed and sealed at the edges, the two streams stay physically separate. A UK government energy technology list describes plate exchangers as having no moving parts and says cross-contamination and leakage are minimal.

Cross-flow vs counterflow

In a cross-flow core the streams run at right angles. In a counterflow core they run in opposite directions through parallel channels, which keeps a more even temperature difference along the plate. The UK list says cross-flow units are typically simpler, cheaper and more common, while counterflow offers greater efficiencies.

For numbers, treat ranges as ranges. The U.S. Department of Energy ventilation guide says the sensible effectiveness of ERV and HRV heat exchangers typically ranges from 55 to 95 percent, with higher values usually costing more. The UK list requires at least 71 percent dry heat recovery efficiency for a plate exchanger to be listed, and says specialist membrane plates may reach up to 90 percent. Use the rated value for the actual model, flows and test conditions, never a rule of thumb.

Sensible-only plates vs enthalpy membranes

Aluminum or plastic plates pass heat but not moisture, so they recover sensible energy only. Enthalpy cores use treated paper or a polymer membrane that lets water vapor move too, so they recover latent energy as well. A utility-funded technology center says fixed-plate enthalpy units can provide up to 50 percent moisture transfer. In humid climates a membrane core also takes some moisture out of incoming summer air. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory modeled retrofit recovery with membrane counterflow plates for humid climates and sensible-only aluminum counterflow plates in drier ones, both with a preheat coil for frost and a bypass.

Frost in cold weather

Warm exhaust air carries moisture. When very cold outdoor air chills the plates, that moisture condenses and can freeze, which blocks the exhaust channels. Frost lowers airflow and recovery, and can damage the core. The Cold Climate Housing Research Center lists three common fixes: preheating the supply air, briefly recirculating indoor air, or routing cold air around the core. Rutgers university says cold-climate recovery ventilators must have a device to prevent freezing and frost.

  • Preheat. An electric or hydronic coil warms the outdoor air before the core. It is simple and works, but it uses energy during the coldest hours.
  • Bypass damper. Outdoor air is diverted around the core. This protects the plates but gives up recovery while it is open.
  • Fan cycling. The supply fan slows or stops so warm exhaust air melts the frost. Pressure falls inside the building during that time, so it is a short, timed event.

A Minnesota energy efficiency nonprofit notes that frost controls and implementations vary broadly, and that in very cold weather (below 0 F outdoor) recovery is reduced to mitigate frost.

The bypass has a second job. The Washington State commercial energy code, built on the model code, says that where an air economizer is required, the energy recovery system must include a bypass of the recovery media.

Cross-leakage versus wheels

Cross-leakage means exhaust air ending up in the supply. The ratings term for it is the Exhaust Air Transfer Ratio, or EATR. A wheel carries a little exhaust air in its rotating media, so a purge section is used to limit it. The UK list says rotating exchangers have greater cross-contamination than plates and are unsuitable for hospital operating theatres and food production. A sealed plate core only leaks where gaskets, edges or the cabinet are damaged. If an odor from the exhaust shows up in the supply, suspect a bad seal, a cracked plate, or a pressure arrangement that pushes air through a leak. Ask for the rated EATR when the application is sensitive.

Worked example: supply temperature and recovered Btu/h

Assumptions (ours, for practice): balanced airflow of 3,000 cfm on both sides, outdoor air 15 F, return air 72 F, sensible effectiveness 70 percent (0.70).

  1. Supply leaving temperature: T_supply = T_out + e x (T_return - T_out) = 15 + 0.70 x (72 - 15) = 15 + 39.9 = 54.9 F.
  2. Temperature rise across the core: 54.9 - 15 = 39.9 F.
  3. Recovered heat: 1.08 x cfm x dT = 1.08 x 3,000 x 39.9 = about 129,000 Btu/h.
  4. Frost check, with balanced flows: exhaust leaving = 72 - 39.9 = 32.1 F. That is right at freezing, and the surface is colder than the leaving air. This example is exactly the condition that needs frost control.

The 1.08 factor covers sensible heat in standard air only. These are our own teaching values, not a maker rating.

Pressure drop and fan energy

Air passing through narrow channels loses pressure, and both the supply and exhaust fans must overcome it. Higher effectiveness tends to mean a bigger drop, and a dirty or frosted core makes it worse. The DOE guide says power use for many residential HRVs and ERVs ranges from 30 to 200 W, and that units above 1 cfm per watt often use variable-speed motors.

Our own estimate: an added 0.6 in. w.c. on each of two fans at 3,000 cfm, with a total fan system efficiency of 60 percent. Air horsepower per fan is cfm x in. w.c. / (6,356 x efficiency) = 3,000 x 0.6 / (6,356 x 0.6) = 0.47 hp. Two fans make about 0.94 hp, or roughly 0.7 kW, running whenever the units run. For more on fan energy see VAV Supply Fan Duct Static Pressure Reset.

Cleaning and condensate drains

Clean or replace the filters ahead of the core, and clean the core as the maker directs. Rutgers says to clean exchangers regularly to keep performance high and prevent mold and bacteria buildup. The DOE guide says higher-performing ERVs need quarterly checks or cleaning of filters, heat exchange media and exterior duct terminations. In cold weather, keep the outdoor intake clear of snow and frost, as the Cold Climate Housing Research Center advises. Condensate forms when the exhaust side cools below its dew point, so the pan and drain need a proper slope and a trap suited to the pressure at that point. The DOE guide says drains should be installed as required by the manufacturers. Check the drain is open before cold weather.

Code context

The Washington State commercial energy code text requires recovery for systems with design outside air above 5,000 cfm (other thresholds apply by climate zone) and at least a 50 percent change in outdoor air enthalpy. Check the code where you work.

Field card: Fixed-plate heat exchangers

No moving parts, low leakage, frost is the winter risk.

  • T_supply = T_out + e x (T_return - T_out); Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT.
  • Counterflow generally beats cross-flow; read the rated effectiveness.
  • Aluminum or plastic plates: sensible only. Membrane cores: heat and moisture.
  • Frost fixes: preheat, bypass damper, fan cycling.
  • Check filters, core, intake, condensate drain, and seals.
  • A dirty or frosted core raises fan power and cuts recovery.
