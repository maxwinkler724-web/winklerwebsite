Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances: Annualizing Method and Repair Deadlines

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a big system keeps needing refrigerant, EPA wants a number, not a guess. This lesson walks through how EPA defines the leak rate, how the annualizing method works with a full example, and what the repair clock looks like once the number is too high.

Check current EPA rules

This is general education, not legal advice. We built it from EPA web pages and the federal regulation text. Rules change, and a compliance decision belongs to the owner, the company's compliance lead or a lawyer. Confirm everything on EPA's pages and the eCFR before you rely on it.

Which appliances are covered, and by which rule

Two federal rules cover leak repair, and the refrigerant decides which one applies. Under Section 608 (40 CFR 82.157), EPA's leak repair page says the rules apply to appliances with a full charge of 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant, meaning CFCs and HCFCs. The regulation text says that since April 10, 2020, the section does not apply to appliances containing only substitute refrigerants, and EPA's page on the 2020 revisions says appliances with 50 or more pounds of substitutes are no longer subject to it.

That is not the end of the story for HFC equipment. EPA's January 2026 fact sheet for the AIM Act rule says appliances with 15 pounds or more of an HFC, or of a substitute with a global warming potential above 53, have leak repair duties starting January 1, 2026, under 40 CFR 84.106. The same fact sheet says residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump equipment is exempt, and that chillers and certain variable refrigerant flow systems are handled under their own subsectors. The EPA pages we read did not explain how the Technology Transitions rule fits in, so this lesson does not describe it. Ask your compliance lead which rule your equipment falls under.

The leak-rate definitions in both rules use the same two methods, so we teach the math once.

Trigger leak rates

EPA gives a trigger rate for each kind of equipment. If the calculated rate goes above it, the repair duties start. In 40 CFR 82.157(c)(2), 84.106(c)(2) and EPA's leak repair page, the numbers are:

  • Commercial refrigeration: 20 percent. EPA defines this as the retail food and cold storage warehouse sectors.
  • Industrial process refrigeration: 30 percent. EPA describes customized appliances tied to industrial processes, plus industrial ice machines, electricity generation and ice rinks.
  • Comfort cooling and other appliances: 10 percent. EPA's definition of comfort cooling is air conditioning for occupied buildings, with chillers, commercial split systems and rooftop units as examples. The HFC fact sheet also puts refrigerated transport in this 10 percent group.

The two calculation methods

EPA's definition of "leak rate" (40 CFR 82.152 and 84.102) says the rate is the loss between refrigerant charges, expressed as the percent of the full charge that would be lost over 12 months if the current loss continued. Two methods are allowed, and the same method must be used for all appliances at a facility that are subject to the leak repair rule. So EPA does not require one method over the other: the owner picks. The regulation lets an owner change methods only in limited cases, such as acquiring a facility that used a different method (40 CFR 84.106(b)(3)).

Annualizing method. EPA describes four steps:

  1. Divide the pounds added to return the appliance to full charge by the pounds the appliance normally holds at full charge.
  2. Take the shorter of the days since refrigerant was last added or 365 days, and divide that by 365.
  3. Divide the step 1 result by the step 2 result.
  4. Multiply by 100 to get a percentage.

Rolling average method. Add up the pounds put in over the previous 365 days (or the shorter time since the last successful follow-up verification test showed all known leaks fixed), divide by the full charge, and multiply by 100.

The rule requires the calculation each time refrigerant is added, except after a retrofit, after a new installation, or for a qualifying seasonal variance (40 CFR 82.157(b)). The person who adds or removes refrigerant must hand the owner documentation when the service ends.

Worked example: a 300 lb comfort cooling chiller

All arithmetic here is Our own, applying EPA's steps above. Assume the chiller's full charge is 300 lb and a technician adds 45 lb to bring it back to full charge. It is comfort cooling, so the trigger is 10 percent.

Case A: last addition was 120 days ago

  1. Our own: 45 lb divided by 300 lb = 0.15.
  2. Our own: 120 days is shorter than 365, so use 120. 120 divided by 365 = 0.3288.
  3. Our own: 0.15 divided by 0.3288 = 0.4563.
  4. Our own: 0.4563 times 100 = about 45.6 percent.

That matches the shortcut (lb added / full charge) x (365 / days) x 100 = 0.15 x 3.0417 x 100. Our own comparison: 45.6 percent is well above the 10 percent comfort cooling trigger, so the repair duties apply.

Case B: the same 45 lb, but 400 days since the last addition

EPA's step 2 says to use the shorter of the days since the last addition or 365. Here 400 is longer, so the number used is 365.

  1. Our own: 45 divided by 300 = 0.15.
  2. Our own: 365 divided by 365 = 1.
  3. Our own: 0.15 divided by 1 = 0.15.
  4. Our own: 0.15 times 100 = 15 percent.

Our own comparison: 15 percent is still above the 10 percent trigger. A shortcut with 400 days would give 13.7 percent, which is not EPA's result, so cap the days at 365.

Repair deadlines after the trigger is passed

EPA's leak repair page says the owner must either repair within 30 days or develop a retrofit or retirement plan. The regulation text counts the 30 days from when refrigerant is added and the appliance is over its trigger (40 CFR 84.106(d)); EPA's page words it as 30 days from the date the leak was discovered. A 120-day period replaces 30 days when an industrial process shutdown is needed. A certified technician does the leak inspection, and the repair is confirmed by the next leak-rate calculation. Extra time is possible for reasons such as unavailable parts or conflicting rules, and the owner asks EPA for it. Timelines are suspended while an appliance is mothballed.

Verification tests

The regulation requires an initial verification test within the repair period to show the repaired leaks are fixed. A follow-up test is then due within 10 days of a successful initial test, or within 10 days of the appliance reaching normal operating conditions (40 CFR 82.157(e) and 84.106(e)). EPA says these tests are essential to confirm repairs worked. See our leak detector lesson for search technique.

Retrofit or retirement plans

A plan is due within 30 days if the owner chooses to retrofit or retire, does not act on the leak, or the appliance still leaks over the trigger after repairs and verification tests. It must include a schedule of no more than one year, be signed and dated, and be kept on site, with the original available to EPA. Work is generally finished within one year of the plan date.

Chronically leaking appliances

EPA's recordkeeping page says an owner whose appliance leaks 125 percent or more of its full charge in a calendar year sends EPA a report describing the efforts to find and repair the leaks. Reports are due March 1 of the following year, and EPA prefers electronic submission. The size threshold is 50 lb for ozone-depleting refrigerant and 15 lb under the HFC rule.

Recordkeeping

EPA's page says technicians give owners an invoice showing refrigerant added plus records of leak inspections and verification tests. Owners keep service records (date, type of service, quantity added) and the inspection and test records. The regulations say records are generally kept at least three years, with full-charge determinations kept until three years after the appliance is retired. See EPA 608 Records: What Technicians Keep.

What to remember

Know the full charge, log every addition with a date and weight, and compute the rate each time. For background, read Leaks and Leak Repair Basics, the EPA 608 Guide, and, for low-pressure chillers that draw in air, Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables.

Field card: Leak Rate Calculation

Pounds added over full charge, annualized, times 100.

  • Covered: 50 lb or more of CFC or HCFC (82.157); 15 lb or more of qualifying HFCs (84.106, from Jan 1, 2026).
  • Triggers: 20 percent commercial refrigeration, 30 percent industrial process, 10 percent comfort cooling and others.
  • Annualizing: (lb added / full charge) divided by (shorter of days or 365, over 365), times 100.
  • Over 365 days since the last addition: use 365.
  • Our own example: 45 lb on 300 lb after 120 days = 45.6 percent; after 400 days = 15 percent.
  • Repair in 30 days (120 for industrial shutdown) or write a retrofit or retirement plan.
  • Initial test, then follow-up within 10 days. Chronic leaker report by March 1.
