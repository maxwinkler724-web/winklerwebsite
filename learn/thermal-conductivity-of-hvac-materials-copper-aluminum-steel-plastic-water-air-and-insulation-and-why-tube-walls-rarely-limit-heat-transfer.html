Thermal Conductivity of HVAC Materials: Copper, Aluminum, Steel, Plastic, Water, Air and Insulation, and Why Tube Walls Rarely Limit Heat Transfer

Last updated: 2026-11-02

Thermal conductivity, written k, tells you how easily heat moves through a solid or still fluid. It spans about five orders of magnitude across the materials in an HVAC system, from copper at the top to foam and still air at the bottom. This lesson gives sourced k values in both unit systems, shows how to turn them into a wall resistance, and explains why a copper or aluminum coil wall is almost never what limits heat transfer, while a plastic or scaled wall can be.

What k is, and the two unit systems

Conduction through a flat wall follows a simple rule: heat flow per square foot equals the temperature difference divided by a resistance, and that resistance is thickness divided by k. We cover the three ways heat moves in our conduction, convection and radiation lesson. Here the only piece we need is R = L / k, where L is the wall thickness.

  • SI units: k in W/m-K (watts per meter per kelvin; a kelvin step equals a Celsius step).
  • Inch-pound units: k in Btu/h-ft-F (Btu per hour, per foot of thickness, per degree F of difference).
  • Conversion: 1 W/m-K = 0.5778 Btu/h-ft-F. To go the other way, divide by 0.5778 (or multiply by 1.731).

Direction rules to keep in mind: a larger k means less resistance and more heat for the same temperature difference; a thicker wall means more resistance and less heat. Resistance in h-ft²-F/Btu is simply the thickness in feet divided by k in Btu/h-ft-F.

Typical values and where they come from

Handbook values depend on alloy, purity, moisture, density and temperature, so we give ranges and the basis for each. Values below come from university and government tables listed in Sources. We converted to Btu/h-ft-F ourselves using 0.5778.

A U.S. Department of Energy lab report on heat exchangers says many metals exceed 100 W/m-K, which is why they are the usual choice, and that polymers sit around 0.1 to 0.5 W/m-K. The chart shows the whole spread.

Two comparisons worth remembering, from our own arithmetic on the table: copper (385) is about 27 times better than 304 stainless (14), and about 2,400 times better than PVC (0.16). Water conducts about 25 times better than still air (0.607 vs 0.024), which is one reason water-side films are so much thinner in resistance than air-side films. Insulation works by trapping still air or low-conductivity gas in small cells so that conduction stays near the air value; our home insulation types lesson covers R-value per inch.

Why coils use copper tube and aluminum fins

Both metals are in the "over 100 W/m-K" group, so heat moves along a fin and through a tube wall with very little temperature drop. For fins this matters twice: heat has to travel from the tube out along the fin, and a fin with a higher k stays closer to the tube temperature along its length, so more of its area does useful work. Our fins lesson works that through. Cost, weight, forming and corrosion also drive the choice (see our lesson on aluminum coil tubing and copper-to-aluminum joints), but this page covers conduction only. Carbon steel at roughly 43 to 64 W/m-K is lower but still a good conductor, which is why steel pipe and boiler tubes work in the hot-water and steam world.

Worked example (our own): wall resistance next to the films

Our own example, with assumptions. Take one square foot of a 0.032 in. wall. Thickness L = 0.032 / 12 = 0.002667 ft. We assume an air-side film resistance of 0.5 h-ft²-F/Btu (a film coefficient of 2 Btu/h-ft²-F) and a water-side film resistance of 0.005 h-ft²-F/Btu (200 Btu/h-ft²-F). These are round numbers we picked for illustration, per square foot of the same area, with no fin area credit. They are not measured values for any product. For k we use 385 W/m-K for copper, 237 for aluminum, 14 for stainless steel and 0.16 for PVC from the table above.

Step 1: convert k. Copper 385 × 0.5778 = 222.5 Btu/h-ft-F. Aluminum 237 × 0.5778 = 136.9. Stainless 14 × 0.5778 = 8.09. PVC 0.16 × 0.5778 = 0.0924.

Step 2: wall resistance R = L / k.

  • Copper: 0.002667 / 222.5 = 0.000012 h-ft²-F/Btu
  • Aluminum: 0.002667 / 136.9 = 0.000019
  • Stainless: 0.002667 / 8.09 = 0.00033
  • PVC: 0.002667 / 0.0924 = 0.0288

Step 3: add the films in series. Total R = air film + wall + water film = 0.5 + wall + 0.005.

  • Copper: 0.505012, wall share 0.0024 percent, U = 1/R = 1.980 Btu/h-ft²-F
  • Aluminum: 0.505019, wall share 0.0039 percent, U = 1.980
  • Stainless: 0.505330, wall share 0.065 percent, U = 1.979
  • PVC: 0.533845, wall share 5.4 percent, U = 1.873 (about 5.4 percent lower than copper)

So with air on one side, switching from copper to stainless changes the overall result by about 0.07 percent. The air film is the limit, by a factor of roughly 40,000 over the copper wall (0.5 / 0.000012). That is why more fin area and good airflow help a coil far more than a thicker or thinner copper wall. Why stainless and plastic heat exchangers are thicker or bigger

Put the same four walls between two water films (0.005 each, so 0.01 total, again our assumption). Copper: 0.010012, U = 99.9. Stainless: 0.010330, U = 96.8, a 3 percent loss. PVC: 0.038845, U = 25.7, and the wall is now 74 percent of the total resistance. To move the same heat at the same temperature difference, area scales with total resistance, so the PVC unit needs about 0.038845 / 0.010012 = 3.9 times the surface of the copper one, and the stainless unit about 3 percent more. Resistance also grows in direct proportion to thickness: a PVC wall twice as thick, 0.064 in., would double the wall term to 0.0577. Plastic is chosen for corrosion resistance and low cost in the right service, and the penalty is paid in surface area. Stainless pays a small penalty in a thin wall, which is why thin stainless plates and tubes are common when the fluid attacks copper or steel.

Why scale and oil films matter

A deposit on a tube is one more resistance in series. Published scale conductivities vary widely with composition and porosity, and we did not find a single sourced value for hard-water calcium carbonate scale in a primary source we could open. So we assume k = 1.0 W/m-K (0.5778 Btu/h-ft-F) for a 0.01 in. calcium scale layer, as our own round number for illustration. Thickness 0.01 / 12 = 0.000833 ft, so R = 0.000833 / 0.5778 = 0.00144 h-ft²-F/Btu.

  • That is about 120 times the resistance of the 0.032 in. copper wall (0.00144 / 0.000012).
  • It is 29 percent of the water film (0.00144 / 0.005).
  • Water to water through copper: total R rises from 0.010012 to 0.011454, a 14.4 percent increase, and U drops from 99.9 to 87.3 (12.6 percent lower).
  • With the 0.5 air film on the other side, the same scale adds only 0.28 percent, so a scaled water side matters most when the other side is also a liquid or a condensing vapor.
  • If scale k were 0.5 instead of 1.0, R would double to 0.0029; at 2.0 it would halve to 0.0007. The direction never changes: lower k, more resistance.

Oil is the same story. A film of oil on the inside of a tube is a poor conductor next to the metal and sits right where heat has to cross, so oil logging in an evaporator or an oil film on a water-side surface adds resistance and cuts capacity. We give no number for oil because we did not source one. Our fouling and series-resistance lesson above covers how plant crews trend it.

Field card: Thermal Conductivity of HVAC Materials

The film beside the wall, not the wall, usually sets the limit.

  • R = thickness / k. Bigger k, less resistance. Thicker wall, more resistance.
  • 1 W/m-K = 0.5778 Btu/h-ft-F.
  • Copper about 385, aluminum 202 to 237, steel 43 to 64, stainless about 14 W/m-K.
  • PVC about 0.16, water about 0.6, still air 0.024, insulation 0.02 to 0.04 W/m-K.
  • Series resistances add: films, wall, scale, oil.
  • A thin scale layer can out-resist a copper wall many times over.
