Mixed-Air Temperature and Percent Outdoor Air: The Temperature Method, Sensor Stratification and Why the Reading Lies

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Mixed-air temperature is the cheapest way to estimate how much outdoor air an air handler is pulling in. It is also easy to fool. This lesson shows the arithmetic, the two situations where the answer goes wrong, and the checks that tell you whether to trust it.

The idea: temperature as a blend

Mixing return air and outdoor air is a weighted average. If 30 percent of the air is outdoor, the mixed temperature sits 30 percent of the way from the return temperature toward the outdoor temperature. Run that backward and a temperature reading gives you the outdoor-air fraction. The PNNL building re-tuning guides write it as OAF = (MAT − RAT) / (OAT − RAT), and an EPA ventilation worksheet writes the same ratio as (RAT − MAT) / (RAT − OAT). Both are the same number, because the signs cancel. We use the second form here:

%OA = (RAT − MAT) / (RAT − OAT) × 100, where RAT is return-air temperature, MAT is mixed-air temperature and OAT is outdoor-air temperature.

Multiply the fraction by total supply airflow to get outdoor cfm. The PNNL guides also use the result as a check: the fraction should roughly match the outdoor-air damper position, and the guide says a value within about 10 to 15 percent of what you expect is generally reasonable. It also says the mixed-air temperature must always land between the return and outdoor temperatures. If it does not, suspect a sensor.

Worked example

These are our own numbers, chosen to be easy to follow. Assumptions: RAT 72 F, OAT 32 F, steady conditions, 10,000 cfm total supply air, and a perfectly mixed stream.

  1. Measured MAT is 60 F.
  2. Numerator: 72 − 60 = 12 F.
  3. Denominator: 72 − 32 = 40 F.
  4. %OA = 12 / 40 = 0.30, or 30 percent.
  5. Outdoor air = 0.30 × 10,000 = 3,000 cfm.

Stratified sensor. Now suppose the same box mixes poorly and a point sensor sits in a warmer layer and reads 62 F while the true average stays 60 F. Numerator: 72 − 62 = 10 F. %OA = 10 / 40 = 0.25, or 25 percent, and 0.25 × 10,000 = 2,500 cfm. A 2 F error produced a 500 cfm miss, which is 5 points of the answer.

Mild day. Change only the outdoor condition: OAT 66 F, RAT 72 F, 10,000 cfm, and assume the true fraction is still 30 percent. The true mixed temperature is 72 − 0.30 × (72 − 66) = 72 − 1.8 = 70.2 F. The denominator is only 72 − 66 = 6 F. Now add a sensor error of half a degree:

  • Reads 0.5 F high (70.7 F): (72 − 70.7) / 6 = 1.3 / 6 = 0.217, or 21.7 percent, which is 2,170 cfm.
  • Reads 0.5 F low (69.7 F): (72 − 69.7) / 6 = 2.3 / 6 = 0.383, or 38.3 percent, which is 3,830 cfm.

A half degree moved the answer from about 22 to about 38 percent, a 1,660 cfm spread, while the winter case shifted only 0.5 / 40 = 1.25 points (125 cfm) for the same error. Error in the result is roughly the sensor error divided by the RAT-minus-OAT spread, and that spread is the weak point.

When the temperature method fails

Small spread. The PNNL minimum outdoor-air guide limits the calculation to times when outdoor and return temperatures differ by about 5 F or more, and advises comparing damper position with the calculated fraction when the two temperatures are well apart. Our mild-day example shows why: at a 6 F spread, ordinary sensor tolerance swamps the signal. Trend the points and use the widest-spread hours.

Leaking dampers and recirculation. A closed outdoor damper that leaks makes the calculated fraction read above zero. The Penn State bulletin below suggests watching the mixed-air trend overnight, when the damper should be closed, and treating a mixed-air temperature that drifts toward freezing as a sign of significant leakage.

Stratification and averaging sensors

Most mixing chambers do not mix completely before the coil, according to the PNNL economizer guide, which recommends an averaging temperature sensor for the mixed-air measurement. That is a long sensing element, usually run back and forth across the section, whose resistance or fluid response reflects the whole length. It hands the controller one blended number instead of the temperature at one spot. The guide does not describe a stratification test, so here is our own habit: with the outdoor damper at minimum, take a handheld reading at several points across the face of the coil and compare them with the controller value. If the readings differ by more than a few degrees, the box is stratified and the single reading is questionable.

The California Title 24 air economizer acceptance procedure (NA7.5.4) tells installers to verify that the mixed-air sensor is the one the economizer is controlled from and that the sensors meet calibration requirements. The reference notes also warn that poor mixing can stratify the air and cause comfort problems, worse at low airflow.

Why stratification trips freezestats

An averaging element can read a comfortable 45 F while a layer of near-freezing outdoor air runs along one side of the coil. A freezestat has its own capillary and trips on the coldest spot it touches. The Penn State bulletin says a layer or stream of cold air can trip one section of the freezestat while the averaging mixed-air sensor is fine, calls it a very common problem, and recommends thoroughly mixing the two streams with air blenders or adjustable baffles. See freezestats, low limits and nuisance trips for placement and resets, and preheat coils for why the coil layout matters. A blender section forces the two streams to swirl together over a short distance; it adds pressure drop, so confirm the fan can carry it.

Compare your minimum-position and low-limit values against economizer settings.

A second opinion: the CO2 ratio

When the temperature spread is too small, measure carbon dioxide instead. The EPA worksheet gives the same blend logic: outdoor air percent = (Cs − Cr) / (Co − Cr), where Cs is the CO2 in supply or mixed air, Cr in return air and Co in outdoor air, all in ppm. The Washington State University energy program article adds the caveats: the space has to be in steady state, occupancy that changes while you measure will skew the result, instruments need occasional checks, and mixing and sample location matter.

A short example with assumed values: return 900 ppm, outdoor 420 ppm, mixed 660 ppm. (660 − 900) / (420 − 900) = −240 / −480 = 0.50, or 50 percent. The 480 ppm spread is large, which helps. Use it as a cross-check, then verify the airflow with a direct measurement as described in outdoor airflow measuring stations. Systems that separate ventilation from space conditioning avoid much of this guesswork; see dedicated outdoor air systems.

Field card: Mixed-air temperature and percent outdoor air

The result is only as good as the spread and the mixing.

  • %OA = (RAT − MAT) / (RAT − OAT); multiply by supply cfm.
  • MAT must fall between RAT and OAT. If not, suspect a sensor.
  • Use it when RAT and OAT differ by 5 F or more; wider is better.
  • Our example: 72, 32 and 60 F gives 30 percent, 3,000 cfm of 10,000.
  • Our mild day: a 0.5 F error moves 30 percent to 22 or 38 percent.
  • Averaging elements, blenders and baffles fight stratification and nuisance freezestat trips.
  • Cross-check with the CO2 ratio at steady state, then verify with a direct airflow measurement.
