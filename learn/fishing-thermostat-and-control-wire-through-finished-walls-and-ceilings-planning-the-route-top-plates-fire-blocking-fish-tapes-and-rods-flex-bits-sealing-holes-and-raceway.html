Fishing Thermostat Wire Through Finished Walls and Ceilings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Replacing a thermostat cable in a finished house means getting a thin wire through cavities you cannot see. The job goes well when you plan the route first, know what is probably inside the wall, and close up every hole you made. This lesson walks through that sequence for low-voltage control wire.

Plan the route before you drill

Start at the two ends: the thermostat location and the air handler or furnace control board. Then ask which direction is easiest. In a house with an accessible attic, the usual choice is to go up from the thermostat wall, across the attic floor, and down the wall behind or beside the equipment. With a basement or crawl space under the equipment, you may instead pull from below and come up inside a wall. Closets stacked above each other, chases and old pipe or flue chases can give a straight shot between floors. Walk the route outside the walls first: note which walls are exterior, where plumbing fixtures and electrical boxes sit, and where the attic floor is walkable. We prefer the route with the fewest horizontal bays to cross.

Ladders are part of attic work. Federal construction rules for portable ladders say the side rails must extend at least 3 feet above the upper landing (or the ladder must be secured at the top with a grab device), that a non-self-supporting ladder should be set at about one-quarter of its working length out from the wall, and that nobody should stand on the top step of a stepladder. Defective ladders are tagged and pulled from use.

What is inside the cavity

A typical stud wall has a bottom plate on the floor and one or two top plates under the ceiling. Where you drill up through the top plate from the room, or down from the attic, you are going through lumber. Inside the cavity you can meet insulation, wiring, pipes and fire blocking. A local building department page quoting the residential model code (International Residential Code section R302.11) says fire blocking is required in concealed wall spaces vertically at ceiling and floor levels and horizontally at intervals not exceeding 10 feet, and around vents, pipes, ducts, cables and wires at ceiling and floor levels. Approved materials include 2-inch nominal lumber, half-inch gypsum board, and mineral wool or glass fiber batts securely held in place, and unfaced fiberglass batts used for this purpose fill the cavity and reach at least 16 inches vertically. So a blocked bay is normal, not a mistake. If your fish tape stops hard a few feet below the top plate, think block before you think force.

Our shop practice, not a code rule: when the tape stops at the same depth twice, stop and find another path or open a small inspection hole. Pushing harder can punch through drywall or push insulation into a pile that the next person cannot get past.

Tools: tapes, rods and bits

  • Steel or fiberglass fish tape. A flat steel tape is stiff enough to push in a straight run and flexible enough to curve with a plate. Hook the wire to its end with a taped, tapered joint so it does not snag on the hole edge.
  • Glow rods and fiberglass rods. These come in sections that screw together. They are good for pushing across an attic floor or down a clear cavity. A glow rod is visible in the dark, which helps when you work from the attic side toward a light in the room. Keep rods away from anything energized, and do not rely on them as protection.
  • Flex bit. A long flexible drill bit with a small hole in the tip lets you drill a top plate from an angle, through the attic floor, or through a plate you can only reach at a slant. A string fed through the tip lets you pull a leader back through the cavity. Drill slowly and stop when the bit feels as if it has cleared the plate.
  • Pull string. Tie the wire to a string or leader and pull the string through, not the wire alone. This also leaves a pull line behind for the next cable.

Staying clear of plumbing and power cables

Hot and cold water lines, drain vents, and non-metallic power cables run through the same walls and plates. Look at the nearest outlet and switch boxes, which tell you the direction of nearby cable runs. In the basement or attic, follow what you can see. A cable that rises through a top plate into the attic is a sign of a nearby power run, so move your hole over. Keep control wire apart from line-voltage wire: do not run the two in the same bay when you can avoid it, and never share a box or fitting. Details on class 2 separation, conductor counts and splicing are in the related lesson on installing and splicing thermostat cable, and the National Electrical Code is the reference for what is allowed in your area.

Old-work low-voltage brackets and when to surface-mount

Where the wall has no box and you do not want to cut a large hole, an old-work low-voltage bracket (a plastic mounting ring that clamps to the drywall) holds the thermostat or a wall plate. It needs a hole just large enough for the wire and bracket tabs. Use it only for low-voltage devices; it is not a line-voltage box.

Sometimes the best fishing job is not fishing. If the wall is masonry or has a full fire-blocked cavity you cannot pass, or the ceiling is plaster over lath that you do not want to cut, mount a small raceway on the surface and run the cable inside it, painted to match the wall. Surface-mounting protects the cable, keeps it visible for the next person, and avoids the hidden holes this lesson is trying to control. Choose the raceway that is designed for low-voltage cable.

Close the holes: air sealing and fire blocking

Every hole through a top plate connects the conditioned house to the attic. A federal job aid on air sealing attic top plates calls for sealing the perimeter of gaps and extending sealant onto the adjacent materials, using a product rated for the location. A companion federal job aid on electrical penetrations picks the material by gap size: caulk for 1/4 inch or less, one-component foam or mastic for 1/4 inch to 2 inches, two-component foam for 2 to 3 inches, and for holes over 1/4 inch, backer rod to support the sealant. State and extension pages agree that wiring penetrations through the top plates of walls are leak points, and that sealing them before adding attic insulation matters because insulation can hide them. One extension page credits plumbing, electrical and HVAC penetrations with about 17 percent of a typical house's air leaks. A thermostat wire hole is tiny, so a small bead of caulk usually covers it; the point is to do it every time.

Do not leave a bigger gap than you need. Where your hole passes through a fire blocking or a rated assembly, restore that material with an approved product and read the related firestopping lesson; do not just fill it with whatever foam is in the truck. Near a flue or other heat source, the federal job aid says to use a high-temperature caulk, and the extension page says professionals use sheet metal and high-temperature caulk at flue penetrations.

Worked example (our own): how much cable to cut

These are our own assumed numbers for a made-up house, not a standard. Assumptions: 8-foot ceilings; thermostat center 4.5 feet above the floor; double top plate plus ceiling drywall allowed as 0.5 foot of travel at each end; 32 feet across the attic floor; the air handler control board 4 feet above the floor in a closet; a 2-foot service loop at each end; and 10 percent spare added at the end.

  1. Up from the thermostat to the ceiling: 8 − 4.5 = 3.5 ft.
  2. Through the plates at the thermostat wall: 0.5 ft.
  3. Across the attic: 32 ft.
  4. Through the closet plates: 0.5 ft.
  5. Down the closet wall to the board: 8 − 4 = 4 ft.
  6. Route length: 3.5 + 0.5 + 32 + 0.5 + 4 = 40.5 ft.
  7. Add the loops: 2 + 2 = 4 ft, so 40.5 + 4 = 44.5 ft.
  8. Add 10 percent spare: 44.5 × 0.10 = 4.45 ft, so 44.5 + 4.45 = 48.95 ft.

Round up and cut from at least 49 feet of cable. If the pull string is going to stay, cut it a few feet longer than the cable so you can tie off both ends. Change any assumption and the total changes: a different attic distance moves the answer foot for foot.

After the pull

Strip and label each end, check continuity, and seal the plate holes from the attic side before blowing or laying insulation back over them. Confirm the thermostat works through a full heating and cooling call before you close the work. Leave a note on the equipment about the route if your company allows it, because the next tech will thank you.

Field card: Fishing Thermostat Wire Through Finished Walls

Plan the route, expect fire blocking, pull a string, seal the holes.

  • Choose attic down, basement up, or closet to closet; walk it first.
  • Fire blocking in a stud cavity is normal; do not force the tape past it.
  • Flex bit through plates; pull a string, then the wire.
  • Stay clear of plumbing and power cable; keep class 2 wire in its own bay.
  • Seal the plate holes: caulk to 1/4 in, foam or mastic to 2 in, backer rod for bigger.
  • Surface-mount in raceway when the cavity will not cooperate.
  • Length = route + service loops + spare percentage.
