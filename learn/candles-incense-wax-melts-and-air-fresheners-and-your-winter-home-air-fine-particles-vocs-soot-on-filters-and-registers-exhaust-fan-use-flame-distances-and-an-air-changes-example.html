Candles, Incense, Wax Melts and Air Fresheners: What They Add to Your Winter Home Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

In winter the windows stay shut and the furnace runs, so whatever you burn or spray stays in the house longer. This lesson covers what candles, incense, wax melts and plug-in or spray fresheners put into the air, why dark soot sometimes shows up on filters and registers, and how a fan changes how long that air hangs around.

What these products release

The federal indoor air page on particles says burning almost anything produces fine particulate matter, and it lists candles, fireplaces and heating and cooking appliances among the sources. It also says candles can add carbon monoxide to the room and advises making sure there is ventilation whenever candles or incense are burned indoors. A federal science summary of candle and incense emissions found that both can release particles, that candles with lead-core wicks can push indoor lead levels up, and that incense smoke exposure has been linked to several health problems. A university news story adds that lead was banned in candle wicks sold here in 2003 but that imported candles may still contain it.

Fragrance products work differently. A university safety office page says sprays, candles, incense and diffusers are all linked to higher indoor levels of volatile organic compounds, or VOCs, including formaldehyde and benzene. It says the VOCs depend mostly on what is in the fragrance, not on the product type, that makers do not have to list every ingredient, and that "green" labels have no regulatory definition. It also says fragrance chemicals can react with indoor ozone to form new pollutants, and that claims about fresheners cleaning the air are hard to verify and do nothing about the odor source. The federal VOC page lists air fresheners among household products that give off VOCs and says many VOCs run up to ten times higher indoors than outdoors.

Wax melts and plug-ins use a warmed or evaporating fragrance and have no flame, but no flame does not mean no emissions. A university story on indoor air monitoring notes that plug-in fresheners can release VOCs too. We did not confirm figures for wax-melt particles from a source we could open, so we make no number claim about them.

Why a tight winter house holds more of it

The federal introduction to indoor air quality explains that buildings built to limit outdoor air leakage may have higher indoor pollutant levels unless they have mechanical ventilation, because too little outdoor air arrives to dilute indoor sources. The ventilation rate is the air exchange rate: how quickly outdoor air replaces indoor air. In winter the windows are closed and we want to keep heat in, so that rate is low. Burn a candle in July with a window cracked and the smoke goes out; burn the same candle in January and it mostly stays.

Soot on filters, walls and registers

A state public health technical brief calls candle burning the most common cause of indoor soot besides tobacco. It says soft wax, added fragrance and long wicks make more soot, and it lists other particle sources: oil lamps, kerosene heaters, cooking without an exhaust fan, and a poorly tuned furnace that puffs soot through the building. It describes deposits on walls and ceilings (especially where wall meets ceiling), around outlet covers and picture frames, on carpet along wall edges and under doors, and on filter media and fan blades where moving air pushes particles onto surfaces. The brief names forced air, static attraction in dry air, and gravity as the driving forces. Our own addition: because a forced-air system moves the whole house volume through the filter, a dark, greasy-looking film on a new filter or around a supply register is a hint to ask what is being burned, in addition to checking the furnace.

The brief's advice is practical: look for candles hidden in drawers or cabinets, switch to hard wax with wicks under one quarter inch and avoid jar candles, seal air leaks where dark streaks show on cold surfaces, fix leaky ducts, and have the furnace inspected and tuned. A furnace puffback or strange furnace noises are a reason to call for service right away. Our related lesson on dark lines at carpet edges and ghosting walks through the leak side of the same problem and when to call a gas technician.

Ventilation and exhaust fans when you burn

The federal particle page says candles are one source that may be possible to stop completely, and the VOC page says to increase ventilation when using VOC-emitting products and to use exhaust fans where labels ask for good ventilation. A kitchen or bath fan only helps if it is ducted outdoors and makeup air can get in somewhere, such as a slightly open window. Our own note: a fan that dumps into an attic or a closed room is not removing anything from the house.

Flames, drafts and registers

A flame in moving air burns unevenly, flickers and smokes. Our own practical note, not from a source we opened: keep candles away from supply registers, ceiling fans, open windows and exterior doors. A register blowing across a flame will make it flicker and can add soot to the air the system then spreads. Fire safety is the bigger reason for care. The fire administration page says to keep candles at least 12 inches from anything that burns, in stable holders where they cannot be knocked over, and to blow them out when you leave the room, leave home or go to bed. It says not to use lit candles in bedrooms, bathrooms and sleeping areas, to use flashlights rather than candles in an outage, and that flameless battery candles are an option. It cites about 20 home candle fires reported each day. A consumer safety agency tips page adds: keep burning candles within sight, use non-flammable holders, and keep them out of reach of children and pets.

Fix the odor at the source

The university page on fresheners lists the alternatives: remove the odor source, open a window or run an exhaust fan, maintain the HVAC system, clean and vacuum regularly, and use an air purifier only if that is not enough. The federal VOC page gives the same order of thinking: identify and, if possible, remove the source, then ventilate. In practice that means taking out the trash, cleaning the drain or pet area that smells, washing the fabric, and checking a musty or burning smell as a real problem, not something to cover. See our lessons on the first heat of the season, the range hood and air purifiers for those cases.

Worked example (our own): how fast does a fan change the room air?

This is our own example. The 0.35 air changes per hour and the 80 cfm fan are assumptions we chose for teaching, not measured values for any house or product.

  • Room: 15 ft by 12 ft with an 8 ft ceiling.
  • Assumed winter leakage: 0.35 air changes per hour (ACH).
  • Assumed fan: 80 cfm of air actually leaving this room's volume (for example a kitchen or bath fan serving it, with makeup air available).

Step 1, volume: 15 x 12 x 8 = 1,440 cubic feet.

Step 2, leakage only: 0.35 ACH means 0.35 x 1,440 = 504 cubic feet per hour, or 504 / 60 = 8.4 cfm. Time to replace the room air once = 1 / 0.35 = 2.86 hours, about 2 hours 51 minutes.

Step 3, with the fan: ACH = cfm x 60 / volume = 80 x 60 / 1,440 = 3.33 ACH. Time to replace the air once = 1 / 3.33 = 0.30 hours, about 18 minutes.

Step 4, compare: 3.33 / 0.35 is about 9.5, so the fan changes the room air roughly nine and a half times as fast. Adding leakage and fan (0.35 + 3.33 = 3.68 ACH) shortens one air change to about 16 minutes.

Our own caution: one air change does not clear a room completely. Because new air mixes with old, about 63 percent of a pollutant is gone after one air change (1 minus e to the power of minus 1), so the last bit takes longer. The fan only runs while someone is home to run it, and it pulls cold outdoor air in, so the home will feel cooler. The same math shows why a candle burned for hours in a sealed room builds up faster than the room can clear it.

Field card: Candles, incense, wax melts and fresheners in winter

Remove the source first, ventilate second, never mask.

  • Burning almost anything makes fine particles; candles and incense are no exception.
  • Fragrance products raise VOC levels, mostly depending on the fragrance.
  • Tight winter homes dilute pollutants slowly.
  • Soot on filters, walls and carpet edges can come from candles, so ask what is burned.
  • Candles: 12 inches from anything that burns, stable holders, out when you leave, none in bedrooms.
  • Our own example: 1,440 ft3 room, 0.35 ACH is 2 h 51 min per air change; an 80 cfm fan is 3.33 ACH, 18 min.
