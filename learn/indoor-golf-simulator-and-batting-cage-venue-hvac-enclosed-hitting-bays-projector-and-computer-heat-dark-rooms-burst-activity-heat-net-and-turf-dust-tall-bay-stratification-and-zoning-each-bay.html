Indoor Golf Simulator and Batting Cage Venue HVAC: Enclosed Hitting Bays, Projector and Computer Heat, Dark Rooms, Burst Activity, Dust, Tall Bays and Zoning Each Bay

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A simulator venue looks like a row of small dark rooms, each with a screen, a projector, a computer and a few people who stand still, then swing hard, then sit down for a drink. That mix is hard on a single thermostat. This lesson walks through where the heat comes from, why the bays and the lounge want separate control, and how to turn a bay's watts and players into a supply airflow.

What makes a hitting bay different

An enclosed bay has almost no outside wall load. It is usually an interior room, so the cooling load is mostly internal: electric equipment and people. The room is also tight on purpose. Curtains or walls keep light and ball strikes inside, and an impact screen sits at one end. That makes it easy to trap heat and hard to move air through it without disturbing the projected image or the ball flight. Compare that with a large open room such as a lane hall, covered in Bowling Center and Family Entertainment HVAC, where one air handler serves one big volume.

Screen, projector and computer heat

Almost every watt that goes into an electronic device ends up as heat in the room. The conversion we use is 3.41 Btu/h per watt, the same factor a university cooling-load lecture applies to lighting watts. A federal energy agency page gives the matching energy figure, 3,412 Btu per kilowatt-hour, which is the same number for a full hour at 1,000 watts. So a 500 watt computer is about 1,700 Btu/h of heat, whether it is idle or drawing hard. In practice the computer, a projector, the lighting, a launch or swing monitor and any display add up to several hundred watts per bay. Read nameplates or measure with a clamp meter rather than guessing, because a gaming-class computer draws far more than an office one. Our Esports and Gaming Lounge HVAC lesson covers computer heat in more depth, so we do not repeat it here.

A projector also needs its own cooling air. Its intake and exhaust are small, so a bay with the projector tucked in a closed ceiling box can run hot even when the room reads fine at the thermostat. Check the equipment instructions for clearances and make sure supply air does not blow straight into the lens path.

Dark rooms and closed curtains

A dark room is not a cool room. With the curtains closed there is almost no sun load, but there is also no daylight to tell a customer that the air feels stuffy. Two things follow for us. First, the sensible heat in the bay is almost all from equipment and players, which makes the load predictable and the calculation below easy. Second, closed curtains and a tight enclosure can block the return path. If the bay has a supply with no return or transfer opening, the room pressurizes, the door whistles, and airflow drops. We plan a return grille or a transfer opening that stays open when the curtain closes. Keep any opening away from the screen face, since moving air can ripple the impact screen.

Bursts of activity

People in a bay do not work at a steady rate. A player takes a few swings, waits, adjusts, drinks, talks. Heat from a person goes up sharply during a swing and settles when they sit. Designers typically size on the group present at the busy hour and accept that the peak moves around the room. For batting cages the activity level is higher and the group is smaller, so each person contributes more heat than a person on a sofa. Our human body heat exchange lesson explains the sensible and latent split for people, and a related play-center lesson in the same family, Indoor Climbing Gym HVAC, shows how active crowds change humidity. A hard-swinging group also adds moisture, so a bay that feels clammy needs the latent side checked too, which this sensible-only example does not cover.

Bar and food areas

Many venues pair the bays with a bar and a small kitchen or snack counter. The bar area has its own people load, plus refrigeration, warmers and sometimes a fryer. Any cooking appliance with a hood needs makeup air, and that air should not be dumped into the nearest bay. A bar that runs warm and noisy next to a quiet bay row will pull complaints in both directions, so we separate the bar from the bays on controls, and we keep kitchen exhaust and makeup air on their own system. The bowling center lesson linked above covers kitchen makeup air for a family venue.

Net and turf dust

Hitting nets, turf mats and curtains shed fibers and rubber crumb, and a swing stirs it up. That dust lands on filters, on projector vents and on computer fans. We suggest a washable or easily changed prefilter on the bay return, a filter check on a short interval, and a clear route for service access to the projector box. Dust loading cuts airflow before the unit seems dirty, and cooler air comes off a coil that has less flow across it. We make a point of telling the owner to keep nets and turf clean as part of the air quality plan, since an HVAC tech cannot fix dust that the room keeps producing.

Tall bays and stratification

Batting cages and golf bays are often tall so a ball can fly. Warm air rises, so in a tall bay the air near the ceiling runs much warmer than the air at the player's height, and the equipment and the projector sit near the warm layer. Mixing the layers with a slow ceiling fan can help in a large cage hall; our HVLS fans and destratification lesson covers the idea and the sprinkler considerations. In an enclosed bay with a net, a fan has to clear the swing path and the ball path, so we usually leave fans out of the bay itself and place supply low on a side wall or near the ceiling aimed along the wall, not at the screen.

Zone each bay separately

A bay is a small room with a large and variable load: empty at one moment, six people and a hot computer the next. If one thermostat serves a row of bays, the empty bays overcool, the busy one overheats, and the person at the far end complains. We prefer one temperature control per bay, or at least one control per group of similar bays, with the bar and kitchen on their own zone. Options include small variable-flow boxes on a central system or a ductless unit per bay. Our forced air zoning lesson explains dampers and bypass, which matter because a damper that closes down on a bay still needs somewhere for its air to go.

Worked example (our own): one bay, sensible load and supply cfm

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not measured data from a real venue. Assume an interior bay with no outside wall, no sun load and no air leaking in. Assume these inputs:

  • Projector: 300 W (our assumption).
  • Computer and monitor: 500 W (our assumption).
  • Lights and swing monitor: 150 W (our assumption).
  • Players: 4 in the bay at the busy hour, with a mix of swinging and sitting, assumed to average 450 Btu/h sensible each (our assumption, not a table value).
  • Supply temperature difference: 20 degrees F below room temperature (our assumption).

Step 1, equipment watts. 300 + 500 + 150 = 950 W.

Step 2, convert to Btu/h. 950 W x 3.41 Btu/h per W = 3,240 Btu/h (3,239.5 rounded).

Step 3, people. 4 players x 450 Btu/h = 1,800 Btu/h.

Step 4, total sensible load. 3,240 + 1,800 = 5,040 Btu/h, about 0.42 ton of sensible cooling.

Step 5, supply airflow. The standard sensible relation is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference. The 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb per cubic foot of air, 60 minutes per hour and 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F. Solve for cfm: 5,040 / (1.08 x 20) = 5,040 / 21.6 = 233 cfm, so we would plan about 235 cfm.

What changes the answer. At an 18 degree difference the same bay needs 5,040 / (1.08 x 18) = 259 cfm. Adding two more players at 450 Btu/h each lifts the load to 5,940 Btu/h and the airflow at 20 degrees to 275 cfm. A bay with latent load, door leakage or an outside wall needs more work than this. Our figure also leaves out fresh air, which should come from the ventilation requirements the local code adopts, not from this calculation.

Field card: Indoor Golf Simulator and Batting Cage Venue HVAC

Bays are interior rooms: the load is equipment and people, so zone each bay.

  • Electric watts become heat: 3.41 Btu/h per watt.
  • Supply cfm = sensible Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature difference).
  • Our own example: 950 W + 4 players = 5,040 Btu/h, about 235 cfm at 20 degrees F.
  • Dark rooms still need a return or transfer path that stays open.
  • Keep supply air off the screen face and out of the projector lens path.
  • Check filters and projector vents often where nets and turf shed dust.
  • Keep kitchen exhaust and makeup air separate from the bays.
