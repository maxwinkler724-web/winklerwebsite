Oil Pressure Safety Controls on Semi-Hermetic Compressors: Net Oil Pressure, Time-Delay Switches, Electronic Sensors and Why They Trip

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A semi-hermetic compressor has a pump that sends oil to the bearings, and a small control that shuts the machine down if that pump stops doing its job. This lesson covers what the control measures, how the common styles work, why a manual reset matters, and how to check one.

Check the unit's own literature

Check the unit's own literature. Settings and wiring differ by compressor and control. The numbers below come from maker documents we read and are examples, not settings for your machine. Use the data for the exact compressor and control you are working on.

Net oil pressure, and why one gauge tells you nothing

The oil pump inlet sits in the crankcase, so the pump pushes against whatever pressure the crankcase already has. What the bearings feel is the difference. A trade-press article on oil safety controllers defines net oil pressure as oil pump discharge pressure minus crankcase pressure. A maker application guide says the same thing in practice: read it by putting one gauge on the pump and a second on the crankcase (or suction service valve) and comparing the two.

That is why a lone oil gauge reading is not enough. As our own illustration, a pump outlet reading of 95 psig sounds healthy until the crankcase is at 90 psig. A reading of 40 psig sounds low until the crankcase is at 10 psig. The same crankcase pressure also moves around during a start, so the reading to trust is the subtraction.

The mechanical differential switch

The classic control is a bellows switch with two pressure connections. Oil pump outlet pressure pushes the switch one way and crankcase pressure pushes it the other, as in the diagram. According to the trade-press article, net pressure has to reach about 9 psi or the contacts stay closed. With the compressor off, net pressure is zero, so the contacts are closed at rest.

The contacts feed a small heater, wired from the line side of the starter so it is dead while the compressor is off. At start the heater is on while pressure builds. If net pressure comes up past the cut-in, the heater shuts off and nothing happens. If it does not, the heater warps a bimetal strip and the timing contacts in series with the contactor coil open. The delay is nominally 120 seconds in a maker bulletin for this type of control, and the same bulletin notes it shifts with line voltage. That pause is deliberate: it lets pressure build at start and rides out short dips, such as a start with refrigerant in the oil.

Electronic sensors

Electronic styles replace the bellows with a pressure sensor at the oil pump and a small control unit with a relay contact. A maker bulletin for one such control gives a trip delay of 120 seconds, plus or minus 15, a cut-out of 7 to 9 psid and a cut-in of 12 to 14 psid. The net pressure can be checked at a service valve on the pump discharge. The bulletin also warns that an inexpensive timer wired in series with the contactor can leak enough current to keep the unit energized and cause a false trip. Electronic controls may also need a field-wired relay so they have power to reset after a trip, so follow the wiring diagram for that model.

The manual reset, and why repeated resets kill compressors

These controls lock out and wait for a person. The mechanical bulletin says the control must be reset by hand, and that repeated trips call for immediate corrective action and should never be accepted as normal operation. The trade-press article puts it plainly: resetting does not solve the problem. A trip means the bearings went about two minutes with too little oil. Every reset is another start on a compressor that may be losing bearing surface. Bypassing the control to stop the nuisance removes the only protection the bearings have. One maker states that proper oil pressure safety control with an approved switch is a condition of its warranty.

Common causes of trips

  • Low oil level. The maker bulletin lists a shortage of oil, or oil logged in the evaporator by low refrigerant velocity. See oil separators and oil management.
  • Refrigerant dilution and foaming at start. One application guide explains that the fast pressure drop at start boils refrigerant out of the oil, which foams it, and that it uses a suction-side oil bore and a crankcase vent valve to slow the pressure drop. It also says to turn the crankcase heater on 12 hours before starting and to limit starts to 10 to 12 per hour. See crankcase heaters and compressor oils.
  • Floodback and migration. Liquid in the crankcase is on the maker's list of causes. See floodback and slugging.
  • Short cycling. Each start repeats the foaming risk, which is why the guide limits starts per hour.
  • Oil pump failure. The bulletin names it directly. A worn pump gives low net pressure that does not improve as the machine warms.
  • Restricted pickup screen. Our own shop reasoning, not a figure from the sources: a plugged screen starves the pump inlet and looks like a worn pump. Compare the gauges before condemning the pump.
  • Shortage of refrigerant or poor metering. The bulletin also lists a refrigerant shortage and improper refrigerant control devices.

One trip that is not about oil: if the motor's internal protector opens while the contactor stays closed, the heater runs with no pressure and trips the control in about two minutes. A current-sensing relay prevents that, per the trade-press article. A valve problem can be separate; see testing compressor valves.

Worked example: reading net oil pressure (our own numbers)

These readings are our own and invented for teaching, taken with the compressor running steadily. The oil pump outlet gauge reads 95 psig and the crankcase gauge reads 40 psig.

  1. Net oil pressure = 95 - 40 = 55 psi.
  2. Compare to the cut-out. The maker bulletin gives a cut-out of 9 psig, plus or minus 2, so the control is far from tripping: 55 - 9 = 46 psi of margin.
  3. Compare to normal. One application guide gives a normal range of 1.05 to 4.2 bar above crankcase pressure. Converting at about 14.5 psi per bar, that is roughly 15 to 61 psi. Our 55 psi sits inside it, toward the top.

Now a foaming start. These numbers are our own assumptions. Twenty seconds after start, the pump outlet gauge reads 58 psig and the crankcase has dropped only to 50 psig, because oil is foamy and the pump is pulling foam. Net = 58 - 50 = 8 psi, below the 9 psi cut-out, so the heater is on and the timer is running. Then the foam clears. At 60 seconds the readings are 88 psig and 38 psig, a net of 50 psi, above the cut-in of 14 psig the maker lists, so the heater shuts off and no trip occurs. If the net had stayed near 8 psi for 120 seconds, the control would trip and lock out. That is the control doing its job. The fix is to find out why the oil is foaming (heater, migration, short cycling), not to reset again.

How to test the control

  • Gauge check. Put gauges on the pump and crankcase, subtract, and compare to the maker's range.
  • Timer check (mechanical). The maker bulletin says to jumper the timer terminals if accessible, start the compressor, and confirm it trips in about 120 seconds.
  • Pressure check (mechanical). With the system pumped down, leave the low side at 0 psig and apply 20 to 60 psig to the high-pressure connection. The contacts should close near 9 psig and reopen at 12 to 14 psig.
  • Electronic control. Per the maker bulletin, with the sensor unplugged the contact should open after 120 plus or minus 15 seconds, and it should not trip with the sensor connections jumpered.

If it fails either check, replace it. Lock out and tag power before touching wiring.

Field card: Oil Pressure Safety Controls

Net, not gauge. Find the cause, then reset.

  • Net oil pressure = pump outlet minus crankcase pressure.
  • Example maker cut-out is about 9 psi net, with a 120 second delay.
  • Manual reset: never accept repeated trips as normal.
  • Check oil level, dilution and foaming, floodback, short cycling, pump and screen.
  • Test with the gauges first, then the timer and pressure checks in the maker data.
