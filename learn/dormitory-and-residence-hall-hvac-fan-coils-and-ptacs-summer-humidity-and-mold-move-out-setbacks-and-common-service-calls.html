Dorm HVAC and Mold: Fan Coils, PTACs, Summer Humidity and Move-Out Setbacks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A residence hall is full for nine months and half empty for three, and the mold calls come when the building is empty or the setpoints are far off. This lesson covers the moisture side of student-room equipment, with one worked example of the water an empty wing takes in.

Check the building first

Check the building first. This page is general education built from public guidance read in 2026. Setpoints and shutdown steps belong to the owner's facilities group and the maker's manual.

The equipment, briefly

Student rooms use two terminal units. A fan coil carries hot or chilled water from a central plant; see Fan Coil Units. A PTAC is a self-contained box in a wall sleeve; see PTAC Units. Closet units and corridor makeup air are in VTAC and Hotel Guest Room HVAC. We will not repeat how they work, only where the water comes from.

Why an empty wing gets wet

A cooling coil only dries air while it runs. In an empty room nobody makes heat or moisture, so a unit with a high setpoint barely runs, and leaking outdoor air brings its moisture in. DOE's Building America work says lowering the thermostat to remove moisture "is not always effective," and that a standard air conditioner must cool the air to remove water, so added dehumidification is typically needed when moisture must come out but little cooling is wanted. That is an empty wing in late summer.

The reverse mistake also happens. A Massachusetts state agency page on preventing mold in hot, humid weather warns that systems set for occupied rooms during long vacancies can chill building parts below the dew point, and says to raise the setpoint until materials stay above it. Its examples: air at 76 F and 30 percent RH has a dew point near 43 F, but air at 85 F and 90 percent RH has a dew point near 81 F, so almost any cooled surface sweats. A setback can fail by being so warm the coil stops drying, or so cold that chilled pipes and cabinets sweat.

The number to hold

EPA's brief mold guide says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, with a hygrometer costing about $10 to $50. It also says to dry water-damaged materials within 24 to 48 hours, use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed, and keep drip pans clean with drain lines flowing. For growth that has already started, see Mold on the AC Coil or in Ducts.

Corridors and bathrooms

Dorm corridors are usually the supply path and bathrooms the exhaust path: conditioned makeup air enters the corridor, flows under the door, and leaves through the bath fan. If the corridor unit is off or not dehumidifying, that path brings in untreated humid air; if the bath fan is off, steam stays in the suite. Our own advice: diagnose the makeup unit, bath fan and room unit as one system.

Student-facing pages agree. One state university says always run the exhaust fan when showering; another says to keep the shower door closed and run the fan 10 to 15 minutes afterward. The Massachusetts page says to keep restroom and shower exhaust running in humid weather, open interior doors, and use floor fans in halls.

Tampering and open windows

Many moisture calls trace back to a person. The residence-hall pages we read ask students to:

  • Keep windows closed while cooling. One page says to do so when outdoor humidity is above 65 percent; another says not to open windows during cooling or heating season.
  • Set the fan to auto or continuous, and keep the thermostat within the posted range.
  • Not tamper with the unit; call housing or facilities.
  • Keep belongings at least 12 inches from a fan coil, with no plants on it, and not block vents.

Our own view for techs: a wet coil, a tripped float switch and a student who set 60 F with the window open are three causes with one symptom. Ask first.

Worked example: what an empty wing takes in

These inputs are our own assumptions, not measurements. A wing of 40 empty rooms is closed for the summer. Assume 600 cfm of outdoor air leaks in through the envelope, an unthrottled corridor unit and bath-exhaust suction. Assume outdoor air at 85 F and 70 percent RH and a target of 75 F and 50 percent RH. Humidity ratio W is pounds of water per pound of dry air; our own sea-level calculation gives about 0.0181 outdoors and 0.0092 indoors.

The infiltration latent formula is Q = 4,840 × cfm × (W outdoor − W indoor), in Btu/h. A continuing-education engineering text by A. Bhatia (plain-text citation below) gives it. Note that 4,840 gives heat, not pounds of water. For pounds per hour use 4.5. Our own derivation: 60 minutes times 0.075 lb/ft³ standard air density is 4.5, and 4,840 ÷ 4.5 is about 1,076 Btu per pound, the heat of vaporization of water.

  1. ΔW = 0.0181 − 0.0092 = 0.0089.
  2. Water: 4.5 × 600 × 0.0089 = 24.0 lb/h.
  3. Latent load: 4,840 × 600 × 0.0089 = 25,846 Btu/h, about 2.2 tons (÷ 12,000).
  4. Per day: 24.0 × 24 = 577 lb, which at 8.34 lb/gal is about 69 gal, or 553 pints a day.

To hold only 60 percent (W about 0.0111), ΔW = 0.0070 and the water is 4.5 × 600 × 0.0070 = 18.9 lb/h. That is far beyond a few portable units, so a wing like this needs central dehumidification, a sealed envelope, or both. The free first step is to close the outdoor-air path: halving the 600 cfm halves the water.

Move-out and turnover inspection

One university facilities page says staff inspect furniture, walls, closets and fan coils and measure temperature and humidity. Our own room checklist:

CheckWhat to look for
FilterDirty, wet or missing.
Condensate pan and drainStanding water, slime, a clog, pitch toward the drain. EPA says to keep pans clean and drains flowing. See Secondary Drain Pans and Condensate Float Switches.
Coil and cabinetVisible growth, stains, damp insulation, wet fins. Do not run a unit you suspect is contaminated; see the mold lesson linked above.
Wall and windowStains on the wall below the unit, damp closet backs, gaps in seals, sleeve seals on a PTAC.
Bath fanSpins, moves air, and is not taped off or disconnected.
Humidity readingRead RH and note it. Above 60 percent means find the source.

Then set the empty-room state your owner specifies: a setpoint that holds RH below the limit and keeps surfaces above the dew point, interior doors open, and bath fans on any schedule the owner has.

Field card: Dorm HVAC and Mold

Dry the air, keep surfaces above the dew point, check the drain.

  • EPA: keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.
  • A cooling coil dries air only while it runs; an empty wing needs dehumidification, not just a high setpoint.
  • Windows closed in cooling season; fan on auto or continuous; 12 inches clear around a fan coil.
  • Run bath and restroom exhaust in humid weather; check that corridor makeup air is treated.
  • Moisture lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW. Latent Btu/h = 4,840 × cfm × ΔW.
