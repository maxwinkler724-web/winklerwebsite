Temporary Heat on Construction Sites: Portable Heaters, CO and Ventilation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before the permanent furnace is running, somebody has to keep the building warm enough for drywall mud, concrete and people. The cheapest way, a direct-fired propane heater, dumps every pound of exhaust into the room. This lesson covers the heater types, the OSHA rules, the CO numbers, and how much water one big heater adds to the air.

Check the manual and the rule

Check the manual and the rule. Every heater has its own listing, clearances and fresh-air figure on the data plate and in the manual. OSHA rules and exposure limits can change. This page was built from sources read in 2026 and is general education, not a site safety plan.

The four ways to heat a job

Direct-fired. A burner blows its flame products straight into the airstream. Rental-company pages describe these as the common "torpedo" or forced-air construction heater, nearly all the fuel becomes room heat, and the exhaust, including carbon monoxide and water vapor, goes into the space. They cannot be used with ductwork and need large, well-ventilated areas (Herc Rentals; United Rentals).

Indirect-fired. Combustion happens inside a heat exchanger. The fumes leave through a flue or chimney, and only clean heated air enters the building. The same rental pages say this keeps CO out of the heated air, adds no moisture, and allows ducting into tighter spaces. It gives up some efficiency.

Electric. No fuel burns in the room, so there is no combustion exhaust to manage. Follow the heater's own rating plate for clearances and power supply.

The building's own new system. Makers are cautious about this. Lennox's installation manual for a condensing gas furnace says the company does not recommend using it as a construction heater during any phase of construction. It allows use for heating a structure under construction only if filters are installed and kept up and replaced at completion, 100 percent outdoor air is supplied for combustion, and return air stays between 60 and 80 degrees F. It warns that very low return air temperatures, harmful vapors and clogged filters will damage the unit, and says the furnace, duct system, filters and coil must be thoroughly cleaned after final construction clean-up. The same manual says to protect the condensate drain from freezing. Other makers may word it differently or tie it to warranty terms, so read the manual for the model in your hands.

What OSHA says about temporary heaters

OSHA 1926.154 is the construction rule. Paraphrased from the posted text:

  • Supply enough fresh air to keep workers healthy and safe, and use mechanical ventilation when natural airflow is not enough. In confined spaces, ventilate enough for proper combustion and worker health.
  • Keep heaters away from combustibles by the Table F-4 distances (for radiant room heaters, 36 inches at the sides and rear), or by their approved clearances if smaller. Keep them at least 10 feet from combustible tarps and canvas, and fasten those coverings so wind cannot move them.
  • Set a heater level, and do not set one that is unsuited for wood floors directly on a wood floor.
  • Solid fuel salamanders are not allowed in buildings or on scaffolds.
  • Oil-fired heaters need a primary safety control that stops fuel flow if the flame goes out.

Heater makers write the same ideas in numbers. The DESA construction heater manual (the Reddy line) asks for at least a three-square-foot opening of fresh outside air for each 100,000 Btu/hr, with more air if you run several units. It also gives 8 feet from the outlet, 2 feet from the sides and rear and 6 feet from the top to combustibles. The Mr. Heater contractor manual asks for openings near the floor and near the ceiling, 2 square feet each for its 125,000 Btu/hr model. Both say the heaters are for temporary heating of buildings under construction, alteration or repair, and not for homes. They also say never to use them where dust, solvents or other flammable vapors may be present.

Propane rules in 1926.153

OSHA allows LP-gas cylinders inside a building only when portable use is necessary and an outdoor location is impracticable. Storage of LP-gas inside buildings is prohibited, so spare cylinders wait outside. The heater must be at least 6 feet from any container and must not blow at a container within 20 feet. Portable heaters need automatic shutoff on flame failure, and heaters above 50,000 Btu/hr need a pilot or electric ignition. Maker manuals add cylinder habits: vapor withdrawal only, use the supplied regulator and hose, inspect the hose each use, check for leaks with soapy water and never a flame. For how a tank makes vapor in cold weather, see the propane tank lesson linked below.

The CO numbers

CO is the reason ventilation matters. The NIOSH pocket guide lists the OSHA permissible exposure limit (PEL) at 50 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA). NIOSH's own recommended exposure limit (REL) is stricter: 35 ppm TWA with a 200 ppm ceiling that should never be exceeded. The guide also lists 1,200 ppm as the level considered immediately dangerous to life or health. An L.B. White manual for its kerosene heaters says CO tests should be made within one hour of the start of each shift and at least every four hours after, more often if conditions call for it. For alarms and symptoms, see the CO alarm and combustion lessons linked below.

Worked example: a 400,000 Btu/hr direct-fired propane heater

Invented job: a 400,000 Btu/hr input heater runs for one hour in a framed building. Heat content comes from the Propane Education and Research Council pocket guide: 91,502 Btu per gallon, 4.2 lb per gallon of liquid at 60 degrees F.

  1. Fuel per hour: 400,000 ÷ 91,502 = 4.37 gallons of propane per hour.
  2. Weight burned: 4.37 × 4.2 = 18.4 lb of propane per hour.
  3. Water from the reaction: burning propane (C3H8 + 5 O2 gives 3 CO2 + 4 H2O) turns 44.1 lb of propane into 72.1 lb of water, a ratio of 1.63. This ratio is our own chemistry arithmetic, not a figure from a manual.
  4. Water per hour: 18.4 × 1.63 = 30.0 lb. At 8.34 lb per gallon, that is 30.0 ÷ 8.34 = 3.6 gallons of water per hour.
  5. Per gallon of propane: 3.6 ÷ 4.37 = 0.82 gallon of water, which fits the rule of thumb of 0.8 to 1 gallon of water per gallon of propane. A secondary web page, citing a 2017 industry source, reports about 1 gallon per hour per 100,000 Btu/hr of heater, or roughly 4 gallons per hour here, so 3.6 is in the right range.
  6. A fresh-air check using the DESA figure: 3 sq ft × (400,000 ÷ 100,000) = 12 sq ft of opening. Use your own heater's manual, not this number.

So one shift of 10 hours puts roughly 36 gallons of water into the building air. That is why direct-fired heat on fresh drywall, flooring or curing concrete can lead to condensation on cold glass and slow drying.

CO reading check. A badge or meter shows an average of 20 ppm over 8 hours.

  1. Against the OSHA PEL: 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40, or 40 percent of the limit. Under it.
  2. Against the NIOSH REL: 20 ÷ 35 = 0.57, or 57 percent. Also under it.
  3. Check peaks: an average hides spikes. The REL ceiling is 200 ppm, so any reading above 200 ppm on the meter is a stop-work and ventilate event even if the average is 20.

Passing does not mean the setup is good. An average of 20 ppm means the fresh-air supply was probably working, but headaches, dizziness or nausea mean getting to fresh air first, then fixing ventilation.

Where this fits for a service tech

You may be asked to start a permanent furnace early. Read the installation manual first, because the maker may require filters, outdoor combustion air, warm return air and a full clean-out afterward. For vented gas heat in shops and warehouses, see the infrared tube heater lesson linked below.

Field card: Temporary Heat and CO

Fresh air in, no flame near stuff, propane outside.

  • Direct-fired: exhaust, CO and water go into the room. Indirect-fired: flue outside.
  • OSHA 1926.154: enough fresh air, listed clearances, tarps 10 ft away, level setup.
  • DESA manual: 3 sq ft fresh air per 100,000 Btu/hr, check your heater's own plate.
  • OSHA 1926.153: no LP-gas storage inside; heater 6 ft from containers.
  • CO: OSHA PEL 50 ppm TWA; NIOSH REL 35 ppm TWA, ceiling 200 ppm.
  • Propane: 91,502 Btu/gal; about 0.8 gal of water per gal burned.
  • Do not run the permanent furnace as a construction heater unless its manual allows it.
