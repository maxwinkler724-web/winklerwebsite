Oil in Refrigerant Recovery: Measuring, Replacing and Disposing of Compressor Oil

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Oil is the part of recovery that nobody plans for. The refrigerant comes out, and a little compressor oil comes with it. That oil ends up in the recovery machine, in the cylinder, or on your hands. This lesson covers where it goes, how to measure it, what to put back, and how the federal rules and exam questions treat it.

Why oil travels with refrigerant

A compressor is lubricated by oil, and some of that oil always leaves with the discharge gas. University research on vapor compression systems says a small portion of the oil circulates with the refrigerant through the cycle components, and each component holds a different amount of it (see the university thesis abstract in the Sources). Another university paper defines the oil circulation ratio as the mass flow of oil divided by the total mass flow of the oil and refrigerant mixture, and notes that no oil separator is perfect, so some oil always escapes with the separated refrigerant.

The practical result for a tech: when you pull refrigerant out, you are also pulling out oil that was dissolved in liquid refrigerant, clinging to coil walls, or sitting in low spots. The federal definitions page says recycling equipment generally uses oil separation and filter-driers that reduce moisture, acidity and particulates. That is why recovery machines have an oil separator, and why that separator needs draining.

Recover, recycle, reclaim: where oil fits

The federal definitions page describes recovering as removing refrigerant from an appliance in any condition and storing it in an outside container, with no testing or processing required. Recycling cleans it for reuse in the same owner's equipment. Reclaiming reprocesses it to a published purity standard, verified by analysis, and it needs specialized equipment that is not available at a job site. Oil is mentioned only under recycling. The reclaimer fact sheet adds that reclaimers must analyze each batch and must dispose of process wastes such as oils under all applicable laws.

For the exam, hold on to this: refrigerant recovered into a cylinder can go back into the same owner's systems, but if it changes ownership it must go through an agency-certified reclaimer. That rule is on the service practice page. Oil in the cylinder is one reason a reclaimer, not a tech, is the one who can certify purity. We cover the reuse rules in detail in the linked lesson on reusing recovered refrigerant.

Measuring the oil you remove

  • Use a graduated container. Drain oil from the recovery machine separator and from the compressor into a clean, marked cup or measuring bottle, and read the level on a flat surface.
  • Keep sources separate. Write down oil from the compressor and oil from the machine separator on separate lines, so the log says where each amount came from.
  • Let the refrigerant outgas. Oil that has touched refrigerant holds dissolved refrigerant. A college compliance plan says most refrigerant oil exposed to a system or recovery process still holds significant dissolved refrigerant, so keep it in a closed container and do not let it foam into a pail.
  • Mind the pressure. The service practice page says it is a violation to change oil at higher than 5 psig. Reduce the pressure to no more than 5 psig first, or drain the oil into a system receiver that is already at or below that pressure.
  • Record it. The college plan says service records should note that oil was recovered and the amount and disposal method.

Adding back the same amount and type

Our rule of thumb: whatever oil left the system should be replaced with the same kind of oil in about the same volume, once the repair is done and the system is evacuated. This is our own guidance, so follow the equipment maker's instruction if it names a different amount. Check the oil type on the compressor nameplate or in the service data; the lesson on compressor oils explains why mixing mineral, alkylbenzene and polyolester oils is a problem. Then confirm the level in the sight glass with the lesson on checking crankcase oil level. Do not pour used oil back in just to save a trip. Oil that sat in a separator is contaminated with refrigerant, moisture and possibly acid.

Oil after a burnout

After a burnout the oil is acidic and carries carbon and varnish. The college plan says acids remain in the oil left in the system after a burnout and calls for a suction line filter-drier to help remove them, with all driers replaced. Acid tests, drier choice and cleanup order are in the burnout cleanup lesson, and we do not repeat them here. Two exam-level points: treat oil from a burned system as contaminated, and do not mix it back with clean refrigerant or reuse it. Wear gloves and eye protection, because acidic oil is a skin and eye hazard.

Oil disposal

Refrigerant oil that has been in a system is not ordinary shop waste. The college plan says do not mix refrigerant lubricants with other wastes, and says oil holding more than 4,000 ppm dissolved refrigerant or certain contaminants can be a hazardous waste unless it is headed for reclamation; the disposal contractor handles testing and the shop keeps records of the oil source and its final disposal. The federal used oil page adds that used oil with more than 1,000 ppm total halogens is presumed to have been mixed with regulated halogenated hazardous waste, and that the safest approach is not to mix used oil with anything. Rules vary by state and city, so ask your local waste authority. This page is not legal advice.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all invented for teaching: a service tech recovers refrigerant from a split system. The cylinder gains 20.0 lb on the scale. We assume 1.5 percent of that weight is oil. The compressor drain gives 6.0 fl oz and the machine separator holds 1.5 fl oz. The oil weighs 0.90 g per mL (an assumed density). Real oil fractions vary widely, so this is not a typical figure.

Step 1, oil to add back (volume). 6.0 + 1.5 = 7.5 fl oz removed. Add back 7.5 fl oz of the same oil type. (Check: 7.5 fl oz x 29.57 mL per fl oz = 222 mL.)

Step 2, oil riding in the cylinder (weight). 20.0 lb x 0.015 = 0.30 lb of oil. Refrigerant in the cylinder = 20.0 - 0.30 = 19.70 lb.

Step 3, why it matters. If you charge back using the gross scale gain of 20.0 lb, you overcharge by 0.30 lb (about 1.5 percent). Charging the corrected 19.70 lb avoids it. In practice most techs charge to superheat, subcooling or nameplate weight rather than by scale gain.

Step 4, the cylinder oil as volume. 0.30 lb x 453.6 g per lb = 136 g. 136 g / 0.90 g per mL = 151 mL. 151 / 29.57 = 5.1 fl oz. That oil is not in your drain cup, so it is not part of the 7.5 fl oz in Step 1, and the reclaimer, not you, deals with it if the cylinder goes out for reclaim.

How exam questions word it

These are our paraphrases of common patterns, not real exam items.

  • "Before changing oil in a system, the pressure must be reduced to no more than ___." Answer pattern: 5 psig.
  • "Refrigerant that is sold or moves to a new owner must be ___." Answer pattern: reclaimed by a certified reclaimer.
  • "Which process uses oil separation and filter-driers for reuse in the same owner's equipment?" Answer pattern: recycling.
  • "After a burnout, oil should be ___." Answer pattern: treated as contaminated, not reused.
  • "Recovery removes refrigerant in any condition without testing." Answer pattern: true.

Watch the key words: recover, recycle and reclaim are three different words with three different meanings.

Field card: Oil in Refrigerant Recovery: Measuring, Replacing and Disposing of Compressor Oil

Measure it, log it, replace the same type, dispose of the rest.

  • Oil leaves with the refrigerant; separators catch much but not all.
  • Oil changes only at 5 psig or less.
  • Drain the machine separator to avoid contaminating refrigerant.
  • Replace the same oil type and about the same volume.
  • Burnout oil is acidic: do not reuse it.
  • Do not mix refrigerant oil with other wastes; keep disposal records.
  • Change of ownership means reclaim.
