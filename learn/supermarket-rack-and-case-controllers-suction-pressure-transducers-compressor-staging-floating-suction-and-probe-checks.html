Supermarket Rack and Case Controllers: Suction Transducers, Staging, Floating Suction and Probe Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A supermarket rack does not run on pressure switches anymore. A controller reads a suction pressure transducer, decides how many compressors to run, and lets each case controller report what the cases need. When a transducer is a little off, the whole rack hunts, overcools or runs hot. This lesson covers the sensors, the staging logic, floating suction and the field checks. The rack hardware is in Supermarket Refrigeration Racks.

Check the maker's literature

Check the maker's literature. Wiring colors, voltage ranges, default settings and failure actions differ by maker and model. The figures below are examples from the documents we read, not values for your controller. Use the data plate and the manual for the unit in front of you.

The transducer: what it sends

A pressure transducer turns pressure into a voltage or a current. Two kinds show up on racks.

  • Ratiometric voltage. A maker's datasheet for a refrigeration pressure transmitter says its output is a fixed share of the supply voltage: the minimum is under 10 percent of supply and the maximum is over 90 percent. At a 5 V supply that gives 0.5 V at the bottom of the range and 4.5 V at the top. A maker's technical bulletin lists the supply for one such family as 5.0 V plus or minus 0.25 V. Because the output follows the supply, a sagging supply drags the signal down with it.
  • 4 to 20 mA current. The signal is a loop current, 4 mA at the bottom of the range and 20 mA at the top, so a broken wire (0 mA) is easy to tell from a real low reading. Scaling, loop supply and meter checks are in 4-20 mA and 0-10 V Signals in a BAS, so we will not repeat them. By our arithmetic, a 0 to 200 psig current transducer reads 12 mA at 100 psig.

For a ratiometric 0 to 200 psig transducer at 5 V, the pressure is P = (Vout - 0.5) / 4 x 200, which is the same as 50 psig for every volt above 0.5 V. A maker's controller manual prints this as a chart: 0.5 V is 0 psi, 1.5 V is 50, 2.5 V is 100 and 4.5 V is 200, with 0.2 V for every 10 psi.

The discharge transducer is the same kind of device on a higher range, wired the same way. It feeds head pressure control and high-pressure alarms, so the checks below apply to it too.

How the rack stages compressors

The rack controller compares suction pressure to a setpoint and adds or removes compressor capacity to hold it. One maker's suction-group manual describes a neutral zone: a band around the target where the controller makes no change. Outside the band, the controller acts, and as the error grows it moves pressure back toward target more quickly. The same manual advises against changing the default values without a thorough understanding of the algorithm. Another maker's manual describes a proportional mode that sets how strongly the output reacts to the error, with a throttling range that usually straddles the setpoint evenly on both sides. Our plain reading: inside the band nothing changes, so the setpoint is a target and not a hard line. Compressors also have minimum on and off times, so staging is slow by design.

Floating suction

A fixed suction setpoint must satisfy the coldest case all the time. With floating suction the controller raises the target when the cases are satisfied and lowers it when one needs more. A maker's white paper says the controller keeps readjusting the suction setpoint so it just meets the requirement of the case that needs the lowest pressure, which keeps suction as high as every case allows. A maker's suction-group manual describes adaptive suction that adjusts the target from case discharge-air temperature, with a limit on how far it may float and a delay after defrost during which the target is not adjusted. Those limits and delays are why a bad case probe can disturb the whole group.

What it saves. Numbers vary with refrigerant, climate and baseline, so treat them as a range.

  • A 2006 energy-efficiency conference paper says floating suction control gave an added 30,000 to 60,000 kWh of annual savings per supermarket in California experience. In a San Diego program the modeled average was 174 kWh per horsepower over 46 stores, with the average store at 175 hp. That paper also found about 40 percent of surveyed stores were not floating head and suction pressures at best efficiency, because of programming errors or missing components.
  • A slide deck from a Department of Energy building-technology event lists floating head and suction pressure together at 4 to 13 percent savings against transparent-door cases that meet current standards, and says benefits are greater in colder climates.
  • A maker's control-system paper gives a rule of thumb of about 2 percent less compressor power for each 1 psi increase in suction pressure, and savings of up to 8 percent with electronic evaporator pressure regulators. A maker's blog gives the same 2 percent per psi, says it only works if the regulators are set properly, and cites no study. Treat both as vendor estimates.
  • A regional power agency's 2022 slide deck gives example savings for refrigeration controls of 30,000 kWh for a small store and 200,000 kWh for a large one.

The California Energy Commission's 2019 commercial refrigeration compliance document says compressors and suction groups must use floating suction logic that resets the target saturated suction temperature from the display case or walk-in temperature setpoint, with listed exceptions such as single-compressor systems, groups designed at 30 F saturated suction or higher, and high-stage groups in two-stage or cascade systems. Floating head pressure is a separate strategy; see Floating Head Pressure Control.

Case controllers

A national laboratory and university conference paper on a booster system says each display case has its own controller that regulates expansion valve opening, air temperature, defrost, lighting and fans and communicates with the system controller. A maker's case-controller guide describes the two air probes: one in the warm return air before the evaporator, one in the cold discharge air after it. Parameters weight the two for thermostat, alarm and display. The same guide offers defrost stop methods by time or by temperature sensor, each with time as a safety limit, and offset adjustments for each sensor. For the expansion valve side see Electronic Expansion Valves.

Alarms. One maker's manual lists input alarms for a sensor that reads open (a severed wire or failed sensor) and one that reads shorted. Suction-group alarms include a high suction limit alarm. A maker's FAQ says a network should show communication quality of at least 80 percent, so a low value points at the network wiring and not the case.

Field checks

  1. Gauge first. With the system steady, put a gauge of known condition on the suction header and compare it with the controller reading.
  2. Supply voltage. Measure supply to common at the transducer. A maker's bulletin gives 5.0 V plus or minus 0.25 V for its family.
  3. Signal voltage. Measure signal to common at the transducer, then at the controller terminals. The two should match. Compute the expected voltage from the gauge reading and compare.
  4. Probes. Power off, disconnect the probe and read ohms. A maker's chart for its temperature sensor lists 11,883 ohms at 70 F and 9,299 ohms at 80 F; yours may differ, so use the chart for your probe type. Our rule: a very high reading is an open and near zero is a short. More in HVAC Thermistors and Temperature Sensors.

What a failed transducer does

This is our reasoning from the control logic. The maker manuals we read did not state it, so confirm the failure action in yours. A transducer reading falsely high looks like extra load, so the controller adds compressors and the real suction pressure falls. One reading falsely low looks like a satisfied rack, so compressors drop out and cases warm. An open or shorted transducer should raise an input alarm, but what the compressors do next depends on the controller's failure setting. Read that setting before you leave site.

Worked example (ours): 1.6 V on a 0 to 200 psig transducer

Assumptions, not field data: a ratiometric 0.5 to 4.5 V transducer, 0 to 200 psig, a 5.00 V supply, and 1.6 V measured between signal and common.

Step 1. Subtract the 0.5 V offset: 1.6 - 0.5 = 1.1 V. Step 2. The span is 4.0 V for 200 psig, so each volt is 50 psig: 1.1 x 50 = 55 psig. The maker's chart agrees: 1.5 V is 50 and 1.7 V is 60, so 1.6 V falls halfway at 55.

Compare to a gauge (assumed readings).

  • Gauge 55 psig, controller shows 61. The transducer and its wiring agree with the gauge, so the error is in the controller: a configured offset or scaling (range set wrong). Fix the controller entry. We would not replace the transducer.
  • Gauge 55 psig, controller shows 55. Everything agrees. Look elsewhere for the problem, such as setpoint or compressor lockouts.
  • Gauge 62 psig, 1.6 V at the transducer. A 62 psig pressure should give 0.5 + 62/50 = 1.74 V, so the output is 0.14 V low (7 psig). The supply is the first suspect. If supply is 5.00 V, the transducer is out of tolerance or the gauge is wrong; check the gauge, then replace the transducer.
  • 1.6 V at the transducer, 1.45 V at the controller terminals. The 0.15 V loss is in the wiring (a loose or corroded splice). Repair the run.

Supply sag. Suppose the supply is 4.75 V while the controller still assumes 5 V and the signal is 1.6 V. The signal share is 1.6 / 4.75 = 0.337. Subtract 0.10 and divide by 0.80: 0.237 / 0.80 = 0.296, times 200 = 59.2 psig. So a 5 percent sag moves the true reading from 55 to 59 psig, a good reason to check supply before condemning anything.

What an offset costs. Suppose a transducer reads 3 psi high (assumed). The controller holds its displayed value at target, so the real suction pressure sits 3 psi below where it should be and the rack gives up 3 psi of float. At the maker's rule of thumb of 2 percent per psi, that is about 6 percent of compressor power (an estimate, not a measurement).

Field card: Rack and case controller checks

Gauge, supply, signal, then controller.

  • Ratiometric 0.5 to 4.5 V at 5 V: P = (V - 0.5) / 4 x range.
  • 0 to 200 psig: 50 psig per volt above 0.5 V. 1.6 V is 55 psig.
  • Supply sag lowers the signal. Check supply to common first.
  • Compare the gauge, the voltage at the transducer, the voltage at the controller.
  • Neutral zone means no change inside the band; staging is slow on purpose.
  • Floating suction raises the target when cases are satisfied.
  • Open or short on a probe gives an input alarm. Check ohms, power off.
