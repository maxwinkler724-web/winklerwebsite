Insulating Home Hot Water, Heating and Cold Water Pipes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Walk through an older basement or crawl space and you may find bare pipes carrying hot water to the radiators or the kitchen, and cold pipes dripping onto the floor. Pipe insulation helps with both, but it is not a cure-all. It does not stop a pipe from freezing, and there are places where wrapping a pipe is a bad idea.

Check the sources

Check the sources. This page was built from federal energy department pages and university extension pages read in 2026. It is general education, not a design or an inspection. Read the label on the insulation you buy and the instructions for your boiler or water heater before you wrap anything near them.

Why bare pipe loses heat

A pipe warmer than the air around it gives heat to that air, all year, whether anyone wants the heat or not. A federal steam tip sheet says to insulate any surface over 120 F, and a federal solution-center guide applies the same threshold to hydronic piping and boiler surfaces. Hot water pipes in a basement or crawl space, and hydronic supply and return lines that run through spaces you do not mean to heat, are the usual candidates. A federal solution-center guide says insulating hydronic distribution and return pipes can cut their heat loss by as much as 90 percent, and the steam tip sheet says insulation typically cuts losses by about 90 percent. Both figures are best-case, for a good installation on bare pipe.

Heat lost in a basement is not always wasted. If the basement is lived in, or the floor above is cold, some of it is useful. The trouble is that it is uncontrolled. It keeps coming in spring and fall, and it does not follow the thermostat. Insulation pays most on long runs through unheated spaces.

Foam or fiberglass?

The federal solution-center guide lists several forms: closed-cell foam sleeves (polyethylene or neoprene, some with an adhesive strip), fiberglass or mineral wool sleeves with a plastic or foil vapor jacket, spiral wrap, and foam tape. It also gives a warning for heating systems: use insulation rated for high-temperature pipe, because other forms can melt. A federal homeowner page on hot water pipes says foam sleeves are the most common choice on electric water heaters.

  • Foam sleeves are easy to cut and slip on, and the closed cells resist moisture. Check the temperature printed on the package against the hottest water in the pipe, with margin.
  • Fiberglass or mineral wool tolerates more heat. The guide describes 1-inch-thick, heavy-density hydronic sleeves and says to wrap batt material loosely, because compressing it lowers its R-value.
  • Fittings and valves lose heat too. The steam tip sheet suggests removable jackets on valves and flanges so you can still reach them.

Seal seams and secure the wrap every one to two feet with tape, ties or wire, as the solution-center guide directs.

Clearances near boilers and flues

Both federal home pages we read give the same rule for a gas water heater: keep insulation at least 6 inches from the flue, and where a pipe runs within 8 inches of the flue, use fiberglass or mineral wool pipe wrap, not foam. The homeowner page specifies fiberglass at least 1 inch thick without a facing in that zone. Those numbers are for water heater piping. For a boiler, follow its installation manual, because the maker states the clearances to the vent and to hot surfaces. See Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters for what else shares that room.

Cold water lines that sweat

A cold pipe cools the air that touches it. When the pipe is colder than the dew point of that air, water collects on the surface, and in a humid basement it drips. That is our own plain-language explanation, and the federal guide agrees on the practical point: insulating cold water pipes protects against condensation and the water damage on floors that follows. The key is the jacket. Moist air can move through open-cell or unsealed wrap and reach the cold pipe, so on cold lines the guide calls for closed-cell foam or a sleeve with a plastic or foil vapor barrier, with the seams sealed (our own advice). A single gap lets moist air in and the sweating returns. Wet insulation also stops working well, so replace any wrap that has soaked through. Refrigerant suction lines sweat for the same reason; see Refrigerant Line Set Insulation, and for plant-size chilled water see Mechanical Pipe Insulation.

Freeze protection: insulation buys time, not safety

The federal guide says insulation can help prevent freezing in ventilated crawl spaces, uninsulated basements and under sinks on exterior walls, and it lists heat tape with a sensor and thermostat for pipes at risk. Our own reasoning is simple: insulation slows heat loss, but a pipe with no heat source still cools toward the air around it, so a long enough cold spell freezes it anyway. Wrap does not add heat. State extension pages add the habits that do: open cabinet doors so room air reaches pipes on exterior walls, let a faucet drip during a hard freeze, and use heat tape only as the maker directs. One university page says to keep the thermostat no lower than 55 F when you are away. Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame. For loops that have already frozen, see Frozen Hot-Water Heating Pipes and Baseboard.

When not to insulate

  • Near a flue or vent. Keep foam and any facing away from it, and hold the clearances above.
  • On boiler parts the maker leaves bare. Do not cover relief valves, gauges, controls, the vent connection or anything the manual says must stay exposed. The steam tip sheet adds that trap jackets must follow the trap maker's directions.
  • Radiators and baseboard. Their job is to give off heat. Insulate the supply and return runs, not the emitters.
  • Old wrap you cannot identify. A federal environmental agency lists hot water and steam pipe coverings among places asbestos may be found. Do not cut or sand old white or gray wrap; have it tested first.
  • Wet or leaking pipe. Fix the leak first. The steam tip sheet says to repair the moisture source before replacing wet insulation.

Worked example (our own): 40 feet of bare hydronic supply pipe

This is our own example, not a published result. The federal steam tip sheet gives a loss of 140 million Btu per year for each 100 feet of bare 1-inch horizontal steel line carrying 15 psig steam, with 75 F room air, no wind and 8,760 hours a year. We borrow that number and then scale it to hot water with an assumption, so treat the result as a rough estimate.

Assumptions (ours): 40 ft of bare 1-inch steel supply pipe; water at 160 F; basement at 65 F; the pipe is hot 4,000 hours per year; insulation cuts the loss by 90 percent (the figure both federal sources use); boiler efficiency 80 percent. We take saturated steam at 15 psig as about 250 F. We assume loss is proportional to the temperature difference, which is rough.

  1. Per foot per year from the table: 140 MMBtu / 100 ft = 1.4 MMBtu = 1,400,000 Btu per ft.
  2. Per foot per hour: 1,400,000 / 8,760 = about 160 Btu per hour per ft (steam at about 250 F, 75 F room, difference 175 F).
  3. Scale to our pipe, difference 160 - 65 = 95 F: 160 x 95 / 175 = about 87 Btu per hour per ft.
  4. Whole run: 87 x 40 ft = 3,480 Btu per hour.
  5. Per year: 3,480 x 4,000 h = 13,920,000 Btu, about 13.9 MMBtu lost.
  6. With insulation: 13.92 x 0.90 = 12.5 MMBtu per year kept in the water; 1.4 MMBtu still leaks.
  7. Fuel to make that heat: 12.5 / 0.80 = about 15.7 MMBtu, which is about 157 therms of gas (1 therm = 100,000 Btu).

Your pipe length, water temperature and hours will differ, so rerun the steps with your own numbers. Remember too that some of that heat warmed the basement rather than being lost outright.

Field card: Insulating Home Pipes

Wrap the long runs, seal the cold ones, keep clear of the flue.

  • Insulate hydronic and hot water pipes (surfaces over 120 F) that run through spaces you do not mean to heat.
  • Insulation can cut bare-pipe loss by about 90 percent in a good installation.
  • Gas water heater flue: wrap at least 6 in away; within 8 in use fiberglass or mineral wool, not foam.
  • Cold lines need closed-cell foam or a sealed vapor jacket; one gap brings the sweating back.
  • Insulation slows freezing but adds no heat. Use the other freeze habits too.
  • Do not wrap emitters, valves the maker leaves bare, or unknown old wrap.
  • Our example: 40 ft, 87 Btu/h/ft, 4,000 h = 13.9 MMBtu lost; 90 percent saved = 12.5 MMBtu.
