HVAC for Fiberglass and Boat Repair Shops: Styrene, Dust, Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A shop that lays up fiberglass or repairs boats is a ventilation job first and a comfort job second. Resin gives off vapor, sanding makes dust, the resin wants a steady warm room to cure, and every cubic foot of air you throw outside has to be replaced and heated in winter. This lesson walks through each piece and ends with a worked example of the winter makeup air load.

What is in the air

Styrene vapor. Polyester and vinyl ester resins are thinned with styrene, and some of it evaporates while you mix, roll, spray and while the part cures. A federal workplace-health pocket guide for chemicals lists styrene with a federal permissible limit of 100 ppm as an 8-hour average, a 200 ppm ceiling, and a recommended limit of 50 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 100 ppm short-term limit. It lists the flash point as 88 °F, and explosive limits of 0.9 percent (lower) and 6.8 percent (upper) in air. It names eye, nose and throat irritation, headache, dizziness, confusion and drowsiness among the symptoms. A federal safety agency page on styrene says the same kind of nervous system complaints and notes that people who make boats, tubs and showers are among those exposed.

What measurements looked like. A federal in-depth survey of a boat builder measured personal styrene exposure in open molding. The highest gun operator group averaged about 52 ppm (only two samples, so treat it as rough), and the gelcoat booth area averaged about 17 ppm. Closed molding areas, kept away from open molding, averaged roughly 2 to 3 ppm. Grinding and cutting samples in the same report ran roughly 4 to 7 ppm. We cite these to show a pattern, not to set a design number: how the work is done and where the air moves matters as much as fan size.

Fiberglass dust. Sanding and grinding cured laminate throws glass fibers and resin dust. The federal chemical pocket guide for fibrous glass dust lists eye, skin, nose and throat irritation and breathing difficulty, and it advises preventing skin and eye contact. That dust is also what loads filters and coils, covered below.

Resin cure needs steady warmth

A polyester resin maker's technical data sheet that we read says curing should not be carried out below 15 °C (59 °F), and that the unopened resin should be stored between 5 °C and 25 °C. Treat that as one maker's written range, not a rule for every product. For your shop, read the data sheet for the exact resin and catalyst on the bench and set the space to that range. The HVAC job is to hold that temperature at night and on weekends too, because a part left to cure in a cold shop can stay soft or tacky. Our own point: an exhaust fan that runs only while people work is easier to live with than one that pulls cold air through the shop all night.

Capture at the source: local exhaust, downdraft and crossdraft

Local exhaust catches vapor or dust near where it is made, before it mixes with the room. That takes far less air than diluting the whole room. Common shapes in a shop are a hood or slot at a layup bench, a gelcoat booth, a downdraft table for small sanding jobs, and a crossdraft setup where supply air enters on one side of the work and is exhausted on the other, so the worker stays on the clean side.

The federal boat-builder survey is a good reminder that these systems fail in plain ways. Smoke tests showed one push-pull system moving smoke away from the workers, while in another building the air was not moving toward the exhaust vent in many areas. In a third, supply louvers were damaged and some floor-level exhaust openings were blocked by boxes. The same report advised that rollers work on the supply side of the gun operator, that gun operators stay upwind, and that workers stiffening inside large hulls get more airflow. A federal controls database entry adds that engineering controls need regular maintenance to keep working, and it describes ventilated gelcoat booths and closed molding as effective in the plants studied. Before you trust any hood, do a smoke test and check that nothing blocks the path.

Flammable vapors: who decides

Styrene has a flash point near room temperature and explosive limits, so vapor from resin, solvents and catalyst can be a fire and explosion concern. Whether part of a shop is a classified (hazardous) area, how large it is, and what electrical equipment, motors, heaters and fans may be used there are decisions for a qualified designer and the adopted code, not for the HVAC tech or the shop owner. If an exhaust fan, motor or heater sits in or near that area, ask for the classification drawing before you quote or wire anything. See the related lessons on spray booths and drying ovens for how those rooms handle interlocks and purge airflow.

Winter makeup air

Every cubic foot per minute the shop exhausts has to come back in as makeup air. In winter that air arrives cold, and the sensible heat to bring it up to room temperature is Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference, with Q in Btu per hour and temperature in °F (see the heat transfer formulas lesson for where 1.08 comes from). Shops often feel this load more than the walls, because it is large while the exhaust runs. A heater of the right kind and in the right place is a design question: a burner or hot surface is an ignition source, so its type and location depend on the classified area decision above. The makeup air units lesson covers how those heaters are built and controlled. Bring makeup air in low-velocity and away from the work so it does not blow vapor out of the hood or across the worker's face.

Dust on filters and coils

Fiberglass and resin dust is abrasive and sticky, so filters in any air handler or makeup unit load quickly and coils can pack with a gray film. Our own advice: keep the sanding dust at its source hood or table, keep return air for the office away from the shop, and check filter pressure drop or look at filters on a schedule that matches how much sanding happens, not on a calendar alone. Wet or resin-contaminated filters are a disposal question for the shop owner under local rules; ask before you toss them. A packed coil shows up as low airflow, high head pressure or a frozen coil.

Keeping vapor out of the office

Our own rule of thumb for layout: keep the office and break room on a separate air system, with the shop held slightly negative so air leaks from the office into the shop, not the other way. Close the door between them, seal gaps and penetrations, and never share a return. If the office has a rooftop unit, keep its outdoor air intake far from the shop exhaust discharge. Test with a smoke pencil at the door to see which way air moves, and recheck after any fan or door change, since a shop that goes positive with a makeup unit running and the exhaust off will push vapor toward the office.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption for teaching, not a measured value or a design.

  • Assumption: shop is 40 ft by 60 ft with a 14 ft ceiling, so 40 × 60 × 14 = 33,600 cubic feet.
  • Assumption: total exhaust is 6,000 cfm (local hoods plus a general exhaust fan), all replaced by makeup air.
  • Assumption: indoor target of 65 °F, chosen to stay above the 59 °F minimum on the resin data sheet we read.
  • Assumption: design outdoor temperature of 10 °F.

Step 1, air changes. 6,000 cfm × 60 minutes = 360,000 cubic feet per hour. 360,000 ÷ 33,600 = about 10.7 air changes per hour.

Step 2, temperature difference. 65 − 10 = 55 °F.

Step 3, peak makeup air heating load. Q = 1.08 × 6,000 × 55 = 356,400 Btu/h. That is output the heater must deliver to the air, before any efficiency losses and before the wall and roof heat loss, which are separate loads.

Step 4, yearly hours that need heat. Assumption: the shop runs 8 hours a day, 5 days a week, 52 weeks, which is 8 × 5 × 52 = 2,080 operating hours. Assumption: 900 of those hours (about 43 percent) fall at outdoor temperatures below 65 °F with the exhaust running, at an average temperature difference of 25 °F. These two numbers depend on climate and schedule; a local weather table would replace them.

Step 5, yearly heat delivered to makeup air. Average load = 1.08 × 6,000 × 25 = 162,000 Btu/h. 162,000 × 900 = 145,800,000 Btu, or about 146 million Btu per year delivered to the air.

What to take from it. The peak load (356,400 Btu/h) sizes the heater. The yearly figure shows why trimming exhaust hours or cfm, for example by running local hoods only while work is happening, saves the most heat. Cutting exhaust to 3,000 cfm would halve both numbers in this example, but any cut must still keep vapor below safe levels, which is the designer's call.

Field card: Fiberglass and Boat Repair Shop Ventilation

Capture at the source, replace what you exhaust, keep vapor away from people.

  • Styrene flash point is 88 °F with explosive limits of 0.9 to 6.8 percent; a qualified designer and the code decide classified areas.
  • Check the resin data sheet for its cure temperature range; one maker said not below 15 °C (59 °F).
  • Smoke test every hood and keep exhaust openings clear.
  • Makeup air heat: Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Our example: 6,000 cfm at 55 °F difference is 356,400 Btu/h.
  • Sanding dust loads filters and coils fast; inspect on a schedule.
  • Hold the shop slightly negative to the office and never share a return.
