Parcel and Mail Sorting Center HVAC: Conveyor Heat, Cardboard Dust, Dock Doors and Worker Heat Stress

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A sorting center is a warehouse with hundreds of motors, a bright ceiling, a crowd of people moving fast, and a wall of dock doors that opens and closes all day. The heat sources are inside, the dust comes from the cardboard, and peak season brings new workers who are not used to the heat. This lesson shows where the load comes from, how to size it with a worked example, and what the HVAC tech can do about heat stress.

Where the heat comes from

In a large sorting building the weather is only part of the story. Most of the load is generated indoors.

  • Conveyor and sorter motors. Every motor turns nearly all of its input power into heat in the building. The DOE motor handbook says the lost power shows up as heat in the motor structure, and that most motor losses are rejected into the surrounding space, so cutting losses cuts cooling load. The driven load ends as friction heat too, so the full input is a fair first estimate.
  • Lights. Every watt of lighting becomes heat. The PNNL warehouse design guide lists a baseline of 1.2 W per square foot for storage areas under an older energy standard.
  • People. Sorters, loaders, drivers and supervisors all give off heat, and harder work gives off more.
  • Dock doors. Open doors let in hot, humid air. The PNNL guide modeled an open 8 ft by 10 ft dock door at 783 cfm of infiltration, against 32 cfm when closed. That was a small-warehouse model, not a sorting center, so treat it as a scale check.
  • Roof and walls. Solar gain still matters. We leave it out of the example so the indoor sources stand out.

Dock doors are a cold-air problem in a freezer building; see Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings for that side.

Cardboard dust, filters and coils

Cardboard sheds fibers and fine dust every time it is cut, dragged or slapped down a belt. That dust loads return filters fast. Our own field expectation is shorter change intervals than an office building uses, so plan on pressure-drop checks rather than a calendar. See One-Inch Filters vs Four-Inch Media Cabinets for how pressure drop guides the change, and use a gauge or a differential switch.

Dust is also a health matter for the people in the building. A study listed in the NIOSH library, built on Michigan surveillance reports from 1988 to 2022 found eight cases of work-related asthma tied to paper dust. Seven of the eight were attributed to cardboard dust, and half involved packaging and shipping products in cardboard containers. That is a small count, so it does not show how common the problem is.

On limits, the NIOSH pocket guide lists 15 mg/m³ total and 5 mg/m³ respirable as the OSHA exposure limits for particulates not otherwise regulated. A 1987 OSHA letter, marked as archived, says a review decision held that those nuisance dust limits could no longer apply to organic dusts such as paper dust, and that OSHA then had no enforceable paper dust limit. That letter is old, so check current OSHA and state rules before you quote a limit to a customer. The tech's part is the equipment: keep filters seated, coils clean and returns unblocked.

Dock doors: heat in, dust out

Open dock doors are a moving hole in the envelope. In summer they add hot, humid air and in winter they add cold. The PNNL guide recommends weatherseals at dock levelers and trailer hinges to limit infiltration when doors are open. We have no sourced numbers for other dock fixes, so we give none. One idea of our own: treat the dock as its own zone and give the people there spot cooling rather than trying to condition the whole door area.

Cooling choices: full air conditioning, evaporative coolers, spot coolers

  • Full air conditioning with rooftop units. Controls temperature and humidity, but the doors and tall space make it costly to run (our view).
  • Evaporative coolers. The DOE says they suit dry climates only, lower the incoming air by roughly 15 to 40°F, and add moisture to the space. They need relief openings; DOE suggests 1 to 2 square feet per 1,000 cfm for houses, so a big building needs its own design. They can work well on an open building in a dry region and badly in a humid one.
  • Portable spot coolers. Good for a pack station or a break area, not a whole floor. See Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers in Warehouses.
  • HVLS fans. They move a lot of air slowly and raise the air speed on workers, which NIOSH lists as an engineering control (increase air velocity). They do not remove heat from the building. See HVLS Fans and Destratification.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Every input is our own assumption unless a source is named.

Assumed building: 250,000 sq ft, 30 ft clear height, 400 conveyor motors at 2 hp each, running at 60 percent load on average. Motor efficiency 86.5 percent, which is the DOE handbook's minimum for a 2 hp, 1,800 rpm motor (a minimum, not any particular model).

Step 1, motors. Shaft power = 400 × 2 hp × 0.60 = 480 hp. One horsepower is 746 W, a figure the DOE handbook gives, and 746 W is about 2,545 Btu/h (our own conversion, using 3.412 Btu/h per watt). Heat = 480 × 2,545 ÷ 0.865 = about 1,412,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, lights. Assume 0.8 W per sq ft: 250,000 × 0.8 = 200,000 W. Heat = 200,000 × 3.412 = 682,400 Btu/h.

Step 3, people, counting every group. Assumed sensible heat per person (our own assumption, not a cited value): 600 sorters and loaders at 500 Btu/h = 300,000; 50 equipment drivers at 300 = 15,000; 20 supervisors and maintenance staff at 250 = 5,000. Total 320,000 Btu/h.

Step 4, add up. 1,412,000 + 682,400 + 320,000 = about 2,414,700 Btu/h. Divide by 12,000 Btu/h per ton: about 201 tons. Motors are about 58 percent of the total, lights 28 percent, people 13 percent.

Step 5, airflow. The sensible heat equation is Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT (see the heat transfer formulas lesson). Pick a 20°F difference between room air and supply air. cfm = 2,414,700 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = about 111,800 cfm, or 0.45 cfm per sq ft.

Step 6, dock doors. Suppose 20 doors stand open on a 95°F day and the room is held at 80°F. Using the PNNL 783 cfm per open door as a scale check only: 20 × 783 = 15,660 cfm. Sensible load = 1.08 × 15,660 × 15 = about 253,700 Btu/h, or 21 tons. Humidity adds latent load that this sensible-only math leaves out. The new total is about 2,668,000 Btu/h, or 222 tons, and 123,500 cfm at the same 20°F.

The lesson: the indoor sources alone can ask for over 200 tons, and the DOE handbook says reducing motor losses cuts cooling loads.

Worker heat stress and the HVAC tech's part

OSHA says almost half of heat-related deaths occur on a worker's first day on the job or first day back after a long absence, and over 70 percent in the first week. Peak season brings exactly those workers. OSHA and NIOSH describe the Rule of 20 Percent for new workers: 20 percent of the normal work time on day one, adding no more than 20 percent each day. NIOSH gives a faster schedule for experienced workers: 50, 60, 80 and 100 percent on days one to four.

NIOSH also suggests shielding and, for water, 1 cup (8 oz) every 15 to 20 minutes for moderate heat work under 2 hours, cool water under 59°F, and a buddy system. OSHA first aid for heat exhaustion is to move the worker to a cooler area and cool them right away; for heat stroke (confusion, slurred speech, seizures, very high body temperature), call 911 and cool the worker with ice or cold water until help arrives.

The tech's part: keep the cooling that exists working, especially before peak season; make sure fans and spot coolers are placed where people stand; report a failed unit as a safety item; and watch for signs of heat illness in the crew. Techs need their own heat plan too; see Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs for HVAC Techs.

Field card: Sorting Center Heat and Dust

Motors, lights and people make the load; dust and doors make it worse.

  • Motor heat: hp × 2,545 Btu/h per hp ÷ efficiency (our conversion).
  • Lights: watts × 3.412 = Btu/h. People: count every group.
  • Tons = Btu/h ÷ 12,000. cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT).
  • Dust: check filter pressure drop, not the calendar. Clean coils with power locked out.
  • Open dock doors add large infiltration; seal levelers and hinges.
  • Evaporative cooling: dry climates only, adds moisture, needs relief.
  • New workers: 20 percent rule. Heat stroke: call 911, cool with ice or cold water.
