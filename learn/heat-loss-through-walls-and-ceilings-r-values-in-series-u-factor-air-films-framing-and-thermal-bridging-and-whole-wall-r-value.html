Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings: R-Values in Series, U-Factor, Air Films, Framing and Thermal Bridging, and Whole-Wall R-Value

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A batt labeled R-13 does not make an R-13 wall. Heat takes the easy path, and in a framed wall the easy path is through the wood. This lesson shows how the layers of a wall add up, why the studs pull the whole-wall number down, and what that does to a load calculation and to the comfort of the room.

Layers in series: R-values add

R-value is a measure of resistance to heat flow, so a higher number means less heat moves through. The Department of Energy insulation page says layer R-values are added together. That works because heat that passes through the drywall must then pass through the insulation, then the sheathing, then the siding, one after another. Resistances in series simply add: R total = R1 + R2 + R3 and so on. A national-lab building-energy glossary describes R-value as the reciprocal of the U-factor, so U = 1 / R total. The two numbers say the same thing in different directions: R is how hard it is for heat to get through, U is how easily it does.

Air films count too

A thin layer of nearly still air clings to every wall surface and resists heat flow a little. Technicians call these air films. A state education page lists an inside film of 0.68 and an outside film of 0.17 (the outside film is lower because wind strips it thin). The same glossary notes that a plain R-value for a material leaves film coefficients out, so when you add up a whole assembly you add the films yourself. Film values change with surface, wind and direction of heat flow, so a ceiling does not use exactly the same numbers as a wall.

Parallel paths: why the label is not the wall

Now look at the wall from the side. Across any stretch of wall, some of the area is insulated cavity and some is solid framing: studs, plates, headers and blocking. Heat does not wait for the insulation. It flows through both at once, side by side, so the two paths are in parallel. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory calculator states the method plainly: it assumes heat flows through each distinct area independently, and it reports an equivalent R-value and equivalent U-factor for the whole. Its example 2x6 wall uses a framing fraction of about 25 percent and shows an R-value of 9.32 at the framing against 21.44 between framing.

Wood is a poor insulator compared with fiberglass. A state education table lists softwood at about 1.25 per inch, while fiberglass runs roughly 2.2 to 3.2 per inch. So a 3.5 inch stud carries far more heat than 3.5 inches of batt next to it. This extra heat flow through a high-conductivity member is thermal bridging.

The effect is measured, not just theoretical. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory paper on whole-wall performance reported that a 2x4 wood-frame wall with R-11 insulation came out 10 percent below its clear-wall value, and a 2x6 wall with R-19 came out 17 percent below. A Department of Energy Building America document compares standard framing (15 to 25 percent framing, some insulation voids, R-13 batts) to an effective average of about R-11.1. Advanced framing, with less wood, reached about R-14.6. The same document notes that continuous foam sheathing reduces thermal bridging through wood studs. The DOE insulation page agrees in general terms: heat moves more easily through studs, joists and other materials, so the overall R-value of a wall or ceiling differs from the insulation alone.

Q = U x A x delta T

Once you have a U-factor for the assembly, heat loss is Q = U x A x delta T, with Q in Btu per hour, U in Btu per hour per square foot per degree F, A in square feet and delta T the indoor-outdoor difference in degrees F. The PNNL calculator also reports UA, the U-factor times the area, which is the heat loss per degree of temperature difference for that piece of the building. A load calculation is largely a long sum of these terms, one per wall, ceiling, floor and window, plus air leakage and ventilation. See Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J for the full picture.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a field measurement. Assumptions: a 2x4 wall (3.5 inch cavity) with a batt labeled R-13 that is installed without gaps or compression; framing fraction 25 percent of the wall area, a figure inside the 15 to 25 percent range and near the 25.4 percent the PNNL page uses for a 2x6 wall; 400 square feet of wall; indoor-outdoor delta T of 60 F. Layer values: inside film 0.68 and outside film 0.17 (state education page); 1/2 inch drywall 0.45 (state education page); 1/2 inch sheathing 0.63 (PNNL example, close to the 1.25 per inch plywood value in the state table); siding assumed as 3/4 inch wood at 0.79 per inch, which is 0.59; stud 3.5 inches at 1.25 per inch, which is 4.38. Windows, doors, corners, headers and plates beyond the 25 percent are left out.

  1. Layers common to both paths: 0.68 + 0.45 + 0.63 + 0.59 + 0.17 = 2.52.
  2. Cavity path R: 2.52 + 13 = 15.52. U = 1 / 15.52 = 0.0644.
  3. Stud path R: 2.52 + 4.38 = 6.90. U = 1 / 6.90 = 0.1449.
  4. Area-weighted U: 0.75 x 0.0644 + 0.25 x 0.1449 = 0.0483 + 0.0362 = 0.0846.
  5. Whole-wall R: 1 / 0.0846 = about 11.8.
  6. Heat loss, 400 sq ft at 60 F: Q = 0.0846 x 400 x 60 = about 2,030 Btu/h.
  7. Label R-13 alone: Q = 400 x 60 / 13 = about 1,850 Btu/h.

The framed wall loses about 185 Btu/h more than the label suggests, roughly 10 percent. The studs are 25 percent of the area but carry about 43 percent of the heat (0.0362 / 0.0846). If you had used only the cavity path with all layers (R-15.52), you would get about 1,550 Btu/h, about 24 percent too low. A real wall also has a label that may not be achieved (compressed batts, gaps), which the DOE Building America figure of R-11.1 for standard framing hints at, so treat 11.8 as a friendly estimate.

Why this matters for load calculations

A load program asks for a wall construction and does this blend for you, so the lesson for a technician is what to feed it. Entering the batt label as the wall R-value understates heat loss and can make the load, and the equipment chosen after it, too small. Entering a number that is too pessimistic leads to oversizing, which has its own problems; see the equipment selection step after the load calculation. For ceilings the same parallel-path blend applies with joists in place of studs, and flat attic floors often hide the joists under loose-fill, which is why depth over the joists matters; see Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for New Jersey Homes. Ducts have their own insulation questions in Duct Insulation R-Values for Attics, Crawl Spaces and Basements. Check which R-value rules your adopted energy code, such as the IECC, uses for the job.

Cold spots

The inside surface temperature depends on the resistance behind it. A simple rule from the series idea is that the temperature drop across the inside film equals delta T times (film R divided by total R). With our own assumed 70 F indoors and 10 F outdoors (delta T 60), the drop across the 0.68 film is about 2.6 F over the cavity and about 5.9 F over a stud. That puts the drywall at roughly 67.4 F over the cavity and 64.1 F over the stud. A three-degree stripe is small, but it shows that the framing line is the coolest part of the wall surface, and more framing, corners and gaps mean more of that. The DOE insulation page also ties air sealing to comfort and says insulation filling cavities reduces airflow and leakage; for leaks see Drafty Doors and Windows.

Fixes that reduce the bridge

  • Continuous sheathing-side insulation covers the studs; the DOE energy-saver page lists combinations such as R-13 cavity plus R-10 continuous for some climates.
  • Advanced framing uses less wood, which the Building America table credits with a higher effective R.
  • Fill the cavity fully; compressed or voided batts lose their rating.

Field card: Heat loss through walls and ceilings

Layers add in series; framing lets heat bypass in parallel.

  • Series layers: R total = R1 + R2 + ...; U = 1 / R total.
  • Add air films: about 0.68 inside and 0.17 outside in our sources.
  • Heat loss: Q = U x A x delta T (Btu/h).
  • Studs and joists lower the whole-wall R below the cavity label.
  • Our 2x4 example: R-13 label, about R-11.8 whole wall, about 10 percent more loss.
  • Feed load programs the real assembly, not the batt label.
  • Stud lines are slightly cooler wall surfaces.
