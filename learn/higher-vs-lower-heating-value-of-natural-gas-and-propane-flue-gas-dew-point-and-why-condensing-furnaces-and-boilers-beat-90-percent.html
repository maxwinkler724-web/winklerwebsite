Higher vs Lower Heating Value of Natural Gas and Propane: Dew Point and Why Condensing Gear Beats 90 Percent

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new condensing furnace is labeled 96 percent efficient, and an apprentice asks the fair question: where does the other 4 percent go, and how did anyone get past 90? The answer is in how we count the fuel. Gas contains energy that leaves in the water vapor of the flue gas, and a condensing appliance is built to catch some of it.

Two ways to count the same gas

When natural gas burns, the hydrogen in it becomes water. That water leaves the burner as vapor, and vapor carries a lot of energy that went into boiling it. Engineers count the fuel's energy two ways. The higher heating value (HHV) assumes the water ends up as a liquid, so the heat given up when vapor condenses is counted. The lower heating value (LHV) assumes the water stays vapor, so that latent heat is left out. The EIA states the same split: gross heat content (higher heating value) versus net heat content (lower heating value), and it says it typically uses gross values.

For natural gas, the DOE's 2015 technology review gives the LHV-to-HHV ratio as approximately 0.9 for methane and most domestic natural-gas mixtures. A university combustion lecture lists methane at 23,900 Btu per pound HHV and 21,500 LHV, and propane at 21,700 HHV and 19,900 LHV. The Alternative Fuels Data Center lists propane at 91,420 Btu per gallon HHV and 84,250 LHV. From those two propane figures we get a gap of about 7.8 percent, smaller than natural gas because propane has less hydrogen per Btu. That comparison is our own arithmetic.

The EIA's conversion page lists natural gas at 1,036 Btu per cubic foot and propane at 91,452 Btu per gallon. Those are gross (HHV-style) figures in the sense the EIA uses, and they vary a little from year to year and place to place. A DOE steam tip sheet puts the water vapor heat in burned methane at about 9 percent of the fuel energy, which agrees with the 10 percent order of magnitude above.

Where the 10 percent hides

In a standard furnace or boiler, flue gases leave hot enough that the vapor stays vapor. That latent heat goes up the vent with the stack losses. The best such an appliance can do is capture the LHV portion, so its efficiency tops out in the 80s on an HHV basis. A university text works one example of a natural gas unit at 0.807 and the same unit with the flue products cooled to 32 C (about 90 F) at 0.964.

A condensing appliance adds a bigger or second heat exchanger and a vent that can handle liquid. Cooling the flue gas further turns vapor into liquid on the heat exchanger surface, and that releases the latent heat into the air stream or the water. It is the same latent-heat idea as in sensible vs latent heat, applied to flue gas instead of refrigerant.

The dew point decides how much you catch

Vapor condenses only when it touches a surface colder than its dew point. A DOE steam tip sheet gives about 135 F as the dew point for the products of combustion of natural gas. The university combustion chapter gives a range of 120 to 140 F depending on excess air, with worked examples near 125 to 128 F. So think of it as roughly the 130 F range, and note that more excess air dilutes the vapor and lowers the dew point. How excess air shows up on an analyzer is in combustion analyzer readings.

The DOE tip sheet table makes the point numerically for a large steam example: recoverable latent heat falls as the leaving flue gas temperature rises, to 9.51 million Btu per hour at 75 F, 2.01 at 125 F, and zero at 150 F. Flue gas that leaves above the dew point gives nothing back, no matter how big the heat exchanger. Condensing is not on or off. It is a sliding scale with a cliff near the dew point.

Why return water temperature matters on a boiler

A hot water boiler's heat exchanger surface runs close to the temperature of the water inside it. Cold return water is therefore what chills the flue gas below its dew point. The DOE Building America guide on condensing boilers says the system should be designed so the return temperature does not exceed 130 F at design load, using a 150 F supply on a 20 F temperature drop as its example, and it plots combustion efficiency rising as return temperature falls. For a water heater tank it says running above 130 F gives efficiencies only in the mid to upper 80 percent range. The practical rules, such as outdoor reset and sizing emitters for low water temperature, are in boiler return temperature and condensing. The furnace version of the same hardware story is in condensing furnace secondary heat exchangers.

Why the label says HHV and the number stays under 100

The DOE efficiency discussion for boilers defines efficiency with HHV and its worked example reports an HHV-based result. The university lecture says the HHV applies to the condensing case, where the exhaust cools toward room temperature and all the moisture condenses. When the fuel input is counted at its full HHV, the energy delivered cannot exceed it, so a condensing furnace lands at 90 to 98 percent AFUE, which is the range the DOE Building America furnace guide gives for high-efficiency units. Counting on LHV would make the same appliance read higher than 100 percent, which is confusing on a label. We did not find a DOE page we read that spells out the AFUE test rule in these exact words, so confirm the basis in the test procedure if you need it for a report.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Assumptions: natural gas at the EIA figure of 1,036 Btu per cubic foot as the HHV, and an LHV equal to 0.9 times that, using the DOE ratio of approximately 0.9. The 0.9 is rounded, so treat the results as estimates.

  1. Btu on an HHV basis for 100 cubic feet: 100 × 1,036 = 103,600 Btu.
  2. LHV per cubic foot: 1,036 × 0.9 = 932.4, about 932 Btu. For 100 cubic feet: 100 × 932.4 = 93,240 Btu.
  3. Gap: 103,600 − 93,240 = 10,360 Btu. As a percent of HHV: 10,360 ÷ 103,600 = 10.0 percent. That is the latent heat in the vapor, by construction of the ratio.
  4. Rating comparison: a furnace that delivers 95,000 Btu from 100,000 Btu of HHV input scores 95,000 ÷ 100,000 = 95 percent on an HHV basis. The same gas on an LHV basis is 100,000 × 0.9 = 90,000 Btu, so the score is 95,000 ÷ 90,000 = 1.056, or about 105.6 percent.

The appliance did not change. Only the denominator did. Reading 105.6 percent is not a perpetual motion claim; it just means the unit recovered more than the LHV portion of the fuel. To check what a furnace really burns per hour, see clocking a gas meter and checking furnace input, since a Btu rating only helps if the input is right.

Condensate is acidic: drains and neutralizers

The liquid that forms is not clean water. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory page says acidic gases mix with the water vapor to form condensate at a pH of about 3, more acidic than typical acid rain, and that the acidic condensate leads to corrosion and fouling problems. That is why condensing equipment uses corrosion-resistant heat exchanger and vent materials and plastic drain lines.

For furnaces, the DOE Building America guide says there are no restrictions it found on draining flue gas condensate into residential drains, and that in cold climates condensate should be drained only into the house sanitary drain system, never outdoors. It prefers a gravity drain below the trap, allows a condensate pump if there is none, and calls for heat tape and insulation on the trap and drain lines in attics that can fall below 32 F. Whether a neutralizer is required depends on the equipment instructions and the local authority, so follow both. How the neutralizing cartridge works and when to check it is in condensate neutralizers.

Field card: HHV, LHV and condensing

The label counts the water vapor; only cold surfaces collect it.

  • HHV counts latent heat in the flue vapor; LHV does not.
  • Natural gas LHV is about 0.9 of HHV (DOE); propane gap is smaller, about 7.8 percent from the AFDC figures.
  • Natural gas flue dew point: about 135 F (DOE tip sheet); 120 to 140 F by excess air (university text).
  • Condensing boiler design: return water no higher than 130 F at design load (Building America guide).
  • Our example: 100 cu ft at 1,036 Btu HHV = 103,600; LHV about 93,240; 95,000 out of 100,000 HHV is 95 percent HHV, about 105.6 percent LHV.
  • Condensate pH is about 3: use a proper drain, and follow the manual and local rules on neutralizing.
