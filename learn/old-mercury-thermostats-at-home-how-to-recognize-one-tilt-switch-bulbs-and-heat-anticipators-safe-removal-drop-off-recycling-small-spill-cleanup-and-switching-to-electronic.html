Old Mercury Thermostats at Home: Spot, Remove and Recycle Safely

Last updated: 2026-11-04

If your house has a round, dial-style wall thermostat from decades ago, there may be a few grams of liquid mercury sealed in glass behind its cover. Sealed and untouched, it is not a problem. Broken, thrown in the trash or dropped in the wrong place, it is. This lesson shows how to recognize one, remove it carefully, get it to a proper drop-off and handle a small spill.

How to recognize a mercury thermostat

Older round or rectangular dial thermostats often use a mercury tilt switch. A state environmental agency page says to remove the front cover and look for one or more glass ampoules, or bulbs, holding a silver liquid. Another state page describes the same thing: a small glass switch with a silver mass inside. A regional guidance document from environmental agencies tells readers to remove the front plate carefully and look for small glass ampules with silver liquid. If you see a bright, rolling bead inside a sealed glass bulb, treat it as mercury. A state health department document adds that digital thermostats are mercury free, and a state agency notes that one state stopped their sale years ago, so units in older homes are the likely ones.

Taking the cover off is a look-only step. Do not touch or press on the bulbs.

Why mercury was used

The same regional guidance says mercury tilt switches give accurate, reliable temperature control and need no power source. That fits a thermostat: a coiled metal strip bends as the room changes temperature, tilts the bulb, and the mercury bead rolls to cover or uncover two contacts. Covered contacts complete the low-voltage heat call, uncovered contacts open it.

Many of these thermostats also carry a heat anticipator, a small adjustable resistor in the heating circuit. A university greenhouse fact sheet describes its purpose: a heat anticipator shuts the heater off just before the room reaches the shut-off point. In our own plain words, the resistor warms the thermostat a little while the burner runs, so the switch opens a bit early and the room does not overshoot. Our related lesson on cycle rates covers how it is set.

Why it should not go in the trash

A state environmental agency says mercury thermostats should not be disposed of in the regular trash. The federal environmental agency says old thermostats should go to a state or local household hazardous waste collection center for recycling, and notes that thermostats are unlikely to break or leak mercury while in use. A state health agency says there is no spill risk while the switch is intact, but a broken switch with mercury tracked around the home becomes a large spill. Trash trucks, compactors and landfills are exactly where bulbs break.

Taking one off the wall without breaking the bulb

  1. Turn off the heating and cooling system at its switch or breaker first. This step is our own practice, so no one is working on a live control circuit.
  2. Remove the cover gently. State pages say to remove the face plate carefully and not to break or remove the ampoules, and another state agency says not to remove the mercury switch from the thermostat.
  3. Photograph the wiring and label each wire with tape before you disconnect anything. This is our own habit; the labels matter later.
  4. Loosen the mounting screws and lift the whole thermostat away, level and slowly, with no knocking or shaking. Return it intact when possible, as that state agency advises.
  5. Pack it in a sturdy sealed container that will not tip over. That state agency says loose bulbs belong in a sealed, labeled container.

If a bulb is already cracked or loose, stop and go to the spill section below. Do not cut the thermostat open to get at the bulb.

Where to take it

A state environmental page lists a household hazardous waste collection event run by a municipality or solid waste district, and a participating electrical or plumbing wholesale supplier that accepts them free regardless of brand. Another state page says the same and recommends calling first to confirm the location will accept your thermostat. State and local sites differ, so check the page for your state environmental agency. At a recycling center, the mercury ampules are taken out and sent to a mercury recycler.

If a bulb breaks

The federal environmental agency's page on a broken mercury thermometer describes a small-spill cleanup. These are the steps it lists, in our own words.

  1. Before cleanup, open windows and exterior doors and shut the doors to the rest of the house. After cleanup, keep ventilating outdoors for at least 24 hours.
  2. Do not use a vacuum cleaner, which blows mercury into the air. Do not use a broom, which breaks beads into smaller droplets and spreads them. Do not pour mercury down a drain, and do not walk around if your shoes may be contaminated.
  3. Wear rubber, nitrile or latex gloves. Pick up broken glass on a paper towel, fold it and seal it in a zip bag.
  4. Gather visible beads with a squeegee or cardboard, or draw them up with an eyedropper onto a damp paper towel. A flashlight held low in a darkened room helps spot stray beads. Tiny beads can be lifted with duct tape or shaving cream on a small brush.
  5. Seal every cleanup item, gloves included, in a bag. Store it outside in a secured spot and ask your local health department, waste authority or fire department how to label and dispose of it.

The same page lists the local health department or poison control line for health questions after exposure, and points to the environmental or health agency for contractors with mercury vapor monitoring equipment. The page is written for thermometer-sized spills; a thermostat bulb can hold more, so if you are unsure, call before you start.

What changes with an electronic thermostat

These points come from our own field practice, so confirm them in the instructions that come with the new thermostat.

  • No anticipator setting. Electronic models handle timing in software, so the adjustable resistor and its dial are gone. Some have a cycle-rate or swing setting instead.
  • Wiring labels. Match your tape labels to the new terminal letters rather than to wire colors, because colors are not guaranteed. See our terminal-letters lesson.
  • The common wire. Mercury thermostats needed no power of their own. Many electronic ones do, and use the 24-volt common (C) wire or a battery. If no spare wire is available, see our lesson on how smart thermostats get power.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a small apartment building collects 12 old mercury tilt bulbs from its thermostats. Assume each bulb holds 3 g of mercury. That matches the "about 3 grams per switch" in the regional guidance and the "3 grams" per thermostat in the state health document; the sources frame the number differently, so we state it as an assumption.

  1. Mass of mercury: 12 bulbs × 3 g per bulb = 36 g.
  2. One gram represents 1 g ÷ 3 g = 0.33 of a bulb, about one third.
  3. Thermostats represented, assuming one bulb each: 36 g ÷ 3 g per thermostat = 12 thermostats. So 1 g of mercury is one third of one thermostat.
  4. Check: 12 × 3 = 36, and 36 ÷ 3 = 12.

Now assume instead the same 12 bulbs came from 6 two-bulb thermostats at 3 g per thermostat. Mercury: 6 × 3 g = 18 g. Thermostats: 18 ÷ 3 = 6. The state page that mentions "one or more" ampoules is why the count of bulbs and the count of thermostats can differ. Either way the total is a few dozen grams at most, and every bulb needs to reach a proper drop-off intact.

Field card: Old Mercury Thermostats at Home

Look, do not touch the bulb. Never trash. Never vacuum.

  • Cover off shows glass bulbs with a silver bead: treat it as mercury.
  • Label wires, remove the whole thermostat intact, seal it in a container.
  • Take it to a household hazardous waste drop-off; call first.
  • Spill: ventilate, no vacuum, no broom, gloves, tape or cardboard, sealed bag, call local health officials.
  • New electronic thermostat: no anticipator, match terminal letters, check for a common wire.

Educational summary only. Follow the instructions of your local agency and the new thermostat's maker. Print this page to keep the card.
