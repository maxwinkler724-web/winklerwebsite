Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC: Tailpipe Exhaust Capture, CO and NO2 Limits, Unit Heater Height and Flammable Vapor Rules

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Auto repair shop ventilation has to solve four problems at once: engines that run indoors, gasoline vapor that sinks, gas heaters that can ignite that vapor, and winter air that must be heated after it comes in to replace what the fans throw out. This lesson ties those together for car, truck and bus bays.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Your state and town adopt the mechanical, fuel gas and fire codes, often with local changes. We name codes in plain text because we do not link copies of standards. This page is general education from public sources read in 2026, not an engineering design. Confirm with the inspector who covers the job.

Start with source capture

An idling engine puts exhaust where the mechanic stands. Room fans are the second line. The first is a hose over the tailpipe that carries exhaust outdoors. OSHA's diesel exhaust hazard alert lists "tailpipe or stack exhaust vents" among the ventilation upgrades for maintenance shops, and it says engineering controls are the most effective approach. It also says to limit idling.

Two NIOSH reports show how these systems fail in real shops. In a dealership evaluation, 14 service-bay ports fed a fan through flexible hoses 6 to 10 feet long. Leaks at the joints and cover plates meant one port measured 24 cfm before repairs, and smoke showed exhaust escaping. NIOSH noted that the ACGIH recommends at least 100 cfm per vehicle for cars and trucks up to 200 hp, and the shop still missed it after sealing work. Its fixes were self-closing port caps, a fan sized for the real static pressure, a roof stack and separate general dilution. It called hoses under 6 feet unreasonable to use. In an earlier study of 11 shops, NIOSH reported that most had hose systems, but performance "appeared to be poor, in part due to the lack of maintenance." It recommended regular hose leak checks and written records. One technician measured 69 ppm of CO.

So the service checklist is simple: look for torn or crushed hose, loose tailpipe connectors, open or damaged floor ports, a fan that does not run when a vehicle is connected, and a discharge point near an intake. Reels and ceiling drops are layouts of the same idea. Vendor guides are cited below as plain text only.

CO and NO2 limits to know

The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists these figures:

  • Carbon monoxide: NIOSH 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling; OSHA permissible limit 50 ppm as an 8-hour average; immediately dangerous level 1,200 ppm.
  • Nitrogen dioxide: NIOSH 1 ppm short-term limit; OSHA 5 ppm ceiling; immediately dangerous level 13 ppm.

OSHA's Table Z-1 shows the same OSHA numbers, and it also shows lower limits from California's agency and from ACGIH. Limits differ by agency, so alarm points are set with the local rules. Our enclosed parking garage lesson covers sensor placement, staging and calibration, so we do not repeat it here.

What NIOSH found in a bus garage

A NIOSH evaluation of a 61,000 square foot bus terminal with 40 diesel buses is a useful picture of the problem. The report described no tailpipe capture, and tailpipes sat about 18 inches above the ground, so NIOSH suggested top-mounted tailpipes on new buses. The building had four air handlers at 10,000 cfm each, eight exhaust fans at 5,000 cfm each, and 19 ceiling unit heaters on hot water.

Measured CO was modest: service lane attendants averaged 5.8 ppm. Nitrogen dioxide was the stronger signal. One driver's 15-minute sample peaked at 4.6 ppm, above the NIOSH short-term 1 ppm, and attendants' full-shift average of 1.2 ppm was above the NIOSH 1 ppm figure. The report did not measure diesel particulate, saying that no exposure limit applies directly to whole diesel exhaust, so it used CO and NO2 as indicators. NIOSH told the owner to have a ventilation engineer verify that the system performs as designed. For the same hazard in apparatus bays, see our fire station apparatus bay lesson.

A NIOSH study of a city public works garage found a working tailpipe system but wall fans switched off over noise complaints. NIOSH advised minimal idling. Controls that people turn off do not control anything.

Gasoline vapor sits low

The CDC's Medical Management Guidelines page for gasoline states that gasoline vapors are heavier than air and may collect in low-lying areas. New Jersey's Right to Know fact sheet adds that the vapor may travel a distance to an ignition source and flash back. A heater burner, pilot or switch can ignite a spill. That is why the mechanical code has an ignition source elevation section (the ICC sample points to it), and why the exhaust pickup for general ventilation is usually considered at the low level. That last point is our own reasoning from the vapor property; NFPA 30A, the code for motor fuel dispensing facilities and repair garages, sets the actual rules for your occupancy and we have not read it.

Unit heater height

An ICC training sample on the 2015 International Mechanical Code quotes section 304.6: appliances in public garages, motor fuel-dispensing facilities, repair garages or other areas frequented by motor vehicles must be installed at least 8 feet above the floor. Where vehicles can pass underneath, the appliance must also sit at the manufacturer's clearances and at least 1 foot above the tallest garage door opening. The stated reason is impact: a vehicle strike could damage the heater and start a fire or explosion. An exception allows a lower height where a barrier prevents impact, but the heater must still meet the code's ignition source elevation section and NFPA 30A. Read the adopted edition.

Impact and vapor are two separate reasons to keep a gas heater high. See our garage and workshop heaters lesson for unit heater selection, and our aircraft hangar lesson for the same high-flame, low-vapor idea with big doors.

Makeup air and door heat loss

Every cubic foot the hose and the low grille remove must be replaced by outdoor air, and in winter that air arrives cold. A NIOSH visit to an auto body shop in winter found it "fairly airtight," with the only exhaust being leakage and open doors, and the report said outside air must be brought in, tempered and partly exhausted. Heating that air is a real part of the load. Open overhead doors add more; our hangar lesson shows how to estimate door loss, so we skip it here. The sensible heat equation is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference; ACHR News explains that 1.08 equals 60 x 0.075 x 0.24 and holds for air near 70°F.

Worked example: what the exhaust costs in heat

These inputs are our own assumptions, not measurements: a two-bay shop of 1,000 square feet; a 2.0 L four-cylinder car idling at 800 rpm with a volumetric efficiency of 30 percent (low because the throttle is nearly closed); exhaust at 600°F; indoor 65°F, outdoor 15°F.

  1. What the engine makes. 2.0 L is about 0.0706 cubic feet. A four-stroke engine takes in that volume once every two revolutions: 800 / 2 = 400 times a minute, so 0.0706 x 400 = 28.2 cfm displacement. At 30 percent, 28.2 x 0.30 = about 8.5 cfm of intake air. Hot gas takes more room: (600 + 460) / (70 + 460) = 2.0, so about 17 cfm leaves the tailpipe.
  2. What the hose moves. The tailpipe flow is small, but the hose must catch revving and tolerate leaks. We use the ACGIH figure NIOSH quoted, 100 cfm per vehicle. For two bays that is 200 cfm. The hose pulls about six times what the engine makes.
  3. General exhaust. NIOSH's body shop report cited an older ASHRAE 62-1989 figure of 1.5 cfm per square foot of shop. As an assumption only, 1,000 x 1.5 = 1,500 cfm. Check the adopted code.
  4. Total makeup air. 200 + 1,500 = 1,700 cfm, with a temperature difference of 65 − 15 = 50°F.
  5. Heating load. 1.08 x 1,700 x 50 = 91,800 Btu/h. Hoses alone: 1.08 x 200 x 50 = 10,800 Btu/h.
  6. Gas input. At an assumed 80 percent efficiency, 91,800 / 0.80 = 114,750 Btu/h of input, before the shop's building heat loss.

Tempering makeup air can rival the heat lost through the walls. Running exhaust only while vehicles run, and closing doors, cuts it. Treat 91,800 as an estimate.

Field card: Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC

Capture at the tailpipe, heat high, exhaust low, and replace every cfm.

  • Check hoses, tailpipe fit, port caps and the fan every service visit; NIOSH found leaky hoses and poor upkeep.
  • ACGIH figure quoted by NIOSH: at least 100 cfm per car or light truck.
  • CO: NIOSH 35 ppm, OSHA 50 ppm. NO2: NIOSH 1 ppm short term, OSHA 5 ppm ceiling.
  • Gasoline vapor is heavier than air and collects low.
  • IMC 2015 304.6: heaters in repair garages 8 ft or more above the floor, and 1 ft above the tallest door.
  • Makeup air heat: 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
