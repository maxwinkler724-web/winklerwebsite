Where AC and Furnace Condensate Goes Outside: Puddles, Mosquitoes and Ice

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A cooling coil and a high-efficiency furnace both make water, and a pipe carries it out of the house. Where that pipe ends decides whether you get a harmless damp spot, a mosquito nursery, a soggy foundation or a sheet of ice on the walk. This lesson is about the outdoor end of the line.

Why the end of the line matters

The indoor part of a condensate system gets most of the attention: the pan, the trap, the slope. See the piping lesson for that. Here we only cover what happens after the water leaves the pipe. A national laboratory specification page says condensate from cooling coils and condensing furnaces should be carried to the outside of the building, and that an outdoor termination should point downward with an elbow at the end. That tells you the usual picture: a short pipe stub coming out of the wall or a pad, aimed at the ground.

How much water are we talking about

On a humid day an air conditioner can drip steadily for hours. Our worked example below puts a number on it. The short version is that a day of hard running can leave tens of gallons at one spot, which is plenty to make a puddle if the ground cannot take it.

Standing puddles next to the foundation

If the line ends right at the wall, the same patch of soil gets wet every cooling day. A university extension page on basement moisture explains why concentrated water near a house is a problem: it says a downspout without an extender or splash block is worse than none, and that discharge extensions should carry water at least 4 feet beyond the wall. That page is about roof runoff, not condensate, but the same logic applies to any pipe that dumps water beside a foundation. The same page says soil next to a house is often backfilled loosely and settles later, and that the ground should slope away from the wall at least 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet. If you see a low spot or a dip where the water sits, that is a grading problem that the drain end will keep feeding.

Mosquitoes in standing water

A puddle that stays wet is a place for mosquitoes to lay eggs. The federal public health agency says to empty and scrub, turn over, cover or throw out anything that holds water once a week. The federal environmental agency's backyard guidance is a little looser but points the same way: anything that can hold water for more than a few days is a mosquito breeding habitat. A condensate line that keeps filling a hollow, a buried bucket, a clogged gutter stub or a planter saucer fits that description.

What that means for the drain end:

  • Do not let the water pool. Let it run off or soak in.
  • Do not catch it in a bucket, jug or pot unless you plan to empty it often. A container under the line is a small pond.
  • If the pipe end is open, a small piece of mesh over it can keep insects from walking in. The public health page suggests wire mesh with holes smaller than an adult mosquito for containers without lids. We apply the same idea to the pipe end, and it must never be fine enough to clog. Choose a coarse screen you can see through, check it each season, and clear lint or webs. If water backs up, the screen is too tight or dirty.

Soggy soil and foundation moisture

Water that soaks in beside the house can keep the soil wet longer than rain does, because it arrives every day the system runs. The extension page notes that a rise in water near a foundation can show up as basement dampness, and that moisture can wick upward through soil by different amounts depending on the soil type, from a few inches in gravel to many feet in clay. So the same water can behave very differently from one yard to the next. If your basement is damp and the line ends by that wall, moving the end is cheap and easy to try first.

Ice patches in winter

A condensing furnace makes water in winter, and when the outside air is below freezing, that water can freeze where it lands. A line that ends over a walkway, step or driveway can build a slick patch, and ice can also grow back into the pipe and block it. The specification page above says to insulate condensate lines to a minimum of R-4, with a vapor retarder, wherever condensation or freezing is possible. We read that as an indoor and attic rule of thumb, since we found no source that gives an outdoor freeze fix for every climate. Practical steps that need no special source: keep the end away from walking surfaces, point it toward soil or gravel, and if the manufacturer's instructions allow a different outdoor route for your model, follow them. If a pipe freezes shut, the furnace may shut down, so do not just chip at it. See the pump lesson and the clog lesson for what blocked drains look like.

Good places for the water to go

  • A splash block. A block under the elbow spreads the stream and keeps it from digging a hole. Extension guidance lists splash blocks as one way to carry water away from the foundation.
  • A gravel area away from the house. A bed of stone lets water spread and drain instead of ponding. Put it on ground that slopes away from the wall.
  • A proper drain. A plumbing drain or other approved disposal point can take the water. The specification page notes that if waste lines are used for condensing furnace condensate, it should go through a neutralizer first. See the neutralizer lesson.

Do not tie into a sewer vent or a rain barrel without checking local rules. A sewer vent is not a drain. A rain barrel stores water that may sit for days, and the public health page says water storage containers such as rain barrels must be tightly covered so mosquitoes cannot get in. Whether either is allowed depends on local code and the equipment maker, so ask the local building department or a licensed contractor first. We do not give legal advice here.

Our own worked example

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume a central air conditioner removes about 1.5 gallons of water per hour on a humid day (we take the middle of a 1 to 2 gallon per hour range), runs 10 hours, and all of it leaves through the one line. Water weighs 8.34 pounds per gallon. These inputs are our assumptions, not measured values for your house.

  1. Gallons: 1.5 gal/h × 10 h = 15 gallons.
  2. Pounds: 15 gal × 8.34 lb/gal = 125.1 pounds.
  3. Check: 125.1 ÷ 8.34 = 15. Correct.
  4. Over 7 such days: 15 × 7 = 105 gallons, or 105 × 8.34 = 875.7 pounds, all at the same spot.

The low end (1 gal/h) gives 10 gallons and 83.4 pounds; the high end (2 gal/h) gives 20 gallons and 166.8 pounds. So the daily load is in the range of 10 to 20 gallons. For more on why humid days fill the drain, see how much water a home air conditioner removes.

When to call a tech

  • Water backs up, the pan overflows, or the system shuts off on a safety switch.
  • The line is frozen, cracked, crushed or full of slime that a gentle flush does not clear.
  • You are thinking of connecting to a vent, a sewer line, a sump or a rain barrel.
  • The drain end cannot be moved without trenching, new pipe or cutting through walls or paving.
  • You see water, rust or stains around the furnace or coil, not just outside.

A simple owner check is safe: look at where the line ends, make sure it is open and the screen is clear, and watch that the water leaves the area on a hot day. Anything inside the equipment is for a trained tech.

Field card: Where Condensate Goes Outside

Open end, away from the wall, never pooling.

  • Weekly rule from public health: do not leave water standing; mosquitoes lay eggs in it.
  • Point the end down, over a splash block or gravel, on ground sloping away from the house.
  • Keep it off walks and steps in winter; ice can also block the pipe.
  • Screen must be coarse enough that it never restricts flow.
  • No vent, sewer or rain barrel hookup without local rules checked.
  • Our example: 1.5 gal/h × 10 h = 15 gal = 125.1 lb.
