How Many Gallons Are in a Hot-Water Heating System? Pipe, Emitter and Boiler Water Content

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Almost every job on a hot-water system, from adding antifreeze to checking the expansion tank, starts with a number nobody wrote down: how many gallons are in it. You can get a good answer with a tape measure, a pencil and a few figures from maker literature. This lesson shows how, including where the "0.0408" in the pipe formula comes from.

Why you need the number

  • Glycol dosing. A percentage of antifreeze is a percentage of the system volume. Guess the volume wrong and the mix is wrong. See Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops for strengths and testing, and glycol makeup feeders for keeping the mix right afterward.
  • Chemical treatment. Inhibitor and cleaner labels give a dose per gallon of system water. See closed-loop water treatment.
  • Expansion tank check. Tank sizing depends on how much water heats and swells. The method is in Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure; this lesson only supplies the volume that goes into it.
  • Low-mass boilers and buffers. A boiler with very little water in it can short-cycle if the loop is small. Makers publish minimum system volumes, and buffer tank sizing picks up from there.

Where 0.0408 comes from

Water in a round pipe fills a cylinder: the area of the circle times the length. For an inside diameter d in inches, the area is (π/4) × d² square inches. One foot is 12 inches long, so each foot of pipe holds (π/4) × d² × 12 cubic inches, which is 9.4248 × d² cubic inches.

The U.S. gallon is defined as 231 cubic inches; NIST Handbook 44, Appendix C, lists it as exactly that. Divide: 9.4248 ÷ 231 = 0.0408. So:

gallons per foot = 0.0408 × ID², with ID in inches.

Check it on a pipe with a 1 inch inside diameter: 0.0408 × 1 × 1 = 0.0408 gal per foot, so about 24.5 feet of it holds a gallon (1 ÷ 0.0408). Use the inside diameter, not the nominal size. A pipe called "3/4 inch" is not 0.75 inch inside, and because the diameter is squared, using the wrong one shifts the answer a lot.

Gallons per foot of common pipe

A published copper tube handbook (2006), Table 2b, lists Type L tube dimensions and gives its own gallons-per-foot column. Our formula column below is our own arithmetic from those inside diameters.

The 1-1/4 inch row differs in the fourth decimal because the printed table rounds its own way; the difference is about 0.3 percent, which does not matter for an estimate.

Steel pipe. Schedule 40 steel pipe (dimensions set by the national steel pipe standard) has a thicker wall, so less water per foot than copper of the same nominal size. An openly posted engineering reference lists inside diameters of 20.93 mm for 3/4 inch, 26.64 mm for 1 inch and 52.5 mm for 2 inch. Dividing by 25.4 mm per inch gives 0.824, 1.049 and 2.067 inches. Our own arithmetic then gives 0.0408 × 0.824² = 0.0277 gal per foot for 3/4 inch, 0.0408 × 1.049² = 0.0449 for 1 inch, and 0.0408 × 2.067² = 0.1743 for 2 inch. A maker data sheet for commercial fin-tube radiation lists 0.174 for its 2 inch steel pipe, which agrees.

PEX. PEX tubing comes in several wall thicknesses and inside diameters, so do not assume one. A Department of Energy building science teaching page (modified from a model plumbing code table) lists ounces of water per foot, and notes 1 ounce = 0.00781 gallon. For PEX CTS SDR 9 it gives 1.18 oz per foot at 1/2 inch, 2.35 at 3/4 inch and 3.91 at 1 inch. Converted, that is 0.0092, 0.0184 and 0.0305 gal per foot. The 3/4 inch figure matches an inside diameter near 0.67 inch by our formula (0.0408 × 0.671² = 0.0184). If your tubing is a different series, use the maker's listed inside diameter in the formula.

The same DOE table lists Type L copper at 3.22 oz per foot for 3/4 inch and 5.49 for 1 inch, which converts to 0.0251 and 0.0429 gal per foot, the same as the handbook. Two sources agreeing is a good sign.

Water in the heat emitters

Emitters are not pipe, so use the maker's literature for the exact model. Typical figures we found:

  • Fin-tube baseboard. A maker data sheet for commercial radiation lists water content of 0.023 gal per foot for a 3/4 inch copper element, 0.040 for 1 inch and 0.063 for 1-1/4 inch, plus 0.077 for 1-1/4 inch steel pipe elements. The 3/4 inch figure is a little under the Type L value (0.0251), so use the maker number for the element. Count the length of the element, not the enclosure, and note that the pipe between enclosures is separate.
  • Cast-iron radiators. A maker catalog lists water content per section: 0.08 gal for a 4-tube, 19 inch tall section, 0.12 gal for 4-tube, 25 inch, and 0.17 gal for 6-tube, 25 inch. A ten-section 4-tube, 25 inch radiator is then 10 × 0.12 = 1.2 gal. Count sections and match tube count and height to the catalog.
  • Steel panel radiators. Water content is given per metre of length by panel type and height. One maker's 2026 technical documentation lists 5.83 litres per metre for a type 22, 500 mm high panel. Dividing by 3.785 litres per U.S. gallon (NIST) gives 1.54 gal per metre, about 0.47 gal per foot. Single-panel types hold far less (2.80 litres per metre for type 11).

Water in the boiler

Read it from the boiler's installation manual, usually in the ratings table as "water content" in gallons. It varies enormously with design. A small cast-iron gas boiler manual lists 4.0 gal for one of its smaller models, while a large sectional cast-iron boiler manual lists 37.5 gal for its smallest size and 141 gal for its largest. Copper-tube and other low-mass boilers hold only a small amount, which is exactly why their makers state minimum flow or system volume. Look for that statement in the same manual.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a real house. Assumptions: a small home loop with 40 ft of 1 inch Type L copper and 60 ft of 3/4 inch Type L copper (both totals include supply and return), 25 ft of 3/4 inch PEX CTS SDR 9, 50 ft of 3/4 inch copper fin-tube baseboard element, and a small cast-iron boiler listed at 4.0 gal. We ignore fittings, the air separator and the expansion tank water side for now.

  1. 1 inch copper: 40 ft × 0.0429 = 1.716, call it 1.72 gal.
  2. 3/4 inch copper: 60 ft × 0.0251 = 1.506, call it 1.51 gal.
  3. PEX: 25 ft × 0.0184 = 0.46 gal.
  4. Baseboard: 50 ft × 0.023 = 1.15 gal.
  5. Boiler: 4.0 gal from its manual.
  6. Total: 1.72 + 1.51 + 0.46 + 1.15 + 4.0 = 8.84 gal.

Glycol at 30 percent by volume (an assumed target for this example): 0.30 × 8.84 = 2.65 gal of glycol. Because the loop is already full of water, draw off about 2.65 gal of water first (or mix the batch in a pail and fill from that), otherwise the system cannot take the glycol. Pure glycol and premixed product are different; follow the label and the glycol lesson linked above.

How much does a bad estimate cost? Suppose the real volume is 10 percent higher, 9.72 gal. The same 2.65 gal of glycol makes 2.65 ÷ 9.72 = 27 percent. That is why we treat the estimate as a starting point and finish with a concentration test.

Checking the estimate

  • Drain and measure. A provincial government technical presentation on closed-system volume describes draining the system, reading the make-up water meter, refilling and reading it again. The volume is the difference. It is accurate if the system is fully drained, but draining exposes wetted metal to air and speeds corrosion, so it fits best when you are flushing or refilling anyway.
  • Tracer. The same presentation describes adding a measured amount of an unreactive tracer, circulating the water, and calculating volume from the change in concentration; it calls the method easy and accurate. The idea is a dilution: volume = amount of tracer added ÷ rise in concentration. Water treatment suppliers run this test; the presentation reports 2 to 5 days of circulation before the final sample.
  • Rule of thumb. The same source calls multiplier shortcuts only a ball-park estimate. Use them to catch a gross error, not to dose.

Common ways the total comes out low

These are our own checks: the expansion tank water side, the air separator, indirect water heater coils, buffer tanks, a forgotten second zone, and long runs hidden in walls. Walk the system with a sketch and tick each one off. For pipe lengths versus flow, see Hot-Water Heating Pipe Sizing.

Field card: Gallons in a Hot-Water Heating System

Inside diameter squared times 0.0408.

  • Gallons per foot = 0.0408 × ID² (9.4248 cubic inches per foot divided by 231 cubic inches per gallon).
  • Type L copper: 1/2 inch 0.0121, 3/4 inch 0.0251, 1 inch 0.0429, 1-1/4 inch 0.0655 gal per foot.
  • Schedule 40 steel: 3/4 inch 0.0277, 1 inch 0.0449, 2 inch 0.1743 gal per foot.
  • PEX varies by series; get the inside diameter or use the maker's table.
  • Emitters and boilers: use the maker's water content, per foot, per section or per metre.
  • Check the estimate by drain-and-measure or tracer, and test glycol strength after mixing.
