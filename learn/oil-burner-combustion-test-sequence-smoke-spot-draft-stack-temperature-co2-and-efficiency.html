Oil Burner Combustion Test Sequence: Smoke, Draft, Stack Temperature and Efficiency

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A combustion test on an oil burner is a short list of readings taken in a set order, because each reading depends on the one before it. This lesson walks the order, shows what each number tells you, and works one example with assumed readings.

Safety and targets. Combustion testing and adjustment belong to a qualified oil technician. Target numbers come from the burner and boiler or furnace makers and from the program or code that applies to the job, never from this page. If there is smoke or fumes in the building or a CO alarm is sounding, get outside and call for help. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Why the order matters

Too little air makes smoke and soot. Too much air wastes heat up the chimney. A weak chimney pull upsets both. So the test starts with a clean, warm, running system and only then moves to adjustment. For how the analyzer works, see Combustion Analyzer Readings: O2, CO2, CO, Air-Free and Excess Air.

Step 1: Let the system reach steady state

A burner maker's service bulletin on proper burner adjustment says to run the system until it reaches a uniform operating level, which it calls steady state. It gives no minute count. Our own rule of thumb is to watch the stack temperature and wait until it stops climbing. A New York City rule for large boilers uses a similar idea: it reads the outlet gas temperature every minute until successive readings differ by no more than 5 °F (see source 5). That rule is written for big commercial boilers, so treat it as an illustration of the idea, not a residential target. Readings taken on a cold boiler are not steady-state readings.

Step 2: Check overfire draft, then breech draft

Overfire draft is the pressure inside the combustion chamber, read through a small hole in the chamber wall or door. Breech draft (also called stack draft) is read in the flue pipe near the appliance. The burner maker's bulletin gives a target of about -0.02 inch of water column for overfire draft, or whatever the appliance maker specifies. It says the breech draft should be slightly stronger than the overfire draft, and that an unusually large gap can point to scale or soot blocking the heat exchanger, which should be cleaned before you adjust anything. For how a draft regulator holds these numbers, see Oil Burner Draft Regulators, Barometric Dampers and Overfire Draft.

Some programs set their own minimum draft, in pascals and tied to outdoor temperature (source 3). Our own conversion: -0.02 inch w.c. is about -5 Pa.

Step 3: Take the smoke spot test

A hand pump draws a set volume of flue gas through a filter paper, and the darkness of the spot is matched to a numbered scale. A Brookhaven National Laboratory report (source 1) describes drawing 0.6 cubic foot of flue gas through the paper and comparing it to a 0 to 9 reference scale, citing the ASTM D2156 method. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory paper (source 6) describes the scale this way: 0 is clean, 3 is marginal, and higher is too smoky. One state weatherization form lists a smoke number of 1 or less for oil burners (source 3).

Take the sample where the maker says to. The New York City rule for large boilers puts the first test hole about one breeching diameter downstream of the boiler outlet (source 5). Confirm the location in the instructions for the unit in front of you.

Step 4: Read stack temperature and compute net stack temperature

Stack temperature is the flue gas temperature in the breech. Net stack temperature is the stack temperature minus the room (combustion air) temperature. Net is the number used in efficiency tables, because it removes the effect of a warm or cold basement. State program forms list net stack temperature as a required entry (sources 2, 3 and 4), and one weatherization form shows a net stack range of 325 to 600 °F for oil gun and flame retention burners (source 3). The unit's own manual comes first.

Step 5: Read O2 or CO2 and find excess air

Excess air is the air supplied beyond what the fuel needs. The Brookhaven report notes that air to fuel ratio can be stated as excess air, percent O2 or percent CO2, and that CO2 is the most common form in residential heating (source 1). A common approximation, which we use here as our own arithmetic, is:

Excess air (%) ≈ O2 ÷ (21 − O2) × 100, where 21 is the oxygen percentage in ordinary air.

The same program forms list O2 or CO2 as a required entry. The weatherization form in source 3 shows O2 ranges of 4 to 9 percent for oil gun burners and 4 to 7 percent for flame retention burners.

Step 6: Check CO

CO is a safety reading as well as a tuning reading. A New York State clean and tune invoice form (source 4) tells the technician to confirm ambient CO is below 35 ppm and, if not, to shut the system off, ventilate and evacuate. The same form asks for an explanation of remedial action if breech CO is over 100 ppm. Those are that program's rules. For general CO safety, follow the rules for your job and your site.

Step 7: Adjust for the lowest smoke, then back off

The maker's bulletin describes this sequence. First adjust the air so the smoke is just a trace. Measure CO2 or O2 at that point; its example is 13.5 percent CO2 or 2.5 percent O2. Then open the air until CO2 falls about 1.5 points, to about 12.0 percent, or O2 rises about 2 points, to about 4.5 percent. Check the smoke again; the bulletin says you should now have zero. The extra air is a safety margin against lower draft, lint buildup or heavier oil showing up as smoke later. The same bulletin says the margin may cost about 1 percent over a heating season. Retest after every adjustment, then recheck draft and the fuel pressure. See Oil Burner Nozzles, Pump Pressure and Firing Rate.

Step 8: Steady-state efficiency, then record

Steady-state efficiency is found from the O2 or CO2 reading, the net stack temperature, and the smoke number. The Oak Ridge paper describes it as calculated from stack O2 or CO2, stack temperature, room temperature and smoke (source 6). Most analyzers do the math. The maker's bulletin also shows a stack loss table; its example is a 300 °F net stack at 4 percent O2 giving about 86.4 percent. Steady-state efficiency leaves out start and stop losses. Record smoke, draft readings, O2 or CO2, CO, net stack temperature and efficiency, as the state forms ask. The Connecticut form (source 2) also asks for nozzle size, pump pressure and a printout.

Worked example

Assumptions (made up for practice, not from any real unit): room temperature 70 °F; stack temperature 480 °F at steady state; O2 4.5 percent after backing off the air; smoke number 0.

  1. Net stack temperature = 480 − 70 = 410 °F.
  2. Air share of the gas that is not oxygen: 21 − 4.5 = 16.5.
  3. Excess air ≈ 4.5 ÷ 16.5 × 100 = 27.3, so about 27 percent.
  4. For comparison, at the earlier trace-smoke setting of 2.5 percent O2: 2.5 ÷ 18.5 × 100 = 13.5, about 14 percent. Backing off roughly doubled the excess air.
  5. Efficiency: look up 410 °F net and 4.5 percent O2 in the unit's stack loss chart, or read the analyzer. We do not give a number here because we have no sourced table value for 410 °F.

What odd readings can point to

  • High net stack temperature. Common suspects are a fouled heat exchanger (soot or scale), too much firing rate from the nozzle or pump pressure, or too much air. Compare against the unit's data plate and manual.
  • Smoke that will not clear. Check for a dirty or wrong nozzle, air leaks or water in the oil, a damaged flame retention head or combustion air problems. See Oil Burner Fuel Filters, Oil Lines and Air Leaks.
  • Positive overfire draft. The maker's bulletin says a slight negative pull removes combustion products from the chamber. Positive pressure there means they are not leaving freely. Look for a blocked heat exchanger or flue and stop before running further; see Oil Burner Puffback and Soot in the House.

Field card: Oil Burner Combustion Test Sequence

Warm, clean, then measure. Adjust, back off, retest, record.

  • Run to steady state; stack temperature stops rising.
  • Overfire draft about -0.02 in. w.c. unless the maker says otherwise; breech slightly stronger.
  • Smoke spot: 0 to 9 scale; one state form lists 1 or less.
  • Net stack temperature = stack temperature minus room temperature.
  • Excess air ≈ O2 ÷ (21 − O2) × 100.
  • Set trace smoke, then add air (about 1.5 points CO2 or 2 points O2) and recheck for zero smoke.
  • Check CO. Record every reading.
