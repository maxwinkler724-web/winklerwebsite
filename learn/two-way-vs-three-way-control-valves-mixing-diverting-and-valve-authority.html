Two-Way vs Three-Way Control Valves: Mixing, Diverting and Valve Authority

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A coil valve has one job: decide how much water the coil gets. A two-way valve does it by throttling the flow. A three-way valve does it by steering water through the coil or around it. Which one you have changes how the pump, the coil and the whole plant behave. This lesson covers the two types, how they are piped, why valve authority decides whether a valve actually controls, and how to size one with Cv.

Two-way valves: the flow actually changes

A two-way valve sits in series with the coil and throttles it. When the valve closes, flow in that branch drops. Bell & Gossett's variable primary flow manual (TEH-910A) notes that most coil valves in a primary-secondary chilled water system are two-way, so they cut flow through the coil and the pumps at part load, and it calls these variable volume systems. Johnson Controls' piping package catalog puts the closed position plainly: water cannot flow through the water coil. That makes two-way valves the natural match for variable-speed pumping, where the pump slows as valves close. The federal lab report on chiller plant optimization (PNNL-38309) says the same from the plant side: it describes converting three-way valves to two-way valves and removing bypass valves as a way to cut wasted water flow, and notes that the full benefit of variable-speed pumping may require valve changes at cooling coils.

Three-way valves: the flow is steered

A three-way valve has two inlets and one outlet or one inlet and two outlets. A modulating one sends part of the water through the coil and the rest through a bypass pipe. Johnson Controls says that when its three-way valve is de-energized, water cannot flow through the coil but is forced through the bypass port. Total flow in the branch stays roughly constant, so the pump sees little change. Honeywell's three-way valve data sheet separates the two bodies. A mixing valve receives flow at two ports and discharges mixed flow at the third. A diverting valve takes flow at one port and splits it to two outlets. Honeywell warns that mixing and diverting valves are not interchangeable and must not be piped backwards, and HPAC's selection guide warns against using a globe mixing valve for diverting service or the reverse, because pressure on the disc can slam it shut and cause loss of control.

Where each one is piped

For a coil bypass, Honeywell says the same function can come from a diverting valve upstream of the coil or a mixing valve downstream of it. It also says almost all new installations can use a mixing valve with a suitable mixing piping arrangement, and Johnson Controls says its factory three-way packages are piped as mixing valves. Some makers go further: Belimo's project planning notes call its characterised three-way valves mixing devices and say they must not be used as diverting valves. So the rule for a tech is to read the body markings and the maker's piping drawing. Do not decide from the word "three-way" alone.

Why three-way valves hurt delta T in variable-speed plants

Our own reading of the sources: a three-way bypass sends supply water straight back to the return without passing through a coil. At light load the return becomes a blend of coil water and untouched bypass water, so the return temperature falls toward the supply temperature and delta T shrinks. PNNL's report says reducing flow raises delta T and that delta T below about 5 °F is common nationwide and treated as a problem. A Kele engineering article on converting to all-variable flow lists, as step one, replacing air handler three-way valves and any two-way valves that do not close tightly, and closing or removing bypasses between supply and return. For the plant side of this story see Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T and Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained.

Flow characteristics: why coils get equal percentage

A valve's characteristic is the relationship between stem travel and flow. Equal percentage valves give the same percentage change in flow for each equal step of stem travel. A coil does not give heat output in proportion to flow: HPAC's guide says large flow reductions through a hot-water coil cause only small reductions in heat output, and that equal percentage valves are used to offset that. Belimo's planning notes say the valve should complement the coil's non-linear curve so heat output changes about in step with valve opening. Siemens' specification text requires equal percentage characteristics for valves serving coils and heat exchangers and linear for steam. Linear characteristics have a place too: the Belimo US sizing guide uses linear for the bypass port of a constant-flow three-way valve, and Honeywell's three-way valves are linear through each port.

Valve authority

Authority tells you how much of the circuit's pressure drop the valve takes. The version used in this lesson is the valve's pressure drop at full open, divided by that drop plus the drop of everything else in the controlled circuit. If the valve is a tiny share, then moving the stem barely changes the total resistance, and the valve is nearly useless at part stroke. Its equal percentage curve gets distorted toward quick-opening. Belimo's US guide says a value of 0.5 gives adequate response and higher is better; its European notes say above 0.5 is desired in everyday practice. Siemens' spec text asks for 0.25 to 0.5, so the makers do not agree on one number. Treat 0.5 as a common target and check your project documents. B&G adds the general point that control valves need an appropriate inherent characteristic and enough authority to limit distortion.

Cv sizing basics

Belimo defines Cv as the gallons per minute of water that flow through a valve with a 1 psi pressure loss. For water that gives gpm = Cv × √ΔP, or Cv = gpm ÷ √ΔP. Our balancing valve lesson shows the equation in more detail. Belimo gives rules of thumb too: about 4 psi at full open for modulating service (so Cv is about half the gpm), or 4 to 9 psi if the supply-to-return differential is unknown. Those are the maker's sizing aids, not a substitute for the project's design data.

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

A coil needs 40 gpm. The coil and its piping drop 6 psi at that flow.

  1. Our own: valve at 6 psi. Cv = 40 ÷ √6 = 40 ÷ 2.449 = 16.3. Authority = 6 ÷ (6 + 6) = 0.50.
  2. Our own: smaller 3 psi valve drop. Cv = 40 ÷ √3 = 40 ÷ 1.732 = 23.1. Authority = 3 ÷ (3 + 6) = 0.33. A larger valve passes the flow with less loss, but it takes a smaller share of the circuit drop. That puts it below the 0.5 target in the sources above and inside Siemens' 0.25 to 0.5 band.
  3. Our own: three-way on the same coil. The bypass leg should drop the same 6 psi at 40 gpm as the coil leg, so total flow stays steady as the valve swings. Balancing valve Cv = 40 ÷ √6 = 16.3. Setting it any more open sends extra flow around the coil, and any less starves the bypass.

In the real piping the three-way body has its own port drop, so the final setting is made by flow measurement, not from this arithmetic alone. Johnson Controls says its balance bypass valve needs manual adjustment and is not calibrated at the valve. Honeywell advises adjusting the balancing cock under actual operating conditions, aiming for equal pressure at the two inlets at midstroke. Belimo's planning notes say a balancing valve in the bypass is not required for the diverting circuit of its valve, so follow the maker's drawing for the valve you have.

What techs check

  • Stroke. Command the valve from closed to open and watch the stem or position indicator move fully and smoothly. Honeywell's data sheet says debris can lodge in the seat and stop proper closing.
  • Actuator signal. Compare the controller output with the actuator's position, and confirm the actuator wiring and signal type match what the maker specifies.
  • Leaking by. A valve that does not close tightly keeps feeding the coil or the bypass. The Kele article says replacements should close tight, and Siemens' spec lists seat leakage limits such as ANSI Class III for the three-way bypass path.
  • Piping. Confirm the three-way is the mixing or diverting body the drawing shows and is not piped backwards.

Field card: Two-way vs three-way control valves

Two-way changes flow. Three-way steers it.

  • Two-way: throttles the coil; suits variable-speed pumping.
  • Three-way: coil port plus bypass; branch flow stays roughly constant.
  • Mixing: two inlets, one outlet. Diverting: one inlet, two outlets. Not interchangeable.
  • Coils pair with equal percentage; bypass ports are linear.
  • Authority = valve drop ÷ (valve drop + circuit drop). Common target about 0.5.
  • Cv = gpm ÷ √ΔP. Example: 40 gpm at 6 psi gives Cv 16.3.
  • Check stroke, actuator signal, and leaking by.
