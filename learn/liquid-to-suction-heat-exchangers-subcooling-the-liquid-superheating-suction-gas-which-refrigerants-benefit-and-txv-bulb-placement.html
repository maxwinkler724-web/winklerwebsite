Liquid-to-Suction Heat Exchangers: Which Refrigerants Benefit and Where the TXV Bulb Goes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A liquid-to-suction heat exchanger (LSHX) is a small device, or sometimes just two soldered tubes, that lets warm liquid refrigerant hand heat to cold suction gas. It sounds like free efficiency, but research shows it helps some refrigerants, does little for others and hurts a few. It also changes where a TXV bulb has to go and how hot the discharge gets.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. Whether an exchanger belongs on a system, and how the valve must be piped around it, is the equipment maker's call. The research below comes from a national measurement lab, university papers and a valve maker's bulletin. The worked example is ours, built on government property tables.

What it does

The liquid line leaving the condenser is warm. The suction line leaving the evaporator is cold. Put them in thermal contact, ideally in counterflow so the two streams run in opposite directions, and heat moves from liquid to gas. The liquid gets subcooled a few more degrees. The suction gas gets superheated a few more degrees. Both lines carry the same mass flow of refrigerant, so the heat the liquid loses is the heat the gas gains (apart from heat leaking in from the room). The lesson on superheat and subcooling covers the two terms.

Why it helps some refrigerants and hurts others

The extra subcooling is useful: each pound of liquid arrives at the evaporator carrying less heat, so it can absorb more. The extra superheat is a cost: warmer suction gas is thinner, so the compressor moves fewer pounds per stroke. Which effect wins depends on the fluid.

  • National lab theory. A National Institute of Standards and Technology paper (Domanski, Didion and Doyle, 1994) evaluated the exchanger for many fluids. It found the benefit depends on operating conditions and on fluid properties, with heat capacity the most influential. Fluids that perform well in the basic cycle are only marginally affected, and the effect on efficiency and capacity may be positive or negative. Fluids that perform poorly in the basic cycle gain.
  • University simulation. A University of Wisconsin paper (Klein, Reindl and Brownell, 2000) found the exchanger beneficial for R-507A, R-134a, R-12, R-404A, R-290, R-407C, R-600 and R-410A when low-side pressure drop is small, and detrimental for R-22, R-32 and R-717 (ammonia). It ties the result to a ratio of latent heat to liquid heat capacity and critical temperature. Fluids with a small value of that ratio, meaning relatively little latent heat compared with their liquid heat capacity, are the ones that gain.
  • Practice. A 2014 Purdue conference paper on an R-290 air conditioner reports the exchanger is rarely used in R-22 and R-410A units because it adds circuit complexity for small gains, and measured a 5.3 percent capacity gain and 4.5 percent efficiency gain with R-290.

Takeaway: the answer is refrigerant and condition specific. The papers differ in method and conditions, so do not treat the lists as a purchasing rule. Use what the equipment maker built in.

Protecting against flash gas and floodback

Klein and colleagues list the exchanger's protective jobs: subcooling the liquid to avoid vapor forming ahead of the expansion device, and boiling off any liquid that carries out of the evaporator before it reaches the compressor. A valve maker's bulletin says a suction-liquid exchanger will usually not provide more than 20 F of subcooling on air conditioning systems, and when the goal is to stop flash gas it should sit near the receiver, before a vertical lift. See the lesson on liquid line pressure drop and flash gas and the lesson on floodback and slugging. The exchanger is a margin of safety, not a cure for overcharge or a feeding problem.

Effect on discharge temperature

Warmer suction gas means warmer gas leaving the compressor. In the Purdue R-290 work, the simulation showed discharge temperature rising from 68 C to 88 C as suction temperature went from 8 C to 28 C, and the test unit's discharge rose 25.4 C with the exchanger. Those are one refrigerant and one machine, but the direction holds generally. Check discharge line temperature after any exchanger is added or bypassed; see discharge line temperature as a diagnostic.

Where the TXV bulb goes

A valve maker's bulletin says to attach the bulb to a horizontal suction line at the evaporator outlet, never in or after a trap. With an exchanger in the suction line, our reasoning is that this puts the bulb upstream of the exchanger, on the line between the evaporator and the exchanger, as the diagram shows. The valve is meant to hold superheat at the evaporator outlet. If the bulb sat downstream, it would feel gas the exchanger already warmed by about 25 F in our example, think the evaporator was starving, and open too far. The evaporator would lose its superheat, and liquid could pass through. That is the floodback you bought the exchanger to avoid. The bulletin we read does not address bulb position relative to an exchanger directly, so follow the exchanger maker's piping drawing if it differs. For mounting position and the equalizer line, see TXV bulb placement, equalizers and adjusting superheat.

Worked example (ours): liquid 100 F in, 85 F out

This is our own calculation, not a measurement. Assumptions we chose: R-134a; liquid pressure about 1.0 MPa (145 psia), where saturation is 104.5 F, so the 100 F liquid is about 4.5 F subcooled before the exchanger; suction pressure 0.3 MPa (43.5 psia), where saturation is about 33 F; evaporator outlet 44 F; no heat gain from the room; no phase change on either side; the same compressor efficiency with and without the exchanger. Property values are from the National Institute of Standards and Technology Chemistry WebBook (isobaric tables, SI units), converted to U.S. units.

  1. Liquid properties: heat capacity is about 1.46 kJ/kg-K near 92 F (the table gives 1.45 at 305 K and 1.48 at 310 K). Converting, 1.46 × 0.2388 = 0.349 Btu/lb-F.
  2. Heat given up by the liquid: 0.349 × 15 F = about 5.2 Btu per pound. The table enthalpies agree: about 253 kJ/kg at 100 F and 241 kJ/kg at 85 F, a difference of 12.2 kJ/kg, which is 5.2 Btu/lb.
  3. Suction vapor heat capacity at 0.3 MPa is about 0.89 kJ/kg-K between 280 and 295 K, or 0.89 × 0.2388 = 0.213 Btu/lb-F.
  4. Same flow, so the gas gains the same 5.2 Btu/lb: temperature rise = 5.2 / 0.213 = about 24.6 F. Shortcut: 15 F × (0.349 / 0.213) = 24.6 F.
  5. Suction leaves at 44 + 25 = about 69 F. The tables confirm it: 404.5 + 12.2 = 416.7 kJ/kg lands near 293.7 K, which is about 69 F.
  6. Effectiveness (vapor-side rise over the largest possible difference, as Klein defines it): 24.6 / (100 − 44) = about 0.44.

The vapor warms by more degrees than the liquid cools because liquid refrigerant holds more heat per degree than thin vapor. A 15 F liquid drop makes a 25 F suction rise. Net effect in this example: refrigeration effect per pound rises about 8 percent (163.6 versus 151.4 kJ/kg), but the warmer gas is about 6 percent less dense (13.44 versus 14.3 kg/m3), so the net capacity gain is only about 1 to 2 percent in this simple comparison. That small, fluid-dependent gain matches the research above.

Field checks

  • Four temperatures. Strap insulated probes to bare tube at: liquid in, liquid out, suction in (after the bulb), suction out. Read all four together under steady running.
  • Energy balance. Suction rise should be the liquid drop times the ratio of liquid to vapor heat capacity (ours: 25 versus 15 F, a ratio of 1.64 for R-134a). Look up the ratio for your refrigerant and pressures. A suction rise much smaller than predicted points to a fouled or bypassed exchanger, poor probe contact, or little flow. A much larger one points to heat gain from the room through bare suction line.
  • No temperature cross. Liquid out must stay above suction in, and suction out below liquid in.
  • Bulb check. Confirm the bulb is ahead of the exchanger and compare bulb-line superheat with compressor-inlet superheat; the second should be higher.
  • Liquid-side bubbles. If the sight glass shows vapor after the exchanger, subcooling was lost to pressure drop before it got there.

Field card: Liquid-to-Suction Heat Exchangers

Liquid gives, gas takes, bulb goes first.

  • Liquid is subcooled, suction gas superheated, same heat both ways.
  • Benefit depends on the refrigerant; research lists some gaining and R-22, R-32 and R-717 losing.
  • Protects against flash gas and small amounts of liquid carryover.
  • Raises discharge temperature; check it.
  • TXV bulb goes on the suction line before the exchanger.
  • Example (ours): liquid 100 to 85 F means suction about 44 to 69 F.
  • Measure four temperatures and compare the balance.
