Draft Beer Systems for Refrigeration Techs: Keg Coolers, Glycol Trunk Lines and Foaming

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A bar or restaurant draft system is a refrigeration job with a pour at the end of it. The walk-in keg cooler, the glycol power pack and the insulated trunk line all exist to deliver beer to the faucet at the same temperature it had in the keg. When that fails, the customer sees foam, and the first call usually goes to the refrigeration tech.

What the industry guide says about temperature

The Brewers Association publishes a free Draught Beer Quality Manual. It says beer should be held between 34 F and 38 F, and it warns that any rise in beer temperature between the cooler and the faucet can cause dispensing problems such as foaming. That is the whole job in one sentence: hold the keg cold, then keep the beer cold all the way to the tap. A maker page on long-draw systems gives the same walk-in range, 34 F to 38 F. For the cooler itself, see Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics; the equipment is ordinary, but the setpoint matters more than usual.

Two ways beer gets from keg to tap

Direct draw. The keg box or walk-in sits close to the tower, so cold air does the work. The manual describes air-cooled towers as insulated, with cold air from the cooler circulating around the beer lines and shanks. Some keg boxes send air up the tower through corrugated tubing connected to the evaporator housing, and that air is typically colder than the keg temperature. If the cooler fan stops or the tubing comes loose, the tower warms and the first pours foam.

Long draw. When the keg cooler is far from the bar, air cannot reach. The manual notes that long-draw systems typically use glycol instead: chilled glycol runs in tubes alongside the beer lines to the faucet. One maker page describes runs "typically, running 25 feet or longer" and says trunk lines have been known to reach 750 feet. The beer in the keg stays cold from the walk-in air, while the glycol keeps the lines cold on the way.

The glycol power pack and the trunk line

The power pack is a small self-contained chiller: compressor, a glycol bath, and one or two recirculating pumps. The manual says polyethylene tubing carries glycol to and from the power pack. The beer lines and glycol lines travel together inside a bundled trunk line, wrapped in an insulating cover. A maker page lists the parts as a vapor barrier, foil tape and insulation, and some makers add a barrier jacket.

Two numbers from maker pages help with expectations. One says the coolant is cycled between 28 F and 34 F by an adjustable thermostat. Another power pack page gives a mix of 1 gallon of glycol to 2 1/2 parts water and identifies the glycol as propylene glycol; it says nothing about bath temperature. Mix ratios differ by product, so follow the maker's label. For freeze protection and testing concepts, see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops, and for the fermenter side of the same idea, Brewery and Winery Glycol Chillers. We do not repeat that glycol strength material here.

Why warm lines or a warm cooler make foam

Dissolved CO2 depends on both temperature and pressure. The manual states that if either varies, carbonation levels change. In our own plain-language reading, warmer beer at the same pressure holds less dissolved gas, so gas breaks out in the line and at the faucet and the glass fills with foam. The practical steps for a tech:

  • Check the walk-in setpoint and the actual air temperature near the kegs. A cooler running above the 34 F to 38 F range warms every keg at once.
  • Check that the power pack is running and that the pumps are circulating. A tripped pump leaves the trunk line warming toward room temperature.
  • Check the trunk line for wet or crushed insulation, an open vapor barrier or a section that sits against a hot pipe. The manual describes barrier tubing bundles for long draw and says vinyl and polyethylene should not be used in long-draw bundles.
  • In our experience, a warm first pour that clears after a few seconds points to a warm tower or line stub rather than a warm keg.

Gas matters only as it affects this. The manual says CO2 gas leaks cause pressure drops that lead to foamy beer, and that pressure set too high or too low causes problems. Blended gas, typically 25 to 30 percent CO2 with the rest nitrogen, is used for nitro beers, and the manual says nitrogen lets you raise system pressure without overcarbonating. Gas settings belong to the draft installer; the refrigeration tech's contribution is a steady, cold system. Line cleaning is also the installer's task and its schedule is outside this lesson, but ask when the system was last cleaned so you know what you are looking at.

Worked example: heat gain into a trunk line

This example is our own. Every input below is an assumption, not a cited figure, so replace each one with the maker's data for the real system.

  • Assumption 1: trunk line length from walk-in to bar tower is 120 ft.
  • Assumption 2: heat gain through a well-insulated trunk line is 10 Btu/h per foot. This is our own planning number, not from a source.
  • Assumption 3: the recirculating pump draws 150 W, and all of that becomes heat in the glycol (conservative). Convert: 150 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = about 512 Btu/h.
  • Assumption 4: the power pack is rated 3,600 Btu/h. A maker page for a two-pump air-cooled unit lists 3,600 BTU with no stated conditions, so we assume it is a rating at mild conditions and take 20 percent off for a hot equipment room.

Step 1, line heat gain: 120 ft x 10 Btu/h per ft = 1,200 Btu/h.

Step 2, add pump heat: 1,200 + 512 = 1,712 Btu/h total load.

Step 3, derated capacity: 3,600 x 0.80 = 2,880 Btu/h.

Step 4, margin: 2,880 - 1,712 = 1,168 Btu/h spare, so the load is 1,712 / 2,880 = about 59 percent of derated capacity. On the nameplate rating alone the load is 1,712 / 3,600 = about 48 percent.

What this leaves out: pulling down warm beer after a keg change or a busy night, a damaged trunk line, and fittings at the tower. A bar that pours steadily all night adds beer heat that this simple total does not include. If the insulation is wet or missing, the 10 Btu/h per foot assumption could be far too low and the margin disappears. That is why the first check on a long-draw complaint is the trunk line, not the compressor.

CO2 leaks and low oxygen in cooler rooms

The manual says breathing high concentrations of CO2 can be deadly, that CO2 tanks should never be kept inside the walk-in, and that concentrations of 10 percent by volume or more can cause unconsciousness or death. It recommends CO2 monitors and alarms in enclosed areas and says to vent beer pump exhaust outside the walk-in or building. OSHA lists a permissible exposure limit of 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average and 30,000 ppm as a short-term limit; NIOSH lists the same two numbers and an immediately dangerous level of 40,000 ppm. Percentages are our conversion: 5,000 ppm is 0.5 percent, 30,000 ppm is 3 percent and 40,000 ppm is 4 percent.

A basement cooler room is a closed space where, in our judgment, a heavy gas leak can build up. OSHA's permit-required confined space rule for general industry (29 CFR 1910.146) calls an atmosphere below 19.5 percent oxygen by volume hazardous, and a space that has the potential to contain a hazardous atmosphere can be a permit space. Treat a keg room with a gas leak the same way: do not enter to look for the leak, ventilate first, and follow the site's entry procedure. The same oxygen-depletion thinking, with monitors and entry checks, is covered in Liquid Nitrogen and Dry Ice Storage Rooms.

Evaporator problems in the same cooler, such as icing, are in Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Defrost. A frozen coil starves airflow and can warm the kegs, so rule it out when kegs run warm.

Field card: Draft Beer Systems for Refrigeration Techs

Cold keg, cold line, cold tower, and never go in a leaking room.

  • Industry guide: hold beer at 34 F to 38 F; any warming before the faucet can foam.
  • Direct draw uses cooler air to the tower; long draw uses glycol alongside the beer lines.
  • Maker page: glycol cycled 28 F to 34 F; follow the maker's glycol mix.
  • Foam first checks: cooler temperature, power pack and pumps running, trunk line insulation.
  • Our example: 120 ft x 10 Btu/h per ft + 512 Btu/h pump = 1,712 Btu/h (assumptions).
  • CO2: 5,000 ppm 8-hour limit, 30,000 ppm short-term; ventilate before entry.
