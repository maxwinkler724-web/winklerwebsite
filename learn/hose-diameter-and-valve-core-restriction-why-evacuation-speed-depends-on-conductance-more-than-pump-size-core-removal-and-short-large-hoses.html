Hose Diameter and Valve Core Restriction: Why Evacuation Speed Depends on Conductance More Than Pump Size

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A tech buys a bigger vacuum pump and the job barely gets faster. The reason is usually not the pump. At deep vacuum, the gas has to squeeze through a hose and past a valve core on the way to the pump, and those parts set the speed. This lesson explains that idea in plain terms and works one example with our own numbers.

Check the equipment instructions

Check the equipment instructions. Target vacuum levels, hose and tool choices and any core-removal steps come from the equipment maker or the job specification. This page explains why flow behaves the way it does. It is general education, not an installation procedure. For the target level and the hold test, see the related lessons below.

Pump speed is not system speed

A pump rating in cubic feet per minute (cfm) describes how fast the pump itself moves gas at its own inlet. Your system does not sit at that inlet. It sits at the far end of a hose, a core and a few fittings. Vacuum engineers handle this with the idea of conductance: how easily gas flows through a part, in the same units as pump speed. A wide, short passage has high conductance. A narrow, long passage has low conductance.

When parts are in a line, the speed the system actually sees is found by adding the inverses. A community-college vacuum technology article gives it as 1/S = 1/Sp + 1/C, where S is the effective speed at the chamber, Sp is the pump speed and C is the conductance of the connection. Its own example is a 150 liter-per-second pump behind a short tube whose conductance is about 304 liters per second. The effective speed at the chamber comes out near 100 liters per second, and a pump twice as large gives only about 151. A university lab handout makes the same point more sharply: a very large pump behind a very small conductance gives an effective speed close to that small conductance.

The takeaway is that the slowest link in the chain controls the result. Adding pump size to a restricted line is like putting a bigger engine in a car that is stuck behind a narrow gate.

Why the hose diameter matters so much

At the low pressures of a deep vacuum, the gas molecules rarely hit each other. They mostly bounce off the walls. The university lab handout gives the conductance of a round tube in this regime as proportional to the diameter cubed divided by the length. A national laboratory technical note gives the same form for a long round tube, and says this conductance does not depend on pressure. Two lessons follow:

  • Diameter counts in the third power. A hose that is 1.5 times wider (1/4 inch to 3/8 inch) passes about 3.4 times more gas, since 1.5 x 1.5 x 1.5 = 3.375.
  • Length counts only once. Cutting a hose in half doubles its conductance. That helps, but widening it helps more. The handout says the line length is not nearly as important as the diameter.

One caution: the full story changes as pressure rises, because the relation applies at the low pressures of a deep vacuum and changes at higher pressure. For that reason we use the relation only to compare options, not to predict absolute times.

The valve core is the pinch point

Service ports hold a Schrader valve core. A hose depressor holds the core open, but the passage around the core stays small and crowded. A maker's tool sheet for a core-removal set says that valve cores are highly restrictive, blocking up to 90 percent of flow, and that without core removal a pump's maximum flow is limited to about 0.2 cfm. That sheet is a maker's claim without test data, so treat it as a sign of the size of the effect and not as a measured value. A maker's article on evacuation tools makes the same practical point: removing the core lets you evacuate through unrestricted lines, which it says gives a faster and higher vacuum.

That is why a valve core removal tool matters more than a larger pump. The tool lets you take the core out of the flow path for the evacuation. The steps and seals differ by tool, so follow the tool instructions. For how the cores seat and why caps matter, see the service valves lesson linked below.

Hose choice in the field

  • Shorter is better. The maker's article says long hoses slow the process.
  • Wider is better. The same article says to choose the widest hose you can, even if the system has quarter-inch fittings, and for its pump suggests a 3/8 inch hose with quarter-inch fittings at the pump end. The tool sheet describes a 1/2 inch inside diameter hose for maximum flow.
  • Put the micron gauge at the far end. The gauge should read the system, not the pump side of the restriction. The decay test lesson covers this.

Rated cfm versus real speed at low microns

The cfm rating describes the pump, not the line behind it. Add the restriction of the hose and core, and the speed the system actually sees can be a small fraction of the number on the label. The maker claim above, about 0.2 cfm without core removal, shows the scale of the gap. We did not find a published pump speed curve in the sources we read, so we give none here. The label tells you about the pump, not about your job. A clean, healthy pump with fresh oil is still needed to reach depth; see the pump care lesson.

Our own worked example: two hose setups, one system

This example is our own. It uses a deliberately simple model, our own simplification, with assumptions we chose to show the pattern. It is not a measurement and not a maker figure.

Our simplifications.

  1. Each hose's conductance is proportional to D³/L (diameter cubed over length), from the deep-vacuum relation above. We use nominal hose sizes as if they were inside diameters. Real hoses have smaller bores.
  2. Parts in a line combine by 1/S = 1/Sp + 1/C1 + 1/C2.
  3. Time for a pump-down stage is t = (V / S) x ln(P start / P end). This ignores moisture and outgassing, which make real jobs slower.

Our assumptions.

  • System volume V = 20 liters. Pressure stage: 5,000 microns down to 500 microns, so ln(10) = 2.303.
  • We set the scale so that one unit of conductance is 0.05 liters per second (about 0.1 cfm, the same order as the maker's 0.2 cfm claim above). The 1/4 inch, 6 ft hose is 1.00 unit.
  • With the core in place, the core restricts like 1.00 unit (our assumed value).
  • The pump delivers 10 units (0.5 L/s, about 1.1 cfm) at its inlet at depth. We also test a pump with twice that.

Hose conductance, relative to Setup A. Setup B hose is 3/8 inch and 3 ft. Ratio = (0.375/0.25)³ x (6/3) = 3.375 x 2 = 6.75 units.

Setup A: 1/4 inch, 6 ft hose, core in. 1/S = 1/10 + 1/1.00 + 1/1.00 = 2.10, so S = 0.476 units = 0.0238 L/s. Time = (20 / 0.0238) x 2.303 = about 1,930 seconds, or 32 minutes. With the pump doubled to 20 units: 1/S = 0.05 + 1 + 1 = 2.05, S = 0.488 units, time about 31.5 minutes. The doubling saved about 2.5 percent.

Setup B: 3/8 inch, 3 ft hose, core removed. 1/S = 1/10 + 1/6.75 = 0.1 + 0.148 = 0.248, so S = 4.03 units = 0.201 L/s. Time = (20 / 0.201) x 2.303 = about 229 seconds, or 3.8 minutes. With the doubled pump: 1/S = 0.05 + 0.148 = 0.198, S = 5.05 units, time about 3.0 minutes.

Result. Setup B is about 8.5 times faster than Setup A with the same pump (1,930 / 229 = 8.4 to 8.5). The relative time for B is about 0.12 of A. If the core were less restrictive than we assumed, for example 2.00 units, the same arithmetic gives A near 1/S = 0.1 + 1 + 0.5 = 1.6 and B stays the same, so B is about 6.4 times faster. The size of the gain depends on our assumed values. The direction does not: fixing the line does more than doubling the pump.

What to remember on the job

  • If a job is slow, check the line before you blame or replace the pump.
  • Rank the fixes: remove the core with a proper tool, shorten the hose, widen the hose, then think about pump size.
  • A bigger pump through 1/4 inch hoses and cores barely helps, because the restriction is in series with the pump.
  • Our worked example is a model to show the pattern, not a time you should expect on a real job.

Field card: Hose Diameter and Valve Core Restriction

The slowest link in the line sets the speed, not the pump label.

  • Effective speed: 1/S = 1/Sp + 1/C. A small C caps S.
  • Deep-vacuum hose conductance goes with D cubed over L: wider helps most, shorter helps next.
  • The valve core is a major pinch point; a core-removal tool takes it out of the path.
  • Rated cfm describes the pump inlet, not what the system sees at low microns.
  • Our example: a 3/8 in, 3 ft hose with cores out was about 8.5 times faster than a 1/4 in, 6 ft hose with cores in, using our own assumptions.
  • Follow the equipment maker for targets, tools and steps.
