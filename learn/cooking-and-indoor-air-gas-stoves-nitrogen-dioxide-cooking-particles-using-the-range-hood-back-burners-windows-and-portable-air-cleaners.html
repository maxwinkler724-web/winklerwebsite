Cooking and Indoor Air: Gas Stoves, NO2, Particles and the Range Hood

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Cooking is one of the few daily activities that puts pollution straight into the room where we breathe. This lesson covers what comes off a stove, why the range hood is the first tool to reach for, what to do when there is no hood that vents outdoors, and why a portable air cleaner only does half the job.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. This page is general education built from government and national-lab pages read in 2026. It is not medical advice. If someone in your home has asthma or another lung condition, ask their clinician. Follow the appliance and hood manuals, and ask the local building department what applies to your house.

What cooking releases

Two different kinds of pollutant come off a kitchen range, and they need different fixes.

  • Fine particles, from any stove. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Berkeley Lab) news page says both gas and electric burners release fine and ultrafine particles, and the lab considers them potentially very harmful because they can enter the lungs. A second lab page says anything with a red-hot element makes particles, including ovens and toasters, and that frying and broiling make more.
  • Gases, from gas burners. The EPA lists unvented combustion appliances such as gas stoves among the main indoor sources of nitrogen dioxide (NO2). It says NO2 mainly irritates the eyes, nose, throat and airways, and that homes with gas stoves often have indoor levels above outdoor levels. A Berkeley Lab researcher says gas burners almost always make significant NO2, while electric burners make only small amounts and no carbon monoxide (CO). The EPA adds that a badly adjusted gas stove can put out much more CO than a properly adjusted one.

The Berkeley Lab news page reports that in California, about 60 percent of homes that cook with gas at least once a week can reach pollutant levels that would not be legal outdoors. Treat that as a state-level estimate, not a prediction for any one house. We cannot tell you your own exposure, but the fixes below are cheap and low risk.

Use the range hood every time

The EPA says to install and use an exhaust fan vented outdoors over gas stoves, and that cooking is a short activity that can make high pollutant levels, so ventilate as much as possible while you do it. The Berkeley Lab advice page points out that people cannot sense most of these pollutants, so they skip the hood when they need it. A lab tip sheet suggests running the hood on high during cooking and for about 15 minutes afterward.

A hood that you will not switch on does nothing. The Berkeley Lab advice is to pick a quiet one you will actually use. For how to size a hood and tell ducted from recirculating models, see Kitchen Range Hoods at Home: Ducted vs Recirculating and Sizing. We do not repeat sizing here.

Back burners first

A hood catches what rises into it. Berkeley Lab tests of a gas range under three hood designs found back burners were captured better than front ones. At top fan speed with the hood against the wall, two hoods captured over 90 percent of back-burner exhaust, while front-burner capture was 73 to 78 percent for those two and 46 to 63 percent for a shallow third design. A separate Berkeley Lab report on over-the-range microwave hoods found about 40 to 85 percent capture for front burners and 60 to 100 percent for back burners, rising with airflow. A lab news page also notes that in installed hoods, less than half of the gas burner pollutants were removed in many cases, so capture varies a lot.

The practical habits: put the pot on a back burner when you can, and run the fan at a higher speed when you use the front. Berkeley Lab says cooking on one back burner at low fan speed captured about 50 to 70 percent.

No ducted hood? Open a window or run a bath fan

A recirculating hood pulls air through a filter and puts it back in the room. A Berkeley Lab researcher says such a hood does not remove the pollutants, and suggests using another exhaust fan such as a bathroom fan, or opening windows. The EPA likewise says fans that vent outdoors remove contaminants directly from the room and that open windows and doors bring in more outdoor air when the weather permits, with care if there are outdoor smoke or other pollution sources nearby.

A Berkeley Lab study of two filtered recirculating hoods found that they substantially reduced particles at all sizes, with larger reductions for gas cooking. That study does not report NO2, so we cannot say these hoods help with that gas. Particle filters are not designed for gases. Our own reading of these sources: a recirculating hood is better than nothing for particles, but do not count on it for NO2 or CO.

See Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting and Moisture Control for how a bath fan is rated and ducted.

Small kitchens and apartments

The sources we read do not give separate numbers for apartments, so this part is our own reasoning. A small kitchen has less air to dilute a given release, so the level climbs faster. Apartments often lack a hood that vents outdoors, and a bath fan or an open window may be the only exhaust you have. If the kitchen is open to the living area, the pollution spreads there too. Keep the bath fan running while you cook and for a while after, crack a window on the far side of the home if the weather allows, and keep doors between the kitchen and bedrooms closed.

Portable air cleaners: particles only

The EPA guide to air cleaners says the CADR rating system is for particles only, that most gas-removal filters have no widely used rating system, and that air cleaners cannot remove all pollutants. The EPA improving-indoor-air page says air cleaners are generally not designed to remove gaseous pollutants. So a portable unit can help with cooking particles, but it is not a fix for NO2 or CO. Treat it as a backup to ventilation and source control, not a replacement. For what works and what is hype, read Air Purifiers and Whole-House Air Cleaners: What Actually Works.

Never heat the house with a gas oven

The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) carbon monoxide page says: never use a gas range or oven for heating. If the furnace is out, see Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages for safer options. CPSC also advises battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms outside sleeping areas; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Worked example (our own): how fast does a hood clear the air?

This is a simple well-mixed box estimate of our own, not a measurement. It treats the kitchen as one box where pollutant is spread evenly. Real kitchens are not evenly mixed, and a hood also captures pollution right at the stove, so the box is only a way to feel the effect of air changes.

Assumptions (ours, chosen for round numbers): kitchen 12 ft by 14 ft with an 8 ft ceiling, a hood that really moves 200 cfm outdoors, replacement air arriving from elsewhere in the home, and no source after the cooking stops.

  1. Volume: 12 x 14 x 8 = 1,344 ft3.
  2. Air moved per hour: 200 cfm x 60 = 12,000 ft3 per hour.
  3. Air changes per hour: ACH = 12,000 / 1,344 = 8.93.
  4. Time constant = 1 / ACH = 0.112 h, which is 6.7 minutes. After one time constant the level falls to e^-1, about 37 percent, of where it started.
  5. Level after time t = start x e^(-ACH x t). After 15 minutes (0.25 h): e^(-8.93 x 0.25) = e^-2.23 = 0.107, about 11 percent left.
  6. Time to reach 10 percent: ln(10) / 8.93 = 0.258 h, about 15.5 minutes.

Compare other airflows in the same kitchen: at 100 cfm, ACH is 4.46, the time constant is 13.4 minutes and 10 percent takes about 31 minutes. At a 50 cfm bath fan, ACH is 2.23, the time constant is 26.9 minutes and 10 percent takes about 62 minutes. With only a leaky-house rate of 0.5 ACH (also assumed), 10 percent would take 4.6 hours. A recirculating hood moves air but removes no gas, so for gases its ACH in this model is close to zero.

Lesson: doubling the airflow halves the time. In the real world capture at the stove matters even more, which is why the hood is turned on before the pan heats.

Field card: Cooking and Indoor Air

Particles from any stove, NO2 and CO from gas, and the hood is the first tool.

  • All stoves make fine particles; gas burners also make NO2 and CO.
  • Run a hood that vents outdoors every time, and for about 15 minutes after.
  • Back burners get captured better than front burners.
  • No ducted hood: open a window or run the bath fan.
  • Recirculating hoods do not remove gases.
  • Portable air cleaners handle particles only.
  • Never heat the house with a gas range or oven.
  • Box model: time constant = 1/ACH; 37% left after one, about 10% after 2.3.
