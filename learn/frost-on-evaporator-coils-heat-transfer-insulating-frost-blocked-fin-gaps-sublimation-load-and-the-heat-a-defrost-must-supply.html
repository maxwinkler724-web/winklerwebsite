Frost on Evaporator Coils as a Heat Transfer Problem

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Frost is a heat transfer problem in three parts. It is a poor conductor between the air and the cold metal, it fills the gaps between the fins, and each pound of it is water vapor that gave up a lot of heat to become ice. Then the defrost has to put heat back in, and most of that heat does not go where you want it. This lesson puts numbers on each part.

Scope. Defrost operation is in Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat, normal ice versus a problem is in Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter, and timers and termination are in Defrost Timers and Termination Thermostats. Specific heat and heat of fusion basics are in Sensible vs Latent Heat. This page is the heat transfer physics underneath them.

When frost forms

Frost needs two things at once: a surface below the dew point of the air touching it, so vapor leaves the air, and a surface below freezing, so what lands turns to ice. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory literature review on defrost technologies says water vapor condenses and freezes when evaporator surfaces are below the dew point of the air, and puts heat pump outdoor-coil frosting in outdoor air from roughly -7 to 5 °C (about 19 to 41 °F) at relative humidity above about 65 percent. That is how a heat pump can frost on a damp 38 °F morning: the air is above freezing, but the coil, running colder than the air to absorb heat, is below 32 °F. A walk-in freezer coil is always far below freezing, so the question there is how much moisture the air brings in.

Frost is an insulator

Heat has to travel from the air, through whatever is on the surface, into the metal and on into the refrigerant. Frost sits first in line. A national laboratory preprint reviewing frost models lists three published equations for frost thermal conductivity as a function of frost density, and says the frost layer acts as extra insulation because it is porous. We ran them at an assumed frost density of 100 kg/m³ (our assumption; real density varies) and got about 0.11, 0.16 and 0.10 W/m-K (our arithmetic). Georgia State University's HyperPhysics conductivity table gives the comparison:

Frost conducts roughly 10 to 16 times worse than solid ice and 1,300 to 2,000 times worse than aluminum (our arithmetic). Resistance to conduction through a flat layer is thickness divided by conductivity. Our own illustration: 3 mm of frost at an assumed 0.10 W/m-K is 0.003 / 0.10 = 0.03 m²-K/W, the same as 0.003 x 205 / 0.10 = 6.15 m of aluminum. The fin metal is not the bottleneck; the frost is. It is the series-resistance idea from Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient U and Fouling Factors, with frost playing the fouling.

Direction matters. Heat flows warm to cold: box air, frost surface, through the frost, fin, tube wall, boiling refrigerant. Heat flow equals temperature difference divided by resistance (see Conduction, Convection and Radiation), so with more frost in that chain, either the metal runs colder or the coil moves less heat.

Frost blocks airflow between the fins

Conduction is only half the damage. The Oak Ridge review says frost hinders airflow and raises the pressure drop through the coil, so capacity drops. The laboratory preprint says excessive buildup can block the air passage, that air shifts toward the less blocked parts of the coil, and that frosting and defrosting cycles can cost up to 13 percent of seasonal heat pump efficiency (citing a published study). Our own geometry check: a coil with 4 fins per inch has fins about 25.4 / 4 = 6.35 mm apart, center to center. Frost grows on both facing fins, and two 3 mm layers use 6 mm of that gap, leaving a slit of about a third of a millimeter before you subtract the fin metal. For coil sizing and box humidity, see Evaporator Design TD in Walk-In Coolers.

Frost adds a latent load: sublimation

Water vapor that lands on a surface below freezing goes straight to ice, and the refrigerant has to carry away the heat that vapor gave up. A National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) paper on ice gives 2,500.8 J/g for vaporization and 333.4 J/g for fusion at 0 °C. Adding them gives about 2,834 J/g for sublimation, or 2,834 / 2.326 = about 1,218 Btu per pound (our arithmetic, with the NIST conversion 1 Btu/lb = 2.326 kJ/kg). Vapor going to liquid dew would give up about 1,075 Btu/lb (2,500.8 / 2.326), and melting the frost later takes just 144. This heat is part of the coil's latent load and is already inside the infiltration and moisture terms in Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Box Load Calculation; it is not an extra charge. The point is the ratio: forming a pound of frost loads the coil with about 8.5 times the heat it takes to melt it (1,218 / 144, our arithmetic).

What a defrost has to pay for

The Oak Ridge review lists where defrost heat goes: warming the coil metal, melting the frost, warming the meltwater, evaporating water left on the coil, and heating the surroundings. It quotes an estimate that only 15 to 25 percent of hot gas defrost heat goes into melting frost, says only a portion of electric heater heat is used for melting on a display case, and reports a maximum reverse cycle defrosting efficiency of about 60 percent in one test. A Purdue conference paper on a small reversible CO2 automotive test coil (not a walk-in) found that early in defrost most energy went to warming the coil metal and melting frost, with the frost gone in the first 20 to 35 seconds; later, meltwater and heat loss took a growing share. For hot gas versus electric, see Hot Gas Defrost in Commercial Refrigeration.

Worked example (our own): one defrost of a walk-in freezer coil

This is our own example. It is a made-up coil, not a measurement of any real unit.

Assumptions (all ours): 12 lb of frost between defrosts; coil and frost at -10 °F at the start; defrost ends with the metal at 50 °F; 18 lb of aluminum fins and 14 lb of copper tubing; electric heaters of 3,000 W running 20 minutes. Property values: ice 2.09, aluminum 0.900 and copper 0.385 J/g-°C from University of Massachusetts Amherst chemistry tables, divided by 4.1868 to give 0.50, 0.215 and 0.092 Btu/lb-°F (our arithmetic); heat of fusion 144 Btu/lb (80 cal/g on the HyperPhysics phase-change page, times 1.8; NIST's 333.4 J/g is 143.3, within half a percent).

  1. Warm the frost from -10 to 32 °F: 12 lb x 0.50 x 42 °F = 252 Btu.
  2. Melt it: 12 lb x 144 = 1,728 Btu.
  3. Warm the aluminum, -10 to 50 °F (60 °F rise): 18 x 0.215 x 60 = 232 Btu.
  4. Warm the copper, same rise: 14 x 0.092 x 60 = 77 Btu.
  5. Total heat the coil needs: 252 + 1,728 + 232 + 77 = about 2,290 Btu. Melting is 75 percent of it, coil metal 14 percent, warming the frost 11 percent.
  6. Heater output: 3,000 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = 10,236 Btu/h; over 20 minutes (one third of an hour) that is 3,412 Btu, which is 1 kWh. The 3.412 comes from the NIST factor 0.2930711 W per Btu/h (our arithmetic).
  7. Heat not needed for the frost or metal: 3,412 - 2,290 = about 1,120 Btu, or 33 percent of the heater output. It goes to the box air and surfaces, the drain pan and line, and the meltwater that runs away, none of which we counted.

Two more results. If every Btu went into the coil, the job would take 2,290 / 10,236 = 0.224 h, about 13.4 minutes; real heaters lose heat, so 20 minutes may be reasonable, but each minute after the ice is gone adds 3,000 W x 1 min = about 171 Btu to the box. After the defrost, refrigeration must remove the 1,120 Btu that leaked into the box plus the 309 Btu stored in the warm metal (232 + 77), about 1,430 Btu. At an assumed four defrosts a day, the heaters alone use 4 x 365 x 1 kWh = 1,460 kWh a year in this made-up case.

Why defrost energy and timing matter

  • Too late: thicker frost means more resistance, less airflow and less capacity, and more ice to melt next time.
  • Too long: once the ice is gone, heat only warms the box and coil; in the example each wasted minute is about 171 Btu to pull back out.
  • Too early or cut short: a lightly frosted coil still pays for 309 Btu of metal warming, and ice left in the fin gaps means the next cycle starts partly blocked.
  • Less frost to start with: frost grows with the moisture coming in, so door discipline and tight gaskets cut the sublimation load and the frost together.

Field card: Frost as a heat transfer problem

Frost insulates, blocks and loads the coil; defrost heat mostly goes elsewhere.

  • Frost needs a surface below the dew point and below 32 °F.
  • Frost conducts about 0.1 to 0.16 W/m-K (assumed density 100 kg/m³), far below ice at 1.6 and aluminum at 205.
  • Heat flows air to frost to fin to tube to refrigerant; more frost means a bigger drop across the frost.
  • Frost narrows or closes the gaps between fins and cuts airflow.
  • Sublimation is about 1,218 Btu/lb; melting is 144 Btu/lb.
  • Our example: 12 lb of frost plus 18 lb aluminum and 14 lb copper needs about 2,290 Btu; a 20 minute, 3 kW heater supplies 3,412 Btu.
  • Leftover heat goes to the box; each extra minute of a 3 kW heater is about 171 Btu.
