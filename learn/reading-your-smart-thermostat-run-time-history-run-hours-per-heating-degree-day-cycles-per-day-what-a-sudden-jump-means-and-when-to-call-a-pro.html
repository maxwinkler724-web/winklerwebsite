Reading Your Smart Thermostat Run-Time History: Run Hours per Degree Day and What a Jump Means

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most smart thermostats keep a log of how many hours the heating or cooling ran each day. That log is a free diagnostic tool, but only if you compare days fairly. A cold day should run longer than a mild day, so the raw hours alone tell you little. This lesson shows how to divide run hours by heating degree days so that weather drops out, what a sudden jump on a mild day usually points to, and when to stop looking and call a pro.

Why raw run hours mislead

Run time follows the weather. On a cold day the house loses heat faster, so the equipment has to run a larger share of every hour. If you only look at the bar chart and see a tall bar, you cannot tell whether the system is struggling or whether it was simply a cold day. Our lesson on how long a system should run covers that load-versus-capacity idea in detail, so we will not repeat it here. This page adds one step: put a number on the weather so you can divide it out.

Heating degree days as the weather yardstick

The U.S. Energy Information Administration explains that a day's mean temperature is the high plus the low, divided by 2, and that in the United States heating degree days are counted against a 65 F base. Its example is a day with a 29 F mean, which gives 36 heating degree days (65 minus 29). Our own plain version: one degree day is one degree of cold held for one day. Our heating degree days lesson explains the base, the balance temperature and where to get daily figures, so read that for the background. Many weather sites and some utility bills list daily or monthly degree days for your area. If you only have the day's high and low, you can work it out yourself with the averaging step above.

The number to track: run hours per degree day

For each day, divide the thermostat's run hours by that day's heating degree days. Our own reasoning is that a house with a steady heat loss per degree and equipment of steady output should produce a roughly constant ratio, because both the heat loss and the run time scale with how cold it is. A house that gives 0.30 run hours per degree day in January should give about 0.30 in February, give or take. Keep these cautions in mind, all of which are our own judgment and not measured rules:

  • Use days when the setpoint did not change. Setbacks and recovery periods distort a single day.
  • Skip days with strong wind, a lot of sun, a party or open doors. Free heat and extra loss both shift the ratio.
  • Very mild days (under about 5 degree days) give a ratio that swings wildly because the numbers are small. Compare several days or a week at a time.
  • This works for heating. For cooling, the same idea works with cooling degree days, but humidity adds load that degree days do not capture.

What you want is a baseline: a week or two of normal days, written down, so you have something to compare against later. A sudden step up in the ratio, one that stays up from day to day, is the signal. A one-day spike is more likely weather or a door left open.

Cycles per day and run length

Many thermostats also show how many times the system started. Divide the run hours by the cycle count to get the average run length. Our run-time lesson gives the benchmarks. The key reading here is how the two numbers move together. Run hours per degree day up while cycles per day fall means each run got longer: the system is taking more time to do the same job, which fits restricted airflow, lost capacity, or a weaker heat source. Cycles per day up with short runs fits short cycling, a different problem covered in our furnace short cycling lesson.

Days 1 to 7. Degree days: 30 + 24 + 18 + 36 + 12 + 20 + 26 = 166. Run hours: 9.2 + 7.3 + 5.6 + 10.8 + 3.7 + 6.1 + 7.9 = 50.6. Ratio: 50.6 / 166 = 0.305 run hours per degree day.

Days 8 to 14. Degree days: 28 + 22 + 14 + 32 + 10 + 18 + 24 = 148. Run hours: 12.1 + 9.6 + 6.2 + 13.8 + 4.5 + 7.9 + 10.4 = 64.5. Ratio: 64.5 / 148 = 0.436.

The jump. 0.436 / 0.305 = 1.43, about 43 percent more run time for the same weather. At the old ratio, the 148 degree days of the second week would have needed 148 x 0.305 = 45.1 hours. The system actually ran 64.5, so 64.5 - 45.1 = 19.4 extra hours. The mildest day shows it plainly: day 12 had 10 degree days, so the old ratio predicts 10 x 0.305 = 3.05 hours, and the log says 4.5.

Now add the cycle counts we assumed: an average of 18 starts per day in week one and 11 per day in week two. Average run length is 7.23 h x 60 / 18 = 24 minutes before, and 9.21 h x 60 / 11 = 50 minutes after. Longer runs, fewer starts, and a ratio up by about 43 percent.

What a sudden, lasting jump suggests

A step like this does not name a cause. It narrows the list. These are the usual suspects, in the order a homeowner can check them:

  • Dirty filter. A federal consumer page on maintaining an air conditioner says dirty filters reduce airflow and efficiency, and that swapping a dirty filter for a clean one can cut energy use by 5 to 15 percent. Check this first; it costs little. See how often to change your air filter.
  • Low refrigerant charge. A federal heat pump page lists low airflow, leaky ducts and incorrect refrigerant charge among the things that reduce performance and efficiency. A system that has lost refrigerant has a leak, and that is a job for a licensed technician with the right tools. A homeowner cannot fix it.
  • Stuck or overused auxiliary heat. On a heat pump, the backup electric heat is meant for extra-cold weather or defrost. The same federal heat pump resource says heat pumps can cut heating electricity use by up to 75 percent compared with electric resistance heating, so backup heat running on a mild day costs a lot. Our own reading: if run hours per degree day jumped and your electric bill jumped with it, suspect the backup. The thermostat may show an aux or emergency heat indicator. See emergency heat vs auxiliary heat.
  • Duct leaks. A U.S. Department of Energy duct brochure from 2004 says ducts in attics or crawl spaces can lose 25 to 40 percent of the energy passing through them. A new leak, such as a disconnected joint or a torn flex duct after work in the attic, raises run time without any noise from the equipment. See sealing duct leaks.

Other causes also fit: an open window or door, a new thermostat setpoint, a failed outdoor fan, or a dirty outdoor coil. The federal maintenance page also advises keeping at least 2 feet of clearance around the outdoor unit. A change in the house, such as new occupants working from home, can also shift the baseline.

When to call a pro

  • The ratio stays about 30 percent or more above your baseline for a week after you have replaced the filter and cleared vents and the outdoor unit. The 30 percent figure is our own rule of thumb, not a published threshold.
  • The backup or emergency heat indicator stays on in mild weather.
  • Run hours are long and the house still does not reach the setpoint.
  • You see ice on the outdoor unit or refrigerant lines, hear hissing, or smell gas or burning. Turn the system off and call for service right away. If you smell gas, leave the building first and call the gas utility from outside.

Show the technician your written baseline and the date the ratio changed. That date helps narrow the cause and saves diagnostic time.

Field card: Reading Your Smart Thermostat Run-Time History: Run Hours per Degree Day and What a Jump Means

Divide out the weather, then watch for a lasting step.

  • Degree days for a day = 65 F minus the day's mean temperature (high plus low, divided by 2), or zero if the mean is 65 F or warmer.
  • Ratio = run hours divided by degree days. Write down a baseline of normal days.
  • Our example: 0.305 before, 0.436 after, a 43 percent jump on the same weather.
  • Longer runs and fewer cycles fit airflow, charge, backup heat or duct problems.
  • Check the filter and vents first. Refrigerant, backup heat and hidden duct leaks go to a licensed pro.
