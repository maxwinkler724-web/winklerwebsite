Boiler Water Tests: pH, Alkalinity, Conductivity, Hardness and Sulfite

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Boiler water testing turns a bucket of hot water into a decision: blow down, feed chemical, or call the treatment company. Here is what each common test tells you.

The big idea

Boiler water gets more concentrated as steam leaves and makeup water enters. Testing checks that the water stays inside the treatment program's limits so the boiler does not scale, corrode or foam. Limits depend on the boiler's pressure, design and maker, and the treatment company sets them. This lesson explains the tests. For blowdown itself see Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics.

Sampling matters most

A boiler-water testing guide says the analysis can never be better than the sample. It advises keeping the sample line short and well cooled, flushing it by letting water run through before sampling, and rinsing clean equipment with the sample water before measuring. Hot samples are a scald hazard, and a cooler is the safe way to get one. Follow the site's sampling procedure.

Test kit instructions add practical points. One kit manual says to analyze at room temperature as soon as possible, and some tests, such as chlorine, should be run immediately. For sulfite, the same manual recommends adding a preservative at collection to stop the metal-catalyzed oxidation of the sample. That is the kit maker's step for its kit, so use the instructions that came with yours.

What each test is for

  • **pH.** Shows how acidic or alkaline the water is. The testing guide lists boiler water as alkaline, with a target band for its example boiler. The kit manual measures pH with color indicators and a color disc. Low pH invites corrosion.
  • **Alkalinity.** The kit manual says alkalinity is controlled to prevent corrosion from low pH and to prevent scaling from calcium precipitates. It is measured by titration, adding a reagent drop by drop until a color changes, and there are phenolphthalein (partial) and total alkalinity results.
  • **Conductivity or total dissolved solids (TDS).** Measures how concentrated the water has become. It is the usual trigger for blowdown, in manual or automatic form. The testing guide gives a maximum TDS for its example boiler, with a lower limit for the higher-pressure class.
  • **Hardness.** Calcium and magnesium cause scale. The kit manual notes hardness in the system can reduce boiler efficiency. Feedwater hardness is expected to be very low when softeners are working, so a rising reading is a softener alarm.
  • **Chloride.** The kit manual says chloride is used to tell when dissolved solids have increased enough to require blowdown. The testing guide shows tighter chloride limits at higher pressure.
  • **Sulfite (or other oxygen scavenger).** Shows whether there is leftover chemical to react with trace oxygen. A deaerator article names sodium sulfite as a common scavenger in lower-pressure systems. See Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics.
  • **Others.** Phosphate, silica and iron tests show up in some programs. Treatment companies decide when they matter.

Numbers are examples

The testing guide lists a set of values for pH, alkalinity, TDS, hardness, sulfite and chloride for boilers in two pressure classes. They are one European guide's example limits, given in different units from many U.S. programs. Do not use them as targets for a real boiler. Use the limits your treatment provider and the boiler maker set.

Reading results as a trend

A single result tells you little. Log each test with date, time and who took it. Look for:

  • TDS drifting up between blowdowns: blowdown is not keeping up, or makeup water is changing.
  • Hardness appearing in feedwater: softener exhausted or bypassed.
  • Sulfite falling: more oxygen is getting in, which points back to the deaerator or condensate.
  • Chloride rising: contamination or a vent not open enough, according to one deaerator reference.

Safety

Boiler water and reagents are hot, caustic or irritating. Use eye protection, gloves and the safety data sheet for each reagent, and never take a sample from a line that is not meant for it. Dispose of used reagents by the kit instructions and the site's rules.

In states with operator licensing, record-keeping rules may apply. See Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules.
