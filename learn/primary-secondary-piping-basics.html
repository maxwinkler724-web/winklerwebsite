Primary-Secondary Piping Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a boiler feeds several zones or loads, each with its own pump, the pumps can interfere with each other. Primary-secondary piping is the classic way to stop that.

The problem it solves

Put two circulators in the same piping and each one changes the flow the other sees. A big pump on one zone can pull flow through a neighboring zone, or push it backward. Primary-secondary piping gives each circuit its own pump and keeps the circuits from steering one another.

How it is built

One loop, the primary loop, runs from the boiler and passes by each load. Each secondary circuit has its own circulator and connects to the primary loop through two tees set very close together, with straight pipe before and after them. Those closely spaced tees are the whole trick: the primary pump pushes water around the primary loop only, and from its point of view the secondary circuits might as well not exist. The secondary pump moves water around its own circuit and takes water from the primary loop only as needed.

The tees need to be close together and have straight pipe around them. Spreading them apart or putting fittings between them breaks the separation, and flow will start to move where it should not.

What happens to temperature

Water in the primary loop gives up heat as it passes each load, so it gets cooler as it goes around. Designers therefore connect the circuits in order of what supply temperature they need, hottest first. For example, fin-tube baseboard that likes hotter water goes near the start, and low-temperature floor heating goes near the end. Each load lowers the primary temperature a little, and with the right order the hotter loads see the hotter water.

The same sources note that the boiler's return temperature still matters. With conventional boilers, designers often watch for return water that falls too low, because it can cause flue gas to condense inside the boiler. About 130 F minimum has been suggested in published articles, though the boiler manual and a modern condensing boiler's rules can differ.

Troubleshooting ideas

  • A zone that heats when it is not calling: check for flow through the common piping, wrong pump size, or tees that were spaced too far apart.
  • Cold zones at the end of the loop: the primary temperature may be dropping too much, or the circuits are in a poor order.
  • One loud or fast circuit: check pump size against the load.
  • Air problems: make sure the loop has air removal and the piping is pitched sensibly.

A hydraulic separator (or a buffer tank) can do the same job in one fitting, as the Caleffi issue describes. The idea is the same: separate the boiler circuit from the distribution circuits so the pumps cannot interfere.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the exact equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints or wiring and piping steps.

Field card: Primary-Secondary Piping Basics

Close tees keep pumps from fighting.

  • Each secondary has its own pump and connects with two closely spaced tees.
  • Straight pipe before and after the tees helps the separation.
  • Order circuits hottest to coolest around the primary loop.
  • The primary pump sees only the primary loop.
  • Check the boiler manual for minimum return temperature.
