Home Safe Rooms and Storm Shelters: Ventilation and Comfort

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A safe room is built to stay shut while a storm passes. That is exactly why it needs a plan for air. People breathe out carbon dioxide and heat, and a tight room holds both. This lesson explains the basics a homeowner or apprentice should know, then works one example with numbers.

Check the design documents

Check the design documents. A safe room is a protective structure, and its ventilation openings are part of that protection. This page is general education built from sources read in 2026. It is not a design, not engineering advice and not a substitute for the plans and the registered design professional behind your room. Do not cut, add or block any opening in a protective room without that person's approval.

Why a sealed room still needs fresh air

Every person in a closed room uses oxygen and adds carbon dioxide and moisture. In a house, leaks in walls, doors and ducts quietly dilute this. A safe room is built to be strong and, in many designs, fairly tight, so the dilution is smaller. A shelter that protects well but runs out of fresh air is not doing its job. The federal emergency management agency's design guidance for community and residential safe rooms has a ventilation section (and a standby power section) in its list of essential features, and it points to the shelter standard it is built on for the details. A university wind research page we read says the same in one line: ventilation should follow that shelter standard. We did not read the numeric ventilation requirements themselves, so this lesson does not teach them. Ask your designer or the authority having jurisdiction what your room was built to.

A student design report from a state university engineering program chose a passive vent, meaning an opening with no fan or power, because it is simple and needs no electricity. It also noted that the vent lets occupants breathe comfortably. Passive openings in a protective room are usually built and protected to resist wind-borne debris, so they are part of the structure, not something to improvise.

Carbon dioxide with several people

Carbon dioxide (CO2) is not the same as carbon monoxide (CO). CO2 is what we exhale. At the levels you would meet in a crowded closet-sized room it does not burn or explode, but it builds up, and high levels cause headache, dizziness, sweating and faster heart rate. The federal occupational safety institute's pocket guide lists a 5,000 ppm limit as a 10-hour average, a 30,000 ppm short-term limit and 40,000 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health. Those are workplace numbers, not comfort numbers; air often feels stuffy well below them.

How much CO2 does a person make? A 2019 journal paper from a Spanish university group measured six sedentary room tests and averaged about 0.0045 liters per second per person (0.00447 L/s, spread 0.00425 to 0.00476). A national measurement laboratory's page notes that older estimating formulas may be off by as much as 25 percent, so treat any single figure as a planning value, not a promise. People who are anxious, active or larger make more.

Heat from occupants

People also give off heat. In a small insulated room with no outdoor air path, that heat has nowhere to go, and a warm day or a hot attic wall makes it worse. We did not source a watts-per-person figure for this page, so we give no number. Plan on the room getting warmer with each person and each hour. Keep water on hand, wear light layers, sit still, and ask your designer whether the room can safely take a battery fan (below). Watch children, older adults and anyone with a heart or breathing condition first; see Keeping Older Adults Safe in Heat Waves and Cold Snaps.

No fuel-burning heaters or generators inside

This is our own safety rule, and it matches what every carbon monoxide lesson says: never run a generator, camp stove, grill, lantern or fuel-burning heater inside a safe room, or in an attached garage or any space that connects to it. Burning fuel uses up the same oxygen the occupants need and produces carbon monoxide, which you cannot see or smell. Flameless battery lights are the safer choice. For the full story on engines and heaters during outages, read Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages and Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Keep the HVAC duct openings out of the protective envelope

If a safe room is built inside a house, the walls, ceiling and door make up the protective envelope. A supply register or return grille cut through that envelope is a hole in it, and it connects the room to attic or crawl space air and to ducts that may be torn open in a storm. A federal fact page we read calls the idea that opening a window or door helps during extreme winds a myth, because it lets wind into the building. The same reasoning is why we suggest, as our own planning advice, that the room's heating and cooling be handled by the rest of the house before the storm, and that any duct opening be left out of the room or kept to what the plans show and approve. Never tape over a protected opening yourself.

Battery fans and planning for long stays

A small battery-powered fan stirs air so that heat and exhaled CO2 do not pool around faces. Stirring is not ventilation; it moves air around inside the room but brings nothing in. Pack spare batteries, a flashlight for each person, water, medicines and a battery or crank radio. The federal guidance describes protection times of at least 2 hours for tornado and 24 hours for hurricane safe rooms, and says community evacuation shelters in one state are generally planned for no more than 72 hours. A home tornado stay is usually short; a hurricane stay is longer and needs more water, more air and more patience. Test the vents and the door latch each season, keep outside openings clear of debris, and practice getting everyone in quickly.

Worked example (our own): CO2 in a closed safe room

This is our own example. The inputs below are assumptions we chose, except the per-person rate, which comes from the journal paper above.

  • Room volume V: 8 ft x 8 ft x 8 ft = 512 ft3, about 14.5 m3 (assumption).
  • People N: 6 (assumption).
  • CO2 made per person g: 0.0045 L/s (sourced).
  • Outdoor CO2, Co: 420 ppm (assumption), which is 0.000420 as a fraction.
  • Time with no ventilation and no leakage: 4 hours = 14,400 s (assumption).

Step 1, total CO2 made. G = 6 x 0.0045 L/s = 0.027 L/s = 0.000027 m3/s.

Step 2, CO2 added over 4 hours. Volume added = 0.000027 x 14,400 = 0.389 m3.

Step 3, rise in concentration. 0.389 / 14.5 = 0.0268, which is about 26,800 ppm. Starting at 420 ppm, the room reaches about 27,200 ppm, close to the 30,000 ppm short-term limit and more than five times the 5,000 ppm average limit. This ignores the space taken by people and furniture and any leakage, so it is an upper bound for a tight room.

Step 4, how fast does it get there? To reach 5,000 ppm the rise is 5,000 - 420 = 4,580 ppm = 0.00458. Time = 0.00458 x 14.5 / 0.000027 = about 2,460 s, or roughly 41 minutes.

Step 5, airflow to hold 5,000 ppm. With steady-state mass balance, the CO2 made equals the CO2 carried out: G = Q x (Cs - Co), so Q = G / (Cs - Co) = 0.000027 / (0.005000 - 0.000420) = 0.0059 m3/s, which is 5.9 L/s total, or about 12.5 cfm. That is about 0.98 L/s (2.1 cfm) per person. Note the room volume drops out of the steady-state answer; volume only sets how long it takes to get there.

Reading the result. Holding a much lower level, such as one you would find comfortable, takes much more air: halving the allowed rise doubles Q. If the true generation rate is 25 percent higher, Q is 25 percent higher. And the 5.9 L/s must actually arrive, which a passive vent only does if wind and temperature drive it, and a protected opening is small. That is why the real vent size comes from the designer, not from this page.

Field card: Safe Room Ventilation and Comfort

Sealed does not mean airless: plan the air before the storm.

  • People make CO2 and heat; a tight room holds both.
  • Our example: 6 people at 0.0045 L/s each in 14.5 m3 gain about 26,800 ppm in 4 hours with no air.
  • Holding 5,000 ppm with 420 ppm outdoors needs about 5.9 L/s (12.5 cfm) for those 6 people.
  • Steady state: Q = G / (Cs - Co).
  • Never run a generator or fuel-burning heater in or next to the room.
  • Do not cut or block protected vents or add duct openings to the envelope.
  • A battery fan stirs air; it does not bring fresh air in.
  • Pack water, spare batteries, lights and medicines, and test vents each season.
