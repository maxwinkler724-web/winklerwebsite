Vacuum Decay Test After Evacuation: Leak Rise vs Moisture Leveling Off, What to Record, Core Tools, Hoses and Next Steps

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Pulling the micron gauge down is only half of an evacuation. The other half is the decay test: close off the pump, leave the gauge watching the system, and see what the vacuum does on its own. A tight, dry system holds. A system with a leak or with water still inside climbs, and the shape of that climb tells you which problem you have.

What the test is

This is the same idea vacuum engineers call a rate-of-rise test. A national laboratory vacuum textbook describes it as evacuating the vessel to its base pressure, isolating the pump, and recording pressure against time. It also notes that the test lumps together every gas source at once: leaks, outgassing and permeation through seals. The test does not separate them by itself, so reading the curve is the skill. Basics of getting down to a deep vacuum are in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics, and pump and gauge upkeep is in Vacuum Pump Care. This lesson starts where those end.

Running the test

  1. Reach your target first. Use the target in the equipment maker's instructions. Do not start the decay clock while the gauge is still falling quickly.
  2. Isolate the pump. Close the valve between the pump and the system, then shut the pump off if the instructions say so. The gauge must sit on the system side of that valve, ideally at the far end from the pump, so it reads the system and not the pump.
  3. Expect a short jump. The reading steps up for a moment as the hoses, the gauge and the system equalize. That is normal. Start timing after it settles, or log the first reading as time zero.
  4. Watch for several minutes. Write down a reading every minute for at least five to ten minutes, or for the hold time the instructions call for.
  5. Keep the temperature steady. Do not warm or cool the lines during the test, because water vapor pressure follows temperature (see the table below).

Why the two curves look different

The OSTI vacuum textbook states that a leak produces a pressure rise that is linear with time, while if outgassing dominates, the pressure rises and then levels off at the equilibrium vapor pressure of the materials inside. A university vacuum-technology lab text agrees on the leak side: a real leak shows as a linear rise after the system is isolated from the pump, with a larger leak giving a steeper slope. Outside air keeps arriving as long as the hole is there, so the reading keeps heading toward atmosphere.

Moisture is different. Liquid water or ice left in the system boils off into the vacuum, but only until the vapor reaches the water's vapor pressure at that temperature. After that, no more vapor can be added, so the curve flattens. If the water is all gone before that ceiling, it flattens lower. Trapped refrigerant dissolved in oil acts the same way: it gasses out, then settles. The same lab text lists water vapor among outgassing sources and warns that vacuum chambers should avoid high vapor pressure fluids such as water.

How high can moisture push the gauge?

The ceiling is the saturation pressure of water at the system temperature. We take it from the NIST Chemistry WebBook water table, then convert to microns. A micron of mercury is one thousandth of a millimeter of mercury, and the NIST conversion guide lists 1 mm Hg (conventional) as 133.3224 pascals. So 1 micron = 0.1333224 Pa. (The unit family is covered in Pressure Units for Refrigerant Work.)

The arithmetic for room temperature: 2.6453 kPa × 1,000 = 2,645.3 Pa. Then 2,645.3 ÷ 0.1333224 = 19,841 microns, about 19.8 mm Hg. Even at the freezing point the ceiling is near 4,588 microns. So pure water can lift the gauge to thousands of microns. A moisture climb that settles at a few thousand microns or less is therefore still consistent with water being present, not with a leak.

Log A. From 250 to 1,950 microns is a rise of 1,700 in 10 minutes, or 170 per minute, and every minute adds the same 170. No slowing means gas is still arriving. It has reached only 1,950 ÷ 19,841 = 9.8% of the room-temperature water ceiling, so it is not near any natural stopping point. This points to a leak.

Log B. The rise is 1,340 in 10 minutes, but it is front-loaded: the minute-to-minute gains are 350, 300, 220, 170, 120, 80, 50, 30, 15 and 5 microns. The last minute added only 5. It is settling near 1,590, which is 1,590 ÷ 19,841 = 8.0% of the water ceiling, as expected if only a small amount of water was present. This points to moisture (or refrigerant in oil), not a leak.

Both logs end within about 360 microns of each other at minute 10 (1,950 minus 1,590). A one-reading check at ten minutes could not separate them. The slope over the last few minutes is what does.

What to record

  • Date, system, and the target from the instructions.
  • Ambient and line temperature (water vapor pressure depends on it).
  • Reading just before isolation, and one every minute after.
  • Where the gauge was connected, hose length and size, and whether core removal tools were used.
  • Which valves were closed, and whether the pump was off.
  • Final reading, total minutes, your interpretation, and what you did next.

Core removal tools and hoses

This section is our own shop reasoning, not a quoted figure. A valve core is a restriction in the access fitting, and a long, thin hose is a restriction in the path to the system. The same restrictions slow the pump from reaching the far end, and they also slow the gauge from seeing what the system is doing. With cores in, the gauge can read lower than the coil's true pressure and then drift up during the test as it catches up, which looks like a leak or moisture when it is neither. A core removal tool lets you pull the core, evacuate through the full opening, and then reseat it without losing vacuum. Short, large-diameter hoses help for the same reason. See Service Valves and Schrader Cores and Hooking Up Manifold Gauges.

Hoses and fittings are also leak candidates. Worn hose seals, loose fittings and a gauge adapter that is not fully seated can all produce a linear climb that has nothing to do with the system. If Log A appears, isolate the hoses and gauge first: close off the system side and watch the gauge on the hoses alone.

What to do with each result

  • Flat or nearly flat at a low reading: the system is tight and dry. Record it and continue with the maker's next step.
  • Rises, then levels off: moisture or refrigerant in oil. Reopen the pump valve and keep evacuating, then repeat the test. If the level will not drop, the maker may call for breaking the vacuum with dry nitrogen and evacuating again. Follow those instructions.
  • Keeps climbing: treat it as a leak. Check your hoses and fittings first, then pressure test the system with nitrogen and look for the leak (see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection). Fix it, then evacuate and test again.
  • Starts low and drifts a little for the whole test: some outgassing from oil or seals is normal. Compare with the instructions rather than guessing.

For the EPA 608 exam, remember that the evacuation levels in the federal rule, 40 CFR 82.158, are written in inches of mercury vacuum, such as 10 or 15 inches for newer high-pressure appliances. They are recovery requirements, not micron targets, and the rule text we read does not use the word micron at all.

Field card: Vacuum Decay Test

Flat is tight. Levels off is wet. Keeps climbing is a leak.

  • Reach the maker's target, isolate the pump, then log a reading every minute for 5 to 10 minutes.
  • Leak: straight-line rise that does not slow.
  • Moisture: rise that bends over and flattens at a vapor pressure.
  • Water ceiling at 22 °C: about 19,841 microns; at 0.01 °C: about 4,588 microns.
  • Record temperature, gauge location, hose and core tool use.
  • Check hoses and fittings before blaming the system.
