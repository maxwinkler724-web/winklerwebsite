Water Heater No Hot Water: Gas and Electric Tank Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tank water heater that makes no hot water usually has one of a handful of problems, and the first few checks are safe for anyone. This lesson starts with what a homeowner can do, then covers what a technician measures on gas and electric tanks.

Start here: safe homeowner steps

Your heater's own label and manual rule over this page. The makers we read agree on the basics.

  • Smell gas? Leave. The Rheem and Bradford White manuals say not to light anything, not to touch electrical switches, and not to use a phone inside. Go out, call the gas utility from a neighbor's phone, and call the fire department if you cannot reach them. Do not go back in until you are told it is safe.
  • Relight only by the label. Bradford White's manual points to the lighting label on the heater or the instructions in its manual and does not repeat the steps. Follow that label exactly. If the label's steps do not work, stop and call a pro.
  • Never plug, cap or remove the T&P valve or its drain pipe. A. O. Smith warns that an explosion could occur if the relief valve or its discharge pipe is blocked. A dripping valve is a symptom to report, not something to fix by capping it.
  • Electric heater, power off first. A. O. Smith says to turn off the breaker and confirm with a non-contact tester before touching anything. Working on an energized circuit can cause severe injury or death.
  • Mind the scald risk. CPSC urges setting water heaters to 120°F, and its table gives about 30 seconds to a third-degree burn at 130°F, about 6 seconds at 140°F and about 2 seconds at 150°F. The Department of Energy also recommends 120°F and says it can save $36 to $61 a year.

Easy checks that need no tools: is the breaker on (electric) or the control knob in the on position (gas)? Did a recent power outage or heavy use come first? Is the pilot or the status light behaving the way the label describes? If the answer points to anything below, call a licensed technician.

Gas tank heaters

An atmospheric gas heater with a standing pilot holds its main gas valve open only while a thermocouple is hot. Weak or failed thermocouple, dirty pilot, or a blocked airway means the pilot goes out when you release the knob. Bradford White's service manual lists poor thermocouple connection, an open ECO (the energy cut-off safety), a defective magnet, and a tripped resettable thermal switch as causes of a pilot that will not stay lit. See Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics for how flame proving works in general.

FVIR units. Newer residential gas heaters are flammable vapor ignition resistant. Bradford White describes a flame arrestor, a flammable vapor sensor, an automatic ignition device and a sight window. A sensor fault gives seven flashes then a pause, and if vapor is present or suspected the maker says leave and call a service professional. Rheem's power-vent manual says its sensor shuts the heater down when it detects gasoline or certain other flammable vapors, and lists paint thinners and vent-pipe cement among possible sources. Do not store those near the heater.

Electronic-ignition and power-vent models. These show lockouts as flash codes. Rheem's table gives failed trial for ignition, often from low gas supply or a carbon-fouled electrode, a flame lost recycle limit, and a pressure switch failed open that is often a vent blockage. Bradford White shows a four-flash excessive-temperature lockout that can come from heavy sediment or a faulty gas valve, and says to wait at least five minutes after turning the knob off before relighting. Rheem also lists low gas pressure and an unplugged blower as no-hot-water causes. For related ideas see Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis and Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories. A blocked vent is also a carbon monoxide risk, so read Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Electric tank heaters

Most residential electric tanks have two elements, upper and lower, each with its own thermostat, plus an ECO high limit with a red reset button on the upper thermostat. A. O. Smith's manual lists no hot water causes as no power, a burned-out upper element, a tripped ECO, reversed inlet and outlet connections, a broken upper thermostat or wiring, and a leak.

  1. Power. Check the breaker, then confirm power is reaching the heater. A non-contact tester only shows that voltage is present, so prove dead with a meter before work, as in the lockout lesson.
  2. ECO reset. With power off, press the red button. A click means it had tripped. A. O. Smith says not to restore power until the reason for the overheating is found and fixed, because a tripped ECO usually points to a bad element or thermostat.
  3. Upper element. A. O. Smith lists a burned-out upper element as a cause of no hot water at all, and notes a new heater's upper element is almost always killed by turning power on before the tank is full (dry firing).
  4. Lower element. A failed lower element gives some hot water that runs out fast. A. O. Smith says the lower element does most of the heating and usually wears out first.

Elements and thermostats are 240 volt parts, so lockout and proving dead matter. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead, Electrical Basics for HVAC and Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.

Running out of hot water fast

  • Setpoint too low. Both Bradford White and A. O. Smith list a thermostat set too low. Raising it has a scald cost, so a mixing valve at the fixtures is the maker-recommended way if you must go higher.
  • Cold incoming water. Bradford White notes very cold inlet water reduces the hot water produced.
  • Sediment. Rheem says sediment and scale can collect in the tank and cover gas control sensors. A. O. Smith says heavy deposits can keep a tank from draining and recommends flushing at least annually, more often in hard water.
  • Dip tube. Bradford White lists an out-of-spec dip tube diluting hot water with cold, and A. O. Smith lists reversed connections or a melted dip tube.
  • Undersized. The Department of Energy says size a storage heater by its first hour rating, the gallons it can supply in an hour, compared with your peak hour of use. DOE's example adds up to 66 gallons for three showers, a shave and a hand wash.

Tech section: measure, don't guess

Electric element resistance. Power off and locked out, wires off one terminal. A. O. Smith says a good element reads between 5 and 25 ohms, and reading outside that range means replace it. A reading of infinity (open) means a burned-out element. Also check each terminal to the tank or flange: any continuity to ground means replace. Heavy lime is a common culprit.

Gas thermocouple and thermopile. Bradford White's service procedure RG-II gives, by control brand: White-Rodgers closed circuit at 10 mV or higher is good; Robertshaw at 13 mV or higher; White-Rodgers open circuit 20 to 30 mV; Honeywell thermopile 350 to 850 mV. The manual's magnet check says the magnet should stay closed for a drop of at least 6 mV. Those numbers differ by control, so confirm yours in the maker's literature. For microamp flame sensing on electronic units see Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps.

Worked example: is a 4,500 W element good?

An element is a plain resistor, so power equals voltage squared divided by resistance. Rearranged, R = V² / W.

  1. Square the voltage: 240 × 240 = 57,600.
  2. Divide by watts: 57,600 ÷ 4,500 = 12.8 ohms.
  3. A cold meter reading near 12.8 ohms says the element is intact. It also sits inside A. O. Smith's 5 to 25 ohm range.
  4. Same element on 208 volts: 208 × 208 = 43,264. Power = 43,264 ÷ 12.8 = 3,380 W, about 75 percent of rating, so recovery is slower. Low supply voltage is on A. O. Smith's list of slow-hot-water causes.

An open reading or a reading well off 12.8 on a rated 4,500 W element points to a failing element. Test each element for continuity to ground too.

Field card: Water Heater No Hot Water

Gas smell: leave. Electric: breaker off before the cover comes off.

  • Gas: relight only by the label; sensor flash codes and vent blockages need a pro.
  • Electric: breaker, ECO reset, upper element, lower element.
  • Setpoint 120°F per CPSC and DOE. Never cap the T&P valve.
  • Element ohms: R = V² / W. 4,500 W at 240 V is 12.8 ohms.
  • Thermocouple mV values vary by control. Use the maker's number.
  • Runs out fast: sediment, dip tube, setpoint, undersized.
