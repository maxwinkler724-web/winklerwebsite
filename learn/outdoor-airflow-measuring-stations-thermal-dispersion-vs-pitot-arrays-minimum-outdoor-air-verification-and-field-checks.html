Outdoor Airflow Measuring Stations: Thermal Dispersion vs Pitot Arrays, Minimum Outdoor Air Verification and Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An outdoor air damper set to 30 percent open does not mean 30 percent outdoor air. The fan speed, wind and louver all change what actually flows. An airflow measuring station reads the cfm directly, and the BAS uses that reading to hold the minimum. This lesson covers the station types, their limits, and how to check one in the field.

Check the issuing source

Check the issuing source. Required outdoor air rates come from the ventilation standard and code your building follows, such as ASHRAE 62.1 and the local mechanical code, and from the design documents. We do not copy standards here. Use the project's required minimum, and follow the station maker's installation sheet.

Why damper position alone fails

In a variable air volume system the supply fan slows down as the load drops. An ASHRAE Journal article on controlling minimum ventilation volume gives an example: a 100,000 cfm system set for 15,000 cfm of minimum outdoor air falls to about 9,000 cfm at 60 percent load, with the damper in the same spot. The same article says estimating outdoor air by subtracting return from supply (fan tracking) can be off by more than 25 percent. LBNL adds that minimum damper settings can be changed by building operators, so the commissioned flow can drift. The California acceptance test for outdoor air also checks that a fixed minimum damper setpoint is not what controls outdoor air, and that a flow station or similar method does. A station turns the question "how open is the damper" into "how many cfm are coming in."

The station types

  • Thermal dispersion. A heated element sits in the air stream. One maker describes it as relating air velocity to the power and temperature rise of the element, with several sensing points averaged for the airflow. That maker states its accuracy as 2 percent of reading, and a second maker states the same figure. Johnson Controls lists a thermal system with up to 128 sensing points. The Air Monitor article says thermal sensors have a wide turndown, but dirt on the sensors can shift their response, and moisture and turbulence hurt performance.
  • Pitot or differential-pressure arrays. These sense pressure and convert it to velocity. At low velocity the pressure signal is tiny. A Paragon data sheet for its averaging-element system gives a stated range of 100 to 2,800 fpm and an accuracy band of 100 to 1,200 fpm, and recommends at least 180 fpm at the measurement point. The Air Monitor article says the sensing holes can clog and need cleaning, and that a transmitter without auto-zero needs calibration.
  • Fan inlet sensors. Johnson Controls lists a thermal fan-inlet system that averages velocity and temperature at the fan inlet. It measures the fan's total airflow, not just outdoor air, so it pairs with a separate outdoor air measurement.
  • Damper-integrated. Johnson Controls lists an airflow measuring damper with a formed shroud. It combines the damper and the sensor in one assembly.

Low velocity, straight runs and louvers

Outdoor air inlets are often oversized, so velocity at minimum flow is low. The Air Monitor article says thermal stations typically want 2 to 8 duct diameters of straight run on both sides, which an outdoor air intake rarely has. The California acceptance test says to take field readings at least six to eight duct diameters from turbulence, and prefers a hot-wire anemometer to a velocity pressure probe below 250 fpm.

LBNL tested electronic velocity probes in a laboratory with one louver. Probes between the louver blades or at the louver outlet measured within about 10 percent (errors from -12 to +11 percent), and accuracy held down to 15 percent of maximum flow. Probes placed behind a honeycomb straightener had errors as high as 100 percent. LBNL tested only one maker's sensors, one louver size, indoors without wind, so treat these as research findings, not a guarantee for any product. The takeaway for us is that placement and louver geometry matter as much as the sensor.

How it feeds the BAS

The station's transmitter sends a cfm or velocity signal to the controller. The Paragon data sheet lists 0 to 5 V, 0 to 10 V or 4 to 20 mA outputs, with BACnet, LonWorks or Modbus options. Johnson Controls lists BACnet and analog outputs on its thermal system. The controller compares that reading with the minimum cfm setpoint and moves the outdoor air damper (or the damper and return damper) to hold it. See the point types lesson below for reading these on a graphic.

Worked example (our own, assumptions stated)

Assumptions: the air handler needs 2,000 cfm minimum outdoor air, and the free area of the outdoor air opening at the measurement plane is 4 ft by 2 ft. Total airflow is 10,000 cfm. Temperatures on the test day are return air 75 °F, outdoor air 35 °F, mixed air 67 °F. All of these are our assumptions.

Step 1, velocity. Area = 4 ft × 2 ft = 8 sq ft. Velocity = cfm / area = 2,000 / 8 = 250 fpm. Compared with the Paragon figures above, 250 fpm is above its recommended 180 fpm minimum and inside its 100 to 1,200 fpm accuracy band. That comparison applies only to that maker's system; other makers publish different limits, and 250 fpm is right at the 250 fpm line where the California test prefers a hot-wire for field readings. Real louvers and dampers have less free area than the opening, so the velocity at the sensor will be higher than 250 fpm.

Step 2, outdoor air fraction. OA fraction = (RA - MA) / (RA - OA) = (75 - 67) / (75 - 35) = 8 / 40 = 0.20, or 20 percent. TSI's application note gives the same formula for percent outdoor air.

Step 3, cross-check. 0.20 × 10,000 cfm = 2,000 cfm. That matches the station's 2,000 cfm.

Step 4, why the temperature spread matters. If the mixed air thermometer reads 0.5 °F off (67.5 or 66.5), the fraction becomes 7.5/40 = 18.75 percent (1,875 cfm) or 8.5/40 = 21.25 percent (2,125 cfm). On a mild day with OA at 65 °F and RA at 75 °F, the spread is 10 °F. Then MA of 73 gives 2/10 = 20 percent, while a 1 °F error (MA of 72) gives 3/10 = 30 percent. TSI's note says to use CO2 when indoor and outdoor temperatures are too close. It also says the return air should be well mixed, and that the mixed reading should be taken before the fan heat. Use the temperature method on days with a large spread.

Verifying against a traverse

The California acceptance test lists reading an installed station, traversing the outdoor air duct, measuring face velocity at the intake, or supply minus return as acceptable ways to measure outdoor air. It requires a measured value within 10 percent of the required volume, at both maximum and near-minimum flow, with demand control and the economizer disabled for the test. It also asks for calibrated sensors, with the calibration within one year. LBNL found a pitot traverse and a reference meter agreed within -1 to +2 percent in its tests. Our duct traverse lesson shows the arithmetic.

Common errors

  • Dirty or wet probes. Dirt coats thermal sensors and plugs pitot holes (Air Monitor). Wipe or blow out per the maker's sheet.
  • Low velocity. A big opening at minimum flow can fall under a maker's stated floor.
  • Turbulence. LBNL's smoke tests showed large eddies behind louvers. Elbows and dampers just upstream do the same.
  • Wrong placement or orientation. Paragon says elements should run perpendicular to the damper blades.
  • Setpoint changed. Someone raised the minimum damper, or the station scaling or area entry in the BAS is wrong.
  • Moving the louver or damper. LBNL noted that a louver changed by a few degrees shifted its test error by 1 to 6 percent, and that a louver too close to the damper is expected to hurt accuracy.

Field card: Outdoor Airflow Measuring Stations

Measure cfm. Do not trust damper position.

  • Fixed minimum damper position lets outdoor air drift as VAV fan speed changes.
  • Thermal dispersion: heated element, several points averaged. Keep sensors clean.
  • Pitot arrays: weak signal at low velocity. Check the maker's minimum fpm.
  • Velocity = cfm / free area. Our example: 2,000 / 8 = 250 fpm.
  • Cross-check: OA fraction = (RA - MA) / (RA - OA). Needs a big temperature spread.
  • Verify against a traverse or a calibrated reference, at near-minimum flow too.
  • Check probes, scaling, area entry and BAS setpoint when the numbers disagree.
