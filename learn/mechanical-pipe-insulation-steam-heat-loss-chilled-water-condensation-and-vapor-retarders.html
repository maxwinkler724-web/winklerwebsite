Pipe Insulation: Steam Heat Loss, Chilled Water Condensation and Vapor Retarders

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Insulation does two different jobs. On a hot line it keeps heat in the pipe and off your skin. On a cold line it keeps the outside surface warmer than the room air's dew point, so water never forms. This lesson covers both, with the arithmetic shown. It describes no brand and does not replace the plans, the spec or the insulation maker's instructions for your job.

Why bare hot pipe costs money and hurts people

A bare steam or hot water pipe is a long radiator you did not mean to install. Every foot gives heat to the room, and the boiler burns extra fuel to make it up. The U.S. Department of Energy's steam tip sheet on insulating distribution and condensate lines (Steam Tip Sheet 2) says surfaces above 120°F should be insulated, and that insulation can cut the loss by about 90 percent. Hot surfaces also burn. We did not find a burn-temperature rule in the sources we read, so follow your site's personnel-protection rules. Valves and flanges matter too. The same DOE sheet notes a 6 inch gate valve can have more than 6 square feet of surface, and a Hartford Steam Boiler article gives a rule of thumb that a 4 inch valve loses about two to three times what a foot of 4 inch pipe loses.

Worked example 1: 100 ft of bare 2 in. steam pipe

The DOE tip sheet table gives heat loss from uninsulated horizontal steel pipe per 100 feet. Its stated conditions are 75°F ambient air, no wind and 8,760 operating hours a year. For 2 inch pipe at 15 psig it lists 235 MMBtu per year per 100 ft. (At 150 psig the same pipe is listed at 480.) The sheet's own example uses $8.00 per MMBtu and an 80 percent boiler, so we use those as assumed numbers here. They are not your plant's numbers.

  1. Our own: bare loss per hour = 235 MMBtu ÷ 8,760 h = 0.026826 MMBtu/h, which is 26,826 Btu/h for 100 ft, or about 268 Btu/h per foot.
  2. Our own: insulated loss, using the DOE figure that insulation cuts the loss by 90 percent = 26,826 × (1 − 0.90) = 2,683 Btu/h.
  3. Our own: Btu/h saved = 26,826 − 2,683 = 24,143 Btu/h.
  4. Our own: at 6,000 operating hours (assumed, steam on for part of the year) = 24,143 × 6,000 = 144,858,000 Btu, or about 144.9 MMBtu per year.
  5. Our own: fuel burned to make up that heat = 144.9 ÷ 0.80 boiler efficiency (assumed) = 181.1 MMBtu.
  6. Our own: cost = 181.1 × $8.00 per MMBtu (assumed) = about $1,449 per year for 100 ft.

The DOE sheet does not tie its 90 percent figure to one thickness. For a thickness-backed comparison, the Hartford article says a bare 4 inch line at 15 psig loses about 530 Btu/h per foot and drops to 57 with 1 inch of fiberglass, with a second inch only getting to about 37. That shows why the first inch does most of the work. For hot water, a University of Massachusetts Amherst fact sheet lists 2 inch steel pipe with 180°F water in 70°F air at 147 Btu/h per foot bare and 23 with 1 inch of foam insulation.

Insulation materials and where each fits

Maker data sheets and trade articles we read give these pictures. They are starting points, not a selection guide.

  • Fiberglass and mineral wool. A Johns Manville data sheet for a molded fiberglass pipe insulation lists 0°F to 850°F, with an all-service jacket (ASJ) that has a vapor retarder rated 0.02 perm maximum. The Insulation Outlook article lists mineral wool up to 1,200°F and fiberglass up to 850°F in Process Industry Practices tables.
  • Calcium silicate. Same article: up to 1,200°F, a high-temperature material.
  • Cellular glass. A distributor page for Pittsburgh Corning's Foamglas says the product is impermeable to moisture in any form and covers -450°F to 900°F. The article lists 800°F on the hot side.
  • Polyiso. The article's tables only extend to 250°F, so it suits lower temperatures. Confirm limits with the maker.
  • Foam (elastomeric). The UMass fact sheet uses foam insulation on hot water lines up to 180°F. Armacell and similar makers publish their own limits. We could not open their pages for this lesson.

Why cold lines need a continuous vapor retarder

Air always carries water vapor. When a surface sits below the air's dew point, the vapor condenses on it. On a cold pipe the vapor also moves toward the cold side, through the insulation. The NAIMA guide to insulating chilled water piping says condensation can form on the cold surface or, when the system is installed incorrectly, on or within the insulation. Wet insulation can shorten service life, corrode pipe and fittings and support mold growth.

The NAIMA guide asks for a continuous, unbroken vapor seal as the outermost layer, with ASJ rated at 0.02 perm or less. Longitudinal laps use the self-seal lap and the circumferential joints get butt strips, rubbed down firmly with a squeegee. Stapling is not recommended; if staples are used they must be outward clinching and covered with mastic or tape. The guide also says vapor dams should be used on all chilled water systems, at every fourth section and at fitting terminations. Johns Manville says the same in its data sheet: pipes below ambient need all joints sealed, with the lap and adhesive kept clean and dry until closed.

Worked example 2: the dew point check

Chilled water line at 44°F in a 75°F room at 60 percent relative humidity. An online calculator page we read gives a worked example for 75°F air at about 60 percent RH and states the dew point as 60°F, using the Magnus formula (constants a = 17.625 and b = 243.04°C). Another calculator page tabulates 61°F. A table can round a degree either way, so we also ran the formula ourselves.

  1. Our own: T = (75 − 32) ÷ 1.8 = 23.89°C.
  2. Our own: α = ln(0.60) + (17.625 × 23.89) ÷ (243.04 + 23.89) = −0.511 + 1.577 = 1.066.
  3. Our own: dew point = (243.04 × 1.066) ÷ (17.625 − 1.066) = 15.65°C = 60.2°F.
  4. Our own: the 44°F pipe wall is 60.2 − 44 = 16.2°F below the dew point. Bare, it would sweat heavily.
  5. Our own: the room is 75 − 44 = 31°F warmer than the water. The jacket's outside surface has to stay above 60.2°F. That means no more than 75 − 60.2 = 14.8°F of the 31°F difference may be lost across the outside air film, about 48 percent. Insulation thickness is what holds the surface up near room temperature.

Two things follow. First, if the surface drops below 60.2°F anywhere, such as at a gap, a hanger, a valve with no cover or a cut jacket, water forms there. Second, the vapor retarder is the second defense: it keeps moist room air from reaching the cold insulation and the pipe. A thickness table does not give you either one. The NAIMA guide notes that the ASHRAE 90.1 thickness tables it shows are based on energy efficiency only and are not intended to prevent condensation. It points to NAIMA's 3E Plus program for condensation-control thickness at worst-case design conditions.

3E Plus and the code tables

NAIMA's 3E Plus is a pipe insulation modeling program from the manufacturers' association; the NAIMA guide points to it for condensation-control thickness. You do not need it for this lesson. Energy code minimums are in ASHRAE 90.1 and the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) tables. The NAIMA guide shows a 90.1-2013 excerpt for 40°F to 60°F fluid: 0.5 in. for pipe under 1.5 in. and 1.0 in. for larger sizes. Adopted codes, edition and climate differ, so read the table your jurisdiction adopted.

Removable jackets, damage and wet insulation

The DOE tip sheet says removable insulating jackets exist for valves, flanges, steam traps and other fittings, and it sends readers to its removable insulation tip sheet. For steam traps, follow the trap maker's directions so the trap can still work. See Steam Traps Explained. On damaged or wet insulation, DOE says to repair or replace it right away, and to fix the leak first. The Hartford article says wet insulation from roof or pipe leaks is virtually useless and must be replaced. The NAIMA guide's spec language says damaged, wet or contaminated insulation shall be replaced, and damaged facing or vapor barrier must be repaired before start-up.

Field card: Pipe Insulation

Hot lines keep heat in. Cold lines keep water out.

  • DOE: insulate surfaces above 120°F; insulation can cut loss about 90 percent.
  • DOE table: 2 in. bare steam pipe at 15 psig, 235 MMBtu/yr per 100 ft (8,760 h, 75°F air).
  • Cold lines: continuous vapor retarder, sealed laps and butt strips, vapor dams.
  • Dew point of 75°F and 60 percent RH is about 60°F. Jacket surface must stay above it.
  • Cover valves and flanges with removable jackets; keep trap function per maker.
  • Wet or damaged insulation: fix the leak, then replace.
  • Check the thickness your specification and adopted code require.
