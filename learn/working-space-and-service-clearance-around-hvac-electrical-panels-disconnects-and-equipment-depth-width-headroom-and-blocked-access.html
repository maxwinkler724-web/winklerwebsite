Working Space and Service Clearance Around HVAC Electrical Panels, Disconnects and Equipment

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A panel or disconnect that cannot be reached safely is a defect, even when it works. This lesson covers the clear space the electrical rules require in front of panels, starters, drives and disconnects, why stored filters and ladders break the rule, and what to do when a new unit or duct lands in front of a panel.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. The figures below come from the federal workplace electrical rule, which is public and which tracks the same idea as the model electrical code (NFPA 70, section 110.26). Your jurisdiction adopts a specific edition, and the inspector there decides. Study use only.

What the rule protects

The working space is the room a person needs to open a door, read a meter, reset a breaker or pull a fuse and still step away fast. OSHA's electrical standard, 29 CFR 1910.303(g)(1), says sufficient access and working space must be provided and maintained about all electric equipment. It covers systems of 600 volts or less. A letter of interpretation from OSHA adds that the space must stay clear for operation and maintenance personnel at all times.

The three dimensions

Depth

Depth is measured from the exposed live parts, or from the enclosure front or opening if the parts are enclosed. The minimum comes from Table S-1 and depends on the voltage to ground and on what is across from you.

Minimum clear depth, 600 V or less (29 CFR 1910.303(g)(1), Table S-1)
Nominal voltage to groundCondition ACondition BCondition C
0 to 150 V3.0 ft3.0 ft3.0 ft
151 to 600 V3.0 ft3.5 ft4.0 ft
  • Condition A: live parts on one side, nothing live or grounded on the other (or guarded by insulation).
  • Condition B: live parts on one side, grounded parts on the other.
  • Condition C: live parts on both sides, with you between them.

The table in the same section allows 2.5 ft for installations built before April 16, 1981. Do not rely on that for new work.

Width

The space must be at least as wide as the equipment or 30 in., whichever is greater. Doors and hinged panels must be able to open at least 90 degrees, so a door that hits a wall at 60 degrees fails.

Height

The clear space runs from the floor up to the headroom limit: 6.5 ft for installations built on or after August 13, 2007 (6.25 ft before that), or the height of the equipment if it is taller. Other equipment above or below may stick out no more than 6 in. into the space.

Dedicated space above the panel

For panelboards, switchboards and motor control centers, 1910.303(g)(1)(vii) reserves a footprint as wide and deep as the equipment, from the floor up to 6.0 ft above the equipment or to the structural ceiling, whichever is lower. Foreign piping, ducts and equipment generally do not belong there. Above that zone, foreign systems must be protected from leaks and condensation. This is why a drain line or a duct run over a panel gets flagged.

Worked example: two panels

Assumptions (ours): both panels are on solid-grounded wye systems, the 480 V panel serving a variable frequency drive (VFD) on a 480Y/277 V system and the other a 208Y/120 V system. Live parts are on the panel side only. We treat the concrete wall as a grounded surface, which is the usual reading, and the sheetrock wall as not grounded. Confirm both with your inspector.

  1. VFD panel, 480Y/277 V, concrete wall opposite. Voltage to ground is 277 V, which falls in the 151 to 600 V row. Grounded surface across the aisle means Condition B. Table S-1 gives 3.5 ft.
  2. Distribution panel, 208Y/120 V, sheetrock wall opposite. Voltage to ground is 120 V, in the 0 to 150 V row. That row reads 3.0 ft in every condition, so 3.0 ft.
  3. Difference: 3.5 ft minus 3.0 ft is 0.5 ft, or 6 in. more clear depth for the 480 V panel.
  4. Width and height for both: at least 30 in. or the panel width if wider, and 6.5 ft headroom for post-2007 work.

If the 480 V panel had live equipment on the opposite wall as well, it would be Condition C and need 4.0 ft. Your own tape measure and the rule table settle it. Note that the table uses voltage to ground, so a 480 V ungrounded system may need a different reading. We left that case out.

Disconnect within sight

Under 1910.305(j)(4), a motor controller needs a disconnecting means within sight of the controller location. The rule defines "within sight" as visible and not more than 50 ft away, and the disconnect must be readily accessible. Under 1910.303(f), disconnects must also be capable of being locked open. The federal text we read does not state a general within-sight rule for other equipment such as an air handler, so for that case follow the manufacturer's instructions and the code your jurisdiction adopted. The lockout side is in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, and the hardware in AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers.

Mechanical service clearance

Separate from the electrical rule, the International Mechanical Code (IMC), section 306, covers access to and service space around appliances and equipment. In general terms it asks for passage to the unit and room to inspect, service, repair or replace components. The ICC code site showed us only a subscription notice for the chapter text, so we do not quote its dimensions here. Read the adopted edition, then add the maker's installation manual, which often asks for more room than the code does.

Why stored items are a violation and a hazard

OSHA's letter says working space may not be used for storage, including intermittent or incidental storage of nonpermanent equipment or furniture. A box of filters, a ladder or a parts cart in that space may look temporary, but it slows an escape or a rescue during an arc event and can be tipped into the panel. See Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs for why seconds and clear footing matter. Floor markings and a no-storage sign help, but the rule is about the space, not the sign.

When a new unit or duct blocks a panel

  1. Measure depth, width, headroom and door swing before ordering or setting equipment.
  2. If the space is short, move the new equipment or the duct. Moving it is usually cheaper than moving a panel.
  3. Do not swap panel positions or relocate feeders unless a licensed electrician does it.
  4. Document the problem with photos and tell the owner and the electrical contractor in writing.
  5. Do not sign off or energize a job you know blocks the space.

OSHA says the requirements apply to equipment installed after March 15, 1972 and to major replacement, modification, repair or rehabilitation after that date.

Nameplate numbers feed the panel and wire sizing, covered in Reading an HVAC Nameplate. For drives, see Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans.

Field card: Working Space Around Electrical Equipment

Measure before you set it, and keep it clear after.

  • Depth: 3.0 ft at 150 V to ground or less; 3.5 ft with grounded surface opposite at 151 to 600 V.
  • Width: 30 in. or the equipment width, whichever is greater.
  • Headroom: 6.5 ft, or equipment height if taller.
  • Doors must open at least 90 degrees.
  • No storage in the space, not even for a minute.
  • Motor controller disconnect: within sight, 50 ft or less.
