Mobile Home Furnace: Downflow Units, Sealed Combustion, Belly Ducts and the Crossover

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A manufactured (mobile) home is heated by a furnace that looks like a small version of a house furnace, but the rules around it, and the way the air travels, are different. This lesson explains why the furnace blows down into the floor, why it must be built for this use, where double-wide ducts go wrong, and what to check before adding air conditioning.

Check the label, the manual and your local rules

Check first. This page is general education built from federal rules and government and maker pages we read in 2026. The federal rules discussed here are the HUD code for manufactured homes; a site-built house follows other rules. Follow the furnace nameplate, the maker's manual and your authority having jurisdiction. Gas work belongs to a licensed tech.

Which rules we mean

Homes built to the HUD code follow the HUD Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards, 24 CFR part 3280. Heating, cooling and fuel burning systems are in Subpart H. The sections we use are 3280.707 (heat producing appliances), 3280.709 (installation of appliances), 3280.710 (venting and combustion air), 3280.714 (cooling appliances) and 3280.715 (circulating air systems). The eCFR calls its own text authoritative but unofficial, so confirm against the current published rule.

Why a downflow furnace, and why one made for this use

In most of these homes the supply ducts run in the floor, between the joists and a bottom board, in the space called the belly. Our own reasoning: a furnace in a closet or hall compartment feeds that floor duct most simply by blowing down, so the common unit is a downflow furnace. A Home Energy article posted by the Department of Energy says furnaces are usually in a hallway, their own compartment or a closet, and that the usual types are electric furnaces or sealed-combustion gas or propane furnaces.

The federal rule is about listing and installation, not a ban on a model name. Section 3280.707 requires heat producing appliances to be listed or certified by a nationally recognized testing agency, and 3280.709 requires each appliance to follow its listing and the maker's instructions. Section 3280.707 also says fuel-burning appliances other than ranges and ovens must be the vented type, vented outside. So a furnace whose listing and manual do not cover manufactured homes is not a legal substitute, even if it fits the closet. Some makers allow a standard model only with an accessory: one Trane installer's guide says its direct vent downflow family is approved for manufactured or mobile housing when used with a named kit, and a second Trane guide says that kit may be used only with direct vent furnaces. Always read your model's own manual.

Sealed combustion

Section 3280.709(d) says most fuel-burning appliances must have the combustion system separated from the home's interior air, either by direct vent (sealed combustion) or by an enclosure with no interior access and sealed penetrations. In a sealed-combustion furnace, a pipe brings combustion air from outdoors and a second pipe carries the exhaust out, so house air is not used. The Trane kit guide describes this as all combustion air coming from outdoors. Section 3280.710 adds that vents may not terminate underneath the home. For the open-closet combustion air rules that apply to site-built houses, see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters; we do not repeat them here.

Belly ducts and the crossover

A double-wide is built in two floors that are joined on site. Each half has its own belly trunk, and a crossover duct carries air under the marriage line from the furnace side to the other half. Section 3280.715(a) says connections must work when a multi-section home is fully coupled, and that crossover ducts must not touch the ground. It also requires supply ducts to have at least R-4.0 insulation, and ducts exposed to outdoor air, including under-chassis crossovers, to have R-8, a vapor retarder rated 1 perm or less, and an exterior-use listing.

The Home Energy article says branch ducts and crossover ducts in double-wides often leak where they connect to the main duct, and that quite a few mobile homes have torn belly boards. Neil Moyer's Home Energy article on moisture reported supply duct leakage in all 25 homes inspected, and one case where a disconnected crossover sent most of the conditioned air into the attic. Leaked air escaped through belly tears, and the resulting negative pressure (about 1 to 3 Pa) pulled in outside air. A Florida Solar Energy Center research page reports a factory goal of 6 percent or less total duct leakage, or 3 percent or less to the outside, and says mastic held up much better than tape.

Other measured numbers show the range. An Ecotope field study of nine efficient Northwest manufactured homes found an average supply leakage fraction of 6.2 percent (its text and table disagree slightly, so treat it as roughly 6 to 9 percent). Lubliner and co-authors modeled standard HUD-code homes at 120 cfm of duct leakage at 25 Pa, against 60 for ENERGY STAR homes. Testing and sealing methods are in Duct Leakage Testing and Sealing Duct Leaks.

Worked example: what a disconnected crossover costs

This is our own estimate, not a measurement. The formula is sensible heat in Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T.

  • Assumption 1, airflow: a 30,000 Btu/h cooling system. Section 3280.715(a) requires at least 300 cfm per 10,000 Btu/h, so 3 x 300 = 900 cfm. We use 900 cfm for heating too (assumption).
  • Assumption 2, split: the crossover feeds half the home, so 50 percent of the air, or 450 cfm, rides through it (assumption).
  • Assumption 3, temperatures: 120 F supply air, 30 F belly or outdoor air, so delta T = 90 F (assumptions; the lost air is treated as fully lost, an upper bound, because belly air is not always fully outdoors).

Step 1, a typical leaky system with about 10 percent loss (the Moyer article's target of about 10 percent): 900 x 0.10 = 90 cfm lost. Step 2: 1.08 x 90 x 90 = 8,748 Btu/h. Step 3, a fully disconnected crossover: 900 x 0.50 = 450 cfm. Then 1.08 x 450 x 90 = 43,740 Btu/h. For scale, delivering 900 cfm at a 50 F rise (120 F supply, 70 F return, assumptions) is 1.08 x 900 x 50 = 48,600 Btu/h. The disconnected crossover wastes most of what the furnace makes, and the far half of the home goes cold. The result changes if any assumption changes, so use it as a sense of scale only.

Return air and the closet door

Many of these homes have no ducted return. The Home Energy article says air returns through a large vented grille in the furnace closet, that without a ducted return the closet door needs at least 1 square foot of free air space, and that interior doors should be undercut at least 2 inches. Section 3280.715(b) calls for return air from all rooms except toilet rooms, return duct area of at least 2 square inches per 1,000 Btu/h of input, and, for rooms without return ducts, permanent openings of 1 square inch per 5 square feet of floor area. Undercut doors (2 to 2.5 inches) may count only up to half their free area. Section 3280.709(e) also says fan-created negative pressure must not affect combustion air or pull combustion products into the circulating air. The Moyer article found closed doors can pressurize bedrooms and starve the main room; see Not Enough Return Air. Never block the closet grille with storage.

Adding central AC or a heat pump

Section 3280.714 requires cooling and heat pump equipment to be listed for its use and installed per its instructions, and requires indoor coils and outdoor sections to be certified, listed and rated together. A coil or blower installed with a furnace must be tested and listed with it. Section 3280.709(e) puts cooling coils downstream of the furnace unless listed otherwise. For heat pumps with resistance backup, 3280.714 requires at least 60 percent of annual heating to come from compression and a control that blocks resistance heat above 40 F except in defrost. The existing ducts matter: Moyer found oversized AC short-cycles and dehumidifies poorly, so size from a load calculation, then seal the ducts before you ask them to carry cooling.

Water heaters

Section 3280.709(h) requires a corrosion-resistant drip pan, drained outside or to a drain, under storage water heaters, and 3280.707(d) points to federal efficiency rules. A HUD memo says tankless heaters do not meet all the standards, and a maker needs HUD approval to install one. The Home Energy article sets tanks to 120 F and recommends at least an R-5 insulating blanket.

Field card: Mobile home furnace

Listed for this home, sealed from its air, ducts connected.

  • Use a furnace whose nameplate and manual cover manufactured homes, with any required kit.
  • Sealed combustion: one pipe in, one pipe out; no house air used.
  • Crossover and belly: check connections, insulation and belly board for tears.
  • Closet door grille: keep it open, at least 1 sq ft free area (Home Energy).
  • Our example: 450 cfm lost x 1.08 x 90 F = 43,740 Btu/h (assumptions stated).
  • AC or heat pump: matched, listed coil and outdoor unit; seal ducts first.
