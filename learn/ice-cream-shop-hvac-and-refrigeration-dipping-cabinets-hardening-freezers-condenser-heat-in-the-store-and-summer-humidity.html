Ice Cream Shop HVAC and Refrigeration: Dipping Cabinets, Hardening Freezers, Condenser Heat and Summer Humidity

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A scoop shop looks like a small retail space, but it is full of freezers. Every one of them pulls heat out of ice cream and pushes that heat, plus the compressor's own work, somewhere else. In many shops the somewhere else is the customer area, and the rooftop unit pays for it twice: once in tons, and again in a humid summer when frost and fogged glass start the service calls.

Three jobs, three temperatures

The International Dairy Foods Association says a retail freezer case should hold ice cream at 0 F or colder and should never exceed 10 F, and that the ideal scooping range is 6 to 10 F (IDFA). That gap explains the equipment. A dipping cabinet is a display freezer run warm enough to scoop. One maker's manual rates its cabinet at -15 F and says its cold-wall system maintains -10 F to +8 F; another maker's manual says operators typically run 5 to 9 F above zero, and sherbets need colder. A storage or hardening freezer is where tubs wait colder, so they come out firm and stay stable. A soft-serve machine is a separate freezer that serves product at a much softer state; see blast chillers and soft serve machines for its cycle.

Why hardening matters

An ice cream textbook from the University of Guelph says a blast freezer runs at -30 to -40 C (-22 to -40 F) because most of the remaining water freezes there, and that colder, faster hardening gives a smoother product. It adds that below about -25 C (-13 F) ice cream is stable indefinitely, while warmer storage lets ice crystals grow at a rate that depends on temperature. It also says to stack product so air circulates, since dead air and a frosted evaporator slow hardening. A South Dakota State University dairy study used -20 F as its control storage temperature and called it the industry standard (abstract only). For the technician: a tub of freshly made product pulled warm into a dipping well, or a hardening freezer starved of airflow, is a quality problem the customer can taste. Softening and refreezing grows large crystals, which IDFA describes as unpalatable lumps.

For defrost mechanics and gaskets, see reach-in and display case defrost and walk-in cooler basics.

Where the condenser heat goes

A refrigeration circuit rejects at the condenser the heat it picked up at the evaporator plus the work of the compressor. Purdue's ME 418 notes give the three component balances; combined, condenser heat equals evaporator heat plus compressor work. In a self-contained cabinet that condenser sits in the same room as the customers. The soft-serve maker's own article says air-cooled machines draw in room air, run it across the condenser and push the warm air back out, and that this adds up in a kitchen already warm from other equipment. Water-cooled machines send the heat down a drain line instead, but need a water inlet, a drain and a check of local codes.

The evaporator heat started as heat leaking into the cabinet from the room, so the cabinet is only moving it. In steady running, the net heat added to the room is close to the electrical power the equipment draws. That is why we use watts times 3.412 Btu/h per watt in the example below. Pulling down warm product, which adds new heat, is on top of that. Count measured or nameplate watts, not cooling capacity.

Where remote condensers help

A foodservice trade magazine report says moving compressors and condensers out of the kitchen keeps the workspace more comfortable, that heat removed from the building is heat the air conditioning no longer has to pull out, and that remoting a lot of refrigeration may allow a smaller HVAC system. It names walk-ins as the first candidates and ice machines next, says prep tables and reach-ins usually are not, and says a compressor of 1/3 hp or smaller generally should not be remoted. Costs rise 25 percent or more in some estimates, lines are longer, and leases that bar wall or roof penetrations can end the idea. EPA describes remote condensing units as assemblies of about 1 to 20 kW that sit outside the sales area and usually release heat outdoors. For a scoop shop, the realistic candidate is a walk-in sized hardening room, not a countertop dipping cabinet. For related head pressure issues, see dirty condenser and head pressure.

Summer humidity, frost and fogged glass

Dipping cabinet makers are blunt about this. One manual says humid air can fog the lid and front glass, that an open lid or high room humidity brings rapid ice buildup, and that frost is an insulator, so frequent scraping or defrosting is needed. Another says interior dewdrops usually come from frequent or prolonged door opening, and that the cabinet is designed for air-conditioned rooms where temperatures do not exceed 75 F and 55 percent relative humidity. It also says to keep the condenser coil clean (at least every three months) and to leave the rear clearance open (at least 5 inches in that model). A summer shop with a propped door can sit well past those conditions, and frost then insulates the cabinet, so it runs longer and adds more heat.

Moisture math uses the humidity ratio, W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw), with the vapor pressure pw as relative humidity times the saturation pressure. For the saturation pressures we read a published water table (Engineering ToolBox), rounded as listed: 0.36341 psia at 70 F and 0.69915 psia at 90 F.

Worked example: how many tons does the scoop-shop add

These inputs are our own assumptions for a small shop, not measured data. Only the physical constants and the table pressures are from sources.

  • Indoor: 70 F and 50 percent RH. Outdoor: 90 F and 60 percent RH. Pressure 14.696 psia.
  • Equipment, average draw: three dipping cabinets at 350 W each, one walk-in sized hardening freezer at 1,500 W, one soft-serve machine at 2,200 W.
  • People at peak: 10 seated customers (250 Btu/h sensible, 200 latent each), 6 customers standing in line (250 and 250), 3 staff on their feet (300 and 400).
  • Outdoor air and door traffic: 400 cfm combined.

Equipment heat. 3 x 350 + 1,500 + 2,200 = 4,750 W. 4,750 x 3.412 = 16,207 Btu/h.

People. Sensible: 10 x 250 + 6 x 250 + 3 x 300 = 4,900 Btu/h. Latent: 10 x 200 + 6 x 250 + 3 x 400 = 4,700 Btu/h. Every group is counted; skipping the line or the staff undercounts the load.

Outdoor air. Humidity ratios: outdoors pw = 0.6 x 0.69915 = 0.41949, W = 0.622 x 0.41949 / (14.696 - 0.41949) = 0.01828. Indoors pw = 0.5 x 0.36341 = 0.18171, W = 0.622 x 0.18171 / (14.696 - 0.18171) = 0.00779. Difference = 0.01049. Moisture in: 4.5 x 400 x 0.01049 = 18.9 lb/h. Latent: 4,840 x 400 x 0.01049 = 20,308 Btu/h. Sensible: 1.08 x 400 x (90 - 70) = 8,640 Btu/h. (Constants are explained in HVAC heat transfer formulas.)

Total for these pieces. 16,207 + 4,900 + 4,700 + 8,640 + 20,308 = 54,755 Btu/h. Divide by 12,000: 4.56 tons. Walls, roof, glass and lights are not included, so this is not a full load calculation; it shows how much of a rooftop unit the freezers and doors take.

Remote condenser on the hardening freezer. Assume only 150 W of fan and defrost heat stays in the room. Equipment drops to 3 x 350 + 150 + 2,200 = 3,400 W = 11,601 Btu/h, saving 4,606 Btu/h. The total becomes 50,149 Btu/h, or 4.18 tons. The remote condenser trims about 0.38 ton, roughly 8 percent of this subtotal. A tight door and air curtain cutting the 400 cfm would help more; see air curtains over commercial doors.

Field card: Scoop shop refrigeration and AC

Count the watts, keep the air dry, keep the coils clean.

  • Case 0 F or colder, never above 10 F; scooping 6 to 10 F (IDFA).
  • Hardening is fast and cold: blast at -22 to -40 F; stable below about -13 F (Guelph).
  • One maker: ambient 75 F max, 55 percent RH max, condenser cleaned every 90 days, 5 inch rear clearance.
  • Fogged glass and frost: check room RH, door habits, lid closing, gaskets, defrost.
  • Room heat from self-contained units is about their watts x 3.412 (our rule).
  • Remote condenser: best on walk-in sized loads; avoid on tiny compressors.
  • Do not stack warm tubs in the dipping well.
