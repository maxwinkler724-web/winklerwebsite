Getting Your HVAC Ready for a Hurricane or Nor'easter, Before and After the Storm

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most of what helps your heating and cooling equipment in a big storm is simple: shut it down in the right order, keep floodwater and fuel away from it, run any generator outdoors, and inspect before you restart. This lesson pulls those steps together from federal, New Jersey and manufacturer pages, with a timeline and a generator wattage check.

The short answer

If you are asking whether to turn off the AC during a hurricane: Trane's storm-preparation article says to cut power to the air conditioner or heat pump before the storm, at both the thermostat and the circuit breaker. The goal is to keep a surge or a flooded unit from being powered. The federal advice on whole-house utilities is narrower. The National Weather Service says to shut off utilities only when authorities tell you to, and New Jersey's Office of Emergency Management guide says to turn off electricity and water and shut off propane tanks before you evacuate. Those sources are written about hurricanes. We found no separate federal page for nor'easters, so treat the same steps as a reasonable fit for a coastal storm with surge, wind and long outages, and follow local officials.

Before the storm: the outdoor unit

Your outdoor condenser or heat pump is built to sit outside. Lennox says modern units are designed to live and operate outdoors, and warns that a cover traps moisture that leads to rust and damages electronics. Lennox also says heat pumps that run year-round should never be covered, and Trane says a heat pump should not get a solid cover and any cover should breathe. Wrapping a unit in plastic for a storm is therefore not something these makers recommend. Our own view: a wrapped unit that someone then switches on is a worse problem. Lennox's suggested alternative for debris protection is a piece of plywood on top with the sides left open for airflow.

What matters more is what flies at it. Trane warns that debris can lodge in the outdoor unit and burn out the motor. The NWS tells you to clear gutters and bring in loose outdoor items, which are the things that become projectiles. Trane also suggests a surge protector made for HVAC equipment, installed by a professional.

Before the storm: flood, basements and fuel

Wind is only half of a coastal storm. FEMA's homeowner mitigation guide says to elevate or floodproof HVAC and mechanical units, ductwork and electrical systems above the base flood elevation (BFE), and FEMA's utility-systems guide P-348 notes the residential building code puts equipment at or above BFE plus one foot unless it is designed to resist flood loads. It gives moving a furnace out of a basement as one example. FEMA fact sheet 3.4.1 calls elevation the most effective approach and shows outdoor compressors raised on platforms or pedestals. In coastal high-hazard zones, platforms need corrosion-resistant connectors. Trane likewise says a pro can elevate a unit in a flood-prone area. Moving equipment is a permit-and-contractor job, not a weekend project. If you already have flood damage, go to Furnace, Boiler or AC Was Flooded: What to Do Before Turning It On rather than restarting anything.

Oil and propane tanks float. FEMA says to anchor fuel tanks to the floor and keep vents and fill-line openings above BFE, because a tank can tip or float, spill fuel and become a fire hazard. It adds that the vent and fill-line work may need your fuel provider's approval. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building-science guide says elevation is the most effective flood protection for fuel tanks, that straps and anchors do much of the job, and that anchors should resist corrosion. For New Jersey oil-tank basics, see Home Heating Oil Tanks in New Jersey. Propane: the NWS and the New Jersey guide both say to turn propane tanks off.

A timeline you can follow

  • Watch: clear gutters, bring in loose items, secure or check any tank straps, and note where your breaker, gas and propane shutoffs are (the New Jersey guide says to know them).
  • Warning: the NWS says preparations should be complete. Switch the AC or heat pump off at the thermostat, then the breaker, as Trane advises. Turn propane off. Leave gas service shutoff to officials or your utility unless they say otherwise.
  • If you must evacuate: unplug appliances and turn off the main electrical switch, per the New Jersey guide.
  • During an outage: a generator goes outside, never in a garage or basement. See below.
  • After: return only when officials say it is safe, then walk the outside of the house first.

Generators and carbon monoxide

The Consumer Product Safety Commission says to run a portable generator outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away, and that open doors or windows do not make indoor use safe. The CDC gives the same 20 feet and adds distance from doors, windows and vents. The New Jersey guide warns that deadly CO can linger for hours after a generator is off. Put a battery CO alarm on each level. For the full treatment read Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages, and for how to hook equipment up safely see Running a Furnace or Boiler on a Generator.

Worked example: a generator check for a blower and a sump pump

A basement with a gas or oil furnace and a sump pump needs both running in a long outage. Honda's wattage guide lists a 1/3 horsepower furnace fan at 700 running and 1,400 starting watts, a 1/3 horsepower sump pump at 800 running and 1,300 starting watts, and an Energy Star refrigerator at 132 to 192 running and 1,200 starting watts. We use the 192 figure to be safe. Generac's sizing method is: add all running watts, then add the single highest starting burst.

  1. Running watts: 700 + 800 + 192 = 1,692 W
  2. Highest single starting burst: the furnace fan at 1,400 W (the pump is 1,300 and the refrigerator 1,200)
  3. Total needed: 1,692 + 1,400 = 3,092 W

So a generator with rated starting watts comfortably above about 3,100 W would cover this list, with room for a few lights. Generac says starting watts are often two to three times running watts, which matches the table (1,400 is twice 700). Honda lists no oil burner, and a boiler circulator or burner motor adds load, so read your own nameplates; the sizing lesson above shows how. This is a planning number, not a guarantee.

After the storm: look before you restart

The NWS says that once home you should check gas, water and electrical lines and appliances for damage, and use a flashlight, never a candle. New Jersey's guide adds: check for loose power lines, gas leaks and structural damage; stay out if you smell gas or floodwater is still around the building; and turn flashlights on outside, since a battery can spark. CPSC warns not to touch wet appliances that are still plugged in.

Then walk the outdoor unit: is it standing level, are coil fins and the fan free of debris, are lines and wires intact? Trane calls inspecting before restarting one of the most important steps, because running a damaged unit can make the damage worse. If water reached any equipment, stop and use the flood lesson. If everything is dry and the power is back, the restart sequence and the compressor delay are in Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start.

When to call a pro

  • Any water on or near a furnace, boiler, condenser, panel or fuel tank.
  • Bent lines, a shifted or leaning unit, a damaged disconnect, or fuel smell.
  • A tripped breaker that trips again, or a unit that makes new noises.
  • Moving equipment or anchoring a tank: the work involves a licensed contractor, your fuel supplier and the local building department, as FEMA notes.

Field card: HVAC and a Big Storm

Shut down in order, keep it dry, generator outside, inspect first.

  • Watch: clear yard and gutters, know your shutoffs.
  • Warning: AC or heat pump off at thermostat, then breaker; propane off.
  • Do not wrap a unit that could run; Lennox and Trane warn against solid covers.
  • Fuel tanks: anchor, vents and fills above BFE.
  • Generator: outdoors, 20 ft from the house, CO alarms working.
  • Sizing: running watts total + biggest single starting burst.
  • After: look first. Wet or damaged equipment means call a pro.
