Refrigerant Machine Rooms: Detectors, Alarms and Ventilation

Part of the A2L Refrigerants Guide

Also in the Commercial Cooling Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A large chiller holds hundreds or thousands of pounds of refrigerant, and it usually sits in a closed room. This lesson explains why that room gets its own refrigerant detector, what the detector is supposed to do, and how to do one piece of the ventilation math.

Check your local code

Check your local code. Which edition of the safety standard and mechanical code applies depends on your jurisdiction, and a designer or inspector decides the details for a given room. This page is built from public summaries read in 2026. It does not reproduce the standards, and it is general education, not design guidance.

What a machine room is

ACHR News describes the ASHRAE Standard 15 term "machinery room" as a space that contains compressors and pressure vessels, and it also counts a room where one circuit's charge is more than the refrigerant concentration limit. The author treats "chiller room" as the same idea. Trane's engineering newsletter says the standard defines a machinery room as a designated space that meets its machinery room sections, which cover construction, ventilation and detection. In plain terms: it is the room built so that a big leak is detected, announced and diluted. For the equipment itself, see Chiller Plant Fundamentals.

Why a closed room is the problem

Most refrigerant vapor is heavier than air. Honeywell's machine room application note says to watch pits, stairwells and trenches for that reason. A leak can sink and pool low, pushing out breathable air with little warning. OSHA describes a confined space as one not designed for continuous occupancy, and it becomes a permit space if it has or could have a hazardous atmosphere. Whether a particular machine room is a permit space is a site decision, so ask before you enter. The general hazards are in Refrigerant Safety Basics.

What the detector must do

Bacharach's summary of the ASHRAE 15 rule says each machinery room has a detector placed where leaked refrigerant would concentrate. Its job has three parts:

  • Alarm. Visible and audible alarms inside the room and outside each entrance, so someone about to walk in is warned. Bacharach says the alarm is manual reset, with the reset inside the room, so nobody can silence it from the hall.
  • Ventilate. The detector starts mechanical ventilation. Honeywell adds that normal ventilation, running whenever the room is occupied, is about 0.5 cfm per square foot of floor, and the emergency rate is separate (see the example below).
  • Shut down ignition sources. Honeywell says combustion equipment sharing the room must shut down on a leak. For A2L refrigerants, ACHR News says detectors trigger shutdown of compressors, pumps and valves at 25 percent of the lower flammability limit, with a manual emergency shutdown control outside the door.

Where the detector goes

Because the vapor sinks, mounting is low. Honeywell suggests about 18 inches above the floor and within 20 feet of the chiller, in the airstream and not in a dead pocket. MSA and Bacharach's catalog recommends at least one detector per chiller, sensors at 30 cm (about 12 inches) or lower for heavier-than-air gases, and never where airflow can be blocked. Lighter-than-air gases are the reverse: sensors go high.

Setpoints and exposure limits

Bacharach says the alarm must sound at a value no greater than the refrigerant's TLV-TWA, which is an 8-hour occupational exposure limit (OEL). For R-134a it gives an 8-hour OEL of 1,000 ppm. It also gives ASHRAE 34's refrigerant concentration limit (RCL) for R-134a as 50,000 ppm, which equals 13 pounds of refrigerant per 1,000 cubic feet of occupied space. Two numbers with two jobs: the OEL is about what a person can breathe over a shift, and the RCL is about a single large release in an occupied space. Detector setpoints sit down near the OEL, far below the RCL, so people are warned early.

Honeywell's table lists example first and second alarms of 250 and 500 ppm for R-134a, and 50 and 150 ppm for R-123, and notes levels can be changed to suit the owner. Bacharach calls its own 500 ppm figure an illustration, not a rule. Your site's setpoints come from the design documents.

A2L and A3 differences

For a non-flammable A1 refrigerant, the detector is about breathing safety, so alarms are set from the exposure limit. For a mildly flammable A2L, ACHR News and Copeland's literature describe a flammability trigger too: 25 percent of the lower flammability limit starts the shutdown and fans. A2L exhaust sizing is much larger, and the fan must be built so it cannot ignite vapor: ACHR News says the motor stays out of the airstream, parts are non-sparking, and the inlet sits within a foot of the expected low point. A3 refrigerants such as propane (R-290) are more flammable; ACHR News says an A2L's lower flammability limit is roughly 8 to 10 times higher than R-290's. See A2L and A3 Refrigerants, A2L Leak Detection Sensors and A2L Charge Limits and Room Size.

Worked example: emergency ventilation rate

ACHR News gives the exhaust sizing for A1 refrigerants as Q = 100 × G0.5, where Q is airflow in cfm and G is the pounds of refrigerant in the circuit. A public IMC study guide states the same equation and says G is the largest single system with any part in the room. Suppose a 1,500 lb R-134a chiller (an example number, not a real unit).

  1. Square root of G: √1,500 = 38.73.
  2. Multiply by 100: Q = 100 × 38.73 = 3,873 cfm.

Check: ACHR News gives 3,162 cfm for a 250-ton R-134a centrifugal. That fits G = 1,000 lb, since 100 × √1,000 = 100 × 31.62 = 3,162. For a made-up 30 ft by 40 ft room with a 12 ft ceiling, the volume is 14,400 cubic feet. At 3,873 cfm the fan moves 3,873 × 60 = 232,380 cubic feet per hour, or 232,380 ÷ 14,400 = 16.1 room air changes per hour. The same page says A2L rates are far higher, 19,000 cfm for the R-1234ze centrifugal in its example, so never use the A1 formula for an A2L machine room.

Breathing apparatus, signs and shutoffs

Honeywell's note says at least one approved self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) is kept outside the machine room, with a second recommended, and warning signs are 12 to 16 inches square. Having an SCBA does not mean you should use one. Only trained, authorized responders enter a leak. The IMC study guide describes two break-glass switches outside the door: one that only turns off compressors, pumps and valves, and one that only turns the ventilation on. That way nobody can restart a leaking system from the hallway. Confirm your own room's layout.

Calibration and bump testing

A detector you never test is a decoration. Honeywell separates a bump test, a quick check that gas reaches the sensor and the alarms work, from a calibration, which sets zero and then span with a known gas. For fixed monitors Honeywell suggests an accuracy check every 4 to 6 months and zero and span at least yearly, but says local rules can override. Bacharach's MVR sensor note is stricter: test or recalibrate every 6 months, replace sensors at 2 years, use the gas listed on the label under the cover, and keep a log for each unit. Follow the maker's manual for your exact model. The MSA pages we read gave no bump interval, so we do not state one. Alarms and trends also show up in BAS Alarms, Trend Logs.

Field card: Refrigerant Machine Rooms

Detector low, alarm outside, fans on, then leave.

  • Detector goes where heavy vapor collects, low and near the chiller.
  • It alarms inside and outside, starts ventilation, and stops ignition sources.
  • Setpoints sit at or below the exposure limit (OEL), far under the RCL.
  • A1 emergency exhaust: Q = 100 × √G cfm. 1,500 lb gives 3,873 cfm.
  • A2L rooms need much more airflow and non-sparking fans. Do not use the A1 number.
  • Test and calibrate to the maker's manual. Log it.
  • If it alarms, do not enter. Report from outside.
