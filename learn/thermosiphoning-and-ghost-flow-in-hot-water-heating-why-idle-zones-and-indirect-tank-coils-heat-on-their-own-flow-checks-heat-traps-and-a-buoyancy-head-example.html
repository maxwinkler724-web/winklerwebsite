Thermosiphoning and Ghost Flow in Hot-Water Heating

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A circulator is off, thermostat is satisfied, and the baseboard in that room is still hot. Nobody called for heat. The water moved by itself, because hot water is lighter than cool water and a piping loop gives it a path. This lesson explains that effect, what it looks like in a house, and how the usual fixes work.

Why water moves with the pump off

Water at 180 F is a little less dense than water at 140 F. If a tall column of hot water sits beside a column of cooler water, the cooler column weighs more and pushes the hot one upward. Engineers call this a thermosiphon or natural circulation. It needs no pump, only a height difference and a temperature difference. The pressure it creates is tiny compared with a circulator, but it only has to beat the friction in the loop. In a short, large-diameter loop with no check valve, that friction can be very low.

Two places show it most often. The first is a zone: boiler water at the top of a vertical supply riser, a cooler return coming back down, and a zone circulator that is off but has no valve to stop gravity flow. The second is an indirect water heater: the tank coil connects to the boiler loop by short pipes, and hot boiler water can rise into the coil and heat the tank, or the tank can lose its heat backward into the boiler loop. The same idea is behind heat traps on a storage water heater. A university extension publication says heat traps prevent warm water from rising out of the tank when it is not in use, and a second extension publication says heat traps or one-way valves reduce convective heat loss through the inlet and outlet pipes.

Symptoms

These are our own field notes, not from a cited source. Ghost flow is often mistaken for a stuck zone valve or a wiring fault, so look for the pattern.

  • A zone gets warm or hot with its thermostat satisfied and its zone valve or circulator proven off.
  • Baseboard or radiant supply pipes feel warm in summer while only the indirect tank is calling for heat.
  • One room runs hotter than thermostat setting, and it is usually the zone closest to the boiler or with a tall supply riser.
  • An indirect tank seems to heat the house in the cold months, or a cold-weather no-heat complaint goes away on its own when the boiler runs for hot water.
  • Pipes near the boiler stay warm long after a burner cycle ends.

The key clue is that the heat is gentle and slow. A leaking zone valve or a circulator wired to run all the time usually gives stronger, faster heating. If you want that side of the story, see the lessons linked below on end switches and no-heat diagnosis; we do not repeat them here.

Worked example: how much push is there? (our own example)

Assumptions, all our own: a 10 ft vertical riser, 180 F water in the supply side, 140 F water on the return side, and densities of about 60.6 lb per cubic foot at 180 F and 61.4 lb per cubic foot at 140 F. We also use a standard conversion of 2.31 ft of water per psi.

  1. Density difference: 61.4 minus 60.6 = 0.8 lb per cubic foot.
  2. Pressure difference between the two columns: 0.8 lb/ft³ x 10 ft = 8 lb per square foot.
  3. Convert to psi: 8 / 144 = about 0.056 psi, or about 0.06 psi.
  4. Convert to head: 0.056 psi x 2.31 ft/psi = about 0.13 ft of water.

That is small. A circulator is designed to make several feet of head, so this is far weaker. But a loop of large pipe with open valves and no check can have a friction loss below 0.13 ft at a very low flow, so the water creeps. A taller riser raises the push in direct proportion, and a bigger density difference does too. Real flow also depends on pipe size and fittings we have not assumed here, so treat the number as an upper-side sense of scale, not a prediction.

Fixes

  • Flow-check (weighted) valves. A weighted check is held shut by its own weight. A circulator pushes it open; a gentle thermosiphon cannot lift it. Because the weight sets how much pressure it takes, it blocks ghost flow where a light swing check would not. See the check valve lesson for types.
  • Spring checks built into circulators. Some circulators have a check in the housing. It is convenient, but it is a part that can wear or hold debris.
  • Heat traps. These are one-way valves or loops that make hot water go down before it can rise. Extension guidance says heat traps on a water heater stop warm water from rising out of the tank and cut convective heat loss through the pipes. A federal home-energy guide also advises installing them on hot and cold pipes at the water heater and notes most new water heaters have them built in. On an indirect tank, a downward loop or a check in the coil piping does the same job.
  • Zone valves. A zone valve that closes fully also breaks the loop, which is why zone-valve systems are less prone to this than zone-circulator systems without checks. See the zone valve lessons below.

A federal combined space and water heating guideline also lists missing or inadequate heat traps on piping as a cause of standby loss in one installation it studied, which shows how common the effect is.

A stuck-open flow check

Our own field notes: a flow check that sticks open behaves as if it were not installed. Debris, scale or a worn seat holds the disc off its seat. The circulator still works normally, because the valve is open when it runs, so the only sign is ghost flow when it is off. The cartridge or valve then needs service or replacement.

Many weighted flow checks have a lever or stem on top. Turned to its open position it holds the disc up, which lets a tech purge air or flush a zone without fighting the weight. It is a manual override. If the lever is left in the open position, it defeats the check and causes ghost flow, so confirm it is back in the normal position after service. The exact motion varies by maker, so read the instructions for the valve in front of you. Shut off and cool the loop first; the water is hot and under pressure.

Field card: Thermosiphoning and Ghost Flow in Hot-Water Heating

No pump does not mean no flow.

  • Hot water rises, cool water falls; a loop lets it circulate.
  • Our example: 10 ft, 180 F vs 140 F is about 0.06 psi or 0.13 ft of head.
  • Symptoms: heat with the stat satisfied, warm summer pipes, an indirect tank warming the house.
  • Fixes: weighted flow checks, circulator checks, heat traps, zone valves.
  • A stuck-open check or a lever left open lets ghost flow back.
  • Cool the loop before opening a lever or servicing a check.
