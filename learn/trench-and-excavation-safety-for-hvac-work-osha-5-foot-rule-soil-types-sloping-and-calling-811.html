Trench and Excavation Safety for HVAC Work: OSHA 5-Foot Rule, Soil Types, Sloping and Calling 811

Last updated: 2026-10-16

HVAC techs dig more than people expect: buried line sets, condensate drains, gas piping, and the header trenches for geothermal ground loops. A trench that looks shallow can bury a person in seconds. This lesson walks through the federal excavation rules in OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P in plain terms, then works one sloped-trench example. Your employer's program and the current rule text come first.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. We read the federal text on the dates this page was built. This is general education, not legal or engineering advice. Confirm requirements against the current regulation, your employer's safety program and the person designated as competent on your site.

Why trenches are different

The federal workplace safety agency says trench cave-ins are prevented by sloping or benching the walls, shoring them, or shielding them with a trench box, and that soil should be analyzed to pick the right method. A state fatality report published through the CDC archive, prepared with NIOSH, describes a laborer killed in a trench about 7.5 ft deep that had not been shielded, sloped or shored. Groundwater was filling it, and the walls gave way while he probed them. That is a depth most tradespeople would call "just a ditch."

Call 811 before you dig

The federal pipeline safety agency describes 811 as the nationwide toll-free number to call before any excavation, and says excavation damage is the leading cause of pipeline incidents. Its page cites research that calling first gives a 99 percent chance of avoiding an incident, injury, environmental harm or death. The OSHA rule in 1926.651(b) goes further: the estimated location of underground utilities such as sewer, telephone, fuel, electric and water lines must be determined before the excavation opens, and utility owners must be contacted within local response times. Wait times and rules differ by state, so we do not give a lead time here; the 811 service in your area will tell you. Also ask the property owner what private lines, such as condensate or gas piping, may be buried, because a utility locate may not cover them (our own caution).

The 5-foot rule

Under 1926.652(a)(1), every employee in an excavation must be protected from cave-ins by an adequate protective system. There are two exceptions: excavations made entirely in stable rock, and excavations less than 5 ft deep where a competent person finds no sign of a potential cave-in. So at 5 ft or deeper in soil, you need a protective system, and under 5 ft you need a competent person's judgment, not a guess. The protective system choices are sloping or benching (1926.652(b)), and support, shield or other systems such as shoring and trench boxes (1926.652(c)). A design that does not fit the tabulated options must be approved by a registered professional engineer. Appendix B also says excavations deeper than 20 ft must be designed by a registered professional engineer.

Soil types A, B and C

Appendix A to Subpart P sorts soil by strength and condition:

  • Type A: cohesive soil with unconfined compressive strength of 1.5 tsf or more, such as clay. It is not Type A if it is fissured, subject to vibration, or previously disturbed.
  • Type B: cohesive soil above 0.5 tsf and below 1.5 tsf, angular gravel, silt and sandy loam, previously disturbed soil, and unstable dry rock.
  • Type C: cohesive soil of 0.5 tsf or less, granular soil such as gravel, sand and loamy sand, and submerged soil or soil with water freely seeping.

Appendix A describes simple manual tests: roll a thread about 1/8 in thick to see if the soil is cohesive, check whether a dried sample crumbles, and press a thumb in. A thumb that goes in several inches easily points to Type C. A competent person makes the call, and when in doubt, treat it as the worse type. Backfilled or previously dug ground, common around buried lines, is never Type A.

Maximum slopes from Appendix B

For excavations less than 20 ft deep, Appendix B Table B-1 gives these maximum allowable slopes (horizontal to vertical):

  • Stable rock: vertical (90 degrees)
  • Type A: 3/4 to 1 (53 degrees)
  • Type B: 1 to 1 (45 degrees)
  • Type C: 1 1/2 to 1 (34 degrees)

The rule also sets 1 1/2 to 1 as the default slope in 1926.652(b)(1) when you do not classify the soil. Flatter is always allowed.

Worked example (our own)

This example is ours. The numbers are assumptions, not a site plan: a geothermal header trench 6 ft deep, 3 ft wide at the bottom so the pipes and a worker fit, in Type C soil, sloped on both sides.

  1. Slope for Type C: 1.5 horizontal per 1 vertical (Appendix B).
  2. Horizontal run per side: 6 ft deep x 1.5 = 9 ft.
  3. Both sides: 9 ft x 2 = 18 ft.
  4. Top width: 3 ft bottom + 18 ft = 21 ft.

What else is required for this trench:

  • Protective system: 6 ft is past the 5-ft line and the soil is not stable rock, so the sloping above is our system. A trench box is the alternative if 21 ft of width is not available, but it must be used per its tabulated data.
  • Engineer: no registered professional engineer is needed for this standard slope at 6 ft, since 20 ft is the Appendix B limit; a nonstandard design would need one.
  • Way out: a ladder, stairway or ramp so no worker travels more than 25 ft laterally to reach it (required at 4 ft deep and more, 1926.651(c)(2)). For a long header trench, that means more than one.
  • Spoil: keep spoil, pipe and equipment at least 2 ft from the edge (1926.651(j)(2)). In our drawing the digging footprint including a 2-ft setback each side is 21 + 4 = 25 ft, before the pile itself. This is why tight yards can force a box.
  • Competent person: daily inspections of the excavation, adjacent areas and protective system by a competent person, looking for signs of cave-in or failure (1926.651(k)(1)).
  • Before digging: 811 call, and a check for water. Water changes soil toward Type C and the soil may need reclassification.

Field card: Trench and Excavation Safety for HVAC Work: OSHA 5-Foot Rule, Soil Types, Sloping and Calling 811

Call first. Classify the soil. Protect at 5 ft. Ladder within 25 ft. Spoil back 2 ft.

  • Call 811 and ask the owner about private lines before opening ground.
  • 5 ft or deeper in soil: slope, shore or shield. Under 5 ft: competent person decides.
  • Type C slope is 1.5 to 1; Type B 1 to 1; Type A 3/4 to 1 (under 20 ft deep).
  • Top width = bottom width + 2 x depth x slope. Example: 3 + 2 x 6 x 1.5 = 21 ft.
  • Way out within 25 ft of travel at 4 ft deep or more.
  • Spoil and equipment at least 2 ft from the edge.
  • Daily inspection by a competent person; over 20 ft needs an engineer.
