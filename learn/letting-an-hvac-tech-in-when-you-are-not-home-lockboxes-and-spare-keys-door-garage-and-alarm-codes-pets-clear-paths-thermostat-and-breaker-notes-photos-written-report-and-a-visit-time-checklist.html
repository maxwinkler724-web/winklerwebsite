Letting an HVAC Tech In When You Are Not Home: A Homeowner Checklist

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many service visits happen while nobody is home. That can work well, and it can also waste a trip or put your home at risk. This lesson walks through the setup we recommend: how to arrange access, what to do with keys and codes, how to make the work areas easy to reach, and what to ask for when the tech leaves.

Decide first: should you be away at all?

Some visits are fine without you, such as a routine tune-up with a clear path. Others go better if you or a trusted adult is there, such as a no-heat call where you need to describe what happened, or a visit where the tech will need a decision about a repair. If you are unsure, ask the company when you book. A federal consumer protection article on home improvement work advises being cautious with anyone who just shows up at the door, and says to start with referrals from people you trust. Arranging a visit ahead of time, with a name and a window, is the opposite of an unannounced knock, and it is what makes an away-from-home visit safer.

Arrange access ahead of time

  • Book it in writing. Ask for the arrival window, the tech's name and the company phone number, by text or email, so you have a record.
  • Name the one person who may enter. Tell the company who is coming and whether anyone else, such as a helper, may come along.
  • Ask for contact on arrival. A short call or text when the tech arrives and again when leaving helps you track the visit even from work.
  • Name a backup person. A neighbor or relative who can answer if the tech cannot get in saves a second trip.
  • Say what the visit is for. Describe the symptom in plain words so the tech goes to the right equipment first. See Calling for HVAC Service: What to Have Ready.

Lockboxes, spare keys and door codes

A state university student-affairs page on off-campus safety says never to hide a spare key under a mat, in a flowerpot or in any other easy-to-find place, suggests leaving a copy with a trusted neighbor instead, and also says not to give keys to repair workers. We agree with the first two points. On the third, our view is that a lockbox or a temporary code, which you control and then reset, is a safer way to give access than handing over a physical key you may never see again. Our own suggestions:

  • Lockbox: mount it where it is not visible from the street, set a code you do not use anywhere else, and change the code after the visit.
  • Smart or keypad locks: if yours supports temporary codes, create one for the visit and delete it afterward.
  • Garage keypad: the same idea applies. Use a one-time or short-term code if the opener allows it, then change it.
  • Spare keys: if a key must be left, hand it to a trusted person, not to the company in a bag at the door. Keep track of every copy.
  • Change codes the same day. It takes two minutes and removes the question of who still knows the number.

Choose codes that are not your birth date, address or phone number. Send the code only by the channel the company says is private, and do not post it in a group chat.

Alarm system codes

If you have a monitored alarm, tell your monitoring contact about the visit window so a tech's arrival does not become a false dispatch. Give the tech the arming steps on a card, including how to disarm, how long the entry delay lasts and how to re-arm on the way out. If your system supports a separate user code, set one for the visit and delete it afterward. If it does not, change your main code after the visit. Remember that motion sensors in hallways and basements can trip while a tech walks to the equipment, so mention any sensors on the route.

Put the pets away

An animal that is friendly with you can behave differently around a stranger, and an open door is an easy escape. Close pets in a room the tech does not need, with water and a sign on the door, and tell the company how many animals you have and where they will be. Our lesson Dogs and Pets on HVAC House Calls covers the details, so we will not repeat them here. Do not rely on a note alone to keep a dog out of a room, because a door that does not latch fully can be pushed open.

Clear a path to the equipment

A university extension home safety checklist says exits and passageways should be kept clear at all times, that nothing should be stored on stairways, even temporarily, that small rugs should be tacked down and that electrical cords should not run under furniture, rugs or carpeting. The same rules protect a tech carrying tools. Before the visit:

  • Furnace or boiler: clear about an arm's length around it so the tech can open panels. Move boxes, laundry baskets and stored items.
  • Attic access: clear the area under the hatch or pull-down stairs, and make sure stored items are not on the way up. If the attic is unfinished, tell the company so the tech comes prepared.
  • Outdoor unit: trim back plants, clear toys and furniture, and unlock the gate. Make sure the dog is not in the yard.
  • Lighting: turn on stair and basement lights. The same checklist says stairways should be well lighted.
  • Route: remove loose rugs and cords from the route from the door to the equipment.

See Your First Week in a New House for how to find these items if you do not know where they are.

Note thermostat and breaker locations

Write a short note, one page at most, and leave it in the lockbox sheet or text it to the company ahead of time. Include:

  • Where thermostat is and any app or schedule the tech should leave alone.
  • Which breakers feed the furnace, the outdoor unit and any attic equipment, and where the panel is.
  • Where the furnace power switch is, usually a wall switch near the equipment.
  • Where any water shutoff or condensate drain is, if you know.
  • What you noticed: sounds, smells, error lights or how long the problem has lasted.

If you smell gas or your carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not wait for a visit. Leave the home and call the emergency number from outside, as our service-call preparation lesson explains.

Photos and a written report

Since you are not there, ask for the record you would otherwise get in person. The same federal article says a written estimate should describe the work, the materials, the completion date and the price, and that any promise about scope or cost should be in writing. For a service visit, ask for:

  • Arrival and departure times.
  • Photos of the equipment label, the problem found and the repair, taken before and after.
  • A written list of what was checked, what was changed and what was left alone.
  • Any repair recommended, described in plain words, with the written estimate before extra work starts.
  • Confirmation that thermostat, power switch and any door or gate were returned to how they were.

Keep these in one folder with your other records. The federal article also says to hold the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied. For more on choosing and questioning a company, see What to Ask an HVAC Contractor Before Hiring.

Safety and privacy tips

  • Ask the tech to show ID and confirm the booked time when they arrive, if you or a neighbor is there.
  • Put away mail, medicine, spare keys, cash and anything you would not want a stranger to see, or close that room's door.
  • Do not leave the alarm code or the Wi-Fi password written beside the equipment.
  • Tell the company about any camera inside the home. Cameras are common, and telling the tech ahead of time is courteous and avoids surprises.
  • After the visit, check that doors are locked, the alarm is armed, the gate is latched and pets are accounted for.
  • Reset the lockbox, keypad and garage codes the same day.

Worked example (our own): the visit checklist and time estimate

This is our own example with stated assumptions, not data from a source. We assume a routine heating check with a furnace in the basement, an outdoor unit and an attic air handler, and these task times, all our own estimates:

TaskMinutes (our assumption)
Enter, disarm alarm, text arrival3
Walk to the furnace3
Furnace check30
Thermostat check10
Outdoor unit check20
Attic check15
Photos and written report10
Re-arm, lock up, text departure5

Step 1, add the prepared-visit tasks: 3 + 3 + 30 + 10 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 5 = 96 minutes. That is about 1 hour 36 minutes.

Step 2, add our assumed delays for an unprepared home: 10 minutes to find the key or sort out the code, 8 minutes to move boxes from the furnace, 5 minutes to find the right breaker and 4 minutes to find a leashed dog or latch a gate. 10 + 8 + 5 + 4 = 27 minutes.

Step 3, add them: 96 + 27 = 123 minutes, or about 2 hours 3 minutes. Our checklist saves about 27 of 123 minutes, roughly 22 percent (27 divided by 123 is 0.22). The saved time is only our assumed example, but it shows where the minutes go: not in the repair, but in finding things.

Use the tasks as a checklist for your own visit: access set, codes planned, alarm card written, pets closed in, path cleared, notes left, report requested, codes reset.

Field card: Letting an HVAC Tech In When You Are Not Home

Plan it, control the codes, clear the path, get it in writing.

  • Book in writing and name who may enter.
  • Use a lockbox or temporary code, never a hidden key, and reset codes after.
  • Give the tech an alarm card and tell any monitoring contact the window.
  • Close pets in a room and clear the path to the furnace, attic hatch and outdoor unit.
  • Leave a note of thermostat and breaker locations.
  • Ask for photos, times and a written report.
  • Gas smell or CO alarm: leave and call from outside.
