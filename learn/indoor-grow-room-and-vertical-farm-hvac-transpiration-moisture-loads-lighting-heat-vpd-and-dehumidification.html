Indoor Grow Room and Vertical Farm HVAC: Moisture Loads, Lighting Heat and Dehumidification

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A sealed room full of plants and electric lights behaves like a pool hall and an oven at the same time. The plants release a steady stream of water vapor, and the lights dump heat into the same air. This lesson explains where the moisture comes from, how to turn irrigation water into a dehumidification load, and why the equipment choice is harder than it looks.

Check the equipment and the code

Check the manufacturer data. This page is general education built from public reports, extension pages and a few maker documents read in 2026. Real rooms are sized by an engineer using the actual crop, lighting and climate. Follow the equipment instructions and your local code.

Why the irrigation water ends up in the air

Plants pull water through the roots and let it leave through tiny leaf pores in a process called transpiration. A USDA Agricultural Research Service abstract on crop water use says the water kept inside plant tissue is generally under 1 percent of the water evaporated over a normal growing season. A trade-press article on indoor growing puts the figure this way: plants transpire or evaporate about 95 percent of the water they receive. We treat that as a planning number, not a law, because runoff, drain-to-waste and leaks change it room by room.

For a technician the lesson is simple. Every gallon the irrigation system delivers is, for the most part, a gallon your equipment must remove from the air again, unless the room is ventilated with dry outdoor air. That is a latent load, and it does not care whether the lights are on.

Lighting heat: LED and HID

Electric lights are a heat source in a closed room. A U.S. Department of Energy thermal management paper says white LEDs convert only about 15 to 25 percent of their input power into visible light and the remainder into heat. Our own reasoning is that the light itself is absorbed by leaves, racks and walls and turns into heat there too, so for load calculations a lighting watt is close to a watt of sensible heat in the room. The DOE lighting campaign says LEDs cut electricity use by 24 to 30 percent compared with high-pressure sodium and similar lamps, and that they give off less radiant heat. Less lighting power means less sensible load, but the plant moisture load stays about the same if the crop and light level stay the same. That is why converting a room from HID to LED often leaves the dehumidification job as large as before while the cooling job shrinks.

Lights on and lights off

A utility-sponsored field evaluation of two rooms compared the two states. With lights on, sensible heat was large. With lights off, it dropped sharply, and the sensible heat ratio of the rooms fell from 0.68 to 0.43 in one room and from 0.67 to 0.20 in the other. A Trane engineering newsletter says a space sensible heat ratio of 0.50 or lower is common in these rooms, and that at night the sensible load nearly disappears while the plants keep releasing moisture, so the ratio can even go negative. A negative ratio means the room needs dehumidification and, often, heat. Standard cooling coils lose sensible capacity when the lights go out, which is the reason these rooms need reheat or a design that can recover heat. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for the sensible heat ratio.

Why growers ask for VPD, not just relative humidity

Vapor pressure deficit (VPD) is the gap between how much water vapor the air could hold at saturation and how much it holds now. The University of Arizona Extension describes it as a measure of the air's demand for water, and notes that it rises with temperature and falls with humidity. The same relative humidity means a different VPD at 70°F than at 85°F, so VPD tells a grower how hard the leaves are being pulled. Low VPD slows transpiration and favors fungal and bacterial disease. High VPD makes plants close their pores and can stress them.

Published targets vary. A USDA review of greenhouse microclimate collects recommendations in the range of 0.3 to 1.0 kPa, and one cited source suggests dehumidifying when VPD falls below 0.45 kPa. A UMass Extension handout gives about 0.8 kPa for high-yield greenhouse tomato. Expect the customer to give you the setpoints; your job is to hold temperature and humidity tightly enough to deliver them, which needs sensors you can trust.

Worked example: irrigation water to dehumidification load

Our own example. Assumptions we chose for illustration: the room is irrigated with 400 gallons per day, 95 percent of it is transpired (the trade-press figure above), and the removal equipment must remove all of it as condensate over 24 hours. We use 8,090 Btu per gallon of condensate, the latent value a utility field report assumed for its rooms, and 8 pints per U.S. gallon.

  1. Transpired water: 400 gal/day × 0.95 = 380 gal/day.
  2. Pints per day: 380 gal × 8 pints/gal = 3,040 pints/day.
  3. Latent energy per day: 380 gal × 8,090 Btu/gal = 3,074,200 Btu/day.
  4. Average latent load: 3,074,200 ÷ 24 h = about 128,000 Btu/h.

Reality check: the field report measured 386 and 502 gallons per day of water removed in its two rooms. At 386 gallons, the same arithmetic gives about 130,000 Btu/h, so our result is the right size for a room of that kind. In practice the load is not flat. It is higher under the lights, so the equipment must be sized for the peak, not this average.

The energy to do it depends on the machine. A UC Davis Western Cooling Efficiency Center study used a baseline dehumidifier at 2.3 liters per kWh. Our own illustration: 380 gal × 3.785 L/gal = 1,438 L, and 1,438 ÷ 2.3 = about 625 kWh/day for that part of the job, which ignores fans and any reheat.

Dehumidifier, air conditioner with reheat, or integrated system

A model-code proposal from the New Buildings Institute defines these categories. A stand-alone dehumidifier is a self-contained unit whose only job is removing moisture. An air conditioner with hot gas reheat has a refrigerant circuit that reheats the air it just overcooled. An integrated system is built to handle both sensible and latent loads, and may include dedicated outdoor air systems and dehumidifiers modified to reject heat outside the room. The same document says an integrated system may have a sensible heat ratio of 0.65 or lower.

The trade-off is heat. A plain dehumidifier rejects its compressor heat into the room, which helps at night and hurts when the lights are on. A cooling coil that dehumidifies overcools the air and needs reheat, which wastes energy unless the reheat is recovered. A Western Cooling Efficiency Center case study describes a system that pre-cools incoming air with an air-to-air heat exchanger so the coil can focus on moisture. An Emerging Technologies Coordinating Council report found a similar heat exchanger retrofit saved 37 to 41 percent energy in container farms, but it sometimes over-dehumidified and was not a one-to-one swap. Desiccant equipment is another option when a very low dew point is needed; see Desiccant Dehumidifiers. For coil basics, see Air Handler Cooling Coils.

CO2 enrichment and sealed rooms

Many growers add carbon dioxide above the outdoor level. The Trane newsletter notes that many recirculate all the air with no ventilation air, because bringing in outdoor air dilutes the enriched space. That is why moisture must be removed mechanically rather than by ventilating, and why these rooms lose the free dehumidification a dry-climate economizer would give. Outdoor air also has to be tempered in cold weather, which adds heating. Treat carbon dioxide as a safety topic too: confirm with the facility how it is delivered and monitored before you work in a room.

Air mixing

A dehumidifier only treats the air that reaches it, and dead spots near the canopy stay wet. A UConn Extension page on horizontal airflow reports that moving air at 50 to 100 feet per minute mixes the space and keeps leaf temperatures near air temperature, which keeps leaves from dropping below the dew point and growing condensation. Racks and tight spacing change the pattern in vertical farms, so measure humidity at the canopy and not only at the return.

Coil frosting at low temperatures

ENERGY STAR notes that frost can form on a dehumidifier coil when air temperature drops below about 65°F, that frost can make the compressor run without removing moisture, and that some units have an anti-frost sensor that shuts the unit off. A cold night room is exactly that condition. Choose equipment rated for the lowest room temperature, and ask the maker how it defrosts.

Condensate reuse

The UC Davis study says all of the water removed from the air can be reused to water plants. A paper on indoor farm HVAC also notes condensate can serve as irrigation or humidification. Confirm water quality, treatment and local rules before piping it to the plants, because those details were not in the sources we read.

Field card: Indoor Grow Room HVAC

Most of the water in is water out, as vapor.

  • Plants transpire most of the irrigation water; a cited planning figure is about 95 percent.
  • Gallons transpired × 8 = pints per day; × 8,090 Btu per gallon = Btu per day; divide by 24 for average Btu/h.
  • Lighting power turns into room heat; LEDs reduce it, not the plant moisture.
  • Lights off: sensible load drops, latent stays. Expect a low or negative sensible heat ratio.
  • VPD ties temperature and humidity to plant water demand. Low VPD invites disease.
  • Sealed rooms mean the equipment, not ventilation, removes moisture.
  • Check frost limits, airflow at the canopy and condensate rules.
