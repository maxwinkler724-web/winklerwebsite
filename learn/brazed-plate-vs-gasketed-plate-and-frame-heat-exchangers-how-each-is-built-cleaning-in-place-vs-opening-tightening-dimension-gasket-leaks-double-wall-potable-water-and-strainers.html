Brazed Plate vs Gasketed Plate-and-Frame Heat Exchangers: Build, Cleaning and Choice

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Two exchangers can look alike on a mechanical room wall and behave very differently when they get dirty or start to leak. One is bolted together with gaskets and can be opened. The other is a sealed, brazed block that can only be cleaned by pumping a cleaning solution through it. Knowing which one you are standing in front of changes how you service it.

How each one is built

Both types are stacks of thin corrugated metal plates. Hot fluid runs through every other gap and cold fluid through the gaps in between, so heat crosses a thin plate over a very large area. A university geothermal center chapter on heat exchangers describes the gasketed (plate-and-frame) type as heavy end plates and tie bolts that compress the plates together, with elastomer gaskets on the plates. It describes the brazed type as having no end plates, bolts or gaskets: the plates are held together by copper brazing.

A university design standard for gasketed units asks for gaskets with relieving grooves "to prevent intermixing of fluids," a frame that lets you reach any plate without removing others, and carrying bars sized for 15 percent more plates later. Another university specification asks for glued nitrile gaskets and a removable shroud around the plates. Those are examples of what owners ask for, not rules for every unit.

Pressure and temperature limits, in general terms

Limits depend on the model, plate material and gasket, so the data plate and the maker's literature set the real number for a given unit. For a rough picture, a university chemical engineering reference says gasketed units reach about 149 °C (300 °F) and 300 psi, while gasket-free designs can go to 550 °C and 780 psi. A national laboratory screening table lists gasketed plate units at about -35 to 200 °C and 25 bar and brazed units at about -195 to 220 °C and 30 bar. The sources differ, which is the point: treat these as ranges and read the nameplate. Brazed units are also limited in size. The university geothermal chapter says standard brazed units were limited to about 100 gpm and 200 square feet of surface, with one maker offering 200 gpm. Always stay inside both the pressure and temperature rating on the plate, at the highest fluid temperature the system can reach.

Cleaning: circulate or open

The geothermal chapter says a brazed unit cannot be disassembled for cleaning, so it is cleaned by circulating a fluid through it until the deposit is gone, much like cleaning a refrigeration condenser. A gasketed unit is cleaned by loosening the tie bolts, sliding the plates apart, cleaning them and reassembling; the same chapter estimated 2 to 3 hours for one worker on a small unit. It assumed cleaning every 2 to 5 years in its geothermal case, which is that report's assumption, not a rule for your building. How to tell a unit is fouled is in Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach; we do not repeat it here.

For circulation cleaning, one university utility-plant standard gives a target velocity of 2 to 3 feet per second and warns that a cleaning chemical must not be used if aluminum or galvanized parts are in the system. That standard is for piping, so use the cleaning product maker's instructions for the exchanger itself, and dispose of the spent solution as local rules require.

Tightening dimension and the risk of overtightening

A gasketed pack is closed to a stated plate pack dimension, measured between the end plates, not to a torque. Our own field practice note: the nameplate or the maker's drawing gives that dimension and a minimum, and a pack closed past its minimum can crush gaskets and distort plates, while a pack left too loose weeps. Tighten the bolts evenly in small steps, measure at every bolt, and keep the plates parallel. We did not find a public number for these dimensions, so there is none here: use the maker's sheet. After any reassembly, bring the unit up to pressure slowly and check the edges for drips.

Gasket aging and leaks to the outside

Gaskets are rubber. Our own general note: heat, time, chemicals in the water and repeated opening make them harden, flatten and lose their seal. Because the groove design is meant to keep the two fluids from intermixing, an aging gasket usually shows up as a drip or weep at the edge of the pack, outside the unit, where you can see it. A brazed unit has no gaskets, but a failed braze joint or corroded plate can leak between circuits, and you cannot see it. The geothermal chapter named corrosion of the copper braze by hydrogen sulfide as the main failure mode for brazed units in its setting. Watch for water on the floor, a falling system pressure, or one loop gaining water.

Potable water: double-wall and vented designs

When one side of an exchanger is drinking water, the question is what happens if a plate fails. A university backflow newsletter explains that a single-wall exchanger separates the fluids by only one wall, so a leak can pass treated heating fluid into the potable side without detection, and a reduced-pressure backflow assembly is the usual protection. A double-wall vented exchanger has two walls with an air path between them and a weep hole, so a failed wall leaks to the open and warns maintenance staff. Which design your job needs depends on the local plumbing rules and the unit approvals, so check them with your inspector and the maker. For the backflow side, see Backflow Prevention on HVAC Makeup Water.

Strainers ahead of narrow channels

The channels between plates are narrow, so grit, weld slag and pipe scale lodge in the inlet and raise pressure drop. Put a strainer ahead of each side that can carry debris, with isolation valves so you can clean it, and check its pressure drop. One university utility standard shows the style: a startup strainer of 40 mesh and a perforated basket with 50 percent free area, a blowdown valve, and cleaning or replacing the screens before pressure testing. See Y-Strainers and Basket Strainers in Plant Piping. Use the mesh the exchanger maker allows.

When each is chosen

  • Brazed: small to mid-size duty, clean closed loops, a small footprint, and no need to open it. If the water is dirty, poorly treated or scale-forming, the lack of an opening is a real drawback.
  • Gasketed: larger duty, fluids that foul, jobs where you may add plates later, and any place where inspection matters. The geothermal chapter notes you can add or remove plates only on the gasketed type. The cost is gasket upkeep and floor space for opening it.

For sizing by outlet temperature, see Heat Exchanger Effectiveness and the NTU Method, and for a free-cooling use see Waterside Economizers.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. Both sides are plain water, so we use 500 as the water factor (about 8.33 lb per gallon x 60 minutes x 1 Btu per lb per °F). Assumptions: the hot side flows 60 gpm and cools from 180 °F to 140 °F. The other side is designed for a 20 °F rise.

  1. Hot-side temperature change: 180 - 140 = 40 °F.
  2. Heat duty: 60 gpm x 500 x 40 °F = 1,200,000 Btu/h.
  3. Other-side flow for a 20 °F rise: gpm = 1,200,000 / (500 x 20) = 1,200,000 / 10,000 = 120 gpm.
  4. Check: 120 gpm x 500 x 20 °F = 1,200,000 Btu/h. It matches, assuming no heat loss.

The lesson in the result: halving the temperature change doubles the flow (40 versus 20 degrees gave 60 versus 120 gpm). A 120 gpm side is above the roughly 100 gpm single-unit brazed size noted earlier, so a designer might look at a gasketed unit or at models rated higher. In the field, if your measured flows and temperatures on the two sides disagree by more than your instrument error, suspect a bad reading or flow, not the physics.

Field card: Brazed vs Gasketed Plate Exchangers

Brazed: circulate to clean. Gasketed: open to clean.

  • Brazed units are sealed blocks with no gaskets; gasketed units have bolts, gaskets and removable plates.
  • Read the nameplate for pressure, temperature and the plate pack dimension.
  • Tighten a gasketed pack evenly to the maker's dimension; overtightening can crush gaskets and bend plates.
  • Gasket leaks usually drip outside; brazed leaks stay inside.
  • Potable side: use the double-wall vented design or backflow protection the local rules require.
  • Strainer ahead of narrow channels; check its pressure drop.
  • Heat duty = gpm x 500 x temperature change (plain water). Check the other side.
