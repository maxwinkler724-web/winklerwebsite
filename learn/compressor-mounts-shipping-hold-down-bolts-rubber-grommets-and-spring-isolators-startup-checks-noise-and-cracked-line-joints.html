Compressor Mounts: Shipping Hold-Down Bolts, Rubber Grommets and Spring Isolators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A compressor shakes every time it turns, and the mounts are what keep that shake out of the frame and the tubing. New condensing units often leave the factory with the compressor clamped solid for the truck ride. If nobody releases it, the unit starts up noisy and the copper lines take the beating. This lesson covers the shipping hardware, the usual mount types, how to check rubber, and the arithmetic of why a soft mount works.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Mount parts, bolt settings and the release steps differ by compressor and by unit. This page was built from maker installation sheets, a university textbook chapter and other public pages read in 2026. It is general education. The label and instruction sheet on the unit you are working on decide what to do.

What mounts are for

A running compressor pushes on its feet at shaft speed and pulses the gas in the discharge and suction lines. A soft mount lets the compressor move a little so less of that push reaches the base, the cabinet and the tubing. A university textbook chapter on vibration isolation says the greater the deflection of the isolator, the lower the resonant frequency of the spring and mass system. That idea is worked through in the example below.

Shipping restraints: what the makers say

Several condensing unit installation manuals we read describe the same job in different words. One says a spring-mounted compressor is rigidly secured for shipping. Its steps are to remove the upper nuts and washers, discard the shipping spacers, install the neoprene spacers (found in the electrical panel or tied to the compressor), put the nuts and washers back, and leave 1/16 inch between the nut and washer and the rubber spacer. It adds that the mounting spring must not be fully compressed when the nut is correctly set. A second manual says to loosen the upper nuts and washers until the compressor floats on its springs, discard the shipping spacers, and keep the same 1/16 inch gap. A third says to loosen the compressor bolts and remove shipping blocks from under the compressor, then tighten all four bolts and loosen each one just enough that its flanged washer can be moved sideways with finger pressure, and to be sure the compressor floats freely on its springs. One of these manuals also warns that shipping brackets must be removed before the equipment is put into operation.

Not every compressor is spring mounted. The same manuals say a rigid-mounted compressor needs a different check: confirm the mounting bolts did not vibrate loose in shipping. So the first job is to find out which kind you have. The steps are not interchangeable, and that is why we do not give one universal rule here.

Three common mount styles

  • Hermetic, internal springs. A U.S. patent that describes the background art says the motor-compressor inside a sealed shell is held on helical springs, not bolted to the shell, and the springs keep compressor vibration from passing into the shell. A 1980 Purdue conference paper title also refers to the internal suspension springs of a hermetic refrigeration compressor during start and stop. You cannot see or service those springs. The outside of the shell has its own feet.
  • Hermetic, external grommets and sleeves. A hermetic compressor maker's mounting guideline lists a kit of four sets per compressor: rubber grommet, steel sleeve, washer, bolt and nut. It says to fit the grommets and sleeves to the base plate and gives a hand-tight setting of 5 N·m (44 in-lb) for the M6 size. Our reading: the sleeve works as a fixed stop so the bolt cannot crush the rubber.
  • Semi-hermetic, external springs. A semi-hermetic application guideline says flexible mounting should be used to cut vibration and start-stop impulses, that a set of coloured springs comes with each compressor, and that rigid mounting is allowed but passes more shock and vibration into the frame. It lists foot-bolt torques by model, so use the table for your model, not a guess.

What happens if the restraints stay tight

We did not find a maker page that lists every symptom, so here is what the pages say and what follows from them. A condensing unit manual says rigid mounting will fatigue copper tubing. A line vibration bulletin says vibration problems can often result in broken refrigerant lines. A troubleshooting table in one manual lists a noisy or vibrating compressor with improper line support as a cause, and the fixes are to relocate, add or remove hangers. Our own reasoning: with the shipping spacers still in, the springs cannot move, so the compressor acts like a rigid mount. The vibration goes into the base, the cabinet panels and the lines, and a brazed joint or an elbow that was never made to flex becomes the weak point. Noise at startup is the first clue. A cracked line can later show up as an oil stain and a low charge.

Checking grommets and sleeves

These checks are our own shop habits, not maker steps. Lock out the unit and keep your hands clear of the fan and the lines.

  • Look for cracks, flaking, flattening or oil-softened rubber. Old rubber that has gone hard no longer isolates.
  • Compare all feet. A grommet much shorter than the others is crushed.
  • Bottoming out means the compressor foot or the washer rests on metal, so no rubber is carrying the load. Look for shiny wear marks where steel touches steel.
  • With the unit off, push the shell gently. It should move a little and return. If it cannot move at all, look for shipping hardware or an over-tight bolt. If it rocks freely or clunks, look for a missing or worn part.
  • Look at the line clearances. A tube touching the cabinet or a neighbor line will rub through even when the mounts are good.

Replacing mounts during a compressor change

The hermetic maker's guideline says to keep the compressor on its base plate until it is fitted, and fit the grommets and sleeves with the compressor standing correctly, not upside down. Use the mount kit that goes with the replacement and the maker's bolt setting. Our own advice is to discard old grommets with the old compressor, because they have been squeezed for years. After the new compressor is in and the lines are brazed, check that the lines meet the fittings without being pulled into place. A line forced into position carries a constant stress. Then run the unit and watch the lines. One manual says to inspect all piping after the equipment is running and add supports wherever line vibration is noticeably greater than in the other lines. For brazing and pump-down on the changeout itself, see the related lesson on testing reciprocating compressor valves.

How mounting ties to discharge and suction lines

A line vibration bulletin says structural resonance, where the pipe's natural frequency matches the gas pulsation frequency, is the most common cause of line vibration. It also says spring mounting raises the vibration risk, so a spring-mounted compressor needs a vibration absorber clamped from the spring platform to the rigid base, kept parallel to the crankshaft. It says to keep lines stiff and light but with enough flexibility for start-stop motion, to use a bend radius over 10 pipe diameters, and to clamp only to something more rigid than the line. For support spacing and clamps, see supporting refrigerant piping. For a pad or isolator under an outdoor unit, see outdoor unit noise and vibration pads.

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

The formula for natural frequency from static deflection is fn = 3.13 / sqrt(d) in hertz, with d in inches. A university textbook chapter on vibration isolation gives that form, and says isolation begins when the frequency ratio goes above the square root of 2, about 1.4. Assumptions are ours: a 3,500 rpm motor, a single-mass model, and two isolators we invented to compare.

  1. Running frequency: 3,500 / 60 = 58.3 Hz.
  2. Soft mount, assumed d = 0.25 in: fn = 3.13 / sqrt(0.25) = 3.13 / 0.5 = 6.26 Hz (about 376 rpm).
  3. Ratio: 58.3 / 6.26 = 9.3. That is well above 1.4.
  4. Best-case transmission with no damping: 1 / (r² - 1) = 1 / (86.8 - 1) = 0.012, about 1 percent. The textbook chapter gives this relation for ratios above the square root of 2.
  5. Stiff mount, assumed d = 0.02 in, like a hard worn grommet: fn = 3.13 / 0.1414 = 22.1 Hz. Ratio 58.3 / 22.1 = 2.64. Transmission 1 / (6.95 - 1) = 0.17, about 17 percent.
  6. Where isolation starts: fn must be 58.3 / 1.414 = 41.2 Hz or lower, so d must be at least (3.13 / 41.2)² = 0.006 in.

The result: going from the soft mount to the stiff one raises the force sent to the base from about 1 percent to about 17 percent, about 14 times as much. With the shipping spacers still in, the deflection is nearly zero, fn is very high, the ratio is below 1.4, and the isolation is gone. Real compressors have damping and sit on flexible bases, and the textbook chapter warns the single-mass relations apply only to simple systems, so treat these as a guide to direction, not a prediction.

The soft mount also means the compressor passes through its natural frequency (about 376 rpm in the example) each time it starts and stops, which is why springs and lines must be able to move briefly. For vibration measurement see vibration analysis basics and for the same arithmetic on pumps and fans see vibration isolators and flexible connectors.

Field card: Compressor mounts and shipping hardware

Release the shipping hold-down, then confirm it floats.

  • Find out if the compressor is rigid, spring or grommet mounted before you touch a nut.
  • Spring mounts: follow the unit sheet, discard shipping spacers, and set the nut gap the sheet gives (1/16 inch in several manuals).
  • Tight shipping hardware sends vibration into the frame and fatigues copper lines.
  • Grommets: look for cracks, flat spots, oil softening and metal-to-metal wear.
  • On a changeout, use the new mount kit and the maker's bolt setting.
  • Run the unit and add supports where a line shakes more than its neighbors.
  • Our example: 58.3 Hz at 3,500 rpm; fn = 3.13 / sqrt(d); isolation needs a ratio above about 1.4.
