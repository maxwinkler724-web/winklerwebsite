Copper Tubing Types for HVAC: ACR vs Type K, L and M

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two pieces of copper tube can both be called "1/2 inch" and differ by an eighth of an inch across. That is not a typo. Refrigeration tube and plumbing tube follow different sizing rules, and mixing them up is a quick way to buy the wrong stock or fit the wrong fitting. This lesson sorts out the types, the sizes, soft versus hard, and which tube belongs in which service.

Check the paperwork

Check the paperwork. Project specs, local codes and equipment makers decide what tube is allowed on a given job. This page is general education built from public documents we read in 2026. Confirm the rules that apply to your site before you order or install.

Two sizing systems

A trade-association copper tube handbook (a university site hosts a copy; we cite it as plain text) explains the split. Types K, L and M, plus drain and medical gas tube, carry a nominal size, and the actual outside diameter is always 1/8 inch larger than that size name. ACR tube is the exception: it is designated by its actual outside diameter. So 1/2 inch Type L measures 0.625 inch across, while 1/2 inch ACR measures 0.500 inch across. The handbook's dimension tables show the same pattern at 3/8 inch: water tube of that nominal size is 0.500 inch OD, while 3/8 inch ACR is 0.375 inch OD.

The tube standards are ASTM B88 for the water tube types (K, L and M) and ASTM B280 for air conditioning and refrigeration field service tube. A Department of Defense unified facilities guide specification for refrigerant piping names ASTM B280 and says the tube must be marked "ACR." We did not read the standards themselves, only public documents that cite them.

What the letters K, L and M mean

K, L and M are wall thickness grades for the same outside diameter. For 1/2 inch nominal, the handbook table lists Type K at 0.049 inch wall, Type L at 0.040 inch and Type M at 0.028 inch, all at 0.625 inch OD. K is the thickest and M is the thinnest, so inside diameter goes the other way: 0.527, 0.545 and 0.569 inch.

Soft and hard temper

The same handbook says drawn temper tube is often called "hard" and annealed tube is called "soft." Soft tube comes in coils and bends by hand or with a bender. Hard tube comes in straight sticks, most commonly 20 feet, and holds its shape. The handbook lists ACR in 20 foot straight lengths from 3/8 inch up and in 50 foot coils for the small sizes.

Government specs add limits on soft tube. The unified facilities guide specification says copper tubing must be soft annealed where bending is required and hard drawn where it is not, and that soft annealed tube must not be used in sizes larger than 1-3/8 inches. A national laboratory refrigerant systems spec we read sets a tighter limit: annealed tube must not be used for pipe with an outside diameter larger than 0.625 inch. Each limit belongs to its own project, so check yours.

Cleaned and capped

That same national laboratory spec calls for ASTM B280 type ACR tube that is "clean, dry, and capped" for drawn-temper piping, and the unified facilities spec says refrigerant piping, valves and accessories must be cleaned, dehydrated and sealed (capped or plugged) before they leave the manufacturer's plant. The reason is that a refrigerant circuit has to stay dry and free of dirt, scale and moisture. We found no document stating that water tube is supplied that way, so do not assume it. After the tube is on the job, keep the ends capped, and purge with nitrogen while brazing to prevent copper oxide scale; both specs say so, and the brazing lesson covers the technique.

Why R-410A makes wall and rating matter

R-410A runs at much higher pressure than the older R-22. A NIST-coauthored trade journal article says the blend operates at condensing pressures approximately 60 percent higher than R-22 for air-cooled systems. The national laboratory spec gives a concrete picture: for R-410A it calls for field test pressures of at least 300 psig low side and 535 psig high side on air-cooled systems. Those are test pressures from that spec, not a limit set by tube wall alone.

The handbook's ACR rating table lists internal working pressure by size and temper at 100°F. For 3/8 inch ACR it shows 914 psi annealed and 1,569 psi drawn. For 1/2 inch ACR it shows 781 psi annealed and 1,341 psi drawn. For comparison, the handbook's tables for 1/2 inch water tube at 100°F show Type L at 722 psi annealed and 1,242 psi drawn, and Type M at 850 psi drawn. The handbook adds that joint ratings (soldered and brazed) are listed separately and can govern the design. Press fitting limits are in the press-fit lesson.

Which tube goes where

  • Refrigerant lines. The specs we read call for ASTM B280 ACR tube. The handbook says copper is the preferred material for most refrigerants and names Type L or ACR "or as specified." Follow the maker and the project spec.
  • Hot-water heating and chilled water. A university construction standard for hydronic piping specifies Type L drawn-temper tube for hot-water heating, chilled-water and makeup piping at 2 inch and smaller, and does not list Type M. The handbook lists K, L and M for domestic water.
  • Domestic water. Another university standard lists Type L hard tube above ground for 2 inch and smaller, and Type K soft tube under a slab. Type M is not in that document.

For flare joints on soft tube see mini-split flare fittings.

Worked example (ours)

A tech has a coil of 3/8 inch soft tubing and a 10 foot stick of 1/2 inch Type M. Which is right for an R-410A liquid line, and which for a hot-water baseboard loop? Assumptions, ours: the coil is marked ACR and arrived capped; the maker's line set table calls for 3/8 inch OD on this liquid line; the project spec follows the sources above.

  1. Coil size. ACR is sized by actual OD, so the coil is 0.375 inch OD. Handbook inside diameter for the standard wall is 0.305 inch, so wall = (0.375 − 0.305) / 2 = 0.035 inch.
  2. Soft allowed? 0.375 is below the 0.625 inch OD limit in the laboratory spec and below the 1-3/8 inch limit in the unified facilities spec, so soft tube is acceptable under both.
  3. Pressure check. Soft 3/8 ACR is rated 914 psi at 100°F in the handbook. The laboratory spec's R-410A high-side test is 535 psig. 914 − 535 = 379 psi of margin. The coil is a fit for the liquid line.
  4. Stick size. Type M is sized nominally: 0.500 + 0.125 = 0.625 inch OD. Wall = (0.625 − 0.569) / 2 = 0.028 inch, matching the table.
  5. Refrigerant? No. It is ASTM B88 water tube, not marked ACR, and we found nothing saying it is supplied cleaned and capped. Its 0.625 inch OD also does not match a 3/8 inch line.
  6. Baseboard? It is the right kind of tube for water, and a 0.625 inch OD, 0.028 inch wall stick is rated 850 psi drawn at 100°F. Whether Type M is accepted depends on the spec: the university standard we read names Type L for hot-water heating, so if the spec says L, we order L.

Answer: the 3/8 inch ACR coil goes on the R-410A liquid line, and the 1/2 inch Type M stick is a hydronic candidate only if the spec and local code allow Type M. Never swap them.

Field card: Copper Tubing Types for HVAC

ACR is sized by outside diameter. K, L and M are nominal, OD = size + 1/8 inch.

  • 1/2 in. Type L or M: 0.625 in. OD. 1/2 in. ACR: 0.500 in. OD.
  • K is thickest, then L, then M (1/2 in.: 0.049, 0.040, 0.028 wall).
  • Refrigerant lines: ASTM B280 ACR, clean, dry and capped. Keep ends capped.
  • Soft tube bends from a coil; hard tube is straight sticks. Check size limits for soft tube in the spec.
  • R-410A runs about 60 percent higher condensing pressure than R-22 in air-cooled systems.
  • Water tube type for heating and domestic water depends on the project spec and code.
