HVAC for Insect Farming Rooms: Heat, Humidity, Air and Escape Screens

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Rooms that raise crickets, mealworms or black soldier fly larvae for feed and food behave like a warm, humid, slightly smelly animal house. The HVAC job is to hold a steady warm temperature, keep moisture in a narrow band, move enough air to dilute ammonia and carbon dioxide, and keep the animals inside. This lesson walks through each piece and ends with a heat-load example of our own.

Check the facts for your species

Check the facts for your species. The numbers below come from research and extension papers on specific species, often in lab incubators or small colonies. A production room, a feed rule or a local authority may ask for something different. This page is general education, not design advice. Confirm targets with the farm's own rearing plan and the people who inspect the operation.

What temperatures and humidity the research reports

For crickets, a 2025 study of an edible cricket species found a narrow best range of 32 to 36 °C (about 90 to 97 °F), with reproduction falling sharply at 38 °C. The growth work there ran at 60 to 80 percent relative humidity (source 1). An older state extension fact sheet for house crickets, written for small colonies, says to keep the container at about 85 °F, that cooling to about 50 °F slows or stops rearing, and that the cage floor should stay dry with only the egg-laying dish damp (source 2).

For mealworms, a 2023 rearing study held cultures at 25 ± 5 °C and 60 to 65 percent relative humidity and suggested 25 to 27 °C for growing, noting that larvae tolerate fairly dry conditions (source 3). For black soldier fly, a colony paper kept 28 ± 1 °C and 65 ± 5 percent relative humidity (source 4), and a state extension guide for home bins gives 50 to 70 percent relative humidity (source 5).

The takeaway for the mechanical side: these rooms run roughly 77 to 97 °F, far above a normal building, so heat loss is large in winter. Humidity is moderate, not saturated, so the job is more about control than about heavy humidification.

Heat from the insects and the substrate

Dense colonies make their own heat. A 2023 study of black soldier fly larvae found that substrate in pans warmed at least 10 °C above the surrounding air, that the largest groups reached about 45.6 °C in a 27 °C chamber, and that the authors suggest measuring substrate temperature, not just room air (source 6). Pans with no larvae still warmed by up to about 10 °C, which the authors link possibly to microbes. The same work cites about 47 °C as a level above which growth falls and larvae can die.

What this means for a tech: a room thermostat can read a comfortable number while the middle of a bin is far hotter. Mixing the air, keeping bin depth within the rearing plan and placing a probe in a representative bin matter more than adding heater capacity. We do not have a sourced heat output per pound of insects, so the worked example below ignores animal heat. That is conservative for sizing a heater but means the cooling case needs its own look.

Ventilation for ammonia, carbon dioxide and odor

Frass (insect droppings) and spoiled feed break down and give off ammonia and odor. The extension guide above ties bin odor to overfeeding and excess moisture (source 5). Federal workplace chemical guidance lists an ammonia time-weighted exposure limit of 25 ppm, a short-term limit of 35 ppm and a legal limit of 50 ppm for an eight-hour average (source 7). Those are limits for people, so worker comfort and safety, not animal tolerance, often set the ventilation target. We do not have sourced animal-side figures for ammonia or carbon dioxide in these rooms, so set room limits with the rearing plan and a sensor, and let the sensor drive fan speed.

Ventilation brings in cold outdoor air, and that air must be heated. The bigger the airflow, the bigger the heater. For the same reasons covered in the barn ventilation lesson, run a minimum fan rate all the time and add more only as the sensors call for it. Fuel-fired heaters that vent into the room add carbon monoxide and moisture, so use sealed or vented equipment.

Dust from frass and keeping coils and filters alive

Dried frass and feed are fine dust. Put a filter on the intake and supply side, keep the return and exhaust paths away from open bins, and wash down or vacuum rather than blow. Allow for frequent filter changes. Insect and feed dust can also build on heater elements and humidifier parts, so keep guards clean and allow service space. Because airborne dust, ammonia and humidity are all present, choose materials that tolerate wet cleaning, and keep electrical gear in sealed enclosures rated for damp, dusty rooms.

Even temperatures across racks

Warm air rises, and stacked bins add their own heat, so top shelves run hot and floors run cold. Common fixes: slow horizontal air movement across every level rather than a jet at one spot, a fan to break up stratification, and heat placed low or along the cold wall. Measure at the top, middle and bottom shelf. If the spread is more than the rearing plan allows, adjust air before you add heat. The greenhouse heating lesson covers low-level heat and air mixing in a similar space.

Heaters and humidifiers

Electric resistance heat is simple and clean in a dusty, damp room. Hot-water unit heaters spread heat well but add piping near wet floors. Whichever you pick, put a high-limit cutoff on it, size it from the load, and keep it away from bedding, loose feed and dust piles. For moisture, a steam or fogging unit must have its own safe discharge so wet air does not hit bins or walls directly, and a humidistat or sensor should control it with the room fans, not alone. The pet store lesson covers small-animal ammonia and failure alarms, and the mushroom room lesson covers high-humidity fresh air control, so we do not repeat that here.

Escape screens on intakes and exhausts

Every opening is an escape path. Black soldier fly work used lids with 1.3 mm by 1.3 mm mesh for container ventilation (source 4), which gives a feel for the fine mesh used on small containers. Extension sources describe sealing gaps with caulk around exits and note that gaps in lids let larvae escape (source 5), and one cricket sheet uses an oiled band at the top of the container as a barrier (source 2). For a room, plan screened intakes and exhausts, sealed door sweeps and sealed duct and pipe penetrations. Screens clog with dust and frass, so size them with low face velocity, make them easy to remove and put a differential pressure check across them. A clogged screen starves the fans and can upset room pressure.

Alarms

These rooms hold live animals. Alarm on high and low room temperature, high humidity, loss of airflow or fan fault, high ammonia where a sensor is used, and power loss. Send alarms to a person who can respond at night, and test them on a schedule. A failed exhaust fan or heater in a closed room can cook or chill a colony within hours; ask the rearing plan for time limits.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example. The room size, loss coefficient and airflow are assumptions, not measured data.

  • Assumed room: 20 ft by 30 ft by 10 ft, insulated, with an overall heat loss coefficient (UA) of 180 Btu/h per °F. This is an assumption.
  • Assumed outdoor design temperature: 10 °F.
  • Target: 90 °F, near the low end of the sourced 32 to 36 °C cricket range (source 1).
  • Assumed ventilation: 300 cfm of outdoor air.

Step 1, temperature difference: 90 − 10 = 80 °F.

Step 2, envelope loss: 180 × 80 = 14,400 Btu/h.

Step 3, ventilation sensible heat: 1.08 × 300 cfm × 80 = 25,920 Btu/h. The 1.08 factor is explained in the 1.08 lesson.

Step 4, total: 14,400 + 25,920 = 40,320 Btu/h. At 3,412 Btu/h per kW, that is about 11.8 kW of electric heat before any allowance for animal heat.

What we learn: ventilation is about 64 percent of this load (25,920 ÷ 40,320). Halving the airflow to 150 cfm would cut that part to 12,960 Btu/h, so smart minimum ventilation with sensor control saves more heat than adding insulation. This also assumes bins and colony heat from source 6 stay off the books, so check the summer cooling case separately.

Field card: HVAC for Insect Farming Rooms: Heat, Humidity, Air and Escape Screens

Hold warm, move air, keep them in.

  • Research targets: crickets about 32 to 36 °C, mealworms about 25 to 27 °C, soldier fly colony about 28 °C.
  • Humidity is moderate: reported values run about 50 to 80 percent depending on species.
  • Dense bins run well above room air, so read substrate temperature too.
  • Ventilation heat: 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Screen every opening; check pressure across screens.
  • Alarm temperature, humidity, airflow and power.
