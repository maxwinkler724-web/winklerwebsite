Quieter Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Units: Sound Ratings, Placement and Neighbors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most complaints about an outdoor unit come from a neighbor's bedroom window, not the owner's. A sound number on a spec sheet helps, but only if you know what it measures. This lesson explains how makers rate outdoor unit sound, what moves that number around, and how to check a planned spot with simple arithmetic.

Check the numbers where you work

Check local rules. Noise limits are set by municipalities and the state, and spec sheets change by model year. This page is general education built from sources read in 2026. Confirm the limit for your address with your town and confirm sound data on the current literature for the exact model.

How the sound rating is made

The industry test standard for outdoor units is AHRI 270. The edition we read says the main rating is an overall A-weighted sound power level, written LwA and measured in decibels against a 1 picowatt reference. "A-weighted" means the meter discounts very low and very high pitches the way human hearing does, which is why you see dBA. The standard also says that a maker who publishes a rating must state that it was "rated in accordance with" AHRI 270, and that tests are run at the standard thermal rating conditions. For variable speed equipment the test is run at one constant speed. So the printed figure is one operating point, not everything the unit does all year.

Sound power describes how much noise the machine puts out. Sound pressure is what a person or a meter picks up at a spot, and it depends on distance and surroundings. A companion standard, AHRI 275, explains how to turn a power rating into an estimate of pressure at a chosen point. Its method starts with the unit's rating, adds a correction for nearby reflecting walls, and subtracts corrections for a barrier, the path the sound takes, and distance. Makers do not all print the same quantity, so read the footnote before you compare.

Reading what the makers print

  • Carrier. The product data for the Infinity 19VS variable speed air conditioner (24VNA9) lists sound power in dBA at minimum and maximum speed. For the 036 size it shows 55 dBA at 1200 rpm and 72 dBA at 4800 rpm. The same sheet says the unit was "tested in compliance with AHRI 270-1995 but not listed with AHRI," and that the stage 2, 3 and 4 sound values are estimates.
  • Trane. The XR15 product data gives an A-weighted sound power of 75 dB(A) for most sizes at high stage, rated under AHRI 270-2008 in cooling at 95 degrees F outdoor air. It lists a "Sound Enclosure" as a feature for some sizes only, and a rubber isolator kit for jobs where sound transmission should be reduced.
  • Mitsubishi. The submittal for the MUZ-FS18NA mini split outdoor unit lists 52 dB(A) cooling and 55 dB(A) heating, tied to AHRI rated conditions. It gives no distance for those values, so you cannot treat them as the level at the fence. It is also a sound pressure style number, not the sound power figure Carrier and Trane print.
  • Lennox. We found no sound figure in the multi-zone outdoor unit instructions we read, but they give placement advice, covered below.

The takeaway: a bigger number from one maker and a smaller number from another may not be comparable. Check whether it is power or pressure, at what speed, and at what distance.

Why inverter units run quieter

The Carrier sheet shows the reason in numbers. At its slowest the 036 unit is rated 55 dBA and at full speed 72 dBA, a 17 dB spread. When such a unit runs at low speed, its sound sits nearer the low figure. The Carrier sheet also advertises "as low as 55 dBA in low speed" for its Silencer System II. See Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors for how the drive controls that speed. Because the standard rates one speed, ask which speed a quoted figure belongs to, and expect a hot afternoon to be louder than the low-speed figure.

Placement: distance, walls and windows

Two rules of thumb from public acoustics sources do most of the work. FHWA's construction noise handbook says sound from a point source drops 6 dB for each doubling of distance, and OSHA's noise page says the same for open space. The Florida Department of Transportation's training lesson adds that on hard ground the drop may be closer to 3 dB and on soft ground 4.5 to 6 dB. So 6 dB is a best case for a single small source in the open, not a guarantee.

Walls matter too. Under AHRI 275 a single reflecting surface within about 3 meters (10 feet) adds a 3 dB location correction, and an inside corner of two walls adds 6 dB. The same standard lists sound paths into a building: 0 dB to a point outdoors, 10 dB through an open window, 17 dB through a closed single-pane window, and 23 dB through a closed double-pane window or solid wall. An open bedroom window is the worst case. Lennox's instructions say the same in plain words: install the unit as far as possible from the property line, since some localities judge sound at the neighbor's property, avoid spots near a bedroom, and do not put it directly outside a window because glass transmits sound readily. Check Outdoor Unit Clearances too, since a quiet spot must still leave room for airflow.

Sound blankets, enclosures and pads

Carrier's 24VNA9 sheet lists a "Deluxe Sound Blanket" as a standard feature but gives no reduction figure. Trane's XR15 sheet shows a "Sound Enclosure" on some models and a rubber isolator kit made to cut transmitted sound. Neither sheet promises a number of dB, so we do not either. Never add a cover or enclosure that blocks the air inlet or outlet. Check the maker's accessory list for your model.

New Jersey rules, in general terms

The state noise rule, N.J.A.C. 7:29, sets sound limits measured at a residential property line: 65 dBA from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. and 50 dBA from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m., for continuous sound read on the slow meter setting. As we read it, the rule says it applies to sound from an industrial, commercial, public service or community service facility. A New Jersey DEP handout on ordinances says the state regulations apply to stationary commercial and industrial properties, that a town ordinance with measurable limits must be approved by the DEP, and that a plain nuisance code cannot set measurable limits. We did not find a page saying how those limits apply to a single-family home's own air conditioner. The DEP handout says towns may have their own ordinance or nuisance code, so call the town and ask. We use the 50 dBA night figure below only as a handy benchmark, not as a statement of what binds a homeowner.

Worked example: two units and a fence line

Assume (our own numbers) that one heat pump measures 52 dBA at 10 feet in the open, toward the neighbor's yard. Use the FDOT addition table: if two levels differ by 0 or 1 dB, add 3 dB to the higher; by 2 or 3, add 2; by 4 to 9, add 1; by 10 or more, add 0.

  1. Two identical units side by side, both 10 feet from the spot: 52 and 52 differ by 0, so add 3. 52 + 3 = 55 dBA.
  2. Move the spot away, doubling the distance to 20 feet. Each unit drops 6 dB (best case, open space): 52 - 6 = 46 dBA each.
  3. Add the two at 20 feet: 46 and 46 differ by 0, so 46 + 3 = 49 dBA.
  4. Compare with the 50 dBA night benchmark: 55 is 5 over, 49 is 1 under. Only the doubled distance clears it, and not by much.
  5. If the units differ, say one reads 52 and the other 46: they differ by 6, so add 1. 52 + 1 = 53 dBA.

Check with the full formula from AHRI 275: 10 log(10^5.2 + 10^4.6) is about 52.97, which rounds to 53. Add a wall within 10 feet and AHRI 275 says to add another 3 dB at the source side. For reference, the EPA's 1974 levels document names 55 dB (day-night average) outdoors as a level to protect public health in residential areas, but says the figures are not standards or regulations.

Noises that mean a fault

A rated sound is the steady whoosh of fan and compressor. A new grind, squeal, rattle, buzz or hum with no start is not a sound-rating problem. Our noise table in Furnace, AC and Boiler Noises says a hum with no start points toward the capacitor or a compressor that cannot start, and a rattle may be a loose panel, bolt or an unlevel outdoor unit. If the unit suddenly gets louder, shut it off and call. For compressor start-up and protection, see Crankcase Heaters.

Field card: Quieter Outdoor Units

Power is not pressure, and distance is not guaranteed.

  • AHRI 270 rates sound power in dBA at one constant speed.
  • Check whether a printed number is power or pressure, and at what speed and distance.
  • Open space: about 6 dB less per doubling of distance; hard ground can be less.
  • Two equal sources add 3 dB, not double.
  • A nearby wall adds about 3 dB, a corner 6 dB (AHRI 275).
  • Keep units off bedroom windows and far from the property line.
  • Never block airflow with a cover or enclosure.
  • Loud new noise means shut off and call.
