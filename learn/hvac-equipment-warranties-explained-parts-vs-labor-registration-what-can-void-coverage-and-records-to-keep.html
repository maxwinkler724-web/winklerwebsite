HVAC Equipment Warranties Explained: Parts vs Labor, Registration, What Can Void Coverage, and Records to Keep

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new furnace, air conditioner or heat pump usually comes with several different promises from different parties, and they do not all cover the same things. This lesson sorts them out in plain English so you know what to ask for, what to keep, and what to check before you need a repair.

General information, not legal advice. Warranty terms vary by maker, by model and by state. Your own paperwork controls. For a legal question, talk to a lawyer or your state consumer protection office.

Three layers, three different promises

Think of coverage as layers (see the sketch below). They can come from different companies and each has its own rules.

  • Manufacturer parts warranty. This comes from the equipment maker. Maker warranty documents we read covered a part found defective in materials or workmanship, and several said plainly that labor is not included. One listed "Labor, freight, or any other cost associated with the service" as excluded. Another excluded labor for diagnosing, repairing, removing or installing parts. Not every maker is the same, so look for the word labor in yours.
  • Installer labor warranty. This is the installing company's own promise about its workmanship, often in the contract or invoice. We did not find a standard length, so do not assume one. Ask for it in writing, and see our estimate and written contract lesson.
  • Extended service plan. The FTC describes an extended warranty or service contract as an add-on you pay for that "costs extra and might cover different issues than a manufacturer's warranty." The FTC also notes the company selling it may not be the manufacturer, and says to assume that anything not listed in the contract is not covered.

Registration: why the date matters

Several maker documents we read tie a longer parts term to registering the equipment online within a set window. The windows we saw were 60 days and 90 days after installation, and the unregistered term in those documents was 5 years. One maker page said that if you miss the window, the applicable warranty period is 5 years, and another said late registration is not accepted. A third page said it does not say what happens when registration is late. That is why we cannot give you one rule: terms vary by maker, so read yours.

Two more details. Some makers say registration is not required where state law does not let coverage depend on it. And the FTC says a warranty should state whether you must return a registration card to qualify. Our practical advice: put the registration on your calendar for installation week, and ask your installer whether they will do it.

Common things that limit or void coverage

These came up in the maker warranty documents we read. Your own may differ.

  • No proof of maintenance. One residential warranty asks for proof of proper maintenance over the warranty life, described as at least annual. Another did not require proof but excluded failures caused by improper maintenance. Routine upkeep such as filter and coil cleaning was listed as the owner's job. See filter and MERV basics, the spring AC startup checklist and the fall heating checklist.
  • Improper or unlicensed installation. One document required installation per the maker's instructions and local codes by a licensed dealer or contractor. Another excluded damage from faulty installation. We make no claims here about any particular state's licensing rules.
  • Wrong or recycled refrigerant. One document excluded damage from unapproved refrigerant types or used or recycled refrigerant, and did not cover the refrigerant itself.
  • Moving the unit. One document said coverage ends once the unit is removed from its original installation location.
  • Removed or defaced rating plate. One document required the plate to stay readable.
  • Purchase channel. One document excluded products bought on the internet.

What federal law says

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act is the federal law on consumer product warranties. The FTC's business guide says it covers written warranties on tangible property normally used for personal, family or household purposes, and that it does not apply to oral warranties or to services alone. It does apply when a warranty covers repair parts and the workmanship on a repair.

Full vs. limited. Per the FTC guide, written warranties on products costing more than $10 must be titled "full" or "limited." A warranty may be called full only if it meets all five standards, including free service, service for anyone who owns the product during the warranty period, and a replacement or refund choice if repair fails after a reasonable number of tries. If any is missing, it is "limited." The same guide gives parts-only coverage, with the consumer paying labor, as an example of a limited warranty. Most equipment warranties you will see are limited.

Tie-in sales. The FTC says federal law generally does not let a warrantor require you to use a particular company's parts or service to keep coverage, unless the item is provided free under the warranty or the FTC grants a waiver. The guide also says a warrantor may still disclaim coverage for damage caused by parts or service it did not provide. So "use only our dealer or the warranty is void" is the kind of statement to question, while "we do not cover damage caused by improper work" is allowed. If you are unsure, ask the maker in writing or contact your state consumer protection office.

Before you buy. The FTC says a written warranty must be available to read before you buy, and that promises from a salesperson should be put in writing.

Selling the house: does the warranty transfer?

It depends, and the documents we read disagreed. One said the longer registered term lasts only while the original registered owner (or spouse) owns and lives in the home. Another said residential parts coverage extends to later owners for 5 years with no registration. A third said the registered term does not transfer automatically, except in states that require it. A limited warranty can end on transfer, and the FTC guide says a full warranty must serve anyone who owns the product during the period. Ask the maker how to transfer, and whether there is a deadline or form. Give the buyer the paperwork either way.

Records to keep

  • Model and serial number from the nameplate (see reading the nameplate). Photograph it.
  • Install date and the final invoice.
  • The maker warranty, the installer's written labor terms, and any service plan contract.
  • Registration confirmation.
  • A maintenance log: date, who did it, what was done. The FTC suggests keeping the receipt with the warranty as proof of purchase date and original ownership.

Worked example (ours, made up)

These inputs are our assumptions, not any maker's terms. Assume the maker gives 10 years on parts if registered within 60 days of install, 5 years if not, and excludes labor. Assume the installer gives 1 year on labor. Assume the homeowner has no service plan.

  1. Day 0: Install. The homeowner registers on day 20. That is inside the 60-day window, so the 10-year parts term applies.
  2. Year 1: Labor warranty ends.
  3. Year 4: A part fails. A technician finds it defective. The part is covered by the maker. The labor to diagnose and replace it is not covered by the maker, and the installer's labor promise ended in year 1, so the homeowner pays the labor.
  4. What would have changed it: With a service plan that lists labor, that plan might have paid it. If registration had been missed, the 5-year term would still have covered year 4 in this example, but not a failure in year 7.

The lesson: parts covered and labor covered are separate questions. Ask both when you get a repair estimate.

Field card: HVAC equipment warranties

Parts covered does not mean labor covered.

  • Three layers: maker parts, installer labor, optional paid plan.
  • Register within the maker's window; windows we saw were 60 and 90 days.
  • Keep model, serial, invoice, registration and a maintenance log.
  • Ask about transfer before you sell.
  • Federal law generally bars requiring a specific brand of service to keep coverage.
