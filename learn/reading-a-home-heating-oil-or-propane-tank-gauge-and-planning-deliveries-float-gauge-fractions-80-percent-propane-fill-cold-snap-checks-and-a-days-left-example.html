Reading Your Heating Oil or Propane Tank Gauge and Planning Deliveries

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A fuel tank gauge is a rough guess, not a precise meter, and the bottom and top of a tank are not fuel you can count on. This lesson shows how to read an oil float gauge and a propane dial, how to turn a reading into days of heat left, and why it pays to order before the needle gets low.

Oil tanks: the float gauge

Most home heating-oil tanks have a float gauge on top. A float rides on the oil surface and moves a pointer or a marked rod, usually in fractions: empty, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4 and full. Some tanks have a sight gauge, a clear tube or window, instead. State tank guides say aboveground tanks should have a working sight gauge, and one state checklist asks whether the gauge is cracked, stuck or frozen (sources 3, 5 and 6).

What the fractions mean (our own explanation): each mark is a fraction of the tank's nominal volume, not a measured number of gallons. Tanks are not perfect boxes. An oval or cylinder tank on its side holds less oil per inch near the top and bottom than near the middle, so the pointer moves unevenly. Treat every fraction as approximate and ask your supplier how your gauge compares with a delivery.

Why the top and bottom are not usable

The top of the tank is air space on purpose. A delivery should stop with room left, and the vent pipe lets air out while the tank fills. Delivery people listen for the vent whistle that warns the tank is nearly full (source 5). The bottom is no better. A state guide notes that sludge can build up in a tank and that a dealer can add a fuel additive to help (source 3). Our own caution: the last stretch of oil at the bottom is where water and sludge collect, and the fuel line does not pick up from the very floor. So an "empty" reading with a bit left is not a safe reserve. Running a burner dry can pull debris into the line and shut the burner down.

Propane tanks: the dial reads percent

A home propane tank usually has a round dial. A state propane safety brochure says the dial shows numbers from 5 to 95, and those numbers are the percentage of gas in the tank (source 1). Ignore any separate pressure scale on the same gauge. The same brochure says if the gauge reads less than 20 percent, order gas now.

Why the dial never reads 100 after a fill: propane is stored as a liquid, and the liquid expands as it warms, so containers are filled only to about 80 percent of capacity. A state fire marshal filling sheet says exactly this and says a fixed liquid level gauge marks the 80 percent point (source 4). The brochure's own chart gives a sense of scale: a 500-gallon tank at 80 percent holds about 400 gallons (source 1). Because the dial is percent of tank volume, your gallons left are the tank size times the percent. For help with cold-weather behavior of propane, see the related lesson below.

Check more often in a cold snap

This part is our own advice. Fuel use rises quickly when outdoor temperatures drop, so a reading that lasted three weeks in October might last one week in a deep freeze. One state guide recommends a monthly tank check as preventive maintenance (source 3). In a cold snap, look at the gauge every few days, and look again before a storm is forecast, since deliveries can slow down when roads are bad. A sudden jump in use is also a clue: one state checklist lists a sudden increase in oil use as a possible sign of a leak (source 6), and another guide says to report any sudden change in product level to your supplier or a heating professional (source 3).

Automatic delivery or will-call

Our own explanation. With automatic delivery, the supplier estimates your use from weather data and your past deliveries and schedules a fill. It saves you from watching the gauge, but it is an estimate, so you should still glance at the gauge. With will-call (also called keep-full or call-in service), you decide when to order, so you control the timing but you carry the risk of forgetting. Ask your supplier how each works, what lead time they need in cold weather, and whether automatic delivery has ever missed a house like yours.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions: a 275-gallon oil tank, a gauge reading 1/4, and a household that used about 4 gallons per day in recent cold weather. These numbers are our own, chosen to show the method. Tank shapes make fractions approximate, so your real number can differ.

  1. Oil left: 275 x 0.25 = 68.75, so about 69 gallons.
  2. Days of heat: 69 / 4 = 17.25, so about 17 days.
  3. Decision: 17 days sounds like plenty, but that is the best case. It ignores the unusable bottom, a colder week that raises use, and a delivery that may take several days to arrive. So order now rather than waiting for the gauge to hit empty.

For propane the arithmetic is the same: gallons = tank size x percent on the dial. Using the brochure's figures for scale, a 500-gallon tank reading 30 percent holds about 150 gallons (our own arithmetic: 500 x 0.30), and that is already close to the 20 percent order-now line.

Keep a simple log

Our own suggestion. Write the date, the gauge reading and any delivery in a notebook or a phone note, every week and every few days in cold weather. After two or three entries you can see the drop between readings and divide by the days. That gives your real daily use instead of a guess. Write down the delivery date and the gallons delivered too. If use jumps with no weather change, call your supplier.

Keep the path clear

State guides say to keep the oil fill pipe accessible and visible to the delivery company, and to keep the vent line clear of snow, ice and insect nests (sources 2 and 3). Our own addition: after a snowfall, shovel a path to the fill pipe or the propane tank and clear around the dome and the shutoff, and keep the gauge face readable. A driver who cannot reach the tank may skip your stop.

Signs of a stuck gauge

Our own checklist, built on the state advice to check for a gauge that is cracked, stuck or frozen (source 6). The gauge may be stuck if it has read the same number for weeks while the house has been heating, if the reading rose or fell by a large jump with no delivery, or if tapping the glass or housing changes it. A cracked window, fog inside the glass or oil around the gauge base also needs attention. A stuck gauge can hide a nearly empty tank, so ask your supplier to confirm the level on the next visit. Do not take apart the gauge or the tank fittings yourself.

If you smell gas or oil

For propane, a state brochure says to treat all odors seriously and not to assume a strong smell only means the tank is nearly empty. It says to put out flames, leave the area, avoid electric switches and appliances, close the tank shutoff, then call your supplier or the fire department from a phone away from the smell (source 1). Another state brochure says to evacuate everyone right away and not to stop near the house or tank to wait for help (source 7). A third page adds that you should not turn the gas back on once it has been shut off for a safety concern (source 8).

For oil, one state guide lists the smell of oil in your home or around the tank as a sign of a fuel release, and says to call your furnace repair company if you find vapors in a basement or crawlspace (source 3). Our own caution: if the smell is strong or anyone feels dizzy or ill, go outside to fresh air first and call from there, and use the related lesson on gas smells below.

Call the supplier; do not refill or relight yourself

Do not try to add fuel to a tank yourself, and do not relight pilots or restart a system after a run-out or a gas smell. The propane brochure says a trained technician should light pilots in most cases, and to never light a pilot if you smell gas (source 1). Another brochure says not to attempt repairs on any propane system or appliance (source 7). After a run-out, air in the oil line and sludge can make restarting a job for a service technician. See the related lesson on running out of heating oil.

Field card: Reading Your Heating Oil or Propane Tank Gauge and Planning Deliveries

The gauge is a guess; order early.

  • Oil gauge fractions are approximate. Top and bottom of the tank are not usable fuel.
  • Propane dial shows percent of tank volume. Tanks are filled to about 80 percent so the liquid can expand.
  • Order propane before the dial gets to 20 percent.
  • Gallons left = tank size x fraction or percent. Days left = gallons / daily use.
  • Check every few days in a cold snap; keep a dated log.
  • Clear snow from the fill pipe, vent and tank path.
  • Gas smell: leave, no switches or flames, call from outside. Never relight or refill yourself.
