Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines: Alloys, Flux and Joint Prep

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A good brazed joint is mostly decided before the torch is lit. The tube has to be cut square, cleaned and seated in a fitting with the right tiny gap, and then the heat has to pull the filler metal into that gap. This lesson covers the alloy choice, the flux question, the prep, the heating, and the safety that goes with an open flame.

Why brazing and not soft solder

The American Welding Society draws the line at 840°F. The Copper Development Association (CDA) data sheet on soldering and brazing copper tube repeats that definition: below 840°F is soldering, above it is brazing. In copper systems, soldering typically runs around 350 to 600°F and brazing around 1100 to 1500°F. The same sheet says soldered joints are generally used for service up to 250°F, while brazed joints are stronger and handle system temperatures up to 350°F. An ACHR News article on the basics adds the rule that matters here: codes require refrigerant lines to be joined with a brazing alloy, and soldering is not approved for refrigerant joints. Your local code and the equipment maker's instructions have the final word.

Picking the alloy: BCuP or silver

The CDA Copper Tube Handbook sorts brazing filler metals into two families. The BCuP family contains phosphorus. The handbook calls it more economical than the silver (BAg) family and better suited to general piping. It says BAg alloys are the ones to use when joining dissimilar metals. It points to BCuP-2 where close tolerances can be held and BCuP-3, -4 or -5 where they cannot, and names BAg-1, BAg-5 and BAg-7 as common silver choices for HVAC refrigeration.

A common refrigeration rod is BCuP-5, which Harris sells as Stay-Silv 15. Harris and the CDA sheet agree on its melting range: it starts to melt (solidus) at 1190°F and is fully liquid (liquidus) at 1475°F. Harris says the wide range lets you fill slightly loose connections. Harris does not recommend it for steel or other ferrous metals.

When flux is needed

Harris says plainly that no flux is required for copper brazing with Stay-Silv 15. Lucas-Milhaupt explains why: the phosphorus in a BCuP alloy reacts with copper oxides, so it acts as its own flux on copper. Lucas-Milhaupt's data page for its 5% silver phosphorus alloy says to use flux when joining copper to brass, and Harris says the same for Stay-Silv 15 on brass, with its white flux. The self-fluxing effect applies to copper-to-copper only.

Steel is different. Harris does not recommend this phosphorus alloy for ferrous metals, the CDA points to silver alloys for dissimilar metals, and ACHR News notes that silver alloys are often called fluxless but flux may still be needed when brazing steel or brass. The CDA says brazing flux is water based and is not interchangeable with soldering flux, and that flux for copper tube should meet AWS A5.31 type FB3-A or FB3-C. ACHR News warns that leftover residue can hold moisture and cause pitting, or hide a pinhole leak.

Joint prep: cut, ream, clean, fit, seat

  • Cut square. The CDA says the cut must be square to the run of the tube so it seats properly in the fitting cup.
  • Ream. Remove the burr the cutter leaves on the inside edge, and any burr on the outside.
  • Clean. Remove oxide and soil from the tube end and the fitting cup. The CDA sheet says to abrade the tube slightly past the cup depth, not to touch cleaned surfaces with bare hands or oily gloves, and not to remove so much copper that the fit goes loose. The MCAA pipe-work guide suggests brazing within an hour of cleaning.
  • Check the gap. The CDA sheet says capillary action works best with a gap of 0.002 to 0.005 inch, and that joint strength depends mainly on holding that clearance. Too much clearance can let the joint crack under stress or vibration.
  • Seat and support. Push the tube to the bottom of the cup and hold the assembly firmly so it stays aligned during the braze.

Heating and capillary flow

The CDA handbook describes the sequence. Heat the tube first, starting about an inch from the fitting, sweeping the flame in short strokes and keeping it moving. Then move to the base of the cup, alternating between tube and fitting. Feed the filler where the tube enters the socket and keep the flame off the filler itself. When the joint is hot enough, capillary action pulls the metal into the gap. Do not overheat: ACHR News says prolonged heating forms copper oxide that can block the joint and contaminate the circuit, and it says overheating also burns flux so it stops working. Stop feeding filler when a continuous fillet shows all the way around, and let the joint cool in air. The CDA warns that shock cooling with water may stress or crack it.

Nitrogen and valves

Inside the tube, heat makes scale unless air is pushed out. The CDA says purging with inert gas displaces oxygen from the inside of the system during brazing. The MCAA guide says to plug all openings except one discharge opening on the far side of the joint and to reduce the flow during brazing so it does not build pressure in the tube. Flow settings and the regulator are covered in Nitrogen Purge Flow While Brazing.

Valves have rubber, plastic and electrical parts that heat can ruin, so read the valve maker's sheet. Sporlan's solenoid valve instructions say the extended copper connections on its E Series valves can be brazed in place if you put a wet cloth or chill block on the extensions at the body, and that its B Series valves with brass connections must be completely disassembled before heat touches the body. Sporlan also says to use a torch tip large enough to avoid long heating and to aim the flame away from the valve body. One service bulletin for a Danfoss stainless steel expansion valve says to wrap the valve in a wet rag, heat the copper tube about 10 to 15 seconds and then the valve socket 2 to 5 seconds, using 15% silver alloy with no flux. That is one valve's procedure only.

Worked example: a 3/4 inch joint, step by step

The tube and fitting sizes below are made-up illustration values. The limits come from the sources above.

  1. Find the clearance. The MCAA guide gives the formula: clearance equals (socket inside diameter minus tube outside diameter) divided by 2. Suppose the tube measures 0.750 inch and the cup 0.754 inch. (0.754 − 0.750) ÷ 2 = 0.002 inch. That meets the CDA 0.002 to 0.005 inch window.
  2. Compare a worn fit. A cup of 0.766 inch gives (0.766 − 0.750) ÷ 2 = 0.008 inch. That is outside the window. Lucas-Milhaupt lists wider clearances of 0.007 to 0.015 inch only for BCuP joints longer than 1 inch, so this small joint would be a poor fit. Use a better-fitting part.
  3. Check the overlap. The CDA says joint strength meets or exceeds the base metal when overlap and filler penetration are at least three times the thinner wall. If the wall is 0.035 inch (assumed), 3 × 0.035 = 0.105 inch minimum. Seat the tube all the way to the cup bottom.
  4. Check the heat window. For BCuP-5 the liquidus is 1475°F and the solidus 1190°F, so the melting range is 1475 − 1190 = 285°F. The solidus is 1190 − 840 = 350°F above the soldering line, which is why a soft-solder joint cannot be a stand-in.
  5. Choose flux. Copper to copper with BCuP-5: none needed. If the other end is a brass valve connection, use the flux the alloy maker names (Harris names its white flux for Stay-Silv 15).

Inspect and test

Look for a continuous fillet all the way around. The MCAA guide says joints with cracks, heavy oxidation or pressure-test failures cannot be fixed by dabbing on more filler. Replace the part, or take it apart, clean it and braze again. The CDA says every finished assembly should be tested for joint integrity to the code that applies. For the pressure-test steps, see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods.

PPE and fire safety

  • Eyes and fumes. Harris warns that infrared heat rays can injure eyes, and that you should keep your head out of the fumes and use local exhaust plus general ventilation. The MCAA guide suggests shade 4 or 5 goggles and says not to braze in a confined space without proper ventilation.
  • Coatings and cadmium. The MCAA guide says to remove paint, galvanizing, cadmium and other coatings first. OSHA 1910.252 requires labels on cadmium-bearing filler metals and sets ventilation or respirator rules for cadmium.
  • Fire. OSHA 1910.252 says combustibles should be moved at least 35 feet from the work, or covered or shielded if they cannot be. A fire watch with extinguishing equipment is required when appreciable combustibles are within 35 feet, and it must continue at least a half hour after the work ends. The MCAA guide adds: do not braze alone.
  • Refrigerant in the line. Brazing near refrigerant, especially A2L, needs extra steps; see A2L Installation and Brazing Practices.

Field card: Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines

Fit decides the joint; the torch only finishes it.

  • Brazing is above 840°F; refrigerant joints are normally brazed; local code governs.
  • BCuP-5: melts 1190 to 1475°F; no flux copper to copper, flux for brass.
  • Not for steel with phosphorus alloys; use the alloy maker's sheet.
  • Cut square, ream, clean, no bare hands, seat to the cup bottom.
  • Clearance goal: 0.002 to 0.005 in. (CDA).
  • Heat tube, then fitting; flame moving; filler at the mouth; look for a full fillet.
  • Nitrogen with an open exit; wet cloth or disassemble valves per the maker.
  • Move combustibles 35 ft, keep a fire watch half an hour after.
