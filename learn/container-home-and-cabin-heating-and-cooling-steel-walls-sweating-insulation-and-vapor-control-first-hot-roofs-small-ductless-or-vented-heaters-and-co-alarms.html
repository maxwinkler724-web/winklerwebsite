Container Home and Cabin Heating and Cooling: Steel Walls, Condensation and Safe Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steel shipping container is strong and cheap to place, but as a house it is a thermal problem. Steel moves heat about as fast as anything you can build with, and it gets cold enough in winter to make indoor air drip. This lesson explains what happens, why the wall comes first and the equipment second, and how to heat and cool a small steel box without creating a safety problem.

Why a bare steel box is hard to keep comfortable

A thin steel wall has almost no resistance to heat flow. Only the thin films of still air on each face slow it down. In winter the inside face of the steel sits close to the outdoor temperature. In summer a dark roof in full sun gets far hotter than the air, and that heat pours straight through.

Think of the wall as layers in series: the more resistance in the path, the less heat moves. Our lesson on heat loss through walls and ceilings shows how R-values add and why framing changes the whole-wall number. For a container the practical point is simple: the insulation is nearly the entire wall.

Why steel sweats, and why rust follows

Condensation forms when warm, moist air touches a cold surface. A land-grant extension guide on controlling condensation describes warm air holding more moisture and moving toward cold surfaces, where the moisture turns into water, frost or ice. Cooking, showers and breathing all add moisture to a small room, and a cabin has very little air volume to absorb it. The same guide suggests keeping indoor humidity around 30 to 45 percent in the heating season.

On bare steel the result is dripping, wet insulation and rust. A federal guide on mold and moisture says moisture control is the key to mold control, and it lists condensation on walls or pipes as a sign of high humidity and a reason to act quickly. Rust is not just cosmetic. Wet steel that never dries keeps corroding from the back side where you cannot see it.

Insulation and the air and vapor control layer come first

Buying equipment before fixing the shell is the common mistake. Sequence matters:

  • Stop moist air from reaching cold steel. The extension guide says to put a low-permeability layer on the warm side of walls, ceilings and floors, and to seal cracks and holes where warm moist air can sneak into cold areas. Closed-cell spray foam applied directly to the steel does both jobs in one layer, because it blocks air movement and slows vapor. Rigid foam board can work if every seam and edge is sealed. Foam that leaves gaps lets moist air reach the steel behind it.
  • Treat thermal bridges. The corrugations, the steel frame and any steel studs conduct heat around the insulation. Foam that covers the frame, or a continuous layer of rigid foam over it, helps. Bridging means the real R-value is lower than the foam alone suggests (see the worked example).
  • Do not forget the roof and floor. The roof is the largest single face a container shows to the sky, and a steel floor over open air loses heat like a wall. Both need the same continuous insulation and air control.
  • Do not trap moisture. The guide warns that vapor barriers on both the warm and cold sides can trap moisture, so plan the layers with one control layer placed on the warm side. This is a point to settle with a local building official or an experienced builder before closing up walls.

Sun on a steel roof in summer

A federal heat island page says high solar reflectance and thermal emittance lower roof, indoor and outdoor temperatures. It reports that in air-conditioned homes a reflective roof can cut peak cooling demand by 11 to 27 percent, and in homes without air conditioning it can lower maximum indoor temperatures by about 2.2 to 5.9 F. Those are studies of ordinary homes, so treat them as a direction, not a promise for a container.

Our own suggestions, based on that principle: use a light, reflective roof color; shade the roof with trees, an awning or a separate frame; or build a vented roof over the box, with an air gap that lets hot air escape before it reaches the steel. Insulation on the underside still matters, because shade only lowers the heat coming in.

Worked example (our own)

These are our own assumptions, not measured data. A 40-foot container is about 40 by 8 by 8.5 feet. We count the walls, the two ends and the roof, and leave the floor out.

  1. Long walls: 2 x 40 x 8.5 = 680 square feet.
  2. Ends: 2 x 8 x 8.5 = 136 square feet.
  3. Roof: 40 x 8 = 320 square feet.
  4. Total area: 680 + 136 + 320 = 1,136 square feet.

Assume a 40 F difference between indoors and outdoors. Heat loss in Btu per hour is area divided by R-value, times the temperature difference.

  • Bare steel. Assume only air films, about R-0.7 in total. Loss = 1,136 / 0.7 x 40 = 1,623 x 40, about 65,000 Btu per hour.
  • Insulated. Assume about 2 inches of closed-cell foam at roughly R-6.5 per inch, which is R-13, plus the films at R-0.7, for about R-14 total, ignoring bridging. Loss = 1,136 / 14 x 40 = 81.1 x 40, about 3,250 Btu per hour.

The insulated box loses about one twentieth as much heat. The real numbers will be higher, because steel thermal bridges and air leaks add loss that this simple arithmetic leaves out. Even if real losses were double, they would still be small, which is why a small ductless heat pump or a small vented heater can serve a well-built container. A bare box needs an oversized system that cycles and still sweats. Our tiny-home lesson covers oversizing and ductless heads for very small loads, and our lesson on AC without ducts compares the options.

Choosing heat and cooling for a small box

  • Small ductless heat pump. One head can heat and cool a tight, insulated container. Size it to the calculated load, not to a rule of thumb, because an oversized unit short-cycles and does not dry the air well.
  • Properly vented heater. A sealed-combustion or direct-vent heater takes air from outdoors and sends exhaust outdoors, so it does not use up indoor air. Follow the maker's instructions for vent clearances.
  • Never use unvented fuel heaters in a small sealed box. A federal indoor air page says unvented kerosene and gas space heaters release carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide and particles, and that combustion appliances add water vapor unless vented outdoors, which is a double problem in a steel cabin. It also warns that blocked or damaged flues and chimneys can release dangerous or even fatal levels of carbon monoxide.

Fresh air, exhaust, and alarms

A tight container needs planned ventilation. Vent kitchen and bath fans to the outdoors, not into the box. The extension guide and the federal moisture guide both say exhaust fans for cooking and bathing should vent outside. Use a bath fan or open a window when showering. See our lessons on bathroom exhaust fans and winter condensation and indoor humidity.

A federal safety agency says to put carbon monoxide alarms on every floor and near sleeping areas, to test them monthly and to change batteries about once a year. The federal fire administration says to put smoke alarms inside and outside each bedroom and on every level, to test them monthly and to replace the whole alarm 10 years after its manufacture date. See also our carbon monoxide alarms lesson.

Check local rules first

Whether a container can be a dwelling, and what foundation, insulation, fire safety, heating equipment and electrical wiring it needs, is set locally. Ask your local building and zoning office before you buy the unit or cut openings, and have wiring and fuel piping done and inspected as your local rules require. Adopted building and mechanical codes differ by place, so confirm which ones apply to you.

Field card: Container Home Heating and Cooling

Shell first, then a small system, never an unvented fuel heater.

  • Bare steel loses heat very fast and sweats when warm moist air touches it.
  • Seal and insulate first: closed-cell foam or sealed rigid foam, roof and floor included.
  • Steel frame and corrugations are thermal bridges, so real loss is higher than foam R-value alone suggests.
  • Light roof color, shade or a vented roof cuts summer heat.
  • Choose a small ductless heat pump or a properly vented heater, sized to the calculated load.
  • No unvented fuel heaters in a small sealed box.
  • Vent kitchen and bath fans outdoors; install CO and smoke alarms; check local rules.
