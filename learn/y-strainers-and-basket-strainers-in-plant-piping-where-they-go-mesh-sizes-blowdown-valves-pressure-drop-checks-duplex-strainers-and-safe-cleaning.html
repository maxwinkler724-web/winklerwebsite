Y-Strainers and Basket Strainers in Plant Piping: Where They Go, Mesh Sizes, Blowdown Valves, Pressure Drop Checks, Duplex Strainers and Safe Cleaning

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A strainer is a cheap part that protects expensive ones. It catches pipe scale, weld slag, gasket bits and sludge before they reach a pump, a control valve, a steam trap or a coil. This lesson covers where they go, how the screen is chosen, how to tell when one needs cleaning, and how to open one without getting hurt.

Y-strainers and basket strainers

A Y-strainer has a straight-through body with an angled leg that holds a cylindrical screen. Water or steam passes through the screen from the inside out, and debris stays in the leg. It is compact, works on liquids and steam, and is usually the choice for smaller pipe. A basket strainer is a larger vessel with a bolted cover and a lift-out basket. It holds more debris and is easier to clean on bigger lines. Two university design standards we read specify both types for hydronic piping, with the same pressure rating for each. Another standard adds a duplex basket type, covered below.

Where they go

The rule is to put the strainer just ahead of whatever the debris would hurt. One university standard calls for strainers upstream of pumps, control valves and the building differential pressure valve on hydronic systems, and upstream of pressure reducing valves, steam traps and control valves on steam. A second spec says to install them ahead of each control valve, steam trap and pressure reducing valve, in a way that leaves room for blowdown and for pulling the basket. The same standard asks for a pressure gauge on the suction side of each pump inlet strainer or suction diffuser. Coil control valves are control valves, so a strainer ahead of each one follows the same logic. For pump suction hardware, see our lesson on suction diffusers and triple-duty valves. Condenser water has its own wrinkle at the tower basin, covered in basin outlet and suction problems.

Orientation matters. One standard has steam strainers lying on their side with the screen chamber at 3 or 9 o'clock, and other strainers horizontal with the chamber at 6 o'clock. Another says water strainers go wye down, and steam strainers ahead of traps wye down too. Check the job spec and the maker's arrow and instructions, because the sources do not agree on every case.

Mesh and perforation sizes

Mesh is the number of openings per inch, so a higher number means finer straining. Perforated screens are rated by hole diameter instead. The numbers below come from the design standards we read. They are examples of what owners ask for, not a code requirement.

  • Hydronic Y-strainers 2 inch and smaller: 20 mesh in two standards. In larger sizes, one standard uses 3/64 inch perforations.
  • Steam: 3/64 inch perforations in general, 20 mesh ahead of traps, and 60 to 100 mesh ahead of control valves in one standard. A different standard uses 30 mesh or a .045 inch perforation on steam.
  • Larger water strainers: 1/16 inch perforations through 4 inch pipe and 5/32 inch for 5 inch and up in one standard; .062 inch for 4 inch and .125 inch for 5 inch and up in another.
  • Basket strainers: a perforated stainless basket with 50 percent free area, with a finer 40 mesh screen for startup.
  • Steam strainer screens: net free area at least 2.5 times the cross section of the entering pipe.

Finer is not always better. A fine screen protects a control valve seat, but it loads up faster, so the pressure drop climbs sooner. That is why the same standards use a coarse screen for the pump and a finer one only where a device needs it.

Blowdown valves

Every strainer in these standards gets a blowdown connection at the bottom of the leg or basket. For larger water and steam strainers that means a full-port ball valve. One standard asks for a 3/4 inch nipple and ball valve on strainers 2 inch and larger. Another asks for a hose connection and cap, and a third says to pipe the blowoff to the closest drain. On steam, one standard puts a shut-off valve and a capped nipple after the blowdown valve. Blowdown is a quick way to flush loose debris out without opening the cover, but it does not replace pulling the screen at scheduled cleanings. Never blow down toward people, and open the valve slowly.

Measuring pressure drop to know when to clean

The best clue that a strainer is loading up is a rising pressure drop, read with a gauge at each of two taps, one upstream and one downstream of the screen. We suggest using the same gauge on both taps, one after the other, or a differential gauge, because two cheap gauges can disagree by more than the drop you are trying to see. Read the clean value at commissioning and write it on the strainer tag or log. One standard sets a maximum clean pressure drop of 1 psig for hydronic strainers, so a clean reading well above that is a clue that the screen is too fine or the strainer is too small. A rising reading at the same flow means debris. The exact reading at which to clean is a decision for the owner and the maker, so ask what they have set.

Fresh piping is dirtier. One university spec has strainers cleaned after initial startup, after 30 days of operation and at final acceptance. Another has the system flushed with clean water and then the screens removed and cleaned or replaced. Our lessons on flushing new piping and strainer habits and pump seals cover those steps in more detail. The standards describe startup strainers as the fine screens used during commissioning that are replaced with permanent ones afterward.

Worked example (our own): what a clogged screen costs

Our own example, not a measured case. A strainer on a 200 gpm chilled water line reads 2 psi drop when clean.

Square law (our own derivation). For a screen, the pressure loss is roughly a loss coefficient times the velocity head, so drop = K x (density x v squared / 2). Velocity through the screen is flow divided by open area, so with K unchanged, drop is proportional to the square of (flow / open area).

Assumptions. Flow stays at 200 gpm (for example a variable speed pump holds it). The open area halves evenly, and K does not change. Water is near 1.0 specific gravity. These are assumed, and real debris loading is rarely this tidy.

  1. Halving the area doubles velocity through the screen. Drop scales with velocity squared, so it is multiplied by 2 squared, which is 4.
  2. New drop = 2 psi x 4 = 8 psi. Extra drop = 8 - 2 = 6 psi.
  3. Convert to head: 6 psi x 2.31 ft per psi = 13.9 ft of water.
  4. Extra fluid power = 200 gpm x 13.86 ft x 1.0 / 3,960 = 0.70 hp. The factor 3,960 and the term fluid power come from a DOE pumping sourcebook, which also defines pump efficiency as fluid power divided by shaft power.
  5. Assume a combined pump and motor efficiency of 60 percent. A DOE tip sheet says efficiencies of 50 to 60 percent are common. Extra input = 0.70 / 0.60 = 1.17 hp = 0.87 kW (1 hp = 0.7457 kW).
  6. Assume 5,000 run hours a year. Extra energy = 0.87 kW x 5,000 h = 4,350 kWh. At an assumed $0.15 per kWh, that is about $650 a year.

One clogged strainer costs real money, and in a real system the flow would also sag unless the controls make up for it. Plot your own clean and dirty readings the same way. If you do not trust your numbers, compare against the pump curve; our chilled water pump lesson shows how.

Duplex strainers

A duplex strainer has two baskets and a diverter valve, so flow can be switched to one side while the other is opened and cleaned with the line still running. One university spec lists a 150 psig duplex basket type for 3 inch and smaller with dual 40 mesh stainless baskets, a diverter valve and a full-port blowdown valve. Use one where the line cannot be shut down for cleaning, such as a loop that feeds a process. Follow the maker's switching and venting steps. The idle side is still a pressure vessel, so isolate and drain it before opening it.

Isolating and cleaning safely

A strainer cover holds back full system pressure and often hot water or steam. OSHA's lockout rule for servicing equipment requires, in order, preparing, shutting down, isolating, applying lockout or tagout devices, relieving or restraining stored or residual energy, and verifying isolation. We read that to include trapped pressure and heat in a pipe. In practice:

  1. Follow your site procedure and lock out the pump or control that feeds the line. See lockout, tagout and verify dead.
  2. Close the isolation valves on both sides, or use the bypass if one is piped around the strainer. A university standard calls for isolation valves and a bypass where several systems depend on one strainer.
  3. Let the line cool, then relieve pressure at the blowdown valve or a drain, slowly, to a safe place. Confirm the gauges read zero.
  4. Loosen the cover a little and watch before removing it. Pull the screen, clean it, check it for tears or dents, and replace the gasket if it is damaged.
  5. Close up, refill, vent the air, open the isolation valves slowly and read your pressure drop. Record it as the new clean value.

Steam strainers upstream of traps are the same job on hotter piping; see steam traps explained and steam trap testing methods for why debris matters there.

Field card: Y-strainers and basket strainers

Strain before you protect, read before you open.

  • Put a strainer just ahead of pumps, control valves, steam traps and pressure reducing valves.
  • Mesh is openings per inch; perforations are hole diameters. Finer screens clog sooner.
  • Every strainer gets a blowdown valve; open it slowly and away from people.
  • Record the clean pressure drop from two taps, and compare at the same flow.
  • Drop goes with the square of velocity: half the open area, about four times the drop (our assumption).
  • Isolate, lock out, cool, relieve pressure and verify before opening the cover.
  • Duplex units let you clean one basket while the other runs.
