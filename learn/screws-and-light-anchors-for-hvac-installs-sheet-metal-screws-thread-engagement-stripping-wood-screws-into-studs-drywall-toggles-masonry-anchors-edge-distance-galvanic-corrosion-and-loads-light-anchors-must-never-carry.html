Screws and Light Anchors for HVAC Installs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A thermostat plate, a line-set cover, a sheet metal transition and a disconnect box all hang on small fasteners. Most callbacks with these fasteners come from the same few choices: the wrong screw for the base material, a screw that missed the stud, two dissimilar metals touching in the rain, or a light anchor asked to do a heavy job. This lesson covers what to pick, how to drive it, and how to check it with simple arithmetic.

Sheet metal screws: number sizes, drill points and hex heads

Sheet metal screws are sold by a number size and a length. Our shop rule of thumb is that a higher number means a thicker screw. A self-tapping screw cuts or forms its own threads but usually needs a pilot hole. A self-drilling screw has a drill point that makes its own hole first, so it goes in with one tool. A hex head takes a nut driver or socket, which keeps the bit seated while you push. These are our own plain-language descriptions of common shop practice. For duct seams, collars and take-offs, see Hand Sheet Metal Work for HVAC Techs, which this lesson does not repeat.

How many threads should engage

A university machine-design reference guide says to design threaded features with at least 5 threads of engagement, and it says that if that is not possible, use a normal nut or a rivet nut instead. It also notes that about twice the major diameter of engagement gives full strength. Thin duct or cabinet metal cannot give you five threads. So treat a screw in a single thin sheet as a light, shear-type connection only: use more screws, not a bigger pull on one, and use a nut or rivet nut where the part must resist being pulled off.

Stripping and overdriving

If a screw suddenly spins freely, the threads it cut in metal or wood have stripped. Our shop practice is to stop, because more turning only makes the hole bigger. Back the screw out, then move to fresh material, step up to the next size, or switch to a nut or rivet nut. Set the drill clutch low and finish by hand. The federal wood handbook adds that wood screws should be turned in, never hammered.

Wood screws into studs

The federal wood handbook chapter on fastenings gives a withdrawal equation for wood screws in side grain: p = 15,700 x G squared x D x L, in pounds, where G is the wood specific gravity, D is the shank diameter in inches and L is the length of threaded penetration in inches. The values are a maximum (ultimate) load, not a safe working load, and apply to the screw sizes in the handbook's table. The same chapter gives these practical points:

  • Pilot (lead) holes should be about 70 percent of the screw root diameter in softwood and about 90 percent in hardwood.
  • For sideways (lateral) loads, penetration into the holding piece should be at least seven times the shank diameter.
  • Keep fasteners from the edge of the wood by at least half the thickness of the side piece, and from the end by at least its thickness.
  • Wood that swings between wet and dry can hold as little as 25 percent of its first withdrawal strength, which the handbook says about nails. We treat that as a warning for screws in damp wood too.

Hitting the stud is the whole game. A federal consumer product safety report on wall anchoring says drywall generally cannot withstand a 50 pound pull, and so a wood screw into a stud is the usual answer. It also says drywall anchors hold best when the pull is parallel to the wall.

Plastic, ribbed and toggle anchors in drywall

Plastic and ribbed anchors expand against the drywall and are for light, mostly sideways loads such as a thermostat plate or a small cover. Toggle bolts and strap-toggle bolts spread their load on the back of the board and hold more, but they still hang on a thin gypsum layer. In that same federal report, staff informally tested two drywall anchors and two toggle types with a 50 pound pull. All held, but each test was run once and the report calls the results anecdotal. It also points out that the standards it reviews put no strength requirement on the anchor in the drywall. Read the maker's data sheet for the rated load and the board thickness, and treat the rating as the ceiling, not the target.

Sleeve and wedge anchors and concrete screws in masonry

A state transportation agency field manual for concrete anchoring says the following about mechanical anchors, and we apply it to the light ones here. Edge distance and spacing must not reduce capacity, and the manual defers to the manufacturer's stated minimums, so the maker's data sheet gives the number. The hole must be cleaned as the maker directs, because poor cleaning can reduce capacity. Tighten to the maker's torque with a calibrated torque wrench, and install at the depth the maker lists. The manual also says overhead installation of mechanical anchors is not recommended, and that they are especially prone to vibration. Because the manual gives no numeric edge distance, we do not give one here either. Concrete screws follow the same rule: the maker's sheet sets the bit size, depth and edge distance. For equipment hanging, see Hanging HVAC Equipment from Strut, Threaded Rod and Anchors.

Stainless or coated screws outdoors

The federal wood handbook reports that copper, silicon bronze and 300-series stainless steel nails performed well in treated lumber, and that stainless steel, aluminum or hot-dipped galvanized nails reduce staining from wood extractives. Plated zinc coatings work by being sacrificial, as a federal fastener design manual puts it: zinc is a sacrificial material. That means a scratched or thin coating wears away first and then the steel underneath is exposed. For outdoor condenser pads, wall plates and cover brackets, pick a screw whose metal and coating suit the weather, and replace any that show red rust.

Galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals

A federal space-center corrosion page says galvanic corrosion needs two dissimilar metals, an electrolyte such as moisture, and an electrical path between them. The farther apart two metals are in the galvanic series, the greater the effect, and the more active metal is the one that corrodes. The page notes that aluminum alloys touching carbon or stainless steel can corrode faster, and shows a stainless screw that corroded aluminum in six months at an outdoor test site. Its prevention list includes using metals closer together in the series and isolating them with insulating sleeves and washers. A federal fastener design manual adds that the anode should be the larger mass: stainless fasteners can be used in carbon steel assemblies, because the carbon steel is the anode, while carbon steel fasteners in a stainless or copper assembly are poor design. In HVAC terms, watch aluminum fin or housing parts touching steel screws, and copper lines touching steel straps or aluminum clamps. What light anchors must never carry

This is our own rule for apprentices. Do not trust plastic, ribbed or drywall toggle anchors with any of these:

  • Equipment weight: air handlers, condensers, water heaters and heavy boxes need structure and the proper hardware.
  • Hangers: rod, strut and trapeze loads belong to the methods in the hanging lesson.
  • Anything overhead: if it falls, it falls on a person. The anchoring manual above does not recommend even mechanical masonry anchors overhead.

Worked example (our own): pull-out check for a line-set cover

This is our own example. All inputs are assumptions we chose for teaching, not data for a real product.

  • The cover and the lines it holds weigh 12 lb. We assume a snag or lean could add 28 lb of pull, so the design pull-out load is 12 + 28 = 40 lb.
  • The cover uses 4 fasteners, and we assume they share the pull equally.
  • Our own safety factor is 6, borrowing the one-sixth working-load idea the federal wood handbook uses for nails.
  • Required ultimate capacity in total: 40 x 6 = 240 lb.

Case A, four #8 wood screws into studs. We assume G = 0.50, D = 0.164 in, and L = 1.25 in of threads in the stud. That meets the seven-diameters guide, since 7 x 0.164 = 1.15 in. Equation: p = 15,700 x G squared x D x L = 15,700 x 0.25 x 0.164 x 1.25 = about 805 lb per screw. Four screws give 4 x 805 = 3,220 lb ultimate, far above 240 lb. Even one screw (805 lb) would pass, so the extra screws mainly spread the load and stop the cover from twisting. Each screw carries 40 / 4 = 10 lb.

Case B, four plastic anchors in drywall only. The handbook equation does not apply to drywall anchors, and we have no source value for them. So we assume a maker sheet lists 40 lb ultimate pull-out per anchor. Four give 4 x 40 = 160 lb, which is less than the required 240 lb. It fails our check. Shared load per anchor is still only 10 lb, but the margin of 4 (40 / 10) is below our factor of 6.

Case C, a mix. If two screws find studs and two anchors stay in drywall, the stiff stud screws take most of the load, so the anchors are not reliable partners. Count only the stud screws, here 2 x 805 = 1,610 lb, and treat the anchors as alignment aids.

Lesson: spread the load across several fasteners, and put as many as possible in studs. Penetration matters too: halving L to 0.625 in halves the screw's holding to about 402 lb.

Field card: Screws and Light Anchors for HVAC Installs

Find the stud, count the threads, keep metals apart.

  • Self-drilling screws make their own hole; self-tapping screws usually need a pilot.
  • Aim for at least 5 threads engaged; if the metal is too thin, use a nut or rivet nut.
  • A screw that spins freely is stripped: stop and move or resize.
  • Wood screws: penetrate at least 7 shank diameters for sideways loads; pilot about 70 percent of root diameter in softwood.
  • Drywall alone is weak in a straight pull. Use studs where you can.
  • Masonry anchors: edge distance, hole cleaning, depth and torque come from the maker's sheet.
  • Keep dissimilar metals apart with sleeves or washers; aluminum is the usual victim.
  • No equipment, hangers or overhead loads on light anchors.
