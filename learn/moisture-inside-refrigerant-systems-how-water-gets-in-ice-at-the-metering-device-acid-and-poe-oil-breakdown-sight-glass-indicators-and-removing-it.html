Moisture Inside Refrigerant Systems: How Water Gets In, Ice at the Metering Device, Acid and POE Oil Breakdown, Sight Glass Indicators and Removing It

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A sealed refrigerant circuit is supposed to hold refrigerant and oil and nothing else. Water is the contaminant that does the most quiet damage: it can freeze in the metering device, it breaks down ester oil into acid, and it leaves deposits in narrow passages. This lesson follows water from the way it gets in to the way it comes out. The same ideas apply whether the system runs R-454B, R-32 or R-448A.

How water gets in

These are our own field-reasoning categories; none of them needs special equipment to happen, only a lapse in habit.

  • Open lines and parts. A line set, coil or compressor left open takes in room air, and room air carries humidity. Cap and plug everything until the moment you braze or connect it.
  • Brazing without nitrogen flowing. Air inside a hot joint makes scale and adds moisture. Flow dry nitrogen through the joint as covered in Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines.
  • Poor evacuation. Stopping the pump when the gauge first dips, instead of when it holds, leaves water behind. The EPA Section 608 test topics list evacuating at the end of service to eliminate air and moisture as a core technique.
  • Leaks while pulling vacuum. A bad hose, core or fitting on the pump side draws room air (and its humidity) in as fast as the pump removes it. The EPA leak guideline notes that valve stems can draw in during a deep vacuum and cause a loss of vacuum that a pressure test will not show.
  • Wet oil. Ester oil left in an open container absorbs water from the air. The DOE report we read says mineral oil resists hydrolysis because water barely dissolves in it, which is the contrast: ester oils hold much more water. See the compressor oils lesson for which refrigerant uses which oil.

What water does

Ice at the metering device. The EPA refrigeration leak guideline states plainly: "Moisture causes expansion valve ice blockage, wax build up, and acid in the oil." The metering device is where refrigerant pressure and temperature drop the most. On a low-temperature system running R-448A, the refrigerant leaving the metering device is well below 32 °F, so free water that arrives there can freeze and choke the flow. The suction pressure falls, the box warms, and the symptom clears when the device warms up, then returns. Our own note: a medium-temperature air conditioning system on R-454B or R-32 runs warmer at the device, so ice is rarer there, but acid is still a problem.

Acid and oil breakdown. POE oil is an ester. A university chemistry text explains that an ester reacts with water to make a carboxylic acid and an alcohol, that the reaction is reversible, and that acid is one of the catalysts. A U.S. Department of Energy report on acid number says water hydrolyzes POE lubricants and releases the organic acids and alcohols used to make them, typically carboxylic acids with five to nine carbons. It lists moisture, temperature, acid concentration and the metals present as drivers of the rate. So the acid that forms helps form more.

Sludge and plugging. The same report says these organic acids are much less corrosive than the mineral acids from older refrigerant and oil pairs, but over long periods they can form metal carboxylate salts that collect in narrow passages such as capillary tubes and can plug them. That is the practical meaning of "sludge" for a small system. For what a failed compressor does to the oil, see Compressor Burnout Cleanup.

How techs detect moisture

  • Moisture-indicating sight glass. A university technical-assistance guide for commercial refrigeration describes the indicator as a color element you compare with a printed ring around the glass; a "Caution" reading means call for service. What each color means is set by the indicator's maker, so read the chart printed on the glass. More in Filter Driers and Sight Glasses.
  • A vacuum that rises, then levels off. After isolating the pump, a leak climbs steadily toward atmospheric, while water boiling off rises and then flattens. The EPA guideline says a system that cannot hold and drifts back to atmospheric points to a leak to find and fix. The full method is in the vacuum decay lesson.
  • Oil acid test. The DOE report says field acid test kits give quick, semi-quantitative results, and lab titration gives total acid number. It cites 0.05 mg KOH per gram of oil as the critical value used for older mineral oil systems, and says no agreed limit exists yet for POE systems. Do not borrow that number for POE; follow the kit maker's color chart.

How to remove it

Evacuate deep and hold. The EPA guideline gives a staged commercial procedure: pull to 1,000 microns and hold 30 minutes, break the vacuum with dry nitrogen, pull to 500, then pull to a minimum of 300 microns. It also says to install the suction and liquid drier cores after the first vacuum holds, and calls for a pump of 8 cfm or larger with a micron gauge. Basics are in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.

Dry with a drier. The DOE report says driers lower free water and that scavenger chemicals neutralize acids, and both slow hydrolysis. A drier has a limited capacity, so size it from the maker's rating and replace it whenever the circuit is opened; see the filter drier lesson linked above.

Keep oil sealed. Pour POE from a small container that you close right after, and add it through a closed path. Our own advice: if a can has been open for a shift, treat it as wet.

Worked example (our own): why water boils off in a deep vacuum

This is our own example. Assumptions: a clean, dry-gauge reading, pure water, and the conversion 1 psi = 51.715 mmHg, with 1 mmHg = 1,000 microns (so 1 psia = 51,715 microns).

  1. Water at 70 °F. The NIST fluid data give a saturation pressure of 0.36336 psia. Multiply: 0.36336 × 51,715 = about 18,791 microns.
  2. The target. 500 microns = 0.5 mmHg = 500 ÷ 51,715 = about 0.0097 psia.
  3. Compare. 18,791 ÷ 500 = about 37.6. Water at 70 °F would boil at a pressure nearly 38 times higher than the target, so at 500 microns any liquid water is boiling hard and leaves as vapor for the pump to carry out. A reading that stalls well above 500 means water or a leak is still present.
  4. Same water at 40 °F. NIST gives 0.12173 psia, and 0.12173 × 51,715 = about 6,295 microns. That is still 12.6 times the target, so it still boils, but the margin is about a third (6,295 ÷ 18,791 = 0.335) of the 70 °F case.

What this means in the field: boiling takes heat from the water left behind, which lowers its temperature and pressure and slows the process. A cold system gives the water less heat to work with, so pulling down takes longer and the gauge may seem to stall. Our own rule of thumb: let the equipment come up to room temperature before you judge a slow pulldown, and do not call it a leak until a decay test says so. Never use heat that could damage components, and follow the maker's instructions.

Field card: Moisture Inside Refrigerant Systems

Keep it capped, braze with nitrogen, pull deep, change the drier.

  • Entry: open lines, no nitrogen while brazing, quit-early evacuation, pump-side leaks, wet oil.
  • Damage: ice at the metering device, POE oil hydrolysis to acid, deposits in narrow passages.
  • Detect: indicator color against its own chart, vacuum that levels off, oil acid kit.
  • Remove: deep staged evacuation, drier cores after the first vacuum holds, sealed oil.
  • Our example: water at 70 F boils near 18,791 microns; at 40 F near 6,295 microns; target 500.
