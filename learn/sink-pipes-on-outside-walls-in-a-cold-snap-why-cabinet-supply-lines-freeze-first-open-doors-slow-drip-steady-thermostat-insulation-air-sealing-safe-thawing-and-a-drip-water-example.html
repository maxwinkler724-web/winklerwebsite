Sink Pipes on Outside Walls in a Cold Snap: Keeping Them From Freezing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When a hard freeze arrives, the sink supply lines in a cabinet on an outside wall are often among the first pipes in the house to freeze. They are cheap to protect. This lesson explains why they are at risk and which steps from federal and university extension guidance help, and it ends with a rough look at how much water a slow drip uses.

Safety first. Never thaw a pipe with a torch or any open flame. Do not heat the house with a gas oven, a camp stove or an unvented fuel heater. See Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

Why sink lines on outside walls freeze first

University extension guides name pipes in exterior walls, cabinets, attics, crawl spaces and garages as the ones most at risk. Three things stack up in a sink cabinet on an outside wall:

  • A cold wall cavity. The pipe runs through or against the wall, so little more than a thin layer separates it from outdoor air. A university guide notes that air leaks let cold air reach pipes along outer walls.
  • Closed doors. Cabinet doors keep the room's warm air out. Several guides say to open the doors so warm air can reach the pipes, which tells us the closed cabinet is where the pipe gets the least room heat.
  • Wind. Wind pushing on the outside wall can drive cold air through gaps around the pipe holes. We did not find a source that puts a number on this, so treat it as our own explanation.

One extension guide says it takes only a few hours below about 25°F for pipes to freeze, and says smaller pipes are more likely to freeze. A sink supply line is small. Another guide says the risk rises sharply during a long freeze.

What to do on a cold night

1. Open the cabinet doors, and move the cleaners

Leave the kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors on the outside wall open so room air can reach the pipes. One university guide cautions that this will not reach attics and crawl spaces, so it is a first step, not the whole plan. Two university guides say to remove harmful cleaners and chemicals from those cabinets first if young children are in the home. We add pets to that list as our own advice. Put cleaners on a high shelf or in a locked cabinet elsewhere.

2. Let the faucet drip

Let cold water drip from a faucet on the at-risk line. The guides say moving water is less likely to freeze. One extension page says a trickle is enough, uses the cold side only, and suggests the faucet farthest from the water meter so water moves through more of the house. Keep dripping until temperatures stay above freezing. A drip is not a guarantee, so keep the other steps too.

3. Keep the thermostat steady, day and night

A university extension guide says to keep the thermostat at the same setting day and night. A federal emergency-management page and two university guides give 55°F as the lowest heat setting to use, for example when you are away. For a cold snap at home, we would skip the night setback altogether (our own advice), because the cabinet pipes need the heat. If the heat fails, see Heat Went Out in Winter.

Longer-term fixes: insulation and air sealing

A university extension guide recommends foam insulation that wraps around the pipe and says to check where pipes enter the home and seal gaps to keep cold air out. Another university guide also says to seal air leaks around pipes. In a sink cabinet that means two jobs: wrap the exposed supply lines, and seal the pipe holes in the cabinet back and floor. Insulation slows heat loss but does not make heat (our own note), so a pipe in a truly cold cavity can still freeze. Sealing keeps wind from blowing straight onto the pipe. If a pipe has frozen before, one guide suggests having a licensed plumber relocate it.

Heat cable: only as instructed

A federal consumer-product-safety agency says heat tapes and cables are involved in about 3,300 residential fires each year (a figure from an older release). Its guidance is to buy the product the maker recommends for your pipe size, follow the maker's directions, never lap the tape over itself unless the instructions allow it, and not use more insulation than recommended. Each fall, unplug it and check the whole length, and replace it if the covering is damaged or you find bare wires or charring. One university guide says to minimize the use of heat tapes and never to overwrap or cross the wires. A university healthy-homes guide says to use them only in exposed or problem areas, choose a product that carries a testing-lab approval, and follow the instructions.

From that we draw these rules. The plastic-pipe rule is our own advice, because the pages we read do not cover it: use only a purchased, listed cable, never homemade heat tape, and do not put heat cable on plastic pipe unless its instructions say it is rated for plastic.

If a pipe may be frozen

Signs. A university extension guide says to suspect a frozen pipe when you turn on a faucet and get nothing or only a trickle. Frost on an exposed pipe is another sign (our own observation). If one pipe has frozen, the guide says others may have too, so check every faucet.

Thaw safely. Keep the faucet open so melting ice and water can run out. The guide lists an electric hair dryer, an electric heating pad or towels soaked in hot water, and says to keep applying heat until full pressure returns. Another university guide says to start at the open faucet and work toward the frozen part, so trapped steam does not burst the pipe. Never use a blowtorch, a propane or kerosene heater, a charcoal stove or any other open flame. Watch for shock risk near standing water, and keep electric devices out of puddles.

Shut the main and call. If you cannot find the frozen section, cannot reach it or cannot thaw it, call a licensed plumber. If a pipe has split, shut off the water at the main valve; a university guide says so, and a federal emergency-management page says to learn where the main shutoff is before an emergency. A university extension guide also says to call your insurer if there is damage, and another warns that thawing a pipe yourself can make damage worse.

Worked example (our own): what does an overnight drip use?

These are our own rough numbers with assumed inputs. They are not measurements. A real faucet's flow depends on the fixture and the water pressure.

Assumptions. The drip runs for 10 hours. A drop is about 0.05 mL. A pencil-thin stream is about 1 cup per minute. One extension page describes a trickle that fills a gallon pitcher in about an hour. 1 gallon = 16 cups = 3,785 mL.

A drip of one drop per second.

  • 10 hours = 10 × 3,600 s = 36,000 s, so 36,000 drops.
  • 36,000 × 0.05 mL = 1,800 mL.
  • 1,800 ÷ 3,785 = about 0.48 gallon, or about half a gallon overnight.

A pencil-thin stream of 1 cup per minute.

  • 10 hours = 600 minutes, so 600 × 1 cup = 600 cups.
  • 600 ÷ 16 = 37.5 gallons.

A trickle of one gallon per hour (the pitcher description). 10 × 1 gallon = 10 gallons.

Reading the result. The same 10 hours uses anywhere from about half a gallon to nearly 40 gallons. A drop-per-second drip may be too slow to keep water moving through the whole line, and a pencil-thin stream uses a lot of water. The extension page's trickle falls between them. This is a rough estimate that ignores pressure changes, and we are not giving a cost.

Field card: Sink Pipes on Outside Walls

Warm air, moving water, steady heat.

  • Why they freeze first: cold wall cavity, closed doors, wind through gaps.
  • Open cabinet doors on cold nights; move cleaners out of reach of children and pets.
  • Drip cold water from the at-risk line; do not turn the thermostat down at night.
  • Foam insulation on exposed lines; seal pipe holes at the cabinet back.
  • Heat cable: purchased and listed, used per its instructions, never on plastic pipe unless rated.
  • Frozen? Open the faucet, use a hair dryer or warm towels, never a flame.
  • Split pipe or cannot thaw: shut the main, call a licensed plumber.
  • Our own: a 10-hour drip of one drop per second is about half a gallon; a stream of 1 cup per minute is about 37.5 gallons.
