Commercial Boiler Burner Controls: Linkage, Parallel Positioning, O2 Trim and Excess Air

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A burner has to deliver the right amount of air for the fuel it is burning at every firing rate, from low fire to high fire. How it does that, and how well it keeps doing it, decides how much fuel a boiler wastes. This lesson compares the three common schemes: single-point (jackshaft) linkage, parallel positioning, and O2 trim.

Why the air amount matters

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) steam tip sheet on combustion efficiency says too little excess air leaves unburned combustibles, while too much air wastes heat because of the increased flue gas flow. Its stated aim is the amount of excess air that minimizes heat lost up the stack, and it says stack temperature and flue gas oxygen (or carbon dioxide) are the primary indicators. For a well-designed natural gas system the sheet says 10 percent excess air is attainable. A second DOE tip sheet, on burners, says an efficient natural gas burner needs only 2 to 3 percent excess oxygen, or 10 to 15 percent excess air, and suggests looking at controls or the burner if oxygen runs above 3 percent. Where the analyzer numbers come from is covered in Combustion Analyzer Readings; here we focus on how the burner holds the ratio.

Single-point (jackshaft) linkage

In a single-point system one actuator drives both the fuel valve and the air damper through a mechanical linkage. A nationwide boiler dealer's blog summary says the fuel/air ratio is set with a characterizing cam on the fuel valve, and that the parts must sit fairly close together. The same article says linkage slop forces extra excess air, so the burner can never be tuned to its maximum efficiency, and that seasonal retuning and wear are drawbacks. It also notes one failsafe trait: if the actuator fails, the burner holds its air-fuel ratio but stays at that firing rate.

The DOE burner tip sheet is blunter. It warns against buying standard burners that use linkages for single-point control, because joints wear and set screws loosen, which causes slippage, poor air-to-fuel ratios and lower efficiency. A 2008 ACEEE paper by Carpenter, Schmidt and Kissock looked at linked boilers and found that excess air usually rose as firing rate fell. In one plant it was 43 percent at high fire, 35 percent at medium and 113 percent at low fire. The paper blames non-linearity in the linkage (air flow falls more slowly than fuel as the valve closes) and says linkages seldom hold a constant ratio unless they are monitored and maintained. A well-calibrated single-point system, it says, can hold 10 to 30 percent excess air, but most do not.

Parallel positioning

Parallel positioning gives the fuel valve and the air damper (or fan drive) their own servo actuators, coordinated by an electronic controller. Cleaver-Brooks describes it as dedicated motorized actuators for the fuel and air valves tied into the electronic firing rate control, and credits it with saving up to 3 percent in fuel cost by eliminating hysteresis. Power Flame says individually driven servos control the ratio more closely than a conventional linkage type system, which lowers the excess oxygen required for complete combustion. Siemens describes its LMV line as needing no mechanical linkage, so actuators can be mounted where they fit. The DOE burner tip sheet says servo-driven controls make tune-ups easier, remove hysteresis and give repeatable performance across firing rates. In the ACEEE case study, linkageless controls with actuators preset at roughly 10 percent firing-rate increments held excess air to 16 percent at high fire and 38 percent at low fire.

The Nationwide article adds a safety caution: older parallel systems without position feedback or cross-limiting are called inherently unsafe. Cross-limiting means the controller watches actuator positions so air leads fuel on a load increase and fuel leads air on a decrease. The burner management system, with its flame safeties and interlocks, is a separate layer; see Boiler Flame Safeguards, Purge and Lockout.

O2 trim

O2 trim adds a flue oxygen probe. The DOE combustion tip sheet describes the trim system as providing feedback to the burner controls to automatically minimize excess combustion air, and suggests it where fuel composition or steam flow varies widely. The ACEEE paper says the probe feeds a controller that moves the air damper toward an oxygen setpoint, and puts equipment cost at $20,000 to $30,000 per system, with calibration three to four times a year. Cleaver-Brooks says O2 trim increases efficiency by up to 2 percent. Trim is a small correction, not a substitute for a good base setup. The Nationwide article says trim moves the damper by up to plus or minus 10 percent and corrects for air density and fuel heating value changes, and Autoflame's guide likewise limits total correction to plus or minus 10 percent by default. Nationwide also recommends checking analyzer calibration at least monthly.

Worked example: what a lower O2 is worth

The DOE combustion tip sheet gives a table of natural gas combustion efficiency by oxygen level and net flue temperature, and the formula Annual Savings = Fuel Consumption x (1 - E1/E2) x Fuel Cost. Its own example, 500,000 MMBtu per year at $8.00, moves from 78.2 to 83.1 percent efficiency and saves 29,482 MMBtu per year ($235,856). We rework the method for a smaller boiler. This is Our own arithmetic using the sheet's table values.

  1. Our own scenario: 20,000 MMBtu per year at $10 per MMBtu, a net flue temperature of 400°F, and a tune-up that lowers flue O2 from 7.0 to 3.0 percent.
  2. From the DOE table at 400°F: E1 = 78.2 percent at 7.0 percent O2 (44.9 percent excess air), and E2 = 80.4 percent at 3.0 percent O2 (15.0 percent excess air).
  3. Ratio: E1 / E2 = 78.2 / 80.4 = 0.9726.
  4. Fraction saved: 1 - 0.9726 = 0.0274, about 2.7 percent.
  5. Fuel saved: 20,000 x 0.0274 = about 547 MMBtu per year.
  6. Dollars: 547 x $10 = about $5,470 per year.

The table assumes complete combustion, so it cannot show what happens if the air is cut so far that CO appears. The savings are real only if CO stays acceptable at every firing rate.

Hysteresis, drift and tuning across the range

Hysteresis is slop: the damper ends up in a slightly different place depending on which way it last moved. Cleaver-Brooks and DOE both say servo control eliminates it, while DOE says worn linkage joints and loose set screws cause slippage and poor ratios. Drift is the ratio moving away from the commissioned setting over time; the ACEEE paper says linkages need monitoring and maintenance to hold the ratio, and Nationwide says air density and fuel heating value changes are what trim corrects. That is why combustion tuning is repeated, not done once. Tuning means setting the fuel-air relationship at several points between low and high fire while a combustion analyzer reads O2, CO and stack temperature. Autoflame's guide describes entering the maximum fuel position first, then descending positions, storing O2, CO and CO2 for each fuel and air pair. The DOE tip sheet says to monitor flue gas composition periodically and tune the boilers. Tuning and setting limits belong to qualified, authorized people with the maker's procedure in hand. Field card: Commercial Boiler Burner Controls: Linkage, Parallel Positioning, O2 Trim and Excess Air

Ratio, trend, then compare.

  • Too little air makes CO and unburned fuel; too much air carries heat up the stack.
  • Jackshaft: one actuator and linkage. Parallel positioning: separate servos and a stored curve. O2 trim: a flue probe corrects the air.
  • Watch stack temperature, flue O2 trend, CO and flame appearance on the log, and compare readings at the same firing rate.
  • A rising stack temperature or O2 at the same load is a flag to report, not to adjust on your own.
  • Target O2 or excess air comes from the burner maker; DOE sheets give 2 to 3 percent O2 as typical for gas.
  • Never bypass a limit or interlock. Combustion adjustments are for qualified, authorized people.

What a facility tech watches

The DOE sheet names stack temperature and flue oxygen as the primary indicators, so a log of both at the same firing rate shows change early. Add CO from the analyzer and a look at the flame. Autoflame's guide notes a higher CO reading can come from either lean or rich combustion, so CO alone does not say which way the air is off. The same DOE sheet puts roughly a 1 percent efficiency gain on each 40°F reduction in stack gas temperature, so a stack temperature creeping up at the same load is worth reporting. Report changes to the person responsible for combustion adjustment.
