Equipment Grounding and GFCI for HVAC: Ground vs Neutral and the Outdoor Unit Rule

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A third wire in a condenser's whip does one job: it gives a fault somewhere to go that is fast, easy and trips the breaker. This lesson explains that wire, why it is not the neutral, how a tech checks it safely, and why the outdoor-unit GFCI rule has been a moving target for inverter heat pumps.

Check the current code first

Check the current code. Electrical code editions, exceptions and dates change, and the local inspector decides what applies. This page is general education built from public pages we read in 2026. It is not an installation guide, and it does not replace the code, the manufacturer's instructions or a licensed electrician.

Two different wires, two different jobs

A 120 V circuit has a hot conductor, a neutral and an equipment grounding conductor (EGC). The hot and the neutral carry the load current in normal operation. The EGC carries nothing in normal operation. It ties the metal cabinet to the panel so a hot wire touching metal has a low-resistance path back to the source.

That matters because a breaker only opens when current is large. A loose hot wire touching a cabinet with no ground path leaves the cabinet energized at line voltage, waiting for a person to complete the circuit. With a good EGC, the fault current jumps to hundreds of amps and the breaker opens in a moment. OSHA's electrical rule says the path to ground from circuits, equipment and enclosures must be permanent, continuous and effective, and that fixed equipment's grounding conductor runs in the same cable or raceway as the circuit conductors.

Why neutral and ground are tied together only at the service

OSHA's rule says the grounded conductor (the neutral on a single-phase three-wire system) and the equipment grounding conductor are both connected to the grounding electrode, and that this connection is made on the supply side of the service disconnecting means. In plain terms, the neutral-to-ground bond is made once, at the service. Downstream, in a sub-panel, a disconnect or the condenser itself, the neutral and the ground are kept separate.

The reason is current. If a second bond existed downstream, part of the neutral's normal load current would take the ground wire as a parallel path, and the cabinet, conduit and any metal piping tied to them would carry current all day. Keeping the bond at one place keeps the EGC quiet so it is ready for a fault. Gas piping bonded to the same system is covered in CSST Yellow Gas Piping: Bonding and Lightning.

Worked example: why the ground wire is a safety device

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Take a 120 V circuit and a 20 A breaker. We assume the whole fault loop (hot wire, cabinet connection, grounding conductor and bond back to the source) totals 0.4 ohm. These are assumptions for teaching, not measurements; real loops vary.

  1. Good ground: I = V / R = 120 / 0.4 = 300 A. That is 300 / 20 = 15 times the breaker rating, so the breaker should open quickly.
  2. Broken ground, person touching the cabinet: assume a body path of 1,000 ohm. I = 120 / 1,000 = 0.12 A = 120 mA. That is far below the 20 A rating, so the breaker does nothing, and the current stays flowing through the person.
  3. Compare to a GFCI: OSHA says a GFCI trips at approximately 5 mA of imbalance. 120 mA / 5 mA = 24 times that level, and a GFCI would cut power in as little as 1/40 second per OSHA's page.

The lesson in the math: a good EGC turns a dangerous hidden fault into a big, obvious one that opens the breaker. A GFCI is a second layer for when the ground is missing or the path is a person.

Checking ground continuity

Ground checks are done with the circuit de-energized. Under OSHA's lockout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, the sequence is shut down, isolate the energy source, apply a lockout device, relieve stored energy and verify the isolation before work starts. Then a meter on resistance or continuity can compare the unit's cabinet to the ground terminal and to the panel's ground bar. A very low reading is what we want; an open or high reading means a loose or missing connection. Do not use the continuity test as a substitute for verifying that the circuit is dead. The meter ratings and PPE side is in Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs.

A compressor whose windings leak to its shell is a different symptom, checked at the compressor terminals; see Checking Compressor Windings and Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing. Disconnect basics are in AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers.

The outdoor GFCI rule and inverter heat pumps

A trade association post from ACCA says the 2020 NEC applied 210.8(F) to outdoor HVACR equipment, and that field reports of nuisance trips came from single-stage equipment, variable-speed equipment and ECM fan motors. It also says listed equipment can leak more current than a GFCI's roughly 5 mA trip level while touch current stays well below injury levels. An earlier interim amendment delayed the date for inverter-driven units, such as mini-splits, to January 1, 2023.

The 2023 NEC then added an exception. Minnesota's Department of Labor and Industry FAQ says a new exception was added so that listed HVAC equipment is not required to have GFCI protection until September 1, 2026, and that garages, accessory buildings and boathouses were added to dwelling outdoor outlets. A distributor association page dated September 30, 2026 says the exception was carried into the 2023 and 2026 editions, that several states amended or deleted the rule, and that in states that did not, GFCI could apply after the September 1, 2026 date.

A proposed NFPA interim amendment (TIA 1924) from an industry association asks to move that date to September 1, 2028. It cites preliminary test work that found startup leakage from inverter equipment can interact with GFCI sensing in ways existing standards do not fully address. That is a proposal, and we could not confirm whether it was approved. Depending on the code edition, state amendments and the inspector, the equipment may be exempt or covered.

Where New Jersey stands

The New Jersey Department of Community Affairs lists the 2023 edition of the National Electrical Code as the electrical subcode of the Uniform Construction Code, with an adoption date shown as August 17, 2026. A DCA newsletter from winter 2023 still refers to the 2020 edition, so ask your local electrical inspector which one applies to your job.

Who does the wiring

New circuits, new disconnects and GFCI devices are electrical work. Under the New Jersey HVACR board's rule, a master HVACR contractor may not do electrical work above 30 volts apart from limited equipment-replacement hookups, as covered in NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers. If a ground is missing, a GFCI trips on a new install or a circuit needs to change, the call goes to a licensed electrician.

Field card: Equipment Grounding and GFCI

Ground is for faults. Neutral is for load.

  • The EGC carries current only during a fault, and its low resistance trips the breaker.
  • Neutral and ground are bonded at the service only, not downstream.
  • Our example: 120 / 0.4 = 300 A (good ground); 120 / 1,000 = 0.12 A (broken ground, person).
  • A GFCI trips at about 5 mA of imbalance (OSHA).
  • Lock out and verify dead before any continuity check.
  • The outdoor HVAC GFCI exception and dates vary; ask the inspector.
  • Wiring changes belong to a licensed electrician.
