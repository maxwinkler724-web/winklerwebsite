Heat Loss From Open Hot Water Tanks: Evaporation, Covers and Room Humidity

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rinse, wash, plating or process tank full of hot water looks like a quiet piece of plant equipment. To the heating system it is a leak, and to the room air handlers it is a steady source of moisture. This lesson shows where the heat goes, what changes it, how much humidity lands on the room, and the fixes that work.

Where the heat goes

A heated, uncovered tank loses heat three ways at the liquid surface, plus conduction through its walls and bottom.

  • Evaporation. Water leaving the surface as vapor takes its latent heat with it, roughly 1,000 Btu for every pound evaporated (a round figure we use in this lesson). A federal steam-system tip sheet on covering heated open vessels says open vessels holding heated liquids often have high heat loss from surface evaporation, and it tabulates the loss for water in Btu per hour per square foot of surface.
  • Radiation. The same tip sheet lists radiation as a secondary loss that rises from about 90 Btu/h per square foot with 110°F liquid to about 290 Btu/h per square foot at 190°F.
  • Convection. Air that touches the surface warms up and rises, carrying sensible heat away. The tip sheet does not give a separate convection number, so we treat it as a smaller loss that grows with air movement.
  • Walls and bottom. Conduction through uninsulated steel or plastic sides loses heat to the room. This is the same idea as the bare piping in bare pipe heat loss in mechanical rooms, only with a flat surface.

Why evaporation is the big one

The tip sheet table for water shows how fast evaporation climbs with temperature. At a 110°F liquid temperature the evaporative loss is roughly 150 to 245 Btu/h per square foot depending on room air temperature. At 170°F it is roughly 1,440 to 1,610 Btu/h per square foot, with 1,566 listed for 75°F room air. That is six to ten times more for a 60°F rise in water temperature, and at 170°F evaporation is several times the 90 to 290 Btu/h radiation figure. The table assumes dry, still air, so real tanks can differ.

The reason is vapor pressure. Evaporation is driven by the gap between the water vapor pressure right at the liquid surface (which climbs steeply with water temperature) and the vapor pressure in the room air. Hotter water widens the gap fast.

Air speed and room humidity

The same tip sheet says the loss is a strong function of both wind speed and ambient humidity. It warns that a fan pulling air over an uncovered tank could more than double the heat loss, and that a 3 mph wind more than doubles the loss rate. In plain terms:

  • Moving air sweeps the damp layer off the surface so more water can leave. Spot coolers, open dock doors, supply diffusers aimed at the tank and exhaust hoods pulling across it all speed evaporation.
  • Humid room air narrows the vapor pressure gap and slows evaporation. This is our own physical reasoning, consistent with the tip sheet statement, not a number from it. Dry winter plant air is therefore the worst case for tank loss.

The load this puts on room HVAC

Every pound of water that evaporates ends up as vapor in the room air unless it is exhausted. For a space served by cooling and dehumidifying equipment, that is a latent load. The same tip sheet notes that when a tank sits in a conditioned space, removing the heat load from the tank can also save electricity. Put another way, heat taken from the tank goes into the room air as latent energy, and the cooling coil has to remove it again when it condenses the moisture. In a plant with mechanical cooling, the tank heat is paid for twice. In winter the vapor can condense on cold windows, roof decks and ductwork, so humidity control and ventilation matter. See aquarium and fish room HVAC for the same effect at smaller scale.

Fixes, in rough order of payoff

  1. Cover the tank. The tip sheet says both energy and liquid losses are reduced by covering open vessels with insulated lids. Its example assumes a cover cuts the surface loss to a negligible value. Lids must still allow the process to run, so many are hinged, sliding or removed only when parts are loaded.
  2. Cut the exposed surface. The tip sheet lists reducing the exposed liquid area as a loss-reduction step. Floating covers such as hollow balls or sheets are one common way to do that where a hard lid will not work, and where the process allows it. How much they save depends on how completely they cover the surface, and we have no government figure to quote.
  3. Lower the setpoint. The tip sheet lists lowering the liquid temperature. The table above shows why: the loss drops sharply as water temperature falls. Check the process need first; do not lower a temperature that a rinse, wash or bath needs.
  4. Insulate sides and bottom. This cuts conduction loss to the room and also keeps outside surfaces cooler to the touch.
  5. Calm the air. Move fans, diffusers and drafts away from the tank, in line with the tip sheet advice to minimize airflow over it.
  6. Local exhaust. Where fumes must be captured, as at many plating and process tanks, a hood at the tank edge pulls vapor out before it spreads. Our own caution: the exhaust draws air across the surface, which can raise evaporation, and it removes conditioned air that makeup air must replace. Design capture to the fumes, not to the moisture, and keep the opening just large enough.

The tip sheet also suggests surveying every open heated vessel and recording its schedule, liquid and room temperatures and exposed area, then estimating the loss for each. That is a good first job for a plant tech. For another case where adding material is not always the answer, see critical radius of insulation.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

These inputs are assumptions made up for this lesson. They are not measurements from a real plant.

  • Tank surface: 3 ft by 8 ft = 24 ft².
  • Assumed evaporation rate: 1.2 lb of water per hour per square foot of surface.
  • Latent heat used: about 1,000 Btu per lb evaporated (our round figure).
  • Room supply air: 5,000 cfm (assumed), standard air.

Step 1, water evaporated. 24 ft² × 1.2 lb/h per ft² = 28.8 lb/h.

Step 2, heat carried off by evaporation. 28.8 lb/h × 1,000 Btu/lb = 28,800 Btu/h. Per square foot, that is 28,800 / 24 = 1,200 Btu/h per ft². This sits between the tip sheet table values at 150°F (about 750 to 890) and 170°F (about 1,440 to 1,610), so the assumption fits a tank somewhere around 160°F in dry, still air.

Step 3, moisture added to the room. The same 28.8 lb/h is the moisture load. At 8.34 lb per gallon that is 28.8 / 8.34 = 3.45 gal/h, or about 27.6 gal over an 8 hour shift (3.45 × 8).

Step 4, what it does to room air. Standard air weighs about 0.075 lb/ft³, so 5,000 cfm × 60 min × 0.075 lb/ft³ = 22,500 lb of air per hour. Humidity ratio rise = 28.8 / 22,500 = 0.00128 lb of water per lb of dry air, or about 9 grains per lb (0.00128 × 7,000 = 8.96).

Step 5, cooling equipment view. If all of that moisture must be condensed out, the latent load is the same 28,800 Btu/h, or 28,800 / 12,000 = 2.4 tons of cooling capacity spent only on dehumidifying this tank, before any sensible heat.

Check. Units cancel in each step: lb/h per ft² × ft² gives lb/h; lb/h × Btu/lb gives Btu/h; lb/h divided by lb of air per hour gives lb of water per lb of air. A good lid that cuts evaporation by, say, 90 percent (our assumption) would leave about 2,880 Btu/h and 2.9 lb/h. The real percentage depends on how well the lid seals.

Field card: Heat Loss From Open Hot Water Tanks

Cover it, cool it, calm the air.

  • Evaporation is the largest loss from an open hot tank; radiation and convection are smaller.
  • About 1,000 Btu leaves with each pound of water evaporated (our round figure).
  • A fan or a 3 mph breeze over the surface can more than double the loss (federal tip sheet).
  • Evaporated water becomes a latent load: Btu/h = lb/h of water × 1,000.
  • Fixes: insulated lids, less exposed surface, lower setpoint, insulated walls, less airflow, right-sized local exhaust.
  • Always confirm the process temperature need before changing a setpoint.
