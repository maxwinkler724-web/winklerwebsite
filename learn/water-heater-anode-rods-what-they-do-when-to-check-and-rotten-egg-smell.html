Water Heater Anode Rods: What They Do, When to Check, and Rotten Egg Smell

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Inside most tank water heaters there is a metal rod that is meant to be eaten away so the tank is not. It is cheap, it is out of sight, and it is the part most often blamed when hot water smells like rotten eggs. This lesson explains what the rod does, how often the makers say to look at it, what the smell really is, and why pulling the rod out is a bad trade.

First, which smell is it?

A rotten egg smell can mean two very different things. Natural gas is given an odor on purpose, so a gas leak can smell like sulfur. If the smell is in the air near the heater, the furnace or the gas piping, treat it as a gas leak: leave, and call the gas utility from outside. Our lessons on what to do when you smell gas and what each furnace odor means cover that. The rest of this page is about a different smell: one that comes out of the hot tap. The Minnesota Department of Health suggests a simple sorting test. If the cold water has no smell and the hot water does, the problem is likely in the water heater.

What the anode rod does

A steel tank would rust through quickly, so a gas or electric tank heater is lined inside with a thin glass coating. The coating is not perfect. Rheem's manual describes the anode rod as designed to prolong the life of the glass-lined tank. A. O. Smith's commercial manual puts the idea plainly: the rod is submerged and corrodes in place of the tank, so the water's ions attack the rod instead of the steel. Bradford White calls it a sacrificial rod that gives up electrons to protect the carbon steel tank.

The reason this works is that metals differ in how readily they give up electrons. Put a more reactive metal (the rod) in the same water as steel (the tank) and the rod takes the damage. When the rod is used up, the water starts working on any bare steel spots. State's service handbook also notes that the rod protects places where glass cannot be applied, such as threaded tank openings.

Magnesium, aluminum, zinc and powered rods

  • Magnesium. Bradford White calls magnesium a leading material because it protects the steel tank best, since it gives up electrons more freely than aluminum. It is the standard rod in many heaters.
  • Aluminum. Bradford White recommends aluminum where water is more conductive, such as softened water or lower pH, and uses it as the less reactive replacement. An A. O. Smith bulletin on aluminum hydroxide adds one caution: in a few areas with water above about pH 8, an aluminum rod can make a gel that shows up in the drain and at faucet screens, and that maker's fix is a magnesium rod.
  • Zinc. A. O. Smith's residential guide says a special zinc rod may reduce or remove the odor in some cases.
  • Powered. A powered rod does not wear away. The maker we read, Corro-Protec, describes a titanium rod, a plug-in power supply and a green light that shows protection is active, and says to confirm the light once a year. That is a third-party product, not a part from the heater maker, so check your heater's warranty terms before adding one.

When to look at it

Water, not the calendar, decides how fast a rod wears. Bradford White lists dissolved solids, sulfur, water softener use, and municipal versus well water as factors. A. O. Smith's softener bulletin says high conductivity speeds up rod decay and that a softener can shorten heater life through faster rod loss. So the makers differ on timing; follow your own label and manual.

  • A. O. Smith, commercial gas manual: inspect after no more than three years, then every year until the rod needs replacing. Softened water means inspect every year. A pitted or corroded rod means check more often.
  • A. O. Smith, residential gas guide: remove and inspect after the first six months, then at least yearly.
  • State, service handbook: inspect yearly and replace when large gouges appear.
  • Bradford White, PDV service manual: no fixed interval; check it during service.
  • Rheem, instruction manual: allows removal for inspection or replacement and gives no schedule on the page we read.

How to judge a worn rod

Bradford White's manual gives a clear test for the outlet-nipple anode in its heater: some wear is normal, replacement is recommended when the rod has lost half its original diameter (about 3/4 inch in that manual), or when any steel core shows. State says to replace when large gouges appear. The work involves draining a hot, pressurized tank, so A. O. Smith says a qualified technician should do it.

Worked example: inspection dates and remaining rod

Part 1: when to look. Say a heater is installed in October 2026 on softened water.

  1. Under the A. O. Smith commercial rule for softened water, inspect every year: Oct 2027, 2028, 2029, 2030, 2031, 2032 = 6 inspections in six years.
  2. Under the same manual without a softener: first look by year 3 (Oct 2029), then yearly: 2029, 2030, 2031, 2032 = 4 inspections.
  3. Under the A. O. Smith residential guide: Apr 2027 (six months), then yearly: Apr 2027, 2028, 2029, 2030, 2031, 2032 = 6 inspections.

So the softener moves the first look from year 3 to year 1, and two extra inspections get done in the same six years (6 compared with 4).

Part 2: how much rod is left. The Bradford White manual says to replace at half the original diameter, about 3/4 inch, which implies a new rod of about 1.5 inches (our inference, not a figure the manual states).

  1. Remaining diameter at replacement: 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 inch.
  2. Cross-section area scales with diameter squared, so the fraction left is (0.75 ÷ 1.5) squared = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.
  3. A rod that has lost half its diameter has lost about three quarters of its metal.

So "half the diameter" means about 25 percent of the rod is left.

The rotten egg smell from hot water

Bradford White says three things are needed: sulfur, hydrogen and bacteria. Remove one and the smell should stop. Sulfur is in most water. Hydrogen comes from corrosion, and Bradford White says the magnesium rod protects the tank but makes enough hydrogen to cause odor. Rheem's manual says sulfate in the water plus the protection process can make a rotten egg odor in heated water. A. O. Smith's bulletin names sulfate-reducing bacteria, which turn sulfate into hydrogen sulfide gas, and says they like warm-but-not-hot tanks (below 138°F), little oxygen, softeners, wells and long periods without water use. The University of Nebraska extension adds that heat lets the gas escape into the air, so a shower smells stronger. A. O. Smith says the smell is detectable far below harmful levels.

What the makers approve

  • Different rod. Bradford White says replacing the magnesium rod with its A420 rod may reduce the problem. The Nebraska extension, Minnesota and Eau Claire pages also suggest an aluminum rod, and A. O. Smith's guide mentions zinc.
  • Shock chlorination. Bradford White publishes a 10-step bleach procedure (drain, take out the rod, add bleach, put the replacement rod in, let it sit at least 3 hours, flush until the chlorine smell is gone). A. O. Smith says it is the simplest option but treats only the surface, so a heavy infection may return. Use the maker's bulletin, not a guess, and have a plumber do wells.
  • Treat the supply. Rheem says chlorinating the water supply should minimize the problem; Bradford White says properly chlorinated public water generally kills the bacteria.
  • Temperature. A. O. Smith's guide suggests 140°F for strong odors with mixing valves for scald protection. Some state health pages mention far higher temperatures; we do not teach that. See temperature settings and mixing valves.

Why you should not just remove the rod

Removal is a commonly suggested fix, and the makers say no. Rheem's manual says taking the rod out of an operating heater will greatly shorten tank life and exclude warranty coverage. Bradford White says complete removal of the anode, magnesium or aluminum, will void the warranty and shorten tank life. A. O. Smith's residential guide says a rod must be installed at all times or the warranty is void. Also, A. O. Smith's commercial manual says rod replacement itself is not covered by warranty. The state health pages we read say removal may shorten heater life significantly. If the smell is bothering you, swap the rod, treat the heater, or fix the water supply.

Hydrogen gas and long absences

A. O. Smith and Bradford White both warn that hydrogen can collect in a hot water system that has not been used for two weeks or more. Their advice is to run a hot faucet for several minutes before using electrical equipment on the system, with no smoking or flames nearby.

Field card: Water Heater Anode Rods

Smell in the air is gas. Smell only from the hot tap is the heater.

  • The rod corrodes so the glass-lined tank does not.
  • Magnesium protects best; aluminum or zinc is used for odor or conductive water; powered rods do not wear.
  • Intervals differ by maker: three years then yearly, six months then yearly, or yearly. Softened water means yearly.
  • Replace at half original diameter or exposed steel core (Bradford White). Half diameter is about 25 percent of the metal left.
  • Sulfur smell needs sulfate, hydrogen and bacteria. Fix with a different rod, shock chlorination or treated supply.
  • Never remove the rod outright: it shortens tank life and can void the warranty.
