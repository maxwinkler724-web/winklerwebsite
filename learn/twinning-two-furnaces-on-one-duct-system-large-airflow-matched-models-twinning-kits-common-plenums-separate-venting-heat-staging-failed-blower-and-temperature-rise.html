Twinning Two Furnaces on One Duct System

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some houses and small buildings need more air moved than one furnace blower can deliver. One answer is to put two furnaces side by side and let them share a single duct system. That is called twinning. This lesson covers how the pieces fit and what a tech checks after setup. It is education, not an installation guide: the maker's instructions for your exact furnaces and your local code rule.

Why twinning is used

A furnace blower is sized for a range of airflow, and even the largest residential furnaces top out at a limit. When a building needs more total airflow than one cabinet can supply, one option is two furnaces moving air into the same ducts. The duct system is built once, with the combined airflow in mind. For the basics of how airflow and static pressure relate, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. For choosing one system or two for a house, see One System or Two for a New Two-Story Home, which we do not repeat here. Twinning is a different idea: two furnaces, but one duct system and one thermostat.

Matched models and the twinning kit

Twinned furnaces are not two random furnaces. One maker's twinning kit instructions say both furnaces must have the same product number, that only two furnaces can be twinned on a common supply and return duct system with a factory authorized kit, and that condensing and non-condensing furnaces cannot be twinned together. The same instructions say a heat pump cannot be twinned with a gas furnace, and that the kit works with certain blower motor types and a mechanical thermostat. Those limits belong to that maker's kit. Another maker's rules may differ, so read the instructions that came with your kit and your furnace listings.

Twin terminals: making both blowers run together

The kit connects a twin terminal on each furnace control board. In the instructions we read, one furnace is the main unit and the other is the secondary. All thermostat wires go to the main board only. The twin wire joins the two boards so both blowers run at the same speed and both furnaces follow the same mode: heat, cool or continuous fan. The instructions also say each furnace needs its own 115 volt supply on the same phase leg, and that LED patterns on the boards show whether phasing is right. Steady lights mean it is correct and rapid flashing points to a polarity, grounding or transformer problem. Set the blower speed or airflow on both furnaces the same, as the instructions direct. Two blowers set differently fight each other in the shared plenum.

Common plenums, separate everything else

The two furnaces share a supply plenum and a return plenum. Everything that touches fuel and flue gas stays separate. The kit instructions we read say each furnace must always be individually vented and that gas connections are made at each furnace. Combustion air also stays per furnace, and two furnaces together burn twice the gas of one, so the room's combustion air has to be checked for the total input. See Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters and Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV. The International Fuel Gas Code and the International Mechanical Code are the model codes that address appliance installation, combustion air and venting. Local adoption and amendments vary, so confirm what applies where you work. A federal energy source also makes the point that sealed-combustion equipment brings outside air to the burner and sends flue gases outside, which keeps combustion products out of the house.

Sequencing the heat stages

The instructions we read describe how stages work. With single-stage furnaces on a two-stage thermostat, the first furnace provides first-stage heat and both provide second stage. With two-stage furnaces, both run low heat on a first-stage call and both run high heat on a second-stage call. With a single-stage thermostat, they run together as one unit. This staging is set by the kit and the board, so confirm it for the model you have.

When one blower fails

The kit instructions say that exceptions can occur if a safety switch on either furnace opens. The other furnace keeps running unless the open switch is the flame rollout, the main limit or the kit's auxiliary limit, in which case both furnaces respond. That design matters. A dead blower leaves its heat exchanger with no air across it, so its limit should open. Our own reasoning, not a source claim: a service call for weak heat on a twinned system should start by checking that both blowers are actually turning and both airflow settings match, because part of the airflow can be missing while a furnace still runs. Never bypass a limit or rollout switch to get heat back.

Checking airflow and temperature rise after setup

The instructions we read leave airflow and rise checks to each furnace's own manual, so use the nameplate range on each furnace. Rise is the supply air temperature minus the return air temperature. Take it in the common plenums, with the system at steady state. For the method and its limits, see Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise, and for measuring CFM see Measuring Airflow (CFM). A university text on combustion heating gives typical supply air temperatures of about 130 to 170 °F for noncondensing furnaces and 100 to 110 °F for condensing furnaces. That text also defines efficiency as useful heat output divided by fuel energy input, which is the relation we use below.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. Assume two identical furnaces, each with 100,000 Btu/h input and 80 percent efficiency. A federal energy page lists 80 to 83 percent AFUE as mid-efficiency, so 80 is a plausible round number, but it is our assumption. Assume each blower delivers 1,600 CFM with matching settings. Assume the nameplate rise range is 35 to 65 °F, also an assumption. We use the standard sensible-heat relation: heat (Btu/h) = 1.08 x CFM x rise, where 1.08 comes from air density, specific heat and 60 minutes per hour (our own derivation).

  1. Output of one furnace: 100,000 x 0.80 = 80,000 Btu/h. Two furnaces: 160,000 Btu/h.
  2. Total airflow: 1,600 + 1,600 = 3,200 CFM.
  3. Rise = 160,000 / (1.08 x 3,200) = 160,000 / 3,456 = about 46 °F.
  4. Compare: 46 °F is inside 35 to 65 °F, so the airflow looks reasonable for this assumed range.

Now a failure what-if. If one blower stopped but both burners kept firing into 1,600 CFM, the rise would be 160,000 / (1.08 x 1,600) = about 93 °F, far above the range. That is our own illustration of why limits exist and why a rise far above the nameplate is a warning. Field numbers come from measured temperatures, not assumed arithmetic. If the field rise sits outside the range, check filters, duct restrictions and the blower settings on both units before touching anything else.

Field card: Twinning Two Furnaces

Two furnaces, one duct system, separate fuel and flue.

  • Use matched models and the maker's twinning kit, per the maker's instructions.
  • Common supply and return plenums; each furnace has its own gas, vent and combustion air.
  • The twin wire makes both blowers run at the same speed; set both to the same airflow.
  • A limit or rollout trip can shut down both furnaces by design. Never bypass them.
  • Rise = supply minus return, in the common plenums. Compare with each nameplate.
  • Total heat = 1.08 x CFM x rise. Add the two airflows for twinned units.
