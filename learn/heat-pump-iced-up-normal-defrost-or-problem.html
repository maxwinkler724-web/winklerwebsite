Heat Pump Iced Up in Winter: Normal Defrost or a Problem?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Seeing frost or ice on your heat pump can be alarming. Sometimes it is exactly what the machine is supposed to do. Here is how to tell the difference.

The short answer

Some frost on a heat pump's outdoor unit in cold, damp weather is normal, and so is a short defrost cycle with steam and a paused fan. Ice that stays, builds thick, or covers the fan is not normal. In that case, turn the system off at the thermostat and call a professional.

What normal defrost looks like

In winter a heat pump pulls heat from the outside air, which makes the outdoor coil very cold. Moisture in the air can freeze on it. To clear the frost, the system briefly switches direction (using a part called a reversing valve) and warms the coil. While that happens you may see steam rise (Lennox says it is just water vapor), hear a whoosh, and notice the indoor air cool slightly. Lennox says a typical defrost cycle lasts about 5 to 15 minutes, and the system checks for frost about every 30 to 90 minutes while heating, defrosting only when sensors detect enough buildup. Cold, damp weather means more cycles; dry or sunny weather may mean few or none. Afterward the unit should look mostly clear and go back to heating.

Timers differ by make and model. As examples only from two trade articles: one Amana example uses a timer of 30, 60, 90 or 120 minutes and ends defrost near 60 F or after a maximum time; a Carrier example uses a 30, 60 or 90 minute timer and ends near 65 F. The two articles disagree on the maximum defrost time, so treat these as examples, not rules for your unit.

What a problem looks like

  • Thick ice covering most of the unit.
  • Ice that does not thaw. Lennox says a frozen unit above freezing should thaw in about 2 to 4 hours; ice still there after about 6 to 8 hours needs a technician.
  • A fan that struggles or is buried in ice.
  • The house is not getting warm even though the unit runs constantly.
  • Ice that returns soon after it melts, or frost cycles that are frequent or run unusually long.
  • Ice on only one side, ice on the refrigerant lines, hissing, grinding or squealing, or an outdoor fan that will not spin.

Lennox lists possible causes such as a failed defrost control, timer or sensor, low refrigerant, restricted airflow (a dirty filter, debris, blocked ducts or closed vents), a clogged drain pan or line, a bad reversing valve or fan motor, and severe weather. The Department of Energy adds that strong winds can cause defrosting problems, which is why a bush or fence upwind of the unit is sometimes recommended.

Things you can safely check

  • Give it a moment. Watch for a few minutes to see if it is a normal defrost with steam.
  • Replace a dirty indoor air filter and open blocked vents. Poor indoor airflow affects performance.
  • Clear loose snow, leaves, and debris away from the outside of the unit by hand. Keep plants and clutter clear (Lennox suggests about two feet of clear space around the outdoor unit).
  • Check the thermostat is set to Heat and the system has power.
  • If it is heavily iced, set the thermostat to off so it can thaw. Lennox notes the emergency heat setting can keep the house warm meanwhile.

Please do not chip or scrape ice off the unit or pour hot water on it. Lennox warns that this can bend coils and fins or crack parts through temperature shock. Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out. If you have a gas furnace as backup and smell gas, leave first.
  • The unit never seems to go into defrost, or it will not return to heating after defrost.
  • Ice comes back soon after thawing, or the unit is frozen solid.
  • You suspect a refrigerant, electrical, or reversing valve problem.
  • The house stays cold even after the checks above. If it is very cold, ask the technician about safe temporary heat.

Field card: Heat Pump Ice in Winter

Light frost that clears itself is normal. Ice that stays is not.

  • Normal: light frost, steam, and a paused fan for a few minutes.
  • Not normal: thick ice, ice that stays, or ice over the fan. A coil still iced after a defrost, or a unit that never defrosts, is the fault.
  • A normal defrost is about 5 to 15 minutes.
  • Safe checks: filter, vents, clear loose debris, thermostat setting.
  • Never chip ice or pour hot water on the unit.
  • Set the thermostat to off if heavily iced, and call a pro if it does not thaw in about 6 to 8 hours.
