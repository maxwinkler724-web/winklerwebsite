Keeping a Home Pantry and Food-Storage Cabinets Cool and Dry

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Canned and dry goods are forgiving, but they are not heat proof. The cabinet you pick matters more than most people think, and it is often an HVAC question: where the heat comes from, where the moisture comes from, and where the air from the registers goes.

Why cool and dry wins

The federal food-safety agency for meat, poultry and egg products says to keep canned foods and other shelf-stable products in a cool, dry place, and that temperatures below 85 °F are best. It also warns that spoilage risk rises as storage temperature rises, and that temperatures over 100 °F are harmful to canned goods. A multi-state extension publication gives the same 85 °F ceiling for a cupboard and calls 50 to 70 °F the optimum. Other university extension fact sheets say canned foods stored above 70 °F will have a shorter shelf life, and that their listed storage times assume 70 °F. In plain terms, 85 °F is the line you do not want a cabinet to cross, and the cooler it stays below that, the longer the quality lasts.

The federal page lists shelf lives measured in months or years: 2 to 5 years for low-acid canned goods and 12 to 18 months for high-acid ones. Those are quality guides, not a reason to ignore a bulging or leaking can. The multi-state publication says bulging cans are spoiled, and that dents on the side or rim seams should not be used.

Cabinets to avoid

Two university extension fact sheets name the same trouble spots: cabinets over the range, near the dishwasher, or by the refrigerator exhaust, where the temperature is too warm for food. The multi-state publication adds cabinets near an oven, stove or hot pipes, and says to keep food out of direct sunlight. Another extension fact sheet adds heating ducts to the list. The federal page also says not to store canned goods above or beside the stove, under the sink, or in a damp garage or basement. Putting those together, our checklist is:

  • Above or beside the range or oven, and next to the dishwasher.
  • On the back or side of the refrigerator where its exhaust heat comes out. See the related refrigerator lesson below.
  • Against a water heater or furnace closet wall, or along hot water pipes and heating ducts.
  • On a sunny exterior wall.
  • An unheated garage or porch in freezing weather: one extension fact sheet says canned goods should be kept cool but not freezing, and that they may freeze in winter. The same sheet says not to keep canned goods above 95 °F, as can happen in a shed or garage in summer.

Of the items above, the sunny wall, the water heater wall and the furnace closet wall are our own additions, built on the sources' heat and sunlight warnings. The sources name pipes, ducts and a furnace for home-canned jars; they do not list the water heater itself.

Humidity, moisture and pests

A fact sheet from a southern extension service says high temperature or humidity can shorten storage time considerably and that insect infestation can occur in any home. The multi-state publication says airtight containers protect food from insects and from humidity levels greater than 60 percent, and that dampness can corrode metal lids and break seals on home-canned jars. It also says many insects can infest pantry foods that sit for more than two months.

A midwestern extension service says pests live and grow inside stored dry goods such as flour, rice, pasta, oatmeal, pet food and chocolate, so inspect packages before you buy and keep them sealed and unbroken. It recommends tightly closed glass, metal or heavy plastic containers, because pests can chew through paper, cardboard and thin plastic. It also says to keep storage areas clean, with no crumbs or spills, and to clean cracks and corners. It lists one generation as taking 1 to 2 months.

Our own HVAC note: a pantry on an outside wall, near a bathroom, or over a damp basement is more likely to run humid than an interior hallway closet. If the room smells musty, fix the moisture source first. The basement dehumidifier lesson below covers that.

Simple thermometer checks

None of the sources we read sets out a method for checking a pantry with a thermometer, so this section is our own. Put an inexpensive thermometer on the shelf where the food sits, not on the wall. Read it at the end of a cooking evening and again in the early afternoon on a hot day, and write the numbers on a card. A digital one that remembers its high and low reading is easier. Repeat on a bright summer day and on a cold night. If a shelf reads above 85 °F on cooking days or on a sunny afternoon, move the food to the cooler spot. If you also own a humidity gauge, the 60 percent figure mentioned above is a useful number to watch.

How supply registers near a pantry matter

This is our own explanation; none of the sources address registers. In summer a supply register in or near a pantry can keep it a few degrees cooler, if the cooling system runs. In winter the same register delivers warm air, which is another reason an extension fact sheet lists heating ducts among places to avoid. Do not block a register with boxes just to protect food, because blocked registers can starve the system. See the lesson on furniture and registers below. If a pantry is always too warm, a technician can look at the air balance, but moving the cans is the first fix.

Worked example (our own): range cabinet vs hallway pantry

This example and every number in it are our own assumptions, except the 85 °F line, which comes from the federal page and the multi-state publication.

Assumptions: the cabinet above the range reaches 95 °F on cooking days. It stays over 85 °F for 1.5 hours on each weekday evening, 3 hours on Saturday (baking) and 2 hours on Sunday (a roast). The hallway pantry stays at 72 °F all week, which is above the 70 °F level used in the extension charts but well under 85 °F.

  1. Weekdays: 5 days × 1.5 h = 7.5 h.
  2. Add Saturday: 7.5 + 3 = 10.5 h.
  3. Add Sunday: 10.5 + 2 = 12.5 h over the line per week.
  4. A week has 7 × 24 = 168 h, so 12.5 ÷ 168 = 0.0744, about 7.4 percent of the week.
  5. Hallway: 0 h over 85 °F.

Reading the result: 12.5 hours a week sounds small, but it repeats every week, and the peak (95 °F in our assumption) is 10 °F over the line. The hallway never crosses it. Store canned goods and flour in the hallway, and keep the cabinet over the range for pots and dishes.

Field card: Keeping a Home Pantry and Food-Storage Cabinets Cool and Dry

Cool, dry, dark and away from appliance heat.

  • Keep canned and dry goods below 85 °F; 50 to 70 °F is the optimum in one extension publication.
  • Avoid cabinets over the range, near the dishwasher, by the refrigerator exhaust and near hot pipes or ducts.
  • Freezing garages and sunny walls are also poor choices.
  • Use tightly closed glass, metal or heavy plastic containers; keep shelves free of crumbs.
  • Put a thermometer on the shelf and check it after cooking and on hot days (our own tip).
  • Our worked example: 12.5 of 168 hours, about 7.4 percent, over 85 °F (our own assumptions).
