HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings: NC Ratings, Silencers and Mechanical Room Sound

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tenant calls about a hiss over the conference table or a rumble in the open office. Before anyone touches a damper, it helps to know what the number on the spec sheet means, where the sound travels, and what a fix really changes. This lesson covers decibels, NC ratings, the usual noise sources, the usual fixes, and the sound levels the mechanics themselves work in.

Check the source documents

Check the source documents. Design targets and exposure rules change. This page was built from agency and maker pages read in 2026 and is general education. The full methods live in the ASHRAE handbook chapters on sound and vibration and in AHRI 885, which we name here in plain text only. Confirm the project specification and current OSHA text before you rely on a number.

Decibels and how levels add

Decibels are logarithmic, so levels are not added the ordinary way. OSHA's technical manual says plainly that decibels are logarithmic values and cannot be summed with arithmetic addition. A CDC chapter on the physics of sound explains the method: convert each level to an intensity, add the intensities, then convert back to a level. That works out to the form L total = 10 log10 of the sum of 10 raised to (each L divided by 10). The same chapter says this applies to random, unrelated noise sources, and that its table method (a 3 dB difference adds 1.8 dB) agrees with the log result. A Canadian occupational health guide gives the everyday meaning: a 3 dB increase corresponds to sound energy doubled, and a source 10 dB or more below another adds essentially nothing.

Two shortcuts follow. Two equal sources add 3 dB. A source 10 dB or more below the loudest one can be ignored. One VAV terminal maker's sound guide uses the same idea: 80 dB plus 74 dB is about 81 dB.

A-weighting versus NC curves

A sound level meter set to A-weighting reports dBA. OSHA's technical manual says the A network strongly reduces the very low frequencies, so dBA tracks hearing risk and everyday loudness. It is one number. Room noise criteria are different. A terminal-unit maker's guide describes NC as a rating drawn from octave-band levels, and says it is a single number that says little about sound quality. The room is rated by the highest octave band that touches an NC curve, so a rumble or a hiss that dBA hides will still set the NC. A trade-press article notes that ASHRAE prefers RC curves, which add the 16.5 and 31 Hz bands, while NC covers 63 to 8,000 Hz and misses very low rumble. The maker guide adds a letter to RC: R for rumble, H for hiss, V for vibration, N for neutral. For a complaint, that quality letter is often the clue.

Typical design targets

Targets come from the project documents, and the VA specification, for example, sends designers to its own room data sheets and to the ASHRAE applications handbook. Examples that are freely published:

  • A federal Whole Building Design Guide page lists small conference rooms and small private offices at NC 30 to 35, general offices at NC 35 to 40, and computer rooms at NC 40 to 50.
  • A terminal-unit maker's table gives executive offices and conference rooms NC 25 to 30, private offices NC 30 to 35, open-plan offices NC 35 to 40, hospital private rooms NC 25 to 30, and laboratories NC 35 to 40.

The two sources do not agree on conference rooms, which is normal. Use the number in the job's specification. The WBDG page also says too quiet is a problem: quiet rooms let nearby conversations carry.

Where the noise comes from

  • Fan tones. A fan makes a pure tone at the blade pass frequency, which one fan-acoustics article gives as number of blades times rpm divided by 60. If that tone lands near the natural frequency of the connected duct, the duct can ring. The same article says backward-inclined wheels run quieter than forward-curved ones. A building-systems engineering article adds that a fan far from its efficient point, or fed with turbulent air at its inlet or outlet, is noisier.
  • VAV boxes. A trade-press article lists high inlet pressure, a nearly closed damper, discharge sound, and radiated sound from the casing as causes, and suggests keeping inlet static pressure under about 2 in. wc, with 1.5 in. wc as a suggested limit. A terminal maker's guide gives one example unit at 2,000 cfm whose radiated NC rises from under 20 at 0.5 in. wc to 33 at 3.0 in. wc. For the controls side of pressure, see VAV Duct Static Pressure Reset.
  • Duct velocity and fittings. The engineering article says high velocity and abrupt transitions cause flow noise, that transitions steeper than 15 degrees can rumble, and that elbows and takeoffs should be spaced at least 4 to 5 duct diameters apart. Registers near fittings add turbulence; see Supply Registers and Return Grilles.
  • Rooftop units over occupied space. A rooftop unit maker's acoustics article describes supply and return duct sound, breakout through duct walls, sound through the roof deck, and structure-borne vibration. In its 60-ton, open-office example the worst path was supply breakout through a large unlined duct.

Fixes, roughly in order

  1. Check the system first. Confirm airflow and inlet pressure match design before buying anything. Lower duct static helps the fan and every terminal at once.
  2. Reduce the source. Reselect or slow the fan, straighten the air entering it, and size terminals for the pressure they actually see. A terminal guide says to control fan speed rather than choke airflow with dampers.
  3. Treat the path. Internally lined duct absorbs high-frequency discharge sound, and a trade-press article notes that outside lining gives little benefit. One maker table lists about 0.4 to 4.9 dB per foot of 1 in. lined duct depending on frequency, with the most loss in the middle and upper bands. Silencers work the same way, so put them close to the source. The rooftop unit article warns they are weak against the low-frequency content of rooftop units, that field results are often below lab ratings, and that their pressure drop can raise fan speed. One engineering article suggests silencers with a static-pressure loss of 0.35 in. wc or less.
  4. Fix the last few feet. A short run of lined flex at the diffuser reduces discharge sound; a terminal guide recommends 3 to 5 ft, kept straight, and warns that flex allows more breakout than metal and makes noise when bent or sagged.
  5. Isolate. For fans and units on spring or neoprene isolators, see Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors. The rooftop article lists common mistakes that defeat springs: rigid conduit or piping bridging the unit and curb, and rigid duct connections without flexible connectors.
  6. Move or relocate. Terminal and rooftop maker guides both say to put the noisy equipment over corridors, closets or service areas rather than over the people.

Measuring with a sound level meter

OSHA's technical manual says to set the meter to slow response for typical occupational measurements, and to calibrate before and after use with a known tone such as 114 dB at 1,000 Hz, treating a reading within 0.2 dB as good. An octave-band analyzer shows which bands drive the problem, which is what you need to compare to NC curves. Meter type matters: the manual refers to Type 1 and Type 2 meters. Note the time, the location, and whether the system was at high load, since the maker articles say design sound for the highest load condition. For equipment that sits outdoors, see Quieter Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Units. For what a given sound means on a service call, see Furnace, AC and Boiler Noises.

Mechanical room sound and OSHA

29 CFR 1910.95 requires protection when levels exceed Table G-16. The table allows 90 dBA for 8 hours, 92 for 6, 95 for 4, 97 for 3, 100 for 2, 102 for 1.5, 105 for 1, 110 for half an hour, and 115 for a quarter hour or less, on the A scale with slow response. A hearing conservation program starts at an 8-hour average of 85 dBA, which the rule calls the action level. Paragraph (b)(1) puts feasible engineering or administrative controls first, and personal protective equipment if those do not bring levels within the table. NIOSH recommends 85 dBA for an 8-hour shift with a 3 dB exchange rate, meaning every 3 dB more halves the recommended time. NIOSH also says noise is likely hazardous if you must raise your voice to be heard by someone an arm's length away. The WBDG page adds a design point: do not put mechanical rooms next to offices or conference rooms.

Worked example: adding sources

Our own example. Assumptions: two machines in one mechanical room, noise is random and unrelated, and the levels are dBA at the same spot with each running alone. These numbers are invented for teaching, not measured.

Two equal sources. Two pumps at 85 dBA each.

  1. Each source: 10^(85/10) = 10^8.5.
  2. Sum: 2 x 10^8.5.
  3. Level: 10 log10 (2 x 10^8.5) = 10 log10 2 + 85 = 3.01 + 85 = 88.0 dBA.

Two unequal sources. A fan at 90 dBA and a pump at 84 dBA.

  1. 10^9 + 10^8.4 = 1.000 x 10^9 + 0.251 x 10^9 = 1.251 x 10^9.
  2. Level: 10 log10 (1.251 x 10^9) = 90 + 0.97 = 91.0 dBA.
  3. Check with the cited table method: a 6 dB difference adds 1.0 dB to the higher level, giving 91.

What it means. Each pump alone sits right at the NIOSH 85 dBA recommended limit and the OSHA action level. Together they make 88 dBA. On the NIOSH 3 dB rule that is 3 dB above 85, so the recommended time at that spot drops to about 4 hours (our arithmetic from the exchange rate). The fan-and-pump case reads 91 dBA, over the OSHA 90 dBA 8-hour value. Silencing the quieter pump saves almost nothing, because removing it only takes 91 down to 90. The louder fan is where the fix belongs. Real exposure depends on time spent at each location, so this is not a dose calculation.

Field card: HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings

Find the path, then fix the source or the path, not the loudest guess.

  • Decibels add by energy: equal sources add 3 dB; a source 10 dB lower adds almost nothing.
  • dBA is one weighted number; NC and RC rate octave bands, so a hiss or rumble can fail even when dBA looks fine.
  • Published office targets: NC 30 to 35 private offices, NC 35 to 40 general or open offices (WBDG and a maker table).
  • Check inlet static pressure at VAV boxes first; sound rises fast above about 2 in. wc in the maker example.
  • Lined duct and silencers help high frequencies, not low rumble from rooftop units.
  • Meter: slow response, calibrate before and after.
  • OSHA Table G-16: 90 dBA for 8 hours; action level 85 dBA; NIOSH 85 dBA with a 3 dB exchange rate.
