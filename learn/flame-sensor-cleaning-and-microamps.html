Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace that lights, runs a few seconds and then shuts off is a classic. Often the burner is fine. The control board simply cannot see the flame, because the flame sensor signal is too weak.

How the board sees fire

The flame sensor is a plain metal rod with a ceramic mount. It makes no power of its own. HVAC School explains that the board puts a small voltage on the rod. When flame covers the rod, the ions in the flame carry a very small direct current from the rod through the flame to the burner and back to the board. That current, called flame rectification, is the board's proof of fire. If the board does not see it within a few seconds, it closes the gas valve and tries again. See Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics for the big picture.

Reading the signal

The current is measured in microamps (millionths of an amp), with a meter that has a microamp DC range, connected in series between the rod and its wire. Make sure your meter resolves at least a tenth of a microamp and that the meter itself is working. Examples from the sources:

  • HVAC School: roughly 0.5 to 10 microamps depending on the furnace, with 2 to 6 common.
  • One manufacturer technical guide: a minimum of 0.5 and typical 4 to 6.
  • One ignition control instruction sheet: at least 1.0 required, nominal 2 to 8.
  • The White-Rodgers 50M56U-843 installation sheet, as one control's example: a minimum flame current for detection of 1 microamp DC, and a maximum current of 0.1 microamp that still counts as no flame. It specifies a DC microammeter in series with the sensor lead, and a maximum leakage resistance of 100 megohms.
  • A service article: under 5 suggests a dirty sensor.

Your furnace's control sets its own minimum (one example: 1 microamp). A service tech reads the signal with a microammeter. A weak signal usually means a dirty or poorly grounded rod or a flame problem. Those numbers do not agree exactly, which is the point: the minimum and typical numbers belong to the control and the furnace. Use the manual for yours, and watch whether the reading is steady.

Cleaning the rod

Homeowners: leave sensor cleaning and any other gas-appliance work to a licensed professional unless your furnace manual says you may do it. The rest of this section is for trained techs. We found trade advice on cleaning, but no maker instruction, so treat what follows as trade practice, not a manufacturer procedure. Let the rod cool, then clean gently. HVAC School suggests very fine steel wool or a non-abrasive pad rather than sand cloth, which can leave grit that causes faster buildup. Another source recommends a non-woven scouring pad and warns that heavy abrasives wear the surface. HVAC School also suggests checking resistance from the rod tip to its terminal afterward, and says it found no evidence of a rub-off coating on typical rods; claims of such coatings that we saw were unsourced. Wipe the residue off, check for cracks in the ceramic, reinstall it, and take a new reading so you can see the improvement. Do not bend the rod out of position.

If cleaning does not fix it

HVAC School lists other causes of a weak signal. Work through them before replacing the board:

  • Rod problems: cracked insulator (short), broken rod (open), or a rod placed so the flame does not cover it.
  • Coating: a glassy silica film or carbon buildup.
  • Dirty or unstable flame: poor combustion air, wrong gas pressure, or airborne contaminants. Laundry or basement rooms with cleaners can foul rods sooner.
  • Poor burner ground: with power off, resistance from neutral to the burner assembly should be a few ohms or less, according to HVAC School and a manufacturer guide.
  • Polarity: hot and neutral swapped can stop rectification.

Safety

Shut off power before reaching into the burner area. If the flame looks wrong, lifts, rolls out, or you smell exhaust, stop and sort out combustion before anything else; see Combustion and CO Safety Basics. Never bridge the sensor to fool the board. Confirm a full normal cycle after the repair.

Field card: Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps

No signal, no gas.

  • The rod makes no power; the board sends a small current that the flame carries back.
  • Measure in microamps, in series, with a meter that reads tenths.
  • Use your model's minimum and typical values; sources differ.
  • Trade advice, not a maker step: a fine, non-grit abrasive pad, gently, then re-measure. Homeowners leave this to a pro.
  • If still weak: check rod position and cracks, burner ground, polarity and combustion.
