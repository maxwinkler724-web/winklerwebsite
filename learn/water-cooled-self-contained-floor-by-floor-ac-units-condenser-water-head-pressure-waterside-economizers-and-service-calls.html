Water-Cooled Self-Contained AC Units: Condenser Water, Head Pressure and Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-29

In many older office buildings every floor has its own air conditioner: one cabinet holding the compressors, a water-cooled condenser, the evaporator and the fan, all fed by one condenser water loop that runs up a riser from a cooling tower. This lesson explains how that arrangement works, what the service calls usually look like, and how much water a 30-ton unit needs.

What a floor-by-floor unit is

A self-contained water-cooled unit is a complete refrigeration cycle in a single cabinet. Return air from the floor passes a filter, an evaporator coil and a fan, and goes back out through ductwork. The heat the evaporator picks up, plus the heat of the compressor, has to go somewhere. Instead of a fan-cooled outdoor coil, it goes into water through a condenser inside the cabinet. Federal rules for commercial package air conditioners treat water-cooled equipment as its own type, alongside air-cooled, evaporatively cooled and water-source types (10 CFR 431.92 definitions).

A common design for the condenser is a shell-and-tube heat exchanger: refrigerant vapor condenses on the outside of the tubes while water flows through them (a university lecture text on heat exchangers describes water-cooled condensers this way). The warm water leaves the cabinet, rides the riser to the cooling tower, and returns cooler.

The condenser water loop

The tower rejects the loop's heat to the atmosphere mainly by evaporating a little of the water. The EPA water-efficiency guide for commercial building mechanical systems puts that at about 1.8 gallons evaporated per ton-hour of cooling, and says tower tons run about 1.25 times the cooling tons because compressors and fans add heat. The nominal tower rating in a national-lab chilled-water design guide is 3 gpm per ton with 95 F water entering the tower, 85 F leaving, and a 78 F wet-bulb. That is a 10 F range, which we use in the example below. For the tower itself, see Cooling Tower Basics, and for what the water does to the tubes, see Condenser Water Treatment.

Parts that control the water side

Water regulating valve. In our own plain description: a regulating valve on the condenser water piping opens more as the refrigerant discharge pressure rises and closes down as it falls, so the unit holds a steady head pressure and does not waste water. Its exact settings, and whether the unit uses one at all, are in the unit's own service manual, so read that before you adjust anything.

Strainer. The DOE and PNNL operations guide notes that a filter in the condenser water line catches debris from the tower, and that if it clogs at higher loads, heat transfer worsens and the refrigerant condensing temperature rises (section 9.4.5). We clean the strainer on a schedule, not only when it causes a call.

Flow proving. The LBNL design guide lists evaporator and condenser "proof of water flow" among the safety controls on water-cooled equipment. The idea is simple: the compressor should not run if the water that carries its heat away is not moving.

Tube velocity and cleaning. The same guide recommends tube water velocities of at least 3 ft/s and no more than 12 ft/s, says tube interiors on open-tower systems may foul and need mechanical cleaning, and links fouling to high dissolved solids and to velocities below 3 ft/s. Tube cleaning is a lockout job: power off, water isolated and drained, before anyone opens the condenser heads.

Waterside economizer coil. ENERGY STAR describes a waterside economizer as a way to use condenser water to cool chilled water directly through a separate heat exchanger when outdoor conditions allow (its page is written for data centers). In a floor unit, the same idea can be packaged as a coil in the air stream ahead of the evaporator, fed with cool tower water through a control valve. When the water is cold enough, the coil does some or all of the cooling and the compressor rests. See Waterside Economizers for the central-plant version. The same ENERGY STAR page says climates with a wet-bulb below 55 F for 3,000 or more hours a year suit this approach, which covers most of the U.S. Our own warning: the coil is another thing that can leak, clog or stick, so a unit that "will not cool" in mild weather may be an economizer valve problem and not a refrigerant one.

Typical service calls

High head pressure

The first suspect is the condenser water: low flow from a clogged strainer, a closed or stuck valve, a tripped pump, or a tower that is warm or off. The second is fouled tubes. The DOE operations guide says scale and sludge insulate the tubes, so a larger temperature difference between water and refrigerant is needed, and that air trapped in the condenser raises discharge pressure much as scale does. A DOE chiller fact sheet adds that dirty evaporator and condenser tubes can raise compressor energy use by as much as 30 percent, and that when condensing temperature rises from 95 F to 105 F the cooling capacity drops 7 percent. A compact way to separate the causes is to compare water in and out temperatures with the load: a big rise suggests low flow, and a small rise with high head points toward fouled tubes or air. Our own habit is to confirm with the unit's instruments and the pressure-switch ideas in AC High and Low Pressure Switches.

Low suction pressure

The usual cause is too little air across the evaporator. The DOE Energy Saver page says clogged, dirty filters reduce airflow and efficiency, and that a dirty evaporator coil is insulated and gets less air; it also tells technicians to check belts for tightness and wear. A DOE fault-detection report shows reduced evaporator airflow as low evaporator temperature with low superheat. Low suction pressure with a low superheat points to airflow first. Low suction with high superheat points toward a refrigerant problem, which is outside this lesson. Look at filters, the coil face, belt tension and the drive before you suspect charge.

Condensate overflow

The DOE Energy Saver page says clogged drain channels prevent a unit from removing humidity and suggests running a stiff wire through them. On a floor unit, an overflowing pan can stain ceilings below, so check the drain, trap and pan during every visit.

Worked example (our own)

This example uses numbers we chose, not data from a particular unit. We assume a 30-ton unit with an EER of 12.0 Btu/h per watt and a design water temperature rise of 10 F. Federal rules define EER as the cooling effect divided by the net work input, in Btu per watt-hour.

  1. Cooling capacity = 30 tons x 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 360,000 Btu/h. The ton definition is from the DOE cooling tower fact sheet.
  2. Electrical input = 360,000 Btu/h / 12.0 = 30,000 W = 30 kW.
  3. Input as heat = 30,000 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = 102,360 Btu/h. The 3.412 comes from the EIA figure of 3,412 Btu per kilowatt-hour.
  4. Heat rejected to the water = 360,000 + 102,360 = 462,360 Btu/h. This is a simplification, since EER includes the supply fan, whose heat goes into the air, not the water.
  5. Flow = 462,360 / (500 x 10) = 462,360 / 5,000 = 92.5 gpm.

Where 500 comes from. One gallon of water weighs about 8.33 pounds (an extension service fact sheet), one Btu raises one pound of water one degree F (EIA), and there are 60 minutes in an hour. So 8.33 lb/gal x 1 Btu/lb-F x 60 min/h = about 500 Btu/h per gpm per degree F. Rearranged: gpm = Btu/h / (500 x dT).

Check: 92.5 gpm / 30 tons = 3.08 gpm per ton, close to the 3.0 gpm per ton rating basis from the LBNL guide, and 462,360 / 30 = about 15,400 Btu/h per ton, near the 1.25 factor from the EPA guide (15,000). Now a service-call twist: if a half-clogged strainer drops flow to 70 gpm, the rise becomes 462,360 / (500 x 70) = 13.2 F, so the leaving water is about 3 F warmer and head pressure climbs. The numbers show why a 10 F rise is a useful check.

Versus central chillers

This is our own summary; the cited material gives no side-by-side figures for floor units. A central plant puts compressors in one room, while floor units spread them through the building, so any compressor service happens on occupied floors. On the other hand, one unit failing affects one floor, not the whole building. Floor units also need condenser water piped to every floor, so a water problem on the riser can hit all units at once. For central plant types see Chiller Types.

Safety

Lock out and tag out power, isolate and drain water before opening condenser heads, and follow the unit's service manual and your site's rules. Cooling tower water has its own biological hazards; see the tower lesson linked above.

Field card: water-cooled self-contained units

No water, no heat rejection.

  • Gpm = Btu/h / (500 x dT). 500 = 8.33 lb/gal x 60 min/h x 1 Btu/lb-F.
  • Our example: 30 tons, EER 12 = about 462,000 Btu/h rejected = 92.5 gpm at 10 F rise.
  • High head: check flow, strainer, valve, tower water temperature, then tubes and air.
  • Low suction: check filter, coil face, belts and fan first.
  • Condensate overflow: clear drain and trap, check the pan.
  • Tube water velocity: 3 to 12 ft/s recommended in the LBNL guide.
