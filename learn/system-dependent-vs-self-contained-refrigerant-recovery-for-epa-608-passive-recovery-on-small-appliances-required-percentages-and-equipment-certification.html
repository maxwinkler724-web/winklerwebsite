System-Dependent vs Self-Contained Refrigerant Recovery for EPA 608: Passive Recovery on Small Appliances, Required Percentages and Equipment Certification

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some recovery equipment does the pulling itself. Other equipment only gives the refrigerant a place to go and relies on the appliance to push it there. The EPA 608 exam asks you to tell these apart, to know where the passive kind is allowed, and to remember the percentages that apply to small appliances.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. This page was built from the federal rule text and EPA pages read in 2026. It is general education, not legal advice. Confirm the current wording of 40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F before you rely on any number here.

The two kinds of recovery equipment

The federal definitions are short. Self-contained recovery equipment is equipment that can remove refrigerant from an appliance without the assistance of components contained in the appliance. System-dependent recovery equipment is equipment that requires the assistance of components contained in the appliance to remove the refrigerant. The rule also defines "recover" as removing refrigerant in any condition from an appliance and storing it in an external container, without necessarily testing or processing it.

The exam shorthand is active for self-contained and passive for system-dependent. Those two words are study labels we use here, not the rule's words. An active machine has its own compressor, so the appliance can be dead and the machine still works. A passive setup has nothing of its own to move vapor, so it leans on the appliance, most often the appliance compressor, to push refrigerant out.

Where passive recovery is allowed

The rule in 40 CFR 82.156(e) says system-dependent equipment may not be used with appliances that have a full charge of more than 15 pounds of refrigerant. The one exception is system-dependent equipment permanently attached to the appliance as a pump-out unit. Paragraph (f) separately exempts people who service only their own appliances that contain pump-out units from the requirement to use certified self-contained equipment.

The small appliance definition in 82.152 is a factory-made, charged and hermetically sealed appliance with five pounds or less of refrigerant. It lists household and commercial refrigerators and freezers, room air conditioners, dehumidifiers, under-the-counter ice makers, vending machines and drinking water coolers. That is why exam questions pair passive recovery with small appliances: it is the group the passive method is aimed at. Notice the two numbers differ. The rule text we read caps passive recovery at a 15 pound full charge, not 5 pounds, so do not answer "5 pounds" to a question about the limit on system-dependent equipment. Larger appliances need self-contained equipment taken to the evacuation levels in the rule table, covered in the recovery machine care lesson.

Required percentages for small appliances

Under 82.156(b), before opening or disposing of a small appliance, you must recover refrigerant with certified equipment. The amount depends on the appliance's date of manufacture and whether its compressor works:

  • Made before November 15, 1993: recover 80 percent of the refrigerant.
  • Made on or after November 15, 1993 with a functioning compressor: recover 90 percent.
  • Made on or after November 15, 1993 with a compressor that is not functioning: recover 80 percent.
  • As an alternative to any percentage, evacuate the appliance to four inches of mercury vacuum.

Why does the working compressor earn a higher number? A running compressor adds pumping power. The more of the job the appliance helps with, the more of the charge you can reasonably get out. That reasoning is our own plain-language explanation, not a quote from the rule. What the rule itself ties to a working compressor is the 90 percent figure.

Helping a passive recovery along

With passive recovery the appliance has to do the work, so a technician makes it easier. Running the appliance compressor gives the push, which is the whole idea behind the definition. Some exam materials also mention heating or tapping the appliance to move more refrigerant out. We did not find either practice in the rule text or the EPA pages we read, so we do not teach them as requirements; confirm them in your equipment instructions. The rule does say recovery equipment must be used in line with the manufacturer's directions, unless they conflict with the subpart. For access fittings and why piercing valves on small appliances cause trouble, see the line-tap lesson, and for whole-unit disposal see the safe disposal lesson.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This example is our own. We assume a small appliance manufactured after November 15, 1993, holding a 6 ounce charge. (6 ounces is 0.375 pound, well under the 5 pound small appliance limit.) We use the rule's percentages.

Case 1: compressor not working.

  • Required: 80 percent of 6 oz = 0.80 x 6 = 4.8 oz recovered.
  • May remain: 6 - 4.8 = 1.2 oz (the other 20 percent).

Case 2: compressor working.

  • Required: 90 percent of 6 oz = 0.90 x 6 = 5.4 oz recovered.
  • May remain: 6 - 5.4 = 0.6 oz (the other 10 percent).

Comparison. 5.4 - 4.8 = 0.6 oz. A working compressor raises the required recovery by 0.6 oz and cuts the allowed leftover in half, from 1.2 oz to 0.6 oz. In real work you do not weigh each ounce in the field this way; the equipment is certified to reach the percentage when used correctly. The point is the size of the numbers.

Equipment certification and dates

Recovery and recycling equipment has to be tested and certified. 40 CFR 82.158 sorts it by date of manufacture or import:

  • Before November 15, 1993: considered certified if it can reach the evacuation level in the rule's table when checked with a calibrated pressure gauge.
  • November 15, 1993 through September 21, 2003: certified under appendix B1, which is based on a 1993 industry standard (ARI Standard 740-1993).
  • September 22, 2003 through December 31, 2016: certified under appendix B2, based on Standard 740-1995.
  • January 1, 2017 and later: appendix B3 for non-flammable refrigerants (based on a 2016 standard) or appendix B4 for flammable refrigerants.

Small appliance recovery equipment must recover 90 percent with the compressor running or 80 percent with it off, tested under appendix C. EPA's equipment certification page says approved third-party organizations certify the equipment, and certified units carry a label stating that they meet EPA's minimum requirements for their category. EPA no longer requires technicians to send in an equipment acquisition form. Equipment with a noncondensables purge must not release more than 3 percent of the refrigerant being recycled, and hoses need low-loss fittings. For the cylinder side of the job, see Recovery, Recycle and Reclaim and push-pull liquid recovery.

Exam recap

  • Self-contained: works without help from the appliance. System-dependent: needs the appliance's components.
  • System-dependent equipment: not on appliances over 15 pounds unless permanently attached as a pump-out unit.
  • Small appliance: factory sealed, 5 pounds or less.
  • After November 15, 1993: 90 percent with a working compressor, 80 percent without. Before: 80 percent. Or four inches of mercury vacuum.

Field card: Passive vs Active Recovery

Passive borrows the appliance's compressor. Active brings its own.

  • Small appliance: factory sealed, 5 lb or less.
  • Passive limit in the rule: no more than 15 lb full charge, unless a permanent pump-out unit.
  • Built after 11/15/1993: 90% with a working compressor, 80% without.
  • Built before 11/15/1993: 80%. Or evacuate to 4 inches Hg vacuum.
  • Our example, 6 oz: 4.8 oz out (1.2 oz left) vs 5.4 oz out (0.6 oz left).
  • Follow the equipment maker's directions.
