Getting Ready for an Entry-Level HVAC Technician Interview: What Employers Check, Technical Questions, Ride-Along Tests and Questions to Ask

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An entry-level interview is mostly a check on three things: will you show up and drive safely, will you work safely, and can you learn. Nobody expects a first-job candidate to know everything. They do expect honest answers and sound habits. This lesson walks through what to prepare.

What employers commonly look for

These are common themes, not a promise about any one company. Every shop weighs them differently.

  • EPA Section 608 certification status. EPA requires certification for technicians who maintain, service, repair or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerant, and its page says the credentials do not expire. The four types are Type I (small appliances), Type II (high- and very high-pressure), Type III (low-pressure) and Universal. EPA also describes an exception for apprentices who are closely and continually supervised by a certified technician. Know exactly what you hold, if anything, and say so plainly. If you have not tested yet, say that and say when you plan to. See EPA 608 Certification Basics.
  • A valid driver's license and a clean-enough record. The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that technicians may need a driver's license to get to jobsites. Many employers also have insurance rules for drivers. Bring your license, and be ready to talk honestly about anything on your record.
  • Basic tools and hand skills. Most employers do not expect a full set. Our apprentice tool list covers what to own first, so we will not repeat it.
  • Reliability. On time, phone answered, a straight explanation when something goes wrong. The BLS lists attention to detail, problem-solving, mechanical skills, customer-service skills and physical stamina among the qualities that matter in this work.
  • Safety habits. Lockout, ladders, eye protection, not rushing. Describe real habits from any past job or training.

Technical questions and honest answers

When you do not know, say what you do know and how you would find out. Guessing on a safety question is the worst move. Interviewers often care more about how you reason than whether you recite a number.

Ohm's law

Voltage equals current times resistance (V = I x R). A simple answer: if a 24-volt control circuit has 0.5 amp flowing, the load is about 48 ohms (24 / 0.5). We chose those numbers for illustration. Add that you would use a meter, with the power state and the meter category in mind. See Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Superheat versus subcooling

Superheat is how far the suction line temperature sits above the refrigerant's saturation temperature at the suction pressure. Subcooling is how far the liquid line temperature sits below the saturation temperature at the liquid pressure. Superheat tells you about the evaporator side and compressor protection; subcooling tells you about liquid at the metering device. An honest entry-level answer: "I can define both and I know the readings need a gauge or probe on each line. I have not set a charge on my own yet." More in Refrigeration Superheat Checks.

What do you do at a gas leak?

The safe answer is simple and does not require heroics: do not create a spark or flame, do not flip switches or use a phone inside, get people out, and report it from a safe distance to the gas utility and your supervisor using the company's procedure. This is our own plain-language summary, and your employer's written procedure and the local utility instructions come first. Never say you would hunt for the leak with a lighter, and never say you would stay to finish the job. Details are in Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Worked example (our own): a spoken answer to "Walk me through a no-cool call on a split system"

Assumptions for this example: a residential split system, homeowner says the house is warm, you are the tech arriving on the call, and you are answering in about a minute. All of this is our own sample wording, not a script from any employer.

  1. Safety first. "I would listen to the homeowner, then check the work area. If anyone smells gas I stop and follow the gas procedure. At the equipment I would turn off power and verify with a meter before opening a panel, and I would use eye protection and a safe ladder setup on any roof."
  2. Thermostat and indoor side. "With power restored for testing, I confirm the thermostat is set to cool and calling, and that the indoor blower runs. A dead thermostat, tripped breaker or float switch is a quick find."
  3. Outdoor unit. "I check whether the outdoor fan and compressor start. If not, I check the contactor and the line and low voltage, with power rules in mind."
  4. Airflow. "I look at the filter, the evaporator coil for ice, the outdoor coil for dirt, and the supply and return vents. Many no-cool calls end here."
  5. Numbers last. "Only if airflow looks right would I put gauges on and compare superheat and subcooling to the maker's data for that system. I would not add refrigerant until I have a reason, and I would call my lead if I am past what I have been trained to do."
  6. Close the loop. "I explain what I found in plain words and write it down." Our customer talk lesson covers that part.

Hands-on or ride-along tests

Some employers add a short practical portion: naming tools, reading a meter, identifying parts, or riding with a tech for part of a day. Treat the ride-along as a working interview. Wear boots and sturdy clothes, bring your own safety glasses, ask before touching anything, stay out of the way on roofs and in attics, and take notes. Do not pretend to know a task. "Can you show me first?" is a good sentence. Safety under supervision is what is being watched.

Questions to ask the employer

  • On-call: Is there an on-call rotation, how often, and would I be paired with someone at first? (Compare our lesson on working alone.)
  • Truck and tools: Will I ride with a lead tech? Which tools does the company supply, and which do I buy?
  • Training: Who trains me, and how will I be checked on my skills over the first months? Is there a registered apprenticeship? The U.S. Department of Labor has apprenticeship information.
  • Safety gear: What PPE is provided? OSHA says employers must provide required safety gear such as gloves and harnesses, and generally pay for it, with few exceptions. Ask how it is issued and replaced.
  • Reporting problems: OSHA says employers cannot retaliate against workers who raise safety complaints. Ask how the company wants hazards reported.

For the bigger picture of the job, the BLS Occupational Outlook page for heating, air conditioning and refrigeration mechanics and installers describes typical education, training, duties and outlook. Its Pay tab is where real wage data lives, so check it directly rather than relying on figures passed around.

What to bring

  • Photo ID and driver's license
  • EPA 608 card or proof of your test result, if you have one
  • Resume with references (names and phone numbers you have already asked)
  • Any training records or certifications you hold, in a folder
  • Notepad and pen with your questions written down
  • Safety glasses, work boots and gloves in case of a ride-along
  • Any basic hand tools you own, clean and in a bag, only if asked

Field card: Getting Ready for an Entry-Level HVAC Technician Interview

Be honest, be safe, be reliable.

  • Know your 608 status and say it plainly.
  • Bring license, ID, resume, notes, safety glasses.
  • V = I x R; superheat is above saturation at suction, subcooling is below saturation at liquid.
  • No-cool order: safety, thermostat, outdoor unit, airflow, then numbers.
  • Gas leak: no sparks, get out, report from a safe place.
  • Ask about on-call, training, truck, tools and PPE.
