CMMS Basics for HVAC Techs: Work Orders, PM Schedules and Close-Out Notes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

In facilities work, the job does not start with a knock on the door. It starts with a work order in a computer system. This lesson explains what that system tracks, how a work order travels from request to close, how preventive tasks appear on their own, and why the notes you type at the end matter to people you may never meet.

What the system keeps track of

A computerized maintenance management system, usually shortened to CMMS, is a database built around equipment. A federal operations and maintenance guide prepared by a federal national laboratory lists what such a system can hold: work orders (generated, prioritized and tracked), records of scheduled and unscheduled maintenance, procedures, warranty information and technical documents for each component, a parts and materials inventory, and capital and labor costs by component. For a tech, that breaks into four things to know.

  • Assets. Each piece of equipment is a record: a rooftop unit, a pump, a boiler, a fan coil. The record usually holds make, model, serial number, location, install date and a history.
  • Equipment tags. The tag is the short ID painted or labeled on the unit, such as the one you also see on mechanical drawings. If the tag on the unit and the tag in the system do not match, the history ends up on the wrong machine. Report a missing or wrong tag; do not guess.
  • Work orders. A work order is the record of one job: what was asked for, where, who did it, what was done and what it used.
  • Parts. Parts used on a work order can draw down a stock count, so a filter you take from the shelf but never record is a part the system thinks is still there.

The life of a work order

A university policy page on maintenance and service requests that we read describes a typical path. A request comes in through an online system, or by phone for an emergency. Staff review it for completeness. If it is accepted it becomes a work order with a number, and a coordinator assigns it to a craft shop through the maintenance management system. When the work is finished, labor and material costs are posted to the order and it is closed. The same page says preventive maintenance requests are entered on a schedule by the maintenance staff themselves. Local systems differ, but most follow this shape.

  1. Request. Someone reports a problem, or the schedule creates a preventive task.
  2. Review. Staff check that it is complete and not a duplicate, and set a priority.
  3. Assign. The order goes to a shop or a person.
  4. Work. You do the job, noting readings, parts and time.
  5. Close-out. You record what was found and done, and mark it complete or note what is still open.
  6. History. The closed order stays on the asset record.

How preventive tasks appear

The same federal guide says a CMMS can generate preventive maintenance work orders based on the calendar or on equipment run time. A calendar trigger fits tasks tied to the season or to inspections, such as a spring coil cleaning or an annual safety check. A run-hour trigger fits wear: a belt or a filter on a unit that runs around the clock wears faster than the same part on a unit that idles half the year. The guide also notes that linking the system to a building controls system can support condition-based monitoring, where a reading, not a date, says it is time to look.

Our own example of the idea: say a filter change is set for every 1,500 run hours. A fan that runs 12 hours a day reaches 1,500 hours in 1,500 / 12 = 125 days. A fan that runs 24 hours a day gets there in 1,500 / 24 = 62.5 days. The same task, set by hours, shows up about twice as often on the busier unit. Those hours and the interval are assumptions made up for this example.

Writing a good close-out note

The note is the part of the work order that outlives the visit. Our lesson on writing clear service tickets and work order notes covers the full pattern, so here are only the habits that matter most in a facilities system.

  • Use the equipment tag exactly as the system shows it.
  • Say what you found, what you did and what state you left the unit in. "Done" tells the next person nothing.
  • Record parts actually used, with quantities, so the inventory stays honest.
  • If a preventive task could not be finished, say why and leave the order open or create a follow-up. Closing it anyway makes the backlog look better than the equipment really is.
  • Put follow-up recommendations in the note, not just in a conversation, so they reach whoever plans repairs.

The preventive side has its own habits; see preventive maintenance checklist habits.

Why the data matters for budgets and repairs

The federal guide describes the history as sortable by equipment, date and responder, and says it can track the shortest, median and longest time to close a work order. It says tracking work orders generated and closed over time helps managers understand workload and schedule staff, and that the backlog of corrective maintenance points to workload problems. It also says operating metrics can help justify purchases and staffing. The guide does not claim that a CMMS alone writes a budget; people read the data and decide. The guide also estimates that preventive maintenance can save as much as 12 to 18 percent on average compared with a reactive-only program, and notes that one study found more than 55 percent of an average facility's maintenance resources were still reactive. Those are estimates from the guide, not promises for any one building.

What this means at the unit: if three work orders in one year each say "low airflow, filter loaded, replaced," a planner can see a pattern and ask whether the interval is too long. If those notes say only "fixed," the pattern is invisible, and the same call keeps coming back. A clean record also helps when a repair-or-replace question comes up for an aging unit.

Worked example: one month of preventive work orders

Our own example, with assumed counts. The numbers below are made up to show the arithmetic; they are not from any real facility.

  • Open preventive work orders carried in from last month (opening backlog): 14
  • New preventive work orders generated this month: 84 by calendar plus 36 by run hours = 120
  • Completed this month: 108, made up of 96 of this month's orders and 12 of the carried-in orders

Step 1, this month's completion rate. 96 completed of 120 generated: 96 / 120 = 0.80, or 80 percent.

Step 2, closing backlog. Opening backlog + new orders - all completed = 14 + 120 - 108 = 26.

Step 3, check. Unfinished from this month: 120 - 96 = 24. Unfinished from last month: 14 - 12 = 2. Total 24 + 2 = 26. It matches.

Step 4, a second view. Counting all work available, 14 + 120 = 134, and 108 / 134 = 0.806, about 80.6 percent. The backlog grew by 26 - 14 = 12 orders.

Step 5, what it means. At 108 completions a month, 26 open orders is 26 / 108 = 0.24 of a month, roughly 7 days of work (0.24 x 30 = 7.2). Not alarming for one month, but if the backlog grows by 12 each month it will keep climbing. A manager would use this to ask whether the task list, the staffing or the parts supply needs attention. Your honest close-outs are what make the count trustworthy.

Field card: CMMS Basics for HVAC Techs

If it is not in the system, it did not happen.

  • Assets, equipment tags, work orders, PM schedules and parts are the five things the system ties together.
  • Work order path: request, review, assign, work, close-out, history.
  • PM can be set by calendar or by run hours.
  • Close-out note: tag, found, done, parts, state left, follow-up.
  • Do not close an unfinished task to clean up the numbers.
  • Completion rate = completed / generated. Backlog = opening + new - completed.
