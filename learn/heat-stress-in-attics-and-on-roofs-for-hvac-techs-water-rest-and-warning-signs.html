Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs for HVAC Techs: Water, Rest and Warning Signs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you search for how to handle heat stress on attic work, the short version from the federal sources is water, rest and shade, plus time to build up to the job if you are new. This lesson is safety information for apprentices, not medical advice. It covers why attics and roofs are hot, the warning signs, the first aid the agencies list, a worked water-intake example, and where OSHA's proposed heat rule stands.

Check the sources

Check the sources. Everything here comes from named agency pages read in 2026. Numbers are the agencies' numbers, not our advice. Your employer's heat plan and a doctor's advice for your own health come first. For a suspected heat stroke, call 911.

Why attics and roofs are different

OSHA's heat hazards page says heat stress is not only air temperature. It adds up air temperature, humidity, radiant heat from sunlight or other sources, and air movement, and it also counts the heat your own body makes while working and the clothing you wear. It lists enclosed spaces, heat sources, reflective surfaces and blocked airflow as things that raise the risk, and it says direct sunlight can add up to 13.5°F to the heat index. The National Weather Service says up to 15°F. A roof in full sun has the sun, reflected heat and hard work. An attic has still air and heat from above. The pages we read do not name attics or give attic temperatures, so we will not guess a number. Treat the weather report as a floor, not a ceiling.

OSHA also says heat illness can happen at moderate temperatures when work is heavy, and that outdoor workers have died of heat stroke on days when the maximum heat index was only 86°F. OSHA says heavy and very heavy work carry the highest risk, so how hard you are working matters as much as the weather. The CDC page on NIOSH heat stress adds that heat can also cause injuries, such as slips on sweat, fogged safety glasses and accidents from dizziness or fatigue.

Heat exhaustion and heat stroke

OSHA's first aid page lists these signs:

  • Heat exhaustion: fatigue, irritability, thirst, nausea or vomiting, dizziness or lightheadedness, heavy sweating, and a raised body temperature or fast heart rate. The National Weather Service adds cool, pale, clammy skin, muscle cramps, headache and fainting.
  • Heat stroke: confusion, slurred speech, unconsciousness, seizures, very high body temperature and a rapid heart rate, with either heavy sweating or hot, dry skin. The Weather Service adds a body temperature above 103°F and calls it a severe medical emergency.

First aid, as the sources give it. For heat exhaustion the Weather Service says to move the person to a cooler place, loosen clothing, apply cool wet cloths, offer small sips of water, and get medical help if the person vomits, gets worse or is not better in an hour. For heat stroke, OSHA says to call 911 right away, move the worker to shade or air conditioning, remove outer layers, put ice or cold wet towels on the head, neck, trunk, armpits and groin, use fans, and never leave the worker alone. OSHA describes a cold water or ice bath as the best rapid cooling in an emergency. The Weather Service says not to give fluids to someone with heat stroke, and says to use a fan only if the heat index is below the high 90s, since a fan can make a person hotter in higher heat. OSHA's own summary is to cool the worker and call 911 when in doubt. OSHA says never to leave the worker alone, so plan for a second person on the job.

Water, rest and shade

NIOSH's 2016 criteria document recommends about one cup (8 oz.) of cool water every 15 to 20 minutes for moderate work in the heat lasting up to two hours, and the CDC page says total intake should generally not go above 6 cups an hour. The criteria call for cool, drinkable water near the work area in individual cups. For sweating that goes beyond two hours, they point to sports drinks with balanced electrolytes. OSHA's water, rest, shade page agrees about electrolytes after two hours and says not to rely on thirst. It says breaks should get longer as heat stress goes up, generally an hourly break when stress exceeds its limits, and that a cooler spot speeds recovery: shade, an air-conditioned vehicle, or a place with fans or misting. Plan the water before you go up. A bottle left in the van does not help in the attic.

New to the heat? Build up to it

OSHA lists workers who have been on a job less than one to two weeks as unacclimatized, a main risk group. The CDC page on NIOSH recommendations gives the schedule for new workers: no more than 20 percent of the usual time in the heat on day one, then no more than 20 percent added each day. For workers with previous experience it lists 50, 60, 80 and 100 percent on days one to four. Supervise new workers closely for the first 14 days or until they are fully acclimatized. The same applies to anyone returning after time away, so tell your lead if the first hot week finds you on the roof.

Heat index and risk levels

The heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity. The National Weather Service names its bands Caution (80 to 90), Extreme Caution (90 to 103), Danger (103 to 124) and Extreme Danger (125 and up). The OSHA guide on using the heat index uses worksite risk levels, modified from the NOAA chart: Lower below 91°F, Moderate 91 to 103, High 103 to 115, and Very High to Extreme above 115. The guide says the values assume shade and light wind, and that full sun can add up to 15°F, so a site may need the precautions of the next level up. OSHA calls a wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) meter the most accurate way to judge heat stress; the heat index is a screening tool. The OSHA-NIOSH Heat Safety Tool app gives heat index and workload guidance but not WBGT.

Worked example: a 4-hour attic job

Invented day: outdoor air is 90°F at 60 percent relative humidity, and you will be in the attic for 4 hours.

Step 1, heat index. The NWS regression equation with T = 90 and RH = 60 has these terms: −42.379, +184.411, +608.600, −1213.679, −55.386, −197.342, +597.168, +276.314 and −58.028. They add to 99.68, which rounds to about 100°F. (We could not read the printed chart image, so this is the equation, not a chart lookup.)

Step 2, risk level. 100°F falls in the OSHA guide's Moderate band (91 to 103) and the NWS Extreme Caution band (90 to 103).

Step 3, sun adjustment. Full sun can add up to 15°F: 100 + 15 = 115°F, the edge of the High band. That is a roof number. Plan the roof as High and treat the attic with the same care.

Step 4, water. 4 hours × 60 minutes = 240 minutes. One cup every 20 minutes: 240 ÷ 20 = 12 cups. One cup every 15 minutes: 240 ÷ 15 = 16 cups. At 4 cups per quart, that is 12 ÷ 4 = 3 quarts to 16 ÷ 4 = 4 quarts. Check against the ceiling: 6 cups an hour × 4 hours = 24 cups, so 12 to 16 cups is well under it.

Step 5, schedule. A cup at each 15 to 20 minute mark, a rest in a cool spot about every hour, and an electrolyte drink in place of some of the water once the job passes two hours. The 15 to 20 minute rate is given for work up to two hours, so for four hours treat 12 to 16 cups as an illustration of the arithmetic, not a prescription. Your employer's plan sets the real schedule.

OSHA's proposed heat rule

OSHA's rulemaking page lists the Heat Injury and Illness Prevention in Outdoor and Indoor Work Settings proposal. It says the notice was published in the Federal Register on August 30, 2024, the comment period closed January 14, 2025, the informal public hearing ran June 16 through July 2, 2025, and a post-hearing comment period ended October 30, 2025. We read no final rule on that page. Check it for current status, and ask your employer what its own heat plan requires now.

Field card: Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs

Water, rest, shade. Heat stroke means call 911.

  • Heat stress adds air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, airflow, workload and clothing.
  • Sun can add up to 15°F (NWS) to the heat index; enclosed spaces raise risk.
  • Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea, cool clammy skin. Cool down, sip water.
  • Heat stroke: confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures. Call 911, cool, no fluids (NWS).
  • NIOSH: 1 cup (8 oz.) every 15 to 20 min for moderate work up to 2 hours; max about 6 cups an hour.
  • New workers: 20 percent of usual heat time on day 1, add 20 percent a day.
  • Example: 90°F and 60% RH is about 100°F heat index, Moderate; 4 hours is 12 to 16 cups, 3 to 4 quarts.
  • OSHA: never leave a heat-ill worker alone.
