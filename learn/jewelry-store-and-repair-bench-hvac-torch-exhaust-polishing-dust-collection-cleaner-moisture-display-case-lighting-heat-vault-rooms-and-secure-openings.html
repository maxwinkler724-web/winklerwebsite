Jewelry Store and Repair Bench HVAC: Torch Exhaust, Polishing Dust, Cleaner Moisture, Vault Rooms and Secure Openings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A jewelry store looks like a quiet retail space, but the repair bench behind the counter is a small workshop: a torch, a buffing motor, cleaning tanks and bright lights, all in a room the owner wants locked tight. This lesson shows what each one asks of the HVAC system and ends with a sizing example of our own.

How to read this page

Check the rules that apply. This page is general education built from federal agency documents read in 2026. Where a number comes from a public source we name it. Where a number is an assumption of ours, we say so. It is not legal advice, and the maker's instructions and the local inspector always win.

The bench is a workshop, not a counter

A federal health survey of a one-person custom jewelry shop is a useful picture. The jeweler melted metal with an oxygen-propane torch in an open-top box with no local exhaust, so the report says the jeweler was exposed to metal fumes during casting. A canopy hood over a stripping bath let chemical vapors escape at the slightest side draft, and the investigators asked for side shields and for a canopy and duct that resist acid vapors and mist. The same shop already had a bench buffer with its own local exhaust and dust collector. The lesson for us is the pattern: capture at the work, not at the ceiling. Our nail salon ventilation lesson makes the same argument for a different trade, and we do not repeat it here.

Torch work: fumes, flux and combustion products

Bench torches burn a fuel gas with oxygen. The combustion products and the metal and flux fumes leave together in the hot plume, so we treat them as one exhaust job. A federal consumer-safety guide for arts and crafts says forging heat can release metal fumes and carbon monoxide, and it advises carbon monoxide alarms. It also says to use lead-free and cadmium-free solder, to avoid fluoride fluxes, and never to mix fluxes, because mixing can form toxic vapors.

The federal welding ventilation rule, 29 CFR 1910.252(c), is written for welding and cutting, not jewelry, but it is the clearest public number set for a torch hood. Movable hoods must hold 100 linear feet per minute in the welding zone at their farthest position. The rule's table gives 150 cfm at 4 to 6 inches from the arc or torch, 275 cfm at 6 to 8, 425 at 8 to 10 and 600 at 10 to 12. It adds that more ventilation may be needed when brazing or cutting cadmium-bearing material, and it asks for local exhaust where cadmium or lead is involved indoors. For torch setup itself, see our brazing torch lesson.

Polishing and buffing dust

OSHA's ventilation rule, 29 CFR 1910.94(b), says that where dry grinding, polishing or buffing exposes workers above the limits in the air contaminants rule, a local exhaust system must be provided and used. Hoods must be placed so the dust is thrown into them, and the rule gives recommended minimum duct velocities of 4,500 feet per minute in a branch and 3,500 in the main. Its buffing table gives a minimum exhaust of 300 cfm for a wheel up to 9 inches in diameter and 2 inches wide. For a wider wheel you multiply by the new width over the listed width. The arts and crafts guide adds a housekeeping point: wet mop or use a HEPA vacuum, and never sweep dust.

For us, the dust goes through a filter, not back into the room. Where the filter sits and who empties it are questions for the owner, because metal sweepings are valuable and the bin is a security item (our own observation).

Cleaners, pickle pots and plating

Ultrasonic and steam cleaners add water vapor to the bench air. We have no sourced figure for how much, so our rule is to treat them as a small moisture source and to put a lid or an exhaust pickup near any heated tank (our own advice). The crafts guide says that when concentrated acids are used for pickling you should use appropriate ventilation or a respirator and have an eyewash available. It advises avoiding cyanide-containing cleaning and plating solutions, and if they are used, ensuring appropriate ventilation and telling the local emergency room.

For plating mist, OSHA's fact sheet on chromium plating says local exhaust is the most effective way to lower exposure, that it must be designed to pull mist away from the breathing zone, and that a qualified person should check its airflow regularly. It says mist rises with higher bath temperature, current and agitation, and that tanks should stay covered when not in use. That fact sheet is about chromium baths, so we use it only as a description of how mist capture works. Any hood and duct over acid baths should be made of acid-resistant material, as the health survey above recommended.

Display lighting heat

A federal energy page says incandescent bulbs release 90 percent of their energy as heat, while LEDs give off minimal heat. Our own note: nearly all electric power used by a light ends up as heat in the space or the case, so connected watts set the load. Display cases that are packed with small spotlights can still make a hot pocket, so check the case before blaming the unit. The retail store lesson covers sales floor lighting and storefront doors, so we stay at the bench here.

Safes, vault rooms and secure openings

A safe or vault room is a security product first. A federal modular vault specification (Amendment 3) says the buyer must state the number and location of conduit openings and vent openings. That tells us the openings are part of the design and are not ours to add. Our own practice: do not cut, drill or enlarge anything on a vault or its panel, keep supply and return outside the vault envelope, and report heat, damp or stale air to the owner and the vault provider. A small closed room has little air, so never work in one with the door shut behind you. Our bank branch lesson has more on vault rooms.

The same thinking applies to the store shell. Exhaust discharge and makeup air both need openings, and each one is a spot someone could try to use. We keep them few, small and as high as the local rules and the intake-to-exhaust spacing allow, and we ask the owner's security provider and the landlord before any roof or wall work (our own advice, no code cited).

Worked example: our own bench

This is our own example with assumed inputs. A small repair bench in a store has a torch that gives off an average of 1,500 W of heat to the room while in use, a bench buffer motor drawing 600 W, 300 W of LED bench and case lighting, and two staff giving off 250 Btu/h of sensible heat each. The torch figure is an assumption. We count all of it as a room load, even though the hood removes some.

  1. Electrical and torch watts: 1,500 + 600 + 300 = 2,400 W.
  2. Convert to Btu/h: one watt is 3.412 Btu/h, from the NIST factor 0.2930711 W per Btu/h. So 2,400 × 3.412 = 8,189 Btu/h.
  3. Add people: 2 × 250 = 500 Btu/h. Sensible load = 8,189 + 500 = 8,689, about 8,700 Btu/h.
  4. Cooling air at a 20 degree F rise (assumed): cfm = 8,689 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 8,689 ÷ 21.6 = about 402 cfm. The sensible heat formula lesson explains the 1.08.
  5. Torch hood: assume a face 18 inches wide and 12 inches high, which is 1.5 ft², and a stated capture velocity of 100 fpm, the same figure as the welding hood rule. Q = 100 × 1.5 = 150 cfm. That matches the rule's 150 cfm at 4 to 6 inches.
  6. Buffing hoods: assume a double-ended buffer with two wheels, each 8 inches in diameter and 2 inches wide. Each needs 300 cfm from the buffing table, so 2 × 300 = 600 cfm with both hoods open.
  7. Exhaust to plan for: 150 + 600 = 750 cfm if the torch and both wheels run together. Our assumption is that blast gates close an idle hood, so the usual draw is lower.
  8. Makeup air cost (assumed 40 degree F winter rise): 1.08 × 750 × 40 = 32,400 Btu/h of heating to replace the exhausted air. That is about four times the 8,700 Btu/h internal load, so the exhaust should run only while the work is being done.

The answer is a starting point for talking to the designer. The hood maker's data and the inspector decide the real numbers.

Field card: Jewelry Store and Repair Bench HVAC

Capture at the bench, replace the air, leave the vault alone.

  • Torch fumes, flux vapors and combustion products go to a hood close to the flame.
  • The federal welding hood table gives 150 cfm at 4 to 6 inches from the torch, at 100 fpm.
  • A buffing wheel up to 9 inches by 2 inches needs 300 cfm and a filter on the exhaust.
  • Cover heated pickle and plating tanks, and use an acid-resistant hood and duct.
  • Cleaners add moisture, and lights add heat equal to their watts.
  • Never alter a vault or its vent, and ask before roof or wall openings.
  • Exhaust needs makeup air, so run it only while the work is done.
