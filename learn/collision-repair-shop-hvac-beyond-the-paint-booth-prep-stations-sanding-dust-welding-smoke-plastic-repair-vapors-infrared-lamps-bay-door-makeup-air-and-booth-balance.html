Collision Repair Shop HVAC Beyond the Paint Booth

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most people picture the paint booth when they think of a collision shop. But the booth is only one fan in a building full of them. Prep stations, sanding, welding, plastic repair and curing lamps all change the air, and every cubic foot that goes out has to be replaced by something. This lesson is about the rest of the shop and how it can upset the booth.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. This is general education built from federal and university pages read in 2026. Local fire and mechanical codes, the equipment maker and the shop's hazard review decide what is allowed. For the booth itself, see Spray Paint Booth HVAC, and for tailpipe capture and unit heater placement see Vehicle Repair and Bus Garage HVAC. We do not repeat those here.

What is in the air besides paint mist

The federal worker safety agency's auto body overview lists "volatile organics from paints, fillers and solvents," diisocyanates and polyisocyanates and hexavalent chromium from spray painting, dust from sanding, and metal fumes from welding and cutting. Each of those sources wants its own kind of air control, and a technician sizing makeup air needs to know which fans are running and when.

Open-face prep stations

A prep station is a ventilated bay, often open on one side, where panels are masked, sanded and primed before paint. It has its own exhaust fan, and that air is usually pulled from the shop. A federal health hazard report on a small collision shop describes a single-bay prep station used for primer, with spraying also done in the open repair area. It found that outside air "needs to be brought into the building, tempered and partially exhausted," and that the shop in winter was fairly airtight, with leakage and open doors as the only relief. Better general ventilation also helped when spraying was done outside the booth. Our own reading for a technician: a prep station exhaust is a real load on the makeup air system, and it must be counted every time it runs.

Sanding dust

A federal worker health institute publication on sanding in auto body shops says dust can carry lead and chromium from old coatings plus abrasive particles. It recommends ventilated sanders with built-in high velocity, low volume exhaust, and says these cut total dust to about one tenth of the level from unventilated sanders during body filler sanding. It also describes a central vacuum with retractable hoses, an air mover, air cleaners and ductwork. The publication gives no airflow figures, so a technician should get the airflow from the equipment maker. The practical HVAC point is that capture at the tool is cheaper than trying to clear dust with room air, and a vacuum system that exhausts outdoors is another fan that needs makeup air. If it returns air to the shop, the filters on it become part of the air quality system and need service.

Welding and plasma cutting smoke at the frame bay

A federal worker safety fact sheet on welding says local exhaust can remove fume from the welder's breathing zone, with hoods, fume extractor guns or vacuum nozzles kept close to the source. Moving fresh air through a space helps too, but the sheet warns that welding in an open space does not guarantee adequate ventilation. Respirators are a backup if work practices and ventilation do not control exposure. It also notes that chromium in stainless steel can form hexavalent chromium fume, and it advises cleaning coatings such as paint and solvent residue off surfaces before welding. For the HVAC side: keep the extractor exhaust pointed away from other workers, and do not let a fume plume drift toward the prep station intake or the booth makeup air intake.

Plastic repair and adhesive vapors

Fillers, adhesives and solvents are on the federal agency's list of volatile organic sources. Our own advice: read the product safety data sheet for the specific material, use the exhaust that sheet calls for, and keep these jobs away from ignition sources and away from the heater inlet. Plastic repair benches are good candidates for a small local hood, not for recirculating shop air.

Infrared curing lamps add heat

An electric curing lamp turns nearly all of its power into heat in the room. The unit conversion is 1 kilowatt = 3,412 Btu/h. That heat is welcome in winter but it is a load in summer, and it can push the shop air toward the lamp zone. Check the nameplate for the lamp's kilowatts and have the cooling or makeup system sized with the lamps included. The lamps and booth heat should be coordinated with the booth's own controls and interlocks, covered in the booth lesson.

Bay doors and makeup air

Every cubic foot per minute of exhaust has to come from somewhere: the makeup air unit, or leaks around the bay doors and walls. An open bay door can wipe out a careful balance in a moment. Our own rule of thumb for the technician: when exhaust fans run, the shop should be slightly negative so odors stay in, but not so much that doors are hard to open, burners backdraft or the booth sucks air from the wrong place. For makeup unit types see Make-Up Air Units.

Keeping shop air from upsetting booth balance

A booth has its own supply and exhaust, and the federal report describes a booth with a separate one-pass exhaust. A booth works well when the air it pulls from the shop is clean, steady and at the right temperature. A big negative shop pressure from prep station, extractor and vacuum fans pulls extra air through door gaps, bringing cold air, dust and fumes toward the booth intake. Keep the booth makeup air tied to the booth fan, size shop makeup for the other fans, and check for doors that slam or hiss when everything runs. Run the whole shop, with all fans on, when you measure pressure.

Worked example (our own): makeup air and tempering load

These numbers are our own assumptions for practice, not a design or a measured shop.

  • Booth exhaust: 8,000 cfm (assumed).
  • Prep station exhaust: 4,000 cfm (assumed).
  • Both run together, and we want the makeup air to match the total exhaust.
  • Cold day outdoor air: 10°F (assumed). We heat the makeup air to 55°F (assumed).

Step 1, airflow. 8,000 + 4,000 = 12,000 cfm of makeup air.

Step 2, temperature rise. 55 − 10 = 45°F.

Step 3, sensible heat. A university lecture note gives the relation Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference. It builds the constant from 60 minutes per hour, 0.075 lb/ft³ air density and 0.24 Btu/lb°F specific heat: 60 × 0.075 × 0.24 = 1.08. So 1.08 × 12,000 = 12,960, and 12,960 × 45 = 583,200 Btu/h.

Step 4, check the other case. With the booth off, the prep station alone is 4,000 cfm: 1.08 × 4,000 × 45 = 194,400 Btu/h. So the heater must turn down to about one third. A makeup unit with modulating controls handles that; a single on and off stage would swing the shop.

What if the makeup air is short? Suppose only 10,000 cfm is supplied. The 2,000 cfm shortfall comes through door gaps and cracks, at 10°F and unfiltered, and the shop goes negative toward the booth. Heating that leaked air is the shop's job: 1.08 × 2,000 × 45 = 97,200 Btu/h. The constant is based on air near sea level and normal room conditions, so a correction applies at high altitude. This example only counts sensible heat of air; it leaves out wall heat loss and the lamp heat from the section above.

Field card: Collision Repair Shop HVAC Beyond the Paint Booth

Count every fan, then replace every cfm.

  • List all exhaust: booth, prep station, extractors, vacuum system.
  • Capture dust and weld fume at the tool, and send it outdoors or through filters that are serviced.
  • Sensible heat: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Our example: 12,000 cfm, 45°F rise, 583,200 Btu/h.
  • Infrared lamps: 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h of heat in the room.
  • Test shop pressure with all fans running and doors in normal use.
  • Follow the product safety data sheets and local codes.
