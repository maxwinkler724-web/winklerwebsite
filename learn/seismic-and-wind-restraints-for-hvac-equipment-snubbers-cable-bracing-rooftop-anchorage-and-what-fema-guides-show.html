Seismic and Wind Restraints for HVAC Equipment: Snubbers, Cable Bracing, Rooftop Anchorage, and What FEMA Guides Show

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fan on soft springs is great at keeping vibration out of a building and poor at staying put in an earthquake. This lesson explains why isolated equipment needs snubbers or restrained isolators, how cable and rigid braces hold up ducts and pipes, how rooftop units are tied to their curbs, and who actually decides all of it.

Check the drawings first

Check the drawings first. This is general education built from federal guides and maker pages read in 2026. Seismic and wind restraint is designed for each project by the engineer of record, using ASCE 7 and the International Building Code as adopted locally. Your job as an installer is to build what the approved drawings, submittals and maker instructions show, not to pick bolts by feel.

Why springs need help in an earthquake

FEMA E-74, the federal guide to nonstructural earthquake damage, describes floor-mounted HVAC equipment on vibration isolators as vulnerable unless it has snubbers, bumpers or vendor restraints. It says open and housed springs do not have enough capacity to resist shear and uplift, and that isolators tend to amplify the shaking the equipment feels. Damage it lists includes sliding, tilting, overturning, broken fuel, electrical and duct connections, and units stopped by misalignment or failed isolators. E-74 shows examples from the 1994 Northridge earthquake, including a failed compressor and rooftop equipment that fell onto its skid.

Two fixes appear in E-74 and in the FEMA 413 installation guide. Use open springs with snubbers or bumpers, or use restrained springs rated for the expected shear and uplift. FEMA 413 puts it bluntly: never use housed isolators for seismic restraint, because they cannot resist uplift, and never use open isolators without snubbers or bumpers. It also says to install only the snubber type specified, since some limit motion in one direction only, and to keep the clearances the maker requires. E-74 adds that if the air gap between the support frame and the restraint is more than 0.25 inch, the seismic design force on the equipment is doubled, and that metal-on-metal contact should be softened with elastomeric surfaces. More than four restrained isolators may be needed on one machine, so check the maker.

This is why a snubber gap is a check item. A gap that is too wide invites impact loads. A gap that is too tight can short-circuit the isolation and bring back the noise. See Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors for Pumps and Fans for deflection and the clearance checks.

Cable and rigid bracing for suspended duct, pipe and equipment

For suspended ductwork, E-74 says the main goal is to keep the system from falling. Unbraced ducts swing and strike other items, and damage concentrates at hard spots such as connections to braced equipment and wall or slab penetrations. The ASHRAE earthquake protection guide from its seismic and wind restraint committee says suspended equipment needs at least four braces, one at each corner, made of rigid steel sections or aircraft-quality cable, usually at about 30 to 60 degrees from horizontal.

FEMA 414 and FEMA 413 give installer-level rules that match. Cables are installed at about a 45 degree slope, with 30 to 60 degrees allowed where something is in the way. For rigid connections the cable is pulled hand-tight, and for vibration-isolated items the guides say to leave about 1/8 inch of slack. Cables brace sideways. FEMA 413 says they should not be used to hang equipment. Do not mix strut and cable systems on one run, and keep the bracing manual that was used for the design on site. Each run needs at least one longitudinal brace, and FEMA 414 says longitudinal spacing is usually a maximum of twice the transverse spacing. Brace spacing and which ducts or pipes need bracing at all come from the design, not from this page. For pipe hangers and insulation saddles, see Supporting Refrigerant and Copper Piping.

Flexible connections at isolated equipment and seismic joints

FEMA 414 states that flexible connections are required when vibration-isolated equipment is attached to rigidly supported piping, and that isolated piping should be kept separate from rigidly braced piping. At a seismic joint between building sections, FEMA 413 says to support conduit so it can move in all directions with the building. For ducts and pipes crossing a joint, FEMA 414 defers to the approved contract documents. The takeaway is simple: a stiff connection to a machine that is allowed to move will be the first thing to fail.

Rooftop units: curb anchorage for seismic and wind

FEMA 413 describes rooftop equipment as held on leveling stanchions, a wood frame, or restrained isolators on stanchions, with the roof structure required to carry the point loads and the support frame bolted or welded to the stanchions. It tells installers to verify structure capacity with the appropriate design professional. One maker of a combined curb and isolation rail for packaged units under 12,000 lb says its spring isolators are rated for seismic and wind loads, includes cable pairs that tie the upper rail to the curb where project codes require them, and offers seismic and wind engineering certification as an option. The same maker lists a restrained spring isolator meant to limit upward travel during high wind loads. Those are maker claims for those products, so use the product data for the actual unit. Curb adapters and lifts are covered in Replacing a Rooftop Unit.

Anchors: the weak link nobody sees

The ASHRAE guide calls anchors one of the most important parts of a restraint system. FEMA 413 says to follow the anchor maker's instructions, locate rebar and post-tension tendons before drilling, avoid oversized holes, and keep anchors away from slab edges and from each other (roughly 1.5 and 2 times embedment depth). It also notes that approved documents may require special inspection to torque or proof-load anchors.

Worked example: Our own, simplified

Our own. This is a teaching sketch. Real design uses ASCE 7 and is done by the engineer of record. The factor below is an assumption for illustration, not a design value, and ignores overturning, uplift and bolt capacity.

  • Assumed: a floor-mounted unit weighs 2,400 lb. Assumed horizontal factor: 0.5. Assumed bolts: 4, sharing shear equally.
  • Horizontal force: F = 0.5 x 2,400 lb = 1,200 lb.
  • Shear per bolt: 1,200 lb / 4 = 300 lb.
  • Now take the E-74 rule that a gap over 0.25 inch doubles the design force: F = 2 x 1,200 lb = 2,400 lb. Shear per bolt: 2,400 lb / 4 = 600 lb.

The same machine, with the same bolts, sees twice the load if the snubber gap is set wide. A designer then compares the load to the anchor rating in the concrete, which is why bolt size, embedment and edge distance are on the drawings.

Design, inspection and New Jersey

USGS says New Jersey's seismic hazard is lower than in many earthquake-prone parts of the country, highest in the northeast of the state, and that some earthquakes have caused damage here. Lower does not mean exempt. E-74 explains that anchorage requirements in the ASCE 7 edition it discusses are tied to seismic design category, with equipment over 400 lb and items over 20 lb mounted more than 4 ft up needing anchorage in categories D, E and F, and that exempt items still need positive attachment and flexible connections. New Jersey's Department of Community Affairs adopted amendments tied to the 2021 International Building Code effective September 6, 2022. A site's category, and any special inspection, come from the building's design. A sample special inspection checklist shows the registered design professional prepares the statement of special inspections and lists items such as designated seismic systems. If the drawings call for it, expect an inspector at your anchors. For permit questions see Does Replacing a Furnace, Boiler or AC Need a Permit in New Jersey? For small split-system pads and brackets, see Heat Pump Outdoor Unit Pads, Stands and Wall Brackets.

Field card: Seismic and Wind Restraints for HVAC Equipment

Springs isolate, snubbers and braces restrain, and the engineer decides.

  • Isolated equipment needs snubbers, bumpers or restrained isolators; never housed springs for seismic restraint.
  • Keep snubber gaps to the maker's setting; E-74 says over 0.25 inch doubles the design force.
  • Cables brace sideways only, about 45 degrees, 1/8 inch slack on isolated items.
  • Use flexible connections where isolated equipment meets rigid pipe or duct.
  • Rooftop units: curb, restraints and roof structure follow the drawings.
  • Locate rebar before drilling anchors; follow anchor maker instructions.
  • Design is by the engineer of record under ASCE 7 and the International Building Code.
