Ceiling Radiation Dampers and Fusible Links: Why a Diffuser Reads Zero

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You hold a flow hood on a diffuser that should be delivering 200 cfm and the display says 3. Before you blame the fan, look up. In a fire-rated ceiling the diffuser may have a small damper above it that has done exactly what it was built to do. This lesson covers what a ceiling radiation damper is, how its fusible link works, and a repeatable way to rule it in or out.

Check the label and the rules that apply

Check the label. The ratings, link temperature and installation details depend on the product and its listing. Read the label and the maker's instructions for the unit in front of you. This is general education, not a substitute for the listing, the code or the facility's procedures. Do not remove, bypass or alter a life-safety device without the people who own it.

What it does in a rated ceiling

Some floor and roof assemblies get part of their fire rating from the ceiling membrane under them. Every hole cut in that membrane for a diffuser or grille is a weak spot. A ceiling radiation damper limits radiant heat passing through such an opening so the assembly still behaves like the tested design. One maker describes its unit as built to limit radiant heat transfer through openings in the ceiling membrane. Maker literature we read describes units for floor/ceiling and roof/ceiling assemblies rated up to 3 hours. The International Building Code and International Mechanical Code are the model codes that set when protection is required, and a fire-code standard for dampers covers their upkeep. The local authority decides what applies in your building.

This is different from the fire damper in a duct wall sleeve. For that device, and for smoke dampers, see Fire Dampers and Smoke Dampers. For the wall and floor holes around pipes and ducts, see Firestopping HVAC Penetrations.

Where techs find them

Look above diffusers and grilles in a lay-in or hard ceiling that is part of a rated assembly. Often the damper is a box or collar between the diffuser and the duct, sometimes with a short flex connection above it. You cannot see it from the room. You find it when you lift a tile, look through an access panel, or read the drawings. Some diffusers are sold as a diffuser and damper pair. One maker sheet we read lists such a unit as classified for use in floor/ceiling or roof/ceiling assemblies with an exposed grid ceiling, so the diffuser, damper and ceiling are meant to be one tested arrangement.

The fusible link

A fusible link is a small heat-sensitive part that holds the blade open. When the air around it reaches the link's rating, the link lets go, a spring or the blade's design closes it, and the opening is covered. Maker literature we read says the link is installed so the blades are held open, and one product ships with its blades closed until the link is fitted. Link temperatures vary. One maker sheet lists 212 F as its standard link and 165 F as an option, so check the label and the sheet for the damper you have. A university fire and life safety paper and a state health department testing guide both say a damaged or painted link is replaced with one of the same size, temperature and load rating. Never fit a link of a different rating because it is what you have in the truck.

Why you cannot swap parts or pull the damper

  • The listing is for a set. The damper, diffuser, ceiling type and connection were tested together. Changing the diffuser style, ceiling or duct type can leave an assembly nobody tested.
  • Flex connections matter. One maker's instructions say flexible duct must not interfere with the blade closing. A flex run that sags into the blade path, or a longer replacement, can pin the blade open or block it later.
  • Access panels count too. A state guide says a wall or ceiling access panel must not reduce the fire resistance rating of the assembly.
  • Removal is not a repair. Pulling a damper to make air move trades a comfort fix for a life-safety hole. Report it and let the building owner and the authority decide.

Inspecting a link without breaking it

  1. Get permission and tell the building contact. If testing involves alarm systems, follow their procedure.
  2. Look first, touch second. With a light and a mirror, check the link is present, not painted over, not corroded and not sagging, and that nothing blocks the blade.
  3. Do not pull on the link, scrape it or heat it. A brush of the wrong tool can release it.
  4. Read the label and note the link rating. Take a photo.
  5. If a formal test is needed, the university and state guides describe removing the link to confirm the blade closes, then reinstalling the link and returning the damper to the open position. That belongs to people trained on that damper. Records should show the location, date, who inspected, and any problem found.

The same guides say initial testing comes one year after installation and then every four years, or six in hospitals. Your local authority sets the actual interval.

Why a closed damper looks like a dead diffuser

From below, nothing is wrong. The face is clean, the ceiling is intact. But the blade above is closed, so air has nowhere to go. A room can get warm or stuffy and the first guess is a bad fan or a closed balancing damper. A closed damper also leaves a clue on its own branch: it should read near zero while neighbors on the same duct read normal.

Worked example (our own): 200 cfm expected, flow hood reads 0 to 10

Assumptions (ours, for practice): a supply diffuser on an 8 inch round neck, design airflow 200 cfm, other diffusers on the same branch reading 180 to 210 cfm, flow hood zeroed and sealed to the ceiling, ceiling in a rated assembly with a damper above.

  1. Size the miss. 10 cfm of 200 cfm is 10 / 200 = 0.05, or 5 percent. A reading that low is a blocked path, not a balancing error.
  2. Check the hood. Recheck zero and seal, and read again. If it still reads low, move on.
  3. Compare neighbors. Neck area for an 8 inch round is 3.14 x (4 / 12)^2 = 0.35 sq ft. At 200 cfm that is 200 / 0.35 = about 570 fpm; at 10 cfm only about 29 fpm. Neighbors on the same branch read normal, so the branch has air. The problem is local to this diffuser.
  4. Look up without removing anything. Remove only the face parts the maker allows, or use the access panel, and shine a light into the neck. A blade across the opening with the link missing or fallen is a closed damper. A blade open with a mashed or kinked flex above is a crushed flex.
  5. Check the flex. Follow the connection from the branch duct to the damper. Look for a kink, a crushed spot under a tile, or a loose connection. This may be easier from above.
  6. Write it up. For a closed damper, record location, damper label and link rating, whether the link was present, and photos. Do not reset or replace anything unless that is your assignment. For a crushed flex, record the spot and the fix. If you see both, note both.

Tools in the cab: flow hood, flashlight, mirror, camera, ladder. See Flex Duct Installation for kinks and Duct Traverse with a Pitot Tube if you need to confirm branch airflow another way.

Field card: Ceiling radiation dampers and fusible links

Near-zero diffuser: look up before you blame the fan.

  • A ceiling radiation damper limits heat through a diffuser opening in a rated ceiling.
  • Link temperature varies. Read the label; replace a link only with the same rating.
  • Do not swap the diffuser, ceiling type or flex, or remove the damper, without the listing in mind.
  • Inspect the link with light and eyes first. Do not pull, paint or heat it.
  • A closed damper reads near zero while neighbors read normal.
  • Write down location, label, link status and photos. Leave the fix to the right people.
