Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare: Air Changes, Pressure Monitors and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In most buildings, pressure between rooms is an accident. In an isolation room it is the product. Air has to move one way through the door, every minute, and a tech may be the person who keeps it that way. This lesson covers the two room types, how exhaust and supply create pressure, the numbers CDC publishes, and what gets checked.

Two room types, two directions

CDC's environmental infection control guideline describes an airborne infection isolation (AII) room as one kept under negative pressure, so air flows from the adjacent space into the room. The goal, in CDC's words, is to keep airborne microorganisms in the room from entering hallways and corridors. A protective environment (PE) room is the opposite. It has positive airflow relative to the corridor, so air moves out of the room, and CDC lists it for severely immune-compromised patients such as certain stem cell transplant patients. Think of one room as a trap for what is inside and the other as a shield for the patient inside.

How pressure is made: offset airflow

Pressure in these rooms is not made by a special fan. It comes from a flow mismatch. If a room is supplied with less air than is exhausted, the missing air has to come from somewhere, and it leaks in under and around the door from the corridor. That is negative pressure. Supply more than exhaust and the extra leaks out, which is positive pressure. A NIOSH walk-through survey report states a practice of exhaust at 10 percent above supply, but not less than 50 cfm. An NIH design article on an isolation suite gives an example of 700 cfm exhaust against 600 cfm supply, which it says provides 100 cfm of inward airflow. See our lesson on airflow and static pressure for why small flow differences make small pressures, and transfer grilles and jump ducts for the same idea in a house, where a closed door can pressurize a bedroom.

The CDC numbers

CDC's air guidance table lists these figures:

  • AII room: pressure greater than -2.5 Pa (0.01 in w.c.), at least 12 air changes per hour (ACH) for renovation or new construction, flow into the room. CDC's key terms page says at least 6 ACH applied to AII rooms built before 2001.
  • PE room: greater than +2.5 Pa (0.01 in w.c.), ideal greater than +8 Pa, more than 12 ACH, flow out of the room, with supply air through a filter rated 99.97 percent at 0.3 micron (HEPA class).

An older CDC/NIOSH survey report used a lower floor of 0.001 in w.c. as its criterion, which shows why you confirm the target your facility actually uses. CDC says self-closing doors are mandatory for both room types. For PE rooms it lists sealed windows and well-sealed rooms. Its web page does not say the same about AII rooms, though the NIH suite article describes sealed walls, penetrations and exhaust duct for its own design. CDC says AII rooms can be built with or without an anteroom. It says HEPA filtering of AII exhaust should not be required if the exhaust is located properly; where air is recirculated, HEPA filtration applies. The NIH suite puts HEPA filters ahead of its exhaust fans. ASHRAE 170 and FGI also set requirements for these rooms; we did not read them here, so check the edition your project follows.

Room pressure monitors and the smoke or tissue test

CDC recommends monitoring airflow direction with manometers or installed visual indicators, and prefers hand-held, calibrated equipment that gives a numerical reading daily. For new PE construction it calls for a permanent visual monitoring device. The NIH suite goes further, with continuous indication and remote alarms at the nurses' station. A cheap, quick backup is a qualitative check: CDC lists smoke tubes, flutter strips, ping-pong balls or tissue held in the air stream. NIOSH used smoke tubes to confirm what a digital micromanometer showed, and one hospital in a NIOSH report did a daily visual smoke or tissue test. The idea is simple. Hold smoke or a tissue at the gap under the closed door. In an AII room it should be pulled inward.

Worked example

All arithmetic below is Our own, using CDC's 12 ACH, the NIOSH survey's 10 percent / 50 cfm practice, and the formula cfm = ACH x volume / 60.

  1. Volume: 12 ft x 14 ft x 9 ft = 1,512 ft³.
  2. Supply for 12 ACH: 12 x 1,512 / 60 = 18,144 / 60 = 302.4 cfm.
  3. Offset: 10 percent of 302.4 = 30.24 cfm. That is below the 50 cfm floor, so 50 cfm governs.
  4. Exhaust: 302.4 + 50 = 352.4 cfm. Check: 352.4 x 60 / 1,512 = 13.98 ACH by exhaust.
  5. If a project instead followed the 100 cfm offset in the NIH example, exhaust would be 302.4 + 100 = 402.4 cfm.
  6. Monitor reading: one inch of water column is about 249 Pa, so 2.5 Pa / 249 = 0.0100 in w.c.
  7. Compare: CDC's figure is greater than 0.01 in w.c. (2.5 Pa) negative. A reading of exactly 2.5 Pa sits right on the line, not clearly past it, so a tech would want a margin and a steady reading, not a borderline one.

What techs check

  • Monitor calibration. A wrong gauge gives false comfort. CDC prefers calibrated instruments. We found no source stating a calibration interval, so follow the maker and the facility's plan.
  • Door closers. Self-closing doors are mandatory per CDC. A NIOSH survey noted that opening the isolation door badly reduced directional control. Check that the door closes fully and latches, and look for gaps.
  • Exhaust fan and path. Confirm the exhaust runs and flow is as designed. A NIOSH report found a television directly below an exhaust grille hindering exhaust flow. Filters, dampers and belts matter too, since less exhaust means less negative pressure.
  • Balancing. Measure supply and exhaust, not just the pressure. The NIOSH survey measured several rooms that missed the 10 percent / 50 cfm practice, so airflow readings catch problems that one gauge reading hides.
  • Recirculating units. A NIOSH report notes portable HEPA units recirculate room air and do not change the room's pressure relationship to the hallway.
  • Documentation. CDC says to monitor and record daily when a room is in use for TB precautions (2005 TB guideline text). Record what you read.

Field card: Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare

Exhaust more than supply pulls air in. Prove it with a gauge and with smoke.

  • AII room: negative, air flows in. PE room: positive, air flows out.
  • CDC table: 2.5 Pa (0.01 in w.c.) either way; 12 ACH or more for new work.
  • cfm = ACH x volume / 60. Exhaust = supply plus offset.
  • NIOSH survey practice: offset of 10 percent, at least 50 cfm.
  • 1 in w.c. is about 249 Pa. 2.5 Pa is about 0.01 in w.c.
  • Check: calibrated monitor, closed and latching door, exhaust fan and grille, measured balance.
  • Smoke tube or tissue at the door gap shows direction.
