Threaded Black Iron Gas Pipe: Thread Sealant, Engagement and Pressure Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most leaks on a new gas line start at a threaded joint: how far the threads went together, what went on them, or a test that was cut short. This lesson explains how tapered pipe threads seal, how many threads sit inside a fitting at common sizes, what the sealant makers say about gas, and how a new line is pressure tested before anyone lights anything.

Licensed work in New Jersey. Cutting, threading, connecting and testing gas piping is work for qualified, licensed people, with the permits and inspections the town requires. A PSE&G piping manual says that under New Jersey law interior house piping is the responsibility of the local building code official, and that a test of new or altered piping is observed by that official. This page teaches you to read the numbers. It is not a how-to for doing the work yourself. If you smell gas, leave and call the utility; see the lesson linked below.

How a tapered thread seals

Gas pipe uses NPT, the American National Standard taper pipe thread, written down in ASME B1.20.1. The thread gets slightly narrower toward the end, so as the male thread is driven into the female thread the two flanks wedge together. That wedge is what makes a metal-to-metal seal. The sealant does not hold pressure by itself. It lubricates the threads so they pull up tight, and it fills small voids at the crest and root.

A Boshart reference page on the standard describes two lengths. The first, called L1, is the hand-tight engagement: how far a clean, perfect male thread goes into a female thread by hand. The second, L3, is the wrench make-up, the extra turning with a wrench. Boshart adds the two for the total engagement of a wrench-tight joint, and notes that a tolerance of about one turn on each part means real joints vary from the chart. An engineering reference reproducing the standard's table says wrench make-up is three threads for 2 inch pipe and smaller.

Engagement by size

A machining-reference site that reproduces the basic dimensions lists these values for the sizes you meet most in house gas piping. Threads engaged is the length divided by the pitch, and pitch is one divided by threads per inch.

These are the standard's ideal numbers for perfect threads. They tell you what a correct joint looks like. They are not a reason to count threads on a real joint and accept it.

Thread sealants listed for gas

The 2021 International Fuel Gas Code, according to an ICC article on the change, now requires a thread sealant on threaded gas joints. It must be non-hardening, resistant to LP gas and the other constituents of the gas, and compatible with the pipe and fitting. PTFE pastes and tapes are the common choices. Older editions did not require a sealant, so check which edition your town enforces.

Pipe dope. RectorSeal's T Plus 2 sheets say it is for natural gas and LP gas in vapor state only, meets ANSI LC 7-2009, is listed with the Canadian Standards Association, and shows a 125 psi maximum for the gas listing. The gas listing covers iron and steel up to 1-1/4 inch, and the sheet says to apply it to the male threads only on pipe through 1-1/4 inch. The sheets did not mention a UL listing.

Yellow PTFE tape. A retailer listing for a Mill-Rose gas tape describes a full-density tape rated for natural gas, propane and butane, made to be wrapped three times, with a UL listing. Never use it on oxygen. The North Carolina State Fire Marshal's office says yellow is recommended for gas and the color itself is not a code rule, white low-density tape is almost always not acceptable for gas, and that some makers prohibit tape because sheared fragments can lodge downstream in regulators and valves.

Whichever you pick, read the label for a gas rating. Neither one repairs a stripped, chipped or corroded thread; the same fire marshal page quotes the code that such pipe shall not be used.

What not to seal

Dormont's connector instructions say never to put pipe thread sealant or PTFE tape on a flare surface, because you will not get a leak-free seal, and to keep flare seats clean of grease, oil and sealant. BrassCraft's instructions say the same in capital letters: do not apply sealant or tape to the flare ends of fittings or the shutoff valve, and sealant goes only on the male pipe threads. A flare seals where two cones meet, so sealant on the seat gets in the way.

Pipe, fittings and what to avoid

The PSE&G manual calls typical house piping black iron and lists approved steel as coated pipe to ASTM A53 Grade B or API 5L Grade A25. Anvil's published catalog data describe its Class 150 black malleable iron fittings as conforming to ASME B16.3, made to ASTM A-197, with ASME B1.20.1 threads and a maximum rated pressure of 300 psi on the open return bend sheet we read. The sizing lesson's table is for Schedule 40 metallic pipe.

Galvanized pipe is not the same answer everywhere. A 2009 Journal of Light Construction column by Mike Casey says the IRC and the National Fuel Gas Code allow both black and galvanized, but some areas, especially in the Northeast, allow only black iron, and that sulfur in gas can attack zinc so flakes clog small openings. He tells readers to ask the local building department. For New Jersey work, use black iron unless your inspector tells you otherwise.

Cutting, reaming and threading

A Ridgid threader manual says to cut the pipe square, ream the end to remove burrs, clamp it in a pipe vise, use plenty of thread cutting oil, and stop when the dies are flush with the pipe end. A loose burr or chip can travel downstream, so the line has to reach the appliance clean.

Worked example: a new line with three sizes

An invented new line in a single-family house: a 1 in. main, a 3/4 in. branch, and a 1/2 in. drop to the range.

Step 1, pitch. 1/2 and 3/4 in. have 14 threads per inch, so pitch = 1 / 14 = 0.0714 in. The 1 in. size has 11.5 per inch, so pitch = 1 / 11.5 = 0.0870 in.

Step 2, hand-tight threads, L1 divided by pitch. 1/2 in.: 0.320 x 14 = 4.48. 3/4 in.: 0.339 x 14 = 4.75. 1 in.: 0.400 x 11.5 = 4.60.

Step 3, add wrench make-up, 3 threads. Check it as L3 over pitch: 1/2 in.: 0.2143 x 14 = 3.00. 1 in.: 0.2609 x 11.5 = 3.00. So the totals are 4.48 + 3 = 7.48 threads for 1/2 in.; 4.75 + 3 = 7.75 for 3/4 in.; 4.60 + 3 = 7.60 for 1 in. Rounded, about 7-1/2, 7-3/4 and 7-1/2 threads.

Step 4, depth in inches. 0.320 + 0.2143 = 0.534 in. for 1/2 in.; 0.339 + 0.2143 = 0.553 in. for 3/4 in.; 0.400 + 0.2609 = 0.661 in. for 1 in.

Pressure test pressure. (Our own note: these figures are a Virginia city's summary of the 2009 IFGC. New Jersey's adopted code edition and your local code official decide the actual test.) Alexandria, Virginia's gas test policy summarizes 2009 IFGC 406.4.1: the test is at least 1-1/2 times the maximum working pressure and never below 3 psig. The house runs at about 0.5 psig, so 1.5 x 0.5 = 0.75 psig. That is under 3, so 3 psig governs.

Gauge. The policy says the gauge scale top must not exceed five times the test pressure: 5 x 3 = 15 psig. A 0 to 15 psi gauge qualifies; a 0 to 30 gauge would not.

Duration. The policy gives 10 minutes minimum for a single-family dwelling or any system under 10 cubic feet, otherwise half an hour for each 500 cubic feet. This is a house, so 10 minutes. The medium is air, nitrogen, carbon dioxide or an inert gas, never oxygen.

Reading the gauge. Start at 3.0 psig. After 5 minutes it reads 2.9. After 10 minutes it reads 2.8. The drop is 3.0 - 2.8 = 0.2 psig, which is 0.2 / 3.0 = 6.7 percent. Section 406.5 as quoted says any reduction shown on the gauge is deemed to indicate a leak, with a narrow exception where another cause readily explains it. Result: fail. The tester brushes leak detection solution on every joint, finds bubbles at one 3/4 in. elbow, repairs it, and repeats the full test. The retest reads 3.0 at the start, 3.0 at 5 minutes and 3.0 at 10 minutes. Drop = 0, result: pass.

Utilities can ask for more. The PSE&G manual lists 15 psi for regulated higher-pressure piping and says all piping is tested to the satisfaction of local code enforcement. Your inspector's number counts.

Field card: Threaded Black Iron Gas Pipe

Clean thread, gas-rated sealant on male threads only, then a held test.

  • NPT is a taper thread: hand-tight L1 plus about 3 threads of wrench make-up.
  • 1/2 and 3/4 in.: 14 threads per inch. 1 and 1-1/4 in.: 11.5.
  • Sealant must be non-hardening and rated for the gas. Yellow PTFE tape is common; read the label.
  • Never put sealant or tape on flare surfaces.
  • Cut square, ream, oil the dies, reject damaged threads.
  • IFGC (as summarized): test at 1.5 times working pressure and at least 3 psig; at least 10 minutes for a house; any gauge drop is a leak.
  • Gas work is for licensed people in New Jersey; the inspector's number governs.
