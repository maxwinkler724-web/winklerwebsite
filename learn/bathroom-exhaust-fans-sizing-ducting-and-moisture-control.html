Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting and Moisture Control

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every shower puts a lot of water vapor into a small room. A bathroom exhaust fan is the cheapest way to get that moisture out of the house, but only if the fan is big enough, quiet enough that people will use it, and ducted to the outdoors. This lesson covers how to size a bathroom exhaust fan and what usually goes wrong.

Why bathrooms need exhaust

The U.S. EPA's guide to mold says the key to mold control is moisture control, and it suggests running the bathroom fan or opening a window when showering. It also suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and venting moisture-producing appliances to the outside where possible. A fan that dumps humid air somewhere else inside the building only moves the problem. The Home Ventilating Institute (HVI), the trade group that publishes fan ratings, lists enclosed toilet rooms as needing an operable window or a fan, and recommends placing the fan away from where makeup air enters so air sweeps through the room.

Sizing: the 1 cfm per square foot rule

HVI's bathroom ventilation page gives a simple rule for small bathrooms, up to 100 square feet: about 1 cfm of fan airflow per square foot of floor area, which it describes as roughly eight air changes per hour. Its example is an 8 ft by 5 ft bathroom, which is 40 square feet and so needs a fan rated at least 40 cfm. The example assumes 8 ft ceilings. If your ceiling is much taller, the room holds more air than the rule assumes, so ask the fan maker or a contractor rather than guessing.

For bathrooms over 100 square feet, HVI switches to counting fixtures: 50 cfm each for a toilet, a shower and a bathtub, and 100 cfm for a jetted tub. Its example is a 20 ft by 12 ft bathroom with a tub, a shower and an enclosed toilet, which totals 150 cfm. You can use one 150 cfm fan or three 50 cfm fans. Notice the limits of the rule: it is a floor-area shortcut with a stated 100 square foot ceiling, it assumes 8 ft ceilings, and it says nothing about duct length, which can cut the real airflow.

Code minimums: ASHRAE 62.2

A Building America Solution Center guide from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) summarizes the code minimums for a mechanical bathroom exhaust: at least 50 cfm if the fan runs only when someone uses the room (intermittent), or at least 20 cfm if it runs continuously. It attributes these to ASHRAE 62.2 and the International Residential Code. The same guide notes that ENERGY STAR advises choosing a fan rated at more than 50 cfm for continuous use and at least 70 cfm for intermittent use, because ducts and the outside cap reduce airflow. For more on the standard, see Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics (ASHRAE 62.2). Editions and local adoption vary, so check what your jurisdiction uses.

Worked example

Using HVI's own 8 ft by 5 ft bathroom:

  1. Area: 8 ft x 5 ft = 40 square feet.
  2. HVI rule: 40 sq ft x 1 cfm per sq ft = 40 cfm.
  3. Code minimum for an intermittent fan: 50 cfm. The code number is higher than the rule, so 50 cfm governs.
  4. Allowing for duct losses, ENERGY STAR (as summarized by PNNL) advises an intermittent fan rated at least 70 cfm. So shop for a fan rated 70 cfm or more.

Now the large bathroom, 20 ft x 12 ft = 240 square feet, over 100. Fixture method: toilet 50 + shower 50 + tub 50 = 150 cfm. A fan rated near 150 cfm, or three 50 cfm fans, matches HVI's example.

Duct to the outdoors, not the attic

PNNL's guide says a bathroom fan should not exhaust into the attic, crawlspace, garage, floor cavity or wall cavity, and that an attic or crawlspace termination is a problem to fix by rerouting the duct outside. Green Building Advisor makes the same point: exhaust air should never be ducted into an attic, because it adds moisture that can cause mold and rot. Note that Green Building Advisor is a trade publication, and the code section numbers appear only in reader comments there, so check the code your inspector enforces. PNNL also says to seal duct joints with mastic or metal tape, to insulate the duct to reduce condensation, and to use a wall or roof cap with a damper. A termination should sit at least 10 ft from a mechanical air intake (IRC M1504.3 as cited by PNNL).

How long to run it, and how loud it can be

HVI recommends running the fan about 20 minutes after bathroom use and suggests a timer. PNNL's guide says to leave it on for an hour for effective humidity control. The sources differ, so a reasonable approach is to run it at least 20 minutes and longer if the mirror is still fogged or the room still feels damp.

Noise matters because a loud fan gets switched off. Fans are rated in sones, and lower is quieter. PNNL reports that ENERGY STAR sets 1 sone or less for continuous fans and 3 sones or less for intermittent fans. As examples from manufacturers, a Panasonic WhisperCeiling listing shows 0.3 to 0.4 sones at 80 to 110 cfm, while a Broan spec sheet for a 70 cfm model lists 4.0 sones. ENERGY STAR, as PNNL describes it, recommends labeled fans for efficiency, low noise and low power draw.

Tech corner: duct length and elbows

Fans are rated at a test pressure. A Broan spec sheet for a 70 cfm fan states its rating is at 0.1 in. static pressure under HVI procedures. A NuTone (Broan-NuTone brand) spec sheet for a 110 cfm model shows airflow falling from 120 cfm at 0.05 in. to 82 cfm at 0.45 in. as pressure rises, and gives performance curves for 10 to 50 ft of 4 in. duct. It says each 90 degree elbow counts as its duct diameter in feet of equivalent length, so a 4 in. elbow is 4 ft. Example: 10 ft of straight 4 in. duct plus two elbows is 10 + 4 + 4 = 18 ft equivalent, so read the manufacturer's curve at 18 ft, not 10. Panasonic's manual excerpt says to use the shortest, straightest routing, avoid ducts smaller than recommended, and notes that fans on existing ducts may not reach rated airflow. Always use the chart for your model. PNNL adds that a 90 degree elbow right at the fan port can significantly reduce airflow, so keep the first 3 ft straight.

Where a fan serves as part of whole-house ventilation, or you also have an energy recovery unit, see Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics and ERV vs. HRV.

Field card: Bathroom Exhaust Fans

Size it, duct it outside, and give it time to work.

  • Small bath (up to 100 sq ft): about 1 cfm per sq ft (HVI), 8 ft ceilings.
  • Large bath: 50 cfm per toilet, shower, tub; 100 cfm jetted tub.
  • Code minimum: 50 cfm intermittent, 20 cfm continuous (ASHRAE 62.2 per PNNL).
  • ENERGY STAR advice: intermittent fans rated 70 cfm or more.
  • Duct to outdoors, never to the attic. Seal and insulate the duct.
  • Run 20 minutes or more after a shower; use a timer.
  • Quiet: 3 sones or less intermittent, 1 sone or less continuous (ENERGY STAR).
  • Elbows count as extra feet; use the maker's chart.
