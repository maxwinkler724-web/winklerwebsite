Dual Fuel Heat Pump With a Gas Furnace: How the Changeover Temperature Is Set

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dual fuel system has two heat sources and one question: at what outdoor temperature should the gas furnace take over from the heat pump? There are two honest answers, one about capacity and one about cost, and the control setting you choose decides which one wins.

What dual fuel means

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a dual-fuel or hybrid system as a heat pump paired with a gas furnace. The heat pump handles most heating in milder weather, the furnace takes over when it gets colder, and the two typically share the same ductwork. In most designs the two sources take turns instead of working together. Trane's installation guide for its XL824 control describes the heat pump running at lower loads and cycling off when the load rises into the auxiliary range, with the furnace cycling on. The same guide shows a "+" on the home screen when compressor heat and indoor heat are called at the same time and says no action is needed, and it has a test mode that runs both. So simultaneous operation exists, but only where the maker's control and equipment allow it. Do not assume it. The rest of this lesson covers the usual alternating setup. For the difference between auxiliary heat and emergency heat, see Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures.

Two balance points, not one

The thermal balance point is the outdoor temperature where the heat pump's output equals the house's heat loss; the lesson on balance point and low-temperature capacity covers it. Below it, the heat pump physically cannot keep up. A Northwest Energy Efficiency Alliance (NEEA) analysis of dual-fuel control says systems were sized to a 30°F thermal balance point in most of its cases.

The economic balance point is a cost question: the outdoor temperature where heating with the heat pump costs the same as heating with gas. Rheem's dual fuel kit guide defines it that way and says that above it the heat pump is cheaper to run. The two points can differ. If gas is cheap and electricity is expensive, the economic point can sit well above the thermal one, and the furnace should take over while the heat pump could still carry the house. If electricity is cheap, the economic point can fall below the thermal one, and then capacity is what forces the furnace on. NEEA's analysis calls the switchover temperature the biggest lever on gas use. Its example shows a single-speed case burning 309 therms at a 40°F switchover, 154 at 35°F and 105 at 30°F, and it recommends a switchover close to, and a few degrees above, the heat pump balance point.

Where the changeover gets set

Every maker words this differently, so read the control's own manual. These are the ones we read.

  • Resideo (Honeywell Home). The FocusPRO installation guide lists a compressor lockout function that serves as the balance point (default no lockout, adjustable from 5°F to 60°F), a separate auxiliary lockout, a "droop" setting for dual fuel control (default 2°F), and an upstage-to-furnace timer (default 1 hour). It says that with fossil fuel backup, the compressor is deactivated before the furnace starts after a 3-minute delay.
  • ecobee. The ecobee4 dual fuel guide has you set Compressor Min Outdoor Temperature (25 to 40°F, 30°F recommended) and Aux Heat Max Outdoor Temperature (30 to 45°F, 40°F recommended, at least 5°F above the compressor setting). It calls disabling aux heat simultaneous operation the recommended setting to keep the heat pump from running at the same time as the gas furnace.
  • Trane. The XL824 guide has Restricted modes using lockouts: a Compressor Lockout (5°F to 70°F, default 30°F) below which only the auxiliary source runs, and an Auxiliary Heat Lockout (default 45°F). It requires an outdoor sensor for lockouts and a 10°F minimum dead band between them. In Non Restricted mode the control picks the source from the load.
  • Carrier Infinity. The Infinity Control installation instructions have a LOCKOUT TEMP setting in the furnace setup menu, for dual fuel only, with a range of NONE or 5°F to 55°F. It sets the outside temperature above which the furnace will not run. The manual does not say what a given number should be.
  • Older kit. Rheem's dual fuel kit guide wires an outdoor thermostat and sets it to the economic balance point from a cost chart; its example comes out at 30°F.

Note that lockouts and switchovers run in opposite directions depending on the maker. A compressor lockout turns the heat pump off below a temperature. A furnace lockout turns the furnace off above one. Same system, different words.

Coil placement and why it matters

The indoor coil of the heat pump sits in the same duct as the furnace. Trane's coil installer's guide says the coil must be installed downstream, in the outlet air, of the furnace. Rheem's kit guide says the same thing for a dual fuel setup and warns that putting the heat pump coil in the return air stream can cause heat exchanger rust, burner malfunction, fire risk and asphyxiation danger. The reason for keeping the two sources separate in operation comes down to what the makers write: the controls above shut the compressor off before the furnace starts, or provide an explicit setting to prevent both running. Unless the equipment maker and control manual allow it, treat heat pump and burner as one-at-a-time.

Computing the economic balance point

Compare the cost of one million Btu (1 MMBtu) delivered to the house. EIA's conversion table gives 1 therm as 100,000 Btu and 1 kilowatthour as 3,412 Btu. So 1 MMBtu is 10 therms, or 1,000,000 ÷ 3,412 = 293.07 kWh. Then:

  • Gas cost per delivered MMBtu = (price per therm × 10) ÷ AFUE
  • Heat pump cost per delivered MMBtu = (price per kWh × 293.07) ÷ COP
  • Break-even COP = electric cost per MMBtu ÷ gas delivered cost per MMBtu

NEEA's analysis uses the same idea, written as break-even COP = (electricity $/MMBtu ÷ fuel $/MMBtu) × combustion efficiency. If you read your own bill in ccf first, see Reading Your Gas Bill. The first comparison, at one COP, is in Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Jersey Home. Here we add the part dual fuel needs: COP falls as it gets colder, so the answer is a temperature.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

These prices and COP values are made up for teaching. They are not NJ rates and not any model's rating. Use your own bill and the maker's capacity and COP table.

Gas: $1.50 per therm, AFUE 95%.
10 × $1.50 = $15.00 per MMBtu of fuel. $15.00 ÷ 0.95 = $15.79 per delivered MMBtu.

Electricity: $0.18 per kWh. 293.07 × $0.18 = $52.75 per MMBtu at COP 1.

Break-even COP: $52.75 ÷ $15.79 = 3.34.

Now assume this heat pump's COP at several outdoor temperatures (hypothetical): 4.0 at 47°F, 3.4 at 35°F, 3.0 at 30°F, 2.8 at 25°F, 2.3 at 17°F, 1.9 at 5°F. Divide $52.75 by each:

  • 47°F: 52.75 ÷ 4.0 = $13.19
  • 35°F: 52.75 ÷ 3.4 = $15.51 (below $15.79, heat pump cheaper)
  • 30°F: 52.75 ÷ 3.0 = $17.58 (above, gas cheaper)
  • 25°F: 52.75 ÷ 2.8 = $18.84
  • 17°F: 52.75 ÷ 2.3 = $22.94
  • 5°F: 52.75 ÷ 1.9 = $27.76

The break-even COP of 3.34 falls between the 35°F value (3.4) and the 30°F value (3.0). Straight-line between them: (3.4 − 3.34) ÷ (3.4 − 3.0) = 0.15 of the 5°F gap, about 0.7°F below 35°F, so the economic balance point is roughly 34°F. If the thermal balance point for this house were 28°F, the economic point is the higher one, so it governs: set the changeover near 34°F, not 28°F. If gas doubled in price, the economic point would drop and the thermal point would take over as the limit. This simple view ignores defrost losses, equipment wear, fan energy, demand charges, and the fixed charges on your bill, so treat the result as a starting point to be refined.

What to do with this on a job

  • Get the maker's capacity and COP table, and the load calculation, before suggesting a number.
  • Find which direction your control's setting works (heat pump off below, or furnace off above).
  • Confirm the coil is downstream of the furnace and that the control keeps the sources from running together unless the maker says otherwise.
  • Write the settings on the equipment so the next tech sees them. A customer who moves the setting changes the bill.

Field card: Dual Fuel Changeover

Thermal balance is capacity. Economic balance is cost. Use the higher temperature.

  • Dual fuel: heat pump plus gas furnace, usually one at a time.
  • Simultaneous running only where the maker's equipment and control allow it.
  • Gas delivered cost per MMBtu = (price per therm × 10) ÷ AFUE.
  • Heat pump cost per MMBtu = (price per kWh × 293.07) ÷ COP.
  • Break-even COP = electric cost ÷ gas delivered cost.
  • COP falls with outdoor temperature, so the break-even is a temperature.
  • Heat pump coil goes downstream of the furnace.
  • Check whether the setting is a compressor lockout or a furnace lockout.
