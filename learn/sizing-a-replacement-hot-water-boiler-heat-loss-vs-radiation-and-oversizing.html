Sizing a Replacement Hot Water Boiler: Heat Loss vs. Radiation and Oversizing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new boiler goes into a house that already has a heating system, so it is tempting to read the old nameplate and order the same size. Boiler makers and trade writers say that is the wrong starting point. This lesson walks through the sizing order for a hot water boiler, with one worked example.

Start with the building, not the old boiler

Weil-McLain's boiler replacement guide warns that sizing "simply by matching the rating of the old boiler" can leave you with a unit that is too big or too small, and it says the building heat loss must be calculated for hot water systems. U.S. Boiler Company says the same thing more bluntly in a hot water sizing article: the proper way is a heat loss calculation, and copying the old boiler, counting radiation, using water volume or using Btu per square foot all lead to oversizing. Their reasoning is that old boilers were often sized with a safety margin, then the house got new windows, insulation and air sealing, so the load fell.

For the heat loss you can use a Manual J calculation (see Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J) or a whole-house envelope calculation. On a replacement, U.S. Boiler says a room-by-room calculation is not needed when the radiation stays. A fuel-use estimate from real winter bills is a useful cross-check, not a substitute. A 2001 ACHR News article adds a field habit: clock the gas meter to learn what the old boiler actually fires, then compare it with the calculated loss, the connected radiation and what the homeowner says about comfort.

Then check the radiation

The boiler can only deliver what the baseboard or radiators can release. For hot water, add up the installed emitters at the water temperature you plan to run. Manufacturers publish Btu/hr per foot at stated water temperatures. Slant/Fin's Fine/Line 30 ratings chart for its 3/4 inch copper element lists 580 Btu/hr per foot at 180°F and 1 gpm, and 510 at 170°F. Its notes say the ratings assume 65°F entering air, are based on finned length (5 to 6 inches shorter than overall length) and already include a 15% heating effect factor. Our heat emitters lesson covers why output falls as the water cools. Steam is different: a steam boiler is matched to radiator EDR, covered in Steam Radiator EDR.

Compare the two numbers. If the radiation at your design water temperature is well above the loss, the house can be heated with cooler water, which suits a condensing boiler (see Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing). If it is below the loss, rooms will run cold on the coldest days no matter what boiler you buy.

Net, gross and the piping and pickup allowance

Makers list several numbers. Input is the fuel burned. Gross output (many makers call it DOE heating capacity) is what the boiler delivers to the water. AHRI's boiler standard (AHRI 1500) defines a net rating as the installed radiation a boiler is recommended to serve, found by dividing gross output by a piping and pickup factor and rounding to the nearest 1,000 Btu/hr. The factor for hot water is 1.15. Navien's data sheet and Lochinvar's Knight submittal both state that their net AHRI ratings assume a 1.15 allowance, and both tell you to check with the maker for unusual piping. A HeatingHelp trade article explains the allowance as the heat to warm the pipes and the water already in the system, and notes that 1.15 became the hot water figure in 1967.

The makers do not agree on which column to size from. Weil-McLain's guide says to pick the boiler by its AHRI net rating, and that the rating can be more than the calculated loss "but never less." U.S. Boiler's article says to use the DOE output instead, not the net. Read the instructions for the exact boiler you are installing, and call the maker when the system has large old iron radiators or long uninsulated mains, where the standard allowance may not cover the load.

Worked example

This house is invented. A Manual J style calculation gives a design heat loss of 46,000 Btu/hr. The house has 90 ft of fin-tube baseboard (finned length) on one loop. The old boiler's nameplate reads 175,000 Btu/hr input at about 80% efficiency.

  1. Radiation at 180°F. Using the Slant/Fin 580 Btu/hr per foot rating: 90 ft × 580 = 52,200 Btu/hr.
  2. Compare with the loss. 52,200 ÷ 46,000 = 1.13. The baseboard can release about 13% more than the house loses at 180°F water, so the emitters are adequate.
  3. How cool could the water run? 46,000 ÷ 90 ft = 511 Btu/hr per foot needed. The chart shows 510 at 170°F, so roughly 170°F average water would just cover the design day, with no margin.
  4. Pick by net rating. From the Weil-McLain full-line catalog (WM1911) table, the smallest hot water net AHRI rating at or above 46,000 is the Evergreen EVG 70 at 57,000 Btu/hr (the next, Ultra 80, is 62,000). Check the allowance: 46,000 × 1.15 = 52,900 gross needed, and the EVG 70's DOE capacity is 65,000, which is above it.
  5. Net against loss. 57,000 ÷ 46,000 = 1.24, so the pick is about 24% above the load.
  6. Old boiler against loss. Old output = 175,000 × 0.80 = 140,000 Btu/hr. 140,000 ÷ 46,000 = 3.0, so the old unit was about three times the load. By input, 175,000 ÷ 46,000 = 3.8.
  7. Short-cycle picture. On a mild day suppose the house needs 40% of design: 0.40 × 46,000 = 18,400 Btu/hr. The old boiler could cover that in 18,400 ÷ 140,000 = 13% of the time, about two minutes of firing in a 15 minute window, then it would shut off. The EVG 70's catalog turndown is 5:1, so its minimum is about 70,000 ÷ 5 = 14,000 Btu/hr (our own division from the catalog turndown). That is below 18,400, so it can modulate and keep running.

What oversizing costs

U.S. Boiler lists short cycling, more fuel use, stress on controls and fan, and higher standby losses, and says efficient operation "is all about run times." Its own example had a 125,000 Btu/hr boiler replaced with a 150,000 Btu/hr unit chosen without a heat loss, which averaged about three minutes per run; a later heat loss found 70,000 Btu/hr. The U.S. Department of Energy's steam tip sheet on short cycling explains the mechanism: fixed losses such as enclosure radiation are a larger share of fuel at low load (it cites 1% of input at full load, 2% at half and 4% at one quarter) and purge cycles pull heat out of the boiler. That sheet is written for large industrial steam boilers, so treat the percentages as an illustration of the idea, not a number for a house. The Building America Solution Center adds that if the design load is at or below a boiler's lowest output, consider equipment that better matches the load. For the fixes, see Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling.

Turndown on modulating boilers

Turndown is maximum input divided by minimum input. Weil-McLain's Ultra boilers list 5:1; Viessmann's Vitodens 100-W page states 10:1 for the line (the B1HE-85 runs 8,500 to 85,000 Btu/hr); Lochinvar's Knight KBX submittal lists 10:1. Higher turndown helps, but it does not rescue a boiler that is three times too big. Check the minimum input against your smallest zone, not only the whole house.

Indirect water heater priority

If the boiler also feeds an indirect water heater, check that load too. Weil-McLain's Aqua Plus manual says that with domestic priority wired, a call for domestic water switches the boiler from space heating to the water heater only, for the fastest recovery, and that its published ratings assume 200°F boiler water at the listed flow. Because heating is paused during the domestic call, the two loads are checked separately rather than simply added. Compare the boiler's output to the heater's rating at your actual boiler temperature. See Indirect Water Heaters on a Boiler.

Sizing order, in short

Heat loss first. Emitter output at the design water temperature second. Then the boiler's net (or the maker's chosen) rating, the turndown floor, and the indirect heater. The old nameplate is only a clue about what the house used to be. New to the trade? Begin at Start Here.

Field card: Sizing a Replacement Hot Water Boiler: Heat Loss vs. Radiation and Oversizing

Size from the house, check the emitters, then read the rating column.

  • Heat loss first. Do not copy the old boiler's nameplate.
  • Baseboard output = feet × maker's Btu/hr per foot at your water temperature.
  • Net rating = gross output ÷ piping and pickup factor (1.15 for hot water, per AHRI 1500 and maker sheets).
  • Makers differ on net or DOE; follow the one for your boiler.
  • Oversizing: short cycling, standby loss, wear.
  • Turndown = max input ÷ min input; compare minimum fire to the smallest zone.
  • Indirect heater: check its load and priority separately.
