Work Gloves for HVAC Techs: Cut Levels, Refrigerant Frostbite, Chemical Gloves and When Gloves Are Dangerous

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Gloves are the most used and the most misused PPE on an HVAC van. The same pair that saves your palm on a duct edge can drag your hand into a drill chuck. This lesson is about matching the glove to the hazard, and knowing when the right glove is no glove.

Check your own rules first

Your employer's written hazard assessment and the glove maker's label come first. This page is general education built from government and university pages we read in 2026. It is not a substitute for your site rules or training.

What OSHA requires: pick by hazard

Under 29 CFR 1910.138(a), the employer must select and require suitable hand protection when hands are exposed to hazards such as skin absorption of harmful substances, cuts, abrasions, punctures, chemical or thermal burns, and extreme temperatures. Paragraph (b) says the selection is based on how the glove performs against the task, the conditions, how long it is worn, and the hazards present or likely. The general PPE rule, 1910.132(d), requires a hazard assessment, and 1910.132(f) requires training on when to use the PPE, how to put it on and take it off, and what its limits are. In plain words: there is no all-purpose glove, and the limits are part of what you are trained on.

Cut-resistant gloves and sheet metal

Cut-resistant gloves are rated under the ANSI/ISEA 105 standard (we name it in plain text and do not link a copy). A university safety office explains that the test measures the gram force of a cutting load a glove can take before it is penetrated, and another says the higher the number, the more force it takes to cut through. A national lab's glove page lists nine cut levels, A1 through A9, and gives "glass or metal sheet handling, automotive assembly, HVAC" as an A5 example use. We could not find a government or university page that gives the gram range for each of the nine levels, so we do not print gram numbers here. Read them from the glove's own packaging and the standard.

Two practical points. First, a cut level says nothing about heat, cold or chemicals. Second, the same university page that recommends cut-resistant gloves for sharp objects also says to wear them over chemical gloves when both hazards are present. Fresh-cut duct edges, drive cleats and sheet-metal screws are the classic HVAC cut hazards. We do not have an injury statistic for them, so we make no claim about how often they happen.

Cold gloves for liquid refrigerant

The NIOSH Pocket Guide entry for a common refrigerant gas lists the exposure route as skin or eye contact with the liquid and the symptom as "liquid: frostbite." NIOSH's skin-protection codes also say that for compressed gases and extremely cold liquids, protective clothing must keep skin from freezing. A national lab's glove page notes that gloves for very cold liquids exist and are sold as cryogen gloves. In practice, choose gloves rated for the cold contact you expect, and do not assume a thin cloth or ordinary work glove is enough. Pair them with eye protection; see Refrigerant Safety Basics for the full protective gear list and how to handle a hose or cylinder. See Cold Stress for HVAC Techs for weather frostbite, which is a different problem from a splash.

Chemical gloves for coil cleaners and acids

No glove protects against every chemical. One university EHS office says to check the SDS for instructions, another says most SDSs name the most protective glove material in their protective-equipment section, and that the glove maker's chemical compatibility chart should also be checked. A university glove table lists nitrile as suitable for hydrochloric acid and phosphoric acid, and another university rates nitrile as a good all-round balance of chemical and physical resistance that performs poorly against many ketones and some solvents. The lesson: nitrile is a common starting point, not a rule. Coil cleaners differ in what is in the bottle, so read the SDS for the exact product, and treat thin disposable gloves as short-contact protection only unless the maker's chart says otherwise. Our own rule of thumb, not from a source: if the SDS names a different material than the pair on your belt, stop and get the right pair.

Heat gloves for brazing

OSHA's welding rule, 29 CFR 1910.252(b)(3), says people exposed to welding, cutting or brazing hazards need PPE under 1910.132, with the clothing depending on the size, nature and location of the work. A national lab's glove page describes leather welding gloves as protecting against sparks and molten metal splashes, and lists other heat materials with their own temperature limits. That page's limits are stated in the lab's own terms, so check your glove's label. See Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines for the technique and fire safety.

When gloves are dangerous: rotating parts

OSHA's woodworking eTool warns that loose clothing can be easily caught in rotating parts and that stock projecting from a lathe chuck can catch hair or clothing and draw the operator in. A university safety page says directly not to wear gloves that could catch in a machine, and to keep hands away from moving parts. California's hand protection rule, 8 CCR 3384, says gloves shall not be worn where they could become entangled in moving machinery. That is a California rule, not a federal one, but it shows the idea in regulation. The federal text we read, 1910.138, does not itself address entanglement. Our own plain-language takeaway: on a drill with a chuck, a belt drive, a fan blade or a bandsaw, take the gloves off, keep sleeves snug and keep hands clear. The cut you risk by bare hands is smaller than the injury from a glove being drawn in.

Electrical gloves, briefly

Rubber insulating gloves are covered by 29 CFR 1910.137, not 1910.138. That rule sorts them by class (00 through 4), calls for an inspection and air test before each day's use, and a retest every six months. Do not substitute work gloves for them, and do not assume cut gloves protect from shock. The full picture is in Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs and Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead.

Worked example (ours): a glove plan for one day

This is our own plan, built from the sources above. Assumptions: the job is a rooftop unit swap with a short duct modification, the technician has the SDS for the acid cleaner on hand, and the employer's hazard assessment agrees. Nothing here is a numbered requirement; it is a way to organize the day.

StepGloveWhen to take them off
1. Cut and fit ductCut-resistant, level chosen from the label for sheet-metal edgesBefore you pick up the drill or a power tool with a spinning bit
2. Recover refrigerantCold-rated gloves with eye protectionAfter the hoses are disconnected; if liquid gets on a glove, do not keep it on a cold-soaked hand
3. Clean the coil with acid cleanerThe material the SDS names (cut gloves are not a substitute)When the product is rinsed and put away, following the label and SDS
4. Drill panel or mounting holesNoneGloves are off before the bit turns; hold the work with clamps

The pattern is that the glove changes four times in four tasks, and one task has no glove at all. If you only own one pair, you will be tempted to use it everywhere, which is the exact mistake the OSHA selection rule is meant to stop.

Field card: Work gloves for HVAC techs

Match the glove to the hazard, and take it off near spinning parts.

  • OSHA 1910.138: the employer selects hand protection by the hazard.
  • Cut levels run A1 to A9 under ANSI/ISEA 105; read the label.
  • Liquid refrigerant can cause frostbite; wear cold-rated gloves and eye protection.
  • Coil cleaners and acids: pick the glove from the SDS.
  • Brazing: heat-resistant gloves, plus fire watch.
  • Drills, belts, fans, saws: no gloves near rotating parts.
  • Rubber insulating gloves for electrical work fall under 1910.137.
