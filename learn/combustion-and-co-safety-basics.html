Combustion and CO Safety Basics

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Burning fuel needs air, needs somewhere for the exhaust to go, and makes carbon monoxide when something is off. This is the safety lesson behind every gas appliance.

What combustion needs

Fuel burns well when it has enough air and a path for the exhaust to leave. In a furnace or boiler, that means three things working together: fuel, combustion air, and a clear vent. When air is short, the flame can burn poorly and form carbon monoxide, which is a toxic gas. When the vent is blocked, exhaust can spill back into the building.

Combustion air and venting

  • Where air comes from. Some equipment draws air from the room around it. Sealed or condensing units commonly bring in outside air through their own pipe, usually alongside the exhaust pipe.
  • Tight buildings. Houses that are well sealed, or have strong exhaust fans, can struggle to supply air to an appliance that uses room air.
  • Vent types. Metal flue pipes rise on natural draft, while fan-assisted and condensing equipment use a fan and often plastic pipe. The material and size depend on the appliance and the rules where you work.
  • Blockages. Birds' nests, snow, leaves, rusted or loose pipe, and collapsed liners can all stop exhaust from leaving.
  • Shared spaces. Changing one appliance, such as replacing a water heater or furnace, can change how the others vent.

Carbon monoxide basics

The CDC describes carbon monoxide as an odorless, colorless gas that can build up indoors, and lists gas and oil furnaces, water heaters, stoves, generators and fireplaces among the sources. Symptoms such as headache, dizziness, weakness and nausea are easy to mistake for the flu, and people who are asleep may not notice anything. Infants, older adults and people with heart or breathing problems are at higher risk.

Why alarms and testing matter

  • You cannot smell it. An alarm is often the only warning.
  • Placement. The CDC advises detectors near every sleeping area, and the maker's instructions say exactly where.
  • Batteries and age. Test regularly, and replace alarms on the schedule the maker gives. The CDC gives roughly five years as a typical guide.
  • Annual service. A qualified tech should check fuel-burning appliances and venting each year.
  • Testing on the job. Techs commonly use a combustion analyzer to measure CO and other readings in the exhaust. What the numbers should be, and the action limits, come from the manufacturer and local rules, not from this page.

If an alarm sounds

Treat it as real. Get everyone out into fresh air, call emergency services, and do not go back inside until responders say it is safe. Anyone with symptoms should get medical help. A tech should never silence an alarm and carry on without finding the cause. Look for blocked vents, cracked heat exchangers, and equipment not getting enough air, and shut the equipment down if it is unsafe.

Safe habits

Never run a generator or grill indoors or in an attached garage, never use a gas oven for heat, and never remove or disable a safety switch. Keep a carbon monoxide alarm working wherever fuel is burned.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, timings, wiring steps or test values, and those vary by manufacturer, model and local rules.

Field card: Combustion and CO Safety Basics

Fuel, air, vent. Lose one and CO can follow.

  • Combustion needs fuel, air and a clear vent path.
  • CO is odorless and colorless, and the flu-like symptoms are easy to miss.
  • Put working CO alarms near sleeping areas, test them and replace them on schedule.
  • Annual service by a qualified tech covers equipment and venting.
  • If an alarm sounds: get out, call for help, find the cause, never just silence it.
