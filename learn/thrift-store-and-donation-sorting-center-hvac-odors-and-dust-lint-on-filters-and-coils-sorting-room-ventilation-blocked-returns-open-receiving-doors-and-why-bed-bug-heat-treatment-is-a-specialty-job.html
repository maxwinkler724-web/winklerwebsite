Thrift Store and Donation Sorting Center HVAC: Odors, Lint, Crowded Sorting Rooms and Blocked Returns

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A thrift store looks like a normal shop on the front side and a small warehouse on the back side. Bags and boxes arrive all day, a crew opens them and sorts what is inside, and the building has to stay comfortable, reasonably clean and safe the whole time. This lesson covers what that does to the HVAC system and the simple fixes a tech can suggest. For the sales floor itself, see the retail lesson linked at the end.

What donated goods bring into the air

Donated clothing, bedding, books and household items carry whatever they sat in before they arrived: attic or basement mustiness, smoke, perfume, pet hair and dust. Opening a bag releases a burst of that into the sorting room. The EPA indoor air page says indoor pollutant levels can climb when a building is not bringing in enough outdoor air to dilute emissions from indoor sources. A sorting room is a clear case of that: the source is large, it is released right at the work tables, and the people are standing in it.

Textiles also shed. Every bag that is shaken out puts fibers and lint into the air, and that lint goes where the air goes. In our experience it shows up in three places: on the filters, on the face of the evaporator coil, and around the return grille. A filter that loads with lint fast does not fail dramatically. Airflow just drops a little each week until the room is stuffy and the coil starts icing or the unit trips on a safety.

Sorting rooms have more people than the building was sized for

A back room that was drawn as storage may now hold a dozen workers, steady lifting, and open boxes. People add heat and moisture, and they are the reason the room needs outdoor air. Workers who lift and carry also feel warm sooner than someone at a register, so the sorting room often draws comfort complaints even when the thermostat reads normal. Check what the room is really used for before judging the equipment. If the room is served by a unit that was set up as a storage space, its outdoor air damper may be closed or sized for almost no people.

Receiving doors left open

Trucks and donors come and go, so the receiving door is often propped open. That does three things: it pulls unconditioned air through the room and into the sales floor, it lets dust and exhaust in, and it fights the economizer and the exhaust fans because the building pressure keeps changing. A closing door on a spring or a simple air curtain helps, but only if staff actually use the door in the way it was set up. See the air curtain lesson linked below for how those work. A small vestibule, or a rule that the door opens only while a truck is being unloaded, usually does more than any equipment change.

Stacks that block the returns

Donated goods get stacked wherever there is room. Pallets, rolling racks and bales of unsorted bags end up against walls, and walls are where return grilles live. A blocked return starves the air handler. The supply fan then moves less air, the coil runs colder, the filter and coil load faster, and the sorting room goes stuffy at the exact time of day when it is fullest. The fix is a floor marking, not a repair: tape a clear zone in front of every return and every wall-mounted unit and train staff to keep it open. Our own practice is to ask for a clear zone at least as wide as the grille and about 12 inches deep, and to keep stock off the tops of supply diffusers too.

Pests and why heat treatment is a specialty job

Used bedding and upholstered goods can carry bed bugs. This lesson is about HVAC, so we only cover what an HVAC tech should and should not do. The EPA do-it-yourself bed bug page says that do-it-yourself heat treatments might not work, that you should not try to raise a home's temperature with a thermostat, space heater or fireplace because it does not work and is dangerous, and that professionals have access to more intensive methods, including whole-house heat treatment.

The Purdue University Extension bed bug page describes professional heat treatment as heating rooms or buildings to at least 45 °C, with the best results at 49 to 52 °C (120 to 125 °F) for 20 to 30 minutes, and says the effect is immediate but not lasting, so bed bugs can return once temperatures fall. It also notes that distorted structures and contents have been reported and advises using a professional trained in heat treatments. For ordinary items, it says washing in hot water and heat-drying on high for at least 20 minutes kills all stages. The Rutgers fact sheet says a building-wide problem will most likely need a pest management professional, and that small non-washable items can be treated in a heat chamber.

What that means for us: do not drive the sorting room up with the heating system or a borrowed heater, and do not promise a customer that a rooftop unit can "cook out" bed bugs. Overheating a building risks damaged contents, scorched stock and a safety problem, and it can miss the pests anyway. If the store suspects an infestation, the job goes to a pest management professional. Our part is to make sure the HVAC does not move pests or dust around: keep the sorting room slightly negative to the sales floor, as described below, and keep return air out of storage and bagging areas.

Simple ventilation fixes

  • Give the sorting room its own exhaust at the tables, with the intake low and near the work, and make-up air from outdoors through the unit.
  • Keep the room slightly negative to the sales floor by exhausting a little more than the outdoor air supply, so odors do not drift to customers.
  • Do not return sorting room air to other spaces. Send it to the exhaust or back to its own unit.
  • Use the best filter the unit can handle without losing airflow, and add a lint-catching coarse pre-filter ahead of it if there is room. See the filter rack lesson for gasket and bypass checks.
  • Use a pressure gauge across the filter so staff change filters by reading, not guessing.
  • Clean the coil on a schedule. Lint mats on the coil face, and a coil that is dirty on the face loses airflow before it shows any other symptom.

Our own worked example: a sorting room

This is our own example. All inputs are our assumptions, not code requirements and not data from any real store.

Assumptions: a sorting room of 2,400 square feet with a 10 foot ceiling and 12 workers at the tables. We plan on 20 cfm of outdoor air per worker plus 0.15 cfm per square foot for the odor and dust load. Both rates are our own planning numbers. Check what your jurisdiction's ventilation rules require, because the required rate can be different. We also assume the unit supplies 3,600 cfm to the room, and the room exhaust is 700 cfm.

  1. People part: 12 workers × 20 cfm = 240 cfm.
  2. Area part: 2,400 sq ft × 0.15 cfm per sq ft = 360 cfm.
  3. Outdoor air: 240 + 360 = 600 cfm.
  4. Share of the supply: 600 ÷ 3,600 = about 17 percent outdoor air.
  5. Room volume: 2,400 × 10 = 24,000 cubic feet. Outdoor air changes: 600 × 60 ÷ 24,000 = 1.5 per hour.
  6. Pressure: exhaust 700 cfm minus outdoor air 600 cfm leaves 100 cfm to be drawn in from the sales floor side. The room stays slightly negative.
  7. Return air: the unit takes in 600 cfm outdoor air, so return = 3,600 − 600 = 3,000 cfm. Check the balance: supply 3,600 plus transfer 100 equals return 3,000 plus exhaust 700.

Filter intervals, our own practice for this room: look at the filters every 2 weeks, change them about every 4 to 6 weeks, and change sooner if the gauge reads well above its clean-filter reading. Look at the coil face and clean it every 3 months. These intervals are our own starting points. Adjust them to what you actually find when you open the unit, because a room that sorts heavy bedding will load faster than one that sorts hard goods.

Field card: Thrift Store and Donation Sorting Center HVAC

Sorting room: dilute, exhaust, keep it negative, keep returns clear.

  • Opened donation bags release odor, dust and textile lint right at the tables.
  • Lint loads filters and coil faces fast; inspect often and read a filter gauge.
  • Keep a marked clear zone in front of every return grille.
  • Keep the sorting room slightly negative to the sales floor and do not return its air elsewhere.
  • Open receiving doors upset building pressure; use closers, vestibules or air curtains.
  • Bed bug heat treatment is a specialty pest job. Do not overheat a building with the HVAC.
  • Our own example: 12 workers, 2,400 sq ft gives 600 cfm outdoor air.
