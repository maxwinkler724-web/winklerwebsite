Proof of Flow on Chillers and Boilers: Paddle, Differential Pressure and Thermal Dispersion Switches

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chiller or boiler that runs without water moving through it can wreck itself. So the controls make the machine prove flow first. This lesson covers the main proving devices, how makers say to install and set them, and one worked example for choosing a differential pressure setpoint.

Why flow has to be proven

Daikin Applied's scroll chiller manual says a flow switch must be mounted in the leaving evaporator and condenser water lines to prove adequate flow, and it lists an evaporator freeze alarm that rapidly stops all circuits. A flow loss alarm in the same manual trips when the pump is commanded, no flow is seen for longer than a short proof timer and a compressor is running. Daikin also says the flow switch is an equipment protection control and should never be used to cycle a unit. The reasoning behind freezing is covered in Chiller Start-Up and Shutdown: Sequence and Safeties.

Boilers have the same problem in reverse: no flow means heat has nowhere to go. A Laars manual for a hydronic boiler says the boiler must have continuous flow through the heat exchanger when firing, that reduced flow causes a high temperature rise across the boiler, and that low flow can make safety switches shut it down. Low-water protection is a separate device; see Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance.

The device types

Paddle (vane) flow switches

A paddle sits in the pipe and is pushed by the moving water until it closes a switch. The Dwyer FS-2 literature says to trim only the layers that are too long for your pipe size, leave the shorter layers to reinforce the vane, and be sure the vane has full range of motion without touching the pipe wall, bushing or tee. A McDonnell & Miller FS250 sheet adds that the paddle should be inspected yearly and the switch replaced after 5 years or 100,000 cycles, whichever comes first. Both are nuisance sources if the paddle is bent, broken or dragging.

Differential pressure (DP) switches

A DP switch compares pressure at two taps, such as across the evaporator, and closes when the difference is high enough. A trade column by Steven Calabrese called pressure sensing the older method that truly confirms flow, with the drawback that it costs more and is harder to install and set up. Makers differ on whether to use them. Trane's support article on its RTWD and RTHD chillers allows a flow switch or a DP switch. Daikin's scroll chiller manual says DP switches are not recommended because they can freeze and fail to indicate no flow, and says to protect any flow switch installed outdoors from freezing and condensation. Daikin's centrifugal manual lists flow switches, thermal dispersion switches or Delta-P switches as acceptable. The lesson: use what your unit's manual names.

Thermal dispersion switches

These sense flow by how fast a heated element loses heat to the water. FCI's chiller literature calls the design all-welded with no moving parts, so nothing to break or clog, and notes it can be field-configured to a chosen setpoint. That is the maker describing its own product. Daikin lists a factory-mounted thermal dispersion switch as an option on its scroll chillers, and a Pacific Northwest National Laboratory report for the U.S. General Services Administration describes replacing existing evaporator and condenser safety flow switches with ones more sensitive to low flow when a chiller plant was converted to variable speed.

Current switches on pump motors

A current switch tells you the motor is drawing amps. Calabrese notes that early manufacturers marketed these as proving flow, but you were only proving the motor was operating, and that most consulting engineers now accept them anyway. Duke University's facilities standard for building automation says to use differential pressure sensors for pump and critical fan status and current transducers only for non-critical pump and fan status. Treat a current switch as weak proof: a slipped coupling or a closed valve can leave the motor running with little or no water moving. That example is our own reasoning, not a quote.

Installation rules, as makers state them

  • Trane says to mount the switch upright, with at least 5 pipe diameters of straight horizontal run on each side, away from elbows, orifices and valves, with the arrow pointing with the flow, and to remove air so the switch does not flutter.
  • McDonnell & Miller's FS250 sheet agrees on 5 diameters, notes that high velocity or thick fluids may need more, and says not to use its liquid flow switches above 10 ft/s.
  • Daikin's scroll manual puts the switch in the horizontal evaporator outlet piping.
  • Dwyer says to mount vertically on horizontal pipe runs with the arrow in the direction of flow.

Straight-run, tee and paddle tables vary by model, so use the table in your switch's own sheet.

Setpoint, delay and nuisance trips

Daikin's scroll manual says the switch should be calibrated to shut off the unit when flow falls below the minimum listed flow rate. Trane says to adjust the switch to open when flow falls below nominal, using the minimum flow in the chiller's data tables. Trane also notes the controller applies a 6 second delay before a loss of flow shutdown, and Daikin's default flow proof time is 5 seconds with a 3 minute recirculation timeout. McDonnell & Miller says not to lower the setpoint below the factory minimum because that can cause erratic operation. If the shutdowns keep happening, Trane says to contact a qualified service organization.

Worked example: choosing a DP switch setpoint

Our own arithmetic, using the square law that pressure drop scales with the square of flow. The design numbers are given; the 40 percent minimum flow is an example only, since no maker minimum applies to this made-up chiller. Always take the real minimum from the unit's data.

  1. Design: 600 gpm, 12 ft of water drop. Our own: 12 / 2.31 = 5.19 psi.
  2. Minimum flow (example 40 percent): 0.40 x 600 = 240 gpm.
  3. Drop at 240 gpm: 12 x (0.40)² = 12 x 0.16 = 1.92 ft. Our own: 1.92 / 2.31 = 0.83 psi.
  4. Lowest flow we expect in normal operation (example 50 percent, 300 gpm): 12 x 0.25 = 3.00 ft = 3.00 / 2.31 = 1.30 psi.
  5. The trip point has to sit between 0.83 psi (below this we are under minimum flow) and 1.30 psi (above this we would trip while running normally). Pick a trip flow of 45 percent, 270 gpm: 12 x (0.45)² = 12 x 0.2025 = 2.43 ft = 1.05 psi.
  6. Check: 1.05 is about 27 percent above 0.83 and about 19 percent below 1.30, so there is margin on both sides. Real switches also have a reset differential, and gauge readings have tolerance, so field-verify.

The square law is an approximation. Use the maker's pressure drop curve when you have it.

Testing with care

Laars's boiler manual tells the installer to verify the flow switch by shutting the circulating pump off and on a few times: the burner should shut off soon after the pump stops and begin an ignition sequence after it restarts. McDonnell & Miller says to cycle flow on and off and confirm the connected device responds before putting the system in service. Daikin's start checklist requires the switch to be installed, wired and calibrated and the water system operated and tested for design flow. Follow your unit's manual and your site's lockout rules. Our own safety note: never jumper, bypass or tie back a flow safety to get a machine running. Find out why flow is not proving.

Field card: Proof of Flow on Chillers and Boilers: Paddle, Differential Pressure and Thermal Dispersion Switches

No proven flow, no compressor or burner.

  • Use the proving device your maker names; makers differ on DP switches.
  • Paddle: right length for the pipe, free to move, 5 diameters of straight pipe, arrow with the flow.
  • A current switch proves amps, not water.
  • Trip setpoint sits above the maker's minimum flow and below normal low flow.
  • Dp scales with flow squared; 2.31 ft of water is 1 psi.
  • Never jumper a flow safety.
