Percent Math HVAC Techs Use on the Job, With Practice Problems

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Percent shows up in almost every reading you take: amps against the rating, airflow against design, a meter against a reference, outdoor air against supply. The arithmetic is short. The mistakes come from dividing by the wrong number or mixing up two kinds of change. This lesson gives you five working formulas, the common traps, a worked example and eight practice problems.

The one habit that fixes most percent mistakes

A percent is always "this, out of that." Before you divide, say the sentence out loud: "The reading is what percent of the rating?" The rating is the "that," so it goes on the bottom. Every formula below is the same move with different names. Put the thing you are comparing against, the base, in the denominator, then multiply by 100.

1. Percent of rated amps

Take the measured current and divide by the rated load current from the equipment label, then multiply by 100. See Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter for how to take the reading safely and what the label values mean. The base is the label value, not your reading. A reading of 100 percent of the rating means the motor is drawing exactly the labeled current. Over 100 percent is a reason to look for the cause, such as a dirty coil, a tight bearing or low voltage. Under 100 percent is common when a machine is lightly loaded, so a low reading is a clue about load, not proof that the machine is healthy.

2. Percent of design airflow

A balance reading gives you measured airflow at a grille, diffuser or duct. The design airflow is the number on the plans or balance schedule. Divide measured by design, then multiply by 100. Reading a Test and Balance Report shows how those columns are laid out, and Measuring Airflow covers how the readings are taken. Here the math is the same as for amps: measured over target. A branch at 85 percent of design is 15 percent short of design, and that is a different sentence from "15 percent of the air is missing from the building."

3. Percent change between two readings

When you compare a before reading to an after reading, subtract first, then divide by the before reading: (after minus before) divided by before, times 100. A positive answer is an increase and a negative answer is a decrease. A university quantitative-skills handout states the same rule: the difference is divided by the initial value. Static pressure that falls from 0.50 to 0.40 inch of water column changed by minus 0.10, and 0.10 divided by 0.50 is 20 percent, so it dropped 20 percent.

4. Percent error of a meter against a reference

When you check a meter against a trusted reference, the reference is the base. Percent error is (meter reading minus reference value) divided by the reference value, times 100. A college physics handout gives this form, with the accepted or standard value in the denominator. A positive result means the meter reads high. The reference should be a better instrument or a known condition; Checking HVAC Meters and Gauges in the Field covers ways to get one.

5. Percent outdoor air as a ratio

As a flow ratio, percent outdoor air is outdoor airflow divided by total supply airflow, times 100. If 300 cfm of a 1,500 cfm supply comes from outdoors, that is 20 percent. The temperature method estimates the same ratio from three temperatures instead of measuring the flows; that method, and why its readings can mislead, is in Mixed Air Temperature and Percent Outdoor Air. This lesson stays with the flow ratio. Units on the top and bottom must match, so do not divide cfm by gpm or by a velocity.

Common mistakes

  • Percent change versus percentage points. If percent outdoor air goes from 20 percent to 25 percent, it rose by 5 percentage points. It also rose by 25 percent in relative terms, because 5 divided by 20 is 0.25. Both statements are true, and they mean different things. Say which one you mean on a report. The quantitative-skills handout makes the same point about a rise from 4 to 5 percent.
  • Dividing by the wrong number. Airflow that drops from 1,200 to 1,020 cfm fell 15 percent (180 divided by 1,200). Going back from 1,020 up to 1,200 is a rise of about 17.6 percent (180 divided by 1,020). The base is whichever reading you started from. Likewise, a meter's error is divided by the reference, not by the meter's own reading.
  • Treating 85 percent of design as 85 percent less. Percent of design and percent short of design add up to 100.
  • Mixing units. Convert first, then divide. Unit Conversions HVAC Techs Use Every Week covers the conversions, and we do not repeat them here.
  • Rounding too early. Carry at least three digits through the arithmetic and round the final answer once.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example, and every input is an assumption we chose for the arithmetic, not a field measurement or a published value. Assume a compressor label lists 18.0 A of rated load current, and a clamp meter reads 14.4 A. Assume the supply branch design is 1,200 cfm and the balance reading is 1,020 cfm. Assume a handheld meter reads 118.0 V while a reference reads 120.0 V. Assume the outdoor air flow is 300 cfm and the supply is 1,500 cfm.

  1. Percent of rated amps: 14.4 divided by 18.0 is 0.80, so 80 percent.
  2. Percent of design airflow: 1,020 divided by 1,200 is 0.85, so 85 percent of design, 15 percent short.
  3. Percent change in airflow, design to measured: (1,020 minus 1,200) divided by 1,200 is minus 0.15, so minus 15 percent.
  4. Percent error of the meter: (118.0 minus 120.0) divided by 120.0 is minus 0.01667, so about minus 1.7 percent. The meter reads low.
  5. Percent outdoor air: 300 divided by 1,500 is 0.20, so 20 percent.

Eight practice problems (our own)

All eight problems and their numbers are our own, made up for practice. Work each one on paper before opening the answer.

  1. A motor label shows 9.6 A. You read 8.4 A. What percent of rated amps is it?
  2. A compressor label shows 21.0 A and you read 23.1 A. What percent of rated amps is it?
  3. A diffuser is designed for 450 cfm and the hood reads 387 cfm. What percent of design is it?
  4. After a damper adjustment, a branch goes from 387 cfm to 468 cfm. What is the percent change?
  5. External static pressure goes from 0.50 to 0.40 inch of water column. What is the percent change?
  6. A test meter reads 24.6 V on a source that a reference reads as 24.0 V. What is the percent error?
  7. Percent outdoor air goes from 20 percent to 25 percent. Give the change in percentage points and in percent.
  8. A unit supplies 3,500 cfm and the plan calls for 20 percent outdoor air. How many cfm of outdoor air is that?
Show answers (our own)
  1. 8.4 / 9.6 = 0.875, so 87.5 percent.
  2. 23.1 / 21.0 = 1.10, so 110 percent, worth investigating.
  3. 387 / 450 = 0.86, so 86 percent of design.
  4. (468 minus 387) / 387 = 81 / 387 = 0.209, so about plus 20.9 percent.
  5. (0.40 minus 0.50) / 0.50 = minus 0.20, so minus 20 percent.
  6. (24.6 minus 24.0) / 24.0 = 0.025, so plus 2.5 percent. The meter reads high.
  7. 25 minus 20 is 5 percentage points. 5 / 20 = 0.25, so plus 25 percent in relative terms.
  8. 3,500 times 0.20 = 700 cfm.

Field card: Percent math for HVAC techs

Say "this out of that," and put "that" on the bottom.

  • Percent of rating: measured / rated x 100.
  • Percent of design airflow: measured cfm / design cfm x 100.
  • Percent change: (after minus before) / before x 100.
  • Percent error: (meter minus reference) / reference x 100.
  • Percent outdoor air: outdoor cfm / supply cfm x 100.
  • Percentage points are a plain subtraction of two percents; percent change divides by the starting percent.
  • Convert units before you divide, and round once at the end.
