Heat and Ventilation for a New Metal or Post-Frame Shop

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A pre-engineered metal or post-frame shop is a big steel skin around cold air. If you plan the insulation, the heater, the exhaust and the rough-in before the panels go up, the building stays dry and heats on its first winter. If you do not, the roof drips on the tools and someone is cutting through a finished wall for a flue. This lesson is a planning guide for the apprentice or helper who gets asked what to ask for.

Check the manual

Check the manual. This is general education built from university extension and federal pages read in 2026, plus our own arithmetic. The heater label, the manufacturer instructions, the fuel gas code adopted where you work and the local inspector decide the actual installation. Gas and electrical work belongs to licensed, qualified people.

Why the roof and walls drip

A university extension article on metal-walled buildings explains the cause: if the metal surface is colder than the dew point of the room air, water condenses on it. It names ventilation as the main way to reduce the chance, and says insulating the room side of the metal stops surface condensation. It also warns that condensation can still form inside the insulation, or between it and the inner metal, unless a vapor barrier is used properly or closed-cell insulation is included. That article is about livestock buildings with a lot of moisture, so a dry shop is an easier case, but the physics is the same. Metal roofs behave like the metal walls in that article, though the page itself does not discuss roofs. Plan for the moisture the shop will carry before you pick the insulation.

Insulation with a continuous vapor retarder

A federal home energy page says "vapor retarder" is the better term and that retarders work best on the warm side of the assembly, which in a cold climate is the interior. It stresses that coverage must be continuous, with tears and punctures sealed, or moisture can condense in the cavity and wet the insulation. It also says air sealing matters as much as the retarder. The extension article agrees on placement for northern climates and adds that the retarder must be protected from penetrations, so an interior liner is usually added, which raises cost. For a metal building, that means the facing or sheet goes on the shop side of the insulation with taped seams, and the metal panel sits outside it. Sealing the retarder at the roof, wall and slab joints is part of the job, because gaps defeat it.

How much insulation? One university extension farm-building guide lists ceilings at R-24 and walls at R-13 for buildings with supplemental heat. A northern extension shop guide suggests, for metal buildings, R-25 draped over the purlins plus R-11 parallel to the purlins, with thermal blocks at the purlins, and walls with at least R-13 between framing plus R-7.5 continuous. It also says perimeter footings need at least R-10, with an additional R-5 if the slab is heated, and slab-edge insulation to at least 24 inches deep for an unheated slab and 48 inches for a heated one. Those are northern-climate suggestions; confirm the numbers for your climate and code. A separate extension publication says to work on air leaks before adding insulation.

Unit heater, tube heater or in-slab heat

The northern extension shop guide sorts the choices this way. Forced-air heaters mount near the ceiling and heat the air. Infrared radiant heaters hang overhead and heat objects in their line of sight, so they suit spot heating at a bench. In-floor hydronic tubing, fed by a boiler or a heat pump, gives warm floors that dry fast and hold heat, but it costs more to install, is hard to add to a finished shop and recovers slowly. Forced-air gas or electric heating recovers quickly and suits infrequent winter use, though it is noisy. That guide also notes that radiant systems heat objects, which then warm the air. Our own addition: in a building with a large door, a tall cold roof peak and a slab that is poured once, the decision should be made before the pour, because tubing and insulation cannot be added after.

Details are covered in other lessons, so we do not repeat them: see Infrared Tube Heaters for Warehouses and Garages, Garage and Workshop Heaters, Radiant Floor Heating Basics and Heating Farm Shops and Machine Sheds, which already covers big-door air leaks, engine exhaust and freeze protection for existing sheds.

Mounting heights and clearances

We give no universal numbers here. Each heater has its own table for distance to combustibles and for mounting height, and the numbers change with input size and orientation. Ask for the manual before the framing is finished, so blocking, hanger points and the flue path are drawn on the plan. The extension guide notes forced-air heaters mount near the ceiling and radiant heaters hang overhead, but the manual sets the actual distances. Allow for the weight of the heater and any hanger load in the framing, and for the swing of the overhead door, tall equipment and a raised hoist, since those are what a heater gets hit by. If the shop will work on vehicles, our lesson on vehicle repair garages covers heater height near flammable vapors.

Makeup air and exhaust

Anything that exhausts air pulls the same amount in somewhere, and in winter that air arrives cold. A federal worker safety fact sheet lists engines in enclosed spaces among common workplace carbon monoxide sources, and says to install effective ventilation to remove CO and to keep gasoline engines out of poorly ventilated areas. For welding, the federal general industry rule says mechanical ventilation is needed when the space is under 10,000 cubic feet per welder, the ceiling is under 16 feet, or partitions block cross-ventilation, at 2,000 cfm per welder unless local exhaust or approved respirators are used. Local exhaust hoods must hold 100 feet per minute of capture velocity at the weld. That rule covers workplaces; a home shop is not an employer, but we use the figures as a yardstick. See Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors. Plan the exhaust fan wall opening, the makeup air opening and its damper before the panels go up, and place them so cold air does not blow on workers.

Rough in before the panels go up

  • Gas. Decide the heater location, the meter or tank location and the pipe route. Sleeves through the slab or wall are far easier before the pour and before panels. Sizing and materials are a licensed gas fitter's job.
  • Power. Heater controls, exhaust fans, a pump for slab heat and welding outlets all need circuits. Run conduit or leave chases while framing is open. Sizing is for a licensed electrician.
  • Flue and combustion air. Mark roof or wall penetrations from the heater manual, not from guesses. Cut and flash them while the roofer is there.
  • Slab. If in-slab tubing is planned, the extension guide's perimeter and edge insulation must go in before the concrete is placed.
  • Vapor retarder. Seal at every penetration listed above, since each is a hole in it.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a design for your building. The extension guide says to do an actual heat loss calculation instead of relying on a rule of thumb, so this shows the method.

Assumptions. A 40 ft by 60 ft shop with 14 ft walls and a flat roof area of 2,400 sq ft. One 14 ft by 14 ft overhead door (196 sq ft) and one 3 ft by 7 ft man door (21 sq ft). Indoor temperature 55 F, outdoor 5 F, so the difference is 50 F. Assumed effective U-values: walls 0.08, roof 0.05, big door 0.20, man door 0.50 Btu/h per sq ft per F. Assumed slab-edge loss of 0.5 Btu/h per foot of perimeter per F over 200 ft. Assumed leakage of 0.75 air changes per hour. Assumed heater efficiency 80 percent. The factor 0.018 Btu per cubic foot per F is the usual volumetric heat of air.

  1. Wall area: 2 x (40 + 60) x 14 = 2,800 sq ft, minus 196 and 21 for doors = 2,583 sq ft.
  2. Wall UA: 2,583 x 0.08 = 206.6. Roof UA: 2,400 x 0.05 = 120. Big door UA: 196 x 0.20 = 39.2. Man door UA: 21 x 0.50 = 10.5. Slab edge: 200 x 0.5 = 100.
  3. Total UA = 206.6 + 120 + 39.2 + 10.5 + 100 = 476.3 Btu/h per F.
  4. Conduction loss = 476.3 x 50 = 23,800 Btu/h (rounded).
  5. Volume = 40 x 60 x 14 = 33,600 cu ft. Leakage = 33,600 x 0.75 = 25,200 cu ft per hour. Air loss = 25,200 x 0.018 x 50 = 22,700 Btu/h (rounded).
  6. Total load = 23,800 + 22,700 = 46,500 Btu/h.
  7. Heater input at 80 percent = 46,500 / 0.80 = about 58,100 Btu/h. Choose the next available size up; do not add a large safety factor on top.

Check. 46,500 / 2,400 sq ft = 19.4 Btu/h per sq ft, which lines up with the extension guide's figure of about 20 Btu per sq ft per hour for a well insulated, well sealed shop. Our example does not include warming the building up from cold, door openings or exhaust. Add one welder's general ventilation at 2,000 cfm and the makeup air load is 1.08 x 2,000 x 50 = 108,000 Btu/h, more than twice the whole shell. That is why local exhaust, run only while welding, and a plan for makeup air matter more than a few extra inches of insulation.

Field card: Heat and Ventilation for a New Metal or Post-Frame Shop

Plan the dry, the heat, the air and the holes before the panels.

  • Metal drips when it is colder than the room air's dew point.
  • Put one continuous, sealed vapor retarder on the warm side of the insulation.
  • Air seal first; then insulate.
  • Choose forced air, radiant tube or slab heat before the pour.
  • Heater manual sets clearances and height; add framing for hangers.
  • Every exhaust fan needs a planned makeup air path.
  • Rough in gas, power and flue openings while the walls are open.
  • Do a heat loss calculation; do not guess.
