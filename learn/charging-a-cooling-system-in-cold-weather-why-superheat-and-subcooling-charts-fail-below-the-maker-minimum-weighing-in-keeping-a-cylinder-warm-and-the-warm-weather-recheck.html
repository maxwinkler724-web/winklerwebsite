Charging in Cold Weather: Weigh-In, Warm Cylinders and the Return Visit

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A new split system is ready to go, the line set is brazed, the vacuum held, and the thermometer says 38 degrees. Most charging charts are not built for that day. This lesson explains why the pressure-based methods stop being trustworthy in the cold, how to charge by weight instead, how to keep a cylinder usable without doing anything dangerous, and why you owe the customer a second visit.

Why the charts stop working

Superheat and subcooling charging both read the system's balance between how much heat the outdoor coil can dump and how much the refrigerant flow is fed. The maker's chart or target table assumes outdoor and indoor air temperatures inside a stated window. Below the lowest outdoor temperature in that window, the numbers on your gauges no longer mean what the chart says.

A federal energy-office measure guideline for air conditioner diagnostics says charge tests should not be attempted below 55°F outdoors or below 70°F indoors, and it gives the reason as compressor damage from flooding and poor lubrication. It allows adjustments on expansion-valve systems down to 65°F outdoors if the indoor dry bulb stays above 70°F, and it says systems with a fixed metering device should not be tested below 70°F outdoors. It also warns that low outdoor temperatures raise superheat, which can hide an overcharge. Your maker's own installation manual sets the real limit for the unit in front of you. Use the manual's number if it differs from these.

The working rule is simple. If the outdoor temperature is under the minimum printed for the chart, do not "chase" the chart by adding or removing refrigerant. A reading that looks like an undercharge may be only the cold.

Weigh the charge in

The same guideline says that for new installations the charge is set by weighing in an amount based on line length and size, and that weighing in is useful when it is too cold for the pressure methods. It adds that makers generally recommend verifying the charge afterward with superheat and subcooling. For a system that already holds refrigerant, weighing in is only valid after the unit has been fully evacuated, as the guideline puts it.

Start with the nameplate charge, which covers a stated line length, then adjust for the actual line set. The lesson Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments covers that arithmetic. For new equipment, the examples here use R-454B or R-32. EPA's list of acceptable substitutes shows both as A2L refrigerants, acceptable with use conditions for new residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps. Read the installation manual for the use conditions on your equipment.

Our own worked example

This is our own example, not a maker's data. We assume a 3-ton R-454B split system with these inputs:

  • Assumed nameplate charge: 100 oz (6 lb 4 oz), stated for 15 ft of line.
  • Assumed manual rate: 0.60 oz per foot of added liquid line.
  • Actual liquid line: 40 ft. Outdoor temperature: 38°F.
  1. Extra line: 40 ft minus 15 ft = 25 ft.
  2. Added charge: 25 ft x 0.60 oz per ft = 15 oz.
  3. Total charge: 100 oz + 15 oz = 115 oz, which is 7 lb 3 oz.

With the unit evacuated, you put the cylinder on a scale, zero it, open the valve and meter in liquid until the scale shows 115 oz gone, adding in stages while the compressor runs and watching the scale, not the gauges. Your real numbers come from the real manual. Never reuse these.

Why a cold cylinder charges slowly

The pressure in a cylinder that holds liquid and vapor follows the temperature of the refrigerant, not how much is left. This is our own explanation of the pressure and temperature relationship; the lesson R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges shows the numbers. As the cylinder sits on a cold truck bed or a cold slab, its pressure drops. As it drains, the liquid boils off, takes heat from the cylinder and cools it further. Both effects shrink the gap between the cylinder and the running system, so flow slows. Technicians sometimes read the slow flow as an empty cylinder. Check the scale first.

Keeping a cylinder warm the safe way

Compressed gas safety guidance from universities is consistent on three points. A fact sheet from one university says not to put cylinders next to heat sources or let a flame contact any part of the cylinder, and not to subject them to temperatures above 125°F. Another university manual says open flame can never be exposed to any part of a cylinder and that cylinder temperature must not exceed 125°F. OSHA's compressed gas rule, 29 CFR 1910.101, requires employers to make sure cylinders under their control are in a safe condition and says on-site handling must follow a compressed gas handling pamphlet that it adopts.

A third university page goes further: never heat a cylinder to raise the pressure of the gas, because that can defeat the safety devices built in by the supplier. We read that as a warning against pushing pressure up. Our own field practice is narrower. You are trying to stop the cylinder from getting cold, not to make it hot:

  • Stand the cylinder in a bucket of warm water, tap-warm or less. Change the water as it cools. Our own rule of thumb: if you cannot comfortably hold your hand in it, it is too hot.
  • Keep a cylinder in the heated cab or a warm building until you need it, and never in a closed hot vehicle.
  • Never use a torch, a heat gun on the valve or an open flame. Heating a cylinder with a flame is what the guidance above forbids, and a flame near a leaking A2L refrigerant is an ignition hazard.
  • Never exceed the 125°F limit cited above at any point.

See Carrying Refrigerant Cylinders in a Service Van for moving and securing cylinders. Also remember the legal side: EPA's Section 608 rules prohibit intentionally venting refrigerant, and they require technicians who service equipment that could release refrigerant to be certified. Do not vent a hose to "clear" it in the cold.

Come back in warm weather

A weighed-in charge is a good start, not a finished check. Plan a return visit when outdoor temperature is back in the maker's window. By the guideline cited above that means at least 55°F outdoors and 70°F indoors for most tests, with 70°F outdoors for fixed metering devices. Then:

  1. Run the system and let it settle. The guideline says to let it run 15 minutes after any charge change before repeating tests.
  2. Read superheat or subcooling the way the maker specifies for that metering device.
  3. Compare to the maker's target. The guideline treats actual superheat within 5°F of target as acceptable.
  4. If readings are off, look for non-charge causes first: airflow, a dirty coil, a restriction.
  5. Record the weighed-in charge and the date of the recheck so the next tech knows.

Field card: Charging in Cold Weather

Under the chart's minimum, weigh it in, then verify when it is warm.

  • Read the maker's minimum outdoor temperature; the cited guideline says no charge tests below 55°F outdoors.
  • Cold air raises superheat and can hide an overcharge.
  • New install: nameplate charge plus line length adjustment, by weight, on a scale.
  • Cylinder pressure follows temperature; a cold cylinder charges slowly.
  • Warm water only. No torch, no open flame, nothing above 125°F.
  • Return when it is warm and verify superheat or subcooling per the maker.
  • Never vent. EPA 608 certification applies.
