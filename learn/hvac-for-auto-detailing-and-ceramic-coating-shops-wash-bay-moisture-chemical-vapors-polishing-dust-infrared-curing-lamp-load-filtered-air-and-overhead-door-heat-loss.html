HVAC for Auto Detailing and Ceramic Coating Shops: Moisture, Vapors, Dust and Curing Heat

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A detailing bay does four jobs in one room: wet washing, chemical wiping, dusty polishing and warm, clean curing. Each job wants different air. This lesson sorts out what the ventilation and heating have to do for each one, and works one example with our own numbers.

Check the product maker's sheet first

Read the written range. Ceramic coating makers publish a temperature and humidity range for application and cure, and a wait time before the car can get wet. Those ranges differ by product, so we give no numbers here. The range on the product's own data sheet is what the HVAC has to hold, and it is the first question to ask the shop owner. This page is general education, not design advice.

Moisture from washing and steam cleaning

Water that is sprayed on a car and then dries, plus any steam, ends up as vapor in the room air. Humidity ratio is the pounds of water vapor carried per pound of dry air, and it is often written in grains, where 7,000 grains make one pound (university psychrometrics notes). In a closed bay that vapor has three places to go: out through an exhaust fan, into a dehumidifier, or onto cold surfaces such as the overhead door, windows and tools. Wet air that sits against cold metal causes rust and fogged glass, and it can spoil a coating that needs dry air during cure. For a sealed bay with a washing session on a cold day, exhaust is the cheaper way to move a large amount of moisture quickly, and a dehumidifier is better for slow background drying. See car wash HVAC for full wash tunnels, which we do not repeat here.

Cleaning chemicals and solvent vapors

Wipe-down solvents, degreasers and coating prep products give off vapors that can be flammable and harmful to breathe. A federal workplace-safety training chapter on ventilation says local exhaust suits point sources and larger particles, while dilution ventilation suits lower-hazard vapors that are spread out through the room. It also says investigations can be triggered when flammable vapor levels get near the lower explosive limit. Read the safety data sheet for each product, keep bottles capped, and do not rely on room air alone for anything marked flammable. Spraying coatings is a different job; see spray paint booth HVAC.

Polishing dust

Machine polishing and sanding put fine particles into the air. A federal occupational-health report on auto body shops says sanders with built-in high-velocity, low-volume exhaust cut total dust to about one-tenth of the levels from unventilated sanders. For a detailing shop the lesson is to catch dust at the tool when you can, then clean the room air with filtered supply and a return that is not placed where dust piles up. Dust that is allowed to settle will land on a fresh coating, so many shops separate the polishing area from the curing area.

Exhaust that pulls dust in

Every cubic foot an exhaust fan removes must be replaced. The federal ventilation chapter says natural make-up through open doors or leaks under doors is acceptable only if planned, and that leaks around cracks and under doors are not. Without make-up air a slight negative pressure builds and the exhaust flow drops. In a detailing shop the leaks are the problem: unfiltered outside air pulls dust, pollen and road grit through the overhead door seals and lands them on wet paint. A dedicated, filtered, tempered make-up opening near the fan keeps the air path where you want it and cuts drafts at doors.

Infrared curing lamps: heat and electrical load

Infrared lamps turn electricity into radiant heat that warms surfaces directly. A federal industrial technology page describes electric infrared processes with over 90 percent conversion to radiant energy in one process, and fast heat-up compared with convection ovens. Nearly all the electricity ends up as heat in the room, so each kilowatt counts as 3,412 Btu/h (a university energy glossary gives this equality). Lamps also load a circuit: amps are watts divided by volts for a resistive load. Check the lamp maker's placement distance and the panel before adding lamps. Keep flammable vapors and rags away from the lamps and out of the bay while they are on, and follow the lamp maker's written distances to the car.

Unit heaters versus radiant heat

A unit heater warms the air and a fan moves it. That works well for fast recovery after the door opens, but moving air stirs dust. Radiant heaters warm people and objects directly, as a state fire-safety information sheet puts it, and warm objects hold that heat after a door opens. The same sheet says to keep gasoline, dust and vapors away from heaters and says infrared heaters are generally not approved for hazardous classified locations. Gas-fired heaters in a vapor-prone bay need special attention; see vehicle repair garage HVAC and garage and workshop heaters. An electric radiant floor or panel avoids open flames, but the coating maker's range still sets the target temperature.

Overhead door heat loss

The overhead door is the largest hole in a bay. A state energy guide says damaged weatherstripping can increase energy loss around a door by many times, and that doors should be checked yearly. The guide covers house doors, not shop overhead doors, so use it only for the idea: seals, bottom gasket and track gaps matter more than a thicker panel. Seal first, then size the heater.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not data from a real shop. Assumptions: one bay; six infrared lamps rated 1,000 W each, all on at once; 240 V supply; a washing session that evaporates 6 lb of water into the room over 1 hour; outdoor make-up air at 40 grains of moisture per pound of dry air; desired bay air at 70 grains per pound; standard air at 0.075 lb/ft³ (university psychrometrics text). We round a pint of water to 1 lb for the pint step; that is our own rounding.

  1. Lamp power. 6 lamps × 1,000 W = 6,000 W = 6 kW.
  2. Lamp heat. 6 kW × 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 20,472 Btu/h added to the bay.
  3. Lamp current. 6,000 W ÷ 240 V = 25 A.
  4. Moisture mass in grains. 6 lb × 7,000 grains per lb = 42,000 grains in the hour.
  5. Moisture each cfm can carry. One cfm moves 0.075 × 60 = 4.5 lb of dry air per hour. The difference between room and make-up air is 70 − 40 = 30 grains per lb, so one cfm removes 4.5 × 30 = 135 grains per hour.
  6. Exhaust needed. 42,000 ÷ 135 = about 311 cfm, if the session's moisture is removed as fast as it is made.
  7. Dehumidifier alternative. 6 lb is about 6 pints in the hour, so the machine would have to remove 6 pints per hour, which is 144 pints per day if run all day. Compare that with the maker's pints-per-day rating at its stated test conditions. A small unit would take many hours, so exhaust handles the burst and the dehumidifier handles the slow drying that follows.

Our conclusion under these assumptions: about 311 cfm of exhaust, balanced by filtered make-up air, controls the wash burst; the lamps add roughly 20,000 Btu/h on top of whatever the heater supplies, so plan the heating for the door-open and lamps-off case. Real bays differ in what evaporates and what the coating maker calls for.

What to remember

  • Hold the coating maker's written temperature and humidity range during cure.
  • Remove wash moisture by exhaust or dehumidifier, and keep wet air off cold surfaces.
  • Replace exhaust air with filtered make-up air so dust does not leak through the door.
  • Convert lamp kilowatts to Btu/h at 3,412 and to amps by dividing by volts.
