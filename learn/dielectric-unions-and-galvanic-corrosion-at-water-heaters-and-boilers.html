Dielectric Unions and Galvanic Corrosion at Water Heaters and Boilers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Look at the top of an older tank water heater and you will often see a short, colorful fitting between the copper pipe and the heater, sometimes with a white crust or rust stain around it. That fitting is there to stop two different metals from eating each other in wet conditions. This lesson explains why that happens, what the fitting does, why it sometimes leaks anyway, and what the makers say to use.

What galvanic corrosion is

AMPP, the corrosion society, describes galvanic corrosion as damage that happens when two different metals are joined in a corrosive liquid. The less noble metal becomes the anode and corrodes faster. The more noble metal becomes the cathode and corrodes more slowly. A facilities bulletin from the National Institutes of Health lists three things that must all be present: metals with different electrochemical behavior, direct contact between them, and an electrolyte touching both.

Take away any one of the three and the reaction stops. A dielectric fitting removes the second one. It puts a non-conducting break between the metals so electrons cannot travel from one to the other. Our lesson on water heater anode rods covers the same idea used on purpose inside the tank. A dielectric union is the opposite job: preventing an accidental sacrifice at the pipe joint.

Which metal loses, and how fast

A galvanic series is a ranking of metals by their measured voltage in a given liquid, from the most anodic (most likely to corrode) to the most cathodic. Two cautions: the order depends on the liquid (the common version is for seawater), and the voltage gap is only part of the story. AMPP says a small anode area joined to a large cathode area is the bad case, because all the current is concentrated on the small piece. A short steel nipple joined to a long run of copper is exactly that.

The Copper Development Association says the risk rises when copper touches steel or iron in the presence of moisture, and lists dielectric unions, sleeves, plastic wraps and plain physical separation as ways to isolate them.

Worked example: ranking the metals and finding the loser

The web page of a corrosion consultancy publishes a table of potentials in flowing seawater, in volts against a saturated calomel reference electrode. Each metal gets two numbers. We will average the two to get one working value. It is a teaching shortcut, and it is seawater data, so use the order and the sizes of the gaps, not the exact voltages, for a home plumbing system.

Step 1, pick the values and average them.

  • Zinc (the coating on galvanized steel, standing in for it): (-0.98 + -1.03) / 2 = -1.005 V
  • Mild steel: (-0.60 + -0.72) / 2 = -0.66 V
  • Brass (red, yellow or naval): (-0.31 + -0.40) / 2 = -0.355 V
  • Copper: (-0.30 + -0.37) / 2 = -0.335 V
  • Stainless 304, passive: (-0.04 + -0.10) / 2 = -0.07 V

Step 2, rank them. More negative means more anodic. From anodic to cathodic: galvanized (zinc), mild steel, brass, copper, stainless. The metal higher in this list corrodes when joined to one lower in the list.

Step 3, test three invented pairings.

  • Copper pipe to a mild steel nipple. Difference = -0.335 - (-0.66) = 0.325 V. Steel is more negative, so the steel nipple corrodes.
  • Brass adapter to copper pipe. Difference = -0.335 - (-0.355) = 0.02 V. Brass is a hair more anodic, but the gap is tiny, so very little is expected.
  • Copper pipe to a galvanized steel nipple. Difference = -0.335 - (-1.005) = 0.67 V. The zinc coating goes first, and once it is gone the steel underneath follows.

Step 4, compare with a cited guideline. A fastener supplier, Carrlane, gives rough limits for the allowable voltage difference: 0.50 V for climate-controlled indoor use, 0.25 V for indoor use that is not climate controlled, and 0.15 V for harsh conditions. It says they should not guide critical applications. By those numbers, the brass and copper pair (0.02 V) is under every limit. The copper and steel pair (0.325 V) is over 0.25 V and 0.15 V but under 0.50 V. The copper and galvanized pair (0.67 V) is over all three. Treat those limits as a sense of scale. The plumbing codes below do not make the decision from a voltage table at all; they require the fitting.

One more pairing to know: copper joined to passive stainless (-0.07 V) gives a gap of 0.335 - 0.07 = 0.265 V, and here the copper is the one that loses. A plumbing trade magazine told of a hospital where stainless mains were joined to copper branches without dielectric fittings, and the copper corroded.

What the fittings are and how they differ

  • Dielectric union. A union with an insulating gasket and a plastic or rubber sleeve inside it. Plumbing codes point to the ASSE 1079 standard for it.
  • Dielectric nipple. A short threaded pipe with a plastic liner and no moving gasket. Penn State's facilities bulletin prefers it over the union because there is no rubber seal to leak. Heat from soldering can melt the liner, so do hot work first.
  • Brass nipple or adapter. Brass sits close to copper in the series, so it is a middle step. The 2021 Uniform Plumbing Code text, as quoted by a plumbing trade magazine, allows a copper alloy adapter or a copper alloy nipple at least 6 inches long when copper meets threaded steel. Not every jurisdiction accepts it.

Why dielectric unions fail

The NIH bulletin says dielectric unions can leak, and a leak lets water recreate the conditions for corrosion. It also warns that gaskets can shift or split when tightened. Penn State calls dielectric unions proven sources of leaking fittings. Tightening a leaking union squeezes the rubber seal, and later heat cycles make new leaks more likely.

One detail from A. O. Smith: its residential electric manual says a dielectric waterway nipple is not the same as a dielectric nipple and will not protect the water heater from corrosion.

What the heater and boiler makers say

  • A. O. Smith (residential electric manual sold through a home center): recommends a dielectric union meeting ASSE 1079 where the heater meets different pipe materials, and its installed-heater drawing says the unions must be dielectric at the inlet and outlet if copper piping is used. It says the heater's inlet and outlet connections are steel and tells installers not to solder pipes while they are attached, because of non-metallic parts inside.
  • Bradford White (a gas model manual we read): says the heater's inlet and outlet fittings are dielectric waterway fittings with 3/4 inch NPT male threads, with plastic-lined nipples, and to connect the lines with unions. It warns not to apply heat to the nipples on top.
  • Rheem (a manual in its RR118 document): says unions are recommended on all water connections. The text we read did not mention dielectric fittings.
  • Weil-McLain (Aqua Pro indirect water heater manual): says to use dielectric unions or couplings to protect hot and cold water fittings from corrosion, and gives copper with galvanized iron as the example. Our lesson on indirect water heaters on a boiler covers how those tanks work.

Wording differs, so follow the manual with the unit in front of you.

What the plumbing codes say

The code text below is as quoted by a plumbing trade magazine, which we read. It is not a copy of the model codes themselves. The 2021 UPC section 605.15 covers dielectric unions where metals are dissimilar and refers to ASSE 1079. Its section 605.16.1 lists, for copper to threaded joints, a copper alloy adapter, a copper alloy nipple at least 6 inches long, a dielectric fitting or a dielectric union. The 2021 International Plumbing Code section 605.23.1 calls for a copper-alloy or dielectric fitting, or a dielectric union meeting ASSE 1079, for copper to galvanized steel. Your local code can differ, so check the edition in force where you work.

Bonding note

An insulating fitting also breaks the metal path. InspectApedia says a jumper wire is installed so the building's metal plumbing stays grounded. Do not remove one without asking an electrician or inspector.

What to do on a service call

  • Look at each joint where copper meets a steel nipple on the heater, and compare what you see with the manual.
  • If you see a drip near the top, check with the leak-source steps in Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom before blaming the tank.
  • For the boiler fill side, see Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer. We found no maker rule on dissimilar metals there.

Field card: Dielectric Unions and Galvanic Corrosion

Different metals + contact + water = one of them corrodes.

  • Anodic (more negative) metal corrodes: zinc, steel, brass, copper, stainless in that order.
  • A dielectric fitting breaks the contact; it does not change the metals.
  • Small steel next to large copper is the worst area ratio.
  • Dielectric unions can leak; dielectric nipples have no gasket to leak.
  • A "dielectric waterway" nipple is not the same as a dielectric nipple (A. O. Smith).
  • Do not solder or heat near lined nipples; sweat the adapter first.
  • The manual and the local code decide the fitting, not this card.
