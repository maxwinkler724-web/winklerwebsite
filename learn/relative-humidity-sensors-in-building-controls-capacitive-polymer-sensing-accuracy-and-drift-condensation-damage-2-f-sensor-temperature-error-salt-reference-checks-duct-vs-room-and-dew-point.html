Relative Humidity Sensors in Building Controls

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most building controllers learn humidity from a small capacitive polymer chip behind a filter cap. It is a good sensor and an easy one to fool. This lesson covers how it works, how far to trust it, how to check it, and when to ask for dew point instead of relative humidity.

How a capacitive polymer sensor works

The sensing element is a thin polymer film between two electrodes, which makes a tiny capacitor. A university review of humidity-sensor technology explains that these films are polar and somewhat water-absorbing: water that enters the film fills the free space between polymer chains and raises the film's dielectric constant, so the capacitance rises as the film takes on water. The same review says the response is roughly proportional to the water absorbed, so the output is close to linear. The electronics read the capacitance and convert it to a percent RH signal, commonly 0 to 10 V or 4 to 20 mA to the controller.

Because the film takes up and gives back water, it has some hysteresis: the reading after a wet spell can lag the reading after a dry one. The same review lists hysteresis as a real design problem for this sensor type and says makers reduce it with cross-linked, less water-hungry polymers.

Accuracy and drift

Do not expect lab numbers in a ceiling or a duct. In a federal test building report, the return-air RH sensor was specified at plus or minus 2 percent RH and the outdoor-air sensor at plus or minus 3 percent RH. The same report found the mixed-air RH sensor took about half an hour to settle after the outdoor damper opened, against about 12 minutes for the mixed-air temperature sensor. RH sensors are slow, so do not adjust a setpoint on a reading taken a minute after you moved the probe.

For drift, a federal ocean-research lab report on a capacitive probe gives a useful picture. The maker's specification was a drift of under 1 percent RH per year, but the lab's before-and-after deployment checks showed a mean change of 1.04 percent RH (sensors read low when recovered) and a root-mean-square change of 2.72 percent RH, about 2.09 percent RH per year when scaled to one year. The lab pointed out that the maker's figure does not include real-world contamination of the sensor or its filter cap. That was ocean service, harsher than an office, but the lesson carries over: a stated accuracy is a starting point, and a sensor that has been in service for years needs a check. A national measurement-lab procedure for calibrating hygrometers lists 24 months as the initial calibration interval for laboratory hygrometers, which gives a sense of how often even careful labs verify them.

What damages them

  • Fouling and contamination. The ocean-lab report says the sensor and its filter can become fouled, which slows response and shifts readings, and it cleans or replaces filter caps between uses. A university encyclopedia entry on humidity measurement says capacitive sensors must be periodically calibrated to deal with hysteresis and contamination.
  • Long time at high humidity. The same entry says prolonged exposure above 85 percent RH can make capacitive readings inaccurate. Liquid water on the film is a harder case than damp air, so we treat condensation as a reason to recheck the sensor afterward. A sensor right after a cooling coil, or one that sees dripping or spray, is in that situation.
  • Chemical vapors. We have not found a federal or university table of which vapors hurt which polymers, so we keep to the general rule from the sources above: anything that fouls the film or filter can shift the reading. Our own practice is to keep the probe out of cleaning-product, solvent and paint vapor, to remove it or cover it during such work, and to check it against a reference afterward.

Why a 2 F error moves RH

Relative humidity compares the water vapor present with the most the air could hold at that temperature. A federal weather service office page says the same moisture gives a higher RH in cooler air and a lower RH in warmer air, and that dew point is the more direct measure of moisture. So an RH sensor is really an RH-and-temperature sensor: if the chip is warmer than the air you care about, it reports the RH of the warmer air. Heat from a nearby power supply, relay or controller board, a sun patch on an outside wall, or a wall that is warmed by pipes can do this. Check the mounting guidance in the temperature sensor mounting lesson and keep the humidity element out of the board's thermal plume.

Worked example (our own): a 2 F warm spot

Assumptions (ours): the room air is 72 F and 50 percent RH; pressure is near sea level; the sensor body is held 2 F warmer by nearby electronics; no moisture is added between the room and the sensor. We use a common exponential approximation for saturation vapor pressure in hectopascals, es = 6.112 × exp(17.62 × T / (243.12 + T)), with T in degrees C. It is an approximation, so treat the answers as estimates.

  1. Convert: 72 F = 22.22 C and 74 F = 23.33 C.
  2. Saturation pressure at 22.22 C: es = 6.112 × exp(17.62 × 22.22 / 265.34) = 6.112 × exp(1.4756) = 26.73 hPa.
  3. Actual vapor pressure at 50 percent: e = 0.50 × 26.73 = 13.37 hPa.
  4. Dew point: ln(13.37 / 6.112) = 0.7826, so Td = 243.12 × 0.7826 / (17.62 − 0.7826) = 11.30 C, or 52.3 F. Check: putting 11.30 C back into the saturation formula gives 13.37 hPa, which matches step 3.
  5. Sensor at 74 F (23.33 C): es = 6.112 × exp(17.62 × 23.33 / 266.45) = 28.60 hPa.
  6. The vapor pressure is unchanged, so RH at the sensor = 13.37 / 28.60 = 46.7 percent.

Result: the sensor reads about 46.7 percent RH, roughly 3.3 points low against the room's true 50 percent. A quick cross-check: saturation pressure rose by a factor of 28.60 / 26.73 = 1.07, and 50 / 1.07 = 46.7. In this range, each 1 F of sensor warming costs about 1.6 RH points, so a 2 F offset is as large as a typical 2 to 3 percent RH accuracy specification, and it is not drift, so recalibrating the sensor will not fix it. If instead you took the sensor's 46.7 percent and paired it with the 72 F room temperature, you would compute a dew point of about 50.5 F, off by roughly 1.8 F. Pair the RH with the sensor's own temperature and the dew point comes out right at 52.3 F.

Checking a sensor

Against a reference hygrometer. Hold a recently verified handheld hygrometer next to the fixed sensor, same height, out of drafts and away from your breath, and wait for both to settle, remembering the slow response above. Compare the two temperatures too. If the temperatures differ by more than a degree, you may be seeing a mounting problem rather than a sensor problem. A federal calibration guide notes that field comparison against a collocated reference is an option but limited in range, so one point near the normal operating range is a spot check, not a calibration.

Against a saturated salt reference. A closed container holding a saturated salt solution with undissolved salt in it holds the air above it at a fixed RH. A 1977 national-lab compilation of such fixed points fits RH to temperature for 28 salt solutions; for common table salt it lists a value in the mid-70s percent RH (75.5 at 0 C in the fit), and a 1957 national-lab circular says these solutions in general use hold RH to no better than about 1 percent RH, and that RH is only roughly independent of temperature. Practical steps from those limits: let the sensor and container sit together at one steady room temperature for hours, never in sun or beside a vent, keep excess solid salt present, and do not breathe into the container. The compilation warns that sources or sinks of humidity or incomplete equilibration can change the solution. Reading the sensor against the salt tells you whether to trust it at that one point, so adjust or replace it only following the maker's instructions.

Duct versus room placement

A room sensor sees the humidity people feel, but it also sees warm walls, sun, equipment and drafts. A duct sensor sees the air the system is actually delivering or returning, with less wall influence, but it needs enough velocity and mixing, and it can sit in near-saturated air after a cooling coil, which is the high-humidity case above. In the federal test building the return-air RH sensor was on a room wall near the air handler, not in the duct, and the mixed-air sensor was in the mixing box about 2 ft past the return damper. Mixed air can stratify, as covered in the mixed-air lesson, so one point may not represent the stream. Follow the controls designer's drawing and the maker's mounting sheet for height, distance from the coil and filter, and cable routing.

When to report dew point instead

Use RH when the question is comfort or material safety at a known temperature, such as a room setpoint. Use dew point when the question is how much moisture is in the air regardless of temperature: deciding if outdoor air is too wet to bring in, limiting supply air moisture, checking against a cold surface temperature for condensation, or comparing sensors at different places. The weather office page says dew point is the more definitive moisture measure, and also that RH cannot be determined from dew point alone, since you must know the air temperature too. Many controllers compute dew point from the RH and temperature channels. That is only as good as the temperature channel, the sensor's own temperature, which is the pairing mistake in the worked example. For the moisture math behind dew point see the humidity ratio lesson, and for the wiring and switching side of a simple on/off humidity control see the humidistat lesson. A reading taken another way is in the wet-bulb lesson.

Field card: Relative Humidity Sensors in Building Controls

RH is moisture divided by what the sensor's own temperature allows.

  • Capacitive polymer film: absorbed water raises the dielectric constant, so capacitance rises.
  • Slow response: allow many minutes to settle before judging a reading.
  • Specs of 2 to 3 percent RH are common in building reports; drift and fouling add to that over years.
  • Keep sensors out of condensation, spray and cleaning or solvent vapors; recheck after exposure.
  • A 2 F warm spot at 72 F and 50 percent RH reads about 46.7 percent (our own example).
  • Check against a verified reference or a salt reference at steady room temperature.
  • Use dew point when you need moisture content, and pair RH with the sensor's own temperature.
