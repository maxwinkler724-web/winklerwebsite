NJ Landlord Heat Requirements: Minimum Temperatures and Heating Season

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tenant calls: the apartment is cold. Is the landlord required to fix it today, and what number counts? New Jersey sets a heating season and two minimum temperatures for rental housing. This lesson walks through them, shows a worked timeline check, and lists what a tech should write down on a no-heat call in a rental.

Rules can change; confirm with your municipality or NJ DCA. This is legal information for trade education, not legal advice. Cities and towns add their own rules, and the state rule was amended in 2023. For a dispute, talk to the local health or code office, the Department of Community Affairs (DCA), Legal Services of New Jersey or a lawyer.

The state rule in one paragraph

The New Jersey housing maintenance rule on minimum temperature is N.J.A.C. 5:10-14.4. As summarized from the text on Cornell LII, it says that from October 1 through May 15 of the following year, each dwelling unit and each habitable room must be kept at least 68°F from 6:00 a.m. to 11:00 p.m. and at least 65°F from 11:00 p.m. to 6:00 a.m. The Cornell page lists the section as amended in 2023 (55 N.J.R. 1687(a), effective August 7, 2023). Older pages, including NJ 211, still say the season ends May 1, so check the current text before you quote an end date to a customer.

Who the state rule covers

N.J.A.C. 5:10 applies to hotels, retreat lodging and multiple dwellings (N.J.A.C. 5:10-1.4). The DCA habitability bulletin says the DCA Bureau of Housing Inspection enforces housing code violations in buildings with three or more units, and that one- and two-unit buildings are outside the Bureau's jurisdiction and follow local ordinances. So the 68 and 65 degree numbers are the state standard for larger rentals; a house with a basement apartment is a local-ordinance question. Many towns have their own heat rules, and they can be stricter or different. Mount Holly Township, for example, has an ordinance for residences with more than two families that requires the landlord to arrange emergency heat when heat is out for more than four hours. Look up the town code where the job is.

Who has to supply the heat

Under 5:10-14.4(c), the owner must supply the required fuel or energy and keep the heating system in good operating condition, even if a contract tries to push that duty onto the occupant or a third party. There is one narrow exception: the owner need not supply fuel for a unit whose occupant agrees in writing to provide it, if the unit has its own exclusive heating equipment and the heat can be separately computed and billed. In plain terms, a tenant paying the gas or electric bill does not by itself shift the duty to maintain the equipment. The written agreement and separate metering are what matter. Subsection (b) also says the owner is not responsible for a temperature drop caused by occupants leaving windows or doors open to the outside.

The rule also says the system must be able to reach the minimum in all habitable rooms without heating adjoining rooms more than 5 degrees above that minimum. That is a design and balance point a tech can see: a 78°F hallway next to a 62°F bedroom is a distribution problem, not a pass.

How a tenant reports no heat

The DCA bulletin says to call the local health agency for lack of heat and hot water. For multiple dwellings, NJ 211 lists the DCA Bureau of Housing Inspection at 609-633-6227 or BHIInspections@dca.nj.gov, and lists direct no-heat lines for some cities (Jersey City and Newark are 24-hour lines). The bulletin also says that if the landlord does not act after proper notice, the local board of health may act as the landlord's agent and order repairs, and that written notice by certified mail is advised. Tenant remedies such as repair and deduct or rent abatement are legal questions; refer the tenant to Legal Services of New Jersey (1-888-576-5529) rather than advising on them.

Worked example: a timeline check

A tenant in a six-unit building logs thermometer readings in the bedroom. Compare each with the minimum for its date and hour.

Result: three of the five in-season readings fall short, by 2, 1 and 1 degrees. The 5:45 a.m. reading passes only because the night minimum of 65 still applies until 6:00. The May 16 reading is outside the season in the current text, so the state minimum does not apply, though a local ordinance might. One caution: a logged reading is evidence, not a ruling. Whether a violation exists is for the inspector to decide, who will use their own measurement.

What an HVAC tech documents on a no-heat call in a rental

  • Time and date, who called, and who let you in (tenant, super, owner).
  • Indoor temperature in each habitable room you can reach, with the thermometer used and where you held it, plus the outdoor temperature.
  • System type and fuel (see what heats older NJ homes), and whether the tenant or owner pays for the fuel or electric.
  • What you found: empty oil tank, tripped switch, locked-out burner, boiler pressure (pressure guide), cold air from a furnace (cold air guide). Report facts, not blame. If the tenant opened windows or turned the thermostat down, note it neutrally.
  • What you did and what is still needed, with parts and a time estimate.
  • Safety findings. If you see signs of combustion or venting trouble, say so on the paperwork and tell the owner. Never leave a rental running on a plug-in heater without telling the owner; see the space heater lesson.
  • Photos of the thermostat, nameplate and any error code, if the customer allows.

Your invoice is not a legal opinion. Do not tell the tenant or owner who is legally at fault; give them the facts and the heating-rule text above, and point them to the local code office or DCA. For the diagnosis side, start with no-heat first checks.

Field card: NJ Landlord Heat Requirements: Minimum Temperatures and Heating Season

Season, two numbers, two hours.

  • Season in the current rule text: October 1 through May 15.
  • Minimums: 68°F from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m.; 65°F from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m.
  • Owner supplies fuel and keeps the system working, unless there is a written agreement plus exclusive, separately billed equipment.
  • State rule is for multiple dwellings (DCA: three or more units); smaller rentals follow local ordinances.
  • Report no heat to the local health agency; DCA Bureau of Housing Inspection for multiple dwellings.
  • Log time, room temps, outdoor temp, fuel, findings and work done.
  • Rules can change; confirm with your municipality or NJ DCA.
