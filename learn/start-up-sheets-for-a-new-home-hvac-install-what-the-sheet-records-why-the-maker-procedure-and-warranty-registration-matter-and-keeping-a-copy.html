Start-Up Sheets for a New Home HVAC Install: What Gets Recorded and Why

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When a new system is turned on, the installer should write down what it is doing. That one or two page record is the start-up sheet, also called a commissioning sheet. This lesson explains what goes on it, why each line is there, and how it helps the next person who works on your system.

What a start-up sheet is for

A start-up sheet is a snapshot of the system on its first day. A federal research review of residential installations found that most systems studied had at least one fault that hurt performance, and that duct leakage pushed the share even higher. Faults such as low airflow and wrong refrigerant charge turned up again and again. Writing the numbers down is how an installer shows those faults were checked, and how a later technician can tell whether a problem is new or was there from day one.

What the sheet records

  • Identity. Model and serial numbers for the outdoor unit, the indoor unit or furnace and the coil, plus the install date and address. These are what warranty registration and any later parts order depend on.
  • Electrical. Line voltage at the equipment and running amps for the compressor, outdoor fan and indoor blower. Compare the amps to the nameplate values.
  • Refrigerant. The charge method used (weighing in, or measuring superheat or subcooling), the readings, and the amount added or removed. See Superheat and Subcooling Basics. A federal measure guide says to let the system run 15 minutes after any refrigerant adjustment before repeating tests.
  • Airflow. Either a measured airflow or the temperature split across the coil, and the total external static pressure. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Gas furnaces. Gas supply and manifold pressure compared with the value on the furnace rating plate, and the temperature rise compared with the rise range on the same plate.
  • Combustion and carbon monoxide. Flue gas readings and a carbon monoxide check at the appliance. A university extension checklist for gas appliances tells the technician to test all combustion appliances for carbon monoxide and tells homeowners to install a detector near sleeping areas. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics.
  • Condensate. A test that water actually runs out of the drain, plus any safety float switch tested.
  • Thermostat. Settings chosen, such as heat pump or furnace staging, fan mode and any lockout temperatures.

Why the maker's procedure and warranty registration matter

Every maker publishes its own start-up steps and target values in the installation instructions, and they differ between models. The sheet should follow those steps, not a generic list. We suggest asking the installer which document they used. Also ask who registers the equipment for warranty and when, because warranty terms can depend on registration. We have not checked any one maker's terms, so read the warranty paperwork that comes with your equipment. Registration uses the model and serial numbers from the sheet, which is one more reason to get them written down correctly.

Why low airflow and charge top the list

A federal energy office summary of field studies reports incorrect refrigerant charge in 57 to 82 percent of the systems examined across several studies, and improper airflow in 44 percent of systems in one study. Another study cited there found 70 percent of units under 350 cfm per ton. The same summary says a properly installed system performs at its rated efficiency while an improperly installed one may perform about 10 percent lower. Those are field averages from research, not predictions for your house. They are why the sheet puts charge and airflow in writing.

Worked example (our own, with assumed readings)

All numbers below are our own assumptions for teaching. They are not from any real install, and your maker's targets will differ.

LineAssumed entry
Line voltage / compressor amps242 V / 15.2 A (assumed nameplate 17.0 A)
Charge methodSuperheat, fixed metering, 12 F; subcooling noted 10 F
Return and supply air at coil78 F and 58 F
Furnace return and supply air68 F and 128 F; assumed rating plate rise 35 to 65 F
Supply static / return static+0.38 in. w.c. / -0.27 in. w.c.; assumed plate limit 0.80 in. w.c.
Manifold gas pressureMatches the plate value (assumed)
Carbon monoxide, condensateWithin maker limit (assumed); drain tested, float switch tested

Check 1, coil temperature split. Return minus supply: 78 - 58 = 20 F. A federal measure guide compares the measured split to a target that depends on the air conditions, and treats a split within 3 F of the target as acceptable. It says a split well below target points to a capacity-reducing defect and one that is too high can point to low airflow. So the sheet should list the maker's or guide's target next to the 20 F. Our assumption does not include a target, so we cannot grade it here.

Check 2, furnace temperature rise. Supply minus return: 128 - 68 = 60 F. The assumed plate range is 35 to 65 F, so 60 F is inside it, with 5 F to spare at the high end. A rise near the top of the range can hint at restricted airflow, so compare it with the static pressure.

Check 3, total external static pressure. Add the size of the two readings, ignoring the sign: 0.38 + 0.27 = 0.65 in. w.c. Against the assumed 0.80 limit that leaves 0.80 - 0.65 = 0.15 in. w.c. of margin. The federal measure guide notes that field homes are often measured against a typical maker maximum of 0.8 in. w.c., and that an evaporator coil alone should not drop more than about 0.25 in. w.c. Recheck with your own unit's plate value.

Math check: 78 - 58 = 20; 128 - 68 = 60; 0.38 + 0.27 = 0.65; 0.80 - 0.65 = 0.15. Each calculation agrees with the table.

Keep a copy with the house records

Ask for a copy of the finished sheet at handoff, or a photo of it, and file it with the manual, warranty papers and permit paperwork. Add the date you registered the equipment. It is also worth taking a photo of the equipment data labels.

How the sheet helps the next service call

If the system acts up in three years, a technician can compare today's readings with the sheet. A higher static pressure suggests a clogged filter or coil. A smaller split suggests an airflow or charge change. A changed temperature rise points to blower or burner trouble. Without a baseline, the technician has only the maker's general numbers to go on. Field card: Start-Up Sheets

If it is not written down, we cannot compare it later.

  • Record model and serial numbers, voltage and amps.
  • Record charge method and readings, split, rise and static pressure.
  • Gas: manifold pressure, combustion and carbon monoxide readings.
  • Test the condensate drain and float switch.
  • Follow the maker's start-up steps and register the warranty.
  • Rise = supply minus return. Split = return minus supply. External static = supply plus return, signs ignored.
  • Keep a copy with the house records.
