Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A basement dehumidifier is cheap to buy and easy to buy wrong. The label on the box changed in 2019, the drain is where most people get frustrated, and a unit that is too small just runs forever. This lesson covers how big a unit to look for, where to set it, and how to get the water out.

Why basements get damp

Basement walls and floors touch cool soil, so the air next to them is cool, and cool air reaches high relative humidity without gaining any moisture. EPA's mold guidance lists the usual sources: plumbing leaks, clogged gutters, ground that does not slope away from the foundation, blocked air conditioner drain lines, and unvented moisture-making appliances like dryers. EPA also says condensation on walls or pipes is a sign of high humidity, and that you can cut it by reducing humidity, moving more air, insulating cold surfaces and raising the air temperature.

One limit matters before you spend money. A utility fact sheet from Minnesota Power puts it plainly: a dehumidifier is not an effective fix for basement seepage. If water is coming through the wall or floor, fix the gutters, grading or drainage first. A dehumidifier handles moisture in the air, not a leak. If the whole house feels sticky in summer, read House Feels Humid With the AC Running first, and see Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics for the bigger picture.

What humidity to aim for

EPA says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and notes that a humidity meter costs roughly $10 to $50. ENERGY STAR gives the same 30 to 50 percent as the generally optimum range and suggests 30 to 40 percent in cold climates during the heating season to avoid window condensation. Buy a small hygrometer, put it in the basement, and let it tell you what the room really does. Do not trust the dial on the machine alone.

Setpoints differ by model. A Whynter portable unit's manual allows 30 to 90 percent in 5 percent steps and suggests going at least 10 points below the current room reading. It also suggests staying under 50 percent for allergy control. One Yaufey manual only allows 40 to 80 percent and restarts the compressor at 4 points above the setting. So a "45 percent" target on one machine may be impossible on another. Check your manual.

The 2019 label change

ENERGY STAR explains that the Department of Energy's new test procedure, effective in 2019, tests portable dehumidifiers at 65°F instead of 80°F, to better reflect a basement. Cooler air holds less water, so the same machine now earns a smaller number. Nothing got worse. The ruler changed. ENERGY STAR publishes a rough, model-dependent correlation, and it matters if you are comparing a used unit or an old review with a new box.

Worked example: old 70-pint label versus the new rating

You find a "70 pint" dehumidifier from a few years back and want to know what it is worth today.

  1. Look it up in ENERGY STAR's table. An old 70 pints per day corresponds to roughly 40 to 45 pints per day under the new test.
  2. Ratio at the low end: 40 ÷ 70 = 0.57.
  3. Ratio at the high end: 45 ÷ 70 = 0.64.
  4. So the new number is about 57 to 64 percent of the old one, a drop of about 36 to 43 percent, from the same hardware.

Now size a basement. Say it is about 1,200 square feet, under ENERGY STAR's 2,000 square foot line, and your hygrometer reads about 80 percent in July. ENERGY STAR's "very damp" band is 75 to 90 percent, which calls for 25 to 40 pints per day for a space under 2,000 square feet (in the new ratings). A unit labeled 40 to 45 new pints, which is that old 70-pint class, sits at the top of the range. ENERGY STAR says it is better to oversize than undersize, since a humidistat will simply cycle a larger unit off sooner. Compare the numbers in the same units: a new "40 pint" box and an old "70 pint" box are close cousins.

Drainage options

  • Bucket. Simple, but a full bucket stops the machine. One manual says the unit alarms and shuts the compressor off when the tank is full, so a summer basement may fill it daily.
  • Gravity hose. Many portable units, including the models cited here, have a drain outlet for a garden hose. The Whynter manual says the unit must sit level or at least 12 inches off the floor, and the hose must run downhill from the outlet without kinks. A Yaufey manual specifies the hose end stay 5.5 inches below the outlet.
  • Built-in pump. If the floor drain or sink is higher than the machine, the Whynter manual describes a pump with a 16.5 foot hose. It will not run if the bucket is full, so empty it before switching over.

Low temperature and coil frost

The coil is cold, and in a cold basement it can ice. ENERGY STAR says frost can form when air is below 65°F and recommends anti-frost sensors for cold spaces. A GE manual says the same 65°F figure and describes auto-defrost, where the compressor shuts off and the fan keeps running until the coil warms. Whynter's manual says to avoid rooms under 53°F and to turn the unit off for a few hours if it freezes up. A basement in January is often a bad place for a compressor dehumidifier, and if the air is already cold, ice may be the machine telling you so.

Filter and coil care

Whynter says to clean the filter at least every two weeks and the bucket on the same schedule: vacuum or tap off dust, rinse with water no hotter than 100°F, and dry fully. A Yaufey manual also says every two weeks. Minnesota Power adds cleaning the coil once a year with a soft brush, using a spray bottle rather than a hose near electrical parts. Unplug first. EPA says to clean the unit regularly and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Portable or whole-house?

Portable units handle one room and can be moved. ENERGY STAR describes whole-home units as ducted into the home's air system, usually permanent, suited to humid climates with central air and relatively well-sealed homes where humidity stays above 55 percent, and installed by an HVAC professional who routes the drain. The same ENERGY STAR table shows whole-home ratings dropped too (for example 80 old to 70 new, 200 old to 170 new). If only the basement is the problem, a portable is usually the simpler first step.

For the tech

  • Read the room first: hygrometer, wall surface, and a look for seepage, leaks and downspouts. Do not sell a dehumidifier for liquid water.
  • Compare capacity on the same test year. Convert old ratings with ENERGY STAR's table and treat results as approximate; the page says changes vary by model.
  • Check the clearance a manual calls for (one lists 10 inches on all sides), level, and drain path.
  • For a pump, confirm it clears the bucket-full lockout and the discharge goes where local rules allow.
  • Defrost cycles are normal in cool rooms. Frost that will not clear points to room temperature, a dirty filter or a blocked coil.

Field card: Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage

Fix the water source, measure the room, then size.

  • Target: below 60 percent RH, ideally 30 to 50 (EPA).
  • Since 2019 portables are rated at 65°F; old 70 pints is about 40 to 45 pints now.
  • ENERGY STAR "very damp" (75-90 percent) under 2,000 sq ft: 25 to 40 pints per day.
  • Drain: bucket, gravity hose (downhill, no kinks), or built-in pump.
  • Frost below about 65°F; some makers say avoid under 53°F.
  • Clean the filter every two weeks, per the manuals cited.
