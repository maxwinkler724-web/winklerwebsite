Locker Rooms and Fitness Centers HVAC: Shower Exhaust Rates, Transfer Air and Humidity

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fitness center asks the air system to do two opposite jobs. The workout floor needs lots of fresh air and cooling for hard-breathing, sweating people. The locker room next door needs to be exhausted, kept slightly negative so steam and odor stay put, and kept dry enough that grout and ductwork do not rot. This lesson shows how the two connect, with a worked example you can follow with a calculator.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Cities and states adopt different editions of the International Mechanical Code (IMC) with their own amendments, and ASHRAE Standard 62.1 is a separate document. Everything below is general education from public pages read in 2026. Confirm rates with the code your inspector uses.

What the code asks for at showers and toilets

The IMC publishes a ventilation table, Table 403.3.1.1, that lists outdoor air rates for occupied spaces and exhaust rates for some rooms. We read Washington State's adopted version on the state legislature's web page. In it, a shower room is listed at 50/20 cfm per showerhead, and a public toilet room at 50/70 cfm per water closet or urinal. The footnotes say the higher number applies where the exhaust runs intermittently, and the lower number is allowed only where the exhaust runs continuously while the room is occupied. The table prints the two shower numbers in the opposite order from the toilet numbers, so we read the shower pair by the footnote: 20 cfm continuous, 50 cfm intermittent. Check the table in your own adopted edition before you size anything.

Another footnote on those rows matters for design: mechanical exhaust is required, and recirculating air from these rooms is generally prohibited. Limited exceptions exist, such as air that stays entirely inside those spaces, or energy recovery return that meets a separate section. The upshot is simple. Showers and toilets get ducted exhaust that goes outdoors, and that air never returns to the gym air handler.

The same table gives outdoor air rates for the workout floor: for a health club weight room, 10 people per 1,000 square feet, 20 cfm per person and 0.06 cfm per square foot. Those values are the code's starting point; the full ventilation-rate method comes from ASHRAE 62.1, which we name here in plain text only.

Where the make-up air comes from

Every cfm exhausted from the locker room has to be replaced by something. A trade-press article on fitness facility IAQ in Consulting-Specifying Engineer notes that locker room make-up air does not have to be 100 percent outdoor air, and that transfer air from adjacent spaces can serve as make-up, often through wall or ceiling transfer grilles. The same article warns that locker room air is a higher-contamination class and cannot be returned and mixed into cleaner spaces such as offices.

That fits how a gym works. The workout floor already receives outdoor air for its occupants, and that air has to go somewhere. Letting it pass through a transfer grille into the locker room, then out the shower and toilet exhaust, uses the same air twice instead of heating or cooling a second outdoor air stream. It is not free: the energy to condition that air was already spent at the gym unit. We just avoid doing it twice.

Keeping the locker room negative

A locker room should end up with slightly more air leaving than arriving by design, so air leaks into it from the gym and the lobby instead of steam and smell leaking out. That means exhaust slightly above transfer air, with the shortfall made up by small leakage at door undercuts or a pass-through. The article above also warns about the other side of the coin: if the whole building runs negative, unfiltered and unconditioned air is pulled in through doors and cracks, so the building air balance should be done on paper and field-checked. See our building pressurization lesson for door and stack effect problems.

Worked example: our own locker room

Every input below is an assumption we chose for teaching, except the code rates quoted above.

  • Locker room has 6 showerheads, 4 water closets and 2 urinals (6 toilet fixtures). Assumption.
  • Gym weight room is 2,000 square feet. Assumption.
  • Winter design outdoor temperature 15°F, indoor 70°F, so a 55°F rise. Assumption.
  • The locker room has no supply of its own. Assumption.

Step 1, exhaust (continuous, lower rates). Showers: 6 × 20 = 120 cfm. Toilets: 6 × 50 = 300 cfm. Total 420 cfm. If the system ran only when someone pressed a switch (intermittent), the higher rates would apply: 6 × 50 = 300, plus 6 × 70 = 420, total 720 cfm.

Step 2, gym outdoor air. Table values for a weight room: 10 people per 1,000 square feet × 2 = 20 people. People part: 20 × 20 cfm = 400 cfm. Area part: 0.06 × 2,000 = 120 cfm. Total 520 cfm of outdoor air. We assume full effectiveness for simplicity, which is an assumption, and real design applies ASHRAE 62.1 adjustments.

Step 3, transfer air available. We assume 100 cfm of gym air leaves through the entry doors and envelope, so 520 − 100 = 420 cfm can move toward the locker room. We design for slightly less so the room stays negative: 380 cfm through the transfer grille, with 40 cfm drawn in at the door undercut (420 − 380).

Step 4, heating load of the air. We use the sensible heat equation Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise, explained in our heat transfer formulas lesson. The arithmetic is ours.

  • Gym outdoor air: 1.08 × 520 × 55 = 30,888 Btu/h.
  • Of that, the part that rides into the locker room: 1.08 × 420 × 55 = 24,948 Btu/h. It is already counted at the gym unit, so it adds no new outdoor air load.
  • If the exhaust ran intermittent at 720 cfm, the shortfall is 720 − 380 = 340 cfm minus the 40 cfm door leak, about 300 cfm that needs more outdoor air, either by raising the gym intake or by adding a small make-up air unit: 1.08 × 300 × 55 = 17,820 Btu/h.
  • For comparison, if all 720 cfm were separate outdoor air: 1.08 × 720 × 55 = 42,768 Btu/h.

So continuous low-rate exhaust, fed by transfer air, adds no new outdoor air load in this example. The intermittent case adds about 17,820 Btu/h. That is why the lower continuous rates are worth a controls conversation. Transfer works only while the gym unit is running, so exhaust and gym supply should be interlocked to run together, including overnight setback.

Sweat loads on the workout floor

People working hard give off far more heat and moisture than people sitting. A general engineering reference table, at 78°F room air, lists a gymnasium or heavy work at about 510 Btu/h sensible and 940 Btu/h latent per person. For 20 people that is 10,200 sensible and 18,800 latent Btu/h. Dividing the latent by about 1,060 Btu per pound of water gives 17.7 pounds of water per hour, roughly 2 gallons, going into the air (our arithmetic, with 1,060 as our rounded value). That is why a gym that is cooled to the right temperature can still feel clammy, and why the cooling coil needs to run cold enough to dry the air, not just cool it. Our natatorium lesson covers the extreme end of the same problem.

Humidity, mold and corrosion

The EPA's moisture and mold guide advises keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and drying wet areas within 24 to 48 hours. A trade article on wet-area maintenance says mold can start in as little as 24 hours under the right conditions and describes rust on light fixtures from steam and shower humidity. Practical points follow:

  • Exhaust grilles over showers see the most moisture, so choose corrosion-resistant grille and duct materials and slope duct runs back toward a drain point or the fan housing so condensation does not run backward into the room.
  • Insulate exhaust ducts that pass through cold spaces so they do not sweat.
  • Use humidity sensors on a controller that boosts exhaust or cooling when humidity climbs; a humidistat is one of the tools the article names.
  • Give each grille a cleaning interval. Sweat, soap spray and lint load them quickly.

For basic bath fan duct and moisture habits, read our bathroom exhaust fan lesson; the commercial case is the same physics at larger scale.

Saunas and steam rooms are separate

Do not treat a sauna or steam room as another shower. A health and fitness association's sauna guidance says to install two vents in a dry sauna, one low behind the heater and one high on another wall. For steam rooms it says to avoid vents or fans inside the room and to put exhaust near the door, and to prop the door open when the facility is closed. Steam also needs vapor-proof construction. In practice each room gets its own exhaust path, not a share of the locker room duct, so steam does not overwhelm the locker room balance. Specific rates for these rooms were not in the public sources we read, so follow the equipment maker's instructions and the local code.

Field card: Locker Room and Gym Ventilation

Exhaust outside, make up with gym air, keep it negative.

  • Showers and toilets need ducted exhaust to the outdoors; no recirculation back to the gym unit (IMC Table 403.3.1.1 note, as adopted in one state).
  • Table pairs: higher number for intermittent exhaust, lower for continuous. Check the pair order in your edition.
  • Example: 6 showers at 20 plus 6 toilet fixtures at 50 is 420 cfm continuous.
  • Transfer air from the gym can serve as make-up. Interlock gym supply and locker exhaust.
  • Exhaust a little above transfer so the room stays negative.
  • Heating load of air: 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise.
  • Keep humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50, and dry wet areas in 24 to 48 hours.
  • Saunas and steam rooms get their own exhaust; steam exhaust goes near the door.
