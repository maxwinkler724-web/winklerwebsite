Blast Chillers and Soft Serve Machines: Cooling Times, Loads and Service Calls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two pieces of kitchen refrigeration get called "just a cooler" and are not. A blast chiller is built to beat a food safety clock. A soft serve machine is a freezer, a pasteurizer-style heat cycle and a cold hopper in one cabinet. Both live or die on airflow and clean condensers.

The cooling rule the chiller exists for

FDA's employee fact sheet on the Food Code says cooked time/temperature control for safety food must drop from 135 F to 70 F within 2 hours, then to 41 F or less in the remaining 4 hours, so 6 hours in total (FDA; the 2026 edition is current per the FDA Food Code page). A state agriculture department handout says the same two stages and adds that you should check the food with a calibrated probe thermometer at regular intervals so you can change methods early. The first stage is the hard one, because the food is hottest and the 2-hour clock is short. If 70 F is reached early, the leftover time carries to the second stage.

The rule is about the food, not the box. Check with your local health department for the version it enforces. We could not confirm the cooling wording of New Jersey's retail food rules on a public state page, so we do not quote it here.

Why a reach-in or walk-in cannot do the job

A normal cooler is sized to hold cold food, not to pull heat out of a big pot. See cold holding at 41 F for the holding side. A 2013 Food Protection Trends study put chili and tomato sauce in 2-inch and 3-inch pans in a walk-in refrigerator, a walk-in freezer and a walk-in with an ice bath. Only the walk-in freezer with 2-inch pans met both the time and temperature limits. The South Carolina handout also warns that putting large hot batches in a refrigerator can raise the unit's temperature, which hurts the food already inside. Dumping hot product into a box built for holding is how a box fails its own temperature check, and it is one reason a reach-in's evaporator ices up and drifts (see reach-in defrost and care).

How a blast chiller cools fast

It is the same refrigeration cycle with two changes. First, a low coil temperature: one maker lists 4,837 W of refrigeration power at an evaporating temperature of minus 10 C on its 40 kg model. Second, high air velocity: a retailer guide says the cabinet uses powerful fans to push cold air across all exposed surfaces at once, which is why pan spacing is generous. Heat leaves a thick food slowly by conduction, so the surface air must stay far colder than the food and be replaced constantly. That is also why shallow pans matter: the Food Code fact sheet lists shallow pans and thinner portions among recommended methods.

Probe cycles

The buying guide describes two ways to end a cycle. A probe-driven cycle stops when the core of the food reaches its target. A timed cycle runs a set time whatever the food does, and the guide calls probe-driven the better default. The maker page above includes a single-sensor core probe as standard. In service terms, a probe that is not in the thickest part of the food, or one that has drifted, makes the chiller stop early or run long. Check it in an ice bath against a thermometer you trust.

Worked example: our own pull-down load

Question: is a chiller of the 40 kg size above big enough for a batch of cooked stew with a 90-minute target cycle? Every input below is an assumption unless a source is named.

  • Food mass: 80 lb of cooked food (Our own assumption; 40 kg on the maker page is about 88 lb).
  • Specific heat: 0.80 Btu per lb per F (Our own assumption for a wet cooked dish; the Engineering ToolBox table lists meats between 0.56 and 0.88 and vegetables near 0.9, so measure or look up your own product).
  • Drop: 135 F to 41 F, which is 94 F.
  • Target cycle: 90 minutes, or 1.5 h (Our own assumption for the machine setting).
  • Ignored: the pans, racks and fan motor heat, and any latent heat. Those add real load, so this is a floor.

Step 1, heat to remove: 80 lb x 0.80 Btu/lb/F x 94 F = 6,016 Btu.

Step 2, rate over the cycle: 6,016 Btu / 1.5 h = about 4,011 Btu/h.

Step 3, tons: 4,011 / 12,000 Btu/h per ton = about 0.33 ton.

Step 4, the chiller's rating: 4,837 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = about 16,504 Btu/h, or 1.38 tons, at the maker's stated evaporating temperature of minus 10 C.

The average load is about a quarter of the rating, so the size looks comfortable on paper. Do not stop there. Load is not even: the first minutes carry the most heat because the food is hottest and the air-to-food difference is largest. Pans, racks and fan heat sit on top. The rated figure only holds with a clean condenser, free airflow and a room within the maker's limits (the page lists 16 C to 40 C). Treat the margin as what is left for a dirty coil or a hot kitchen, and check the Food Code stage times with a probe, not with arithmetic. For the first stage alone: 80 x 0.80 x 65 F = 4,160 Btu in 2 h, about 2,080 Btu/h.

Why cycles run long

  • Condenser. A self-contained unit dumps its heat into the kitchen, and a greasy coil raises head pressure, as in dirty condenser and head pressure. Clearances are model-specific; the maker page above lists 5 cm at the sides and back.
  • Airflow inside. Crowded or covered pans, blocked evaporator fan discharge or an iced evaporator choke the air that does the work.
  • Loading. Batches over the rated weight, or food loaded at far above 135 F, or in deep pans.
  • Probe. Misplaced or drifted, as above.

Soft serve and frozen yogurt machines

A soft serve machine has two cold systems: a freezing cylinder, where mix is chilled and whipped with a beater and scraper blades, and a hopper that stores mix. One maker says its model keeps the mix under 41 F in the hopper and runs a daily heating and cooling cycle so dairy can stay in the machine up to 28 days before full disassembly and cleaning. Its manual gives the heat treatment as reaching 151 F within 90 minutes, holding above 151 F for at least 35 minutes, then cooling below 41 F within 90 minutes on one control version or 120 minutes on another. A failed cycle drops the machine to standby and locks out Auto. Standby keeps hopper and cylinder under 40 F. These are one maker's figures; follow the manual for your model and your health department's rules.

Air-cooled condenser clearances

The same manual lists 6 inches at the sides and 0 at the rear for one model and 3 inches on all sides plus a supplied deflector for another, and says too little clearance can cut capacity and may damage the compressor. It calls for brushing the condenser monthly, never with a screwdriver or metal probe between fins, and vacuuming air filters monthly. Compare with cleaning a condenser coil.

Common service calls

  • Compressor high-pressure cutout. The manual says to power off, wait 5 minutes, power on and restart. If it repeats, look for a dirty condenser or blocked airflow first.
  • "Compressor on too long." The manual's fault triggers after 11 minutes of run time; it says to replace scraper blades at the next brush-clean, and to call a technician if that does not fix it.
  • Beater overload. Power off, wait 5 minutes, press the reset, restart.
  • Hopper or barrel thermistor failure. The manual says call an authorized technician.
  • Hopper warm. The manual faults the unit if the hopper stays above 45 F for over an hour or above 41 F for over four hours, so a warm-mix call is a food safety call first.

The manual is a good model for what to ask on a call: error text, when the last heat cycle ran, and when the condenser was last cleaned. Freezing-cylinder refrigerant work follows the same evaporator and cycle ideas you already know.

Field card: Blast Chillers and Soft Serve Machines: Cooling Times, Loads and Service Calls

Fast air, cold coil, clean condenser, probe in the middle.

  • Cooked food: 135 F to 70 F within 2 h, then to 41 F, 6 h total (FDA).
  • Walk-ins and reach-ins hold cold food; they are not cooling equipment.
  • Long chiller cycle: check condenser, clearances, pan spacing, evaporator ice, probe.
  • Load: lb x specific heat x temperature drop, then divide by hours; 12,000 Btu/h = 1 ton.
  • Soft serve: hopper under 41 F, daily heat cycle, monthly condenser brushing, follow the model manual.
