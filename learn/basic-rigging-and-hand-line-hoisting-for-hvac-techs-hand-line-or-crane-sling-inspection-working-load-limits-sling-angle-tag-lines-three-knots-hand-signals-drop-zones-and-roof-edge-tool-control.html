Basic Rigging and Hand-Line Hoisting for HVAC Techs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Sooner or later a tech has to get a motor, a coil, a toolbag or a section of duct up to a roof or into a mechanical room without a truck-mounted lift. A rope and a pulley can do that for light parts. They can also drop a load on a coworker. This lesson covers the rigging basics that sit between "carry it up the ladder" and "call a crane," and where to draw the line.

Check your own rules first

This is study material, not a rigging qualification. Hands-on training from a qualified person, your site rules and the equipment markings come first. Federal rules cited below are for construction work; your job may fall under other rules or contract requirements.

Hand line, gin wheel, or something bigger

A hand line is a rope run over a sheave, often a single pulley called a gin wheel hung from a sound anchor at the roof edge. A tech on the ground ties on a bucket or bundle, and a second tech hauls it up. It suits light, compact, easy-to-tie loads: tools, small parts, fittings, a few lengths of tubing.

Our own rule of thumb for deciding to stop and call for help: if the load is heavy enough that one person cannot hold it steady by hand at the haul rope, if it is awkward or has a high center of gravity, if you cannot read its weight, if the anchor is not clearly sound, or if the load must travel over people, a doorway or a walkway, do not use a hand line. Use a qualified rigger, a crane or a manufactured lifting device. Weigh the part from its nameplate or submittal, then compare it with the marked limit of every piece of gear in the chain. A rooftop unit swap is a crane job; see Replacing a Rooftop Unit. Carrying and team lifts are covered in Moving Heavy HVAC Equipment Safely, so we do not repeat them here.

Inspect rope and slings before every shift

The federal construction rigging rule, 29 CFR 1926.251, says rigging equipment must be inspected before use on each shift and as needed during use, and that defective equipment must be removed from service. Slings are checked by a competent person designated by the employer, and damaged or defective slings are removed from service immediately.

  • Look for the tag. The rule calls for permanent, legible identification markings with the rated capacity. For wire rope and fiber rope slings the marking must also show the hitch and the angle the capacity is based on. No tag, no lift.
  • Fiber rope and web slings: cuts, tears, punctures, broken stitches, broken or cut fibers, rot and abnormal wear mean the sling comes out. A university safety office adds melted or burned spots, embedded particles, exposed warning yarn and knots to its removal list.
  • Wire rope slings: kinks, crushed or distorted rope, burns, corrosion, and broken wires beyond the rule's limit (more than 10 percent of the wires in one eight-diameter length).
  • Hardware: do not use job-made hooks or links. Never shorten a sling with a knot, bolt or makeshift device.
  • Protect the sling from sharp corners of the load, and keep nylon and polyester slings below the rule's 180 degree F limit.

Working load limit and never exceeding it

Every sling, shackle and hook has a rated capacity. University safety offices call it the working load limit, or WLL. The federal rule says rated capacity may not be exceeded and that shock loading is prohibited. Shock loading means a jerk or drop that makes the real force far larger than the weight. Haul smoothly, and lift a few inches first to check balance and the rigging before you continue.

Sling angle: why flatter legs pull harder

When two sling legs share a load, each leg carries part of the weight along its own length. The flatter the leg, the more of its pull goes sideways instead of up, so the tension climbs. A university rigging page puts it simply: the smaller the angle, the less load a sling can safely carry. A second university page says to allow for the increased tension caused by sling angles. That is why capacity tags state an angle, and why we measure the angle from horizontal when we do the math below.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, ours, for illustration only: a 600 lb part hangs from a two-leg sling; the legs are equal in length; the load is balanced; the weight of the sling and hardware is ignored; each leg is rated for at least the tension we compute at the angle used. Angles are measured from horizontal.

Each leg carries half the load vertically, so its vertical pull is 600 / 2 = 300 lb. The leg tension is that vertical share divided by the sine of the angle from horizontal: T = 300 / sin(angle).

  • 60 degrees: sin 60 = 0.866. T = 300 / 0.866 = 346 lb per leg.
  • 30 degrees: sin 30 = 0.500. T = 300 / 0.500 = 600 lb per leg.

At 30 degrees each leg carries a force equal to the entire weight of the part, even though two legs are sharing it. The flatter angle raised the leg tension by 600 / 346 = 1.73 times. If the tag says 500 lb at that angle, the lift is not allowed, even though a 600 lb part looks like it is well within the "two legs" idea. The fix is longer legs, a spreader device, or a larger sling rated for the angle used. Always take the actual capacity from the tag and the maker's tables.

Tag lines, signals and a clear drop zone

  • Tag lines. A university safety manual describes tag lines as a way to position a load while keeping people at a safe distance, and to control swing during travel. Attach them before the lift. Do not wrap the line around a hand or body.
  • One signal person. For crane work the federal rule says only one person may give signals at a time, with the exception that anyone may give a stop signal. Standard hand signals are the expected method. Agree on signals before the lift, even on a hand line, and pick one person to call them.
  • Never stand under a load. The federal crane rule keeps employees out of the fall zone while a load is suspended, with narrow exceptions for hooking, unhooking and guiding by a qualified rigger. Treat the area under any hoist as off limits.
  • Barricade the drop zone. Where objects can fall from a roof, federal fall-protection rules call for barricading the area below and keeping materials far enough back from the edge not to go over if bumped. Use tape or cones with someone watching the doorway.
  • Keep clear of power lines. One university guide says to keep at least 10 feet from energized lines. Ask the line owner before working closer.

Three knots, in plain words

We will not try to teach knots from text; learn them hands-on with someone who ties them for a living. In plain terms:

  • Bowline: makes a fixed loop that does not slip or tighten on itself. It is the usual way to make an eye in the end of a rope.
  • Clove hitch: two wraps around a post or pipe that grip under steady pull. It can loosen if the pull comes and goes, so back it up.
  • Two half hitches: a simple way to tie a rope end off to a ring, rail or load. Seat them snug against the object.

A knot weakens rope, so a tied rope is rated lower than a straight one, and we never put knots in a sling.

Roof-edge housekeeping

Most dropped objects are not hoisted loads. They are a socket, a panel screw or a roll of tape that slid off a curb. Keep tools and parts well back from the edge, tie off hand tools you carry near it, use a bucket with a lid or a bag, and set a barricade below before the first trip. Fall protection for you is in Ladder and Roof Fall Safety for HVAC Work.

Field card: Basic Rigging and Hand-Line Hoisting

Light loads, sound anchor, marked gear. Anything else, get a rigger.

  • Inspect rope, slings and hardware before every shift; remove damaged gear.
  • No tag, no lift. Never exceed the working load limit.
  • Flatter sling angle means higher leg tension: 600 lb on two legs is 346 lb per leg at 60 degrees and 600 lb at 30 degrees.
  • Use tag lines to control swing; lift a few inches to test.
  • One signal person; anyone can call stop.
  • Barricade the drop zone; never stand under a load.
  • Keep tools and parts back from the roof edge.
