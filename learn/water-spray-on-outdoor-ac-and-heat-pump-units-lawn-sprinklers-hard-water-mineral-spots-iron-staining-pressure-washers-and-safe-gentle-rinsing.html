Water Spray on Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Units: Sprinklers, Hard Water, Iron Stains and Pressure Washers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An outdoor air conditioner or heat pump is built to sit in the rain. It is not built to be hit by a sprinkler every morning, soaked with mineral-rich water, or blasted with a pressure washer. This lesson explains what that water does to the coil and the electrical parts, how to rinse the unit safely, and when crust on the fins is a job for a technician.

Rain is fine, daily spray is not

Rain falls, runs off and dries with clean water. A sprinkler head that reaches the cabinet does something different: it wets the same fins at the same time every day with whatever the water supply carries. When that water dries, the dissolved minerals stay behind. Over a summer, a thin white or tan film builds on the fins, and under it the aluminum and copper can corrode. The coil is the part of the unit that moves heat into the air, so anything that clogs the narrow gaps between fins costs capacity. A coil that has lost area makes the compressor work harder, as the worked example below shows.

A state university extension page on home irrigation says sprinkler heads should stand upright, perpendicular to the soil, and that tilted heads warp the spray pattern. It also says heads should not be placed so they throw water toward hard surfaces, and should sit at the edge of a hard surface throwing water back into the landscape. That is the lawn-care reason to fix overspray, but it works for the unit too. Heads that get knocked crooked by a mower are a common cause of spray reaching the cabinet.

Hard water leaves spots that build into crust

A state university extension page says hard water carries high levels of calcium and magnesium picked up from limestone and other rock, and lists mineral deposits called scale as a common nuisance. A federal geological survey page sorts water by hardness in milligrams per liter as calcium carbonate: 0 to 60 is soft, 61 to 120 moderately hard, 121 to 180 hard, and above 180 very hard. The same page says scale forms when hard water is heated and builds up over time in pipes. Coil fins are not heated by the water, but the spots left behind as droplets evaporate are the same minerals, and a daily wet and dry cycle adds a new layer every time. The harder the supply, the faster the film grows. Your water utility or a well test can tell you which category you are in.

Reclaimed and well water can stain

Some irrigation systems run on well water or on a separate non-drinking supply. A state university extension page on iron in turf irrigation water says high iron mostly causes staining of concrete, plugging of irrigation equipment and clogged drainage tile. Rust-orange spray on a white cabinet or on aluminum fins is the same effect. Iron stains are usually cosmetic at first, but the film is still a deposit on the coil, and the water that causes it is the water that keeps hitting the unit. The same page notes that a lab report can understate iron in a rust-colored sample if the sample was filtered, so a clean-looking report does not clear water that visibly stains. Fixing the aim of the sprinklers is cheaper than treating the water.

Pressure washers and siding or deck washing

A university energy extension page on cleaning condensing units warns against a conventional pressure washer or any high-pressure spray, because it can damage the fins and permanently reduce airflow. It calls the aluminum fins fragile. A flattened fin area blocks the air as much as dirt does, and it does not wash out. See Bent and Flattened Coil Fins for what a technician can and cannot straighten.

There is a second danger. A hard jet can force water past door seals, wire openings and panel gaps into the electrical box, where it can wet the contactor, capacitor and wiring. Even a good-looking unit can trip a breaker or fail days later after water gets in. The same risk applies when you wash siding or a deck near the unit. Before washing, turn off the unit at its disconnect and breaker, then cover or shield the cabinet from overspray and from the wash water that runs off the wall. Chemical cleaners meant for siding or decking can also corrode coil metal, so keep them away from the unit. For cleaners that are and are not suited to coils, see Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil: What to Use and What to Avoid. This lesson does not repeat that list.

Safe, gentle rinsing

The university energy extension page says to turn off all electrical connections before cleaning, that judicious use of a garden hose is often all that is needed, and to use a mist or rain setting rather than a hard stream. It also says to spray from the inside of the coil to the outside when possible. If that is not possible, aim downward so water pushes dirt down and away. The federal energy agency maintenance page says outdoor condenser coils can get very dirty near dust and foliage, and recommends trimming plants back at least 2 feet from the unit.

  1. Turn the unit off at the thermostat, then open the outdoor disconnect and switch off the breaker. See AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers.
  2. Remove leaves and cobwebs by hand. Do not take off the cabinet or touch wiring.
  3. Use a garden hose on a soft shower or mist setting, held back from the fins, and rinse gently.
  4. Where you can see through the coil from the fan side, rinse from the inside toward the outside, which pushes debris out the way it came in. If you cannot reach the inside without removing panels, rinse from the outside at a downward angle and stop.
  5. Keep the spray off the electrical box and the wire openings.
  6. Let the unit dry before restoring power.

Rinsing helps with loose dirt and fresh film. It does not dissolve baked-on mineral crust.

Fix the cause: aim the sprinklers away

  • Straighten tilted heads so they are upright, as the extension page describes.
  • Turn the spray pattern or the arc away from the unit, or switch to a head with a smaller throw near the pad.
  • Check that spray cannot reach the cabinet in wind. Mist drifts farther than the main stream.
  • Keep soaker lines and drip lines from soaking the pad or running water into the cabinet.
  • If a head cannot be aimed away, ask the irrigation installer to cap or move it. Do not leave a head throwing at the unit.

A mower or trimmer can knock a head crooked again, so look again after lawn work.

When mineral crust needs a pro

Call a technician when the fin gaps are visibly bridged with hard white or tan crust, when rust-colored staining has spread across the coil, when fins are bent over an area wider than a hand, when the unit trips a breaker after getting wet, or when the cabinet has been hit by a pressure washer. The technician can check airflow, fin condition and the electrical compartment, and can choose a coil-safe cleaning method. Do not scrape crust with a knife or wire brush; that bends the fins.

Worked example (our own): what lost coil area costs

This is our own simplified example. Every input below is an assumption, not a measurement, and it is not a prediction for any real unit.

Assumptions. (1) A clean unit condenses refrigerant 20°F above the outdoor air. (2) Heat rejected stays the same, and heat transfer is proportional to coil area times temperature difference, so if effective coil area drops, the temperature difference must rise in proportion. (3) As a rule of thumb, compressor power rises about 1.5 percent for each 1°F rise in condensing temperature. This 1.5 percent figure is our assumed rule of thumb, not a sourced value. (4) A compressor drawing 2.4 kW when clean. (5) Two cases: 15 percent and 30 percent of coil area effectively lost to mineral film or bent fins.

Case A, 15 percent lost. Remaining area is 100 - 15 = 85 percent, or 0.85. New temperature difference = 20 / 0.85 = 23.53°F. Rise = 23.53 - 20 = 3.53°F. Power rise = 3.53 × 1.5 = 5.3 percent. Extra power = 2.4 × 0.053 = 0.127 kW, about 0.13 kW.

Case B, 30 percent lost. Remaining area is 0.70. New temperature difference = 20 / 0.70 = 28.57°F. Rise = 8.57°F. Power rise = 8.57 × 1.5 = 12.9 percent. Extra power = 2.4 × 0.129 = 0.31 kW.

Read the result. Losing 15 percent of the coil raises the condensing temperature by about 3.5°F, and losing 30 percent raises it by about 8.6°F, nearly 2.4 times as much from only twice the lost area. The effect grows faster than the loss. A real coil loses airflow as well as surface, so the true effect can differ. The point is the direction and the shape of the curve, not the exact numbers.

Field card: Water Spray on Outdoor AC and Heat Pump Units: Sprinklers, Hard Water, Iron Stains and Pressure Washers

Soft water on fins, never hard spray at the box.

  • Daily sprinkler spray leaves mineral film on the coil. Aim heads away.
  • Hard water is calcium and magnesium. Iron in well water leaves rust stains.
  • No pressure washer. It bends fins and forces water into the electrical box.
  • Power off first. Garden hose on mist, inside to outside.
  • Bridged crust, spreading rust or wide bent areas need a technician.
  • Our example (assumed inputs): 15 percent area lost gives about 3.5°F higher condensing temperature and 5.3 percent more power.
