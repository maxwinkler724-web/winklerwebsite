PEX in Hot Water Heating: Oxygen Barrier, Expansion and Support

Last updated: 2026-10-16

PEX is easy to run, but plastic tubing behaves differently from copper once it carries hot boiler water. It lets a little oxygen through its wall, it grows noticeably when it heats up, and it sags if it is hung too far apart. This lesson covers all three, using the makers' own documents.

Check the maker's paper

Check the maker's paper. Every number below comes from a manufacturer or industry document and applies to that product. Tubing from another maker, or a different wall thickness, can differ. The printed marking on the tubing and the installation guide for that exact product win over this page.

PEX-a, PEX-b and PEX-c

PEX is cross-linked polyethylene. The Plastics Pipe Institute (PPI) says the letters name the way the polymer chains are linked, not a quality grade: PEX-a uses organic peroxides during extrusion, PEX-b uses a silane process that finishes with moisture and heat after extrusion, and PEX-c exposes the extruded pipe to an electron beam. PPI adds that every approved method must meet the same standards, including ASTM F876. For heating work the better questions are whether the tubing has an oxygen barrier and what its rating is.

Why iron parts need an oxygen barrier

Polymer pipe is slightly permeable to oxygen. REHAU's bulletin on oxygen diffusion notes that radiant and snow melt systems use hundreds or thousands of feet of pipe, so the wall area is large, and concrete does not stop the gas. Oxygen that gets into a closed heating loop can corrode iron and steel parts such as the boiler, pumps, valves and piping. That is why heating tubing is sold in two kinds: plain plumbing tubing, and barrier tubing with a thin oxygen diffusion layer (REHAU describes a co-extruded EVOH layer; Uponor describes the same kind of layer on its hePEX tubing).

The yardstick is the German standard DIN 4726. REHAU's bulletin gives the current limit as 0.32 mg of oxygen per square meter of pipe wall per day at 40°C (about 104°F). The bulletin says that if pipe is worse than that, you need another protective measure. Uponor lists hePEX as meeting DIN 4726 and REHAU lists its barrier pipe as below the limit. Look for those words on the tubing and data sheet. SharkBite's guide shows the barrier marking as optional on the printed line, so read the print, not just the color.

Do not assume a bare PEX-a, b or c coil from the plumbing aisle is barrier tubing. If it is not, the sources we read point to one of three answers covered in Radiant Floor Heating Basics: use barrier tubing, isolate the tubing from the iron parts with a heat exchanger, or treat the water. For what oxygen does inside a loop once it arrives, see Air Elimination and Dirt Separation.

Reading the printed ratings

PEX is rated by pressure at a given water temperature. Uponor's hePEX specification sheet lists 160 psi at 73.4°F, 100 psi at 180°F and 80 psi at 200°F, and cites ASTM F876 and ASTM F877 for the tubing and fittings. PPI gives the same 160 psi at 73°F and 100 psi at 180°F for SDR 9 tubing and says to check the maker's literature because the rating depends on wall thickness. REHAU's data sheet matches, adds a design factor of 0.50, and calls the 200°F line an elevated-temperature application. REHAU's plumbing guide says its warranty covers operation at or below 180°F.

The hotter the water, the lower the pressure the tubing may see. Check that the boiler's high limit and relief valve settings stay inside the tubing's rating. For the flow side, see Hot Water Heating Pipe Sizing.

How much PEX grows

Uponor's specification sheet states a linear expansion rate of 1.1 in. per 10°F per 100 ft of tubing. SharkBite's guide gives roughly 1 in. for the same 100 ft and 10°F. REHAU gives a coefficient that changes with temperature: 9.33 x 10-4 in. per foot per °F at 68°F, rising to 1.33 x 10-3 at 212°F. A long run tied down tight at both ends has nowhere to grow, so it can buckle or push on its fittings.

REHAU's plumbing guide says that anchors, guides and expansion loops should be used to control the movement, and SharkBite's guide leaves a small amount of slack between supports. In practice that means an offset, loop or gentle snake, with the tubing free to slide in loose hangers rather than pinched to the framing.

Worked example: a 60 ft run of 3/4 in. PEX

Invented job: a straight 60 ft run of 3/4 in. barrier PEX, installed at 60°F and heated to 180°F. Use Uponor's 1.1 in. per 10°F per 100 ft, and the 32 in. horizontal spacing that REHAU and SharkBite both give for tubing 1 in. and smaller.

  1. Temperature change: 180 − 60 = 120°F.
  2. Number of 10°F steps: 120 ÷ 10 = 12.
  3. Growth of 100 ft: 12 x 1.1 in. = 13.2 in.
  4. Scale to 60 ft: 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6, and 13.2 x 0.6 = 7.92 in., about 7 7/8 in.
  5. Cross-check with REHAU's coefficient: 60 ft x 120°F = 7,200 ft-°F. At 9.33 x 10-4 that is 6.7 in., and at 1.33 x 10-3 it is 9.6 in. The 7.9 in. answer sits between them, so it is reasonable.
  6. Convert the run to inches: 60 x 12 = 720 in.
  7. Spaces between supports: 720 ÷ 32 = 22.5, so round up to 23 spaces.
  8. Hangers: 23 spaces need 24 support points counting both ends.

Result: the run lengthens by about 7.9 in. when it reaches 180°F, and it needs about 24 supports. Nearly 8 inches is far too much to bury in a rigid run, so the layout must give that growth somewhere to go (an offset, a loop or a gentle snake), with the hangers loose enough for the tubing to slide. This is a planning estimate; the maker's guide and local code set the final spacing.

Support spacing and rises

Spacing varies by maker and use. REHAU's plumbing guide gives 32 in. horizontal for sizes up to 1 in., 48 in. for larger, and 10 ft maximum vertical, with a support at each floor on risers. SharkBite gives 32 in. for sizes 1 in. and smaller. REHAU's outdoor wood boiler guide says every 3 ft when nothing else is specified. Because the numbers differ, use the one in the guide for the product you are installing.

Sunlight

PEX is not made for sunlight. The ASTM F876 designation gives a UV allowance of 1, 3 or 6 months or more, according to PPI, and that counts storage and transport time as well as the job. SharkBite says its pipe and fittings are UV resistant for one month, REHAU's code "3" means 6 months, and Viega's catalog lists 6 months for ViegaPEX Barrier. PPI's UV guidance says to store tubing indoors in its package and not to install it outdoors where it will be left exposed. Keep coils boxed until they go in.

At the boiler

No single clearance applies. Boiler and tubing makers set the distance from the boiler, flue and any hot surface, and the answer is in the boiler's manual. As an example of what such rules look like, REHAU's plumbing guide keeps PEX at least 6 in. from the flue of a gas tankless or storage water heater, or uses metal transition pipe if that cannot be met, and asks for 18 in. of metal pipe off a water heater to meet code. Those are water heater rules, not boiler rules, so treat them as an example and follow the boiler maker. Never run PEX near a flue or hot vent just because it fits. Field card: PEX in Hot Water Heating

Barrier on iron loops, room to grow, loose hangers, away from the flue.

  • PEX-a, b and c name the cross-linking method, not a grade. Check the barrier and the rating.
  • Oxygen barrier tubing meets DIN 4726 (0.32 mg per square meter per day at 40°C).
  • Typical print: 160 psi at 73.4°F, 100 psi at 180°F, 80 psi at 200°F (ASTM F876/F877).
  • Uponor expansion: 1.1 in. per 10°F per 100 ft.
  • Example: 60 ft, 60 to 180°F: 7.9 in. of growth, 24 hangers at 32 in.
  • Keep coils out of sunlight. UV allowance is months, not years.
  • Boiler distance and flue clearance come from the boiler maker.
