Urgent Care and Walk-In Clinic HVAC: Waiting Room, Isolation Room and Night Setback

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A walk-in clinic is a small building with an odd mix: a crowded waiting room, a few exam rooms, an X-ray room, a toilet, a dirty utility room and sometimes one isolation room. This lesson is the facility tech's view of that mix: how much outdoor air the waiting room needs, which rooms must stay at a pressure, and what to do with the system at closing time.

Check the design documents

Check the design documents. Rules differ by state, license type and the edition adopted. This page is general education from public sources. The mechanical drawings, the commissioning report and the clinic's infection-control staff decide what your building must do.

Waiting room: outdoor air and exhaust

The waiting room is the highest-occupancy space and the one most likely to have a sick person in it. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 sets outdoor air from two rates added together: a rate per person (Rp) and a rate per square foot (Ra). We did not find a row named "waiting room" in the public pages we read. The nearest row is "Reception areas" in the office group, which one conference paper reproduces from the 2010 edition as 5 cfm per person, 0.06 cfm per square foot and a default density of 30 people per 1,000 sq ft. Washington State's adopted ventilation table shows the same reception-area numbers. Treat that row as an assumption for a waiting room, and check which row your designer used.

The same ASHRAE addendum that carries the outpatient health care rows lists urgent care rooms. An urgent care exam room is 7.5 cfm per person and 0.12 cfm per square foot, a triage room is 10 and 0.18, and a Class 1 imaging room is 5 and 0.12. Those are breathing-zone rates, before the distribution correction that designers apply.

Worked example: a 700 sq ft waiting room with 25 people

All inputs below are assumptions unless marked as cited. The rates are the reception-area row above. The 6 total air changes per hour (ACH) is our own illustrative choice; the CDC and NIOSH ventilation FAQ describes rooms at 6 ACH and higher as generally well mixed, but that is not a requirement for this room.

  1. Room volume: 700 sq ft x 9 ft = 6,300 cu ft (our own arithmetic).
  2. Outdoor air: 25 people x 5 cfm = 125 cfm. Area: 700 x 0.06 = 42 cfm. Breathing-zone total = 125 + 42 = 167 cfm. We assume a distribution effectiveness of 1.0, so the zone needs 167 cfm, about 6.7 cfm per person.
  3. Outdoor ACH: 167 cfm x 60 / 6,300 = 1.6 ACH of the room is outdoor air.
  4. Total air at 6 ACH (example): 6 x 6,300 / 60 = 630 cfm supplied. Outdoor air is 167 / 630 = 27 percent of that, so about 463 cfm is recirculated.
  5. Portable HEPA unit: the CDC and NIOSH FAQ gives a 2/3 rule, choosing a unit whose cfm is at least two thirds of the floor area, and it says to scale the room size by ceiling height divided by 8 when the ceiling is taller than 8 ft. Here 700 x 9 / 8 = 787.5, and two thirds of that is 525 cfm.
  6. Equivalent ACH added: 525 x 60 / 6,300 = 5.0 ACH. A smaller assumed unit at 300 cfm adds 300 x 60 / 6,300 = 2.9 ACH.
  7. Combined: 6 + 5.0 = 11 ACH equivalent with the 525 cfm unit, or 6 + 2.9 = 8.9 with the 300 cfm unit.
  8. Time to remove 99 percent of airborne particles, with perfect mixing (our own arithmetic, 4.605 / ACH): at 6 ACH about 46 minutes, at 8.9 ACH about 31 minutes. The FAQ gives 69 minutes at 4 ACH on the same basis, which matches the formula. Real rooms mix less well.

What the HEPA unit does not do: CDC says portable HEPA units do not satisfy fresh-air requirements. The 167 cfm of outdoor air still has to come through the rooftop unit's damper, and a carbon dioxide or dilution problem is not solved by a filter. CDC also describes portable HEPA units at 300 to 800 cfm and an equivalent of 12 ACH or more for its isolation-room use; matching 12 ACH in this waiting room would take 12 x 6,300 / 60 = 1,260 cfm of HEPA airflow, which is more than one large unit.

Triage and screening

CDC's tuberculosis guideline for health care settings says triage protocols should identify, separate and either transfer or manage people with suspected infectious disease, and that these patients should not wait in an open waiting area. They should wear a surgical or procedure mask if possible and should not stay longer than needed to arrange transfer or an isolation room. For us this means the triage room, not the waiting room, is where a coughing patient is held. Keep its supply and exhaust working and its door closed against the corridor.

Exam rooms and the one isolation room

In the ASHRAE 170 addendum text we read, general examination rooms have no required pressure relationship; a specialty infection-control exam room is negative. The isolation room has its own rules: CDC lists negative pressure of at least 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 Pa) and at least 12 ACH for new or renovated airborne infection isolation rooms, with air exhausted outdoors if possible or HEPA filtered. We cover that room in our airborne infection isolation lesson and do not repeat it. In a clinic, the tech's questions are plain: is the exhaust fan running, is the door gap intact, is the monitor reading, and does anyone log it? CDC says to check and record the airflow direction daily when the room is in use.

X-ray room heat

An imaging room is a small room with a lot of electronics. One digital radiography maker's site-planning page lists heat output by component: a patient table at 640 W, a wall stand at 800 W, a generator cabinet at 278 W, a ceiling tube head at 800 W and a workstation at 400 W. The sum is 2,918 W, or about 9,956 Btu/h at 3.412 Btu/h per watt (our own sum). The same page also shows an older table with different figures, so use the submittal for the machine actually installed. This is equipment heat on top of people and lights, so an imaging room often needs its own cooling or a dedicated zone. Its ventilation rate row, Class 1 imaging at 5 cfm per person and 0.12 cfm per square foot. That is a ventilation rate, not a cooling load, so size the cooling from the equipment heat.

Soiled utility and toilet exhaust

These two rooms are held negative so odors and splash aerosols stay in. In the ASHRAE 170 addendum text we read, the general outpatient table shows soiled holding rooms at 6 total ACH and toilet rooms at 4, both negative, both exhausted, neither recirculated. Washington's adopted table lists public toilet exhaust at 50 or 70 cfm per fixture, the higher figure where exhaust runs intermittently. If your exhaust fan is on a light switch, it is probably the low-use case. Treat these numbers as examples and read your plans.

Night setback without losing pressure

The 170 addendum we read allows air changes to be reduced when a space is unoccupied, but only where the table's turndown column says yes, only if the required pressure relationship is kept, and not for the first 20 minutes after the space empties. Soiled holding rooms are marked "No" for turndown. Minimums return as soon as the space is occupied. Switching a space between positive and negative is not allowed. The CDC environmental guideline also notes that hospital and surgical-center air systems should not be shut down except for maintenance, filter changes and construction.

Our own reasoning from that: pressure comes from the gap between supply and exhaust, as in our pressure room lesson. If a night schedule shuts the rooftop unit off but leaves the toilet and soiled exhaust fans on, those fans pull the whole clinic negative to outdoors and the isolation room reads wrong. Pick the night plan on purpose: keep exhaust fans and their makeup air tied together, keep isolation and soiled rooms off the turndown list, and test the monitors after the first night schedule change. Waiting-room and exam-room supply can usually drop to a low or unoccupied rate, as the operating room setback lesson shows for a bigger case.

Field card: Urgent Care and Walk-In Clinic HVAC

People up front, pressure in back, exhaust always on.

  • Waiting room outdoor air: Rp x people + Ra x area (reception row: 5 and 0.06).
  • Example: 25 people, 700 sq ft gives 167 cfm outdoor air, 1.6 outdoor ACH.
  • Portable HEPA ACH added = cfm x 60 / room cu ft. It adds no fresh air.
  • Soiled and toilet rooms: negative, exhausted, not recirculated.
  • Isolation room: follow the isolation lesson and check the monitor daily when in use.
  • After hours: keep pressure relationships; do not strand exhaust fans without supply.
  • X-ray room: get heat output from the installed machine's submittal.
