Silica Dust for HVAC Techs: Coring and Cutting Concrete, OSHA Table 1 Controls and Respirators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Running a pipe sleeve through a block wall, coring a floor for a condensate drain, or chasing a slot for a line set looks like ordinary mechanical work. Concrete, block, brick and mortar all contain crystalline silica, and cutting them throws fine dust into the air you breathe. OSHA has a construction standard for that dust. This lesson covers what it asks of an HVAC tech.

Check the rule and your employer's plan

Check the current text. This page is general education built from federal pages read in 2026, not legal advice. The standard is 29 CFR 1926.1153. Your employer or the general contractor may have a stricter site plan, and some states run their own OSHA programs. Read the current rule and follow your site's written plan.

Why HVAC work is in scope

NIOSH lists concrete, brick, cement, mortar and other masonry among the common sources of respirable crystalline silica, and names drilling, cutting and grinding as the activities that release it. It describes silicosis as a lung disease that cannot be reversed but can be prevented, and links heavy exposure to lung cancer, COPD, autoimmune disease and kidney failure. OSHA's construction page applies the standard to construction work in general, so an HVAC tech on a building site is covered by the same rules as the masons. Typical HVAC jobs that touch silica materials:

  • Coring floors, roof decks and block walls for pipe, duct, refrigerant line and condensate penetrations.
  • Cutting block or brick for a louver, a sleeve or a wall-hung unit.
  • Drilling anchors into a concrete roof pad or slab for equipment, hangers or rail supports.
  • Chasing or chipping a wall for a line set or conduit.

The numbers: action level and PEL

Under 1926.1153, the action level is 25 micrograms of respirable crystalline silica per cubic meter of air (25 µg/m³), averaged over 8 hours. The permissible exposure limit (PEL) is 50 µg/m³, also an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA). The standard does not apply where exposure will stay below the action level under any foreseeable conditions. Exposure is counted as if you were not wearing a respirator, so a mask does not lower the number being compared to the limit.

Two ways to comply

OSHA describes two paths. The simpler one is Table 1, a list of common tasks with the controls to use and, where needed, the minimum respirator. An employer who fully and properly follows Table 1 for a task does not have to measure exposure for it. The other path is the alternative exposure control method: the employer assesses exposure with air sampling or objective data and keeps it at or below the PEL. If any part of a Table 1 task is not followed, for example the tool has no water feed, you fall out of Table 1 and into that second path.

Table 1 tasks an HVAC tech meets

These are our plain-language summaries of the Table 1 rows, read on the eCFR in 2026. APF means assigned protection factor; "up to 4 hours" and "over 4 hours" mean time on that task per shift.

TaskControlRespirator
Rig-mounted core saw or drillTool with integrated water to the cutting surface, kept up as the maker saysNone, either duration
Handheld or stand-mounted drill (rotary hammer, impact)Commercial shroud or cowling with a dust collector that meets the maker's airflow, has a 99 percent filter and a filter-cleaning mechanism; HEPA vacuum to clean holesNone, either duration
Handheld power saw, any blade sizeIntegrated water feed to the bladeOutdoors: none up to 4 hours, APF 10 over 4 hours. Indoors or enclosed: APF 10 either duration
Jackhammer or handheld chipping toolWater at the point of impact, or a commercial shroud and dust collectorOutdoors: none up to 4 hours, APF 10 over 4 hours. Indoors or enclosed: APF 10 either duration

The table also has rows for walk-behind saws, stationary masonry saws, grinders and others. If you add up several Table 1 tasks in one shift and the total passes 4 hours, the over-4-hour respirator level applies to each. Table 1 also says to provide exhaust ventilation as needed to keep visible dust from building up indoors, and to use enough water to keep visible dust down. Where Table 1 says to use a vacuum or water, that is the control, not the respirator.

Indoors changes things

OSHA's fact sheets warn that wet methods and vacuum collectors may not reliably keep dust low indoors or in enclosed spaces, and suggest added ventilation such as exhaust trunks, portable exhaust fans or ducts, set up so that airflow carries dust away from your breathing zone and is not blocked by people or open doors. In the table above, that is why the handheld saw and chipping rows add a respirator indoors. A mechanical room or a crawlspace is an enclosed space for this purpose.

Water or vacuum, not a mix of rules

OSHA's rig-mounted core drill fact sheet says that if you use a vacuum dust collection system instead of water on that tool, Table 1 no longer applies, and an exposure assessment (and possibly a respirator program) is required. Pick the Table 1 control for the tool, and do not swap one for another on your own. Where water is used, the same fact sheet calls for GFCI protection and watertight connectors, and says to clean up slurry before it dries, using shovels or a wet HEPA vacuum.

Respirators: when Table 1 asks for one

APF 10 in the table means a respirator rated at least 10. In 29 CFR 1910.134 the APF 10 class includes half-mask air-purifying respirators, which covers filtering facepieces and elastomeric half masks. Table 1 sends you to the respiratory protection standard, so the employer must have a program: a medical evaluation before use, a fit test of the same make, model and size you wear (repeated at least yearly), and no facial hair between the seal and your skin. You do a seal check each time you put it on. A respirator is a last layer and does not replace the water or the vacuum.

Written exposure control plan and competent person

Every covered employer needs a written exposure control plan. Paragraph (g) says it must describe the tasks that create exposure, the controls and respirators for each task, the housekeeping measures, and how access to the work area is restricted, including exposure from other employers' work. It is reviewed at least once a year, and available to employees on request. The employer also names a competent person, someone who can spot silica hazards and has the authority to fix them quickly, to inspect the site, materials and equipment regularly. A tech's practical job is to know where the plan is kept and to ask before starting a task it does not list.

Housekeeping: no dry sweeping, no compressed air

Paragraph (f) bars dry sweeping and dry brushing where they could contribute to silica exposure, unless wet sweeping, HEPA vacuuming or another low-exposure method is not feasible. It also bars using compressed air to clean clothing or surfaces where that could expose anyone, unless the dust cloud is captured by ventilation or there is no other feasible way. Plan on a wet wipe-down or a HEPA vacuum, including for your clothes and the inside of the drilled hole.

Protecting building occupants

The standard protects employees, and the plan has to restrict access to work areas. Beyond that, keeping dust away from a tenant, a patient or a customer is our practical suggestion and not a line quoted from the rule: ask the site contact before starting, close off the work area, keep people and open doors out of the airflow path, wet-clean before leaving, and never blow dust off a surface with air. Coring through a fire-rated assembly has its own rules, so see the firestopping lesson linked below before you open a wall.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own illustration, not a sample from a real job. Assume a tech cores and chips for part of a shift, and a personal air sample is worn for 6 hours (360 minutes). The lab reports an average of 70 µg/m³ for those 6 hours. We assume no exposure to silica for the other 2 hours, and treat it as zero.

  1. 8-hour TWA = (exposure x time) divided by 480 minutes.
  2. (70 x 360) = 25,200.
  3. 25,200 / 480 = 52.5 µg/m³.
  4. Compare: 52.5 is above the action level of 25 and above the PEL of 50.

Result: the assumed day is over the PEL, so the controls were not enough, and the employer has to fix them and sample again. Now assume the crew switches to a core rig with working water and sets up an exhaust fan, and a new 6-hour sample reads 30 µg/m³. Then 30 x 360 / 480 = 22.5 µg/m³, which is under both numbers. Remember that the standard's follow-up rules depend on where results land, for example sampling again within three months above the PEL or within six months at or above the action level. The numbers 70 and 30 are assumptions, not typical values.

Medical surveillance and training

An employer must offer medical exams, at no cost, to employees who are required to wear a respirator for 30 or more days a year under the standard. The first exam is within 30 days of assignment and repeats every three years; it includes a chest X-ray, a breathing test and a TB test. Employers must also train workers on the hazards, the tasks, the controls and the plan, and give them a copy of the standard on request.

Field card: Silica Dust for HVAC Techs

Wet or vacuum the dust at the cut, then clean up wet.

  • Action level 25 µg/m³; PEL 50 µg/m³; both 8-hour averages.
  • Rig-mounted core drill: water to the cut. Hammer drill: shroud and 99 percent collector.
  • Handheld saw and chipping tool: indoors, APF 10 respirator; over 4 hours outdoors, APF 10.
  • Do not swap water and vacuum on a tool unless the plan allows it.
  • No dry sweeping, no compressed air on dust.
  • Know where the written plan is and who the competent person is.
  • Cleaning up slurry: before it dries, with a wet HEPA vacuum.
