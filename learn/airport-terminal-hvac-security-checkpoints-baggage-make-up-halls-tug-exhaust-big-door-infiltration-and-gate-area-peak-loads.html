Airport Terminal HVAC: Checkpoints, Baggage Halls, Tug Exhaust and Gate Peaks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A terminal never closes, and its load never sits still. Gate rooms fill and empty on the flight schedule, security lanes throw off equipment heat, and the baggage hall has a door to the ramp that is open more than anyone admits. This lesson walks through each zone from the tech's side of the ceiling, with one worked example of our own.

Check first

Check the design documents. Owners, codes and security agencies set their own rules, and many areas need authorized access. This page is general education, not engineering advice. Follow the site's lockout, ramp safety and escort rules before you open any panel.

Peaks drive everything

The FAA's terminal planning circular says planners need to know peak activity because the terminal space program follows it, and it uses a "peak hour of the average day of the peak month" measure. It also notes that holdrooms are sized for the largest aircraft that can park at the gate, and that not every passenger waits at the gate before boarding. For you, that means the room is built for a crowd that arrives in waves, so a unit that looks oversized at 2 a.m. may be just right at 10 a.m.

Gate holdrooms

A holdroom is a transportation waiting space with a flight-sized crowd. ASHRAE 62.1, in Addendum w to the 2022 edition, lists "Transportation waiting" in Table 6-1 at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 100 people per 1,000 square feet and air class 1. The standard is copyrighted, so confirm the row in the edition your jurisdiction adopted. Our train and bus station lesson covers the same crowd-surge logic for a main hall, and our demand-controlled ventilation lesson covers CO2 sensors. CO2 rises slowly, so a crowd that boards in ten minutes may be gone before the sensor reacts; a boarding schedule signal works better.

At the other end of the gate, the aircraft cooling is a separate job. See our jet bridge and preconditioned air lesson. We do not repeat it here.

Security checkpoints

The FAA circular describes the checkpoint as a queuing area, document check, divestiture area, screening area, recomposure area and administrative space. It gives a planning example: 300 passengers in the peak hour at 150 passengers per lane per hour means at least two lanes. Crowds queue, shed coats and bags, and stand close to screening equipment, so these spaces can run warm even in winter. Collect equipment nameplate watts, because every watt becomes heat in the room (ACHR News gives 3.41 Btu/h per watt), and ask when lanes open and close.

Baggage make-up halls and the ramp door

The FAA circular says outbound baggage processing includes the area and equipment needed to accommodate, sort and screen bags, and that systems range from stand-alone screening to fully automated inline systems with multiple conveyor belts. Bags and carts have to reach the ramp, so the hall has big openings. Those openings let in outdoor air, ramp weather and exhaust.

PNNL's vestibule study gives a feel for how door traffic behaves. Its model used a 21 square foot automatic door and found that door-opening frequency mattered far more than outdoor temperature. In one restaurant case, moving outdoor air from 60°F to -40°F raised peak infiltration only 7.0 percent, while changing openings per hour by 30 percent moved infiltration by about 25 percent. A baggage door is far larger than that model door, so use the study as a lesson in method, not as a number. Our air curtain lesson covers mounting height and sizing, and the hall is a place where a curtain, a faster door, or both may be worth asking about.

Tug and vehicle exhaust

OSHA's carbon monoxide fact sheet says the internal combustion engine is one of the most common workplace sources of CO, and that incomplete burning of gasoline, diesel-type fuels, propane and natural gas produces it. It lists forklift operators among the at-risk jobs and recommends effective ventilation, electric or battery equipment where it can be used safely, and fixed CO monitors with alarms. For powered industrial trucks, 29 CFR 1910.178(i)(1) says CO concentrations from their operation must not exceed the levels in 1910.1000. A baggage tug or belt loader is not always a powered industrial truck, so confirm with the site safety office which rules apply.

The numbers to know, from the NIOSH Pocket Guide: carbon monoxide has an OSHA limit of 50 ppm as an 8-hour average, a NIOSH recommended limit of 35 ppm average with a 200 ppm ceiling, and 1,200 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health. Nitrogen dioxide has an OSHA ceiling of 5 ppm, a NIOSH short-term limit of 1 ppm, and 13 ppm as immediately dangerous. EPA's diesel page notes that burning fuel at high temperature produces nitrogen oxides and that diesel exhaust carries fine particles; a CO sensor does not see those.

For fan staging and sensor checks, see our garage CO and NO2 lesson. The sensor ideas apply here, but take trip points from the design documents, not from a parking garage.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Every input below is an assumption of ours, not field data.

Baggage door. Opening: 10 ft by 12 ft = 120 sq ft. Average air speed through the open door: 100 ft/min. Open airflow = area × velocity = 120 × 100 = 12,000 cfm. Assume the door is open 25 percent of the hour, so the time-average is 12,000 × 0.25 = 3,000 cfm. Outdoor air 20°F, hall held at 55°F, so ΔT = 55 − 20 = 35°F. Using Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT from our heat transfer formulas lesson: 1.08 × 3,000 × 35 = 113,400 Btu/h. Propped open all hour: 1.08 × 12,000 × 35 = 453,600 Btu/h. The difference is 453,600 − 113,400 = 340,200 Btu/h. ACHR News says 1.08 comes from 60 × 0.075 × 0.24 and is accurate near 70°F, with about 1.24 at 0°F, so at 20°F our numbers run a little low.

Holdroom crowd. Room: 1,800 sq ft. Occupant groups: 140 passengers (an assumed 175-seat aircraft at 80 percent), 2 gate agents and 1 cleaner, 143 people in all. Using the 62.1 row above: 7.5 × 143 = 1,072.5 cfm people part, plus 0.06 × 1,800 = 108 cfm area part, for 1,180.5 cfm. After boarding, only the 2 agents and the cleaner remain: 7.5 × 3 = 22.5, plus 108 = 130.5 cfm. The outdoor air need drops by about 9 times in minutes.

Checkpoint heat. Per FAA's example, 300 passengers an hour at 150 per lane needs 2 lanes. Assume 3 kW of equipment per lane: 2 × 3,000 W = 6,000 W, and 6,000 × 3.41 = 20,460 Btu/h of heat while the lanes are powered.

24/7 operation and phased maintenance

An ACRP energy report says motors, pumps, fans and air handlers often run on a 24/7 schedule when tenants or the operating plan do not require it, and that setup and setback strategies often pay back in under a year. Another airport in the report closed off one wing because of flight reductions to cut heating, cooling and lighting. A DOE peer-review summary of a large-airport project reports fault detection on air handlers and a central plant control trial where average efficiency went from 0.75 to 0.70 kW/ton, a 7 percent cost saving. For your work, plan around the schedule: take one air handler or lane zone at a time, confirm with operations what happens to the crowd, and put back every damper, door closer and sensor before you leave.

Field card: Airport Terminal HVAC Peaks and Exhaust

Schedule first, door second, exhaust third.

  • Get the flight schedule and gate crowd times before you chase a comfort call.
  • Holdroom outdoor air follows people: 62.1 row is 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 per sq ft (check the adopted edition).
  • Checkpoint heat: equipment watts × 3.41 = Btu/h.
  • Door load: area × velocity = cfm, then 1.08 × cfm × ΔT. Open time matters most.
  • CO limits: 50 ppm OSHA 8-hour, 35 ppm NIOSH, 200 ppm ceiling. NO2: 5 ppm OSHA ceiling, 1 ppm NIOSH short-term.
  • Wear a personal CO monitor in the baggage hall and check door closers, curtains and sensors on every visit.
