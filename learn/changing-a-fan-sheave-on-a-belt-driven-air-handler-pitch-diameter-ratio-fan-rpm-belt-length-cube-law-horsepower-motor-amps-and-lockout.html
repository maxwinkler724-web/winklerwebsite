Changing a Fan Sheave on a Belt-Driven Air Handler

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many older commercial air handlers still turn the supply fan with a motor, a pair of sheaves and a V-belt. When the airflow is wrong, swapping or adjusting the motor sheave is the usual fix. It is also an easy way to overload a motor if you skip the math. This lesson walks through the ratio, the belt length, the cube-law power jump and the amp check, all with one worked example.

Lock it out first

A sheave change puts your hands next to a belt, a fan wheel and a motor that can start without warning. The federal lockout rule, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147, lays out the order: prepare by knowing what energy the unit has, shut it down in an orderly way, operate every energy isolating device, apply a lock to each one (a lock must hold the device in the off position), relieve or restrain any stored energy, and then verify that the equipment is isolated before work starts. On an air handler, the stored energy part matters: a fan wheel can keep coasting, and a wind-milling wheel can turn from airflow created by another fan. Follow your site's written procedure, and read the lesson on belt guards and nip points for what to put back before the unit runs. OSHA 1910.219 covers guarding for pulleys and belts, so the guard goes back on before you restore power.

Pitch diameter sets the speed

A V-belt rides in the groove of each sheave. The circle where the belt effectively rides is the pitch diameter, and it is a bit different from the outside diameter you can measure with a tape. If the belt does not slip, the belt moves at the same speed over both sheaves. That gives us the working relation (our own reasoning from that no-slip assumption):

Fan rpm = motor rpm × (motor sheave pitch diameter ÷ fan sheave pitch diameter)

A smaller motor sheave slows the fan. A larger one speeds it up. Real belts slip a little, so the measured fan speed usually lands slightly under the calculated value. Always measure with a tachometer after the change. The basics of grooves, belt types and tension are in Belt Drives and Sheave Basics.

Adjustable sheaves and turns

Many motor sheaves are the adjustable (variable pitch) type. One flange screws in or out on the hub, which spreads or closes the groove. When the groove closes, the belt rides higher and the pitch diameter grows, so the fan speeds up. When it opens, the belt sinks, the pitch diameter shrinks and the fan slows. The sheave maker publishes how far the pitch diameter changes per turn and the allowed range of turns open, and those values differ by model, so use the data for the sheave in your hands. Do not run the sheave outside its listed range, and re-tighten the set screw on the flat or keyway as the maker directs. Because an adjustable sheave changes the belt line a little, recheck alignment with a straightedge and recheck belt tension after any change.

The fan itself also has a maximum safe speed set by its maker. Never raise rpm past that limit, no matter what the airflow target is.

The approximate belt length formula

A new sheave size can leave the old belt too tight or too loose. A common approximation for an open belt on two sheaves, with D the larger pitch diameter, d the smaller pitch diameter and C the center distance, all in the same units, is:

L ≈ 2C + 1.57(D + d) + (D − d)² ÷ (4C)

This is the same relation a university engineering slide set gives in terms of radii. It estimates pitch length, not the outside length printed on a belt, so convert to a stock belt size using the belt maker's table. It also shows that a modest change in the small sheave moves the belt length only a little. Most real jobs end up using the same belt and adjusting the motor base, but run the numbers when you are changing a fixed sheave to a very different size.

What the fan laws do to CFM and horsepower

For a centrifugal fan in the same duct system, a U.S. Department of Energy tip sheet says airflow varies directly with fan rotational speed and input power varies as the cube of the speed ratio. A Department of Energy motor and drive guide makes the same point in plain numbers: doubling speed takes about eight times the power. Pressure rises with the square of speed (the standard relation in the full lesson on fan laws). So a small speed bump is cheap in airflow terms but expensive in power, and that is why the amp check below is not optional. The same guide also says belt drives are typically used because motor speeds are usually too high for direct drive, and it names a variable frequency drive as often the most efficient way to adjust fan output when the load varies.

Worked example (our own example)

These are our own numbers, not from a manufacturer. The assumptions are: a 7.5 hp, 460 volt, three-phase motor with a nameplate of 11.0 amps; a motor speed of 1,750 rpm; a fixed fan sheave with a 11.0 inch pitch diameter; an adjustable motor sheave now at 4.4 inch pitch diameter; a measured airflow of 10,000 CFM; a measured fan brake horsepower of 5.0; a measured motor current of 6.1 amps; a center distance of 20 inches; and an adjustable sheave that changes pitch diameter by 0.20 inch per full turn (assumed, so the real value comes from the sheave data). Duct and filters stay the same, and the new target is about 10 percent more airflow.

  1. Current fan speed. 1,750 × 4.4 ÷ 11.0 = 700 rpm.
  2. Target speed. 700 × 1.10 = 770 rpm.
  3. Motor sheave pitch diameter needed. 770 × 11.0 ÷ 1,750 = 4.84 inches. That is 0.44 inch bigger, or 2.2 turns closed at the assumed 0.20 inch per turn.
  4. Use whole turns. Two turns gives 4.8 inches. New fan speed = 1,750 × 4.8 ÷ 11.0 = 763.6, about 764 rpm. Speed ratio = 764 ÷ 700 = 1.091.
  5. New airflow. 10,000 × 1.091 = about 10,900 CFM.
  6. New horsepower. Cube of the ratio: 1.091 × 1.091 × 1.091 = 1.298. So 5.0 × 1.298 = about 6.5 brake horsepower. That is a 30 percent power increase for a 9 percent airflow gain, and it is still under the 7.5 hp motor rating (before drive losses).
  7. Expected motor current. As a rough guide, current moves about with load here: 6.1 × 1.298 = about 7.9 amps, below the assumed 11.0 amp nameplate. This is only an estimate, so you must measure it.
  8. Belt length check. Before: 2(20) + 1.57(11.0 + 4.4) + (11.0 − 4.4)² ÷ 80 = 40 + 24.18 + 0.54 = about 64.7 inches. After: 40 + 1.57(11.0 + 4.8) + (6.2)² ÷ 80 = 40 + 24.81 + 0.48 = about 65.3 inches. The difference is about 0.6 inch, which the motor base adjustment should absorb. Confirm against the belt table.

Pressure would also rise by about 1.091 squared, or 1.19, so check that duct leakage, damper and coil limits are fine with 19 percent more pressure.

Check the amps against the nameplate after the change

With the guard on, the area clear and power restored by the lockout procedure, start the unit and measure the running current on each leg with a clamp meter. Compare it with the full-load amps on the motor nameplate. Readings above nameplate mean the motor is overloaded, so slow the fan back down. Do not just reset the overload relay higher. The Department of Energy motor guide says motors generally run best at about 75 to 100 percent of rated load, and a reading well below that tells you the motor is oversized. Check that all three legs are close to each other, listen for belt squeal, and measure the fan rpm and airflow to confirm that the change did what you wanted. For a broken-belt alarm after the change, see current switches for fan status, since the trip point may need adjusting. Background on the equipment is in Air Handling Unit Basics.

Field card: Changing a Fan Sheave

Lock out, calculate, change, align, measure.

  • Lock out and verify isolation; wait for the wheel to stop; guard goes back on.
  • Fan rpm = motor rpm × motor pitch dia ÷ fan pitch dia.
  • Airflow follows speed. Horsepower follows speed cubed. Pressure follows speed squared.
  • Stay inside the sheave's turn range and the fan's maximum speed.
  • Belt length is about 2C + 1.57(D + d) + (D − d)² ÷ 4C.
  • Realign, retension, then measure amps on every leg against the nameplate.
