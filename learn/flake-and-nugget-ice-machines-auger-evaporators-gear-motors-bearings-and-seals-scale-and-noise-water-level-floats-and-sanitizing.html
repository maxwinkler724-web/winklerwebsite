Flake and Nugget Ice Machines: Auger Evaporators, Gear Motors, Bearings and Seals, Scale and Noise, Water Level Floats and Sanitizing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cube machine builds a sheet of ice, warms it and drops it. An auger machine never stops: water freezes on the inside wall of a vertical tube and a turning screw scrapes it off and pushes it out the top. That one change moves most of the service problems from valves and timers to a motor, a gearbox, a bearing and a seal.

How it differs from a batch cube machine

Our lessons Ice Machine Basics: The Freeze and Harvest Cycle and Ice Machine Cycle: Three Makers Compared cover freeze, hot-gas harvest, thickness probes and cycle timers, so we will not repeat them. An auger machine has no sheet to release and, in the small nugget model training sheet we read, the refrigerant pressures stay steady while ice is being made. The manufacturer service manual for a modular flake and nugget line says the controller will not start ice making unless it senses water. So the questions a tech asks change. Is water reaching the evaporator? Is the auger turning freely? Is ice leaving the head? Is the bin control seeing ice?

The ice also differs. Flake ice is pushed through a few oblong slots. Nugget ice is pushed through small round holes, so it comes out compressed. The manual we read gives six slots for flake and eight or sixteen round holes for the two nugget versions of the same line. A rotating sweep above the head breaks the ice into pieces and moves it to the chute.

Auger, gear motor, gearbox, bearings and seals

The training sheet for a small nugget machine describes a solid stainless double-flighted auger turning inside a stainless evaporator tube, with the refrigerant coil wrapped around the outside of the tube. The auger runs about 11 rpm through a gear reducer. The service manual for a modular flake line gives about 1,500 rpm at the motor reduced to about 11 to 13 rpm at the auger. That is a lot of torque at the auger, which is why the weak points are the gearbox, the bearings and the seal.

  • Gear motor and gearbox. The same manual gives a gearbox oil charge of 14 fluid ounces for that line and says to check the level through a vent hole. It says too much oil may mean water has entered the case and too little may mean a leak. Water in the gearbox means replacing the assembly, according to that manual.
  • Upper bearing. In the nugget training sheet, the head holds a non-metallic top bearing that needs no lubrication, and wear is checked with a 0.015 inch pin gauge: the pin must not fit between the auger shaft and the bearing. The flake manual tells you to look for rust, rough spots, excessive play and damaged bearing seals. These are examples from two machines; use the check in your unit's manual.
  • Lower bearing and water seal. The seal keeps water out of the drive. The flake manual says to replace the water seal every time the auger is pulled and to use a food-grade sealant where it specifies one.

Lock out and verify power is off before any of this work; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. For general bearing habits on ordinary motors see Greasing Fan and Pump Motor Bearings; do not grease a part the maker calls lubrication-free.

Water reservoir and float

Water enters through a float valve into a small reservoir that also feeds the evaporator. The float sets the water level in the tube, so it is a production control as well as a plumbing part. For one modular flake line the manual gives a level from 1/8 inch above to 1/4 inch below a molded line in the reservoir, checked while making ice, and says to adjust by bending the float arm. The nugget sheet adds that its water level sensor reads conductivity and needs at least 10 microsiemens per centimeter, so very pure water can look like no water to the control. Check both the float and the sensor before blaming the board.

Bin control

The flake manual describes photo-eyes at the chute: ice is made until it blocks an infrared beam. The controller also expects a beam break at least every 10 minutes after the first 6 minutes of running, or it shuts down for 10 minutes, and three failures in a row lock it out until reset. The nugget sheet uses an ultrasonic level sensor that times an echo and cannot be adjusted. Scale on a photo-eye can give a false bin-full or no-ice reading, so wipe the sensors when you descale. Never bypass a bin control.

Scale: why it loads the gear motor

Scale builds on the evaporator wall and the auger as the water freezes and the minerals stay behind. It thickens the wall, insulates it from the refrigerant, and roughens the surface the auger has to push ice along. The nugget sheet says scale reduces capacity, increases the load on the drive and makes the unit louder. The flake manual lists scale as the cause of low ice-making capacity. More friction means more motor current, which is why the controller watches the auger amps. For one flake line the manual lists 1.8 to 2.2 amps at 115 volts with a control cut-out of 3 amps. Over the limit, the machine shows a fault, tries again after 4 minutes, and then needs a manual reset. Do not assume your unit matches; use the table for your model.

Noise and squealing

The sources we read do not tie any one sound to one failure. They do say scale makes the unit louder, and the flake manual tells you to look for rattles, rubbing wires and tubes, and a worn or rough top bearing. So treat a squeal or groan as a prompt to run a short list, not a diagnosis: take the auger amps, compare them with the model range, inspect the top bearing for play and rust, look at the evaporator for scale, and check gearbox oil. A new noise with normal amps points toward a bearing or loose part. A new noise with high amps and falling production points toward scale or a failing bearing, and you should stop before the auger drags on the tube wall. The flake manual says to replace an auger that has contacted the evaporator wall, which leaves very rough flight edges.

Our own worked example: production against demand, with scale

All inputs below are our assumptions for teaching, not figures from any maker. A site serves 200 drinks a day at 1.5 lb of ice each. The machine is rated 500 lb per 24 hours at the maker's test conditions, and we assume the site's warm water and room air cut that to 80 percent. The bin holds 110 lb usable. Assume 60 percent of the day's ice is used in a 5 hour rush.

  • Demand: 200 x 1.5 = 300 lb per day.
  • Clean machine in the field: 500 x 0.80 = 400 lb per day, which is 400 / 24 = 16.7 lb per hour. Run time needed: 300 / 16.7 = 18 hours. That leaves 100 lb per day, or 25 percent, of margin.
  • Rush: 0.60 x 300 = 180 lb. The machine makes 16.7 x 5 = 83.3 lb in the rush, so the bin supplies 180 - 83.3 = 96.7 lb. A full 110 lb bin covers it with 13.3 lb to spare.
  • Scaled machine, assumed to lose 25 percent: 400 x 0.75 = 300 lb per day, or 12.5 lb per hour. Run time needed is 24 hours, so there is no margin. In the rush it makes 62.5 lb, so the bin must supply 117.5 lb, which is 7.5 lb more than the bin holds. The site runs out.
  • Current check, using the example range above: assume a clean reading of 2.0 A and a scaled reading of 2.5 A. That is 25 percent higher, and 2.5 / 3 = 83 percent of the 3 A cut-out. Any further load trips the fault.

The lesson for the customer: a machine that "just keeps up" on day one has no room for scale. Cleaning on schedule protects the motor and the ice supply.

Water filtration and sanitizing

The flake manual recommends water filtration for suspended solids and says to change cartridges when they have been installed more than 6 months or when pressure drops too much during ice making. It says not to filter the water supply to a water-cooled condenser. It sets scale removal at least twice a year, more often (about every 3 months) for some water, with sanitizing each time scale is removed, sensor cleaning each time, and a top bearing check at least twice a year. The descaler is an acid that can burn, so wear gloves and eye protection and never mix chemicals. Our lesson Ice Machine Water Quality, Scale and Sanitizing covers the order of descale, sanitize and rinse.

Ice is food

The FDA Food Code, a model that states and local agencies adopt on their own schedules, requires in section 3-202.16 that ice used as food or as a cooling medium be made from drinking water; Washington State's rule repeats this. A Florida state bulletin, written around the 2009 edition, lists 4-602.11 for cleaning equipment at the maker's specifications and 3-304.11 for scoops and utensils, and says water filters should be maintained per the maker. Check the edition your jurisdiction uses. In health care, CDC's environmental infection control guideline lists ice and ice machines as a water-related risk, advises cleaning periodically, using an automatic dispenser and avoiding open chest storage in patient areas, and says to disconnect ice machines during a water emergency when possible. If you see slime or mold in a bin, treat it as a sanitation problem and tell the owner in writing.

Field card: Flake and Nugget Ice Machines

Water in, scale off, amps down, ice out.

  • Auger machines make ice continuously: no hot-gas harvest sheet, so look at water, auger, drive and bin control.
  • Parts at risk: gear motor, gearbox oil, upper and lower bearings, water seal.
  • Scale raises auger amps and cuts output; compare amps with your model range and cut-out.
  • Noise plus high amps: stop and inspect the bearing, the gearbox oil and the evaporator.
  • Check the float level and water sensor; wipe the bin sensors when descaling.
  • Descale, sanitize, rinse, with the maker's chemicals and PPE.
  • Ice is food: drinking-water source, clean utensils, never bypass a bin control.
