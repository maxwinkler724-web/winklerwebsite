Protecting a Customer Home During HVAC Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A customer lets us into the place where they sleep, cook and keep their things. Good repair work can still leave a bad memory if the carpet is gray with footprints, the hallway is full of drywall dust or a drip stain shows up on the ceiling a week later. This lesson covers the habits that keep a home clean and undamaged, and what they cost in minutes.

Start before the first tool comes out

Walk the path from the front door to the work area with the customer. Agree on which entrance we use, which rooms are off limits and where we may park the tool bags. Then move what is in the way. Ask first, and move furniture, rugs and wall hangings yourself only with permission. Rather than guessing, ask the customer to move fragile or valuable items. Clearing a three-foot lane on each side of the path means we are not squeezing a heavy air handler past a table.

Before moving anything, photograph what is already there: scuffs on floors, chips in door trim, cracked tiles, existing stains on ceilings under the attic, dents in the wall near the hatch. Take wide shots and close shots, and keep them with the job record. Photos taken first protect the customer from a misunderstanding as much as they protect us. For how to talk through findings and photos with the customer, see the linked lesson on talking with customers.

Floors and stairs

  • Shoe covers go on at the door, every time, including for a quick return trip to the truck. Carry spare pairs, because a torn pair tracks mud just as well as a bare boot.
  • Floor runners (adhesive-backed film or a rolled mat) cover the path from the door to the work area. Cover stair treads and turns too, since that is where weight and grit concentrate. Check that runners do not slide on hardwood or tile, because a slipping runner is a fall hazard for us and for the customer.
  • Drop cloths go under the air handler or furnace before it is disconnected, and under the attic hatch before anyone climbs. Insulation fibers, old dust and screws all fall straight down from a hatch. Lay the cloth wider than the opening you think you need.
  • Tool and parts placement. Set tools on a cloth or tray, not directly on a finished floor. Set a hot torch, a metal tool or a wet part where it cannot mark a surface.

Dust control

EPA guidance written for do-it-yourself renovators gives a good model for any dusty job: seal off the work area with plastic sheeting, cover floors with sheeting, turn off the forced-air system and cover its vents with sheeting taped in place, mist surfaces before disturbing them, and clean with a vacuum that has a high-efficiency filter and with damp cloths. That page also says to wrap items you cannot move in sheeting and tape the edges. Dry sweeping is not part of that cleanup method, and a plain shop vacuum with a standard bag or filter can blow fine dust back into the room.

Put that into practice on common HVAC tasks:

  • Cutting drywall or plaster for a new return, a register boot or a line-set pass-through. Hang sheeting across the doorway, tape the edges and cut with a vacuum nozzle held at the cut line, or use a tool with a dust port. Cut only what the job needs.
  • Opening or removing ducts. Cover open duct ends and open returns with sheeting or caps as soon as they come apart. Sealing a return opening while you work keeps dust from being pulled through the system and out of every register once it restarts.
  • Disturbing attic insulation. Move as little as possible and put it back where it was. Walk only on framing or on boards laid across it, never directly on the insulation. Wear the respiratory protection your employer requires for dusty attic work.
  • Cleaning coils, wheels and filters. Do the dirty part where the dirt can be caught: under a drop cloth, with a vacuum, and with the drain pan protected.

Old homes: lead paint and asbestos-containing materials

Older homes can hide materials that must not be treated like ordinary dust. EPA says about three-quarters of U.S. homes built before 1978 still contain some lead-based paint, and that paid work disturbing painted surfaces in those homes falls under a federal rule requiring lead-safe work practices from certified firms and trained workers. EPA also lists materials in homes that may contain asbestos, including vermiculite attic insulation, vinyl floor tiles and their adhesives, and textured paint and patching compounds, and says exposure generally happens when such material is disturbed or damaged. For attic insulation, its guidance on vermiculite is to assume it contains asbestos and not disturb it, and to hire a qualified professional if remodeling would disturb it.

What this means for us: if the house is old and the job would sand, cut or scrape paint, or break into suspect materials, stop and tell the customer and your supervisor. The decision belongs with the trained people and the rules, not with the tech on a ladder. This page is not legal advice; use the EPA pages listed below and your employer's procedures. See also the linked lesson on asbestos and lead paint around old heating systems.

Spills and drips

Refrigerant oil, condensate and system water stain and spread. Put an absorbent pad or shallow tray under any connection you are about to open. Keep rags and a small wet-dry vacuum near the work. After a repair, check pans, traps and joints for drips before you leave the area, and look at the ceiling or floor below an attic or upper-floor unit. Wipe oil spills at once, with a rag you then bag, because oil left on a surface is difficult to remove later. If a spill reaches carpet, wood or drywall, tell the customer before you leave and write it in the job record. Hiding it makes the problem larger.

Leave it cleaner than you found it

Pick up all packaging, cut ends, screws and wire scraps. Remove sheeting by rolling it inward so dust stays inside. Vacuum the work area and the runner path, then wipe the surfaces the work touched, such as the top of the unit, the wall by the hatch and the door handles you used. Put furniture back where it was, using the photos as a guide. Take the trash with you unless the customer has told you otherwise.

The final walk-through

Walk the same path with the customer, in the order you used on arrival. Show the work, show the cleanup and show that the system runs. Review the photos of any pre-existing marks only if a question comes up. Ask whether anything looks different from this morning. If the customer points out damage, listen, photograph it and report it to your supervisor instead of arguing about whose it was. Our linked lesson on staying safe with upset customers covers what to do when a conversation turns tense.

Worked example (our own example, not from any source)

Assumed job: replace an air handler in a hallway closet, with an attic hatch in the hallway ceiling above it. We assume a 6-hour job (360 minutes), six trips through the front door, one 6-inch hole cut in the closet drywall for a line-set pass-through, and one return duct opened. All times below are our own assumptions for a careful two-person crew, not measured figures.

StepAssumed minutes
Before: walk the path, photograph floors, walls and ceilings6
Before: move furniture, lay runners from door to closet (about 30 ft)5
Before: shoe covers on and off (6 trips at 0.5 min)3
Before: drop cloths under the unit and at the hatch3
During: sheeting barrier and taped edges around the closet5
During: vacuum at the drywall cut, cap and seal the open return4
After: check for drips and wipe any oil or water3
After: vacuum, wipe, remove sheeting and runners, restore furniture10
After: final walk-through with the customer5

Step-by-step total: 6 + 5 = 11; 11 + 3 = 14; 14 + 3 = 17; 17 + 5 = 22; 22 + 4 = 26; 26 + 3 = 29; 29 + 10 = 39; 39 + 5 = 44 minutes. Share of the job: 44 ÷ 360 = 0.122, or about 12 percent. With these assumptions the habits add well under an hour to the day. Your own numbers will differ, so time your own jobs and adjust.

Field card: Protecting a Customer Home During HVAC Work

Photos first, covers on, dust contained, clean out.

  • Walk the path with the customer; photograph existing marks before moving anything.
  • Shoe covers at the door; runners on floors and stairs; drop cloths under the unit and the hatch.
  • Seal the work area with sheeting; vacuum at the source with a high-efficiency filter.
  • Older home and paint or suspect materials will be disturbed: stop and ask your supervisor.
  • Pads under connections; check for drips before leaving.
  • Clean, restore furniture, then walk through with the customer.
