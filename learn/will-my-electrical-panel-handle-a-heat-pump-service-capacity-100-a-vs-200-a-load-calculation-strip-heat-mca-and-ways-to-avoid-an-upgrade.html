Will My Electrical Panel Handle a Heat Pump?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The heat pump itself is rarely the problem for a 100 amp house. The electric backup heat that rides along with it often is. This lesson explains how service capacity gets checked, how to see which part of the new equipment eats the headroom, and what can be tried before anyone prices a bigger service. We also run a small example with assumed numbers.

Start Here if you are new to the site.

Who decides

The electrician and the utility decide. A licensed electrician does the load calculation and signs off on the circuit, and the local electrical inspector and the utility have the final word on the service itself. Nothing here replaces that. It only helps you read their numbers and ask good questions.

Service size: 100 A, 150 A, 200 A

A 100 amp service at 240 volts can deliver 24 kW, a conversion Berkeley Lab uses in its heat pump demand study. A 200 amp service doubles that. In a Berkeley Lab review of about 18,000 electrification projects, mostly single-family homes in California, roughly 30 percent had 100 A service, about 50 percent had 200 A and about 15 percent had 125 A. The authors note the data lean toward one state, so treat the split as a rough picture. A 150 A service sits between the two, and the same arithmetic applies to it.

Berkeley Lab also reports that in one California program, 480 of 10,446 heat pump upgrades (4.6 percent) came with a panel replacement. Most homes did not need one, but a 100 A house with a gas range, gas dryer and no air conditioning is a different starting point from a 100 A house that is already full of electric loads.

How the existing-home load calculation works

The National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 220 covers load calculations. Two parts matter when adding equipment to a house that already stands. Both are named here in plain text because the code itself is the authority and you should read the edition your area has adopted.

  • The optional method for existing dwellings (NEC 220.83). Berkeley Lab slides describe it as a connected-load sum: 100 percent of the first 8,000 W plus 40 percent of the rest. When the new equipment is heating or cooling, the larger of the new heating load or cooling load is added to the calculated total. It needs no meter data, so it is the default when no history is available.
  • The existing-load-by-measurement method, often called the 12-month demand data method (NEC 220.87). Instead of adding up nameplates, the existing load comes from recorded demand. In Berkeley Lab's description the metered peak is multiplied by 1.25 and the new load is added. Because real homes seldom run everything at once, measured data can show more spare capacity than the connected-load sum. The record period and metering details are set by the code and the utility, so ask your electrician which data the inspector will accept.

Berkeley Lab also checked how much a heat pump actually raised a house's peak. Across about 9,000 Vermont homes with smart-meter data, the typical added heat pump rating was 3.6 kW, yet the median change in the home's maximum demand was only 0.20 kW. A few homes did worse: about 8 percent saw the peak rise by more than the heat pump's rating. Those numbers are why a measured approach can differ so much from a nameplate sum, and also why nobody should assume the best case for a particular house.

Why backup strip heat usually dominates

A compressor-driven heat pump moves heat, so a 3-ton unit draws a few kilowatts. Electric resistance heat makes one kilowatt of heat per kilowatt of power, so it draws far more for the same job. Berkeley Lab lists typical heating loads of 2 to 5 kW for a heat pump and more than 10 kW once resistance backup is included, and it recommends avoiding resistance backup where possible to keep the load low. A Berkeley Lab paper estimates a typical 3-ton unit at about 5.4 kW by minimum circuit ampacity (MCA) on the nameplate, which is 22.5 A at 240 V.

The strips are sized for the coldest hours, but they are wired as if they could run any time. A national lab field study of cold-climate prototypes found total backup capacity of roughly 15 to 20 kW, staged in steps with the first about 5 kW. Backup began to appear around 30 to 35 F, and in the coldest bins (-10 to 0 F) it ran about a quarter of the time. The same report saw about 15 kW of backup switch on after a utility curtailment event ended, which shows how fast a setback recovery can pile onto the service. See Heat Pump Thermostat Setback for the controls side.

Reading MCA and MOCP

Two nameplate numbers feed the electrician's math. MCA is the minimum circuit ampacity: the wire must carry at least that much. MOCP is the maximum overcurrent protection: the breaker may be no larger than that. Heater kits have their own MCA and MOCP, and the maker's table gives them for each air handler. The nameplate lesson shows how, and the strip heat sizing lesson walks the kit tables. For this page, remember only that MCA is the number used to size the added load on the service, and that a bigger strip kit means a bigger MCA.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

This is our own simplified example, not a code calculation. Every input below marked as an assumption is made up for practice.

  1. Service: 100 A at 240 V, so the limit is 100 A (24 kW).
  2. Existing calculated load (assumption): 12,000 VA. At 240 V that is 12,000 ÷ 240 = 50.0 A.
  3. 3-ton heat pump (assumption): nameplate MCA of 22.5 A, in line with the 5.4 kW estimate above. We also assume the blower is counted inside this figure.
  4. 5 kW strips: 5,000 ÷ 240 = 20.8 A.
  5. 10 kW strips: 10,000 ÷ 240 = 41.7 A.

We add the heat pump and the strips together because staged backup can run along with the compressor. That is a cautious choice, and the maker's wiring diagram may show a lockout that changes it.

  • With 5 kW strips: added load = 22.5 + 20.8 = 43.3 A. Total = 50.0 + 43.3 = 93.3 A. Headroom = 100 − 93.3 = 6.7 A, so the house is at about 93 percent of the service.
  • With 10 kW strips: added load = 22.5 + 41.7 = 64.2 A. Total = 50.0 + 64.2 = 114.2 A. That is 14.2 A over the 100 A limit, about 114 percent.

The strip choice alone moves the house from fitting to not fitting. To fit 10 kW strips with this heat pump, the existing calculated load would have to drop to about 100 − 64.2 = 35.8 A, roughly 8,600 VA, or the electrician would have to find a measured or managed way to show the peak is lower. A real calculation also applies the code's demand factors, so a licensed electrician's answer can be higher or lower than this sum.

Options short of a service upgrade

Berkeley Lab lists several approaches for avoiding panel and service upgrades. None is automatic, and each needs the electrician and the inspector to agree.

  • Smaller strip heat. As the example shows, halving the kit removes about 21 A. Match the kit to the design-day gap, not to the biggest kit in the catalog (see the balance point lesson).
  • Cold-climate heat pumps that need less backup. The U.S. Department of Energy describes cold-climate models as designed to perform in temperatures as low as 5 F. A unit that holds capacity in the cold leaves a smaller gap for strips to fill. See Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.
  • Load management devices. Generic current-sensing devices can turn off or pause a chosen load when total draw nears a set fraction of the service. Berkeley Lab describes pausers that act at 80 percent of a set point. For heat pump backup, a controller that lets only one stage of strips run at a time is the same idea, if the maker allows it.
  • Circuit-sharing devices. A splitter lets two big loads share one 240 V circuit and counts only the larger one. These are mostly used for dryers and vehicle chargers, so ask whether one is acceptable for your equipment and your inspector.
  • Measured demand. If the connected-load sum fails, recorded demand data under the NEC 220.87 approach may show room, as described above.
  • Fewer other electric loads. Moving a large load to a lower-power version, or keeping a gas appliance, leaves room for the new equipment.
  • Thermostat habits. Berkeley Lab notes that reducing or removing deep setbacks limits the added peak. Our generator lesson covers another case where strip lockout matters.

What to ask before the quote

Ask the electrician which method they used (connected load or measured demand), what existing load they assumed, whether the strip kit is included in the calculation, and what the inspector or utility needs to see. If the answer is a service upgrade, ask what changed when a smaller kit or a managed circuit was tried.

Field card: Will My Panel Handle a Heat Pump?

Strips, not the compressor, usually decide it.

  • 100 A at 240 V is 24 kW; 200 A is double.
  • Optional method (NEC 220.83): 100 percent of the first 8,000 W plus 40 percent of the rest, plus the larger of new heating or cooling.
  • Measured method (NEC 220.87): metered peak × 1.25 plus the new load.
  • Amps = watts ÷ 240 for a 240 V circuit: 5 kW is 20.8 A, 10 kW is 41.7 A.
  • MCA sizes the wire; MOCP caps the breaker. Both are on the nameplate and in the kit table.
  • Before upgrading: smaller strips, cold-climate unit, load management, circuit sharing, measured demand.
  • The electrician and the utility decide.
