Air Purifiers and Whole-House Air Cleaners: What Actually Works

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Do air purifiers work? Some do, for some pollutants, in the right room, when sized correctly. Others do little, and a few can make the air worse. This lesson follows federal guidance so you can tell the difference before you spend money.

Start with the order that works

The EPA says the most effective way to improve indoor air is to reduce or remove the sources of pollution and to ventilate with clean outdoor air. It describes filtration as a supplement to those two steps, not a replacement. So before shopping for a machine, ask: can I stop the pollution at its source (a smoker, a gas stove without a vent hood, a damp spot), and can I bring in or exhaust air where it makes sense? Our lesson on indoor air quality and ventilation basics covers the first two steps in more depth, and the one on bathroom exhaust fans covers one common fix.

Two kinds of cleaners

The EPA separates portable air cleaners, also called air purifiers, which filter the air in one room or area, from furnace or HVAC filters, which filter air throughout the home. Both can reduce some indoor pollution, but the EPA states that neither can remove all pollutants from the air.

Portable HEPA units and the CADR number

The EPA says portable cleaners often reach a high delivery rate by using a high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter, and it notes that studies of portable HEPA units have found small cardiovascular and respiratory improvements. The number to look for on the box is the Clean Air Delivery Rate, or CADR. It comes from a standard test method from the Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers (AHAM), and it is reported separately for smoke, dust and pollen. In simple terms, it is how much cleaned air the unit delivers per minute, in cubic feet per minute (cfm). The EPA says a higher CADR means the unit removes more particles and can serve a larger area. ENERGY STAR, an EPA program, advises picking the rating that matches your main concern, such as smoke or pollen.

Sizing it: the two-thirds rule

ENERGY STAR gives a general rule: choose a unit whose CADR is at least two-thirds of the room's floor area in square feet. The EPA's own sizing chart agrees closely. It lists a minimum CADR of 65 cfm for 100 square feet, rising to 390 cfm for 600 square feet, and says the chart is an estimate that assumes an 8-foot ceiling. With taller ceilings, the EPA advises choosing a unit sized for a larger area.

Worked example

A bedroom measures 15 feet by 20 feet with an 8-foot ceiling.

  1. Floor area = 15 ft x 20 ft = 300 square feet.
  2. Two-thirds rule: 300 x 2/3 = 200 cfm minimum CADR.
  3. Check against the EPA chart: the 300 square foot row lists 195 cfm. Both methods land at about 200.
  4. Shopping answer: look for a unit with a CADR of 200 cfm or more for the pollutant you care about. A unit rated 250 gives some margin.

If the same room had a 10-foot ceiling, the EPA guidance means you should aim higher than 200, so a unit sized for a larger room is the safer pick.

Whole-house: your furnace or air handler filter

For the whole home, the EPA recommends a filter rated at least MERV 13, or as high as your system will accommodate. It says MERV 13 and above must show at least 50 percent removal of the smallest particles tested. The EPA also says most furnaces can handle MERV 13 if the filter is changed often, but since systems are rarely labeled with a filter recommendation, a technician should confirm. Running the fan longer to get more filtering can raise electricity costs and may affect humidity control in cooling season. For change schedules and what the MERV numbers mean, see How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics. If someone is selling you duct cleaning as an air quality fix, read what the evidence says first.

Ozone generators: avoid

The EPA advises avoiding portable air cleaners and furnace filters that intentionally produce ozone. Ozone is a lung irritant. Even fairly low amounts can cause chest pain, coughing, shortness of breath and throat irritation, and it can worsen asthma. The EPA says that at levels within public health standards, ozone has little potential to remove indoor contaminants, does not remove particles such as dust or pollen, and can react with indoor chemicals to form irritating by-products. It also says no federal agency has approved ozone generators for occupied spaces, and that smelling ozone is not a reliable safety check.

California goes further. The California Air Resources Board (CARB) requires indoor air cleaners sold or shipped in that state to emit less than 0.050 parts per million of ozone and to be certified, and it strongly advises against home use of ozone generators and uncertified air cleaners. CARB keeps a public list of certified devices, and the EPA points to lists kept by AHAM and CARB of cleaners tested to emit little or no ozone. Even if you live elsewhere, that list is a handy way to screen a product.

Ionizers and electronic cleaners: use caution

The EPA says electrostatic precipitators, ionizers, ultraviolet lights without adequate lamp coatings, and plasma devices may emit ozone. It also says research has not shown plasma, photocatalytic oxidation or UV to remove gases effectively in portable residential units. CARB covers ionizers, UV devices and electronic in-duct cleaners in its ozone limit as well. If you consider one, look for CARB certification on the label or list. A filter-based unit with a published CADR is the safer buy.

For apprentices: higher MERV and static pressure

A denser filter pushes back harder on the blower. That resistance is part of the total static pressure the blower must overcome, which we explain in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. Too much restriction can mean less air across the coil or heat exchanger. So before you tell a customer to jump to MERV 13, check the manufacturer's rated external static pressure for the equipment, measure the system with the current filter, and look at the filter slot. A thin one-inch slot may not suit a dense filter, and a deeper media cabinet with more surface area often will. The EPA notes a technician can modify a system to use a higher-efficiency filter. Do not guess: follow the equipment's instructions.
