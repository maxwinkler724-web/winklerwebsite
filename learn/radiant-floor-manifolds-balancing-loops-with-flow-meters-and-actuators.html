Radiant Floor Manifolds: Balancing Loops With Flow Meters and Actuators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A radiant floor is usually many loops of tubing fed from one manifold. If you leave them alone, the short loops take most of the water and the long loops stay cool. This lesson shows what is on a manifold, how makers say to set each loop, and how to do the arithmetic.

Check the maker's instructions

Check the maker's instructions. This page is general education built from manufacturer literature. Your manifold, tubing and fluid may have different limits. The installation guide for the exact manifold in front of you always wins.

What is on a manifold

A manifold is a pair of headers. The supply header sends hot water out to each loop and the return header collects it. If you have not read how a floor is built, start with Radiant Floor Heating Basics. The parts you will meet:

  • Isolation valves. Uponor's EP manifold line pairs isolation valves with balancing valves and flow meters, and the main supply and return ball valves let you cut the whole manifold off from the boiler piping. Caleffi's radiant manifolds likewise have shut-off valves on the return side that can seal off a single circuit.
  • Balancing valves. An adjustable valve on each loop that adds or removes resistance. On Uponor's EP manifold the flow meters sit on the supply header and the balancing valve adjusts the flow; REHAU's manifold puts the balancing valve on the return header.
  • Flow meters or sight gauges. A small window per loop that shows gallons per minute (or liters per minute).
  • Fill and drain (purge) valves. Hose connections on the end caps so you can push water through and carry air out.
  • Actuators. Small electric heads on the return valves that open or close a loop when a thermostat calls.
  • Air vent. Caleffi's manifold has an automatic air vent on the supply header that stays connected. See Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents for the system-level picture.

Why loops need balancing

Caleffi's Idronics manifold issue explains that circuits differ in flow resistance because of different lengths or tube sizes, and that the shorter the branch, the larger its share of the total flow. Without adjustment, flow is not guaranteed to match what each space needs. Balancing adds resistance to the easy loops so the hard ones get their share. REHAU's installation guide puts it plainly: balancing does not mean every circuit gets the same flow. Flow requirements are set by the heat loss of the area the loop serves.

Makers also try to avoid the problem up front. The Radiant Company's manual recommends keeping loops within about 10 percent of each other in length, and Uponor's guide has you measure each installed loop from the length marks printed on the tubing and record it as the effective length on a balancing form. Those recorded lengths are the starting numbers for balancing. The general method is covered in Hot-Water Balancing Basics, and the reason a manifold behaves as a parallel system is in Series vs. Parallel Piping and Zoning.

Step 1: total flow from the heat load

Water carries heat according to flow and temperature drop. The usual shop formula is GPM = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). A boiler-maker article explains where 500 comes from: water at 60°F weighs about 8.33 pounds per gallon, its specific heat is 1 Btu per pound per degree, and there are 60 minutes in an hour, so 8.33 × 1 × 60 is about 500. That article notes the constant is exact only for water near 60°F, so treat it as a close working value, and use your maker's data for antifreeze mixes. Uponor, for one, allows only propylene glycol in its EP manifold, never ethylene glycol.

Step 2: split flow among the loops

Our own simplification: where the loops have the same tube, spacing and floor covering, heat per foot of tubing is about the same, so the share of heat each loop delivers is about its share of the total length. That lets you split the flow in proportion to length. This is a simplification built on the makers' rule that flow follows heat loss. If one loop crosses a cold glass wall or a bare floor and another crosses a rug, the room-by-room heat loss should win over the footage.

Worked example

A zone needs 36,000 Btu/h from the floor. The designer picks a 20°F drop between supply and return. Four loops are on the manifold: A is 150 ft, B is 200 ft, C is 250 ft and D is 300 ft.

  1. Total flow: 36,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 36,000 ÷ 10,000 = 3.6 GPM.
  2. Total length: 150 + 200 + 250 + 300 = 900 ft.
  3. Loop A: 3.6 × 150 ÷ 900 = 0.60 GPM.
  4. Loop B: 3.6 × 200 ÷ 900 = 0.80 GPM.
  5. Loop C: 3.6 × 250 ÷ 900 = 1.00 GPM.
  6. Loop D: 3.6 × 300 ÷ 900 = 1.20 GPM.
  7. Check: 0.60 + 0.80 + 1.00 + 1.20 = 3.60 GPM, matching step 1.

If the meters read in liters per minute, multiply by 3.785: loop A is about 2.3 L/min and loop D about 4.5 L/min. One Caleffi manifold meter scale runs 1 to 5 L/min with a stated accuracy of plus or minus 15 percent, so these settings sit inside the scale, but a reading will not be exact. Also remember that REHAU says to check the flow meter after setting, and to recheck all loops at the end, because changing one valve changes the others.

Reading the flow meters

Read the meter the way the maker's sheet describes. REHAU's instructions say to use the meter to check flow and not to adjust it, and to keep it fully open during fill, purge and normal running. They set the lowest-flow loop to its target plus about 50 percent, adjust the others, then recheck every loop. Uponor's steps: turn the balancing valve until the desired flow shows, check the meter window, then lift, turn and push down the setting ring to lock it. Record each valve setting and flow on the form. Uponor's steps start with the system running and water flowing.

Purging air loop by loop

Air trapped in a loop stops flow. The Radiant Company's manual calls trapped air the most common cause of poor radiant performance. Uponor's guide and REHAU's instructions describe the same idea: connect a hose to the fill valve on the supply end cap and a drain hose to the return end cap, close the other loops, open one loop and run water until the stream is clear with no bubbles, then close it and go to the next. The Radiant Company gives a rule of thumb of one minute of flow for every 100 feet of tubing in the zone. Uponor lists a maximum fill pressure of 30 psi on its figure, so follow the maker's pressure for the system. For where fill pressure comes from, see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.

Actuators and end switches

An actuator on a return valve opens the loop on a call for heat. Caleffi's literature describes them as normally closed, with opening and closing times of 120 to 180 seconds, so wait a few minutes before deciding a loop is dead. In the Idronics issue, a four-wire actuator also has an end switch that closes when the valve is fully open. Several end switches can be wired in parallel so any open loop starts the pump or boiler, and you should check the total VA against the transformer rating. REHAU notes a gray position indicator rises when the actuator is on, so you can see that the valve is open. Mount actuators hand-tight and remove the protective cap first, as the maker directs. For how zone controls differ, see Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators.

Low water temperature for the manifold

A floor wants far cooler water than most boilers make. A manifold is therefore often fed through a mixing valve or injection mixing. Caleffi describes a motorized three-way valve that blends supply water to protect a condensing boiler and can be controlled by outdoor reset. See Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics and Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation.

Common problems

  • One loop cold. Check that its actuator is open and allowed enough time, that its isolation valve is open, and that the flow meter shows flow. Zero flow points to air, a closed valve or a blockage; purge that loop first.
  • Air. Noise, gurgling and uneven heat suggest air. Purge again, loop by loop.
  • Kinks and debris. REHAU warns that a kinked pipe restricts flow. Uponor warns that tubing shavings can clog manifold valves, so cut tubing with a proper cutter.
  • Short loop taking all the flow. The balancing valves were never set or were reset. Redo the flow settings and record them.
  • Leaks at valves. REHAU says to keep the control spindle thread below the hex nut or the valve will leak, and to check compression connections after several hours of running.

Field card: Radiant Floor Manifolds

Short loops hog flow. Set each loop, then recheck them all.

  • Total GPM = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). 500 is about 8.33 × 60 for water near 60°F.
  • Loop GPM = total GPM × loop length ÷ total length, when loops are alike.
  • Example: 36,000 Btu/h at 20°F is 3.6 GPM; loops of 150, 200, 250, 300 ft get 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2 GPM.
  • Purge one loop at a time with all others closed, until the stream is clear.
  • Actuators open slowly (about 2 to 3 minutes); wait before calling one dead.
  • Follow the maker for fluid, pressure and temperature limits.
