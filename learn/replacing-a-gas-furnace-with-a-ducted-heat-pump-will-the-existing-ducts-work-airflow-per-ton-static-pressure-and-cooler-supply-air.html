Replacing a Gas Furnace With a Ducted Heat Pump: Will the Existing Ducts Work?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A homeowner asks to swap a gas furnace for a ducted heat pump and keep the ducts. Sometimes that works well and sometimes the new system is loud, starved for air and blowing air that feels cool. The ducts and the blower decide it, so we check them before we quote.

Two systems, two airflow jobs

A gas furnace heats the air a lot, often 130 to 140 degrees F at the supply, according to a national lab resource guide (source 3). It needs only enough air to keep the heat exchanger from overheating. A heat pump puts out far less heat per cubic foot, so it has to move more air to deliver the same Btu. The same guide says heat pumps provide air at about 80 to 115 degrees F, and that ducts may need to be larger for a heat pump than for a gas or oil furnace.

A DOE-hosted duct retrofit guide says 350 to 400 cfm per ton is usually the minimum for a heat pump, and to check the manufacturer data first (source 1). A federal airflow lookup guide from a regional power agency uses a target band of 325 to 500 cfm per ton (source 2). So a 3-ton heat pump needs somewhere around 1,050 to 1,200 cfm, and the exact number comes from the installation instructions for that model.

Why the supply air feels cooler

Warming a heat pump's supply air only 20 to 30 degrees above the return is normal. A university program page describes a boost of about 25 degrees on 70 degree room air, so a supply near 95 degrees, close to body temperature, and says it can feel cool if you sit in the airflow (source 4). Nothing is broken. We tell the customer before install day, and we consider registers that throw air away from seating.

The math we use is the sensible heat equation: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT. The 1.08 comes from the density of standard air (0.075 lb per cubic foot), 60 minutes per hour and the specific heat of air (0.24 Btu per lb per degree F). Solve for dT by dividing the heat delivered by 1.08 x cfm.

Measure total external static pressure first

Total external static pressure (TESP) is the resistance the blower works against outside the equipment cabinet. The lookup guide describes measuring return static and supply plenum static with a static pressure probe and adding the two readings (source 2). Its own example adds 0.28 and 0.34 to get 0.62 inches of water column. It says most manufacturers suggest 0.5 inches w.c. or less. Then you find the blower speed and pressure on the maker's fan table, read cfm, and divide by tons.

  • Take readings with the filter in place. The lookup guide notes that wet coils can reduce airflow below the dry-coil fan table values.
  • If filter pressure drop is over 0.1 inches w.c., the DOE-hosted guide says change the filter and retest (source 1).
  • Compare to the rating on the new unit, not to the old furnace.

Returns are the usual weak spot

The DOE-hosted guide says return ducts must be designed for heating and cooling and must have capacity above the airflow required (source 1). It cites a friction rate of no more than 0.1 inches per 100 feet and average velocity typically 1,500 feet per minute as design limits. A single hallway return with a 14 inch grille that served a quiet furnace can be too small for 1,200 cfm. Our own filter check: a nominal 20 x 25 inch filter has about 3.5 square feet of face area, so 1,200 cfm gives roughly 345 feet per minute across it. Squeezing that same air through a 14 x 20 inch filter (1.9 square feet) means about 620 feet per minute and a noticeably higher pressure drop.

The same guide notes that compressing a flexible duct just 15 percent can raise pressure loss by 400 to 800 percent, so crushed or kinked runs matter more than most people expect.

Sizing by load: Manual J, Manual D and Manual S

Three named procedures govern this. Manual J calculates the heating and cooling loads. Manual S selects equipment against those loads; the national lab guide states that listed total cooling capacity at design conditions should be 95 to 115 percent of the design total heat gain, or 95 to 125 percent for heat pumps in climate zones 4 through 8 (source 3). Manual D sizes the ducts. The DOE-hosted guide says ducts should be sized to the larger of the heating and cooling loads (source 1). In a cold climate the heating load usually wins, which is why a duct system built for a modest air conditioner can fall short.

Attic and crawl space ducts

A DOE report on unvented attics says heating and cooling energy typically rises about 10 percent when ducts are in an attic, and leaky ducts can lose as much as 20 percent of conditioned airflow to it (source 5). The DOE-hosted guide says on average 25 percent of air is lost through leaks (source 1). A heat pump's lower supply temperature makes every degree lost in an unconditioned space count for more, because there is less to give up. Sealing and insulation help; see the related lessons below.

Worked example (our own)

A house has an 80,000 Btu/h input gas furnace and a 3-ton cooling coil, and the owner wants a 3-ton heat pump on the same ducts. These inputs are our own assumptions, not measurements from a real house.

  1. Furnace airflow. Assume 80 percent efficiency, so output is 80,000 x 0.80 = 64,000 Btu/h. Assume a 55 degree F temperature rise. Airflow = 64,000 / (1.08 x 55) = 64,000 / 59.4, about 1,077 cfm.
  2. Heat pump required airflow. At 350 to 400 cfm per ton, 3 tons need 1,050 to 1,200 cfm.
  3. Measured static (assumed). Suppose we measure return 0.28 plus supply 0.32 = 0.60 inches w.c. total.
  4. Blower table (assumed). Use the example fan table from the lookup guide (source 2) as a stated stand-in for the real unit: at 0.60 inches w.c., medium speed gives 1,078 cfm and high speed gives 1,412 cfm.
  5. Check. Medium: 1,078 / 3 = 359 cfm per ton, inside the 350 to 400 range. High: 1,412 / 3 = 471 cfm per ton, above it, so we would not need it.
  6. Supply temperature rise. Assume 34,000 Btu/h output at 47 F outdoors for the heat pump. dT = 34,000 / (1.08 x 1,078) = 34,000 / 1,164, about 29 F, so supply near 70 + 29 = 99 F. Assume 22,000 Btu/h at 17 F outdoors: dT = 22,000 / 1,164, about 19 F, supply near 89 F. The furnace, by comparison: 64,000 / (1.08 x 1,077) = 55 F rise, so 125 F.

The ducts handle the same airflow in both cases, since the furnace ran about 1,077 cfm too. The comfort change is the 99 versus 125 degree supply, and the risk is that 0.60 inches w.c. is already above the 0.5 that most makers suggest, so one more restriction (a dirty filter, a closed damper) could pull the medium-speed cfm below target.

Fixes when the ducts fall short

  • Add a return or enlarge the return path, and use a larger filter grille or a deeper media filter to lower filter pressure drop.
  • Seal ducts and replace crushed flex; see sealing duct leaks.
  • Insulate attic and crawl space runs; see duct insulation values.
  • Choose variable-speed equipment, which can hold target airflow as static rises (we give no airflow-versus-static number for this). For a fixed-speed blower, see PSC blower speed taps.
  • Keep the furnace as a dual-fuel backup; see dual-fuel changeover. Electric strip heat is covered in backup heat strips.

Field card: Furnace to ducted heat pump

Measure before you promise.

  • Target airflow: about 350 to 400 cfm per ton; confirm in the maker's instructions.
  • Measure TESP with a probe; most makers suggest 0.5 inches w.c. or less.
  • Heat pump supply air is roughly 80 to 115 F; furnace about 130 to 140 F.
  • Heat: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT.
  • Check returns, filter drop, crushed flex and duct leaks first.
  • Run Manual J, Manual D and Manual S for new equipment.
