Home Heating Oil Tanks in New Jersey: Basics, Leaks and What Owners Should Know

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your house heats with oil, the tank is part of the system. Here is what the state tells owners about leaks, reporting and removal, in plain English.

Why these tanks get attention

Many New Jersey homes heat with oil, and the oil sits in a steel tank that may be in the basement, outside above ground, or buried. New Jersey's environmental agency (NJDEP) says older home heating oil tanks, whether underground, above ground or in a basement, can rust and leak, which can foul soil and groundwater. Because most home tanks fall outside the state's regular tank rules, the state runs a separate program for what it calls unregulated heating oil tanks. NJDEP training material describes those as above-ground or underground tanks holding heating oil for use on the property, and says residential tanks are covered whatever their size.

Above ground or underground

An NJDEP FAQ on regulated tanks explains that a tank system counts as underground if a set share of its volume sits below the surface, and that heating oil tanks used only to heat a residential building are exempt from the regulated-tank program. Exempt does not mean harmless. A leak from a home tank can still need a certified cleanup.

Warning signs worth a call

NJDEP lists several clues of a possible leak: a jump in oil use you cannot explain, water found in an underground tank, burner trouble that keeps coming back, stains on basement walls or floors, an oil sheen in a sump pit or French drain, and fuel odors away from the burner. Its advice is to have your heating service company rule out ordinary maintenance causes first. If a leaking tank is still possible, the next step is a DEP-certified environmental professional.

Safety first. Never try to clean up a spill, open a tank, or enter a tank pit yourself. The state says certified people are required for tank closure and cleanup work. If you smell fuel strongly or see oil running, keep people and pets away and call for help.

If oil has reached the environment

NJDEP training slides say a discharge of heating oil to the environment should be reported to the DEP hotline right away, with no minimum amount. They also say that surface spills below a stated size are still reported but are passed to county or local health departments. The DEP page on this program lists the hotline as 877-927-6337; confirm the number on the DEP site before you call. After a confirmed leak, the program page describes the path: hire a contractor certified in subsurface evaluation or a Licensed Site Remediation Professional, investigate and clean up, file a final report, and, once DEP reviews it, receive a No Further Action letter. If there was no discharge, the slides say you should not call the hotline and no such letter is issued.

Removing a tank

The state says an owner who plans to remove one of these tanks must hire a certified individual, a point that often comes up when a house is sold. NJDEP describes the usual flow as hiring a certified closure contractor, getting any local permit, and having the contractor check for holes or signs of a discharge after removal. If none are found, the town closes the permit. Ask any firm for its state certification and compare it against the state's lookup tools.

Money and insurance, in general terms

NJDEP's program page says owners of leaking tanks may be able to apply for state grants or loans, and notes a long review time because of demand. A separate state page lists funding contacts for leaking and non-leaking tanks. The New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance says cleanup costs from leaking tanks can be very large, that many homeowner policies exclude oil tank pollution liability, and that some insurers offer limited buy-back coverage or decline homes with tanks or older tanks. Policies differ, so read yours and ask your agent. This is general information, not legal or insurance advice.

Field card: Home Heating Oil Tanks in New Jersey: Basics, Leaks and What Owners Should Know

Know your tank, watch for clues, use certified help.

  • Older oil tanks can rust and leak, whether buried, outdoors or in the basement.
  • Clues: unexplained oil use, water in the tank, repeat burner trouble, stains, sheen in a sump, odors away from the burner.
