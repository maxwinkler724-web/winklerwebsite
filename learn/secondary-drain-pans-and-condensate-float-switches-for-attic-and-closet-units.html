Secondary Drain Pans and Condensate Float Switches for Attic and Closet Units

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your AC quit and someone says "the float switch tripped," that is usually a safety doing its job. When an air handler sits over a ceiling, in an attic or in a closet, a blocked drain can ruin drywall within hours. This lesson covers the backup pan, the switch that watches for water, and why you never bypass it.

Check your local code

Check your local code. Code sections are renumbered and amended by state and town. What follows comes from code summaries and state or city pages read in 2026, not from the official code books, so confirm the wording and edition your inspector enforces. The first lesson to read is Water Leaking from the AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain Basics, which explains why drains clog.

What the code summaries say

The International Mechanical Code (IMC) has a section titled "Auxiliary and secondary drain systems," numbered 307.2.3. A Wichita Falls, Texas ordinance adopting the 2015 IMC shows the lead-in: it applies where overflow from the primary condensate removal system could damage building components, and one protection method must be provided for each cooling coil. The residential code has a matching section: Baltimore's 2024 residential chapter lists M1411.3.1 under the same title but only adopts the model text.

A study summary of the 2024 IMC lists four options. We could not read the code book itself, so treat these as a summary:

  1. An auxiliary pan under the coil with its own separate drain that ends at a conspicuous spot, so a person notices water dripping. The summary gives a pan at least 1 1/2 inches deep and at least 3 inches larger than the unit in length and width, in galvanized steel of at least No. 24 gage or in nonmetal at least 0.0625 inch thick.
  2. A separate overflow drain tied into the equipment's own pan, higher than the main drain connection, also ending at a conspicuous spot.
  3. An auxiliary pan with no separate drain, plus a water-level detection device that shuts the equipment off before the pan overflows.
  4. A water-level detection device alone, which shuts the equipment off if the main drain blocks. It goes above the primary connection and below the overflow rim.

The summary says these devices must meet UL 508. Oklahoma's state notes on the 2018 codes add a rule for down-flow units and other coils with no secondary drain: the monitoring device goes inside the primary pan, and devices installed in the drain line are not permitted. That is one state's reading of the 2018 edition.

Types of overflow switch

Three styles show up, per the makers' pages:

  • In-line trap switch. RectorSeal's SS1 is a float inside a small Tee glued into the drain, and a reed switch opens when the float rises. Little Giant's ACS-4 and ACS-5 are also in-line and rated 24 volts AC. RectorSeal's SS2 is a right-angle model for an auxiliary pan outlet or in-line, listed at 24 volts AC. The SS1 maker warns that it cannot detect a clog upstream of where it sits.
  • Pan-mounted float. Lennox sells a drain pan overflow switch kit with a float, a bracket that pushes onto the end of the drain pan, and wire nuts. Little Giant's ACS-2 and ACS-3 mount inside the pan. Their ratings differ (48 volts for the ACS-2, 125 to 250 volts for the ACS-3), so read the label and the maker's wiring page before choosing one.
  • Wet (sensor) switch. An AquaGuard sensor (a different maker) sits in the pan and trips when water touches it, with a time delay of up to 60 seconds. The maker rates it 5 amps at 24 volts AC and lists UL 508. A second sensor can sit in a secondary pan.

How the switch stops the system

Every one of these is a switch in series with a low-voltage wire. The switch is normally closed. When water rises, it opens and the circuit goes dead. RectorSeal's instructions say to cut the red thermostat wire and connect the two switch leads across the cut. Putting it in the red wire stops the whole unit, because the 24 volt power to the thermostat is cut. Putting it in the yellow wire stops cooling but leaves the fan on, which RectorSeal says helps limit mold during a long absence. Lennox's kit instructions use the red wire loop and describe the blower stopping when the float is lifted. For meter readings, see the float switch case in Outdoor AC Unit Not Running but the Indoor Fan Is, and for the transformer side, Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

Why the AC stopped and how to reset

When the switch opens, the thermostat or the outdoor unit gets no signal, so the system stays off even though the house is warm. Neither RectorSeal's nor Lennox's instructions describe a reset button. Lennox says the blower runs again once the float is allowed to fall back to its down position. So the real fix is to remove the water. Turn the system off at the thermostat and follow the drain lesson. If the unit drains to a pump, the pump lesson covers its own overflow switch. Call a technician if water returns, reaches electrical parts, or the pan is cracked.

Finding the clog and false trips

The SS1 instructions suggest a useful test: plug the drain downstream and run the unit, and the float should rise and stop the system before overflow. The maker advises inspecting, cleaning and testing it at least every 30 days and after each drain line cleaning. Algae growth in standing condensate is a common cause of clogs, according to a forensic engineering article that also urges regular service of pans, traps and piping.

A false trip can come from a pan that is not level. Lennox says the unit must be level and not pitched away from the float end. A float that is stuck or coated with slime can hold the switch open. The SS1 stem sets how soon it trips, but raising it so it trips later is not a fix. Check the drain first. Sweating ducts can add water near the unit; see that lesson.

Pan rust and why the pan matters

A home-care article says metal drip pans can corrode or crack, galvanized steel can rust over time, and cracks or holes mean replacement. A pan that is rusted through will not hold the water the switch is meant to protect against. Check the pan at each service visit.

Why bypassing the switch is wrong

A tripped switch is a warning, not a defect. Jumping across it, taping the float up or removing it lets the unit run into a flooded pan. If a switch trips again and again, the drain is the problem. Fix the drain, and let a licensed technician handle any wiring change. Wire colors are covered in Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Worked example: how fast does a pan fill?

This is a constructed teaching case with a published rate and assumed sizes. Green Building Advisor says a 12,000 Btu per hour air conditioner (one ton) typically removes 3 to 4 pints of water per hour. It gives no test conditions, so treat it as rough. We assume the rate scales with size, so a 2 ton system removes about 6 to 8 pints per hour.

  1. Pan size: the coil is 20 inches by 20 inches. A pan 3 inches larger each way is 26 inches by 26 inches.
  2. Usable water depth before overflow: assume 1 inch. Volume is 26 × 26 × 1 = 676 cubic inches.
  3. Convert to gallons: 676 ÷ 231 cubic inches per gallon = 2.93 gallons.
  4. Convert to pints: 2.93 × 8 pints per gallon = 23.4 pints.
  5. Time to fill with the main drain fully blocked: 23.4 ÷ 8 = 2.9 hours at the high rate, and 23.4 ÷ 6 = 3.9 hours at the low rate.
  6. If the system ran all day: 6 to 8 pints per hour × 24 hours = 144 to 192 pints, which is 18 to 24 gallons.

Result: a full block overflows a 1 inch pan in about 3 to 4 hours of run time. A tripped switch is not something to put off.

Field card: Secondary Pans and Float Switches

A tripped switch means water. Find the water.

  • Code summaries list a secondary pan with its own drain, an overflow drain, or a water-level device that shuts the equipment off.
  • Switch types: in-line in a Tee, in the pan, or a wet sensor. Check the voltage on the label.
  • Wired in series with the red wire stops the whole unit. In the yellow wire stops cooling only.
  • No reset button in the instructions we read. Remove the water and the float drops.
  • An in-line switch cannot see a clog upstream of it.
  • Test and clean on the maker's schedule. Never jump, tape or remove the switch.
  • A 2 ton system could fill a 1 inch deep, 26 by 26 inch pan in about 3 to 4 hours when fully blocked.
