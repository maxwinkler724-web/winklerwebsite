Heating and Cooling a Basement Apartment or In-Law Suite

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A basement apartment or in-law suite is a second home inside the first one. The people downstairs want their own temperature, and everyone wants to be safe if something burns or leaks. This lesson covers the comfort and safety questions we hear most, and ends with a rough heat-loss check we made up for practice.

Give the suite its own comfort control

If one thermostat runs the whole house, the upstairs resident and the basement resident will fight over it. There are three common fixes. The first is a second thermostat on a separately zoned part of the duct system, which needs dampers and a control panel (see forced-air zoning). The second is a ductless heat pump head in the suite, which the federal energy department's homeowner HVAC guide describes as providing zoned heating and cooling without ducts, with a unit that can simply be turned off in rooms nobody uses. The third is a small electric or direct-vent heater for a very small space. That guide notes electric room heaters are often not cost-effective for a whole average-sized home but can make sense for well-insulated spaces or rarely used rooms.

The ductless route also solves a second problem: it keeps the suite on its own air path. That brings us to the safety question.

Why one shared duct system is a poor idea

Air that moves through a shared supply and return can carry cooking smells, tobacco smoke, and smoke from a fire in one unit into the other. The federal environmental agency's multifamily indoor air guidance says units typically share a floor or ceiling and walls, and it treats limiting uncontrolled airflow between spaces as the goal. For smoke from smoking units it recommends isolating them by air sealing the walls, floors and ceilings, while admitting that no engineering technique fully eliminates the transfer. It also warns that mechanical rooms can quickly spread gases and odors through the systems they contain. We read that as: the fewer connections between the two homes, the better.

Carbon monoxide is the larger worry. The federal consumer product safety agency says CO comes from the incomplete burning of fuels and that malfunctioning furnaces, water heaters, ranges and room heaters are a major source. The federal health agency adds that CO cannot be seen, smelled or heard. A furnace in the basement that shares ducts with an upstairs unit can send CO to people who have no idea where it started. Whether and how an existing shared system can be separated is a job for a licensed contractor and your local building department.

Keep fuel-burning equipment in a closed, supplied room

Furnaces, boilers and water heaters need air to burn. The federal environmental agency's multifamily guidance says tightening a building must not lead to improper venting of combustion appliances, because that can raise carbon monoxide exposure. When we finish a basement, the equipment room is often boxed in. The room needs a door that closes, a way for outside air to reach the burner, and no return grille pulling room air out of it. Our lesson on combustion air covers the details. The consumer product safety agency also says to have the heating system serviced every year and to install appliances to the maker's instructions and local codes.

Smoke and CO alarms in each unit

The consumer product safety agency says smoke alarms belong on every level, outside sleeping areas and inside bedrooms, tested monthly, with alarms over 10 years old replaced. For CO it says to put alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, to prefer interconnected alarms, and to avoid spots near heating vents or above fuel-burning appliances. In a two-unit house, each unit needs its own set, and a fuel-burning appliance in the basement makes that non-negotiable. See CO alarms and what to do.

Basement humidity

Basements are damp, and a bedroom in a damp basement invites mold. The federal environmental agency says the key to mold control is moisture control, and suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, using air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed, and drying water-damaged items within 24 to 48 hours. A ductless unit in cooling mode removes some moisture, but a leak or a wet wall must be fixed first. See finished basement options.

Egress, permits and code questions

Bedroom exits, windows, smoke alarm requirements, separate entrances and whether the unit is allowed at all are decided by your local building department, which enforces whichever edition of the International Residential Code (IRC) your area adopted, plus local amendments. Zoning rules for rental units are local too. Call before you build or alter equipment; we can't tell you what your town requires, and this page is not legal advice.

Noise near bedrooms

A furnace blower, a water heater burner or a compressor on the other side of a thin wall will be heard in a bedroom at night. Put the equipment room away from sleeping areas when you can, keep the door closed, and ask the installer where the outdoor ductless unit will sit relative to bedroom windows. Ductless heads are quiet in a room but they are not silent, and the tubing and outdoor unit need solid, vibration-free mounting.

Worked example: rough heat loss against a ductless head (our own example)

This is our own practice example, not a load calculation. A real sizing job needs a room-by-room calculation done by a qualified contractor. We assume the following:

  • Basement unit floor area: 600 square feet (assumption).
  • Rough heating rule: 15 Btu/h per square foot (our assumption, only for practice).

Step 1. 600 sq ft × 15 Btu/h per sq ft = 9,000 Btu/h of rough heat loss on a design-cold day.

Step 2. Convert to tons using the energy department's 1 ton = 12,000 Btu/h: 9,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.75 ton.

Step 3. Compare with ductless heads. A national laboratory report for the federal energy department lists the smallest indoor unit on a multi-head system as 6,000 Btu/h, and single-head systems starting at 9,000 Btu/h of rated cooling capacity. The energy department's homeowner HVAC guide gives one example model that modulates from 3,100 up to 24,000 Btu/h in heating. Our 9,000 Btu/h estimate is 9,000 ÷ 6,000 = 1.5 times the smallest head and about the size of the smallest single-head system.

Step 4. Against the example model's low end, 9,000 ÷ 3,100 is about 2.9, so a unit that can turn down to 3,100 Btu/h would spend mild days running well below our estimate. Our reading: a single small head is the right order of magnitude for this suite, and the question is not "can it be small enough" but whether one head can reach the bedroom, which is a layout and load calculation question. Cooling load is a different number and was not calculated here.

Real basements vary with wall insulation, window area, ceiling height, and how much heat comes from the equipment room, so treat 9,000 as a sanity check only.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Basement Apartment or In-Law Suite

Own controls, own alarms, closed equipment room.

  • Give the suite its own thermostat, zone or ductless head.
  • Avoid shared supply and return ducts between living units.
  • Keep fuel-burning equipment in a closed room with a combustion air supply.
  • Install smoke and CO alarms in each unit.
  • Fix moisture first; aim for indoor humidity under 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50.
  • Ask the local building department about egress, permits and the IRC.
  • Rough example: 600 sq ft × 15 = 9,000 Btu/h, not a load calculation.
