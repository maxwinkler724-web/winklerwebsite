Storm Doors and Home Heating and Cooling: Still Air, Sun Heat and Sealing the Main Door

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A storm door is a second door mounted outside the main entry door. Homeowners ask us whether it is worth having, and the honest answer is "it depends on the door behind it." This lesson explains what a storm door does, where it can cause trouble, and why the seals on the main door matter more than the storm door in front of it.

What a storm door actually does

A storm door creates a pocket of air between itself and the main door. That air is not moving much, and still air is a poor conductor of heat, so the pocket slows the flow of heat from your warm entry door to the cold outdoors. The same idea is behind storm windows, which we cover in our storm window lesson. A university extension publication on storm windows calls still air one of the best insulators and recommends an air space of about 3/4 inch to 4 inches for windows. That figure is for windows, so we use it only to show the principle. A second effect is wind. In our own reasoning, the storm door also blocks some wind from hitting the main door, so less wind pushes air through gaps around it. Treat that as a bonus, not a fix.

Does the main door already do the job?

A federal energy-agency page on doors says a storm door can be a good investment if the existing door is old but still in good condition. It adds that putting one on a newer insulated door is not generally worth the expense. The same page lists R-5 to R-6 insulating values for steel and fiberglass entry doors without a window. So a storm door gives the biggest gain on an older, thinner or weaker door, and the smallest gain on a modern insulated one. Keep that in mind before recommending one.

Glass in winter, screen in summer

Most storm doors let you swap the glass panel for a screen panel (some have a self-storing screen or a removable glass panel, according to the federal doors page). In cold weather, keep the glass in place so the air space stays closed. In mild and warm weather, switch to the screen so you can open the main door for fresh air without letting in insects. If you leave the glass in during a hot spell, remember that a closed glass door is not cooling anything. We suggest labeling the stored panel and storing it flat so it is not scratched or cracked.

The sun-heat warning for the main door

A full-glass storm door in direct sun can act like a small greenhouse in front of the main door. The federal doors page warns that a glass storm door could trap heat against an entry door and cause damage if the entry door gets several hours of direct sun a day, and says to check the door maker's recommendations if that is a concern. Some door makers give a similar warning for certain door finishes. We have not confirmed any specific maker wording, so check the paperwork for the door in question. In our view, simple ways to lower the risk are to swap in the screen panel in warm weather and to think twice before adding full glass on a sunny south or west entry.

Low-e glass on storm doors

Low-e stands for low emissivity: a thin coating on the glass that reduces radiant heat flow. The federal doors page says high-quality storm doors use low-e glass or glazing, and that low-e coatings cut the heat a storm door gains from the sun. Low-e in a storm door can also help with the trapped-heat problem because it passes less solar heat to the main door, though we would still follow the maker's warning above. Low-e glass is a feature to ask about, not a guarantee.

The main door seal matters most

A storm door does not replace weatherstripping. A university extension publication calls air leaks the greatest source of wasted heating and cooling energy in a home and, in its Figure 1, puts doors at about 11 percent of the air leaks in a typical home. It also says two exterior doors without weatherstripping can have a 1/4 inch gap along the meeting edge, which it equates to a 20 square inch opening to the outside. Another university extension bulletin says air infiltration can account for as much as 30 percent of a home's heating costs, and lists signs a door needs weatherstripping: daylight showing around the closed door, a sheet of paper that slides through the closed door, or a thread or tissue that moves near the edge.

Some practical points from those sources and from a federal installation guide on exterior door weatherstripping:

  • Check the weatherstripping every year and replace it when it is worn. The federal doors page says the same.
  • Fix loose hinges, knobs or strike plates first, then add the seals. The federal guide describes this order.
  • Install the weatherstripping and the bottom sweep as a set, so the door still closes smoothly.
  • Foam rubber and EPDM rubber stay flexible for years, according to the federal doors page, while self-adhesive foam and felt last only a year or two in that extension bulletin's chart.
  • Test after the work: with the door closed, a dollar bill or sheet of paper should drag when pulled, and no daylight should show.

The storm door has its own seals, too. Check the sweep on the bottom and the bulb or foam strip on the latch side. A storm door with a worn strip and a loose fit lets cold air into the air space, which gives up the benefit of the still air.

Door closers and keeping the door shut

The still air space only works while the storm door is shut. A pneumatic closer, the cylinder that pulls the door closed, keeps the door from being left ajar or slamming. If the storm door hangs open, the main door is back to doing the whole job. Our own tip: set the closer so the door latches without a bang, replace a weak closer, and tell household members the storm door is part of the weather seal. A door that does not latch is a leak.

Our own worked example: heat loss through a 20 square foot entry door

This is our own example. The U-factors are assumptions for illustration, not measured values for any particular door. We assume a 20 square foot entry door with a U-factor of 0.50 Btu per hour per square foot per degree F when alone, and a combined U-factor of 0.35 with a storm door added. The day is 70 F inside and 20 F outside. Conduction heat loss is Q = U x A x delta T.

  1. Temperature difference: 70 F - 20 F = 50 F.
  2. Door alone: Q = 0.50 x 20 x 50 = 10 x 50 = 500 Btu per hour.
  3. Door with storm door: Q = 0.35 x 20 x 50 = 7 x 50 = 350 Btu per hour.
  4. Difference: 500 - 350 = 150 Btu per hour, or 150 / 500 = 30 percent less conduction loss.

This counts only conduction through the door. It leaves out air leakage around the edges, which can be larger than the conduction numbers for a poorly sealed door. That is why the seals come first. We do not put a price on any of this; the point is the comparison.

Storm door or main door seals first?

Our order of work: fix the main door seals and the sweep first, because that costs little and stops air movement, which a storm door cannot fully do. Then look at the door itself. If the door is old, thin or leaky even after sealing, a storm door can add a still air space, as in the example above. If the main door is a modern insulated one, the federal page suggests the storm door is not generally worth it. If the door gets hours of direct sun, think about the heat trap before adding glass. For other draft sources around the house, see our lesson on drafty doors and windows.

Field card: Storm Doors and Home Heating and Cooling

Seal the main door first; add a storm door where it earns its place.

  • A storm door adds a still air space and shields the main door from wind.
  • Best gain: an older door in good condition. Smallest gain: a newer insulated door.
  • Glass in cold weather, screen in mild weather.
  • Full-glass storm door plus several hours of direct sun can overheat the main door. Check the door maker's guidance.
  • Low-e glass reduces the sun heat a storm door passes through.
  • Check main door and storm door weatherstripping every year; light or a loose paper strip means replace it.
  • Keep the closer working so the door stays shut.
  • Example (our own, assumed U values): 500 vs 350 Btu per hour, a 150 Btu per hour difference.
