Power-Vent and Direct-Vent Gas Water Heaters: Vent Limits and Faults

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas tank water heater has to get its flue gas outdoors, and there are three common ways to do it. This lesson compares a natural draft tank, a power-vent tank with a blower and plastic pipe, and a direct-vent tank with sealed combustion, then covers vent length and elbow limits, condensation, common faults, terminations and carbon monoxide safety.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not instructions for any one heater. Vent length, pipe size, elbow counts and clearances come from the installation manual for the exact model, and the adopted fuel gas code and local rules apply. The figures below come from spec sheets and service documents for residential power-vent models, a city fuel gas code and government pages read in 2026. Your model may differ.

Three ways to vent a gas tank

An atmospheric (natural draft) tank uses a draft hood, takes combustion air from the room, and relies on hot gas rising through a vent connector and a chimney or vent. A U.S. Department of Energy building-science page notes that atmospheric units draw room air and depend on an open flue, so exhaust fans, hoods and dryers can overcome the weak upward flow (backdrafting). Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's building-science page describes the safer alternatives, which use a fan to exhaust gases and keep them isolated from the indoor air.

A power-vent tank puts a blower on top of the tank. The blower pushes flue gas through a small-diameter plastic pipe, so the vent can run sideways through a wall. It still takes combustion air from the room unless it is a direct-vent model. A direct-vent (sealed combustion) tank brings combustion air in through one sealed pipe and sends gases out another. A PNNL guide says the risk of backdrafting is eliminated for sealed direct-vent equipment, and the DOE page calls a sealed chamber with a separate air intake the preferred option. Many "power direct vent" tanks combine both: a blower and two pipes.

Power-vent models also need power. One maker's spec sheet lists a 110/120 volt supply at 3 amps or less with a cord and plug; a service manual lists a dedicated 115 volt, 15 amp supply. No power means no blower, and the sequence of operation requires proof of airflow before the burner will light.

Why owners switch

  • Orphaned chimney. After a furnace or boiler gets its own plastic vent, the old water heater may be left alone in a flue sized for two appliances. See Orphaned Water Heater Chimney Liner: Sizing Basics. A PNNL page also warns that when appliances share a flue, changing one can leave the other exposed to backdrafting.
  • No chimney. A sidewall pipe avoids building or lining a chimney.
  • Basement finish. A tight, finished space with a fan, a dryer or a fireplace is a poor place for room air combustion. Sealed combustion takes air from outdoors. Our own point: boxing in a heater makes combustion air supply harder, so see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters in Closets and Basements.

Vent categories and pipe material

PNNL describes direct-vent condensing equipment as Category IV with flue gases cool enough for plastic pipe. Residential power-vent tanks vary, so the manual decides. The spec sheets we read call for 2, 3 or 4 inch Schedule 40 PVC, CPVC or ABS for the 40 and 50 gallon models, and one says Canadian jobs need PVC or CPVC approved to the ULC S636 standard. One service manual lists "PVC, CPVC or ABS" too. Do not assume another brand of pipe or a different wall thickness is allowed. The category system itself is covered in Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained.

Condensation is real in plastic vents. One maker's service handbook states that condensate will form in the vent pipe in normal operation, calls for a condensate tee, and tells you to avoid layouts that trap water because trapped water causes blocked exhaust or blocked intake lockouts. It adds a check for low points where water collects. Tank models differ, so follow the manual for slope and drains.

Vent length and elbow limits

Makers rate vent runs in equivalent length: straight pipe plus a length credit for every elbow, because elbows slow the gas. The two spec sheets we read list a maximum of 40 equivalent feet with 2 inch pipe, 120 with 3 inch and 160 with 4 inch for the 40 and 50 gallon models, and say 2 and 3 inch elbows count as 5 feet each and 4 inch elbows as 8 feet each. A different service manual does it as a table, with maximum straight length falling by about 5 feet per elbow for its 3 and 4 inch cases. Exceeding either the length or the elbow limit is a known cause of blocked exhaust faults, according to one handbook, and it covers the intake pipe on direct-vent models too.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Assumptions: a 40 gallon power-vent tank; the manual allows 40 equivalent feet of 2 inch pipe and 120 of 3 inch, with each 2 or 3 inch elbow counted as 5 feet (the values above, used here as an assumed typical manual). Our planned run has 18 ft horizontal plus 10 ft vertical, and 4 elbows, counting the one at the termination.

  1. Straight pipe: 18 + 10 = 28 ft.
  2. Elbows: 4 × 5 = 20 ft.
  3. Equivalent length: 28 + 20 = 48 ft.
  4. Check against 2 inch: 48 is more than 40, so 2 inch fails by 8 ft.
  5. Check against 3 inch: 48 is not more than 120, so 3 inch passes with 72 ft to spare.

The fix is 3 inch pipe or a shorter route. Always recompute with your model's own figures, and check any minimum length or intake limit the manual gives.

Common faults

  • Blower pressure switch. The switch is normally open and closes when the blower creates suction or pressure, proving venting before ignition. One service manual says that if it does not close within 30 seconds the control flashes a fault code, and lists kinked or blocked tubing, a blocked tap or a bad switch as causes. Setpoints are small, for example -0.75 inch water column on one model, so do not jumper a switch to "test" it.
  • Blocked termination. The same manual says a frozen exhaust terminal keeps the burner from running until it clears. A handbook says to check the outside termination for debris and to keep the intake and exhaust terminations apart, because recirculated flue gas causes poor combustion, ignition failure and icing in cold weather.
  • Power outage. A blower tank cannot fire without power. After power returns the control runs a short start-up check; one manual gives about 5 seconds, and another requires cycling power to clear some lockouts.
  • Flammable vapor sensor. Some models have a sensor that shuts down the blower and ignition when it detects flammable vapor. One manual says a high-resistance reading shuts off all outputs and leads to a lockout that requires a power cycle. That sensor is a safety device, so find the vapor source first.
  • Long or undersized vent. Too much length, too many elbows or undersized pipe cause blocked-exhaust codes, as above.

Termination clearances

Clearance rules protect windows, intakes and walkways from hot, wet exhaust. The model code in force sets them, and your manual may be stricter. As one example, the 2021 Seattle fuel gas code (Section 503.8, Table 503.8) lists 12 inches above grade for direct-vent and nondirect-vent terminals, and for an openable window or door it lists 6, 9 or 12 inches for direct-vent appliances by input size, against 4 feet below or beside (or 1 foot above) for nondirect-vent. That is one city's adopted text, not a national rule. Intake and exhaust must also be kept apart, and cold-climate guidance from PNNL says to keep terminations above the expected snow line and to keep condensate drains indoors.

Carbon monoxide safety

The EPA lists backdrafting from gas water heaters and blocked, undersized or leaking flues as sources of carbon monoxide (CO). The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends CO alarms on every level outside each sleeping area, and EPA recommends alarms with battery backup. During an outage, never run a generator indoors or near a door, window or vent, which EPA warns can produce deadly CO. If a CO alarm sounds or anyone feels sick, get outside, call for help and do not restart the heater. For gas odors, see Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do. Atmospheric tanks with automatic dampers are covered in Automatic Vent Dampers on Boilers: How They Work and Common Faults.

Field card: Power-Vent and Direct-Vent Gas Water Heaters

Count the elbows, keep the termination clear, never bypass the switch.

  • Atmospheric: room air, draft hood, chimney. Power vent: blower and plastic pipe, needs power. Direct vent: sealed air in, gas out.
  • Equivalent length = straight pipe + elbows times the manual's per-elbow credit.
  • Compare to the manual's maximum for that pipe size; stay under it.
  • Condensate forms in plastic vent; avoid low spots and trapped water.
  • Pressure switch fault: check tubing, tap, blockage and termination first.
  • No power means no heat; never run a generator indoors.
  • Clearances come from the manual and adopted code.
