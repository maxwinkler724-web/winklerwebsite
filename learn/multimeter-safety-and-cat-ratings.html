Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings

A meter is your eyes for electricity, and a bad or wrong meter can fail at the worst moment. The label near the jacks tells you where the tool is built to be used.

What a CAT rating tells you

CAT stands for measurement (overvoltage installation) category. It says how much energy a circuit could dump into the meter if something goes wrong, and so where the meter is meant to be used. Published references describe the levels this way: CAT II is for plug-in loads and branch circuits, such as appliances and outlets. CAT III is for permanently installed distribution, such as panels, motors and feeders. CAT IV is for the origin of an installation, such as the service entrance, where the available fault current can be very high. Higher categories are built to handle more energy, and category-rated meters include protection that makes an internal arc less likely.

Pick the meter for the highest category you might ever use it in, not the easiest job of the day. Rooftop units and condensing units are often fed from distribution equipment, so a plug-in-circuit rating alone may not match the place you are working.

Voltage rating is a second number

The category comes with a voltage, such as a CAT III with a stated maximum. The voltage rating must match or exceed the circuit you are testing. One manufacturer guide gives, as a labeled example, a 480 volt feeder panel needing at least a CAT III 600 volt meter. Both numbers are normally printed near the input jacks. If you cannot read them, do not guess.

Look before you measure

  • Inspect the meter and leads for cracks, bare metal and loose plugs. A damaged meter or lead is out of service.
  • Fuses matter. Most meters have internal fuses on the high current range, and fused test leads are sold as an extra layer. Replace a blown meter fuse only with the correct authorized type, and never bridge one.
  • Use leads rated for the job, with shrouded connectors and finger guards.

Prove the meter, then the circuit

A broken meter can read zero on a live circuit. Safety practice built on NFPA 70E, as described by Fluke, is live-dead-live: check the meter on a known live source (or a proving unit), test the circuit, then check the meter on the known source again. It is the same idea in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

Probe and body habits

  • Set the function and range, and plug into the correct jacks, before touching anything. Leaving a meter on resistance or current and connecting it across a live voltage source can destroy an unfused meter, and a fused one may survive.
  • Keep one hand out of the work, and keep the other hand off the probe tips. Fluke suggests a hand in a pocket to reduce the chance of a current path across the chest.
  • Do not work alone near exposed energized parts, and avoid damp conditions.
  • Treat a circuit as energized until you have proven otherwise.

Never read ohms or capacitance live

Resistance, continuity and capacitance modes put a small voltage of their own on the circuit. They are used with power off and the part isolated, otherwise other paths will skew the reading and the meter can be damaged. For capacitor and contactor checks see Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter.

When live work is allowed

OSHA's electrical work-practices rule says only qualified persons may work on or near exposed energized parts. Taking a voltage reading in an energized panel is that kind of work. Students should do it only with an instructor and the proper protective equipment.

Field card: Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings

Rate the meter for the worst place you will use it, then prove it every time.

  • CAT II = plug-in loads and branch circuits; CAT III = panels, motors, feeders; CAT IV = service entrance. Higher means more energy handling.
  • The voltage rating must be at or above the circuit voltage. Read both numbers by the jacks.
  • Inspect meter and leads before every job. Damaged means out of service. Never bypass or upsize a fuse.
  • Right function, right range, right jacks, then touch the circuit.
  • Live-dead-live: known source, circuit, known source again.
  • Ohms, continuity and capacitance: power off, lockout applied, part isolated.
  • One hand out of the work; no working alone near exposed live parts.
