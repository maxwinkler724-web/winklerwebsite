Condensate Quantities: Furnace, Boiler and AC Coil Gallons per Day

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A condensing furnace and an air conditioner coil both drip, and both drips get blamed when a drain backs up. Before you pick a drain size, a pump or a neutralizer, it helps to know how many gallons are really coming. This lesson works the numbers for both, starting from the chemistry of burning gas and from the latent load of a cooling coil.

Where combustion water comes from

Natural gas is mostly methane. Burning it follows one reaction: one methane molecule and two oxygen molecules make one carbon dioxide and two water molecules. A university combustion chapter does the weight math: two water molecules at about 18.02 each over one methane at 16.04 gives 2.25 pounds of water for every pound of methane burned. A federal steam tip sheet states the same 2.25 figure. Every gas appliance makes this water. Most of it leaves up the vent as vapor. Only a condensing furnace or boiler cools the flue gas enough to turn some of it into liquid.

Pounds of water per therm and per 100 cubic feet

The same university chapter lists methane at 23,857 Btu per pound on a higher heating value basis, the basis that counts the water as liquid. A federal energy agency page gives natural gas at 1,036 Btu per cubic foot and a therm as 100,000 Btu. Our own arithmetic:

  • Per therm: 2.25 ÷ 23,857 × 100,000 = 9.43 lb of water, or 9.43 ÷ 8.34 = 1.13 gallons.
  • Per 100 cubic feet: 100 × 1,036 = 103,600 Btu, so 1.036 therms × 9.43 = 9.77 lb, about 1.17 gallons.

This assumes pure methane. Real pipeline gas has other components, so treat the numbers as close estimates, not meter-grade values. It also assumes all the vapor condenses, which never happens.

A quick check: the water vapor carries latent heat. The same chapter says the gap between its higher and lower heating values equals the latent heat of water times 2.25. Working that backward, (23,857 − 21,500) ÷ 2.25 = about 1,048 Btu per pound of water. We round to 1,050 Btu/lb for the rest of this lesson. Then 9.43 lb × 1,050 = about 9,900 Btu per therm, or about 9.9 percent of the fuel energy. That matches the 9.9 percent latent loss the chapter shows for a non-condensing example. See Higher vs Lower Heating Value for the full story.

How much of it condenses

Condensing starts only below the flue gas dew point. The chapter works an example with 50 percent excess air and puts the dew point near 125 F, and says it falls between 120 and 140 F depending on excess air. The tip sheet gives about 135 F. Above that, none condenses, however large the heat exchanger.

Below the dew point the share climbs as the flue cools. The chapter's condensing example cools the products to 32 C (about 90 F), where the water vapor drops from 2 to 0.65 moles per mole of fuel. Our arithmetic: (2 − 0.65) ÷ 2 = 67.5 percent condensed. The tip sheet gives recoverable latent heat for a large steam boiler example: 9.51 million Btu/h at 75 F, 7.00 at 100 F, 2.01 at 125 F and zero at 150 F, against about 10,938 lb/h of vapor. Dividing by 1,048 Btu/lb is our own conversion. It suggests about 83, 61 and 18 percent condensed at those three flue temperatures. That boiler is not a home unit, so use the table for its shape: a steep slope and then nothing above the dew point.

For a hot water boiler or a furnace, the practical lesson is that the colder the surface the flue gas touches, the more water you collect. How return water temperature controls this is in Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing.

Worked example, furnace (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measured install. Assumptions: an 80,000 Btu/h input condensing furnace; pure methane chemistry; 8 hours of firing on a cold day; and either 67.5 percent condensed (the chapter's 90 F case) or 30 percent condensed (our own guess for a poorly cooled flue, such as a high return temperature).

  1. Firing rate: 80,000 Btu/h is 0.8 therm per hour.
  2. Water made if all condensed: 0.8 × 9.43 = 7.54 lb/h, or 0.90 gal/h.
  3. At 67.5 percent: 7.54 × 0.675 = 5.09 lb/h. That is 5.09 ÷ 8.34 = 0.61 gal/h, and 0.61 × 8 = 4.9 gallons per day.
  4. At 30 percent: 7.54 × 0.30 = 2.26 lb/h, 0.27 gal/h, about 2.2 gallons per day.

Condensate scales with firing rate. Assuming the same 67.5 percent, a 300,000 Btu/h boiler (3 therms/h) running at full fire would make 3 × 9.43 × 0.675 = 19.1 lb/h, about 2.3 gal/h. A boiler that fires lightly most of the season makes far less per day than that.

Cooling coil condensate from latent load

A coil removes water by taking latent heat out of the air. Pounds per hour is latent Btu/h divided by the heat of condensation, about 1,050 Btu/lb here. Our own example: a 3-ton system (36,000 Btu/h nominal) on a day when we assume 25 percent of its capacity goes to latent load, so 9,000 Btu/h.

  1. 9,000 ÷ 1,050 = 8.57 lb/h.
  2. 8.57 ÷ 8.34 = 1.03 gal/h.
  3. 8 hours of running: about 8.2 gallons per day.

Per ton that is about 0.34 gal/h. A 20-ton unit under the same assumption makes about 6.9 gal/h. Our figure is a size estimate for a typical house, not a prediction. For the airflow-based version of this calculation, see How Much Water a Home AC Removes Per Day. Furnace and coil water are seasonal opposites, so they seldom peak together, even when they share a drain.

What the numbers mean for the drain

Drain line size. Our peak flow is about one gallon per hour, a trickle. A city building handout that summarizes a state mechanical code says condensate waste pipes must be at least three-quarter inch and slope at least one-eighth inch per foot, with a trap per the maker's instructions. The volume is not what clogs a line. Slime, sagging runs and kinks do. A federal furnace guideline says a shared drain can serve a furnace, humidifier and air conditioner together. It says a gravity drain is preferred and, where none exists, a pump can lift the water.

Pump capacity. Compare the pump's rated lift and flow with the peak hourly figure above, not the daily total. A small residential coil needs about a gallon an hour, so almost any pump has the flow. Lift height, a working float and an interlock matter more. The same handout says pumps are allowed if they are interlocked so the air conditioner stops when the pump fails. Troubleshooting is in Condensate Pump Not Working.

Neutralizer life. A national laboratory page reports furnace condensate near pH 3, more acidic than typical acid rain. The more gallons that pass through a neutralizer, the faster its media is used up. At our 4.9 gal/day, thirty cold days is about 147 gallons. Ask the neutralizer maker how many gallons its media handles. Mixing coil water into the same line adds volume without adding acid, so check the maker's instructions. See Condensate Neutralizers.

Freeze risk. Furnace condensate runs mostly in winter, in the very weather that freezes pipes, and the flow is a thin trickle, not a stream. A 10 foot run of three-quarter inch pipe holds only about 0.23 gallon (our geometry), so a sag that traps a small pool is enough to freeze. The federal furnace guideline says to keep attic drain lines as short as possible, to weatherize them where temperatures can fall below 32 F, to wrap heat tape on the trap and lines in a cold climate and to use a controller that shuts the tape off above 40 F. It says tape can draw 40 to 100 watts around the clock, and it says flue condensate should go to the house sanitary drain, never outdoors, in cold climates. See Secondary Drain Pans and Float Switches for overflow protection.

Field card: Condensate Quantities: Furnace, Boiler and AC Coil Gallons per Day

Gas: about 9.4 lb of water per therm. Coil: latent Btu/h divided by 1,050.

  • Methane makes 2.25 lb water per lb fuel; about 9.43 lb (1.13 gal) per therm, about 9.8 lb per 100 cubic feet at 1,036 Btu/ft³.
  • Natural gas flue dew point is roughly 120 to 140 F; no condensing above it.
  • Our example: 80,000 Btu/h, 8 hours, 67.5 percent condensed gives 0.61 gal/h and 4.9 gal/day.
  • Our example: 9,000 Btu/h latent gives 8.6 lb/h, 1.0 gal/h and about 8.2 gal/day.
  • Gallons = pounds ÷ 8.34.
  • Size pumps on the hourly peak; drains fail from blockage and sag, not volume.
  • Furnace condensate is acidic (pH about 3) and freezes in cold spaces: short runs, slope, weatherize.
