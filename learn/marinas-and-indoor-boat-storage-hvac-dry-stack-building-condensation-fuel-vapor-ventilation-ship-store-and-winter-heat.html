Marinas and Indoor Boat Storage HVAC: Dry-Stack Building Condensation, Fuel Vapor Ventilation, Ship Store and Winter Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A big metal building full of boats on racks is a strange place for HVAC. The roof is cold, the air can be damp, the boats carry fuel, and the ignition sources we install have to stay away from the floor. This lesson covers what a facility tech should check in dry-stack and winter storage buildings, plus the small offices and ship store attached to them.

Check the rules that apply to you

Check the rules that apply to you. Marina and boatyard fire safety is covered by NFPA 303, the fire code, the mechanical and fuel gas codes and the heater maker's manual, and each state and city adopts its own editions. We could not open the text of NFPA 303 for this page, so we do not quote its heater or ventilation rules. Ask the authority having jurisdiction before you hang a heater or cut in a fan.

What NFPA 303 is, and what we can say

NFPA 303 is the fire protection standard for marinas and boatyards. A city fire code we read (Seattle, 2015) points to Chapter 7 of NFPA 303 for berthing and storage and to Chapter 5 for electrical equipment in wet, damp and hazardous locations. A trade-press article in Fire Engineering, citing the 1995 edition, says no Class I flammable liquids are to be stored in an indoor boat storage area, and notes that boat fuel tanks are filled nearly to capacity in winter. We treat every stored boat as a possible vapor source.

Why the roof drips

Building enclosure writers describe the same pattern in metal buildings: warm, moist inside air rises to the underside of a colder metal roof, cools, and drops water when the metal falls below the dew point. Building Science Corporation puts it as keeping the cold surface warmer than the interior dew point, and gives an example where air at 70 F and 50% relative humidity has a dew point of about 50 F. On a clear night the roof loses heat to the sky, so the deck can run colder than the outdoor air. A drip onto a hull is often not a roof leak but a moisture and surface temperature problem, which we check with numbers. Our psychrometrics lesson covers the basics.

Worked example: dew point and ventilation (Our own)

Every input here is an assumption we chose for practice: sea-level pressure p = 14.696 psia, inside air at 50 F and 70% relative humidity, the roof deck at 35 F, outdoor air at 34 F and 80% relative humidity, and 5 lb/h of moisture released in the building. Saturation pressures come from the Engineering ToolBox water vapor table: 0.17814 psi at 50 F, 0.12170 psi at 40 F, 0.09624 psi at 34 F and 0.11800 psi at 39.2 F.

  1. Inside vapor pressure: pw = 0.70 × 0.17814 = 0.12470 psi.
  2. Humidity ratio: W = 0.622 × pw / (p − pw) = 0.622 × 0.12470 / (14.696 − 0.12470) = 0.00532 lb water per lb dry air.
  3. Dew point: the saturation pressure 0.12470 psi falls just above the 40 F value (0.12170 psi). A straight line between the 40 F and 50 F table values gives about 40.5 F. A line is only an approximation of a curve, so call it roughly 40 F.
  4. Roof deck at 35 F: its saturation pressure, by straight-line interpolation between the 34 F and 39.2 F table values, is about 0.1004 psi. That air can hold W = 0.622 × 0.1004 / (14.696 − 0.1004) = 0.00428. The room air carries 0.00532, so about 0.00104 lb of water per lb of air condenses on contact. The deck is about 5 F below the dew point, so it drips.
  5. Outdoor air: pw = 0.80 × 0.09624 = 0.07699 psi, so W = 0.622 × 0.07699 / (14.696 − 0.07699) = 0.00328.
  6. Difference: ΔW = 0.00532 − 0.00328 = 0.00205.
  7. Airflow: cfm = lb/h / (4.5 × ΔW) = 5 / (4.5 × 0.00205) = about 543 cfm. The 4.5 is 60 minutes × 0.075 lb per cubic foot (the same method ACHR News shows for 1.08).

So in this example about 540 cfm of dry outdoor air, carried to where the moisture is, removes 5 lb/h. Warming that air from 34 F to 50 F takes roughly 1.08 × 543 × 16 = 9,400 Btu/h by the sensible heat formula, which is an estimate because 1.08 drifts away from 70 F air. Real buildings differ, so measure with a data logger.

Fixes, in order

  • Find the source. Wet floors, snow melting off hulls and unvented fuel-burning heaters add moisture.
  • Keep the roof underside warm. The Building Enclosure article we read lists insulation under the metal, vapor retarders, ventilation and dehumidification, and says ventilation alone is usually not enough.
  • Exhaust at the right place, described next.

Fuel vapor stays low

OSHA's shipyard eTool says vapors from flammable and combustible liquids are often heavier than air and can concentrate in confined spaces, that adequate ventilation must be maintained, and that the exhaust location must be tested so exhausted vapors do not build up to hazardous levels. Boats themselves are built for this problem: federal rule 33 CFR 183.610 requires gasoline engine compartments to be open to the atmosphere or have an exhaust blower whose intake duct sits in the lower third of the compartment, with a label warning that gasoline vapors can explode and calling for a four-minute blower run before starting. If a boat needs a low intake, the building does too. Our reasoning: low exhaust pickup, with makeup air entering high and away from the pickup, follows the way the vapor behaves. We could not confirm a specific code figure for it, so ask your AHJ.

Unit heaters: keep the flame high

The ICC commentary we read for the International Mechanical Code (2015, section 304.6) says appliances in public garages, motor fuel-dispensing facilities and repair garages are installed at least 8 feet above the floor, and at least 1 foot above the tallest vehicle door opening where vehicles pass beneath. An exception exists where a barrier prevents vehicle impact, but the appliance must still meet the ignition source elevation rule in section 304.3 and NFPA 30A. A boat storage building may fall under different sections, and your jurisdiction may use another edition. Check the maker's clearances to hulls and racks. Our hangar lesson and vehicle repair lesson go further on heater height and vapor zones.

Salt air and the condensing unit

Carrier states that salt water can be carried as far as two miles inland, that no single distance decides whether coastal equipment is needed, and that regular maintenance cannot eliminate corrosion but helps catch problems early. Its coastal line uses a two-layer coating and zinc-plated hardware. A Navy facilities amendment (a government document written for its own sites, not a rule for yours) calls for copper tube and aluminum fin on small outdoor units, copper tube and copper fin at 3 tons and above, a 10,000-hour ASTM B117 salt spray rating, factory E-coat, stainless fasteners and no field-applied coating, with equipment on the leeward side where possible. Federal unified guide specifications likewise call for a coil coating in corrosive or coastal environments. For the pitting leaks salt and other contaminants cause, see our corrosion lesson. We found no maker rinse interval to quote, so follow the nameplate manual for cleaning agents and frequency.

Ship store and office units

The small spaces on the end of the building often get a split system or packaged unit. Treat them as ordinary light commercial work with two differences: the condenser sits in salt air, and shop odors may be in the air. Keep intakes away from the storage bay and its exhaust.

Freeze protection for water lines

CDC's winter safety page for homes advises keeping water taps slightly open so they drip, keeping the space heated, letting heated air reach pipes, and thawing slowly with warm air from a hair dryer instead of a torch. Storage buildings are kept cool on purpose, so water lines for washdown and hose bibs are better drained or heat traced. Our heat trace lesson covers cable choices. A torch is also an ignition source in a vapor building.

Field card: Marinas and Indoor Boat Storage HVAC: Dry-Stack Building Condensation, Fuel Vapor Ventilation, Ship Store and Winter Heat

Roof cold, vapor low, flame high.

  • Metal roof drips when the deck is below the dew point of the room air.
  • Our example: 50 F and 70% RH gives W of 0.00532 and a dew point near 40 F; a 35 F deck condenses.
  • Moisture removal: cfm = lb/h / (4.5 × ΔW); our example needs about 540 cfm for 5 lb/h.
  • Fuel vapor is often heavier than air; keep ignition sources high and exhaust low.
  • Check the adopted code for heater height; a 2015 IMC commentary shows 8 ft for garages.
  • Salt air attacks coils: coatings, material choice and leeward placement help.
  • Drain or trace cold water lines; never use a torch.
