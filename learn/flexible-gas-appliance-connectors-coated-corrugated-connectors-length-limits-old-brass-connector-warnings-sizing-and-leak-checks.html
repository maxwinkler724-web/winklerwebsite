Flexible Gas Appliance Connectors: Length, Sizing, Old Brass Warnings and Leak Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The flexible connector is the last few feet of a gas appliance hookup, and it is one of the parts people replace the least. This lesson covers how modern coated corrugated stainless connectors are chosen and installed, why a federal safety agency told households to replace old uncoated brass ones, and how to leak check the finished job.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Gas fitting is licensed work, local codes differ from the model codes, and the appliance maker's instructions can be stricter than either. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. It is not a substitute for the adopted code, the connector instructions or your inspector.

Where the connector fits

Gas comes to an appliance through rigid pipe, then a shutoff valve, then (on most appliances) a sediment trap, then the connector to the appliance gas control. The connector exists so a range can slide out, a water heater can be serviced, or a furnace does not transmit vibration into the pipe. It is not a way to extend a gas line. For the valve, trap and union layout, see Gas Sediment Traps, Drip Legs, Shutoff Valves and Appliance Connectors; this lesson picks up at the connector itself.

What a modern connector is

The connector the federal consumer safety agency recommends as a replacement is either stainless steel or plastic-coated brass, both of them corrugated (ridged) so they bend. Today the usual choice is corrugated stainless tubing with a plastic coating and brass end fittings. The connector is built to a product standard for metal connectors for gas appliances (ANSI Z21.24), which we name here in plain text and do not link. Look for that listing marking and read the instruction sheet that came with it, because the sheet sets the bend radius, the allowed length and the sealant rules for that product.

Length limits and where it may not go

Length rules come from the code your jurisdiction adopted, so treat these as examples of what is out there:

  • Federal manufactured-home rule. The HUD rule at 24 CFR 3280.705 says listed appliance connectors must be 3 feet or less, or 6 feet or less for cooking appliances, and must not run through walls, floors, ceilings or partitions, except for cabinetry.
  • Recent model-code inspection checklists. A county and a city building department each publish a residential gas piping checklist based on a recent edition of the International Residential Code or International Fuel Gas Code. Both say the connector length is whatever the manufacturer's instructions allow and never more than 6 feet (IRC section G2422.1.2.1), and that the connector is not concealed within, and does not pass through, walls, floors, partitions, ceilings or appliance housings (G2422.1.2.3). Older code editions and some local amendments use the shorter 3 foot and 6 foot split, so ask your inspector which applies.

The reasoning is easy to remember. A connector can be damaged, and a leak is only found if someone can see or smell it. Hiding the connector in a wall or cabinet takes away both. Keep it short, in plain view, in the same room as the appliance, without kinks or sharp bends.

The shutoff valve at the appliance

The same checklists list an accessible shutoff within 6 feet of the appliance and in the same room (G2420.5). The federal manufactured-home rule is tighter: within 6 feet of a cooking appliance and within 3 feet of any other appliance. Whichever rule applies, the point is that you can stop the gas before you touch the connector. A connector that is longer than the distance to the valve is a sign something is wrong with the layout.

Sizing by BTU/h and length

Connectors are sized for the full demand of the appliance (checklist item G2422.1.2.2). Makers publish a capacity table: rows are connector sizes, columns are lengths, and the entries are gas flow, usually in cubic feet per hour (cfh), for a stated gas and pressure drop. To use one:

  1. Find the appliance input rating on its data plate, in BTU/h.
  2. Divide by the heating value of the gas to get cfh. Natural gas is commonly taken as about 1,000 BTU per cubic foot, but your gas supplier or the appliance manual gives the real figure.
  3. Pick the connector length you actually need, then round up to the next length shown in the table.
  4. Choose the smallest size whose entry at that length is at least your cfh. Never use the table for a different gas or a different pressure than it states.

The connector must also be sized with the rest of the piping in mind; the pipe run is sized by a separate method (see Sizing Natural Gas Piping: The Longest Length Method). A large connector on a starved line does not fix the line.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Every number in this example is an assumption of ours, made up to show the steps. It is not taken from any maker's table, so on a real job read the table that came with your connector.

  • Appliance: natural gas water heater rated 40,000 BTU/h (assumed).
  • Distance from the valve outlet to the heater gas control: 4 feet (assumed).
  • Gas heating value: 1,000 BTU per cubic foot (assumed).
  • Our imagined table row, at the 5 foot length column (the next length at or above 4 feet): 3/8 inch connector, 55 cfh; 1/2 inch connector, 100 cfh (both assumed).

Step 1: 40,000 BTU/h ÷ 1,000 BTU/ft³ = 40 cfh needed.
Step 2: 4 feet is not a column, so use the 5 foot column.
Step 3: 3/8 inch gives 55 cfh, and 55 is at least 40, so 3/8 inch is enough.
Step 4: Check it with a bigger load to see the rule work: a 75,000 BTU/h heater needs 75 cfh, which is more than 55, so the same table would send us to the 1/2 inch size (100 cfh).

Also check that the connector's end threads match the shutoff valve and the appliance inlet, and that the connector is still a short, visible run. If the table is for a different gas or pressure than your system, do not use the entry.

The old brass connector warning

In October 1996 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission warned about older, uncoated brass corrugated connectors, the kind most often used on gas ranges, ovens and clothes dryers. Its release said the solder holding the end fittings to the corrugated tube can fail, causing a leak, fire or explosion, and it reported 200 connector failures tied to 35 deaths and 59 injuries. It said you cannot reliably tell by looking whether the fittings were soldered, so any uncoated brass connector should be replaced right away with a stainless steel or plastic-coated brass one. Its later consumer sheet adds that these connectors have not been made for more than 30 years, that a qualified professional should check or replace them, that you should not move the appliance to inspect the connector (even slight movement can make a weak one fail), and that connectors should be replaced whenever the appliance is replaced or moved.

When to replace

  • Any uncoated brass connector, on sight.
  • Whenever the appliance is replaced or moved, per the consumer agency's sheet. Connector instruction sheets also say not to reuse a connector on another appliance.
  • A connector with kinks, dents, a cracked coating, corrosion or any bubbles at its fittings.

Leak checking the finished job

With the gas on and the appliance connected, wet every joint you touched with a leak detection solution made for gas, such as bubble solution. The federal manufactured-home rule calls for testing appliance connections with soapy water or bubble solution, and the checklists accept an approved leak detector or soap suds. Bubbles that grow mean a leak: shut the valve, fix the joint and test again. Never use a flame. Rinse and dry fittings afterward, since some solutions can corrode metal. If you smell gas at any time, follow Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do.

Two joint rules help. Flared connector ends seal metal to metal, so the inspection checklists say no pipe dope on the flare. The threaded pipe side of the hookup is covered in Threaded Black Iron Gas Pipe: Thread Sealant, Engagement and Pressure Testing. And do not confuse a corrugated appliance connector with yellow gas piping run through a building; that is a different product with its own bonding rules, in CSST Yellow Gas Piping: Bonding and Lightning, What to Know.

Field card: Flexible Gas Appliance Connectors

Short, visible, same room, new.

  • Use a listed coated corrugated stainless (or coated brass) connector, never uncoated brass.
  • Never run it through or hide it in a wall, floor, partition or appliance housing.
  • Length: per the maker, and not over 6 ft in the checklists we read; some rules use 3 ft, 6 ft for cooking.
  • Valve accessible in the same room, within 6 ft in the checklists we read.
  • Size: BTU/h ÷ heating value = cfh; round the length up; pick the entry at or above cfh.
  • Replace with the appliance; do not reuse or kink.
  • Bubble solution on every joint, no flame, rinse after.
