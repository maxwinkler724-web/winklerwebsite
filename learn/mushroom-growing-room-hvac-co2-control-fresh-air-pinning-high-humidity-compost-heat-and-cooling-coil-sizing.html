Mushroom Growing Room HVAC: CO2, Fresh Air and Cooling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A mushroom growing room is a box where the crop makes heat and carbon dioxide, the air is kept nearly saturated, and the air handler is asked to hold tight targets in all three. This lesson follows the room through its phases for the common white button type (Agaricus), shows how to size fresh air from a CO2 target, and covers the gas hazards a tech should know before entering.

Check the source and the farm

Check the source. Targets here come from Penn State Extension articles read in 2026 and vary by cultivar, crop stage and grower. A farm's own growing plan and the equipment maker's instructions come first. Other mushroom types (oyster, shiitake) run on different targets that we did not research for this page.

The phases and what the air handler does in each

Spawn run. After the compost is seeded with spawn, Penn State Extension says the spawn-growing period is normally 10 to 18 days, with spawn growth best at 75 to 76°F and compost kept in the high 70s without passing 80°F. It puts the spawn death point near 104°F. Relative humidity is wanted at 95 percent or more, and little outside air is used, so the room recirculates. Extension notes that CO2 can run high here: spawn growth rate increased with CO2 up to 10,000 ppm. The compost makes its own heat, with output rising 6 to 9 days after spawning, so the coil is mainly removing heat the crop generates, not heat coming through the walls.

Pinning and cropping. A separate Penn State Extension article gives room air at 60 to 66°F and CO2 at 1,000 to 2,500 ppm during pinning and cropping. It says the air-handling system regulates the fresh air entering the room and the room temperature, and that uniform air movement prevents pockets of stale, high-CO2 air. Harvest begins 15 to 21 days after casing. The sources we opened gave no relative humidity number for pinning or cropping, so we do not state one; take it from the farm's plan.

Fresh air as a pinning trigger

Penn State Extension's production article says fresh air is introduced to the growing room to develop pins, with CO2 at 0.08 percent or lower depending on cultivar. That is 800 ppm, only a little above outdoor air, which is why the damper opens wide. For the HVAC tech, this means the outdoor air damper and fan are not a "ventilation minimum" as in an office. They are a crop control. If the damper sticks closed, or the CO2 sensor drifts high, pinning stalls. If it sticks open, the room loses temperature and humidity control. Compare how an office uses the same sensor in demand-controlled ventilation; here the setpoint is a crop target, not a comfort one.

Worked example: fresh air cfm for a CO2 target

The equation is a mass balance on the room: cfm = generation / (Cin - Cout), where generation is in cubic feet of CO2 per minute (cfm), and the concentrations are volume fractions (ppm divided by 1,000,000). Our own assumptions, not measured data:

  • The crop and compost in the room release 30 cubic feet of CO2 per hour. That is 30 / 60 = 0.5 cfm of CO2.
  • Outdoor air carries 420 ppm CO2.
  • Case A, cropping hold: room held at 2,000 ppm.
  • Case B, pinning drop: room pulled to 800 ppm.

Case A. Cin - Cout = 2,000 - 420 = 1,580 ppm = 0.00158. Fresh air = 0.5 cfm / 0.00158 = about 316 cfm.

Check by hand. 316 cfm x 0.00158 = 0.499 cfm of CO2, which matches the 0.5 cfm generated. Good.

Case B. Cin - Cout = 800 - 420 = 380 ppm = 0.00038. Fresh air = 0.5 / 0.00038 = about 1,316 cfm. Check: 1,316 x 0.00038 = 0.500 cfm. Good.

What it tells us. Going from 2,000 ppm to 800 ppm takes about 4.2 times the outdoor air (1,316 / 316), because the difference between room and outdoor CO2 is what does the work. That swing lands on the coil and the heater or reheat. In a hot, wet summer the outdoor air arrives warm, so the cooling coil must handle that extra load too.

Compost heat and coil sizing

Penn State Extension says cooling capacity is typically 2 to 5 tons per 1,000 square feet of growing space, depending on location, weather, air volume and distribution, and how dense the substrate is. Our own illustration: a 1,500 square foot room (an assumption) works out to 1.5 x 2 = 3 tons at the low end and 1.5 x 5 = 7.5 tons at the high end. Extension also says more airflow with drier air cools the compost more, and that hot spots are handled with fans aimed at them.

The coil is mostly working in the sensible range, but the air is held very damp, so condensate and coil wetting are constant. See air handler cooling coils for face velocity and carryover limits, and the indoor grow room lesson for the moisture side of plant rooms in general.

Near-saturated air, corrosion and cleanability

With humidity held at 95 percent or more in the spawn run, steel fans, cabinets and fasteners stay damp. This is our own reasoning, not a cited figure: choose coated or corrosion-resistant coils, sealed motors, and cabinets that can be rinsed, and keep ductwork accessible. Coating choices and failure patterns are covered in formicary corrosion and salt air. Extension also notes that after the crop the room is steamed, with temperature no less than 150°F for at least 12 hours, so anything inside the room must tolerate that heat or be isolated from it.

Worker safety: CO2 and compost gases

NIOSH lists the OSHA permissible exposure limit for carbon dioxide at 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average, the NIOSH recommended limit at 5,000 ppm average and 30,000 ppm short term, and the immediately dangerous to life or health value at 40,000 ppm. A room held near 2,000 ppm is below these, but a room with the fresh air stopped, or a sealed spawn-run room, can climb toward them.

Compost also gives off ammonia. NIOSH's pocket guide lists ammonia with a 25 ppm average and 35 ppm short-term recommended limit, an OSHA limit of 50 ppm average, and 300 ppm as immediately dangerous. Penn State Extension also warns that ammonia in the compost above 0.2 percent of dry weight kills spawn, so growers watch it for the crop's sake too. Treat a closed room as a confined-space question, check the farm's safety plan, lock out the air handler before coil work, and follow your employer's confined-space rules. For detector basics, see produce cold storage and controlled atmosphere, which covers a related low-oxygen entry hazard.

Field card: Mushroom growing room HVAC

The room is a CO2 and heat source; the air handler controls both.

  • Spawn run: about 75 to 76°F, stay under 80°F, RH 95% or more, little fresh air (Penn State Extension).
  • Pinning and cropping: air 60 to 66°F, CO2 1,000 to 2,500 ppm.
  • Pins develop at 0.08% (800 ppm) CO2 or lower: open fresh air.
  • Fresh air cfm = CO2 generation (cfm) / (room - outdoor fraction).
  • Cooling: 2 to 5 tons per 1,000 sq ft of growing space, farm records win.
  • CO2: 5,000 ppm OSHA 8-hr limit, 40,000 ppm IDLH; ammonia IDLH 300 ppm.
  • Coat or select coils for constant damp air; expect steam-off at 150°F or more.
