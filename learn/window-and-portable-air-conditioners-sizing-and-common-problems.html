Window and Portable Air Conditioners: Sizing and Common Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your window AC is not cooling, the cause is usually one of five things: the unit is the wrong size for the room, the filter is clogged, the coil has iced over, the sun is overwhelming it, or air is leaking around it. This lesson walks through each one, explains why single-hose portable units disappoint so many people, and covers the electrical and installation safety points that matter most.

Start with size: the ENERGY STAR chart

ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner page tells you to measure the floor area of the room in square feet and read the matching capacity from a chart. Capacity is in BTU per hour (BTU/h), a measure of how much heat the unit can remove each hour. The chart assumes an 8-foot ceiling, so a room with a taller ceiling may need more. Here are the lower rows of that chart.

Room area to capacity (ENERGY STAR chart, 8-foot ceiling)
Area (sq ft)Capacity (BTU/h)
100 to under 1505,000
150 to under 2506,000
250 to under 3007,000
300 to under 3508,000
350 to under 4009,000
400 to under 45010,000
450 to under 55012,000
550 to under 70014,000

ENERGY STAR then gives four adjustments. Take off 10 percent if the room is heavily shaded. Add 10 percent if it is very sunny. Add 600 BTU/h for each person beyond two who regularly use the room. Add 4,000 BTU/h if the unit will cool a kitchen.

Worked example: a sunny family room

A family room measures 12 feet by 15 feet, gets strong afternoon sun, and usually holds four people.

  1. Area: 12 x 15 = 180 square feet.
  2. The chart row for 150 to under 250 square feet gives 6,000 BTU/h.
  3. Very sunny room: add 10 percent. 6,000 x 0.10 = 600, so 6,000 + 600 = 6,600 BTU/h.
  4. Four people means two beyond the first two: 2 x 600 = 1,200. So 6,600 + 1,200 = 7,800 BTU/h.
  5. Not a kitchen, so no 4,000 added. Shop for a unit at or just above 7,800 BTU/h, such as an 8,000 BTU/h model.

Without the adjustments, a 6,000 BTU/h unit would have looked right on paper and then struggled all afternoon. ENERGY STAR also warns that bigger is not always better: an oversized unit costs more, wastes energy and does not cool better. The U.S. Department of Energy adds that an oversized unit cools the room too quickly without dehumidifying it properly, which leaves the air clammy. For a deeper look at how whole-house loads are figured, see Heat Load Calculation: An Intro to Manual J.

Why single-hose portable units underperform

A portable air conditioner cools the room air across its evaporator coil, then has to get the heat outdoors through a hose. A single-hose unit pulls the air it uses to cool its hot coil from the room itself and blows that air outside. The DOE test-procedure rulemaking explains the consequence: the unit creates slight negative pressure in the room, so warm air leaks in from outdoors and other spaces to replace what was exhausted. DOE calls this infiltration, and said the effect is most pronounced for single-duct units. DOE also counts heat that moves through the hot exhaust duct.

DOE's test therefore does not just report the lab number. It adjusts measured cooling capacity for duct heat transfer and infiltration, and combines results from two outdoor conditions into a seasonally adjusted cooling capacity (SACC). DOE's sample found the exhaust duct effect alone averaged about 11 percent of the earlier industry-rated capacity. In plain terms, DOE's adjusted capacity is lower than the raw measured number, because the raw number ignores these losses. If you compare portables, compare SACC where it is listed. A dual-hose unit draws its coil air from outdoors through a second duct and causes less of this infiltration, though DOE notes single-duct units are popular because they are portable and can cool just one room.

Common problems and what to check

  • Dirty filter. GE's room air conditioner manual says to check and clean the filter at least every 30 days, more often if needed, wash it in warm soapy water, dry it, and never run the unit without it. A clogged filter cuts airflow and cooling. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV.
  • Iced coil. The same manual says ice blocks airflow and stops cooling. Its fix is to set the mode to high fan or high cool with the temperature control at 1 or 2 and wait about an hour. If the ice returns, check the filter and for blocked airflow first. Central systems freeze for similar reasons, covered in AC Evaporator Coil Frozen: Causes and Fixes.
  • Blocked front. GE's manual lists curtains, blinds or furniture in front of the unit as a reason for poor cooling.
  • Room too large or too sunny. Recheck the chart and adjustments above. A correctly working small unit cannot keep up with a large, sunny room.
  • Poor seal. ENERGY STAR says an improperly installed unit can leak as much air as a 6-square-inch hole. Use the foam and side panels that came with it, and seal the window sash seal as the manual directs. Leveling matters too: GE's manual says the unit should tilt slightly outward so condensed water drains.

If a unit runs but the room never reaches the setting on a mild day, also read AC Running but Not Cooling and House Feels Humid With the AC Running.

Electrical safety

GE's manual says the unit should always be plugged into its own individual outlet that matches the rating label, so it does not overload house wiring. It says never to cut or remove the ground prong, and that where there is only a two-prong outlet, it is the owner's responsibility to have it replaced with a properly grounded three-prong outlet. It strongly recommends against extension cords and says that if one must be used, it should be a UL-listed 14-gauge, 3-wire grounding appliance cord, and that no extension cord should be used with its 230/208-volt models. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) explains why cords are a risk: overheating is usually caused by connecting appliances that use more watts than the cord can handle, and a cord that is hot in use is a warning sign. Always follow your own unit's manual, because it overrides general advice. If a breaker keeps tripping, see AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker and Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Installing in a window safely

GE's manual uses security brackets on each side that attach the unit to its support, and says all supporting parts must be secured to firm wood, masonry or metal. Its instructions cover a standard double-hung window and must be modified for other types. The manual does not address upper-floor windows, and this page does not offer city-specific rules for them. If you live above the ground floor, follow your unit's manual exactly, get a second person to help lift, and ask your building owner or local building department what is required.

For techs: what to check on a service call

Confirm the airflow basics first: filter, front grille, evaporator face and condenser side. Find the reason a unit iced up. GE's manual ties icing to blocked airflow, so clear the filter and front first, and send repeat icing to a sealed-system diagnosis. Wait at least 3 minutes before restarting a unit after switching it off, as GE's manual directs. Ask the customer how big the room is and apply the ENERGY STAR chart before blaming the unit. For portables, note the hose length, bends and whether the window kit seals fully. Sealed-system work on any of these units involves refrigerant, so it needs EPA 608 certification; see EPA 608 Basics.

Field card: Window and Portable ACs

Size it, seal it, clean it, plug it in right.

  • Measure the room in sq ft, then use the ENERGY STAR chart (8-foot ceiling).
  • Adjust: sunny +10%, shaded -10%, +600 BTU/h per person beyond two, +4,000 BTU/h for a kitchen.
  • Oversized units cool fast but dehumidify poorly.
  • Single-hose portables pull warm air in; compare SACC.
  • Clean the filter at least every 30 days (GE manual).
  • Iced coil: high fan or high cool, wait about an hour.
  • Own outlet, no cut ground prong, avoid extension cords.
