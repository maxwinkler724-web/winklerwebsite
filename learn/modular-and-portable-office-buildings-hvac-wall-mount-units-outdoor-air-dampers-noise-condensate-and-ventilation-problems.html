Modular and Portable Office Buildings: HVAC Wall-Mount Units, Outdoor Air Dampers, Noise, Condensate and Ventilation Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Job site offices and portable buildings usually get a packaged unit bolted to an outside wall. It is simple and cheap to place, and it has a short list of repeat problems: a closed outdoor air damper, a loud unit next to the people it serves, a drain that empties under the floor, and a filter nobody changes. This lesson covers those problems and shows how closing the damper changes CO2 in a room.

Why these buildings go wrong the same way

A portable building is a small, tight box with no mechanical room and often no one assigned to look after it. The wall-mount unit is the whole system. For the unit itself, read the existing telecom shelter lesson; we do not repeat it here. A maker's installation instructions for one wall-mount air conditioner series say that a portable or modular building must be placed so the unit has a constant supply of outdoor air to work properly, and that units should not go in enclosed areas that limit airflow around the cabinet.

The best public data comes from California classroom studies, because the state measured a lot of portable buildings. A 2003 report to the California Air Resources Board by RTI International found wall-mounted units in 79.8 percent of portable classrooms versus 9.3 percent of traditional ones. A later Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory paper (Chan and coauthors, 2020) looked at 104 classrooms and found 31 of 33 portable rooms had wall-mount systems. These are classroom numbers, not job site offices, but the equipment and the failure modes are the same, so we use them as our evidence.

The outdoor air damper

On many wall-mount units the outdoor air damper is a plain manual blade. One maker's manual says the blade is locked in the closed position at shipment, that the installer must set the position stops before it will work, and that a blank-off plate is recommended for best efficiency, with the ratings assuming the plate is in place. Motorized damper control is described there only for the energy recovery ventilator and economizer options. So a building can be built, inspected and occupied with no outdoor air at all, and nothing will alarm.

The Berkeley paper found the same thing in service. Three fixed-position ventilators had no power or control signal, so the damper stayed shut. Seven economizer-only systems were wired so the damper never opened. Six units with non-powered spring dampers averaged 17 L/s (35 cfm), which the authors said cannot meet code for 30 students and a teacher. In 22 rooms the fan was set to "auto," so it ran only while heating or cooling. The paper says equipment needs the fan running continuously during occupied hours. Only about 15 percent of rooms met the 7 L/s-per-person requirement, and 93 percent of wall-mount systems had at least one problem, against 24 percent of rooftop units.

The result shows up in CO2. In that paper, portable rooms averaged 4.0 L/s per person and 1,111 ppm, against 5.7 L/s and 784 ppm in permanent rooms. The 2003 report measured a mean indoor CO2 of 1,070 ppm against 427 ppm outdoors, and found the air intake blocked in 10.8 percent of portable rooms versus 2.7 percent of traditional ones. For sensor placement and the 62.1 ventilation recipe, see Demand-Controlled Ventilation.

Worked example: damper closed versus open (our own)

These inputs are our assumptions, not measurements: a 24 by 40 ft portable building with an 8 ft ceiling (7,680 ft³); 20 seated adults; outdoor air at 420 ppm; leakage alone equal to 0.5 air changes per hour; and, with the damper open, an extra 150 cfm of outdoor air. For CO2 from a seated adult we use 0.0052 L/s, the office-work value (1.2 met) that the NIST paper by Persily and de Jonge quotes from ASTM D6245. NIST notes such estimates may be off by as much as 25 percent, and individuals differ.

  1. CO2 generated: 20 × 0.0052 = 0.104 L/s. At 0.4719 L/s per cfm, that is 0.2204 cfm of CO2.
  2. Damper closed: leakage is 0.5 × 7,680 / 60 = 64 cfm. Steady-state rise = 0.2204 / 64 = 0.003444, or about 3,444 ppm. Room settles near 420 + 3,444 = 3,864 ppm.
  3. Damper open: 64 + 150 = 214 cfm. Rise = 0.2204 / 214 = 0.00103, or about 1,030 ppm. Room settles near 1,450 ppm.
  4. Airflow to hold the room near 1,000 ppm (580 ppm rise): 0.2204 / 0.000580 = about 380 cfm, or 19 cfm per person.
  5. Time matters. The time constant is volume over airflow: 7,680 / 64 = 120 min closed, and 7,680 / 214 = 36 min open. Starting at 420 ppm, the closed room reaches about 1,775 ppm after 1 hour and 2,600 ppm after 2 hours. The open room reaches about 1,260 ppm after 1 hour.

A short meeting may never reach the closed-damper steady state, but an all-day office will get close. Our 0.5 air changes per hour is a guess; a tighter building does worse. Opening the damper is not free, because the air must be cooled, heated and dried. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load before setting a position by feel.

Noise near occupants

A wall-mount unit sits a few feet from desks, with a compressor and fan on the other side of a thin wall. In the 2003 report, teachers in portable rooms turned the HVAC off because of noise more often than in traditional rooms (68.3 percent versus 42.2 percent), and about half of indoor noise measurements in portable rooms exceeded 55 dBA, the outdoor nuisance standard the report cites. Switching the unit off also ends ventilation, so noise leads back to the CO2 problem.

The wall must be stiff enough to carry the unit without passing on vibration. One maker's manual says wood frame walls must be strong and rigid enough for that, and that attachment is to the weight-carrying structural members, not just to siding. It lists indoor sound plenums as an option that can provide sound isolation and indoor sound reduction. Our own habit: check loose panels and mounting bolts before blaming the unit. For room-side sound ratings, see HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings.

Condensate and moisture under the building

The same maker's manual describes a clear flexible hose from the drain pan out through the base, extending 1 to 2 inches below it, and says condensate can freeze after it leaves the hose in cold weather. If it is piped to a drain, the system must be open or vented. The 2003 report found portable rooms less likely to have clean drain pans and lines (30.0 percent versus 56.7 percent), with standing water in 55.3 percent versus 11.1 percent, blocked drains in 36.6 percent versus 6.8 percent, and failed drain tests in 58.5 percent versus 12.4 percent. Over two-thirds of teachers in portables reported musty odors at times.

HUD's guide on moisture in manufactured homes is the best public source for the space under a raised, skirted building. It says water vapor from the ground is often the largest moisture load, calls for a 6-mil polyethylene ground cover over 100 percent of the ground, and says an air conditioner condensate line should drain to a point beyond the skirting. It also warns that a central system can put out 10 gallons or more on a hot, humid day, and that a puddle on the ground cover can persist all cooling season. For vented versus sealed spaces, see Crawl Space Ducts and HVAC Equipment, and for drain faults on the unit see Water Leaking From an AC Condensate Drain.

Filter neglect

The maker's manual says a 1 inch throwaway filter is standard on its wall-mount series and is serviced from outside through an access panel. That ease does not mean it gets done. In the Berkeley paper, 30 of 100 inspected filters were due for change or past their service life, and 26 of those 30 were in wall-mount units. Rooms with only filter problems averaged 3.5 L/s per person versus 6.1 with none. EPA's mold guide says to keep drain pans clean, flowing and unobstructed, keep humidity below 60 percent (ideally 30 to 50), and change filters regularly. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter.

A walk-around for a portable building

Our own order of checks, as practical advice: set the thermostat fan to continuous in occupied hours if the controls allow it; confirm where the damper blade is and whether it is blanked off; look at the intake for blocked louvers; read CO2 with a meter on a working day; pull the filter; run water in the drain pan and watch where it ends; and look under the skirting for puddles or a torn ground cover.

Field card: Wall-Mount Units on Portable Buildings

Shut damper, loud unit, wet floor, dirty filter.

  • Many wall-mount dampers ship locked closed; a blank-off plate can be fitted. Confirm the position.
  • Fan on "auto" runs only while heating or cooling; occupied hours need continuous fan.
  • CO2 rise above outdoors = CO2 cfm / outdoor air cfm. Our example: 3,444 ppm closed, 1,030 ppm open.
  • The room takes time to settle: time constant = volume / airflow.
  • Noise leads people to switch units off; check mounting and panels first.
  • Condensate should end beyond the skirting, not on the ground cover.
  • HUD guide: 6-mil ground cover over 100 percent of the ground.
  • Check filters often; they are on the outside of the cabinet.
