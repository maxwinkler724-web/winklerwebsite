Ice Thermal Storage for Building Cooling: Ton-Hours, Night Charging and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some buildings make ice at night and melt it the next afternoon to carry the cooling load. Ice is a compact way to store cooling because melting soaks up a large amount of heat at a steady 32°F. This lesson covers the unit you size it in (the ton-hour), how much ice that takes, why night charging costs the chiller some capacity, and the difference between partial and full storage.

Why ice stores so much cooling

Melting a pound of ice at 32°F absorbs about 144 Btu without changing the ice-water temperature. That figure, the heat of fusion, is given in the federal thermal energy storage overview we cite below. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu per hour, so one ton-hour of cooling is 12,000 Btu. Dividing gives the key rule of thumb (our own arithmetic):

  • 12,000 Btu ÷ 144 Btu per pound = about 83 pounds of ice melted per ton-hour.
  • Water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon, so 83 pounds is roughly 10 gallons of water frozen and melted per ton-hour.

Real storage tanks hold a bit more than the latent figure, because the meltwater can also warm a few degrees and give up more cooling. One thesis from a university in our source list describes a rated tank capacity that includes both the latent part and the sensible part. When you size from manufacturer data, use the rated ton-hours, not the bare ice weight.

How the system works

At night the chiller does not make 44°F chilled water. It cools a water and glycol mix to below freezing and sends it through coils or tubes in a tank of water, where ice builds up. During the day, warm return fluid from the building goes through the tank and melts that ice, so the stored cooling does part or all of the work the chiller would do. The federal overview says ice storage needs a charging fluid at least 15°F below the normal operating range of conventional cooling equipment, and that a water and glycol mix or similar antifreeze solution is a common secondary coolant. Ice-on-coil internal melt, where warm fluid melts the ice from the inside out, is described there as one of the most common ice storage designs.

The capacity penalty of making ice

A chiller that normally makes 44°F water has to run a much lower evaporating temperature to make ice. A university paper on storage with a small packaged unit states the result plainly: the system loses capacity and efficiency because of the lower evaporating temperature. In that paper the charging supply was about 25°F, against 45°F when discharging. A university thesis on ice tank modeling used a 25 percent ethylene glycol mix and example tank inlet temperatures in the low 20s °F during charging, and notes that both capacity and efficiency of the chiller drop at those low temperatures.

Neither source gives one universal percentage, and we are not going to invent one. The penalty depends on the machine, the glycol temperature and the condenser conditions. Take the ice-making capacity from the maker's performance data for the exact chiller. The nighttime help is that outdoor air is cooler then, which eases the condenser side, but do not count on that to cancel the penalty.

Partial storage and full storage

  • Full storage. The chiller is off, or nearly so, during the occupied cooling period, and the ice meets the whole day's load. The thesis above describes it as a larger chiller than partial storage but smaller than a conventional plant, with more tanks.
  • Partial storage. The chiller runs during the day and the ice covers the part the chiller cannot. The same thesis describes the tank as meeting the portion of the load the chiller cannot meet. A common version is chiller-priority control: the chiller runs first and the ice is used only when the load exceeds the chiller's output.

The federal overview points out that storage lets chillers be sized closer to the average load instead of the peak, and that many office buildings have daytime cooling loads of two or more times their average load. That is the whole idea behind partial storage: a smaller chiller running more hours, with ice taking the peak.

Worked example (our own example)

All inputs here are our own assumptions for teaching, not data from any real building or product.

  • Design-day peak load: 400 tons.
  • Daily cooling energy: 2,400 ton-hours over a 10-hour occupied period (an average of 240 tons).
  • Charging window: 12 hours at night.
  • Assumption: in ice-making mode the chiller delivers 70 percent of its nominal tons. Use the real figure from maker data on a real job.
  • Assumption: no tank heat gain, no pump heat and a perfectly flat 10-hour load, which a real design would not have.

Conventional plant: 400 tons of chiller.

Full storage:

  1. Ice needed: 2,400 ton-hours × 12,000 Btu ÷ 144 Btu per pound = 200,000 pounds.
  2. Water frozen: 200,000 ÷ 8.34 = about 24,000 gallons.
  3. Charge rate: 2,400 ÷ 12 hours = 200 tons in ice-making mode.
  4. Nominal chiller: 200 ÷ 0.70 = about 286 tons.

Partial storage (chiller runs all day and makes ice at night): Let N be the nominal chiller size. Daytime output is N for 10 hours. Night ice storage is 0.70 × N × 12 hours = 8.4 N ton-hours. The two must add to 2,400:

  1. 10 N + 8.4 N = 2,400, so N = 2,400 ÷ 18.4 = about 130 tons.
  2. Storage: 8.4 × 130.4 = about 1,096 ton-hours.
  3. Ice: 1,096 × 12,000 ÷ 144 = about 91,300 pounds, or roughly 10,900 gallons of water.
  4. Peak hour check: 400 − 130 = about 270 tons must come from ice at the same hour, so the tank must be able to melt that fast.

Result in this example: full storage needs a chiller about 29 percent smaller than conventional, partial storage about 67 percent smaller, and partial storage needs less than half the ice. The step-4 check matters: ton-hours tell you how much the tank holds, but the discharge rate tells you whether it can deliver at the peak.

What to check in the field

  • Glycol strength and freeze protection: see the glycol lessons linked below.
  • Charging supply temperature and the controls that stop charging when the tank is full.
  • Tank discharge temperature near the end of the day, which rises as the ice runs out.
  • Ice-mode versus cooling-mode chiller readings, since the two modes behave very differently.

Educational context: a research facility at a national lab pairs an 80-ton chiller with three ice canisters and circulates glycol through them to make ice, to study how to schedule cooling and ice-making. It shows this is an active field of study, not just old equipment.

Field card: Ice thermal storage

144 Btu per pound, about 83 pounds per ton-hour.

  • 1 ton-hour = 12,000 Btu, about 83 lb of ice, about 10 gal of water.
  • Charging fluid runs well below normal chilled-water temperatures, so the chiller makes fewer tons.
  • Full storage: ice carries the whole day. Partial: chiller plus ice.
  • Size the tank in rated ton-hours and check the discharge rate at the peak hour.
  • Get the ice-making derate from the maker's data, not a rule of thumb.
