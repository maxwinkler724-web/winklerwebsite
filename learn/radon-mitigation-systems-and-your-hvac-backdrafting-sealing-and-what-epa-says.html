Radon Mitigation Systems and Your HVAC: Backdrafting, Sealing, and What EPA Says

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "radon mitigation and furnace backdrafting," here is the plain version: a radon fan works by creating suction under the floor, and a house that is already short of air can be pulled harder by exhaust fans and leaky ducts. This lesson explains how the system works, what to keep apart from your gas or oil appliances, and how the pros check for spillage afterward.

Check the rules where you live

Check the rules where you live. This is general education from EPA, New Jersey DEP and fan-maker pages read in 2026, not a ruling on any house. Radon testing, mitigation and combustion safety checks belong to trained, certified people. Standards change between editions, so confirm with your state radon office.

What EPA and NJ DEP say about the number

EPA's Health Risk of Radon page says to fix a home when the level is 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) or more, and to consider fixing at 2 to 4. It lists some risk at every level it shows, including the outdoor average of 0.4 pCi/L. New Jersey DEP's radon basics page also recommends mitigation at 4 pCi/L or more and recommends that all homes be tested. For a short-term test (2 to 7 days) DEP says the device goes in the lowest livable level, at least 20 inches off the floor, and that closed house conditions start at least 12 hours before a test shorter than four days. It also says fireplaces and wood stoves (unless they are the main heat source) and attic and window fans should not run during a test, because they change the air pressure in the house and so the radon reading.

How sub-slab depressurization works

Soil gas moves toward the lower pressure. DEP's information sheet says reduced indoor air pressure draws radon from the soil, and it lists exhaust fans, furnace venting, clothes dryers and the stack effect as things that lower indoor pressure. Sub-slab depressurization (SSD) flips the situation under the floor. A pipe is set into the gravel or soil under the slab, a fan pulls on it, and the soil gas goes up the pipe and out above the roof instead of into the basement. EPA's consumer guide calls this active subslab suction the most common method and says the fan creates negative pressure under the slab. DEP says the same method is the most common in New Jersey and that cracks and openings are sealed as part of the job. EPA adds that it does not recommend sealing alone.

Where the fan and pipe must not go

EPA's consumer guide says the exhaust fan must not be in or below a livable area; an attic or garage is typical. It says the discharge goes above the roof surface, 10 feet or more above the ground, and at least 10 feet from windows, doors or other openings unless it rises at least 2 feet above them. EPA's Radon Mitigation Standards say much the same, and add that fans may not be installed below ground or in conditioned space. The standard (ASTM E2121, in the edition we read) uses the same 10-foot and 2-foot figures. The point for HVAC work is that the radon system is its own pipe with its own fan, away from living space and away from openings. Where the radon pipe, a chimney and a flue outlet sit near each other on the roof or wall is a layout question for the mitigator and your heating tech together.

Why depressurization matters to a furnace

The PNNL Building America Solution Center explains that exhaust fans draw air out of the house and compete with fireplaces, gas water heaters, furnaces and boilers for air, and that fan pressures can reach 60 pascals (Pa). Negative pressure, it says, can cause backdrafting and prolonged spillage, and the same page says negative pressure can pull soil gases such as radon from a basement or crawlspace. It also says leaky return ducts where combustion appliances sit have been found to cause spillage and backdrafting. So one cause, a basement that is short of air, can raise radon entry and also weaken the draft at the water heater. EPA's 1994 Radon Mitigation Standards define backdrafting as depressurization overwhelming the buoyant force on hot flue gases. See Water Heater Backdrafting and Combustion Air for the appliance side.

Sealing return ducts in a basement

For radon, sealing is a helper, not the cure. For combustion, a leaky return in the furnace room is a spillage risk by the PNNL page above. Mastic and UL 181 listed tape on return joints, the furnace cabinet seams and any panned joist bay are covered in Sealing Duct Leaks. Sealing is not a replacement for a combustion safety test. The Radon Mitigation Standards also say proposed HVAC changes for radon control should be reviewed by the original designer or a licensed mechanical contractor, so call your HVAC company before anyone cuts into the ductwork.

Checking for backdrafting after mitigation

EPA's Radon Mitigation Standards give a test sequence before a fan system goes in. With windows and doors closed and registers open, the tester turns on exhaust and air distribution fans and the other combustion appliances, waits 5 minutes, and measures the pressure in the appliance room. A negative 5 Pa or more means a potential for backdrafting. Then the appliance under test runs for 5 minutes and the tester checks the draft hood with a smoke tube or a carbon dioxide analyzer. If spillage is found, a fan-powered system cannot be installed until it is corrected. After the fan is switched on, the standards require the same backdraft test, and any backdrafting caused by the system must be fixed before the system is placed in operation. The consumer guide points the same way: a contractor with backdrafting concerns should have a qualified inspector check the appliances. The posted E2121 text also asks the contractor to recommend a qualified person inspect natural draft appliances where backdrafting is a concern. EPA's 1994 standards are old, and the current edition of E2121 may differ, so ask your mitigator which edition they follow.

New Jersey: who may do the work

DEP's radon basics page says it is against the law for uncertified contractors to do mitigation work in New Jersey, and its information sheet says only certified individuals or the homeowner can conduct radon testing. Check DEP's current listing for a business and its certification date before you hire. DEP also says the mitigator should do a post-mitigation test, and that you can ask the DEP Radon Section for a free post-mitigation test by sending your contract.

Worked example: results and a fan curve

These radon numbers are made up for teaching. A basement tests at 8.0 pCi/L, double the 4 pCi/L action level. After an SSD system goes in, a test taken 3 days after startup (EPA says wait at least 24 hours and test within 30 days) reads 1.5 pCi/L.

  1. Drop: 8.0 - 1.5 = 6.5 pCi/L.
  2. Percent reduction: 6.5 / 8.0 = 0.8125, or about 81 percent.
  3. Check against the action level: 1.5 is below 4. It is also within EPA's "up to 99 percent" ceiling, which would have meant 8.0 x 0.01 = 0.08.

Now a fan number. RadonAway's Pro Series curve for the RP145 (4-inch duct) lists 124 cubic feet per minute (CFM) at 0.5 inch of water column (WC) static pressure and 81 CFM at 1.0 inch WC. The maker's listed maximum operating pressure is 1.7 inch WC.

  1. Airflow lost when the pipe resistance doubles: 124 - 81 = 43 CFM.
  2. As a percent: 43 / 124 = 0.347, about 35 percent.
  3. Convert pressure: 1 inch WC is about 249 Pa, so 0.5 inch WC is about 125 Pa.

That curve describes the fan working against its own piping, not the pressure in your house. For scale, the backdraft screening number above is 5 Pa, about 0.02 inch WC. The takeaway: a fan can pull much harder than a house's exhaust fans, and the fan system should be measured, not guessed. EPA's standards require the contractor to measure suction or flow in the piping. The posted E2121 text gives a sub-slab goal of 0.025 to 0.035 inch of water (6 to 9 Pa) under the slab.

Field card: Radon Mitigation Systems and Your HVAC

Fan under the floor, flue kept separate, test for spillage.

  • Action level: 4 pCi/L or more (EPA, NJ DEP). Test the lowest livable level.
  • SSD: pipe under the slab, fan in an attic or garage, discharge above the roof.
  • Discharge 10 ft from openings unless 2 ft above them.
  • Exhaust fans and leaky returns can depressurize a basement.
  • Backdraft screen in EPA standards: negative 5 Pa or more.
  • Spillage before or after install must be corrected.
  • NJ: mitigation by certified contractors only, per DEP.
  • Retest every two years.
