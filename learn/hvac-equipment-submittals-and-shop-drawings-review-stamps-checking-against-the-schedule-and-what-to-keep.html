HVAC Equipment Submittals and Shop Drawings: Review Stamps, Checking Against the Schedule, and What to Keep

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before a commercial unit is ordered, the contractor sends the design team a submittal: the maker's paper on the exact unit being proposed. The team marks it up and sends it back with a stamp. That returned package is the closest thing to a birth record for the equipment, and it is the document a tech or facility owner should be able to find years later.

Check the owner's documents

Check the project documents. Every project has its own specifications, stamp wording and deadlines. This page is general education built from public federal and university procedures read in 2026. Your contract and your design team decide what applies.

What a submittal package holds

Public specification sections split submittals into two groups. Action submittals need a decision from the design team. Informational submittals are filed and returned only if they do not comply (University of Houston and University of North Texas master specifications). For equipment, the university specifications we read ask for these pieces:

  • Product data: catalog sheets and maker specifications, marked to show which model and which options apply.
  • Performance data: printed performance curves and operating-range diagrams.
  • Wiring diagrams: the North Texas text asks that factory-installed wiring be shown apart from wiring the installer adds in the field.
  • Dimensions and clearances: the clearances needed to other construction, when the drawings do not already show them.
  • Shop drawings: project-specific drawings to scale that show how the unit fits and attaches to adjoining construction, with field-measured dimensions where they matter.

Electrical data (voltage, phase, MCA, MOCP), weights, refrigerant type and filter sizes normally sit in the maker's data sheet. The specifications do not list those words, so we treat them as our own reading of what a good package carries. The nameplate lesson linked below explains the electrical terms.

The contractor reviews first

Both university texts require the contractor to check each submittal for coordination with other work and for compliance with the contract documents, and to stamp it before sending it on. The North Texas text says the design team returns a submittal lacking that stamp. The Houston text adds that incomplete submittals are treated as nonresponsive and returned without review. The Army Corps of Engineers regulation ER 415-1-10 puts the same duty on the contractor's quality control manager and says government review is not a substitute for it.

The review stamps

There is no single national wording. The Corps quality-control system uses letter codes: A approved as submitted; B approved except as noted; C approved, resubmission required; E disapproved. Codes C, E, G and X are marked as needing resubmittal. The University of North Texas text uses "Reviewed," "Revise and Resubmit," "Rejected," "Furnish As Corrected" and "No Action Taken." The Houston and Colorado texts say only that an action stamp will be applied and send you to the project legend. So read the legend on your own job.

In plain terms: an approved or "approved as noted" return lets work go ahead, and the notes are orders. "Revise and resubmit" or a rejection means the unit may not be ordered yet. Both the Houston and North Texas texts allow only final submittals carrying an approval notation to be used for construction, and the Corps regulation says construction covered by a submittal does not start until it is properly approved.

Checking a submittal against the schedule

The drawing schedule and the specification say what was designed. The submittal says what is being offered. Compare these line by line:

  • Capacity at the design conditions on the schedule, not the maker's nominal rating.
  • Voltage and phase, then MCA and MOCP against the circuit shown on the electrical drawings.
  • Refrigerant type and the options called for, such as economizer, controls interface or coil coating.
  • Filter size and count, so the spares and the rack match.
  • Service clearances against the roof or mechanical-room layout.
  • Operating weight against the roof structure and the curb.

Worked example (made up, labeled as ours)

Our own example. Every number is an assumption for practice, not from a real project. Schedule row for RTU-3: 460 V, 3 phase; cooling 112 MBH total at the scheduled design conditions; four 20x25x2 filters; roof allowance 1,050 lb for the unit. Submittal line for the proposed unit: 208 V, 3 phase; cooling 108 MBH at the same conditions; four 20x24x2 filters; operating weight 1,150 lb.

  1. Voltage: 208 V against 460 V. This is a mismatch. The feeder, disconnect and breaker were designed for 460 V.
  2. Capacity: 112 minus 108 is 4 MBH short. 4 divided by 112 is about 3.6 percent below the schedule. Whether that is acceptable depends on the tolerance in the specification, so it is flagged, not decided by us.
  3. Filters: 20x24 against 20x25 does not match the filter rack or the owner's stock.
  4. Weight: 1,150 minus 1,050 is 100 lb over the roof allowance. The structural engineer needs to see it.

This line should not get an approved stamp. It gets "revise and resubmit" or "rejected," with each comment written next to the item. If the contractor wants the 208 V unit, that is a substitution request, not a quiet change.

Substitutions and "or equal"

The Houston substitution section describes a substitution as a contractor-proposed change in products or equipment from what the contract documents require, and it does not allow substitutions made for the contractor's convenience. A request there needs a detailed comparison of significant qualities such as performance, weight, size, durability and warranties, plus product data, and the contractor must certify that the substitute complies and fits. The contractor also gives up the right to ask for more money or time if it fails to perform. "Or equal" is a bid-time phrase that varies by project, so ask the design team what it means in your documents. We did not find an "or equal" definition in the texts we read.

Why the approved copy is the one to keep

The first submittal is not what was bought. The final returned copy, with the stamp and every note, shows what was accepted. The Corps regulation says the contract is not normally substantially complete until useable O&M documentation reaches the owner's staff, and says as-built drawings should be kept up continuously. A tech chasing a part number, a clearance or a wiring question years later needs that package, not the first bid sheet. Ask for it at turnover, scan it, and file it with the nameplate photos. Field card: HVAC equipment submittals

Schedule says what was designed; submittal says what is offered.

  • Package: product data, performance data, wiring diagram, dimensions and clearances, options.
  • Read the project stamp legend; wording varies.
  • Check capacity at design conditions, voltage and phase, MCA and MOCP, refrigerant, filters, clearances, weight.
  • Approved as noted means the notes are orders.
  • A different product is a substitution request.
  • Keep the final stamped copy with the O&M file.
