Walk-In Evaporator Fan Motors: Shaded-Pole vs PSC vs ECM

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A walk-in evaporator has several small fan motors that run nearly all day, every day. Each one uses a little electricity, and every watt it uses ends up as heat inside the box. This lesson shows why those watts count twice, what federal rules say about the motors, and what to check on a retrofit.

Three motor types in one small package

A shaded-pole motor is simple and cheap, but it wastes most of what it draws. In dynamometer tests at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, a 12 W output shaded-pole motor drew 46.7 W and measured 25.7 percent efficient. A PSC (permanent split capacitor) motor has a run capacitor and does better; an older Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory brief put shaded-pole efficiency near 35 percent and PSC at about 50 to 65 percent for the motors of its day, so treat those as older, rougher figures. An ECM (electronically commutated, brushless DC) motor has a permanent-magnet rotor and built-in electronics. The same Oak Ridge test measured three 12 W ECMs drawing 18.0 to 19.6 W, at 61 to 67 percent efficiency. For a larger 38 W output size, the shaded-pole motor drew 160.3 W and the ECMs 52.4 to 61.1 W.

For the full ECM story in air handlers, see ECM Blower Motors. The idea is the same here, only smaller.

Why the watts count twice

The motor sits inside the airstream. Whatever electrical power it draws and does not turn into moving air ends up as heat in the box. Oak Ridge states that motor losses are released as heat and increase the refrigeration load. So a wasteful motor costs you once at the meter and a second time when the compressor must remove that heat. DOE uses the same thinking elsewhere: in its walk-in final rule it assumes 75 percent of a door device's power becomes added heat load on the compressor. Our own assumption for a motor mounted inside the box: all of its input power ends up as heat in the cooled space.

What the federal rules say

DOE's walk-in cooler and freezer rules are in 10 CFR Part 431, Subpart R. Section 431.306(a) lists the prescriptive component requirements. As we read the eCFR text, evaporator fan motors under 1 horsepower and under 460 volts must be electronically commutated or 3-phase. Condenser fan motors under 1 horsepower must be ECM, PSC or 3-phase. Shaded-pole motors are not on either list. The Federal Register notice for the amended standards gives a compliance date of June 5, 2017, and notes that DOE knew of no other motor type matching ECM performance. Our own plain-language reading: a new walk-in with a shaded-pole evaporator fan in that size range should not be sold, and a replacement fan motor in an existing box is a separate question. Check the current eCFR text and your local requirements before you decide, and see Walk-In DOE Efficiency Rules for the rest of Subpart R.

Fan speed and off-cycle control

Evaporator fans often keep running when the compressor is off, which just stirs room-warm air over the coil and adds fan heat. Controls that slow or cycle the fans during off cycle can cut that. The Texas Technical Reference Manual (Volume 3, 2024) assumes fans are off 46 percent of the time when controls are fitted, and 0 percent without. Treat 46 percent as that manual's planning number, not a field measurement. Fans must still run through defrost termination logic; see Walk-In Defrost: Fan Delay and Icing.

Worked example: four shaded-pole motors to ECM

Assumptions (labeled). Motor power uses the Oak Ridge 12 W output motors: 46.7 W shaded-pole, 19.6 W ECM. Hours are 8,760 a year (the Texas manual also uses 8,760 for coolers and freezers). Our own assumptions: four motors, a cooler refrigeration COP of 2.0, and no fan controls. For comparison, the Texas manual uses COPs of 1.94 for a cooler and 2.51 for a freezer in one climate zone.

  1. Watts saved per motor: 46.7 W minus 19.6 W is 27.1 W.
  2. Electric kWh per motor: 27.1 W x 8,760 h / 1,000 = 237.4 kWh per year.
  3. Heat no longer in the box: 27.1 W x 3.412 = 92.5 Btu/h per motor.
  4. Compressor energy avoided: 237.4 kWh / 2.0 COP = 118.7 kWh.
  5. Total per motor: 237.4 + 118.7 = 356.1 kWh per year (the same as 237.4 x (1 + 1/2.0)).
  6. Four motors: 356.1 x 4 = about 1,424 kWh per year.

Real motors will differ. Actual draw changes with voltage, blade load and motor size, and our own 100 percent heat assumption ignores any motor outside the airstream. The Texas manual's larger 1/20 horsepower row, 123 W versus 53 W, gives a bigger per-motor saving. A cooler with fan controls would save less on the motor change alone, and more on the control itself. The result is an estimate, not a bill guarantee.

Retrofit checks

  • Rotation. Match the original airflow direction. Confirm rotation against the motor label and the arrow on the old one.
  • Mounting. Check the bracket, shaft length and orientation so the blade sits where the shroud expects it.
  • Blade pitch and size. A different blade can change airflow and amps, so match the original blade.
  • Voltage. Confirm line voltage matches the motor label.
  • Airflow. Look for even air through the coil and throw across the box after the swap.
  • Defrost interaction. Confirm the fan delay and termination still work, and the fans do not blow meltwater into the box.
  • Capacitors. Follow the new motor's instructions on whether a capacitor is used, and do not leave an unneeded one in the circuit. See Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures.

Rebates

Many utilities offer a rebate for replacing shaded-pole evaporator motors with ECMs, usually with an application, motor details and a receipt. Programs vary and change, so ask the utility before you buy. Do not count on one when you quote a job.

Field card: Walk-In Evaporator Fan Motors

Motor watts become box heat, so count them twice.

  • Shaded-pole: about 26 percent efficient in the Oak Ridge test. ECM: 61 to 67 percent.
  • DOE Subpart R: evaporator fan motors under 1 hp and under 460 V are ECM or 3-phase.
  • Condenser fan motors under 1 hp: ECM, PSC or 3-phase.
  • Total kWh saved = fan kWh saved x (1 + 1/COP).
  • Check rotation, mounting, blade, voltage, airflow and defrost fan delay after a swap.
  • Rebates vary; ask the utility first.
