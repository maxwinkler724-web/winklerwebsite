Ideal Gas Law for Nitrogen Pressure Tests: Why a Standing Test Drops Overnight, the Psia and Rankine Correction, Sun on Line Sets, and When a Drop Is a Leak

Last updated: 2026-11-03

You pressurize a new line set with nitrogen on a warm afternoon, leave it, and find a lower reading in the morning. Before you call that a leak, ask how much of the drop the cold night alone would cause. A sealed volume of gas follows a simple rule, and with a calculator you can check it in two minutes.

Why a tight system still loses pressure

Nitrogen in a closed set of lines is a fixed amount of gas in a fixed volume. When that gas cools, its molecules slow down and push less on the walls, so the pressure falls even though nothing escaped. When it warms, the pressure rises. A university gas-law primer states the rule for a sealed container: with volume and amount of gas held fixed, pressure rises in direct proportion to temperature. NASA Glenn's equation-of-state page gives the full ideal gas law and says the temperature in it must be an absolute temperature. Our lesson on nitrogen pressure testing and leak detection methods covers the setup, test pressures and holds. This page goes deeper on the one thing that most often fools a standing test: temperature.

The formula, and why it needs absolute numbers

For a fixed amount of gas in a fixed volume, the ideal gas law reduces to P1 / T1 = P2 / T2. Two details make or break it.

  • Pressure must be absolute (psia). A gauge reads relative to the atmosphere. A college physics text says absolute pressure is the sum of gauge pressure and atmospheric pressure, and lists atmospheric pressure as 14.7 pounds per square inch. So psia = psig + 14.7 at sea level. Our lesson on pressure units for refrigerant work covers psig versus psia and why altitude shifts the 14.7.
  • Temperature must be absolute (Rankine). NASA Glenn defines the Rankine scale as the absolute scale with Fahrenheit-sized degrees and gives R = F + 459.69. We round to F + 460, which does not change any answer on this page. Plugging Fahrenheit straight into the formula is the classic mistake: it would predict that 300 psig at 85 F becomes about 212 psig at 60 F, which is nonsense.

Then convert the answer back to a gauge reading by subtracting 14.7.

Worked example A: an overnight drop that is only temperature

Our own example, with assumed inputs. We assume a tight, dry system with fixed volume, filled with nitrogen to 300 psig at 85 F in the afternoon, and sea-level atmospheric pressure of 14.7 psi. In the morning the gauge reads 288 psig and the lines are at 60 F.

  1. Convert the starting pressure: P1 = 300 + 14.7 = 314.7 psia.
  2. Convert the temperatures: T1 = 85 + 460 = 545 R. T2 = 60 + 460 = 520 R.
  3. Solve for the expected pressure: P2 = 314.7 × 520 / 545 = 300.26 psia.
  4. Back to gauge: 300.26 − 14.7 = 285.56, or about 285.6 psig.

Temperature alone should have taken the reading from 300 down to about 285.6, a drop of 14.4 psi. The gauge actually shows 288, which is 12 psi down, and 2.4 psi above the no-leak prediction. That is not a leak signal. As a cross-check, solve the other way: 288 psig is 302.7 psia, which matches a line temperature of 545 × 302.7 / 314.7 = 524.2 R, or 64.2 F. A line set a few degrees warmer than the air is easy to believe, especially if it ran through a wall or sat near a warm slab. Verdict: temperature explains the drop, so the test passes.

Worked example B: the same morning, but it is a leak

Our own example, with the same assumptions. Same fill, same lines at 60 F, but now the gauge reads 276 psig.

  1. The no-leak prediction is unchanged: 285.6 psig (300.26 psia).
  2. The shortfall is 285.6 − 276 = 9.6 psi below prediction.
  3. What line temperature would make 276 psig normal? 276 psig is 290.7 psia, so T2 = 545 × 290.7 / 314.7 = 503.4 R, or 43.4 F. If the lines are really near 60 F, they are not 43 F, so cooling cannot explain the reading.
  4. For gas lost, compare the amount of gas to what should be there: 290.7 / 300.26 = 0.968, so roughly 3 percent of the nitrogen has gone.

Verdict: leak. Only about 3 percent of the gas is missing, which is why a flat "it went from 300 to 276 overnight" story needs the correction to be read correctly. Without the correction you would see a 24 psi drop and not know how much was temperature and how much was loss.

A quick rule of thumb

At this pressure each degree Fahrenheit moves the reading by about 0.58 psi (our own: 314.7 psia / 545 R = 0.577 psi per degree). The slope grows with absolute pressure, so a 150 psig test moves only about 0.30 psi per degree (164.7 / 545), while a 450 psig test moves about 0.85. A 25 degree overnight swing on a 300 psig test is therefore worth roughly 14 psi, which is the same size as many "mystery leaks".

Sun on a line set

The same math runs in reverse in the afternoon. Using the same starting point, if a sunlit line set warms from 85 F to 120 F, the pressure climbs to 314.7 × 580 / 545 = 334.9 psia, or about 320.2 psig. That is 20 psi above the fill pressure with no added gas. Two practical points follow. First, never read a rising gauge as proof the system is tight. Second, treat the added pressure as real stored energy: stay under the lowest rated pressure of any component, and use a regulator and relief device as your procedure requires. OSHA has stated there is no federal standard specifically about gas-line leak testing, and points to general rules, including that cylinders be used only as the supplier intended and that damaged ones not be used, so follow the maker's test-pressure limits.

Limits of the correction

  • Gauge resolution. Many analog gauges have 2 psi divisions on a 0 to 500 range. At 0.58 psi per degree, one division is worth about 3.5 degrees (our own: 2 / 0.577). A one-division difference is inside your reading error.
  • Mixed temperatures along the run. The gas has no single temperature. Part of the run may sit in a hot attic, part in a cool basement, part in the sun. Treat the average, weighted by how much gas volume sits in each zone, as the best guess. Our own illustration: if half the volume is at 85 F and half at 60 F, the average is 72.5 F and the predicted reading is 314.7 × 532.5 / 545 − 14.7 = 292.8 psig. A single air temperature can mislead you by many psi.
  • Slow response. Metal, insulation and large coils lag the air. A reading taken at sunrise may lag the overnight low. Measure the pipe surface at several points rather than trusting the air thermometer.
  • Not ideal at every pressure. The ideal gas law is an approximation; at test pressures used in the field it is good enough for a go or no-go decision, not for measuring a leak rate.
  • Altitude and weather. The 14.7 changes with altitude and with the barometer, but a one or two psi shift in atmosphere changes the predicted drop only slightly.

A method you can use on site

  1. Record the gauge reading, the pipe temperature at several points and the date and time when you stop adding gas.
  2. Record the same values at the end of the hold.
  3. Convert both pressures to psia and both temperatures to Rankine.
  4. Compute the expected end pressure with P1/T1 = P2/T2 and compare it with the gauge reading.
  5. If the reading is clearly below the prediction by more than your gauge resolution and temperature uncertainty, hunt for the leak. Our lessons on nitrogen pressure testing and trace-gas leak detection cover how to find it. If it is close to the prediction, document the correction on the job record.

For equal start and end temperatures, no correction is needed, which is why a short test on a steady-temperature day is more convincing than a long one across a cold night.

Field card: Temperature correction for a nitrogen standing test

Absolute pressure, absolute temperature, then subtract 14.7.

  • P1 / T1 = P2 / T2 for sealed nitrogen at fixed volume.
  • P in psia = psig + 14.7. T in Rankine = F + 460.
  • Expected gauge reading = P2 (psia) minus 14.7.
  • About 0.58 psi per degree F at 300 psig (smaller at lower pressure).
  • Example: 300 psig at 85 F becomes 285.6 psig at 60 F with no leak.
  • A reading at or above the prediction is not a leak; well below it is.
  • Check gauge resolution and mixed line temperatures before judging.
