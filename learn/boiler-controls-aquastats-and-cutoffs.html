Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

A boiler holds hot water or steam under real energy. A few small controls keep it from overheating, running dry or building too much pressure, and they are worth understanding well.

The big picture

A boiler heating system has two kinds of controls. Operating controls decide when to heat in normal use. Safety controls step in when something goes outside safe limits. Operating controls can be adjusted by a tech for comfort. Safety controls exist to stop the boiler, and they should not be adjusted, bypassed or removed to keep it running.

The aquastat

An aquastat senses boiler water temperature and switches circuits based on it. Technician references describe a few common functions:

  • High limit. The burner shuts off when the water reaches a set temperature, even if the thermostat still wants heat. The burner restarts after the water cools by some amount.
  • Differential. The gap between where the burner shuts off and where it comes back on. A small gap can cause short cycling and a large gap can cause swings in temperature.
  • Low limit. Keeps the boiler water warm all the time, often so a coil can make domestic hot water on demand. It acts without a call from the thermostat.

Some aquastats also work with a circulator, so the pump runs only when there is heat to move. Settings depend on the system design and the manufacturer, so look them up and do not guess.

The low-water cutoff

A boiler that fires without enough water can overheat and be damaged, and in the worst case fail. A low-water cutoff (LWCO) watches the water level and stops the burner if it drops too low. Some use a float, and others use probes that sense water electrically. Because sediment and scale can stick a float or foul a probe, manufacturers usually call for regular checks and blowdown. A burner that will not light with a low-water lockout is doing its job, so the question is why the water is low.

Pressure and temperature protection

  • Relief valve. The last line of defense. If pressure or temperature rises past its rating, it opens to release. It should never be capped, plugged or replaced with a different rating.
  • Pressure control or limit. On steam boilers, a pressure control shuts the burner off at a low pressure that is far below the relief valve rating.
  • Expansion tank and fill valve. Water expands when heated, and the tank gives it room. A waterlogged tank or a leaking fill valve can cause pressure trouble that looks like a controls problem.
  • Gauge. The combined pressure and temperature gauge is the quickest way to see what the system is really doing.

Safe habits

Hot water and steam can scald in an instant, and a relief valve discharge is dangerous. Let the system cool and relieve pressure the way the maker describes before opening anything. Use lockout/tagout on the power. If a relief valve is leaking or discharging, find the cause rather than replacing it with a bigger one. Never jump out a low-water cutoff or limit. And any boiler work that involves gas or oil firing falls under the rules of a licensed tech where you live.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, timings, wiring steps or test values, and those vary by manufacturer, model and local rules.

Field card: Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs

Operating controls decide, safety controls protect.

  • An aquastat's high limit shuts off the burner at a set water temperature.
  • The differential is the gap between off and on points.
  • A low-water cutoff blocks firing when the water level is too low.
  • The relief valve is the last line of defense, never cap or swap it.
  • Never bypass or reset limits and cutoffs to keep a boiler running.
