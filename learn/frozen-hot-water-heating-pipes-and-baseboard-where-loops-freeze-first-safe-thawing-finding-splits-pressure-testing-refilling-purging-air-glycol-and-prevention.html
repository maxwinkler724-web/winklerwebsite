Frozen Hot-Water Heating Pipes and Baseboard After a No-Heat Event

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a boiler is off for two days at 10 F, the water in the pipes stops moving and the coldest pipes can freeze solid. A frozen loop can look fine until it thaws, then leak. This lesson covers where loops freeze, how to thaw them safely, how to find the damage, and how to refill and purge a loop.

Why a hot-water loop freezes

Freezing needs two things: a cold spot and still water. With the circulator off, water sits in the pipe and the coldest section drops to freezing first. Water gets bigger when it freezes. The USGS says the density of ice is about 9 percent lower than liquid water. Our own arithmetic: 1 ÷ 0.91 = 1.10, so the water wants roughly 10 percent more room. A closed copper tube or a fin-tube element has none to give, so the pipe stretches or splits. The USGS also says ice is about 90 percent as dense as water, and that figure works out to about 11 percent, so we say "roughly 10 percent."

Where loops freeze first

State extension pages for homeowners name the same trouble spots for water pipes: pipes near outer walls, in crawl spaces and attics, in unheated basements and garages, and in cabinets against exterior walls. Hot-water heating piping follows the same rule. Our own map of those spots for a hydronic system:

  • Baseboard on an outside wall. Spot 1 in the drawing. The element sits against the coldest wall, often behind a drape or furniture.
  • Pipes in a crawlspace. Spot 2. Supply and return mains run through unheated space, sometimes with thin or missing insulation.
  • Cantilevered floors. Spot 3. A room that hangs over open air has cold air under the floor, and pipes in the floor cavity sit next to it.
  • Garage and unheated rooms. Spot 4. Pipes in or against the garage wall, or in a porch or addition, see outdoor air.

First steps: stop the damage before you thaw

Our own working order, which follows the general advice in the extension pages below to know your shutoffs and to call a plumber when you cannot find or reach the frozen section:

  1. Turn the boiler off at its switch. Do not fire a boiler into a frozen loop.
  2. Shut the water fill (the valve on the cold supply to the automatic fill) so a split does not keep feeding water onto the floor.
  3. Look and feel for the frozen section. A baseboard that stays ice-cold along one run, frost on a pipe, or a bulge in a pipe are all clues.
  4. If a pipe has burst, shut the water off and call a licensed plumber and your insurer. Be careful of electric shock near standing water; the Illinois extension page warns about this.

Thawing safely

The extension pages agree on the basics. Never use an open flame or torch: the Illinois and Iowa State pages say so plainly, and the Utah State page adds fire hazard as the reason. Do not use boiling water either (Utah State, burn risk). Heat the pipe gradually. Utah State says that when frozen plumbing is heated slowly the trapped water will not boil, which helps prevent pipe damage. Acceptable sources named by Iowa State and Utah State include a hair dryer, an electric heating pad wrapped around the pipe, towels soaked in hot water, and a space heater kept away from anything flammable. North Carolina State advises starting thawing at the open end and working toward the frozen section so steam can escape.

Heat tape. Utah State lists heat tape as a way to thaw, and North Carolina State says to choose listed (lab-tested) heat tape and follow the manufacturer's instructions. We do not have a cited limit on length or wrapping, so read the instructions that came with the tape, and do not leave it unattended on a frozen pipe. In a hot-water loop, keep heat off valves, circulators and plastic parts unless the maker says they can take it.

Our own note for loops: you cannot open a "faucet" on a sealed loop. Opening the bleeder on the baseboard (or a drain at a low point) gives thawing water and steam a place to go. Thaw from the end nearest that opening.

Finding the split

  • Split copper. Look for a lengthwise slit, usually a few inches long, in the pipe that was coldest. The pipe may not leak until the ice melts, so keep watching as the loop thaws.
  • Cracked fin-tube. Look at the tube inside the cover, especially at elbows and low points. Bent fins, rust stains or a wet spot on the floor point to a crack.
  • Cast iron. A cast iron radiator can crack through the casting. Look for a seep or weeping line on a section.

Mark every damaged spot with tape. Frozen systems usually freeze in more than one place, and Iowa State notes that one frozen pipe often means others are frozen too.

Pressure test before you refill

Our own practice: repair or replace the damaged pieces first, then isolate the repaired section if you can, and fill it with water to the normal cold-fill pressure. Watch the gauge and the repairs. A steady gauge with dry joints means the loop holds; a falling gauge means keep looking. The Massachusetts Department of Public Safety residential HVAC basics document gives a minimum boiler water pressure of 12 psi for a two-story house, and says its rules of thumb are not for designing a system. Your boiler's label and manual set the actual fill pressure for your equipment; see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure and the fill valve lesson.

Refilling and purging air

Open the fill slowly and bleed each high point and baseboard until water, not air, comes out. Air trapped in a thawed loop stops the flow just like ice did. Work from the lowest zone up, then run the circulator and bleed again. For symptoms of leftover air, see Cold Air in a Hot Water Heating System.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers, not from a real job. A house loses heat for two days with 10 F outside. The north-wall baseboard loop is frozen and the rest of the house is fine.

  1. Assume 10 gallons of water in the frozen section. Freezing swells it by about 10 percent: 10 ÷ 0.91 = 11.0 gallons of ice volume. That is about 1 gallon more than the pipe can hold.
  2. The boiler and fill are shut off. The heated section is thawed with a hair dryer starting at the open bleeder, and a split shows in one fin-tube elbow.
  3. The elbow is replaced. The loop is isolated and filled to 12 psi cold (our assumed example fill; the Massachusetts document gives 12 psi as a two-story minimum).
  4. Reading A, no leak: 12.0 psi at fill, 12.0 psi after 30 minutes. Change = 12.0 − 12.0 = 0 psi.
  5. Reading B, leak: 12.0 psi at fill, 9.0 psi after 15 minutes. Change = 12.0 − 9.0 = 3.0 psi, which is 3.0 ÷ 15 = 0.2 psi per minute. Keep looking for a second split.
  6. With Reading A, the loop is purged until air stops, the fill is reopened and set to 12 psi cold, and the boiler is restarted. Pressure rises as the water warms; see the expansion tank lesson.

When glycol makes sense

Glycol is useful for loops that cannot be kept warm or drained: a garage unit, a snow-melt run or a seasonal building. A government-lab paper on inhibited glycols (OSTI) says they are used for protection against corrosion and freezing, discusses the difference between burst and freeze protection, and states that automotive antifreeze should not be used in HVAC systems. It also discusses declines in heat transfer, so a glycol loop moves less heat per gallon. It does not replace insulation or heat in the house. See Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops for testing and strength.

Prevention

  • Insulate pipes in crawlspaces, garages and cantilevers, and seal cold air leaks near them. Utah State warns that insulation alone does not protect pipes from prolonged freezing.
  • North Carolina State advises keeping the heat on and not setting it lower than 55 degrees; see away-from-home settings.
  • Do not block baseboard with drapes and furniture on a cold wall.
  • Test the no-heat checks early: Hot Water No Heat: Circulator, Zone Valve and Air.
  • Know where the boiler switch and fill shutoff are before the cold arrives.

Field card: Frozen Hot-Water Heating Loop

Moving, warm water does not freeze. Still water in a cold spot does.

  • Cold spots: outside-wall baseboard, crawlspace pipes, cantilevered floors, garage walls.
  • Boiler off and fill shut before any thawing.
  • No open flame or torch, no boiling water. Heat gradually.
  • Look for splits in copper, fin-tube and cast iron; there may be more than one.
  • Repair, then pressure test before the full refill.
  • Purge air at every high point and baseboard.
  • Burst pipe: shut the water, call a licensed plumber and your insurer.
