Ventilating Horse Stables and Riding Arenas: Ridge Vents, Eave Openings, Moisture and Ammonia, Unheated Barns, Tack Room Heat, Summer Fans, Arena Dust and Fire Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A horse stable is not a poultry house. The animals are big, the building is usually open and unheated, and the best ventilation is often just good openings placed in the right spots. This lesson covers how those openings work, what moisture and ammonia do to a stable, why cold is less of a problem for horses than for people, and where a technician gets pulled in: tack room heat, wash stalls, waterers, summer fans, arena dust and fire risk.

Check the sources

Check the sources. This page was built from university extension pages read in 2026 and is general education. Building rules, insurance requirements and the horse owner's own vet advice come first. Confirm sizes and rates with the designer of the actual building.

Start with natural ventilation

Most horse barns breathe by buoyancy and wind. Warm air rises and leaves high, cooler air comes in low, and wind pushes more through. A University of Minnesota Extension page says a naturally ventilated barn needs inlets, such as sidewall vents or eave openings, and outlets, such as a ridge or roof chimney opening. It adds that these barns cost less to build but give less control over airflow. A Penn State Extension page notes that once wind passes about 1 mph it matters more than buoyancy, so openings must work in still air and in a breeze.

That Penn State page gives these sizing rules of thumb for cold weather: at least 1 square foot of permanent opening per stabled horse, open all year; about 1 inch of continuous eave slot for each 10 feet of building width; and a ridge opening about the same size as the eave opening. Without a ridge vent the eave slot has to double. It suggests eave openings about 10 to 12 feet above the floor so incoming air mixes with warmer air before it reaches the horses, and says to avoid fine insect screen or perforated soffit at the eaves because dust and chaff clog them within months. A hinged panel can narrow the slot in winter, but not more than 75% of it should be covered.

For warm weather the same page recommends openings equal to 5% to 10% of each stall's floor area, such as a Dutch door top or a window. In a hot barn, open stall doors and windows let the building act like a shed. Keep a clear path: the page also warns that the middle stalls of a double-aisle barn can get almost no fresh air.

Moisture and ammonia

Penn State Extension says moisture comes from horse breath, bathing and cleaning, and that a buildup raises condensation, odor, ammonia and germ risk. It recommends 4 to 8 air changes per hour for stables in general, treats condensation on inner surfaces as a sign the building is too closed, and says to check air at floor level as well as head height because dust and ammonia collect near bedding and manure. Minnesota Extension adds that air exchange also carries away dust, germs and fumes. Wet bedding is the usual ammonia source, so a stable that smells of ammonia is telling you both the bedding and the ventilation need attention.

Why many horse barns are left unheated

Penn State Extension says horses are most comfortable between 45°F and 75°F, that acclimated horses with a long coat and good nutrition can tolerate temperatures below 0°F, and that in winter the stable should stay no more than 5 to 10 degrees F warmer than outdoors. It says freezing inside is acceptable in northern climates. A Rutgers fact sheet explains the reason: horses grow a thicker coat as days shorten, a dry coat insulates well, and fermenting hay in the gut makes heat. The same sheet warns that a wet coat loses heat fast and that shelter lets a horse keep up to 20% more body heat than full exposure. It also quotes cattle figures (comfortable to 18°F with a dry winter coat, but 59°F when wet or clipped) and says horse numbers are likely similar, though those are not horse data.

So heating a whole barn tightly shut works against the animals. A warm, closed barn is a damp barn. Minnesota Extension says a barn that is mechanically ventilated needs well-insulated walls and ceiling, while a naturally ventilated one needs only a modest roof R-value, and Penn State says an unheated barn needs about R-5 on the roof to limit condensation. The point of roof insulation here is stopping drips, not holding heat.

Heat only the small spaces

  • Tack rooms and grooming areas. Penn State's advice is to heat a grooming or tack area rather than the whole barn. A small, separate room that is insulated and sealed from the aisle makes sense, and our suggestion is any heater on its own circuit and kept clear of stored gear.
  • Wash stalls. Bathing adds moisture, so give the wash stall its own exhaust or open outlet and floor drainage, and do not let that damp air drift into hay storage.
  • Waterers and lines. Minnesota Extension says heated water lines and tanks help prevent freezing in naturally ventilated barns. Penn State says buried water lines should match the depth of the supply plumbing. A frost-free hydrant or heated bucket is a small electrical load in a dusty, damp place, so it needs proper wiring and regular inspection.
  • Supplemental heat. The same Minnesota page gives about 4,000 Btu per hour (gas) or 1 kW (electric) per horse as a rough size for supplemental heat in a barn that is kept warm. Treat that as a ceiling for planning, not a recommendation to heat.

Summer: fans and heat stress

For hot weather Minnesota Extension lists mechanical figures per 1,000 pounds of horse: about 25 cfm in cold weather for moisture control, about 125 cfm total in mild weather and about 325 cfm total in hot weather. Penn State gives the same pattern as 25, 100 and 200 to 350 cfm. If a mechanical system is ever used, Penn State sizes inlets at 1.7 square feet per 1,000 cfm of fan capacity. Penn State says mechanical ventilation is not commonly needed for horses. Interior fans only stir existing air, so Minnesota Extension says to use them to cool animals, not to ventilate. A University of Georgia Extension page recommends fans to speed sweat evaporation on an overheated horse, notes direct sun can be up to 15°F hotter than shade, and says moving air helps even in humid weather. Our own suggestion: mount barn fans out of reach, guarded, and on protected circuits, because chaff and cobwebs collect on them.

Indoor arenas and dust

An indoor arena shares air with the stable if it is attached, so dust there affects the barn too. Penn State Extension says clay and silt cause most arena dust, that footing with more than 5% of material passing a 200-mesh sieve is likely too dusty, and that moisture must be managed by the operator since indoor arenas get no rain. It suggests keeping footing evenly moist to about 3 inches deep with frequent short watering, using ceiling-mounted mist sprayers rather than side sprinklers that can overwater, and notes that freezing weather makes watering harder. Its figure for a resting horse is about 16 gallons of air breathed per minute, rising to roughly 600 gallons per minute during hard work, so dusty air matters. A University of Kentucky page reports that about 77% of more than 450 survey respondents were concerned about dust, moisture or low air movement in indoor arenas. For the technician, the practical points are open ridge and sidewall louvers that can be opened wide, fans that move air without whipping up the surface, and electrical gear kept clear of dust.

Fire safety with heaters and electrical

Minnesota Extension says space heaters are the leading cause of barn fires. Penn State's fire page advises avoiding portable heaters and dangling heat lamps. If one cannot be avoided, as in a tack room, keep it far from anything flammable, clean off dust regularly, never leave it running unattended, and use only models that shut off when tipped. It says dust, chaff, bird nests, insect colonies and cobwebs can start electrical fires, so sockets, lights and heaters should be inspected and kept clear, frayed cords taken out of service, and wiring checked by an electrician. It also advises storing hay in a separate dry building, keeping hay under 15% moisture, and treating hay at 150 to 170°F inside as dangerous: stay away and call 9-1-1. We add one service rule of our own: a repair person should not leave a heater plugged into a shared barn outlet as a temporary fix.

Worked example (our own): how much air to hold the humidity down

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not sourced data. Moisture per horse is our assumption and varies widely with bedding, washing and manure handling.

Assumptions (ours): 12 horses; each adds 0.3 lb of water vapor per hour to the air; outdoor air 20°F at 70% relative humidity, which we figure as about 0.0015 lb of water per lb of dry air; target stable air 35°F at 75% relative humidity, about 0.0032 lb per lb; dry air takes up about 12.5 cubic feet per lb at these temperatures. We computed those humidity values from standard vapor pressure relations and rounded them.

  1. Moisture to remove: 12 horses × 0.3 lb/h = 3.6 lb/h.
  2. Moisture each pound of incoming air can carry off: 0.0032 − 0.0015 = 0.0017 lb.
  3. Dry air needed: 3.6 ÷ 0.0017 = about 2,120 lb/h.
  4. Volume: 2,120 × 12.5 = about 26,500 cubic feet per hour, or 26,500 ÷ 60 = about 440 cfm.
  5. Per horse: 440 ÷ 12 = about 37 cfm, close to the 25 cfm per 1,000 pounds cold-weather moisture figure the Minnesota page gives. A horse of about 1,000 pounds is our own assumption here.

Air change version (ours): a 48 ft by 36 ft barn with a 14 ft average height holds 48 × 36 × 14 = 24,192 cubic feet. At the 4 to 8 air changes per hour from the Penn State page, 4 × 24,192 ÷ 60 = about 1,610 cfm and 8 × 24,192 ÷ 60 = about 3,225 cfm. Our moisture-only answer of 440 cfm is only about 1.1 air changes per hour. That gap is the lesson: moisture sets a floor, but ammonia, dust and summer heat push a stable toward the higher rate, and open eaves and ridge are how an unheated barn gets there without fans. Doubling the assumed moisture, for example by washing horses indoors, doubles the cfm.

Field card: Ventilating Horse Stables and Riding Arenas

Open and cool beats closed and warm.

  • Natural ventilation needs low inlets (eaves) and a high outlet (ridge).
  • Rule of thumb: about 1 sq ft of permanent opening per horse and about 1 inch of eave slot per 10 ft of width.
  • Moisture and ammonia come from breath, washing and wet bedding; check air at floor level.
  • Heat the tack room and wash area, not the whole barn.
  • Fans cool animals; they do not bring in fresh air.
  • Dust: keep arena footing evenly moist and fines low.
  • Fire: keep dust and cobwebs off electrical gear, and avoid portable heaters.
  • Our example: 12 horses at 0.3 lb/h each needs about 440 cfm at 35°F inside and 20°F outside.
