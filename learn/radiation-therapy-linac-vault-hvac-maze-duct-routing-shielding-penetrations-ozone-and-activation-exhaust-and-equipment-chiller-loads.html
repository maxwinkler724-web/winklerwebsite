Radiation Therapy Linac Vault HVAC: Maze Ducts, Shielding, Ozone and Chiller Loads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A linear accelerator (linac) sits in a concrete vault built to stop radiation. That one fact explains most of the odd HVAC details: ducts that enter by the maze, pipe and conduit that bend instead of running straight, and a rule that nobody services anything while the beam is on. This lesson is for the facility tech, not the physicist.

Check the site rules first

Check the site rules first. The radiation safety officer and the medical physicist control access to a treatment vault. Radiation rules for these machines are set by your state radiation control program and the facility license, and they differ. This page is general education from public sources, not a procedure. If your site procedure disagrees with this page, the site procedure wins.

Why the maze is the duct route

Most vaults have a bent entry corridor called the maze, so radiation scatters off a wall or two before it reaches the door. The IAEA safety report on radiotherapy facility design (Safety Reports Series No. 47) says the maze also gives a route for ventilation ducts and electrical conduit without compromising the shielding. Its guidance is to run the duct above a false ceiling along the maze and bring it out at or near the outer maze door, where the radiation is lowest. It also says to keep HVAC ducts out of the primary barrier (the wall the beam can point at) because of their large cross-section, and it says no duct larger than about 30 mm should penetrate the primary shielding. A duct should not run straight through any barrier.

Where a duct must pass a secondary barrier (the walls that see only scatter and leakage), the report says it should be a flat, high-aspect-ratio shape, aimed as close to square-on to the leakage direction as possible, and placed high on the wall to reduce scatter to people outside. For high-energy rooms it describes wrapping the duct section above the maze in lead and polyethylene, and a baffle where the maze is short. Those details are the designer's call. Your job is to recognize that the wrap and the odd shape are shielding, not insulation or a mistake.

Penetrations that zig-zag

Conduit and small pipe sleeves follow the same idea. The IAEA report describes conduits as inclined sleeves that pass through the secondary barrier, not the primary, and it says openings at least 300 mm above the floor are easier to use. A straight hole would give radiation a clear line out; an angled path does not. For you, that means:

  • Do not drill, core, or enlarge any vault wall, floor or ceiling opening. Any new hole needs the physicist's approval and shielding review first.
  • Do not pull out or replace shielded sleeves, lead wraps, or baffles while repairing a duct or pipe. Report damage instead.

Shielding design, such as that done under NCRP Report 151, belongs to the qualified physicist.

Ozone and activation: why there is exhaust

High-energy photon beams make a little ozone in the room air. A 2022 measurement study in two treatment rooms found room ozone started below the 0.001 ppm detection limit, rose to about 0.010 to 0.015 ppm during a working day, and peaked at 0.015 ppm. Those rooms exchanged their air about three times an hour, by the authors' estimate, and the authors noted the levels were far below the 0.1 ppm limit they cited. Another study using a phantom saw ozone rise with photon energy (15 MV higher than 6 MV) and with dose, with a peak reading of 0.161 ppm that stayed elevated for roughly six minutes, and it called for better room ventilation. We take from them only that higher energy means more ozone and the vault needs a working air path.

For reference, the NIOSH Pocket Guide lists the OSHA permissible exposure limit for ozone at 0.1 ppm as an 8-hour average, a NIOSH ceiling of 0.1 ppm, and an immediately dangerous level of 5 ppm.

High-energy beams can also activate the air itself. A measurement paper on a 15 MV linac found nitrogen-13 the main contributor to air activation, with a half-life near 10 minutes. We do not size anything from that paper. The practical meaning for the tech is to ask the physicist whether any wait or extra exhaust time applies before you enter after high-energy treatment.

The exhaust path is part of the design, so do not block, throttle or re-route it. We could not confirm an ASHRAE 170 row for these rooms from public pages, so we give no values. Use the drawings and the engineer of record. For the pressure idea in general, see the healthcare pressure lesson linked below.

The chiller load and leaks

A linac has its own cooling loop for the machine, usually a closed water circuit with a heat exchanger or a dedicated water cooler. A planning document we read for one maker's linac (a reposted copy, so cited as plain text) shows the water cooler as a separate cabinet with at least 1.5 m clearance at the sides and above for hot air, and a maximum connecting line length of 50 m. It did not give heat load or chiller size; those come from the maker's site planning guide for the specific model. Cooling the machine loop and cooling the room air are two separate jobs.

Keep flow and temperature steady, keep strainers clean, and watch for leaks. Water is the danger in a vault because leaks can reach electronics. Treat any weep at a fitting as urgent. See the process chiller lesson linked below for flow and delta T on equipment loops, and the MRI suite lesson for a similar chiller-uptime story.

Patient comfort on the couch

In our experience (not from a cited source), the patient lies still on the couch for many minutes, so a draft or cold supply air lands on someone who cannot move or add a layer. When a patient says the room feels cold, the fix may be a supply temperature or diffuser change, which needs the facility team and the physicist (room temperature also affects the machine). Do not move diffusers or change the maze duct yourself.

Never enter during beam-on

  • Beam on means nobody inside. Treatment is delivered from the control area with the door closed and the interlock set. Never defeat a door interlock, warning light, or search-and-secure step.
  • Get the physicist's sign-off every time. For a vault, we recommend this routine: contact the physics team, state the work and the area, wait for them to put the machine in a service state with its key or lock control, and get their release before you enter.
  • Lock out your own energy. The OSHA lockout rule requires you to know the energy, shut down, isolate, lock, relieve stored energy, and verify isolation. It names "other" energy sources, not radiation specifically, so the machine's own radiation lockout is the facility's procedure, and your fan, chiller and pump lockout is separate. See the lockout lesson linked below.
  • Access control is a regulatory idea. For example, the NRC rule at 10 CFR 20.1601 asks licensees to control entrances to high radiation areas with an interlock, an alarm, or a locked door. That rule applies to NRC licensees; linacs are usually under state rules, so your site's program governs.

Worked example: vault airflow and heat

Our own example, with assumed inputs, not a design. Assumptions: vault 25 ft by 25 ft with a 12 ft ceiling, an example air-change goal of 6 ACH (an assumed value, not from a standard), linac and equipment heat released into the room of 10 kW, and lights 1 kW.

  1. Volume: 25 x 25 x 12 = 7,500 cubic feet.
  2. Air for 6 ACH: 7,500 x 6 / 60 minutes = 750 cfm.
  3. Heat: 10 kW + 1 kW = 11 kW. Using the standard conversion of 3,412 Btu/h per kW (our own arithmetic): 11 x 3,412 = 37,532 Btu/h.
  4. Supply cfm at 20 F delta T with 1.08: cfm = 37,532 / (1.08 x 20) = 37,532 / 21.6 = 1,738 cfm.
  5. Check: 1,738 cfm x 60 / 7,500 = 13.9 air changes per hour.

The heat load, not the air-change goal, sets the supply here. That is why we quote both numbers and why the real design starts with the maker's heat release data, which we left out because we could not confirm it. A real design also sets the supply-to-exhaust balance, which the engineer of record decides.

Field card: Linac Vault HVAC

Maze is the route. Shielding is not insulation. Beam on means out.

  • Ducts and conduit use the maze or angled paths; never core or move them without physicist approval.
  • High-energy beams make ozone (OSHA 8-hour limit 0.1 ppm); keep the vault exhaust working.
  • Ask the physicist about any wait after high-energy treatment.
  • Machine water loop and room air are two separate cooling jobs.
  • Leaks near a linac are urgent.
  • Get physics release and lock out your own energy before entry.
  • Example: 11 kW is 37,532 Btu/h, about 1,738 cfm at 20 F.
