How Much Refrigerant Is Left in a Cylinder?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A cylinder gauge looks like a fuel gauge, but it is not one. As long as any liquid refrigerant is left inside, the needle tells you the temperature of the cylinder and almost nothing about how full it is. To know what is left, you weigh it. This lesson covers why, how to read the weights on the cylinder, and what happens to the cylinder when you draw vapor.

Why the gauge shows temperature, not amount

Liquid and vapor sit together in a closed cylinder, and the liquid keeps evaporating and the vapor keeps condensing until the two are in balance. Thermodynamics notes from a university engineering department say that in this two-phase condition pressure and temperature are not independent: each pressure has one matching saturation temperature. Pour in more liquid or take some out and the balance point does not move. Only the temperature moves it.

That is why a cylinder with 20 lb inside and one with 2 lb inside, sitting in the same room, read the same pressure. The federal environmental agency lists pressure-temperature relationships among its technician test topics for exactly this reason: the chart is a temperature tool. The gauge only starts to say something about quantity when the last liquid is gone and you are left with vapor, and by then the cylinder is nearly empty. See also pressure-temperature basics.

Weighing is the only reliable way

A refillable cylinder has its empty weight, called the tare weight, stamped into the collar or shown on a tag. Read it before you ever connect a hose. Then:

  • Gross weight is what the scale reads with the cylinder, valve and contents on it.
  • Tare weight is the stamped empty weight.
  • Refrigerant left = gross minus tare.

A federal agency analysis of refillable and disposable cylinders describes refillable cylinders as roughly 15 to 17 lb empty and disposable ones as roughly 6 to 7 lb, so the shell is often a large share of what the scale shows. Disposable cylinders are not weighed against a stamped tare. They show a net weight of refrigerant when new, and you track what you remove from that figure. The same agency document notes that cylinders should not be filled past 80 percent of their capacity, and that a nearly empty cylinder still holds a small vapor heel. Details on stamped markings are in the refillable cylinder lesson and the disposable cylinder lesson. Federal transportation rules for cylinder markings and filling are the place to confirm what must be stamped; we could not open those rule pages for this lesson, so we describe stamped tare only as a general practice.

How a charging scale works, zeroing and hose errors are covered in the charging scales lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

Why the cylinder cools when you draw vapor

Take vapor out of the top and the pressure dips a hair, so some liquid boils to replace it. Boiling needs latent heat, and the only places that heat can come from are the liquid itself, the steel shell, and the room around it. The cylinder gets cold, sometimes frosty, the saturation temperature falls, and so does the pressure. Pull too fast and the pressure can fall so far that flow slows to a crawl, which is the real reason a cylinder sitting in a cold truck is slow to charge from. Keeping the cylinder at room temperature helps, and a cylinder should never be heated with a flame.

Liquid and vapor valves, and why blends are charged as liquid

A single-valve cylinder gives vapor when upright. Two-port cylinders have one valve on a dip tube that reaches the liquid and one on the vapor space. Which valve you open decides whether liquid or vapor leaves. Blends are charged as liquid because the components have different boiling points, so drawing vapor from the top can pull off more of the lighter components and shift the composition. Taking liquid keeps the mix as made. Because liquid should not slam into a running compressor, it is usually metered through a gauge manifold or throttling device into the low side. See the valves and dip tubes lesson and the blend fractionation lesson.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. The inputs marked "assumed" are ours, not stamped or measured values.

Assumptions: R-134a; cylinder and contents at 80.4 F (assumed); stamped tare 17.0 lb (assumed); latent heat of boiling 75 Btu per lb (assumed round figure); steel specific heat 0.11 Btu per lb per F (assumed); no heat enters from the room (assumed, which gives the largest possible drop). From the saturation data for R-134a: 102.10 psia at 80.427 F, and liquid specific heat 0.342 Btu per lb per F at that row.

Step 1, contents. The scale reads 37.0 lb gross. 37.0 minus 17.0 = 20.0 lb of refrigerant. Later the scale reads 19.0 lb gross: 19.0 minus 17.0 = 2.0 lb.

Step 2, pressure. At 80.4 F the saturation pressure is 102.10 psia in both cases. Subtract atmospheric pressure of 14.7 psi, the conversion the agency's test topics page gives: 102.10 minus 14.7 = 87.4 psig with 20 lb inside and with 2 lb inside. The gauge cannot tell them apart.

Step 3, boil off 1 lb of vapor from the 20 lb cylinder. Heat drawn = 1 lb x 75 Btu per lb = 75 Btu. The thermal mass left to supply it: steel 17.0 x 0.11 = 1.87 Btu per F; liquid 19.0 lb x 0.342 = 6.50 Btu per F; total 8.37 Btu per F. Temperature drop = 75 / 8.37 = about 9.0 F, so the cylinder falls to about 71.4 F.

Step 4, new pressure. The property data give 86.398 psia at 70.405 F and 101.12 psia at 79.836 F. Interpolating to 71.4 F gives about 88.0 psia, or about 73 psig, down roughly 14 psi from 87.4 psig.

Check. Units: lb x Btu per lb = Btu; Btu divided by Btu per F = F. Same draw from the 2 lb cylinder: 1 lb is left, 1.0 x 0.342 + 1.87 = 2.21 Btu per F, and 75 / 2.21 = about 34 F, so a small remainder chills much faster. That is why the last pounds seem so hard to get out.

Keep a running log while charging

Write the numbers down as you go, because you cannot trust the gauge to tell you the amount. A simple log: date, refrigerant type, cylinder ID, tare, starting gross, target charge, then gross after each step and the running total added. Subtract each new reading from the starting gross, not from the last reading, so small slips do not add up. Record the ending gross and any weight returned. See what technicians keep for records.

Field card: How Much Refrigerant Is Left in a Cylinder?

The gauge shows temperature. The scale shows refrigerant.

  • With any liquid inside, pressure follows temperature, not fill level.
  • Refillable: gross minus stamped tare = refrigerant left.
  • Disposable: track what you remove from the printed net weight.
  • Drawing vapor boils liquid, which cools the cylinder and drops pressure.
  • Dip-tube or liquid port gives liquid; vapor port gives vapor. Charge blends as liquid.
  • Log starting gross, each reading and the total added.
