Fire Station Apparatus Bay HVAC: Diesel Exhaust, Capture Hoses, Exhaust Fans and Zones

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fire station is a house attached to a garage full of diesel engines. The HVAC job is to keep exhaust out of the people, keep the bay from leaking into the bunk rooms, and keep the building heated when 14-foot doors keep opening. This lesson covers the pieces: capture at the tailpipe, general exhaust, pressure, heat, and the gear room.

Check the issuing authority

Check the issuing authority. Station design is set by the local code, the fire department's own safety program and the engineer of record. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. It is not a design and not a code opinion.

Why this room gets special treatment

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified diesel engine exhaust as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) in June 2012, based on sufficient evidence of an increased lung cancer risk, mostly from studies of highly exposed workers. NIOSH had already recommended in Current Intelligence Bulletin 50 that whole diesel exhaust be regarded as a potential occupational carcinogen and that exposure be reduced. Fire engines and ambulances start, idle and back out inside a building that has beds in it, so the bay is a place where a mechanical designer is protecting people who sleep a few doors away.

Two fire-service rules are worth knowing by name. NFPA 1500 (2018 edition), section 10.1.5, as quoted by the International Association of Fire Chiefs, says the fire department must prevent exposure of firefighters and contamination of living and sleeping areas by exhaust emissions. NFPA 1585 is named in the NFPA's own committee response document in the same discussion of apparatus bay exhaust, though the bay exhaust wording quoted there comes from NFPA 1500. We name both so you know what the fire department may be working from; we do not reproduce either standard.

Source capture: catch it at the tailpipe

A source capture system puts a flexible hose on the exhaust pipe and pulls the exhaust to a fan that discharges outdoors. One magazine description says a disconnecting nozzle lets the truck leave with the hose attached, and an inline pressure switch starts the fan. The same article makes the point that the system is only as good as the crew's habit of connecting the hose. A second type is an onboard unit under the truck that filters exhaust and starts with the engine. The Massachusetts Department of Labor Standards lists both, plus ceiling filters, downdraft ventilation and exterior wall fans where direct capture is not used.

The NIOSH health hazard evaluation of a two-bay station shows how this fails in practice. The station had four ceiling hoses, total measured flow about 2,000 cfm, with the four ranging from 236 to 471 cfm each. One hose was out of service, the fire engine's tailpipe did not fit the hose so much of its exhaust was not captured, the system started mostly on dispatch rather than on engine start, and crews attached hoses to engines already running. Loose hoses also whipped when they failed to release. For a technician, the takeaways are fit, activation, hose condition and release hardware, not just fan size.

General exhaust with CO and NO2 sensors

Capture is never perfect, so the bay also needs room-level exhaust. The International Association of Fire Chiefs recommends building ventilation exhaust in apparatus bays and active monitoring of carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide there. NIOSH found one station's ceiling exhaust fan ran on a CO sensor or a manual wall timer. The federal facilities criteria we read treat the bay as an enclosed parking garage for ventilation, running continuously, and the NIOSH reports cite ASHRAE Standard 62.1 at 0.75 cfm per square foot for that use. Sensor staging, calibration and fan control follow the same logic as our enclosed parking garage ventilation lesson, so we do not repeat it here. We give no alarm setpoints because the sources we read gave none for bays.

Filtration units mounted high in the bay help but are not a substitute. In a large bay, NIOSH measured one ceiling air cleaner set at 87 percent particle reduction in a single pass, yet said the units may supplement filtration but are not effective as the primary source control, and particle levels sometimes took about two hours to return to background.

Hot, warm and cold zones, and keeping the living side positive

Fire-service contamination control sorts spaces into hot, warm and cold zones. The federal facilities criteria we read call them red, yellow and green. Its HVAC rule is that systems keep a negative pressure relationship between the red and yellow zones and the green zones at all times. In plain terms: air should move from clean space toward dirty space. The Massachusetts page says the same thing for ordinary stations: airflow should travel from occupied areas toward the apparatus bay, with self-closing doors and covers at fire pole openings acting as air barriers.

Real measurements show how thin that margin is. At one station NIOSH measured the bay negative to the living quarters by 0.007 to 0.015 in. w.c. at four doorways, confirmed by smoke test, and saw no diesel increase in the living area. At another, the bay was positive to the living quarters by 0.005 to 0.008 in. w.c. when bay doors were open or when the fans were off, so exhaust drifted into the dorm. NIOSH told that station to run the general exhaust whenever capture fails, adjust the energy recovery ventilator for positive pressure in the living areas, and fit sweeps to the bay-to-living doors. The principle is the one in our pressure room lesson: the pressure difference is only a few hundredths of an inch, so door fit, door closers and exhaust balance decide it.

Gear (turnout) rooms

NIOSH's guidance on contaminated firefighter gear says to keep gear in a dedicated storage area, away from diesel exhaust in the apparatus bay and out of living and office areas, and to use exhaust fans in that area that discharge outdoors. The federal criteria we read go further for their facilities: ventilated gear storage separate from the bay, held negative to occupied spaces, on an exhaust system separate from occupied-space exhaust, with laundry dryers vented directly outside. Neither source gives an air change rate for the room, so we do not offer one.

Heating a bay with the doors opening

Exhaust fans pull outdoor air in, and open doors let it in faster. One trade article says mechanical exhaust is the cheapest approach but needs radiant heat to limit heat loss except in warm climates. The federal criteria call for gas-fired radiant heat where fuel is available and an optional floor radiant element at each bay door, with heated make-up air introduced above apparatus level because diesel exhaust is heavier than air. One NIOSH-evaluated bay was heated by radiant equipment for large-volume bays. Radiant heat warms floors, trucks and people directly, so it recovers faster after a door cycle than waiting for a ceiling-height air volume to warm up. For clearances, venting and maintenance of those heaters see our infrared tube heater lesson. Make-up air options are in the make-up air unit lesson.

Worked example: general exhaust and an open door

All of the inputs below are our own assumptions except the exhaust rate, which is the 0.75 cfm per square foot ASHRAE 62.1 garage figure quoted in the NIOSH reports. The 1.08 factor is explained in our heat transfer formulas lesson.

  • Assumed bay (our own): 60 ft deep by 80 ft wide, 20 ft ceiling.
  • Floor area: 60 x 80 = 4,800 sq ft. Volume: 4,800 x 20 = 96,000 cu ft.
  • General exhaust at 0.75 cfm/sq ft: 4,800 x 0.75 = 3,600 cfm. That is 3,600 x 60 / 96,000 = 2.25 air changes per hour. For comparison, NIOSH measured 3.9 air changes per hour of total airflow (including recirculating air cleaners) in a 270,000 cu ft bay, and a ceiling exhaust fan of nearly 5,000 cfm, about 4.6 air changes per hour, at its two-bay station. Those are measured values at other buildings, not requirements.
  • Assumed weather (our own): 68 F bay setpoint (the federal criteria's design heating temperature) and 20 F outdoors, so delta T = 48 F.
  • Heat to warm the exhaust make-up air: 1.08 x 3,600 x 48 = 186,624 Btu/h while the fan runs.
  • Assumed open door (our own): one 14 ft by 14 ft overhead door open 5 minutes, with an assumed air exchange of 2,500 cfm through the opening. Heat rate: 1.08 x 2,500 x 48 = 129,600 Btu/h. For 5 minutes: 129,600 x 5 / 60 = 10,800 Btu per opening.
  • Six openings in an hour: 6 x 10,800 = 64,800 Btu in that hour, about 35 percent of the 186,624 Btu/h the exhaust make-up needs.

The point is scale, not precision. The 2,500 cfm figure depends on wind, truck movement and fan operation, and the bay's real heat loss through walls and roof is not included. Even so, a few door cycles an hour cost real heat, which is why radiant floor heat at the doors and shutting bay doors during outdoor idling make sense. NIOSH recommended closing bay doors when equipment runs outside.

Field card: Fire Station Apparatus Bay HVAC

Capture at the pipe, exhaust the room, hold the bunks positive.

  • Diesel exhaust: IARC Group 1 (2012); NIOSH says treat as a potential occupational carcinogen.
  • Check hose fit, hose condition, release and activation (engine start, not just dispatch).
  • General exhaust on CO and NO2 sensors; our example used 0.75 cfm/sq ft, which is the ASHRAE 62.1 garage rate quoted by NIOSH.
  • Air should flow from living quarters toward the bay and gear room; check door sweeps and closers.
  • Gear room exhaust discharges outdoors, separate from living areas.
  • Open door heat loss: 1.08 x cfm x delta T. Radiant heat helps recovery.
  • NFPA 1500 and NFPA 1585 are fire-department documents; ask who holds the compliance duty.
