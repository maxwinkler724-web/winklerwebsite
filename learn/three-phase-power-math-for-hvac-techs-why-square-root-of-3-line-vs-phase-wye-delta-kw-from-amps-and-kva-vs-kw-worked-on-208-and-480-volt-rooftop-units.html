Three-Phase Power Math for HVAC Techs: Square Root of 3, kW and kVA

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every commercial rooftop unit and chiller you service runs on three-phase power, and the nameplate, the clamp meter and the electric bill all speak in different units. This lesson shows where the 1.732 comes from, how line and phase quantities differ in wye and delta, and how to turn the amps you measure into kW.

Why the square root of 3 appears

In a three-phase supply, the three voltages are the same size but peak one-third of a cycle apart, which is 120 degrees. A line-to-line voltage is the difference between two of those phase voltages. When you subtract two equal-size quantities that sit 120 degrees apart, the result is not twice as big and not the same size. It comes out 1.732 times as big, which is the square root of 3. A university lecture set on three-phase systems says the same thing in plain terms: line voltage in a wye connection is found from the phasor sum of two phase voltages, and the analysis of three-phase circuits reduces to deciding where to put the square root of three. Our own arithmetic: the square root of 3 is 1.732 to four digits, and its inverse is 0.577.

Line and phase, wye and delta

A line value is what you measure on the supply conductors: line-to-line voltage across two of them, line current through one. A phase value is what each winding or heating element sees inside the load. Which one gets the 1.732 depends on how the windings are tied together.

  • Wye. One end of each winding goes to a common point. Line current equals phase current. Line voltage is 1.732 times phase voltage.
  • Delta. Windings are connected end to end in a triangle. Line voltage equals phase voltage. Line current is 1.732 times phase current.

Two university lecture sets (listed in Sources) give these same relationships. Nothing about this changes what your clamp meter reads: it always reads line current when you clamp one supply conductor.

Where 208 V and 480 V come from

A wye system with 120 V from each line to the common point gives 120 x 1.732, about 208 V, line to line. A wye system with 277 V line to common gives 277 x 1.732, about 480 V. These are our own calculations, shown to explain the two nominal voltages. That is why a building with 208 V three-phase also has 120 V outlets, and one with 480 V three-phase also has 277 V lighting. It also explains why a motor wired for 480 V will not run correctly on 208 V: the winding voltage is nowhere near what it was built for. Always match the unit's nameplate voltage to the supply you measure.

The power formula

For a balanced three-phase load, real power is the line voltage times the line current times the power factor times 1.732. The same relationship appears in a U.S. Department of Energy motor fact sheet as input power in kW equals volts times amps times power factor times the square root of 3, divided by 1000, using the line-to-line voltage and the average of the three phase currents. The university lecture notes give it as P = sqrt(3) x V(line) x I(line) x PF.

kW = V x A x 1.732 x PF / 1000

Use the line-to-line voltage and the line current from your meter. Because it uses line values, the same formula works for wye and for delta. You do not need to know how the windings are tied.

kVA versus kW

Multiply volts, amps and 1.732 and leave out the power factor, and you get apparent power in kVA. That is what the wiring, breaker and transformer have to carry. Multiply by the power factor and you get real power in kW, the part that does work and shows up on the meter as energy. The lecture notes define power factor as real power divided by apparent power. The gap between the two is reactive power, which comes from the magnetic fields of motors. A motor at light load can draw nearly full-size current with a poor power factor, so amps alone overstate the kW. See Power Factor on HVAC Motors for the reactive side.

Worked example: two rooftop units, our own numbers

This is our own example. The amp readings and power factor are assumptions we chose to show the arithmetic, not data from a real unit or a maker.

Assumptions. Two similar-size rooftop units are running with the compressor and fans on. Unit A is supplied at 208 V and Unit B at 480 V. Use the measured line-to-line voltage, taken as exactly nominal. Power factor for the whole unit is assumed to be 0.85. Clamp readings, all three legs, are balanced: Unit A reads 28.4, 27.9 and 28.0 A. Unit B reads 12.3, 12.1 and 12.2 A.

Step 1, average the amps. Unit A: (28.4 + 27.9 + 28.0) / 3 = 28.1 A. Unit B: (12.3 + 12.1 + 12.2) / 3 = 12.2 A.

Step 2, kVA. Unit A: 208 x 28.1 x 1.732 = 10,123 VA, or 10.1 kVA. Unit B: 480 x 12.2 x 1.732 = 10,143 VA, or 10.1 kVA.

Step 3, kW. Unit A: 10.12 x 0.85 = 8.6 kW. Unit B: 10.14 x 0.85 = 8.6 kW.

The same load draws 28.1 A at 208 V and 12.2 A at 480 V. The ratio of currents is about 2.3, which matches the ratio of voltages, 480 / 208 = 2.3. Same power, higher voltage, lower current. In our own words, that is the appeal of higher voltage: the same kW with less current in the conductors. Do not expect a nameplate or a meter to match these round numbers exactly; real units have power factor that changes with load.

Checks and cautions

  • Clamp one conductor at a time. Clamping all three, or a cable with several conductors, reads near zero. See Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter.
  • Average the three legs, as the fact sheet does. If the legs differ a lot, the formula is only an estimate. See Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance.
  • A nameplate rating such as full-load amps is a limit, not a measurement. Running amps are usually lower.
  • This formula assumes a balanced load and a sine-wave supply. Variable-speed drives and electronic loads distort the current, so a plain clamp reading may not give true kW. Use a power meter for that.
  • Energized panels are dangerous. Follow your site's lockout and electrical safety rules, and leave live work inside disconnects to qualified people.

Field card: Three-Phase Power Math

Line values in, 1.732 in the middle, power factor last.

  • Wye: I line = I phase; V line = 1.732 x V phase.
  • Delta: V line = V phase; I line = 1.732 x I phase.
  • kVA = V x A x 1.732 / 1000.
  • kW = kVA x PF = V x A x 1.732 x PF / 1000.
  • Our example: 208 V at 28.1 A and 480 V at 12.2 A, both about 10.1 kVA and 8.6 kW at an assumed PF of 0.85.
  • Clamp one conductor, average three legs, use line-to-line volts.
