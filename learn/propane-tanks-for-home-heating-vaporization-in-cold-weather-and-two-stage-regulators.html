Propane Tanks for Home Heating: Vaporization in Cold Weather and Two-Stage Regulators

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A propane furnace does not burn liquid. It burns vapor, and the tank in the yard has to make that vapor on the coldest night of the year. This lesson explains how the tank does it, why a cold or nearly empty tank can fall short, how to check a tank size against a heating load, and what the two regulators between the tank and the furnace do.

How a tank makes vapor

Propane is stored as a liquid under pressure. Inside the tank, the liquid sits at the bottom and vapor sits on top. When the appliances call for gas, vapor leaves the top and the pressure drops a little, so some liquid boils to replace it. Boiling takes heat. The tank pulls that heat from the liquid itself and, through the steel wall, from the surrounding air. Emerson's LP-Gas Serviceman's Handbook says the vaporization rate depends on the liquid temperature and on the wetted surface, meaning the area of steel touched by liquid. Colder air or less liquid lowers the rate. It also warns that heavy withdrawal chills the liquid and can drop the container pressure substantially.

That explains the two winter problems. A cold night gives the tank less heat to draw from the air. A low liquid level leaves less wetted steel. When the furnace asks for more vapor than the tank can make, pressure falls, the flame weakens and the furnace may shut down.

Tank sizes you will see

Home heating tanks are of two kinds. DOT cylinders are the portable, refillable bottles, from the 20 pound grill size up to the 100 pound and 420 pound (120 gallon) cylinders. ASME tanks are the larger stationary tanks, commonly 250, 500 and 1,000 gallons, set above or below ground. The gallon sizes are water capacity, which is how the tank is rated, not how much propane it holds. A supplier page lists a 250 gallon tank as holding about 200 gallons of propane and a 500 gallon tank as holding about 400.

The 80 percent rule

Tanks are filled to roughly 80 percent of water capacity, which leaves about 20 percent as vapor space. Liquid propane expands as it warms, and the empty space keeps the tank from filling solid and forcing the relief valve open. Retailers describe this as the 80-20 rule, and it traces to NFPA 58, the Liquefied Petroleum Gas Code. One New Jersey fire-service handbook chapter instead says containers are filled to about 85 percent of water capacity, so the exact limit depends on the container and the filling method.

Reading a vaporization table

Makers publish tables of how many Btu per hour a tank can deliver. The Emerson handbook's table for ASME tanks lists the maximum intermittent withdrawal rate without tank frosting, by tank size and by the lowest average outdoor temperature over 24 hours. Its stated conditions are a tank one-half full and 70 percent relative humidity. Frost matters because ice on the shell insulates it and lowers the rate. For continuous loading, the handbook says to multiply the table values by 0.25, and notes that continuous loading is not very common on domestic installations. Two rows from the table:

  • 500 gallon tank at 10°F: 329,700 Btu/hr
  • 500 gallon tank at 0°F: 300,100 Btu/hr

For 100 pound cylinders, the same handbook lists output by pounds of propane left. At 0°F a full cylinder (100 lb) gives 71,000 Btu/hr and one with 20 lb left gives 22,000. That is the low-level effect in numbers.

Worked example: does a 500 gallon tank cover 140,000 Btu/hr?

An invented house has a 100,000 Btu/hr furnace and a 40,000 Btu/hr water heater, on one 500 gallon ASME tank. The coldest 24-hour average outdoor temperature is 10°F and the tank is half full.

  1. Add the loads. 100,000 + 40,000 = 140,000 Btu/hr if both run flat out together.
  2. Look up the tank. The table gives 329,700 Btu/hr at 10°F for a 500 gallon tank at half full.
  3. Compare, intermittent. 329,700 − 140,000 = 189,700 Btu/hr to spare. The load is 140,000 ÷ 329,700 = 0.42, or about 42 percent of the table value. On the intermittent basis the tank covers it.
  4. Compare, continuous. 329,700 × 0.25 = 82,425 Btu/hr. That is less than 140,000, a shortfall of 57,575. A furnace and water heater that truly ran nonstop together would exceed the continuous rating.
  5. Gallons per hour at full load. The handbook gives 91,502 Btu per gallon of propane. 140,000 ÷ 91,502 = 1.53 gallons per hour. Over 24 hours: 1.53 × 24 = about 36.7 gallons.
  6. Reserve. Half of 500 gallons is 250 gallons of liquid. 250 ÷ 1.53 = about 163 hours, roughly 6.8 days at constant full load.

The honest reading: the table passes the 140,000 check as intermittent withdrawal, and the continuous factor shows how little margin a smaller or lower tank would have. At 0°F the same steps give 300,100 intermittent and 75,025 continuous, and the 250 gallon tank at 10°F gives 198,400 and 49,600. The table was read at half full only, so a lower level means less than these figures. Ask the supplier to size and monitor the tank. The furnace's own input rating comes from its nameplate; see Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.

Two-stage regulation

Tank pressure rises and falls with temperature, and it is far too high for a burner. Two regulators bring it down in steps. The first stage sits at the tank and lowers the pressure to 10 psig or less, with 10 psig the usual setting according to Emerson. RegO adds a cold-weather limit: if temperatures can reach -20°F, the first stage should not be set above 10 psig, and at -35°F or colder not above 5 psig. The second stage sits at the building and drops the pressure to the appliance level. RegO's LV4403B second-stage regulator is set at the factory to 11 inches water column with 10 psig on its inlet, adjustable from 9 to 13 inches, and accepts 5 to 20 psig in. RegO's service tips say second-stage regulators should be mounted 12 to 18 inches above the ground and above snow, with the vent pointing down where there is no cover. Once the pressure is at the house, appliance and manifold readings work the same as on any gas job. See Checking Gas Inlet and Manifold Pressure With a Manometer, and for the burner parts that change from natural gas, Converting a Gas Furnace or Boiler to Propane.

Who owns and services what

Unlike a utility meter, the tank is usually the propane supplier's. One national supplier says that for a leased tank the provider owns, installs and maintains it. A customer can buy a tank instead, and then takes on its upkeep. Either way, the propane council's safety guidance says never to install, modify or repair your propane system or appliances yourself, and that a qualified professional must leak-check the system when gas is turned back on after a run-out. Never turn the gas on at the tank yourself. For the utility version of this question, see Gas Meter and Regulator Basics.

Leaks and odor

Propane has an odorant added so leaks can be noticed, and the council tells every household member to learn the smell. Odor can fade as soil, water or rust absorbs it, and a person's nose can tire or be dulled by a cold, so the council also recommends propane gas detectors. If you smell gas: get everyone out, do not touch switches, outlets, appliances or a landline phone, do not smoke or light flames, and call your propane retailer, 911 or the fire department from a safe distance. Close the tank valve only if you do not suspect a leak at the tank. Have the system checked before using any appliance. The general steps are in Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do.

Field card: Propane tanks, vaporization and regulators

The furnace burns vapor; cold and low levels cut how much the tank can make.

  • Vaporization depends on liquid temperature and wetted surface; colder air or less liquid lowers it.
  • Gallon sizes are water capacity; tanks are filled to about 80 percent (some sources say 85 percent by container).
  • Emerson table: 500 gal, half full, 10°F = 329,700 Btu/hr intermittent; multiply by 0.25 for continuous.
  • Propane: 91,502 Btu per gallon. 140,000 ÷ 91,502 = 1.53 gal/hr.
  • First stage at tank about 10 psig; second stage at house about 11 in. w.c. (RegO LV4403B).
  • Supplier owns and services leased tanks and regulators. Do not repair them yourself.
  • Smell gas: leave, touch nothing, call from outside.
