Skylights and Roof Windows at Home: Heat Gain, Heat Loss and Condensation

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A skylight brings daylight into a dark room, and it also puts a sloped sheet of glass in the hottest and coldest part of the house. That glass sees the sun at its strongest in summer and the cold night sky in winter. This lesson explains what a skylight or roof window does to heating and cooling, what to do about it, and when the room underneath needs its own supply air or a ductless head.

Why a roof opening is different from a wall window

The federal energy department's page on skylights says a low-slope skylight admits more summer solar heat and less winter heat, the opposite of what most homes want. Sun hits a flat or gently sloped pane more squarely in summer, when the sun is high, than it hits a vertical wall window. A wall window also has eaves, trees or neighbors shading it for part of the day. A roof opening rarely does.

The same page says skylights lose heat in winter too, and that insulated glazing, low-e coatings and translucent insulation between layers reduce that loss. Two ratings describe a skylight: the U-factor (how fast heat flows through it per degree of temperature difference) and the solar heat gain coefficient, or SHGC (the fraction of sun energy that gets in as heat). Lower is better for both when cooling is the main concern. For the full picture on those two numbers see the related lesson on window ratings below.

Summer: solar gain through sloped glass

In a hot spell, a skylight shows up as a hot patch of ceiling and a room that never catches up. The federal page lists the compass effects: east-facing glass admits maximum light and heat in the morning, west-facing in the afternoon, and south-facing gives the most winter passive heat but often unwanted summer heat. North-facing glass gives fairly constant, cool light. The page also suggests shade from leaf-shedding trees or movable coverings, and says exterior shades are more effective at reducing summer heat gain than interior ones, because the heat is stopped before it passes through the glass.

The same page offers a sizing rule of thumb: skylight area of no more than about 5 percent of the floor area in rooms with many windows, and up to about 15 percent in rooms with few windows. If a room is already sunny and hot, the smaller end is the wiser choice.

Winter: heat loss and the cold downdraft

Skylight glass is colder than a wall in winter, and its inside surface can drop well below room temperature. Air touching that surface cools, gets denser and slides downward, which people feel as a draft falling from the ceiling. It is not a leak; it is convection. You can tell the difference by holding a tissue near the glass edge: a steady sideways flutter near the frame points to an air leak, while a cool, slow sinking feeling in the middle of the room points to cold glass. Better glazing, a tight shaft and an insulated shade reduce it.

That cold surface also pulls the room's warm, moist air toward it, which brings us to condensation.

Condensation and dripping on the frame

A university extension article on window condensation says water or frost on the inside of a window points to too much indoor moisture, a cold window surface, or both, and that it can lead to damage and mold on the frame. It recommends keeping winter indoor humidity under 40 percent, shutting off humidifiers, and using outside-air exhaust fans while cooking and bathing. A second extension publication suggests about 30 to 45 percent during the heating season, vents for cooking, bathing and laundry that go to the outdoors, and storm windows with an air space of at least half an inch, sealed at the edges. Neither one is written specifically about skylights, so we treat their advice as general principles that apply to any cold glass.

A skylight is the worst place for this because warm moist air rises straight to it and the glass is often the coldest surface in the room. Typical clues are a wet frame, stained or peeling paint on the shaft, and drips in the same spot every cold morning. A drip on the frame is not always a roof leak, so check for condensation first, especially when it appears on cold clear nights and not during rain. If the stain shows up during rain, the flashing is the suspect. The federal page notes that leaks come from poor installation, and recommends a curb above the roof, flashing, sealed joints and sheet waterproofing.

The light shaft: a heat and leak path

Many skylights sit at the top of a shaft that runs from the roof, through the attic, to the ceiling. That shaft is a chimney-shaped hole in your air barrier. Warm room air can travel up the shaft walls into the attic, carrying moisture that condenses on cold sheathing. In summer the shaft walls pick up attic heat and radiate it down. Seal the gap where the shaft passes the ceiling, insulate the shaft walls on the attic side, and keep the insulation from blocking soffit vents. The second extension publication gives a vent rule of thumb for attics: 1 square foot of vent for each 150 square feet of attic floor for attics without a vapor barrier, with inlets at the eaves and outlets near the ridge.

The federal page also describes tubular skylights. A small roof collector feeds a mirrored pipe and a diffusing lens in the ceiling, so they bring in daylight without much daytime heat gain or nighttime heat loss. They give no view and no ventilation.

Shades, blinds and venting

  • Exterior shading works best in summer. Interior shades help less, because the glass has already warmed.
  • Interior insulating shades, such as cellular types that fit closely, cut winter loss at night. The federal windows page recommends insulated cellular shades for drafty windows. Close them on cold nights and in the hottest part of summer afternoons.
  • Films reduce solar gain. The federal windows page says sun-control films help on south-facing glass and on east- and west-facing glass in hot climates, but gives no figures.
  • Venting skylights let the hottest air near the ceiling escape. The federal page says operable models usually open outward at the bottom or through a small hinged panel, and can be moved by pole, chain, crank or motor, and some close automatically when it rains. Open one on a cool evening and a low window on the same side of the house or another floor so air can enter. Close it before the heat of the day.

Use a pole or a rated operator rather than reaching from a ladder or a table. Larger roof windows near floor level can serve as emergency exits, so keep the path clear.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption for teaching, not a measurement of your home.

  • Skylight glass area: 6 ft². Wall window of the same area: 6 ft².
  • SHGC for both: 0.40.
  • Peak summer sun on the sloped glass: 250 Btu/h per ft². On the vertical east or west window at its peak: 140 Btu/h per ft².
  • U-factor: skylight 0.50, wall window 0.30 (Btu/h per ft² per °F).
  • Winter: 70°F inside, 30°F outside, so a 40°F difference.

Summer gain = area × SHGC × sun intensity.

  1. Skylight: 6 × 0.40 = 2.4; 2.4 × 250 = 600 Btu/h.
  2. Wall window: 6 × 0.40 = 2.4; 2.4 × 140 = 336 Btu/h.
  3. Difference: 600 − 336 = 264 Btu/h more through the roof, or 600 ÷ 336 = 1.79 times as much.

Winter loss = area × U-factor × temperature difference.

  1. Skylight: 6 × 0.50 = 3.0; 3.0 × 40 = 120 Btu/h.
  2. Wall window: 6 × 0.30 = 1.8; 1.8 × 40 = 72 Btu/h.
  3. Difference: 120 − 72 = 48 Btu/h, or 1.67 times as much.

What it means: at that peak hour the skylight adds 600 Btu/h, which is 0.05 ton of cooling (600 ÷ 12,000), the same as about 176 W of heat. That sounds small, but it lands in one room, and a small room with a closed door may have only a few hundred Btu/h of design load to begin with. The loss figure ignores the shaft, air leaks and the cold-glass draft, so real comfort is usually worse than the arithmetic. Compare the numbers with a rated product's label and your local sun conditions before relying on them.

When the room needs more than a skylight fix

Shades and better glass can only do so much. A room under a skylight may need its own air if it is always the hottest in summer even with shading, or the coldest in winter with the door open. Check these in order:

  1. Supply air. Does the room have at least one supply register, and is there a return path or a door undercut? A closed door with no return starves the room.
  2. Duct fix. Is the duct to that room long, kinked or in a hot attic? Fix that first. See the finished attic lesson linked below.
  3. Dedicated supply. If the main system cannot be balanced for that room without upsetting others, a contractor may add a branch or a separate supply from a load calculation for the room.
  4. Ductless head. When the duct route is hard, a ductless head on the ceiling or wall serves that room alone and can be set to its own temperature. Have a licensed contractor size it from a room load, not from the floor area alone.

A frequent mistake is oversizing the head for the peak hour, which leaves the unit cycling most of the day. A skylight load peaks briefly, so use shading to trim that peak before sizing equipment.

Field card: skylights and roof windows

The glass is hot in summer, cold in winter, and wet when the room is humid.

  • Sloped glass admits more summer sun and less winter sun than a wall window.
  • Exterior shading beats interior shading for summer heat.
  • Insulated shades cut night heat loss.
  • Keep winter indoor humidity under about 40 percent; wet frames mean humidity, cold glass or both.
  • Seal and insulate the light shaft at the ceiling and in the attic.
  • Tubular skylights gain and lose less heat but give no view or ventilation.
  • Our example: 6 ft², SHGC 0.40, 250 Btu/h per ft² gives 600 Btu/h; U 0.50 and 40°F gives 120 Btu/h.
  • Hot or cold room anyway? Check supply, return and duct first, then consider a ductless head.
