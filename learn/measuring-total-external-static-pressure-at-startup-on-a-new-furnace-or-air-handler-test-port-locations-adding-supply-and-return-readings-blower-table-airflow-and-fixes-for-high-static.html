Total External Static Pressure at Startup on a New Furnace or Air Handler

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A new furnace or air handler can light, run and cool without any complaint and still be moving the wrong amount of air. Total external static pressure is the one reading that tells you how hard the blower is working against everything outside its own cabinet. This lesson walks through where the two test ports go, how to read the manometer, how to add the readings, and what to do when the number is high.

What the reading tells you

A national-lab heat pump installation guide describes total external static pressure as a way to evaluate ductwork performance, find restrictions and confirm the equipment is operating within its specifications. Too much of it can cut airflow, lower efficiency and cause comfort problems. A national-lab installation report compares it to blood pressure: a high value is a warning sign about the health of the duct system. That report says a total external static pressure of 0.5 inches of water column or less is good. Treat that as a rule of thumb. The number that matters on your job is the range printed in the equipment manufacturer's instructions. For the basics of what static pressure is, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Where the two test ports go

You need one port on the return side and one on the supply side. The national-lab guide puts the return port just after the filter. On the supply side it puts the port just after the fan in a fan-coil air handler, or between the heat exchanger and the indoor coil in a furnace with a coil on top. It says to drill the holes only as large as the probes, to stay away from important components, and to seal each hole with a rubber plug when you finish.

Reading the manometer

The steps in the national-lab guide are short. Zero the probe, put it in the return tap and record the return static pressure. Zero it again, put it in the supply tap and record the supply static pressure. Then add the absolute values, which means you add the two numbers and ignore the minus sign on the return side. The return side sits below room pressure, so most meters show it as a negative number; the supply side shows positive. The guide does not give a probe orientation in its text, so follow the instructions that come with your probe and meter. Take the readings with the blower at the speed or setting the manufacturer's table is written for, with the access panels on, and with the filter and every other accessory in place. A university housing-research handout for new-home installations says the reading is taken with the installed system running and all accessories and components in place.

What is inside and what is outside the cabinet

The word external means outside the equipment cabinet. In our own experience the blower table covers the parts inside the cabinet, so you do not add those back in; confirm that in the unit's instructions. Everything else the blower pushes or pulls through is outside: return grilles and ducts, the filter in its rack or slot, a coil that sits outside the cabinet, any damper, humidifier or add-on, the supply plenum and the supply ducts. Because both readings are taken against room pressure, the return reading includes the filter and the return ducts, and the supply reading includes the supply ducts and any coil past the port. Where a coil is built into an air handler cabinet, it is on the inside, so check the notes under the table, since some tables list a separate allowance for a wet coil or a filter. Those two points are our own advice, not a rule from a source.

The filter is often the biggest single piece. A national-lab study of homes found that the highest-efficiency filter tested nearly doubled the pressure drop across the filter, from an average of 0.29 to 0.60 inches of water column. In homes with a standard blower motor of the permanent split capacitor type, airflow fell by an average of 22 percent (188 cfm) between a low-efficiency filter and a clean filter of that top grade. Blowers with variable-speed motors held airflow by drawing more power until they ran out of capacity. See Air Handler Filter Racks and Filter Bypass Gaps for the rack side of this.

Using the blower table to estimate airflow

A national-lab laboratory report notes that if a manufacturer publishes airflow versus static pressure, then measuring static pressure alone gives you the airflow, and it presents this as quicker and cheaper than measuring airflow directly. The idea is to find your speed tap or setting in the table, find your total external static pressure, and read the cfm. If your reading falls between two table rows, split the difference. A university handout lists the manufacturer's cfm versus pressure-drop table among the accepted ways to confirm airflow, next to a duct traverse, a flow grid, pressure matching and, for heating only, temperature rise. The same handout asks for airflow within 15 percent of design. A national-lab installation report uses a guideline of about 400 cfm per ton, plus or minus 15 percent, which is roughly 340 to 460 cfm per ton. Cross-check the answer with the temperature rise method when you can.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption we made up to show the arithmetic; none of it comes from a real manufacturer table.

  • A 3-ton cooling system on a new furnace with a coil on top, blower set for cooling.
  • Assumed blower table at that setting: 0.50 in. w.c. gives 1,270 cfm, and 0.60 in. w.c. gives 1,180 cfm.
  • Measured return port reading: negative 0.22 in. w.c. Measured supply port reading: positive 0.31 in. w.c.

Step 1, add the readings without signs: 0.22 + 0.31 = 0.53 in. w.c.

Step 2, interpolate in the table: 0.53 is 0.03 of the way up a 0.10 step, which is 0.3 of the way. The table drops 90 cfm over that step (1,270 minus 1,180). So 0.3 x 90 = 27 cfm, and 1,270 minus 27 = 1,243 cfm.

Step 3, divide by tons: 1,243 / 3 = about 414 cfm per ton. That is inside the 340 to 460 range from the guideline above.

Step 4, compare to the limits: 0.53 is near the 0.5 rule of thumb, but the real test is the manufacturer's range, which we did not invent here. If the printed limit were 0.50, this job would be slightly over and worth a look.

What to do when it is high

The national-lab installation report says high readings are brought down by repairing or upgrading the ducts. Our own field order of checks, starting with the cheapest:

  1. Look at the filter. Is it the right size, installed the right way, and not a more restrictive type than the manufacturer allows?
  2. Look at the coil and blower wheel for dirt. See Cleaning a Furnace Blower Wheel and Indoor Coil.
  3. Open every damper and register, and check for crushed or kinked flex duct.
  4. Take the two readings separately. A large return reading points to the return side: an undersized return, a small filter slot or too few grilles. A large supply reading points to supply ducts, the plenum or the coil.
  5. If the return is the problem, a larger filter area or a deeper filter can lower the drop. The same laboratory study found four-inch deep pleated filters averaged 0.38 in. w.c. (94 Pa) against 0.44 in. w.c. (110 Pa) for one-inch filters. Other work cited there found the depth effect small on clean filters, so do not count on it alone.
  6. Consult the unit instructions for any allowed change of blower speed. A faster setting moves more air but raises the static and the power draw.

Write the final readings, the speed setting and the estimated airflow on the startup sheet; Recording Startup Readings has a layout. For variable-speed units, setting airflow is done with switches or menus, and the same static check still applies.

Field card: Total External Static Pressure at Startup

Two ports, two readings, add them with no signs.

  • Return port just after the filter; supply port after the fan, or between heat exchanger and coil on a furnace.
  • Zero the meter, read each port, add the absolute values.
  • Filter and outside coil are external; the blower table covers what is inside the cabinet.
  • Use the manufacturer table at your speed setting to estimate cfm.
  • Guideline: about 400 cfm per ton, plus or minus 15 percent; reading near 0.5 in. w.c. or less is a good sign.
  • High? Check filter, coil, dampers, ducts, then each side separately.
