Condensation on Windows in Winter: Indoor Humidity and What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You wake up in January and the inside of the glass is wet, maybe frosted at the edges. That water came out of your own indoor air. This lesson explains why the glass sweats, what indoor humidity to aim for as the weather turns colder, and which fixes actually remove the moisture.

Why the glass sweats

Air always carries some water vapor. Warm air can hold more than cold air. If that air touches a surface colder than its dew point, the vapor turns into liquid on the surface. Simonton, a window maker, describes it the same way: condensation forms when vapor meets a surface colder than the dew point, as on a bathroom mirror after a hot shower. Andersen's help center adds that windows are typically the coolest interior surfaces in a room, even with storm panels, Low-E glass or triple panes. Pella says interior fogging shows up when indoor humidity is high and outdoor temperatures drop.

So the glass is not leaking. Andersen says interior condensation does not mean the glass has failed. It is a warning that the room air holds more moisture than the cold glass can tolerate. The physics behind dew point is in Psychrometrics for Technicians.

What humidity to aim for

The colder it gets outside, the colder the inner glass, so the humidity you can live with goes down. The EPA's general mold guidance says to keep indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. In winter you often need to sit at the low end or below it to keep windows clear. The University of Minnesota Extension moisture page lists window condensation among the symptoms of too much moisture and says problems may occur when relative humidity is above 50 percent. It does not print an outdoor-temperature chart.

The window makers do. Renewal by Andersen, a separate company that carries the Andersen name, prints the maximum indoor humidity by outside air temperature, and says the figures come from engineering studies at 70 F indoors. Simonton prints the same steps and credits University of Minnesota studies.

Outside air temperatureMaximum indoor relative humidity
20 F to 40 F40%
10 F to 20 F35%
0 F to 10 F30%
-10 F to 0 F25%
-20 F to -10 F20%
Below -20 F15%

Marvin's humidity guide gives a similar table with some ranges, such as 30 to 35 percent at 10 F, and suggests 30 to 50 percent for comfort. Andersen's own blog says to aim for 30 to 35 percent in winter, and its help center says at or below 30 to 35 percent. Treat the chart as a starting point, not a promise. Marvin and Andersen both say a hygrometer (a small humidity meter) is the way to know your real number, and Simonton notes that readings near a window can differ from the middle of the room. Keep in mind that the humidity in a weather report describes outdoors, not your house.

Where the moisture comes from

Marvin says a family of four adds about half a pint of water to the air every hour just by breathing and perspiring. Renewal by Andersen says cooking three meals a day puts eight or ten cups of water into the air and each shower adds roughly a cup. Other sources named by the makers and by University of Minnesota Extension include dishwashing, laundry, clothes dryers vented indoors, stored firewood, houseplants, humidifiers set too high, damp crawl spaces and plumbing leaks. Marvin and Andersen both note that a new home can release extra moisture as building materials dry, which Marvin says usually lasts a year or less.

Fixes, in a sensible order

  1. Remove the source. University of Minnesota Extension says to find and remove the moisture source first; if that is not possible, use a dehumidifier or ventilate. Store firewood outside and vent dryers and gas appliances outdoors, as Andersen and Marvin advise.
  2. Use the bath fan. The EPA says to run the bathroom fan or open the window when showering, and Renewal by Andersen says to keep it running until the mirror clears. The ducting and sizing details are in Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting and Moisture Control.
  3. Use the kitchen hood. The EPA suggests exhaust fans or an open window whenever you cook or wash dishes. A hood that vents outdoors removes water vapor; see Kitchen Range Hoods at Home. Marvin also suggests covering pots.
  4. Lower the humidifier. Andersen's help center says to lower the furnace humidifier setting or turn it off, and Simonton says to adjust a humidifier for the outside temperature. Match the setting to the chart above, and see Whole-House Humidifiers for how the controls work.
  5. Move air across the glass. Andersen and Simonton suggest opening blinds or curtains during the day, and Andersen suggests ceiling fans, so room air reaches the glass.
  6. Add controlled ventilation. University of Minnesota Extension suggests considering a whole-house ventilation system. Marvin suggests an air-to-air heat exchanger to replace stale air, and Andersen suggests one for very airtight homes. ERV vs HRV explains what each type recovers.
  7. Dehumidify if needed. Andersen, Pella and University of Minnesota Extension all list a dehumidifier as an option.

University of Minnesota Extension says to open windows only slightly to let moist air out, and Marvin calls opening them a temporary measure. It wastes heat, so treat it as a stopgap.

Worked example: will this glass fog?

The Magnus formula estimates dew point from air temperature and relative humidity. A University of Miami note by Brian McNoldy gives the August-Roche-Magnus form with a = 17.625 and b = 243.04 C, valid for air from 0 to 60 C and 1 to 100 percent RH. In the formula, T is in Celsius:

g = ln(RH/100) + aT/(b + T)   then   dew point = b × g / (a − g)

Given: the room is 70 F at 30% RH. Suppose a thermometer taped to the glass reads 35 F. (That glass temperature is a made-up example, not a source figure.)

  1. Convert the air to Celsius: (70 − 32) / 1.8 = 21.11 C.
  2. ln(30/100) = ln(0.30) = −1.2040.
  3. aT/(b + T) = 17.625 × 21.11 / (243.04 + 21.11) = 372.1 / 264.15 = 1.4087.
  4. g = −1.2040 + 1.4087 = 0.2047.
  5. Dew point = 243.04 × 0.2047 / (17.625 − 0.2047) = 49.75 / 17.42 = 2.86 C.
  6. Back to Fahrenheit: 2.86 × 1.8 + 32 = 37.1 F.

Compare: the glass is 35 F and the dew point is about 37 F. Glass below dew point means water forms. Now repeat with 20% RH: ln(0.20) = −1.6094, g = −1.6094 + 1.4087 = −0.2007, dew point = 243.04 × (−0.2007) / (17.625 + 0.2007) = −48.78 / 17.83 = −2.74 C = 27.1 F. The glass at 35 F is now above the dew point, so it stays dry. The point: dropping humidity from 30 to 20 percent lowered the dew point about 10 degrees, which is why the chart says to run drier as the glass gets colder. The McNoldy note lists dew points above 0 C as its valid range, so the 20% result sits just outside it. The formula is an approximation, so treat both results as close estimates.

Mold risk

The EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control, and that condensation can be a sign of high humidity. It advises acting quickly to dry wet surfaces and reduce the moisture source, and says mold cleaned up without fixing the water problem will likely return. Water that sits on sills and frames every morning is a reason to act, not to wipe and forget. University of Minnesota Extension lists mold in closet corners, kitchens and bathrooms among the symptoms of excess moisture, and says mold can cause allergies and respiratory problems. Simonton also says heavy interior condensation can point to moisture collecting in walls and ceilings, which is a reason to bring in a qualified inspector if it persists.

Fog between the panes is a different problem

If the haze or droplets are inside the sealed glass unit, no humidity setting will clear it. Andersen's blog says condensation between panes means the window's seal has failed, that it cannot be prevented, and that replacement is the ideal solution. Pella says moisture gets between the panes when the seal fails, that it typically occurs in older windows, and that eventually fogging may develop in any insulating glass product. Check your warranty and ask the maker or a window professional about repair or replacement. Condensation on the outside of the glass, by contrast, is dew from outdoor conditions, and Andersen says it is not a sign of failure.

Field card: Condensation on Windows in Winter

Wet glass means the glass is colder than the dew point of the room air.

  • Cold glass plus too much indoor moisture equals condensation on the inside.
  • Lower the target humidity as it gets colder: about 40% at 20 to 40 F outside, 30% at 0 to 10 F, 20% at -20 to -10 F (Renewal by Andersen chart).
  • EPA general range: below 60%, ideally 30 to 50%.
  • Remove sources, run bath and kitchen fans, lower the humidifier, move air across the glass.
  • Check with a hygrometer, not a guess.
  • Fog between the panes means the sealed unit has failed.
