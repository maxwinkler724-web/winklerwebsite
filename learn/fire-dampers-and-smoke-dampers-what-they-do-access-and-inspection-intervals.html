Fire Dampers and Smoke Dampers: What They Do, Access, and Inspection Intervals

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every commercial building has dampers that you never touch in normal service, because they sit in the ductwork waiting for a fire. They are tested on a calendar, not when a customer complains. This lesson explains the three types, how they are told apart, why access matters, and how the common test intervals turn into a real schedule and a real labor number.

Check your local rules

Check the rule that applies to your building. The test intervals below come from NFPA 80 and NFPA 105 as quoted by manufacturers, a state fire marshal and hospital trade groups. The local code, the fire marshal and the building owner's insurer decide what is enforced. Do not bypass, disable or alter one, and do not shut off detection or alarm equipment, without the facility's procedure and the people who own that system.

Three kinds of damper

A fire damper closes to keep flame from traveling through a duct that passes through a rated wall or floor. Most are held open by a fusible link, a small heat-sensitive part that lets go and frees the damper to close. Greenheck's link instructions list 165°F as the standard rating with 212°F available, and tell the installer to replace a link with the same temperature rating. Ruskin literature for an electric resettable system also lists 165°F or 212°F set points.

A smoke damper limits smoke movement. The International Code Council's CodeNotes describes smoke dampers as built to work against air velocity and fan pressure, with a leakage class, and with an elevated-temperature rating of at least 250°F. A smoke damper does not wait for heat. It has an actuator, and the building's smoke detectors or fire alarm panel tell it when to close. That is why the building automation system and the fire alarm system both show up in damper work; see Building Automation Basics.

A combination fire/smoke damper does both jobs. Pottorff's product page for one such model lists a 1-1/2 hour UL 555 fire rating and a 165°F fire closure temperature, plus a UL 555S smoke leakage class. AMCA's damper subcommittee notes that combination dampers usually use a thermostat-type heat device rather than a fusible link, and that thermostats can often be reset in the field after inspection. UL 555 is the fire damper standard and UL 555S is the smoke damper standard; a combination damper carries both.

Static or dynamic

AMCA and the ICC use the same idea. A static fire damper is for duct systems that shut down in a fire, so it only has to close in still air. A dynamic fire damper must close against the airflow and pressure the fan still produces. The ICC says dynamic dampers may be used in static systems, but static dampers should not be used in dynamic systems. A dynamic damper carries marked airflow and closing-pressure ratings, and an inspector compares the label to the plans.

Where they are required

The ICC CodeNotes summary points to International Building Code section 717.5 and International Mechanical Code section 607.5 for ducts and air transfer openings in walls, and to 717.6 and 607.6 for floors and other horizontal assemblies. In general, a duct crossing a fire-rated wall or floor needs a damper unless an exception applies, and smoke barriers and smoke partitions have their own rules. The required fire rating follows the assembly: 1-1/2 hours for assemblies rated less than 3 hours, and 3 hours for assemblies rated 3 hours or more.

Access doors and labels

A damper nobody can reach is a damper nobody can test. An FCIA presentation, quoting NFPA 105, says dampers with fusible links or internal operators must have an access door, or a removable duct section as an alternative, and quotes NFPA 80 for labeling the access point in letters at least one inch high. A paper from the AMCA ASET meeting says access must allow inspection and testing and be marked permanently, and that some newer proposals allow remote inspection where space is tight. Greenheck's dynamic fire damper submittal adds a maker detail: if you use an access door, put it on the side the maker marks. A University of Colorado fire safety summary says the commissioning test must confirm full, unobstructed access to the damper and every component.

Test and inspection intervals

Ruskin's smoke and combination damper O&M guide, Greenheck's article in ACHR News, and a Minnesota State Fire Marshal handout that quotes the standards all give the same pattern. After installation, an operational test proves the damper works as installed. Then:

  • Test and inspect each damper 1 year after installation.
  • Then repeat every 4 years.
  • In a building containing a hospital, repeat every 6 years.

The hospital interval is also in the hospital world's own documents. A federal memo from CMS (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) in 2009 let hospitals use the NFPA six-year cycle if their testing met the 2007 editions of NFPA 80 and NFPA 105. The American Society for Health Care Engineering lists the same timing under a Joint Commission standard, EC.02.03.05. These sources cite 2007 editions, so ask which edition your jurisdiction currently enforces. Ruskin itself says the required frequency varies by local jurisdiction. The University of Colorado summary adds that smoke dampers in some large spaces and smoke control systems are tested more often, so a damper on a smoke control system may not follow the simple cycle.

What goes wrong

  • Painted or damaged links. The University of Colorado summary lists damaged or painted fusible links, which should be replaced with the same size, temperature and load rating.
  • Blocked closure. The same page says nothing may stop the blades from closing.
  • Bent or rusted frames, blades and hinges, and objects poking through the frame.
  • Missing or buried access. A door behind a new pipe or above a ceiling that cannot be opened makes the test impossible.
  • Field changes. FCIA warns against modifying a damper in the field without approval, and against installing it racked or with screws in the track.
  • Dead actuators. AMCA says NFPA 80 and 105 call for repair as soon as possible.

Worked example: a schedule and a labor number

An invented office building is commissioned in March 2021. It has 40 dampers. We build two schedules, one for a non-hospital building and one for a building containing a hospital, using the intervals above. We assume each later test comes the stated number of years after the previous test.

Step 1, the first test. One year after March 2021 is March 2022. Both buildings do it. The March 2021 commissioning test is the installation test and is not counted below.

Step 2, non-hospital, every 4 years. March 2022 + 4 = March 2026. March 2026 + 4 = March 2030. March 2030 + 4 = March 2034, which is after 2033. So the periodic tests through 2033 are March 2022, March 2026 and March 2030: 3 cycles.

Step 3, hospital, every 6 years. March 2022 + 6 = March 2028. March 2028 + 6 = March 2034, again after 2033. The tests are March 2022 and March 2028: 2 cycles.

Step 4, damper inspections. 40 dampers × 3 cycles = 120 for the non-hospital building. 40 dampers × 2 cycles = 80 for the hospital building.

Step 5, labor per cycle. This lesson assumes 20 minutes per damper for opening the access door, looking at the link, blades and frame, running the closure test, resetting and logging it. That figure is an assumption for the example, not a published number; real time swings with access and how the dampers are tested. 40 × 20 minutes = 800 minutes. 800 ÷ 60 = 13.33 hours per cycle.

Step 6, labor through 2033. Non-hospital: 3 × 13.33 = 40.0 hours. Hospital: 2 × 13.33 = 26.67 hours. The difference is one whole cycle, 13.33 hours, which is the whole reason the six-year interval matters to a facilities budget.

Field card: Fire and Smoke Dampers

Fire closes on heat, smoke closes on a signal, combination does both.

  • Fusible link rating is commonly 165°F (212°F optional); replace with the same rating.
  • UL 555 is fire dampers; UL 555S is smoke dampers; combination has both.
  • Static: system shuts down. Dynamic: closes against airflow.
  • Test 1 year after install, then every 4 years; every 6 years in hospitals. Local rules decide.
  • Need access, a label, and no painted links or blocked blades.
  • Example: 40 dampers at 20 min = 13.33 h per cycle; 3 cycles by 2033 = 40.0 h, hospital 2 cycles = 26.67 h.
