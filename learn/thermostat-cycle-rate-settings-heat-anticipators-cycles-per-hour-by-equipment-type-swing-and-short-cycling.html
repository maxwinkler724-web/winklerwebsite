Thermostat Cycle Rate Settings: Heat Anticipators, CPH, Swing and Short Cycling

Last updated: 2026-10-28

A thermostat does not just wait for the room to hit the setpoint. It turns the heat off a little early, or lets the room drift a little past the setpoint, and one adjustment controls how much. On an old mechanical thermostat that adjustment is the heat anticipator. On a digital one it is a cycle rate or a swing. Get it wrong and the equipment short cycles or the house rides up and down.

What the setting is really doing

A mechanical heating thermostat has a small resistor in series with the W circuit. Current through it warms the sensing element a few degrees above room temperature, so the contacts open before the room reaches the setpoint and the leftover heat in the equipment finishes the job. That is the heat anticipator. A trade training article on the topic describes it exactly that way, as a resistor in the heat call circuit that ends the cycle a little early so residual heat and the fan off-delay cover the remaining gap. A higher anticipator setting lengthens the cycle and a lower one shortens it, which matches a thermostat maker's installation sheet that says counterclockwise lengthens heating cycles and clockwise shortens them.

Digital thermostats model the same idea in software. Installer menus call it a cycle rate (cycles per hour, CPH), a swing, or a differential, and one maker's manual says the swing setting is often called cycle rate, differential or anticipation. See Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats for the terminals involved.

Setting a mechanical anticipator

The rule is to set the anticipator to the current the W circuit actually draws. Anticipators are rated in amps, not ohms. Typical installation sheets list the adjustment range as roughly 0.15 to 1.2 amps and give example draws for 24 volt primary controls: about 0.23 A for a gas valve, 0.35 A for a zone valve and 0.43 A for an oil primary. Those are examples from one maker, so measure the real circuit.

  1. With the thermostat off the wall plate or its cover open, twist R and W together at the base so the anticipator is out of the circuit and the equipment sees a steady call for heat. Stay with the equipment while it runs.
  2. Wrap the thermostat wire 10 times around the jaw of a clamp meter that reads low amps. The trade article describes this wrapped-coil method, because a clamp meter cannot read a single 24 volt control wire with any accuracy.
  3. Power up and let the sequence run until everything that pulls on the circuit is energized, such as the gas valve and any relay. The largest draw is usually when the valve opens.
  4. Divide the meter reading by the number of wraps.
  5. Set the pointer to that value, then watch a few cycles.

Two cautions from the maker sheets we read: move the pointer in small steps (one maker says no more than half a marking at a time, another says 0.1 A at a time), and do not drop below 0.3 A on that maker's guide. Pass the same wire through the jaw in the same direction every time, so the currents add. A multi-stage or zoned system may have more than one thing on the W circuit, so measure with everything that can be on at once.

Typical anticipator settings by equipment type

One maker's installation sheet lists starting values when you cannot measure. The values below are from that sheet, quoted as typical amps:

  • Steam: 1.2
  • Hot water heat: 0.8
  • High-efficiency warm air: 0.8
  • Standard warm air: 0.4
  • Electric heat: 0.3

Treat the table as a starting point only. The same sheet tells you to nudge the pointer down 0.1 A if the equipment keeps running past the setpoint and up 0.1 A if it shuts off before the room gets there. The equipment nameplate or control rating beats any generic table, and the measured current beats both.

Cycles per hour on digital thermostats

One maker defines a cycle rate as the maximum number of times a system will run in an hour while it is carrying about half of its capacity. The setting is a ceiling, not a schedule: in cold weather the run times get longer and the count drops. Several makers publish a similar short list in their installer setup menus. Two separate maker documents we read agree on these heating values:

  • 5 CPH: gas or oil furnaces under 90 percent efficiency
  • 3 CPH: hot water systems and furnaces over 90 percent efficiency
  • 1 CPH: steam or gravity systems
  • 9 CPH: electric furnaces

Another maker's table lists 3 CPH for a single-stage heat pump, 6 CPH as the factory setting for conventional systems and 9 CPH for electric heat, so the numbers are not universal. Use the table printed in the installation instructions for the exact thermostat and equipment in front of you. Compressors get their own setting: a common recommendation is 3 CPH for most compressors, with a selectable range of 1 to 6, and a separate compressor protection delay that defaults to 5 minutes on the models we read. That protection delay is a minimum off time, so a compressor cannot restart the moment the call returns. It is a safeguard, not a substitute for a correct cycle rate.

Swing, differential and minimum times

Some thermostats ask for a swing in degrees instead of a CPH number. One manual gives a range of plus or minus 0.2 to 2 F and says plainly that a smaller swing causes more frequent cycles and a larger swing causes fewer. In heating, a swing of 0.5 F means the heat comes on about 0.5 F below the setpoint and goes off about 0.5 F above it. The same manual's tip is to use a swing as large as the occupants will accept. Do not confuse swing with auto-changeover differential, which is a separate setting: the gap needed before the thermostat jumps from heating to cooling.

How the wrong setting shows up

  • Anticipator set lower than the real current, or CPH set high: runs get shorter and starts per hour go up. You get frequent short runs.
  • Anticipator set higher than the real current, or swing set wide: the equipment runs long, the room overshoots, and occupants notice big temperature swings.
  • CPH too high on a hot water or steam system: the boiler fires many times an hour on a system that is slow to deliver heat, which wastes fuel and wears the burner. See Steam Boiler Short Cycling Causes.

The trade article cautions that the anticipator is only one possible cause of short cycling. Rule out airflow, limits, placement and sizing before you touch the dial. Our related lessons cover each: Why Is My Furnace Short Cycling?, AC short cycling causes and where to put a thermostat.

Worked example (ours)

Anticipator. All numbers in this example are our own assumptions for practice. A forced air furnace has a mechanical thermostat set at a guessed 0.8 and it overshoots the setpoint. We twist R to W, wrap the wire 10 times around the clamp, and the meter reads 4.0 A.

4.0 A on the meter / 10 wraps = 0.40 A actual circuit current.

We set the pointer to 0.4. That matches the 0.4 typical for standard warm air above, which is the sort of agreement you hope to see, though your own meter reading is the one that counts.

Cycle rate. We assume a digital thermostat set to 5 CPH and a load that is about 50 percent of equipment capacity.

60 minutes / 5 cycles = 12 minutes per complete cycle.

At a 50 percent load the burner is on half of that: 12 x 0.5 = 6 minutes on and 6 minutes off.

Change the setting to 3 CPH: 60 / 3 = 20 minutes per cycle, so about 10 on and 10 off. At 9 CPH it is 60 / 9, about 6.7 minutes per cycle, roughly 3.3 on and 3.3 off. A lower CPH means longer runs and fewer starts, but also a wider drift in room temperature. Pick the number the equipment type calls for.

Field card: Thermostat Cycle Rate Settings: Heat Anticipators, CPH, Swing and Short Cycling

Match the setting to the equipment, not to a guess.

  • Anticipator: set to the measured W circuit amps; 10 wraps, divide the meter by 10.
  • Example maker values: steam 1.2, hot water 0.8, high-efficiency warm air 0.8, standard warm air 0.4, electric 0.3 A.
  • Cycle rate is the ceiling on cycles per hour at about 50 percent load.
  • Typical heat CPH on two maker sheets: 5 gas/oil under 90 percent, 3 hot water or over 90 percent, 1 steam, 9 electric.
  • Cycle time = 60 / CPH. At 50 percent load, half is on and half is off.
  • Smaller swing = more cycles; larger swing = fewer cycles and more temperature drift.
  • Compressor protection delay: 5 minutes on the models we read.
  • Change one thing at a time and watch a few cycles.
