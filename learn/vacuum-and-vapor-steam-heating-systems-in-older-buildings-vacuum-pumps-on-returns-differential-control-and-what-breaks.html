Vacuum and Vapor Steam Heating in Older Buildings: Pumps, Traps and Failures

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many large older buildings, such as apartment blocks, offices and churches, were piped as two-pipe steam systems with a pump holding the return side below atmospheric pressure. This lesson explains why that setup exists, what the vacuum does to steam temperature, and the few faults that cause most of the trouble.

Vapor systems and vacuum systems

Both are two-pipe, low-pressure designs. For the basic split between one-pipe and two-pipe piping, see One-Pipe vs. Two-Pipe Steam Systems; here we only cover what makes these two types different. A 1948 heating guide chapter we read describes a vapor system as one where each radiator discharges condensate and air through a thermostatic trap to a dry return main. A trade article on these systems says they were designed to run at very low pressure, "ounces in fact," not pounds. The same guide says a vacuum system adds a vacuum pump to maintain sub-atmospheric pressure. The pump removes air and water from the return, separates them, vents the air and sends the water to the boiler or a receiver.

Both depend on one rule from that guide: the supply and return sides must not connect at any point except through a trap. The trade article above adds the reason: without a trap on each radiator outlet, steam passes into the return and the pressure difference that moves it is lost.

What the vacuum does

Bell and Gossett's guide FHD-205 says an ordinary two-pipe vacuum system uses a pump to create a pressure below atmospheric, usually 3 in. to 8 in. Hg, and that vacuum removes air from the piping and heating units so the system picks up heat quickly. Domestic Pump's manual TES-676B says vacuum also helps trap performance, because a larger pressure difference across the trap speeds drainage. Peerless adds that the return piping on a vacuum system can be smaller because the vacuum pulls air through quickly, and that the system has to be airtight for the pump to do its job.

The second effect is on temperature. Water boils at a lower temperature when the pressure over it drops, so steam in a system held under vacuum is cooler than steam at atmospheric pressure. The worked example below puts numbers on that. The 1948 guide says vapor systems can run under vacuum at lower steam temperatures for about four to eight hours, depending on how tight the piping is.

How the vacuum is controlled

Pumps are not sized to chase a deep vacuum. A Hoffman Specialty manual (TES-181A, a distributor copy) says low-vacuum pumps are rated to hold an average vacuum of 5.5 in. Hg and are controlled to cut in at 3 in. Hg and out at 8 in. Hg. Domestic's TES-676B gives the same 3 in. to 8 in. working range and says the pump starts and stops on a vacuum switch responding to system needs. It does not publish switch setpoints, so read the setpoints on the unit in front of you.

TES-676B sizes air capacity in cfm per 1,000 sq. ft. of EDR (see Steam Radiator EDR), and sets it higher for systems that leak, so leaks are expected. Vertical lift also costs vacuum: a trade article on checking these systems says each foot of lift takes roughly one more inch of mercury.

Newer designs push further. A FacilitiesNet article describes a "vacuum boost" that pulls 10 to 18 in. Hg at the start of a heating cycle, and a trapless type that holds 25 to 27 in. Hg. Those are trade-press descriptions, not what you will find in an older building.

Parts you will see

  • Thermostatic trap at each radiator. Peerless says these hold condensate until it cools 10 to 30 F below steam saturation temperature and pass air quickly. TES-676B says most steam heating applications use thermostatic traps that drain condensate at roughly 160 F to 180 F.
  • Packless radiator valves. Hoffman calls packless valves "ideal" for vacuum two-pipe systems because their construction prevents air leakage, and Peerless says packed valves are for pressurized systems only.
  • Vacuum breaker or check valve. TES-676B says traps draining into a submerged inlet need a vacuum breaker, or a check valve acting as one, so condensate can drain; without it the return can fill and hammer.
  • Vacuum pump unit. TES-676B describes a receiver in two compartments. The lower one collects condensate under vacuum; the upper one separates air and must stay open to atmosphere. Water circulated through a nozzle and venturi creates the vacuum. Receiver sizing is in our boiler feed pump lesson, so we skip it here.

Worked example: steam temperature at 10 in. Hg (Our own)

Assumption: the return side is held at a steady 10 in. Hg vacuum, and the steam side is close enough to that pressure that saturated steam there is at the same pressure. We take standard atmosphere as 101,325 Pa from the NIST guide to SI conversion factors, which is 14.696 psi, equal to 29.92 in. Hg.

  1. Convert the vacuum to psi: 10 ÷ 29.92 × 14.696 = 4.91 psi below atmosphere.
  2. Absolute pressure: 14.696 − 4.91 = 9.78 psia.
  3. Read saturation temperature at 9.78 psia from the NIST Chemistry WebBook water table. It lists 191.31 F at 9.6157 psia and 192.44 F at 9.8478 psia. Interpolating: 191.31 + (9.784 − 9.6157) ÷ (9.8478 − 9.6157) × 1.13 = about 192.1 F.
  4. Atmospheric check: at 14.696 psia the same table interpolates to about 212 F.
  5. 2 psig check: 14.696 + 2 = 16.696 psia. The table lists 218.24 F at 16.624 psia and 219.36 F at 16.990 psia, which interpolates to about 218.5 F.

Result: steam at 10 in. Hg is about 192 F, about 20 F cooler than steam at atmospheric pressure (212 F) and about 26 F cooler than steam at 2 psig (about 218 F). A Trerice steam properties table lists 192 F at 10 in. Hg and 212 F at 0 psig, which agrees. It also follows that condensate hotter than about 192 F entering a receiver held at 10 in. Hg will flash to steam.

What breaks

  • Air leaks. Leaks at valve packing, pipe joints, trap bodies and pump seals let air in. The pump then runs long and never reaches its cut-out vacuum, and radiators heat unevenly. The Heating Help article on checking vacuum systems adds that vents or breakers someone added later work against the vacuum.
  • Traps failed open. Peerless says a failed-open radiator trap lets steam into the returns, air-binds other radiators, causes tank cavitation from hot condensate and can cause severe hammer. The Heating Help article says failed traps cut the pressure difference, slow the condensate return and heat the condensate until the pump cavitates and the boiler loses water. Radiator and end-of-main traps are the usual culprits.
  • Traps failed closed. Peerless says a failed-closed main or riser drip blocks air elimination, so the affected part of the system will not fill with steam.
  • Hot condensate in the pump. TES-676B lists the vacuum return units at a maximum of 160 F condensate, with some models marked for factory consultation on higher temperatures, and notes that a limit switch can stop the air pump when condensate gets too hot.
  • No seal water. The Heating Help article says the pump's upper chamber needs its water to make vacuum, so check the float switch and the solenoid valve that supplies it.
  • Closing a radiator on both sides. Holohan warns that a radiator closed at both valves can condense its steam into a vacuum that pulls steam from nearby returns through its trap and ruins the element.

How techs test it

  1. Read the gauge. Compare the vacuum to the 3 in. to 8 in. Hg working range above. Weak or slow-building vacuum points to leaks or a weak pump.
  2. Isolate the pump. The Heating Help article suggests closing the inlet valve to the pump receiver. If the pump pulls vacuum with that valve closed but not with it open, the leak is in the system, not the pump.
  3. Check the return temperature. That article says returning condensate ideally should not be hotter than 180 F; hot return usually means failed traps or leaks.
  4. Check the receiver vent. It must connect to a dry return downstream of the traps, because a vent open to the room kills vacuum.
  5. Test traps. See Steam Trap Testing Methods and Steam Trap Types. A DOE tip sheet says systems not maintained for 3 to 5 years may have 15 to 30 percent of traps failed, and recommends annual checks on low-pressure systems (under 30 psig).

Peerless advises against replacing traps while the system is running and suggests waiting until summer; the Heating Help article gives the same reason, that bad traps in service can damage new ones. Every failed-trap fix also needs a way for the system to breathe, as covered in Steam Main Venting Basics.

Peerless adds that converting a vacuum system to a plain pumped return means changing the piping.

Field card: Vacuum and Vapor Steam Heating

Vacuum lowers steam temperature and pulls air out. Leaks and failed traps defeat it.

  • Typical working vacuum is 3 in. to 8 in. Hg; pumps cut in near 3 and out near 8 (check the unit).
  • 10 in. Hg is 4.91 psi below atmosphere, 9.78 psia, and saturated steam near 192 F. Atmospheric is 212 F. 2 psig is about 218 F.
  • Thermostatic traps at radiators keep steam out of the returns; failed-open traps heat the condensate and cavitate the pump.
  • Packless valves keep air out of the vacuum side.
  • Pump will not pull vacuum: close the receiver inlet to see whether the pump or the system leaks.
  • Return condensate ideally stays at or below 180 F; vacuum units are listed for about 160 F.
