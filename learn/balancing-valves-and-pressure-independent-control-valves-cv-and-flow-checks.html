Balancing Valves and Pressure-Independent Control Valves: Cv and Flow Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A balancing valve is a throttle with a built-in way to measure flow. A pressure-independent control valve goes further and holds the flow for you. This lesson shows the one equation behind both, gpm = Cv × √ΔP, and what happens when a valve does not get the pressure it needs.

What a circuit setter style valve does

A balancing valve of the circuit setter type is an adjustable valve with two small test ports, one on each side of a restriction. The restriction creates a pressure difference that grows as flow grows. You read that difference and turn it into gpm. A Deppmann-hosted balancing article describes two common builds: a variable-orifice valve, where the setting you dial in changes the opening, and a venturi type, where a fixed, smooth-edged orifice does the measuring and a separate ball or butterfly valve does the throttling. The article says the variable-orifice kind reads to about 5 percent and has trouble with very low flows, because getting a readable difference at low flow means a large pressure loss.

Caleffi's balancing issue (Idronics 8) adds that a venturi takes its readings at the inlet and throat, and that this gives a steadier pressure-to-flow relationship than taps across a plug. It also notes that these valves need straight pipe ahead of them because turbulence upsets the reading. Its figure is 10 pipe diameters; the Deppmann article gives smaller numbers for some valves. Makers differ, so use the straight-pipe distance printed for the valve in front of you.

Reading flow with a meter and the maker's chart

You connect a differential pressure meter to the two ports. Older practice used a manometer or an accurate gauge. Newer electronic meters convert the reading to flow on the screen. Caleffi lists the drawbacks: one meter serves one valve at a time, a little water escapes when you connect, and the meter can be costly.

The Bell & Gossett instruction manual for its Circuit Sentry Flo-Setter says that when the reading is above the valve's minimum, you take flow from the maker's graph for that valve size and handle setting, and below the minimum you use the flow formula with Cv. The graphs live in a separate flow curve book or the submittal sheet. The graph is for that model, so a chart from another valve gives a wrong answer. Pre-set the handle, lock it if the valve allows it, and write the setting and reading down.

The Cv equation

Cv is a valve's flow coefficient. Belimo's explainer on Cv defines it as the gallons per minute of 60 °F water that flow through a fully open valve with a 1 psi pressure difference. Caleffi uses the same definition. Rearranged for water, the relationship is:

gpm = Cv × √ΔP, with ΔP in psi. The other way around: ΔP = (gpm ÷ Cv)².

Belimo's own worked example runs the equation backward: 10 gpm at 4 psi needs a Cv of 10 ÷ √4 = 5. Belimo's text defines the variables for water systems with a specific gravity of 1; the formula itself is an image on that page, so this lesson writes the water form from the definition above. Caleffi notes that the rated Cv is the fully open value and that Cv falls toward zero as the stem closes, so for a throttled valve use the Cv for the actual setting from the maker's data.

Worked example: three questions

Use a valve with a stated Cv of 5.0. This is a teaching number, not a rating for any particular product.

1. Flow at a 4 psi drop.

  • √4 = 2
  • gpm = 5.0 × 2 = 10 gpm

2. Pressure drop needed for 12 gpm.

  • gpm ÷ Cv = 12 ÷ 5.0 = 2.4
  • ΔP = 2.4² = 5.76, so about 5.8 psi
  • Check: √5.76 = 2.4, and 5.0 × 2.4 = 12 gpm.

Notice the square root. Going from 10 to 12 gpm is 20 percent more flow, but the drop climbs from 4 to 5.76 psi, about 44 percent more. Flow goes with the square root of pressure, so pressure goes with the square of flow.

3. A PICV with a 5 psi minimum in a branch with 3.5 psi available.

A pressure-independent valve has an internal regulator. Caleffi's Idronics 34 says the minimum differential pressure for its example valve ranges from 3.6 to 4.4 psi depending on flow setting, and that pressure-independent balancing valves have a lower threshold of roughly 2 to 5 psi depending on model. Danfoss's data sheet for its valve lists a minimum Δp for each size and says that at the minimum the valve reaches at least 90 percent of nominal flow. Below the threshold, Idronics 8 explains, the internal mechanism does not move and flow rises with pressure, like a fixed opening.

  • Stated minimum: 5 psi. Available: 3.5 psi. Shortfall: 5 − 3.5 = 1.5 psi.
  • 3.5 is less than 5, so the valve is out of its regulating range. It stops holding the set flow.
  • It acts like a fixed opening, so a rough estimate scales with the square root: √(3.5 ÷ 5) = √0.7 = 0.84. A valve set for 12 gpm might pass about 12 × 0.84 = 10 gpm.

That last step is our own teaching estimate, not maker data; the real shortfall depends on the model. The point is the direction: the coil gets less than design, the space may fall behind on a hot day, and the control valve may sit wide open without catching up. The fix is more pressure at the branch (check pump head, strainers, a partly closed isolation valve, or a differential pressure setpoint), not a bigger valve.

Automatic flow limiters and PICVs

An automatic flow limiter is a fixed or presettable valve with a spring-loaded regulator that caps flow across a pressure range. Danfoss describes its valve as having an internal controller that holds a constant pressure drop across the valve, and says that without an actuator it works as a plain flow limiter. A pressure-independent control valve (PICV) adds an actuator that modulates the valve, while the regulator keeps the flow per position steady. Belimo describes an electronic version that uses a flow sensor instead of a spring regulator. Caleffi says pressure-independent balancing valves hold flow to about plus or minus 5 percent within their working range, and that flow climbs again above the upper threshold.

Both Belimo pages say one such valve can take the place of a separate balancing valve and control valve, and that you size it by the flow the coil needs rather than by valve authority.

Why balancing matters for low delta-T

Delta-T is the temperature difference between supply and return. Low delta-T means too much water for the work done. Belimo ties overflow through coils to low delta-T in chilled-water systems and says pressure-independent valves limit that overflow and the pumping energy it wastes. Caleffi's example shows the mechanism: raising flow through a 180 °F coil from 1 to 3 gpm cut its temperature drop from about 40 °F to 16 °F. More gpm, smaller drop. Our low delta-T lesson covers plant effects, and Hot-Water Flow Rate Math shows the gpm and temperature-drop relationship.

Field card: Balancing Valves and PICVs: Cv and Flow Checks

gpm = Cv × √ΔP. Pressure goes with flow squared.

  • Cv: gpm of 60 °F water at 1 psi drop. Use the Cv for the actual valve setting.
  • Cv 5.0 at 4 psi: 5.0 × 2 = 10 gpm.
  • 12 gpm at Cv 5.0: (12 ÷ 5)² = 5.76 psi.
  • Read ΔP at the two ports, then use the maker's chart for that model and setting.
  • Keep the straight pipe the maker specifies upstream of the valve.
  • A PICV needs its minimum ΔP. Below it, flow falls short of the setpoint.
  • Overflow lowers coil delta-T. Record settings before changing anything.
