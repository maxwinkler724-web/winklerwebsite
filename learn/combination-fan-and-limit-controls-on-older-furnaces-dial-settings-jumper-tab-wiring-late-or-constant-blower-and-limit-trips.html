Combination Fan and Limit Controls on Older Furnaces

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before circuit boards, one little dial in the plenum decided when the blower started, when it stopped, and when the burner was shut off for being too hot. It is still found on plenty of older gas and oil furnaces. This lesson explains how it works, how it is wired, and how to read a complaint like "the fan keeps cycling at the end of the call."

What the control is

A combination fan and limit control is two temperature switches in one case, driven by one sensing element that sits in the warm air plenum or bonnet. Maker instructions describe the element as a coiled (helix) bimetal strip. As plenum air warms, the strip winds or unwinds a little, and that motion pushes switch contacts open or closed. There is no electronics and no timer, so the blower follows plenum temperature, not the clock.

One switch is the fan switch. It starts the blower when the plenum reaches the fan-on point and stops it when the plenum falls to the fan-off point. The other is the limit switch. It opens the burner control circuit if the plenum gets too hot, and it closes again on its own when things cool. One maker describes the limit as having a fixed differential while the fan side has an adjustable one.

The dial, the three pointers and the button

The dial carries three pointers: fan off, fan on, and limit. Maker literature says to hold the dial still while you move the pointers, because forcing the dial strains the sensing element. Typical settings are not universal. Two of the maker instructions we read give no recommended fan temperatures at all and say to follow the furnace maker. One oil furnace owner's manual lists fan on at 120 F, fan off at 90 F and limit at 200 F. An inspector reference lists factory defaults of fan off 90 F, fan on 100 F and limit 200 F. A control label we read says the limit is factory set at 200 F. Always use the number on the furnace nameplate or in its manual.

The manual-auto button is the usual reason a blower "never shuts off." Pushed in (MAN) it runs the fan constantly and overrides the fan pointers. Pulled out or slid to AUTO, the fan follows the pointers. Some people leave it on MAN on purpose for summer air circulation or a continuous filter, so ask before you change it.

Line voltage, low voltage and the jumper tab

The fan side usually switches the blower motor at line voltage (120 V). The limit side can sit in a line voltage burner circuit, such as an oil burner control, or in a low voltage or millivolt circuit, such as a gas valve on a 24 V transformer. Maker instructions say one brass jumper tab ties the two sides together for line voltage use and must be removed when the limit is used in a low voltage or millivolt circuit. One maker warns of electric shock if the jumper is left in on a low voltage hookup, and another notes the jumper cannot be put back once it is broken out. Check both by meter: if the tab is in and you think the limit is 24 V, the line voltage side may be live. For how the 24 V side is made, see the related lesson on relays and transformers.

Why the blower comes on late or runs constantly

Late: the blower waits for the plenum to reach the fan-on pointer. A high fan-on setting, a sensing element that does not see the heat, or low burner output all delay it. One maker says the element must not touch internal furnace parts, and another says to keep it at least 3 inches from hot heat exchanger surfaces unless the furnace maker approves a closer spot.

Constant: first look at the manual button. Next check whether the fan-off pointer has been set so low that the plenum never falls to it, and whether the burner is staying on because of a bad call for heat.

What a limit trip means

The limit is a symptom detector. When it opens, the plenum got hotter than the maker allows. The usual cause is that not enough air crossed the heat exchanger: a dirty filter, a blocked or closed return, collapsed or disconnected duct, or a blower that is slow or not running. An oversized burner can do it too, because heat goes in faster than the blower can carry it away. The owner's manual we read says a 200 F reading "indicates a malfunction" and lists excessive restriction of heated air, a failed blower motor, an obstructed blower inlet and too much oil being fired. An inspector reference adds a bad switch or a flue blockage. Frequent short cycling during heating often traces to the plenum overheating from reduced airflow. See why furnaces short cycle and how a cracked heat exchanger is inspected, since repeated overheating is hard on the metal.

Safety rule. Never bypass a limit, jumper it, or tape the contacts. Never set the limit pointer higher than the furnace maker allows. A maker instruction says the limit pointer should never be set higher than the furnace manufacturer recommends, and one scaleplate we read is marked as a safety stop that must not be altered. An inspector reference warns that bypassing a limit can lead to heat exchanger damage, fire or carbon monoxide hazards. See combustion and CO safety basics. A limit that trips is telling you something. Find the airflow problem.

Worked example (ours): a blower that cycles at the end of the call

Our own example furnace has fan on at 120 F, fan off at 90 F and limit at 200 F. The customer says that when the thermostat is satisfied, the blower stops and starts again a few times. Walk through it:

  1. The call ends and the burner shuts off. Plenum air is still warm, so the blower keeps pushing air until the element cools to 90 F.
  2. With the blower off, the hot heat exchanger is no longer being cooled. Heat soaks back into the plenum and the element warms again.
  3. If the element climbs past 120 F, the fan switch closes and the blower restarts. It runs the plenum back down toward 90 F, shuts off, and the cycle repeats until the metal settles below 120 F.

This is our reasoning from how the switch works, not a maker statement. Two things make it worse: low airflow (the metal holds more heat) and a sensing element mounted close to the heat exchanger. Fixes to consider: clear the airflow restriction first, then confirm the element position per the maker. The oil furnace manual we read says the fan settings may be adjusted if the blower cycles unusually, but to talk to the dealer first to keep the warranty. If you move a pointer, never touch the limit pointer.

Now the airflow side. The sensible heat relation is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T. A building science article we read explains the 1.08 as air density 0.075 lb/cu ft x specific heat 0.24 Btu/lb F x 60 min/h, for air near sea level and room temperature. A federal strategy guide uses a rounded 1.1 for similar conditions. Rearranged, delta T = Btu/h / (1.08 x cfm).

Assumptions (ours, not from a nameplate): furnace output 56,000 Btu/h, return air 70 F, normal airflow 1,000 cfm.

  • Normal: 56,000 / (1.08 x 1,000) = 51.9 F rise, supply about 70 + 51.9 = 121.9 F.
  • Dirty filter, 500 cfm: 56,000 / (1.08 x 500) = 103.7 F rise, supply about 173.7 F.
  • Badly blocked return, 400 cfm: 56,000 / (1.08 x 400) = 129.6 F rise, supply about 199.6 F, right at the 200 F limit.

So with the burner unchanged, losing 60 percent of the airflow can push the plenum to the limit, and the element sits near hot metal so it may see even more. The limit opens, the burner stops, the plenum cools, the burner relights and the story repeats. Compare the actual rise against the furnace nameplate range, not our numbers.

How modern boards replaced them

Newer furnaces use an integrated control board. One maker instruction describes a selectable blower-off delay of 90, 120, 150 or 180 seconds, factory set at 150, and a fixed 30-second blower-on delay, with safety circuits monitored by the board. The limit is a separate switch in the heat exchanger area, and the fan timing is a timer instead of plenum temperature. That removes the rebound cycling, but a tripped limit still means the same thing: look at airflow first.

Field card: Combination fan and limit controls

Fan follows plenum temperature; the limit protects the heat exchanger.

  • Three pointers: fan off, fan on, limit. Hold the dial while moving them.
  • MAN pushed in = constant fan. AUTO = fan follows pointers.
  • Jumper tab ties fan side to limit side; remove it for low voltage or millivolt limits.
  • Late blower: high fan-on, poor element position, weak burner.
  • Limit trip: look at filter, returns, ducts, blower, then burner size.
  • Never bypass the limit or exceed the maker maximum.
  • Rise = Btu/h / (1.08 x cfm).
