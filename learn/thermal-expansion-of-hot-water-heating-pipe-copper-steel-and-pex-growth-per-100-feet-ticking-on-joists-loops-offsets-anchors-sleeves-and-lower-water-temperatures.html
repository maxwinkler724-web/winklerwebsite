Thermal Expansion of Hot-Water Heating Pipe: Copper, Steel and PEX Growth, Ticking and Loops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Heating pipe is a few degrees long in the morning and a few more in the afternoon. A hot-water system fills at cellar temperature and then runs far hotter, and every foot of pipe grows when that happens. This lesson puts numbers on the growth for copper, steel and PEX, explains the ticking and creaking it causes, and shows how loops, offsets, anchors and sleeves give the pipe room.

Why pipe grows, and why it makes noise

Almost every solid gets longer as it warms. The change in length is the pipe length, times a material constant called the linear expansion coefficient, times the temperature change. A federal measurement lab reported a mean coefficient for copper of about 17.8 millionths per degree Celsius between 20 and 100 degrees Celsius. Another federal agency's accident analysis used 12 millionths per kelvin for steel, and a state university system's hydronic piping guide lists carbon steel at 6.5 millionths per inch per degree Fahrenheit. Those are small numbers, but they multiply by a long run and a big temperature swing.

The noise comes from the pipe sliding. A pipe that is clamped tight, or that passes through a snug hole in a joist, cannot grow quietly. It sticks, builds up force, then slips with a tick, a snap or a long creak. The sound often starts a few minutes after the boiler fires, as the pipe warms, and again as it cools after the call for heat ends. The noise is usually harmless in itself, but a pipe that is forced to move against something rigid can wear through insulation, scar a hanger, loosen a fitting or stress a joint. For the other common ticking source, loose fins and enclosures on baseboard, see our baseboard cleaning and ticking lesson. This page covers the pipe itself.

Worked example (our own): 100 feet, three materials

This is our own example, with assumed inputs, not field data. Assume a straight 100 foot run, which is 1,200 inches. Assume the system is filled at 60 degrees Fahrenheit and later runs at a 180 degree supply temperature, so the temperature change is 180 minus 60, which is 120 degrees Fahrenheit. The formula is: change in length = length x coefficient x temperature change.

  • Copper. The federal lab value of 17.8 millionths per degree Celsius converts to 17.8 x 5/9, about 9.9 millionths per degree Fahrenheit. Growth = 1,200 x 0.0000099 x 120 = 1.43 inches.
  • Steel. Use 6.5 millionths per degree Fahrenheit. Growth = 1,200 x 0.0000065 x 120 = 0.94 inch. The 12 millionths per kelvin figure is 6.7 millionths per degree Fahrenheit, which agrees.
  • PEX. The coefficient of PEX is not one number, because it climbs as the pipe warms. The hydronic piping guide tabulates one PEX-a product at 1.11 ten-thousandths per degree Fahrenheit at 120 degrees and 1.00 ten-thousandth at 100 degrees. Using 1.11 ten-thousandths as a midpoint-style estimate, growth = 1,200 x 0.000111 x 120 = 15.98, or about 16 inches. Using 1.00 ten-thousandth gives 14.4 inches. We would plan on roughly 14 to 16 inches for this example.

Check: 1,200 x 120 = 144,000 degree-inches. Copper 144,000 x 0.0000099 = 1.43. Steel 144,000 x 0.0000065 = 0.94. PEX 144,000 x 0.000111 = 15.98. So PEX grows about 11 times as much as copper and about 17 times as much as steel in the same conditions. The specific values for any product come from its maker, so treat these as learning numbers.

Plastic pipe needs more room than metal

The same hydronic guide says plastic pipes expand and contract dramatically with temperature, and it advises anchoring a plastic system where it passes through a building wall or floor. In practice that means PEX gets generous clearance, supports that let it slide and snake gently, and loops or offsets where a metal pipe would be fine running straight. The federal housing research office's PEX design guide says a section on expansion and contraction is part of its installation chapter and quotes model-code support spacing of 32 inches horizontal and 10 feet vertical for PEX; follow the pipe maker's instructions for heating applications. Another reason PEX is easy to support loosely is that it is much more flexible than copper, but flexible does not mean it can be pinned tight and left.

Ways to absorb movement

  • Offsets and swing joints. A branch that leaves a main with a few elbows and a short length of pipe at right angles can twist a little, so the main moves and the branch flexes instead of binding. A university facilities guideline we read requires at least five fittings, including the tee, where a riser connects to a main, and at least four where a riser or branch connects to a terminal unit. Those counts are the guideline's own minimums for large buildings, not a residential rule.
  • Expansion loops. A U-shaped detour in a long main lets the legs bend as the run grows. The same guideline asks for two guides on each side of a loop or expansion fitting, with the nearest guide not more than four pipe diameters away.
  • Anchors. An anchor stops all movement at one point, so growth is forced to go toward the loop or joint you planned for. The guideline says to place anchors so the pipe stress stays within the limits the piping code allows and so loads do not pass to connected equipment. The boiler, a pump or a heat exchanger connection is not an anchor.
  • Guides. A guide holds the pipe on its line while letting it slide lengthwise. Without guides a hot pipe can buckle sideways instead of growing along its axis.
  • Sleeves and oversized holes. Where pipe passes through framing, a hole drilled larger than the pipe, or a sleeve lined with insulation or a soft liner, lets the pipe slide without ticking against wood. Keep the hole large enough for pipe plus insulation, and do not notch or weaken joists beyond what the local building code and the framing designer allow.
  • Hangers. Use hangers that support the pipe without gripping it. A hanger clamped hard to copper turns into an accidental anchor. Plastic isolating sleeves or insulation at the contact point quiet the squeal.

Long risers in apartment buildings

A tall riser carries the same growth per foot, over many floors. A steel riser 100 feet tall, heated by 120 degrees Fahrenheit, grows about 0.94 inch by our example, and if the riser is held at each floor it has nowhere to go. Building designers use floor-by-floor guides, one anchor point near the middle, and flexible connections to each unit so the movement is split and absorbed. If you service an older building where the connections to each radiator were rigid, a ticking or cracking tee can be a sign of that movement. See our apartment hot-water lesson for riser balancing.

What changes with lower water temperatures

Growth follows the temperature change, so a system that runs cooler moves less. Rerun our example with a 120 degree supply and the same 60 degree fill. The change is 60 degrees Fahrenheit, half as much. Copper grows 1,200 x 0.0000099 x 60 = 0.71 inch, and steel 1,200 x 0.0000065 x 60 = 0.47 inch. For PEX, using the 100 degree table value of 1.00 ten-thousandth, growth = 1,200 x 0.0001 x 60 = 7.2 inches. Lower temperature heating, such as outdoor-reset operation or heat pump supply, cuts the movement, and often the noise, but it does not remove the need for clearance, because the pipe still cycles from cold fill to warm. See our lesson on emitter output versus water temperature for what lower water means for heat output.

A short field routine for ticking pipe

  1. Listen as the system heats and note where the sound starts, then trace the pipe to a joist hole, hanger or strap.
  2. Check for pipe touching wood, a tight strap, a tight sleeve or a fitting pressed against framing.
  3. Look for places where the pipe cannot move because it is held at both ends with no offset, loop or flexible leg.
  4. Free the contact with an oversized hole, a larger sleeve, insulation or a loose hanger, keeping access and fire stopping requirements in mind.
  5. Do not cut or drill structure on your own judgment. Ask the building owner or a qualified person if framing is affected.

Field card: Thermal expansion of hot-water heating pipe

Growth = length x coefficient x temperature change.

  • Our 100 ft example, 60 to 180 degrees F: copper about 1.4 in, steel about 0.94 in, PEX about 14 to 16 in.
  • Halve the temperature change and the growth roughly halves.
  • Ticking is pipe sticking, then slipping, on wood, straps or tight holes.
  • Give pipe room: offsets, loops, oversized holes or sleeves, loose hangers.
  • Anchors push growth toward the loop; guides keep the pipe on line.
  • PEX needs the most room. Follow the maker's instructions.
