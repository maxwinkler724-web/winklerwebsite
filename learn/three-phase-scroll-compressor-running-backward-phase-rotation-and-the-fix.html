Three-Phase Scroll Compressor Running Backward: Phase Rotation and the Fix

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A new three-phase rooftop unit starts, runs loud, and never cools. The gauges look strange and the amp reading is low. Nothing is broken in the refrigerant circuit. The compressor is spinning the wrong way, because two of the three power wires are in the wrong order. This lesson shows how to recognize that, prove it with a meter, and fix it safely.

Why a scroll can run backward

A single-phase motor only runs one direction. A three-phase motor does not care: it turns whichever way the order of the three phases tells it to. Danfoss explains that its scroll compressors compress gas only while turning in one direction, counter-clockwise when viewed from the top, so the order of the supply phases decides whether the compressor works at all. Copeland puts the odds bluntly in its scroll bulletin: wiring a three-phase compressor is a 50/50 chance of backward, which is why it asks installers to put instructions on the equipment.

Trane says the compressor motor is wired for correct rotation when the supply is phased A, B, C. If the building supply arrives in a different order, the unit runs backward. Typical triggers are a brand-new install, a utility or electrician working on the service, a replaced breaker or panel, or a generator hookup. One chiller maker notes the same thing: after startup, reversal can happen whenever any two supply legs get swapped, such as after a breaker or distribution panel is replaced.

What it looks and sounds like

  • Pressures. Copeland says correct rotation shows discharge pressure rising and suction pressure falling. Carrier says the same, and adds that in reverse the suction pressure does not drop and the discharge pressure does not rise to normal. Danfoss lists no pressure differential between suction and discharge, with a suction line that warms instead of cooling. Trane says suction and discharge are roughly equal.
  • Noise. Copeland says the sound level is above that of correct rotation. Trane adds more vibration, and Carrier reports an elevated noise level.
  • Amps. Copeland describes substantially reduced current draw compared to tabulated values. Trane says about 50 percent below normal rated current. A reversed compressor is not compressing, so it is not doing much work.
  • No cooling. Carrier says the unit provides no heating or cooling.
  • Protector trips. Copeland says the reverse running continues until the internal protector trips, after several minutes of operation. Copeland also warns the compressor can overheat.

One note on noise: Danfoss says a brief reverse-rotation sound for under 3 seconds when a scroll shuts down is normal and does not hurt reliability. The problem is reverse running while the motor is energized.

How long it can be tolerated

Treat it as "not at all." Carrier's manual says compressor damage will occur if rotation is not immediately corrected. Copeland says a backward compressor could overheat. Danfoss warns that reverse rotation can go unnoticed for long periods, which is why it recommends phase monitors on its HR and HL scrolls and requires them on its HC series. Use the numbers on your unit's data plate and manual, not a guess.

Prove it with a phase rotation meter

Do not guess from the wires. A phase rotation meter, a small tester with three leads, shows the order of the three phases. Trane tells you to confirm the sequence with a meter designed for phase rotation before the machine is started, and to open the disconnect first when connecting the meter at the unit. Danfoss likewise says to use a phase meter to establish the order, then connect L1, L2 and L3 to T1, T2 and T3. Tech tip: write down the meter result on the startup sheet so the next person knows the order was checked. For more on meters and live-work habits, see Electrical Basics for HVAC.

The fix: swap any two, with power locked out

Because the problem is only the order, swapping any two of the three leads reverses the direction. Do not swap one and then a second pair: two swaps put it back. Steps, in plain order:

  1. Shut the unit down. Carrier says to turn off power and install a lockout tag; Copeland says to disconnect and lock out the supply. Follow Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, including a test of your meter and a dead check on every leg.
  2. Swap two leads where the maker says. Carrier says to reverse any two of the unit power leads. Copeland and Daikin say to switch any two leads at the unit contactor. A chiller maker lists the breaker, the service disconnect or the unit's power connection. Read the manual for the unit in front of you; the point is to follow it, and have a qualified person do the work.
  3. Restore power, restart, and check the gauges and amps again.

Why does the location matter? On an RTU, the supply fan or blower and the condenser fans share the same incoming power. Carrier notes that when the compressor is backward, the evaporator fan is probably turning the wrong way too. If you swap only at the compressor leads, the compressor is right and the fans stay wrong. If you swap the incoming unit power leads, everything flips together. Whichever you do, check that every motor ends up turning the correct way, usually shown by an arrow on the motor or the fan housing. Carrier says plainly to be sure the fans rotate in the proper direction. Keep tidy: relabel the wires so the next tech is not surprised.

Phase monitors and reverse phase protection

A phase monitor is a small relay that watches the incoming power and keeps the contactor from closing if the order is wrong or a phase is missing. Lennox describes its A42 monitor kit this way: if the phases are out of sequence, or one is missing, the contacts never close, and a red LED shows the fault. Danfoss says the monitor chosen for its HC models should lock out the compressor from operation in reverse. A monitor will not swap wires for you, but it stops the unit from running backward for weeks before anyone notices. See RTU Refrigerant Circuit and Compressor Protection for the other protection devices.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

These readings are hypothetical, chosen to be realistic. They are not from a real job or a real data plate. A 208 volt three-phase RTU has a compressor RLA of 16.0 A. On first start the tech reads gauges and a clamp on one compressor lead.

Hypothetical readings, same unit, same day
ReadingWrong rotationCorrect rotation
Suction138 psig126 psig
Discharge146 psig345 psig
Compressor amps6.8 A13.5 A

Step 1, pressure difference. Wrong: 146 - 138 = 8 psi, basically equalized. Correct: 345 - 126 = 219 psi.

Step 2, amps against RLA. Wrong: 6.8 / 16.0 = 0.425, about 43 percent of RLA. Correct: 13.5 / 16.0 = 0.84, about 84 percent of RLA, normal for a compressor that is working below its worst case.

Step 3, compare the two. 6.8 / 13.5 = 0.50. The reversed reading is about half the correct one, which fits Trane's statement that backward current is about 50 percent below normal. Do not use a fixed percent as a pass mark; compare to the unit's own correct reading and its data plate. See Measuring Amp Draw with a Clamp Meter. Lesson: low amps on a loud compressor with equal pressures means check rotation before anything else, and before blaming charge. A low reading is also possible with other faults, so the phase meter gives the answer. If the windings need a check, see Checking Compressor Windings.

Field card: Scroll Running Backward

Loud, equal pressures, low amps: check rotation first.

  • Three-phase scrolls turn either way; only one way compresses.
  • Signs: loud, suction and discharge near equal, amps well below normal, no cooling.
  • Do not let it run: damage is possible, and the protector trips after minutes.
  • Prove the order with a phase rotation meter, power off to connect it.
  • Lock out, verify dead, swap any two leads where the unit manual says.
  • Recheck pressures and amps, and make sure every fan and blower turns the right way.
  • Consider a phase monitor so a bad order never starts the unit.
