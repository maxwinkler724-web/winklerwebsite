Surge Protective Devices for HVAC Equipment: What Surges Do to Inverter Boards and ECM Motors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump with an inverter drive, an ECM blower and a control board holds a lot of electronics, and electronics do not tolerate a short, sharp voltage spike the way an old contactor and motor did. This lesson covers where surges come from, what they do to modern HVAC boards, where surge protective devices (SPDs) go, what they cannot do, and why a few feet of wire can decide whether one works.

Check before you touch anything

This is electrician work. Installing or replacing an SPD at the service entrance, in the main panel or in a disconnect means working on live-bus equipment and following the local electrical code. This page is general education so a tech can read, explain and recommend. Follow the maker's instructions, lock out and test before you open any enclosure, and let a qualified electrician do the install.

What a surge is and where it comes from

A surge, or transient, is a very brief jump in voltage. The NIST consumer booklet Surges Happen! says surges last only a few millionths of a second, and that they come from lightning arriving by way of the wires and from switching, which happens when electrical loads are turned on or off. A NIST paper on end-user protection adds that surges also come from the utility side (capacitor switching, for example) and from inside the building, including load switching and fuses or breakers clearing a fault.

  • Lightning. The booklet cites a mid-range strike of about 20,000 amperes. It does not need to hit the house: a nearby strike can couple into wiring, and a 1997 NIST-coauthored paper notes it can also arrive through other systems, including the grounding electrode system.
  • Utility switching. Capacitor banks and other switching on the utility system send disturbances down the service. The NIST end-user paper reports capacitor switching on a 23 kV system that caused varistor failures in 480 V drives 3 km (2 miles) away.
  • Large motors cycling inside the building. New York's Clean Heat program says heat pump electronics are susceptible to surges from the grid or from household appliances. The 1997 paper names a furnace igniter and a light switch as surge generators. Those internal events are why one device at the service entrance is not the whole answer.

Why inverters, ECM motors and boards are the weak spot

New York's Clean Heat page explains that a heat pump control board converts AC to variable-frequency DC to drive the compressor, and that microprocessors manage the compressor, fans and metering valves. The page says a surge can impair or disable the system and that a damaged board cannot be repaired and must be replaced. An Oak Ridge National Laboratory field report describes the same architecture in test houses: an inverter-driven compressor outdoor unit with a control board, and indoor units with a variable-speed blower using a brushless permanent magnet motor, which is the same class of motor as an ECM. See Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors and ECM Blower Motors for how those parts work.

Our plain reading: a board full of semiconductors has a voltage it can take, and a spike above that for even a few millionths of a second can finish a part. A capacitor and contactor were far more forgiving. A surge may also leave a board working but weakened, which is why a failure can show up weeks later. The 1997 NIST paper also warns that the power port may not be the path of damage: surges can come in over control or communication wiring, so a power-side device alone is not complete protection.

Device locations: entrance, panel, disconnect

Devices are described by where they sit in the circuit. The electrical code's service-equipment rule, section 230.67, calls for a whole-home SPD on new dwelling services and when service equipment is replaced, according to New York's Clean Heat page (2023 edition; the edition your area enforces is a local question). We cite the code by number and do not reproduce it.

  • Service entrance or main panel. The NIST booklet says service-entrance protection diverts large surges coming on the power line before they enter the house. Clean Heat describes whole-home protection at the main panel as sending excess voltage to ground, and says it may not cover surges created inside the house.
  • At the equipment disconnect. Clean Heat says component protection for a heat pump typically goes at the electrical disconnect, and that some disconnect boxes have surge protection built in. Because it sits near the unit, it handles what the panel device leaves.
  • Plug-in point-of-use devices. The booklet notes these are the easiest option and need no electrician, but a hardwired condenser has no plug, so they do not apply to the outdoor unit.

The booklet answers whether a service-entrance device is enough for the whole house with "Yes for one-link appliances, No for two-link appliances." Our reading: a heat pump also has control wiring and an indoor unit, so it behaves more like the second kind, which is why two layers (panel and disconnect) are the usual advice. For storm paperwork see HVAC Damage From Storms, Floods, Freezes and Power Surges; for the homeowner view see Surge Protection for AC, Heat Pumps and Furnaces.

What an SPD does and does not do

  • It diverts, it does not destroy. The NIST booklet says a protector does not stop a surge; it diverts it to ground. The 1997 paper adds that effectiveness depends on the grounding system and its bonding.
  • No direct-strike guarantee. Clean Heat says no device available can protect against a direct lightning strike.
  • Not a fix for brownouts or sustained overvoltage. Clean Heat says SPDs are not built to handle continuous overvoltage from a poorly regulated grid or aging wiring, and notes that on a 208/230 V heat pump circuit continuous voltage above 253 V can overheat and fail boards. The NIST booklet says sags are different from surges and that the remedies are generally different. For low-voltage protection see Line Voltage Monitors and Brownout Protection.
  • Not phase protection. An SPD clamps spikes. It does not watch for a missing leg or a reversed sequence. Those need a monitor relay; see Phase Loss and Single-Phasing.
  • Not an arc-fault or ground-fault device. Clean Heat notes a GFCI breaker does not protect from line surges. See AFCI Breakers and HVAC Equipment for what those breakers do detect.

Indicator lights and replacement after a big hit

The NIST booklet says status indications are not standardized and can be confusing, and tells readers to read the instructions and keep any explanatory card. A New Mexico State University safety sheet agrees: a light shows whether the protection parts are working, but the light may be on or off in normal operation depending on the maker. The same sheet says the varistors inside wear out with use, shorten in life with each diverted surge, and fail with no forewarning, and that a unit can keep passing power as an ordinary strip after protection is gone. The NIST booklet adds that components age and that most protector failures it saw came from excessively high line voltage rather than from a single huge surge.

What to do with that: read the indicator against the maker's key, write down what it shows at every visit, and tell the customer when it shows protection lost. The sources we read give no rule for replacing a device after a known big hit, so follow the maker's instructions. After a nearby strike, we would treat the device as suspect and have the electrician check it. Photograph the indicator and the nameplate for any claim.

Grounding and short lead length

The NIST booklet puts it bluntly: the best surge protector in the world can be useless if grounding is not done properly. A NIST paper on end-user protection says an excellent SPD can be made useless by poor installation, in particular excessive lead length and the impedance of the ground connection. The booklet's one number is that a protector should sit within about 30 cm, or one foot, of the service panel or its voltage-limiting effect is degraded. The 1997 paper calls incorrect grounding the primary problem in residential surge protection and warns that separate grounds for power and communications violate the code. Bonding, electrode connections and conductor routing are for the electrician to settle.

Worked example (our own): why lead length matters

A wire has inductance, and when current changes fast, the wire drops voltage: V = L × di/dt. The 1997 NIST paper uses this same relation in an example with L = 10 µH and di/dt = 500 A/µs. We borrow that rise rate and add our own inductance figure. These are assumptions, not measurements:

  • Inductance of straight wire: 0.3 µH per foot (our assumption, about 1 µH per meter).
  • Surge current rise rate: 500 A/µs (the rate used in the NIST paper's example).
  • The SPD clamps at 600 V at its own terminals (our assumption).
  • Total lead length counts both leads: the one to the bus and the one to ground.

Step 1, voltage per foot: 0.3 µH × 500 A/µs = 150 V per foot (the units cancel because µH times A per µs is volts).

Step 2, short install, 1 ft of total lead: 150 V × 1 = 150 V added. The board sees about 600 + 150 = 750 V.

Step 3, long install, 8 ft of total lead: 150 V × 8 = 1,200 V added. The board sees about 600 + 1,200 = 1,800 V.

Step 4, compare: 1,800 ÷ 750 = 2.4, so with these assumptions the long install lets through about 2.4 times the voltage even though the device is the same. Real wiring and surges vary, so treat this as the idea, not a prediction. It is also why a device at the disconnect should be wired with the shortest, straightest leads the maker allows.

What a tech does and does not do

Do: look for a device at the panel and at the disconnect, read and record its indicator, explain what an SPD can and cannot do, and refer a customer to an electrician. Do not: open the service panel, add a device to a live circuit or lengthen leads. Check voltage when boards keep failing, since a steady high reading is not a surge problem.

Field card: Surge protective devices

Two layers, short leads, good ground, and read the light.

  • Sources: lightning, utility switching, motors and loads inside the house.
  • Inverter boards, ECM motors and control boards are the parts at risk.
  • Panel device: incoming surges. Disconnect device: surges made inside.
  • An SPD diverts surge current to ground. It does not fix brownouts, overvoltage or phase loss.
  • No device protects against a direct strike.
  • Indicator meanings differ by maker; read the key and record it.
  • Rough example: 0.3 µH/ft at 500 A/µs is about 150 V per foot of lead (our own assumption).
  • Install and replacement are electrician work.
