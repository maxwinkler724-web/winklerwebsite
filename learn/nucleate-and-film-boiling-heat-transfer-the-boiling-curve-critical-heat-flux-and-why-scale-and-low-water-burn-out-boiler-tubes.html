Nucleate and Film Boiling: The Boiling Curve, Critical Heat Flux, and Why Scale and Low Water Burn Out Tubes

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A pot of water on a hot burner boils gently. Turn the burner up and it boils harder, until one day the surface gets so hot that the water cannot keep up. That last step is the one that ruins boiler tubes. This lesson walks through the whole boiling curve and shows why scale and low water push a tube toward it.

The boiling curve in one picture

Engineers plot how much heat a hot surface passes to a pool of liquid (heat flux, in Btu/h-ft²) against how much hotter the surface is than the liquid's boiling temperature (the excess temperature). The result is the boiling curve, first measured by Nukiyama in 1934 according to a university lecture on two-phase heat transfer. It has four regions after the quiet start, and the shape is the whole story.

Region by region

Natural convection. With the surface only a little hotter than the liquid, no bubbles form. Warm liquid rises off the surface and cooler liquid replaces it. Heat flux is low and climbs slowly. A university lecture notes that boiling begins at the onset of nucleate boiling, with a partial boiling zone where natural convection and bubbles share the work.

Nucleate boiling. Bubbles form at tiny spots on the metal, grow, and break away. Each departing bubble pulls cooler liquid back onto the surface, and the bubbles carry latent heat away (see sensible vs latent heat). The result is a very high heat transfer rate for a small temperature difference. For water at atmospheric pressure, one university lecture gives the nucleate range as an excess temperature of about 5 °C to 30 °C (9 °F to 54 °F, our conversion of the temperature difference). That is a narrow, forgiving window: the wall only has to run a few tens of degrees above the water to move a lot of heat.

Critical heat flux (CHF). This is the peak of the curve and the end of nucleate boiling. The same lecture describes it as the end of unbroken contact between liquid and surface; push the flux past it and parts of the surface dry out. A worked example in the other lecture, for water on a large horizontal surface at atmospheric pressure, gives a peak of about 1,260,968 W/m², reached at an excess temperature of roughly 20.4 °C. Converting with 1 W/m² = 0.317 Btu/h-ft² (our conversion), that is about 400,000 Btu/h-ft². Treat it as a textbook order of magnitude, not a design limit for any boiler.

Transition boiling. Past the peak, the surface is dry part of the time and wet part of the time. More wall temperature now means less heat flux, because more of the surface is covered in insulating vapor. This region is unstable. The university lecture notes that Nukiyama could follow this part of the curve only by controlling the wall temperature; when the heat input is fixed instead, the system cannot ride the curve down and simply jumps.

Film boiling. At the minimum film boiling point the surface is covered by a continuous vapor blanket with no direct contact between liquid and metal. Vapor conducts heat poorly, so the wall must get very hot before much heat crosses the film. A physics article on the Leidenfrost effect shows the longest-lived water drops on a plate at about 200 °C, the same vapor-cushion effect that lets drops skate across a hot pan.

Why a heat-flux-controlled tube burns out

A burner or furnace does not control tube wall temperature. It delivers a heat input, and the tube wall finds whatever temperature it needs to pass that heat to the water. That is called heat-flux control. If the flux stays under CHF, the wall settles in nucleate boiling at a modest excess temperature. If something pushes the flux above CHF, the tube cannot follow the descending transition branch. It jumps to the film boiling branch, where the wall temperature needed to pass the same heat is far higher. That sudden climb is called burnout. Steel that was comfortable at its nucleate boiling temperature can soften and bulge or rupture at the film boiling temperature.

This is why steam boilers, evaporators and water-cooled heat exchangers are designed to live in the nucleate region: the heat transfer coefficient is high, the wall stays near the water temperature, and there is margin below the peak. Refrigerant evaporators work the same way, as covered in boiling and condensing heat transfer in evaporators and condensers.

How scale and low water cause overheating

Scale. Calcium, magnesium and silica in the water can deposit on the waterside of the tube as a hard layer. The U.S. Department of Energy tip sheet on firetube boilers says scale typically has a thermal conductivity an order of magnitude lower than bare steel. Because the scale sits between the metal and the water, the metal must run hotter to push the same heat through. Heat crosses the scale by conduction, and for a flat layer the rule is simple: temperature drop equals heat flux times thickness divided by conductivity. The same tip sheet says even thin scale slows heat transfer and can overheat tube metal and cause tube failures. Scale also wastes fuel: for a boiler without stack heat recovery, it lists about 2.0 percent more fuel for 1/32 inch of normal scale. For the noise side of the story, see boiler kettling and scale.

Low water. If the water level drops and a tube section is no longer covered, steam instead of liquid touches the inside of that tube. There is no nucleate boiling left to cool the metal, so the tube behaves like the film boiling or dry case: the metal temperature climbs toward the flame-side temperature. The same fire that boiling water absorbed easily now heats steel with almost nothing carrying the heat away. That is why the low-water cutoff is a critical safety device; see low-water cutoff testing and maintenance.

Worked example: tube metal temperature with and without scale (our own example)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption unless a source is named.

  • Heat flux through the tube wall: q = 20,000 Btu/h-ft² (assumed round number; about 63 kW/m², our conversion, far below the textbook CHF of about 400,000 Btu/h-ft² above, so the waterside is in nucleate boiling).
  • Clean carbon steel wall: thickness 0.25 in = 0.25 / 12 = 0.02083 ft; conductivity k = 25 Btu/h-ft-°F (assumed typical value for carbon steel; see the conductivity lesson).
  • Scale layer: thickness 1/32 in = 0.03125 / 12 = 0.002604 ft; k = 0.2 Btu/h-ft-°F, the value a national-lab report gives for porous calcium carbonate scale (that report studied cooling-tower scale, so we borrow it as a low-end case). A denser scale could be nearer 1 Btu/h-ft-°F, which we also show (assumed).
  • Water-side surface temperature in boiling: 400 °F (assumed).

Step 1, the steel wall alone: ΔT = q × t / k = 20,000 × 0.02083 / 25 = 16.7 °F across the steel.

Step 2, the scale layer at k = 0.2: ΔT = 20,000 × 0.002604 / 0.2 = 260.4 °F.

Step 2b, the scale layer at k = 1.0: ΔT = 20,000 × 0.002604 / 1.0 = 52.1 °F.

Step 3, add them up. Clean, the hot-side metal surface is 400 + 16.7 = about 417 °F. With the k = 0.2 scale it is 400 + 260.4 + 16.7 = about 677 °F. With the k = 1.0 scale it is 400 + 52.1 + 16.7 = about 469 °F.

So one thirty-second of an inch of scale adds about 260 °F at the low conductivity (about 52 °F at the higher one), compared with the 16.7 °F that a quarter inch of clean steel adds. The scale is about one eighth as thick as the wall (0.03125 / 0.25) yet adds about 16 times the temperature drop in the low-conductivity case (260.4 / 16.7 = 15.6). Why it matters: steel gets weaker as it gets hotter, and the tube is under internal pressure the whole time. A tube that was comfortably stressed at 417 °F may be much weaker at 677 °F, and the weakest spot is under the thickest scale. This assumes the same heat flux, which is what a fired boiler delivers. Real heat flux and scale conductivity vary, so use the example to see the trend, not to predict a failure.

What a tech should take from this

  • Boilers and evaporators work best in nucleate boiling: high heat transfer coefficient, low wall excess temperature.
  • The danger is the jump from the nucleate peak (CHF) to film boiling when heat flux is fixed. It is fast and does not reverse on its own.
  • Scale is an insulating layer. A small thickness adds a large temperature rise at a given flux. Keep makeup water treated and tubes clean (see fire-tube and water-tube boilers for what inspectors look at).
  • Low water removes the liquid cooling entirely. Never bypass or defeat the cutoff.

Field card: Nucleate and Film Boiling

Stay in nucleate boiling; scale and low water push you out.

  • Boiling curve order: natural convection, nucleate, critical heat flux (peak), transition, film.
  • Nucleate boiling moves a lot of heat for a small wall temperature rise.
  • Past CHF with fixed heat input, the wall jumps to film boiling and overheats.
  • Scale: ΔT = q × thickness / k. Scale k is far below steel.
  • Low water leaves steam against the tube and removes the cooling.
  • Our example: 1/32 in scale at k = 0.2 and 20,000 Btu/h-ft² adds about 260 °F.
