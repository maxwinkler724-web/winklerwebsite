Cabinet Unit Heaters in Vestibules and Stairwells: Types, Fan Control, Freeze Risk and Service Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cabinet unit heater is a small fan and coil behind a steel front, tucked into a wall at an entry vestibule or stair landing. It looks like a grille, but it is the only thing between a cold door and a frozen pipe. This lesson covers types, fan control, freeze risk and service checks.

What it is and where it goes

A cabinet unit heater has a casing, a filter, a small fan and a finned coil or electric element. Room air enters the inlet grille, passes the filter and fan, crosses the heat source and leaves through the discharge grille. One maker's manual describes a floor-mounted pedestal style, plus a wall or ceiling style that can be partly or fully recessed. A federal guide specification describes vertical units with an exposed front panel and horizontal units with an exposed bottom panel, in sheet steel at least 0.053 inch thick. A university specification lists recessed or semi-recessed mounting as an option that adds steel recessing flanges.

Recessed units suit vestibules, stair landings and corridor ends because they keep the footprint small. They are spot heaters, not a building heating system.

Hot water, steam and electric

  • Hot water. A university specification calls for a copper-tube, aluminum-fin coil rated for at least 200 psig and a maximum entering water temperature of 220 F, with a manual air vent and drain. A second university specification expects an isolation valve and a balancing valve at each unit.
  • Steam. One maker limits its cabinet units to 10 psig and says steam is allowed only on its one-row coils. A university specification asks for a union, strainer and trap on the steam connection. For trap and stall problems, see the warehouse unit heater lesson.
  • Electric. The same federal specification describes electric-resistance coils with fuses and limit controls for high-temperature protection. Electric units have no water to freeze, so they are common where piping cannot reach.

Fan control: so it does not blow cold

A hydronic unit that starts its fan with a cold coil blows cold air at the people in the doorway. Three controls fix that.

  • Aquastat. One maker's manual describes an aquastat strapped to the return pipe that keeps the fan off when no heat is being supplied. See boiler aquastats and cutoffs. A loose strap can make the fan start late or never.
  • Fan delay on electric units. A distributor-hosted spec for an electric recessed unit describes a bimetal snap-action fan delay, plus a "comfort start" that holds the fan off until the element is warm and keeps it running on residual heat after the element cycles off.
  • Room thermostat or BAS. A university specification calls for a wall thermostat with heat-off and fan-auto switches; with a DDC controller the fan runs continuously when occupied and cycles to hold setback when unoccupied. A public sequence-of-operation article describes a 55 F heating setpoint where the fan starts and the hot water valve opens below it, and warns that building automation alarms in freezing weather should not be ignored.

Why vestibule coils freeze

A vestibule has an outside door that opens all day. Each opening dumps outdoor air onto the unit's inlet and coil. If the control valve has closed because the thermostat is satisfied, water in the coil sits still and can freeze, split the tubes, and flood the wall when it thaws.

The protection comes in layers. First, keep water moving: a federal-lab technical bulletin on air handler preheat coils requires circulation pumps so water keeps flowing at reduced load, and uses a glycol mix. Second, a low-limit freezestat. That bulletin describes a typical trip near 37 F and a manual reset, and a trade article gives 38 F, adding that on a trip the hot water valve goes fully open and the fan stops. These are air handler practices; the same idea applies to a vestibule unit. Third, keep the isolation valves open and the heating water on all winter. Fourth, glycol where freezing is possible. Compare freezestats on air handlers and preheat coils for details.

An air curtain or a tighter door closer cuts the infiltration.

Energy code limits on vestibule heat

Some states cap vestibule heating. The California Energy Commission page on nonresidential vestibules says heated vestibules need a thermostat inside the vestibule that limits heating to 60 F or less, and controls that shut off heating when the outdoor air is above 45 F. Minnesota's rule, in the version we read, limits vestibule thermostats to 68 F and shuts off the heat source above 60 F outdoors. These values differ by state and code year, so confirm the adopted code.

Worked example: heat for one vestibule

Use the sensible heat formula, Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT. A trade article derives 1.08 from 60 min/h x 0.075 lb/ft3 x 0.24 Btu/lb-F and says it fits air near 70 F.

  • Assumption (our own): average door infiltration of 300 cfm during busy periods.
  • Assumption (our own): outdoor air at 10 F, vestibule setpoint 55 F, so dT = 55 - 10 = 45 F.
  • Assumption (our own): selected unit puts out 20,000 Btu/h at the job's actual water temperature and entering air.

Step 1: 1.08 x 300 x 45 = 14,580 Btu/h.
Step 2: 20,000 / 14,580 = about 1.37, so the unit covers the infiltration load with about 37 percent to spare. That spare has to cover wall and glass loss, which we did not calculate here.
Step 3: with a busier door at 600 cfm (our own assumption), 1.08 x 600 x 45 = 29,160 Btu/h. The same unit falls short by 9,160 Btu/h and the vestibule drifts below 55 F.

Rated output drops when the water is cooler than the rating condition, so read the maker's table at your water temperature.

Filter and fan maintenance

  • A university specification calls for pleated filters with a MERV 7 rating. Another lists removable 25 mm (1 inch) throwaway fiberglass filters.
  • One maker's manual describes permanent cleanable aluminum filters and says to clean them every three months, sooner in dusty places.
  • The same manual says to clean the coil at least once a year, to avoid solvents and liquid or steam sprays, and to blow out open-frame motors each heating season.

Common complaints

  • Blows cold. Check supply water temperature, whether the valve opens, a loose or misplaced aquastat strap, air in the coil (the university specs call for an air vent), and a clogged filter. On electric units, check the fan delay and limit.
  • Noisy. Loose casing panels, a dirty fan wheel, dry motor bearings, or ticking piping.
  • Frozen or burst coil. Look for a closed isolation valve, a stuck valve, a dead circulation pump, a failed thermostat, or a door left propped open. Check that the freezestat and BAS alarm work before you restore heat.

Field card: Cabinet unit heaters in vestibules

Keep water moving, keep the valve able to open, keep the filter clean.

  • Aquastat on the return holds the fan until the coil is warm.
  • Freezestat: typical trip 37 to 38 F; hot water valve goes open, fan stops, manual reset.
  • Heat load: Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x dT.
  • Confirm your state's vestibule heating cap before you change a setpoint.
