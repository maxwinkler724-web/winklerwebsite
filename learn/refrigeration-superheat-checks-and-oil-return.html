Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two quiet ideas protect a refrigeration compressor: the gas coming home should be warm enough that no liquid arrives, and the oil that left with the gas should come back with it. Superheat covers the first. Line design covers the second.

Superheat in one sentence

Superheat is how far suction gas has been warmed above its boiling point. An ACHR News explainer walks the measurement in four steps: read suction pressure, convert it to the refrigerant's boiling temperature, clamp a probe on the suction line near the gauge, and subtract. In its R-22 example, a line reading of 75 degrees F at a pressure that boils at about 7 degrees F gives 68 degrees of superheat. That example happened to come from a badly undercharged air conditioner, and it shows the pattern: high superheat means the coil is starved, low superheat means too much liquid is arriving.

Commercial TXV systems

A trade article on adjusting valves in market refrigeration gives a goal of six to twelve degrees of superheat for a case TXV. It also makes a point that applies to every system: the valve is the last thing you adjust. Check the other causes of a warm case or floodback first, including a dirty inlet screen, a failed power head, ice on the coil and bad airflow. The article says the valve itself rarely fails. If the valve reads zero superheat, it describes resetting it by turning to the closed end and backing out a brand-specific number of turns, then waiting about fifteen minutes before measuring again. A glass-door freezer manual we read makes the same point in different words: adjust no more than half a turn at a time, and wait fifteen minutes between checks. Numbers like these are examples from those sources, so use the valve maker's data for yours.

Measuring well

  • Use a calibrated probe with the clamp on clean bare tubing, insulated from room air.
  • Place it near the pressure reading point so both readings describe the same spot.
  • Measure with the case or box in a steady operating state, not just after a defrost.
  • Remember that suction line temperature rises as the line runs away from the evaporator. A suction line article from the same publisher notes that heat pickup along the line makes the compressor-inlet superheat higher than the evaporator-outlet superheat.

The same source also mentions that compressors are more likely to overheat as suction line temperature climbs above about 65 degrees F, and that some manufacturers set that as a limit. Read your compressor maker's limit rather than relying on one figure.

Why oil return matters

Oil leaves the compressor with the discharge gas. An ACHR News article on oil return explains that oil and refrigerant mix well as liquids but poorly as vapors, so the oil depends on gas velocity to sweep it back. If it stays in the system, the compressor can run short of lubrication, and oil pooling in the evaporator reduces heat transfer. Cold oil is thick, so low-temperature systems have a harder job.

Velocity, slope, traps and risers

The article gives design targets as examples: roughly 700 feet per minute in horizontal suction lines and 1,500 in vertical ones, with horizontal runs pitched toward the compressor. It describes P-traps at the foot of a vertical riser when the compressor sits well above the evaporator, with more traps for tall risers, kept shallow to limit oil buildup. A compressor manufacturer's technical page gives metric guideline velocities, 4 meters per second horizontal and 8 meters per second vertical, and adds that rising lines often need two parallel sections because at part load an oil column can close one pipe, leaving the other to carry the gas at a high enough velocity. That page also says some widely branched medium and low temperature systems use an added oil separator. These are design questions for the system designer, but you should recognize them when a rack runs short on oil or a trap fills.

Low load and field clues

At part load, gas velocity drops, so an oversized suction line that works at full load can fail at low load. Signs of oil trouble include low oil level at the compressor, oil staining at joints that is not a leak, and a compressor that trips on oil pressure. Fix the cause: velocity, trap geometry, defrost recovery or a stuck valve. Do not just add oil. Related reading: Superheat and Subcooling Basics and Undercharge vs. Overcharge Symptom Table.

Field card: Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return

Warm gas home, oil home, valve last.

  • Superheat is suction line temperature minus the boiling temperature for suction pressure.
  • High superheat points to a starved coil; low points to flooding.
  • Check screens, ice, airflow and charge before adjusting the TXV.
  • Oil needs gas velocity to come home; low load makes it hardest.
  • Traps and double risers are design details; use the maker and designer data.
