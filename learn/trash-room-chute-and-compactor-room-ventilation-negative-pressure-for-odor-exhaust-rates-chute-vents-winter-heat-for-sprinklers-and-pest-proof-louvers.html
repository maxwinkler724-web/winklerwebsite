Trash Room, Chute and Compactor Room Ventilation: Negative Pressure for Odor, Exhaust Rates, Chute Vents, Winter Heat for Sprinklers and Pest-Proof Louvers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The trash room is the room nobody designs carefully and everybody notices when it goes wrong. In an apartment or commercial building, a chute drops bags from every floor into a dumpster or compactor, and whatever smell is down there wants to travel up the shaft or out into the corridor. The HVAC job is simple to say: pull the room slightly negative so air flows in and odor stays put. This lesson covers how that is done, how much exhaust to expect, and the winter, summer and pest problems that show up later.

Why the room must stay negative

A state-funded research guide to multifamily ventilation, prepared for the Minnesota Department of Commerce, describes a trash chute as a vertical shaft, typically about 2 feet in diameter, with a door on each floor and a dumpster or compactor at the bottom. It says the best exhaust arrangement creates negative pressure in the trash room relative to the adjoining building area. It also warns that dumpster rooms not isolated from building air can cause odor problems and excessive chute exhaust flows, and that on upper floors, any penetration into a disposal room can let odor through. The same guide notes that dumpster room doors are often propped open for convenience, which defeats the whole idea. Federal medical-center design criteria from the Department of Veterans Affairs, read on a third-party mirror, say the same thing in plain terms: exhaust air, hold the room negative, and run the system continuously.

How much exhaust

No single number covers every building, so we show what the sources give. The Minnesota guide lists trash room exhaust as 1 cfm per square foot of room area, continuous. It also says that rate is not in the 2015 Minnesota mechanical code and was taken from a national ventilation standard, so treat it as a design practice, not as a rule that applies everywhere. The older VA criteria ask for a minimum of 10 air changes per hour of exhaust air with negative pressure. We did not find a trash room figure in a model code that we could read and cite. The International Mechanical Code and the International Building Code are the model codes most states start from, but your state or city amends them, so ask the local code office what applies.

The two numbers do not land in the same place. In a room with a 9 foot ceiling, each square foot of floor has 9 cubic feet of air above it, so 10 air changes per hour is 9 × 10 ÷ 60, which is 1.5 cfm per square foot. So the VA figure is a bit higher than the Minnesota one. Pick the higher number when odor is a known complaint, and size the fan for the real duct and louver pressure loss, not a catalog free-air rating.

The chute vent at the roof

The Minnesota guide says the chute extends to the roof and ends in a hooded termination, so stack effect and wind can pull air up the chute without a fan. The size of that rooftop opening sets how much air leaves. Too large and it draws a lot of conditioned air out of the building. Too small or blocked and it may not draw enough, so trash odors spread into the building. The guide calls adjusting the opening a low-cost energy saving technique. In the field, that means checking the hood is open, screened as the code allows, and not choked with leaves or nests, and checking that the roof opening and the room exhaust are not fighting each other.

The guide also says the chute outlet at the compactor affects how much air escapes through the chute, and that where the room cannot be isolated, the outlet can be sealed to the compactor top with sheet metal plenums. See the figure.

Transfer air and door undercuts

Every cfm the fan removes must come from somewhere. If nothing is provided, the fan pulls through gaps you did not plan, sometimes backward down the chute. The usual plan is transfer air: a door undercut or a transfer grille in the wall or door that lets air from the adjoining space flow in. There is a catch. The VA criteria say make-up air may come from building air, but that for a fully enclosed trash room, corridor air may not be used as make-up air. Whether your project may borrow corridor air is a design and code question, so confirm it on the drawings. Whatever path is used, a rated door may have limits on gap size or on added grilles, so check the door listing before cutting anything. Our own rule of thumb: the transfer path should be sized so the air moves slowly, so it does not whistle or make the door hard to open.

Winter heat and sprinkler piping

A continuous exhaust fan in January pulls cold air in. If the room has sprinkler piping and the heat is off or undersized, the pipe can freeze and split. The VA criteria call for thermostat-controlled heat to hold a trash collection area at 50°F in winter. A Texas State Fire Marshal's Office notice, written about dry sprinklers on wet systems, says the interior temperature must stay at or above the design value and never below 40°F, and it refers to the sprinkler installation standard by section. We read that as a floor, not a target. Keep pipe and heads away from the louver, damper and door gap where the coldest air enters, and test the thermostat in the fall. A frozen head in a trash room also means a flooded room and a fire watch.

Summer heat and odor

Heat speeds decay. A Cornell composting fact sheet says decomposition runs fastest between 90°F and 140°F and drops off noticeably below 90°F. That page is about compost, not trash rooms, so we use it only to show the trend: a warm closed room breeds odor faster than a cool one. We do not have a sourced trash room temperature limit. In summer, check that the fan is actually running, the damper opens fully, and the room is not baking from a nearby mechanical room or a sunny wall. Cooling the room is rarely worth it; moving the air and cleaning the floor and chute are.

Rodents and pests at louvers

An exhaust louver or a gap under a door is also an entry for mice and rats. The CDC says a mouse can fit through a hole 1/4 inch across. A National Park Service rodent exclusion manual says rats can enter a hole larger than 1/2 inch, recommends closing gaps of 1/4 inch or larger, and recommends 1/4 inch hardware cloth, 16 to 19 gauge, over vents, plus tight door sweeps and checking closed doors for daylight. The EPA's page for housing managers says to keep common areas clean and sanitary, especially trash chutes and dumpsters, and to store dumpsters on concrete or asphalt and never within three feet of a building wall. For louvers, our own caution is that fine mesh clogs with lint, leaves and grease. Pick a screen the fan can pull through, and clean it on a schedule. See Outdoor Air Intake Louvers for free area.

Cleaning and maintenance

  • Fan: check rotation, belt or motor, and blade buildup. Measure the flow now and then, because the Minnesota guide tells assessors to verify exhaust flow rather than assume it.
  • Backdraft damper: trash room air carries grease and sticky residue. A damper that sticks shut starves the room and one stuck open leaks odor backward. Free the blades and check the stop.
  • Screen and louver: clear debris and look for chewed or bent mesh.
  • Doors: closers working, latches holding, no wedges. A door that never closes cancels the fan.
  • Controls: the fan should run continuously. If there is a switch or timer, find out why.

Fire dampers, where a duct crosses a rated wall or floor, need their own access and inspection. See Fire Dampers and Smoke Dampers.

Fire-rated chute construction, in general terms

We keep this general because it varies by location. Model building codes treat a refuse chute as a shaft that runs through floors, so the shaft walls are fire-rated, the doors on each floor are rated and self-closing, and sprinklers are placed at the top of the chute and in the discharge room. A New York City buildings bulletin we read says an approved automatic sprinkler system goes at the top of gravity chutes and in chute access and termination rooms; that is one city's rule, not a national one. As the person on the HVAC side, do not cut openings in a chute shaft or its rated walls without approval, and use rated products wherever you penetrate a rated wall.

Our own worked example: fan size and door undercut

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design for any real building.

  • Room: 10 ft by 12 ft, 9 ft ceiling (assumed).
  • Air-change rate: 10 per hour (assumed, picked to match the VA figure above).
  • Velocity through the transfer opening: 400 fpm (assumed, our own choice for a gentle flow).

Volume: 10 × 12 × 9 = 1,080 cubic feet. Exhaust: 1,080 × 10 ÷ 60 = 180 cfm. As a check, 1 cfm per square foot of 120 square feet is only 120 cfm, so the air-change method is higher.

Free area: 180 cfm ÷ 400 fpm = 0.45 square feet, and 0.45 × 144 = 65 square inches.

Door undercut: a 36 inch door needs 65 ÷ 36 = about 1.8 inches of gap. That is too big for most doors, and a rated door may limit the gap. A 3/4 inch undercut gives 36 × 0.75 = 27 square inches, which is 0.19 square feet, so the velocity at 180 cfm would be 180 ÷ 0.19 = about 960 fpm. So we would add a transfer grille with about 65 square inches of free area, for example a 10 by 12 inch grille at 60 percent free area gives 72 square inches. In real life leakage through the chute and cracks takes part of the flow, so the final numbers need field measurement.

Field card: Trash room and chute ventilation

Pull the room negative, keep the pipe warm, keep the pests out.

  • Exhaust continuously. Sources give 1 cfm per sq ft or 10 air changes per hour; confirm the local number.
  • Room negative to the corridor. Doors closed and not propped.
  • Roof chute hood: open, not choked, not oversized.
  • Transfer air through a grille or undercut sized for low velocity: cfm ÷ fpm = sq ft.
  • Winter heat: 50°F target in the VA criteria; never below 40°F for sprinkler fittings in the Texas notice.
  • Pests: 1/4 inch hardware cloth, tight door sweeps.
  • Clean screen, free the damper, measure flow.
  • Our example: 10 × 12 × 9 ft at 10 ACH is 180 cfm, about 65 sq in of transfer opening at 400 fpm.
