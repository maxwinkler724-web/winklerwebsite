Air-Handler Filter Racks and Bypass: Gaps, Gaskets, Holding Frames and Gauges

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A filter only cleans the air that goes through it. In a commercial air handler, air takes the easiest path, and a loose rack, a missing blank or a worn gasket gives it a path around the media. This lesson covers how filter racks are built, where bypass happens, how a differential pressure gauge tells you when to change filters, and what a bigger MERV number does to the fan.

How filter racks are built

Two layouts show up most. A side-access housing is a welded steel box with filters sliding in from the side through hinged doors. A university facilities design guideline we read describes these with replaceable pile seals on the downstream flange, so the filter's own pressure drop presses it against the seal, plus door latches and gasketed doors. A front-loading holding frame is a steel frame, built up into a bank, where filters are pushed in from the upstream side and held by spring clips. The same guideline calls for foam gaskets on those frames, at least four tool-free clips per frame, and caulked joints where field-assembled frames meet the plenum.

Many banks run two stages. A thin prefilter (the guideline's rack channels take 2-inch panel prefilters) catches lint and large dust, and a deeper final filter behind it does the finer work and lasts longer because the prefilter shields it. Changing the cheap stage on schedule is the usual way to protect the expensive one.

Where bypass happens

CDC/NIOSH guidance on filtration defines bypass as air that goes around the filter instead of through it. It lists poorly fitting filters, poor sealing in the frames, missing panels, and leaks between the filter bank and the blower as causes. It also says that upgrading filter efficiency without fixing bypass gives little if any benefit. Common field findings:

  • Missing blanks. A rack sized for a full grid but loaded with fewer filters, or a gap where a half-size filter should sit.
  • Wrong or undersized filters. Nominal size is not actual size. See the filter fit lesson linked below.
  • Dead or missing gaskets and clips. NIOSH says clamping alone may not make an airtight seal, and that deteriorating or distorted gaskets should be replaced and checked for leaks.
  • Held only by suction. NIOSH suggests asking whether filters are held only by negative pressure from the downstream fan or sealed against the rack.
  • Leaks downstream of the bank, such as a loose access panel between the filters and the blower.
  • Bent or damaged frames, especially heavy metal-framed cells that need proper support.

The same NIOSH document says a light behind a filter can show tears or voids, and that a particle counter or aerosol photometer is the better leak test.

How much air does a small gap pass?

More than it looks like, because the pressure across a filter is the push that drives air through any opening beside it. Air through a plain opening moves at roughly 4005 times the square root of the pressure difference in inches of water, in feet per minute, times a discharge factor. The worked example below uses that relation.

Worked example: a gap beside one filter

Assumptions (labeled, not measured):

  • One 24 x 24 inch filter, 2000 cfm through the bank, so face velocity is 2000 / 4 ft² = 500 fpm. (Our own setup.)
  • Resistance values borrowed from a university guideline's example of a 2-inch MERV 8 filter: initial 0.30 in. w.g. and final 1.0 in. w.g. at 500 fpm. Those are that guideline's limits, not a rule for every filter.
  • Discharge factor 0.6 for a rough slot (Our own assumption).
  • We ignore that bypass lowers the pressure a little and shifts flow. This is a rough estimate, not a measurement.

Case A: a 1/8 inch gap along one 24 inch edge.

  1. Area: 24 in x 0.125 in = 3.0 in² = 0.0208 ft².
  2. At 0.30 in. w.g.: 4005 x √0.30 = 2194 fpm, x 0.6 = 1316 fpm. Flow: 1316 x 0.0208 = 27 cfm, about 1.4 percent of 2000.
  3. At 1.0 in. w.g.: 4005 x 1 = 4005, x 0.6 = 2403 fpm. Flow: 2403 x 0.0208 = 50 cfm, about 2.5 percent.

Case B: a 1/4 inch gap all the way around the frame.

  1. Area: perimeter 96 in x 0.25 in = 24 in² = 0.167 ft².
  2. Clean: 1316 x 0.167 = 219 cfm, about 11 percent.
  3. Loaded: 2403 x 0.167 = 400 cfm, about 20 percent.

What it does to cleaning. Say the media captures 90 percent of a certain particle (an assumed figure). With 20 percent of the air bypassing, only about 80 percent is filtered, so the overall result is 0.80 x 0.90 = 72 percent. The gap costs 18 points, and it gets worse as the filter loads, because a dirtier filter raises the pressure that pushes air through the gap. A bypass can also hold the gauge reading lower than it should be (our own reasoning, since the leaking air carries less resistance). Fix the gaps first. That is the same point NIOSH makes about upgrading without sealing.

Reading a filter differential pressure gauge

A differential pressure gauge reads the pressure drop across the bank, with one tap upstream and one downstream. NIOSH says mechanical filters get more resistant as they load, so pressure drop is often used to signal change-out, and it recommends a manometer or similar device for an objective replacement signal. It also says electrostatic filters' pressure drop rises slowly, so the gauge is a poor guide for them.

The university guideline calls for a gauge on each filter or bank at 2000 cfm or more, with a scale reaching no more than 1 inch w.g. above the maker's recommended final resistance, plus an adjustable flag and an alarm contact. A useful habit is to write the clean (initial) reading on the gauge or in the log when new filters go in, then change when the reading nears the maker's final resistance. NIOSH gives typical final values of 125 to 250 Pa (0.5 to 1.0 in. w.g.) for lower-efficiency filters, with better ones starting above 125 Pa and ending as high as 325 Pa (1.5 in.). Use the number printed by the filter maker, not these ranges.

Reading example. Using the guideline's MERV 8 example (initial 0.30, final 1.0), a gauge showing 0.55 in. w.g. is (0.55 - 0.30) / (1.0 - 0.30) = 36 percent of the way from clean to final. Not due yet. A reading that never rises, or drops suddenly, can mean a torn filter, a bypass or a bad tap line, so look before you assume all is well. Rated resistance also applies only at a stated face velocity (NIOSH: typically 250 to 500 fpm), so if airflow is off, the reading is off.

MERV upgrades and the fan

A higher MERV filter is not automatically a higher-resistance one: a national lab building-science guide notes that deeper or more numerous pleats cut pressure drop without changing the rating. Still, the filter commonly takes 20 to 50 percent of the system's pressure drop, according to that guide, and added resistance reaches the fan. NIOSH notes many light-commercial rooftop units lack the fan capacity for high-efficiency filters, and may not deliver design airflow. Its example raising filter drop from 250 to 500 Pa (1.0 to 2.0 in.) puts system drop from 1000 to 1250 Pa and raises fan horsepower roughly 40 percent. The lab guide adds that PSC-motor blowers lose airflow as resistance rises, while BPM (ECM) blowers raise speed and power to hold airflow. Either way, check airflow and static pressure after a change; see the fan law and static pressure lessons below. For the very high end of filtration, see the cleanroom lesson.

Filter change checklist

  1. Shut down and lock out the unit. NIOSH says to do filter maintenance only with the system shut down.
  2. Record the gauge reading before you open the door.
  3. Check the filter size, rating and airflow arrow. Install the arrow toward the equipment, as the lab guide says.
  4. Remove all old filters, then look at the rack for bent frames, dead gaskets, missing clips.
  5. Replace worn gaskets and seals; fill any empty position with a proper blank or filter.
  6. Seat each filter fully so clips or the downstream seal pull it tight. Do not tape or caulk the access doors shut.
  7. Change prefilters on their schedule, and check the final bank.
  8. Close and latch every door and panel.
  9. Restart, record the new clean (initial) reading, and compare with last time.
  10. Bag the used filters and record the date.

Field card: Air-Handler Filter Racks and Bypass

Air takes the easy way around.

  • Bypass is air going around the filter: gaps, missing blanks, bad gaskets, leaks before the fan.
  • Better media without a sealed rack gives little benefit (NIOSH).
  • Log the clean gauge reading; change near the maker's final resistance.
  • A stuck or falling gauge may mean a tear or a bypass, so look.
  • Higher resistance can cut airflow or raise fan power. Recheck after any MERV change.
  • Lock out before opening. Seat, seal and latch before restart.
