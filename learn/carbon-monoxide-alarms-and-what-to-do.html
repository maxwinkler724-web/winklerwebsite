Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A carbon monoxide alarm that goes off can be frightening. Knowing the steps ahead of time makes it much easier to act calmly.

The short answer

Carbon monoxide, or CO, is a gas you cannot see, taste or smell. Any fuel-burning appliance can make it. A CO alarm is your warning. If it sounds, do not look for the source. Following the CPSC steps: get everyone outside to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, count heads, and do not go back in until responders say it is clear. Do not run the suspect appliance until a professional has serviced it. If the alarm sounds again within 24 hours of going back in, repeat these steps and have a technician inspect all of your fuel-burning equipment.

What CO is and where it comes from

The CDC names gas- and oil-burning furnaces, portable generators, and charcoal grills as common sources. It says never to burn charcoal indoors, never to use camp stoves or flameless chemical heaters indoors, never to run a generator inside a home or garage, and never to run a vehicle in an attached garage even with the door open. Furnaces and boilers are a common source when something goes wrong, which is why this page belongs next to our heating guides.

Symptoms include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea or vomiting, chest pain, and confusion, and they are easy to mistake for the flu. The CDC notes that people who are asleep can die before they notice symptoms, and that infants, older adults and people with heart or breathing problems are at higher risk. The EPA says to think of CO if several people feel sick at about the same time.

Alarms: where, how many, how long

  • Put alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas (CPSC). Interconnected alarms sound together and are best.
  • Keep alarms away from heating vents and do not cover them with furniture or drapes. CPSC does not recommend kitchens or spots directly above fuel-burning appliances.
  • The test button checks the alarm's circuitry, not its sensor, so follow the maker's test and replacement-age instructions.
  • Use battery-powered or battery backup models. Test them monthly, as CPSC recommends, and check the batteries when you change the clocks.
  • The CDC suggests replacing a detector as the manufacturer says, or about every five years, and setting a reminder.
  • The EPA suggests looking for UL certification and following the maker's placement instructions. It also cautions that detectors vary in performance and are a backup, not a replacement, for proper appliance use and upkeep.
  • An alarm is a backup. It does not replace yearly checks of fuel-burning equipment.

Things you can safely check

  • Press the test button and look at the date printed on every alarm.
  • Make sure vents and chimneys outside are not blocked by snow, leaves, or nests, if you can see them from the ground.
  • Never run a generator, grill, or car in or near the house, garage, or windows.
  • Keep furnace, water heater, and boiler areas clear of stored items.
  • Arrange a yearly professional check of heating equipment, including chimneys and flues for blockage, corrosion and loose connections, before winter.
  • Make an escape plan with a meeting spot outside.

Never open panels, touch gas piping or valves, handle refrigerant, or work on wiring. Those jobs belong to a licensed professional. Never silence an alarm and keep using the equipment, and never remove an alarm because it is annoying.

When to call a pro

Safety comes first. If you smell gas (a rotten-egg odor), hear a hiss, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not look for the cause. Leave the building with everyone, stay out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not use switches or lighters on the way out.
  • A CO alarm sounds, with or without symptoms: get everyone out into fresh air and call 911 or the fire department. Do not go back in until cleared.
  • An alarm sounds and you cannot tell why: leave anyway, call from outside, and stay out until they say it is safe. Do not search for the source.
  • The alarm sounds again within 24 hours of returning: repeat the steps and have a technician inspect all fuel-burning equipment.
  • You smell gas: leave at once and call from outside. Do not turn lights or switches on or off, and do not search for the leak.
  • After any alarm, do not use the suspected appliance until a qualified technician has inspected it.
  • You see soot or sooty stains around an appliance.

Field card: Carbon Monoxide Alarms

No smell, no warning. The alarm is the warning.

  • Alarms on every level and near sleeping areas. Test and check batteries.
  • Replace alarms by the maker's date, or about every five years per CDC.
  • Alarm sounds: do not look for the source. Everyone outside, call 911, count heads, stay out.
  • Gas smell: leave, call from outside, no switches.
  • Yearly professional inspection of heating systems, including chimneys and flues for blockage, corrosion and loose connections.
