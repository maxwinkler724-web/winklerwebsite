Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit: Nominal vs Actual

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Buying a filter is easy once you know three things: where the slot is, what size the label really means, and which way the arrow points. This lesson is about fit. For how often to change a filter and what MERV means, see the lesson linked below.

The short answer

The arrow on the filter frame points the way the air is traveling, toward the furnace, air handler or blower and away from the room. Carrier's homeowner guide puts it this way: the arrows should point toward the blower motor. Buy by the nominal size printed on the old frame, but remember that the filter is cut a little smaller than that number so it can slide in. The filter must fit snugly enough that air cannot leak around it, without being bent or crushed.

Step 1: Find the filter slot

Filters sit on the return side, between the air coming back from the house and the blower. Carrier says a furnace filter is typically found in the blower compartment of the furnace. The PNNL Building America guide says the slot is usually between the return air plenum and the air handler cabinet, or in the first few feet of ductwork, or at a return grille. In plain terms there are two common homes for a filter:

  • In the furnace or air handler area. A slot in the side of the cabinet, or in the return duct right next to it. Many basement, closet and attic units are built this way.
  • Behind a return grille. A big grille in a wall, ceiling or floor, with the filter held in a frame behind the face. Some houses have both, or several grilles. If you find more than one filter, do not assume the first one is the only one.

Turn the thermostat off before you pull anything out. Do not open any panel that needs tools or that exposes burners, wiring or gas parts. A filter slot is built for homeowners; the rest is not.

Step 2: Read nominal vs actual size

Every filter has a nominal size, a rounded name like 20x25x1 (width, length, depth in inches). The actual size is what a tape measure finds, and it is smaller. Filter maker Filterbuy explains that the filter is cut about half an inch under nominal in length and width, and a 1-inch filter is really about three quarters of an inch thick. The frame usually prints both numbers, the nominal in big type and the actual in small type. Brands also vary slightly, so a new filter with the same nominal label can still fit a little differently.

Notice the last three rows do not follow the "half an inch smaller" pattern exactly. That is the point: depth and width rules differ by maker and by filter type, so read the label of the filter you actually buy. Filterbuy also notes that a true fractional slot, such as 22.25 inches, usually means a custom size.

Worked example: measure a slot and pick a size

Measure the empty slot, not the old filter, because a used filter often bows and reads short (Filterbuy's advice). Say the slot in a furnace closet measures 20 inches wide, 25 inches long and 1 inch deep.

  1. Round each side to the nearest inch: 20, 25, 1. The nominal size is 20x25x1.
  2. Look it up in the table: the actual size is 19.5 x 24.5 x 0.75 inches.
  3. Width gap: 20 - 19.5 = 0.5 inch total, so 0.5 / 2 = 0.25 inch per side.
  4. Length gap: 25 - 24.5 = 0.5 inch total, so 0.25 inch per side.
  5. Check against Filterbuy's guide: about 1/4 inch per side is a normal gap, and more than about 3/8 inch per side means the filter is too small.

Now see what a wrong buy does. A 19x24x1 filter would have an actual size near 18.5 x 23.5. Then 20 - 18.5 = 1.5 inches total, or 0.75 inch per side, twice the too-small limit. A filter that much smaller leaves a wide open path for air. And if you pick up a 21x26x1, it will not go in at all; do not force it. Match the nominal size to the slot and you are done.

Step 3: Point the arrow toward the blower

Before you remove the old filter, look at which way its arrow points and how it sits, as Carrier suggests. Then install the new one the same way. The same rule covers upflow, downflow and horizontal furnaces and ceiling, wall or floor returns. Only the physical direction changes. At a wall or ceiling return grille the arrow points away from the room, into the duct. PNNL's text says the arrows point into the duct and its figure caption says toward the furnace, which agree in practice. If a filter has no arrow, check the product instructions and the furnace documentation instead of guessing.

Why gaps matter

Air takes the easy path. EPA's guide to air cleaners tells you to make sure the filter fits snugly so air does not leak around it, and that you should not need to bend or crush it to make it fit. Air that goes around a filter is not cleaned, and it carries dust to the blower and coil. Filterbuy's fit guide cites modeling that assumes roughly 10% of the air bypasses a loose filter and roughly 25% for a large gap; treat those as rough illustrations, not measurements of your house. A published modeling study on filter bypass (AIVC) found that small gaps of about 1 millimeter matter little for most filters, but filters with a high pressure drop lose performance with small gaps and badly lose it with large ones. An ASHRAE Journal article on in-home filter tests lists bypass among the main factors, and warns that a high-efficiency filter is not guaranteed to perform well if it is not installed correctly.

That means a higher MERV number on the box does not fix a poor fit. MERV comes from the ASHRAE 52.2 test, which counts particles upstream and downstream of the filter across 12 size ranges from 0.30 to 10 microns, as a NAFA summary describes. The rating describes the filter media tested, not the gap in your slot. A PNNL tip for a loose slot is foam weatherstripping tape to reduce bypass, while keeping the cover from being taped or caulked shut so you can change filters easily.

Quick fit check: the filter should slide in without bending and stay seated. Look along the frame with a flashlight for light or gaps, and look for gray streaks on the clean side or a whistle at the slot, as Filterbuy suggests.

Media cabinets: the 4 to 5 inch filters

EPA says filters commonly come in thicknesses of 1 to 5 inches. The thick ones usually live in a media cabinet, a box bolted to the furnace or return duct that holds a deep filter. 3M's Filtrete product page for a 20-inch nominal media filter lists the actual size as 19.88 x 19.88 x 4.31 inches, and says it fits systems calling for a filter thickness of 4.31 inches and larger, designed to fit Honeywell media air cleaners. These filters have more surface area, so they can last longer between changes, but a media cabinet takes a specific size. Do not try to jam a thick filter into a 1-inch slot. PNNL advises using the thickest filter the slot can accommodate, and asking an HVAC company before moving up to MERV 13 or higher.

One filter, not two

Run one filter in the return path. Stacking two restricts airflow, which can strain the system and raise energy bills, according to filter retailer Remember the Filter, and it also upsets the balance between filtering and airflow. If you want better filtering, use one better filter that the system allows. Check the equipment manual or ask a technician, and see the airflow lesson linked below for why restriction causes trouble.

Field card: Furnace Filter Fit

Right slot, right size, arrow toward the blower, no gaps.

  • Find the slot on the return side: furnace cabinet, return duct, or return grille.
  • Order by the nominal size (20x25x1); expect the real size about 1/2 inch smaller and 1/4 inch thinner.
  • Measure the empty slot, round each side to the nearest inch.
  • Arrow points toward the blower or into the duct, the way air flows.
  • Fit snug with no bending, no forcing, no gaps.
  • Thick 4 to 5 inch filters go in a media cabinet only.
  • One filter at a time. Do not stack.
