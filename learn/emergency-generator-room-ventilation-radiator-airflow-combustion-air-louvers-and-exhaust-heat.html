Emergency Generator Room Ventilation: Radiator Airflow, Combustion Air, Louvers and Exhaust Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A standby generator turns fuel into power, and a lot of the rest becomes heat. If the room cannot get rid of that heat, the set derates, overheats or shuts down at the moment the building needs it. This lesson walks through where the heat goes, how air should travel through the room, and how to check a ventilation airflow with the sensible heat equation.

Check the maker and the code

Check the maker's data. Airflow, heat rejection and restriction limits are different for every model. Use the generator maker's spec sheet and the rules in force where you work, including NFPA 110 for emergency power systems. This page is general education, not a design.

Where the heat comes from

An engine rejects heat in several streams. Most goes to the cooling system and leaves through the radiator, but some radiates from the engine, the alternator and the exhaust piping into the room. Kohler's cooling paper says the room still needs enough air to remove radiant engine, exhaust and alternator heat, and to supply combustion air, even when the radiator is remote. A Caterpillar dealer's engine room guide (cited as plain text below) says up to about 10 percent of the fuel an engine burns can be dissipated to the surrounding area as heat. Treat that as a rough idea, not a design number. The real figure is on the maker's data sheet as heat rejection to room or radiated heat.

Exhaust is the hot spot. Caterpillar's article says flue exhaust can exceed 900 F at full load and radiator discharge air can exceed 160 F. POWER magazine and the Teksan layout guide both say the exhaust pipe and silencer inside the room should be insulated, because otherwise exhaust heat raises room temperature and reduces performance. Kohler likewise says to wrap exhaust piping and silencer sections inside the room.

How air should move

With a unit-mounted radiator, the fan pulls room air in through a vent behind the alternator, across the alternator and engine, through the radiator, and out through a hot-air outlet in front of the radiator (POWER magazine). Caterpillar adds that ventilation air for unit-mounted radiators should enter low so it flows over the whole set, and that bypass air that goes straight to the discharge raises room temperature. POWER magazine says a duct with a flexible connection should join the radiator to the discharge opening.

Radiator airflow and allowed restriction

The radiator fan can only push against so much resistance. Caterpillar says radiator fans handle an external static pressure of 0.5 to 0.7 in. wc, and that attenuators add restriction. Go past what the fan can do and airflow drops and the set must be derated. Because every added restriction cuts airflow, the fan laws matter here; see the fan laws lesson linked below. The maker publishes the allowed restriction for each model, so read it before judging a louver or duct.

Louvers: free area, type and motion

  • Free area. A louver face is not all open, because fins and frames block part of it. Openings are sized by free area from the louver maker's data. We found no maker-published free-area ratio, so none is given here.
  • Right louver type. POWER magazine calls using louver fins designed for transformer rooms the biggest mistake in this area, because restricted louvers cause backpressure and overheating.
  • Open on start. Generac's cold climate paper says motorized louvers are preferred over those that open with fan pressure, because sleet and snow can disable fan-pressure louvers, and that movable louvers should open in a positive manner. Caterpillar says to keep damper actuators out of the hot airstream, since they are often not rated for discharge temperatures, and to use quick-acting actuators that fail open.
  • Fire systems. POWER magazine says automatic louvers should be used where a gaseous fire extinguishing system is installed.

Recirculation

Hot discharge air that finds its way back to the intake makes every number worse. Caterpillar warns that recirculating those streams can push ventilation air above ambient, and that assuming ventilation air equals ambient "can be a critical design flaw." Wind drives much of it. Its example was a data center with a 99 F design ambient where intake air was predicted at 127 F, which would have limited each 1.5 MW set to 1.2 MW until openings were added on the opposite side of the building. Kohler advises placing the radiator upstream of the exhaust and room air discharge and away from structures that block airflow.

Combustion air

The engine draws combustion air from the room unless it is ducted from outside, so that air counts in the room airflow. The Caterpillar dealer guide adds engine combustion air to the radiated heat in its airflow formula. Kohler notes that combustion air that gets too hot makes the engine derate, and Caterpillar says room temperatures above 104 F typically require derating, about 10 to 15 percent per 18 F above that. Use the maker's combustion air figure for your model.

Worked example (Our own)

Question: how much room airflow keeps the temperature rise across the room to 15 F?

Basis. The sensible heat equation is Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x delta T (ACCA), where 1.08 comes from air density 0.075, specific heat 0.24 and 60 minutes per hour. ACCA says it fits air near sea level and room temperature. The Caterpillar dealer guide describes a usual allowed rise of 10 to 20 F.

Our own assumptions (not from a maker): sea level; heat rejected to the room of 150,000 Btu/h, standing in for the spec-sheet value; allowed rise of 15 F; engine combustion air of 1,500 cfm taken from the room; a face velocity of 500 feet per minute through the louver free area; a louver that is 50 percent free area.

  1. Cfm for room heat = 150,000 / (1.08 x 15) = 150,000 / 16.2 = about 9,259 cfm.
  2. Add combustion air: 9,259 + 1,500 = about 10,759 cfm.
  3. Free area = 10,759 / 500 = about 21.5 sq ft.
  4. Gross opening at 50 percent free area = 21.5 / 0.5 = about 43 sq ft.

Check. At a 20 F rise the heat part drops to 150,000 / (1.08 x 20) = about 6,944 cfm, so a tighter limit costs a lot of air. For a unit-mounted radiator, the maker's radiator fan airflow is normally far larger and controls louver size; this calculation checks the room portion and applies most directly to remote radiator rooms. Above sea level, the Caterpillar dealer guide raises airflow 10 percent for every 2,500 feet.

Cold weather

Caterpillar says to keep the room above freezing so standby sets start reliably and fuel and pipes are protected. Generac says block heaters should keep coolant above 60 F, take about 1 to 2 hours to bring an engine up to temperature, and must disconnect when the start sequence begins. It also says movable louvers can seal the openings while the set is stationary. The Caterpillar dealer guide describes louvers that send engine heat back into the room until jacket water reaches 190 F, then close so ventilation air exhausts.

Exhaust leaks and carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide has no smell or color, and the CDC and NIOSH hazard card says it can reach deadly levels in less than 1 minute. A leaking exhaust joint is a poison source. Never run gas-powered generators inside a building or semi-enclosed space (CDC and NIOSH). See the carbon monoxide lesson linked below.

Field card: Emergency Generator Room Ventilation: Radiator Airflow, Combustion Air, Louvers and Exhaust Heat

Heat out equals air times 1.08 times rise.

  • Room heat load comes from the maker spec sheet.
  • cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T), sea level, near room temperature.
  • Add combustion air to the room airflow.
  • Radiator fan limit is about 0.5 to 0.7 in. wc external static, so louvers cost airflow.
  • Use generator-room louvers, motorized, fail open.
  • Keep discharge away from the intake; consider wind.
  • Insulate exhaust pipe and silencer in the room.
  • Coolant above 60 F with a block heater; louvers shut when the set is off.
