EPA 608 Core Exam: The Refrigeration Cycle and Pressure-Temperature

Last updated: 2026-11-03

The Section 608 core test asks where the refrigerant is a vapor or a liquid, and at what pressure, as it travels around the system. This lesson builds that picture from the pressure-temperature relationship, then ties it to superheat, subcooling and the reason a cylinder gauge tells you temperature, not how much is inside.

What the core test asks about the cycle

EPA's test-topics page lists, under Refrigeration, the refrigerant states (vapor versus liquid) and pressures at different points of the cycle, how and when cooling occurs, and refrigeration gauges with their color codes and ranges. Under Safety it lists reusable versus disposable cylinders and the risk of filling cylinders more than 80 percent full. The word superheat appears on that page only in the Type 2 list, so on the core you are more likely to meet the cycle-state questions than a superheat calculation. We cover superheat and subcooling anyway because they explain the states. EPA says tests are given by EPA-approved certifying organizations, and the core must be taken proctored (not open book) to count toward Universal certification. For the basics of the cycle itself, start with Refrigeration Cycle Basics; this page is about the exam view.

The four components and the state at each point

Follow the arrows in the diagram. A university thermodynamics reading on the vapor-compression cycle describes the same four steps: the compressor raises pressure and temperature, the condenser rejects heat, a throttling device (an expansion valve or capillary tube) drops the pressure, and the evaporator absorbs heat.

  • Compressor outlet (A): high-pressure, hot, superheated vapor. The compressor does not make liquid. It squeezes vapor to a pressure high enough that the condenser can reject heat to warmer-than-refrigerant surroundings. The Purdue reading puts this state at a temperature above the hot reservoir.
  • Condenser outlet (B): high-pressure liquid. In the ideal cycle it is saturated liquid; the same reading notes that real cycles often leave it as compressed (subcooled) liquid. That extra subcooling is what you want ahead of the metering device.
  • Metering device outlet and evaporator inlet (C): low-pressure, cold liquid-vapor mixture. The pressure drop makes some of the liquid boil off, so the mixture is mostly liquid with a little vapor, and it is cold because it is now at the low saturation temperature.
  • Evaporator outlet and compressor inlet (D): low-pressure vapor, usually slightly superheated. The reading says the compressor inlet state is often in the superheated vapor region. That is the margin that keeps liquid out of the compressor.

Two quick sorting rules help on the exam. First, the high side is everything from the compressor outlet to the metering device; the low side is everything from the metering device to the compressor inlet. Second, the only place a big pressure drop happens on purpose is the metering device, and cooling happens in the evaporator because the refrigerant there is colder than the air or water it is cooling. Do not mix up the two kinds of pressure on a question: high pressure vapor exists only between the compressor and the condenser's condensing zone, while a low-pressure liquid exists only between the metering device and the point where it finishes boiling.

Saturation and the pressure-temperature relationship

When liquid and vapor of one pure refrigerant sit together, each pressure has exactly one temperature, called the saturation temperature. The Purdue properties reading says pressure and temperature are not independent during a phase change. Raise the pressure and the boiling or condensing temperature rises with it. That is why the evaporator runs cold: low pressure means a low boiling point. It is also why the condenser runs warm enough to dump heat outdoors: high pressure means a high condensing temperature.

At a fixed pressure the temperature stays at saturation while the refrigerant boils or condenses, even though heat is moving in or out. Only after the last liquid has boiled (or the last vapor has condensed) does the temperature move away from saturation. See Pressure-Temperature Basics for the chart habits.

Superheat and subcooling, defined by the saturation line

Both terms are measured against the saturation temperature for the pressure you read.

  • Superheat is how far a vapor is above its saturation temperature at that pressure. Measure at the suction line: line temperature minus saturation temperature at suction pressure.
  • Subcooling is how far a liquid is below its saturation temperature at that pressure. Measure at the liquid line: saturation temperature at liquid-line pressure minus line temperature.

The Purdue reading states the same test: a subcooled liquid is below the saturation temperature for its pressure, and a superheated vapor is above it. Both are temperature differences, so they are in degrees F, not psi. For blends whose saturation temperature depends on whether you are at the bubble point or dew point, use Refrigerant Temperature Glide: Bubble and Dew Points, and for field reading see Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Worked example (our own): R-134a, medium pressure

These line temperatures and gauge readings are our own made-up numbers for practice. The saturation values come from the NIST Chemistry WebBook fluid tables for R-134a (1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane), which list pressure in psia. We turn gauge pressure into absolute by adding the standard atmosphere, about 14.7 psi, and assume the gauges are accurate and the site is near sea level.

Low side (superheat). Assume the suction gauge reads 18.5 psig and the suction line is 31 F.

  1. Absolute pressure: 18.5 + 14.7 = 33.2 psia.
  2. NIST row: 33.247 psia is saturated at 20.175 F. So the saturation temperature is about 20 F.
  3. Superheat: 31 F − 20 F = 11 F. The vapor is above saturation, so it is superheated.

High side (subcooling). Assume the liquid-line gauge reads 116 psig and the liquid line is 86 F.

  1. Absolute pressure: 116 + 14.7 = 130.7 psia.
  2. NIST row: 130.55 psia is saturated at 95.955 F. The 0.15 psi difference moves the answer by under 0.1 F, so the saturation temperature is about 96 F.
  3. Subcooling: 96 F − 86 F = 10 F. The liquid is below saturation, so it is subcooled.

Sanity check on direction: the vapor reading (31 F) is warmer than its saturation (20 F), and the liquid reading (86 F) is cooler than its saturation (96 F). If either came out the other way you would have swapped the subtraction.

Why a sealed cylinder's pressure tells you temperature, not amount

A cylinder that holds some liquid refrigerant and some vapor is a closed container with both phases present. Its pressure sits on the saturation line for whatever temperature the contents are at. Same refrigerant, same temperature, same pressure, whether the cylinder is nearly empty or nearly full, as long as some liquid remains. From the NIST table, R-134a at about 80 F (80.427 F) is saturated at 102.10 psia, which is roughly 87 psig. A cylinder of R-134a that has been sitting in 100 F air (100.25 F in the table) reads 139.38 psia, about 125 psig, with the same refrigerant inside.

So the gauge answers "how warm is the refrigerant?" For "how much is in there?" you weigh the cylinder on a scale. This ties to the safety item on the test: EPA lists the risk of filling cylinders more than 80 percent full, because liquid that fills the cylinder leaves no room for vapor. Fill limits and stamped markings are covered in the cylinder lessons; here the exam point is that pressure is not a fill gauge. One caveat: if the cylinder is down to vapor only, the pressure drops as vapor is drawn off and no longer follows the saturation line.

Practice-style questions (our own)

  1. In a working system, the refrigerant leaving the compressor is best described as: (a) low-pressure liquid, (b) high-pressure superheated vapor, (c) high-pressure liquid, (d) low-pressure saturated mixture.
  2. Where does the refrigerant first become a cold low-pressure liquid-vapor mixture? (a) compressor discharge, (b) condenser inlet, (c) just after the metering device, (d) liquid line before the filter drier.
  3. Two sealed R-134a cylinders sit in the same 80 F room. One is about a quarter full of liquid and the other is about two-thirds full. What do you expect their gauges to show? (a) the fuller one reads higher, (b) the emptier one reads higher, (c) about the same pressure, roughly 87 psig, (d) zero on both.
  4. Saturation temperature at the suction pressure is 40 F and the suction line reads 52 F. What is the superheat?
  5. Saturation temperature at the liquid-line pressure is 105 F and the liquid line reads 95 F. What is the subcooling, and is the liquid subcooled?
  6. True or false: while refrigerant boils in the evaporator at a steady pressure, its temperature keeps rising as it absorbs heat until all the liquid is gone.
  7. A technician says a cylinder reading 60 psig must be about half full. What is wrong with that reasoning?

Answers. 1: (b). 2: (c). 3: (c), because both hold liquid and vapor at the same temperature. 4: 52 − 40 = 12 F of superheat. 5: 105 − 95 = 10 F of subcooling; yes, the liquid is below saturation. 6: False; during boiling at a steady pressure the temperature stays at saturation, and it rises only after the liquid is gone. 7: pressure follows temperature when liquid is present, not the amount, so only a scale shows how much refrigerant is in the cylinder.

For more questions in test format, use the EPA 608 Core Practice Test.

Field card: EPA 608 Core Exam: The Refrigeration Cycle and Pressure-Temperature

Pressure sets the boiling point; the cylinder gauge reads temperature.

  • Compressor outlet: high-pressure superheated vapor.
  • Condenser outlet: high-pressure liquid, ideally with some subcooling.
  • Metering device outlet: low-pressure, cold liquid-vapor mixture.
  • Evaporator outlet and compressor inlet: low-pressure vapor, slightly superheated.
  • Superheat = vapor temperature minus saturation temperature. Subcooling = saturation temperature minus liquid temperature.
  • Temperature stays at saturation while boiling or condensing at steady pressure.
  • A cylinder with liquid in it reads saturation pressure for its temperature. Weigh it to know the amount; never fill past 80 percent.
