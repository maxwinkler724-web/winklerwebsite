HVAC in Paper Mill Machine Rooms: Dryer Hoods, Humid Exhaust and Heat Recovery

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A paper machine dries a wet sheet by pressing it against steam-heated cylinders, and every pound of water that leaves the sheet becomes vapor in the air above it. That makes the dryer section one of the biggest heat and moisture sources in any plant. This lesson covers how the machine hood and its exhaust are arranged, what the supply air does, why roofs and walls drip, and how to size the airflow.

Why the dryer section matters to an HVAC tech

A federal-hosted review of paper industry efficiency says drying is by far the most energy-intensive step on a paper machine, roughly two-thirds of total papermaking energy. A national-lab-hosted study of four mills adds that the dryer section is the largest thermal energy user and that the sheet loses roughly 1.0 tonne of water per tonne of paper in the mills it examined (values from 0.92 to 1.19). Almost all of that heat ends up in the air as latent heat, so the hood and room air systems are really water-vapor handling systems.

The same ideas appear in other wet-process rooms. See open hot water tanks and print shops and paper storage, which cover related paper-handling and hot-water vapor loads.

Open, semi-open and closed hoods

The study describes three hood types: open, semi-open and closed. A closed hood needs only about one-third as much air as an open hood to remove the same moisture. In one machine it compared, a semi-open hood used about 47.6 kg of dry air per kg of water evaporated and a closed hood about 10.4. The closed hood ran a much hotter, wetter exhaust: roughly 80 C and 120 g of water per kg of dry air, with a dew point near 56 C, against 45 C, 45 g/kg and 38 C for the semi-open hood. The review adds that enclosed hoods need about half the air per tonne of water evaporated compared with canopy hoods. Less air means less air to heat and smaller exhaust fans, which is why closing the hood saves both steam and fan power.

Supply air to the hood and the room

The hood needs supply air, and in the study the closed hood used supply air heated to about 95 C, with a humidity of 24 g/kg. Heating that air with recovered heat, not live steam, is where savings come from. The hood is not sealed, so pressure balance matters: if the hood runs too negative, it pulls room air in around the edges; too positive, and humid air spills into the room. Hood balance is a number the study tracks (30 percent for the semi-open hood, 80 percent for the closed one). Hood supply and exhaust fans are normally set by the machine controls, and building techs should coordinate with the operators before changing any damper or fan setting on that system.

Why roofs and walls drip

Whatever vapor escapes the hood rises in the machine room. Where it meets a roof deck, wall or skylight colder than the air's dew point, it condenses and drips onto the floor or sheet. Winter makes this worse. The cure has three parts, all general principles:

  • Capture more at the hood. Vapor that goes up the exhaust duct never reaches the roof.
  • Keep surfaces warm. Warm room supply air washed along the underside of the roof, insulation and vapor retarders keep surface temperature above the room dew point. Refer to the humidity ratio and dew point lesson.
  • Sweep the space. Ventilation air moves through the roof zone and out through roof exhausters, carrying moisture out. A similar roof-condensation problem is covered in the sugar house lesson.

Heat recovery from humid exhaust

Hood exhaust is warm, humid, and sometimes carries fiber and fine droplets, so heat exchangers foul and need cleaning. The efficiency review says typical air-to-air systems recover only about 15 percent of the energy in the hood exhaust, and that with maintenance and extensions 60 to 70 percent is possible. The lab study says more than 60 percent of dryer exhaust heat can be recovered in a modern, efficient system, and reports a machine whose 70 C-plus exhaust had no recovery at all. Recovered heat commonly warms hood supply air, machine-room make-up air, or process water, and a coil loop can be used where the streams must stay apart (see run-around coil loops). The lab study also noted that adding humidity control to the exhaust, so the flow follows the season, was estimated to save electricity and thermal energy.

Ventilating the rest of the building

The rest of the mill building needs ordinary ventilation: make-up air for the exhaust taken from the hood, relief paths, and exhaust for areas away from the machine such as chemical rooms or locker rooms. Exhaust taken from the hood must be replaced, so the make-up air supply has to be sized with it. Operators and maintenance people also work in hot rooms near the dryers, so heat stress controls matter; follow the plant's written program.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These inputs are our assumptions, not data from a particular mill.

  1. Assumed production: 25 short tons per hour of finished paper, which is 50,000 lb/h at 93 percent solids (7 percent moisture).
  2. Assumed sheet solids entering the dryer section: 45 percent.
  3. Bone-dry fiber: 50,000 x 0.93 = 46,500 lb/h.
  4. Sheet mass entering: 46,500 / 0.45 = 103,333 lb/h.
  5. Water evaporated: 103,333 - 50,000 = 53,333 lb/h. That is about 1.07 lb per lb of paper, close to the roughly 1 tonne per tonne in the study (a check on reasonableness).
  6. Latent load at about 1,000 Btu per lb: 53,333 x 1,000 = 53.3 million Btu/h.
  7. Assumed humidity rise from supply to exhaust: 500 grains per lb of dry air (7,000 grains = 1 lb). Water carried per lb of air = 500 / 7,000 = 0.0714 lb.
  8. Dry air needed: 53,333 / 0.0714 = 746,700 lb/h, which is about 14 lb of air per lb of water. This sits between the study's closed (10.4) and semi-open (47.6) values, so it is plausible.
  9. Airflow at 0.075 lb/ft3 standard air density: 746,700 / 0.075 / 60 = about 166,000 cfm. Real hot exhaust occupies more volume, so fans are sized from actual conditions.
  10. Check: 1,000 x 0.075 x 60 / 7,000 = 0.643 Btu/h per cfm per grain. 0.643 x 166,000 x 500 = 53.4 million Btu/h, matching step 6.
  11. If we assume half of that latent heat could be recovered (an assumption, below the study's 60 percent figure): 0.5 x 53.3 = about 26.7 million Btu/h.

Hold the humidity rise lower, or let the exhaust get wetter, and the airflow changes in proportion: doubling the rise to 1,000 grains per lb halves the airflow to about 83,000 cfm. This is the reason closed hoods run such wet exhaust.

Field card: HVAC in Paper Mill Machine Rooms

Every pound of water out of the sheet is about 1,000 Btu in the air.

  • Drying is the largest energy user on a paper machine; the water leaves as vapor.
  • Closed hoods use about one-third the air of open hoods and give hotter, wetter exhaust.
  • Roof drips mean a surface is below the room dew point: capture, warm and sweep.
  • Airflow, lb/h = water, lb/h x 7,000 / humidity rise, grains per lb.
  • Replace exhausted air with make-up air.
  • Heat recovery fouls; plan for cleaning.
  • Coordinate with machine operators before touching hood dampers.
