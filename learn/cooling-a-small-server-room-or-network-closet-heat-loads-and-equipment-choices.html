Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet: Heat Loads and Equipment Choices

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A rack of switches, a firewall and a couple of servers can sit in a closet for years, then fail on the first hot weekend. This lesson covers why small IT rooms overheat, what temperatures the references aim for, why the building's own air conditioning usually cannot be trusted with the job, and how to pick and size a dedicated cooler.

Where the heat comes from

Nearly every watt an IT device draws ends up as heat in the room. Schneider Electric's (APC) paper on wiring closets says the power equipment consumes is a practical stand-in for the heat it produces, and that every kilowatt of IT load creates a kilowatt of heat that has to be removed. The paper also notes that more distributed devices (phones, routers, switches, servers) packed into small rooms is why overheating is more common now.

To size cooling you need Btu/h, the unit air conditioners are rated in. NIST's SP 811 conversion table lists 1 Btu(IT)/h as 0.2930711 W, which inverts to 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h. APC's cooling-requirements white paper rounds the factor to 3.41. A 1,000 W closet is about 3,412 Btu/h, a little over a quarter of a "ton."

One trap: use real draw, not the nameplate. APC's closet paper cites configurable routers rated at 5 to 6 kW that draw only 1 to 2 kW as configured. Check the maker's power specification or measure with a plug-in meter before you buy a cooler for a load that does not exist.

What temperature to aim for

The number that matters is the air entering the equipment, not the thermostat reading across the room. ENERGY STAR's data center page says ASHRAE widened its recommended range from 68 to 77°F (the 2004 level) to 64.4 to 80.6°F, measured at the inlet at the front of the server. The DOE's data center design guide lists the same 64.4 to 80.6°F (18 to 27°C) for the common equipment classes. APC's closet paper uses the older 68 to 77°F recommended band and sets a goal of no higher than 77°F for important closets, with up to 90°F tolerable for non-critical ones. It also quotes 104°F as a typical vendor maximum, which is a limit, not a target.

Heat also costs money in other ways. The same paper says UPS batteries at 104°F may last about 1.5 years instead of 3 to 5. Your equipment's own manuals win over any general range, so read them.

Why the building's comfort system falls short

It is tempting to run a supply duct into the closet. APC's paper rates that approach as not recommended, and gives the reasons:

  • Setback. The thermostat is outside the closet and follows the office schedule. In its example, a night and weekend setback from 68 to 85°F leaves a critical closet with no supportable load.
  • Cycling. A closet that rides the zone's on and off cycles sees temperature swings. The paper says swings stress equipment more than steady, somewhat higher temperatures.
  • Capacity. Typical comfort systems deliver about 4 to 5 W per square foot, roughly 150 W per rack, far under a loaded rack.
  • Winter. A supply duct can pour heat into the closet when the building is heating. The paper advises removing or shutting off such ducts.

APC's blog on the topic frames the dilemma: give the closet its own thermostat and the system runs all night just for it, or share the zone and accept swings.

The options, smallest load to largest

APC's paper sorts rooms by watts. For a closet that must stay at or below 77°F, wall conduction covers under about 400 W, passive vents to cooler building air up to about 700 W, and fan-assisted ventilation (it assumes a 480 cfm fan) roughly 700 to 2,000 W. Above about 2,000 W it points to a dedicated air conditioner. For non-critical closets allowed up to 90°F, those lines move to 1,000, 1,750, and 1,750 to 4,500 W. These limits assume the surrounding space stays cool and that the room is not in direct sun; the paper's own example adds 8 to 12°F on a 100°F day with an exposed wall. It also says dedicated cooling is the answer, even at low loads, when outside air is dusty, swings widely, or ducts cannot be added.

Exhaust or transfer fan. Cheapest, but it only works when the air it pulls in is cooler than you want. Use a thermostat-controlled fan, filter the intake, and plan on more than one fan so one failure does not cook the room, as APC advises.

Ductless mini-split. The usual answer above the fan range. A wall or ceiling indoor unit sits in or near the closet and the outdoor unit goes outside, so it works whatever the building system is doing. See Mini-Split Basics and Ceiling Cassette and Ducted Mini-Splits vs Wall-Mounted Heads for the indoor unit choices.

Year-round cooling is the catch. In winter the outdoor air is cold, and ordinary cooling operation may lose head pressure. Maker data varies by model: the Mitsubishi PKA-AL18 submittal gives a cooling range of 115°F down to 23°F outdoors, and says wind baffles are required below 23°F in cooling mode, with operation listed to -40°F with the advanced side baffle. A Mitsubishi M-Series catalog says its systems can cool when it dips to 14°F. Those are different products with different numbers, so read the sheet for the exact model, and see Low Ambient Head Pressure Control for why the limits exist. Also check the indoor side: that submittal lists a minimum intake air of 66°F dry bulb for cooling, which is a reason not to overcool a closet.

Portable and spot coolers. A portable unit is a stopgap. APC groups portable units among self-contained dedicated air conditioners, but the DOE says single-duct portables pull their condenser air from the room, which draws warm outside air in; DOE says this infiltration lowers capacity and efficiency. Hot exhaust must also be ducted out of the space, and condensate must go somewhere. Use one to survive until a permanent unit is in. See Window and Portable Air Conditioners.

Condensate, power and alarms

Water is the main risk of cooling a room full of electronics. The PKA-AL18 submittal lists a drain pan level sensor and condensate pump accessories, so plan the drain route and a pump with a safety switch before the unit goes in; see Mini-Split Leaking Water. APC notes that air conditioners can pull four to six times nominal current at compressor start, which matters if cooling must ride through a UPS event.

Finally, alarm on temperature. APC's catalog describes sensors on PDUs and UPS network cards and a NetBotz monitor with customizable thresholds and notifications for temperature, leaks, doors and more. Whatever you use, send alerts to a person who will see them on a weekend.

Worked example: sizing a closet unit

Closet is 8 ft by 10 ft (80 ft²) with this list, using measured draw:

  • 2 servers at 350 W each = 700 W
  • 1 PoE switch = 400 W
  • 1 firewall = 60 W
  • 1 storage box = 120 W

Step 1, IT load: 700 + 400 + 60 + 120 = 1,280 W.

Step 2, UPS loss. APC White Paper 25 gives UPS heat as 0.04 times the rating plus 0.05 times the IT load. With a 1,500 W UPS: 0.04 × 1,500 = 60 W, and 0.05 × 1,280 = 64 W, so 124 W.

Step 3, lights and people. The same paper lists 2.0 W per square foot for lighting and 100 W per person. Lights: 2.0 × 80 = 160 W. One person: 100 W. This is a generous allowance, since the closet paper suggests few or no lights, but it is a safe one.

Step 4, total: 1,280 + 124 + 160 + 100 = 1,664 W.

Step 5, convert: 1,664 × 3.412 = 5,678 Btu/h.

Step 6, cross-check and pick a size. White Paper 25 suggests a cooler rated at 1.3 times the IT load for network rooms. 1.3 × 1,280 W = 1,664 W, which happens to match our total, so both methods give 5,678 Btu/h. Nominal mini-splits come in steps such as 9,000 and 12,000 Btu/h (the Mitsubishi catalog lists 9,000 and 12,000 Btu/h single-zone models). A 9,000 Btu/h unit covers 5,678 with margin, while a 12,000 would just be bigger than needed. Pick a nominal 9,000 Btu/h unit, then confirm the maker's cooling range for your climate and low ambient needs.

Note that 1,664 W is inside APC's 700 to 2,000 W fan-assisted band for a critical closet, so a fan could work if cool building air is always available. Where it is not, the mini-split is the sounder choice.

Field card: Server Closet Cooling

Watts in is heat out. 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h.

  • Add measured equipment watts, UPS loss, lights and people, then multiply by 3.412.
  • Aim for equipment inlet air in the maker's range; ASHRAE recommends 64.4 to 80.6°F at the inlet.
  • Building comfort air fails on setback, cycling and winter heating.
  • Small loads: fans if cool air is available; larger or year-round: a dedicated mini-split.
  • Check the model's low ambient cooling limit and any wind baffle requirement.
  • Plan the drain, a pump with a safety switch, and a temperature alarm that reaches a person.
  • Portable coolers are a stopgap.
