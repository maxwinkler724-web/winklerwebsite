Writing a Useful HVAC Service Ticket: Readings, Findings, Refrigerant Records and Recommendations

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A service ticket is the only memory a building has of what happened on a call. Months later, a different tech, a manager or an auditor will read it with none of the context you had on the roof. This lesson covers what to write down, how to write it so a stranger can use it, and why refrigerant leak records live or die on the ticket.

Check current rules

Check current rules. This is general education built from EPA pages, the federal regulation and facilities guidance we read in 2026. Your employer, customer contract and local rules decide what your tickets must contain. Confirm legal requirements with EPA and the eCFR.

Who reads your ticket

Write for three readers: the next tech, who needs to know where you stopped; the owner or facility manager, who needs to decide whether to spend money; and anyone checking refrigerant compliance later. A DOE federal facilities guide says a maintenance management system can track work orders by piece of equipment and keep a sortable history of them. That history only helps if each entry holds real information.

What goes on the ticket

  • The complaint, in the customer's words. Quote it: "Third floor is warm after 2 pm." Your diagnosis may differ, but the original words show what the occupant actually experienced. University facilities offices ask requesters for the building, room and as much problem detail as possible; the tech should keep that detail, not shrink it to "no cool."
  • Equipment identity. Tag or unit number, location, make, model and serial from the nameplate, and the refrigerant type and factory charge if printed there. See Reading an HVAC Nameplate. Model and serial are how parts get ordered and how a unit is told apart from its twin.
  • Readings with units and conditions. A bare "suction 70" is nearly useless. Write the value, the unit, where it was taken and what the unit was doing: "suction 70 psig at the service port, compressor running, outdoor air 91 F, return air 78 F." Add the time. Readings are only comparable when conditions are recorded.
  • What you found. Separate observation from conclusion. "Condenser coil matted with cottonwood fluff" is an observation. "Cause of high head pressure" is your conclusion. Label which is which.
  • What you did. Actions in order, including what you adjusted, cleaned, replaced or reset, and the result afterward.
  • Parts used. Part, quantity, and old and new part numbers where you have them.
  • Refrigerant added or recovered. Type, pounds and ounces, which circuit, and why. More on this below.
  • Recommendations with priority. Say what you would do next, why, and how soon. A simple scale works: safety or failure risk now, fix soon, watch. One university facilities office publishes four request tiers from emergency to specialized, each with a response expectation, which shows how owners already think about urgency.
  • What was not checked. "Did not inspect the economizer; roof access blocked." This protects you and warns the next tech not to assume it was cleared.

Plain language or jargon?

Use trade terms where they are exact (superheat, subcooling, static pressure) and give the number. Then add one plain sentence for the owner: "The refrigerant level is low, so the unit cannot absorb enough heat." Avoid shorthand only you understand, such as "LOP" or "bad TXV?" with no reading behind it. If you suspect something you did not prove, write "suspected" and say what test would confirm it.

Photos

A photo of the nameplate, the failed part and the gauge or meter display, taken before and after, answers questions words cannot. Name or tag each photo with the unit number and attach it to the ticket. Keep people's faces out of frame and follow your employer's photo policy.

Why refrigerant records depend on the ticket

Under 40 CFR 82.157, the owner or operator of an appliance with a full charge of 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant must calculate the leak rate every time refrigerant is added, with exceptions for retrofits, new installations and qualifying seasonal variances. The regulation's service record list includes the appliance identity and location, the date, the parts and type of work, the name of the person who did it, and the amount and type of refrigerant added, plus the full charge and the leak rate with its method. EPA says a technician must give the owner an invoice or other documentation showing the amount of refrigerant added, plus records of leak inspections and verification tests. EPA also says technicians are not required to supply the full charge or leak-rate results; the owner keeps those. But the owner cannot work them out from a ticket that says "added some gas." The regulation text we read sets a minimum three-year retention for these records.

Newer federal rules for HFC equipment add leak repair duties for larger charges, described in EPA's Section 608 materials and in our leak-rate lesson; ask your compliance lead which rule covers a given unit. The math is in Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate. Here the point is simple: that math needs pounds added, the date, and the full charge. Each missing item turns the number into a guess, and a wrong number can mean a missed repair deadline.

A ticket layout

Worked example: weak ticket to good ticket

Both tickets below are made up by us for teaching. They are not from a real job.

The weak ticket: "RTU-3 not cooling. Low on gas. Added some. Running OK now. Tech: J."

What is wrong: no customer words, no model or serial, no readings, no amount added, no date or full charge, no reason for the shortage, no leak search, no recommendation, and nothing about what was not checked.

The rewrite (all details are our assumptions):

  • Complaint: Manager says "The east offices were warm all week."
  • Equipment: Chiller CH-1, rooftop plant; model and serial copied from nameplate (photo 1); R-134a; nameplate charge 120 lb.
  • Readings, 10:15 am, outdoor air 91 F: leaving chilled water 52 F against a 44 F setpoint; bubbles in the liquid line sight glass; subcooling 2 F.
  • Found: Observed: sight glass bubbling, oil residue at the evaporator flare joint (photo 2). Concluded, not yet proven: slow leak at that joint.
  • Done: Tightened the joint, checked it with an electronic leak detector (no signal after), added refrigerant to clear the sight glass, and recorded readings afterward: leaving water 45 F, subcooling 8 F.
  • Refrigerant: Added 9 lb of R-134a, circuit 1, to return the chiller to full charge. Last addition was 150 days ago per the owner's log.
  • Recommendation: Priority 2, fix soon: schedule a verification leak test and recheck the joint in 10 days. Priority 3, watch: condenser fins are dirty.
  • Not checked: Oil analysis; the second circuit was not opened.

Now the owner's compliance person can do the arithmetic from the ticket alone, using the annualizing method. Step 1: 9 lb added divided by 120 lb full charge is 0.075. Step 2: 150 days divided by 365 is 0.411. Step 3: 0.075 divided by 0.411 is 0.1825. Step 4: times 100 is about 18.3 percent. EPA's trigger for comfort cooling and other appliances is 10 percent, so under these assumptions the number is over the line and the repair-deadline questions begin. Whether a rule covers this unit is a call for the owner's compliance lead. The weak ticket would have left every one of those inputs blank.

Habits that make tickets good

  • Write while you are still on site; memory fades fast.
  • Use the same order every time so readers know where to look.
  • Do not guess pounds. Weigh or measure and write the real figure.
  • Keep it factual and polite; the owner may read it, and so may a lawyer.

Boiler plants have their own log habits; see Boiler Operator Rounds, Logs and Test Schedules. For what owners should receive at handover, see HVAC Project Closeout Documents. When a ticket is about a fault hunt, Troubleshooting shows the order of checks worth recording.

Field card: Writing a Useful HVAC Service Ticket

If the next tech cannot repeat your work from the ticket, rewrite it.

  • Customer's words first, then equipment tag, model and serial.
  • Every reading gets a unit, a location and the conditions.
  • Separate what you saw from what you concluded.
  • Refrigerant: type, pounds, circuit, reason, date.
  • Recommendations carry a priority.
  • Write down what you did not check.
  • Attach photos of nameplate, failed part and meters.
