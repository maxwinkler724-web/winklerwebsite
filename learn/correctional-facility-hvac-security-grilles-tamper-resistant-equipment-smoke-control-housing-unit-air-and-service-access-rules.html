Correctional Facility HVAC: Security Grilles, Tamper-Resistant Equipment, Smoke Control, and Service Access

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A jail housing unit is still a building with air to move, heat to remove and smoke to control. What changes is who can reach the equipment and what a person could do with a grille, a screw or a borrowed tool. This lesson explains those differences in plain terms for a tech who may someday be escorted into one.

Check the facility's own rules

Check first. Codes, state jail standards and each facility's procedures differ and change. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. It is not legal advice and not a design guide. Follow the facility's written rules and your employer's procedures.

Why the grille is different

In an ordinary room a grille is a metal face with slots. In a cell, a slot or a loose edge can become a place to tie something, or a hiding place, or a way into the duct. Public jail standards therefore treat grilles as part of suicide prevention. One state rule we read, 10A NCAC 14J .1215, says supply and return grilles in a cell used for special watch must have openings no wider than 3/16 inch, and it puts that grille item in a list of features that "decrease suicide hazards." The same list limits holes in bunk platforms to 1/8 inch. Another state's local-jail construction rule, 6VAC15-81-1060, requires security fasteners on air diffusers, thermostats and similar items in inmate-accessible areas. It exempts items behind secure locked access panels and inside secure pipe and duct chases, and equipment mounted higher than 15 feet above the floor or within six feet of the tier is also exempt.

For the tech, this means three habits. Count and keep every fastener. Use only the special driver bits the facility allows. Never leave a grille open, even for a minute, unless staff are in control of the space. Airflow math still applies, but we found no public number for the pressure drop of a small-opening grille, so use the maker's data for that.

Keep the equipment outside the secure area

A common design idea is to put the service side of the system where service people do not have to walk among residents. The Virginia rule above says the main mechanical room "shall be located outside the secure perimeter and shall be accessible from the outside," and so shall a separate maintenance shop (6VAC15-81-880). Walk-in plumbing chases must have lights and outlets for maintenance (6VAC15-81-900), and the design must allow replacement of larger mechanical equipment (6VAC15-81-890). That is why the figure below shows a pipe chase and service corridor behind the housing wall: valves, dampers and fan coils are reached from the service side, not from the cell.

Contractor escort and tool control

Tools in a secure building are treated as security items. One state corrections policy (DOC 3.1.14) says staff must directly supervise contract and non-facility workers at all times, the tool control officer inspects and inventories their tools on entry and reconciles them on exit, and the inventory sheets are kept for 30 days. The same policy has tools hang on shadow boards in a tool crib, one tool per outline. Other states have their own versions. We looked at the National Institute of Corrections jail administration topic page and found officer-safety material but no tool control procedure, so we do not cite it for this point.

Heat in housing units

CDC says people 65 and older, people with chronic medical conditions, and people without air conditioning at home are at higher risk in hot weather. CDC clinician guidance also lists medication classes that can raise heat risk, including diuretics, some psychiatric medications, beta blockers and stimulants, and it says that list is not comprehensive. People in custody cannot step outside, open a window or choose the thermostat, so the building has to do the work. A state corrections agency page we read describes staged responses keyed to temperature: at indoor readings above 90 °F, fans, extra water and ice, more nurse checks and moves to air-conditioned spaces; above 95 °F, nurse checks on each person at least every two hours. For the building side of heat plans, see our nursing home heat emergency lesson, which we do not repeat here.

Smoke control in housing

The International Building Code puts detention and correctional buildings in Group I-3. The California text of IBC Section 408 that we read says I-3 buildings use smoke barriers to split each resident floor into at least two smoke compartments, with no more than 200 residents in a compartment and at least 6 net square feet per occupant on each side of a barrier. It also says windowless buildings need an engineered smoke control system under Section 909, with an exception when an engineering analysis shows an acceptable safe egress time. Residents often cannot leave on their own, so the plan is to move people to the other compartment and keep the smoke out of the occupied side. For the tech, that means smoke dampers and control wiring are life-safety parts: see fire and smoke dampers and smoke control system testing. Never disable or bypass them to quiet a nuisance alarm.

TB and airborne isolation cells

CDC's 2006 correctional TB recommendations say people known or suspected to have TB disease should be placed in an airborne infection isolation (AII) room or cell. Air from such rooms should be exhausted directly to the outdoors, or cleaned with HEPA filters if that is not feasible. Air should flow from the corridor and supply grille across the worker and then through the patient area. Monitoring matters: CDC says failure to routinely monitor pressure differences left many rooms under positive pressure, and recommends daily smoke testing of the room even when a pressure device is installed, pressure devices sensitive to 0.001 inch of water, and measuring supply and exhaust airflow at least annually. The page we read gives no single air-change figure for AII cells, so we give none here. Design and checks are covered in our isolation room lesson.

Worked example: air and heat for one housing unit

Assumptions (ours, not code values): 40 residents, floor area 3,000 sq ft, ceiling 10 ft, and a design target of 4 air changes per hour of exhaust. Heat per person is 115 W total for an adult seated at rest, split 80 W sensible and 35 W latent at 22 °C, from the IES Virtual Environment reference data (adult male, 2 square meter body surface). We use 3.412 Btu/h per watt.

  1. Volume = 3,000 × 10 = 30,000 cu ft.
  2. Airflow = 30,000 × 4 ÷ 60 = 2,000 cfm.
  3. Occupant heat, total = 40 × 115 = 4,600 W × 3.412 = 15,695, about 15,700 Btu/h.
  4. Sensible = 40 × 80 = 3,200 W × 3.412 = 10,918, about 10,900 Btu/h. Latent = 40 × 35 = 1,400 W × 3.412 = 4,777, about 4,800 Btu/h.
  5. If all 2,000 cfm carried only the occupant sensible heat, the air temperature rise would be 10,918 ÷ (1.08 × 2,000) = 5.05 °F. See our 1.08 formula lesson.

This ignores lights, solar and envelope gains, and the outdoor air load, so a real load calculation would be larger. People in a dayroom may be more active than "seated at rest," which raises the numbers. The point is the method: volume and air changes give cfm, and headcount gives heat.

Field card: Correctional Facility HVAC: Security Grilles, Tamper-Resistant Equipment, Smoke Control, and Service Access

Fixed grilles, outside access, counted tools.

  • Cell grilles: small openings and security fasteners; one state rule says 3/16 inch maximum for special-watch cells.
  • Service equipment sits outside the secure perimeter where the design allows.
  • Staff supervise contractors at all times; tools are inventoried in and out.
  • Heat: CDC lists older adults and chronic conditions as higher risk; medications matter too.
  • I-3 housing uses smoke compartments and, in windowless buildings, engineered smoke control.
  • TB cells: exhaust outdoors or HEPA, daily smoke test, pressure monitoring.
  • Our example: 30,000 cu ft at 4 ACH is 2,000 cfm; 40 people give about 15,700 Btu/h.
