Pump-Down Cycle Control: Liquid Line Solenoids, Low-Pressure Settings, Short Cycling and Manual Pump-Down

Last updated: 2026-10-16

On many walk-in coolers and split systems the thermostat does not start the compressor. It controls a solenoid valve in the liquid line, and a low-pressure control on the suction line does the starting and stopping of the compressor. This lesson covers why that is done, how to pick the cut-out and cut-in pressures, what makes it short cycle, and how a manual pump-down for service differs.

Read the unit's own instructions first

Pressures, delays and wiring differ by maker and model. Anything marked "our own" below is our trade practice or worked example, not a rule. Check the wiring diagram and the maker's literature before you change a control.

The sequence, step by step

Here is the usual arrangement, shown in the diagram.

  1. The box warms. The thermostat calls and energizes the liquid line solenoid valve (LLSV), which opens.
  2. Liquid flows through the expansion valve into the evaporator. Suction pressure rises above the low-pressure control's cut-in point, so its contacts close and the compressor starts.
  3. The box reaches setpoint. The thermostat opens and the LLSV closes, so liquid stops entering the coil.
  4. The compressor keeps running and pulls the refrigerant out of the evaporator, moving it toward the condenser and receiver. Suction pressure falls.
  5. At the cut-out pressure the low-pressure control opens and the compressor stops, with most of the charge on the high side.

The solenoid valve itself, its coil, and its pressure-drop ratings are covered in Refrigeration Solenoid Valves.

Why keep the refrigerant out of the compressor

An academic paper on refrigerant migration says vapor moves to the coldest part of the system during an idle period, and after a long off-cycle the compressor shell can be that cold spot. Refrigerant condenses there and dissolves in the oil. The authors say that thins the oil, makes it foam, and can lead to liquid slugging and poor lubrication at startup. Their remedy is an oil heater; see Crankcase Heaters and Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging.

A pump-down cycle attacks the same problem from the other side. If the charge is stored in the condenser and receiver at the end of every cycle, there is little liquid left on the low side to migrate into the crankcase. A university extension report on marine refrigeration gives the same reason for a shutdown procedure: close the king valve and pump the system partly down so liquid does not reach the crankcase at startup and push oil out.

Single pump-down versus continuous (recycling)

Our own trade practice, with the terms as we use them:

  • Single pump-down. The compressor stops at cut-out and stays off until the thermostat opens the solenoid again. Wiring is the low-pressure control in series with the compressor contactor, and the thermostat feeds only the solenoid. The control is set so that pressure creeping up while the solenoid is closed does not reach cut-in.
  • Continuous or recycling pump-down. Any pressure rise above cut-in while the solenoid is closed (warming coil, oil releasing refrigerant, a small leak) restarts the compressor for a short run. It is protective but costs starts, so frequent recycling is a reason to check settings and valves.

Setting cut-out and cut-in

A low-pressure control opens at its cut-out pressure and closes at a higher cut-in pressure; the gap is the differential. A federal sample exam question shows the arithmetic: a cut-in of 5 psig and a differential of 7.5 psi gives a cut-out of 5 minus 7.5, or -2.5 psig, which is about 5 inches of mercury vacuum. So cut-out equals cut-in minus differential. Our own working rules, to be checked against the maker's limits for the compressor:

  • Cut-in must sit below the saturation pressure at the box temperature where the thermostat calls, or the compressor can never start when the solenoid opens.
  • Cut-out must sit well below the normal running suction pressure at the coldest box and coil conditions, or the control will stop the unit during normal running.
  • A higher cut-in tends toward single pump-down. A lower one tends toward recycling.

Worked example (our own): 35 F walk-in cooler, R-404A

Assumptions, all ours. Box setpoint 35 F with a 4 F thermostat swing (closes the solenoid at 33 F, opens it at 37 F). Evaporating temperature while running 15 F below the box, so 20 F. Pressures are read from a refrigerant maker's published R-404A pressure-temperature chart (cited in plain text, not linked), which lists a "liquid" and a "vapor" column. We use the vapor column for the coil. Confirm every number on the chart for your refrigerant and on your own gauges.

  1. Running suction: 20 F evaporating. The chart gives 55.3 psig (vapor column).
  2. Pressure at the box when the thermostat calls: 37 F. The chart gives 80.3 psig (vapor column). When the solenoid opens, the coil warms toward that value, so suction rises toward it.
  3. Pick cut-in: 70 psig. The chart gives 69.3 psig at 30 F (vapor column), so 70 psig is about 30 F saturation. Check: 80.3 minus 70 = 10.3 psi of headroom, so the compressor will start when the solenoid opens.
  4. With the solenoid closed and the box at 33 F, the chart gives 73.9 psig (vapor column), 3.9 psi above cut-in, so leftover liquid warming up could restart the compressor (recycling). Step 2 limits how high cut-in can go.
  5. Pick cut-out: 20 psig. The chart rows we read start at 0 F, which is 32.6 psig (vapor column), so 20 psig is colder than 0 F saturation. Check: 55.3 minus 20 = 35.3 psi of margin below normal running suction.
  6. Differential: 70 minus 20 = 50 psi. Check: cut-out = cut-in minus differential = 70 - 50 = 20 psig, matching step 5.

R-448A instead. We do not reuse the R-404A numbers for R-448A. A national-lab report on a blend with the R-448A composition gives a bubble-point pressure of 717.1 kPa absolute at 277.6 K (40.0 F). Converting, 717.1 kPa is 104.0 psia, or about 89.3 psig. The same maker chart lists 86.9 psig (liquid column) for R-404A at 40 F, so R-448A runs about 2.4 psi higher at that one point. The same report lists a temperature glide of 5.74 K, about 10.3 F (our conversion) so read the right bubble or dew column on the maker's R-448A chart before copying our example.

Short cycling from leaks

Our own field logic: with the solenoid closed and the compressor off, suction pressure should stay near the pumped-down value or climb slowly. Two leaks raise it fast.

  • Leaking liquid line solenoid. Liquid creeps past the seat into the coil, pressure climbs to cut-in, the compressor runs briefly and pumps it out, then it repeats. Starts happen with the thermostat satisfied. Debris on the seat or a worn seat are the usual suspects.
  • Leaking compressor valves. High-side gas leaks back to the low side after shutdown and lifts suction pressure. An Air Force training guide, hosted in a federal education database, says high suction pressure most likely points to a leaking suction valve and describes checking it by front seating the suction service valve and watching the low-side gauge. The same guide says low head pressure most often means leaking discharge valves. A Coast Guard sample exam also keys "higher than normal suction pressure" as the sign of leaking suction valves.

Gauge checks

With gauges on the suction port, satisfy the thermostat and watch suction fall to cut-out. Then wait. A slow rise is normal; a quick return to cut-in points to the solenoid or compressor valves. Closing the king valve (below) separates them: if pressure still rises with liquid cut off, suspect the compressor.

Manual pump-down for service

Government and university sources point to the king valve as the isolation point. A Coast Guard sample exam question keys "close the king valve" as the way to start a pump-down when testing the low-pressure cut-out. The same exam keys storing the whole charge in the receiver for lay-up. Our own general steps, which you adapt to the equipment:

  1. Install gauges. With the compressor running, close the king valve slowly (clockwise).
  2. Watch the low-side gauge fall. Many systems stop at cut-out; the maker's procedure governs any lower pull.
  3. Stop the compressor and watch whether the low side stays down.
  4. Isolate the part you are working on, and follow the recovery and refrigerant-handling rules that apply to you before opening the circuit.

Cautions. A university extension report notes that suction going into vacuum is bad from a leakage standpoint, because air can be drawn in. Keep the low side positive unless the maker's procedure says otherwise. Do not leave liquid trapped between closed valves; see Liquid Receivers in Commercial Refrigeration for receiver capacity. When you finish, open the king valve fully (back seat it) before restoring power. For how pressure controls behave and are tested, read AC High and Low Pressure Switches.

Field card: Pump-Down Cycle Control

Thermostat closes the solenoid, the low-pressure control stops the compressor.

  • Order: thermostat opens the liquid line solenoid, suction rises, compressor starts at cut-in; thermostat satisfied, solenoid closes, compressor pumps the coil out until cut-out.
  • Why: keeps liquid refrigerant out of the crankcase during the off cycle.
  • Cut-out = cut-in minus differential.
  • Cut-in below the saturation pressure at the thermostat call temperature; cut-out well below running suction.
  • Our example, R-404A 35 F box: cut-in 70, cut-out 20, differential 50 psig.
  • Fast rise after shutdown: suspect the solenoid or compressor valves.
  • Service: king valve closed, compressor running, watch the low side, follow the maker.
