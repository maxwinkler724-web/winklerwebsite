1 Inch vs 4 Inch Furnace Filter: Pressure Drop and Change Intervals

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A 1 inch filter and a 4 inch media filter can carry the same MERV number and still behave very differently in your ductwork. The thick one usually pushes back on the blower far less. This lesson uses published maker numbers to show how much less, and what else changes when you add a cabinet.

Same MERV, different resistance

MERV is a rating of how well the media catches particles. EPA's MERV page says it comes from an ASHRAE test method and covers particles from 0.3 to 10 microns. The rating does not tell you how hard the blower must work to pull air through the filter. That is a separate number, the pressure drop, in inches of water column (in. w.g. or in. w.c.). The basics of that unit are in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Why does depth matter? Pleats fold the media like an accordion. A deeper frame holds deeper pleats and more total media inside the same face opening. The same airflow then passes through more media area, so it moves slower through each square inch, and the resistance is lower. AAF's data sheets for its Pre Pleat MERV 13 line show it. The 25x25x1 size lists 8.3 square feet of media, and the 28x30x4 size lists 32.6 square feet. Divide each by its face area (nominal width times length) and you get about 1.9 square feet of media per square foot of face for the 1 inch, and about 5.6 for the 4 inch, nearly three times as much.

The same sheets give the initial resistance at 300 feet per minute (fpm) of face velocity: 0.25 in. w.g. for the 1 inch MERV 13 and 0.13 in. w.g. for the 4 inch MERV 13, about half. At 500 fpm the 4 inch is listed at 0.23 and the 1 inch has no value. Those are clean-filter numbers. Resistance climbs as dust loads the media, and AAF recommends a final resistance of 1.0 in. w.g. for all depths. Your furnace will not tolerate anything near that, as the worked example shows.

What the cabinet makers publish

A media cabinet adds its own pieces, so the makers give cabinet-plus-filter numbers. Aprilaire's 2000 series sheet lists the 2210, a 20x25 unit with 31.4 square feet of MERV 13 media, at 0.08 in. w.c. at 1,000 CFM, 0.11 at 1,200 and 0.22 at 2,000. Its older 2210/2410 sheet says new media runs from 0.06 in. w.c. at 800 CFM up to 0.27 at 2,000 CFM and recommends airflow up to 2,000 CFM. Honeywell's F100 installation manual lists a MERV 8 media filter at 0.177 in. wc at 492 fpm, while its product data lists the MERV 11 media at 0.23 in. w.c. at 500 fpm. Same cabinet family, so stepping up in efficiency cost pressure. Higher MERV in any depth tends to cost some resistance.

Sizing matters. Honeywell lists maximum airflow by cabinet size: 1,200 CFM for 16x20, 1,400 for 16x25 and 20x20, and 2,000 for 20x25. Lennox's PureAir S cabinet (a MERV 16 filter system, not the same as a plain media cabinet) shows the smaller sizes climbing from 0.15 in. w.g. at 1,000 CFM to 0.25 at 1,400, and Lennox marks the 14 inch model "not recommended" above 1,400 CFM and the 16 inch model above 1,600 because of excess pressure drop. Carrier's filter cabinet manual ties its 16 inch cabinet to systems of 4 tons of cooling or less, with 20 or 24 inch cabinets for larger ones, and warns that the 4-5/16 inch media filter can cut airflow by up to 7 percent. Read your blower's CFM from the furnace table, then pick the cabinet whose limit is above it.

Worked example: how much of the furnace's budget each one uses

Take a 20x25 filter face. A furnace maker states how much external static pressure the whole duct system, filter included, may add. Lennox's G40UH installation instructions say not to run that furnace above 0.5 in. w.c. external static, and its newer EL296DF lists 0.8 in. w.g. for heating. We use 0.5 as the conservative budget.

  1. Face area: 20 in. x 25 in. = 500 sq in. Divide by 144 sq in. per sq ft = 3.47 sq ft.
  2. Airflow at 300 fpm: 3.47 sq ft x 300 fpm = about 1,040 CFM. (AAF uses the same method: its 10x20 size at 300 fpm is 417 CFM.)
  3. 1 inch MERV 13: 0.25 in. w.g. 0.25 / 0.5 = 0.50, so half the budget is gone before the coil, ducts or grilles.
  4. 4 inch MERV 13 (AAF): 0.13 / 0.5 = 0.26, so about a quarter.
  5. Aprilaire 2210 cabinet at 1,000 CFM: 0.08 / 0.5 = 0.16, so about 16 percent. This is another maker tested its own way, so use it as a rough comparison, not a head-to-head.

Against the EL296DF's 0.8 heating figure the shares are 31, 16 and 10 percent. The deeper filter frees up about 0.12 in. w.g. (0.25 minus 0.13) at the same airflow, which is a large gain on a ductwork budget this small. All figures are clean-filter values; a loaded filter uses more. Lennox's furnace instructions warn that dirty or plugged filters will trip the limit control.

Bypass and gasket leaks

Air takes the easiest path. A filter that does not fill its slot, or a cabinet door that does not seal, lets air skip the media entirely. The fit basics are in Furnace Filter Size, Direction and Fit. For cabinets, makers address it directly. Aprilaire says its SelfSeal media reduces bypass. Honeywell says the redesigned door and corners on its current cabinets are tighter, which cuts the need for added sealant, and its manual says to seal all joints between the cabinet and furnace. Carrier's cabinet manual recommends silicone or mastic on joints and foam tape gaskets on the flanges and door.

Why a high-MERV 1 inch can choke a system

Put the numbers together. A dense 1 inch MERV 13 can use half of a 0.5 in. w.c. budget when clean, and the filter only gets worse as it loads. Less air crosses the coil and heat exchanger, so you see the problems in the static pressure lesson: icing, overheating and limit trips. EPA suggests MERV 13 or the highest your fan and slot can handle, and says most systems manage MERV 13 if the filter is changed often. The honest answer is that a 1 inch MERV 13 may work in some systems and not in others. If your goal is better filtration, the thicker frame is the way to get it with less resistance, and a technician can measure static pressure before and after.

Change intervals by maker

  • Honeywell F100 manual: every six months, at least annually, or when the drop reaches 0.5 in. w.c. Resideo's page says 6 to 12 months.
  • Aprilaire 213 (MERV 13): 12 months on the store page. An older sheet says one to two years depending on airflow.
  • Carrier cabinet: media filter before each heating and cooling season, at least once a year. The 1 inch cleanable filter: check monthly.
  • Lennox PureAir S: annual filter replacement, sooner in heavy dust.
  • Trane: most filters every 1 to 3 months. Its Comfort Performance 1 inch filter page says every three months.
  • EPA: manufacturers typically say 60 to 90 days for common filters. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter.

A thick filter lasts longer because it has more media to load, but follow the label and look at it. Smoke, pets and construction dust shorten any interval; see Wildfire Smoke Days.

Installing a cabinet

  • Put it on the return side. Honeywell's manual says return duct only, never the supply.
  • Match arrows. Carrier says the arrows on the cabinet sections must agree, and Honeywell says the cartridge arrow must point the way air flows.
  • Leave clearance. Honeywell asks for at least 26 inches in front to slide the filter out, and gradual transitions of no more than 20 degrees per side.
  • Seal every joint with mastic or the tape the maker names, and do not run without the filter or door.
  • Remove the old 1 inch filter and clean the blower compartment first (Honeywell), and ask a technician to check static pressure afterward.

Field card: 1 in. vs 4 in. Filters

Same MERV, but depth sets the resistance.

  • Deeper pleats hold more media, so air moves slower and the drop is lower.
  • AAF MERV 13 at 300 fpm: 1 in. 0.25, 4 in. 0.13 in. w.g.
  • Face area x fpm = CFM. A 20x25 face at 300 fpm is about 1,040 CFM.
  • Check the cabinet maker's CFM limit against your blower.
  • Seal every joint, match the arrows, return side only.
  • Intervals run from 1 to 3 months for 1 in. up to 6 to 12 months for media. Follow the label.
