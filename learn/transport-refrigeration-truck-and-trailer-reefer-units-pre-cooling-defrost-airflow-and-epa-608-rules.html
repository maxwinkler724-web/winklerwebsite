Reefer Units: Pre-Cooling, Defrost, Airflow and EPA 608

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A reefer is a refrigerated truck body or trailer with its own cooling unit. The unit is built to hold product at temperature on the road, not to pull warm product down. This lesson covers how the unit moves air, why the empty box gets pre-cooled, what defrost does, and which federal rules apply.

Check the rules that apply to you

General education only. Federal, state and local rules change. This page was built from agency and maker pages read in 2026. Confirm current requirements with the agency, your employer and the unit maker before you act on anything here.

What the unit is

EPA describes a transport refrigeration unit, or TRU, as the equipment that keeps cargo cold in refrigerated trucks and trailers, and says most are powered by small diesel engines. Newer designs can also plug into grid power while parked at a warehouse or store. That plug-in mode is often called electric standby. The cooling side is the same vapor compression cycle you meet in any refrigeration job, with the evaporator inside the box and the condenser outside; see Refrigeration Cycle Basics. EPA's low-GWP fact sheet says road vehicles, trailers and intermodal containers typically hold charges of roughly 4.5 to 7.5 kg, and that R-404A and other HFCs were common choices.

Pre-cool the empty box, but do not count on the unit to cool warm product

The USDA handbook on protecting perishable foods in truck transport says to set the thermostat to the transit temperature, close the doors and run the unit until heat flow through the body settles. It says that can take two hours or more in hot summer weather. It also prefers that carriers accept only product that is already at temperature, because otherwise the unit has to absorb the extra heat. Units have some reserve capacity, but the handbook says that if product is well above transit temperature, the load should be estimated and the trailer not used when the load exceeds the unit's rating.

The reason is simple. A reefer is sized to remove the heat that leaks in through the body and doors during the trip. A pallet of warm product dumps far more heat than that into the air stream, so the box sits warm while the unit runs flat out. The top layer near the discharge can freeze while the middle stays warm.

Pre-cooling is also part of the federal food rule. Under the FDA Sanitary Transportation rule (21 CFR Part 1, Subpart O), a shipper of food that needs temperature control must give the carrier a written operating temperature, including any pre-cooling phase (section 1.908(b)(2)). A carrier using a vehicle with an auxiliary refrigeration unit must pre-cool each mechanically refrigerated compartment to the temperature the shipper specified before offering it for the load (section 1.908(e)(3)). FDA's summary page lists exemptions, including businesses under $500,000 in average annual revenue, so check whether the rule covers a particular shipment.

Airflow and load pattern

In the common top-air trailer, the unit discharges cold air into a chute along the ceiling. The air moves toward the rear, spills down through gaps around the load, and returns along the floor to the bottom of the bulkhead behind the unit. The USDA handbook calls airflow one of the most important factors in keeping product at temperature and gives these practical points:

  • Keep the bulkhead at least 3 inches from the front wall, with at least 6 inches of open space below its bottom edge for return air.
  • Keep a gap at the top of the bulkhead to mix air and feed return air to the controller; the handbook gives 30 to 50 square inches of open area.
  • Keep chutes intact. Holes or missing sections let air short-circuit over the load, freezing the top layer and leaving warm spots below.
  • Do not load so high that the chute is crushed. The handbook suggests a painted maximum load line on the walls.
  • Keep at least 4 inches between the load and the rear doors for return air, and brace the load so it cannot shift and block airflow.
  • Place palletized loads in rows off the walls. The handbook says ribbed walls or spacers at least 1 inch thick let air flow down the sides.

For a technician, the takeaway is to look at the chute and the load pattern as well as the unit when temperature is off.

Defrost and door-open losses

Frost builds on the evaporator coil as moisture condenses and freezes. One maker's operator manual explains that the unit melts it by passing hot refrigerant gas through the coil, and the meltwater drains out through tubes. Cycles can start on a timer or on demand. In that manual, timed intervals are programmable from 2 to 12 hours, and demand defrost starts when the differences among return air, discharge air and coil temperatures pass set limits. A manual defrost is allowed only when coil temperature is below 45°F. Details differ by maker, so use the manual for the unit in front of you. For the same ideas in a building, see Refrigerated Warehouses and Freezer Buildings and Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics.

Doors matter just as much. The USDA handbook notes that cumulative door-open time on multi-stop deliveries can reach an hour or more in warm weather, and advises shutting the unit off when loading from an open dock so ice does not form on the coil and block airflow. Check that the doors seal when closed.

Worked example: heat leaking through the walls

This is a rough estimate of conduction through the body only. The surface area and the U-value are our own assumptions, not measured values.

  1. Assumed surface area (Our own): a 53 foot trailer with an inside of about 52 ft long, 8.2 ft wide and 8.8 ft high. Area of all six surfaces = 2 x (52 x 8.2 + 52 x 8.8 + 8.2 x 8.8) = 2 x (426.4 + 457.6 + 72.2) = about 1,912 ft². We round to 1,900 ft².
  2. Assumed U-value (Our own): 0.5 W/m²K. This is an assumption chosen inside the range in a Cemafroid study summarized by the International Institute of Refrigeration: tested in-service bodies were far under 0.7 W/m²K, and about 30 percent were below 0.4. Converted with 1 W/m²K = 0.17612 Btu/h·ft²·°F (our conversion), U = 0.5 x 0.17612 = 0.0881 Btu/h·ft²·°F.
  3. Assumed temperatures (Our own): outside 95°F, box held at 34°F, so ΔT = 95 - 34 = 61°F. We ignore sun on the roof and walls, air leakage, the product and the fan heat.
  4. Heat gain: Q = U x A x ΔT = 0.0881 x 1,900 x 61 = about 10,200 Btu/h.
  5. In tons: one ton of refrigeration is 12,000 Btu/h, so 10,200 / 12,000 = about 0.85 ton.

If the body has aged and U is 0.7 W/m²K (0.1233 Btu/h·ft²·°F), the same math gives about 14,300 Btu/h, or 1.2 tons. Real loads are higher once sun, door openings and product heat are added, which is why the handbook tells carriers to estimate the full load when product arrives warm.

Which EPA rule covers the refrigerant side

Section 609 is the motor vehicle air conditioning program. EPA's rule at 40 CFR 82.32 defines a motor vehicle air conditioner as equipment used to cool the driver's or passenger's compartment, and the definition leaves out sealed refrigeration systems for refrigerated cargo. The Section 608 rule (40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F) defines an appliance as any device that contains and uses a refrigerant for household or commercial purposes. Reading the two together, we understand a cargo reefer to fall under Section 608 and not under Section 609. EPA's Section 609 summary also has a chart that lists refrigerated trailers, and its text does not clearly say how each vehicle type is sorted, so confirm with EPA or your certifying organization if a specific unit is in question. For the basics of 608 certification and refrigerant handling, see EPA 608 Certification Basics.

California note

CARB's page says its TRU program covers diesel-powered transport refrigeration units on truck vans, semi-trailers, shipping containers and railcars, under 13 CCR sections 2477 to 2477.24. It applies in California only. That is an engine emissions rule, separate from the refrigerant rules above.

Field card: Reefer Units: Pre-Cooling, Defrost, Airflow and EPA 608

Pre-cool empty, load cold, keep the air moving.

  • The unit holds temperature. It is not sized to pull warm product down.
  • Pre-cool the empty box at the shipper's temperature; allow 2 hours or more in hot weather per USDA.
  • Top-air: discharge along the ceiling chute, return at the bottom of the bulkhead.
  • Keep chutes intact and leave return gaps at bulkhead, ceiling and rear doors.
  • Defrost is hot gas through the coil, by timer or on demand; follow the unit manual.
  • FDA Subpart O: shipper specifies temperature and pre-cool; carrier pre-cools.
  • Cargo refrigeration is outside the MVAC definition; Section 608 applies to the refrigerant work.
  • Our worked estimate: about 10,200 Btu/h (0.85 ton) body conduction at 95°F outside and 34°F inside, with assumed inputs.
