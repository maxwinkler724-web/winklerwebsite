Solar Thermal Water Heating: Collectors, Glycol and Drainback Loops, Controllers, Stagnation and Freeze Protection

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A solar thermal system uses sunlight to heat water directly, with no electricity generation at all. It is a different thing from solar PV panels, which are covered in Solar Panels and Heat Pumps. This lesson walks through the collector types, the loop types, the controller that runs the pump, and the three things that go wrong most: summer overheating, freezing and scalding.

Check the manual first

Check the manual. Every collector, tank and controller has its own limits for temperature, pressure and fluid. This page is general education from federal, laboratory and university sources read in 2026. Follow the maker's instructions and your local rules for anything you install or service.

Flat-plate and evacuated-tube collectors

A flat-plate collector is an insulated, weatherproof box holding a dark absorber plate under glass, with fluid tubes attached to the plate (North Carolina State University clean-energy guide). The U.S. DOE Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) technology alert puts flat-plate collectors in the mid-temperature class, generally heating water up to about 160 degrees F. An evacuated-tube collector is a bank of glass tubes with a vacuum between the layers, which cuts heat loss. FEMP lists evacuated tubes as reaching up to 350 degrees F. The federal ENERGY STAR page calls them the most efficient type, notes that they keep working in overcast weather and that single tubes can be replaced, and says they cost about twice as much per square foot as flat-plate. The NC State guide cautions that in warmer regions flat-plate collectors can match or beat the yearly efficiency of evacuated tubes.

Passive systems: batch and thermosiphon

Passive systems have no pump. A batch (integrated collector-storage) unit heats water in dark tanks or tubes inside an insulated box and stores it there until it is used. ENERGY STAR says the water can get very hot when demand is low, so a tempering valve mixes in cold water, and that batch units are not compatible with closed-loop designs and are generally not recommended for cold climates. A thermosiphon system relies on natural convection: warmer water becomes lighter and rises to the tank, so the tank normally has to sit above the collector (our own plain-language note). The NC State guide says direct thermosiphon collectors generally cannot tolerate freezing, indirect thermosiphon collectors are rated freeze protected to 19 degrees F, and a batch system does not give adequate freeze protection where temperatures fall below 10 degrees F. FEMP adds that indirect thermosiphon systems offer no stagnation protection.

Active closed-loop glycol systems

Active systems use electric pumps and controllers, which lets the tank sit away from the collectors (NC State guide). In a closed-loop (indirect) design, a non-freezing fluid circulates through the collector and gives up its heat through a heat exchanger to the potable water in the storage tank. FEMP says these antifreeze systems usually circulate non-toxic propylene glycol. The loop is pressurized, so it needs fill and drain valves (NC State guide). For the expansion tank side see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.

Drainback systems

A drainback loop is also closed, but its fluid is usually distilled water. When the pump stops, gravity pulls the fluid out of the collector and piping into an unpressurized tank, and the collectors and piping must be sloped so they empty completely (NC State guide). ENERGY STAR and the NC State guide agree that no electric valves or expansion tanks are needed. The Florida Solar Energy Center manual adds that the pump must overcome gravity as well as friction. FEMP notes that drainback can use demineralized water, so scaling on the heat exchanger is less of a worry, and its freeze-tolerance table rates drainback good and glycol flat-plate excellent.

The differential controller

The controller is a temperature comparer. The Florida manual describes one sensor on top of the collector, the hottest point, and one at the bottom of the storage tank, the coldest point. When the collector is warmer than the tank bottom by the on differential, the pump runs. The manual says the on setting can be 5 to 20 degrees F, with 9, 12, 15 or 20 as installer choices on some controllers, and that the pump stops when the difference falls to about 3 to 5 degrees F. FEMP describes a typical start at about 12 degrees F. The gap between on and off keeps the pump from short cycling when the difference hovers near the start point (our own note).

Many controllers also have a high limit that stops the pump at a preset tank temperature. The Florida manual stresses that this reading is the temperature at the bottom of the tank, which may be cooler than the top. A separate freeze sensor, where used, goes on the collector plate at the middle or bottom of the collector. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) suggests a weekly check that fluid leaving the collector is warmer than fluid entering it.

Stagnation and summer overheating

Stagnation means the pump is off but the sun is still on the collector. The Florida manual says collectors must tolerate stagnation temperatures and that some reach 400 degrees F. In a glycol loop the fluid can boil and flash to steam; PNNL says the expansion tank takes the pressure and should be drained and flushed annually. FEMP warns that stagnation damages antifreeze fluid and that glycol should be replaced sooner if the system has excess capacity and runs hot. It also advises sizing a cost-effective system to meet at most about two-thirds of total hot water use. FEMP says solar-heated water can reach 180 degrees F, which is the reason tempering matters. The Florida manual says a tank temperature-and-pressure relief valve is usually set at 150 psi and 210 degrees F by the maker. If a system trips its relief valve in summer, our own advice is to look at the controller, pump and sensors and ask the maker, rather than adjusting the valve.

Glycol breakdown and pH checks

Glycol does not last forever. FEMP says it should be replaced every 5 to 10 years because the corrosion inhibitors break down. The NC State guide says to inspect it every 1 to 2 years and replace it when it becomes too acidic, and PNNL lists an annual pH test with adjustment or replacement as needed, on the maker's replacement schedule. The Florida manual says pH can be checked with test strips or a color chart. We do not give a numeric pass-fail pH here, since the sources we read did not state one: use the fluid maker's limit.

Freeze protection

The NC State guide points out that collectors can freeze on a clear night even when the air stays above 32 degrees F, because they radiate heat to the sky, and that most of the Southeast must use drainback or glycol systems. FEMP's table rates unglazed and direct thermosiphon collectors as no freeze tolerance, batch and indirect thermosiphon as moderate, drainback as good and glycol flat-plate as excellent. In our own words: drainback relies on the fluid leaving the collector, while glycol relies on the fluid being strong enough to stay liquid. For more on testing glycol strength see Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops.

Tempering valves and the backup heater

Hot water from a solar tank can be much hotter than a conventional heater is set to. The Alabama Fire College writes that 120 degrees F takes about 5 minutes to cause a severe burn, but 140 degrees F can do it in about 5 seconds. FEMP describes mixing solar-heated water with cold water in a tempering valve. A mixing valve on the outlet (see the figure) holds the supply to the fixtures near the setting; check the valve with a thermometer at a tap after it is installed and again after any service. See Central Domestic Hot Water Plants for mixing valves in bigger buildings.

The backup heater covers nights and cloudy spells. ENERGY STAR lists an electric or gas tank or a tankless unit as options. In a one-tank design the Florida manual says the electric element is deliberately placed in the upper third of the tank, and FEMP says systems generally keep a conventional heater as backup. For a boiler feeding an indirect tank see Indirect Water Heaters on a Boiler.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

Every input below is an assumption chosen for practice, not data from a real house. Energy to heat water is gallons x 8.33 lb per gallon x temperature rise in degrees F, in Btu.

  1. Draw. Assume 4 people x 16 gallons = 64 gallons a day. Assume 55 F cold water and 120 F delivered, so the rise is 65 F.
  2. Daily energy. 64 x 8.33 x 65 = 34,653 Btu per day.
  3. Solar share. Assume a solar fraction of 60 percent. 34,653 x 0.60 = 20,792 Btu from the sun.
  4. Backup left over. 34,653 - 20,792 = 13,861 Btu. As electric resistance at 3,412 Btu per kWh, that is 13,861 / 3,412 = 4.06 kWh a day. If the backup is gas at an assumed 60 percent efficiency, the fuel input is 13,861 / 0.60 = 23,102 Btu.
  5. Sanity check on sizing. Assume two 32 square foot collectors, 64 square feet. The NC State guide says 40 to 72 square feet and 60 to 140 gallons of storage for a family of four, so 64 square feet with an assumed 80 gallon tank fits, and the tank holds more than the 64 gallon daily draw, which FEMP and ENERGY STAR describe as roughly one day of use. The 60 percent fraction is also inside the 50 to 80 percent range in the NC State guide.

Collector loop flow check (assumed). Use Btu/h = 500 x gpm x temperature rise for water, and assume the glycol mix carries 0.9 of water's heat per gallon. Assume a clear midday gain of 150 Btu/h per square foot, so 64 x 150 = 9,600 Btu/h. For a 15 F rise through the collector: gpm = 9,600 / (500 x 0.9 x 15) = 9,600 / 6,750 = 1.42 gpm, about 0.022 gpm per square foot. If a clogged strainer or trapped air drops flow to 0.7 gpm, the rise becomes 9,600 / (450 x 0.7) = 30.5 F. The collector runs hotter and wastes heat, and a rise far above what you expect is a clue to look for flow trouble. These figures are our teaching numbers; use the maker's flow range for a real job.

Field card: Solar thermal water heating

Sun to collector, collector to coil, coil to tank, tank to a mixing valve.

  • Flat-plate: up to about 160 F. Evacuated tube: up to about 350 F. Both can stagnate far hotter.
  • Controller: top-of-collector vs bottom-of-tank sensors; on around 12 F, off around 3 to 5 F.
  • Glycol: check pH every year, replace per the maker; FEMP says 5 to 10 years, sooner if it runs hot.
  • Drainback: slope the pipes, keep the tank level correct, use the water type the maker names.
  • Freeze: glycol and drainback, not direct or batch, in freezing climates.
  • Scald: 140 F water can burn in about 5 seconds. Install and test a mixing valve.
  • Our own: daily Btu = gallons x 8.33 x rise; backup = total minus solar share.
