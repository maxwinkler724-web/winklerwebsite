Outdoor Wood Boilers at the House: Pipe Loss, Heat Exchangers, Controls and Smoke

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An outdoor wood-fired hydronic heater is a firebox in a shed with a short stack, sending hot water through buried pipe to a house. The unit is outside, but the house side still has coils, pumps, controls and a backup furnace that need the same care as any hot-water system. This lesson follows the water from the firebox to the house and puts a number on what the buried pipe costs in wood.

Check local rules first

Check local rules first. State and local rules apply to where these units may be placed, how tall the stack must be and whether new units are allowed at all. This page is general education, not legal advice. Ask your local building and air-quality offices before you plan an installation.

How the unit makes hot water

A conventional unit is a firebox surrounded by a water jacket, inside a weatherproof cabinet with a short smokestack, as one state air agency describes it. Fire heats the steel walls, the walls heat the water in the jacket, and a circulator moves that water to the house. The federal environmental agency adds that these units can be inside or outside a building, and that outdoor models look like a small shed with a short stack. Most are sold for rural, cold-climate areas where wood is easy to get.

One maker's owner manual says water below 150°F can cause condensation inside the firebox, which leads to corrosion. That is why the supply and return lines in that manual use a thermostatic valve to keep the unit water warm, with a recommended setpoint of 185°F. Treat that as one maker's instruction, not a rule for every unit, and follow the manual for the unit in front of you.

Open versus pressurized systems

The same manual says the unit must not be pressurized: the vent cap sits loosely on the vent pipe, and automatic float vents elsewhere in the system must be capped off tightly. So many of these units are open, vented systems, which differs from the sealed, pressurized boiler loops in most houses (see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure). If the unit water boils, the manual says to check the level and restore it to the full mark. It also says the water jacket can deteriorate if water is added too often, so the manual expects topping off no more than about once a year. Frequent refills point to a leak or repeated boiling.

Because the unit is open to air, it is usual to keep house piping and the unit loop apart with a heat exchanger. Do not connect an open unit loop directly to a pressurized house loop unless the maker's instructions say how.

Antifreeze and water treatment

The manual calls for a nontoxic propylene glycol boiler-type antifreeze, never automotive or RV antifreeze, mixed with softened water to at least 30 percent, and checked after 24 hours of circulation to confirm protection to 0°F or lower. It also calls for a corrosion inhibitor at the first fill, good-quality water and a retest after any water addition. Glycol changes pump and heat-delivery numbers, which Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops explains.

Buried pipe and heat loss

The insulated supply and return pipes are the part of the system that loses heat all season. A University of Maryland Extension fact sheet reports research that found a 20 percent Btu loss over a 100-foot run of well-insulated pipe, and says the ground, at about 54°F, draws heat out of poorly insulated lines. It advises placing the unit as close to the home as is workable. The same maker's manual calls for trenching at 10 to 28 inches deep, air-testing lines at 50 psi before backfill, and avoiding drain tile or other makeshift insulation. It also warns against burying other piping where water stands or the water table is very high.

Getting heat into the house

There are two common connections. A water-to-air coil mounts in the plenum or duct of an existing furnace, so the furnace blower pushes house air through hot water instead of through a flame. The manual says the coil must be level front to back and notes that a snap disc may be needed so the air conditioner does not run while the coil water is above 140°F. Our lesson on hydro-air coils covers how those coils behave.

The other is a flat plate heat exchanger, which keeps the unit water and the house water in separate loops, either to a closed boiler loop or to domestic water. The manual says water-to-water exchangers deliver temperatures about 20°F below the unit water. Plan emitters and controls around that lost 20°F.

Circulator and aquastat controls

The maker's manual says at least one circulator must run continuously and should sit in the supply line with its arrow pointing away from the unit. At the house, a water temperature switch (an aquastat) or a thermostat call normally decides when the house-side pump or the plenum blower runs. For how those switches work on ordinary boilers, see Boiler Controls, Aquastats and Cutoffs. Keep the house-side controls separate from the unit's own controller, and follow each maker's wiring instructions.

When the power fails

A wood fire does not stop when the electricity does. The manual says that during a long outage a generator can keep the lines from freezing, and that a backup heat source should be in place if the unit runs out of fuel, is unattended or needs service. If the water boils, check the level afterward and bring it back to full. Heat that cannot be carried away is how a unit overheats, so a pump that stops while the fire is burning deserves attention right away. Our lesson on winter outages and carbon monoxide covers the generator side.

Smoke, stack height and setbacks

The federal environmental agency says these units make heavy smoke released close to the ground, because of low, smoldering fires and short stacks, and that their stacks are usually not above the roofline. Its figures: most unqualified units emit about 2 pounds of fine particles per million Btu of heat input, cleaner qualified units are rated at 0.32 pounds per million Btu of heat output or less, and outdoor heaters often burn about 10 times as much wood as wood stoves. It names fine particles as a health concern for lungs and heart, especially for children, older adults and people with heart or lung disease. Burn only seasoned, untreated wood or other fuels the maker lists. Never burn trash or construction debris, and never burn driftwood.

The agency gives no single national distance. As one example of how rules look, a state air agency sets a stack at least 10 feet above the ground for all units, and sets setback distances that depend on the unit's emission rating. Your state and town may have different numbers, so do not assume that example applies to you.

Carbon monoxide in the house

Carbon monoxide inside the house is not usually from the outdoor unit. The fire is outdoors and its smoke goes up its own stack. The risk is the furnace that serves as backup or carries the coil. A state emergency agency warns that a wood-burning appliance and a fuel-burning furnace should not share one chimney, because creosote can clog the chimney and the furnace exhaust can back up, giving dangerous carbon monoxide levels. Because the plenum coil sits in the same ductwork, the furnace deserves a yearly check of its vent, heat exchanger and burners, and working carbon monoxide alarms. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. The related lesson on wood and pellet stoves with central heat covers indoor solid-fuel appliances, which we do not repeat here.

Worked example (our own): what the pipe run costs in wood

This is our own example with stated assumptions, not a measurement.

  • Assumption: 120 feet of trench with supply and return pipes in it.
  • Assumption: heat loss of 30 Btu per hour per foot of trench, supply and return together. This is our own round number for well-insulated pipe. Real values depend on insulation, soil, water temperature and moisture.
  • Sourced: air-dried hardwood holds about 7,100 Btu per pound (Missouri extension guide).
  • Assumption: the unit turns 50 percent of that into heat in the water. The extension table also uses 50 percent for wood. Part-load operation can be worse.

Step 1, loss per hour: 120 ft × 30 Btu/h per ft = 3,600 Btu/h.
Step 2, loss per day: 3,600 × 24 h = 86,400 Btu per day.
Step 3, usable heat per pound of wood: 7,100 × 0.50 = 3,550 Btu per pound.
Step 4, wood per day: 86,400 ÷ 3,550 = about 24 pounds of wood per day.

Over a 150-day heating season that is about 3,650 pounds. The same guide puts a cord of air-dried hardwood at about 3,200 pounds, so the pipe alone would eat a bit over one cord. If the true loss were 60 Btu/h per foot, double everything. As a sanity check on scale, the Maryland fact sheet's 20 percent loss over a 100-foot run suggests our per-foot figure may be on the low side for some installations, so measure when you can.

Field card: Outdoor wood boilers at the house

The fire is outside; the house side still needs checks.

  • Firebox in a water jacket, short stack, buried supply and return.
  • Many units are open and vented: do not pressurize them or cap their vent.
  • Connect through a plenum coil or a plate heat exchanger; expect about 20°F lost across the exchanger on one maker's data.
  • Antifreeze: boiler-type propylene glycol, never automotive, then test the mix.
  • Buried pipe loses heat all season; shorter runs and good insulation save wood.
  • Power failure: plan backup heat and watch for boiling and low water.
  • CO indoors usually points to the furnace or a shared chimney, not the yard unit.
  • State and local rules apply to siting and stack height.
