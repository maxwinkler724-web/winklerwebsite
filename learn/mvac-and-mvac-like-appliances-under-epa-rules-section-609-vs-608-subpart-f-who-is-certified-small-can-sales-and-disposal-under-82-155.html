MVAC and MVAC-Like Appliances Under EPA Rules: Section 609, Subpart F, Small Cans and Disposal

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A pickup truck air conditioner and a farm tractor air conditioner can look almost the same under the hood, yet federal rules put them in different boxes. The first is a motor vehicle air conditioner (MVAC). The second is usually an MVAC-like appliance. This lesson shows how to sort them, which technician certification each one needs, what the small-can sales rule says, and what happens when the vehicle or machine is scrapped.

Check the current rule

Check the current text. Federal rules change. We read the eCFR and EPA pages named in the sources in 2026. This is general education, not legal advice. Confirm details in the regulation itself before you rely on them.

What an MVAC is

The MVAC definition lives in 40 CFR 82.32(d), which is part of 40 CFR part 82, subpart B. That subpart is the regulatory home of Clean Air Act Section 609. The definition says that motor vehicle air conditioners are "mechanical vapor compression refrigeration equipment used to cool the driver's or passenger's compartment of any motor vehicle." It then says it is not meant to cover the hermetically sealed refrigeration systems used on motor vehicles for refrigerated cargo, or the air conditioning systems on passenger buses that use HCFC-22.

The test is what the system cools. If it cools the place where people sit in an on-road vehicle, it is the MVAC. If it cools cargo, it is not.

What an MVAC-like appliance is

The term is defined in 40 CFR 82.152, in subpart F, which is the Section 608 appliance rule. An MVAC-like appliance is a "mechanical vapor compression, open-drive compressor appliance" with a full charge of 20 pounds or less of refrigerant, used to cool "the driver's or passenger's compartment of off-road vehicles or equipment." The definition adds that this includes, but is not limited to, air-conditioning equipment on agricultural or construction vehicles, and that it is not meant to cover appliances using R-22.

Notice the four tests packed into that one sentence: an open-drive compressor, a full charge of 20 pounds or less, an operator or passenger compartment, and an off-road vehicle or machine. Miss any one and the appliance falls back into the ordinary appliance rules.

Which technician certification applies

MVAC. Under 40 CFR 82.161, persons who maintain, service or repair MVACs for consideration must be certified in accordance with 40 CFR part 82, subpart B. Section 82.34 also requires approved recovery and recycling equipment for that work. EPA describes the Section 609 route as training from an EPA-approved program followed by a test, and says that test differs from the Section 608 test. A Section 608 card alone does not cover an MVAC.

MVAC-like appliance. Section 82.161 gives two options. The technician must either be certified as a Type II technician or be certified in accordance with subpart B. EPA's overlap page says the same thing in plainer words and adds that it recommends technicians servicing MVAC-like appliances consider Section 609 certification. For the Type II side of that choice, see our lesson on who needs certification, linked below.

Everything else. A system that is neither an MVAC nor an MVAC-like appliance is an ordinary appliance, and the usual Type I, II, III or Universal sorting by pressure class applies.

The small-can sales rules

Two eCFR provisions matter here. First, 40 CFR 82.34(b) bars selling or offering to sell a class I or class II refrigerant suitable for MVACs in a container holding less than 20 pounds, unless the buyer is trained and certified under the subpart B program, or the container is for resale only and the buyer certifies that to the seller. Second, 40 CFR 82.154(c) covers the broader refrigerant sales restriction. For MVACs it allows a buyer who is certified under subpart B, or who employs at least one person certified under subpart B and gives the seller proof, when the refrigerant is acceptable for use in MVACs.

There is a consumer exception for small cans. Paragraph 82.154(c)(1)(ix) lets a non-exempt substitute intended for an MVAC be sold in a container designed to hold two pounds or less. EPA's sales restriction page says such cans must have unique fittings and self-sealing valves, and that they can continue to be sold to people without certification for do-it-yourself use on their own vehicles. Paragraph 82.154(c)(2) sets the self-sealing valve requirements starting January 1, 2018. Seen from the study side, the exception is about the can, not the buyer: two pounds or less, unique fitting, self-sealing valve. A larger container of MVAC refrigerant is back under the certified-buyer rules.

One more point from EPA: a Section 609 technician cannot use that certification to buy refrigerant meant for stationary equipment, regardless of container size.

Disposal: where the two categories join up

Servicing splits MVACs and MVAC-like appliances. Disposal brings them back together. Section 82.155 in subpart F covers safe disposal and names small appliances, MVACs and MVAC-like appliances together. Paragraph (a) says persons recovering refrigerant from these for purposes of disposal must evacuate it using recovery equipment that meets the standards in the rule. Paragraph (b) puts a duty on the final processor of these appliances: either recover the remaining refrigerant, or verify through a signed statement or contract that recovery already happened. EPA's overlap page confirms that MVAC disposal falls under Section 608 rather than Section 609.

Two practical points follow. Section 82.161 says persons who dispose of small appliances, MVACs and MVAC-like appliances are not required to be certified, so the disposal rule is about equipment and proof, not a card. And venting the refrigerant is still not allowed; see the venting lesson linked below. Our safe-disposal lesson explains the verification statement idea in more detail for household appliances, and the same final-processor logic is what a scrapped truck or retired machine goes through before it is crushed or cut up.

Worked example: sorting four items (our own example)

This is our own made-up example. Assumptions we are stating, not facts about any real product: the tractor and forklift air conditioners each have an open-drive, belt-driven compressor, a full charge of 12 pounds, and do not use R-22. The trailer unit is a diesel-powered refrigeration unit that cools cargo. Nobody is dealing with a bus.

  1. Pickup truck air conditioner. It cools the cab of a motor vehicle, so it meets "mechanical vapor compression refrigeration equipment used to cool the driver's or passenger's compartment of any motor vehicle." It is an MVAC. Servicing for pay needs subpart B (Section 609) certification and approved recovery equipment. Disposal: 82.155.
  2. Farm tractor cab air conditioner. Check the four tests: open-drive compressor (assumed yes), 12 pounds is at most 20 pounds (yes), operator compartment (yes), off-road farm machine (yes, and agricultural vehicles are named in the definition). It is an MVAC-like appliance. The technician needs Type II or subpart B certification. Disposal: 82.155.
  3. Forklift cab air conditioner. Same four tests, same answer: MVAC-like. Change one assumption to see how fragile the label is. If the charge were 22 pounds, then 22 is greater than 20, the "20 pounds or less" test fails, and the unit drops into the ordinary appliance rules, where the Type I, II, III or Universal sorting applies by pressure class (a unit this size is not a small appliance).
  4. Refrigerated trailer unit. It cools cargo, not the driver's or passenger's compartment, so it fails the MVAC test and the MVAC-like test. The MVAC definition also steps aside for hermetically sealed refrigerated-cargo systems on motor vehicles. Treat it as an ordinary appliance under Section 608, with the certification type that matches its pressure class; our transport refrigeration lesson covers the unit itself.

The arithmetic is only the charge check: 12 is less than 20, so the tractor and forklift pass; 22 is more than 20, so the changed forklift does not.

Field card: MVAC and MVAC-like appliances

Ask what it cools, where it rides, and how big the charge is.

  • MVAC: cools the driver's or passenger's compartment of a motor vehicle. Subpart B (Section 609) certification for servicing for consideration.
  • MVAC-like: open-drive compressor, 20 lb or less, off-road vehicle or equipment, not R-22. Type II or subpart B certification.
  • Cargo refrigeration and HCFC-22 buses are outside the MVAC definition.
  • Small cans: two pounds or less, unique fitting, self-sealing valve may be sold for do-it-yourself use on a vehicle.
  • Disposal: 82.155 covers small appliances, MVACs and MVAC-like appliances; the final processor recovers the refrigerant or gets a signed statement or contract. No certification is required to dispose.
