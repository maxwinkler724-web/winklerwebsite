Variable Primary Flow Chilled Water: Minimum Flow and Bypass Valves

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In a variable primary flow (VPF) plant, one set of pumps moves water through the chillers and the building, and the flow through the chiller evaporators goes up and down with the load. That saves pump energy, but every chiller still needs a minimum flow to stay safe. This lesson covers how that minimum is protected, what the bypass valve does, and what to check in the field.

Check the maker's numbers

Check the maker's numbers. Flow limits and rates of change differ by chiller model and by maker. This page quotes published literature to show the kinds of numbers that exist. Your chiller's submittal, installation manual and controls sequence rule on your job. This is general education, not a design.

How VPF differs from primary-secondary

In a primary-secondary (decoupled) plant, small constant-speed primary pumps push a fixed flow through each running chiller, and separate secondary pumps send a varying flow to the coils. Our primary-secondary piping basics lesson shows that layout. A Trane article on variable primary flow describes the difference this way: the decoupled system holds evaporator flow constant and varies only the flow through the coils, while the VPF system varies the flow through each operating chiller's evaporator as well as the coils, so no separate distribution pump is needed.

That is the whole appeal. The same article lists the benefits as removing the secondary pump along with its piping, electrical service and drive, and lowering pumping energy. In its Atlanta medical office example, annual pumping energy fell from 52,588 kWh with constant flow to 32,819 kWh decoupled and 26,614 kWh with VPF. The same article notes that chiller power is roughly the same either way, so the savings come mainly from the pumps.

Chiller flow limits

Every evaporator has a lowest and a highest flow it can accept. The Trane article says each chiller has a minimum and maximum, that catalog ranges are typically expressed as a tube velocity of 3 to 11 feet per second, and that you should confirm the number for your machine with its maker. Daikin's centrifugal chiller fundamentals guide gives different figures for its water-only evaporators: a lower limit between 2.3 and 3.0 feet per second and an upper limit of 12 feet per second. That guide also says to confirm limits against the submittal, and that flow outside the range can damage the chiller. A McQuay conference paper hosted by the U.S. Department of Energy's OSTI found evaporator performance became unstable below 2 feet per second in its tests, which used internally rifled tubes. Notice that these sources do not agree on one number. That is why the nameplate and submittal come first.

Low flow matters because it slows the water in the tubes, which lowers the heat transfer. The Trane article warns that cutting flow faster than the chiller's safeties can respond can cause nuisance shutdowns, freezing and ruptured evaporator tubes.

How fast flow may change

The Trane article gives two limits: no more than 10 percent of design flow per minute where chilled-water temperature must be tightly controlled, and up to 30 percent per minute being permissible for most comfort cooling. Daikin's guide states that its chiller can vary evaporator flow up to 50 percent per minute, and adds that depending on loop design, leaving-water temperature control could be compromised. These are different makers talking about different machines, so treat each as that maker's statement, not a rule. When a tech sees pumps or bypass valves slamming open and closed, our own advice is to look up the rate-of-change limit.

The minimum flow bypass valve

When the coils call for less water than the chillers need, something has to carry the difference. The Trane article says a control valve in a bypass line keeps return flow to the operating chillers above the minimum, and that the bypass may sit upstream or downstream of the coils. A Trane application guide for modular chillers advises placing it toward the end of the loop, or at least two thirds of the way down the line, and sizing the dedicated bypass line for about 1.5 times the largest single chiller unit's design flow. That guide is about modular chillers, so use its numbers as an example of practice, not a universal rule.

Control comes from a measurement. The Trane article says evaporator flow measurement is critical, using either a direct flow meter or an estimate from the water-side pressure drop with maker data. The modular chiller guide recommends electromagnetic flow meters wired directly to the controller and says differential pressure sensing is less reliable with fouled strainers or heat exchangers. Three-way valves at the coils can also act as the bypass, but the Trane article says they raise system flow and reduce the pump savings.

Staging with changing flow

Because flow changes through every running chiller, adding or dropping a machine disturbs the others. The Trane article calls choosing chiller-off control points a critical design task and says staging gets harder as the number or range of chiller sizes grows. The modular chiller guide stresses that rapid bypass valve and pump speed response is critical during staging, so the machines still running are not starved. It also sets the bypass response on a stage up to finish within 10 seconds and delays the minimum flow setpoint decrease by 90 seconds on a stage down. Those are that guide's example timings. For the load-based logic itself, see chiller plant staging and sequencing.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example: a 500 ton chiller designed for 2.4 gpm per ton, with a minimum evaporator flow of 40 percent of design. The 40 percent is an example value for this lesson, not a published figure; use your chiller's data.

  1. Design flow. Our own: 500 tons × 2.4 gpm/ton = 1,200 gpm.
  2. Minimum flow. Our own: 0.40 × 1,200 gpm = 480 gpm.
  3. Bypass flow when the coils need only 300 gpm. The chiller must still see 480 gpm. Our own: 480 − 300 = 180 gpm goes through the bypass valve.
  4. Delta T at 150 tons and 480 gpm. Using tons = gpm × dT ÷ 24, rearranged to dT = tons × 24 ÷ gpm. Our own: 150 × 24 = 3,600; 3,600 ÷ 480 = 7.5°F.
  5. Compare to design. Our own: at 500 tons and 1,200 gpm, dT = 500 × 24 ÷ 1,200 = 10°F. So holding minimum flow at a light load gives a smaller delta T than design.

The 24 in the tons formula comes from the water constant; see the heat transfer formulas lesson. Our own reasoning: water that goes through an open bypass does no cooling, so it mixes with the coil return and lowers the return temperature the chiller sees. That feeds the low delta T problem described in chilled water pumps and delta-T.

Benefits and risks as sources state them

The Trane article lists easier retrofit and lower first cost among the benefits. It lists as cautions: not recommended where chilled-water temperature is critical, where only three-way valves are used, where the owner is unlikely to run the plant as designed, or where controls are old and inaccurate. A FacilitiesNet column by David Brooks adds that poor controls setup removes most of the benefit, and that a plant-only retrofit is a poor application. A university design guideline from the University of Michigan (2005) only allows VPF for small single-building plants, under 1,000 tons, with approval, and requires a volume-controlled bypass to maintain evaporator minimum flow.

Field checks

  • Flow meter. The modular chiller guide lists calibrating the temperature sensors, pressure sensors and flow meter. Our own suggestion: compare the meter against a second reading if your site allows one.
  • Bypass valve stroking. The same guide lists verifying bypass valve responsiveness and stability. Our own suggestion: watch the valve travel and the flow reading while you change the setpoint, and compare the timing to the written sequence.
  • Flow switches. A PNNL report on a federal building says standard flow switches are designed for constant flow and were replaced when the chillers were converted to variable flow. Check that switches suit variable flow.
  • Low-flow trips. Our own suggestion: read the trip log and the flow trend before the trip. Clogged strainers raise pressure drop and can mislead a differential-pressure reading, per the modular chiller guide.

Field card: Variable Primary Flow Minimum Flow

Flow varies through the chillers, so protect the minimum.

  • VPF has one set of pumps and varies flow through the evaporators; decoupled plants hold chiller flow constant.
  • Find your chiller's minimum, maximum and rate-of-change limits in its submittal.
  • The bypass valve keeps flow at or above the minimum; it needs a trusted flow or DP reading.
  • Bypass flow = minimum flow − coil flow (example: 480 − 300 = 180 gpm).
  • dT = tons × 24 ÷ gpm (example: 150 tons at 480 gpm = 7.5°F).
  • Check meter calibration, valve stroking, flow switches and strainers.
