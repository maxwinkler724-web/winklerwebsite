Warm Weather Shutdown on Hot-Water Boilers: Setpoints, Sensors and DHW

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In July nobody needs the boiler to keep the radiators warm, yet many boilers sit there holding a hot water temperature all summer. Warm weather shutdown, usually shortened to WWSD, is the control feature that lets the boiler and heating pumps go to sleep when the weather says heat is not needed. This lesson covers how it works, what it does not stop, and how to pick a setpoint.

What WWSD is

WWSD is a decision made from the outdoor temperature. When the outdoor sensor reads above the WWSD setpoint, the control stops calling for space heat: the burner is not fired for the heating loop and the heating circulators stop. It is related to outdoor reset, which lowers the target water temperature as the weather warms. Reset trims the supply temperature; WWSD is the point where the target goes to nothing. On many controls the two share one outdoor sensor.

A control maker's 1999 data brochure for a house control says WWSD occurs when the outdoor temperature is above the setting and all zones are satisfied, and that the WWSD point normally lines up with the start of the heating curve. Not every control behaves the same way, so read the sequence of operation for the one you are working on.

The setpoint and the differential

Ranges differ by control. A boiler control maker's installation instructions (2021 revision) for an outdoor reset add-on list a WWSD adjustment from 40 F to 70 F in 10 F steps, with the feature OFF as shipped. A set of 1990s data brochures from a hydronic control maker ties the WWSD point to the occupied and unoccupied temperature dials, with a range of 35 F to 105 F. Two designs, two ranges. The setpoint is just a number in a menu or on a dial, and its range and default are matters for the manual.

A differential (hysteresis) keeps the control from flipping in and out of shutdown when the outdoor temperature hovers at the setpoint. The documents we read gave no WWSD differential, so we do not quote one here. Look for it in the control's settings list.

What WWSD does not stop: DHW

WWSD shuts down space heating, not domestic hot water (DHW). Controls handle that in different ways, and this is where mistakes happen:

  • One 1999 brochure says that during WWSD the injection pump and zone relays turn off, and the system pump turns off unless it is needed for domestic hot water.
  • A brochure for a stage control says its DHW setup has no WWSD provision at all: the pump and boilers stay off until the DHW aquastat calls for heat.
  • The 2021 instructions say that with WWSD active, the boiler answers a hot water call only if the zone-request terminal is set up as a DHW request. They also say to disable WWSD on the aquastat when a zoning panel has a priority DHW zone.

The takeaway: after turning WWSD on, run a hot water call and watch the boiler fire. See indirect water heaters on a boiler for how that load is piped and controlled, and boiler controls for the aquastat side.

Pump exercise during summer

A circulator that sits for months can stick. Some controls run an exercise routine during WWSD. The brochures we read describe a system pump run of 20 seconds after 3 days of shutdown on one stage control, 15 seconds every 3 days on a mixing control, and 10 seconds every 3 days on a house control, with zone valves exercised on the same schedule. The 2021 instructions and the 2012 aquastat guide we read did not describe a summer exercise. If your control has none, a seized pump is a late-September surprise, so check the manual and note it on the service tag.

Where the sensor goes

Everything depends on the outdoor sensor. A sensor kit's instructions say to mount it on the north-facing exterior wall, out of direct sun, high enough to avoid damage and drifting snow, and away from heat sources such as exhaust fans, windows and doors. A sensor in the sun reads warm and can shut the heat off on a cool morning. A sensor near a dryer vent reads warm for the same reason. A 1995 brochure also notes that if the sensor opens or shorts, that control assumes an outdoor temperature of -8 F, which fails toward heat rather than toward shutdown. The same kit says to route the sensor cable away from other wiring to avoid interference.

Picking a setpoint for a house or building

Our own reasoning: the right setpoint is near the outdoor temperature at which the building stops needing heat on its own, called its balance point. Heat from people, lights, appliances and the sun covers the rest. A tight, well-insulated building has a lower balance point, so a lower WWSD number fits. A drafty building with little internal heat has a higher one. Start with the manual's setting or a value inside the range you read above, then adjust over a couple of weeks of shoulder weather based on what the occupants report. Change one setting at a time and record the old value.

Energy savings: standby losses

A boiler held hot at idle loses heat through its jacket, piping and flue. A U.S. Department of Energy fact sheet on residential hydronic systems says long-term data show standby losses can be as large as 20 to 30 percent. A Brookhaven National Laboratory report says low-load and idle losses can have a very large impact on annual energy use. Those are whole-system findings, so they do not turn straight into a savings number for your job. For that, do your own estimate.

Worked example (all inputs are our own assumptions)

Assumptions, picked for easy arithmetic and not measured: a boiler with 100,000 Btu/h input; average standby loss of 1 percent of input while hot and idle; 1,800 hours a year that the boiler would otherwise sit hot with no space-heat need, which WWSD now covers; gas at 100,000 Btu per therm and $1.80 per therm.

  1. Standby loss rate: 100,000 Btu/h x 0.01 = 1,000 Btu/h.
  2. Energy over the hours saved: 1,000 Btu/h x 1,800 h = 1,800,000 Btu.
  3. In therms: 1,800,000 / 100,000 = 18 therms.
  4. Cost: 18 x $1.80 = $32.40 a year.

So the savings are real but modest for one small boiler. Larger plants, higher standby losses and longer idle periods raise it, and fewer burner starts and pump hours add some wear savings. Change any assumption and the answer moves in direct proportion.

Problems to watch

  • Cold mornings in shoulder season. Outdoor temperature is a rough guide. A frosty 50 F morning that turns 68 F by noon can leave a house cold if the setpoint is too low. Raise it a step before reaching for a workaround, and see whether the control has a manual override.
  • Short cycling after wake-up. When WWSD releases, the first calls can be short. See short cycling, oversizing and zoning.
  • Return temperature. Low water temperatures matter differently for condensing and non-condensing boilers; see boiler return temperature.
  • Bad sensor placement or wiring. Covered above.
  • Local heat rules. Many places set heating seasons or minimum indoor temperatures for rental buildings. A fixed WWSD setting could conflict with them. We make no claim about any particular jurisdiction: check the local requirements and keep a record of the settings you leave.

Field card: Warm Weather Shutdown on Hot-Water Boilers

WWSD stops space heat, not necessarily hot water.

  • Outdoor above the WWSD setpoint: no space-heat call. Check the manual for range and default.
  • Test a DHW call after any change.
  • Sensor: north wall, out of sun, away from vents and doors.
  • Pump exercise varies: 10 to 20 seconds every 3 days on some controls, none on others.
  • Pick near the building balance point, then adjust over weeks.
  • Check local heat rules for occupied buildings.
