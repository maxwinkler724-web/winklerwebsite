Heat Pump Thermostat Setback: Recovery, Strip Heat and Adaptive Recovery Settings

Last updated: 2026-10-28

With a gas furnace, turning the thermostat down at night is a classic way to save. With a heat pump it can backfire. The morning climb back to the daytime setpoint is where the electric strips can wake up, and strip heat costs far more per Btu than the heat pump does. This lesson explains why, what the research says, and which settings help.

What the research says

The U.S. Department of Energy says that turning a thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees F for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling. The same page also says programmable thermostats are generally not recommended for heat pumps, because setting one back in heating mode can make the unit run inefficiently and cancel the savings. It adds that heat-pump-specific thermostats exist and typically use special algorithms to limit the use of backup electric resistance heat.

Two federal laboratory reports point the same way. A simulation study from a national laboratory compared constant 72 F with a 68 F daytime setting and several night settings for heat pumps with resistance backup, in Atlanta and Minneapolis climates. It reported savings of 14 to 29 percent for a 68 F night setting, 20 to 40 percent for 60 F, and 22 to 42 percent for 55 F (each range spans the two cities). Note that this compared against a warmer constant setting, so part of the saving comes from the lower daytime temperature, not only the setback. An older journal simulation study from a manufacturer's lab, indexed by the same federal records site, found about 4 percent seasonal saving per degree F of setpoint reduction in Minneapolis, but warned that nighttime setbacks of 5 F or more can increase heat pump energy use and cause high morning power demand, and so did not recommend general setback for air-source heat pumps. These are old simulations, not field measurements of today's variable-speed equipment, so treat them as direction, not as a prediction for your house.

A recent national-lab (NREL) measure for commercial rooftop heat pumps is more recent. It explains that manufacturers often recommend a setback of no more than 2 F in heating mode, notes that some buildings use setbacks of 10 F or more and others none, and models a 2 F setback. In its Wyoming test building, supplemental heat was needed during morning warm-up with the standard setbacks because the compressor capacity was not enough, and the reduced setback cut the peak supplemental heating power by about 45 percent. The trade-off is real: the same report found a 4.6 percent increase in electricity use in that location, and about 5 percent more heating electricity across the modeled stock. That is a commercial-building study, so we use it for the mechanism, not as a home number.

Why recovery brings on the strips

At a mild outdoor temperature the heat pump can raise the house a few degrees in an hour or two. In the cold, its output is lower and the house loses heat faster, so there is little spare capacity to climb with (see Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity). A thermostat that sees a big gap between room and setpoint may call for the second stage, which is the strips. Those strips turn every watt into one watt of heat, a coefficient of performance (COP) of 1. The Department of Energy says a properly installed air-source heat pump can deliver up to three times as much heat as the electricity it uses. So a deep setback is not just a bigger recovery; it can be a recovery done by the most expensive heat source in the system.

Worked example (our own example)

This example uses our own assumed numbers, not measurements. The only sourced input is the conversion: the U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kWh of electricity as 3,412 Btu.

  • Heat to add during the morning recovery (assumption): 20,000 Btu.
  • Heat pump COP during that recovery (assumption): 2.5. The Department of Energy says up to 3 in good conditions; a cold morning is lower, so we picked 2.5.
  • Strip heat COP: 1.

Step 1, heat in kWh: 20,000 Btu ÷ 3,412 Btu/kWh = 5.86 kWh of heat.

Step 2, heat pump electricity: 5.86 kWh ÷ 2.5 = 2.34 kWh.

Step 3, strip electricity: 5.86 kWh ÷ 1 = 5.86 kWh.

Step 4, difference: 5.86 − 2.34 = 3.52 kWh more by strips for the same heat, about 2.5 times as much. At an assumed price of $0.17 per kWh (our assumption; use your own rate), that is about $0.40 versus $1.00 for one recovery. Over a long winter of daily recoveries the gap adds up, and the strips also raise the peak electric demand. A real recovery usually mixes both sources, so the true cost sits between the two.

How thermostats limit strip heat

  • Adaptive (gradual) recovery. The thermostat learns how long the house takes to warm and starts the climb early so the heat pump alone gets there by the set time. The Bonneville Power Administration (a federal power agency) notes that this only works if the homeowner avoids deep setbacks and adjusts the thermostat infrequently, and that control of the strips cannot be left up to adaptive recovery alone.
  • Outdoor lockout. A temperature below which the strips may run, and above which they may not. That agency's residential thermostat specification sets the lockout at 35 F or lower and says a balance point of no more than 30 F. With an outdoor sensor the cut-out is programmed at the thermostat. See Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures.
  • Stage delay and differential ("droop"). Many thermostats bring on the strips only after the room has lagged the setpoint by a set amount or for a set time. Setting names vary, so read the thermostat's manual. A discharge-air sensor is another approach: the same agency describes one that brings on strips after a delay of about 4 to 5 minutes and says the staging maximum on multi-stage or zoned systems should be 85 F.
  • Two-stage wiring. The 2012 North Carolina Energy Conservation Code (named here as plain text) requires a thermostat whose set points keep supplementary heat on the second stage at a lower set point than compressor heat, and a state page says standard heat pump thermostats meet it. An outside thermostat to lock out strips was described there as an added measure.
  • Balance point logic. An NREL standard work specification says to prevent supplementary heat when the heat pump can meet the load, and to select a balance point following the manufacturer and ACCA Manual S (named as a standard).

Small setback or none

A regional nonprofit's consumer guide says modest nighttime adjustments are fine but deep setbacks may cost energy and money with cold-climate heat pumps, and that settings should be kept steady. Together with the NREL discussion of manufacturer guidance, that points to a setback of about 2 F, or none, in cold weather. Our reading: if a house sits near its balance point on the coldest nights, the safest setting is no setback at all.

Setback in cooling

In summer the heat pump is an air conditioner and no strips are involved. The Department of Energy says to keep the house warmer than normal when you are away, because a higher interior temperature slows heat flow into the house.

What to set

  • Homeowners: use a small night change (about 2 F) or none; do not hunt for the thermostat several times a day; never use EM HEAT to speed up recovery; and ask a tech if the aux light shows every morning.
  • Techs: turn on adaptive recovery if present; set the outdoor lockout at or below the balance point per the maker (35 F or lower in the agency specification above); set the stage delay or droop so the strips are the last resort; confirm the second stage is on W2 or its equivalent and that the stat is a heat pump type; and watch a recovery with the amp clamp on the strip circuit.

Field card: Heat Pump Thermostat Setback

A deep setback can be paid back by the strips.

  • DOE: setback saves up to 10% a year (7 to 10 F for 8 h) but is generally not recommended for heat pumps in heating.
  • Old simulations: setbacks of 5 F or more can raise heat pump energy use and morning demand.
  • NREL commercial work: about 2 F setback; reduced setback cut peak strip power by about 45% in its test.
  • Strips are COP 1. Example (ours): 20,000 Btu = 5.86 kWh by strips vs 2.34 kWh at COP 2.5.
  • Tech settings: adaptive recovery, outdoor lockout (35 F or lower in one federal agency spec), stage delay or droop.
  • Cooling: raising the setpoint while away saves energy and uses no strips.
