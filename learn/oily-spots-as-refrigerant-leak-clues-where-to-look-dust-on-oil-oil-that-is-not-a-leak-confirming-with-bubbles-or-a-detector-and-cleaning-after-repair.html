Oily Spots as Refrigerant Leak Clues: Where to Look and What to Confirm

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Refrigerant is invisible, but the oil that rides with it is not. A greasy, dark, dusty spot on a joint is one of the oldest leak clues in the trade. It is only a clue, though. This lesson covers why the clue works, where to look, which oily spots mislead you, and how to confirm before anyone calls it a leak.

Why oil shows where refrigerant escapes

A compressor needs oil, and some of that oil leaves the compressor with the refrigerant and circulates through the lines and coils. A federal research summary on lubricant circulation in residential split systems describes the whole job as managing oil return, because oil that leaves the compressor has to travel around the circuit and come back. Our own plain-language reading: oil and refrigerant are mixed in the circuit, so a hole lets the mix out. Refrigerant boils off into the air. Oil does not. It stays on the metal as a thin film, and over weeks the film builds into a stain. Our oil-in-refrigerant lesson covers the same travel from the recovery side.

Where to look

A federal leak-prevention guide for refrigeration systems lists the places that leak most and says to look for oil seepage on piping, valves and equipment, then confirm with soap bubbles or an electronic detector. Using that list plus our own field habits, work through these spots:

  • Brazed joints and fittings. The federal guide names brazed and mechanical fittings and brazed connections throughout the piping.
  • Service valve caps and access cores. The guide points to valve stem packing and to Schrader cores, especially when the cap is missing. Always put the cap back with its seal.
  • Coil return bends and fin edges. The guide says to check beneath air-cooled coils, the finned surfaces, and the return bends and manifolds.
  • Tubing rubbing on supports. The guide notes copper tubing that rubs against steel supports. This covers a capillary tube that vibrates against a bracket or its neighbor and wears through.
  • Flare nuts on mini-splits. Our own checklist item: an over- or under-tightened flare or a poor flare face often weeps oil at the nut. See flare fittings on mini-splits.
  • Compressor terminals and fittings. Our own checklist item: look at the terminal block area and the suction and discharge fittings on the compressor shell for a wet shine.

Dust makes the stain easier to see

Our own field observation: a fresh oil film is clear and easy to miss. Dust and lint stick to it and turn it into a dark, fuzzy patch, so a dusty smear on an otherwise clean line is worth a closer look. The federal guide makes the related point from the other side. Dirty racks, coils and condensers make leaks harder to spot, so keep equipment clean and wipe up oil spills as they occur.

Oil spots that are not leaks

Do not let a stain talk you into a conclusion. Our own list of common false alarms:

  • Factory oil. Some parts and tubing leave the maker with a light film of oil from forming or protection. A film on a new part with no dust buildup and no growth over time is not by itself a leak.
  • Spilled oil from a past repair. Oil that dripped when a compressor or a line was opened can sit on a base pan or a line for years.
  • Motor bearing oil. Fan motors with oil ports can throw or weep a little oil, which lands on nearby surfaces. It has nothing to do with the refrigerant circuit.

The test is simple: a leak stain is at a place where refrigerant-bearing metal is joined or worn, and it grows. A stain somewhere else needs a different explanation. Wipe it, mark the date, and look again.

Confirm before you call it a leak

The federal guide says to use soap bubbles or an electronic leak detector to confirm a refrigerant loss and pinpoint the spot, and not to stop at a small seep if it cannot account for the charge that was lost. Three confirmation tools, in the order we usually reach for them:

  1. Bubble solution. Brush it on the suspect spot, with the system holding pressure, and watch for growing bubbles.
  2. Electronic detector. Sweep slowly near the spot. See electronic refrigerant leak detectors for technique. Dye is another option, covered in the dye lesson.
  3. Pressure test. With the charge recovered, a nitrogen standing test shows whether the circuit holds. A drop overnight is not always a leak because temperature moves pressure, as explained in the nitrogen test lesson.

After the repair: clean the spot

Once the repair is made and verified, clean the old oil off the area with a cleaning agent that is compatible with the parts. The federal guide warns to use only compatible cleaners, especially around evaporators and piping. Then the next visit starts with a clean slate: any fresh oil film or dust smear is new information. If you leave the old stain, nobody can tell whether the repair failed. The federal guide also says to check the repaired area again after the repair.

The federal rule in general terms

A federal environmental agency page on stationary refrigeration leak repair says that, for appliances with a full charge of 50 pounds or more of ozone-depleting refrigerant, owners must repair a leak within 30 days of discovering it once the leak rate passes a trigger. Trigger rates differ by appliance type: 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, 20 percent for commercial refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other appliances, measured over 12 months. The owner may instead write a plan within 30 days to retrofit or retire the appliance and finish within one year. Repairs are followed by initial and follow-up verification tests. The point for a technician is the direction of the rule: where it applies, the job is to repair the leak, not to keep adding refrigerant. Whether a given system is covered depends on its size, refrigerant and use, so check the agency page and your employer. Our verification test lesson goes deeper. Regardless of the rule, topping off a leaking system just delays the next call.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. These inputs are assumptions chosen for easy arithmetic, not measured data and not from any source.

  • Assumption A: a small split system with a 96 oz (6 lb) charge.
  • Assumption B: a slow leak of 0.25 oz of refrigerant per week.
  • Assumption C: a 26-week cooling season.
  • Assumption D: the leaking refrigerant carries oil at 1 percent by mass.

Step 1, refrigerant lost in the season: 0.25 oz per week x 26 weeks = 6.5 oz.

Step 2, as a share of the charge: 6.5 oz / 96 oz = 0.0677, about 6.8 percent of the charge.

Step 3, oil carried out with it: 6.5 oz x 0.01 = 0.065 oz of oil by weight.

Step 4, in a size you can picture: 0.065 oz x 28.35 g per oz = 1.84 g. At about 0.9 g per mL (an assumed typical oil density, also our assumption), 1.84 / 0.9 = about 2 mL, well under half a teaspoon, which is about 5 mL.

Check: 6.5 oz of refrigerant is a noticeable loss, yet the oil it leaves behind is only a couple of milliliters spread over a joint. That is why leak stains are small and why a spot the size of a coin can match a real loss. It is also why the stain can be hard to see on a clean day and easy to see once dust sticks to it. If the oil share were 2 percent, the oil would double to 0.13 oz, still small. Annualized, this assumed leak would be 0.25 x 52 = 13 oz, or 13/96 = 13.5 percent of the charge per year. This home-size system holds well under 50 lb, so the federal trigger rates above would not apply to it. They are shown only to compare scale.

Field card: Oily Spots as Refrigerant Leak Clues: Where to Look and What to Confirm

Oil is the clue. Bubbles, a detector or a pressure test is the proof.

  • Oil rides with refrigerant. A leak lets refrigerant boil off and leaves oil behind.
  • Look at brazed joints, caps and cores, coil return bends, rubbing tubing, flare nuts and compressor fittings.
  • Dust on oil makes a dark, fuzzy patch.
  • Factory oil, old spills and motor bearing oil can mislead you.
  • Confirm with bubbles, an electronic detector or a pressure test.
  • Clean the spot after repair so a new leak shows.
  • Where the federal rule applies, repair the leak instead of topping off.
