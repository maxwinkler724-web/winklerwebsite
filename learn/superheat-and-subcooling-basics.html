Superheat and Subcooling Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the EPA 608 Guide

Pressure alone does not tell you how a refrigerant circuit is doing. Superheat and subcooling add the second half of the story, and together they are among the most useful diagnostic readings a tech has.

Superheat

Superheat is how many degrees a refrigerant vapor is above its boiling (saturation) temperature at the pressure you measure. It is checked on the suction side near the compressor, and tells you the refrigerant fully boiled off in the evaporator and then picked up a little more heat. You get it by reading suction pressure, converting that to the saturation temperature for that refrigerant, then subtracting that from the actual line temperature.

Labeled example only: if the saturation temperature at the measured pressure is 40°F and the suction line reads 55°F, superheat is 15°F.

Subcooling

Subcooling is how many degrees a liquid refrigerant is below its condensing (saturation) temperature at the pressure you measure. It is checked on the liquid line near the condenser outlet or the metering device, and tells you the refrigerant fully turned to liquid and then cooled a little more. You subtract the liquid line temperature from the saturation temperature at the liquid pressure.

Labeled example only: if saturation is 100°F and the liquid line reads 90°F, subcooling is 10°F.

What the readings can suggest

  • Very low superheat can mean liquid is flooding back toward the compressor, which can damage it.
  • High superheat can point to an undercharge or too little refrigerant reaching the evaporator.
  • Low subcooling can point to low charge or flash gas in the liquid line.
  • High subcooling can point to an overcharge or a restriction.

These are clues, not verdicts. A dirty coil, low airflow, or a bad metering device can move the numbers too, so check airflow and coils before touching charge.

A general method

  • Run the system steadily for the time the maker requires, with normal airflow and clean filters.
  • Attach gauges and temperature clamps carefully, following refrigerant-handling rules.
  • Convert pressures to saturation temperatures for the exact refrigerant, using its table or your meter.
  • Calculate superheat and subcooling.
  • Compare them with the target on the unit's data plate or manual. Targets depend on the metering device and conditions.

Some sources quote typical ranges for superheat and subcooling. Treat any such range as general background, because the target for your equipment comes from the manufacturer, and different metering devices and refrigerants use different methods.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: refrigerant handling requires proper certification and recovery practices, and the manufacturer's charging procedure governs. This lesson explains meaning, not charging steps.

Field card: Superheat and Subcooling Basics

Two numbers that say what the refrigerant is really doing.

  • Superheat: suction vapor temperature minus saturation temperature.
  • Subcooling: saturation temperature minus liquid line temperature.
  • Low superheat can mean flooding. High superheat can mean starved coil.
  • Low and high subcooling hint at low charge and overcharge or restriction.
  • Check airflow and coils first, then follow the maker's target, not a rule of thumb.
