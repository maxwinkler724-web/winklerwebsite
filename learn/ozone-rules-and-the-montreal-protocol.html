Ozone Rules and the Montreal Protocol in Plain English

The EPA 608 exam asks about rules that can feel random until you know the story behind them. The short version: scientists found that certain chemicals in refrigerants damage the ozone layer, countries agreed to stop making them, and the United States wrote that agreement into its own law.

The problem

The ozone layer high in the atmosphere shields life from harmful ultraviolet light. Chlorine-containing chemicals, especially chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), were found to damage it. CFCs were widely used in refrigeration, air conditioning, foam and aerosols, which is why technicians are part of the solution.

The treaty

According to a general reference, the Montreal Protocol was signed on 16 September 1987 and took effect on 1 January 1989. It set schedules to phase out ozone-depleting substances such as CFCs and halons, and later phased down HCFCs. It has been strengthened by several amendments, with wealthier countries given earlier deadlines and developing countries extra time and help. EPA's own pages describe the treaty as the international agreement that sets these schedules, with the United States going faster in some cases under its own law. The Kigali Amendment of 2016 added HFCs, which do not harm the ozone layer but are strong greenhouse gases.

The U.S. law

In the United States the Clean Air Act (as amended in 1990) is the legal vehicle, and EPA writes the rules. Section 608 covers stationary refrigeration and air conditioning, which is why technicians need EPA 608 certification. EPA describes two classes of ozone-depleting substances: Class I, mainly CFCs, which are fully phased out apart from limited exemptions, and Class II, the HCFCs, which were interim replacements and are being phased out in steps. A concrete milestone from EPA: on January 1, 2020, U.S. production and import of HCFC-22 ended. Existing systems may still be serviced using recovered or reclaimed R-22 or R-22 produced before 2020.

What this means in the field

  • No venting. Section 608 prohibits knowingly releasing ozone-depleting refrigerants and substitutes like HFCs during maintenance, service, repair or disposal.
  • Recovery and reclaim. Recovered refrigerant can be returned to equipment with the same owner. If ownership changes, it must be reclaimed by an EPA-certified reclaimer first. See Refrigerant Recovery, Recycle and Reclaim.
  • Certification. The Type I, II and III structure is in Type I, II, III Explained.
  • Refrigerant families. CFC, HCFC and HFC are different generations. Newer low-GWP choices appear in A2L and A3 Refrigerants.

Keep rules current

The treaty and EPA rules change, and some details (such as HFC regulations) were under revision at the time this lesson was written. Check EPA's current pages and the free EPA 608 course for updates. Practice with the 608 quiz and flashcards.

Field card: Ozone Rules and the Montreal Protocol in Plain English

Why the rules exist, in one page.

  • CFCs and HCFCs damage the ozone layer; HFCs do not but are strong greenhouse gases.
  • The Montreal Protocol (signed 1987, in effect 1989) is the international treaty.
  • In the U.S., the Clean Air Act and EPA rules, including Section 608, carry it out.
  • HCFC-22 production and import ended January 1, 2020; servicing with reclaimed R-22 continues.
  • No venting; recover, and reclaim if ownership changes.
