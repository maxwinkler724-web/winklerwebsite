Cooling Tower Side-Stream Filtration: Sand Filters, Centrifugal Separators and Sizing by Percent of Flow

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An open cooling tower is an air scrubber that happens to cool water. Dust, pollen and grit blow in, settle in the basin and ride the loop into the condenser. Side-stream filtration is the common fix: pull some of the water out, clean it, and put it back. This lesson covers how that works, which equipment does the cleaning, and how to size the stream.

Why open loops collect dirt

A tower moves a lot of air across falling water, and that air carries dust. One maker's application page says a tower acts as an air scrubber, so airborne dust, sand and pollen get mixed into the tower water (Lakos). The same page quotes the ASHRAE Handbook as saying a typical 200-ton tower running 1,000 hours may take in upwards of 600 lb of particulate. It also says that less than 1/16 inch of accumulated solids can interfere with water treatment, because chemicals do not reach the metal under the deposit. DOE's Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) tower guidance makes the same basic point: filtering silt and suspended solids limits fouling, which helps most in dusty locations.

Suspended dirt is a different problem from dissolved minerals. Blowdown controls dissolved solids; it does not do much for silt. For that side of the story see cycles of concentration and blowdown and condenser water treatment.

Full-flow versus side-stream

Lakos describes a full-flow arrangement as filtering 100 percent of the tower flow, which it calls the most effective way to protect downstream equipment. A side-stream arrangement filters only part of the flow; the maker calls it an economical option that reduces suspended solids in the main stream and is easy to retrofit. Side-stream suits large or variable flows where full flow is not practical. The trade-off is simple: a side stream never cleans the water in one pass, it cleans the loop over time.

Three kinds of equipment

Media (sand) filters

Water passes down through a bed of sand. As dirt collects, pressure drop rises, and the filter is backwashed. One maker's sand filter page gives a standard filtration rating of 5 microns, a guideline of 15 to 25 gpm per square foot of media area, and says to backwash when pressure drop climbs about 7 psi above the clean value, or at least once a day if that does not happen. The same page says sand filters remove finer solids and improve clarity but need more maintenance than separators.

Centrifugal separators

Water enters tangentially and spins. Heavy particles move to the wall and drop into a collection chamber, while cleaned water leaves through the center. Lakos reports up to 98 percent removal of solids 74 microns and larger in a single pass, and says removal depends on the solids being heavy compared with the water (specific gravity at least 1.5 higher). Because it relies on weight, it is weaker on light material such as fine fibers or biological growth. Pressure drop depends on flow, not pipe size. The unit has no moving parts but has to be purged. The maker says purge until the discharge changes from dark to light, and never let the time between purges exceed the time to fill one-third of the collection chamber.

Bag and cartridge filters

These are disposable elements in a housing. We found them on the maker pages mainly as the item that separators replace in closed loops ("direct replacement for side stream bags or spiral wound cartridges") and as the bag in a solids recovery vessel that catches purge water. We did not find sizing data for them in the sources read, so we leave them as a small-flow option and send you to the manufacturer for ratings.

Sizing by percent of flow

You will hear a single rule of thumb quoted. The one we can cite is from a maker page: traditional side-stream systems take generally 10 to 25 percent or less of the flow from the main line, through a bypass, and return the filtered water to the main line (Lakos). The same page says that adding basin sweeping pushes the total to 20 percent or more. The FEMP page we read gives no percentage. Treat the percent as a starting point; the product's flow range and the dirt load decide the real size, and the maker will select the model from flow rate.

Basin sweeper piping

Dirt that settles in the basin never reaches a filter by itself. Sweeper piping runs filtered water back through nozzles in the basin floor so settled solids are pushed toward the suction of the side-stream pump, away from the condenser water pump. The maker sizes this by basin area, not by percent of flow: its rule of thumb is 1 gpm per square foot of basin (length times width), with heavy industrial loads referred to the maker. Another cooling tower maker lists basin sweeper piping with the filtration system as automatic maintenance (BAC).

Worked example

Our own arithmetic, using the cited 10 to 25 percent range from above and an assumed loop volume.

  1. Recirculating flow: 1,000 gpm.
  2. Side stream at 10 percent: 1,000 x 0.10 = 100 gpm.
  3. Per hour: 100 gpm x 60 min = 6,000 gal/hr.
  4. Our own assumed system volume (basin, piping, condenser and fill): 10,000 gal. Count it for your own plant; this number is a placeholder.
  5. Turnovers: 6,000 / 10,000 = 0.6 turnovers per hour. That is one full pass about every 1.7 hours (10,000 / 6,000).
  6. At 25 percent: 250 gpm x 60 = 15,000 gal/hr, so 1.5 turnovers per hour.
  7. Basin sweeping, for a basin we assume is 10 ft by 12 ft: 120 sq ft x 1 gpm/sq ft = 120 gpm.

The 100 gpm and 120 gpm streams are not the same water path in every design, so the maker's drawing decides whether they add. Because the side stream is a partial pass, expect the water to clear over many hours, not in one.

Backwash and purge water: the blowdown link

A sand filter that backwashes sends dirty water somewhere, and a separator purges a small stream. FEMP's water balance says make-up equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift. Our own reading: any water you send to drain from a backwash or purge leaves the loop as well, so it adds to make-up, and you can count it with blowdown when you estimate water use. Lakos says its solids recovery vessel catches purge water in a bag filter and returns the water to pump suction, so no liquid is lost; the bag is then the item you handle. One maker page notes the sand filter can draw backwash water from the system or from city water, which changes how much fresh water you use. We did not find a source giving typical backwash volumes, so ask the maker for the figure on your model.

Maintenance checks

  • Sand filter: log pressure drop before and after backwash, and follow the maker's trigger (one maker says 7 psi above clean, or daily).
  • Separator: confirm the purge controller was set at installation (they are not set at the factory) and watch the discharge color change from dark to light.
  • Keep solids load low: the maker says stay below 1 percent by volume.
  • Basin: flush dirt through the drain or sump strainer and clean the strainers (BAC), and look for dead spots the sweeper does not reach.
  • Check the maker limits on the nameplate: one separator line lists 150 psi and 180 degrees F maximums.
  • Do not open basin or filter equipment without your site lockout and water-hygiene procedures; see water management and Legionella awareness.

Heavy fouling that gets past the filter shows up in the condenser; see tube cleaning and testing and fan, fill, drift and basin checks.

Field card: Cooling Tower Side-Stream Filtration

Clean the loop a little at a time, and sweep the basin.

  • Towers scrub dust out of the air; it settles in the basin and fouls the loop.
  • Full-flow filters all the water; side-stream filters a share and cleans over time.
  • One maker cites generally 10 to 25 percent or less of flow for a traditional side stream.
  • Sand filters go finer but need backwash; separators have no moving parts but need purging.
  • Basin sweeping is sized by basin area (one maker: 1 gpm per sq ft).
  • Water sent to drain from backwash or purge adds to make-up.
  • Follow the maker's purge and backwash settings and your site procedures.
