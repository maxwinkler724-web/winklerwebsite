Slime and Clogs in AC and Furnace Condensate Drains: Why They Grow and How to Clean Safely

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The condensate drain is the one pipe on a cooling system that is wet, warm and dark most of the summer. That is a good place for a slimy film to grow. This lesson explains why it grows, how a slow drain announces itself, what you can safely do as an owner, and how to tell slime from a slope or trap problem.

Check the equipment maker first

Follow the manual for your equipment. Makers differ on drain size, trap shape and what may be poured into a pan. This page is general education, not a service procedure. If you are unsure at any step, stop and call a qualified technician. If water is already reaching the ceiling, wiring or insulation, turn the system off and call.

The basic leak story is in Water Leaking from the AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain Basics. This lesson goes deeper on the slime and the pipe layout.

Why a drain grows a film

Federal indoor air guidance says that where water stands in a drain pan, molds and bacteria will multiply unless the pan is cleaned often. A condensate drain has everything that growth needs:

  • Warm. Cooling-season air in the equipment area and in the drain line is mild, not cold.
  • Wet. The coil makes water whenever it cools and dries the air, so the line is damp for months.
  • Dark. Pan and pipe are closed in, with no light to slow growth.
  • Fed. Air passing the coil leaves dust and spores on it. A federal energy page says dirt on the evaporator coil builds up and should be checked and cleaned. Some of that dirt rinses off the fins with the condensate and rides down the pipe.

The result is a slick film on the pan and the pipe wall. A thin film flows fine. Over time the film catches lint and grit, thickens, and narrows the bore until water moves slowly or stops. A clogged drain also stops the coil from removing humidity, and backed-up water can stain walls or carpet, according to the same federal energy page.

Signs of a slow drain

  • Gurgling from the drain line or the indoor unit while it runs. Air and water are fighting through a narrowed spot.
  • Standing water in the pan you can see through the access opening, or a pan that is still wet well after the system has been off.
  • The system shuts off and will not restart. A float switch in or near the pan opens when water rises. A trip is the drain asking for help.
  • A musty smell at the vents or near the unit. Our furnace and AC smells lesson covers other odors.

The trap, the vent tee and why the trap stays primed

Federal indoor air guidance says condensate leaves the pan through a trap, and it is important that condensate lines be trapped and charged with liquid. A trap holds a plug of water in a low bend. If that water dries out, air passes freely, and a poorly trapped line can pull unwanted air into the supply. On a draw-through unit the blower pulls the pan below room pressure, so with no water seal the drain can inhale instead of drain. That is the reason the trap must stay filled. The depth rules are in AC Coil Condensate Traps.

Many installs add a vertical tee just past the trap, often capped or open at the top. In our plain-language description it does two jobs: it lets air in so the water seal cannot be siphoned, and it gives you a place to clean from. A slime plug inside the trap is the usual way a full trap goes bad.

Safe cleaning steps for an owner

These steps follow common practice and the safety rule that water and electricity do not mix. They are general education, so read your own manual first.

  1. Power off. Turn the thermostat to off and shut off the equipment switch or breaker. Keep water away from wiring.
  2. Find the cleanout. Look for the capped vertical tee near the indoor unit. Unscrew the cap by hand if it moves easily. If it is glued or stuck, stop.
  3. Clear the outdoor end. Find where the pipe ends outside. Remove leaves, mud and spider webs by hand. A wet-dry vacuum held on the end with a cloth around the joint can pull a soft plug out. Pull for a short time, then check what came out.
  4. Flush with warm water. Pour a small amount of warm tap water, not boiling, into the tee. Watch for it to run out at the far end. Boiling water can soften or distort plastic pipe, so we leave it out.
  5. Recap and restart. Replace the cap, restore power, and watch the outdoor end for steady flow while the system cools.
  6. Dry up. Dry wet surfaces quickly. A university extension publication says to solve the moisture problem to control mold for good.

If the water will not move, there is no access tee, or the pan is full and the unit will not run, stop and call a technician. Never open panels, touch gas piping, handle refrigerant or work on wiring.

What to avoid

  • Never mix cleaners. The extension publication warns that bleach and ammonia must not be mixed because the fumes are toxic.
  • Do not pour bleach or other strong chemicals into the pan if metal parts or the coil are nearby, unless the equipment maker says that product is allowed. Our caution is simple: the maker knows which materials are in your pan and coil, and we do not. Check the manual or ask your technician.
  • Do not bypass the float switch. It is a safety device.

Drain pan tablets: what they can and cannot do

These are our own plain-reasoning points, not tested claims. Tablets and similar pan treatments dissolve slowly and spread through the standing water. They may slow new growth in the pan and the first stretch of pipe. They cannot remove an existing plug, they do not fix a sag or a missing trap, and they do not replace cleaning. Read the label and the equipment manual first, since the product must suit the pan material. A pan that needs a tablet every month is telling you the drain is not draining completely.

When it is not slime

Federal indoor air guidance says standing water builds up when the pan system is not designed to drain completely, and that pans should slope toward the drain. The same idea applies to the pipe.

  • Slope. If the line is level or tilts the wrong way, water sits and grows slime again soon after you clean it. A fast return of slow-drain signs points here.
  • A sagging line. A long run of unsupported pipe droops and makes a belly that holds water. You may see a sag from a basement or attic.
  • A missing or too-shallow trap. On a draw-through unit, this can leave the pan full even though the pipe is clean. See the trap lesson above.
  • A frozen or kinked line, or a failed condensate pump. See Condensate Pump Not Working.

For a condensing furnace, condensate comes from the heat exchanger and has its own traps. See the collector box lesson. Repairing slope, sag or trap problems is a technician job.

Worked example (our own): how much water, and how fast does a pan fill?

Our own example with assumed numbers. These inputs are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. Your house will differ.

  • Assumed latent load while running: 8,000 Btu per hour.
  • Assumed run time on a humid day: 8 hours.
  • Assumed heat removed per pound of water condensed: about 1,050 Btu (a round value for room-temperature air).
  • Assumed water weight: 8.34 pounds per gallon.
  • Assumed pan: 24 in by 20 in, with 1.5 in of usable depth below the overflow level.

Step 1, latent energy per day: 8,000 Btu/h x 8 h = 64,000 Btu.
Step 2, pounds of water: 64,000 / 1,050 = about 61 lb.
Step 3, gallons per day: 61 / 8.34 = about 7.3 gal per day (about 58 pints).
Step 4, flow while running: 7.3 gal / 8 h = about 0.91 gal per running hour.
Step 5, pan volume: 24 x 20 x 1.5 = 720 cubic inches. One gallon is 231 cubic inches, so 720 / 231 = about 3.1 gal.
Step 6, time to overflow with the drain fully blocked: 3.1 / 0.91 = about 3.4 running hours (about 3 hours 25 minutes).

What it means. With these assumptions the pan would be full in one long afternoon of running. If the system runs 8 hours spread over a full day, the clock time is about 10 hours (3.1 / 7.3 x 24 = 10.2). A float switch should trip before that, but a pan that runs near full is exactly where it trips. For scale, a national lab report on a dedicated dehumidifier unit described a target of under 75 pints per day, so 58 is a plausible size.

Field card: Slime and Clogs in AC and Furnace Condensate Drains: Why They Grow and How to Clean Safely

Warm, wet, dark and dusty: the drain grows slime unless it drains fully.

  • Signs: gurgling, water in the pan, float switch trips, musty smell.
  • The trap holds a water plug and must stay filled; the vent tee keeps the seal from being siphoned and gives a cleanout.
  • Power off first. Cleanout cap, outdoor-end vacuum, warm (not boiling) water flush.
  • Never mix cleaners. Do not put strong chemicals in pans with metal or near the coil unless the maker allows it.
  • Tablets slow growth only. They do not clear a plug or fix slope, a sag or a missing trap.
  • Slime that returns fast usually means a slope, sag or trap problem. Call a technician.
  • Our example: 8,000 Btu/h latent x 8 h gives about 7.3 gal/day; a 3.1 gal pan fills in about 3.4 running hours if blocked.
