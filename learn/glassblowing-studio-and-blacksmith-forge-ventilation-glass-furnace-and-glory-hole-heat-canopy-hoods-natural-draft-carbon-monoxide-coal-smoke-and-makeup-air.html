Glassblowing Studio and Blacksmith Forge Ventilation: Furnace Heat, Canopy Hoods, Carbon Monoxide and Makeup Air

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A glass furnace never really cools down, and a forge makes carbon monoxide whenever it burns. Put either one in a room with people and the room needs a plan for heat, for combustion gases and for replacement air. This lesson covers the equipment, the heat stress it causes, canopy hoods and natural draft, coal smoke, and one worked example you can redo with your own numbers.

What is running, and for how long

A hot shop is built around three heat sources. A university glass hot shop procedure we read gives the furnace operating range as 2,000 to 2,400°F and says it is operated around the clock, shut down only for maintenance. The same procedure lists annealers from room temperature up to 950°F. A second university glass handbook describes three furnaces that run twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week, apart from seasonal breaks, and a bank of glory holes, the small reheating chambers a glassblower returns to again and again. That handbook also says a glory hole is never left unattended and that one color technique means briefly turning off the glory hole fan.

The point for the HVAC tech is the duty cycle. A kiln fires and cools. A glass furnace is a standing load, so the room gets its heat every hour of every day, including the hottest afternoon of the year.

Forges: gas and coal

A university extension overview of blacksmithing for youth instructors says two types of forge are commonly used in metalworking, propane and coal, and that coal brings smoke and dust. It also warns that heating scrap that is painted, coated, galvanized or plated can release fumes that harm health, and it says ventilation and filtration must be set up to avoid dangerous situations. A federal art and craft safety guide lists forging or smithing separately: furnaces may release fumes and gases, ventilation must be appropriate, and carbon monoxide alarms should be installed.

Coal is a different fuel from the coke most people picture. A national park service teaching page says coal is a poorer forge fuel than coke, that coke makes less smoke, and that sulfur and other impurities are driven off when coal is turned into coke. A university cooperative extension book on heating with wood and coal adds that a strong draft is particularly important for coal, that combustion air must pass up through the bed of coals, and that soft coal brings much more pollution risk than anthracite because of sulfur. It gives a draft rule of thumb for a chimney: keep the average flue gas about 200°F above outdoor temperature. Soot in the flue interferes with draft and, per the same book, can hold acids that corrode steel chimneys faster.

Carbon monoxide

OSHA's carbon monoxide fact sheet names forges, blast furnaces and coke ovens as producers of the gas, and lists incomplete burning of natural gas, propane, coal and wood as the cause. It gives the OSHA limit as 50 ppm over an 8-hour average and tells employers to install an effective ventilation system that removes carbon monoxide from work areas. Early symptoms it lists are headache, fatigue, dizziness, drowsiness or nausea; a CDC page adds confusion, vomiting and chest pain and says all fuel-burning appliances must be properly vented.

An art studio safety guide from another university says gas-fired furnaces give off carbon monoxide and that furnaces should be locally exhausted to remove combustion gases and fume. Our takeaway: a studio is not a place to rely on open doors. Vent every burner, put alarms where people work, and read Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do for what an alarm should trigger.

Radiant heat and heat stress

The same art studio guide says furnaces produce intense heat and infrared radiation that can lead to heat stress, skin burns and cataracts, and advises frequent rest breaks and slow acclimation. The federal craft guide recommends infrared goggles, that glassworkers cool off frequently, and a canopy hood for glassblowing. The glass handbook mentions a shield that protects the gaffer from the radiant heat of the glass.

OSHA's heat page lists furnaces, ovens and foundries among hot indoor settings, and its controls page lists engineering fixes first: reflective shields to redirect radiant heat, increased general ventilation, local exhaust at points of high heat production, and cooling fans. It also asks for mandatory rest breaks in a cooler place that get longer as heat stress rises, and shorter shifts for new or unacclimatized workers, increasing over the first one to two weeks. OSHA's technical manual chapter says engineering controls are the most effective way to cut heat stress and describes acclimatization over 7 to 14 days. Those pages give no single temperature limit, so we do not state one.

The HVAC lesson: room air movement does not remove radiant heat from a person standing at a glory hole. Shields and distance do that. Ventilation handles the heat carried by the air.

Canopy hoods and natural draft

A canopy hood over a furnace works because hot gas rises. The heat itself drives the flow, which is why a hood over a glory hole can work without much fan help, and why it can fail when a cold draft or a strong makeup air jet knocks the plume sideways. The studio guide says capture efficiency drops dramatically with distance and says to confirm that local exhaust is running with enough airflow before work begins. The federal craft guide says adequate ventilation means clean air flows toward the artist and contaminated air flows away, and that blowing air around with a fan without a source of clean air is not adequate and can increase exposure. Hood sizing for a given furnace comes from the equipment maker or an engineer; we found no sourced cfm rule for hot-process hoods, so none is given. For hood basics, see Pottery Kiln Rooms and Art Studio Ventilation, and for capture and source extraction ideas see Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors.

Makeup air

Every cubic foot the hood removes must come back in. In our own reasoning, makeup air should enter low and slow, away from the hood face and the burners, and it must never be pulled down a flue or chimney, because a starved room can reverse a natural-draft vent and spill carbon monoxide into the room. We found no sourced airflow numbers for studio makeup air, so the worked example below shows the arithmetic and leaves the equipment choice to the maker and an engineer. In cold weather this air must be tempered so workers are not standing in a draft. For the airflow measurement itself, see Measuring Airflow: CFM, Flow Hoods, Anemometers and the Temperature Rise Method.

Quench and finish fumes

We did not find a gov or university source that gives numbers for quench tank or forge finishing fumes, so the page gives none. The federal craft guide does say that many patina chemicals may be toxic by inhalation, ingestion or skin and eye contact, and that pickling chemicals call for protective clothing, eye protection and an eyewash fountain. Check the safety data sheet for each quench liquid, patina and finish, keep these tasks away from the hot work area, and give them their own exhaust if the sheet calls for it.

Worked example (our own example)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption we chose for illustration, not a measured studio.

  • Furnace gas input: 150,000 Btu/h, running continuously (assumption).
  • Glory hole gas input: 100,000 Btu/h, running continuously (assumption).
  • Fraction of that input that ends up in the room air rather than the hood, glass or flue: 40 percent (assumption).
  • Allowed room temperature rise over the makeup air: 15°F (assumption).
  • Sensible heat relation: Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT. The 1.08 comes from our own arithmetic with standard air at 0.075 lb/ft³, 0.24 Btu per lb per °F and 60 minutes per hour: 0.075 × 0.24 × 60 = 1.08.

Step 1, total input. 150,000 + 100,000 = 250,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, heat released to the room. 250,000 × 0.40 = 100,000 Btu/h.

Step 3, airflow for a 15°F rise. cfm = 100,000 ÷ (1.08 × 15) = 100,000 ÷ 16.2 = about 6,170 cfm.

Step 4, test the assumption. A 10°F rise needs 100,000 ÷ 10.8 = about 9,260 cfm. A 20°F rise needs 100,000 ÷ 21.6 = about 4,630 cfm. Cutting the room fraction to 25 percent (62,500 Btu/h) at 15°F gives 62,500 ÷ 16.2 = about 3,860 cfm.

What it tells us. Even a modest pair of burners needs several thousand cfm to keep the rise small, and the answer swings by a factor of two with the assumed room fraction. That is why capture at the source matters: a hood that catches more of the plume cuts the room load more cheaply than a bigger fan. This calculation covers air-carried heat only. It does not make a worker comfortable beside a glory hole, and it does not replace a carbon monoxide plan.

Field card: Glass furnace and forge room ventilation

Heat out, gas out, clean air in.

  • A glass furnace is a standing load: it runs around the clock, so size for the hottest day.
  • Forges and gas-fired furnaces make carbon monoxide; vent every burner and install alarms.
  • Coal needs strong draft and combustion air through the bed; soft coal adds sulfur.
  • Shields and distance handle radiant heat; ventilation handles air-carried heat.
  • Heat stress: engineering controls first, rest breaks, shorter shifts for new workers.
  • Makeup air must never be drawn down a flue; temper it in cold weather.
  • Our example: 100,000 Btu/h to the room and a 15°F rise gives about 6,170 cfm (Q = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT).
