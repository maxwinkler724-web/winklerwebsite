Screw Compressors in Commercial Refrigeration and Chillers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Screw compressors have a few moving parts, but they carry a built-in design number that decides whether they run efficiently on a given job. This lesson covers how a twin-screw machine compresses gas, why its volume ratio should match the system, how the slide valve and oil system work, and what we look at on a routine visit.

How a twin-screw compressor works

Two meshing rotors turn inside a close-fitting casing. The male rotor has lobes and the female rotor has grooves. In a test compressor described by a university research group, the male rotor had four lobes and the female five. Gas enters at the suction end and is trapped in the pockets between the lobes and the casing. As the rotors turn, each pocket shrinks and moves toward the discharge end, so the gas is squeezed until a port uncovers and releases it. A 1972 Purdue conference paper on oil-injected plants reports that the male rotor carries about 85 percent of the input torque and the female about 15 percent, which helps explain why rotor wear is low when the oil system is healthy.

Built-in volume ratio (Vi)

The built-in volume ratio is the pocket volume when it closes off from suction divided by the pocket volume when it first opens to discharge. A paper from City, University of London defines it this way and says a standard discharge port limits it to about 4, with a reduced port reaching higher values in their test case. Because Vi is fixed by the port shape, the machine always compresses the gas to the same internal pressure no matter what the system is doing. That internal pressure is set by Vi, the suction pressure and the gas.

If the system needs a different discharge pressure than the machine builds inside, the gas is corrected abruptly when the port opens, and that costs power:

  • Over-compression happens when Vi is too large for the job. The pocket pressure rises above the system discharge pressure, then falls as the port opens. The compressor did work it did not need to do.
  • Under-compression happens when Vi is too small. The port opens while pocket pressure is below system pressure, so high-pressure gas rushes back into the pocket, and the rotors must then push that gas out again.

A Purdue conference paper from a Xi'an Jiaotong University team measured pressure against volume in a semi-hermetic R-134a screw compressor with a built-in ratio of 2.4. They saw under-compression at 5 C/50 C and 5 C/65 C and over-compression at 5 C/29 C (evaporating/condensing). They found efficiency peaked when the built-in end pressure was close to the condensing pressure. The City paper reaches the same conclusion: only a matched built-in volume ratio and pressure ratio give the best performance, and both mismatches raise specific power.

Worked example (ours): a 3.5 Vi machine on an ammonia plant

This is our own example, not a manufacturer rating. The pressure ratio and matching Vi use the isentropic relation Vi = (Pd/Ps)^(1/k), where k is the ratio of specific heats, Cp/Cv. We use NIST Chemistry WebBook fluid data for ammonia.

Assumptions (ours): ammonia; evaporating at -20 C (253.15 K); condensing at 35 C (308.15 K); no line losses, so the compressor sees the saturation pressures; absolute pressures; a machine with a built-in Vi of 3.5.

  1. NIST gives saturation pressure at -20 C as 1.9003 bar absolute, and at 35 C as 13.500 bar absolute.
  2. Actual pressure ratio = 13.500 / 1.9003 = 7.10.
  3. NIST gives the suction vapor Cp = 2.4305 and Cv = 1.7798 J/g-K at -20 C, so k = 2.4305 / 1.7798 = 1.366.
  4. Matching Vi = 7.10^(1/1.366). The natural log of 7.10 is 1.961; divided by 1.366 it is 1.436; the exponential is about 4.2.
  5. Check the machine we have. Internal pressure at the discharge port = 1.9003 x 3.5^1.366 = 1.9003 x 5.53 = about 10.5 bar absolute.

Result: the machine builds about 10.5 bar inside, but the system sits at 13.5 bar, so a 3.5 Vi machine under-compresses here. When the port opens, discharge gas flows backward into the pocket, which is the situation the Purdue paper described at its high-condensing conditions. The matching Vi is about 4.2, so a machine near 4 would be a better fit. Two cautions: k is not constant (the NIST vapor value at 35 C is about 1.51, which gives a lower matching Vi near 3.7), and with k = 1.3 the answer would be about 4.5, so treat the matching Vi as an estimate. The City paper also notes that injected oil takes up volume in the pocket and raises the effective built-in ratio, so a real machine does not follow the ideal numbers exactly.

Conditions change through the year, so a fixed Vi cannot match every one. Which Vi values a given model offers is in its maker's literature.

Slide valve capacity control

The Purdue paper from Xi'an Jiaotong describes the slide valve as a part that moves along the rotors. At full load it sits closed against its stop. At part load it opens a bypass port, so some of the trapped gas is vented back to suction before it is fully compressed. Capacity and COP fall as load drops. Power does not fall as fast as capacity, so a compressor idling at low slide position is running less efficiently than one at full load. For our purposes, remember that slide position is a good indicator of load, and a technician can compare it with the plant's actual demand.

Oil injection, separator and cooler

The same research group lists three jobs for injected oil: it seals the clearances between rotors and casing, it absorbs heat as the gas is compressed, and it lubricates the bearings. Too much oil drawn in from the suction line takes up working volume and cuts efficiency. The 1972 Purdue paper adds an ammonia example at a 10:1 pressure ratio: without oil, an adiabatic discharge temperature near 380 F is predicted, and with oil about 170 F. It also states a design goal that discharge temperature should not exceed 212 F.

Because the oil leaves with the gas, an oil separator must remove it at the discharge. Separation is never perfect, so some oil carries over into the system. That paper describes gravity settling in the tank and a knitted-wire demister, with a secondary coalescer for very low carryover. The separated oil is cooled in an oil cooler, which may use water, air or refrigerant, and returned to the compressor. In that design the oil tank sits at discharge pressure and a pump delivers oil to the bearings and injection points at about 30 psi above discharge. The paper says the unit shuts down if manifold oil pressure falls, which is the idea behind low oil pressure differential safeties. Always use the settings in the maker's manual for the actual machine.

What we check on a routine visit

This list is our own checklist. We compare each reading with the maker's manual and the plant log, not with a number from this page.

  • Oil level and temperature. The 1972 paper lists oil level, oil temperature control and filter pressure drop as the main routine items.
  • Separator and filter differential. A growing pressure drop across the filter or separator points to a plugged element. Compare with the last visit.
  • Discharge temperature and superheat. High readings mean poor oil cooling or poor oil flow; see Compressor Discharge Line Temperature as a Diagnostic.
  • Slide valve position against load. If the valve sits fully open while the plant is lightly loaded, or hunts constantly, look at the controls and the load.
  • Vibration and noise. Compare with the baseline taken when the machine was healthy.
  • Oil analysis. Take samples on the plant's schedule; see Chiller and Compressor Oil Analysis.
  • Safeties. Confirm the oil pressure differential and high discharge safeties as the plant procedure requires, following lockout rules.

For how screws compare with other chiller compressors, see Chiller Compressors Compared. For parallel rack plants, see Supermarket Refrigeration Racks.

Field card: Screw Compressors in Commercial Refrigeration and Chillers

Match the built-in Vi to the job, and watch the oil.

  • Vi = pocket volume at suction close divided by volume at discharge opening.
  • Matching Vi = (Pd/Ps)^(1/k), absolute pressures.
  • Vi too large: over-compression. Vi too small: under-compression, with backflow at the port.
  • Our ammonia example: ratio 7.10, matching Vi about 4.2, so a 3.5 machine under-compresses.
  • Slide valve opens a bypass to unload; capacity falls faster than power.
  • Oil seals, cools and lubricates. The separator and cooler return it.
  • Check oil level, oil temperature, filter and separator differential, slide position, vibration.
