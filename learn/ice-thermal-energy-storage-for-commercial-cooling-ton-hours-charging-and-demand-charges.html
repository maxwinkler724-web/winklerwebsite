Ice Thermal Energy Storage for Commercial Cooling: Ton-Hours, Charging and Demand Charges

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some big buildings run their chillers at night and cool the building from a tank of ice the next afternoon. This lesson explains why that can pay, how stored cooling is counted in ton-hours, and what an operator watches on a plant that makes ice.

What thermal energy storage is

The U.S. Department of Energy describes thermal energy storage as holding cooling (or heat) in a medium and releasing it later to cut peak electric use. For cooling, the medium is chilled water or ice. The idea is to separate when the chiller runs from when the building needs cooling. Because the chiller no longer has to chase the afternoon peak, it can be sized closer to the average load. DOE notes that many office buildings have daytime loads two or more times their average. See Chiller Plant Fundamentals for the machine itself.

Ice or chilled water

A chilled water tank stores cooling as a temperature change. DOE says these tanks typically hold supply water at 39 to 42 F, while return water comes back at 55 to 60 F or higher, so a chilled water tank must be large; DOE says stratified tanks are the most common design. Ice stores cooling as latent heat: DOE gives 144 Btu per pound for ice melting. That is far more energy per gallon of tank, but the chiller has to make a fluid at least 15 F colder than a conventional cooling plant uses, which is why ice plants run glycol.

Ice tanks come in a few forms. EVAPCO's guide describes ice-on-coil tanks, where glycol runs through coils submerged in water and ice builds on the tubes. In an external melt tank, ice grows in a ring around each coil to roughly 65 percent ice, and warm return water circulates over the outside and melts it from the outside in, so tank water can leave near 34 F. In an internal melt tank the container holds roughly 65 to 80 percent ice, the coils are spaced closer so ice bridges between them, and warm glycol runs through the coils to melt the ice from the inside, while the tank water stays still. Encapsulated-ice tanks also exist, but we did not find a source we could read for them, so we leave them out here. Product choice belongs to the engineer who designs the plant.

Ton-hours: the unit for stored cooling

CALMAC's technical introduction (published by Trane) explains that a cooling load is measured in tons, but a storage system is measured in ton-hours: tons multiplied by hours. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu per hour, so one ton-hour is 12,000 Btu. EVAPCO adds that a full ton of ice (2,000 lb at 144 Btu per lb) holds 288,000 Btu, the same as 24 ton-hours.

Charging at night

During off-peak hours the chiller cools a glycol solution below freezing and sends it through the tank coils. CALMAC's example uses a 25 percent ethylene glycol solution cooled to about 25 F, which freezes roughly 95 percent of the tank water. EVAPCO's example builds with glycol leaving the chiller near 22 F, with a glycol temperature drop of 5 to 7 F across the tank. Making ice costs capacity. CALMAC says that cooling the solution to 25 F instead of 44 to 45 F reduces the chiller's nominal capacity by about 30 to 35 percent, and EVAPCO's guide uses a 35 percent derate for ice-build duty. CALMAC adds that this is a capacity derate, not much of an efficiency penalty, because cool night air helps the condenser; for air-cooled chillers it cites efficiency gains of 2 to 8 percent. Glycol also thins heat transfer slightly, so the supply setpoint may need to go 1 to 2 F lower. See Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops for how concentration is tested.

Full storage and partial storage

In full storage the chiller sits idle during the on-peak window and the ice carries the whole load. The compressors and tower fans stay off at the expensive hours. EVAPCO says this costs more for the tank and the chiller and fits where on-peak energy is scarce or costly. In partial storage a smaller chiller runs during the day alongside the ice. EVAPCO puts the partial-storage chiller at roughly 60 percent of the conventional size; CALMAC says chiller capacity can often drop 50 to 60 percent or more. Partial plants often behave like full storage on mild days.

Why the rate structure makes it pay

Two charges matter. A demand charge bills the highest kW you pull in a short window, often 15 or 30 minutes, once a month or once a year. CALMAC's example uses $13 per kW per month, and a DOE and NREL ice storage webinar shows a $14 per kW example. Time-of-use rates charge more per kWh in the afternoon than at night; CALMAC says many suppliers offer night energy prices that are 20 to 90 percent lower. DOE says the savings depend on your demand charges and on the gap between on-peak and off-peak energy rates. If neither exists on the bill, storage has little to save.

Worked example

These are teaching values we chose, not utility data. A building has a stated peak of 300 tons for 6 afternoon hours.

  1. Our own: ton-hours = 300 tons × 6 h = 1,800 ton-hours.
  2. Our own: Btu = 1,800 × 12,000 = 21,600,000 Btu.
  3. Partial storage: a chiller covers a stated 150 tons for the same 6 hours, so it delivers 150 × 6 = 900 ton-hours. Storage must supply 1,800 − 900 = 900 ton-hours (10,800,000 Btu). That is 37.5 tons of ice at 288,000 Btu each, or 75,000 lb at 144 Btu per lb, counting only the latent heat.
  4. Demand savings, with a stated 0.7 kW/ton chiller and a stated $15 per kW demand charge: the 150 tons shifted would have drawn 150 × 0.7 = 105 kW during the peak window. Our own estimate: 105 kW × $15 = $1,575 per month, about $18,900 over a 12-month year if the charge applied every month.
  5. Charging check: if 900 ton-hours are made over a 10-hour night, the chiller must average 90 tons at ice-making conditions. With a 35 percent derate, a chiller needs about 90 ÷ 0.65 = 138 tons of nominal capacity. The 150-ton machine in the example is just enough, before losses.

Our own caveats: the estimate ignores the glycol pump, the tank's heat gain, and the kW the chiller draws at night, which are billed at the off-peak rate and, if the utility meters demand only on-peak, do not hit the demand charge. A real design must come from the utility tariff and a load profile, not this sketch.

Operator checks

  • Glycol concentration. BAC's maintenance manual for an internal melt tank calls for inhibited ethylene glycol, typically 25 percent by weight, no automotive antifreeze, and testing with a refractometer every 6 months or at seasonal start-up. It also wants the glycol supplier to verify the inhibitor level once a year, and it lists freeze points of about 14 F at 20 percent and about 3 F at 30 percent (−10 C and −16 C). It says to keep the coldest operating temperature at least 3 to 4 C above the freeze point.
  • Tank inventory. Ice is usually measured from the rise in tank water level. EVAPCO describes a device that signals the end of the build when the water level shows storage is full. In BAC's manual a 4-20 mA signal reads 0 percent at 4 mA and 100 percent at 20 mA, and the operator re-zeros it by melting all ice and checking the sight tube at least monthly in operation. Do not overfill a tank; the manual says to fill only to the zero-ice mark.
  • Charge and discharge. Watch that each build finishes before the on-peak window and that each discharge melts out the ice, because ice left behind is wasted energy. BAC says not to start a new build until roughly 15 percent of the ice has melted, and to melt any ice at the top or walls of a tank that saw subfreezing air before building.
  • Controls. CALMAC notes that a chiller priority or ice priority strategy may be changed with rates, season and occupancy, so log which mode the plant is in.

The plant's free cooling from the tower is a different strategy that can work with or against storage; see Waterside Economizers and Cooling Tower Fan VFDs, Staging and Condenser Water Reset.

Field card: Ice Thermal Energy Storage

Make ice when power is cheap, melt it when power is dear.

  • Ton-hour = 1 ton of cooling for 1 hour = 12,000 Btu.
  • Ice stores latent heat: 144 Btu per lb; 288,000 Btu per ton of ice.
  • Charge with sub-freezing glycol (examples: 22 to 25 F); expect a 30 to 35 percent chiller capacity derate.
  • Full storage: chiller off at peak. Partial storage: smaller chiller plus ice.
  • Payback depends on demand charges and on-peak versus off-peak rates.
  • Check glycol percent and inhibitor, ice inventory, and that each charge and discharge completes.
