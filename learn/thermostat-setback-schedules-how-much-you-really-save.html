Thermostat Setback Schedules: How Much You Really Save

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Customers ask it constantly: "How much does turning down the thermostat save?" The honest answer is a range, not a promise. The U.S. Department of Energy gives one headline number, and it comes with conditions. This lesson shows what the number means, how to do the math for a real house, and when a setback backfires.

What a setback is

A setback is a scheduled change to the thermostat setpoint during hours when comfort matters less, usually overnight or while nobody is home. In heating season the setpoint goes down. In cooling season it goes up. The schedule then returns to the comfort setting before people need it. A programmable or smart thermostat does this automatically, which is the whole point: people rarely remember to adjust a thermostat by hand every day.

DOE's number, and its conditions

The Department of Energy's thermostat page says you can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling by turning your thermostat back 7 to 10 degrees F for 8 hours a day from its normal setting. Read the wording carefully. It says "as much as," so 10% is a ceiling, not an average. It assumes a drop of 7 to 10 degrees. And it assumes about 8 hours a day, which is one third of the day. The same page says the savings are greater in milder climates than in extreme ones.

The reason a setback saves energy is simple. A house loses heat to the outdoors faster when the inside is much warmer than the outside. Hold the house cooler for several hours and it leaks less heat during those hours, so the system burns less fuel or uses less electricity.

The "it works harder to catch up" myth

A common belief is that the furnace has to work extra hard to bring the house back up, wiping out the savings. DOE calls this a common misconception. Its explanation is that once the house cools below its normal temperature it loses heat more slowly, so the energy saved while it is cool is not given back during recovery. The system does run for a longer stretch in the morning, but it does not burn more than it saved. Do not tell customers that setbacks are a waste because of recovery.

Worked example

This example uses made-up numbers to show the arithmetic. The percentages come from DOE. The dollar amounts are assumptions you should replace with the customer's real bill.

  1. The schedule. Comfort setting 70 F. Overnight setback to 62 F from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m.
  2. Check the drop. 70 F minus 62 F = 8 F. That is inside DOE's 7 to 10 F range.
  3. Check the duration. 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. = 8 hours, which matches DOE's 8 hours.
  4. Assume a yearly bill. Say heating and cooling together cost $1,800 a year.
  5. Apply the ceiling. $1,800 x 0.10 = $180 per year at most.
  6. Be realistic. Suppose the house only reaches half of that ceiling because the schedule is often overridden or the setback runs fewer hours than planned. (DOE notes that milder climates tend to see larger percentage savings, not smaller.) $180 x 0.5 = $90 per year.

So the answer to the customer is "somewhere from nothing up to about $180 for this bill, and probably less than the maximum." A different house, climate, thermostat habit or equipment type will give a different result. DOE's figure is a planning ceiling for a typical schedule, not a guarantee for any one home.

The heat pump catch

Everything above assumes a furnace or boiler. Heat pumps are different. DOE's thermostat page says programmable thermostats are generally not recommended for heat pumps, because lowering the setting in heating mode can make the unit run inefficiently and cancel the savings. It says a moderate, steady setting is most cost effective, and it notes that some companies sell programmable thermostats made for heat pumps that limit use of the backup electric resistance heat.

DOE's page on operating a heat pump is more direct: do not set back the heat pump's thermostat if it causes the backup heating to come on. The same page says backup heat such as electric resistance or electric strip heaters is usually more expensive to operate because it is less efficient. In plain terms, if the morning recovery calls for strip heat, the setback costs more than it saves.

For the field, that means asking what kind of system it is before suggesting a deep setback, and keeping heat pump setbacks small. Learn how backup heat is staged in Emergency Heat vs. Auxiliary Heat and Lockout Temperatures, and why long run times are often normal in Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter: Is That Normal?.

Smart thermostats

ENERGY STAR describes a smart thermostat as a Wi-Fi device that adjusts heating and cooling settings automatically. Some models learn your habits and shift to energy saving temperatures when you are asleep or away, and some use geofencing to detect that you left and set the system back. The program says labeled models have been independently certified, based on field data, to deliver energy savings. It also tells buyers to confirm the thermostat is compatible with their heating and cooling system. For the wiring side, including the common wire, see Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Slow systems

DOE's page mentions that some manufacturers offer thermostats that track the performance of the heating system so heat can be timed for steam and radiant floor systems. It does not give savings numbers for them. These systems respond slowly, so recovery needs a head start. See Radiant Floor Heating Basics and follow the equipment maker's guidance.

Short tech section: setting up a schedule

  • Identify the equipment first: furnace, boiler, heat pump with or without electric backup, or electric resistance.
  • Set the recovery time so the house is back at the comfort setting before people wake or return.
  • For a furnace, a drop of 7 to 10 F for about 8 hours matches DOE's example.
  • For a heat pump, keep the drop small and watch whether the backup heat energizes during recovery. If it does, shrink the setback.
  • Check that the thermostat type matches the system. DOE notes electric resistance heat needs line-voltage thermostats.
  • Teach the customer how to override without wrecking the program.

Field card: Thermostat Setback Schedules

10% is a ceiling, and heat pumps are the exception.

  • DOE: up to 10% a year for 7 to 10 F back, about 8 hours a day.
  • Savings vary with climate, house and habits. Milder climates save more.
  • Recovery does not erase the savings; DOE calls that a misconception.
  • Example: 70 to 62 F = 8 F; 8 hours; $1,800 x 10% = $180 maximum.
  • Heat pump: do not set back if it brings on backup heat.
  • Electric strip heat costs more to run than the compressor.
  • Match the thermostat to the equipment.
