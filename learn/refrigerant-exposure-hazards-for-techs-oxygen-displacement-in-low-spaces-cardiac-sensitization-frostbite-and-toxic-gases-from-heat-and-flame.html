Refrigerant Exposure Hazards for Techs: Oxygen Displacement in Low Spaces, Cardiac Sensitization, Frostbite and Toxic Gases from Heat and Flame

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most refrigerants are not very toxic at the amounts you smell in a normal service call. The danger is different: a big release in a small low room, a heart that overreacts at high concentrations, liquid that freezes skin, and the poisonous gases that form when refrigerant touches a flame. This lesson explains each hazard in plain terms so you can see why the usual shop rules exist.

Heavy vapor and thin air

Many refrigerant vapors are much heavier than air. The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists a relative gas density of 4.2 for dichlorodifluoromethane and 5.93 for dichlorotetrafluoroethane, with air at 1. An international chemical safety card for the first one says the gas collects in low spaces, that it reduces oxygen, and that leaks in confined areas can cause suffocation. It also tells people to check the oxygen content before entering. The same idea holds for the newer compounds. The molecular weight of R-134a is 102.03 according to the NIST Chemistry WebBook, and air averages roughly 29, so by our own arithmetic that vapor is about 3.5 times as heavy as air. Check the safety data sheet for the exact refrigerant on your job; this lesson on reading one shows where to look.

Heavy vapor does not mix quickly. It flows downhill like water: into pits, stairwells, basements, crawlspaces, mechanical rooms and the corners of walk-in coolers. OSHA's confined space rule treats an atmosphere with less than 19.5 percent oxygen by volume as oxygen deficient, and it counts oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent as a hazardous atmosphere. Because the vapor lies low, a person can be standing in a bad layer while a person at the top of the stairs feels fine. See Confined Spaces in HVAC Work for the air-testing order, and the liquid nitrogen storage room lesson for the same oxygen-depletion idea with a different gas.

Our own worked example: how much does a leak take away?

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measured event. Assumptions: a basement equipment room 10 ft by 12 ft with an 8 ft ceiling and no ventilation running; 20 lb of an R-134a-type refrigerant escapes as vapor; the vapor mixes evenly with the room air (the simple case, and the most favorable one); and the vapor density is 0.26 lb per cubic foot. We got that density from the ideal gas relation: 102.03 g/mol (NIST) divided by about 24.05 L/mol at room temperature gives 4.24 g/L, which is about 0.26 lb/ft³. Real saturated vapor and cold air differ a little.

  1. Room volume: 10 × 12 × 8 = 960 ft³.
  2. Vapor volume: 20 lb ÷ 0.26 lb/ft³ = 76.9 ft³.
  3. Concentration by volume: 76.9 ÷ 960 = 0.080, or 8.0 percent (about 80,000 ppm).
  4. Oxygen left, using oxygen = 20.9 × (1 − fraction): 20.9 × (1 − 0.080) = 20.9 × 0.920 = 19.2 percent.
  5. OSHA's oxygen-deficient line is 19.5 percent, so 19.2 percent is below it. Working backward, 19.5 ÷ 20.9 = 0.933, so a fraction of 0.067 (6.7 percent, about 64 ft³, or roughly 17 lb) is enough to cross the line in this room.

Two lessons come from the numbers. First, a charge that fits on a service scale can push a small closed room past the oxygen line. Second, uneven mixing is worse. If the same 76.9 ft³ settled as a pure layer over the 120 ft² floor, it would be 76.9 ÷ 120 = 0.64 ft, about 8 inches deep, and air in that layer would have almost no oxygen. That is the layer drawn in the figure. Also notice that 8 percent is far above the 1.5 percent (15,000 ppm) that NIOSH lists as the immediately dangerous level for the older refrigerants in the pocket guide pages above. For those compounds the toxic effects can matter before the oxygen number does.

Cardiac sensitization

At high concentrations some halogenated compounds make the heart overreact to adrenaline. NIOSH lists cardiac arrhythmias and cardiac arrest among the symptoms for the older refrigerants above, and the international card says short exposures may affect the heart. A conference paper on testing for this effect describes it as increased sensitivity of the heart to adrenaline after exposure to high concentrations, and reports that in animal tests, fright or exercise that raised the animal's own adrenaline also triggered arrhythmias. The National Institute on Drug Abuse describes the same pattern with inhaled solvents and sprays: irregular, rapid heart rhythms and fatal heart failure within minutes, even after one use.

Practical meaning: if you are in a refrigerant cloud, do not sprint, struggle or panic; walk out calmly if you can, and do not make a crisis worse by running back in to grab tools. Not every refrigerant behaves the same. An archived OSHA chemical sampling page for R-134a notes that human volunteers breathing up to 8,000 ppm for one hour showed no effects on pulse, blood pressure or electrocardiogram. We take two points from that: potency varies by compound, and 8,000 ppm is still not a number to aim for.

Frostbite from liquid

Refrigerant liquid boils far below freezing at atmospheric pressure, so contact takes heat from skin and eyes fast. NIOSH lists frostbite from liquid contact, and the international card adds that rapid evaporation of the liquid can cause it. Splash goggles and insulated gloves matter when you connect hoses or release a cylinder. Treatment is covered in First Aid for HVAC Jobsite Injuries, and glove choices are in Work Gloves for HVAC Techs.

Flame, hot surfaces and acid gases

The refrigerant itself is usually not the worst thing in the air; what it turns into can be. The international card for dichlorodifluoromethane says that contact with hot surfaces or flames produces toxic and corrosive gases, naming hydrogen chloride, phosgene, hydrogen fluoride and carbonyl fluoride. The chlorine-based products come from refrigerants that contain chlorine. Refrigerants with only fluorine make the fluorine-based gases. A 2014 university lab paper heated R-32, R-1234yf and R-134a mixed with air and found hydrogen fluoride was the main toxic product, appearing at roughly 500 to 590 degrees C depending on the refrigerant, and carbonyl fluoride appearing around 600 to 650 degrees C or higher. The authors did not study combustion or real torch flames, so we use the study only to show the products are real, not to predict amounts.

The NIOSH pocket guide entries show why these gases matter. Hydrogen fluoride has an immediately dangerous level of 30 ppm and causes eye, skin and nose irritation, pulmonary edema and burns. Hydrogen chloride has a ceiling of 5 ppm and an immediately dangerous level of 50 ppm. Phosgene has an immediately dangerous level of 2 ppm and a limit of 0.1 ppm. The federal medical guideline for hydrogen fluoride says lung injury can show up 12 to 36 hours after exposure, so feeling fine after leaving is not a clearance.

That is the reason for the shop rule: recover refrigerant and purge the line before you braze, and keep open flame away from a leaking system. A leak you did not notice, a torch near a burst line or any hot surface in a refrigerant-filled space can make acid gas. Read Hot Work Permits and Fire Watch and Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines for the work routine.

Smell and eye irritation as warning signs

Do not depend on your nose for the refrigerant. The international card states that the odor warning is insufficient when the exposure limit is exceeded. Irritation is a better clue for the decomposition gases: NIOSH lists eye, nose and throat irritation for hydrogen fluoride and hydrogen chloride. A sharp, stinging smell or burning eyes near a torch or hot equipment is a stop signal, not something to push through. Also, a CDC emergency card for hydrogen fluoride gas says not to rely on odor to warn you.

After a large leak

  • Leave first. Walk out, stay upwind and out of low areas, and warn others. Do not re-enter to be a hero. The CDC card for hydrogen fluoride says to stay out of low-lying and confined areas and stay upwind, and OSHA's rule is why entry needs air testing.
  • Call for help. Dial 911 for anyone dizzy, confused, with trouble breathing or passed out. The poison center number on the CDC card is 1-800-222-1222.
  • Ventilate from outside. Open doors and start fans so the vapor leaves the low spot; the international card recommends ventilation or local exhaust, or breathing protection for entry.
  • Test before you go back. Check oxygen and the refrigerant level with a meter, as the card advises, before anyone enters.
  • Get checked later. Lung effects from acid gases can be delayed.

Rooms with big charges use fixed refrigerant detectors, low intakes and alarms; see Refrigerant Machine Rooms. The figure shows the principle: the detector and fan intake are low because the vapor is.

Inhalant abuse and locking caps

Some people deliberately breathe refrigerant from outdoor units to get high, and NIDA warns that inhalants can cause sudden death from cardiac arrest even on first use. Techs cannot fix that, but we can avoid supplying it: leave access caps on, use locking caps where the customer or code asks, close cylinder valves, and never leave open cylinders around. See Locking Refrigerant Access Caps.

Field card: Refrigerant Exposure Hazards

Heavy vapor pools low. Flame turns it into acid.

  • Most vapors are heavier than air: pits, basements, crawlspaces, walk-ins and machine rooms trap them.
  • OSHA oxygen-deficient line: below 19.5 percent. Our example: 20 lb in 960 ft³ leaves 19.2 percent.
  • High concentrations can set off heart rhythm problems. Stay calm, walk out.
  • Liquid contact freezes skin and eyes. Wear goggles and insulated gloves.
  • Recover refrigerant before brazing. Flame or hot surfaces make hydrogen fluoride, hydrogen chloride and carbonyl halides.
  • Smell is not a reliable alarm. Eye and throat burning means leave.
  • After a big leak: leave, call 911, ventilate from outside, test the air, then re-enter.
  • Keep caps on and cylinders closed.
