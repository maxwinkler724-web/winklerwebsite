Window and Portable Air Conditioners: Sizing, CEER and Single vs Dual Hose

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The box says 8,000 BTU, the efficiency number says CEER 15, and the portable next to it says it has one hose or two. This lesson turns those labels into a decision: how big a unit your room needs, what the efficiency rating means in kilowatt-hours and dollars, and how to plug in, mount and clean the unit safely.

Check the sources

Check the sources. Ratings, rules and product lines change. This page was built from federal pages and maker manuals read in 2026 and is general education. Your unit's own manual and nameplate always win.

Step 1: Size the room with ENERGY STAR's table

ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner page gives a chart of floor area against cooling capacity in BTU/hr, based on an 8-foot ceiling. A few rows: 150 to 250 sq ft needs 6,000; 250 to 300 needs 7,000; 300 to 350 needs 8,000; 350 to 400 needs 9,000; 450 to 550 needs 12,000. Area is length times width, in feet.

Then it lists adjustments. Heavy shade: reduce capacity by 10 percent. Very sunny room: increase by 10 percent. Occupants: add 600 BTU/hr for each regular occupant beyond the number the table assumes (the page's wording on that threshold is not perfectly clear, so treat it as a small nudge). Kitchen: add 4,000 BTU/hr. Higher ceilings may call for a larger unit.

ENERGY STAR and the DOE's federal purchasing program both warn against oversizing. A unit that is too big costs more, uses more energy, and cools in short bursts that remove humidity poorly, so the room can feel clammy.

Worked example: a sunny 15 x 20 ft bedroom, two people

This is an invented room. Follow each step.

  1. Area: 15 ft x 20 ft = 300 sq ft.
  2. Table row: 300 sits on the edge of the 250-to-300 and 300-to-350 rows. Take the lower row: 7,000 BTU/hr.
  3. Sun adjustment: very sunny, so add 10 percent. 7,000 x 1.10 = 7,700 BTU/hr.
  4. Occupants and kitchen: two people, no kitchen. Even if you added 600 for one person, 7,700 + 600 = 8,300, which still points to the same size class.
  5. Pick a size: the nearest standard size at or above 7,700 is 8,000 BTU/hr.

Now the energy. Midea lists an 8,000 BTU window unit, model MAW08V1YWT-S, at CEER 15.0 and ENERGY STAR certified. CEER is in BTU per watt-hour (the Federal Register defines it in Btu/Wh), so watts = BTU/hr divided by CEER.

  1. Average watts: 8,000 / 15.0 = 533 W (533.3).
  2. Invented runtime: 8 hours a day for 100 summer days = 800 hours.
  3. Energy: kWh = BTU/hr / CEER x hours / 1000 = 8,000 / 15.0 x 800 / 1000 = 426.7 kWh.
  4. Cost at an invented $0.18 per kWh: 426.7 x 0.18 = $76.80 for the summer.

This is a planning estimate. CEER is a test-lab number; your real draw rises on the hottest afternoons and falls when the compressor cycles off. The method still lets you compare two models: the one with the higher CEER and the same BTU uses fewer kWh for the same hours.

What CEER means

CEER is the Combined Energy Efficiency Ratio. DOE's test rule describes it as a combination of cooling output with the energy used while cooling and in the standby and off modes, so it counts more than just the cooling hours. Higher is better: DOE's purchasing page lists a CEER 14.7 ENERGY STAR example using about 612 kWh a year against 826 kWh for a CEER 10.9 model in its own comparison. Compare CEER only between units of similar size and type, because ENERGY STAR sets different baseline values by capacity and cabinet style.

Why portable ratings look lower

DOE's portable air conditioner test (10 CFR 430, Appendix CC) came from a June 2016 rule, and ACHR News reported that makers had to use it for efficiency claims from late November 2016. DOE found that the older industry test did not fully account for real-world losses such as air infiltrating into the room and heat gaining through the hoses. The new test subtracts those effects, then reports seasonally adjusted cooling capacity (SACC), a weighted average of two outdoor conditions: 20 percent at 95°F and 80 percent at 83°F. A portable's SACC is therefore lower than a capacity figure from the older industry method. When you size a portable, use the SACC figure, not the headline BTU.

DOE says single-hose units typically cause more infiltration air than comparable dual-hose units, and that it is most noticeable for single-hose designs. The Appliance Standards Awareness Project summarizes DOE as estimating that portable units use about twice the energy of a typical new window unit. Federal efficiency standards for portables apply to units made or imported from January 10, 2025.

Single hose or dual hose?

  • Single hose: the room is pulled slightly negative, so warm air slips in around doors and windows to replace what the unit exhausts.
  • Dual hose: a second hose carries outside air through the condenser, so less pulled-in air to cool again.
  • Either type: do not add hose length. One maker's portable manual says extensions reduce cooling efficiency and may damage the unit, and that the hose must not bend past 45 degrees or be blocked.

If a window unit fits your window, the ASAP summary above suggests it is the lower-energy choice. A portable earns its place where windows will not take a unit.

Electrical safety

One window unit maker's manual says plainly: "Do not use an extension cord or an adaptor plug." Another maker's portable manual specifies a 115 V, 15-amp, grounded three-prong outlet on a dedicated circuit and says not to run other appliances on it. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's general guidance is that extension cords are for temporary use only and should never be loaded beyond their rating. That CPSC sheet does not single out air conditioners, so follow your unit's manual. Never remove the ground prong. If the cord or its built-in protection plug is damaged, stop using the unit and get a replacement from the maker.

Window installation safety

Maker manuals treat the window as a fall hazard. One says to use two people, never leave the unit unattended while installing, fit the support bracket and anti-tip brackets with the supplied screws, lock the sash, and keep the rear of the unit outdoors. It also says to tilt the unit slightly outward so water drains. In an apartment, ask your landlord or building manager before drilling into a sill or frame, because your lease may have rules.

Filter cleaning

Both manuals read say to clean the filter about every two weeks while running. One window maker says to wash it in warm water no hotter than 104°F, or vacuum it, and let it dry fully; a portable maker says to use warm water with a neutral cleaner and air-dry in the shade. Unplug first and never run the unit without the filter.

Field card: Window and Portable Air Conditioners

Size by area and adjustments, then compare CEER.

  • Area = length x width; look up BTU/hr in ENERGY STAR's table (8 ft ceiling).
  • Shade: minus 10 percent. Very sunny: plus 10 percent. Kitchen: plus 4,000 BTU/hr.
  • kWh = BTU/hr / CEER x hours / 1000. Cost = kWh x rate.
  • Portables: size by SACC, not the headline BTU. Dual hose loses less to infiltration.
  • No extension cords or adapters; follow the maker's outlet rating.
  • Use the window brackets, work in pairs, and clean the filter every two weeks.
