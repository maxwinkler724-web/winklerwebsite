Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits: Long Condenser Feeders and 24-Volt Control Wire Runs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every wire has resistance, so every wire eats a little voltage on the way to the load. On a short run you never notice. On a 120-foot condenser feeder or a 150-foot thermostat run, it can be the reason a compressor struggles or a contactor will not pull in. This lesson gives the formula, a worked example for each case, and a way to measure the problem.

What voltage drop is

Voltage drop is the voltage lost across the conductors while current flows. If the panel puts out 240 V and the unit sees 232 V, the drop is 8 V. The energy lost becomes heat in the wire, and the equipment gets less than it was built for. A university engineering tech note says excessive drop can mean the equipment gets too little voltage to operate properly and that premature motor burnout is a common result (Source 2).

The formula

For a single-phase circuit, both conductors carry the current, so the drop is twice the one-way run:

VD = 2 × K × I × L / CM

  • K is the resistivity of the metal in ohm circular mils per foot. The university note gives 10.79 for copper at 25°C and 12.87 at 75°C (Source 1). The same publisher's voltage drop note uses 12 for copper at 50°C (Source 2). We use 12.9 below, the 75°C value rounded. That is our own choice, and it is on the cautious side for a warm conductor.
  • I is the load current in amps.
  • L is the one-way length in feet.
  • CM is the conductor area in circular mils. From the university table, copper: 18 AWG is 1,620 CM, 14 AWG is 4,110, 12 AWG is 6,530, 10 AWG is 10,380 and 8 AWG is 16,510 (Source 1).

For three phase, replace the 2 with 1.732 (the university note writes 1.73 for the three-phase drop equation, Source 2). Percent drop is VD divided by the source voltage, times 100 (arithmetic is our own).

The 3% and 5% guideline

NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code, puts voltage drop guidance in informational notes, not in mandatory rules. A trade magazine article quoting the note under Section 210.19(A) says branch circuits should be sized so the drop at the farthest outlet does not exceed 3%, and that the total for feeders plus branch circuit should not exceed 5% (Source 5). The university note describes the same split, about 2% on feeders and 3% on branch circuits, within 5% overall, and says the Code leaves it to the installer (Source 2). A federal tip sheet on motors also says the National Electrical Code calls for a 3% limit (Source 4). Treat 3% as the target. Your local code adoption and the equipment maker's installation instructions still govern the actual installation.

Why compressors and contactors suffer

A federal motor tip sheet lists what happens to a general-purpose motor at 90% of nameplate voltage: starting and maximum running torque fall about 19%, full-load current rises about 5 to 10%, efficiency drops about 1 to 3% and slip rises about 22% (Source 3). Torque falls with the square of the voltage ratio, so a modest sag costs more than it looks. Another tip sheet says undervoltage can bring higher currents, reduced starting torque and lower efficiency (Source 4). For a compressor that means a hard start (low starting torque against full head pressure), more heat in the windings and a motor protector that trips. These tip sheets describe general motors; a hermetic compressor is not the same machine, so read its maker documents for the stated limits. We have not confirmed a published pull-in or drop-out voltage for contactors, so read the maker's coil data before you set a pass-fail number.

Voltage drop is also worst when current is highest, at compressor start. Our own field rule: if a unit runs but fails or chatters at start, check the voltage during the start attempt, not before it.

Nameplate range

The same DOE tip sheet notes that motors are designed for plus or minus 10% of nameplate voltage (Source 3). Do not read that as permission to run at the bottom of the range. The nameplate current and the torque losses above are the reason to keep the drop small. For how to read MCA, MOCP and the voltage range on a unit, see Reading an HVAC Nameplate. On three-phase units also see three-phase voltage imbalance.

Worked example 1: 25 A condenser, 120 ft, 10 AWG

Assumptions (our own, for teaching): 240 V single-phase, 25 A running load, 120 ft one way from the panel, copper, K = 12.9.

  1. 10 AWG: VD = 2 × 12.9 × 25 × 120 / 10,380 = 77,400 / 10,380 = 7.46 V.
  2. Percent = 7.46 / 240 = 3.1%. That is slightly over a 3% target.
  3. 8 AWG: VD = 77,400 / 16,510 = 4.69 V. Percent = 4.69 / 240 = 1.95%. Yes, 8 AWG fixes it.

The result is close to the line. With K = 12 (Source 2) the 10 AWG answer is about 2.9%. That sensitivity is the point: when a run is near 3%, upsize rather than argue about K. Also remember that conductor size is first set by ampacity and the nameplate MCA, and that voltage drop is a second check. A larger wire may need a lug size the disconnect accepts. See condenser disconnects, fuses and breakers.

Worked example 2: 24 V control wire, 150 ft, 18 AWG

Assumptions (our own): 24 V transformer output, 1 A load through the control wire (assumed; real coil and relay loads vary, so check yours), 150 ft one way, copper, K = 12.9.

  1. 18 AWG: VD = 2 × 12.9 × 1 × 150 / 1,620 = 3,870 / 1,620 = 2.39 V.
  2. Percent = 2.39 / 24 = 10%, leaving about 21.6 V at the far end.
  3. 14 AWG: 3,870 / 4,110 = 0.94 V, or 3.9%.

A 10% drop on a 24 V circuit is only 2.4 V, but that is a large share of a small voltage, so the relay or contactor coil sees much less margin. When a long run, a heavy coil load or several devices on one wire pair push the drop above your target, move to a heavier conductor or add a separate control transformer at the remote end. Use only wire and listed cable rated for the application. For how the transformer, relay and thermostat fit together, see Relays, Transformers and 24V Control and Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

How to measure it

These steps are our own practice, using standard meter technique. Follow your site's safety rules for live work and use a rated meter.

  1. Read the supply voltage at the panel or disconnect line side with the unit off, and note it.
  2. Start the unit. At the unit's line terminals, read the voltage while the compressor is running, and also during the start attempt if you can.
  3. Subtract. The difference is the loaded drop in volts. Divide by the supply voltage for percent.
  4. For 24 V, read between R and C at the transformer and again at the contactor coil terminals while the call for cooling is active. A reading taken with the circuit unloaded or open will look fine and tell you nothing.

If the measured drop is higher than the formula predicts, suspect a loose or burned termination, a worn contactor, or an undersized splice, not just wire size.

Field card: Voltage drop

Meter it loaded, at the unit, not just at the panel.

  • VD = 2 × K × I × L / CM single phase; use 1.732 for three phase.
  • K about 12.9 for copper at 75°C; CM: 10 AWG 10,380, 8 AWG 16,510, 18 AWG 1,620.
  • Target 3% branch, 5% feeder plus branch (NFPA 70 informational note).
  • Example: 25 A, 120 ft, 10 AWG = 7.46 V, 3.1%; 8 AWG = 1.95%.
  • Example: 1 A, 150 ft, 18 AWG on 24 V = 2.39 V, 10%.
  • Low voltage means less torque, more current and more heat.
