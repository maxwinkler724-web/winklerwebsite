Replacing a Hot-Water Heating Circulator Pump Step by Step

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A circulator swap looks like a one-hour job, and it can be. It also goes wrong in quiet ways: the wrong pump for the loop, a pump that goes in backward, a system left full of air, or a boiler that now runs with the wrong temperature drop. This lesson walks the job in order, from proving the pump is the problem to proving the new one moves the right amount of water.

Step 1: Confirm it is the pump

Do not buy a pump on a guess. Our separate lesson on a boiler circulator that is humming, seized or has no power covers those diagnostics, so we do not repeat them here. If you reach this page, you have already decided the pump is bad or too old to trust.

Step 2: Match flow and head

A replacement should do the same hydraulic job as the old one. Two numbers describe that job. Flow is how many gallons per minute (GPM) the loop needs. Head is the pressure the pump must overcome, expressed as feet of water column. University technical documents on pump curves describe head this way and say a pump gives high flow at low head and low flow at high head. A state university lesson adds that the system curve shows the head the piping needs at each flow, and the loop settles where it crosses the pump curve.

At a general level, find your flow on the flow axis, go up to the pump curve, and read across to head. If that matches what the piping needs, the pump suits the loop. A university water-resources document on reading centrifugal pump curves also advises running a pump within roughly 80 to 110 percent of its best-efficiency flow, and warns that operating outside the recommended range can shorten bearing and seal life. Choose a model whose curve passes through your flow and head near that best-efficiency region, not at the far end of the curve.

To get flow, work from the boiler output and the design temperature drop (see the worked example below). To get head, either add up the pipe, fitting and boiler losses (our lesson on adding up head loss shows how) or, in the usual replacement case, match the old pump's published curve. Gauge readings across a running pump give a field estimate of its head. A state environmental agency conversion table lists 1 psi as 2.31 feet of water, which is what lets you turn a pressure difference across the pump into feet of head. Our lesson on estimating circulator flow from gauge readings covers that method, and the ECM circulator modes lesson covers variable-speed setups, so we leave both there.

Before you order, also match the physical details: the flange or union style, the port-to-port length, the voltage on the nameplate and the pipe size.

Step 3: Isolate the pump, or drain the system

Look at the piping on both sides of the pump. If you see isolation valves, usually a pair of valves with flange faces on the pump side, closing both lets you remove the pump with only a cup or two of water to catch. If there are no valves, the section of the system around the pump has to be drained below the pump height, which means a hose, a drain valve, a way to let air in so water can leave, and a plan for where that water goes. Our own general practice: if the pump has no valves, consider adding a pair while it is out, so the next swap does not need a drain.

Hot water scalds. Let the boiler and piping cool before you open anything, and relieve any pressure. Water inside a closed loop may still be hot long after the burner stops.

Step 4: Power off and lockout

Turn off the boiler switch and the pump's own power, then apply your lock and tag to the disconnect. The federal workplace-safety standard on controlling hazardous energy (29 CFR 1910.147) says that after lockout or tagout devices are applied, stored or residual energy must be made safe, and that the authorized person must confirm the equipment is isolated and de-energized before starting work. In practice that means you meter the pump's terminals for zero volts rather than trusting a switch. See our lockout, tagout and verify dead lesson. The standard also says push buttons and selector switches are not energy isolating devices, so a thermostat or pump switch alone is not a lockout.

Step 5: Remove the old motor or the whole pump

Some circulators have a replaceable motor or cartridge head on a pump body that stays in the piping. If the replacement uses the same body, you may only unbolt the motor head and leave the body alone. If the new pump has a different body, take the whole pump out. Either way:

  • Photograph the wiring, then disconnect the wires one at a time.
  • Keep a rag and a pan under the flanges.
  • Support the pump as you loosen the last bolt.
  • Check the flange faces; pitted faces will not seal even with a new gasket.

Step 6: New gaskets and flange bolts

Use new gaskets, never the old ones. Our own general practice is to confirm the new gasket seats flat in its groove or on the flange face, snug the bolts a little at a time in an alternating cross pattern, and then finish tightening to the pump maker's instructions. A pump maker's instruction sheet is the correct source for any torque or bolt size, and we are not giving numbers here because they vary by model. Replace bolts that are rusted or stretched.

Step 7: Check the flow arrow and rotation

Almost every circulator body has a cast arrow showing the direction of flow. Point it the way the water travels through that pipe, away from the pump suction and toward the discharge. Which side of the boiler the pump belongs on, and which way its motor may face, are set by the pump maker and boiler maker, so follow their instruction sheets rather than assuming. Rotation on a small circulator is normally built in, so the main job is installing the body the right way round. Our own tip: after power is back, confirm the pump is running by feel and sound, and confirm the flow later by temperature drop (step 11).

Step 8: Wiring, at a general level

Connect the line, neutral and ground to the terminals marked on the new pump, matching the voltage on the nameplate. Do not guess. If the pump has a terminal box cover, replace it before you restore power. If it is a variable-speed model that takes a control signal, our ECM modes lesson explains what the settings do. Follow the electrical rules that apply where you work.

Step 9: Refill and purge the air

Open the isolation valves slowly. If you drained the system, close the drain, open the fill, and let the system build to its normal cold pressure, which is the number on the boiler's gauge from the last service or the boiler's instructions. Then purge: run water through the loop with the fill open, bleed the vents, and cycle the pump. Air is the most common reason a new pump is noisy or moves no water. See our lesson on air separators and automatic air vents for where air collects. Our own tip: do not run the pump dry; check the maker's instructions.

Step 10: Check for leaks

With the system full, hot and at pressure, look at both flanges, each isolation valve stem and the drain. Wipe them dry with a paper towel and look again after ten minutes, because a weeping gasket may show only when the metal warms and expands.

Step 11: Verify flow by temperature drop

A pump that hums and feels warm is not proof of flow. The proof is the temperature drop across the boiler: supply temperature minus return temperature once the system is warm and calling for heat. Because heat output equals 500 times GPM times temperature drop (a constant for plain water that appears in a federally funded ground-source heat pump report as "K = 500 for water"), a given output with a bigger drop than design means less flow than you wanted, and a smaller drop means more.

Our own worked example

These are our own numbers, with every input stated as an assumption.

  • Assumption: the boiler delivers 80,000 Btu/h to the water.
  • Assumption: the system is plain water with a design temperature drop of 20 degrees F.

Flow needed. GPM = Btu/h / (500 x temperature drop). Step 1: 500 x 20 = 10,000. Step 2: 80,000 / 10,000 = 8.0 GPM. So the pump should move about 8 GPM at the head the loop needs. If we also assume our piping adds up to 12 feet of head (an assumed figure), then we look for a pump curve that passes close to 8 GPM at 12 feet, ideally not near the far end of that curve. That also equals about 5.2 psi (12 / 2.31).

Check after the swap. Assume we measure 180 F supply and 163 F return with the system warm. Step 1: temperature drop = 180 - 163 = 17 degrees F. Step 2: if the output really is 80,000 Btu/h, actual flow = 80,000 / (500 x 17) = 80,000 / 8,500 = about 9.4 GPM. Step 3: compare with the design: 9.4 GPM is about 18 percent more than the 8.0 we wanted (9.4 / 8.0 = 1.18). A drop of 17 degrees against a design of 20 suggests the new pump is moving a bit more water than needed, or the burner is not putting out the full 80,000 Btu/h. Check the pump speed setting and the actual firing rate before concluding anything. Thermometer error of a few degrees matters a lot here, so, as our own tip, use two sensors of the same type, held the same way on the pipe surface under insulation. Our lesson on estimating circulator flow covers glycol corrections and cross-checks.

Field card: Replacing a hot-water heating circulator

Match it, isolate it, point it, purge it, prove it.

  • Confirm the pump is the problem first.
  • Match flow (GPM) and head (feet) to the loop; run near best-efficiency flow.
  • Isolation valves or drain the system. Let the water cool.
  • Lock out, tag out and meter for zero volts.
  • New gaskets; tighten flange bolts evenly in a cross pattern per the maker.
  • Flow arrow with the water; follow the maker for mounting.
  • Refill to the correct pressure, purge air, check for leaks hot.
  • Verify with temperature drop: GPM = Btu/h / (500 x drop).
