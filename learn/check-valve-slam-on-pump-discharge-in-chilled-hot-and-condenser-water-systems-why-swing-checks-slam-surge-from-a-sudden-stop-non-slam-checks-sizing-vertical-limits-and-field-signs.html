Check Valve Slam on Pump Discharge: Causes and Fixes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A loud bang in the pump room right after a pump stops is often a swing check valve closing too late. The water has already started backward, the disc is still open, and when it finally swings shut it stops that moving water all at once. This lesson explains why that happens, which piping layouts invite it, and what the fixes are.

Why a swing check slams

A swing check has a hinged disc. Forward flow holds it open. When the pump stops, forward flow dies away, and the water in the discharge line (and in a tall riser, gravity) begins to run backward. A plain disc cannot close until that backward flow pushes it. By then the water is already moving the wrong way, so the disc is driven into the seat at speed. The backward-moving water is stopped within a fraction of a second, and that sudden stop is what makes the pressure surge you hear as a bang.

The bigger the reverse velocity at the moment of closure, the harder the hit. A federal facilities manual warns that a swing check or the pipeline could be damaged or fail from hydraulic transients, and suggests an outside lever and weight (or a damper) on swing checks as one way to reduce slam. It also calls for care where frequent, rapid flow reversals can happen.

Installs that are prone

  • Parallel pumps. When one of two pumps stops, the running pump keeps the header pressurized, and the stopped pump's check sees a sharp reversal across the disc. Staging and trips are covered in the parallel and series pumps lesson linked below.
  • Tall risers. A high column of water above the check pushes hard backward the moment the pump quits. This is common on condenser water lines running up to a rooftop tower.
  • Fast pump trips. A power loss or safety trip stops the pump far faster than a controlled ramp-down. A variable speed drive that ramps the speed down gives the check more time to close than a sudden power loss does. Slowly ramping also helps because slower flow changes make smaller surges; a state university irrigation publication notes that a slower valve closure produces less surge than an instant one.
  • Long runs. More water in motion means more energy to stop.

The fixes

Spring-loaded non-slam checks. Silent or nozzle-type checks use a spring that pushes the disc toward the seat, with a short stroke. The disc closes as forward flow falls off, before flow reverses. One university facilities guideline we read calls for spring-loaded silent checks on condensate pump discharge and non-slam checks on pump discharges generally, and allows non-slam wafer checks in either a horizontal or a vertical position.

Swing check with lever and weight. The outside weight helps the disc start closing early. It is a fix for an existing swing check, as the federal manual above describes, but it does not make the valve as quick as a spring design.

Slow the stop. Where the controls allow it, ramp the pump down rather than cutting power, and close the discharge valve slowly. The state university irrigation publication says no valve should be closed in under 10 seconds and prefers 30 seconds or more; that is advice for a different kind of system, but the principle carries over.

Sizing so the disc opens fully

This part is our own field explanation, not a sourced figure. A check valve should be chosen for the actual flow, not just the pipe size. Line-size checks on an oversized pipe may only crack open at low flow, so the disc floats and flutters against its stop. That repeated motion wears the hinge pin and the disc arm and can give a rattle or hum. When a pump is much larger than the real load, or when variable speed pumps run slow, check the maker's data for the flow needed to hold your valve fully open. If the data say the valve will not fully open at your normal flow, a smaller check (with a reducer) or a different style is the usual answer.

Orientation limits for vertical lines

Swing checks rely on gravity to close, so their position matters. The university guideline above says swing checks go in a horizontal position with the hinge pin level, and that only wafer-style checks are allowed in a vertical position. Always follow the arrow on the body and the maker's orientation notes, and never put a plain swing check where flow runs downward.

Worked example (our own): how big can the surge get?

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not field data. The size of a sudden-stop surge is estimated with the standard wave-speed relation: head rise equals wave speed times velocity change, divided by g. The state university irrigation publication describes the same idea: the worst case is when velocity drops to zero, and the formula assumes an instant change.

Assumptions (ours): wave speed a = 4,000 ft/s (a round figure for water in steel pipe; the real value depends on pipe wall and mounting), velocity stopped from 4 ft/s to 0 in a time shorter than the pipe round trip, g = 32.2 ft/s², and 2.31 ft of water per psi.

  1. Velocity change: 4 − 0 = 4 ft/s.
  2. Head rise = 4,000 × 4 ÷ 32.2 = 16,000 ÷ 32.2 = about 497 ft of water.
  3. Convert to psi: 497 ÷ 2.31 = about 215 psi.

So a 4 ft/s stop could add about 215 psi on top of the normal pressure, and halving the velocity to 2 ft/s halves the rise to about 108 psi. We checked the math twice. The round trip for a 100 ft line is 2 × 100 ÷ 4,000 = 0.05 s, so any stop faster than that is a full-size surge. Real systems often see less because of air, pipe flex and a slower disc, but this is why compare-to-rating checks matter: add the surge to the operating pressure and compare it to the lowest-rated part on the line.

Field signs

  • A bang or thump a moment after a pump stops, not when it starts.
  • Pipe shake or hanger movement near the pump at shutdown.
  • A worn hinge pin, scored disc arm or a rattling valve found when you open the cover.
  • Leaks at flanges or gauges near the pump after repeated hits.

Lock out and relieve pressure before you open any check valve cover, and follow your site procedure and the maker's instructions.

Field card: Check Valve Slam on Pump Discharge: Causes and Fixes

The bang means the disc closed after the water reversed.

  • Slam: late closure, reverse flow, sudden stop, pressure surge.
  • Prone: parallel pumps, tall risers, fast trips.
  • Fixes: spring-loaded non-slam check, lever and weight, ramped stops.
  • Swing checks horizontal with hinge pin level; wafer non-slam checks may go vertical.
  • Our own example: 4 ft/s stopped suddenly gives about 215 psi rise (a = 4,000 ft/s assumed).
