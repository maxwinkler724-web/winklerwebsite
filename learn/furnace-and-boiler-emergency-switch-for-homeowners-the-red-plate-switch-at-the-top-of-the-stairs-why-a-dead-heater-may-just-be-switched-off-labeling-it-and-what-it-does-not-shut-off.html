Furnace and Boiler Emergency Switch: What It Does and Does Not Do

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many homes with a furnace or boiler have a switch on a wall, often with a red plate, that looks just like a light switch. It is an emergency switch for the heating equipment. If it gets flipped off, the heat goes out, and nothing is actually broken. This lesson explains what the switch is, what it does and does not shut off, and when to stop and call a pro.

What the switch is

A town fire department page on oil burner emergencies says that in a private home the burner emergency switch is typically found at the top of the cellar stairs, with a red switch plate labeled for oil burner emergency shutoff. In commercial buildings or multiple dwellings, the same page says it may be outside the burner room or just inside the door. The page describes its job as shutting off the burner's electrical supply.

Our own description (not from a cited page): homes vary. Gas furnaces and electric air handlers often have a switch that looks like a light switch on or near the unit itself, sometimes on a metal box fastened to the furnace or a nearby joist. Some units have one switch on the wall and another at the unit. Check what is on your own equipment before you need it in a hurry.

Why a heater that seems dead may just be switched off

This is the most common reason we mention it. Someone clearing the basement stairs, or a guest hunting for the light, flips the switch. The thermostat calls for heat, but the equipment has no power, so nothing happens: no blower sound, no clicks, no flame. Our own field observation: this is a frequent no-heat story, and it is worth checking early. See No Heat First Checks: Homeowner and Tech Checklist for the full order of checks.

If you find the switch off and nothing looks wrong (no smoke, no burning smell, no water around the unit, no gas odor), turning it on once is a reasonable step. If the equipment runs, you are done. If it shuts off again, or a reset button on an oil burner trips, stop. A university extension publication on oil heating advises pressing the reset button only once to restart a burner that has shut off, and contacting a heating contractor if it trips again. The same publication says unusual odors, discoloration, excess dirt or unusual cycling should be reported to your heating contractor, and tells readers to shut off electricity to the unit before doing its maintenance procedures.

How to tell which switch is which

  • Wall switch with a red plate near the stairs or door: usually the burner emergency switch described above.
  • Switch or box on or beside the unit: the equipment's own service switch (our own description). It cuts power at the unit so a technician can work on it.
  • Light switch for the basement light: a different circuit. Flipping it does not control the heater.
  • Breaker in the panel: another place the equipment can be turned off. See Electrical Basics for HVAC.

The fire department page also gives a caution worth knowing: the emergency switch is not always a sure way to shut off the electrical supply. It may be defective, may not be connected to the burner's electrical system, or may be mounted upside down so the up and down positions are reversed. Do not assume the label is right. A technician can verify it.

Labeling your switches

Our own suggestion: with the equipment off and no one in danger, ask your service technician to confirm which switch does what. Then put a clear label on each plate, such as "Furnace emergency switch. Leave ON." A label does not replace a technician's check, but it stops the next person from flipping the wrong one. A tag on the basement light switch saying "Light only" can also help. Write the locations on your records sheet (see the new-house first-week lesson).

When to use the emergency switch

These are our own general safety notes, not code requirements:

  • Smoke or visible fire: get everyone out and call the emergency number from outside. Do not go into a smoke-filled basement to reach a switch.
  • Burning smell or unusual noise near the unit: if you can reach the switch safely without passing smoke, turning it off is reasonable, then call a pro.
  • Water around the unit: do not step into standing water near electrical equipment. If the floor is dry where you stand and the switch is dry, turn it off. If water is deep or reaches outlets, leave it and call a professional or the utility.
  • Gas odor: leave the building and call from outside. Do not operate switches. The fire department page notes, in an oil context, that a spark from throwing a safety switch may provide an ignition source in some situations.

If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, the switch is not the answer. Get fresh air first and see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds. A federal consumer product safety release describes the early symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning as flu-like but without fever, including headache, fatigue, shortness of breath, nausea and dizziness.

What the switch does not do

It cuts electricity to the equipment. It does not shut off gas or oil. The fire department page says fuel must be shut off separately and notes that two oil shutoffs may exist, one at the tank and one at the burner. For gas, the shutoff is a separate valve on the gas line; see Gas Sediment Traps, Drip Legs, Shutoff Valves and Appliance Connectors. Also, turning the switch off does not fix a leak, remove soot, or clear a blocked flue. After a puffback or soot event, see Oil Burner Puffback: Soot in the House, Causes and What to Do. For a homeowner facing a real emergency, the safe move is to leave and call for help.

Worked example (our own): how much of a circuit does the furnace use?

This is our own example. The assumptions are ours: a 120 V furnace circuit protected by a 15 A breaker, a blower motor drawing 600 W, and controls drawing 100 W. We treat watts as volt-amperes for simplicity; real motors have a power factor, so a technician's meter reading will differ.

  1. Total power: 600 W + 100 W = 700 W.
  2. Current: amps = watts / volts = 700 / 120 = 5.83 A.
  3. Share of the breaker rating: 5.83 / 15 = 0.389, or about 39 percent.

Check: 5.83 A × 120 V = 700 W. So under these assumptions the furnace uses well under half of the breaker rating. The point for this lesson: the switch sits in this circuit and carries that current, so it is a real electrical device and not a decoration. If the equipment is dead and the breaker is fine, a switch left off is quick to check.

When to call a pro

  • The equipment shuts off again after you turn the switch on, or the burner reset trips more than once.
  • You see soot, smell oil or burning, or hear unusual noises.
  • The switch is loose, warm, buzzing, cracked or sparks.
  • You are unsure which switch controls the equipment, or whether it works.
  • Water has been near the unit or the electrical parts.
  • It has been a year or more since a professional cleaned and inspected the equipment. A federal fire administration page and a federal consumer product safety release both urge a yearly professional inspection of fuel-burning heating equipment.

The fire administration page also advises keeping anything that can burn at least 3 feet from heat sources, which includes clearing storage away from the furnace and its switch.

Field card: Furnace and Boiler Emergency Switch

It cuts power. It does not cut fuel.

  • Red plate near stairs or burner room door: usually the burner emergency switch.
  • Heat dead and nothing wrong? Check whether someone flipped the switch off.
  • Label each switch; do not trust a label until a technician confirms it.
  • Smoke or fire: leave and call the emergency number.
  • Gas smell: leave, do not touch switches.
  • Repeat shutdowns or a tripped reset more than once: call a pro.
  • Example: 700 W / 120 V = 5.83 A, about 39 percent of a 15 A breaker.
