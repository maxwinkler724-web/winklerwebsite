Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A rotten-egg smell near a furnace or water heater is the one gas problem where you do not troubleshoot first. This lesson explains what that smell is, what the New Jersey gas utilities tell you to do, what happens after you call, and how propane differs.

Safety first

If you smell gas right now, stop reading, leave the building and call from outside. The steps below are general education. Your utility's current instructions and the person on the emergency line outrank anything here.

What the smell is

Natural gas has no smell of its own. The U.S. Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) says gas distribution systems are odorized with mercaptan or a similar chemical, and calls mercaptan "a harmless non-toxic chemical that is added to make it easier to detect a gas leak." South Jersey Gas names mercaptan as its odorant in a pipeline safety notice, and its safety page says the added chemical makes gas smell like rotten eggs. PSE&G adds a caution worth remembering: do not rely on smell alone, because the odorant can be weak or missing even when there is a leak.

New Jersey Natural Gas (NJNG) lists why you might miss the smell: a reduced sense of smell, other odors covering it, or odor that has faded. So "I cannot smell it now" does not mean the air is clear.

What to do, per the New Jersey utilities

The four utility pages we read agree on the core. Leave the building right away. Do not create a spark. Call from a safe place.

  • PSE&G: exit and move at least 350 feet away, then call PSE&G or 911 from a safe distance. Do not look for the source, use light switches or doorbells, use a phone or cell phone, light matches, start a car or pull plugs.
  • New Jersey Natural Gas: get everyone out, call from a safe location and say where you are, and do not go back in until NJNG personnel say it is safe. Avoid phones, matches, lighters, flames, light switches, flashlights, doorbells and garage door openers near a suspected leak.
  • South Jersey Gas: leave the building immediately, call 911 and South Jersey Gas, and do not light a match, smoke, turn appliances or lights on or off, use a flashlight, start a car or use a telephone near the leak.
  • Elizabethtown Gas: leave the building immediately and call 911 and Elizabethtown Gas, with a similar list of things not to do.

The phone is on the "avoid" list because of spark risk, so make the call after you are out. Several pages also say not to hunt for the leak yourself.

Emergency numbers, as listed on each utility's page when we read them

  • PSE&G: 1-800-880-PSEG (7734)
  • New Jersey Natural Gas: 800-GAS-LEAK (800-427-5325)
  • South Jersey Gas: 1.800.582.7060 or 1.844.570.LEAK
  • Elizabethtown Gas: 1.800.492.4009 or 1.844.571.LEAK

Numbers can change, so also confirm the one on your bill and keep it somewhere you can find it fast. PSE&G, South Jersey Gas and Elizabethtown Gas all list 911 as an option.

What the utility does

PSE&G says it provides free 24-hour emergency service every day, does not charge for reporting a possible leak, and that its technicians respond to gas leak emergencies within 60 minutes. It tells you to wait outside until they arrive. NJNG says it investigates suspected leaks around the clock. PHMSA's general description is that pipeline company personnel are sent to isolate the emergency and limit how much gas escapes, and it says not to operate any pipeline valve yourself.

Where the furnace and water heater fit in

You do not need to work out which part is leaking. The utility pages tell you not to look for the source. For why a pilot goes out and what the sensor does, see Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit.

Gas smell versus carbon monoxide

They are different problems. The odorant warns you about gas. Carbon monoxide comes from incomplete burning, has no smell, and is caught by a CO alarm. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds. In both cases the first move is to get everyone outside.

Propane is a little different

If your home uses propane, your propane supplier takes the utility's place. The Propane Education & Research Council's safety page says: "IF YOU SUSPECT A LEAK OR SMELL GAS: Call your propane retailer, 911, or the fire department." Propane has no odor of its own, and a council safety booklet says manufacturers add a strong smell to it. The same booklet says propane vapor is heavier than air and settles in low places such as basements and crawl spaces, and that air currents from HVAC systems can carry it elsewhere. It also describes "odor fade," a loss of smell strength that can come from air, water or rust in a tank, from gas passing through soil, or from odor clinging to the inside of piping. It adds that people can fail to notice the smell because of colds, allergies, smoking, age or long exposure. Connecticut's utility regulator says that after a shutoff you should never turn the gas back on yourself; call your propane company.

Gas detectors

A fuel gas detector is a backup, not a replacement for the odorant or for leaving. A trade-journal summary of National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) guidance says fuel gas alarms should be tested by a third-party lab to standards such as UL 1484 (Residential Gas Detectors), and that they are designed to alarm at or below 25 percent of the lower explosive limit. The same summary says NFPA 715 covers selection, installation and maintenance of fuel gas detection but does not itself require that detection be provided. SoCalGas, a California utility, tells shoppers to look for UL 1484 listing and calls a detector "an extra layer of protection." If one sounds, treat it like a smell: leave, then call.

Worked example: a decision sequence

You walk into the basement and notice a rotten-egg smell by the water heater. Work the sequence in order.

  1. Do you smell gas or have a detector alarming? Yes, so go on. Do not stop to check anything.
  2. Do not flip a switch, ring a doorbell, use your phone or unplug anything. Walk out.
  3. Get everyone else out with you.
  4. Distance: PSE&G says at least 350 feet. 350 ÷ 3 = about 117 yards, a bit longer than a football field.
  5. From there, call the utility's emergency line or 911. Give your address and what you smelled.
  6. Wait outside. Do not go back in until the utility says it is safe (NJNG states this directly).

Nothing in that list asks you to measure, light or test anything. That is deliberate.

After the utility leaves

The pages we read do not give a natural gas relighting procedure. Elizabethtown Gas says to follow the owner's manual for appliance instructions, and for propane Connecticut's regulator says never to turn the gas back on yourself. Our own advice, not a utility rule: do not relight anything until the utility or a licensed contractor has cleared the system, then follow the appliance manual. The logic of a safe pilot is in the pilot light lesson.

To see how gas input is measured when everything is working normally, read Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input. Never run that test while you smell gas.

Field card: Smell Gas Near the Furnace or Water Heater: What to Do

Leave first. Call second. Touch nothing.

  • Mercaptan is the added rotten-egg odorant. It can fade or be missed.
  • Leave at once. PSE&G says go at least 350 feet away.
  • No switches, phones, flames, flashlights or car starts near the smell.
  • Call the utility or 911 from outside. Check the number on your bill.
  • Do not go back until the utility says it is safe.
  • Propane: call your propane retailer or 911. Vapor is heavier than air.
  • A gas detector is a backup. If it sounds, leave and call.
