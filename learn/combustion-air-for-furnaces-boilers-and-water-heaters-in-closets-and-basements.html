Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters in Closets and Basements

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "combustion air requirements furnace closet," here is the plain version: a gas appliance in a small room needs a way for fresh air to get in, and the manuals give a volume test and opening sizes to check it. This lesson shows the test, the opening rules as the manuals print them, and one worked example with the arithmetic.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on any house. The installation manual for the exact appliance and the fuel gas code adopted where you work decide the answer. The manuals we read point to the National Fuel Gas Code (ANSI Z223.1 / NFPA 54) and print the same style of rules; we did not read the code itself, so we cite each manual by name and edition. Rules differ between editions and local amendments.

Why burners need air

Our Combustion and CO Safety Basics lesson covers the idea: fuel, air and a clear vent have to work together. This lesson is about the air part. A U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) training deck on combustion safety codes says that when indoor air is not enough, outdoor openings are required, and that natural-draft appliances need a "vent dilution air" supply as well. That is the room air that enters the draft hood and mixes into the vent; see Water Heater Backdrafting for how that hood works.

The manuals also warn about what is in the air. A. O. Smith's gas water heater guide says air for combustion must not come from a corrosive atmosphere, and that corrosion failures are excluded from warranty coverage.

Confined or unconfined: the volume test

Add up the input of every gas appliance that draws air from the room, in Btu/h. Then compare the room's volume to 50 cubic feet for each 1,000 Btu/h. Rheem's 80+ furnace manual (part 708275A) calls a space unconfined when its volume is greater than that, and confined when it is less. It says rooms not separated by doors can be counted together. The Carrier 58TUA furnace manual and the A. O. Smith water heater guide give the same 50 cubic feet figure. The DOE slides say it rests on an old assumption of about one-half air change per hour, and that it assumes natural-draft appliances. Manuals may print the test with different wording, so read the one in the box.

In practice a furnace closet, a small utility room and a tight mechanical room are usually confined. The Trane J962 manual lists "furnace closets, small equipment rooms and garages" as examples.

Opening rules as the manuals print them

For a confined space, the manuals we read agree on these points:

  • Air from inside the building: two permanent openings, one within 12 inches of the ceiling and one within 12 inches of the floor. Each needs at least 1 square inch of free area per 1,000 Btu/h of total input, and not less than 100 square inches (Rheem; Carrier 58TUA; A. O. Smith).
  • Air from outdoors, direct or by vertical duct: two openings, each 1 square inch per 4,000 Btu/h (Rheem; Trane; A. O. Smith).
  • Air from outdoors by horizontal duct: two openings, each 1 square inch per 2,000 Btu/h (Rheem; Trane; A. O. Smith).
  • One opening only: 1 square inch per 3,000 Btu/h, starting within 12 inches of the top of the space. Carrier adds that it must be at least the total area of the vent connectors in the space. Trane says this method applies only where local codes allow it.
  • Ducts must match the opening's area, and rectangular ducts cannot be under 3 inches in their smaller dimension (Carrier; A. O. Smith).

Weil-McLain's PFG Series 6 boiler manual (part 550-110-640/0703) is shorter. It points to Section 5.3 of the National Fuel Gas Code and gives the same 4,000, 2,000 and 1,000 Btu/h-per-square-inch figures, with a 9 square inch duct minimum and the warning not to install an exhaust fan in the boiler room. It does not define confined or unconfined spaces.

Louvers and free area

A grille never gives you its full opening, because the slats and screen block part of it. Carrier's manual says that if the free area is not known, assume 20 percent for wood louvers and 60 percent for metal louvers or grilles. Screens should be no finer than 1/4 inch mesh, and louvers must not be closable. A. O. Smith gives 20 to 25 percent and 60 to 75 percent, and says louvers must be locked open or interconnected so they open when the equipment runs. Both manuals say to keep them clean and unblocked. Trane says openings in closet doors or walls must never be restricted.

Direct vent: bring the air in a pipe

Trane's J962 manual states the difference: a direct vent appliance draws combustion air from outdoors and vents products back outside, while installing with air taken from around the furnace is called a conventional, one-pipe install. The manual says direct vent isolates the system from house depressurization, and that it keeps an airtight path from the air inlet to the flue outlet. Even so, it says clearances to combustibles apply whether the space is confined or not. Category terms are in Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained.

Finishing a basement around a furnace

No manual we read talks about basement finishing by name, but its rules apply directly. Walls and a door around the equipment shrink the volume that counts under Rheem's "rooms not separated by doors" wording, and that can turn an unconfined space into a confined one. Then the openings in the worked example below come into play. Trane's warning applies to new framing and doors: openings must never be restricted. Carrier's manual adds that if a furnace sits on a raised platform that serves as a return plenum and takes return air from an adjacent space, all combustion air must come from outdoors. Before closing in the equipment, check the manual and ask the local code official.

Contaminated combustion air

Rheem lists substances that may call for outdoor combustion air, including chlorinated waxes and cleaners, chlorine pool chemicals, water softener products, permanent wave solutions, antistatic dryer sheets, paint removers, cements and glues. Carrier lists halogen compounds from aerosols, detergents, bleaches, solvents and air fresheners, and says not to run the furnace while adhesives, sealers or new carpet are curing. A. O. Smith names beauty shops, photo labs, indoor pools and laundry or hobby rooms. Weil-McLain's manual says not to store hydrocarbon sources such as bleaches, cleaners and fabric softeners in the boiler area.

Worked example: a closet and an open basement

A 100,000 Btu/h furnace and a 40,000 Btu/h water heater share a room. Rules used: Rheem and Carrier (50 cubic feet per 1,000 Btu/h; openings per the lists above) and Carrier's louver free areas. Dimensions are made up for the example.

Step 1, total input. 100,000 + 40,000 = 140,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, required volume. 140,000 / 1,000 = 140. Then 140 x 50 = 7,000 cubic feet.

Step 3, compare. A closet 5 ft x 6 ft x 8 ft is 240 cubic feet. That is far below 7,000, so it is confined. An unfinished basement 30 ft x 24 ft x 8 ft is 5,760 cubic feet. That is also below 7,000, so by this rule it is confined too, unless adjoining rooms without doors can be counted.

Step 4, openings from inside. 140,000 / 1,000 = 140 square inches of free area each, and 140 is more than the 100 minimum. Two openings, one high and one low.

Step 5, openings from outdoors. Direct or vertical duct: 140,000 / 4,000 = 35 square inches each. Horizontal duct: 140,000 / 2,000 = 70 square inches each. Single opening: 140,000 / 3,000 = 46.7 square inches.

Step 6, louver size. Gross area = free area / free fraction. For the 140 square inch inside openings: wood at 20 percent is 140 / 0.20 = 700 square inches; metal at 60 percent is 140 / 0.60 = 233 square inches. For 35 square inch outdoor openings: wood is 175 square inches; metal is 58 square inches.

Carrier's Table 2 shows the same math at 40,000 Btu/h (10.0, 20.0 and 13.4 square inches), which matches dividing by 4,000, 2,000 and 3,000.

Plain limits: this uses the simple volume rule. The DOE slides say a known air infiltration rate can change the calculation, and fan-assisted equipment is treated differently there. Follow the manual in the box, and have the inspector sign off.

Field card: Combustion Air in Closets and Basements

Add the Btu/h, check the volume, size the openings, mind the louver.

  • Total input of every appliance in the room, in Btu/h.
  • Volume needed = total input / 1,000 x 50 cubic feet. Less volume means confined.
  • Confined, inside air: two openings, 1 sq in per 1,000 Btu/h each, 100 sq in minimum, within 12 in. of top and bottom.
  • Outdoor air: 1 sq in per 4,000 Btu/h (direct or vertical duct) or per 2,000 Btu/h (horizontal duct), two openings; one opening is 1 per 3,000.
  • Louver gross area = free area / free fraction (Carrier: wood 20 percent, metal 60 percent).
  • Never block or close the openings. Keep chlorine, bleach and solvents away from the air supply.
  • Direct vent brings combustion air in by pipe.
