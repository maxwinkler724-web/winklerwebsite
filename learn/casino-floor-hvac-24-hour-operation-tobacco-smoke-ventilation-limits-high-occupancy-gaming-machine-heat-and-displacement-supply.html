Casino Floor HVAC: 24-Hour Operation, Smoke Ventilation Limits and Machine Heat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A casino floor is one big room with hundreds of heat-making machines, a crowd that changes all day, and doors that never close for the night. Add tobacco smoke in some places and the HVAC job gets odd. This lesson sorts out what the equipment can do, what it cannot, and how to size the heat.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Smoking law, ventilation rates and gaming regulations change by state and by property. This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It is not engineering design or legal advice.

A floor that never closes

For this lesson we assume the floor runs around the clock, every day. That changes how you work. The points below are our own reasoning, not a code requirement.

  • There is no night setback to lean on. The crowd thins in the early morning, but the machines keep running, so the cooling plant rarely gets a quiet window.
  • Maintenance has to be planned by zone. Isolate one air handler, hold the floor with the others, and keep spare cooling capacity for the day a unit is down.
  • Filter changes, coil cleaning and belt checks belong on a written schedule. The federal health study described below recommended a preventive maintenance plan for the ventilation systems, including testing, balancing and regular calibration of return-air CO2 sensors.

What ventilation can and cannot do about smoke

This is the part people get wrong, so we quote the public health sources.

  • Surgeon General. The CDC's notice about the 2006 Surgeon General's report on involuntary exposure to tobacco smoke says that eliminating indoor smoking fully protects nonsmokers, and that separating smokers from nonsmokers, cleaning the air and ventilating buildings cannot eliminate exposure.
  • CDC. A 2007 CDC weekly report says that other approaches, including separating smokers from nonsmokers and ventilating buildings, are not effective, and that only eliminating smoking in indoor spaces fully protects nonsmokers.
  • ASHRAE. The ASHRAE Position Document on Environmental Tobacco Smoke, reaffirmed in 2026, says ventilation and air cleaning cannot be relied on to control exposure, and that building systems can reduce only odor and discomfort when smoking is allowed. We name it in plain text and do not link a copy.
  • NIOSH. NIOSH studied dealers at three large Las Vegas casinos where patrons could smoke throughout the gaming area. It found the dealers were exposed to secondhand smoke, and their urine levels of a tobacco-specific carcinogen marker rose over an 8-hour shift.

So what is the tech's job? The same NIOSH report gives the practical side. Peak CO2 ran more than 700 ppm above outdoors, which suggested not enough outdoor air for acceptable odor control, and smoke compounds would be expected to run higher when less outdoor air is brought in. Minimum outdoor air settings differed from casino to casino (one was set at 40 percent), and the report could not say whether the duct CO2 sensors had been calibrated. The takeaway is narrow. Good ventilation, clean filters and working sensors are worth keeping up for odor, comfort and dilution. They are not a health safeguard against smoke. Do not promise a customer that they are.

New Jersey: what the law says about casinos

We state this without opinion. The New Jersey Smoke-Free Air Act lists exemptions from its smoking ban at N.J.S.A. 26:3D-59(e). One covers the area within the perimeter of a casino, as defined in the state casino law, that meets the statute's size and gaming thresholds. Another covers approved casino simulcasting facilities. A January 26, 2026 Appellate Division opinion, in a case brought by a union and a group of casino workers, affirmed the denial of a preliminary injunction and sent part of the equal-protection claim back to the trial court. Bills in the Legislature, including A2151 in the 2022 session, would delete the exemption. The law and the case can change, so read the current statute and the court record before you rely on this paragraph.

Gaming machine heat

Nearly every watt that goes into a gaming machine ends up as heat in the room, because lights, screens, computers and power supplies all turn into warmth. We did not find a public source that lists watts per machine, so the machine wattage in the example below is our own assumption. If you have a measured or nameplate value, use it and treat the example as a method only. The conversions are standard: 1 watt equals 3.412 Btu/h, and 1 ton of cooling equals 12,000 Btu/h.

Worked example (our own assumptions)

Every input here is an assumption made up for teaching. None of it measures a real floor.

  1. Machines. Assume 600 machines at 300 W each, all of it becoming heat in the space. 600 × 300 W = 180,000 W. 180,000 W × 3.412 = 614,160 Btu/h. 614,160 ÷ 12,000 = 51.2 tons, all sensible.
  2. People, sensible. Assume 400 occupants at 250 Btu/h sensible each (our own assumption for seated, light activity). 400 × 250 = 100,000 Btu/h, or 8.3 tons.
  3. People, latent. Assume 200 Btu/h of moisture load per person. 400 × 200 = 80,000 Btu/h, or 6.7 tons.
  4. Total internal load. 614,160 + 100,000 + 80,000 = 794,160 Btu/h. 794,160 ÷ 12,000 = 66.2 tons. Lighting, envelope and outdoor air are not counted yet.
  5. Outdoor air, sensible only. Assume 15,000 cfm brought in and a 20°F difference between outdoors and the supply air target. The common sensible formula is Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, so 1.08 × 15,000 × 20 = 324,000 Btu/h, or 27 tons, before any latent load.

Two things stand out. The machines are the largest single piece of the internal load, and outdoor air is a second large piece. That is why a floor with high outdoor air rates is a good place to ask about heat recovery, which we do not repeat here; see ERV vs HRV. Scaling outdoor air to the actual crowd is covered in demand-controlled ventilation. On a floor where smoke is a concern, turning outdoor air down to save energy needs careful thought, given the NIOSH finding above. Rates come from the ventilation standard in force, named in Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics.

Low supply and high exhaust

Many large rooms use low-velocity supply near the floor and exhaust high up. The basics are in underfloor air distribution and displacement ventilation, and the crowd version is in theater and auditorium HVAC. In our own understanding, warm plumes rise off machines and people, and the high exhaust takes the warm air at the top. That helps remove heat and can carry some warm contaminants up and away from faces. But smoke that has cooled, or that is released at breathing height, does not always rise, so we do not treat this layout as a smoke solution. It fits the NIOSH point: it helps comfort and odor, not health protection.

Field card: Casino Floor HVAC

Ventilation helps with odor and comfort. It does not protect health from smoke.

  • Surgeon General, CDC and ASHRAE all say ventilation cannot eliminate secondhand smoke exposure.
  • Keep outdoor air settings, filters and CO2 sensors maintained and calibrated.
  • Watts × 3.412 = Btu/h. Btu/h ÷ 12,000 = tons.
  • Sensible outdoor air load: 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Plan maintenance by zone because the floor does not close.
  • Never promise a customer that ventilation makes smoke safe.
