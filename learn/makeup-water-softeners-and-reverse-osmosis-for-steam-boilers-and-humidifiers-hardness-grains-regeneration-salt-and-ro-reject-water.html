Makeup Water Softeners and RO for Steam Boilers and Humidifiers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Boil raw city water and its calcium and magnesium stay behind as scale. This lesson covers two ways to clean up makeup water for steam boilers and humidifiers: ion-exchange softening and reverse osmosis (RO).

Why makeup water gets treated

Makeup water is the water added because steam or condensate was lost. An Oak Ridge guide used by the U.S. Department of Energy says waterside fouling is best handled by prevention: makeup water treatment, condensate conditioning, chemical addition and blowdown. The same guide lists scale, foaming and oxygen pitting as results of poor treatment. DOE's blowdown tip sheet puts typical blowdown at 4 to 8 percent of feedwater flow, as high as 10 percent when makeup water has a high solids content. For the boiler-side chemistry and tests, see Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics and Boiler Water Tests.

Steam units leave scale in the cylinder or tank, and ultrasonic units can throw minerals into the air as white dust. A federal (NIH) technical note says ultrasonic humidifiers must use mineral-free, deionized or RO water, and that clean-steam systems need RO or deionized makeup.

What hardness is, and the two units

The USGS says hardness is caused by compounds of calcium and magnesium, plus some other metals, picked up as water moves through soil and rock. It reports hardness in mg/L as calcium carbonate: soft is 0 to 60, moderately hard 61 to 120, hard 121 to 180, and very hard above 180. Water treatment gear is sold in grains per gallon (gpg). Penn State Extension and North Dakota State University Extension both give one gpg as about 17 mg/L, which is the same as 17 ppm. We use 17.1 ppm per gpg (Our own working factor, which keeps the example arithmetic clean).

Formulas: gpg = ppm as CaCO3 ÷ 17.1 and ppm = gpg × 17.1.

How the softener works

A softener is a tank of resin beads that hold sodium ions loosely. Hard water passes through, and calcium and magnesium displace the sodium, so hardness stays on the resin and sodium goes into the water. North Dakota State Extension says about 8 mg/L of sodium is added for each grain of hardness removed. When the beads fill with hardness, the unit regenerates: it backwashes, then draws strong salt brine from the brine tank across the resin so sodium replaces the calcium and magnesium, then rinses. The EPA says a typical household softener uses 20 to 70 gallons of water per regeneration, all of it salty waste.

Softeners are rated by capacity in grains, the hardness they can remove between regenerations (the EPA guide's example is 32,000 grains). Plant units use the same principle in bigger tanks, and a University of Michigan facilities drawing shows a duplex softener with a brine tank ahead of a humidifier RO system.

Worked example: how often does it regenerate?

Every input below is an assumption of ours, not a measurement from a real plant.

  1. Hardness in gpg. Assume city water at 171 ppm as CaCO3. 171 ÷ 17.1 = 10 gpg.
  2. Hardness load. Assume 2,000 gallons of boiler makeup a day. 2,000 gal × 10 gpg = 20,000 grains per day.
  3. Days between regenerations. Assume the softener is rated at 60,000 grains. 60,000 ÷ 20,000 = 3 days.
  4. Salt per regeneration. Assume 2 cubic feet of resin dosed at 15 lb of salt per cubic foot. 2 × 15 = 30 lb.
  5. Salt per month. 30 days ÷ 3 days = 10 regenerations. 10 × 30 lb = 300 lb. A 40 lb bag is the size North Dakota State Extension quotes, so 300 ÷ 40 = 7.5 bags.

The EPA guide says time-based regeneration wastes salt and water when it runs too often, so it favors demand-initiated regeneration.

Salt bridging and the hardness check

The EPA guide warns against filling the brine tank to the top, because the weight can form a salt bridge: a crust that leaves a gap above the water so no brine forms and the resin is not recharged. Its fix is a broom handle to break the crust, then run a regeneration. It also says to use softener-specific salt pellets, not rock salt, road salt or table salt.

The check that catches both a bridge and an exhausted bed is a hardness test at the softener outlet. A Canadian university facilities procedure takes a 100 mL sample from the softener outlet sample point, adds a buffer and an indicator, and calls the water soft if the sample turns blue. It sets no test interval, so follow your site procedure and log each result. Hardness showing up in feedwater means the softener is exhausted or bypassed. Wear eye and hand protection with the test chemicals.

RO and DI for humidifiers

RO forces water through a semi-permeable membrane and makes two streams: treated water (permeate) and reject water (concentrate). The reject carries the minerals the membrane held back and goes to a drain. EPA WaterSense says a typical point-of-use RO unit sends five or more gallons to drain for each gallon of treated water, with some worse. Those are drinking-water units. For larger systems, a trade-press report on a maker's launch (plain text, not linked) quotes recovery of 50 percent on hard feed water and 70 percent on soft water. So softening ahead of RO raises the recovery.

The membrane can foul. The Connecticut sheet says particles such as iron can foul or clog it and reduce flow, and that iron, manganese, turbidity, hydrogen sulfide and chlorine reduce RO efficiency, and that a sediment pre-filter helps. The University of Kentucky's humidification standard requires all incoming water to pass a softener first, then RO, and says RO output drops about 3 percent for each degree below 77 F, so feed water is tempered with a mixing valve. The Michigan drawing shows carbon and prefilters ahead of the RO unit too, and Kentucky lists RO output resistivity and TDS as controller points.

The NIH note says deionized water is not conductive enough for an electric heating element to work, while softened water is often too conductive and may cause arcing. Maker manuals decide which water a given unit accepts: our steam humidifier lesson compares limits across manuals, and some say untreated water only. Read the manual for the exact model.

The worked RO numbers are also our assumptions. If a humidifier needs 100 gallons of treated water a day and the RO recovers 50 percent, the feed is 100 ÷ 0.50 = 200 gallons and the reject is 200 − 100 = 100 gallons a day. At 70 percent recovery the feed is 100 ÷ 0.70 = 143 gallons and the reject is 43 gallons.

Discharge and the makeup line

Regeneration brine and RO reject both go to a drain. The EPA says cation-exchange softeners discharge salt to wastewater and can affect local water quality, and the Minnesota Department of Health lists environmental effects of softener salt. Check local sewer rules before adding a unit. The potable supply to any boiler or humidifier also needs backflow protection; see Backflow Prevention on HVAC Makeup Water.

Field card: Makeup Water Treatment

Soft water in the boiler, the right water in the humidifier.

  • gpg = ppm as CaCO3 ÷ 17.1. Grains per day = gallons × gpg.
  • Days between regenerations = softener grains ÷ grains per day.
  • Test the softener outlet for hardness and log it. Hardness in feedwater means exhausted, bypassed or bridged.
  • Do not overfill the brine tank. Break a bridge with a broom handle, then regenerate.
  • RO makes treated water plus reject. Feed = product ÷ recovery.
  • Softening ahead of RO raises recovery. Cold feed water cuts RO output.
  • DI is too pure for some electric units, and softened water can be too conductive. Follow the maker's manual.
