Vents Aimed at Your Outdoor Unit: Dryer, Fan and Furnace Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many houses have an outdoor air conditioner or heat pump sitting a few feet from a dryer hood, a bathroom fan outlet or a white plastic furnace vent pipe. Each of those blows something at the unit. This lesson covers what each one does to the coil, why the installation instructions set vent clearances, and what a homeowner can safely check.

The short answer

Lint, moisture and acidic exhaust all hurt an outdoor coil, and a vent too close to a window or an air intake can send flue gas back indoors. A homeowner can look from outside, keep the coil clean and keep plants, snow and debris off the vents. A homeowner should not block, extend, cap or redirect a gas appliance vent. Venting changes go to a licensed pro.

What each exhaust does to the outdoor unit

Dryer vent: lint. The federal fire agency says a dryer is at higher risk of fire when the lint filter and vent are not cleaned, and it tells owners to check that the outdoor vent cover opens while the dryer runs. A dryer hood aimed at a condenser coil adds a second problem: lint can mat on the fins and choke airflow. That matting is our own observation of how fins behave, not a measured figure. Our dryer vent lesson covers the fire side.

Bathroom fan: warm, moist air. The federal environmental agency recommends venting moisture-producing appliances outdoors where possible, and running a bath fan to control moisture. That moisture has to go somewhere. Our own reasoning: in winter a heat pump coil runs colder than freezing outside, so humid air blown onto it can freeze there. The heat pump already frosts on its own in cold weather (see our iced-up heat pump lesson), so extra moisture from a vent close by only adds to the frost the defrost cycle must clear. We did not find a measured case, so treat this as a reasoned risk.

High-efficiency furnace or water heater pipe: acidic, wet exhaust. A condensing appliance pulls so much heat out of its exhaust that water vapor turns to liquid. A national laboratory publication says that when flue gases cool below the dew points of acidic gases, the gases mix with water to form acidic solutions that cause serious corrosion and fouling in natural gas condensing furnaces. Our own reasoning follows: a plume that is hit by the fan of a nearby outdoor unit can wet the fins and cabinet with that mild acid, and aluminum fins and thin metal corrode over time. The exhaust is also cooler and wetter than an older appliance's, so it makes a visible white plume in cold weather. Our vent pipe lesson covers snow and ice at the pipe.

Why clearances exist

The installation instructions from the equipment maker, and the model codes behind them, set how far a vent outlet must sit from windows, doors, air intakes, walls, corners and the ground. We did not open a maker manual for this lesson, so we give no distances here. Use the manual for your own model. The reasoning is simple, and it is our own: exhaust gas leaving a pipe is carried by wind and by whatever fan is pulling air nearby. An outdoor unit fan, a dryer, a window that is open or a fresh air intake can all pull that exhaust back toward the house. Flue gas contains carbon monoxide, which is why the federal consumer product safety agency stresses annual professional inspection of heating systems, checks of flues and vents for blockage, corrosion and disconnection, and carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.

That agency also says birds, insects and animals sometimes nest in vents and block exhaust gases, which can push fumes into the home, and that ventilation air openings should never be blocked.

Worked example (our own): water vapor from a furnace in one hour

This is our own example with stated assumptions. How much water does a gas furnace put into its exhaust? Assumptions: the furnace burns natural gas at an input of 60,000 Btu per hour; the gas holds about 1,000 Btu per cubic foot; burning each cubic foot makes about 2 cubic feet of water vapor (our own approximation, treating the gas as mostly methane); and a pound-mole of any gas fills about 385 cubic feet at room temperature (our own rounded value).

  1. Gas burned: 60,000 Btu/h divided by 1,000 Btu/ft³ = 60 ft³ of gas in one hour.
  2. Vapor made: 60 ft³ times 2 = 120 ft³ of water vapor.
  3. Moles: 120 ft³ divided by 385 ft³ per lb-mole = 0.31 lb-mole.
  4. Pounds: 0.31 lb-mole times 18 lb per lb-mole (the weight of water) = about 5.6 lb.
  5. Gallons: 5.6 lb divided by 8.34 lb per gallon = about 0.67 gallon.

Check. Burning methane gives 2 water molecules per methane molecule, so each pound of methane makes 36/16 = 2.25 lb of water. The 60 ft³ of gas is 60/385 = 0.156 lb-mole, or 2.5 lb of methane. Then 2.5 times 2.25 = 5.6 lb, which matches. So a furnace at full fire makes roughly two-thirds of a gallon of water per hour. A condensing furnace captures much of it as liquid and sends it to a drain; a non-condensing one sends it up the flue as vapor. Real gas varies, so this is an estimate.

What a homeowner can safely do

  • Look from outside. Check whether any hood or plastic pipe points at the outdoor unit or ends near a window or an air intake. Photograph it for your technician.
  • Keep the coil clean. Brush away lint and debris gently with the power off, and keep plants, snow and leaves back. See our outdoor unit clearance lesson.
  • Keep vents clear. Clear snow and nests from the area, but do not poke into a gas appliance vent.
  • Keep the dryer duct clean and the vent cover free to open.
  • Test your carbon monoxide alarms and replace them on schedule.
  • Never block, cap, extend or redirect a gas appliance vent yourself. Call a licensed pro for any venting change, and get the maker instructions followed.

Warning signs to call for: white crust or green corrosion on fins near a vent, ice that keeps coming back at the same spot, a furnace pipe or hood that sits close to an open window, or any carbon monoxide alarm. If an alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call emergency services.
