Child Care Center HVAC: Ventilation, Nap Rooms, Diaper Room Exhaust and Hot Surfaces

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A child care center puts many small people in rooms that are not very big, for hours, with naps, diaper changes and radiators within reach of curious hands. That mix makes ventilation, quiet, odor control and burn protection the main HVAC jobs. This lesson walks through each one and includes a worked outdoor air and CO2 example.

Check the local rules first

Check the adopted codes. Child care licensing is set by each state, and the mechanical code your jurisdiction has adopted sets the ventilation and exhaust numbers. This page is general education built from public government pages read in 2026, not legal or engineering advice. Confirm every requirement with the authority where the job is.

Why crowded rooms of small children need outdoor air

Ventilation standards for commercial buildings, such as ASHRAE Standard 62.1, set outdoor air with two parts: an amount per person and an amount per square foot of floor. The per-person part covers what people breathe out and shed. The floor-area part covers odors from the room itself. Child care rooms have a lot of people per square foot, so the per-person part usually dominates. Name the standard in the design file, but use the rates from the edition your code adopted.

The Minnesota Department of Health says outdoor CO2 is about 400 ppm or higher, and that indoor CO2 can pass 1,000 ppm in crowded, long-occupied rooms with limited outdoor air. It describes CO2 as a common, low-cost screening measure of ventilation, because it roughly tracks how much outdoor air reaches the occupants. The same page notes that CO2 itself is usually not the contaminant causing comfort problems, and that workplace limits were written for healthy adults. So treat a CO2 reading as a clue about airflow, not as a pass or fail test of air quality. A cheap meter in the room is useful during a service call. See Demand-Controlled Ventilation: CO2 Sensors in Offices and Meeting Rooms for how sensors are used to control dampers.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Every input below is an assumption we chose for teaching. The outdoor air rates are of the kind a design table might list for a child care room; use the table in the code edition that applies to your job.

  • Room: 30 ft by 30 ft by 9 ft ceiling, so 900 ft² and 8,100 ft³.
  • Occupants: 20 children and 3 staff, 23 people.
  • Assumed outdoor air rates: 10 cfm per person and 0.18 cfm per ft².
  • Assumed CO2 generation: 0.005 cfm per child and 0.011 cfm per adult.
  • Assumed outdoor CO2: 420 ppm.

Step 1, outdoor air. People part: 23 x 10 = 230 cfm. Area part: 900 x 0.18 = 162 cfm. Total: 230 + 162 = 392 cfm.

Step 2, air changes. 392 cfm x 60 = 23,520 cubic feet per hour. Divided by 8,100 ft³ gives about 2.9 air changes per hour of outdoor air.

Step 3, CO2 steady state. Generation: 20 x 0.005 + 3 x 0.011 = 0.100 + 0.033 = 0.133 cfm. Rise above outdoor = 0.133 / 392 x 1,000,000 = about 339 ppm. Indoor CO2 = 420 + 339 = about 760 ppm.

Step 4, what a weak damper does. If the damper is stuck and only half the air arrives (196 cfm), the rise doubles to about 679 ppm, so the room settles near 1,100 ppm. That is above the 1,000 ppm level the Minnesota page mentions, which is the kind of clue that sends a tech to check the damper, the filter and the fan.

Step 5, what the air costs in winter. Using the usual sensible-heat relation, 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference, with an assumed 70 F room and 10 F outdoors: 1.08 x 392 x 60 = about 25,400 Btu/h just to warm the outdoor air. This is why heat recovery or demand control is often considered. See Unit Ventilators in Classrooms for equipment that often supplies this air.

Nap room comfort and quiet

Children nap on cots near the floor, so the air at floor level matters. Michigan's licensing rule for centers, as shown in the state's technical assistance manual, measures the 68 F minimum two feet above the floor, which is a hint to check where a thermostat or a thermometer is reading. Keep supply diffusers away from the cot rows so cold or hot air does not land on a sleeping child. Low air velocity and a low-noise fan matter, because a unit that ramps up or cycles loudly will wake the room. As a rough comparison, EPA notes that about 50 dB is the operating noise of a modern refrigerator, a useful target to keep in mind for any fan running in a quiet room. Put the ventilation unit or fan coil in a mechanical closet if you can, with a ducted return and a sound-lined supply.

Diaper-changing and toilet room exhaust

The goal is simple: odor and moisture should be pulled out and not spread into play and nap rooms. We design that as a room under slight negative pressure, with an exhaust fan sized to the code rate for the adopted edition, ducted to outdoors and not into a ceiling space, with makeup air entering from the adjacent room through a door undercut or transfer grille. Check that the fan runs whenever the center is open, and that the exhaust discharge is not near an outdoor air intake. A weak or dead fan is a common service call, and the symptom is odor in the next room. See Adult Day Center HVAC for more on toilet exhaust and schedules in a similar occupancy.

Temperature ranges in licensing rules (examples only)

These two states are examples of how rules read. They are not a summary of any other state, and we make no claim about any state we did not read.

  • Example, Washington: WAC 110-300-0165 says the temperature of indoor early learning licensed space must be between 68 and 82 degrees Fahrenheit.
  • Example, Michigan: Rule R400.8152 says indoor temperature must be at least 68 F at a point two feet above the floor, and that if temperatures exceed 82 F the center must take measures to cool children.

The practical lesson is that rules like these have a floor and a ceiling, and a building that cannot hold both in winter and summer is a licensing problem for the operator as well as a comfort problem. A setback schedule must bring the rooms back above the minimum before the first child arrives.

Hot surfaces, heater guards and controls

Washington's WAC 110-300-0170 says furnaces and other heating devices must be inaccessible to children in care, that any appliance or heating device with a surface that can reach 110 F must be inaccessible to children (with an exception for a supervised program activity), that combustible materials must be at least three feet from heating devices, and that portable heaters must not be used inside program space during operating hours. WAC 110-300-0165 adds that equipment or products that may be hot enough to injure a child must be made inaccessible. Michigan's manual adds that children must not have access to fans.

For the tech, that translates into these checks:

  • Hot-water and steam radiators and baseboard: look for a solid or perforated enclosure that is fixed in place, with no gaps a hand can reach into, and covers that do not trap heat against the wall.
  • Unit heaters and cabinet heaters: see Cabinet Unit Heaters. Recessed or high-mounted units keep hot grilles out of reach.
  • Exposed piping near the floor: insulate or enclose it.
  • Thermostats and control panels: mount them high or use a locking cover or sensor guard so a child cannot change setpoints.
  • Do not add a portable heater as a fix for a cold room. Find the cause.

Whether a particular surface counts as inaccessible is up to the inspector, so ask the licensing agency when in doubt.

Filtration and portable air cleaners

EPA's guide says the best first steps are source control and ventilation with clean outdoor air, and that filtration can add to them. It advises a furnace or HVAC filter rated at least MERV 13, or the highest the system can take, and says most systems can use MERV 13 if filters are changed frequently; it gives typical replacement every 60 to 90 days, sooner if soiled. Check that the fan can move air through the denser filter before you switch. For a portable air cleaner, EPA gives a minimum particle CADR of 65 cfm for a 100 ft² room with an 8 ft ceiling, scaling to 390 cfm for 600 ft². A 900 ft² play room would need more than one unit. EPA says to avoid units that intentionally make ozone. Those figures come from EPA's home guide, so use them as a starting point in a commercial room, not a design basis. Portable units add noise, so check the rating in a nap room. See How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics.

Humidity and mold

EPA says the way to control indoor mold is to control moisture and recommends keeping indoor humidity between 30 and 60 percent. It also says items that get wet should be dried within 24 to 48 hours. In a child care center, the risk spots are cold supply diffusers that sweat, a clogged condensate drain, wet ceiling tile near a leaking coil, and a toilet room with a dead exhaust fan. Fix the water source first, then clean up.

Common service calls

  • Room too hot or cold at floor level: thermostat location, stuck valve, broken fan.
  • Odor from the diaper room: fan off, blocked duct, no makeup air path.
  • Stuffy room, CO2 meter high: damper stuck closed, filter loaded, fan speed too low.
  • Noise complaints in the nap room: loose panel, dirty blower wheel, high fan speed.
  • Tampered controls: setpoint changed, cover missing.
  • Water on the floor or stains: plugged condensate drain, leaking coil.

Field card: Child Care Center HVAC

Air, quiet, odor, burns.

  • Outdoor air = people part + floor-area part; our example: 392 cfm for 23 people in 900 ft².
  • CO2 is a clue to airflow, not a full air quality test; outdoor is about 400 ppm.
  • Example state rules: 68 to 82 F (Washington); 68 F or more at two feet up (Michigan).
  • Surfaces that can reach 110 F must be out of reach in the Washington example.
  • Keep thermostats high or guarded; no portable heaters.
  • Toilet room: exhaust fan on, makeup air path open.
  • Humidity 30 to 60 percent; dry wet items in 24 to 48 hours.
  • Filters: MERV 13 if the fan can handle it; change on schedule.
