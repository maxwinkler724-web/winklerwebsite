Thermal Mass in Homes: Why Brick, Stone and Slab Houses Heat and Cool Slowly, Time Lag, Setback Recovery and Night Cooling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A brick house on a concrete slab can feel steady all day, then stay warm long after sunset or stay stubbornly cool after you raise the thermostat. That is thermal mass at work. It is easy to confuse with insulation, and the two do different jobs. This lesson explains the difference, what time lag means, why big setbacks can be slow to recover in a heavy home, and when mass helps with night cooling.

Mass stores heat, insulation slows it

Heat capacity is how much heat a material soaks up for each degree its temperature rises. R-value is how strongly a layer resists heat flowing through it. A solid masonry wall can be very good at the first and only modest at the second. The national lab team that runs the Building America Solution Center defines thermal mass as the ability of a material to store heat, and notes that insulation is what slows heat flow. An ORNL paper on massive walls makes the same point from the other side: the steady-state R-value commonly used to rate a wall does not capture how a massive wall behaves as outdoor temperatures change through the day.

Our own plain-language version: R-value tells you how fast heat leaks through a wall when conditions hold still. Heat capacity tells you how much heat the wall, floor and plaster have to absorb or give up before the room temperature moves. For the leak side, see Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings; this page does not repeat it.

Slow is not the same as efficient. The ORNL authors warn that a massive wall with a low R-value can use more energy in a cold climate, and that earlier comparisons looked better than they were because the walls did not have equal R-values.

Which parts of the house count

Brick, stone, concrete block, poured concrete, a slab floor and plaster all store a lot of heat per cubic foot. The Building America page says mass works best when it is spread over a large area and exposed to the room air. Compact features such as a lone column are less effective. It also says a concrete wall that is insulated on the inside will not help with passive solar heating or a night cooling strategy, because the insulation sits between the room and the mass. A thin surface layer does most of the work in daily cycles: the same lab's night cooling guide says only the first one or two inches below a surface contribute meaningfully to night flushing. A thick wall is not fully used every day.

Damping and time lag

Two things happen when the outdoor temperature swings through a day and the wall has mass. The swing indoors gets smaller, which is called damping. And the indoor peak arrives later than the outdoor peak, which is called time lag. ORNL describes massive building parts as delaying and flattening thermal waves caused by outdoor temperature swings. In a lab test of a mass timber wall, the ORNL report found a delay of about six hours in heat flow in summer, and the indoor surface temperature moved about 1 F, compared with 2 to 2.5 F for lightweight walls. Mass timber is not brick, so treat that as an example, not a number for your house. The Building America page adds that damping is greater where the day-night temperature difference is larger.

The lag cuts both ways. A heavy house can stay comfortable longer if power or heat fails, which the Building America page notes. But it also keeps giving back heat when you do not want it, such as a hot evening after a sunny afternoon.

Why big setbacks are slow to recover in a heavy home

When you turn the thermostat down at night, the air cools first. Then walls, floor and plaster slowly give up their stored heat to the air. The ORNL authors report that mass increased the time for the indoor temperature to fall during a setback and also extended the time to get back to the setpoint afterward. In their simulations, the energy penalty from this was very small. So the practical issue is comfort and timing, not a large bill increase.

When you raise the setpoint again, the equipment must reheat the air and also pull the walls and floor back up. The thermostat measures air, which warms quickly, so it may say the room is at setpoint while the floor and walls are still cool. The room can feel chilly even though the number on the wall is right. Our own explanation: this air-versus-surfaces gap is why heavy houses seem to "never catch up" after a deep setback. Heat pumps add another wrinkle, because a long climb can bring on backup heat; that is covered in Heat Pump Thermostat Setback Recovery and Strip Heat. For general savings math, see Thermostat Setback Schedules.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a published case. We want to know how much heat a concrete slab stores when it warms 5 F, how that compares with the air in the same house, and how long a furnace needs to supply just the slab's share.

Assumptions. One-story house, 1,000 sq ft, with a 4 in. concrete slab floor. Concrete density about 145 lb/ft3 and specific heat about 0.2 Btu/lb-F (a California Energy Commission building materials table lists 144 lb/ft3 and 0.2, so these are close to published values). Air density about 0.075 lb/ft3 and specific heat about 0.24 Btu/lb-F (our assumed standard values near sea level). 8 ft ceilings. The furnace delivers 40,000 Btu/h of heat to the house. We ignore heat loss during the warm-up, so this is a best case.

  1. Slab volume: 1,000 sq ft x (4 / 12 ft) = 333.3 ft3.
  2. Slab weight: 333.3 ft3 x 145 lb/ft3 = 48,333 lb.
  3. Slab heat capacity: 48,333 lb x 0.2 Btu/lb-F = 9,667 Btu per F.
  4. Heat to warm the slab 5 F: 9,667 x 5 = 48,333 Btu.
  5. Air volume: 1,000 sq ft x 8 ft = 8,000 ft3. Air weight: 8,000 x 0.075 = 600 lb.
  6. Air heat capacity: 600 lb x 0.24 = 144 Btu per F. Heat to warm the air 5 F: 144 x 5 = 720 Btu.
  7. Ratio: 48,333 / 720 = about 67. The slab alone stores about 67 times as much heat as the air.
  8. Furnace time for the slab: 48,333 Btu / 40,000 Btu/h = 1.21 h, about 72 minutes. Furnace time for the air: 720 / 40,000 = 0.018 h, about 1 minute.

What it means. Warming the air takes about a minute of furnace output, but warming just the slab by the same 5 F takes over an hour even in this ideal case. Real recovery takes longer. The house is losing heat to outdoors the whole time, and heat soaks into a thick slab slowly from the surface down. The walls, plaster and furniture add more mass. If your slab is also your radiant heating, see Radiant Floor Heating Basics.

Night cooling: dry climates yes, humid climates no

Night flush cooling means opening the house to cool night air, letting the interior mass absorb the cool, then closing up during the day so the mass soaks up daytime heat. The Building America cooling guide says it needs interior thermal mass, a high rate of airflow (it cites recent recommendations of roughly 12 to 23 air changes per hour), and a fairly dry climate with cool nights. It gives nights below about 67 F and summer daily swings over about 25 F as the sweet spot, and says night flush is least effective in humid regions. The mass page likewise says thermal mass is not recommended for passive cooling in warm-humid climate zones. Our reasoning: in humid weather the nights stay warm and damp, so there is little cool to bank, and opening up lets moisture in. For humidity control, see Humidistats and Dehumidistats.

The ORNL study named Phoenix and Bakersfield as the most favorable cities. An MIT report on concrete buildings says mass helps most when daily outdoor temperatures swing above and below the building's balance point.

Practical thermostat habits for a heavy house

  • Use smaller, gentler setbacks. A few degrees is usually easier to recover from than a deep drop. This is our own advice, supported by the ORNL finding that mass slows recovery.
  • Start recovery earlier. If the house needs to be comfortable at 6 am, plan the schedule for the slow climb, not for the setpoint time.
  • Trust surfaces, not just the number. If the room feels cold at setpoint, the walls and floor may be behind. Give it time rather than raising the setpoint far above where you want it. A higher setpoint only makes the equipment run longer.
  • Check the thermostat location. A sensor that reads air near a vent or sunny wall reacts faster than the mass does; see Where to Put a Thermostat.
  • In a dry climate, open up at night and close up by morning while the outdoor air is cooler than indoors, then keep shades and windows closed through the afternoon.

Field card: Thermal Mass in Homes

Mass stores heat. Insulation slows it. They are not the same thing.

  • Heavy walls and slabs damp the indoor swing and delay the peak by hours.
  • Deep setbacks take longer to fall and to recover in a heavy house; the energy penalty is small in ORNL simulations.
  • Our example: a 4 in., 1,000 sq ft slab holds about 48,000 Btu per 5 F, about 67 times the air; a 40,000 Btu/h furnace needs over an hour for the slab alone.
  • Night cooling works with interior mass, big airflow and dry climates with cool nights (swing over about 25 F); least effective in humid regions.
  • Use gentle setbacks and start recovery early.
