Getting Heating and Cooling Ready to Sell Your Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Before a home goes on the market, the furnace and air conditioner are two of the things a buyer and a buyer's home inspector will ask about. You cannot make old equipment young, but you can show it was looked after, fix the cheap and obvious problems, and hand over clear papers. This lesson walks through that preparation in plain steps.

Start with the paper trail

Gather everything about the heating and cooling equipment into one folder, paper or digital. Useful items are service and repair invoices, the installation paperwork, any warranty or product registration papers, the owner's manuals, and a note of the filter size and the date it was last changed. If the original papers are gone, photograph the nameplate on each unit. It carries the model, the serial number and usually a date code; our lesson on reading the serial number and nameplate date shows how to turn that into an age. Our lesson on documents to leave with a new system has a records sheet you can copy for this folder.

Warranty terms differ by maker and by whether a registration was filed, so read the actual papers instead of guessing. Do not promise a buyer that a warranty transfers. Whether it does is written in the warranty itself, and your real estate agent or the maker can tell you.

Fix the obvious items first

Small problems look worse to a buyer than they are, and they are the ones a home inspector finds fastest. A short list we would work through:

  • Dirty filter. A federal energy guide written for homeowners says to inspect and clean filters monthly or as the maker instructs. A university extension tip sheet says to check the filter once a month in the heating season and clean or replace it as needed, because a clogged filter keeps the system from running efficiently. Put in a clean filter of the right size and write the date on the frame edge. See furnace filter size, direction and fit.
  • Condensate drain. The same federal guide's heat pump checklist says to confirm the drain pan is draining correctly. A drain that is backed up or stained is a common find, so clear it or have a technician clear it.
  • Carbon monoxide alarms. The federal consumer safety agency says CO alarms belong on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and that batteries should be replaced annually and alarms tested monthly. A missing or dead alarm is an easy fix. Read the date or replacement note on the alarm itself and follow the alarm maker's instructions. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.
  • Loose or missing covers, damaged insulation on refrigerant lines, thermostat that does not respond. Note them for the technician rather than opening electrical or gas parts yourself.

A pre-listing tune-up

The federal environmental agency's indoor air page says it is most important to keep combustion equipment maintained and properly adjusted, and advises a trained professional to inspect, clean and tune up the central heating system, including the flue and chimney, annually. The national-laboratory guide for homeowners likewise recommends a technician tune-up for a furnace, with checks that include flue gas spillage and the heat exchanger. A tune-up done shortly before listing gives you two things: a chance to have problems found on your schedule, and a dated invoice for the folder. Ask the technician to write down what was checked and measured. Our tune-up checklist lists what a good visit includes, so we do not repeat it here.

If the technician finds a safety problem, such as a cracked heat exchanger or a blocked flue, treat it as a repair for the listing process and not as something to hide. Whether and how a problem must be told to a buyer is a disclosure question, covered below.

What a buyer's home inspector commonly looks at

A general home inspector looks at a whole house in a few hours, so the heating and cooling check is mostly visual and functional. As one example, a state legislature's published home-inspector standards say the inspector operates the heating and cooling with normal controls, opens the access panels meant for routine homeowner maintenance, and describes the equipment, controls and visible venting. The same standards say the inspector is not required to look inside flues, to check a heat exchanger unless it can be seen without taking things apart, to measure how evenly rooms are heated or cooled, to check coolant pressure or find leaks, or to run cooling when weather could damage the equipment. That is one state's text, written for that state. We use it only to show the typical scope: a quick, limited look. Buyers sometimes hire a heating and cooling technician for a deeper check, and that visit will find what the quick look misses.

For the buyer's side of the same conversation, see checking heating and cooling when buying a house.

Why equipment age matters to buyers

Buyers use age as a quick guess at how soon they may face a replacement. A national-laboratory study of survey data estimated median lifetimes of about 22.6 years for gas furnaces, 18.0 years for central air conditioners and 14.6 years for heat pumps. These are medians for the whole country, so half the units lasted longer and half did not, and a single unit can differ a lot. The federal homeowner guide also uses 15 years as a decision point, asking "Is the system over 15 years old?" in its chart for deciding what to do about a system.

Age can also affect refrigerant. The federal environmental agency's phaseout page shows production and import of the older air conditioning refrigerant HCFC-22 ended in 2020, with an exception for equipment manufactured before January 1, 2010, and says servicing of existing equipment is a continuing use. A buyer who sees an older unit may ask which refrigerant it uses, which is printed on the nameplate. You do not need to explain the market; just have the nameplate photo ready.

Disclosure questions go to your agent or attorney

What you must tell a buyer about the heating and cooling, and in what form, is a legal question that varies by place and by the kind of sale. We do not give legal advice and this lesson does not describe any disclosure rule. Bring your folder and your technician's written findings to your real estate agent or an attorney and ask them what to say and what to attach. Giving them accurate, dated papers is the most useful thing you can do.

Worked example: a seller checklist (our own example)

This is our own example, with assumptions we made up for illustration. Assume we are listing in 2026. Assume the gas furnace was installed in 2009 and the central air conditioner in 2008. Assume the filter is a 16 by 25 by 1 inch size, last changed on 2026-10-20, and that the CO alarms were tested this month.

  1. Age of each unit: furnace 2026 - 2009 = 17 years. Air conditioner 2026 - 2008 = 18 years.
  2. Compare with the national medians from the laboratory study: furnace 17 / 22.61 = 0.752, so about 75 percent of the median lifetime. Air conditioner 18 / 18.04 = 0.998, so about 100 percent.
  3. Time left if each unit hit the median: furnace 22.61 - 17 = 5.61 years. Air conditioner 18.04 - 18 = 0.04 year. A median is not a prediction for one unit, but the air conditioner is clearly at the point where a buyer will ask about replacement.
  4. Federal 15-year question: both units are over 15 years, so both would get a "yes" in that chart, and a buyer is likely to ask.
  5. Refrigerant note: the air conditioner dates from before January 1, 2010, so we would photograph the nameplate to show the refrigerant type.

Seller checklist from these assumptions: folder holds the two nameplate photos, ages of 17 and 18 years, the filter size and date, last service invoice, and warranty papers if any. Clear drain. Fresh filter dated. CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas. Pre-listing tune-up scheduled, with written results added to the folder. Disclosure questions sent to the agent or attorney. Change the numbers to your own years to build yours.

Field card: Getting Heating and Cooling Ready to Sell

Papers, easy fixes, tune-up, then ask your agent.

  • Collect invoices, warranty and registration papers, manuals, nameplate photos.
  • Write down the filter size and the last change date.
  • Fresh filter, clear drain, working CO alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas.
  • Book a tune-up and keep the written result.
  • Inspectors look at normal operation, not hidden parts.
  • Age: ages are national medians, not promises. Our example: 17 and 18 years.
  • Disclosure goes to your real estate agent or attorney.
