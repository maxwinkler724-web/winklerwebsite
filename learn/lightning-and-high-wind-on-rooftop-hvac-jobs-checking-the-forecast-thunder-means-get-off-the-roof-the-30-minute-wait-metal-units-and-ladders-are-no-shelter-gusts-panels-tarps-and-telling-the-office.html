Lightning and High Wind on Rooftop HVAC Jobs: When to Get Off the Roof

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rooftop unit job can turn dangerous quickly when a storm builds or the wind picks up. The roof is the tallest thing around, and a tech holding sheet metal or a ladder is a bad place to be when either one shows up. This lesson covers the decisions that keep you off the roof at the right time and how to tell the office why work stopped.

Check the forecast before going up

The federal weather service advises checking the forecast before outdoor activities and postponing them if thunderstorms are imminent. For a roof job, that means looking at the hourly forecast and any posted watches or warnings before the ladder goes up, and again when you break for lunch. Look at two things: the chance of thunderstorms during the hours you expect to be up there, and the wind, including the gust figure, not only the steady speed. The weather service issues a wind advisory for sustained winds of 31 to 39 mph lasting an hour or more, or gusts of 46 to 57 mph, and a high wind warning for sustained winds of 40 mph or more or gusts of 58 mph or more (these are the criteria posted by one regional office, so offices may differ). Those are public warning levels, not a work rule. Your employer's written procedure and the equipment maker's limits decide when you stop.

Thunder means get off the roof

The federal weather service says that if you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike you, and that you should move to safe shelter right away. A university safety office says the same: if you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to harm you, and distant thunder still means move. Do not wait to see lightning. Do not finish the one more screw. Lower tools by hand line or leave them, climb down, and get inside.

Wait after the last thunder

Both the weather service and the university safety office say to stay in shelter at least 30 minutes after the last sound of thunder. The clock restarts with each new rumble. Assign one person to watch the sky and keep the log of last thunder, as the weather service suggests for outdoor groups. Do not go back up just because the sun came out. A metal unit and a ladder are no shelter

The weather service names only two safe places: a substantial enclosed structure and an enclosed, metal-topped vehicle with the windows up. It warns against small sheds and against tall objects such as towers, fences and poles. A rooftop unit is not an enclosed building you can step into. It is a tall metal object on the highest surface around. A ladder leaning on the building is also a tall metal or fiberglass object that you would be standing on in the open. The university safety office lists metal tools among items to stay away from outdoors. We take that to cover your hand tools and a metal ladder; the sources do not mention ladders by name, so do not treat a ladder as a shelter or a place to wait.

High wind: panels, sheet metal and ladders

The federal workplace safety agency says high winds, wet weather and icy conditions are especially hazardous for roofing workers and tells employers to consider stopping work in bad weather. Sheet metal makes the problem worse. A removed access panel, a section of duct or a flat of insulation board has a large area, so a gust can catch it like a sail and push you or pull it away. Practical habits that follow from that:

  • Keep removed panels flat, held down with something heavy and screwed back loosely if you must leave the unit.
  • Carry large pieces low, with both hands, and set them down instead of holding them in a gust.
  • Put the ladder up only when you plan to use it, and stop if the wind is moving it. Follow the setup and tie-off steps in the ladder and roof fall lesson.

Gusts at roof edges

Wind speeds up and swirls where it meets the edge of a building, and a gust can arrive without warning. Stay away from the edge when the wind is up and keep your body low when carrying anything wide. Edge protection and fall arrest are covered in the lesson linked above and are not repeated here. If a gust makes you stagger, stop and set the load down.

Secure loose parts, tools and tarps

The federal workplace safety agency says to secure tools when not in use so they cannot fall off the roof, and warns that loose debris is a trip hazard and a danger to people below. In wind, add parts to that list: screws, panels, filter media, rolled paper and especially tarps. A tarp or plastic sheet spread over an open unit to keep rain out will catch wind and can pull a person off balance. Our own rule of thumb is to secure it fully or do not use it; if you cannot, close up and come down. Keep drop zones clear, and use the hand-line steps in the rigging lesson to lower loads.

Ice and wind chill together

The federal workplace safety agency lists ice as a roofing hazard and says some metal roofs are especially slippery when wet. When it is also windy and freezing, the roof gets slick and you lose body heat faster. The agency's cold-work schedule pairs air temperature with wind speed and calls for shorter work periods and more warm-up breaks as the temperature drops and wind rises. Details on frostbite and warm-up breaks are in the cold stress lesson.

Tell the office why work stopped

Stopping is a safety decision, not a failure. Tell the office right away, in writing if your company has a way to do it (a text works). Include the time, the address, what you saw (thunder heard at what time, wind and gusts, ice), the job state (unit open or closed, panels secured, power off), and your plan for return. Customers hear a clear reason better than a vague delay. Do not promise a return time until the wait is over.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example. All times and speeds are assumptions, not measurements.

Part 1, return time. Assume the crew hears thunder and gets off the roof, and the last thunder is at 2:47 pm. Assume a 30-minute wait, as both safety sources state.

  1. 2:47 plus 30 minutes is 3:17 pm.
  2. Now assume thunder is heard again at 3:05 pm, before 3:17. The wait restarts from the new last thunder.
  3. 3:05 plus 30 minutes is 3:35 pm.
  4. If nothing further is heard, the earliest return is 3:35 pm. Any new thunder moves it again.

Part 2, wind units. Assume the forecast says sustained wind of 25 mph with gusts to 46 mph, and the maker's sheet lists limits in meters per second. Our own arithmetic uses the exact definition 1 mile equals 1,609.344 meters, and 1 hour equals 3,600 seconds, so 1 mph is 0.44704 m/s and 1.609344 km/h. One knot is 1 nautical mile per hour, or 1.852 km/h, so 1 mph is about 0.869 knots.

  1. 25 mph x 0.44704 = 11.2 m/s. In km/h, 25 x 1.609344 = 40.2. In knots, 25 x 0.8690 = 21.7.
  2. 46 mph x 0.44704 = 20.6 m/s. In km/h, 46 x 1.609344 = 74.0. In knots, 46 x 0.8690 = 40.0.
  3. For a feel for the public criteria quoted above, 40 mph is 17.9 m/s, or 64.4 km/h.

Field card: Lightning and High Wind on Rooftop HVAC Jobs

Thunder heard means off the roof. Wait 30 minutes after the last one.

  • Check the hourly forecast and gust figures before going up and again after lunch.
  • Thunder heard means lightning is close enough to strike. Go to a substantial building or a closed metal-topped vehicle.
  • Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. New thunder restarts the clock.
  • A rooftop unit, a ladder and hand tools are not shelter.
  • In wind, keep panels and sheet metal flat and held down. Do not carry large pieces in gusts.
  • Secure or remove tarps, loose parts and tools. Stay clear of the edge.
  • Ice plus wind means slick footing and faster heat loss.
  • Tell the office what stopped work and when.
  • Unit conversions: 1 mph is 0.447 m/s or 1.609 km/h.
