Pipe Labels and Color Codes in Mechanical Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk into a mechanical room and every pipe looks alike until it has a label. A good label tells you what is inside, which way it flows and which valve stops it. This lesson covers the common color scheme, label sizes, spacing, flow arrows and valve tags, and ends with a label count you can follow.

Why piping gets labeled

Labels do three jobs. First, safety: a technician about to cut into a line needs to know whether it holds chilled water, steam or fuel gas. Second, isolation: before work starts, you have to find the right valve, which ties directly to lockout/tagout thinking. Third, emergency response: when a leak starts, someone who has never seen the room needs to find the shutoff fast.

On the federal side, the OSHA page for 29 CFR 1910.1200 (hazard communication) says the term "container" does not include pipes or piping systems. So that rule does not simply require a label on every pipe. It does allow signs, placards, process sheets and similar written materials in place of labels on stationary process containers, and it requires a written program at each workplace. OSHA 1910.144 sets yellow for caution and red for danger, but neither page spells out pipe marking. In practice, owners require pipe marking in their own design standards, and those standards usually point to ASME A13.1, the pipe identification scheme. We name it in plain text only; we did not read the paid standard itself, so the details below come from public design standards and a label maker's summary.

The color scheme by contents

A label maker's public summary of ASME A13.1 groups colors by the hazard of the contents. Color is meant to be used with a legend that names the fluid.

ContentsBackground / text
Flammable and oxidizing fluidsYellow / black
Fire quenching fluids (sprinkler, standpipe)Red / white
Toxic and corrosive fluidsOrange / black
Water (potable, cooling, boiler feed, other)Green / white
Compressed airBlue / white

Owners add their own details. One university guide lists white on green for potable cold water and white on red for fire protection. Another paints exposed gas piping yellow and fire piping red. A third spec gives separate colors for steam pressure ranges, chilled water and glycol, so colors differ from one owner to the next. Read the posted legend before you trust a color alone.

Label size by pipe diameter

Bigger pipe needs bigger labels so they can be read from a walkway. The size is based on the outside diameter of the pipe including insulation. A university facilities spec and the label maker summary give the same table.

The label maker lists the same bands in tenths of an inch (for example 0.7 to 1.3 in, 1.3 in letters for 2.5 to 6.7 in) and says pipe under 3/4 in, plus valves and fittings, should get a permanent tag instead. Because the measure includes insulation, see pipe insulation when you size a label for a covered line.

Where labels go

The public standards we read agree on the places:

  • Next to each valve and branch (tee).
  • On both sides of every wall, floor or ceiling penetration.
  • At access doors and panels, direction changes and near major equipment.
  • At regular intervals on straight runs. The maximum spacing varies by owner: 10 ft in an open mechanical room in one spec, 20 ft in another, 25 ft in a university design guide, and up to 50 ft (25 ft in congested areas) in a third. The label maker suggests 20 to 25 ft.

For pipe far above or below eye level, the label maker says to put the label on the side facing the viewer; a university guide says markers must stay visible from floor level.

Flow arrows

A label that names the fluid but not the direction leaves you guessing, especially on a chilled water loop where supply and return run side by side. The state university spec says each marker shall carry an arrow showing flow direction. Another spec lets the arrow be built into the lettering so it works either way, or placed as a separate arrow on each label. Our own habit: write supply or return in the legend text too. See chilled water pumps and delta T for why supply and return matter.

Valve tags and valve charts

Pipe labels tell you what a line is. Valve tags tell you which valve to close. Several university specs call for brass tags, 1-1/2 in across, stamped with a system abbreviation in 1/4 in letters and a valve number in 1/2 in letters, hung on a corrosion resistant chain or hook. For the valves themselves, see isolation and check valves.

The tag number points to a valve chart. One spec asks for a chart listing tag number, location, function and the maker's model, framed and posted. Another spec asks for a schedule per system on letter size paper listing valve number, system, location and normal operating position. A normal position column lets the next person spot a valve left shut.

Worked example: sizing and counting labels

These inputs are assumptions (Our own), chosen to show the arithmetic. A mechanical room has a 60 ft straight chilled water run with these features: a valve at 5 ft, a branch tee at 12 ft, a wall penetration at 45 ft (labels on both sides, at 44 and 46 ft) and a valve at 58 ft.

Step 1, feature labels. Valve (5), tee (12), wall (44 and 46), valve (58) gives 5 labels.

Step 2, find the longest gap. The gap from 12 ft to 44 ft is 32 ft. Other gaps are shorter: 7 ft, 12 ft, 2 ft and 2 ft.

Step 3, apply each rule. Extra labels needed in a gap = round up (gap divided by the maximum spacing) minus 1.

  • 50 ft rule: 32 / 50 rounds up to 1, so 0 extra. Total 5.
  • 25 ft rule: 32 / 25 rounds up to 2, so 1 extra. Total 6.
  • 20 ft rule: 32 / 20 rounds up to 2, so 1 extra. Total 6.
  • 10 ft rule: 32 / 10 rounds up to 4, so 3 extra in that gap. The 12 ft gap (46 to 58) needs 12 / 10 rounds up to 2, so 1 extra. The 7 ft gap needs none. Total 5 + 3 + 1 = 9.

The same piping needs 5 to 9 labels depending on the owner's rule, so get the spacing rule before you buy labels. Add 2 valve tags for the two valves.

Step 4, pick sizes. Assume covered outside diameters (Our own) of 1.5 in, 4 in and 8 in. Using the table above: 1.5 in gives an 8 in color field and 3/4 in letters; 4 in gives 12 in and 1-1/4 in; 8 in gives 24 in and 2-1/2 in. The label maker's tenths-of-an-inch bands (2.5 to 6.7 in for the 12 in field) cover odd sizes such as a 6.5 in insulated line.

Field card: Pipe labels in mechanical rooms

Contents, direction, valve.

  • Label: fluid name, flow arrow, and color from the owner's legend.
  • Size from outside diameter including insulation: 8, 12, 24 or 32 in color field.
  • Place at valves, tees, both sides of penetrations, access points and along runs.
  • Run spacing is set by the owner: 10, 20, 25 or 50 ft in the specs we read.
  • Valve tag: brass, 1-1/2 in, number matches the valve chart.
  • Before work: find the tag, check the chart, then lock out. See the lockout lesson.
