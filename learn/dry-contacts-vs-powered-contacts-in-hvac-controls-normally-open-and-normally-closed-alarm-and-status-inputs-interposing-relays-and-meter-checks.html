Dry Contacts vs Powered Contacts in HVAC Controls: Alarm and Status Inputs, Interposing Relays and Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An equipment panel says "alarm: dry contact" and the controls drawing shows a pair of wires to a building automation system (BAS). Does anything on those wires carry voltage? Which way does the contact rest? This lesson explains what "dry" means, how normally open and normally closed contacts read on a meter, how to land an alarm or status contact on a BAS input, and how an interposing relay lets a small 24 volt output start a 120 volt fan.

Check the equipment drawing

Check the equipment drawing. Terminal names, ratings and input types differ by maker and model. This page is general education. Use the controller and equipment documents for your job, and your site procedures, before you wire or test anything.

What "dry" means

A dry contact is a switch that supplies no voltage of its own. It is a pair of terminals that are either connected or not, and nothing else. A powered (wet) contact is the opposite: the device puts its own voltage on the terminal when the switch closes, so the receiver sees a voltage appear rather than a connection being made. A university construction standard for building controls tells installers to "provide dry contact from local device to digital input at controller," and another university standard lists "dry contact closure" as one kind of digital input, next to pulse counting and voltage sensing. Those are the two ideas to keep straight: a dry contact makes or breaks a path, and the input that receives it supplies the small voltage that makes the path mean something.

Mixing the two up causes trouble: a powered 24 or 120 volt signal on an input built for a dry contact can burn the input, and a dry contact on an input that expects a voltage never shows anything. Read the input type on the controller drawing first.

Normally open and normally closed

The word "normally" means the resting state, with the device unpowered or the equipment healthy and idle. A relay lecture from a state university puts it plainly: the contacts of a normally open (NO) relay are open when no current passes through the coil, and a normally closed (NC) relay is the reverse. On a meter, with power off and the contact isolated from other circuits:

  • NO contact at rest: ohms mode reads open (OL, or a very large number). Closed: near 0 ohms.
  • NC contact at rest: reads near 0 ohms. Open: OL.

Alarm contacts are often wired NC on purpose: a healthy device holds the circuit closed, so a cut wire or loose terminal looks like an alarm (our own practice note). Run-status contacts are usually NO (closed when running), but which one you have is a drawing question, not a guess.

Using an alarm or status contact as a BAS input

A BAS digital input is a pair of terminals, a returned reference (common) and a small voltage the controller supplies to watch for a closure. Wire the equipment's dry contact across that pair and the controller sees a connected path (closed) or no path (open). The point list should say what each state means, for example "closed = normal, open = alarm." For a run status that must prove a fan is really turning rather than only commanded on, see the current switch lesson.

Interposing relays

An interposing relay sits between a controller output and the thing it cannot drive directly. Two common reasons:

  • Different voltage or size. A BAS output may switch only a small 24 VAC load. The relay coil takes that small load and the relay contacts switch a bigger one, such as a 120 volt exhaust fan.
  • Isolation. The coil and the contacts are separate circuits. Two control circuits with their own transformers can talk through a relay without being joined, which avoids tying two transformer secondaries together. That hazard, coil VA and the transformer side are covered in the fan center relay lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

For a motor load, the contact rating that matters is the horsepower or full-load rating, not just the resistive amp number. The contact ratings lesson explains how to read those; the example below uses assumed ratings and checks them against the fan.

Why heavy-duty contacts can fail on tiny signals

A relay with a big resistive amp rating is built to switch big loads. Each time such a contact opens or closes under load it can arc a little, and the arc helps break through any thin film that has formed on the contact surface. A paper on relay failures, published by NASA, cites a minimum arcing current of about 0.4 A for silver contacts and says an arc needs roughly 10 V to start. A signal of 5 volts and half a milliamp is far below both of those numbers. Our explanation (not a statement from that paper): with no arc, a film that has built up over time is not broken through, so the contact can read as intermittent or high resistance even though it looks fine. Contacts meant for low-level signals are built for it, and their labels may show a minimum load or low-level rating. If a BAS input chatters from a contact that tests fine on a meter, check for a low-level rating before replacing wire. A meter in ohms mode pushes very little current, so a contact can look good on the meter and still be unreliable on the job (our own observation).

Power off or power on: two different checks

Power off (continuity). This tells you the state of the contact itself. Lift one wire so the meter does not read through the rest of the circuit. A federal workplace rule requires that live parts be de-energized before work on or near them unless leaving them live is justified, and that circuits that are off but not locked out and tagged be treated as energized. Follow your lockout procedure for line-voltage parts, then test your meter on a known source, test the circuit, and test the meter again.

Power on (voltage). This tells you whether the contact is doing its job under real conditions. For a powered circuit, a good open contact has the full circuit voltage across it, and a good closed contact has almost none. Place the meter across the contact: 120 volts across a contact that should be closed means it is open or burned; near 0 volts across a contact that should be open means it is stuck or welded. The same federal rule limits work on or near live parts to qualified persons using the right protective equipment and insulated tools.

Worked example (our own): boiler alarm to a BAS input, and an interposing relay for an exhaust fan

This is our own example. Every number here is an assumption we picked to show the arithmetic, not a measurement from a real job.

Part A: boiler alarm dry contact to a BAS digital input

Assumptions: the boiler has an NC alarm contact on terminals A1 and A2 that opens on lockout. The BAS digital input is a dry-contact input with terminals DI1 and COM. The input supplies 5 V DC (open circuit) through a 10,000 ohm resistor. Current when the contact is closed: 5 V / 10,000 ohms = 0.0005 A = 0.5 mA. Wires A1 to DI1 and A2 to COM; the BAS and the boiler control are powered.

Meter on A1 to A2 with the boiler powered and the wires still landed gives the same readings as DI1 to COM. With the wires lifted at the boiler end, the boiler terminals read 0 V in both states, because a dry contact supplies nothing. If you see 24 or 120 volts there, the contact is not dry; stop and read the drawing before you land it on that input. With both the boiler and BAS powered down and one wire lifted, ohms across A1 and A2 read near 0 ohms (closed) or OL (open). With the wires still landed and the BAS powered, do not use ohms mode; use DC volts as in the table.

Part B: BAS 24 VAC output to a 120 V exhaust fan through an interposing relay

Assumptions: the BAS output can switch 24 VAC at up to 0.5 A. The relay coil is 24 VAC, 3 VA. Coil current = 3 VA / 24 V = 0.125 A, well under 0.5 A. The relay contacts are rated 10 A resistive at 120 V and 1/2 horsepower at 120 V. The fan is a 120 V motor with a nameplate current of 4.4 A and a horsepower below 1/2. Check: 4.4 A is under 10 A, and the fan is under the 1/2 horsepower rating, so the contact is a fit for this load. Terminals: coil X1 and X2; contact COM, NO, NC. L1 (120 V) feeds COM; NO feeds the fan; the fan returns to neutral. NC is unused. Wire X1 to BAS DO1 and X2 to BAS 24 VAC common, as in the figure.

Power off, locked out, wires lifted: COM to NC reads near 0 ohms and COM to NO reads OL with the coil off. X1 to COM reads OL, which confirms the coil and contact circuits are not joined. How to read the results: 24 volts at X1 and X2 but 120 volts still across COM and NO means the coil has power but the contact is not closing, so suspect the relay. 0 volts at X1 and X2 with the BAS commanding the fan on means the problem is on the 24 V side (the output, its wiring or its transformer), not the relay.

Field card: Dry Contacts vs Powered Contacts in HVAC Controls

Dry = no voltage from the device. The receiving input supplies it.

  • Read the input type first; never put 24 or 120 V on a dry-contact input.
  • NO is open at rest; NC is closed at rest. Alarms are often NC so a broken wire alarms.
  • Meter on a dry contact, device side: 0 V. Across an open powered contact: full circuit voltage.
  • Interposing relay: small coil circuit on one side, a bigger or separate load on the other, no shared wire.
  • Heavy-duty contacts can be unreliable on tiny signals; look for a low-level rating.
  • Power off: continuity with one wire lifted. Power on: volts across the contact.
