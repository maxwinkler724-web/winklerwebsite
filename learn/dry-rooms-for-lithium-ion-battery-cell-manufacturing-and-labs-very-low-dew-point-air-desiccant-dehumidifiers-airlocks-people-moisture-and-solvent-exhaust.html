Battery Cell Dry Rooms: Very Low Dew Point Air, Desiccant Units and Airlocks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Lithium-ion cells do not tolerate water. That is why the rooms where electrodes are finished and cells are assembled are held at a dew point far below anything a comfort system sees. For the HVAC tech, a dry room is a desiccant system, an airtight envelope and a lot of discipline about moisture sources, all at once.

Why the air has to be this dry

Public pages from labs that run pilot battery lines give the targets. A university pilot line page describes its mixing, coating, calendering and pouch cell assembly as housed in a dedicated dry room at a -40 °C dew point. A second university open-access lab lists a 1,600 square foot space with a dry room at the same -40 °C dew point. A national lab news page describes an ultra-dry chamber at -40 °C dew point, about 0.5 percent relative humidity, and says the dryness keeps out moisture that can degrade battery cells. A national lab slide deck lists a pouch cell dry room held at a -53 °C dew point. A national lab report on a lithium powder additive says that material is safe to handle in a dry room of -30 °C dew point or better. So the target depends on the process step and the materials, and the number is set by the cell maker, not by the HVAC contractor. Our job is to hold the number we are given and to prove it with a sensor we trust.

If dew point and grains of moisture are new to you, read the humidity ratio lesson linked below first. At these conditions there is only a fraction of a grain of water in each pound of air, so tiny sources matter.

The air path: cooling coil, desiccant, then the room

A cooling coil alone is a poor way to reach this dryness. Coil leaving air is limited by the coil surface temperature, and a surface well below freezing would frost over. So the usual arrangement puts a cooling coil ahead of a desiccant dehumidifier. The coil removes the bulk of the moisture from the outside or makeup air cheaply, and the desiccant wheel takes it the rest of the way down. For how wheels, reactivation air and coil comparisons work in general, see the desiccant lesson linked below. We do not repeat it here.

Two features of dry room units are worth knowing. First, reactivation heat is a major energy use, because the wheel must be heated to drive the water back out into a separate exhaust stream. Second, a wheel typically leaves the air warm, so a cooling section may follow it to bring supply air back to room temperature. Check the sequence on the drawings for your job.

People, airlocks and door discipline

In a room this dry, the largest uncontrolled moisture source is often the workforce. Breath and skin add water vapor continuously, and carts and materials can carry moisture in from the corridor. Airlocks (also called vestibules or pass-throughs) exist so that only one door is open at a time. Techs working in these rooms should learn the site entry routine: keep doors closed, never prop interlocked doors, and keep wet or damp items out. A defeated door interlock can wipe out hours of dew point recovery. Report any such fault to the owner rather than bypassing it.

Room leakage and pressure

Dry rooms are normally kept at positive pressure relative to the surrounding space, so most leakage is outward and the supply air carries the moisture load of the people. Positive pressure only helps if the envelope is tight. Gaps at ceiling penetrations, conduit and pass-through boxes weaken the barrier and make the dehumidifier work harder. Because the pressure difference is what keeps moist air out, watch for door-closer problems, changes in exhaust and loading filters that shift room pressure. Leakage can still run inward when a door is open or the room is briefly negative, so the worked example below includes an inward leakage term to show how large that can become. Pressure cascade ideas are in the cleanroom lesson.

Dew point sensors and drift

Everything about control depends on the dew point reading. Sensors at very low dew points respond slowly, and they can read wrong after a wet excursion such as a door left open. Drift is a normal behavior of humidity sensors, which is why the owner should have a calibration schedule and may use a reference instrument. Where you can, compare two sensors in different places, check the sample line for leaks, and treat a sudden shift as a possible sensor problem as well as a real process problem. Never adjust a setpoint to make a doubtful reading look good.

Solvent exhaust from coating lines

The national lab slides we read show electrodes made with a solvent-based slurry as the baseline process, and describe aqueous processing as a way to cut the cost of the coating and drying steps. A solvent coating line needs exhaust to carry vapor away from the drying oven and coating head. That exhaust removes already-dried air from the building, and it must be made up. The solvent in those baseline electrodes is N-methylpyrrolidone, and the federal environmental agency has determined that it presents an unreasonable risk to human health, assessing workers among the exposed groups. The agency also states that it does not assume workers always and appropriately wear protective equipment. Those are reasons to treat line exhaust as a safety system, not a comfort system. Do not tie into it, rebalance it or shut it down without the owner's procedure, and do not recirculate it. Exhaust and makeup air are linked, so a change on one side shows up as a pressure and dew point change in the room. For lithium cell storage and charging areas, see the battery room and battery storage enclosure lessons.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for easy arithmetic, not a measured value or a design rule.

  • People: 8 working in the room, each adding 60 g of water per hour (assumed).
  • Conversion: 1 g is 15.43 grains.
  • Leakage: 60 cfm of air entering from the adjacent space at 20 grains of water per pound of dry air (assumed), with air density 0.075 lb per cubic foot.
  • Room air: 0.55 grains per pound (assumed, a very dry room).
  • Supply air: 0.10 grains per pound (assumed), so each pound of supply air can absorb 0.55 - 0.10 = 0.45 grains.

People: 8 x 60 g/h = 480 g/h. 480 x 15.43 = 7,406 grains per hour.

Leakage: 60 cfm x 60 min/h x 0.075 lb/ft³ = 270 lb of air per hour. Each pound brings 20 - 0.55 = 19.45 grains more than the room holds. 270 x 19.45 = 5,252 grains per hour.

Total: 7,406 + 5,252 = 12,658 grains per hour.

Dry air needed: 12,658 / 0.45 = 28,129 lb per hour. In cfm: 28,129 / (60 x 0.075) = 6,251 cfm.

So under these assumptions, about 6,250 cfm of dry supply air removes the moisture from people and leakage. Each extra person adds 60 x 15.43 = 926 grains per hour, which needs 926 / 0.45 / 4.5 = about 457 cfm more. Two lessons come from the arithmetic. The smaller the difference between room and supply moisture, the larger the airflow. And a tight room with strict entry limits cuts the load without adding any equipment. The example leaves out moisture from materials, door openings and makeup air for exhaust, which add to the load in a real room.

Field card: Battery Cell Dry Rooms

Dry room: coil first, desiccant second, tight room, strict doors.

  • Targets such as -40 °C dew point come from the cell maker; we hold and prove them.
  • A cooling coil alone is a poor way to get this dry; the desiccant wheel finishes the job.
  • People and leakage are major moisture loads: grains per hour divided by grains per pound of dry-air capacity gives dry air flow.
  • Never prop interlocked airlock doors.
  • Treat a sudden dew point shift as a possible sensor fault and as a real excursion until checked.
  • Coating line exhaust is a worker safety system; follow the owner's procedure.
