Reading an HVAC Replacement Estimate in New Jersey: What a Written Contract Should Show

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A replacement estimate is the document you will be held to, so it should say more than a price. This lesson walks through what to look for on an HVAC replacement quote in New Jersey, which items the state's home improvement rules say a written contract must carry, and how to compare two quotes with simple arithmetic.

General information, not legal advice

This page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It is not legal advice, and it cannot tell you whether a particular contract is fair or enforceable. Rules change and facts differ. For questions about a contract you are about to sign, talk to a New Jersey lawyer or contact the Division of Consumer Affairs, and check the current rule text yourself.

Start with the contractor, not the equipment

New Jersey regulates home improvement contractors through the Contractors' Registration Act. The Division of Consumer Affairs (DCA) FAQ says a contractor who has not received a registration number is prohibited from selling or making home improvements, and that the number must be displayed on business documents. The DCA compliance checklist adds that the number starts with "HIC reg.#" and that the word "license" should not be used for it. The rule on registration numbers, N.J.A.C. 13:45A-17.11, says the number must appear on contracts and on correspondence with consumers.

So the first line to check on any estimate is a registration number you can look up with the DCA. Registration is separate from trade licensing. For the state HVACR board license, see the NJ HVACR contractor license lesson linked below. Whether a given HVAC firm needs both is a question for the DCA, not for this page.

What the state says a written contract must contain

N.J.A.C. 13:45A-16.2(a)12, as published by Cornell LII, requires a home improvement contract over $500, and every change to it, to be in writing and signed by all parties, in understandable language. It must state:

  • the seller's legal name and business address, and those of any sales representative who negotiated the deal;
  • a description of the work and the principal products, including each product's name, make, size, capacity, model and model year where applicable;
  • the total price, including finance charges;
  • the dates or time period for starting and finishing;
  • any mortgage or security interest tied to the job;
  • any guarantee or warranty the seller makes on products, materials, labor or services.

The DCA's contractor compliance checklist lists the same core items and adds the registration number, a copy of the contractor's general liability insurance proof (at least $500,000 per occurrence) with the insurer's phone number, and the Division's toll-free number. It also describes a cancellation notice: you may cancel before midnight of the third business day after receiving a copy of the contract, by signed, dated written notice sent by registered or certified mail or delivered in person, with a full refund due within 30 days. Ask a lawyer or the DCA how that right applies to your signing situation.

Other parts of the same rule matter to a replacement job. Work may not start before all required permits are issued. A written copy of each guarantee or warranty is to be given when the bid is presented and again at signing, though manufacturer warranties may be provided at installation. Failing to disclose that the price leaves out permit fees or financing charges is listed as an unlawful practice. So is asking for final payment before the work is done as the contract describes. And a missed start or finish date without a cause beyond the seller's control, or without timely written notice of the delay, is also listed.

Equipment lines: model numbers and the AHRI reference

The state rule asks for the model of principal products. For a split system, that means separate lines for the outdoor unit and for the indoor coil, air handler or furnace, each with a full model number, not a brand and a tonnage. Ratings belong to the combination. The AHRI directory describes a certified reference number as a unique number identifying a condenser and evaporator combination, and says the installing contractor is the best source for it. Ask for that number on the estimate, then look it up yourself. The directory page advises entering only the first three to five characters of the outdoor model, since letters change from year to year. The AHRI matched systems lesson explains why the pairing matters.

Load calculation and scope

The Department of Energy's Building America guide for homeowners (PNNL-20421) describes ACCA Manual J as the method for estimating a home's heat loss and heat gain, says older rules of thumb often led to oversized systems, and says the contractor should check local codes and handle required permits. The ENERGY STAR quality installation FAQ lists correctly sized and matched equipment based on load calculations, a well-sealed duct system and the proper refrigerant charge as required elements. On an estimate, that translates to a line saying a load calculation will be done for your house, with a copy to you, and a statement about duct work and charge.

Permit, removal and refrigerant

Under the state rule the work cannot start before permits are issued, so the estimate should say who pulls the permit and whether the fee is in the price. Local rules decide what is needed; the New Jersey permit lesson linked below covers that. Also look for a line on removal and disposal of the old equipment.

For refrigerant, federal rules apply to the people doing the work. EPA's Section 608 page says the program prohibits intentionally venting refrigerants, including HFCs, while servicing air conditioning equipment. The regulation at 40 CFR 82.161, on Cornell LII, requires EPA 608 certification for anyone whose work could break into a refrigerant circuit, with apprentices allowed only under close supervision. A good estimate states that the old refrigerant will be recovered by a technician holding EPA 608 certification. If the estimate is silent, ask.

Warranty, dates and payments

Look for two warranty lines. One is the manufacturer's: which warranty applies, and who registers the equipment and by when (see the warranties lesson linked below). The other is the contractor's own labor warranty: how long it lasts and what it excludes. The state rule says guarantees must be specific and clear, and must state exclusions or limits. Then look for a start date or window, a completion date or window, and a payment schedule tied to stages of work. The rule text we read set no percentage for deposits, so ask the DCA or a lawyer if a deposit looks high to you.

Worked example: two invented quotes side by side

These quotes are made up. Quote A comes from Firm A and quote B from Firm B, for the same house and a similar-size system. A checkmark means the item is printed on the estimate.

#ItemQuote AQuote B
1HIC registration numberYesYes
2Total priceYesYes
3Outdoor unit model numberYesYes
4Indoor coil or air handler model numberYesMissing
5AHRI reference numberYesMissing
6Load calculationYesMissing
7Permit and who pulls itYesMissing
8Removal and disposal of old unitYesYes
9Refrigerant handlingMissingMissing
10Warranty registrationYesMissing
11Labor warrantyYesMissing
12Start and completion datesYesMissing
13Payment scheduleYesYes

Step 1: count the items. Quote A has 12 of 13. Quote B has 5 of 13, so 13 - 5 = 8 are missing.

Step 2: price difference. Quote A is $14,200. Quote B is $11,900. $14,200 - $11,900 = $2,300, so B is cheaper by $2,300.

Step 3: deposit as a percent of total. Quote A asks $4,260 down: $4,260 / $14,200 = 0.30, which is 30%. Quote B asks $5,950 down: $5,950 / $11,900 = 0.50, which is 50%. On quote B you would have $5,950 at risk before any start date is written down.

Step 4: cost per year over an invented expected life. Suppose the equipment on A is expected to last 15 years and the lower-grade equipment on B 12 years. These are assumptions for the exercise, not published figures. A: $14,200 / 15 = $946.67 per year. B: $11,900 / 12 = $991.67 per year. $991.67 - $946.67 = $45.00, so B costs about $45 more per year even though it is $2,300 cheaper up front.

Step 5: read the result. The arithmetic leans toward A only because of the assumed life spans. Change them and the answer changes. The checklist counts matter more: you cannot compare prices when quote B does not say what equipment, what permit work or what warranty the $11,900 buys. Ask B to fill in the 8 blank lines in writing, and ask A to add the refrigerant line, then compare again. This example also leaves out energy use, which depends on the rated efficiency of each listed system.

Field card: Reading an HVAC Replacement Estimate

If it is not in writing on the estimate, you cannot point to it later.

  • Contractor name, address and an HIC registration number you can look up with the DCA.
  • Over $500: written, signed, changes in writing (N.J.A.C. 13:45A-16.2).
  • Full model numbers for the outdoor and indoor units, plus the AHRI reference number.
  • A load calculation, a permit line, removal of the old unit and a refrigerant recovery line.
  • Manufacturer warranty registration and the contractor's labor warranty, in writing.
  • Start and completion dates, total price, and a payment schedule.
  • Deposit percent = deposit / total price. Cost per year = price / expected years.
  • Contract questions: ask a New Jersey lawyer or the DCA. This card is not legal advice.
