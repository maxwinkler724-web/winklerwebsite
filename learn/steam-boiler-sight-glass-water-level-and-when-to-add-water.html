Steam Boiler Sight Glass: Water Level and When to Add Water

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you own a steam boiler, the glass tube on its side is the one gauge you can read without tools. It tells you how much water is in the boiler right now. This lesson explains what the line should look like, why adding water to a hot boiler is dangerous, what an automatic feeder does, and when a number you can count tells you something is wrong.

Check your own manual first

Your boiler's manual wins. The wording below comes from published manuals for a few boilers and controls. Yours may differ. Look for the manual by model name on the boiler's rating plate, and follow it.

Reading the glass

The glass shows the water line, the boundary between water below and steam space above. Where that line belongs is set by the maker, not by a rule of thumb. The Weil-McLain SGO steam manual says the normal waterline is halfway up the gauge glass, and that the cold-fill level should match a line stamped on the jacket. The Peerless 63 and 64 manual gives a different spot: for gravity returns, about 25-7/8 inches above the boiler foundation, which it describes as about two-thirds of the glass. Two makers, two answers. Find the mark or the line in your own manual.

The line is not supposed to sit still. Weil-McLain says the level fluctuates while the boiler runs. Water also leaves the boiler as steam and comes back through the return pipes as condensate, so some movement is expected. Weil-McLain's troubleshooting table lists water disappearing from the glass and flowing back into the system through the return piping as a condition for a technician to check, so write down what you see and when.

A line that jumps up and down hard is a different story. Peerless says dirt, oil or other contaminants can cause foaming and surging and an unstable water line, and Weil-McLain lists violent waterline swings with a technician skim as the fix. See Wet Steam and Carryover Basics for what is going on inside, and Steam Boiler Short-Cycling: Causes and Where to Look if the burner is also starting and stopping quickly.

A dirty or unreadable glass

A glass full of rust or sediment can hide the line. Weil-McLain's manual says to clean the glass when needed and describes flushing it by closing the lower cock and opening the petcock below the glass. It also warns that boiler pressure must be low to avoid severe burns. That is a service task. Do not open anything on a running boiler. If the glass is cloudy and you cannot see a line, treat the level as unknown and call a technician.

Safety: never add cold water to a hot boiler

Weil-McLain's SGO manual states it directly in its water-system tips: do not add cold water to a hot boiler, because thermal shock can cause the sections to crack. The same manual also says not to fire the boiler without water or the sections will overheat, and its start-up checklist lists "water not visible in gauge glass" as a problem to correct, with a call to a qualified technician if the burner still will not fire. The Peerless manual adds a related caution for boil-out cleaning: do not let make-up water enter too quickly.

Our own advice (not the manuals' wording) for an empty or nearly empty glass on a hot boiler is simple. Do not feed it. Shut the boiler down at the switch, leave the valves alone, and call a heating technician. The manuals we read do not give owners a procedure for topping off a hot boiler. They put that on trained people.

Manual fill versus an automatic feeder

Some boilers have a manual fill valve you open by hand. Others have an automatic feeder that adds water on its own. Hydrolevel's VXT-120 instructions explain the feeder idea: it works with a probe or float-type low-water cutoff, waits through an adjustable delay (2 minutes is the factory setting, adjustable from 30 seconds to 10 minutes) so condensate has time to return before it feeds, and can be set to add water only until the cutoff lets the burner run again, or a maximum of 5 gallons. Hydrolevel also says boiler flooding can occur if the feeder is not set properly. Weil-McLain agrees on the risk: a feeder that is installed or adjusted wrongly can add water before condensate returns, causing overfill and flooding when it does return.

Hydrolevel describes the VXT as acting on the cutoff's signal, so it supports the cutoff rather than replacing it. Its setup involves lowering the water on purpose and timing the refill, which is work for a trained technician.

The low-water cutoff and your part in testing it

The low-water cutoff (LWCO) shuts off the burner when water is too low. Our full lesson on it is Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance. Here is what the makers say about float types. McDonnell & Miller's Series 67 instructions say to blow down the control weekly while the boiler runs, with the water at its normal level and the burner on, and to confirm the burner shuts off. If it does not, shut the boiler off immediately and correct the problem. The company also calls for a discharge line from the blowdown valve to a safe place so no one is scalded. The Series 63 sheet gives a different schedule (daily above 15 psi, weekly below) and says not to use an automatic water feeder with a manual-reset cutoff.

Weil-McLain's residential manual treats float testing as a weekly item per the control maker's instructions and says not to blow down the cutoff unless blowdown piping was installed per the manual. If your boiler does not have that piping, this is a job for a technician, not a bucket. Owners should know the control exists, ask when it was last opened and cleaned (Weil-McLain says at least annually), and never bypass it.

Too much make-up water

Here is the part many owners miss. Weil-McLain's manual says make-up water is only needed when the system is leaking or when make-up water is administered incorrectly, so a steady need for water is a symptom. It also says continual make-up water reduces boiler life and tells owners to have leaks repaired at once. Peerless says leaks increase the volume of make-up water and significantly shorten the life of the boiler. The U.S. Boiler Company Independence manual says that if you have to add water more than once a month, a qualified technician should check the system for leaks. Hydrolevel explains the why: adding water raises the oxygen level and builds up lime and mineral deposits over time.

Weil-McLain's weekly checklist includes looking for water leaks in the boiler and piping, and its troubleshooting table lists a wrongly installed or adjusted automatic feeder as a cause of heavy make-up use. If the glass keeps needing water, call.

Worked example: counting make-up water (hypothetical numbers)

Hydrolevel's instructions give a guideline for late-model boilers during the heating season, in gallons per month by net IBR rating (91,000 to 120,000 Btu/hr: 8 gallons per month). It excludes water added during maintenance, and the boiler maker's own limit overrides it. Its feeder has a built-in counter, and the company says to check it annually. If your feeder has no counter, a small water meter on the make-up line is one way to get the same number. That idea is ours, not the maker's.

Say a boiler rated 100,000 Btu/hr net (hypothetical) has four weekly meter readings of 3.0, 3.5, 3.5 and 4.0 gallons.

  1. Total: 3.0 + 3.5 + 3.5 + 4.0 = 14.0 gallons in 28 days.
  2. Per day: 14.0 ÷ 28 = 0.5 gallons a day.
  3. Scale to a 30-day month: 0.5 × 30 = 15 gallons.
  4. Compare with the 8-gallon guideline: 15 ÷ 8 = 1.9, so nearly double.

Hydrolevel says that when feed amounts significantly exceed its limits, a qualified technician should check for leaks. The numbers do not say where the water goes. They say it is worth a visit.

When to call

  • No water is visible in the glass, or the burner will not fire and the glass looks empty.
  • The line swings hard, the glass fills with dirt, or you hear banging.
  • You add water more than about once a month, or the feeder counter or meter runs high.
  • The burner stays on when the control should have shut it off.

Field card: Steam Boiler Sight Glass

Read the glass, never feed a hot boiler, count the make-up water.

  • Normal water line is set by the maker: halfway up the glass on a Weil-McLain SGO, about two-thirds on a Peerless 63/64 with gravity returns.
  • Do not add cold water to a hot boiler; thermal shock can crack sections.
  • Do not fire a boiler without water.
  • A feeder takes its cue from the cutoff and can flood the boiler if set wrong.
  • Blow down float cutoffs only per the maker, with discharge piping in place.
  • Needing water more than once a month points to a leak. Call for service.
  • Hypothetical example: 14 gallons in 28 days is about 15 a month, nearly double an 8 gallon guideline.
