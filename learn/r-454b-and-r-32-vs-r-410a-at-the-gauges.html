R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges

Hook up gauges to a new A2L heat pump and the numbers look familiar. That is the trap. They are close enough to feel the same and different enough to matter.

Pressures: close, not equal

HVAC School reports that A2L suction pressures sit within about 10 PSIG of R-410A and discharge pressures within about 40 PSIG, a much smaller jump than R-22 to R-410A. At the same suction pressure the saturation temperatures differ: its example of about 117 PSIG gives roughly 39 F for R-410A, 38 F for R-32 and 45 F for R-454B. The point is not those numbers. The point is that the right chart for the right refrigerant changes your superheat and subcooling.

Use the right PT chart

The same article notes pressure-temperature curves are similar but says to use the chart or app for your application. A cylinder is not a guide: R-454B equipment should only be serviced with R-454B, and R-410A systems with R-410A. Hardi, an industry group, explains that R-454B equipment operates at slightly lower pressures and its metering device is calibrated for it, so substitution can starve the evaporator.

Glide: small, but know the terms

Glide is the temperature window between the boiling points of a blend. R-32 is a single refrigerant and has none. R-410A has very little. HVAC School puts R-454B at around 2 F in one article and about 0.2 F in another, so the sources disagree on the figure but agree it is small. For real glide, the rule of thumb in HVAC School's glide article is bubble point for subcooling, dew point for superheat, and bubble point for static pressure. Use the refrigerant maker's chart for exact values.

Other things you will notice

  • Oil: R-410A and R-454B use POE-32; R-32 needs a higher-viscosity POE-46 because discharge temperatures run higher, per HVAC School. R-32 vapor is also heavier than air (its safety data sheet gives a relative density of about 1.86), so a leak pools low.
  • Discharge temperature: HVAC School repeats the 225 F stay-alive rule, six inches from the compressor outlet.
  • Parts: expansion valves and other pressure-set parts carry different part numbers.
  • Service ports: red caps help identify A2L equipment.
  • Charging: follow the maker's method and charts, and charge a blend the way the maker says, usually as a liquid.
  • Safety class: A2L, not A1. Ignition control, ventilation and A2L-rated recovery gear apply.

Why the equipment changed

A maker's regulatory guide describes EPA's 2023 Technology Transitions rule as setting a GWP limit of 700 for new residential and light commercial air conditioners and heat pumps from 2025, and R-410A is above that limit. That is why new equipment moved to R-32 and R-454B. Rules about installing older R-410A equipment changed after that rule, so this page does not give an install deadline; read EPA's current notice (linked in Sources) and ask your supplier what applies to the equipment you are quoting.

Habit to build

Before you connect gauges, read the nameplate for the refrigerant and open the matching chart. Do not trust a chart saved from last week's job.

Field card: R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges

Close pressures, different charts. Read the nameplate first.

  • A2L pressures are close to R-410A but not the same; use the right PT chart or app.
  • R-32 has no glide; R-454B has a small amount.
  • Subcooling uses bubble point, superheat uses dew point, per HVAC School.
  • Oil, discharge temperature and part numbers can differ (R-32 notably).
  • Never swap refrigerants; A2L work follows A2L habits.
