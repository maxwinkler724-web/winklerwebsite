Whole-House Fans vs Attic Fans: Do They Cut Cooling Costs?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two fans in the attic sound alike and do different jobs. A whole-house fan flushes your living space with cooler air. A powered attic ventilator only exhausts the attic. This lesson explains the difference, what federal and university sources say about each, and the sizing and safety checks that matter in a New Jersey house.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on your house. Fan installs involve wiring, ceiling and roof openings and combustion safety. Follow the fan maker's instructions and local code, and have a qualified person check gas and oil appliances before you run a large exhaust fan.

The difference in one picture

What each fan does

The U.S. Department of Energy fact sheet on whole-house fans (prepared with Southface and Oak Ridge National Laboratory) says the fan cools the house by drawing cooler outdoor air in through open windows and exhausting warm indoor air through the attic. It suggests running it when outdoor temperatures are lower than indoor ones. A powered attic ventilator is a different product: it moves attic air outdoors and is not set up to pull outdoor air through your rooms. That difference drives the verdicts below.

What research says about powered attic fans

The Building America Solution Center (Pacific Northwest National Laboratory) says the cooling benefit of attic fans is "usually much less than might be expected" and that passive attic ventilation is generally recommended over fans. It explains why: the fan creates a negative pressure zone in the attic, which draws conditioned air from the living space into the attic through gaps in the ceiling. In winter it warns that warm, moist indoor air can move into the attic and cause moisture damage.

The Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC) studied photovoltaic attic fans in 2000. The paper says powered ventilation does not typically give a net energy savings unless the attic is uninsulated, and that conventional units often draw 250 to 300 watts. The solar units cut air conditioning use about 6 percent (roughly 460 kWh per year), which the authors called a payback of over twenty years. Allison Bailes of Energy Vanguard sums it up as "air conditioning your attic" and, in most cases, a waste of money. His article points to FSEC-CR-1496-05, a literature review on attic ventilation.

A well-insulated, air-sealed attic leaves a fan little to do. See Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for New Jersey Homes for the better first step.

What research says about whole-house fans

The DOE fact sheet treats the whole-house fan as a way to cool when outdoor air is cooler than indoor air, and suggests spring and fall use in homes that also have air conditioning. The Building America guide copy on energy.gov likewise says these fans work best when outdoor air is cooler, and lists poor humidity control, no air filtration and noise as drawbacks. A fan moves air, it does not dry it, so a muggy New Jersey night will bring in cooler but still damp air. We did not find a New Jersey specific study, so treat "run it only when outdoor air is cooler and not damp" as a rule of thumb. Neither source gives a dollar savings figure, so this page does not either.

For related comfort tools that only move air in a room, see Ceiling Fans: Summer and Winter Direction and Real Savings.

Worked example (hypothetical house)

This is a hypothetical house, not a measured one. A 2,000 square foot house with 8 foot ceilings has a volume of 2,000 x 8 = 16,000 cubic feet. Suppose a whole-house fan is rated 3,000 CFM (cubic feet per minute) at its highest speed.

  1. Air moved per hour: 3,000 CFM x 60 minutes = 180,000 cubic feet per hour.
  2. Air changes per hour (ACH): 180,000 / 16,000 = 11.25. Real airflow is lower when windows are restricted or vents are small, so this is an upper estimate.
  3. Attic net free vent area at the DOE fact sheet ratio of 1 square foot per 750 CFM: 3,000 / 750 = 4.0 square feet.
  4. At the AirScape ratio of 1 square foot per 500 CFM: 3,000 / 500 = 6.0 square feet. AirScape's own worked case for 3,000 CFM is also 6 square feet.
  5. Counting existing vents: AirScape suggests treating a screened vent as about 50 percent net free (a 2 square foot louver counts 1.0) and a ridge vent as 13 percent of its length in feet (30 ft x 0.13 = 3.9).

Two 2 square foot gable louvers (2 x 1.0 = 2.0) plus the 30 foot ridge (3.9) total 5.9 square feet. That clears the 4.0 target and falls just short of the 6.0 one. The fan maker's number for your model governs, and published net free ratings beat rules of thumb.

Why vent area matters

The AirScape manual says less net free vent area than recommended decreases airflow and creates back-pressure that can substantially reduce the fan's performance. The DOE fact sheet adds that continuous ridge and soffit vents are usually more than adequate, and that attic air must not be forced back into the living space through cracks and gaps.

Combustion appliance and backdraft caution

A large exhaust fan can pull the house below outdoor pressure. The DOE fact sheet warns that a fan may easily backdraft a water heater located inside a louvered closet door, especially when too few windows are open, and recommends against combustion appliances that draw air from the room. The AirScape manual says never run the fan without opening a window or door, since that can excessively depressurize the home, and to turn off the furnace and air conditioner first. The Building America page on attic fans makes the same point: combustion appliances other than sealed combustion units may not draft well or could backdraft. Read Water Heater Backdrafting: How to Check the Draft Hood and Spill Switch, and have a trained tester check the draft before you run a whole-house fan in a house with a gas or oil appliance.

Fire and smoke

The AirScape manual says a fire alarm may call for the fan's interlock, which disables the fan and sets the damper doors to the off position. We did not find a sourced ember or fire-rating requirement for fan openings, so read your fan's manual and ask your local building department.

Winter air sealing of the opening

The DOE fact sheet calls a whole-house fan essentially a large, uninsulated hole in the ceiling when it is off and advises louver covers. The Building America guide copy says fans need an air-tight cover for winter use. Some fans have an automatic drop-down cover that seals at ceiling level, shown on the Building America Solution Center with a Tamarack Ventilation photo. In a New Jersey winter an unsealed opening lets heated air leak into the attic.

Which one fits?

The sources point to sealing and insulating the attic first and skipping a powered attic ventilator. A whole-house fan can help on cooler, drier evenings if you have the vent area, a safe combustion setup and a winter cover.

Field card: Whole-House Fans vs Attic Fans

Whole-house fan moves house air. Attic fan moves attic air.

  • Whole-house fan: outdoor air in through windows, out the attic. Use when outdoor air is cooler.
  • Powered attic fan: little or no net savings in the sources read; can pull air from the rooms.
  • Vent area: 1 sq ft net free per 750 CFM (DOE) or per 500 CFM (AirScape).
  • Air changes per hour = CFM x 60 / house volume.
  • Open windows first. Check gas and oil appliance draft.
  • Cover the opening in winter.
