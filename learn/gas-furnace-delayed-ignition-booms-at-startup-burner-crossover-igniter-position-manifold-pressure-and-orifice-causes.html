Gas Furnace Delayed Ignition: Why It Booms at Startup

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas furnace that lights with a thump, boom or bang is telling you that gas collected in the burner area before the flame caught it. The sound may be mild or startling, but the cause is the same: ignition came late. This lesson explains how that happens, what usually causes it, what a homeowner should do, and what a technician checks.

Check the manual and the tech

Educational summary. Pressure numbers, settings and test steps vary by model. The furnace's own installation and service instructions and the rating plate rule. We could not find a government or university page that ranks the causes below, so they come from our own explanation of how the burner system works. Treat them as a checklist of places to look, not a diagnosis.

How gas builds up before it lights

When the thermostat calls for heat, the control runs the inducer fan, then warms the igniter, then opens the gas valve. Gas flows from the manifold through an orifice at each burner and mixes with air. The burner closest to the igniter should catch first, and flame should then spread burner to burner along the row through small slots or channels between them, usually called crossover or carryover slots. (The diagram below shows the layout.)

Gas only burns when it is mixed with air in the right range. NOAA's chemical database lists methane, the main part of natural gas, at a 5 percent lower and 15 percent upper explosive limit in air. Natural gas also has other components, and the limits it lists date from 1992, so treat 5 to 15 percent as a rough guide. What matters is this: if gas keeps flowing while nothing lights it, the burner box fills with a burnable mix. When a flame finally reaches it, that mix burns all at once instead of in a steady stream. The result is a pressure pulse, which you hear as a boom.

Common causes

  • Dirty or rusted crossover slots. Rust flakes, lint or soot narrow the slots, so flame crawls across slowly or skips a burner. The far burners fill with gas before flame reaches them.
  • Weak, cracked or misaligned igniter. A hot surface igniter that is aged, loose, or bent out of the gas stream may take too long to light the first burner. Gas is already flowing when it does.
  • Blocked burners or orifices. Rust, spider webs and debris change how gas and air mix, so one burner lights late.
  • High manifold pressure. Too much pressure pushes gas out faster than the air mix can handle, so flame can lift or light unevenly. A regulator that has drifted or been set wrong is the usual reason.
  • Low gas pressure. Weak flow gives a slow, lazy flame that carries across the row more slowly than it should.
  • Wrong orifices after a propane conversion. Natural gas and propane need different orifice sizes and pressures. A furnace converted with the wrong parts, or not converted at all, can light badly. See the conversion lesson.

Why it matters

A hard light is not just noise. The pressure pulse can push flame back out toward the burner area, which is what a rollout switch is there to catch (see the rollout switch lesson). Repeated hard lights stress the heat exchanger and cabinet parts, and a rattled door or a scorched burner box is a sign of a strong event. We have no data on how often delayed ignition cracks a heat exchanger, so we do not claim it does. The practical point is that a furnace that bangs should be looked at before it is trusted again, and the exchanger inspection is part of that (how technicians inspect it).

One recall is on record from the federal safety agency. In March 2023 the Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall of about 185 commercial natural gas boilers, sold from June 2020 through November 2022, saying delayed ignition and/or flame rollout could occur and cause a burn hazard. The page reported two flame rollouts and no injuries. It involves boilers, not home furnaces, and we found no CPSC furnace recall that names delayed ignition. If you want to know about your own model, search the agency's recall list with the model number from the rating plate.

What a homeowner should do

  • If the furnace bangs, turn the thermostat to off and switch off the furnace power switch. Do not keep cycling it to see if it happens again.
  • If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility from outside.
  • Do not remove burner parts, bend the igniter or adjust the gas valve yourself.
  • Call a qualified heating technician. The CPSC advises a yearly professional inspection of all fuel-burning home heating systems, and a university extension heating guide lists soot at the burner and odors as warning signs to get help.
  • Mention when the noise happens: every start, only on cold starts, or only since a repair or a fuel change.

What the technician checks

  1. Observe a start. Watch the lighting sequence through the sight port or with the door off as the manual allows. Note whether the first burner lights promptly, how long the rest take, and whether any burner lights late.
  2. Power and gas off, then burners. Look for rust, lint, webs and blocked ports. Clean or replace as needed.
  3. Crossover slots. Check every slot is open and clean and the burners sit square.
  4. Igniter. Check its condition, mounting and position in the gas stream of the end burner.
  5. Orifices. Confirm the size matches the fuel and the rating plate, and that none is partly blocked or damaged.
  6. Gas pressure. Measure inlet and manifold pressure with a manometer. See the manometer lesson.
  7. Flame pattern. After the repair, look for steady, even flames that settle into the heat exchanger without lifting or rolling out.

Worked example (our own)

This example uses assumed numbers. Real values come from the rating plate and manual of the furnace in front of you.

Assumptions: a five-burner furnace with an input of 80,000 Btu/h on natural gas, and a rating plate manifold pressure of 3.5 inches of water column (in. w.c.). These are typical-looking values we picked for teaching, not a standard.

Step 1, input per burner. 80,000 ÷ 5 = 16,000 Btu/h each.

Step 2, burner inspection. With power and gas off, the tech pulls the burners and finds burner 5 has a slot that is half filled with rust flakes. That slot is the path flame must take to reach the last burner, so it lights late. It is cleaned, and the burner is reseated square.

Step 3, manifold reading, case A. Measured while firing: 3.4 in. w.c. The difference is 3.5 − 3.4 = 0.1 in. w.c., and 0.1 ÷ 3.5 = 0.029, about 3 percent low. That is close to the plate value in this made-up case, so pressure is not the cause here. Whether it is acceptable is for the manual to say.

Step 4, manifold reading, case B. Suppose the tech measured 4.6 in. w.c. instead. The difference is 4.6 − 3.5 = 1.1 in. w.c., and 1.1 ÷ 3.5 = 0.31, so about 31 percent high. As a rough guide (our own rule of thumb), flow through a fixed orifice rises with the square root of pressure: √(4.6 ÷ 3.5) = √1.314 = 1.146. So the burners would pass about 15 percent more gas, and 80,000 × 1.146 is about 91,700 Btu/h. That is over the plate input, so the regulator needs correcting before the furnace goes back in service, and the actual gas input should be verified.

Step 5, result. Case A with the cleaned slot and an igniter in the right spot should light all five burners quickly. Case B means pressure and cleaning both need work.

Lesson 699. The worked example is our own illustration with assumed inputs. It is not a repair procedure.

Field card: Gas Furnace Delayed Ignition: Why It Booms at Startup

A boom means gas waited too long for a flame.

  • Gas builds up when ignition or flame spread is late.
  • Look at: crossover slots, igniter position, burner and orifice blockage, manifold pressure, orifice size for the fuel.
  • Homeowner: shut it off, call a qualified tech.
  • Tech: watch a start, clean, check, measure, then re-check the flame.
  • Methane burns in about 5 to 15 percent in air (NOAA, 1992 source).
