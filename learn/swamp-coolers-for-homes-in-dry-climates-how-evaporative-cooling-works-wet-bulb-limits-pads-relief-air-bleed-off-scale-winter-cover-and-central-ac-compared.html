Swamp Coolers for Homes in Dry Climates: How They Work and When They Do Not

Last updated: 2026-11-04

If you moved to the desert Southwest or you are visiting family there, you may find a big box on the roof instead of a condensing unit, and a house that feels cool but a little damp. That box is an evaporative (swamp) cooler. It cools by evaporating water, not by running a refrigerant cycle, so it behaves very differently from central air conditioning. This lesson explains how it works, when it fails, and what upkeep it needs.

How a swamp cooler cools

When water evaporates it takes heat from the air around it. A swamp cooler uses a fan to pull outdoor air through pads kept wet by a small pump, and the cooled, moister air is blown into the house. The federal energy page on evaporative coolers says the air drops roughly 15 to 40 degrees F in the process. A university extension guide describes the same idea: a fan pulls outside air through a wet pad, and the result is lower temperature and higher humidity. There is no compressor and no refrigerant, which is why the federal page says these units use about one-quarter the energy of central AC and cost about half as much to install.

One difference matters for daily life. Central AC recirculates the same indoor air. A swamp cooler brings in a steady supply of fresh outdoor air, and that air has to leave the house somewhere. More on that below.

When it works and when it does not

The cooling is limited by the wet-bulb temperature, the lowest temperature air can reach by evaporating water into it. The difference between dry-bulb and wet-bulb is called the wet-bulb depression. A national laboratory resource guide says the greater the depression, the better the cooler works, and gives a general rule that places with wet-bulb temperatures of 70 F or lower suit evaporative cooling. It contrasts a desert city at 108 F dry-bulb and 70 F wet-bulb (a 38 degree depression) with a humid coastal city at 92 F and 79 F (13 degrees), where evaporative cooling does not suit. At 100 percent relative humidity the wet-bulb equals the dry-bulb and no evaporative cooling is possible.

In plain terms: on a hot, dry afternoon the house feels pleasant. During a humid monsoon-type spell, or any muggy day, the same unit delivers air that is only a few degrees cooler and clammy. The federal page says plainly that these coolers are only suitable for low-humidity areas and do not cool a house as low as an air conditioner would. If your visit lands on a humid week, expect that, and do not assume the unit is broken. A unit that cools poorly on a dry day is a different story; see the upkeep section.

Window units and roof units

Small horizontal-flow coolers can sit in a window and cool one room or one part of a house. Most whole-house units are installed on the roof and blow down through a ceiling duct. The federal page notes that many experts prefer ground-mounted horizontal units because they are easier to maintain and less likely to cause roof leaks. Either way, the fan, pump and water line are the parts that matter to you as a homeowner. The university extension pamphlet on cooler water use lists eight major parts: housing, blower, recirculating pump, water reservoir, float valve, pads, water distribution lines and an electric motor.

Pad types

Pads are the heart of the unit. A national laboratory guide says media comes in treated cellulose, fiberglass, plastic foam or shredded aspen fibers, and that newer thick media (8 to 12 inches) can reach 75 to 93 percent effectiveness, while aspen fiber is about 70 percent. The university extension pamphlet says aspen pads offer the least airflow resistance and last about a season, though they can shed debris into the reservoir. Cellulose pads are more uniform and can last several seasons. Cheaper spun metal or plastic pads may need replacing several times a season. The federal page suggests replacing pads at least twice per cooling season, or about monthly with continuous use, and says some paper and synthetic pads can be cleaned following the maker instructions. Dry spots on a pad greatly cut cooling, so a pad that looks dry in places needs attention.

Open a window, or cooling stalls

A swamp cooler pushes a lot of air into the house, and the house needs somewhere for it to go. The federal page says to open windows or vents on the leeward side, about 1 to 2 square feet of opening per 1,000 cfm of cooling capacity. Open too little and humidity builds up indoors; open too much and hot outside air gets in. Open windows in the rooms you want cooled and close them in unoccupied rooms. The national laboratory guide gives 2 square feet per 1,000 cfm. The university extension pamphlet notes that open windows can be a security concern and says window stops (limiting the opening to about 6 to 8 inches) or ceiling vents to the attic are alternatives, provided the attic is ventilated. Shading windows from direct sun also helps.

Water use, bleed-off and scale

Evaporative coolers keep using water, which the federal page flags as a concern where supplies are limited. As water evaporates, minerals stay behind and concentrate in the pan. Some units have a bleed-off valve that drains part of the water to limit that buildup. The university extension pamphlet says bleed-off valves are controversial because they are estimated to raise water use by 10 to 50 percent, and the national laboratory guide gives the same range and describes a timed pan dump as another option. The university extension pamphlet also warns that bleed-off water can harm garden plants and that scale can rust metal cabinets and form on pads, causing uneven wetting and hot spots. It estimates scale can shorten the life of a metal cooler by 50 percent, says additives do not prevent scale, and says softened water adds sodium that builds up on pads. Cleaning at least once per cooling season is advised.

Pump, float and seasonal upkeep

  • Monthly in hot weather: the federal page says to check the pads, filters, reservoir and pump. Pad condition and a free-floating float valve are the usual keys to a unit that cools poorly.
  • Each season: the federal page calls for a major cleaning, draining the unit to remove sediment and mineral buildup. The national laboratory guide recommends service in spring and again at winter shutdown.
  • Power off first: the federal page says to disconnect the electricity before servicing. Roof work carries fall risk, so use proper ladder and roof safety, or hire a service company.
  • Idle water: the laboratory guide says to empty the pan if the unit sits idle for more than a few days, since standing water can breed mosquitoes and bacteria, including Legionella.

Winter shutdown and covering the unit

For winter, the national laboratory guide says to shut off the water line, drain and clean the pan, and install a cover on the unit. Our own practical reasoning: a cooler on the roof is also an open path into the house, so cold air can fall through the ceiling duct all winter. Ask whoever services your unit how the duct opening should be closed off, and do not run the pump in freezing weather. Visitors should not switch an off-season unit on.

Swamp cooler or central AC?

The federal page puts it simply: a swamp cooler costs about half as much to install and uses about one-quarter the energy, but it needs water, needs more frequent maintenance, and does not cool as low as an air conditioner. Central AC keeps indoor humidity down and works in any weather, while the swamp cooler adds humidity and brings in fresh air. The university extension guide adds that raising airflow gives diminishing returns for cooling and always uses more water and energy, so a two-speed or variable-speed fan helps. A swamp cooler is not a good fit for a humid summer, and if a house also has central AC, ask which system to use on a given day. For other ways to cool without a compressor, see the window fan lesson linked below.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not a measured house. Assumptions: outdoor air at 100 F dry-bulb and 64 F wet-bulb, pad effectiveness of 0.80 (inside the 75 to 93 percent range cited above), airflow of 4,000 cfm, and 1,050 Btu per pound for the heat of vaporization of water.

  1. Wet-bulb depression: 100 minus 64 = 36 F.
  2. Supply air: 100 minus 0.80 × 36 = 100 minus 28.8 = 71.2 F.
  3. Heat removed from the air (sensible): 1.08 × 4,000 × 28.8 = 124,416 Btu/h. The 1.08 is a standard factor for air (0.24 Btu/lb-F × 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h).
  4. Water evaporated: 124,416 / 1,050 = 118.5 lb/h. Water weighs about 8.34 lb/gal, so 118.5 / 8.34 = about 14.2 gal/h.
  5. Cross-check by humidity: air mass flow = 4,000 × 60 × 0.075 = 18,000 lb/h; humidity gain = 0.24 × 28.8 / 1,050 = 0.00658 lb water per lb air; 18,000 × 0.00658 = 118.5 lb/h. Same answer.
  6. With bleed-off, the cited 10 to 50 percent increase gives 14.2 × 1.1 = 15.6 up to 14.2 × 1.5 = 21.3 gal/h.

Compare a humid day, same unit and airflow: 95 F dry-bulb and 79 F wet-bulb gives 95 minus 0.80 × 16 = 82.2 F supply, which feels warm and sticky. That is why the wet-bulb matters more than the thermometer. The university extension pamphlet reports measured averages near 7.6 gal/h of operation in a desert-city study of 46 homes; ours is higher because we assumed full airflow on a very dry day. Real use depends on fan speed and weather.

Field card: Swamp Coolers for Homes

Dry air cools well, humid air does not, and the house needs a way out for the air.

  • Cooling is limited by wet-bulb; wet-bulb of about 70 F or lower suits evaporative cooling.
  • Open windows or vents: about 1 to 2 sq ft per 1,000 cfm.
  • Check pads, reservoir, float and pump monthly in hot weather; clean each season.
  • Bleed-off limits scale but raises water use by 10 to 50 percent.
  • Winter: shut off water, drain, clean, cover the unit, close off the duct.
  • Power off before servicing; use safe roof access.
