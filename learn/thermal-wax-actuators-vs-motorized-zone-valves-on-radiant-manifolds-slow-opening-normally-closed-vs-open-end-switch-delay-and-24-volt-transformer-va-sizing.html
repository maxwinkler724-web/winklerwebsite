Thermal Wax Actuators vs Motorized Zone Valves: Slow Opening, End Switches and 24 V Transformer Sizing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Radiant manifolds usually use small thermal actuators, while many baseboard and radiator zones use motorized zone valves. Both are 24 V devices that open a water path on a thermostat call (a federal energy department page on home heat distribution describes automatic valves controlled by thermostat in each zone), but they behave differently, and the difference matters for pump timing and transformer size.

How a wax actuator pushes a pin

A wax (also called thermoelectric) actuator is a sealed capsule of wax, a pin and a small electric heater. A university digital-fabrication reference on thermal actuators explains that wax expands considerably as it melts, about 5 to 20 percent in volume, and that this expansion moves the plunger with a lot of force. The same reference notes the cost of that design: because it depends on a phase change, the motion is slow, and a bias spring is needed to push the piston back. On a manifold, the pin presses on the valve insert at the loop port. Power warms the wax, the pin moves, and when power is removed the wax cools and the spring returns the pin. A motorized zone valve instead drives a small motor through gears, so its travel is usually quicker. How long each device takes is set by the model, so read the data sheet instead of guessing. Expect a wax actuator to need noticeable time to finish moving, and do not judge a loop by the first few seconds after a call.

Normally closed versus normally open

A normally closed (NC) actuator holds the valve shut with no power and opens when energized. A normally open (NO) actuator does the reverse. A university controls text describes the same idea for any heating valve: the controller action must match whether the valve is normally open or closed, and it warns that a reverse-acting controller never properly controls a normally open heating valve. For radiant manifolds, NC is the common choice because loops stay off when the control loses power, but check the housing label and the control panel. Putting NO actuators on a panel that expects NC will give loops that run when thermostat is satisfied.

Why the pump and boiler should wait

Because the wax takes time to open, a thermostat call does not mean flow is available. If the circulator starts at once, it can run against closed loops, and the boiler can fire with almost no flow. That is our own reasoning, and the fix is simple: many actuators include an end (auxiliary) switch that closes only after the pin has traveled, and the control uses it to start the pump and boiler. Where the actuators have no end switch, a control with a built-in start delay does the same job. The wiring logic is covered in the end switch lesson, so we will not repeat it here.

Adapters for different manifold threads

Manifold valve inserts and actuator bodies come with different thread types and sizes. An adapter is a small ring or collar that lets one actuator body fit another thread. In general terms: match the actuator to the thread named in the manifold or actuator instructions, use the adapter the maker lists for that pairing, and seat the actuator the way the instructions say. Do not force a mismatched thread or use a tool on a plastic nut unless the instructions allow it.

Sizing the 24 V transformer by VA

A university power-engineering note defines a transformer rating as rated voltage times rated current, in volt-amperes (VA). Add the VA of everything the secondary feeds. With many actuators on one transformer, the question is how many can be powered at once. Because each actuator heats an element, its draw can be higher at the start; we call that an inrush factor. The nameplate or data sheet gives the real VA figures, so use those.

Worked example (our own, assumptions stated). Assume eight actuators, each 3 VA while running (assumption), an inrush factor of 1.5 on the running load (assumption), and all eight energized together as the worst case.
Running load: 8 x 3 VA = 24 VA.
With inrush: 24 VA x 1.5 = 36 VA.
Add a 25 percent margin: 36 VA x 1.25 = 45 VA.
A 40 VA transformer is under 45 VA, so it is too small for this case. A 75 VA transformer clears it with room for a control panel.
If the inrush were brief and the actuators started staggered, running load plus margin would be 24 x 1.25 = 30 VA, and 40 VA would pass. Because we do not know that, size for the worse case or add a second transformer. Also add the VA of the panel and any relays on that secondary. The adding method is in the transformer sizing lesson.

Failure signs

  • Loop never gets warm: actuator stuck closed, no 24 V at its leads, or an NO type on an NC panel.
  • Loop stays warm after thermostat is satisfied: stuck open, or the wax has not cooled yet. Give it time, then check again.
  • Pump runs but zones are cold: circulator started before the loops opened, or the end switch is not releasing it. See hot-water no heat diagnosis.
  • Low voltage under load: a transformer too small for all actuators at once, which can keep several from moving fully.
  • Loose or cracked actuator: a cracked housing or a wrong thread fit that never seats.

For flow balancing at the manifold, see radiant manifold balancing. For choosing zone valves versus zone pumps, see zone valves vs zone circulators.

Field card: wax actuators and zone transformers

Slow to open, so wait for the end switch.

  • Wax melts, expands and pushes a pin; a spring returns it.
  • Slow motion is part of the design.
  • Match NC or NO to the control.
  • Pump and boiler start on the end switch or a delay.
  • Transformer VA = sum of loads, with inrush and 25 percent margin (our example: 45 VA).
