Textile Mill HVAC: Steady Humidity, Machine Heat, Lint Filtration and Spray Washers in Spinning and Weaving Rooms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A spinning room is a strange HVAC job. The air must hold a steady relative humidity, the machines dump a lot of heat into it, and the air is full of cotton lint that wants to coat every filter and coil. This lesson walks through why textile plants condition air this way, how a spray-washer system works, and what a tech should check when lint starts to win.

Why fiber needs steady humidity

Cotton and other fibers take up and give off moisture with the air around them. A federal farm research agency notes that too little moisture leaves cotton prone to damage, while too much makes it stick to machinery parts, and that the right amount depends on the process. In plain terms, dry fiber is brittle and builds static charge, so it clings to rolls and guides and breaks more often. Damp fiber gums up the equipment. The goal is a narrow band that stays put all shift, not a single magic number.

A national-lab guidebook on textile energy use lists typical relative humidity ranges by process, averaged from plants in one surveyed country. For cotton, carding runs about 45 to 55 percent, spinning about 50 to 65 percent, winding and twisting about 60 to 70 percent, and weaving about 70 to 85 percent. The same table shows a spinning-room temperature of about 24 to 29 degrees C (roughly 75 to 84 degrees F). Treat these as examples of how setpoints differ room to room, and follow the plant's own process specification for real work.

Machine heat is the cooling load

Spinning frames, drives and fans all end up as heat in the room, because the electric power they draw is turned into friction, air movement and warmth. The same guidebook reports that ring frames are the largest energy users in ring spinning, with spindle rotation dominating, and that humidification plants use about 15 to 25 percent of a textile plant's energy. So the system has two big jobs: carry away machine heat and keep moisture steady. The two jobs interact, which is why one air system usually does both.

Lint: the air is a filter problem first

Fiber processing throws off fly and dust. The guidebook describes return air pulled through tunnels and rotary drum filters on each return fan inlet to strip fibers and particles, and overhead traveling cleaners that patrol the machines to collect fluff. A typical layout puts coarse lint removal first, before the air reaches the washer, so the sprays and coils see mostly clean air.

Lint is also a health matter. The federal workplace safety agency has a cotton dust standard, covering yarn manufacturing, slashing and weaving, and waste houses. It sets a limit of 200 micrograms per cubic meter (8-hour average, lint-free respirable dust) for yarn manufacturing and 750 for slashing and weaving, and requires engineering and work-practice controls to hold exposure down. It also calls for vacuum or low-dispersal floor cleaning and prohibits compressed-air blow-down cleaning where other means are feasible. A federal occupational health institute bulletin reports that this standard helped cut byssinosis, an airways disease from cotton dust, to under 1 percent among US cotton workers. For a tech, the takeaway is practical: do not blow lint off a coil or filter bank with compressed air in a mill, and follow the plant's cleaning rules.

Spray washers and adiabatic humidification

In many mills the main air handler is a spray washer, also called an air washer. Recirculated water is sprayed into the airstream. As water evaporates into the air, the air picks up moisture and its dry-bulb temperature drops, while its total heat stays about the same. That is adiabatic humidification: cooling and humidifying in one step. The guidebook says air washers clean, cool and humidify incoming air before it goes to ceiling ducts. The mist also scrubs some remaining fine lint out of the air and into the wash water, where strainers catch it.

A washer is not a cooling coil. Its leaving air temperature is limited by the entering wet-bulb temperature, so on humid days it may need chilled water or a coil to hold the room. For wetted media, fogging and the water-treatment and Legionella side of these systems, see Adiabatic Humidifiers in Air Handlers rather than repeating it here. For a separate lint-and-heat case, see Commercial Laundry Rooms.

Recirculation with filtering

Because the room load is mostly machine heat, plants recirculate most of the air and bring in only the outdoor air needed for ventilation and pressure. Recirculation saves conditioning energy, but it means the lint filters must keep up, or fibers return to the room. Fan speed control is one place energy is saved: the guidebook calls inlet guide vanes and outlet dampers highly inefficient on these fans, and recommends variable speed drives. That ties to Airflow and Static Pressure Basics, since a loading filter changes system pressure and airflow.

Worked example (our own): airflow for a spinning room

This is our own example with assumed numbers. It is not from a source and does not describe any real plant.

Assumptions: the machines, drives and fans in the room draw 150 kW, and we assume all of that becomes heat in the room air (lights, people and walls are ignored). The supply air is 12 degrees F cooler than the room air. The room is 200 ft by 100 ft by 20 ft.

  1. Convert kW to Btu/h using 1 kW = 3,412 Btu/h: 150 x 3,412 = 511,800 Btu/h.
  2. Use the standard sensible-heat relation for air, Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference. The 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb/ft³ x 0.24 Btu/lb-F x 60 min/h, which is for standard air.
  3. Solve for airflow: cfm = 511,800 / (1.08 x 12) = 511,800 / 12.96 = about 39,500 cfm.
  4. Check: 1.08 x 39,500 x 12 = 511,920 Btu/h, which matches the load within rounding.
  5. Room volume = 200 x 100 x 20 = 400,000 ft³. Air changes per hour = 39,500 x 60 / 400,000 = about 5.9.

What it shows: if the supply air is only 12 degrees F cooler, the load needs nearly 40,000 cfm. If we allowed a 20 degree F difference, the same load needs 511,800 / (1.08 x 20) = about 23,700 cfm. A smaller temperature difference means more air, more fan power and more lint passing through the filters. Real designs also have to count latent load, outdoor air and the room's required humidity, so this is a sizing sketch, not a design.

Keeping lint off the coils

  • Check the first filter stage first. A loaded lint screen or drum filter drops airflow and pushes lint on to the next component. Read the pressure gauge across it, as in Air Handler Filter Racks and Bypass Gaps.
  • Look for a felt mat on coil faces. Wet lint mats against fins, raises air pressure drop and cuts capacity. Clean with the plant's approved method: vacuum, low-pressure water or wet cleaning, not compressed air. See Commercial Duct and Air Handler Cleaning and Air Handler Cooling Coils.
  • Watch washer strainers and nozzles. Lint in the recirculated water clogs spray nozzles and pump strainers, which makes humidity drift.
  • Respect lockout and dust rules. Lint is also a fire fuel and a breathing hazard. Ask the plant about its housekeeping and respirator rules before opening a casing.
  • Watch for drift. A room that slowly gets drier or hotter usually means a loading filter, a clogged nozzle or a lint-matted coil.

Field card: Textile Mill HVAC

Steady humidity, machine heat, lint first.

  • Dry fiber is brittle and static-prone; wet fiber sticks. The band matters, and it differs by process.
  • Machine kW becomes heat: kW x 3,412 = Btu/h.
  • Sensible airflow: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature difference).
  • A spray washer cools and humidifies by evaporation; leaving temperature is limited by wet-bulb.
  • Filter lint before the washer and coil; check filter pressure drop often.
  • No compressed-air blow-down of lint where other means work; follow the plant's cleaning rules.
