Heating a Garage or Workshop From the House Boiler

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A cold garage or workshop sits right next to a boiler that already makes hot water. It is tempting to run two pipes over and add a unit heater. It can work well, but only if the boiler has room to spare, the freeze risks are handled, and the heat loss of the building and the pipe is understood first. This lesson walks through those checks.

Two ways to put heat in the building

The usual choices are a hydronic unit heater, which is a coil with a fan that blows warm air, and a loop of tubing in the floor slab. A federal energy page on radiant heating describes hydronic floors as water pumped from a boiler through tubing under the floor, with room temperature controlled by zone valves or pumps and thermostats, and notes that a thick concrete slab responds slowly, which makes setbacks hard. A university extension publication on heating farm shops says boiler-fed floor heat costs more up front, is hard to add to an existing shop, and recovers slowly, but gives a warm surface to stand on. Those traits explain the trade-off. A slab is comfortable for a workshop that is kept warm all winter. A unit heater recovers quickly after a door is opened, which suits a garage you heat only when you use it. Either way, give the garage its own zone with its own thermostat and circulator or zone valve, so a cold building never pulls on the house loop.

For gas-fired and electric alternatives and for placement of a unit heater, see Garage and Workshop Heaters. This lesson is about the hot-water option only.

Does the boiler have spare capacity?

Start with the building, not the boiler. The same extension publication says heat loss must be calculated to size a shop heater properly, and that older rules of thumb near 50 Btu per square foot per hour have often been replaced by about 20 for well-insulated, air-sealed shops. Calculate the garage load, then add it to the house load and compare the total to the boiler's rated output. Two points matter:

  • Boilers are usually sized close to the house design load. If the house load plus the garage load is more than the boiler output, the house will lose on the coldest night.
  • Check the circulator and the piping, not only the burner. A small boiler loop may not deliver the extra flow, and an older boiler with a low-temperature setting may not make water hot enough for the unit heater coil.

If the margin is thin, give the house priority with a controls arrangement that lets the garage zone wait, or keep the garage on a separate heat source. A high-mass slab held at a steady temperature is gentler on the boiler than a unit heater that calls hard after a cold start.

Freeze risk: open doors and power failure

A garage has big doors that open to outdoor air, and often a heater that depends on electricity. Two events cause trouble:

  • Door open in cold weather. Cold air can sweep across a unit heater coil. If the coil flow is low or a valve is closed, water in the coil can freeze and split it. The extension publication lists how often the large doors will be open as a factor in sizing a shop system.
  • Power failure. When the power goes out the circulator and the fan stop, the water in the coil and the exposed pipes stops moving, and a cold building cools toward outdoor temperature. A plain water loop with nothing to keep the building above freezing can freeze solid. See Frozen Hot-Water Heating Pipes and Baseboard.

This is why a plain water loop in a building that is not kept heated is risky: the pipe is the first thing to freeze, and the break is often found only when it thaws. A federal energy page for a related application says pipe insulation can protect pipes from freezing and cracking in winter, and that heat trace on pipes in unheated spaces is an option, but insulation only slows heat loss. It does not make heat.

Separating a glycol loop with a plate heat exchanger

The safer plan for a building that may sit cold is to run the garage loop with a glycol and water mix and to keep it apart from the house water. A small plate heat exchanger next to the boiler transfers heat from the house loop to the garage loop without mixing the two. The house loop keeps its water. The garage loop carries the freeze protection and can be fitted with its own expansion tank, relief valve, circulator and fill arrangement. Never connect a glycol loop to city water through an automatic filler. Details are in Glycol in Chilled Water and Hydronic Loops. For how plate exchangers are built and cleaned, see Brazed Plate vs Gasketed Plate-and-Frame Heat Exchangers.

The exchanger has a cost: the garage water has to be a little hotter than the house water it takes heat from, and the glycol mix carries somewhat less heat per gallon and is thicker, so it needs more flow and more pump head. Many installations also use a glycol-rated pump and follow the fluid maker's concentration chart. Check the loop concentration with a test method suited to the glycol used, since a mix that is too weak will not protect the loop. Glycol protects against a split pipe, but the building is still cold, so a low-temperature alarm is worth adding.

Buried pipe to a detached building

For a detached garage, the two pipes run underground. Use pipe rated for burial and for the fluid temperature, keep it insulated, and keep the insulation dry. Wet insulation loses much of its value. The same pipe also loses heat all winter, whether or not the garage is calling for heat, so a long run is a real load on the boiler. Our worked example below shows the size of that loss. Bury the pipe below the local frost depth, keep a marked path so no one digs into it, and pressure test the loop before backfill. Local code and the pipe maker's instructions govern depth, bedding and fittings.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These are our own assumptions for practice, not field measurements. Confirm every number against the actual building.

Assumed building. A two-car garage, 24 ft by 24 ft with 9 ft walls, a slab floor, and two 9 ft by 7 ft insulated overhead doors. Indoor temperature 55 F, outdoor design temperature 5 F, so the difference is 50 F. We assume wall R-14 (U = 0.07), doors U = 0.25, ceiling R-33 (U = 0.03), a slab edge factor of 0.5 Btu/h per foot of perimeter per F, and 0.5 air changes per hour.

Step 1, heat loss.

  • Net wall area: 4 × 24 × 9 = 864 ft², minus 2 × 63 = 126 ft² of doors = 738 ft². Walls: 738 × 0.07 = 51.7 Btu/h per F.
  • Doors: 126 × 0.25 = 31.5.
  • Ceiling: 576 × 0.03 = 17.3.
  • Slab edge: 96 ft × 0.5 = 48.0.
  • Air leakage: volume 576 × 9 = 5,184 ft³; 5,184 × 0.5 = 2,592 ft³/h; times 0.018 Btu per ft³ per F = 46.7.
  • Total: 51.7 + 31.5 + 17.3 + 48.0 + 46.7 = 195.2 Btu/h per F. Times 50 F = about 9,760 Btu/h. We round to 9,800 Btu/h.

Step 2, flow. For plain water, gpm = Btu/h divided by (500 × temperature drop). With an assumed 20 F drop: 9,800 / (500 × 20) = 0.98 gpm. Glycol carries less heat per gallon, so we apply an assumed correction of 1.10 (a stand-in for a mix of roughly 30 percent glycol; use the fluid maker's data for the real mix): 0.98 × 1.10 = about 1.07 gpm. That is a small flow, so the garage zone adds little pumping demand. The pipe and pump must still be sized for the pressure drop of the actual run.

Step 3, buried pipe loss. Assume two 1-inch copper tubes (outside diameter 1.125 in) run 60 ft each, 120 ft of pipe total, each with 2 in of insulation of conductivity 0.025 Btu/h per ft per F. For a cylinder, loss per foot per F = 2 × π × k / ln(outer radius / inner radius). The radii are 0.5625 in and 2.5625 in, so ln(4.556) = 1.516, and the loss is 6.283 × 0.025 / 1.516 = 0.1036 Btu/h per ft per F. Assume an average fluid temperature of 140 F and ground at 35 F, a difference of 105 F. That gives 0.1036 × 105 = 10.9 Btu/h per ft, and 10.9 × 120 = about 1,300 Btu/h. We left out the soil resistance, so this slightly overstates the loss, but wet or compressed insulation could make it worse.

Check. The pipe loss is about 1,300 / 9,800, or 13 percent of the garage load, and it runs even when the garage is not calling for heat. The boiler must supply about 9,800 + 1,300 = 11,100 Btu/h at design conditions, before any exchanger or distribution margin.

What to remember

  • Calculate the garage load first, then compare it to the boiler output left after the house load.
  • Use a separate zone, and plan for an open door and for a power failure.
  • A plain water loop in a building that may be cold is risky; a glycol loop behind a plate exchanger is the safer pattern.
  • Buried pipe loses heat all winter, so insulate it and keep the insulation dry.

Field card: Heating a Garage or Workshop From the House Boiler

Load first, then boiler, then freeze plan.

  • Own zone, own thermostat, own circulator or valve.
  • Heat loss = total U times area, plus slab edge and air leakage, times the temperature difference.
  • Water flow in gpm = Btu/h / (500 x drop); glycol needs more, so use the maker's data.
  • Glycol loop behind a plate heat exchanger; no automatic city-water filler on the glycol side.
  • Buried pipe: insulated, dry, below frost depth, pressure tested.
