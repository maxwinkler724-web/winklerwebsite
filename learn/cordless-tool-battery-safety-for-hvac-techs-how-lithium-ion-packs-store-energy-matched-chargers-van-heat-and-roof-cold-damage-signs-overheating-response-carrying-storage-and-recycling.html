Cordless Tool Battery Safety for HVAC Techs: Charging, Heat, Cold, Damaged Packs and Recycling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Cordless drills, impact drivers, saws and work lights make a service call faster. Each one runs from a lithium-ion pack that holds a lot of energy in a small case. This lesson covers how that energy is stored, how to charge, carry and store packs, how heat in the van and cold on the roof affect them, and what to do with a pack that has gone bad.

How a pack stores energy

A lithium-ion pack is a group of small cells wired together, with a circuit board that watches the cells. The pack label lists nominal volts and amp-hours. Volts times amp-hours gives watt-hours, the amount of energy stored. A bigger number means more run time and also more energy that can be released if something goes wrong. A federal consumer product safety release warns about loose cells taken out of packs: the cells are made as parts of packs, they do not have protection circuits on their own, and exposed terminals can short against metal and start thermal runaway, which can cause fires and serious injury. The takeaway for a tech is to use packs as built, and never rebuild, open or mix cells from different packs.

Worked example (our own)

These numbers are our own assumptions for practice, not a rating for any real tool or pack. Assume a pack labeled 18 V and 5.0 Ah, and a cordless saw that draws 300 W while cutting.

  1. Energy: 18 V x 5.0 Ah = 90 Wh.
  2. Ideal run time: 90 Wh / 300 W = 0.3 hours, which is 0.3 x 60 = 18 minutes of continuous cutting.
  3. Real packs do not give all of the label energy at a hard draw, so assume only 80 percent is usable. That is 0.80 x 90 = 72 Wh, and 72 / 300 = 0.24 hours, or about 14 minutes.

Use the same steps for a different pack: multiply volts by amp-hours, then divide by the tool watts. If a tool says amps instead of watts, multiply amps by pack volts first. A pack with twice the amp-hours stores twice the energy, which is also twice the energy to respect in a fire.

Charge only with the matched charger

Federal and university guidance agree on this point. The federal workplace safety agency tells workers to use chargers and batteries made or approved for the device, to take batteries off the charger when they are full, and to avoid ultra-fast chargers unless the maker lists them. A university safety office says to use only chargers approved by the manufacturer and never to build your own charging circuit. An extension article says the wrong charger can damage batteries and raise fire risk, and suggests charging on a hard, flat surface and putting larger tools outside and away from exits. The federal consumer product safety agency advises being present while charging and not charging overnight or while away, noting that many home battery fires happen at night while charging.

For the van and shop that means: one charger family per pack family, no universal or homemade chargers, charging on a bare non-combustible surface away from paper, insulation and cardboard, and unplugging when the light says full.

Heat in a summer van, cold on a winter roof

The federal workplace safety agency says heat is a major risk and that damage to lithium batteries can occur at high temperatures, such as above 130 degrees F, and that outside heat can speed up failure in a damaged or defective cell. It also says not to charge when the battery or its surroundings are below freezing (32 degrees F), because charging in the cold can cause lithium plating inside the cell, which raises the chance of failure. A university safety office adds that batteries should be kept in a dry, cool place with stable temperatures, away from direct sunlight.

  • Summer: do not leave packs in direct sun on the dash or on a hot roof deck. Keep them in a shaded bin and take them inside when the job ends. Let a hot pack cool before putting it on the charger.
  • Winter: a cold pack gives less run time, so keep spares in an inside pocket. Bring a cold pack indoors and let it warm up before charging it, since the federal workplace safety agency says not to charge below freezing.

Heat stress and cold stress for you are covered in heat stress in attics and on roofs and cold stress on winter roofs.

Signs of a damaged pack

Look at every pack before you use it. The federal workplace safety agency lists warning signs of bulging or cracking, hissing, leaking, rising temperature and smoking. A university safety office adds odor, odd noises and corrosion, and says to stop using a battery that bulges, swells or changes shape. An extension article says to stop using a device that becomes very hot, swells, smokes or gives off a strange smell. In plain terms:

  • A swollen, puffy or bulging case, or a case that no longer snaps in square.
  • A cracked, dented or melted case, or a pack that was dropped hard or crushed.
  • A pack that gets very hot in the tool or on the charger.
  • A sweet, chemical or burning smell, or any leaking or hissing.

Any of these means the pack comes out of service. Do not charge it, do not put it back in the tool, and keep it away from anything that burns.

If a pack overheats or smokes

These steps come from the guidance we read, and your employer's emergency plan comes first. The federal workplace safety agency says to remove a battery showing these signs from service and keep it away from flammable materials, and that the emergency plan should include lithium-specific steps based on manufacturer instructions. A university safety office says to move an overheating, hissing or bulging battery away from combustibles if it is safe to do so, to put it on a non-conductive surface or in a non-conductive container, and to call 911 if you feel in danger, pulling the fire alarm before trying to put out a fire and using an extinguisher only if trained. Do not carry a smoking pack through the house, and do not pick it up bare handed. Our own rule: when in doubt, get out, and let the fire department handle it. See fire extinguishers for HVAC techs if you want to review extinguisher types.

Storing and carrying packs

  • Store packs disconnected from tools, dry and cool, away from sunlight, away from other batteries and away from flammable material, as the university guidance says.
  • Keep terminals from touching metal. The federal environmental agency says to tape the terminals with non-conductive tape or put each battery in its own plastic bag. Do not toss loose packs in a bin with screws, wire nuts and snips.
  • Use the case or slot the pack came with, and keep the bin secured in the vehicle.
  • Do not stack damaged packs with good ones. The federal workplace safety agency says damaged batteries go in a fire-resistant container, such as a metal drum with sand or another extinguishing material.

Recycling and disposal

The federal environmental agency says lithium-ion batteries and devices that contain them should not go in household garbage or recycling bins. They go to a battery recycler, a take-back location, or a household hazardous waste collection point, and the federal environmental agency suggests that medium and large batteries go back to the maker, dealer or installer. For a damaged battery, the federal environmental agency says to contact the battery or device maker for handling information. Businesses that produce this waste should look at the federal universal waste rules, which send it to a permitted facility or recycler. The federal consumer product safety agency also says never to throw lithium batteries into the trash or general recycling. Ask your employer how your shop handles dead packs, and tape the terminals before they leave your hands.

Field card: Cordless tool batteries

Matched charger, cool and dry, and bad packs leave service.

  • Watt-hours = volts x amp-hours. Run time = watt-hours / tool watts.
  • Our example: 18 V x 5 Ah = 90 Wh; at 300 W that is 18 minutes ideal.
  • Charge only with the matched charger; unplug when full.
  • Do not charge below freezing; keep packs out of hot sun.
  • Swelling, heat, smell, hissing, leaking or a cracked case: stop using it.
  • Tape terminals or bag each pack; never put lithium-ion in the trash.
