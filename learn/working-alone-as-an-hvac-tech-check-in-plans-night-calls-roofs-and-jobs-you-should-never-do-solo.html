Working Alone as an HVAC Tech: Check-In Plans, Night Calls, Roofs and Jobs You Should Never Do Solo

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most service techs spend part of every week out of sight and out of voice range of anyone else: on a roof, in a basement mechanical room, under a house, or alone in a dark building at night. This lesson explains what the law does and does not say about working alone, then builds a simple check-in plan you can copy.

What the rules say

There is no single federal working-alone standard. A NIOSH bulletin on lone workers says the United States "does not have a comprehensive legal standard or rule addressing working alone," that neither NIOSH nor OSHA has an official definition of lone work, and that some specific rules restrict lone work in particular situations, such as confined spaces. Its working definition is "a potentially hazardous working condition that occurs when an individual cannot be seen or heard by another worker." The bulletin also says OSHA and NIOSH formed a partnership in 2023 to improve safety and health for lone workers.

That does not mean an employer has no duty. The General Duty Clause, Section 5(a)(1) of the OSH Act, requires an employer to furnish employment and a place of employment "free from recognized hazards" that are causing or are likely to cause death or serious physical harm. If a hazard of a lone job is recognized, such as a fall with no one to call for help, the employer cannot ignore it just because no standard names it. We are explaining the text, not giving a legal opinion on any case.

Specific standards still apply when you are alone. OSHA wrote in a 2001 interpretation about underground construction that none of that subpart's provisions depend on more than one employee being present, and that check-in and check-out rules matter just as much for one worker. That letter is about a different industry, but the idea carries over: a lone worker still has to meet every rule that covers the task.

States can differ. OSHA says State Plans are programs run by individual states or territories and must be at least as effective as federal OSHA, and in states without a plan federal OSHA covers most private employers. As an example of a state going further for one narrow group, Washington's labor department requires employers of hotel, motel, retail, security and janitorial isolated workers to provide a panic button. That rule does not cover HVAC techs. Check your own state plan or federal OSHA office for what applies where you work.

The text on confined spaces

The clearest "not alone" rule in our trade is the permit-required confined space standard, 29 CFR 1910.146 (general industry). Its permit space program must "provide at least one attendant outside the permit space into which entry is authorized for the duration of entry operations." The duties of attendants include that the attendant "remains outside the permit space during entry operations until relieved by another attendant." In plain terms: if a space needs a permit, a second person must be standing outside the whole time, so one tech cannot do the entry alone. The standard also requires procedures for summoning rescue. Our confined spaces lesson covers how to tell a permit space from other tight spots. Construction work falls under a separate standard, so ask which one applies.

Violence and the unknown site

A NIOSH fast fact card written for home healthcare workers (publication 2012-118, February 2012) is not about HVAC, but its advice fits any tech who visits strangers' property. It says to always let your employer know your location and when to expect you to report back, to be sure of the address and directions, to park in a well-lit area, to stay in the car and contact your manager if something seems threatening, and to schedule visits during daylight when possible. It advises using a cell phone to call your employer or 911 depending on severity, and to shorten the visit and leave if you cannot gain control of the situation. The card gives no required check-in interval or missed-check-in procedure, so those details are up to you and your company.

Building a check-in plan

Every plan has four parts. Who: one person who is awake, has a phone and knows they are your contact. Where: the street address, which door you will use, and which part of the building you will work in (roof, basement mechanical room, crawlspace). When: your expected finish time, written down before you start. What if: what that person does if you stop answering.

Interval is a judgment call. We suggest shorter gaps for riskier locations and longer ones for a clean ground-floor job, and we have no agency number to back a particular value. Our own rule of thumb: no more than about 45 minutes of silence on an ordinary call and about 20 minutes during a roof, crawlspace or tight-room task. Both numbers are ours.

Phone signal

Basements, steel-framed mechanical rooms and rooftop screen walls can drop a signal. Check your bars at the spot where you will work before you start, not after something goes wrong. If the room has no signal, tell your contact before you go in, shorten the interval, and send a message each time you come back up. Do not assume a text was delivered until you see a reply. A cell phone is not a rescue plan; see our ladder and roof fall lesson for equipment that keeps you from needing one.

Worked example (ours): 9 PM no-heat call, vacant commercial building

These times and the building are an invented scenario with assumed inputs: a 9:00 PM arrival, a two-hour job, a dispatcher as contact, and a key holder who is reachable.

  1. 8:40 PM. You text the dispatcher: street address, which side door you will use, that the building is vacant, a basement boiler room and a roof unit are in play, and a finish estimate of 11:00 PM.
  2. 9:00 PM. You park in a lit spot, look around, and text "arrived, going in." If anything feels wrong you stay in the truck and call.
  3. 9:45 PM. Check-in text from the boiler room doorway where you have signal: "basement, working on controls, next stop roof."
  4. 10:30 PM. Second check-in: "back down from roof, clearing, expect 11:00."
  5. 11:00 PM. "Done, leaving, home by 11:30." The contact stands down.

Arithmetic: 8:40 to 11:00 is 2 hours 20 minutes, or 140 minutes. Check-ins fall at minutes 20, 65 and 110 of that window, with gaps of 45 and 45 minutes between them and 30 minutes from the last check-in to the finish.

If the 10:30 check-in is missed: at 10:40 the contact calls and texts. At 10:45 they call the building's key holder or owner if they have one. At 10:50 they call 911 and give the address, entrance, areas of the building and when you last answered. Those steps are ours, not a standard. Write whatever escalation you pick before the shift, and make sure the contact has actually agreed to it.

Jobs a tech should not do alone

  • Permit-required confined space entry. Needs an attendant outside under 1910.146, as quoted above.
  • Energized electrical work where your employer's electrical safety program, built on NFPA 70E (named in plain text), calls for a second person. We did not find the exact wording of that requirement on a public government page, so check your company's written program. Our arc flash lesson explains labels and PPE.
  • Work at unprotected roof edges or high ladders without a plan for how someone would know you fell.
  • Anything where one person might be overcome, such as a refrigerant leak in a closed room. See our first-aid lesson.

Winter adds one more reason to keep time on your side. Cold slows your hands and judgment; see cold stress for techs for warm-up breaks.

Field card: working alone

Tell someone where, when, and what to do if you go quiet.

  • No single federal working-alone standard exists; the General Duty Clause and task standards still apply.
  • Permit confined space needs an attendant outside. Do not enter alone.
  • Send address, entrance, area of the building and finish time before you start.
  • Check signal where you will work. No signal means shorter, announced gaps.
  • Write the no-answer steps down and get your contact to agree.
  • Stay in the truck and call if the site feels wrong.
