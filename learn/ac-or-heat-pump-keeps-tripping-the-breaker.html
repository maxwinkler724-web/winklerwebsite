AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker: Causes and What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

It is a hot afternoon, the house is warming up, and the breaker for the outdoor unit has flipped again. A breaker is a safety device, so a repeat trip is a message, not a nuisance. This lesson explains what the message can mean, what a homeowner should and should not do, and how a technician finds the cause.

Two ways a breaker trips

A breaker opens the circuit for one of two broad reasons. An overload is too much current for too long: the equipment is working, but it is pulling more than normal. A short circuit or ground fault is current taking a path it should not, such as hot wire touching another conductor or the metal frame. An E-T-A white paper on breakers describes overloads as fairly modest, typically two to five times the breaker rating, and says a breaker should ride through brief ones such as a motor start-up surge but trip on a sustained one. A short circuit is limited only by the wiring itself, so the current is far higher. Thermal-magnetic breakers handle both: a heat-sensitive element that trips slowly on an overload, plus a magnetic element that trips almost at once on a high-current fault.

That gives a useful clue. A breaker that holds for a while and then trips, especially on the hottest part of the day, points toward overload. A breaker that slams off the moment the unit starts, or the moment you reset it, points toward a fault. Neither is a diagnosis, but both tell a technician where to begin.

Common HVAC causes

  • Dirty or blocked outdoor coil. ACHR News explains that a fouled condenser or a failed fan raises condensing pressure, the compressor works harder, and amp draw climbs. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Weak or failed capacitor. ACHR News reports that a defective run capacitor can make a compressor draw more current than normal and trip on its overload. See Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures.
  • Hard-starting or worn compressor. A compressor that cannot turn pulls very high locked-rotor current. See Compressor Hums and Won't Start rather than repeating it here.
  • Shorted or grounded motor. Insulation in a compressor or fan motor can break down and let current reach the shell or frame. That is a ground fault and often trips right away.
  • Loose or burnt connections. hvac.com lists loose connections and damaged wiring as causes of intermittent trips, and also worn or loosely connected breaker wiring. A loose lug heats up and can also fail the breaker connection itself.
  • Other load problems. The same hvac.com article lists a clogged filter, a frozen evaporator coil and low refrigerant as things that make the system draw more power.
  • Wrong or weak breaker. A breaker too small for the load will trip; one that is oversized hides problems and is dangerous (see below). An aging breaker can also trip below its rating, which is why a tech measures the actual current before blaming it.

What a homeowner should do

  1. Reset once, and only once. hvac.com says a single trip can be a temporary surge, so one reset is reasonable. If it trips again, stop.
  2. Leave it off and call a professional. ESFI lists repeated breaker trips among the signs of trouble and says to contact a qualified electrician. For an air conditioner or heat pump, a qualified HVAC technician is the right first call because the cause is often in the equipment, not the house wiring.
  3. Never tape, jam or upsize a breaker. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's guide to home wiring hazards says not to install a higher-amperage fuse or breaker, and ESFI calls an oversized fuse a dangerous fire hazard. The breaker is sized to protect the wire, not to be a nuisance.
  4. A burning smell, discolored switch or warm outlet means leave it off. ESFI lists these among electrical warning signs. Do not keep resetting; have it inspected before anyone turns it back on.
  5. Simple safe checks only. Replacing a dirty filter is fine. Do not open the unit's electrical panel or the main service panel. CPSC advises that panel changes and defects found there be handled by an electrician.

How a technician sorts it out

First comes safety: lock out and verify dead before opening anything, as covered in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, and use a properly rated meter (Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings). Then the usual order is:

  1. Read the nameplate. Find RLA, LRA, MCA and MOCP. Reading an HVAC Nameplate explains each one.
  2. Check voltage. ACHR News notes that most compressors tolerate about plus or minus 10 percent, so low or high supply voltage can cause overload trouble by itself.
  3. Measure running amps on the compressor and fan leads and compare them with RLA. Note the head pressure and outdoor temperature at the same time.
  4. Look at starting current. A hard-starting compressor draws locked-rotor current for longer than normal. A weak capacitor shows up here and in the running amps.
  5. Test insulation to ground with the unit de-energized and the motor leads disconnected. ACHR News describes a megohmmeter normally applying 500 V dc, with windings reading at least 100 megohms to ground, and warns never to megger a motor under vacuum. A podcast at hvacrschool.com adds that scroll compressors often read differently to ground, with about 0.5 megohm as a general concern level, so follow the compressor maker's limits.
  6. Check the breaker against the nameplate. Per a North Carolina Department of Insurance interpretation of NEC 440.6, the nameplate sets a minimum circuit ampacity and a maximum overcurrent device, and the device cannot exceed that maximum. A Lennox manual says the same thing in its tables: the MOP is the largest protective device allowed, and the breaker is sized from the MCA. If the load is normal and the breaker is within its rating, suspect the breaker or its connection.

Worked example: amps versus nameplate

A unit's compressor nameplate shows RLA 18 A. On a 95 degree afternoon with a matted condenser coil, a clamp meter on the compressor lead reads 23 A.

  1. Difference: 23 A minus 18 A = 5 A over.
  2. Percent over RLA: 5 A divided by 18 A = 0.278, or about 27.8 percent over.

The technician cleans the condenser and rechecks. Now the clamp reads 16 A.

  1. Difference: 16 A minus 18 A = minus 2 A.
  2. Percent: minus 2 A divided by 18 A = minus 0.111, about 11.1 percent below RLA.

The current dropped by 7 A (23 minus 16), about 30 percent of the high reading (7 divided by 23 = 0.304). Cleaning the coil lowered the head pressure and the amps, the pattern ACHR News describes. The numbers are an illustration, not a limit: RLA is a nameplate value, and actual current moves with voltage, outdoor temperature and pressures, so judge readings with the maker's data. Also note that a good breaker could still hold at 23 A on a circuit sized for the unit. Tripping often appears only when high amps combine with a hot day, a loose lug or an aging breaker, which is why the cause has to be measured rather than guessed.

Electrical work and licensing

Control wiring and line-voltage circuit work are not the same job in New Jersey. See NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers for what an HVACR contractor may and may not do, and call an electrical contractor when the problem is in the house wiring or panel.

Field card: AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker

Reset once. If it trips again, leave it off.

  • Overload: too much current for too long. Short or ground fault: current on a wrong path.
  • Common causes: dirty condenser, weak capacitor, hard-starting compressor, grounded motor, loose or burnt connections.
  • Homeowner: one reset only, then leave it off and call a pro. Never tape or upsize a breaker.
  • Burning smell, discolored switch or warm outlet: leave it off.
  • Tech: lock out, verify dead, read the nameplate, then measure volts and amps against RLA.
  • Megohm test to ground with leads off; follow the compressor maker's limits.
  • The breaker may never exceed the nameplate maximum overcurrent device.
  • Percent over RLA = (measured minus RLA) divided by RLA.
