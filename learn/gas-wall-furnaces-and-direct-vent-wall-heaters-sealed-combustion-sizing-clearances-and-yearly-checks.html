Gas Wall Furnaces and Direct-Vent Wall Heaters: Sealed Combustion, Sizing, Clearances and Yearly Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A gas wall heater is a small furnace that lives in the room it heats, with no ductwork. Some are sealed from the room and breathe through the outside wall. Others are older designs that use room air and a flue. This lesson explains the difference, how to size one, what to keep clear of it, and what to check every year.

How a direct-vent wall heater works

A direct-vent (sealed-combustion) appliance is built so the flame never uses room air. The U.S. Department of Energy describes it as having one sealed pipe that brings combustion air directly from outside to the combustion chamber and a second sealed pipe that carries exhaust gases outdoors. Wall heaters usually package those two paths as one fitting that goes straight through the exterior wall: a pipe inside a pipe, called a concentric or coaxial vent. Outside air flows in through the outer passage while flue gas flows out through the inner one. The exact vent parts and terminal location come from the maker's instructions, so follow them for the model in front of you.

The room is heated in one of two ways. A gravity-type wall heater relies on natural convection: warm air rises off the heat exchanger and cooler air flows in below. A fan-type unit adds a blower to move more air across the exchanger. DOE lists both types in its direct heating equipment standards and sets higher minimum efficiency for the fan type.

Older gravity wall furnaces and spillage risk

Many older wall furnaces take combustion air from the room and send flue gas up a B-vent (a double-wall metal flue) using only buoyancy. That is an atmospheric, or Category 1, appliance. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's building guide says such appliances draw air from the room and are susceptible to backdrafting, especially in airtight homes where exhaust fans or a fireplace can pull the house under negative pressure. It recommends that combustion appliances be direct vented or mechanically drafted.

Spillage means flue gas comes out of the draft opening into the room instead of going up the vent. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report notes that a spill lasting five minutes or more fails the draft test in the National Fuel Gas Code, and that a negative pressure reading alone can be misleading, because an undersized vent can draft and still spill. In its field tests, a wall furnace in Stockton, California did not spill at slight positive house pressure but spilled for the whole five-minute test at 9.0 pascals of depressurization. That is one test, not a rule of thumb, but it shows why exhaust fans, tight houses and blocked flues matter for these units.

If a CO alarm sounds or a flue looks blocked, damaged or disconnected, shut the unit off and have a qualified technician run a draft and combustion test. The combustion air lesson covers why tight rooms starve these appliances.

Vent-free heaters are a separate category

A vent-free (unvented) heater has no chimney or vent, so all its combustion products enter the room. The New York State Department of Health says these heaters should be professionally installed, should not be your main heat source even in a power failure, and that overusing one or choosing one that is too large can raise carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide in the air. The EPA's carbon monoxide fact sheet advises avoiding unvented fuel-burning space heaters indoors if possible. Rules on their use vary by state and city, so check local code or the fire official. DOE's direct heating equipment standards cover vented equipment only. Do not treat a vent-free unit as a substitute for a wall furnace; see the gas fireplace lesson for the same three vent types on the hearth side.

Sizing for a room

Sizing starts with the room's heat loss at design conditions, then picks an input rating whose output meets it. Output equals input times steady-state efficiency. A catalog label on a direct-vent unit gives input in Btu/hr, and DOE's federal minimums are annual (AFUE) figures, not steady-state, so use the label or the maker's data for the real number.

Worked example (our own example, with assumptions). A 400 sq ft room.
  1. Assumption: design-day heat loss of 25 Btu/hr per sq ft. This is our own round number, not a measured value; a real job uses a room-by-room load calculation.
  2. Heat needed: 400 sq ft × 25 Btu/hr per sq ft = 10,000 Btu/hr.
  3. Assumption: the unit has a steady-state efficiency of 75 percent (0.75).
  4. Required input: input = output ÷ efficiency = 10,000 ÷ 0.75 = 13,333 Btu/hr.
  5. Pick the next size up from the maker's list, for example a 14,000 Btu/hr input rating.
  6. Check: output = input × efficiency = 14,000 × 0.75 = 10,500 Btu/hr, which covers the 10,000 Btu/hr load with a small margin.
Another room, a drafty room or an open doorway to other rooms changes the answer. A unit far larger than the load cycles on and off and, for a vent-free heater, raises the pollutant risk the health department describes.

Clearances and furniture

The Washington State Patrol, which houses the state fire marshal, says to keep anything that can burn at least three feet from furnaces, fireplaces and space heaters. The New York health department likewise says to keep heaters away from furniture, rugs and curtains and to follow the manufacturer's placement guidance. The nameplate and manual give the minimum clearances to the sides, front and floor for that model; those numbers win over any rule of thumb. Keep the grilles clear so air can circulate.

CO alarms

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says to put a CO alarm outside bedrooms and on each level and to press the test button monthly. If it sounds, get outside and call the fire department. The EPA fact sheet adds that alarms should be at least 15 feet from fuel-burning appliances to limit nuisance trips, and should be interconnected if possible. An alarm is a backup, not a substitute for inspection. See carbon monoxide during winter outages for outage-specific hazards.

Yearly checks

  • Professional service once a year. The EPA and the New York health department both advise annual inspection of fuel-burning heaters by a qualified professional.
  • Pilot or igniter. The flame should light promptly and burn steadily. A pilot that will not stay lit points to a thermocouple or draft problem; see pilot light won't stay lit.
  • Burner and heat exchanger. Look for lint on burners, rust, soot and cracks.
  • Vent terminal. Make sure the concentric terminal outside is clear of snow, ice, leaves and shrubs. PNNL advises placing intake and exhaust terminals above the anticipated snow line in cold climates. For an older B-vent unit, the New York health department says to check the flue each fall for obstructions such as bird nests.
  • Room. Clear the 3 feet around the heater and the grilles.

When to replace

We did not find a government figure for the service life of a wall furnace, so we give no age. Practical triggers are a cracked heat exchanger, repeated flame or ignition failures, a failed draft or combustion test, heavy corrosion, or parts the maker no longer supplies. When an old gravity unit with a B-vent fails its tests, PNNL's guidance to replace atmospheric appliances with direct-vent equipment points to a sealed unit as the usual upgrade. Compare any replacement's efficiency against the DOE minimums: 75 percent AFUE for a fan-type wall furnace up to 42,000 Btu/hr input (76 percent above that), and 65 to 67 percent for gravity types depending on size.

Field card: Gas Wall Heaters

Sealed means outside air in, flue out, no room air in the flame.

  • Direct vent: concentric pipe, outside air in, flue out; no backdraft path.
  • Gravity wall furnace with B-vent uses room air and can spill.
  • Vent-free: all exhaust enters the room; not a main heat source.
  • Input × efficiency = output. Our example: 14,000 × 0.75 = 10,500 Btu/hr.
  • Keep burnables 3 ft away; label clearances win.
  • CO alarm on each level and outside bedrooms; test monthly.
  • Yearly: pilot or igniter, burner, vent terminal clear of snow and shrubs.
