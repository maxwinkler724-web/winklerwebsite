Vacuum Pump Care: Oil Changes, Gas Ballast and Micron Gauge Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A vacuum pump can only pull as deep as its oil and seals allow. If your system will not drop below a few hundred microns, the pump is one of the first things to rule out. This lesson covers what the makers say about oil, the gas ballast valve, and a quick blank-off test that tells you whether the pump and gauge are healthy before you blame the system.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. Oil types, fill amounts, ballast settings and test limits differ between models. The numbers below come from the manuals named in the Sources list and are examples. Your pump's manual wins.

Why oil condition sets the depth

A two-stage rotary vane pump seals its vanes and chambers with a thin film of oil. During evacuation, water vapor and other contaminants are pulled out of the system and end up in that oil. JB Industries' pump-care guide says moisture and acids collect in the oil and, if left in the pump, abrade and corrode its internal surfaces. It also says a deep vacuum needs clean, moisture-free oil all the way through the job, and that even oil a teaspoon low can affect the ultimate vacuum. Milky oil is the visible sign of water. Yellow Jacket's manual says that a pump that will not pull and hold a good vacuum should be run hot and given fresh oil.

How often to change the oil

The makers agree on the habit, not on one fixed interval. None of the manuals read gives an hours-based interval.

  • Robinair (models 15401, 15601, 15605) recommends changing the oil after each use.
  • Yellow Jacket (SuperEvac) says to change oil after each usage.
  • JB Industries says pump oil should be changed after each use, with extra changes on large or heavily contaminated jobs.
  • NAVAC (NP4DP and NP7DP) recommends changing it just before evacuating each system, and changing it promptly if it looks opaque, dirty or full of moisture.
  • Fieldpiece (VP67, VP87, VPX7) says to change as needed during the job, to start with fresh oil and to drain it after every job. Its pumps have a built-in oil change system.

The short version for a new tech: start each job with clean oil, look at the sight glass during the job, and change it when it turns cloudy. Use the oil the maker names. Yellow Jacket's manual says the wrong oil type voids the warranty. For how refrigerant oils differ from pump oil, see Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE.

An oil change, in general steps

The exact method depends on the pump. Common points from the manuals:

  1. Warm the oil so it flows. NAVAC says to run the pump about one minute and not longer. JB Industries also says to drain while warm and thin.
  2. Drain into a container you can seal, and dispose of used oil under local rules (Yellow Jacket's manual says to do so). Tilting the pump drains the rest.
  3. Refill to the level mark on the sight glass. Robinair says to run the pump for a minute with the inlets capped, then top up to the level line while it runs. Yellow Jacket says to check the level between the marks while running.
  4. Fieldpiece's RunQuick system drains and refills with the pump running, but its manual warns that the pump holds vacuum only about 30 seconds after draining. Running longer without refilling can exceed temperature limits and void the warranty.

Many makers allow flushing a dirty pump with small amounts of new oil. Robinair and Yellow Jacket both describe it.

The gas ballast valve

Our own explanation: a gas ballast lets a little air into the pump's compression stage so water vapor is less likely to condense in the oil. Check your pump's manual for how its ballast works. That helps on a wet pulldown and keeps oil from going milky, but the extra air limits how deep the pump can pull. So the usual pattern is open early, close for depth.

  • Robinair: open while pulling down until the reading is roughly 1,000 to 3,000 microns, then close it so the pump can reach its ultimate vacuum.
  • Yellow Jacket: open one full turn after evacuation starts, close at 1,000 to 2,000 microns, and keep it closed when measuring ultimate vacuum.
  • NAVAC: open at the start, then tighten once the gauge reaches 2,000 microns.
  • Fieldpiece: open for the initial pulldown above about 3,000 microns on wet systems, and close at about 2,000 to 3,000 microns.

Notice the closing points differ, from 1,000 to 3,000 microns. Follow your pump's manual. JB Industries adds that leaving the ballast open hurts performance, and that at shutdown you can open it fully and run the pump a few seconds to break the vacuum.

The blank-off test

A blank-off test asks one question: can the pump, by itself, reach its rated vacuum? You take the system out of the picture so any bad number must come from the pump, its oil, or the gauge.

Fieldpiece's manual gives a clear recipe: connect the vacuum gauge directly to one pump port, seal the other ports, make sure the gas ballast is closed, and switch the pump on. The gauge should read below 200 microns within one minute. If it does not, check the gauge, the pump, or both. Yellow Jacket's manual describes the same idea: gauge on the inlet fitting, intake capped, ballast closed, and the gauge shows the pump's ultimate vacuum. If the pump will not pull and hold a good vacuum, it says to run it until hot and change the oil. Robinair's manual does not describe a separate blank-off test, and NAVAC's says only that any leak makes the deep vacuum impossible, so for those pumps use the pump's rating and a capped inlet as your check.

Why connect the gauge directly? JB Industries tested this: a direct connection to the intake reached 20 microns, while a new charging line on the same pump read 297 microns. Hoses and fittings add their own load, which is why a hose or core can hide a good pump. How hose size and valve core removal change pulldown speed on a real system is covered in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics, so it is not repeated here. JB Industries also states gauge accuracy as roughly 10 percent between 250 and 6,000 microns and 15 percent between 50 and 250 microns, so do not chase small differences near the rating.

Worked example: blank-off, then a decay test

Part 1, blank-off. A Fieldpiece VP87 is rated at 15 microns ultimate vacuum at its input ports. The same manual's blank-off check calls for less than 200 microns within 1 minute. A tech caps three ports, closes the ballast, and puts the gauge on the fourth port.

  • Reading after 1 minute: 85 microns.
  • Compare with the limit: 85 < 200, so it passes.
  • Compare with the rating: 85 is 70 microns above the 15 rating (85 − 15 = 70). That is fine for a one-minute check, and it should creep lower with more run time. Gauge accuracy of about 15 percent at this range also blurs small differences.

If it had read 350 microns, 350 is above 200, so it fails. The next steps are to fresh-oil the pump, run it warm, check for a loose cap or dirty gauge, and test again.

Part 2, decay test on a system. This is a different test with a different pass line. A New York state clean heat commissioning checklist for heat pump installs says to evacuate to 500 microns or the manufacturer's target, hold vacuum at least 15 minutes (or per manufacturer), and investigate if the level rises by more than 200 microns. That is a program form, not a pump maker's spec, and an equipment manufacturer's value overrides it. Say the tech isolates the system at 450 microns with the gauge at the system.

  • Reading at 15 minutes: 610 microns.
  • Rise: 610 − 450 = 160 microns.
  • Compare with the limit: 160 < 200, so it passes.

If the reading had been 780, the rise would be 780 − 450 = 330 microns, which is over 200, so it needs investigation. A steady climb points toward a leak, and a rise that levels off points toward moisture, as covered in the evacuation lesson. Both tests need the pump proven good first, which is the reason to run the blank-off test.

Storing the pump

Fieldpiece says to empty or replace oil at the end of every job, store the pump empty with the ports capped in a dry, clean place, and keep oil sealed until use. NAVAC says to store indoors, away from rain, with the air entry and exhaust caps closed tightly. Yellow Jacket says to cap the intake to keep out contaminants. Oil left in a pump holds moisture, so draining or changing it before storage protects the pump.

Safety

  • Never connect a pump to a pressurized system. NAVAC says so in capitals, and Robinair says to recover refrigerant first because pulling at high pressure can damage the pump.
  • Check what the pump is rated for. Fieldpiece and Robinair exclude flammable refrigerants, and NAVAC does not allow A3 or flammable refrigerants. Some newer models are marked for A2L, so read the label.
  • Do not expose skin to the vacuum port. Yellow Jacket warns the deep vacuum can harm human tissue.
  • Pump housing, motor and oil can be hot. Let it cool or wear gloves.
  • Do not run the pump with the exhaust blocked. Remove any shipping cap first. NAVAC limits running with the intake open to air to 3 minutes.
  • Wear goggles, ground the pump, keep it dry, and do not connect or disconnect it while energized.
  • Check the start temperature. NAVAC lists 30 to 104 F, and Yellow Jacket says the pump and oil must be at least 30 F to start.

Field card: Vacuum Pump Care

Prove the pump, then blame the system.

  • Fresh oil at the start of the job. Change it when it looks milky or dirty.
  • Use the maker's oil and fill to the sight-glass mark.
  • Gas ballast: open for wet pulldown, close at about 1,000 to 3,000 microns per your manual.
  • Blank-off: gauge on a pump port, other ports capped, ballast closed. Fieldpiece wants under 200 microns in 1 minute.
  • Decay example: hold 15 minutes, investigate a rise over 200 microns (NY form), or use the maker's value.
  • Drain, cap and store dry. Never connect to a pressurized system.
