Dissolved Air in Hot-Water Heating and Henry's Law

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Cold tap water is full of dissolved air. Heat it and drop its pressure, and that air comes out as bubbles. This lesson explains the physics behind that (Henry's law), puts real numbers on it for a 50 gallon fill, and shows why the boiler outlet is where the bubbles show up first.

Henry's law in plain words

Henry's law says that at a fixed temperature, the amount of a gas dissolved in water is proportional to that gas's pressure above the water. Double the pressure and the water can hold about twice as much. A standard reference paper on gases in water defines the Henry's constant, kH, as the gas's fugacity (think of it as its effective pressure) divided by its mole fraction in the water, in the dilute limit. A big kH means the gas is not very soluble. The temperature matters just as much, because kH is not fixed. The NIST WebBook lists Henry's constants for oxygen and nitrogen with a temperature factor, so the same gas dissolves differently at 50 F and at 180 F.

What cold fill water holds

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and both dissolve in water. Fill water that has sat in contact with air at room pressure is close to saturated with both. For a worked number, see the example below: 50 F water saturated at 14.7 psia holds about 11.3 mg/L of oxygen and 18.1 mg/L of nitrogen, about 29.4 mg/L in all (our own calculation). That sounds tiny, and it is. A 50 gallon system is about 189 liters, though, so those milligrams add up to about 5.6 grams of dissolved gas looking for somewhere to go. The U.S. Geological Survey publishes dissolved oxygen solubility tables built on the same temperature and pressure dependence, but its method is valid only from 0 to 40 C (32 to 104 F), so it stops short of boiler temperatures. We needed a source that reaches hot water, and used the Henry's constants in a NIST-hosted paper (Fernandez-Prini, Alvarez and Harvey, 2003), whose fit covers about 274 to 617 K.

Why heat pushes air out

For oxygen and nitrogen in water, kH rises as the water warms. The paper's table gives oxygen about 4,490 MPa at 300 K (27 C, 80 F) and about 6,575 MPa at 400 K (127 C, 260 F); nitrogen goes from about 8,770 to about 10,490 MPa. A bigger kH means less gas in solution at the same pressure, and that trend continues across the whole range a hot-water heating system uses. Our own evaluation of the paper's equation puts oxygen at about 3,290 MPa at 50 F and 6,940 MPa at 180 F. Hot water is a poor place to keep air. There is a second effect inside the equation: as water heats, its own vapor pressure takes up more of the total pressure, leaving less pressure for the dissolved gases. At 180 F, we calculate water vapor takes about 51.8 kPa (7.5 psi) of the pressure.

Why low pressure pushes air out

The other half of Henry's law is pressure. A system at 30 psia (about 15 psig at sea level, our assumed fill) can hold roughly twice the gas of water at 14.7 psia, at the same temperature. Anywhere the pressure falls, such as the suction side of a circulator, the water is suddenly holding more gas than it can keep, and the extra comes out. See Pumping Away for why the pump placement controls which part of the loop sees the lowest pressure.

Where the microbubbles appear

Put the two effects together: gas comes out of solution where the water is hottest and the pressure is lowest. In most boiler loops, the hottest water leaves the boiler and the lowest pressure is near the circulator suction, so the boiler outlet and the pipe just after it are the usual place to look. In our reading, bubbles also tend to start on the hottest surfaces, since the water film against a heat exchanger wall is hotter than the bulk water. That is why a microbubble air separator does its best work at the boiler outlet: the gas is already out of solution and in bubble form there, so there is something to catch. Farther around the loop, the water cools, can take gas back in, and the bubbles that are left shrink or dissolve. The hardware types and the dirt side are covered in Air Elimination and Dirt Separation, so we do not repeat them here.

Why a system keeps making air

  • The first heat-up after a fill. Everything the fill water was carrying gets one chance to come out as it warms. That is normal.
  • Fresh makeup water. A tight closed loop should need almost no new water. Each gallon of new water brings its dissolved gas with it (about 0.02 g released per gallon in our example). A leak, a weeping relief valve, or a fill valve that keeps opening repeats the first heat-up again and again. Check Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure and look for the leak before you buy another vent.
  • Pressure problems. A federal technical bulletin on closed loops says oxygen can enter through aerated water, pressurization imbalances or non-barrier pipes, and that proper pressurization and limited flushing are key to preventing air ingress.
  • Draining and opening the system. Every repair that drains part of the loop lets in air and fresh water.

The customer symptom is usually a cold radiator, gurgling or a noisy pump. See Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold for the service side.

Oxygen is the corrosion problem

Nitrogen is inert in the loop. Oxygen is not. The same NIH bulletin series says dissolved oxygen drives corrosion in closed-loop systems, that excess oxygen accelerates oxidation, and that corrosion can produce iron oxide sludge. Its second bulletin says oxygen in makeup water can cause flash rusting on carbon steel. The first bulletin also gives a rule of thumb that a 10 C (18 F) rise in temperature doubles the reaction rate, so hot boiler water is a harsher place for oxygen than cold water. In our reading, the oxygen that reacts with iron is used up and the nitrogen is left to build up as bubbles. We do not teach dosing or inhibitors here; see Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics for how steam plants deal with oxygen.

Our own worked example: 50 gallons from 50 F to 180 F

This is our own example, not a manufacturer or code figure. The constants come from the cited paper; the rest are assumptions we chose.

Assumptions (ours): 50 U.S. gallons (189.27 liters) of fill water at 50 F (10 C), saturated with air at 14.7 psia (101.325 kPa) and then sealed in. The system runs at 180 F (82.2 C) and 30 psia (206.84 kPa), and nothing else is added or lost. Air is taken as 20.95 percent oxygen and 78.08 percent nitrogen of the dry gas, and the argon and other trace gases are ignored. Gas fugacity is treated as equal to its pressure. The pressure correction is our own: we subtract water vapor pressure from the total, about 1.23 kPa at 50 F and 51.8 kPa at 180 F, from a standard steam correlation we calculated ourselves.

  1. Gas pressure. Fill: 101.325 − 1.23 = 100.10 kPa, so oxygen is 20.97 kPa and nitrogen 78.16 kPa. Hot: 206.84 − 51.85 = 155.00 kPa, so oxygen is 32.47 kPa and nitrogen 121.02 kPa.
  2. Henry's constants (our evaluation of the cited equation): oxygen 3,291 MPa at 50 F and 6,937 MPa at 180 F; nitrogen 6,708 MPa and 12,003 MPa.
  3. Mole fraction = pressure divided by kH. Fill: oxygen 20.97 ÷ 3,291,366 = 6.37 × 10-6, nitrogen 78.16 ÷ 6,708,407 = 1.165 × 10-5. Hot: oxygen 32.47 ÷ 6,936,879 = 4.68 × 10-6, nitrogen 121.02 ÷ 12,002,930 = 1.008 × 10-5.
  4. Convert to mg/L. Water at 50 F is about 55.49 mol/L (our rounding, from a density of 0.9997 kg/L), and oxygen is 31.998 g/mol, nitrogen 28.014 g/mol. Fill: oxygen 11.31 mg/L, nitrogen 18.11 mg/L, total about 29.4 mg/L. Hot: oxygen 8.31 mg/L, nitrogen 15.67 mg/L, total about 24.0 mg/L.
  5. Release. 29.4 − 24.0 = about 5.4 mg/L (oxygen 3.00, nitrogen 2.44). For 189.27 liters that is about 1.03 g of gas, or 0.0342 mol. At 14.7 psia and 68 F that gas takes about 0.82 liter, or 0.22 gallon, which is about 0.43 gallon per 100 gallons of water.

Read the result. About 52 percent of the released gas is oxygen (0.0178 of 0.0342 mol), against 21 percent in air, because oxygen's kH rises faster with temperature than nitrogen's (a factor of 2.11 against 1.79 between 50 F and 180 F, our calculation), so a larger share of the oxygen leaves. The release is small because the system pressure rose from 14.7 to 30 psia and kept more of the gas in. If instead the fill water had been saturated at 30 psia (an assumption we make only to show the size of the effect), the same heating would release about 36.5 mg/L, about 5.6 liters or 1.5 gallons for the 50 gallons. And if a low-pressure spot dropped the 180 F water to 14.7 psia, the equation gives only 7.7 mg/L capacity, so the 24 mg/L it was holding would be far over the limit. Real systems differ, since supply water may be under- or over-saturated, but the direction is always the same.

Field card: Dissolved Air and Henry's Law

Hot and low pressure push gas out; the boiler outlet is where it shows.

  • Henry's law: gas held in water is proportional to that gas's pressure at a fixed temperature.
  • Oxygen and nitrogen hold less as water heats and as pressure falls.
  • Our example: 50 gal from 50 F to 180 F at 30 psia releases about 5.4 mg/L, about 1 g of gas.
  • Bubbles come out first at the hottest, lowest-pressure spot, usually at or just after the boiler outlet.
  • New makeup water brings new gas; hunt the leak or constant refilling.
  • Oxygen drives corrosion of steel; nitrogen just collects as bubbles.
