Converting Steam Heat to Hot Water: Radiator Output, Pressure Testing and What Changes

Last updated: 2026-10-28

Many older houses still have cast iron steam radiators, and a worn-out steam boiler raises the question: can we keep the radiators and feed them hot water instead? Sometimes yes. This lesson covers what the radiators give up, which ones can be reused, how to leak-test before filling, what comes out of the basement piping, and when we would leave the steam system alone.

Check before you plan. The sources here are trade articles and one university guideline, and they disagree on some details. Follow the new boiler maker's instructions, local code and a heat loss calculation. This is general education, not a design.

The radiators lose output

Steam gives off its heat when it condenses, and the radiator surface sits near the steam temperature. A trade article by a steam specialist puts 1 psi steam at about 215°F and says one square foot of EDR (equivalent direct radiation) gives about 240 Btu/hr on steam but only about 150 Btu/hr on hot water. A 2023 boiler-industry column says the same pair of numbers, which makes hot water output 62.5 percent of steam (150 ÷ 240). For the EDR basics see Steam Radiator EDR.

The hot water number depends on the water temperature, so we need a table, not one number. A distributor's 2014 cast iron radiator sheet lists output per square foot by average water temperature:

Notice a mismatch. The two trade articles pair the 150 figure with water of about 180°F, while the chart gives 150 at 170°F and 170 at 180°F. Treat any single figure as an estimate and use one table consistently. The same chart says a house heated entirely by cast iron radiators generally does not need supply water above 140°F (90 Btu/hr per sq ft). That only holds if the radiators are oversized for the rooms, which is why the room check below matters. A university facilities guideline agrees on direction and gives no single percentage.

Can one-pipe steam radiators be used?

A one-pipe steam radiator has one tapping at the bottom, where steam enters and condensate leaves, and an air vent near the top of the end section. Hot water needs a flow path, so it needs supply and return connections. A boiler-industry column says it is possible but "not practical," because it means drilling and tapping an outlet on the opposite side of every radiator and running return piping. The university guideline lists the requirements for any reused radiator: top and bottom connections, two-pipe design (not a pipe-in-pipe type), enough capacity, and strength for the new static pressure.

Column radiators with no top push nipples between sections are a particular problem. Those two articles say that without them, water has trouble moving through the sections and conversion is neither easy nor practical. Radiators that do have them can be tapped at the top of the end section for a new air vent, after the old steam vent hole is plugged. The steam radiator valve has to be replaced with a hot water valve, according to the steam specialist, and traps on two-pipe radiators come out or get gutted.

Pressure test before you fill

Steam runs at only a few psi, so small weeps never show. Hot water adds static pressure from the height of the house. The trade articles give 12 psi at least for a two-story system, about 18 psi for three stories, and 15 to 20 psi as a general range. Two trade articles suggest a practical first step: raise the steam boiler to roughly 10 to 12 psi and look through the house for leaking steam or condensate before any water goes in.

The university guideline treats the water test as a written procedure. It says to test reused piping with water to at least the circulator's deadhead pressure, to state the test pressure, fluid, duration and test section in advance, and to plan for leaks. It gives no number for the test pressure, and the sources give no rated working pressure for old radiators, so we do not offer one here. Ask the radiator and boiler makers, or an engineer. One boiler-supplier article fills the boiler and piping until a 30 psi relief valve opens, then checks for leaks.

Near-boiler piping and the steam piping

If the boiler is replaced, the steam near-boiler piping goes with it, and the new boiler is piped the way hot water boilers normally are. Two trade articles list the steam trim to remove: gauge glass, low-water cutoff, pressure control and old near-boiler piping. See Hartford Loop and Near-Boiler Piping for what that piping did on steam. Return piping that sits below the old water line should come out too, so sludge does not reach the new boiler and circulators, and flushing is not a substitute.

Pipe reuse is a judgment call. The university guideline allows reusing two-pipe condensate lines if they are large enough, in good condition and suited to the new design, and warns they are often smaller than the steam supply and can corrode faster. The boiler-industry column warns that steam condensate pipes carry little volume, so the main return may need upsizing to carry full water flow. A boiler-supplier article goes further and says all old steam piping should be replaced, while an engineering firm lists good existing piping as a reason to stay on steam.

Expansion tank and air removal

A closed hot water loop needs an expansion tank and a fill pressure, covered in Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure. Two trade articles say to size the tank from the total water volume, including the radiators and old steam piping, because that is larger than people expect. The steam specialist gives 0.045 gal per foot for 1 inch pipe and 0.17 for 2 inch. Use the tank maker's sizing method.

Steam systems vent air through radiator vents or traps. Hot water has no such outlet, so an air separator is needed, and a high-point vent on each radiator. The steam specialist adds that old column radiators with no top connections can rely on the water slowly absorbing trapped air, which may take months.

Worked example: one room, our own numbers

This is our own example. A room has radiation of 60 sq ft EDR. We assume the room's design heat loss is 8,000 Btu/hr; this is an assumed input, and a real job needs a room-by-room calculation. We use the distributor chart values above.

  1. On steam at 240 Btu/hr per sq ft: 60 × 240 = 14,400 Btu/hr. That is 80 percent more than the load (14,400 ÷ 8,000 = 1.8), which is why the room is probably fine on steam.
  2. On hot water at 170°F average: 60 × 150 = 9,000 Btu/hr, 62.5 percent of steam. Against the 8,000 load, the margin is 1,000 Btu/hr (about 12 percent).
  3. On hot water at 140°F average: 60 × 90 = 5,400 Btu/hr, 37.5 percent of steam. That covers only 67.5 percent of the load (5,400 ÷ 8,000), so the room runs cold.
  4. Water temperature needed: 8,000 ÷ 60 = 133 Btu/hr per sq ft. The chart gives 130 at 160°F and 150 at 170°F, so by straight-line interpolation the room needs about 160 + (3.3 ÷ 20) × 10, or roughly 162°F average water.

The same radiator can be oversized on steam and just adequate at 170°F. If the coldest room needs 162°F, the whole zone has to run that hot, which limits efficiency. The two trade articles pair 150 with 180°F water instead. If the true 150 point is at 180°F, then 170°F water is worth only about 130 per sq ft (a guess shifted by one 10°F chart step), 60 × 130 = 7,800 Btu/hr, and the margin disappears. Do this check for every room before committing.

When it is not worth it

  • The radiators cannot meet the load. The steam specialist says conversion is a poor choice if existing radiation cannot meet the heat loss on the coldest day, so run the room checks first. See Sizing a Replacement Hot Water Boiler.
  • Wide column or pipe-in-pipe radiators with no top connections, or lots of one-pipe radiators that each need new tapping and return piping.
  • Steam piping and radiators that work. An engineering firm says a complete conversion is not always the best solution and that savings are not automatic, especially when it means heavy demolition or a tight budget. One boiler-supplier article advises against it when only the boiler needs replacing in a period home.

On the other side, a 2019 report on New York steam systems found fuel savings of 40 to 50 percent in some conversions, while noting costs vary with piping access.

Field card: Converting Steam Heat to Hot Water

Fewer Btu per square foot, more places to leak.

  • Steam EDR is 240 Btu/hr per sq ft; on hot water it is about 150 (chart: 150 at 170°F, 90 at 140°F).
  • Check room by room: radiator output at the planned water temperature against the room heat loss.
  • One-pipe radiators need a return tapping; column types with no top nipples are poor candidates.
  • Leak-test first and get the test pressure from the makers or an engineer.
  • Remove steam trim and near-boiler piping; replace the radiator valves and gut the traps.
  • Size the expansion tank on total water volume; add an air separator and radiator vents.
  • If the radiators fall short or the steam system works, leave it steam.
