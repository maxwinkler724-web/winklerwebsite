Control Valve Flow Characteristics on Water Coils: Equal Percentage, Linear and Quick Opening

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Two valves can have the same size and the same actuator and still control a coil very differently. The difference is the shape of the curve that links stem position to water flow. This lesson explains the three common shapes, why a water coil pairs well with one of them, and what a tech sees when the valve is too big.

Inherent characteristic: the valve on a test bench

The inherent characteristic is how flow changes with stroke when the pressure drop across the valve is held constant. University lecture notes on HVAC controls describe three common shapes. A quick-opening valve has a flat disc plug, so flow jumps up as soon as the plug lifts off the seat; the notes say this suits on/off service. A linear valve gives flow proportional to stem position. An equal-percentage valve starts with small flow and each equal step of stroke raises flow by the same percentage of the flow it already had. The same notes give the relation as maximum flow times R raised to the power (stroke fraction minus 1), where R is the rangeability. A process-control text from another university gives R as usually 20 to 50 for equal-percentage trim.

Why the coil matters

A coil does not turn flow into heat in a straight line. The lecture notes describe a heating coil as having a convex curve: output rises quickly at low flow, then flattens as flow keeps increasing. Hot water at low flow stays in the coil longer, so it gives up almost all of its available temperature drop, and doubling a small flow nearly doubles output. At high flow the water leaves still hot and extra flow adds little. The notes say a cooling coil is closer to linear, because of its smaller water temperature change, and so cooling coils may want a different valve shape than heating coils.

An equal-percentage valve is concave: small flow change per inch of stroke at the bottom, large change at the top. Put a convex coil in series with a concave valve and the two offset, and the notes say the result is an approximately linear signal-to-output relationship. That is the reason this shape is the usual pick for two-way valves on heating coils. A linear valve on the same coil gives too much heat for the first bit of stroke, and a quick-opening valve is worse, which pushes the loop toward on/off behavior.

Rangeability and turndown

Rangeability is the ratio of full flow to the smallest flow the valve can still control, measured on a bench at constant pressure drop. In the lecture notes' example, 100 gpm at full flow and 2 gpm at the minimum gives 50 to 1. Turndown is the same idea for the installed valve, and the notes say it is always smaller than rangeability and is reduced by oversizing. They give turndown as rangeability times the square root of valve authority, and recommend authority above 0.5. Authority itself, along with two-way and three-way selection, is covered in the lesson on two-way versus three-way valves, so we do not repeat it here.

Installed versus inherent

On a real system the pressure across the valve is not constant. As the valve closes, the pipe and coil lose less pressure and the valve takes a bigger share, so flow at low stroke is higher than the inherent curve predicts. The process-control text recommends sizing so the valve takes a suitable share of the total drop (it gives 25 to 33 percent as an example) and then checking that the installed curve is reasonably linear near design flow. The practical lesson: an equal-percentage valve with poor authority starts to behave more like a linear one, and a linear one starts to behave like quick opening. How flow is held steady against pressure swings is covered in the balancing and pressure-independent valve lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume an equal-percentage valve with rangeability R = 50, and read it from its inherent curve only (constant pressure drop, authority ignored).

  1. At 50 percent stroke the flow fraction is R^(0.5 minus 1) = 50^(-0.5) = 1 divided by 7.07, about 0.14 of full flow.
  2. A linear valve at 50 percent stroke passes 0.50 of full flow, more than three times as much.
  3. Now assume the valve is oversized, so its full-open flow is four times the coil's design flow. Design flow is then 0.25 of the valve's maximum, which needs stroke = 1 + ln(0.25) / ln(50) = 1 minus 1.386 / 3.912 = about 0.65, or 65 percent open.
  4. Assume the coil load falls to 10 percent of design, so the coil needs 0.10 times 0.25 = 0.025 of the valve's maximum flow. Stroke = 1 + ln(0.025) / ln(50) = 1 minus 3.689 / 3.912 = about 0.057, or 6 percent open. A linear valve would need 2.5 percent.
  5. The smallest controllable flow for R = 50 is 1/50 = 0.02 of maximum. Our 0.025 is barely above that floor.

So an oversized valve at light load is asked to work in the bottom few percent of stroke, where it is least precise. Real hardware makes it worse: one national laboratory test found a valve whose command signals below 25 percent produced no measurable response, which is a dead zone right where the oversized valve wants to operate.

What oversizing looks like in the field

The lecture notes put it this way: when a valve is too large, the maximum required flow is already supplied while the valve is only partly open, so a small stem move causes a large change in heat, especially at low and average load. The controller sees a big temperature swing, backs the valve off, overshoots the other way, and repeats. This is hunting. A national laboratory report defines a hunting actuator as one that oscillates around the position it is seeking, and ties it to poorly tuned control constants; the same report lists a wrongly sized reheat valve as a fault category. A university test of coil valves found hysteresis and nonlinearity, and noted that with a conventional controller these can give severe temperature oscillation and control chattering.

The tech sees these symptoms:

  • The actuator or the valve position reading on the building controls swings back and forth, often between about 5 and 15 percent open, while the load is light. (That range is our own field observation, not a published figure.)
  • Discharge air temperature or space temperature oscillates in a regular pattern around the setpoint.
  • Actuator wear and noise: the motor runs nearly all the time making tiny moves.
  • Trend graphs show a sawtooth in valve position that gets worse as the load drops.

Checks and fixes to consider

Before blaming the valve, look at the loop. Poor tuning, a noisy or slow sensor and a sticking linkage all cause hunting. If the trend is clean and the valve is simply working in the bottom 15 percent of its stroke, ask the designer or controls contractor whether a smaller trim, a two-valve split, or a wider-range arrangement fits. Check the real flow against the design flow, since a coil that is oversized or running at low water temperature difference can look like a valve problem. A national laboratory chilled-water plant report calls low temperature difference an undesirable condition that afflicts many chilled-water plants, so watch it alongside valve position.

Field card: Control Valve Flow Characteristics on Water Coils

Heating coil is convex, equal-percentage valve is concave, and together they come out nearly straight.

  • Quick opening: big flow right away, for on/off. Linear: flow follows stroke. Equal percentage: small flow at the bottom, each step adds the same percent.
  • Equal-percentage flow = R raised to (stroke minus 1). With R = 50, half stroke is about 0.14 of full flow; linear is 0.50.
  • Rangeability is the bench ratio; installed turndown is smaller, and oversizing shrinks it.
  • Installed curves differ from inherent curves because pressure across the valve changes as it closes.
  • Oversized valve at light load: hunting, with valve position swinging near closed.
  • Check tuning, sensors and linkage before replacing the valve.
