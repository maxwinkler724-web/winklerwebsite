HFC Leak Repair and Reclaim Rules for Field Techs (40 CFR Part 84, Subpart C)

Last updated: 2026-11-04

For years, the federal leak repair rules for refrigerant appliances applied to ozone-depleting refrigerants only. A newer federal rule written under the AIM Act reaches HFC and many substitute refrigerants too, starting with systems that hold 15 lb or more. This lesson explains what that means at the unit, what a tech has to do or hand off, and how it differs from the older rule you already know from EPA 608 study.

Check the current text first

Check the current text. Dates, thresholds and exemptions in this area phase in and have been revised. This page is general education built from the federal eCFR as we read it in 2026. It is not legal advice. Before you rely on any number or date below, open the current eCFR text for 40 CFR Part 84, subpart C [1], and for Part 82, subpart F [2], and check the federal environmental agency's pages for updates.

Two rules, two sets of refrigerants

The older rule is in 40 CFR Part 82, subpart F, section 82.157. Since April 10, 2020, it covers appliances with a full charge of 50 lb or more of an ozone-depleting refrigerant, and the eCFR text says it does not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants [2]. That is why a 60 lb system running only an HFC was outside it.

The newer rule is in 40 CFR Part 84, subpart C, section 84.106. The eCFR text applies it from January 1, 2026 to appliances with a full charge of 15 lb or more that hold a regulated HFC, or a substitute refrigerant with a global warming potential above 53 [1]. It excludes appliances containing solely an ozone-depleting substance (Part 82 still handles those) and excludes residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump appliances [1]. So the two rules sit side by side: one by refrigerant family and a 50 lb line, the other by a 15 lb line and a GWP line. Note the exclusion carefully. Whether a given rooftop unit counts as light commercial is a question for the facility's compliance contact and the rule's definitions, not something to guess at the unit.

What stays familiar

If you know the Part 82 routine, the Part 84 routine will look familiar. The trigger leak rates in the eCFR text are 20 percent for commercial refrigeration, 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other covered appliances [1]. Repair is due within 30 days of adding refrigerant that puts the appliance over its trigger, or 120 days if an industrial process shutdown is needed [1]. A certified technician does the leak inspection to find the leaks [1]. The same trigger percentages and 30 and 120 day windows appear in the Part 82 text [2].

What is new for HFC systems

  • Leak rate calculation at each addition. The rule calls for a leak rate calculation each time refrigerant is added, except after a retrofit, a new installation, or a seasonal variance [1]. A facility uses one calculation method for all its covered appliances [1]. Our leak rate calculation lesson covers the arithmetic in full.
  • Verification tests. An initial verification test is done after the repair, within the repair window, and a follow-up test comes within 10 days of a successful initial test [1]. See the verification test lesson.
  • Scheduled inspections. For commercial or industrial refrigeration of 500 lb or more, the rule calls for quarterly inspections until four consecutive quarters come in under the trigger. Smaller commercial and industrial refrigeration, and comfort cooling, get annual inspections until one year comes in under the trigger [1]. More on that in the inspection schedule lesson.
  • Retrofit or retire plans and chronic leakers. If repairs fail, or the owner chooses not to repair, a retrofit or retirement plan is due within 30 days. An appliance that loses 125 percent or more of its full charge in a calendar year is reported to the agency by March 1 of the next year [1].

Automatic leak detection for very large systems

Section 84.108 requires an automatic leak detection system on industrial process or commercial refrigeration appliances with a full charge of 1,500 lb or more of a refrigerant with a GWP above 53. A system installed on or after January 1, 2026 needs it at installation or within 30 days. A system installed between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2025 needs it by January 1, 2027 [1]. A direct-detection system must alert at 100 ppm and detect 10 ppm. A condition-monitoring system must alert at a loss of 50 lb or 10 percent of full charge, whichever is less. The detection system is installed to the maker's instructions and is audited and calibrated annually [1]. This is a large-plant item: comfort cooling and small rooftop units are not in it.

Recovery cylinders and reclaimed refrigerant, in general terms

Three pieces matter to a tech. First, section 84.104 says recovered refrigerant from stationary equipment may not be sold, distributed or transferred to a new owner unless a certified reclaimer has reclaimed it to the listed specification, or it is transferred only to be reclaimed or destroyed [1]. Second, as of January 1, 2026, refrigerant may not be sold or reported as reclaimed if it contains more than 15 percent virgin regulated substance by weight, and reclaimers must label containers they fill [1]. Third, from January 1, 2029, servicing supermarket systems, refrigerated transport and automatic commercial ice makers must use reclaimed refrigerant that meets those paragraphs [1]. Our reuse versus reclaim lesson goes further.

On disposable cylinders, section 84.116 says that as of January 1, 2028, a person who uses a disposable cylinder and does not plan to reuse it must send it on so the leftover refrigerant, the heel, is removed. A certified technician can instead remove the heel and evacuate the cylinder to 15 in-Hg, with a signed statement for the final processor [1]. We read these sections for general terms only. The details, such as who the allowed recipients are, are in the text.

Record keeping

Records are kept for at least three years in paper or electronic form unless the text says otherwise. Full-charge records are kept until three years after the appliance is retired. Service records show the refrigerant amounts and the leak rate method, and verification and inspection records are kept as well [1]. In practice, write down the amount added, the date, the appliance's full charge and your name and certification, every time. The owner or operator keeps the file, so hand your notes in clean.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a real service call. Assumptions: a rooftop comfort cooling system with a 40 lb full charge of an HFC refrigerant; it takes 6 lb at a top-off; the last addition was 120 days earlier; it is not excluded as a light commercial appliance, and the addition is not after a retrofit, a new installation or a seasonal variance. Method: the annualizing method as the eCFR text describes it [1].

  1. Pounds added divided by full charge: 6 ÷ 40 = 0.15.
  2. Days since the last addition (120, which is shorter than 365) divided by 365: 120 ÷ 365 = 0.3288.
  3. Step 1 divided by step 2: 0.15 ÷ 0.3288 = 0.4563.
  4. Times 100: about 45.6 percent a year.

The comfort cooling trigger in the rule is 10 percent [1]. 45.6 is about 4.6 times that, so the appliance is over the trigger. Under the rule, repair is due within 30 days of the addition, followed by an initial verification test and then a follow-up test within 10 days [1]. Note that the arithmetic works because the interval is under a year; one top-off after a very long gap would produce a smaller rate, which is why the dates in the record matter.

Field card: HFC Leak Repair and Reclaim Rules

Part 82 is by refrigerant family at 50 lb; Part 84 adds HFCs at 15 lb.

  • Part 84 leak repair applies from January 1, 2026 at 15 lb or more with a regulated HFC or GWP above 53 [1].
  • Residential and light commercial AC and heat pump appliances are excluded [1].
  • Triggers: 10% comfort cooling, 20% commercial refrigeration, 30% industrial process [1].
  • Repair in 30 days (120 days with an industrial process shutdown); follow-up test within 10 days [1].
  • Automatic leak detection: 1,500 lb or more of commercial or industrial process refrigeration [1].
  • Reclaimed refrigerant: no more than 15% virgin by weight [1].
  • Keep records at least three years. Check the current eCFR text for dates.
