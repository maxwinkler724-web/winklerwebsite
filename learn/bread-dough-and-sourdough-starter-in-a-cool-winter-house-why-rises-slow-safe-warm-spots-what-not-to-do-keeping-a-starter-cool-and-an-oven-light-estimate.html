Bread Dough and Sourdough Starter in a Cool Winter House

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Turn the thermostat down for winter and the bread dough slows right along with the house. Nothing is wrong with your yeast. It is just cold. This lesson covers why that happens, which warm spots are safe, which popular tricks are not, how to keep a sourdough starter calm, and a rough estimate of what an oven light can do.

Why dough rises slowly in a cool house

Yeast is a living thing, and its speed follows temperature. A university extension bread-baking summary lists 80 to 85 F as the best range for dough to rise and 84 to 86 F as the best dough temperature, and says yeast dies at about 135 F. A university extension guide to sourdough says a starter generally works best between 70 and 82 F, and that in the cold the yeast becomes sleepy while the acid-making bacteria keep going, so dough may turn sour before it puffs up. A state-university breadmaking guide simply says to rise dough in a warm place free from draft until it has doubled, and notes that a typical rise takes about an hour. Our own note: that hour assumes a warm kitchen. A house set back to the low 60s F can stretch it a lot, so judge by the dough (doubled in size), not the clock.

Safe warm spots

  • Top of the refrigerator. Warm air from the appliance collects there. Our own tip: put a thermometer next to the bowl and keep it clear of the wall and cabinet edges so air can move.
  • Near, not on, a toe-kick heater or register. A bowl on a counter or chair a few feet from a warm outlet gets gentle heat. Keep it off the grille and out of the airflow so the dough skin does not dry. See the related lesson on hydronic toe-kick heaters below.
  • An oven with only the light on. Our own method, with the estimate below. Never turn the oven on, and tape a note to the door so nobody preheats it with dough inside.
  • A cooler with a jar of warm water. Our own idea: a closed insulated cooler holds heat well. Put the covered bowl and a sealed jar of warm tap water inside, close the lid, and check the temperature with a thermometer. Refresh the water if it cools.

What not to do

  • Dough on radiators or baseboard covers. Surface temperatures vary and can run far hotter than the 135 F that kills yeast, so the bottom of the bowl can cook while the top stays cold. Our own rule: keep it off hot hydronic and electric baseboard surfaces. Heated surfaces are also a burn risk, as the childproofing lesson below explains.
  • Space heaters. A federal fire-prevention agency says to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from all heat sources, including radiators and space heaters, and to switch space heaters off when you leave the room or go to sleep. Dough bowls, towels and plastic wrap are things that can burn. Do not park a bowl beside a heater and walk away.
  • Anything near a gas oven pilot area left unattended. Our own caution: do not tuck dough in or under a gas appliance, and never leave a heat source running to warm food. The same federal fire agency says, "Never use an oven to heat your home." That also means do not warm the kitchen by running the oven or stove burners; carbon monoxide risk is covered in the winter outage lesson below.
  • Covering a floor register with a bowl. Our own caution: a bowl blocks the supply air the system is designed to deliver, can hold the dough in a hot, dry draft, and can be kicked. Use a spot beside the register instead.

Worked example (our own): how warm does an oven light get it?

This is a rough estimate we made ourselves, not a measured result and not from a source. Every input is an assumption. Your oven will differ, so use a thermometer.

  1. Assumption, cavity: a 16 in wide, 16 in tall, 24 in deep cavity. Area of six sides = 2 x (16x16 + 16x24 + 16x24) = 2 x 1,024 = 2,048 square inches = 2,048 / 144 = 14.2 ft2.
  2. Assumption, bulb: 25 W or 40 W, with all of the power ending up as heat inside the box. Conversion: 1 W = 3.412 BTU/h. So 25 W = 85.3 BTU/h and 40 W = 136.5 BTU/h.
  3. Assumption, loss: an effective insulation value (R-value, a measure of resistance to heat flow) of R-4 for a decent oven, or R-3 for a leaky one (door glass, seal gaps, vent). Loss per degree = area / R. R-4: 14.2 / 4 = 3.55 BTU/h per F. R-3: 14.2 / 3 = 4.74 BTU/h per F.
  4. Steady rise above room: heat in divided by loss per degree. 25 W at R-4: 85.3 / 3.55 = 24 F. 40 W at R-4: 136.5 / 3.55 = 38 F. 25 W at R-3: 85.3 / 4.74 = 18 F. 40 W at R-3: 136.5 / 4.74 = 29 F.
  5. In a 64 F kitchen: about 82 F to 102 F, depending on bulb and oven. Check: 64 + 18 = 82 and 64 + 38 = 102.

The low end lands in the 80 to 85 F rising range. The high end overshoots it. A real oven also takes hours to reach a steady value, so the dough sees less warmth early on. Because our inputs are guesses, put an oven thermometer or probe next to the bowl, look again after 30 minutes, and use a timer so a rise never runs unchecked. If it is too warm, prop the door open a crack or use the smaller bulb. Some ovens only switch the light on with the door open; read the manual, and never leave the door open on a gas oven that is lit.

Keeping a starter cooler to slow it

A cool house helps if you bake once a week. The sourdough guide says a weekly baker can keep the starter in the refrigerator and take it out the day before baking, with no need to bring it to room temperature first. For monthly baking, take it out a day ahead and expect to feed it a couple of times about 12 hours apart. It also says higher flour-and-water ratios slow a starter, a ready starter at least doubles within 4 to 6 hours after feeding, and people baking three or four times a week or more should feed daily and keep it on the counter or in a warm place. Freezing can damage a starter. A cold kitchen counter that sits around 60 F is therefore a free way to slow a starter between feedings, but expect a sluggish rise after feeding until it warms.

Food safety for fillings and leftovers

Plain dough is not the concern. Perishable fillings are: cheese, meat, eggs, cream and custard. A federal food-safety agency says bacteria grow fastest between 40 F and 140 F and can double in as little as 20 minutes, that perishable food should not sit out more than 2 hours, and that the limit drops to 1 hour when the air is above 90 F. It says to keep the refrigerator at 40 F. A warm oven or cooler used for rising can sit in that range, so do not put filled dough in a warm spot for a long rise. Our own practice: rise the plain dough in the warm spot, then add perishable fillings, shape quickly, and bake or chill. A rise in the refrigerator is an option: a state-university breadmaking guide describes covered cool-rise dough chilled for 2 to 24 hours. For the starter, the sourdough guide says to discard it if mold appears and to use a loosely covered, non-reactive container.

Humidity and drafts near cold windows

A sill by a cold window looks like a nice sunny spot, but the glass is cold, and the draft there chills the dough surface. Our own advice: keep dough off cold sills and away from the leak around doors. Cover the bowl so the dough skin does not dry, and expect moisture on cold glass if you also run steaming pots. See the lessons on window condensation and drafty doors below.

Field card: Bread Dough and Sourdough Starter in a Cool Winter House

Warm the bowl gently, check with a thermometer, set a timer.

  • Yeast likes about 80 to 85 F; a cool house just slows it.
  • Safe: top of the refrigerator, a spot near (not on) a warm outlet, oven light only, a cooler with warm water.
  • Not safe: dough on radiators or baseboard covers, space heaters, a gas oven pilot area unattended, a bowl on a register.
  • Keep flammables 3 feet from heaters. Never use the oven to heat your home.
  • Perishable fillings: 2 hours out, 1 hour above 90 F.
  • Our own estimate: an oven light adds roughly 18 to 38 F; check it.
