BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A building automation system is only as good as what its sensors tell it. Sensors rarely fail with a bang. They drift, get swapped for a near match, or get overridden, and the building slowly runs worse.

Thermistors and RTDs

Most temperature points in a BAS use one of two element types. A thermistor changes resistance with temperature along a curve that is not a straight line. An RTD (resistance temperature detector) has a more linear relationship. Kele, a controls supplier, says RTDs hold accuracy and stability better in long-runtime or tight-tolerance jobs, while thermistors are common and cost-effective for comfort control. Other sensor families in a BAS include humidity, pressure, CO2 and current sensors, and the same principles of checking against a known reference apply.

Why "10K" does not mean interchangeable

The Kele article makes a point worth remembering: names like "10K Type II" and "10K Type III" are shorthand, not universal standards. Manufacturers define their own curves, and two sensors that both read 10,000 ohms at 77 degrees F can disagree elsewhere. The article's field example is a substitute that read correctly near 72 degrees F but was off by 5 to 8 degrees F at 55 degrees F. Before replacing a sensor, confirm what input type and curve the controller expects and verify the replacement matches the original. A shifting error across the range suggests a curve mismatch; a fixed offset suggests a configuration problem, such as an input set for the wrong sensor type or a scaling error.

How drift shows up

  • Zones that never settle or sit a few degrees off setpoint.
  • Occupant complaints that do not match what the screen says.
  • Simultaneous heating and cooling, or a damper that never economizes (see economizer faults).
  • A reading that looks plausible but disagrees with a handheld meter.

A controls article from a trade magazine notes that occupant complaints are often the first sign of a worn sensor reporting bad data, and recommends replacing a failing sensor rather than working around it. Another operator guide suggests inspecting and calibrating temperature, humidity, pressure and CO2 sensors on a regular schedule, since dirty or faulty sensors cause inefficient operation.

Checking a sensor in the field

  • Compare the BAS reading to a calibrated handheld at the same spot, after letting both settle.
  • Measure the sensor's resistance or signal and compare to the manufacturer's table for that exact sensor.
  • Look at location: a sensor near heat-producing equipment, in sun or in a draft reads the wrong thing even if it is accurate. Move or shield it.
  • Check wiring, connectors and the controller's configured input type.
  • Record the as-found reading, the correction and the date.

Setpoint and override habits

Writing down the baseline before changing anything is a recurring recommendation. One operator guide suggests documenting schedules and setpoints as a building baseline so you can compare later. Overrides are another quiet problem. The same sources say overrides that drift from design intent are among the most common causes of poor performance, that overrides are fine for testing or emergencies but should be tracked, and that equipment should be returned to automatic control when finished. Many systems can limit the damage with PIN-protected changes, setpoint limits and timed reversion. Check recent overrides first in any troubleshooting job.

A caution

A BAS typically cannot fix a mechanical problem. If a zone is cold because a damper is jammed or a coil is dirty, a better setpoint will not help. Verify with your own eyes and instruments, and follow the site's procedures before changing live equipment settings.

Field card: BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits

Sensors lie slowly. Check them against something you trust.

  • Thermistors are nonlinear; RTDs are more linear and stable.
  • "10K" curves differ by maker. Match the curve, not just the resistance.
  • Verify against a calibrated handheld, in a representative location.
  • Log baselines and track overrides; return to auto when done.
  • A BAS cannot fix a mechanical fault.
