Condensate Pump Not Working: High-Efficiency Furnace and AC

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A small pump in the basement or closet can stop a whole furnace or air conditioner. When it fails, the house goes cold or warm and the cause is a cup of water in a plastic tank. This lesson explains what the pump does, why it can shut the system off on purpose, what is safe for a homeowner to look at, and what a technician checks.

What the condensate pump does

Air conditioners remove moisture from the air, and high-efficiency (condensing) gas furnaces make water when they cool their exhaust gas. If the drain cannot slope down to a floor drain, a condensate pump collects the water in a small tank and pushes it up and away through a thin tube. Little Giant's manual for its VCMX-20 describes the pump as one that removes condensation from air conditioners and high-efficiency gas furnaces. For why the water forms and how a plain gravity drain clogs, read Water Leaking from the AC or Furnace: Condensate Drain Basics first; this page covers the pump itself.

Why the system stops: the safety switch

Pumps like the ones cited here have a main float plus a separate overflow safety switch. The main float starts and stops the motor. Little Giant's manual gives numbers for its model: the pump turns on when the water reaches about 1.6 inches, off at about 1.2 inches, and a separate overflow switch activates at about 2.2 inches. That second switch is the one that matters when you have no heat or no cooling. When it is wired into the thermostat's low-voltage circuit, as the manuals describe, a tank that is too full tells the equipment to stop before water spills. Sauermann's manual for its Omega pump says connecting the safety switch cable is indispensable to avoid any risk of overflow.

So a dead furnace or AC next to a pump with a full tank is often the safety working correctly, not a second failure. Do not jumper or remove the switch to get the system running. Water on the floor, or on a furnace that is hot, is the problem the switch exists to prevent.

Common reasons a pump stops working

  • Dirty tank and floats. Slime and debris can hold a float up or down. Little Giant says to inspect and service the pump at least once a year, make sure the floats move freely, and wash the tank with warm water and mild soap. Sauermann says to clean its tank at the start of the season and regularly if the system runs year-round.
  • Kinked or clogged tubing. Sauermann's troubleshooting steps start with confirming the discharge lines are neither obstructed nor kinked. Little Giant says the same for inlet and discharge lines.
  • Check valve problems. A check valve keeps water in the discharge tube from running back into the tank. If it sticks or is dirty, the pump may cycle again and again. Little Giant notes the valve can be unlocked and removed for service without draining the discharge line.
  • No power. The pump needs a grounded outlet that stays powered. Little Giant says to connect to a constant power source, not a fan or other device that runs only part of the time, and recommends a GFCI.
  • Air or loose connections. Sauermann says that a pump running with no water usually means the suction hose is not connected and airtight.

What a homeowner can safely do

  1. Look, do not open the furnace. Check whether the pump tank is full and whether the pump's cord is plugged in.
  2. Check the thermostat is calling and the breaker is on.
  3. If you are comfortable, unplug the pump first. Little Giant's service steps begin with unplugging it. Then look for a kinked tube.
  4. Do not disassemble the furnace.
  5. If the tank is full, the pump hums but does not move water, or the water level keeps rising, turn the system off at the thermostat and call a technician. Water near electrical parts is a reason to stop.

If it is a heating emergency and you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave first; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds. For a furnace that runs only part of the way and then stops, No Heat First Checks shows the order to look.

Acidic condensate and neutralizers

Condensing furnaces make acidic water. Oak Ridge National Laboratory describes furnace condensate as having a pH of about 3, more acidic than typical acid rain, and says it leads to corrosion. A trade article in Contracting Business gives a range of 2.9 to 4.0 and warns it can damage plumbing, concrete and wastewater systems, especially older cast iron. That is why many installations add a neutralizer, a small container of limestone-type media that raises the pH before the water reaches a drain.

Axiom's neutralizer manual says to check the media level, test pH at the outlet once a year with test strips or a meter, replace the media when the pH falls below the minimum set by the local water authority, and check with that authority about discharge rules. Spent media can restrict flow and make the pump's job harder, so a neutralizer is part of the same yearly check as the pump. For the vent side of a condensing system, see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained; condensing boilers have the same issue, covered in Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers.

Worked example: how big a gap is pH 3 to pH 7?

pH is a logarithmic scale, so each whole number is a factor of ten in hydrogen ion concentration. Untreated furnace condensate at pH 3 compared with neutral water at pH 7:

  1. Difference in pH: 7 - 3 = 4 units.
  2. Each unit is a factor of 10, so 10 x 10 x 10 x 10 = 10,000.
  3. The raw condensate has about 10,000 times the hydrogen ion concentration of neutral water.
  4. A neutralizer that brings it only up to pH 5 (2 units) would still leave it 10 x 10 = 100 times more acidic than neutral. That is why the outlet test, not just seeing media in the container, matters.

Technician section

Pump and switch wiring. Little Giant says the safety switch should connect to a Class II low-voltage circuit. To shut down heating or cooling, the COM and NO leads go in series with the low-voltage thermostat circuit. If the system cannot be interrupted, the COM and NC contacts can drive an alarm instead. It ships wired to COM and NO. Sauermann's Omega is a normally closed contact and its manual says to verify the wiring diagram and the customer's application before using it to cut power. Goodman's installation manual for the GMVC96 describes an auxiliary alarm switch and, on non-communicating setups, says any auxiliary alarm switch must be used to interrupt the Y1 signal. Read the furnace manual for where its alarm input is. For reading 24 V controls, see Relays, Transformers and 24V Control, and Thermostat Terminal Letters.

Discharge piping. Little Giant calls for discharge tubing no larger than 3/8 inch inside diameter to limit backflow, an inlet that enters the tank 1 to 2 inches so it clears the float, and a drop to at or below the pump base after rising so a siphon forms; where the line cannot slope down, form an inverted U-trap at the high point. Do not install a pump where it can be splashed, or inside an air plenum.

Match the pump to the liquid. Little Giant says to use only liquids compatible with the pump materials and states its warranty is void if it circulates anything other than water. Sauermann's manual describes its pump as designed for neutral-phase, non-oily condensate. Axiom says where there is no gravity drain, use a condensate pump designed for condensing boilers and furnaces, with an overflow switch so the appliance shuts off if the pump fails. Confirm the pump's rating and where the neutralizer sits in the line before installing.

Gravity side. On the furnace drain, Goodman's manual calls for a primed trap, no extra traps, and a downward slope of 1/4 inch per foot toward the drain. For a 12 ft run that is 12 x 0.25 = 3 inches of drop. It also says to protect the line from freezing. Axiom recommends a Y or T fitting as a safety overflow in case the drain clogs, and says the neutralizer's inlet and outlet must be lower than the appliance's drain.

When the furnace shows a pressure switch fault. A blocked trap or drain can back up and affect the pressure switch; walk the condensate path before replacing parts, as in Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis and No-Heat Furnace: From LED Code to Part. On an AC, a full pump tank with ice on the coil points at airflow or charge; see AC Evaporator Coil Frozen.

Where a failed pump could cause damage, Little Giant says a backup system or alarm should be used. RectorSeal's catalog likewise lists overflow switches that shut off the system on a clogged drain.

Field card: Condensate Pump Not Working

A full tank and a dead system usually means the safety switch did its job.

  • The pump moves water from a furnace or AC when gravity cannot.
  • The overflow switch is wired into the thermostat circuit and stops the equipment.
  • Never bypass the switch.
  • Common causes: dirty floats, kinked tube, bad check valve, no power or GFCI trip.
  • Clean the tank at least yearly (unplug first).
  • Condensing furnace condensate is acidic (pH about 3 to 4); test neutralizer pH yearly.
  • Check manual wiring: COM and NO in series with the thermostat circuit, or the furnace alarm input.
