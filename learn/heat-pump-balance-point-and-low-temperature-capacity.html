Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house loses more heat as it gets colder outside, and a heat pump makes less heat at the same time. The temperature where those two lines cross is the balance point, and it explains most "why is the backup heat on" calls.

Two lines crossing

An ICC article defines the balance point as the outdoor temperature where heat pump output equals the building's heat loss. Above it, the heat pump can carry the building alone. Below it, the building needs more heat than the unit can deliver, and the gap must come from backup heat. A tighter, better insulated building has a lower balance point, so it leans less on backup.

Why capacity falls in the cold

As the outdoor air cools, the unit has to lift heat across a bigger temperature difference. Frost on the outdoor coil and the defrost cycles that remove it cut output further. The same article gives an example of a standard 3-ton unit rated at 36,000 Btu/h at 47 degrees F: roughly 31,000 at 35 degrees F, 27,000 at 25, 22,000 at 17 and 17,000 at 5. A cold-climate inverter version in the same article falls much more gently, to roughly 30,000 at 5 degrees F. These are the article's illustrations, not rating data for any particular product.

Reading it against the load

The load calculation (see Manual J) tells you how much heat the building needs at its design temperature. Compare that to the equipment's published capacity at that temperature. Where capacity is short, backup heat covers the difference. ICC notes that backup is expected during the coldest hours in climates with design temperatures roughly between 0 and 20 degrees F, and that hybrid systems with a boiler or furnace are another way to handle those hours. The DOE page on air-source heat pumps adds that newer designs have become a legitimate space heating option in colder regions and recommends choosing the highest HSPF that is practical.

What it means on a service call

  • A heat pump in backup mode at 10 degrees F may be doing exactly what it should.
  • Backup heat running at 45 degrees F is a symptom. Check airflow, charge, sensors, settings and the outdoor coil first. See the emergency vs. auxiliary heat lesson.
  • Replacing a unit with a bigger one is not the only fix; sealing the building, fixing ducts and adding insulation lower the load, which lowers the balance point.
  • One published example of a target: a Pacific Northwest utility program (Bonneville Power Administration, Performance Tested Comfort Systems) asks for a balance point no higher than 30 degrees F, and says an undersized system shows comfort problems when strip heat is locked out. That is one program's requirement, not a code or a maker rule.
  • Do not promise a customer a balance point number without the load calculation and the manufacturer's low-temperature capacity data.
  • Equipment ratings and cold-climate performance requirements are covered in Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Setting expectations

Customers often expect heat to feel like a furnace. A heat pump delivers cooler supply air, which can feel less hot even when it is working well. Explaining that the unit is sized to carry most of the winter, with backup for the coldest stretch, avoids a lot of callbacks.

Field card: Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity

Balance point: where unit output meets building heat loss.

  • Below the balance point, backup heat covers the gap.
  • Capacity drops as outdoor temperature drops; cold-climate models drop more slowly.
  • A tighter building lowers the balance point.
  • Use the load calculation and manufacturer capacity data, not a rule of thumb.
  • Backup in mild weather is a symptom to investigate.
