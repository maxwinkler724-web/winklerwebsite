Heating and Cooling an Insulated Concrete Form (ICF) Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An insulated concrete form (ICF) wall is a stack of foam blocks that is filled with concrete. The foam stays in place on both sides, so the wall is foam, concrete, foam. That makes a quiet, steady, well-insulated house that is usually very tight. It also means the heating and cooling plan needs a few different habits than a framed house.

Small loads, easy to oversize

Foam on both faces and few air leaks cut heat loss, so an ICF house usually needs less heating and cooling capacity than a framed house of the same size. The risk is that equipment gets picked from a rule of thumb or copied from the old house. Oversized equipment short cycles, which means it turns on and off quickly, and a cooling system that never runs long enough does a poor job of removing humidity. Our own advice: ask for a room-by-room load calculation that uses the actual wall, window and air leakage numbers for your house, and then choose equipment that matches that result. See Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings for how whole-wall R-value is built up, and use the worked example below to feel the size of the difference.

Mass between two layers of foam

The concrete core can hold a lot of heat, but it sits between two foam layers. That is not the same as a thick exposed wall such as adobe, where the mass is in direct contact with the room air. In an ICF wall the inner foam layer (usually covered with a finish) slows heat moving between the room and the concrete. Our own reading of this: the mass still helps to smooth swings, for example when the sun goes down or when the heat is set back, but you should not expect adobe-style behavior or count on the concrete to store solar heat and give it back at night without careful design. For the idea of time lag in general, see Thermal Mass in Homes, and for the exposed-earth case compare Heating and Cooling an Adobe or Cob Home.

A tight house needs planned fresh air

A federal environmental agency's introduction to indoor air quality says that buildings designed and built to limit outdoor air leakage may have higher indoor pollutant levels unless they have special mechanical ventilation. That fits an ICF house: there are few accidental leaks to bring in fresh air, so the air has to be planned. Common approaches, which we cover in Choosing Whole-House Ventilation for a New Home, are:

  • Exhaust fans plus a supply path. A quiet fan pulls stale air out, and a deliberate opening lets outdoor air in so the house does not pull air backward through vented appliances.
  • A balanced heat recovery ventilator or energy recovery ventilator. It brings in and exhausts about the same amount of air, and passes some heat (and with the energy type, some moisture) between the two streams so the fresh air costs less to condition.

Kitchen and bath fans that vent outdoors still matter. The same agency's guide to mold and moisture says to run the bath fan when showering and use exhaust fans when cooking or dishwashing.

Humidity in summer

Because the house is tight and the load is small, a cooling system can satisfy the thermostat quickly without drying the air. The moisture guide recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and says to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. A humidity meter is the simple way to check. If the house feels clammy at a comfortable temperature, that points to oversized cooling, too much outdoor air in humid weather, or a missing dehumidifier. A ventilation system that brings in humid air needs a plan for it. Our own note: a technician can compare run time, humidity and equipment size together rather than guessing.

Plan the holes before the pour

It is easy to run a line set, a vent or a duct through a stud wall later. It is not easy through a concrete core. Our own planning advice is to settle the locations of the indoor and outdoor units, the line set path, combustion air or vent terminations, dryer and exhaust fan ducts, and the ventilator intake and exhaust before the walls are poured, and to have the builder place sleeves in the forms. Coring a finished concrete wall in the wrong place can hit steel reinforcing bar and is slow work, so ask the installer and the wall contractor to agree on the plan. We did not find a source that gives sleeve sizes, so we give none; follow the equipment maker's instructions and your local rules.

Radon testing

A federal environmental agency's radon page says that testing is the only way to know your level of exposure and that all homes should be tested for radon. An ICF house with a basement or other below-grade walls is no exception: the concrete is not a reason to skip the test. Our own note: test after the house is lived in and the heating and cooling are running as usual, and follow the testing instructions from your state radon office. A tight house can hold radon differently than a leaky one, so the result tells you more than a guess does.

Vented combustion and CO alarms

If you burn gas, oil, wood or propane in an ICF house, the vent system must work properly, and the tight shell means makeup air is not accidental. Use sealed-combustion or power-vented equipment where the equipment maker calls for it, and have a qualified person install and check the vents. A federal consumer product safety agency says to install carbon monoxide (CO) alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and notes that interconnected alarms are best: when one sounds, they all sound. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do for the details.

Worked example: wall heat loss (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed round numbers, not a measurement of any real house. Assumptions: 1,800 ft2 of wall, an indoor-outdoor temperature difference of 50 F (70 F inside, 20 F outside), an assumed whole-wall R-22 for the ICF wall, and an assumed whole-wall R-14 for a framed wall. Whole-wall R means the value for the whole assembly including studs, ties and other bridges, not just the foam or the cavity. Wall heat loss in Btu/h is area times temperature difference divided by R.

  1. Multiply area by difference: 1,800 x 50 = 90,000.
  2. ICF wall at R-22: 90,000 / 22 = 4,090.9, or about 4,091 Btu/h.
  3. Framed wall at R-14: 90,000 / 14 = 6,428.6, or about 6,429 Btu/h.
  4. Difference: 6,429 - 4,091 = 2,338 Btu/h less.
  5. Percent less: 2,338 / 6,429 = 0.364, or about 36 percent less. Check: 1 - 14/22 = 1 - 0.636 = 0.364, the same answer.

This is wall conduction only. Windows, roof, floor, air leakage and ventilation add to a real load, which is why a full room-by-room calculation is still needed. With assumed numbers like these, the ICF wall loses about 2,300 Btu/h less than the framed wall at the same conditions.

Field card: Heating and Cooling an Insulated Concrete Form (ICF) Home

Tight and well insulated: size it right and plan the air.

  • Foam, concrete, foam gives steady walls and usually a tight house.
  • Loads are small, so oversizing is easy. Ask for a room-by-room load calculation.
  • Mass between foam smooths swings but is not exposed mass like adobe.
  • Plan fresh air: exhaust fans plus a supply path, or a balanced heat or energy recovery ventilator.
  • Keep indoor humidity ideally 30 to 50 percent, below 60 percent.
  • Plan penetrations before the pour.
  • Test for radon; all homes should be tested.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; vent combustion properly.
  • Our example: about 4,091 vs 6,429 Btu/h, about 36 percent less.
