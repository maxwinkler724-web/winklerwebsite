Compounding Pharmacy HVAC: USP 797 and 800 Buffer Rooms, Ante-Rooms and Negative-Pressure Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A sterile compounding room is a small cleanroom inside a pharmacy, where staff mix medicines that go into a patient's body. The HVAC is part of the safety system. This lesson explains the room layout, the ISO classes, the air change and pressure numbers that state boards and USP publish, and what a service tech should leave alone.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. The standards here are USP General Chapter 797 (sterile compounding) and USP General Chapter 800 (hazardous drugs). They are standards, not copies we can hand you, so we name them and cite public pages that describe them. State boards adopt and enforce them, and some state rule text is older than the current chapters. The pharmacy's designated person and its certifier decide the exact setpoints. Nothing here is a design or a code ruling.

The rooms and what they are for

A cleanroom suite has at least two rooms. Staff enter the ante-room first, wash hands and put on gowns. USP's 797 FAQ says the ante-room has a line of demarcation, with the dirty side at the entrance and the clean side nearest the buffer room. Then they step into the buffer room, where the primary engineering control (the hood or isolator, ISO Class 5 inside) sits. The Texas State Board of Pharmacy rule defines the buffer area as an ISO Class 7 area holding the primary engineering control, and the ante-room as ISO Class 8 or better. USP's 797 FAQ says an isolator used for Category 2 preparations goes in a positive-pressure ISO Class 7 buffer room with an ISO Class 8 or better positive-pressure ante-room. Another FAQ answer says one ante-room may be shared by a Category 2 and a Category 3 buffer room, and also by a hazardous and a non-hazardous buffer room.

Lower ISO number means cleaner air. The cleanroom lesson linked below explains the scale and HEPA filters, so we do not repeat it here.

Air changes, HEPA supply and pressure for non-hazardous rooms

The North Carolina Board of Pharmacy's 2018 sterile compounding guidance, written while the older edition of USP 797 was in force, lists a minimum of 30 air changes per hour (ACPH) for an ISO Class 7 buffer room, with at least 15 ACPH of that supplied through the HEPA filters in the room. It recommends 20 ACPH for an ISO Class 8 ante-room and 30 ACPH if the ante-room is classified ISO 7. Treat these as the numbers a certifier has commonly worked to, and confirm the current figure with the pharmacy and its state board.

Pressure is what keeps the dirty side out. The Texas board's rule text calls for a minimum positive differential of 0.02 to 0.05 inch water column between the buffer room and the space outside it. The North Carolina guidance gives the same magnitude, to be measured between the buffer room and ante-room and between the ante-room and general pharmacy. Our own conversion: 1 inch water column is about 249 pascals, so 0.02 inch is about 5 Pa. That is a very small pressure, which is why a propped door, a changed damper or a dirty filter can erase it. USP's FAQ says an analog gauge is acceptable and the readings must be reviewed and documented at least daily on days when compounding happens.

Two layout details come from the same FAQ. HEPA filters go in the ceiling of the buffer room and ante-room, because that avoids contaminating the air after it has been filtered. Air returns in the suite must be low on the wall unless a visual smoke study shows otherwise, and the smoke study must be repeated whenever equipment is moved.

Hazardous drugs: negative pressure under USP 800

Hazardous drugs such as chemotherapy agents are handled so the room contains them. USP's 800 FAQ says the containment secondary engineering control, meaning the room, must hold a negative pressure of 0.01 to 0.03 inch water column, and the hood must exhaust to the outdoors. External venting means air is exhausted outside the building, and the FAQ says the room does not need HEPA filtration on that exhaust. Fixed walls are required, and the FAQ ties them to preventing contamination from leaving the room and from entering the ISO Class 7 hazardous drug buffer room.

The FAQ gives air change values as examples of the room minimum, "12 or 30 ACPH". It states at least 12 ACPH for externally ventilated negative-pressure storage rooms. The North Carolina guidance lists 30 ACPH for an ISO 7 hazardous buffer room, or a non-classified space with an isolator at 12 ACPH. The FAQ also says a hazardous drug sterile hood must be externally vented, and that sterile and non-sterile, hazardous and non-hazardous work generally need separate rooms.

Worked example: a 10 x 12 x 9 ft buffer room

The room size is given. The air change rate and diffuser rating are our assumptions, called out as such.

  1. Our own: volume = 10 x 12 x 9 = 1,080 cubic feet.
  2. Assumption: 30 ACPH, matching the North Carolina guidance figure above.
  3. Our own: supply cfm = ACPH x volume / 60 = 30 x 1,080 / 60 = 540 cfm.
  4. Assumption: each 2 x 4 ft HEPA diffuser delivers 150 cfm. Real units have a maker's rating that varies with filter loading, so a design uses the published figure.
  5. Our own: diffusers = 540 / 150 = 3.6, so round up to 4. Three would give 450 cfm, only 25 ACPH.
  6. Our own: with 4 diffusers, airflow = 600 cfm and ACPH = 600 x 60 / 1,080 = 33.3.
  7. Our own: the HEPA minimum of 15 ACPH (North Carolina guidance) is 15 x 1,080 / 60 = 270 cfm, which 2 diffusers (300 cfm) would already cover. Four diffusers therefore meet both the total and the HEPA-supply numbers.

The room reaches its pressure only if the air leaving through returns and leakage is a little less than the supply. That offset is a balancing-contractor task, not a number you set from this example.

Temperature, humidity and monitoring

USP's 797 FAQ says the chapter sets no specific temperature or humidity requirement for an unclassified segregated compounding area, and suggests the cleanroom suite requirements as a reasonable guide. We found no setpoint on the government and USP pages we read, so we do not give one. USP's FAQ says temperature and humidity sensors and pressure monitors are monitoring equipment, calibrated as the maker says or every 12 months if the maker is silent.

The North Carolina guidance says secondary engineering controls (the rooms) and primary engineering controls (the hoods) are certified at least every six months. USP's FAQ says the room must be independently certified before use, that testing is done under dynamic operating conditions and covers ACPH, and that changing the ceiling HEPA filters brings required air and surface sampling.

What a tech must not do

  • Do not shut off or slow a fan without telling the pharmacy first. USP's 800 FAQ says the negative-pressure room and the storage room must stay under negative pressure at all times, and the air change rate may not be set below its minimum when the room is not in use. Our own rule of thumb: ask the pharmacy to schedule any shutdown.
  • Do not change dampers, setpoints, return locations or diffuser positions without the certifier and pharmacy knowing. Moving things can force a new smoke study and recertification.
  • Do not open or swap a ceiling HEPA filter on your own. See the sampling note above.
  • Do not prop doors or open pass-throughs to move tools. The pressure gap is only a few pascals.
  • Do not recirculate or redirect hazardous drug exhaust. It must go outdoors, and your work on it may need PPE and a procedure from the pharmacy.

Report alarms and a drifting gauge to the pharmacy contact. For general healthcare pressure rooms, see the linked lesson.

Field card: Compounding Pharmacy HVAC

Clean room positive, hazardous room negative, never turn it off unannounced.

  • Buffer room: ISO 7. Ante-room: ISO 8 or better. Hood: ISO 5.
  • Non-hazardous: positive, 0.02 to 0.05 in. w.c. (Texas board rule).
  • Hazardous drug room: negative, 0.01 to 0.03 in. w.c.; hood exhausts outdoors (USP 800 FAQ).
  • North Carolina guidance: 30 ACPH ISO 7, 15 ACPH of it through room HEPA; 20 ACPH ISO 8 ante-room.
  • Rooms and hoods certified at least every six months.
  • ACPH = cfm x 60 / room volume in cubic feet.
  • Tell the pharmacy before any fan, damper or filter work.
