Kennel and Animal Room HVAC: Air Changes, Odor, Pressure and Humidity

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A room full of dogs, cats or lab animals is a different air problem from an office. The animals add heat, moisture, odor and germs, and the floors get hosed down. This lesson covers what the published guidance says about air changes, temperature and humidity, room pressure and waste anesthetic gas, then works one kennel room through to a cfm and a winter heating number.

A vet clinic, a boarding kennel, a shelter and a research room can each answer to different authorities, and the room's designer or veterinarian sets the target. This page is general education built from public documents read in 2026. It does not replace the facility's design documents or the engineer of record.

What goes wrong in animal rooms

Four things drive the design: heat and moisture from the animals, odor and ammonia from waste, airborne germs that move between animals, and water from washdown. Ammonia is a good example of why this is hard. A 2006 National Academies workshop report on environmental controls says current guidelines "provide no criteria to judge air quality" for animal rooms, so there is no single number to hold the room to. In practice a tech sees the symptoms: a sharp smell, condensation on walls, and animals that cough or sneeze.

Air changes: a starting guide, not a design answer

The Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition, from the National Academies) says that providing 10 to 15 fresh air changes per hour in animal housing rooms is an acceptable guideline. It adds a caution that matters to us: the guideline does not take into account the range of possible heat loads, among other factors. The 2006 workshop report says the same range has historically worked for most thermal and breathing loads, that higher rates are allowed with justification, and that in some cases less than 10 may be adequate. It also says the Guide expects calculations of the air change rate needed to remove heat and moisture, plus any make-up air for hoods or safety cabinets.

For shelters, a shelter infection-control resource from a university public health center suggests an air exchange rate "ideally 8-15" per hour and says to minimize excess moisture, dust and odor. A national animal welfare group's shelter facility checklist, based on the shelter veterinarians' guidelines, gives no air change number. It says only that ventilation must be high enough for adequate air quality and must not compromise the recommended temperature. So the safe reading is that 10 to 15 is a common reference point for research housing, and other facility types use it loosely.

Temperature and humidity by species

Table 3.1 of the Guide lists dry-bulb temperatures: 68 to 79 °F for mice, rats, hamsters, gerbils and guinea pigs; 61 to 72 °F for rabbits; 64 to 84 °F for cats, dogs and nonhuman primates; and 61 to 81 °F for farm animals and poultry. It calls 30 to 70 percent relative humidity acceptable for most mammals, with some primates, reptiles and amphibians needing more. The shelter checklist uses 64 to 80 °F and the same 30 to 70 percent. A policy from a university animal care office for dogs repeats the Guide values and requires temperature alarms where dogs are housed. See psychrometrics if relative humidity is new to you.

Supply high, exhaust low

Dogs and cats sit and lie near the floor, and in our own experience odor and moisture tend to collect down there. Our own design habit, shown in the drawing, is to deliver tempered air high and pull exhaust near the floor so air sweeps past the animals and out. That layout is our own illustration, not a requirement quoted from the Guide; the 2006 report says only that deviations from the air change guideline should account for air movement, diffusion and the placement of supply and exhaust devices. A failure we watch for is short-circuiting: if supply and exhaust sit close together, the room reads as ventilated while the runs stay stale.

Pressure and where the air goes

The 2006 report says cross-contamination between holding rooms is usually controlled by pressurization. It says quarantine, isolation, biohazard and nonhuman primate rooms should be negative, while pathogen-free rooms, surgery and clean storage should be positive. It does not give pressure numbers. For how healthcare spaces monitor pressure, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare and Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms; we do not repeat them here. A kennel room is usually held negative to the corridor so odor stays in. A surgery suite is the opposite case and is held positive.

100 percent outside air or recirculation

The Guide says recycling air may save energy but carries risks, chiefly cross-contamination between rooms on the same system, and that recycled air should be filtered with at least 85 to 95 percent ASHRAE efficiency filters. It says exhausting straight to the building exhaust is generally preferred. The shelter checklist says air from isolation areas should be exhausted outside and not recirculated. The cost of going all outside air shows up in winter, which is the next section. Energy recovery can offset it; see energy recovery wheels.

Washdown and corrosion

The checklist calls for sealed, impermeable flooring such as resinous epoxy or urethane, floors sloped to drains, and surfaces that withstand repeated cleaning. The Guide's physical plant section is where room finishes and drains are covered. For HVAC, the point is that equipment in or serving these rooms sees moisture and cleaning chemicals, so the designer should say what the room's cleaning routine is before grilles, fan housings and controls are chosen. We did not find a published corrosion rating we can cite, so we leave that to the maker's literature.

Anesthetic gas and the vet clinic

OSHA's waste anesthetic gas page says its standards do not specifically address waste anesthetic gases. The NIOSH veterinary prevention page lists installing an effective waste anesthetic gas scavenging system as an engineering control. A NIOSH hospital document says to place the scavenging exhaust where waste gases will not be pulled back into intake air and to maintain machines, circuits and scavenging systems to minimize leaks. It is written for human operating rooms, and we use it only for those two ideas. Room air change numbers in it are for human hospitals and do not carry over to a vet clinic.

Worked example: one kennel room

All inputs below are our own assumptions for teaching, except the 10 to 15 range (the Guide) and the 1.08 factor (see the 1.08 lesson).

  • Room: 20 ft by 15 ft by 9 ft ceiling (assumed).
  • Rate: 15 air changes per hour of fresh air, the top of the Guide's range (assumed choice).
  • All outside air, no recirculation (assumed).
  • Indoor 70 °F, within the Guide's 64 to 84 °F for dogs (assumed). Outdoor design 15 °F (assumed).
  1. Volume: 20 × 15 × 9 = 2,700 ft³.
  2. Airflow: cfm = ACH × volume ÷ 60 = 15 × 2,700 ÷ 60 = 675 cfm. At 10 ACH it is 10 × 2,700 ÷ 60 = 450 cfm.
  3. Temperature difference: 70 − 15 = 55 °F.
  4. Heating load of that air: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT = 1.08 × 675 × 55 = 40,095 Btu/h. At 450 cfm: 1.08 × 450 × 55 = 26,730 Btu/h.
  5. Negative offset (assumed 75 cfm, about 11 percent): exhaust 675 cfm, supply 600 cfm, and 75 cfm is drawn in from the corridor.

So about 40,000 Btu/h of heat goes just to warm the air, before wall and roof losses, and the dogs' own heat is not credited. Dropping from 15 to 10 changes the load by about 13,000 Btu/h, which is why the Guide's caution about heat load matters: more air is not free. The 1.08 factor holds for sea-level air near room temperature, per the ACCA blog.

Field card: Kennel and Animal Room HVAC

Air, pressure, humidity, and a plan for the washdown.

  • The Guide: 10 to 15 fresh air changes per hour is an acceptable guideline, not a design for every heat load.
  • cfm = ACH × room volume ÷ 60.
  • Heating the air: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Dogs and cats: 64 to 84 °F, 30 to 70 percent RH (Guide).
  • Isolation and quarantine rooms negative; surgery positive; isolation air not recirculated.
  • Waste anesthetic gas: scavenge it, and keep the exhaust away from intakes.
  • Our own habit: supply high, exhaust low; watch for short-circuiting.
