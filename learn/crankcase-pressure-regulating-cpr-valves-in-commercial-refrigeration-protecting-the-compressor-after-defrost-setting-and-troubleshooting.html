Crankcase Pressure Regulating (CPR) Valves in Commercial Refrigeration: Protecting the Compressor After Defrost, Setting and Troubleshooting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A low-temperature compressor can run for hours inside its amp limit and then trip its overload every time a defrost ends. The coil has warmed up, the suction line is full of warm vapor, and the compressor sees a pressure far above what it was built to pull. A crankcase pressure regulator (CPR) is the valve that puts a ceiling on that pressure. This lesson covers what it does, where it sits, how it is set, and what it looks like when it is wrong.

Check the maker's instructions

Check the maker's instructions. The valve maker's bulletin and the compressor maker's data set the real numbers and the adjustment direction. Working on a charged system takes EPA 608 certification. We found no federal, university or national-lab page on CPR valves, so this lesson rests on trade-magazine articles and maker material, listed as plain text in Sources. We give no setpoints because none apply to every machine.

What a CPR does

A trade-magazine article on CPRs says the valve keeps a compressor motor from overloading when crankcase pressure climbs above the pressure the compressor was designed to work at. That can happen during or after a defrost, or after a normal shutdown, when the evaporator has warmed and its pressure is high. The same article calls it an outlet pressure regulator and a common accessory on low-temperature equipment such as walk-in and reach-in freezers. A maker's valve guide puts it this way: installed in the suction line, it throttles the vapor leaving the evaporator until the compressor can handle the load.

Why does high suction pressure overload a motor? Denser suction vapor means more refrigerant mass pumped on every stroke, and the motor has to do more work to compress it. The valve does not remove load. It meters the vapor in, so the compressor sees a limited inlet pressure while the coil pulls itself back down.

Where it sits and how it senses

The article places the CPR in the suction line between the evaporator and the compressor, and says nothing else should sit between the valve outlet and the compressor, so the outlet senses true crankcase pressure. It is a close-on-rise valve: the outlet pressure pushes toward closing, and an adjustable spring pushes toward opening. If the pressure at the outlet rises above the spring setting, the valve closes down toward that setting. The inlet pressure acts on both sides of the internal parts equally, so it cancels. That is why a CPR responds to what the compressor sees, not to what the coil is doing.

CPR versus EPR

An evaporator pressure regulator looks similar from the outside, and the two get mixed up on parts counters. They protect different things, and the difference is which pressure each one watches.

  • EPR: an article on EPRs says it senses inlet (upstream) pressure. It sits on the evaporator side and keeps coil pressure from falling below its setting. It protects the product and the coil. See our EPR lesson.
  • CPR: senses outlet (downstream) pressure at the compressor end. It keeps suction pressure from rising above its setting. It protects the motor.

One holds a floor under the coil, the other holds a ceiling over the compressor. A system can carry both, as the diagram shows. The CPR article adds two interaction warnings: it is not recommended on a system that also uses a maximum-operating-pressure expansion valve, since pulldown can lengthen, and on a system with a discharge bypass valve for capacity control the CPR setting must be higher than the bypass setting. For the bypass side, see our hot-gas bypass lesson.

Setting a CPR with an ammeter and gauge

A trade article on servicing CPRs describes the method. Let the system sit off long enough for the evaporator pressure to rise, which imitates the end of a defrost or a startup. Turn the adjustment screw fully out (normally counterclockwise). Start the system and watch the crankcase pressure gauge and the compressor current with an ammeter. Slowly turn the screw in (clockwise) until the current reaches the highest value the compressor maker allows. Another article says to set the valve at start-up, when evaporator pressure is normally highest, low enough to protect the compressor but not so high that it hurts capacity or pulldown time. A third piece gives the alternative of setting several pounds below the compressor maker's maximum allowable suction pressure on a low-side gauge. That article does not say how many pounds. Do not guess; take the figure from the compressor maker.

The ammeter reading needs care. Read each leg of a three-phase unit, and compare to the maker's rated current, not just to a nameplate you assume. See our clamp-meter lesson. If two CPRs are piped in parallel, the article says to adjust both at the same time and rate so the load splits evenly.

Worked example (ours, with assumed numbers)

Assumptions, all ours: a walk-in freezer with one compressor whose maker lists a maximum running current of 12.0 A. The compressor trips on overload a few minutes after each defrost. With the CPR screw all the way out, we measure 15.5 A at the peak. The compressor maker's maximum allowable suction pressure is a number we do not have, so we do not use one.

  1. Compare the amps. 15.5 A minus 12.0 A is 3.5 A over the limit. As a fraction, 3.5 / 12.0 = 0.29, about 29 percent over. The motor is overloaded, which fits the trip.
  2. Confirm the cause. Gauge on the suction side. The peak current comes in the first minutes after defrost ends, when the gauge shows its highest suction pressure. That matches a high-suction overload, not a bad capacitor or low voltage. Check line voltage and the run capacitor before touching the valve.
  3. Simulate the worst case. Switch the system off and let the coil warm until the gauge settles. Turn the CPR screw fully out. Restart with the ammeter on the compressor lead.
  4. Turn in slowly. Make a small turn, wait for the pressure to settle, read the amps again. Stop when the peak is at 12.0 A and not over. If the peak reads 11.4 A and the next small turn gives 12.3 A, back it off to the 11.4 A setting.
  5. Check pulldown. Time the pulldown to the normal box temperature. The valve should lengthen pulldown only a little. If it takes much longer, the setting is lower than needed.
  6. Record it. Write the final gauge reading and amps on the service tag. That pressure belongs to this machine and these conditions, not to any other.

Our numbers are illustrations. The goal is the sequence: measure, find the limit, simulate the worst case, adjust in small steps, stop at the maker's limit.

Symptoms of a mis-set or stuck CPR

We found no trade article that gives a full symptom list, so the entries below come from what the valve does, with the sources noted.

  • Overload trips after every defrost, amps over the limit: set too high, or the valve is stuck open or not regulating. The servicing article says the main test is whether the valve regulates outlet pressure, and that dirt, solder or brazing residue lodged inside causes many failures. Our reading of the valve is that a stuck-open valve gives no protection.
  • Long pulldown, low suction pressure, box slow to recover: set too low, or stuck partly closed. A guide article warns against setting it so high that it penalizes capacity, and the converse of that is a setting so low that pulldown is slow. Compare to the same box on a day the valve is wide open.
  • A refrigerant leak at the spring housing: the servicing article says a leak around the spring housing means replace the valve.
  • Pressure swinging: we found no source; check the expansion valve first, as in our floodback lesson.

For a stuck valve, the servicing article suggests turning the adjustment fully in with the system running to open the valve and maybe dislodge debris, and replacing the valve if that fails. It also says few field repairs can be made on these valves.

Why you see them less on newer equipment

We did not find a source that counts CPRs on new equipment or explains a trend, so we will not claim one. What the sources give are reasons the valve is not always chosen: it lengthens pulldown if set low, it is not recommended with a maximum-operating-pressure expansion valve, and and, in our reading, it adds a restriction to the suction line. Whether a given walk-in has one depends on the maker, so look at the suction line and the equipment data before assuming. Compare it to the defrost method in play; our defrost lesson covers how a warm coil is produced in the first place.

Field card: CPR valves

The CPR watches its outlet and puts a ceiling on suction pressure.

  • Protects the compressor motor after defrost or a shutdown, when suction pressure is high.
  • Sits in the suction line near the compressor; nothing between its outlet and the compressor.
  • EPR senses inlet and holds a floor; CPR senses outlet and holds a ceiling.
  • Set: valve fully out, warm coil, start, turn in slowly until amps reach the maker's maximum.
  • Over amps after defrost: set high or stuck open. Slow pulldown: set low or stuck closed.
  • Do not pair casually with a MOP expansion valve; follow the maker.
