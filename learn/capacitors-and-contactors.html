Capacitors and Contactors

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two of the most commonly replaced electrical parts in an air conditioner or heat pump are the capacitor and the contactor. Understanding what each one does makes the symptoms make sense, and makes the safety rules easier to remember.

What a capacitor does

A capacitor stores electrical energy by holding charge on two closely spaced conductors with an insulator between them. It can give that energy back quickly. In an HVAC motor it helps a single-phase motor start and run: the capacitor shifts the timing of the current in a second winding, which creates the rotating magnetic field a single-phase motor cannot make on its own. A start capacitor helps get the motor going and is dropped out of the circuit once it is up to speed. A run capacitor stays connected while the motor runs. Many outdoor units use a dual-run capacitor that serves the compressor and the condenser fan from one can, with separate terminals for each.

What a contactor does

A contactor is a heavy-duty electrically operated switch, a close cousin of a relay that is built for higher current and voltage. A small coil becomes an electromagnet when it is energized. It pulls the contacts closed so power can flow to the compressor and fan. When the coil is de-energized, a spring opens the contacts again. The coil is usually fed by the thermostat's 24 volt circuit, which is how a low-voltage signal controls a line-voltage load. See Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

Signs of trouble, at a general level

Capacitor symptoms people report include a hum with no start, a motor that starts slowly or stalls, a fan that must be pushed to spin, and a compressor that trips on overload. Physical clues include a bulging or domed top, oil leakage or a burnt smell. Contactor clues include a buzzing or chattering sound, contacts that are pitted, burned or stuck, and a unit that will not start or will not shut off. None of these alone proves the part is bad. Other faults can mimic them, so diagnosis follows the manufacturer's procedure and a meter reading, not a guess.

Stored energy and discharge safety

A capacitor can keep its charge after power is removed. Energy, not just voltage, determines how dangerous it is, and capacitors can also rebound a little after being discharged. A damaged capacitor can also fail violently under stress. For all these reasons, the standard practice is:

  • Open the disconnect and follow your lockout procedure first.
  • Prove the power is off with a meter you have checked.
  • Treat every capacitor as charged until it has been discharged by the method your employer and the manufacturer approve. This lesson does not teach a discharge technique. Learn it hands-on from a qualified person.
  • Never discharge it by shorting with a bare screwdriver or tool unless that is your trained, approved procedure. A violent spark can injure you and damage the part.
  • Keep your hands off the terminals and keep the capacitor can pointed away from your face.

Replacement habits

Use a replacement with the ratings the manufacturer specifies. Mismatched capacitance or voltage ratings can overheat motors or shorten their life. Never replace a contactor or capacitor with something made to look similar, and never jumper a contactor closed to make a unit run.

Field card: Capacitors and Contactors

A capacitor starts and runs a single-phase motor. A contactor is the coil-operated power switch.

  • Capacitor = starts or runs single-phase motors. Contactor = coil-controlled switch for power.
  • Humming without starting, bulged tops and chattering are clues, not proof.
  • Capacitors can stay charged after power is off.
  • Lock out, prove dead, then discharge only by your approved method.
  • Match the manufacturer's ratings on replacement.
  • Never jumper a contactor closed.
