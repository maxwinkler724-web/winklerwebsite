Aircraft Hangar Heating and Ventilation: Big Door Heat Loss, Radiant and In-Floor Heat, Fuel Vapor Rules and Destratification

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hangar is a very tall, very leaky box with a door the size of a wall, and the thing parked inside it is full of fuel. That combination changes how we pick heaters, where we hang them and what we exhaust. This lesson covers the heating and ventilating side for small airports and corporate hangars, using public military design criteria, a state safety order and maker pages.

Check the rules that apply to you

Check the rules that apply to you. Hangar heating is controlled by the fire code, the mechanical and fuel gas codes, NFPA 409 and the heater maker's instructions, and each state and city adopts its own editions. Nothing here replaces the adopted code, the authority having jurisdiction or the manual on the heater. We could not open the ICC code text for this page, so we do not quote the IMC, IFGC or IFC hangar sections here. Look them up on your state's code page.

Why a hangar is different

Three things make hangar work different from a warehouse. First, the door opening is huge, so every time it opens the room swaps air with outdoors. Second, the people and equipment sit at the floor, but the hot air wants to sit at the roof, 20 or 30 feet up. Third, fuel vapor is heavier than air and settles low, so anything that can ignite it needs to stay out of the low zone. The Department of Defense hangar design standard, UFC 4-211-01N, builds its mechanical chapter around exactly these problems: infiltration, overhead radiant heat, floor heat, destratifiers and exhaust.

The door is the biggest heat loss

UFC 4-211-01N tells designers to assume an infiltration rate of two air changes per hour in the hangar bay, and ties that number to nylon brush seals on the sliding doors. That is a closed-door leakage figure. When the door stands open the exchange is far higher, and nobody has a published number that fits every hangar, so we treat open-door airflow as an assumption you must state.

The same UFC adds two control ideas worth copying in concept. A switch on the hangar doors overrides the space thermostat and stops bay heating while the doors are open, and a minimum-temperature thermostat set at 34°F overrides that shutdown in freezing weather. After the doors close, the room thermostat takes over again, and the UFC says to plan for heating recovery in 60 minutes. One maker of tube heaters also suggests concentrating the heat near the main door, and using two-stage units so they can recover door losses but cycle less at other times. See Air Curtains Over Commercial Doors for another way to cut door losses.

Worked example: one door-open event (Our own numbers)

Every input below is an assumption we chose for practice. Your hangar will differ.

  • Bay: 100 ft long, 80 ft wide, 24 ft to the roof, so volume = 100 × 80 × 24 = 192,000 cubic feet (assumed).
  • Inside air held at 55°F, outdoor air at 25°F, so ΔT = 30°F (assumed).
  • Door stays open 15 minutes (assumed).
  • During those 15 minutes, one quarter of the bay's air is swapped with outdoor air (assumed): 192,000 × 0.25 = 48,000 cubic feet in 15 minutes, which is 48,000 / 15 = 3,200 cfm.

Step 1, heat lost while open. Sensible heat = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT = 1.08 × 3,200 × 30 = 103,680 Btu/h. The ACHR News article on this formula shows where 1.08 comes from (60 minutes per hour × 0.075 lb per cubic foot × 0.24 Btu per lb per degree F) and warns it drifts when the air is far from 70°F, so treat the answer as an estimate. Fifteen minutes is a quarter hour, so heat lost = 103,680 × 0.25 = 25,920 Btu.

Step 2, energy to reheat the air afterward. Mass of air in the bay = 192,000 × 0.075 = 14,400 lb. Heat to raise it 1°F = 14,400 × 0.24 = 3,456 Btu per degree F. If the 25,920 Btu came out of the whole bay's air, the air would drop 25,920 / 3,456 = 7.5°F, and getting it back needs 14,400 × 0.24 × 7.5 = 25,920 Btu. The two steps agree, which is a good arithmetic check.

Step 3, time to recover. If the heaters put a net 100,000 Btu/h into the room (assumed), 25,920 / 100,000 = 0.26 hour, or about 16 minutes. In practice it takes longer, because the slab, walls and aircraft are cold masses that soak up heat. One therm is 100,000 Btu, so this event is about 0.26 therm at best by definition (Our own conversion). Run it several times a day in January and the door cycle, not the walls, drives the gas bill.

Radiant tubes, in-floor heat and unit heaters

Overhead radiant tube heaters. Radiant heat warms floors, tools and the aircraft skin instead of the air, so it works when the room air keeps leaving through the door. The UFC calls for natural gas-fired tube heaters in the aircraft servicing area when overhead radiant heat is used, and says the flame or element must be shielded so that it does not set off optical flame detectors or release the fire suppression system. For clearances, venting and upkeep of tube heaters, see Infrared Tube Heaters for Warehouses and Garages.

In-floor hydronic heat. Warm tubing in the slab keeps the floor, and the cold-soaked aircraft wheels and lower surfaces, warmer, and it has no flame in the bay. The UFC says to investigate under-floor heating when the outdoor design temperature is below −10°F. A slab is heavy mass, so in our own judgment it responds slowly and suits steady holding temperature better than fast recovery after a door cycle. Basics of slab loops are in Radiant Floor Heating Basics.

Unit heaters. Small garage-type gas unit heaters are common in tiny private hangars, but they blow hot air toward the roof and use an open flame or hot element. Check the adopted code before putting one in any bay that stores or services aircraft. See Garage and Workshop Heaters for the general picture.

Heater height and the fuel vapor zone

NFPA 409 is the standard on aircraft hangars; we name it in plain text because we do not link copies of it. In the 2011 edition we read, the heating and ventilating section does not allow equipment with an open flame or glowing element in the storage and servicing areas except as allowed for suspended or elevated heaters. Those heaters must be at least 10 feet above the upper surface of wings or engine enclosures. In shops and offices, the bottom of a heater must be at least 8 feet above the floor. A heater maker's hangar page repeats the 10 foot figure, and recommends high-output tube heaters for these high mounts. Editions and local amendments differ, so confirm the edition your authority has adopted.

The same edition also keeps return-air openings at least 10 feet above the floor and does not allow supply openings in the floor.

The low zone has a published shape. California's hangar safety order classifies the whole hangar floor as Class I, Division 2, up to 18 inches above the floor. Around aircraft engines and fuel tanks, the classified area runs from the floor up to 5 feet above the upper surface of wings and engine enclosures. The order also says aircraft battery chargers and their controls must not be located or operated in hazardous areas. Those rules are written for electrical equipment, but they show why a heater, its controls and any ignition-capable equipment belongs high and away from the floor band shown in the drawing.

Ventilation for fuel vapor and exhaust

The UFC says toxic fumes and combustible vapor made in work areas must be exhausted directly to the outside, and names the airframe, corrosion control and electric shops as likely sources. Where fuel system work is done, it calls for a fuel line and tank purge system plus fuel low-level vapor exhaust and fuel cell vapor exhaust systems. Because heaters and vapor exhaust fight each other for the same air, a vapor exhaust fan that pulls air from the floor level also pulls cold outdoor makeup air in, which adds to the load in the example above.

Destratification in a tall bay

Warm air collects under the roof, so a slow, large ceiling fan can push it back down. One maker article on hangar moisture says running a large fan in reverse in winter moves warm air trapped near the ceiling down to floor level. The UFC also says designers should consider cold jet destratifiers, if an economic analysis supports them. Fans near sprinkler heads and flame detectors need care; the full discussion is in HVLS Fans and Destratification in Warehouses and Gyms. A fan stirring air near a radiant tube heater does not change the 10 foot clearance above the wing.

Condensation on aircraft and on the slab

Water forms on any surface that sits below the dew point of the air touching it. A heater maker's hangar page says overnight cooling pushes the aircraft skin below the dew point, and that keeping surfaces warmer than the dew point stops condensation. The same page gives under 45 percent relative humidity as the point where corrosion slows, according to most experts. A fan maker says the cold slab "sweats" in humid fall and spring weather when door opening lets in warm, humid air. So when a warm humid day follows a cold night, the problem is often worse after you open the door, not before. Both makers sell the fix they describe, so treat their claims as maker views, not independent findings.

What to remember

  • Assume the door, not the walls, is your biggest load, and state your open-door airflow.
  • Radiant heat warms surfaces; in-floor heat is slow but flame free; unit heaters need a code check.
  • Keep flame and glowing heaters high; the floor band is a vapor zone.
  • Exhaust fumes and fuel vapor outdoors; do not recirculate them.
  • Move warm air down and keep surfaces above dew point.

Field card: Aircraft Hangar Heating and Ventilation

High flame, low vapor, big door.

  • Heat lost through an open door = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT; state your cfm assumption.
  • Reheat the air volume = volume × 0.075 × 0.24 × ΔT.
  • NFPA 409 (2011 edition we read): suspended heaters at least 10 ft above wings and engine enclosures; confirm your adopted edition.
  • Floor band up to 18 in is the classified low zone in California's order.
  • UFC 4-211-01N: two air changes per hour design infiltration, door switch overrides heat, 34°F minimum, 60 minute recovery.
  • UFC: look at under-floor heat below −10°F design; exhaust fumes and vapor outdoors.
  • Keep surfaces above dew point to avoid sweating aircraft and slab.
