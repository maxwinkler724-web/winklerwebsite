Window Coverings and Your Heating and Cooling: Blinds, Shades, Drapes, Film and Awnings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A window is the thinnest part of the building envelope, so what you hang on it matters to your furnace and your air conditioner. This lesson covers what each kind of covering does in summer and winter, where it goes wrong, and how to put numbers on it.

Why windows matter

A university extension fact sheet on energy-saving window treatments says that in a typical single family home, 25% to 35% of the heat is lost through windows. A federal energy agency page (plain text, not linked here) puts it at about 30% of a home's heating energy. In summer the same page says about 76% of the sunlight that falls on standard double-pane windows enters as heat. A covering cannot make a window as good as a wall, but it can shave both numbers.

Summer: block the sun before it gets through the glass

Sunlight that passes through glass turns to heat once it lands on a floor, sofa or the back of an interior shade. A university extension guide to home shading recommends exterior shading first on the east, west and south sides, using trees, awnings, solar screens or window film, because it blocks solar radiation before it enters. It treats blinds and drapes as the secondary option: they stop the light near the window but the heat is already inside the glass. The federal energy agency page agrees that exterior shutters and shades are the most effective option for cutting solar heat gain, and it says awnings can cut summer solar heat gain by up to 65% on south-facing windows and 77% on west-facing windows.

Summer choices, one at a time

  • Awnings and shade screens. The federal energy agency page says to use light-colored, opaque, tightly woven fabric, to leave ventilation openings so hot air is not trapped, and to roll up or remove awnings in winter so the sun can warm the house.
  • Window film. Film is rated by solar heat gain coefficient and visible transmittance, both from 0 to 1; a lower solar heat gain number blocks more heat. The page says films suit long cooling seasons, help most on east and west windows, and also block winter sun. It also warns that tinted films can damage sealed double-pane units and that most window makers void warranties if film goes on one. Ask the window maker first.
  • Interior blinds, shades and drapes. They help, but less than exterior shading. Keep sunlit windows covered in summer and open the shaded ones for daylight.

Winter: trap a layer of still air

At night the job reverses: you want to slow heat leaving through the glass. The university extension fact sheet says every treatment works by holding air between itself and the window, so it must fit tightly at the edges and sit about an inch from the glass. Its figures for heat loss reduction show how much fit matters:

  • Standard slat blinds: only about 6% to 7%. They are for light and airflow, not insulation.
  • Ordinary unsealed drapes: no more than about 10%, because room air circulates behind them like a tunnel. Lined drapes sealed on all four edges reach about 25%.
  • A roller shade with side tracks or a reflective coating: about 45%.
  • Roman shades: roughly 65% to 75%, with R-values of 3 to 4. Insulated panels and shutters: about 75% to 90%.

A federal building-technologies fact sheet on cellular (honeycomb) shades, from field tests and modeling, reports up to 20% less heating energy and up to 15% less total heating and cooling energy than no shades. It adds that cooling-season benefits need more research. It gives no window U-factor, so the example below uses an assumption.

Open and close with the sun

The extension fact sheet says to open treatments on sunny windows during the day to admit solar heat and close them at night. On sunny winter days, open the south-facing coverings; north windows get little sun, so those coverings can stay closed. The federal energy agency page notes that 75% of residential window coverings stay in one position every day, so moving them twice a day is already better than most households.

Keep coverings off heat sources

If a warm-air register sits below a window, the extension fact sheet warns against letting its airflow run between the drapery and the glass, where the heat is wasted. A metal or plastic deflector over the register sends the air into the room. Our own advice: do not let fabric rest on a baseboard heater or radiator cover, and do not let drapes cover a floor register or a return grille, since blocked airflow hurts comfort and can starve the system of air. Hanging drapes so they stop short of the heater is the simple fix.

Condensation behind tight coverings

A university extension page on moisture says problems can appear when indoor relative humidity is above 50%, that in cold weather the humidity must be lower to prevent condensation, and that 25% in winter to 50% in summer is generally considered healthy. Our own reasoning follows from that: a tight, well-insulating covering keeps room heat off the glass, so the glass behind it runs colder, and the trapped air can reach its dew point. If you see water or mold on the glass or sill, first reduce moisture (exhaust fans, less indoor drying) and then open the covering for part of the day. See Condensation on Windows in Winter. Our own caution: do not lower the humidity so far that the air feels uncomfortably dry; adjust a little at a time.

Child cord safety

A federal consumer product safety agency page says children have died by strangulation on window covering cords, that it can happen within moments even with an adult nearby, and that it is often silent. Its advice: go cordless where young children are present, keep cribs, beds, furniture and toys away from window cords, shorten dangling pull cords so children cannot reach them, check that cord stops sit 1 to 2 inches from the head rail when the blind is fully lowered, anchor continuous-loop cords to the wall or floor, and replace older blinds, because most blinds sold before November 2000 have inner lift cords that can form a loop. The page says these steps reduce the hazard but only cordless coverings remove it. A cordless cellular shade is therefore a good match for a home with small children. More in Childproofing Your Home Heating and Cooling.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions. A 3 ft by 5 ft window, 70 F inside, 20 F outside, for a 12 hour night. Heat loss through a window is Q = U x A x temperature difference, where U is in Btu/hr-ft2-F.

  • Assumed: bare window U = 0.5. This is our assumption, not a measured value. The same assumption sets the cellular shade case: with a shade closed and sealed at the edges, we assume U = 0.3 (a 40% cut). No source we read gives a cellular shade U-factor, so treat this as an estimate. It sits inside the range of the extension figures above (about 25% to 75% for tight coverings).
  • Area A = 3 x 5 = 15 ft2. Temperature difference = 70 - 20 = 50 F.

Bare window: Q = 0.5 x 15 x 50 = 375 Btu/hr. Over 12 hours: 375 x 12 = 4,500 Btu per night.

Cellular shade closed: Q = 0.3 x 15 x 50 = 225 Btu/hr. Over 12 hours: 225 x 12 = 2,700 Btu per night.

Difference: 375 - 225 = 150 Btu/hr, or 4,500 - 2,700 = 1,800 Btu per night saved on this one window. Multiply by the number of windows and you can see why the effect adds up, and why fit and edge sealing matter. This ignores air leakage and sun on the glass, which change the real result.

Field card: Window Coverings and Your Heating and Cooling

Shade outside in summer, seal inside at night in winter.

  • Exterior shading beats interior shading in summer: it blocks the sun before the glass.
  • Winter coverings work by holding still air about an inch from the glass; seal the edges.
  • Slat blinds barely insulate; cellular and Roman shades and sealed panels do more.
  • Close at night; open south windows on sunny winter days.
  • Keep fabric off registers and heaters; use a deflector over a register under a window.
  • Tight coverings can bring condensation: manage indoor humidity first.
  • Go cordless where young children live.
  • Q = U x A x temperature difference.
