Underfloor Air Distribution and Displacement Ventilation: Stratification, Supply Temperature and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most commercial air systems blow cold air from the ceiling and mix the whole room. Two other designs do the opposite: they deliver air near the floor at low speed and let heat carry the stale, warm air up and out. This lesson explains underfloor air distribution (UFAD) and displacement ventilation, why their supply air is warmer, where they fit, and what a tech can check.

Mixing versus stratified

A mixing system, the common kind, throws air from ceiling diffusers fast enough that the room air ends up at nearly one temperature. A stratified system lets the room layer itself: cooler air near the floor, warmer air near the ceiling. The Center for the Built Environment (CBE) at UC Berkeley describes the trade-off plainly in a paper by Webster and Bauman: more stratification, from less airflow or less mixing for a given heat load, saves energy, but can cost comfort. A trade article on UFAD in HPAC makes the same point from the other side, saying a UFAD room mixes air only in the lower 4 to 6 feet or so, the part people occupy.

The part people occupy is called the occupied zone. Titus's displacement design guide treats a seated person's head level as 3.6 feet above the floor and keeps the temperature difference across the occupied zone to about 3.6 F. The height where the cool lower layer meets the warmer upper layer is the stratification height, and the guide says it has to stay above the occupied zone for the design to work.

Underfloor air distribution

In UFAD the space between the structural slab and a raised floor is the supply duct. An air handler pressurizes that plenum slightly and floor diffusers or fan-powered terminals release the air into the room. Titus's UFAD application guide says a pressurized plenum is the most common type and typically runs between 0.05 and 0.10 in. w.g. above the room. It calls the neutral plenum, which uses fans in the diffusers or ducted supply, rare. A 2002 paper by Luskay and Sellers of Portland Energy Conservation puts the range at 0.05 to 0.2 in. w.c.; the spread across sources shows the design value belongs to the project, not to a rule of thumb.

Because the outlets sit where people are, the supply is warmer. Titus's guide gives about 63 to 68 F for UFAD, compared with around 55 F for a conventional overhead system. Luskay and Sellers give the same 63 to 68 F range and add that in humid climates the coil may need to make 55 F air to dry it, and then return air is blended in to bring it up. Price Industries' manual separately describes recirculating fan terminals that mix room and plenum air for perimeter zones.

Displacement ventilation

Displacement ventilation is a wall-level version. Titus's guide describes large, low-velocity diffusers on the wall or on columns that spread cool air across the floor, with the return high in the space. It gives 50 to 70 fpm as typical outlet velocity and 62 to 70 F as typical supply, and discourages supply below 65 F where seated people are close to an outlet. An article in Consulting-Specifying Engineer keeps supply above 63 F to avoid drafts and holds floor-level velocity near or below 50 fpm.

Where each one fits

  • Offices (open plan): Titus calls the open plan office the typical UFAD application. Conference rooms and perimeter zones with changing loads need separate handling, such as fan terminals.
  • Theaters, lecture rooms and meeting halls: Titus's displacement guide names large, tall spaces such as theaters, lecture halls and atriums as good fits for displacement ventilation.
  • Limits: The same guide says ceilings under 9 feet and rooms cut up by partitions are poor fits. The trade article says heat gains above about 15 Btu/h per sq ft need extra care or added cooling. Titus's guide also says not to use displacement where hazardous chemical spills could happen.

The UFAD field results from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Fisk and others, 2005) were favorable but modest: only about 2 to 4 F floor-to-ceiling rise in one office, above-average thermal satisfaction, and air change effectiveness near that of overhead systems. The authors say other studies should confirm it.

Plenum leakage and temperature loss

The plenum is part of the supply duct, so a leak is a duct leak. Luskay and Sellers measured an estimated 16 to 18 percent leakage in one case study and wrote that getting under 20 percent was hard even in new construction, so they recommend a leakage test at turnover and again every few years or after remodels. HPAC reports about 5 percent measured at one newer building, and notes the worst leaks are usually through wall cavities under the floor, not through floor panels. Titus's guide says to seal every knockout and hole in drywall below the floor. It also says runs longer than roughly 50 to 65 feet from the supply connection to the farthest diffuser see thermal losses, which means the air warms up on the way; the farthest diffusers can run warmer than the near ones.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own arithmetic. A 5,000 sq ft office zone has a stated sensible load of 60,000 Btu/h. We use cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT).

Mixing system. Supply 55 F, room 75 F.

  1. ΔT = 75 − 55 = 20 F.
  2. 1.08 × 20 = 21.6.
  3. 60,000 ÷ 21.6 = 2,778 cfm.
  4. 2,778 ÷ 5,000 = 0.56 cfm per sq ft.

Stratified system. Stated supply 63 F, stated return/exhaust 82 F.

  1. ΔT = 82 − 63 = 19 F.
  2. 1.08 × 19 = 20.52.
  3. 60,000 ÷ 20.52 = 2,924 cfm.
  4. 2,924 ÷ 5,000 = 0.58 cfm per sq ft.

What changes. The stratified case needs 2,924 − 2,778 = 146 cfm more, about 5 percent. The airflow is nearly the same because the warm return makes up for the warmer supply. But the supply is 8 F warmer, so in our reading the coil can run warmer, and the air going back at 82 F is warmer than the room. The return/exhaust figure here is a stated assumption: real designers pick it from the stratification the room will actually produce, using a maker's or CBE's method, and the load that counts toward the occupied zone is not always the whole room load. Treat this as a feel for the numbers, not a design.

Field card: Underfloor Air and Displacement Ventilation

Warm supply, low speed, floor level in, high out.

  • UFAD: pressurized plenum (sources: about 0.05 to 0.10 in. w.g. typical) with floor diffusers.
  • Displacement: low wall diffusers (about 50 to 70 fpm), high return.
  • Supply runs about 62 to 68 F, not 55 F. Check the design.
  • Check plenum pressure against the design value, with floor panels in place.
  • Look for diffusers blocked by furniture, boxes, carpet or rugs. The maker defines the clear area.
  • Look for plenum debris and open knockouts or holes below the floor.
  • Confirm the room sensor sits where the design says. Sources we read gave no UFAD sensor height.
  • Ceiling under 9 ft or lots of partitions is a bad fit for displacement.

Educational summary only. Follow manufacturer instructions and your site procedures. Print this page to keep the card.

Service checks

  • Plenum pressure. Price's manual describes a relative pressure sensor that reads the pressure between the plenum and the room, and says to put floor tiles back before checking outlet flow in a pressurized plenum. Compare readings to the project's design value, not to a number from this page.
  • Blocked diffusers. Furniture, cabinets, stored boxes or rugs over a floor diffuser starve that spot. Titus notes that the maker defines the clear area around each diffuser, and that diffusers are installed through the floor panel after carpet goes in, so check the cut-out and the carpet edge.
  • Plenum cleanliness. Price's manual says its fan terminals are not designed to run in a dusty construction environment because dust affects the fan wheel and motor. A dirty plenum also puts debris into the supply air, so inspect during access panel removal.
  • Leakage. Open knockouts, unsealed wall bases and gaps at duct connections waste conditioned air.
  • Sensors. In stratified rooms air temperature changes with height, so a sensor mounted at the wrong height reads a different temperature than the occupants feel. Titus uses 3.6 feet for seated head level. Where the sensor belongs for a given UFAD job is a design question.
