Chiller and Compressor Oil Analysis: Moisture, Acid, Wear Metals and Trending Results

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A chiller compressor hides its bearings and gears inside a case, but its oil carries clues about them. A lab report on that oil is a cheap way to look inside without opening anything. This lesson shows what the report lists, how a clean sample is pulled, and why a run of results tells you more than any one line.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. Sample points, intervals, bottle types and alarm limits differ by machine and by lab. This page is general education built from the pages listed at the bottom. Your chiller's manual and your lab's instructions win.

Why plants sample the oil

Oil circulates through the bearings and carries away whatever they shed. A lab's description of chiller oil testing says regular analysis helps judge internal mechanical condition, and that chemical analysis can pick up harmful acids, water that causes corrosion, and abnormal metal wear particles. Another lab page puts it plainly: results "may signal potential problems when they deviate from their normal state." A trade article quotes one service contractor who calls the test a benchmark to begin monitoring from, and recommends it at least yearly. The same article says an oil change should happen only when analysis indicates one.

Pulling a clean, representative sample

The lab can only judge what you send. A trade article on sampling mistakes says field quality matters most, because the lab analyzes what you send and nothing can fix a bad sample afterward. What the sources say about technique:

  • Where. Trane's support page names the oil separator of the circuit or chiller as the best place to pull a sample for analysis, with the bottom of the compressor (once isolated) as a second choice. It also says to use the same location every time if you are trending a chiller.
  • Running or stopped. A contractor quoted in a trade article advises taking the sample while the chiller is operating. A general industrial article agrees that oil should be sampled at normal operating temperature, because oil that sits still settles and separates. If a unit has to be shut down first, it says to sample within minutes.
  • Bottle. Trane says sealed glass is standard and sealed plastic is acceptable if it ships and gets processed quickly. A lubricant maker's bulletin says to confirm the bottle is clean and not to overfill it.
  • Flush the line. The same bulletin says to purge stagnant oil from the valve first so you collect oil that was actually circulating. Our own note: a refrigerant system holds pressure, so the actual port procedure is the maker's job to define. Follow the manual and site safety rules.
  • Label. A sampling article says mislabeled samples attach results to the wrong machine and create false trends. It recommends recording location, operating state, load and runtime on the lab form. Our own habit: write the date and the compressor hours on the bottle and the form, since the trend depends on them.

What a typical report lists

One lab lists its chiller oil tests as kinematic viscosity at 40 °C (ASTM D445), acid number (ASTM D664), water content (ASTM D1533) and metals by spectroscopy (ASTM D5185): iron, silicon, copper, aluminum, lead and tin. Another lab page adds a ferrous density test that tracks iron wear particles. Reading them one at a time:

  • Viscosity. A lab page calls it the most important physical trait of a lubricant. It says out-of-range viscosity can indicate the oil separator is not working properly, and that refrigerant dissolved in the oil can slow the measurement.
  • Moisture. Water reduces efficiency and can cause corrosion and freezing problems. The page names Karl Fischer testing as one way to measure water (ppm means parts per million). A fluid-condition article calls water a common and potentially harmful contaminant that speeds lubricant breakdown and damages metal.
  • Acid number (TAN). A lab page says it matters because it affects how well the lubricant mixes with the refrigerant. For what burned-out acid does to a system, see the burnout lesson linked below.
  • Wear metals. The lab pages say elemental metals help identify the source of contamination and that excessive wear debris can point to bearing failure. They do not map each metal to a part, so we will not either. Ask your lab and your maker which metals match which parts on your model. One lab warns of a sharp increase in ferrous wear or particle size, which it says signals abnormal wear that is getting worse.

The numbers on a report are not pass or fail. A maker's sample report states that property values should not be construed as specifications. Caution and alarm lines are set by the lab or the chiller maker. We give none here.

Why the trend beats one result

A single number says where you are. A series says where you are heading. A lubricant company's basics paper says trending shows gradual changes in fluid properties, contaminants and wear debris so corrective action can be planned instead of rushed, and calls it far superior to hit-or-miss sampling. It also says an alert result does not automatically mean imminent failure, so re-sample to confirm before major action. That is why the first clean report on a healthy machine matters: it becomes the baseline the later ones are compared with.

Worked example: four samples over two years

Our own made-up numbers for teaching, not from any real chiller. A centrifugal chiller is sampled four times over two years from the same point. Suppose every value below sits under the caution line your lab uses for that test (the lab sets those lines, and we are not quoting any). Sample 1 is the baseline.

Iron (ppm): 4, 5, 9, 16.

  1. Our own change, sample 2 vs 1: 5 − 4 = +1 ppm. Percent: 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25, so +25%.
  2. Sample 3 vs 2: 9 − 5 = +4 ppm. 4 ÷ 5 = 0.80, so +80%.
  3. Sample 4 vs 3: 16 − 9 = +7 ppm. 7 ÷ 9 = 0.778, so about +78%.
  4. Overall: 16 − 4 = +12 ppm. 12 ÷ 4 = 3.00, so +300%. Iron is four times the baseline (16 ÷ 4 = 4).

Moisture (ppm): 40, 45, 60, 110.

  1. Our own change, sample 2 vs 1: 45 − 40 = +5 ppm. 5 ÷ 40 = 0.125, so +12.5%.
  2. Sample 3 vs 2: 60 − 45 = +15 ppm. 15 ÷ 45 = 0.333, so about +33%.
  3. Sample 4 vs 3: 110 − 60 = +50 ppm. 50 ÷ 60 = 0.833, so about +83%.
  4. Overall: 110 − 40 = +70 ppm. 70 ÷ 40 = 1.75, so +175%. Moisture is 2.75 times the baseline (110 ÷ 40 = 2.75).

The call. Each jump in ppm is bigger than the one before it. Iron's jumps went +1, +4, +7 ppm and moisture's went +5, +15, +50. A single sample 4 might still sit under a lab's caution line, but a trend that speeds up is a reason to act: pull a confirming sample, tell the lab the history, and ask the maker or service contractor what to inspect. We do not claim a cause from these numbers. As the sources stress, one alert is not proof, and the lab and maker interpret it.

What rising moisture and metals can point to

We keep to what sources state. Rising water means moisture is getting in, and the lab pages warn of corrosion and freezing problems. A rising ferrous reading is called a sign of worsening wear. The sources we read do not say what rising copper means; one lab page lists copper only as part of general elemental analysis. Ask the lab before assuming a part is failing.

Where purge units and POE come in

A low-pressure chiller runs below atmospheric pressure, so leaks pull air and moisture in. The purge unit lesson covers how the purge removes non-condensables, and its pump-out run time is the plant's own early signal before the next lab report. POE oil raises the stakes on moisture: a trade article says POE is more hygroscopic than mineral oil, so it absorbs moisture faster when exposed to air, and that moisture plus heat can form acid. See Refrigerant Oils for why POE gets special handling, and Compressor Burnout Cleanup for acid testing.

Field card: Chiller and Compressor Oil Analysis

A trend is a story. One number is a snapshot.

  • Sample from the same point every time, with a clean sealed bottle.
  • Write the date and compressor hours on the bottle and the form.
  • Typical tests: viscosity, water (Karl Fischer), acid number, wear metals by spectroscopy.
  • Compute change and percent change between each pair of samples.
  • A speeding-up trend deserves a re-sample and a call, even under a caution line.
  • The lab or maker sets the limits. Do not invent your own.
  • POE is hygroscopic: keep systems and containers closed.
