Combining Run Capacitors in Parallel and Series: Microfarad Math, Voltage Ratings, Replacing a Dual Capacitor with Two Singles, and Safe Discharge

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The exact dual run capacitor is not on the truck, but a drawer of singles is. Parallel math is simple and forgiving. Series math is a trap for run duty. This lesson covers the arithmetic, the voltage and tolerance rules, how to land the wires when two singles replace one dual, and how to discharge the old part safely.

Parallel: microfarads add

Capacitors wired in parallel share the same two connection points, so the same voltage appears across each one. University physics notes state it plainly: the equivalent capacitance is the sum, C = C1 + C2 + ..., and the total charge divides among the parts in proportion to their sizes. A 35 microfarad (µF) part and a 10 µF part wired side by side behave like one 45 µF part. That is why parallel is the usual field method for building a value you do not have in stock.

Series: reciprocals add

In series the same charge passes through each capacitor, so the equivalent capacitance is smaller than the smallest part: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ... This is the same shape as resistors in parallel. For two parts the shortcut is C = (C1 x C2) / (C1 + C2). A university lab page works a 5 µF and 8 µF case: in series the pair is about 3.08 µF, in parallel 13 µF.

The voltage does not split evenly unless the parts match. Because the charge is equal, each part gets a voltage share inversely proportional to its capacitance, so the smaller part carries the larger share.

Voltage rating rules

  • Parallel: each capacitor sees the full circuit voltage, so each one must be rated at least that high. The label rating on a run capacitor is a ceiling. A higher rated part (for example 440 V in place of 370 V) is fine. A lower one never is.
  • Series: the total is shared, but unequally if values or real-world tolerances differ, and the smaller part can end up above its own rating even when the pair looks fine on paper. A run capacitor stays energized every minute the motor runs, so series is a poor choice for run duty. We do not recommend it.

Tolerance

Capacitor labels give a microfarad value with a tolerance, and values such as plus or minus 5 or 6 percent are common to see printed. Read the tolerance on the part in your hand. Our own arithmetic for a 45 µF target at plus or minus 6 percent: 45 x 0.06 = 2.7, so the acceptable range is 42.3 to 47.7 µF. At plus or minus 5 percent: 45 x 0.05 = 2.25, so 42.75 to 47.25 µF. A 5 µF fan part at plus or minus 6 percent runs 4.7 to 5.3 µF. When you combine two parts, the combined value lands inside the combined tolerance band, so test the finished assembly against the nameplate value rather than assuming. Testing methods are in Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter.

Dual run capacitors: HERM, FAN and C

A dual run capacitor is two capacitors in one can with three terminals. C is the common side shared by both. HERM goes to the compressor run circuit, and FAN goes to the condenser fan motor. The label shows something like 45/5 µF, with the compressor value first, though always confirm by the terminal markings on the can and the unit wiring diagram. How duals serve the circuit and why they fail is in Capacitors and Contactors and Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures, so we do not repeat it here.

Never swap a start capacitor in for a run capacitor

A start capacitor is built for a few seconds of duty and then drops out of the circuit. It is not made to stay connected while the motor runs. Put in a run circuit, it overheats and wears out, and its value usually does not match the run value anyway. Use run-rated parts only. See PSC Blower Motors, Speed Taps, Run Capacitors and Amp Checks for how run capacitors serve PSC motors.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions: the failed part is a 45/5 µF, 440 V dual. The truck has singles in 35, 10 and 5 µF, all rated 440 V. All values are our own assumptions for the arithmetic.

  1. Compressor side, 45 µF needed. Parallel: 35 + 10 = 45 µF. This matches exactly. Both parts are 440 V, equal to the rating of the original.
  2. Fan side, 5 µF needed. One 5 µF single does it directly. No math required.

Series contrast, same assumptions. Suppose the fan side only had two 10 µF singles. Series: 1/C = 1/10 + 1/10 = 2/10 = 1/5, so C = 5 µF. The number works, but it takes two parts and extra connections to do what a single 5 µF does, and it leaves a hidden hazard. With a matched pair the 440 V splits 220 V each. If the pair is unmatched, say 10 µF in series with 40 µF (40 x 10 / 50 = 8 µF total), the 10 µF part takes 40 / (10 + 40) = 80 percent of the voltage: 0.80 x 440 = 352 V, and the 40 µF part takes 88 V. Tolerance and aging shift the shares further. Parallel has no such sharing problem, so series stays a last resort at best.

Wiring two singles in place of a dual

  1. Note which wire was on HERM, which on FAN, and which on C. Photograph it.
  2. Mount the singles so they cannot move, in a bracket or strap, not hanging on the wires.
  3. Compressor side: put the 35 and the 10 in parallel. One terminal of each takes the old HERM wire, and the other terminal of each takes the old C wire.
  4. Fan side: one terminal of the 5 takes the old FAN wire, and the other takes the old C wire.
  5. The C wire now needs to reach three terminals. Use a pigtail or jumper to bring it to each, with firmly seated connectors. Do not stack loose connectors on a bent terminal.
  6. Restore power, check the amps and compare to the nameplate, using Checking a Run Capacitor Under Load with Amps and Volts.

Safe discharge

The federal work-practice rule for electrical safety says stored electric energy that could endanger personnel must be released, that capacitors must be discharged, and that high-capacitance parts must be short-circuited and grounded where the stored energy poses a risk. A capacitor handled for that purpose is to be treated as energized. A qualified person must also verify with test equipment that the parts are de-energized. In our shop practice: open the disconnect, apply your lock, and confirm dead with a meter you proved on a known source, as covered in Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Then discharge each capacitor through an insulated resistor tool and check across the terminals with the meter before touching them. Do not bridge the terminals with a bare screwdriver: the spark can burn you or damage the part.

When to tell the customer it is a temporary fix

  • You used a series pair, or any part rated below the circuit voltage.
  • The values are off from the nameplate beyond the stated tolerance.
  • The singles are not mounted securely, or the jumper wiring is crowded.
  • The old part showed heat damage, a bulged top or oil leaks, and the cause is not found.

Say it plainly: it runs now, and the exact part is still needed. Write it on the invoice, list the values installed, and schedule the proper replacement.

Field card: Combining Run Capacitors

Parallel adds, series shrinks, voltage rating never goes down.

  • Parallel: C = C1 + C2. Example: 35 + 10 = 45 µF.
  • Series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2. Two 10 µF give 5 µF, with unequal voltage sharing if mismatched.
  • Each part in parallel needs a rating at or above the circuit voltage.
  • Dual: HERM to compressor, FAN to fan motor, C common to both.
  • Never use a start capacitor in a run circuit.
  • Lock out, verify dead, discharge, then check with a meter.
  • Tell the customer when it is a temporary fix.
