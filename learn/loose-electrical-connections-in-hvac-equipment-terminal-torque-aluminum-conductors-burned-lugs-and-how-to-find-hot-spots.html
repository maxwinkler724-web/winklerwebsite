Loose Electrical Connections in HVAC Equipment: Torque, Aluminum and Hot Spots

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A loose wire under a screw rarely trips anything. It just gets warm, then hot, then burns the lug. Condensers and rooftop units shake and heat up and cool down every day, so terminations loosen. This lesson covers why that happens, how to tighten correctly, what is special about aluminum, and how to spot trouble early.

Why a loose connection heats up

Current flows through a connection only where the two metal surfaces actually touch, and those touching spots are microscopic high points. A materials-testing paper from a utility-equipment conference says applied force flattens those high points, and more force lowers contact resistance, with diminishing returns. A loose screw means fewer, smaller contact spots, so the resistance at that joint goes up.

Heat in any resistance follows P = I2R, where P is watts, I is amps and R is ohms. Because current is squared, a motor or compressor pulling full load makes a small resistance matter a lot. The heat has nowhere to go but into a lug the size of your thumb, which raises the joint temperature, which oxidizes the surfaces, which raises resistance further. That feedback loop is why a warm lug turns into a burned one. Federal safety writers describe the same pattern for aluminum home wiring: the consumer safety commission says increased resistance at connections causes overheating, and that such connections have failed with no prior indications.

Why condensers and RTUs loosen

  • Vibration. Compressors and fan motors shake the control box all day.
  • Thermal cycling. Metal expands when loaded and warm, then shrinks when the unit cycles off. Over thousands of cycles a screw can relax.
  • Relaxation. The conference paper reports that roughly 85 percent of the relaxation in an aluminum termination happens within 5 minutes of the first tightening, so a joint can lose clamping force soon after it is made.

The code rule: torque to the listed value

NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code, section 110.14(D) (added in the 2017 edition) says that where the equipment marking or installation instructions give a numeric torque value, a calibrated torque tool must be used to reach it, unless the maker provides another method. An electrical-inspector association magazine reports this, including that the panel behind the change cited testing showing installers use wrong torque values in up to 75 percent of installations when no torque tool is used. Edition adoption varies by state and city, so check the one you work under. Separately, OSHA's rule at 29 CFR 1910.303 requires listed equipment to be used per its instructions and requires a good connection that does not damage the conductors.

In practice:

  • Find the number on the equipment label, the terminal block or in the instructions. Torque values differ by terminal size and conductor, so do not guess.
  • Use a calibrated torque screwdriver or wrench with the correct bit. Keep it in its case and send it for calibration on the maker's schedule.
  • Tighten once, to the value. The conference paper cautions that re-torquing a severely overheated joint restored it less than 15 percent of the time, and calls re-termination the sound choice there. Cut back to clean, undamaged conductor and use a new or undamaged lug.
  • After a proper torque, a paint-pen stripe across screw and lug lets the next tech see movement at a glance (a practice that paper also suggests).
Verify dead first. Opening a control box to torque or inspect terminals requires lockout and a meter check on every line and load terminal before you touch anything, including after the disconnect is open. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. For live-parts hazards and labels, see Arc Flash and Shock Safety.

Aluminum conductors

Aluminum feeders and larger branch conductors still appear on older and bigger equipment. Aluminum forms a surface oxide quickly, and it expands and relaxes more under load cycling than copper does. The consumer safety commission describes several deterioration processes at aluminum connections that raise resistance, with cumulative damage. Its survey found pre-1972 aluminum-wired homes were 55 times more likely than copper-wired homes to have outlet connections at fire-hazard conditions, defined partly as a cover plate screw at 149 C (300 F).

  • Use only lugs marked for aluminum, usually shown as AL/CU or AL. OSHA's rule also calls for terminals for aluminum to be identified as such.
  • Clean the strands per the lug maker's instructions, and use antioxidant compound only where the lug or equipment maker allows it.
  • Torque to the aluminum value, which can differ from the copper value on the same lug.
  • Never put an aluminum conductor in a copper-only lug or push-on terminal.

Spade and push-on terminals

Capacitors and contactors often use quick-connect spade terminals, which grip by spring tension instead of a torqued screw. A discolored or loose female terminal should be replaced, not squeezed tighter with pliers. Crimp new terminals with the right crimper for the wire size, and check that the tab on the part is not burned. Meter and part checks are in Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays With a Meter, and the parts themselves in Capacitors and Contactors.

Worked example: how much heat is that?

Our own assumptions, not measurements: a condenser circuit draws 30 A. A good termination is taken as 0.001 ohm. A loose, oxidized termination is taken as 0.05 ohm.

  1. Good joint: P = 30 x 30 x 0.001 = 900 x 0.001 = 0.9 W.
  2. Loose joint: P = 900 x 0.05 = 45 W.
  3. Convert: 45 W x 3.412 = about 154 Btu/h.

Our own comparison: a small plug-in heater is rated about 1,500 W (our own typical figure), so 45 W is 3 percent of it. That sounds small, but a heater spreads its heat over a large element, while these 45 W are released in a spot about the size of a screw head, roughly 50 times the heat of the good joint. If the resistance doubles to 0.10 ohm as the joint oxidizes, the same 30 A gives 90 W, twice as much.

Signs to look for

  • Brown, blue or black discoloration on a lug, terminal block or wire end.
  • Insulation that is brittle, shrunk back, bubbled or melted near a screw.
  • Pitted, burned or blackened contactor lugs and contacts.
  • A terminal that moves when pushed, after lockout and verifying dead.

The U.S. Fire Administration reports that electrical malfunctions such as short circuits or wiring problems caused 8 percent of nonresidential building fires in 2017 to 2019. That is statistics on all such buildings, not only HVAC equipment.

Finding hot spots before they fail

An infrared camera shows a hot lug on a loaded unit without opening anything. Scan under normal load and compare similar phases. Emissivity, delta T and safe scanning technique are in Infrared Thermography for HVAC and Electrical Gear, so we do not repeat them here. A hot spot is a reason to schedule a lockout and a torque check, not to poke around live.

Field card: Loose Connections

  • Loose joint means high resistance; heat = I squared times R.
  • Lockout and verify dead before touching terminals.
  • Use a calibrated torque tool to the number on the label or instructions (NFPA 70 section 110.14(D)).
  • Aluminum: AL/CU rated lug, aluminum torque value, antioxidant only if the maker allows.
  • Replace burned lugs and brittle terminals; do not re-torque them.
  • Burned or loose spade terminals get replaced, not squeezed.
  • Mark torqued screws with a paint pen; scan loaded gear with infrared.
