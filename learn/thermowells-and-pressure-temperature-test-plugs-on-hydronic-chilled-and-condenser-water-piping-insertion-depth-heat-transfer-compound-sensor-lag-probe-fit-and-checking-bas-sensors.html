Thermowells and P/T Test Plugs on Water Piping

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every chilled water, condenser water and hot water loop is full of temperature readings that the building automation system (BAS) trusts without question. Most of those readings come from a sensor sitting inside a thermowell, and the easiest way to check one is with a probe pushed through a pressure-temperature test plug a few inches away. This lesson covers both fittings: what they are, how deep they go, why a loose sensor reads slow, and how to check a BAS sensor without opening the pipe.

What a thermowell is and why the sensor lives in one

A temperature measurement reading from a university engineering department describes a thermowell as a closed-end metal tube that sticks into the fluid, with the thermometer sitting inside it against its walls. The reason is practical: the pipe stays sealed. The water pressure pushes on the well, not on the sensor, so a technician can pull a failed sensor or a dial thermometer out of the well while the loop keeps running. A university construction specification describes wells as pressure-tight socket fittings made to screw into a piping tee, and a second university specification asks for a pressure rating not less than the piping system design pressure and a threaded cap on a chain so the cap does not get lost.

The price of that protection is a slower reading. A temperature measurement paper from a university warns that a well should protect the sensor without adding so much mass that it creates thermal lag. Heat now has to pass from the water, through the metal wall, across whatever gap is inside the well, and into the sensor.

Insertion depth and placement

The tip of the well has to be in moving water that represents the loop. The second university specification says the socket should reach at least 2 inches into the fluid or to the pipe centerline, whichever is shorter, and on insulated pipe the well needs an extension at least as long as the insulation is thick. The first university specification says to set wells in tees in a vertical position and to match the well's insertion length and bore to the sensor or thermometer stem. A third university hydronic piping specification calls for thermometers with a 2½ inch insertion length.

Depth matters because of stem conduction. The engineering reading explains that a thermometer immersed too shallow reads wrong, and the university paper notes that a long metal probe can conduct heat along its length and spoil the reading. A short well in a big pipe mostly measures the pipe wall and the room around it.

Placement matters as much as depth. In our own experience the common mistakes are a well in a dead leg or capped branch where water barely moves, a well right after a tee where two streams at different temperatures have not mixed yet, and a well on a bypass line that only flows part of the time. A well like that reports a temperature that is real, but it is not the loop temperature. When we find a reading that does not make sense, the first thing we trace is where the well sits relative to flow.

Heat transfer compound and loose fits

A sensor bulb is usually a little smaller than the bore of its well. Whatever fills that gap decides how fast heat gets across. Air is a poor conductor, so a dry, loose sensor touching the well on one side can take much longer to settle. Both university specifications call for filling wells with a graphite and glycerin heat transfer mixture, and the university paper recommends heat sink paste in wells for the same reason. Habits from our own field work: when a sensor is replaced, clean the old dried compound out, add fresh compound, push the sensor to the bottom of the well, and make sure the sensor and well actually match in size.

Response lag: a well versus a bare probe

The university paper defines a sensor's time constant as the time it takes to reach 63.2 percent of a step change, and it notes that response in water can be up to 100 times faster than in air. A bare probe in moving water responds quickly. The same sensor inside a well responds more slowly, and inside a dry, loose well more slowly still. We cover the time constant itself, and how long to wait for a steady reading, in the sensor time constant lesson linked below; here we only apply it to a well.

Worked example (our own, with assumed values)

This is our own example. Every input is an assumption chosen for teaching, not a measured or published value.

  • Assumed time constant of a supply water sensor in a well filled with compound: 10 seconds.
  • Assumed time constant of the same sensor in a dry, loose well: 45 seconds.
  • Assumed event: chilled water supply steps from 44°F to 46°F; return stays at 54°F.
  • Assumed flow: 600 gpm. We read the BAS 30 seconds after the step.

Step 1, fraction of the change seen after 30 s. For a first-order sensor the fraction is 1 − e−t/τ.
With compound: 1 − e−30/10 = 1 − e−3 = 1 − 0.0498 = 0.9502.
Dry and loose: 1 − e−30/45 = 1 − e−0.667 = 1 − 0.5134 = 0.4866.

Step 2, reading. With compound: 44 + 2 × 0.9502 = 45.90°F. Dry and loose: 44 + 2 × 0.4866 = 44.97°F. The true supply is 46.00°F.

Step 3, delta-T. True: 54 − 46.00 = 8.00°F. With compound: 54 − 45.90 = 8.10°F. Dry and loose: 54 − 44.97 = 9.03°F.

Step 4, load from 500 × gpm × delta-T. The 500 comes from roughly 8.33 pounds per gallon times 60 minutes per hour times 1 Btu per pound per °F for water (our own rounding). 500 × 600 = 300,000.
True: 300,000 × 8.00 = 2,400,000 Btu/h.
With compound: 300,000 × 8.10 = 2,430,000 Btu/h, about 30,000 Btu/h high (about 2.5 tons at 12,000 Btu/h per ton).
Dry and loose: 300,000 × 9.03 = 2,709,000 Btu/h, about 309,000 Btu/h high (about 25.8 tons).

What it shows. The error is temporary and fades once the sensor catches up, but a slow sensor on a moving load always trails reality. Five time constants is 50 seconds for the packed well and 225 seconds (3¾ minutes) for the dry one. A trend log or plant control loop that reacts inside those windows reacts to stale data.

Pressure-temperature test plugs

A pressure-temperature test plug, often called a P/T plug, is a small fitting with a self-sealing rubber core. A thin probe from a test thermometer or a gauge needle pushes through the core into the water, and the core closes again when the probe comes out. The second university specification describes nickel-plated brass plugs in a ½ inch fitting, long enough to clear the insulation, rated at least 500 psig, with a gasketed threaded cap on a chain. It lists neoprene cores for 20°F to 200°F service and EPDM cores for −30°F to 275°F on air and water. The first university specification calls for EPDM self-sealing inserts rated at least 500 psig at 200°F, and a test kit with a low range thermometer (at least 25 to 125°F) and a high range one (at least 0 to 220°F), with probes long enough to reach into the pipe. The third university specification puts test plugs next to gauges, thermometers and sensors and at coil inlets and outlets.

Match the probe. The second university specification says its core inserts take a 1/8 inch (3 mm) outside diameter probe. In our own experience a probe that is too fat can tear the core so it weeps after you pull out, and one that is too thin may not seal while it is in. Use the probe size the plug is made for.

Checking a BAS sensor against a reference probe

One university's facilities commissioning standard asks the mechanical contractor to install a P/T plug at each water sensor that is a control system input, so there is always a place to check it. That standard says field sensors are checked against a calibrated handheld instrument at least as accurate as the sensor, near enough to the sensor to give a reliable reading, and near the middle of the expected operating range. It sets a tolerance of plus or minus 0.2°F for chilled and condenser water sensors and plus or minus 0.5°F for heating water, and says a sensor outside tolerance gets an offset or is replaced. It also says not to check thermistor sensors with handheld thermocouple instruments.

The steps we follow (our own habits): insert the reference probe in the plug nearest the well, let both readings settle for several time constants while flow is steady, read the BAS value and the reference at the same moment, and record both. If the two disagree, check the well (compound, depth, fit) before typing in an offset. An offset hides a slow or misplaced sensor instead of fixing it.

Leaks and safety on hot, pressurized lines

The plug core is the only thing between you and the loop water. Check that the plug and core are rated for the water temperature (see the core ranges above) before you insert anything. Our own safety habits, not drawn from a cited source: wear eye protection and gloves on hot water lines, push the probe in straight and steady without twisting, keep your face out of line with the plug, and never force a probe into a plug that is damaged or already weeping. If a core leaks after you pull out, put the cap back on and report it so the plug can be isolated and the core replaced under the site's procedure. Do not pull a thermowell itself unless the line has been isolated and relieved, because the well is part of the pressure boundary. Follow your site procedures and the plug maker's instructions.

Field card: Thermowells and P/T Test Plugs on Water Piping

A well protects the pipe; compound and depth make it read true.

  • Thermowell: sealed tube in a tee; sensor comes out, water stays in.
  • Depth (one university spec): at least 2 in into the fluid or to centerline, whichever is shorter.
  • Keep wells out of dead legs, unmixed tees and part-time bypasses.
  • Fill wells with heat transfer compound; match sensor and bore.
  • Slow sensor plus moving load equals a delta-T error until it catches up.
  • P/T plug: self-sealing core; use the probe size it is made for (1/8 in in one spec).
  • Check BAS sensors against a reference in the nearest plug; fix the well before adding an offset.
  • Never pull a well on a live line; cap and report a weeping plug.
