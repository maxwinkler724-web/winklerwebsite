Vibration Analysis Basics for Fans and Pumps: Velocity, Imbalance, Misalignment and Bearings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fan or pump that is about to fail usually gets rougher first. Vibration readings turn "it feels rough" into a number you can write down, compare with last quarter and act on. This lesson covers the basics a facilities tech needs: the units, where to put the sensor, what the common faults look like on a spectrum, and what to do when a reading climbs.

Why facilities trend vibration

The U.S. DOE operations and maintenance guide lists vibration analysis among the predictive tools that can find problems in rotating equipment, including unbalance, misalignment, looseness, bearing problems, and gear and belt drive problems. The same guide warns that diagnosing them takes trained, skilled people. The NASA reliability-centered maintenance guide ties this to bearing life: it says only 10 to 20 percent of rolling-element bearings reach their design life, and it names imbalance and misalignment as leading causes of excess vibration and extra bearing load. It also says a fan at 0.2 in/s overall is closer to failure than the same fan at 0.1 in/s and should be checked more often.

The three units

The DOE guide describes three ways to express vibration: displacement (the total travel of the vibrating part), velocity (how fast it moves back and forth) and acceleration (the rate of change of velocity). It says the best choice depends on machine speed and design. A maintenance guide from DAK Consulting gives the usual units: displacement in mils or microns, velocity in in/s or mm/s RMS, and acceleration in g. Emerson lists the same families, noting velocity may be quoted in RMS or peak. Always check which one your meter is set to before comparing numbers.

Why velocity? The DAK guide says velocity gives a good picture of condition across a wide speed range and suits general machine condition, imbalance, misalignment and looseness. SKF's spectrum guide uses velocity in in/s RMS for overall severity. The DAK guide prefers acceleration for higher speeds and for bearings. So: velocity for the overall number you trend, acceleration when you hunt bearing trouble.

Where and how to take readings

  • Mark the spot. The DAK guide calls it standard practice to mark each measurement point so every reading comes from the same place. Take them at the drive end and non-drive end of the motor and of the fan or pump.
  • Three directions. SKF notes that a sensor's response depends on its orientation: horizontal, vertical or axial. Horizontal and vertical are radial, across the shaft; axial runs along it. DAK says imbalance is usually radial and misalignment shows both radial and axial components.
  • Mount it right. DAK says the transducer should measure square to its mounting surface. Emerson says stud mounting is preferred and hand-held probes give the poorest results.
  • Same conditions. DAK says to read under consistent speed and load and to record rpm. SKF says to compare against past readings taken under consistent operating conditions.

Our own: before you reach near a running machine, follow your site's lockout and guard rules. Do not open a guard to place a sensor.

What common faults look like

"1x" means a peak at the shaft's running speed in Hz; "2x" is twice that. The sources agree on the basics:

  • Imbalance. Emerson says it always appears at 1x. SKF describes a single 1x peak with the same amplitude in all radial directions, with little at higher multiples. Fluke says to look for rising 1x in the radial direction but not axial.
  • Misalignment. Fluke lists high 1x axial and 2x radial, with peaks at both machines. Emerson says a high 2x peak points to offset misalignment and a high 1x to angular. SKF treats a high 2x as a key indicator.
  • Looseness. Fluke says higher harmonics of 1x in any direction. SKF looks for several multiples of running speed, 2x to 10x, above 20 percent of 1x. Emerson adds a raised noise floor.
  • Bearings. Fluke says non-synchronous peaks, ones that are not whole multiples of running speed. SKF names four defect frequencies: outer race, inner race, rolling element and cage. Emerson says early defects are high-frequency and low-amplitude, and broad "mounds" signal advanced wear. DAK says bearing problems show at high frequency and low amplitude early on. Fluke adds that bearing wear is usually driven by another fault, so check the first three.
  • Belts. DAK says belt vibration relates to rotational speed and its multiples. SKF says to know belt lengths to identify belt peaks. Emerson says belt problems are often dominated by the 2x belt frequency.
  • Blades and vanes. DAK lists blade or vane pass at blade count times 1x.

Severity charts and the baseline

Charts tied to ISO 10816 (and the later ISO 20816 series) give velocity ranges by machine class. We do not reproduce any table here; use the copy your site or employer licenses. The DOE guide cautions that its severity chart is a guide, not an absolute limit above which a machine fails, and that it is not for setting acceptance criteria on new or rebuilt machines. It says such charts "can serve as an excellent guide to get started" when you have no history.

The better yardstick is the machine's own history. DAK calls trending against normal values the most accurate way to judge condition and describes a baseline as the data gathered when monitoring begins. NASA says commissioning and acceptance testing are used to build a baseline set. SKF says to compare with a baseline from installation and past readings, which shows how fast a machine is degrading. NASA recommends at least three monitoring points to confirm a trend.

Worked example: motor 1x versus fan 1x

All arithmetic below is Our own. A fan motor turns at 1,770 rpm. The fan wheel is belt driven with a 9 in motor sheave and a 12 in fan sheave.

  1. Motor running speed in Hz: 1,770 rpm ÷ 60 = 29.5 Hz. That is the motor 1x.
  2. Motor 2x: 2 × 29.5 = 59 Hz.
  3. Fan rpm: a belt drive moves the belt at the same surface speed on both sheaves, so fan rpm = motor rpm × (motor sheave ÷ fan sheave) = 1,770 × (9 ÷ 12) = 1,770 × 0.75 = 1,327.5 rpm. Belt slip would make it slightly lower.
  4. Fan 1x: 1,327.5 ÷ 60 = 22.125 Hz, about 22.1 Hz.
  5. Fan 2x: 2 × 22.125 = 44.25 Hz.

On a spectrum, a peak near 22 Hz points at the fan wheel, a peak near 29.5 Hz points at the motor, and a high 2x at 44 or 59 Hz tells you which shaft to suspect for misalignment or looseness. Our own note: the belt sits between them, so it can show peaks of its own, which is why SKF says to know the belt length.

What a tech does with a rising reading

  1. Confirm it. Recheck the same spot and direction with the same meter setting, speed and load.
  2. Compare it with the baseline and the last few readings. NASA says that once an alert limit is exceeded, the monitoring interval should drop to about one-third to one-quarter of the prior interval, and may be lengthened if readings settle.
  3. Look at direction and frequency if you have a spectrum: 1x radial suggests imbalance, 1x axial with 2x suggests misalignment, many harmonics suggest looseness. Fluke says to check those three first when a bearing shows wear.
  4. Check the simple causes: loose mounts, a worn or slipping belt, buildup on a fan wheel (our own suggestion), then alignment and lubrication. Emerson says high-frequency analysis can detect lack of lubrication.
  5. Write it up and escalate to a trained analyst when the cause is not obvious, since the DOE guide says diagnosis needs skilled people.

Field card: Vibration basics for fans and pumps

Same spot, same direction, same conditions, then compare.

  • Velocity (in/s or mm/s) is the usual overall trend number; acceleration (g) is used for bearings.
  • Read horizontal, vertical and axial at each bearing, at marked spots.
  • Imbalance: 1x radial. Misalignment: 1x axial and 2x. Looseness: many harmonics.
  • Bearing defects show at non-synchronous frequencies.
  • Motor 1x = rpm ÷ 60. Fan rpm = motor rpm × motor sheave ÷ fan sheave.
  • Trend against the baseline; severity charts are a guide, not a pass or fail.
  • Rising reading: recheck, compare, find the cause, shorten the interval.
