Hex Keys and Setscrews on HVAC Fans and Motors

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A lot of HVAC fans are held on their shafts by one or two small setscrews. Blower wheels, fan hubs, pulleys and couplings all depend on that tiny contact. This lesson covers the hex keys that turn those screws, the shaft flat they seat on, and the habits that keep a wheel from slipping and scoring the shaft.

Where setscrews show up

A setscrew is threaded through the hub of a part and pushes against the shaft. On a furnace or air handler, that part is the blower wheel. On a belt-driven fan it is the pulley on the motor and the pulley on the fan shaft. On a direct-drive link it is a coupling half. An agricultural extension article on fan pulley alignment describes the usual arrangement: the pulley is held on the fan shaft by a setscrew, and to move the pulley you loosen that screw, slide the pulley until it lines up, and tighten the screw again. It adds that a stuck pulley may need penetrating spray and a gear puller, and that the maker's manual is the place to find setscrew sizes and torque values. We agree with that last point: this page gives no torque numbers, because they depend on the part.

Lock out first

A fan that looks stopped can start without warning, so the first step is energy control. The federal workplace safety agency's overview says lockout and tagout practices protect workers from the unexpected startup or release of hazardous energy during servicing, and that employees must not try to restart or re-energize locked or tagged equipment. Its minimal procedure for general industry (in the appendix to 29 CFR 1910.147) runs through these steps: tell affected people, stop the machine normally, switch off every isolating device, put your personal lock on each one, release stored energy, then try the normal start controls to prove it will not start, and return the controls to off. For a fan, stored energy includes a wheel that is still coasting and a belt that can turn the motor from the fan side. Our lockout and verify-dead lesson covers the full routine. The fan pulley article also says to shut off the breaker, wear safety glasses and gloves, and keep tools and loose parts out of the fan before power returns.

Inch versus metric keys

Hex keys come in inch and metric sets, and the two overlap closely enough to fool you. A key is sized by the distance across its flats, so a 4 mm key is 4 mm across and a 5/32 inch key is 5/32 inch across. Fans from different makers and countries mix both systems, and a motor shaft setscrew may not match the fan hub screw on the same unit. Keep both sets in the bag, and test fit by feel before you push on anything.

Why a near fit strips the socket

A hex key only turns a screw well when its flats sit flat against the socket walls. If the key is slightly too small, it rocks until its corners press on the socket walls, and all the turning force lands on six thin edges instead of six wide faces. Under load the soft socket rounds off. This is our own explanation of why a near fit is worse than it feels, and the worked example below puts numbers on it.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example. Assumption: a blower wheel was built to a metric standard, and the screw sockets measure 4 mm, 5 mm, 6 mm and 8 mm across the flats. We have only inch keys: 1/8, 5/32, 3/16, 7/32, 1/4 and 5/16.

Step 1, convert each metric size to inches by dividing by 25.4 (there are exactly 25.4 mm in an inch).

  • 4 mm / 25.4 = 0.1575 in
  • 5 mm / 25.4 = 0.1969 in
  • 6 mm / 25.4 = 0.2362 in
  • 8 mm / 25.4 = 0.3150 in

Step 2, convert the inch keys to decimals (divide the top by the bottom): 1/8 = 0.1250, 5/32 = 0.1563, 3/16 = 0.1875, 7/32 = 0.2188, 1/4 = 0.2500, 5/16 = 0.3125.

Step 3, pick the closest key that still fits, meaning one equal to or smaller than the socket.

  • 4 mm (0.1575): 5/32 is 0.1563. Gap = 0.1575 − 0.1563 = 0.0012 in. Close, so it fits, but with a small rattle.
  • 5 mm (0.1969): 3/16 is 0.1875. Gap = 0.0094 in. That is a poor fit, about eight times the 4 mm gap. 7/32 (0.2188) is bigger than the socket and will not go in.
  • 6 mm (0.2362): 7/32 is 0.2188, a gap of 0.0174 in. A 15/64 key (0.2344) would be tighter but is not in our set.
  • 8 mm (0.3150): 5/16 is 0.3125. Gap = 0.0025 in.

Step 4, read the result. The 4 mm and 8 mm sockets have an inch key within a few thousandths, which can pass for a fit. The 5 mm socket has a gap of nearly 0.01 in on every flat pair, so an inch key will round it. Even the small gaps are a risk: a gap that small still lets the key rock onto its corners when the screw is tight. When we cannot get the exact size, we should stop and get the right key instead of forcing the closest one. A gap of 0.0012 in is a number from this example, not a rule for every screw.

Ball-end versus straight keys

A ball-end key can enter a screw at an angle, which helps in a crowded blower housing. The ball touches the socket at only a few points, so it should not be used to apply the last bit of force. Our own shop advice: use the ball end to run the screw in and out, then switch to the straight end for the final snug and for loosening a tight screw.

Cup points and the shaft flat

Many shafts have a flat machined where the setscrew lands. A cup-point screw has a hollow end with a rim that bites a little into the metal. On a flat, the rim sits square and holds. On a round shaft with no flat, the screw can sit on a curve and roll, which is why you should line the screw up with the flat before tightening. Check the flat with your eyes and a finger: if the wheel was installed by turning the shaft until the screw hit the flat, the screw will feel it seat.

Why slipping scores the shaft

If a screw is loose, the wheel does not just fall off. It turns a little on the shaft each time the motor starts, and the screw point drags across the metal. That wears a groove in the shaft or the flat. The next screw will land in that groove, but the groove has a burr on its edge that keeps the wheel from seating square. A scored shaft often needs a repair or replacement before a new wheel will hold. Our own rule: if the old screw left a burr, dress it flat with a fine file before putting a new wheel on, and do not retighten an old one over the same groove without looking.

Rust, thread locker and tightening

Rust in the hex socket hides its depth, so key engagement is shallow and strips. Brush or pick the rust out before loosening, and add penetrating oil if the fan pulley article's advice applies. Whether a screw should get thread locker is a question for the fan maker's manual, so we do not make that call here. If it does call for it, clean oil off the threads first and follow the product instructions.

Check wheel position and end play afterward

Once the screws are snug, do not close the cabinet yet. With the power still locked out, spin the wheel by hand. It should turn freely, with no scrape on the housing or cutoff. Then push and pull the shaft along its length. Some end play is normal; check how much the manual allows. The wheel should sit where the manual says relative to the inlet ring, or where it sat before you started. For a belt-driven fan, recheck pulley alignment: the extension article says to recheck it after replacing a pulley or motor and not to judge it by eye before tightening the mount bolts. Our belt drive lesson covers belts and sheaves.

Field card: Hex Keys and Setscrews on HVAC Fans and Motors

Lock out, clean the socket, fit the key, seat on the flat, then check the wheel.

  • Lock out and try the start controls before reaching in.
  • Brush rust out of the socket before you loosen.
  • Check the key by feel: if it rocks, it is the wrong size.
  • Divide millimeters by 25.4 to compare with inch keys.
  • Ball end to run it, straight end to finish.
  • Seat the screw on the flat. Burrs go off before a new wheel goes on.
  • After tightening: spin by hand, check end play and wheel position, recheck pulley alignment.
