Radiant Floor Tubing: Slab, Thin Slab, Plates and Panels

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The tubing in a radiant floor can sit in four very different places: inside a thick slab, inside a thin pour on a wood floor, in metal plates hung under the subfloor, or in grooves in panels laid on top. Each choice changes how fast the room responds, how hot the water must be, how much weight the framing carries and how much insulation matters below the tubing.

This lesson compares the four. It does not repeat the basics, so start with radiant floor heating basics if the idea is new. Floor coverings are covered in the lesson on radiant under tile, wood and carpet.

The four installation methods

1. Tubing in a slab on grade

A federal energy department HVAC guide says tubing can go in a traditional concrete slab, a thin layer of concrete, or pre-grooved wood panels, and that the slab under the tubing must be insulated. A university housing research center handout adds the model-code numbers it cites for residential work: at least R-5 under the piping for a heated slab on grade, plus edge insulation that depends on climate zone (for example R-10 for 2 feet in the colder zones, with the heated-slab add-on of R-5 in the code table). A federal research report on hydronic distribution describes a field home with the slab underside and edges insulated to R-10 and notes that slab-on-grade systems have high mass that stores energy.

High mass is the trade-off. A university finite-element study modeled 50 °C pulsed water and a starting slab at 10 °C. A slab with the pipe 37.5 mm deep and 100 mm spacing took about 1,000 seconds to bring the surface to 28 °C, while the same slab with the pipe 75 mm deep took about 3,300 seconds. Deeper pipe gave a more even surface but a slower response. The same study found that under-slab insulation raised the overall slab temperature by buffering the ground but did little for surface temperature spread. These are modeled results, not field measurements.

Because the slab stores heat, it can run at low water temperature for long periods. It is a poor fit for a room that needs quick recovery after a night setback.

2. Thin slab over a wood subfloor

Here the tubing is laid on the subfloor and covered with a shallow pour. The slab is thinner, so it responds faster than a thick slab, but it still has real mass. The cost is dead load on the floor framing, which the worked example below puts into numbers. The same university study that modeled slabs also notes that a thinner slab, needed for suspended floors, loses the benefit of putting the pipe at the best height in the slab.

Insulation is a different question than on grade. The housing research center handout cites a code minimum of R-11 in the joist bay under the heated space for a suspended floor. Without it, much of the heat goes down into the crawl space or the room below instead of up.

Weight is not only the pour. Anyone proposing a thin slab on an existing floor should have the framing checked by a person qualified to do that. This page does not give a safe-load rule.

3. Aluminum plates under the subfloor

With plates, tubing is held against the underside of the subfloor between joists and metal plates spread the heat sideways so more of the floor surface is warmed. Nothing is poured, so the added weight is small, and the system has little mass, so it responds quickly. Our own reasoning, from how heat has to move: the heat must cross the air gap or contact joint, the subfloor and the floor covering, so these systems usually need warmer supply water than a slab for the same output, and the covering matters a lot. The federal HVAC guide does not address plates, so we do not give a temperature for them here; use the manufacturer instructions for the plate and tubing you install.

Insulation below is not optional. The same R-11 joist-bay code figure applies; install it as the maker says. One of the models in the university study, a lightweight aluminum trough with one to three pipes, never got the surface near 28 °C at 50 °C water, which shows how output can fall short when little metal touches the floor.

4. Grooved panels on top of the subfloor

Grooved panels sit on the subfloor and the tubing snaps into the grooves, often with a heat-spreading surface in the groove. Our own reasoning: they add little weight and little mass, so they should respond faster than a slab, and the tubing sits above the subfloor, so less heat has to cross it than with plates underneath. The floor covering still sits on top. The federal HVAC guide lists pre-grooved wood panels as an accepted way to install tubing. Insulation below the subfloor is still the same joist-bay question as in method 3.

The response and water temperature columns are our own summary of the sources above and the reasoning in each section, not measured values. The federal research report gives a hydronic product range of 40 to 125 °F supply water, and the federal HVAC guide says radiant floors need lower water temperature than radiators. For how output changes with water temperature, see the emitter output lesson and, for blending water down, the mixed-water temperature lesson.

Worked example (our own): floor load of a thin slab versus plates

This is our own example with assumed inputs. None of these values come from a source or a design document.

Assumptions: a 200 square foot room; a 1.5 inch thin slab; two assumed slab densities, 150 lb per cubic foot for a normal-weight mix and 100 lb per cubic foot for a lighter mix; and an assumed 0.5 pound per square foot for tubing, plates, fasteners and water in a plated system.

  1. Thickness in feet: 1.5 in ÷ 12 = 0.125 ft.
  2. Volume per square foot of floor: 0.125 ft × 1 ft² = 0.125 ft³.
  3. Normal-weight slab: 0.125 × 150 = 18.75 lb per ft². For 200 ft²: 18.75 × 200 = 3,750 lb.
  4. Lighter slab: 0.125 × 100 = 12.5 lb per ft². For 200 ft²: 12.5 × 200 = 2,500 lb.
  5. Plated system: 0.5 lb per ft² × 200 ft² = 100 lb.
  6. Difference, normal-weight slab minus plates: 18.75 − 0.5 = 18.25 lb per ft², or 3,750 − 100 = 3,650 lb in total.

The normal-weight thin slab adds about 37 times the plated system’s assumed load in this example. That is 3,650 pounds on one room’s framing, before the finished floor. Whether the floor can carry it is a framing question for a qualified person, not something this arithmetic answers.

Field card. Before you pick a method, check: (1) what the floor framing or ground can carry; (2) the finished floor covering; (3) the highest water temperature the system will see; (4) whether insulation can be installed against the underside and at the slab edge; (5) the maker’s instructions for the tubing and plates.
