Replacing Electric Baseboard Heat With a Heat Pump: Heads, COP, Backup and Circuits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house with electric baseboard has a simple heating system and a high bill. A heat pump can deliver the same heat for much less electricity, but the swap is not one-for-one. Heads, closed doors, backup heat and the electrical panel all need a plan. This lesson walks through those choices for a baseboard home.

Why baseboard has a COP of 1

Coefficient of performance, or COP, is heat delivered divided by electrical energy used. A university physics reference describes a resistance heater as delivering 1 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity, and a heat pump as moving about 3 kWh of heat for each 1 kWh it uses. The Department of Energy says the same thing in plainer terms: electric baseboards convert all incoming electric energy to heat, and a properly installed air-source heat pump can deliver up to three times more heat energy than the electrical energy it consumes. So baseboard is "100% efficient" at the room and still expensive, because every kilowatt-hour you pay for becomes exactly one kilowatt-hour of heat. A heat pump pays for the electricity to run a compressor and fans, then brings in the rest of the heat from outdoor air.

What that means for the bill is simple: electricity used equals heat needed divided by COP. The DOE electric heating page says heat pumps can roughly halve electricity use compared with resistance heating. COP is not a fixed number. It drops as the outdoor air gets colder, so the number that matters for a bill is a seasonal average, not the best-case rating. See Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity for how it changes with temperature.

Ductless heads or one ducted unit?

A baseboard house usually has no ducts, which is why ductless heat pumps are the common retrofit. The DOE says a ductless system needs only a small hole through the wall for the line connection to each indoor unit, and that each indoor unit creates its own zone, so individual rooms can be heated or cooled. That matches how baseboard already works: one room, one control.

The alternative is ducted indoor equipment. A DOE sizing guide for air-source systems says a single compact-ducted system is preferred for effective air distribution to low-load rooms such as bedrooms. In a baseboard home that means you would be adding duct runs where none exist. In a house with an attic or basement chase where ducts can be run and sealed, one ducted unit can be neater and quieter in the rooms. In a house with plaster walls, finished ceilings and no space, heads are usually easier. The right answer depends on the building, and a contractor should check it with a room-by-room load calculation.

How many heads, and what about closed-off rooms?

The DOE guide says to size each zone to the heating load of the area it serves, so the combined head capacity will not match the whole-house design load. It also says not to pick multi-zone equipment only by counting zones: consider more than one outdoor unit, each smaller and serving fewer zones, because that improves part-load performance and adds resilience. It warns against a multi-zone outdoor unit sized above 120% of the heating design load where possible, and against a pairing in which the outdoor unit's minimum capacity is higher than the smallest indoor unit's heating capacity. See Multi-Zone vs Single-Zone Mini-Splits for the sizing details.

Closed doors are the practical problem. A head in the living area does not heat a bedroom behind a shut door. Your options, in our view, are:

  • A head in each bedroom. Small heads exist, but each one still has a minimum output that can exceed a well-insulated bedroom's load, so it may cycle on and off.
  • Leave the door open or add a transfer path. This works for rooms close to a head, but it shifts the comfort to whoever sleeps with the door closed.
  • Keep the baseboard in that room. It is the simplest choice for a small, rarely used room.
  • A ducted unit with short runs. Best when the building allows it.

Bathrooms, closets and small rooms are often left on baseboard because a head is not worth it there.

Keeping baseboards as backup

Keeping the baseboards is allowed and common. The DOE guide says supplemental heat should be shut off unless needed, and suggests setting the backup thermostat about 4 to 10 degrees F lower than the heat pump setpoint when possible. In a baseboard house that is the key rule. If the baseboard thermostat is set to the same temperature as the head, the baseboard will fight the heat pump any time the head falls behind, and you lose the savings. Set the heads to the comfort temperature and drop each room's baseboard thermostat below it, so the baseboard comes on only when the room falls past that gap.

The DOE electric heating page also notes that many line-voltage baseboard thermostats built into the heater do not sense room temperature accurately, and recommends a remote thermostat on an interior wall. If you are leaving baseboards as backup, the thermostat is the control that decides when they run, so check it. A baseboard that never turns on will not be there when you need it. For troubleshooting a dead heater, see Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working.

Breaker space and new circuits

Removing baseboards frees breakers, but that does not mean you can reuse them. A heat pump outdoor unit and each indoor head are sized by the nameplate on the equipment, and the installation manual calls for a circuit sized to that nameplate, often a dedicated one with a disconnect near the outdoor unit. A baseboard circuit is sized for a heater, not for a compressor, and the wire gauge, breaker size and routing are likely to be wrong. If you keep some baseboards, their circuits stay in place and still count toward your panel's load.

The question to ask is whether the panel has enough total capacity, not only free slots. A service load calculation done by a licensed electrician under the electrical code answers that, and removing baseboards usually helps it. Our panel capacity lesson covers how that works. The electrical work is for a qualified electrician, not for a homeowner experiment.

Cold-climate capacity drop

The DOE factsheet on air-to-air heat pumps says that historically, cold-weather performance has been an issue, with both efficiency and heating capacity significantly lower in colder conditions, and that manufacturers have made great progress with cold-climate models. The DOE says cold-climate heat pumps are designed to perform at temperatures as low as 5 degrees F. The sizing guide tells designers to check maximum heating capacity at 5 degrees F in the published cold-climate specification rather than relying on the rating at 47 degrees F. For a baseboard home this is the main reason to keep some backup heat: the head has a limit, and the baseboard covers the gap on the coldest hours.

What homeowners notice

  • Air movement. Baseboard heat is nearly silent and still. A head moves air from the wall. Some people notice a gentle breeze or cooler-feeling air on startup, and others find it comfortable. Placement matters.
  • Head placement. A head is usually high on a wall, needs clear space around it and a path for the line set and condensate drain to the outside. Furniture and bed placement should be discussed before the install.
  • Noise. Indoor heads run a fan, quiet but not silent. The DOE advises placing the outdoor unit away from windows and neighbors and picking a model with a lower outdoor sound rating.
  • Even temperatures. Rooms without a head may run cooler than before, because baseboards no longer keep every room at its own setpoint.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measured case. We assume:

  • Heat the home needs over the season: 12,000 kWh delivered (assumption).
  • Baseboard: COP of 1.
  • Heat pump seasonal COP: 2.5 (assumption, lower than the best-case rating to allow for cold hours).
  • Electricity price: $0.20 per kWh (assumption; use your own rate).

Step by step:

  1. Baseboard electricity = 12,000 ÷ 1 = 12,000 kWh.
  2. Heat pump electricity = 12,000 ÷ 2.5 = 4,800 kWh.
  3. Baseboard cost = 12,000 × $0.20 = $2,400.
  4. Heat pump cost = 4,800 × $0.20 = $960.
  5. Difference = $2,400 − $960 = $1,440 a season, or 7,200 kWh less. That is 60% less electricity, because 1 ÷ 2.5 = 0.40.

If some of the heat still comes from baseboards, the savings shrink in proportion. If a quarter of the delivered heat (3,000 kWh) came from baseboards at COP 1, the heat pump would deliver 9,000 kWh using 3,600 kWh, for a total of 6,600 kWh and $1,320, saving $1,080. That is why the backup baseboard thermostats should be set low.

Field card: Replacing Electric Baseboard Heat With a Heat Pump: Heads, COP, Backup and Circuits

Same heat, less electricity, but plan the rooms and the panel.

  • COP = heat delivered divided by electricity used. Baseboard is 1.
  • Electricity = heat needed divided by seasonal COP.
  • Ductless heads suit homes without ducts; one ducted unit suits bedrooms if ducts can be run.
  • Closed-off rooms: head, open door, ducted run or keep the baseboard.
  • Set backup baseboard thermostats lower than the heat pump setpoint.
  • Heat pump circuits are sized by nameplate; do not reuse baseboard circuits blindly.
  • Capacity falls in the cold; check the 5 degrees F figure.
