Through-the-Wall Room Air Conditioners at Home: Sleeve, Seal, Cord

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Older apartments, condos and additions often have a metal box set into the wall instead of a unit hanging in a window. It looks like a window air conditioner, but the wall around it, the sleeve it slides into and the air path behind it change how you shop, install, seal and maintain it. This lesson covers those differences for homeowners and apprentices.

How it differs from a window unit

A through-the-wall unit rides in a permanent sleeve, a boxlike metal frame fixed in an opening cut through the wall. A window unit rests on a sill and is held by a sash and brackets. In our own plain-language terms, the sleeve is the part that stays; the chassis (the cooling works) normally slides out of the front for service or replacement. Many models are fixed instead and are built to be installed one way. Check which type you have before you plan any work.

The air path matters too. The back of the chassis, with its rear louvers or grille, must stay open to outdoor air past the outside face of the wall. If the louvers sit inside a wall cavity, behind a deck rail, a shrub or a decorative panel, hot air recirculates and the unit loses capacity. This airflow point is our own, based on how the condenser side works. A federal weatherization job aid says the unit must fit the opening without blocking vents, and a federal energy-lab case study says to clean the intake and exhaust grills on both the indoor and outdoor sides.

Replacing the chassis: match the sleeve

A replacement unit has to fit the sleeve you already have. A city government primer on sealing sleeve units says to make sure the unit is sized appropriately for the sleeve, and to install any trim kit and gasket it as the maker directs. A federal weatherization job aid says to confirm the new unit fits the existing opening without blocking vents, and that the wall thickness must not exceed the non-vented portion of the unit or its mounting assembly. Our own rule on top of that: measure the sleeve opening width, height and depth, and compare the new unit's rear grille and louver layout with the old one, because a grille that does not line up with the sleeve slots chokes the airflow. Also match the voltage and plug type to the old label, and the new cooling capacity should be no larger than needed (see sizing below).

Slope and drain

Water pulled from the room air collects in the base pan. The federal job aid says the unit should end up tilted slightly to the outside and that any condensate drain should slope away from the unit and the building, with a supplied drain hose attached to the outside of the unit. A federal energy-saving guide for consumers says to keep the unit level for efficient drainage and operation, so use the sleeve and the maker's instructions to settle the exact angle, checking left to right for level. Water dripping inside usually points to a sleeve or chassis that slopes the wrong way, which is our own field observation.

Sealing and insulating around the sleeve

The sleeve is a hole in the building envelope. The city primer says to caulk all edges between the sleeve and the wall, and to gasket trim kits per the maker. The job aid says to seal the adjacent framing and the perimeter after installation. On a sleeve in a thick wall, filling the gaps with backer material and caulk, then insulating around the sleeve from inside, are our own suggestions and not drawn from those sources; do not block the sleeve's own vents or drain slots.

Winter air leaks and covers

A well sealed sleeve unit can still leak a lot in winter when it is not running. The city primer recommends a seasonal routine: remove or cover units, and use insulated covers over the sleeve to block drafts. An energy-lab case study on room units says that after the cooling season you should either remove the unit or seal it completely on the inside; otherwise air leaks through the unit itself. Use an inside cover made for your sleeve, or follow the maker's winter instructions. Check that nothing blocks the louvers or drain when you uncover the unit in spring.

Filters and coils

A federal energy-saving guide advises choosing a unit with a filter that slides out easily for cleaning. Wash or replace it as the manual says (our own: check it monthly in heavy use). Coil cleaning is for the manual and, when the chassis must come out, for a technician: switch the power off, unplug the unit, keep water off the electrical parts, and do not bend the fins. This coil steps list is our own advice.

Cord, plug and circuit basics

Read the label first. It lists the voltage, the amps and the plug shape; our own advice is to never swap the plug or force it into a mismatched outlet. A federal energy-saving guide says units under 7.5 amps can plug into a standard 15 or 20 amp, 120 volt circuit that is not shared with major appliances, larger units need a dedicated 115 volt circuit, and the largest need 240 volts. A federal fire-prevention page says never use an extension cord with a major appliance, and a federal weatherization job aid says it is not allowable to plug a room air conditioner into an extension cord. A federal product-safety guide to home wiring adds that extension cords are for short-term use only and that a loose plug that pulls out easily can overheat. If the cord does not reach, move the unit or have an electrician add an outlet. For the wider picture of circuits, see Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Heat-and-cool models with electric heat

Some through-the-wall units add heating. A federal purchasing page notes that reverse cycle room units exist (a heat pump version) and treats models with electric resistance heating elements separately. Electric heat draws more current than cooling alone, which is our own point; use the amps on the label, and do not assume the cooling-only circuit rules above fit a heating model. If the label shows 240 volts, you need the matching outlet and plug, installed by an electrician.

Sizing: worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a case study. A federal energy-saving guide gives a rule of thumb: typically about 20 BTU for each square foot of living space.

  1. Assumptions (ours): a bedroom 14 ft by 16 ft, an 8 ft ceiling, normal shade, no kitchen, two occupants.
  2. Area: 14 × 16 = 224 sq ft.
  3. Capacity: 224 × 20 = 4,480 BTU/h.
  4. Pick the closest common size: about 5,000 BTU/h.

Adjustments: a federal sizing table says to add 10 percent for a very sunny room (4,480 × 1.10 = 4,928, still about 5,000), subtract 10 percent for heavy shade, add 600 BTU/h for each person beyond two, and add 4,000 BTU/h for a kitchen (4,480 + 4,000 = 8,480). That table is built on an 8 ft ceiling and lists capacity in size bands, so it may give a slightly different figure than the rule of thumb; compare both. Oversizing raises cost and energy use and cycles too often to dry the air, per a federal purchasing page. Also check that the new chassis fits the sleeve at that capacity.

When a ductless unit makes more sense

Consider it when the sleeve leaks and is hard to seal, the unit is loud, or you want heat and cooling for more than one room. These are our own points. A ductless heat pump moves the noisy part outside and serves a room or several, with a small line set hole instead of a large sleeve. See Mini-Split Basics and Adding AC to a House Without Ducts. Whether it is worth it depends on the building, the wall type and any rules at your property, so ask a licensed professional.

Field card: Through-the-Wall Room Air Conditioners

The sleeve stays; the louvers must breathe; no extension cords.

  • Match a new chassis to the sleeve size, rear grille and the old label.
  • Slope slightly to the outside and drain away from the building.
  • Caulk the sleeve edges; cover or remove in winter.
  • Read plug type and amps on the label; use no extension cord.
  • Rule of thumb: about 20 BTU/h per sq ft; 224 sq ft gives about 4,480, so about 5,000.
  • Single room and a leaky sleeve? Consider a ductless unit.
