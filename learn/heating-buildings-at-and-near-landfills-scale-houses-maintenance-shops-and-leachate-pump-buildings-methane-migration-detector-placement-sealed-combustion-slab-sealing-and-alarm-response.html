Heating Buildings at and Near Landfills: Methane Migration, Detector Placement, Sealed-Combustion Heaters and Alarm Response

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A scale house, a maintenance shop and a leachate pump building sit close to buried waste. The waste makes methane, and methane can travel through soil and enter a slab. That changes how we pick, place and protect a gas heater. This lesson covers how the gas gets in, where detectors go, which heaters to avoid, and what to do when an alarm sounds.

Why methane reaches buildings near a landfill

A federal environmental agency page on landfill gas describes it as roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide, with a small amount of other organic compounds. A federal health agency landfill gas primer lists four things that drive movement. Gas diffuses from high concentration to low. Gas pressure in the ground can be higher than the pressure inside a building, which pushes gas toward indoor air. Gas takes the path of least resistance, so sand and gravel carry it more easily than clay or silt. And methane, being lighter than air, tends to move upward, but compacted waste or a clay cover can block that and send it sideways instead. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and can pool in low spots such as utility corridors.

The same primer says gas can enter buildings through cracks, gaps, drain pipes and duct work, and that basements, crawl spaces and buried utility entries are the most common places for it to collect. It also lists furnaces and pilot lights among the possible ignition sources and advises assuming an ignition source is always present. For a technician, the point is simple: a heater that burns gas inside a room is an ignition source in the one place where gas may collect.

The numbers that matter

Methane burns in air only between about 5 and 15 percent by volume, according to the federal health primer and a university extension note. Inside the landfill the gas is near 50 percent, above the upper limit, so it is not the landfill itself that explodes. The danger starts after the gas migrates and thins into the explosive range inside a closed space. Detectors for this job read in percent of the lower explosive limit (LEL), where 100 percent LEL is 5 percent methane by volume. The primer also reports that landfill owners must keep methane below 25 percent of the LEL in facility structures, which is 1.25 percent by volume, and below 5 percent by volume at the facility boundary. Treat those as limits on the owner, not as targets for how a technician sets an alarm.

The federal workplace safety rule for permit-required confined spaces calls an atmosphere hazardous when flammable gas exceeds 10 percent of its lower flammable limit, and says to test oxygen first, then combustible gases, then toxic gases. A pump pit or vault at a leachate building may be such a space. Entering one is a permit job, not a quick look. See our confined spaces lesson for the testing order.

Detector placement for a lighter-than-air gas

A university extension note on methane advises placing detectors near the ceiling because methane is lighter than air. That fits a heated building: warm air and methane both rise, so gas that leaks in at the slab tends to collect under the roof deck, in the peak of a shop, or in any ceiling pocket that a beam or duct makes. Our practical points, drawn from that rule:

  • Put the main detector high, near the ceiling and away from supply air that would blow past it, or from an open door that dilutes the reading.
  • In a building with a raised ceiling pocket or a loft, add a detector at the highest point where gas could sit.
  • Methane is not the only gas. Carbon dioxide from the same source is heavier than air and can collect low, and it can displace oxygen. The health primer cites below 19.5 percent oxygen as oxygen deficient. A methane detector will not see it, so low-lying pits and sumps need their own atmosphere testing before anyone enters.
  • Check that the detector is rated for the gas, is calibrated on a schedule, and that its alarm output does what the site plan says. The primer says continuous methane monitors with appropriate alarms belong in buildings where explosive gas could accumulate, with frequent safety checks and maintenance. See our leak detector lesson for setpoints and mounting at home.

Heaters, pilots and ignition sources

Here is our reasoning from the ignition-source rule above. An open-flame unit heater, a pilot light, a gas water heater with a draft hood and a standing-pilot furnace all pull room air into the flame. If that air carries methane, the flame meets fuel. Keep these away from known entry points such as slab cracks, floor drains, sumps, pipe penetrations and cable entries, and do not place them in a pit or low room.

Better choices for a building at risk are separated-combustion or sealed-combustion equipment, which takes combustion air from outdoors through a closed path and exhausts outdoors, so the flame does not draw room air. Electric heat, or a hot-water or electric unit heater fed from a boiler in a separate building, takes the flame out of the room altogether and is worth asking about for a pump building. Even a sealed unit still has a gas valve, an electrical control and a spark or hot surface igniter, so a sealed heater does not make a gassy room safe. See our garage and workshop heater lesson and our sealed-combustion wall heater lesson. The digester side of this problem is in our digester heating lesson.

Ventilation and slab sealing basics

The health primer explains that gas enters when pressure inside a building is lower than in the surrounding soil. Its control options fall in two groups. Leakage controls, such as caulking cracks and gaps, fixing plumbing penetrations and adding a low-permeability liner, are said to have had limited success on their own. Pressure controls, such as passive or active venting of the ground below the building, work on the pressure difference. Passive venting works only where underground gas concentrations are low; active venting uses fans and needs maintenance and power. Sealing is still worth doing, but we treat it as a helper to venting and detection, never a replacement. A building-side fan that exhausts air can lower indoor pressure and pull more gas in, so ask the site gas engineer before adding exhaust. Related soil-gas ideas are in our radon mitigation lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers, not site data. Assume a detector, calibrated for methane, reads 20 percent LEL in a shop. Assume the room is 20 ft by 30 ft by 10 ft, and the alarm is set at 10 percent LEL. Assume methane enters the slab at a steady 0.5 L/min.

  1. Percent by volume. 100 percent LEL is 5 percent methane, so 20 percent LEL is 0.20 x 5 = 1.0 percent by volume.
  2. ppm. One percent is 10,000 ppm, so the reading is 10,000 ppm. The 10 percent LEL alarm is 0.5 percent, or 5,000 ppm. The reading is twice the alarm.
  3. Dilution airflow. Target half the alarm level, 5 percent LEL = 0.25 percent = 0.0025. With good mixing, methane fraction = inflow / (inflow + air flow), which is close to inflow / air flow when the gas is a small share. Air flow = 0.5 L/min / 0.0025 = 200 L/min. Convert: 200 / 28.317 = 7.06 cfm.
  4. Check. 0.5 / (200 + 0.5) = 0.00249, which is 0.25 percent, or 5 percent LEL. Holding exactly at the alarm (0.005) would need 100 L/min (3.5 cfm), so a half-alarm target leaves margin.
  5. Room perspective. The room holds 6,000 ft3, about 169,900 L. With no ventilation at all, reaching 1.0 percent takes 1,699 L of methane, which at 0.5 L/min is 3,398 minutes, about 57 hours. The 7.06 cfm of dilution is only 0.07 air changes per hour (7.06 x 60 / 6,000).

The lesson in the numbers: a slow leak can be handled by modest airflow, but real inflow varies and gas does not mix evenly, because it layers near the ceiling. That is why we rely on detectors and engineered controls, and why we never rest a safety decision on a mixing calculation like this one. It is a study example only.

When a detector alarms

Follow the site emergency plan first. As a general sequence: leave the area without turning light switches, tools or vehicles on or off, since they can spark. Warn others and keep people out. Call the site contact or emergency number from a safe distance outdoors. Do not re-enter to silence the alarm or to check the heater. Only people with the right gas instruments and training should test the atmosphere, and the health primer cautions that sampling equipment must not introduce an ignition source. Find the cause and fix the entry path before the building is restored to service, and log the event.

Field card: Heating buildings near landfills

Gas enters low, collects high, and flames do not belong in the way.

  • Landfill gas is about half methane; it migrates through soil, cracks, pipes and utility entries.
  • Methane burns between about 5 and 15 percent by volume. 100 percent LEL = 5 percent by volume = 50,000 ppm.
  • Detector goes near the ceiling; carbon dioxide can pool low and displace oxygen.
  • Keep open flames, pilots and draft-hood appliances away from entry points; prefer sealed combustion or no flame in the room.
  • Sealing helps but has had limited success alone; venting and detection do the real work.
  • Alarm: leave, keep people out, call from a safe place, do not re-enter.
