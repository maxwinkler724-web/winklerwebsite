Home Insulation Types Explained: R per Inch, Air Sealing and HVAC Effects

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Insulation is sold by type, thickness and an R-value, and the numbers get confusing fast. This lesson sorts the common materials, gives approximate R per inch from government and university tables, and explains how insulation, air sealing, vapor control and spray foam change what your furnace or air conditioner has to do.

What R-value means

R-value is resistance to heat flow. A bigger R means less heat moves through the same area. Because it adds up layer by layer, thickness matters as much as material: R per inch times inches gives the total R. For how layers combine in series with air films and framing, see Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings. The R per inch figures below are approximate ranges. Real products vary with density, age and how well they are installed, so the label on the product governs.

The common types and their R per inch

The U.S. Department of Energy Energy Saver page on insulation types groups the materials by form: blankets (batts and rolls), loose-fill and blown-in, rigid foam board, and sprayed or foamed-in-place. The per-inch numbers in this table come from Table 3.2 of a DOE Building Technologies insulation guide, which it labels as approximate values for general comparison.

Material and formR per inch (approximate)
Fiberglass batts, standard density2.9 to 3.8
Fiberglass blown-in, loose-fill (attics)2.2 to 2.7
Cellulose blown-in, loose-fill2.9 to 3.4
Mineral wool (batts, loose-fill)3.7
Expanded polystyrene (EPS) board3 to 4
Extruded polystyrene (XPS) board5.0
Polyisocyanurate board5.6 to 8 (7.1 to 8.7 foil-faced)
Spray foam, open-cell, low density3.5 to 3.8
Spray foam, closed-cell, medium density7 to 9

Other sources differ a little, which is normal. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory loose-fill page lists 2.2 to 2.7 for loose-fill fiberglass, 3.2 to 3.8 for loose-fill cellulose and 3.0 to 3.3 for mineral wool. A University of Maryland Extension page lists about 3.7 for loose-fill cellulose and about 3.1 for mineral wool. An older DOE materials page gives 5.5 to 6.5 for closed-cell spray polyurethane and about 3.6 for open-cell, and notes closed-cell foam can lose some R as its cell gas escapes over time (thermal drift). When two reliable tables disagree, we show the range and do not pretend to a single number.

What each type is good for

  • Fiberglass batts and loose-fill. Low cost and easy to add over an attic floor. Loose-fill fiberglass is the lowest R per inch here, so it needs the most depth. The PNNL page says fiberglass is the easiest to under-fill ("fluff"), which cuts the R you actually get.
  • Cellulose. Higher R per inch than loose-fill fiberglass. The same PNNL page says it settles 10 to 20 percent over time, versus 2 to 4 percent for fiberglass, so it is installed deeper to end at the target.
  • Mineral wool. Rock or slag fibers with a mid-range R per inch, sold as batts and loose-fill.
  • Rigid foam boards. Foam board is often used on exterior walls, rim areas and roofs. DOE says it can resist heat flow up to twice as well as most materials at the same thickness.
  • Spray foam. It is both insulation and an air barrier. DOE describes closed-cell foam as higher R and better at resisting moisture and air, but costlier, and open-cell as cheaper and lighter. It must be covered by a thermal barrier equal in fire resistance to half-inch gypsum board.

Air sealing is not the same as insulation

Insulation slows conduction. It mostly does not stop air. The DOE guide says air sealing before you add insulation is essential, because leaks carry heat and moisture and most insulation does not stop airflow. A University of Maryland Extension page lists the usual attic leak points: plumbing stacks, recessed lights, wiring holes, ductwork penetrations, wall tops and the attic hatch. Spray foam handles both jobs because it fills the gap and insulates. Sealing first often cuts drafts and makes rooms feel steadier, which can change how long the system runs. Our attic insulation and air sealing lesson goes deeper on attics.

Vapor control basics

A vapor retarder slows water vapor moving through a wall or ceiling. The DOE guide classes them by permeability: Class I at 0.1 perm or less, Class II above 0.1 up to 1.0, and Class III above 1.0 up to 10. It reports that Building America scientists do not recommend Class I retarders except in the coldest climates, and that facing on batts goes on the warm-in-winter side and should never be sandwiched between layers of added insulation, where it can trap moisture. The Energy Saver page notes some codes do not count sprayed foam as a vapor barrier, so an added retarder may be required. Local code and climate decide the details, so ask the building department before you add a plastic sheet or paint-on retarder.

Our own worked example: R-38 in an attic

This is our own example, not a published case. Assumptions (ours): an attic floor needs R-38; the whole R comes from one material; we use the midpoint of the DOE guide range for each; we ignore settling, framing and the small R of other layers.

Midpoints: loose-fill fiberglass (2.2 + 2.7) / 2 = 2.45 R per inch. Loose-fill cellulose (2.9 + 3.4) / 2 = 3.15. Closed-cell spray foam (7 + 9) / 2 = 8.0.

Thickness = target R / R per inch.

  • Loose-fill fiberglass: 38 / 2.45 = 15.5 inches. Range check: 38 / 2.7 = 14.1 inches up to 38 / 2.2 = 17.3 inches.
  • Cellulose: 38 / 3.15 = 12.1 inches. Range check: 38 / 3.4 = 11.2 up to 38 / 2.9 = 13.1 inches.
  • Closed-cell foam: 38 / 8.0 = 4.75 inches. If we instead used the older DOE figure of 6.0 per inch, 38 / 6.0 = 6.3 inches.

The takeaway: the higher the R per inch, the thinner the layer, which matters where depth is limited, such as a cathedral ceiling or an attic edge. It does not mean the foam is automatically the better buy, since cost, moisture behavior and installation conditions also count.

Why spray foam can move the ducts into conditioned space

Instead of insulating the attic floor, a crew can spray foam on the underside of the roof deck. A Building America solution page from PNNL describes this as an unvented, conditioned attic, and says one reason to build it is to provide a conditioned space for locating HVAC equipment in the attic. Ducts and air handler then sit inside the thermal boundary, so duct losses go into the house's own space instead of a hot or cold attic. The same page warns that ducts and air handlers in the attic can be a source of major air leaks, so a compact layout with short straight runs, sealing and leakage testing are recommended, and low-efficiency heating equipment that draws combustion air from the attic should not be installed. It also says the roof deck must stay warm enough to avoid condensation, and in cold climates heat from attic ducts or equipment can warm the deck and cause snow melt and ice dams. Moving the ducts is a design decision for the installer and local code, not a bonus. Compare with air handlers and ducts in unconditioned attics and duct insulation R-values.

Spray foam safety: ventilation and staying out

EPA guidance on spray polyurethane foam is plain: during installation only workers wearing appropriate protective equipment should be present, and other trades and building occupants should leave. EPA says to restrict re-entry until the foam has cured, and for occupants to return only after the building has been ventilated and cleaned. EPA does not set one fixed time. It says re-entry time depends on the product and its cure rate, notes that some makers cite about 24 hours for two-component professional systems, and says to get ventilation and re-entry guidance for the specific product from the maker or supplier. Its ventilation guidance for applicators calls for keeping the work area enclosed and under negative pressure, exhausting to the outdoors away from people and air intakes, and sealing HVAC openings. EPA also lists failing to shut down the HVAC system as a bad practice that can spread chemicals through the building. Children and people with asthma are especially vulnerable. If anyone develops breathing problems after foam work, EPA says to seek medical attention. Disturbing foam that has not cured, such as by cutting it, without ventilation can also expose people.

Heating and cooling effects in plain terms

  • More R and less leakage lower the heat the equipment must add or remove, which can allow smaller equipment on a proper load calculation.
  • Tighter homes need a planned ventilation approach; sealing does not remove the need for fresh air or combustion air for fuel-burning equipment.
  • Heavier masonry walls respond differently; see thermal mass in homes.

Field card: Home insulation types

Seal the air, then add R, and keep out of the house during foam.

  • R per inch times inches equals total R; tables give ranges, so read the product label.
  • Loose-fill fiberglass about 2.2 to 2.7, cellulose about 2.9 to 3.4, closed-cell foam about 7 to 9 (DOE guide table).
  • Air sealing first; most insulation does not stop air.
  • Class I vapor retarders are not recommended outside the coldest climates.
  • Spray foam on the roof deck can put ducts in conditioned space; sealing and moisture design matter.
  • EPA: occupants stay out during application and until ventilated, cured and cleaned; follow the maker's re-entry guidance.
