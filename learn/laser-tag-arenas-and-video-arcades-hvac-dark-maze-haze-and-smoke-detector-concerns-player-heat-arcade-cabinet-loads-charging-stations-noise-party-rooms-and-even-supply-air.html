Laser Tag Arena and Arcade HVAC: Haze, Heat and Airflow

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A laser tag arena and a video arcade sit side by side in many family entertainment spaces, and they stress an air system in opposite ways. The arcade is a crowd of small electric heaters. The arena is a dark maze full of running players and, often, drifting haze that makes the beams visible. This lesson explains what to check and how to size the arcade-side load.

Why these rooms are different

Most of the floor is one of two things: dense electronics, or fast-moving people in a maze. Both put heat into the air all evening, and both are usually crowded on the same busy weekend afternoons. The maze also has walls and partitions that block airflow, so a diffuser that looks fine on a plan can leave one corner hot and another still.

This page does not repeat the small closed-room carbon dioxide and fog details already covered in Escape Rooms and Haunted Attractions HVAC, the computer-heat details in Esports and Gaming Lounge HVAC, or the big-box jumping-room details in Trampoline Parks and Indoor Play Centers HVAC. Read those for the overlap.

Haze and detectors

A federal measurement laboratory explains that photoelectric detectors shine light inside a chamber and alarm when particles scatter some of it onto a sensor, while ionization detectors alarm when particles reduce a small electric current. The same page says a detector sensitive enough to catch nearly all fires will also respond to harmless particles such as cooking smoke and dust. Haze is made of fine droplets or particles, so it is the same kind of nuisance source. We read that page for those points only; it does not discuss haze effects specifically.

What follows from that is practical, and it is a coordination job, not a trick you do alone:

  • Never cover, bag or disable a detector to get through a show. That is a fire-safety and code matter for the fire official and the alarm contractor.
  • Ask who owns the alarm system and the effect machines before you move any diffuser or return. Where haze is allowed, the approved arrangement is set with the local authority.
  • Note that supply air carries haze. A detector close to a diffuser, or a return that pulls haze toward a detector, changes what that detector sees. Placing air devices is part of the same conversation.

Keeping haze from blowing around

Haze that hangs evenly looks good; haze that is shoved into one corner by a strong jet does not. Use many small, low-velocity supply points spread across the maze instead of a few large throws. Keep returns away from the zones where haze is generated, and avoid aiming grilles along the main corridors. Our own advice for the commissioning visit is to run the effect machine with the air handler on and watch which way the haze travels, then adjust. We have no measured velocity target to quote, so we do not give one.

Heat sources

Arcade cabinets and redemption games. Nearly all electricity a cabinet draws ends up as heat in the room. A federal energy agency lists 1 kilowatt-hour as 3,412 Btu, which works out to about 3.412 Btu/h for each watt running continuously. Read the nameplate or measure the draw on a few typical cabinets, because cabinets vary a lot. Redemption games add motors, lights and ticket printers.

Vest and gun charging stations. Racks of chargers are small loads each but run for hours and are packed into one wall or back room. Treat that closet as its own zone: it needs a path for its heat to leave, such as a transfer grille or a small exhaust, not just the general arena supply.

Players. Running players give off much more heat than seated ones, and they sweat, so humidity rises with a crowd. Our own working figure for the example below is rounded and light; a real arena of sprinting teens can be higher, so size with margin.

Noise

Arcade rooms are loud and arenas use music and sound effects, so people assume the air noise does not matter. It still does in the party room and at the front counter, where staff speak to guests. Keep duct velocity modest, line or oversize runs near those spaces, and keep rooftop or ceiling units away from the party rooms where you can.

Party rooms

Party rooms fill for short, intense periods, then sit empty. Give them their own thermostat or a zone damper so a full room of guests with food and cake does not drive the whole system, and so the room can be set back when empty. See Bowling Center and Family Entertainment HVAC for how event crowds and kitchens behave in a larger entertainment building.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not measured data from any venue. Estimate the sensible internal load in the arcade zone only.

  • Assumption: 20 cabinets, 250 W each, all of it becoming heat.
  • Assumption: 30 players in the zone, each adding 250 Btu/h of sensible heat.
  • Assumption: a ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h, the trade convention.

Step 1, cabinets: 20 x 250 W = 5,000 W. Then 5,000 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = 17,060 Btu/h.

Step 2, players: 30 x 250 Btu/h = 7,500 Btu/h.

Step 3, total sensible load: 17,060 + 7,500 = 24,560 Btu/h.

Step 4, tons: 24,560 / 12,000 = 2.05 tons.

That is only this slice of the load. It leaves out the building envelope, lighting, outdoor air, moisture from people, the charging room and the arena itself. Do a full load calculation for the building; use this to see how large the internal part is.

Field card: Laser Tag Arena and Arcade HVAC

Many small heaters, running people, and haze that must not be disturbed carelessly.

  • Cabinet watts x 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Never cover or disable a detector; coordinate with the fire official and alarm contractor.
  • Use many gentle supply points and keep returns away from haze sources.
  • Give the charging room and party rooms their own relief or zone control.
  • Our example: 20 cabinets at 250 W plus 30 players gives 24,560 Btu/h, about 2.05 tons, before everything else.
