Boiler Circulator Pump Not Working: Humming, Seized or No Power

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The boiler is hot and the radiators are cold, and you suspect the circulator. A wet-rotor circulator can fail three ways: it gets no power, it hums but does not turn, or it turns but moves no water. Each one points to a different next step, and each maker's own sheet tells you what is safe to do.

Start with safety

Grundfos warns that the pumped liquid may be scalding hot and under pressure, and its instructions say to drain the system or close the isolation valves before removing screws. Grundfos also says never to make connections in the pump terminal box unless the supply is off. Taco's cartridge sheet adds that before opening the system you drop pressure to 0 psi and let it cool to room temperature. Treat the pump housing as hot, and kill power with a proper lockout first; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC.

None of the Taco, Grundfos or Armstrong sheets we read gives a "feel the housing" or "listen for the hum" test, so this lesson does not teach one. Use meters and the shaft check below instead.

Which pump is it?

All three families here are wet-rotor circulators: the motor rotor spins in the system water, which cools and lubricates it. Taco's Model 007 submittal describes a cartridge design with no mechanical seal and a replaceable cartridge that holds all the moving parts. It lists a 115 V, 3250 RPM motor drawing about 0.74 A (cast iron), with a maximum of 23 GPM and 10 feet of head. Grundfos UPS and Armstrong Astro pumps are the same basic idea. Armstrong and Taco both describe the motor as impedance protected, so a stalled rotor should not burn it up quickly, but it can sit there humming and warm.

Sort the failure: no power, humming, or seized

  • No power. Grundfos's troubleshooting chart for a pump that will not run with no indicator lights says to check fuses, the external breaker and any ground fault interrupter, and that the thermal overload may have tripped. On a boiler, also ask whether the control is calling at all. A relay, aquastat or zone end switch may be holding the pump off; see Boiler Controls: Aquastats and Cutoffs, Zone Valves vs. Zone Circulators and Hot-Water No-Heat Diagnosis. Relay and 24 V basics are in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.
  • Humming, not turning. Grundfos's chart lists a blocked rotor as a possible cause when power is present. Its instruction is to cut the power first, then clean or repair the pump.
  • Turning, but little flow. Air in the pump or piping is the usual suspect. Grundfos lists air in the pump as a cause of pump noise, and air in the system as a cause of system noise.

Checking whether the rotor is free

Grundfos and Armstrong both document the same slot test, with the power off. Grundfos says the vent plug sits at the center of the nameplate. Remove it, insert a medium flat-blade screwdriver in the slot at the exposed end of the shaft and turn it gently. Armstrong's Astro sheet describes the same slot in the end of the rotor shaft once the back plug is out. If the shaft turns freely, the rotor is not the problem. If it does not, Grundfos says to repeat its reinstallation steps and, in its troubleshooting chart, to clean or repair the pump. Expect water to come out of the open plug hole, so isolate the pump first and have a plan for the water. We did not find a Taco sheet with this slot procedure, so do not assume it applies to a Taco.

Replacing a Taco cartridge

Taco's instruction sheet for its 00 Series cartridge circulators lists the order: disconnect power, reduce pressure to 0 psi and cool the system, isolate the circulator by closing service valves or draining, remove the body bolts and swing the motor assembly away, pull the cartridge, and install the new one. The sheet stresses that the cover plate must sit between the cartridge flange and the motor. It also says to use only the O-ring gaskets provided, not flat rubber gaskets. The 007 submittal says the design is field serviceable, which is why replacing the cartridge can be cheaper than replacing the pump. Check the cartridge part number against the pump nameplate first.

Air in the pump

Taco says to run the circulator for 5 minutes immediately after filling the system to clear air from the bearing chamber. Grundfos says to fill, pressurize and vent the piping by suitable means and not to vent the piping through the pump. Armstrong's manual method is to turn the power off, loosen (but not remove) the plug on the back of the motor until water flows with no bubbles, and retighten it before restarting. All three say never to run the pump dry. For where the air comes from, see Air Elimination and Dirt Separation and Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure.

Mounting and isolation

All three makers want the motor shaft horizontal. Grundfos allows about 5 degrees of tilt on the model whose instructions we read, and Taco allows motor-up vertical mounting only when system pressure is at least 20 psi. Taco's sheet also says the body arrow shows flow direction. Taco and Grundfos say to close isolating or service valves, or drain the system, before servicing. We did not find a maker sheet that calls them "isolation flanges," so install service valves or flanges on both sides when you can, so the next change-out does not mean draining the system.

ECM circulators and their modes

Grundfos's ALPHA1 data booklet lists three control modes: proportional pressure (PP1 and PP2, with PP2 the factory setting), constant pressure (CP1 and CP2) and constant curve (speeds I, II and III). Another Grundfos instruction we read describes a "mini Eye" light display where one rotating yellow light is a warning and two flashing red lights are an alarm that stops the pump. It also lists constant curve as the mode for a zone-pump layout and constant pressure for a zone-valve layout. A pump set to a low curve for a big system can look like a failed pump, so read the display and the mode before you condemn the unit. Armstrong's three-speed Astro uses a rotary switch, and setting 1 runs at about half the speed of setting 3.

Worked example: why low flow feels like a dead pump

MEP Academy gives the water heat formula as BTU/hr = 500 x GPM x ΔT, where 500 comes from 8.33 lb/gal x 60 min/hr (499.8). Say a zone needs 30,000 BTU/hr and was designed for a 20 °F drop.

  1. Flow needed: GPM = 30,000 / (500 x 20) = 30,000 / 10,000 = 3 GPM.
  2. Compare with the Taco 007's published maximum of 23 GPM: 3 / 23 is about 0.13, so the pump needs only a small part of its range.
  3. Now suppose air or a dragging rotor cuts flow to 1.5 GPM and the zone still tries to deliver 30,000 BTU/hr. ΔT = 30,000 / (500 x 1.5) = 30,000 / 750 = 40 °F.

The supply-to-return gap doubled, which is the same clue the existing diagnosis lesson uses. A pump that "runs" but delivers half the water shows up as a hot supply and a cold return. At zero flow the heat moved is zero, however loud the boiler gets. The 500 constant is for plain water; MEP Academy notes it drops for glycol mixes.

Field card: Boiler Circulator Not Working

Power, rotor, flow, in that order.

  • Lock out first. Pump housing and water can scald, and the system may be under pressure.
  • No lights or no run: fuses, breaker, GFI, thermal overload, then the control call (Grundfos chart).
  • Hums and will not turn: suspect a blocked rotor. Cut power, then clean or repair.
  • Slot test: power off, remove the vent or back plug, turn the shaft slot with a flat screwdriver (Grundfos, Armstrong).
  • Taco: replaceable cartridge, cover plate between flange and motor, O-ring gaskets only.
  • Air: run 5 minutes after filling (Taco), or loosen the back plug until bubbles stop (Armstrong). Never run dry.
  • Flow check: BTU/hr = 500 x GPM x ΔT. Half the flow doubles the ΔT.
  • Shaft stays horizontal on all three makers.
