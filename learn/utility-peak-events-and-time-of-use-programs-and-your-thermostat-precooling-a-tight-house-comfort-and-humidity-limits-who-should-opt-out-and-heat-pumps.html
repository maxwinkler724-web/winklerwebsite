Utility Peak Events and Your Thermostat: Precooling, Comfort Limits and Who Should Opt Out

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Some utilities invite homeowners to let a connected thermostat ease off the air conditioner for a few hours on the hottest afternoons. This lesson explains what a peak event is, what the thermostat may do, how precooling helps, and who should think twice before joining.

Check your own program terms

Rules vary by utility. Event length, how often events happen, how far the thermostat may move and whether you can override all differ from one program to the next. We do not describe any particular program here, and we make no promise about bills or incentives. Read the terms before you sign up for anything, and ask the utility if a term is unclear. This page is general education, not a substitute for those terms.

What a peak event is

Electric demand is not flat. On a hot afternoon, many air conditioners run at once and the grid is stressed. A federal energy statistics agency says demand response programs aim to decrease customer demand during times of very high system demand or emergencies, and that they can lower peak demand, which reduces blackout risk and the need for new generation. It also notes that these programs can let the utility cycle customers' air conditioners when needed. A federal energy regulator, in a staff report, describes demand response as customers changing their normal electricity use in response to time-varying prices or incentive payments, so that use drops when wholesale prices are high or when grid reliability is at risk. A time-of-use rate works the same way in a quieter form: the price changes by time of day, and you decide whether to shift your use.

Cooling matters here because it is a big load. The federal energy statistics agency reports that air conditioning was 19% of residential site electricity use in 2020, the largest single share, and that about 89% of homes had air conditioning that year.

What your thermostat may do

If you join, the thermostat may take one or both of these actions during an event, which usually lasts a few hours:

  • Raise the cooling setpoint by a few degrees, so the AC waits longer before starting.
  • Cycle the compressor, running it for part of each period and resting it for the rest.

Either way the house warms slowly instead of the AC stopping dead. Whether the fan keeps running, and whether you can press a button to override, depends on the program and the thermostat. Check both before the first event.

Precooling and why a tight house coasts longer

Precooling means lowering the setpoint a bit in the hours before the event, so the house starts the event cooler. The event then spends that stored coolness instead of starting from the normal setpoint. Two things set how long you coast: the starting temperature and how fast the house gains heat. A house with sealed air leaks, good attic insulation and shaded windows gains heat slowly; a leaky house with sun on the glass gains it fast. See our lesson on finding air leaks for a start on sealing. Precooling is a plan for the AC you already have, not a reason to oversize or run it nonstop, and we do not claim it saves money.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumptions we chose for illustration. We assume the house warms about 1.5 F per hour with the AC off, the event lasts 3 hours, the AC is off the whole time, and the event raises the cooling setpoint to 78 F. Real houses vary, and warming is faster late in the afternoon when the sun hits the walls and roof.

  1. Warming over the event: 1.5 F per hour × 3 hours = 4.5 F.
  2. Precooled start of 72 F: 72 + 4.5 = 76.5 F at the end of the event.
  3. Normal start of 76 F: 76 + 4.5 = 80.5 F at the end.
  4. Difference at the end: 80.5 − 76.5 = 4 F, which is just the 72 versus 76 starting gap, since both warm at the same rate.
  5. When does each reach the 78 F event setpoint? Precooled: (78 − 72) ÷ 1.5 = 4 hours, longer than the 3-hour event, so it never gets there. From 76 F: (78 − 76) ÷ 1.5 = about 1.3 hours, so the house passes the setpoint about 1 hour 20 minutes in.

In practice the second house would see the thermostat try to start the AC at that point, and whether it may depends on how the program handles setpoints. The example shows the idea: a few degrees of precool bought most of the event at a comfortable temperature. Cooling the house to 72 F also takes the AC extra running time beforehand, so it is a trade, not free comfort.

Comfort and humidity limits

Temperature is only half of comfort. When the AC pauses, it also stops pulling moisture out of the air, so indoor humidity can creep up and the house feels clammy even at a modest temperature. If the program lets you set a limit, pick a setting you can live with for the full event, not the most aggressive one. Fans can help while it is mild indoors, but the federal public health agency says to use fans only when indoor temperatures are below 90 F, because above that a fan can raise body temperature. Do not use that as a target; it is the line where a fan stops helping.

Who should opt out or choose a milder setting

The CDC lists infants and young children, adults 65 and older, and people with chronic medical conditions such as asthma or heart disease as being at higher risk in extreme heat, and the national weather agency names the same groups plus pregnant women, and notes that older adults living alone or on certain medications are especially at risk. If anyone in the home is in these groups, or relies on medical equipment or medication that needs a cool room, think hard before joining, and consider the smallest setpoint change or no participation. The same caution applies to pets, which cannot open a window or ask for water; confirm how hot the house may get while you are away. Ask a clinician if you are unsure about a medical need. You can also check the older-adult heat lesson. Whatever you choose, check on neighbors who live alone.

Heat pumps in winter events

Some programs also run in cold weather. A heat pump is different from an AC because raising its heating setpoint can wake the backup electric heat, which pulls much more power. A thermostat that tries to recover quickly after an event can trigger that backup. Read your thermostat manual and program terms on how events are handled in heating mode, and see our lesson on setback recovery and auxiliary versus emergency heat. In very cold weather, check that the house stays safe for people and plumbing; the away-from-home settings lesson covers temperatures to avoid.

If the system does not resume after an event

  1. Look at the thermostat screen for a message such as an event or hold notice, and read it. The event may simply not be over, or a schedule hold may be active.
  2. Check the mode (cool, not off) and setpoint, and clear any hold or override.
  3. Check that the thermostat has power (display lit, new batteries if it uses them).
  4. Look at the breaker for the air handler and outdoor unit, and the filter.
  5. Wait several minutes. Many systems have a built-in delay before the compressor restarts.
  6. If it still does not run, call a licensed technician. If you are in a program, tell the utility so they can check the thermostat setup.

Commercial buildings do the same thing on a larger scale; see demand limiting and load shedding.

Field card: Peak Events and Your Thermostat

Precool, set a limit you can live with, and know who should opt out.

  • A peak event asks homes to use less power for a few hours; rules vary by utility.
  • The thermostat may raise the cooling setpoint or cycle the AC.
  • Our example: 1.5 F/hr x 3 hr = 4.5 F; 72 F start ends at 76.5 F, 76 F start at 80.5 F.
  • Tight, shaded, insulated houses coast longer.
  • Infants, older adults, medical needs and pets: choose mild settings or opt out.
  • Heat pumps in winter: watch for backup heat after an event.
  • If the system does not restart: check messages, mode, power, breaker, filter, then call a technician.
