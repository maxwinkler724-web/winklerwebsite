Type III Low-Pressure Chiller Leak Testing: Raising Pressure, Rupture Disc Limits and Recovery Freeze Risk

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most refrigeration systems leak refrigerant out. A low-pressure chiller can do the opposite and pull room air in, so finding the leak takes a different plan: first raise the shell pressure a little, gently, without ever threatening the rupture disc. This lesson covers the Type III ideas on leak checks, purge run time, water in the refrigerant and the freeze risk during recovery.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This is general study help built from EPA pages and the federal regulation text read in 2026. It is not a procedure for your machine. Your chiller maker's service literature and your site rules come first, and the EPA and eCFR pages listed below are the place to confirm wording.

Why a low-pressure machine pulls air in

The federal definition (40 CFR 82.152) calls an appliance low-pressure when its refrigerant has a liquid saturation pressure below 45 psia at 104 °F, and it lists R-11, R-123, R-113 and R-245fa as examples. A shell holding refrigerant like that, at chilled-water temperatures, sits below atmospheric pressure for much of its life. The pressure outside is higher than the pressure inside, so any gasket, flange, fitting or pinhole on the low side lets air and moisture in, not refrigerant out. A published exam-prep manual for EPA Section 608 (2012 edition) says chillers using CFC-11 or HCFC-123 run below atmospheric and need a purge unit to remove non-condensables. The diagram shows the picture. The purge idea itself is in Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables, so this page only uses it as a clue.

How techs raise pressure for a leak check

You cannot find a leak that only leaks inward by looking for refrigerant escaping from a vacuum, so the shell is brought up from its vacuum first, to a low pressure that gives refrigerant a reason to push out of a leak. EPA's Type III test topics list the order of preference for leak-test pressurization of low-pressure systems: first the hot water method or a built-in heating and pressurization device, then nitrogen. The idea is simple. Warm water run through the tubes, or a built-in heating system, warms the refrigerant so it makes its own vapor pressure. That raises pressure with nothing foreign added to the shell.

Nitrogen is the second choice for a good reason. The regulation at 40 CFR 82.156(a)(1)(ii) covers pressurizing a low-pressure appliance before it is opened. It says a person using a refrigerant whose boiling point is at or below 85 °F must not use methods such as nitrogen that require subsequent purging. For a refrigerant with a boiling point above 85 °F, heat is used to raise the pressure as much as possible, and nitrogen may take it from there up to atmospheric pressure. That paragraph is about getting ready to open the machine, not about the leak-check pressure itself, so read it for what it is: nitrogen adds a gas that later has to be purged, and the rule keeps it as a last step. Look up your refrigerant's boiling point before you assume nitrogen is allowed.

The ceiling: stay under the rupture disc

A low-pressure chiller has a rupture disc on the shell. The exam-prep manual says its typical design burst pressure is 15 psig. The disc is a one-time safety part, so the leak-check pressure has to stay under it with room to spare. EPA's Type III topics include "maximum leak test pressure for low-pressure centrifugal chillers," but the page lists the topic without a number, and we did not find a figure in the regulation either. So we do not teach one here. The real limit is on the machine's data plate and in the maker's manual, and it can be lower than the disc rating. Remember also that the regulation sets 0 psig as the highest pressure for opening a low-pressure appliance, so a leak check is not the pressure you open the machine at.

  • Use a regulator and gauge that read in a low range, not a tank gauge.
  • Raise pressure slowly and watch the gauge as you go.
  • Never connect a full cylinder straight to the shell. For the general nitrogen test setup, see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods.

Purge run time as a leak indicator

EPA lists "signs of leakage into a low-pressure system (e.g., excessive purging)" as a Type III topic. The exam-prep manual says frequent purging, high discharge pressure or moisture in the purge unit can point to air leaks or tube leakage. So the minutes per day the purge runs is a free leak gauge. One day means little. A trend means a lot. Compare it with similar load and weather, and write it down every round.

Water in the refrigerant

Moisture is the other thing that comes in through a leak. The manual links excess moisture collected in the purge unit to tube leakage, which means water may be crossing from the tube side into the refrigerant. The equipment standards rule at 40 CFR 82.158(k) sets a maximum of 20 ppm moisture by weight for refrigerant recycled by equipment advertised as recycling, for the low-pressure refrigerants it lists. That number is a purity limit for recycling equipment, not a leak-check reading. Treat any water in the system as a reason to find the source before the next season.

Recovery: liquid, then vapor, and the freeze risk

The regulation requires evacuating "all the liquid refrigerant" to the Table 1 level (82.156(a)), and Table 2 of 82.158 sets that level at 25 mm Hg absolute for low-pressure appliances. EPA's Type III topics add the practical order: recovering liquid at the start speeds the job, you still must recover the vapor, and after reaching the vacuum you wait a few minutes to see if pressure rises. A rise means refrigerant is still there. If leaks keep you from reaching the level, the regulation says leaking components are evacuated to the lowest level that can be reached without contaminating the refrigerant, and that level may not exceed 0 psig (82.156(a)(2)). Compare how leak repair clocks work in Calculating Refrigerant Leak Rate for EPA 608 Appliances.

The freeze risk comes from the water side. Pulling a deep vacuum drops refrigerant temperature, and the manual warns that liquid in a deep vacuum can freeze water in the tubes, and that a large vacuum pump can freeze trapped water. EPA's topics state the precaution: circulate or remove water from the chiller during refrigerant evacuation to prevent freezing. The same logic runs in reverse on recharging: introduce vapor before liquid, and charge through the evaporator charging valve. Ice can damage tubes, and damaged tubes make a water-to-refrigerant leak.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example. The log values and the pressures below are assumptions we made up for teaching, not figures from EPA or a maker.

Part 1, reading a purge log. A tech writes down purge run minutes per day for one chiller, at similar load.

WeekPurge minutes per day
Start5
After 1 week12
After 2 weeks24
After 3 weeks45
  1. Change over three weeks: 45 − 5 = 40 minutes per day more.
  2. Ratio: 45 ÷ 5 = 9 times the starting run time.
  3. Average climb: 40 ÷ 3 ≈ 13 minutes per day for each week.
  4. The weekly steps are 7, 12 and 21 minutes, so the climb is speeding up.

How the tech reads it: a steady, growing trend that the load does not explain points to a growing inward leak, not a one-off. The steps are getting bigger, so the leak is likely getting bigger. The tech checks moisture collected in the purge unit, since the manual ties it to tube leakage. Then the plan is a leak check with warm water or the built-in heater first, nitrogen only if allowed for the refrigerant and the maker's manual allows it. The log does not say where the leak is. It only says there is one.

Part 2, checking a nitrogen pressure against the disc. Assume the rupture disc is rated 15 psig, taken as the manual's typical value. Assume the maker's manual allows a test pressure of 8 psig. This 8 psig is our assumption, not a published limit.

  1. Margin to the disc: 15 − 8 = 7 psig.
  2. Share of disc rating used: 8 ÷ 15 = 0.53, about 53 percent.
  3. Check: 8 psig is less than 15 psig, so the test pressure stays below the disc. A 12 psig target would leave only 3 psig and 12 ÷ 15 = 80 percent, which is a thin margin for a gauge error or a regulator that drifts.

The answer rule: pick the lowest of the maker's limit and your margin under the disc, and read the real disc rating off your own machine.

Field card: Type III leak testing and recovery

Air in, not refrigerant out.

  • Low-pressure machines run below atmospheric, so leaks pull air and moisture in.
  • EPA lists leak-test pressurization order: hot water or built-in heater first, then nitrogen.
  • Rupture disc: typical design burst 15 psig per the exam-prep manual. Stay well under it and follow the data plate.
  • Excess purging is a sign of leakage. Log run minutes and watch the trend.
  • Recover liquid first, then vapor. Low-pressure target is 25 mm Hg absolute.
  • Circulate or remove water from the chiller during evacuation so tubes do not freeze.
  • Introduce vapor before liquid when recharging.
