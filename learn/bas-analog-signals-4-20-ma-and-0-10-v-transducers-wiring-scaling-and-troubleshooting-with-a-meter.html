4-20 mA and 0-10 V Signals in a BAS: Wiring, Scaling, Meter Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most sensors and actuators on a building automation system do not talk in digits. They send a signal that is just a current or a voltage, and the controller turns it into a number. If you can read that signal with a meter, you can tell a bad sensor from a bad wire from a bad controller input.

Analog inputs and outputs

An analog input (AI) is a controller terminal that reads a changing signal from a field device, such as a duct static pressure transducer or a humidity sensor. An analog output (AO) is a terminal where the controller sends a changing signal to a device, such as a damper actuator or a drive speed reference. Our BAS point types lesson covers how these show up on a graphic. Here we look at the signal itself.

The two common signal types are a voltage, usually 0-10 V DC, and a current, 4-20 mA. A third variation, 2-10 V DC, is a voltage that starts at 2 V instead of 0. Johnson Controls' actuator selection guide lists input signals written as 0(2) to 10 VDC, meaning the range can start at 0 or 2 V, and lists 0(4) to 20 mA actuator inputs that need a 500 ohm resistor.

How a 4-20 mA loop works

In a loop-powered two-wire transmitter, the same two wires carry power in and signal out. Acromag's application paper explains that the transmitter does not supply current. It regulates how much current flows through it, while the power supply sources that current. 4 mA means 0 percent of the range and 20 mA means 100 percent. Because the loop is in series, the current is the same everywhere in it. Seametrics' technical bulletin makes the same point: an ammeter put anywhere in the loop reads the same value.

A typical loop uses a 24 V DC supply. BAPI's application note says a 2-wire transmitter's supply current equals its signal current, and that the transmitter needs a minimum voltage across it (in BAPI's example, 15 V). Carrier's installation sheet for one differential pressure transducer, for example, lists a 16 to 36 V DC supply for the 4-20 mA option with a 250 ohm maximum load, and a 22 to 36 V DC supply for a 500 ohm maximum load. Always check the actual transmitter sheet.

Why 4 mA and not 0

The 4 mA floor is called a live zero. Acromag calls it a failsafe: if a lead breaks, the current falls to zero, which is outside the 4-20 mA signal range, so the controller can tell a dead loop from a true zero reading. A live zero also gives a two-wire transmitter some current to run on. The other big selling point is noise. Acromag says the current loop has low sensitivity to electrical noise, and BAPI calls it a robust standard that resists noise. A plain 0-10 V signal has no live zero, so by our own reasoning a reading of 0 V could mean zero pressure or a broken wire; a 2-10 V signal avoids that.

Turning current into volts: the 250 ohm resistor

Many BAS inputs read volts, not milliamps. A precision resistor across the input converts the loop current to voltage by Ohm's law. BAPI says 250 ohms is the most common value and drops 1 V at 4 mA and 5 V at 20 mA, and it notes a 500 ohm resistor gives 2 to 10 V for a 0-10 V input. Honeywell's VisualLogic installation guide says its 4-20 mA inputs need an external 250 ohm, 1/4 W, 1 percent resistor across the input and common, with the input jumper set to 0-5 V, and that a 0-10 V device must go only to an input with a jumper setting for 0-10 V. Some controllers build the resistor in and select it with a jumper or software, so read the controller's own manual.

Scaling to engineering units

The controller needs to know what 4 mA (or 0 V) and 20 mA (or 10 V) mean. Honeywell's guide describes a scaled analog input with a zero value and a range, and the controller applies the line between them. For a 4-20 mA signal on a transducer with range R:

Value = (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × R. For a 0-10 V signal that starts at zero: Value = V ÷ 10 × R. Same idea for 2-10 V, with 2 V as the start and 8 V as the span. Honeywell notes that a 4-20 mA output can be made from a 0-10 V style output by scaling the output so it runs 20 to 100 percent, and the same method yields 2-10 V.

Worked example (our own assumptions)

Assumptions, ours and not from a particular product: a duct static transducer ranges 0 to 5 inches of water column (in. w.c.). Its output is linear, 4 mA at 0 and 20 mA at 5. The 0-10 V version puts 0 V at 0 and 10 V at 5.

  1. Current version, reading 9.6 mA. Subtract the live zero: 9.6 − 4 = 5.6 mA. Divide by the 16 mA span: 5.6 ÷ 16 = 0.35. Multiply by the range: 0.35 × 5 = 1.75 in. w.c.
  2. Voltage version, reading 3.2 V. 3.2 ÷ 10 = 0.32. Multiply by the range: 0.32 × 5 = 1.6 in. w.c.
  3. Voltage across a 250 ohm resistor at 9.6 mA. V = I × R = 0.0096 A × 250 ohm = 2.4 V. Check: 1 V is the live zero, so 2.4 − 1 = 1.4 V above it, over a 4 V span, is 0.35, matching step 1.

So a clamp-on look at 9.6 mA, 2.4 V across the resistor, and 1.75 in. w.c. on the graphic should all agree. If the BAS graphic says something else, suspect the scaling entered in the controller (wrong zero or range) before suspecting the transducer.

Measuring: series for current, parallel for voltage

  • Voltage is measured in parallel. Put the meter leads across the part you care about, such as the 250 ohm resistor or the supply terminals. You do not open the circuit.
  • Current is measured in series. The meter becomes part of the loop. Seametrics describes breaking the loop at the transmitter's positive terminal and inserting the ammeter. Use the meter's milliamp input and range for this, and follow its manual. For meter setup and safety, see our multimeter safety lesson.
  • Fluke notes that opening the loop to hook a meter in series usually means de-energizing the circuit, then re-energizing it, which is why its clamp-style tools measure without breaking the loop.

Common faults

  • Open loop. Seametrics says a 0 mA reading may mean a broken wire, reversed polarity or low supply voltage, and to check supply voltage first. Acromag notes an open loop reads as zero current, clearly below the signal range.
  • Reversed polarity. Only the supply terminals take matching polarity, and a wrong connection stops the loop. Seametrics warns devices may be damaged, though it adds that well-designed products usually have protection.
  • Stuck low or high. If current stays at 4 mA while the measurement rises, Seametrics suggests checking for a misconfigured, unpowered or defective transmitter. If it will not rise above some value under 20 mA, the loop likely has too much voltage drop. Acromag says an under-sized supply can cause a roll-off at the top of the range.
  • Wrong input type. Honeywell says to connect 0-10 V devices only to inputs with a jumper setting for 0-10 V, so check the jumper or configuration against the signal type.
  • Shared common problems. Honeywell's guide says to use the COM terminal closest to the I/O terminal, to connect only input return grounds to input COM terminals, and not to wire an analog output common to an input COM. Johnson Controls says each controller's and peripheral's COM should go to the same side of the transformer, and that connecting two half-wave supplies to the same transformer without keeping polarity causes a short circuit.

Cable and grounding basics

Use twisted pair. Seametrics says twisted pair generally beats shielding for current loops, and shielding can hurt if it is terminated wrongly. Where shielded cable is used, ground the shield at one point only. Carrier's installation sheet says to ground the shield only at the controller end, and Honeywell says to ground one end of the drain wire and not to land it on a controller terminal. Johnson Controls says to keep analog signal cables separated from other cable types.

Field card: 4-20 mA and 0-10 V signals

Current in series, voltage in parallel.

  • 4 mA is 0 percent, 20 mA is 100 percent; under 4 mA means a fault, not a low reading.
  • Value = (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × range. For 0-10 V: V ÷ 10 × range.
  • 250 ohm across the input turns 4-20 mA into 1-5 V.
  • Check supply voltage first when you read 0 mA.
  • Match the input type setting to the signal type.
  • Twisted pair, shield grounded at one end only, no shared commons across inputs and outputs.
