Does Closing Vents in Unused Rooms Save Energy?

Last updated: 2026-10-16

It sounds logical: shut the vent in the spare room and stop paying to heat or cool it. Research says the logic breaks down inside the duct system. This lesson covers what the studies found, why pressure is the reason, and what to do instead.

The short answer

For a typical forced-air house, closing registers in rooms you do not use does not reliably save energy, and it can cost more. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report by Iain Walker (2003), Register Closing Effects on Forced Air Heating System Performance, combined lab measurements of how duct leakage changes as registers close with energy modeling. Its abstract says closing registers increased energy use across a wide range of climates, leakage levels and numbers of closed registers. The smaller heating load from conditioning less of the house was outweighed by higher duct losses, mostly from more leakage. The author did not recommend the technique for houses with ducts outside the conditioned space, such as attic or crawlspace ducts. Advanced Energy, a North Carolina nonprofit, summarized the same study the same way and added that it knew of no published study that had monitored real energy use in occupied homes.

Note: we read the abstract and summaries of the report, not its full text. The detailed test numbers are not on this page.

Why it backfires: pressure

A duct system is a sealed box in theory and a leaky one in practice. The blower pushes air into the supply ducts, so the ducts sit at a positive pressure. Every gap in the duct, at seams, boots and plenum joints, leaks in proportion to that pressure. A closed register adds resistance. The blower has the same job to do through fewer outlets, so pressure in the ducts goes up. Advanced Energy puts it plainly: closing some registers increases the pressure in the ducts, which pushes more air out through leaks, so the equipment runs longer.

The Berkeley report adds a useful detail. The penalty was smallest when the registers farthest from the air handler were closed first, because that mostly affects the closed branch. Closing registers near the air handler raised pressure and leakage across the whole system.

Worked example: how far past the limit are we?

The numbers below come from the sources, except the airflow, which we assume for illustration (a 1,200 cfm system, a common size for a 3-ton air conditioner at 400 cfm per ton).

  1. Pressure: 0.8 iwc measured minus 0.5 iwc design = 0.3 iwc over.
  2. Percent over: 0.3 / 0.5 = 0.60, so about 60 percent above the design maximum. Energy Vanguard says that in a system like this, closing even one vent could push it over the edge.
  3. Leakage: ENERGY STAR says a typical house loses about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through its ducts to leaks and poor connections. 1,200 cfm x 0.20 = 240 cfm lost. 1,200 x 0.30 = 360 cfm lost.
  4. Meaning: before any vent is touched, roughly 240 to 360 cfm may never reach a room. Closing registers raises duct pressure, which the sources say increases leakage from that starting point. The conditioned air you saved by not serving one room can leak into the attic instead.

This is an illustration, not a measurement of your house. Real leakage and pressure need testing.

Safety and equipment risks, as the sources state them

  • Limit on how many. The Berkeley abstract discourages closing more than about 60 percent of registers because the added resistance restricts airflow enough to create safety problems, such as a furnace cycling on its high-limit switch or a cooling coil freezing.
  • Frozen coil and compressor. Energy Vanguard says low airflow can freeze the evaporator coil and damage the compressor. Advanced Energy likewise says closing registers can reduce airflow over the coil and risk compressor failure.
  • Heat exchanger. Energy Vanguard says low airflow can overheat a furnace heat exchanger, which can crack and allow carbon monoxide into the supply air. We did not find this stated in the Berkeley abstract, so treat it as one trade writer's claim and ask your furnace maker.
  • Blower type. Energy Vanguard says a PSC blower moves less air against higher pressure, while an ECM blower uses more energy to hold airflow up.

Better alternatives

  • Seal the ducts. ENERGY STAR recommends mastic sealant or metal tape rather than ordinary duct tape, insulating accessible ducts in attics, crawlspaces, unfinished basements and garages, and sealing register and vent connections. Advanced Energy's advice on closing-register products is the same: seal your ducts instead.
  • Zoning. A zoned system is designed to send air where it is needed, with pressure management. See Zoning and Zone Dampers Basics.
  • Balancing. If one room is too hot or cold, a technician can adjust balancing dampers and measure airflow instead of guessing. See Upstairs Too Hot.
  • Fix the design. Undersized ducts and leaks are covered in Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.

Tech section: static pressure in this question

Static pressure is the push of the air on the duct walls, measured in inches of water column. A technician reads it with a manometer at test points on the equipment and duct, and compares it with the rated limit in the manufacturer's literature. Closing a register moves the system along its fan curve toward higher pressure and lower airflow. Duct leakage rises with pressure, so a leaky system loses more as it is restricted. Read the full method in Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. Always use the rated static pressure for that exact equipment, not a general figure.

What a homeowner can do today

If a room is truly unused, leave its supply open or partly open, and have the system's static pressure measured. If you must restrict a room, a technician can balance the system with the pressure in mind. Never close more registers to fix a problem you have not diagnosed.

Field card: Does Closing Vents in Unused Rooms Save Energy?

A closed vent moves the problem into the ducts.

  • Berkeley Lab (Walker, 2003): closing registers increased energy use in modeled California houses.
  • Closed registers raise duct pressure, so leaky ducts lose more air.
  • Blowers are commonly designed for about 0.5 iwc; a typical field system reads about 0.8 iwc (NCI via Energy Vanguard).
  • Berkeley abstract: do not close more than about 60 percent of registers.
  • Risks named by sources: high-limit trips, frozen coil, compressor damage.
  • Better: seal ducts, balance, consider zoning. Measure static pressure.
