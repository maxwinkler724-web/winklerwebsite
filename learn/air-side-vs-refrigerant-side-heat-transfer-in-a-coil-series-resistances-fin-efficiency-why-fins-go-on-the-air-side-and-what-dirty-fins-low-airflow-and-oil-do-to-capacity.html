Air-Side vs Refrigerant-Side Heat Transfer in a Coil

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Heat leaving the room air and entering the refrigerant has to cross four layers in a row: a film of air, the metal, and a film of refrigerant. They are not equal. On a fin-and-tube coil the air film is by far the biggest hurdle, which is why the coil is covered in fins and why a dirty or starved coil loses so much. This lesson puts numbers on that with a worked example of our own.

The resistance chain

When layers sit in series, their resistances add. The general idea, with fouling terms, is in the lesson on overall U. For a clean coil we need only three terms, all measured per square foot of outside tube surface:

1/U = air film + tube wall + refrigerant film

The air film is 1 divided by (air-side h, times the overall surface efficiency, times the area ratio). The wall is thickness divided by conductivity. The refrigerant film is 1 divided by (refrigerant-side h, times the inside-to-outside area ratio). Whichever term is biggest sets most of U. Speed up the small terms and you gain almost nothing; speed up the big one and U climbs.

Why the two film coefficients are so far apart

Colorado State University heat transfer notes list rough convection coefficients: about 100 W/m²-K for forced convection in a gas such as air, about 1,000 for forced convection in a liquid, and about 20,000 for boiling or condensation. The Purdue heat exchanger chapter uses 14.4 Btu/h-ft²-F (81.8 W/m²-K) for the air side of a finned coil example, close to the 100 figure, and says the air-side resistance is large because of that low coefficient. Air is thin, carries little heat per volume, and moves through a coil slowly. Refrigerant that is boiling or condensing in a tube is a far better heat carrier. The 20,000 figure is a general table value, not a refrigerant tube number, and real in-tube refrigerant values are lower and vary with mass flow and quality, so the example below assumes a more modest value. A university research paper on evaporating refrigerant in smooth and grooved tubes (cited below as plain text) states the same conclusion for coils: the air-side thermal resistance dominates the total.

For why air films behave this way, see natural vs forced convection.

What the fins do

You cannot easily raise the air-side coefficient much, so coil makers add area on the air side instead. Resistance falls as area rises. The Purdue chapter says fins are generally designed for efficiencies between about 0.6 and 0.9. Efficiency is below 1 because the fin tip runs closer to air temperature than the root. The overall surface efficiency blends the fin and the bare tube between fins:

overall efficiency = 1 − (fin area / total area) × (1 − fin efficiency)

Check with the Purdue example numbers: fin area 0.9338 and total 1.114 ft² per foot of tube, fin efficiency 0.763. That gives 1 − 0.838 × 0.237 = 0.801, which matches the overall value the chapter reports. Details on fin shape, spacing and material are in the fins lesson, so we do not repeat them here. The refrigerant side needs no fins because its coefficient is already high; it is the tube's inside surface that does the work.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not data from a coil maker. It is a clean, dry coil. Basis: 1 ft² of outside bare tube surface.

Copper wall0.016 in thick, k = 385 W/m-K = 222.5 Btu/h-ft-Fk from a Georgia State University table; thickness assumed

Step 1, overall surface efficiency: 1 − 0.917 × (1 − 0.85) = 1 − 0.1375 = 0.8625.

Step 2, air film: effective area = 12 × 0.8625 = 10.35 ft². Conductance = 14.4 × 10.35 = 149.0 Btu/h-F. Resistance = 1/149.0 = 0.006710 h-F/Btu.

Step 3, wall: 0.016 in = 0.001333 ft. Resistance = 0.001333 / 222.5 = 0.000006.

Step 4, refrigerant film: 1 / (700 × 0.92) = 1/644 = 0.001553.

Step 5, add: 0.006710 + 0.000006 + 0.001553 = 0.008269 (0.008268 unrounded). U = 1/0.008268 = 121 Btu/h-ft²-F on outside tube area.

Shares: air film 81 percent, refrigerant film 19 percent, copper wall 0.07 percent. Without fins the air film would be 1/14.4 = 0.0694, ten times larger, and U would be about 14. The fins lift U about 8.6 times.

The same 30 percent loss costs U about 26 percent on the air side but only about 7 percent on the refrigerant side, because the air film is the larger share. A resistance that is 81 percent of the chain moves the total; one that is 19 percent barely does. The wall term is so small that even doubling the metal thickness would change U by under 0.1 percent. This is the point of the conductivity lesson.

What this means in the field

  • Dirty fins and low airflow. Real dirt does two things: it coats surfaces and, more importantly, it plugs the fin pack and cuts airflow. A U.S. Department of Energy OSTI report on coil fouling modeled airflow loss of about 5 to 7 percent, and said a 5 to 10 percent flow drop in a properly tuned system cuts capacity, efficiency and power by roughly 2 to 4 percent, with marginal systems losing 10 to 20 percent or more. Clean the air side first. See the fins lesson for bent and fouled fins.
  • Oil logging. Oil that rides with the refrigerant can thicken the inside film. Two OSTI abstracts on in-tube boiling of R-407C found that any oil tended to lower the coefficient in a grooved tube, by 50 percent or more at high vapor quality, and in a plain tube cut it roughly 80 to 90 percent at quality above 70 percent. Those are laboratory tubes and a local effect, not a whole-coil number. As an extreme illustration in our example, an 80 percent cut in refrigerant h (to 140) gives a refrigerant film of 1/(140 × 0.92) = 0.007764, a total of 0.014480 and U of 69.1, down 43 percent. The refrigerant side matters once it is badly degraded. Oil return is covered in the oil management lesson.
  • Tube metal. Copper and aluminum are such good conductors that wall thickness is not what limits capacity. Leaks, kinks, crushed tubes and corrosion are separate failures of the coil, not heat-transfer-through-the-wall problems.
  • What U does not tell you. U is not capacity. Capacity also depends on area, air and refrigerant temperatures and airflow. Our example is a dry coil with no condensate or frost on the fins, and it ignores fouling layers.

Field card: Air-Side vs Refrigerant-Side Heat Transfer in a Coil

The air film is the bottleneck; fins exist to fix it.

  • Coil resistance = air film + tube wall + refrigerant film, all in series.
  • Forced air h is about 100 W/m²-K; boiling or condensing is far higher (a table value near 20,000).
  • Fins add air-side area; fins run about 0.6 to 0.9 efficient.
  • Our example: air 81 percent, refrigerant 19 percent, copper wall under 0.1 percent.
  • A 30 percent drop in air h cuts U about 26 percent; on the refrigerant side, about 7 percent.
  • Clean the fins and restore airflow first; heavy oil logging hurts the refrigerant side too.
