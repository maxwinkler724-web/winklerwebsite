The Common Pipe (Decoupler) in Chilled Water Plants: Flow Direction, Deficit Flow and Staging

Last updated: 2026-11-04

In a primary-secondary chilled water plant, one short piece of pipe quietly decides whether the buildings get the water temperature they asked for. It is the common pipe, also called the decoupler or bridge. This lesson covers what it does, which way water moves through it, and what that tells an operator. The general idea of primary-secondary piping is in Primary-Secondary Piping Basics, and the causes of weak temperature rise are in Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained, so we do not repeat them here.

What the common pipe does

The primary circuit is the chillers and their pumps. The secondary circuit is the distribution pumps, piping and coils. The two circuits meet at a short, large pipe that links the supply header to the return header. Its job is to let the two pump sets move different amounts of water without fighting each other. A university design guideline says the pipe should be sized for the full flow of the largest chiller, and one campus metering guide describes the connection as a pipe with low pressure loss that is common to both circuits.

Which way the water goes

The common pipe carries only the difference between the two pump flows. Think of it as a relief path.

  • Supply to return (excess primary flow). The chillers are pumping more than the building is pulling. The extra chilled water has nowhere to go but across the pipe and back to the chillers. The secondary supply stays at the chiller leaving temperature, but the chillers see a cooler return than the building sent, so their temperature rise shrinks.
  • Return to supply (deficit flow). The building is pulling more than the chillers are making. The missing water comes across the pipe from the return header. Warm return water mixes into the supply, so the secondary supply temperature rises above the chiller setpoint.
  • No flow. The two pump flows match.

Excess flow wastes pumping and hides a chiller that could be shut off. Deficit flow is the one that shows up as warm supply water and complaints from the building, so it gets the attention.

How deficit flow raises the supply temperature

The mix at the secondary supply is a flow-weighted average. Multiply each stream's flow by its temperature, add them, and divide by the total flow. The coils then receive warmer water than designed. Their control valves open further to make up the lost capacity, which pulls more flow and can deepen the deficit. Our worked example below shows the numbers.

Why coils drive the deficit

Each coil returns water at some temperature rise. When coils return water cooler than designed, each gallon carries less heat, so the building needs more gallons for the same load. A national-lab report prepared for a federal agency says it is not uncommon to find plants across the country running a loop temperature difference below 5 F, and it suggests trending and alarming when the loop difference stays below roughly 3 F to 5 F. Low coil temperature rise is the usual starting point. More flow is needed for the same cooling, the secondary pumps outrun the primary pumps, and the common pipe starts flowing in the deficit direction. The causes of that low rise are covered in the related low delta-T lesson.

Staging chillers and primary pumps from the common pipe

Because the pipe shows the mismatch directly, it is a natural input for staging. One university guideline states that chiller staging shall be via decoupler flow volume. In general terms, a sustained deficit flow says add a chiller and its primary pump, and an excess flow larger than the flow of one running chiller, with some margin, says one can be dropped. Delays matter, so a brief swing does not cause hunting. The thresholds and delays belong to the designer and the plant controls, so do not change them in the field without the engineer. Another campus guideline calls for variable speed drives on both primary and secondary pumps to minimize flow balance through the decoupler. Staging by kW per ton is covered in the staging lesson.

Check valves in the common pipe

This part is our own reasoning, not a quoted rule. A check valve passes water one way. The common pipe has to pass water either way, because the mismatch can flip. A check valve in it blocks one direction, adds pressure drop in the other, and can chatter at the low flows the pipe usually sees. Blocking the deficit direction would starve the secondary pumps. Blocking the excess direction would push the primary pumps toward a dead head. If a drawing shows one, ask the design engineer why before you replace or remove it. Check valves do belong on pump discharges, and the slam problems there are in the check valve slam lesson.

Sizing for low pressure drop

The pipe is sized so that the pressure difference between its two ends stays small at any flow it carries. If its pressure drop were large, the primary pumps and secondary pumps would see each other through it, and the pipe would no longer decouple them. The pipe size follows the guideline above (full flow of the largest chiller). Keep it short and straight, and keep pumps and branch connections away from the junctions, as the piping basics lesson shows.

Flow meter and temperature sensor placement

Where designers read the common pipe, they usually want two things: which way water moves and how much, and what temperature the mixed stream is. These points come from general practice and need the maker's instructions:

  • A meter in the common pipe has to read both directions and accurately at low velocity, since the pipe is large and the flow is often small. Ask the meter maker about the range.
  • A campus metering guide for building meters says a magnetic meter must not be installed downstream of a control valve, wants 5 pipe diameters of straight pipe upstream and 2 downstream for same-size piping, and wants a return sensor at least 7 pipe diameters downstream of the last connected tee so the streams mix before they are read. Those rules are for building metering, but they show why sensor spacing matters near tees.
  • A sensor placed right at a junction can read one stream, not the mix. Compare it with a sensor farther downstream before trusting it. See Chilled-Water Pumps and Delta-T.

Worked example (our own example)

These are our own assumptions for teaching, not data from any plant. A plant has chillers that each pump 1,000 gpm and make 44 F water. Two chillers run. The building return is 50 F, a low 6 F temperature rise. The secondary pumps are moving 2,500 gpm.

Step 1: flow through the common pipe. Primary flow is 2 x 1,000 = 2,000 gpm. Secondary flow is 2,500 gpm. The difference is 2,500 - 2,000 = 500 gpm, so the pipe carries 500 gpm return to supply. That is a deficit.

Step 2: mixing temperature at the secondary supply. (2,000 gpm x 44 F + 500 gpm x 50 F) / 2,500 gpm = (88,000 + 25,000) / 2,500 = 113,000 / 2,500 = 45.2 F. The supply is 1.2 F warmer than the chiller setpoint.

Step 3: the load. Using the common relation, tons = gpm x temperature rise / 24: 2,500 x 6 / 24 = 625 tons. The two chillers actually make 2,000 x 6 / 24 = 500 tons at that return, which is why the mix is warm.

Step 4: add a third chiller. Primary flow is now 3,000 gpm, which is 500 gpm more than the building pulls. The common pipe now carries 500 gpm supply to return, and the secondary supply is back at 44 F. Check: the chillers receive (2,500 x 50 + 500 x 44) / 3,000 = 147,000 / 3,000 = 49.0 F. Their rise is 5 F, and 3,000 x 5 / 24 = 625 tons, matching the load.

What it shows. Assume each chiller is built for a 10 F rise at 1,000 gpm, about 417 tons. Three chillers are 1,250 tons, so the plant runs at 50 percent load with all three on. At a 10 F rise, 625 tons would need only 625 x 24 / 10 = 1,500 gpm, and two chillers would cover it with no deficit. The low rise cost us a whole extra chiller and its pump. Now dropping back to two chillers would only reopen the deficit, since the 500 gpm excess is less than one chiller's 1,000 gpm.

Field card: The Common Pipe (Decoupler)

The common pipe carries only the mismatch between primary and secondary flow.

  • Supply to return: excess primary flow. Supply stays at the chiller temperature.
  • Return to supply: deficit flow. Warm return mixes into the secondary supply.
  • Mix temperature = sum of (flow x temperature) divided by total flow.
  • Sustained deficit flow points to adding a chiller; excess larger than one chiller's flow points to dropping one. Thresholds belong to the designer.
  • Keep the pipe large and short so its pressure drop stays low.
  • Ask the engineer before adding or removing a check valve in it.
  • Low coil temperature rise is the usual cause of deficit flow.
