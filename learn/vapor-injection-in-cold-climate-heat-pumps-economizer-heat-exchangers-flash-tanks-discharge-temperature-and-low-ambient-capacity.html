Vapor Injection in Cold-Climate Heat Pumps: Economizers and Flash Tanks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many cold-climate heat pumps use a trick called vapor injection. A second, smaller flow of refrigerant vapor is fed into the compressor partway through compression. It is one of the reasons a heat pump can keep real capacity at 5 F and below, and it adds parts a tech needs to know how to check.

Check the maker's data

Check the maker's data. Injection details, set points and limits differ by compressor and by unit. The numbers below come from named lab and field reports and one compressor maker's technical bulletin, or are marked as our own. Use the unit's service manual for the equipment in front of you.

The problem at low outdoor temperature

When it is cold outside, the evaporating pressure drops while the pressure needed to heat the building stays high. The compressor must squeeze the gas through a bigger pressure ratio. Two things follow. The gas coming in is thin, so each stroke moves less mass of refrigerant, and capacity falls just as the load rises (see Heat Pump Balance Point and Low-Temperature Capacity). And more compression work heats the gas, so discharge temperature climbs (see Compressor Discharge Line Temperature as a Diagnostic). A Department of Energy presentation from the cold-climate heat pump project at Oak Ridge National Laboratory names high pressure ratio at low ambient as a problem for typical heat pumps and notes that the project's heating-optimized compressors tolerate up to 280 F discharge temperature.

What injection does

A scroll or similar compressor traps gas, then squeezes it. Vapor injection opens a port into the chamber after the suction gas has already been partly compressed. Extra vapor enters there, at a pressure between suction and discharge. Two benefits result. The compressor handles more total refrigerant flow, which means more heat delivered. And the injected vapor is cooler than the gas it joins, so it brings down the temperature of what leaves the compressor. A 2016 Purdue compressor-conference paper (Moesch, Bahman and Groll) tested a vapor-injection scroll with R-407C and reported that discharge temperature generally fell as injection pressure rose and that cooling capacity rose 12 to 30 percent over the no-injection baseline, though compressor power rose 7.5 to 22.5 percent. Lab numbers like these are not field numbers, but they show the trade: more heat, more input power, lower discharge temperature.

Two ways to make the injection vapor

Economizer (plate heat exchanger)

In the diagram, liquid leaves the condenser and splits. The small branch goes through the injection expansion valve, drops in pressure and gets cold, then runs through a brazed plate heat exchanger. The main liquid flows through the other side of the same exchanger and is subcooled. The branch boils into vapor, picks up some superheat, and goes to the compressor's injection port. A compressor maker's technical bulletin on enhanced vapor injection describes exactly this arrangement, with a counterflow plate exchanger acting as a subcooler for the main flow and an evaporator for the injected flow.

Flash tank

The other way is to expand the liquid in two steps with a tank between. The first valve drops the pressure to the injection pressure, the tank lets vapor separate from liquid, saturated vapor goes to the injection port, and the liquid goes on through the second valve to the outdoor coil. The Oak Ridge cold-climate prototype used this layout. Its report describes two fixed orifices around the flash tank and an electronic expansion valve ahead of the receiver that sets injection pressure, with the aim of keeping the low-stage and high-stage pressure ratios about equal.

When the control turns injection on

Injection helps most when the pressure ratio is high. At mild conditions it costs little to leave it off. The Oak Ridge report shows one example of staging: with one compressor running, its expansion valve was fully open above 47 F outdoors and at minimum opening below 17 F; with two compressors, fully open above 17 F and at minimum opening below -13 F. That is one prototype, not a rule. The compressor bulletin we read goes further. It says to close the injection line when the pressure ratio is below 2, to avoid backflow, and it limits running without injection to conditions above set evaporating temperatures that depend on the model. The control board, not the tech, normally decides all this from outdoor temperature, pressures and discharge temperature.

What the field and lab numbers show

  • Lab. The DOE project presentation reports a lab tandem vapor-injection prototype at 88 percent of its 47 F capacity when tested at -13 F, versus 76 percent for the same tandem design without injection.
  • Field, Fairbanks. The Oak Ridge report on a six-month field test says the prototype ran down to -30 F, delivering about 27,000 Btu/h, about 75 percent of its 36,000 Btu/h rating at 47 F, with a COP of about 1.8; at -13 F COP was about 2.0. Discharge temperature at -30 F was around 230 F, below the 280 F limit.
  • Lesson learned. A low-suction-pressure cutoff set above 30 psia stopped the unit near -30 F. A protective setting, not the refrigeration cycle, set the floor.
  • Bigger picture. A 2023 Brookhaven National Laboratory report for DOE on 21 occupied homes credits variable-speed compressors, multistage systems and optimized thermal design for better low-temperature capacity. We did not find it breaking out vapor injection by itself, so do not read it as a vapor-injection result.

For how inverter speed fits in, see Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors; for the overall picture, Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Worked example (ours): 5 F outdoors

This is our own illustration, not a measured unit. Assumptions we chose: R-410A; evaporating temperature 15 F below outdoor air, so -10 F; condensing temperature 110 F; atmospheric pressure 14.7 psi. Saturation pressures are approximate readings from a federal agency's R-410A pressure-temperature chart (gauge pressure).

  1. Suction: 36 psig at -10 F, so 36 + 14.7 = 50.7 psia.
  2. Condensing: about 364 psig at 110 F, so 364 + 14.7 = 378.7 psia.
  3. Pressure ratio with no injection: 378.7 / 50.7 = 7.5. Always use absolute pressure.
  4. If two stages shared the work equally, each would take the square root of 7.5, which is 2.73. The middle pressure would be 50.7 × 2.73 = 138.6 psia, about 124 psig.
  5. Check: 138.6 / 50.7 = 2.73 and 378.7 / 138.6 = 2.73.

Why it helps: gas compressed from 50.7 to 138.6 psia is partly done. Injected vapor arrives at about that middle pressure, so it does not have to be compressed through the first stage. The second stage then squeezes a ratio near 2.7, not 7.5, over a mixed, cooler flow. Real injection pressure is not exactly the geometric mean; the Purdue paper above found best capacity near 94 percent of it. A Purdue paper from the same group on a multi-port compressor reported coefficient-of-performance gains over a baseline cycle that grew with pressure ratio, up to 19 percent at a ratio of 8 and a 43.3 C condensing temperature.

Service notes

  • Extra parts. Expect an economizer or tank, an injection expansion valve (the maker's bulletin prefers an electronic one, with a shutoff valve added if it does not close fully), and temperature or pressure sensors on the injection line. Pulse-modulating valves are called unsuitable and can cause liquid overfeed.
  • Injection superheat. The bulletin's data assume about 5 K injection superheat, which is about 9 F. Measure it as the injection line temperature minus the saturation temperature at injection pressure. Many units do not have a service port there; read the controller's service display if it shows it. Use the maker's target for the model.
  • Discharge protection. The bulletin says discharge gas temperature protection is required; some compressors have it built in and others need an external device.
  • Installation. The bulletin gives mounting details for the exchanger and valve bulb and says to keep and insulate the injection lines. Follow the unit's own instructions.

Symptoms when injection fails (our reasoning)

No source we read lists fault symptoms as a table, so the following is our own reasoning from how the circuit works. Confirm with the maker's fault codes.

  • Injection valve stuck closed or line plugged: the unit acts like a plain heat pump. Mild weather may seem fine. In cold weather capacity sags and discharge temperature runs high, sometimes ending in a high-discharge lockout.
  • Injection valve flooding or overfeeding: low or zero injection superheat, noise, and risk of liquid reaching the compressor. The bulletin says backflow and overfeed cause performance loss and noise.
  • Bad injection sensor: the control may close the valve or run a fixed position, so symptoms look like the two above.

The first checks are the same as for any low-capacity call: airflow, frost and the defrost cycle, charge, then the injection circuit.

Field card: Vapor Injection in Cold-Climate Heat Pumps

Split the compression, cool the discharge, keep the capacity.

  • Cold outside means a high pressure ratio, less refrigerant flow and a hot discharge.
  • Injection adds intermediate-pressure vapor partway through compression.
  • Economizer: injection branch subcools the main liquid in a plate heat exchanger. Flash tank: two-step expansion with a vapor tank.
  • Control decides when to inject; one maker closes injection below a pressure ratio of 2.
  • Example (ours): 7.5 ratio at 5 F outdoors splits into about 2.7 per stage.
  • Check injection superheat against the maker's target, about 5 K in one bulletin.
  • Stuck-closed injection looks like a weak heat pump in the cold.
