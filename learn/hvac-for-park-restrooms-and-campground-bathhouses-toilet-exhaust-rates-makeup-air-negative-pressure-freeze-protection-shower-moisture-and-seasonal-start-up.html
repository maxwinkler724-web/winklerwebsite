Park Restrooms and Campground Bathhouses: HVAC Basics

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A park comfort station looks simple: a few toilets, a few showers, a block building. But it has wet rooms, a heavy exhaust load, freezing weather, cleaning chemicals and people who may not be gentle with the equipment. This lesson walks through the ventilation and freeze-protection basics and ends with a heating-load example of our own.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Codes differ by state and by year, and a park building may fall under a different rule set than a store or office. The numbers below come from a state government web page that prints a mechanical code ventilation table, read in 2026. Confirm what your local building department has adopted before you size anything.

What the code table asks for in public toilet rooms

The mechanical code chapter we read lists public toilet rooms at 50 or 70 cfm per water closet or urinal. Its notes say the rate is counted per fixture, and that the higher number, 70, applies where the exhaust fan is designed to run intermittently. The lower number, 50, is allowed only where the exhaust is designed to run continuously while the room is occupied. A second note says toilet rooms and shower rooms need mechanical exhaust, and recirculating their air is prohibited, with only narrow exceptions for energy recovery. Exhaust fans are also to be ducted directly outdoors. The same page says an interior room joined to a neighboring space is exempt from its own direct supply air if a permanent opening of 25 square feet, or a 30 cfm transfer fan, connects the two.

That page does not give a rate for shower heads in a public room. Where this lesson needs a shower number, we label it as our own assumption.

Where the makeup air comes from

Every cubic foot the fan throws out has to come back in from somewhere. In a small standalone building, that is usually a wall louver, a door undercut or a gap at the door. If nothing is provided, the fan will struggle, doors will pull shut or whistle, and air may be drawn backward through floor drains and vent stacks. A good plan gives the air a path: outdoor louver, past the heater, through the rooms, then out the fan. A single-room station with a door to the outside is the simplest case. A building with separate men's, women's and family rooms needs a path to each room.

Keeping the room negative

Toilet and shower rooms should be at slightly lower pressure than the spaces around them so odors and moisture flow into the room and then out through the fan. The state extension page on bathrooms gives a simple field test: while the exhaust fan runs, you should be able to feel air entering the bathroom under the door. In a park building, check that test with the doors in their normal positions. If a louver is blocked by leaves, a bird screen is clogged or a door is propped open, the test will tell you quickly.

Fan controls

The extension page recommends running the fan during a shower and for a few minutes afterward, and notes that a timer switch can handle the run time. In a public building the choices are broadly these: continuous run during open hours, a timer on a switch or a key, or an occupancy sensor that starts the fan when someone enters. Each has a trade-off. Continuous run matches the lower code rate but costs heating energy all day in winter. Timers and sensors save energy but must be set long enough for shower steam to clear. Whichever you choose, the choice drives the rate in the table above, so the design and the control have to match.

Unheated and seasonal buildings

Many park buildings close for the winter. A state park release said parks begin winterizing by shutting off water in the campgrounds and closing the shower houses. For the building itself, two state agencies we read give different advice on antifreeze, so follow your own health or environmental agency and the owner's written plan. A shutdown guide from one state says to drain every pipe and appliance that could freeze, including water heaters and water fountains, to exercise the isolation valves to confirm they work, and to add an environmentally safe antifreeze to sink and shower traps and toilet bowls. It says never to use automotive or RV antifreeze in a water system. A guide from another state says antifreeze is not needed if all fixtures are completely drained, and warns against using it to protect plumbing at all. Both agree the first step is draining the water out of the system.

Heating a cold-climate bathhouse that stays open

A building that stays open in freezing weather needs enough heat to keep water lines, traps and floor drains above freezing, and it needs to deal with the cold makeup air its fan pulls in. Small electric or gas unit heaters are common choices, and we cover how they differ in the garage and workshop heater lesson linked below. A heater sized only for the walls will fall behind once exhaust fans run, which is what the example below shows.

Shower moisture, mold and cleaning chemicals

The federal mold guide says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and to run a bathroom fan when showering. The extension page notes that mold grows in warm, humid places such as bathrooms, and that soap scum and skin cells feed it. Our own practical note: bathhouses are scrubbed with strong cleaners and sprayed with water, so galvanized housings, bare steel and thin painted metal will rust. When you choose or replace equipment, ask the maker what finish or housing is suited to wet, cleaned areas. Tamper-resistant fan grilles, locking covers and heaters mounted out of reach are also worth asking about, since public buildings get abused.

Worked example: our own bathhouse

This is our own example, not a design. Our assumptions are labeled so you can swap in your own.

  • Our own assumption: one bathhouse has 4 water closets, 2 urinals and 4 showers.
  • From the code table above: 70 cfm per water closet or urinal, because the fans are intermittent. That is 6 fixtures.
  • Our own assumption: 50 cfm per shower, since the table we read gives no shower rate.
  • Our own assumption: outdoor temperature 20°F and the building held at 50°F, so the temperature difference is 30°F.
  • Our own assumption: all fans run at once, which is the worst case.

Step 1, toilet exhaust: 6 fixtures × 70 cfm = 420 cfm.

Step 2, shower exhaust: 4 showers × 50 cfm = 200 cfm.

Step 3, total exhaust and makeup air: 420 + 200 = 620 cfm.

Step 4, heat to warm the makeup air: The sensible heat relation for outside air is Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference, which a university page explains as the heat to warm 1 cfm of air by 1°F for an hour. Q = 1.08 × 620 × 30 = 20,088 Btu per hour. That is roughly 20,000 Btu per hour, or about 5.9 kW, since one kilowatt is about 3,412 Btu per hour.

If the toilet fans ran continuously at the lower 50 cfm rate instead, the toilet part would be 300 cfm, the total 500 cfm, and Q = 1.08 × 500 × 30 = 16,200 Btu per hour. That is before any heat for the building shell, so the heater has to cover both. The example shows why heater size, fan size and control choice all belong in the same conversation.

Seasonal start-up and shutdown checks

Shutdown. Turn off pumps, drain pipes and tanks, drain water heaters and fountains, exercise valves, treat traps as your agency directs, cap open lines to keep out insects and animals, and shut off or protect heaters, fans and controls per the maker's instructions.

Start-up. Our own checklist, built from the topics above: open the louvers and check they are clear, confirm each fan spins and moves air, test timers and sensors, do the under-the-door airflow test, check heaters and thermostats before cold weather returns, look for new corrosion or rust, and confirm the plumbing is refilled and leak-free per your water system's rules. Keep a short written log of what you found.

Field card: Park Restrooms and Campground Bathhouses

Give the fan an air path, keep the room negative and keep the water out of the cold.

  • Code table we read: 70 cfm per fixture for intermittent exhaust, 50 for continuous run while occupied.
  • Exhaust ducts straight outdoors, no recirculating toilet-room air.
  • Test: you should feel air entering under the door while the fan runs.
  • Humidity: below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.
  • Heat for makeup air: Q = 1.08 × cfm × temperature difference.
  • Our example: 620 cfm at a 30°F difference is about 20,088 Btu per hour.
  • Closing for winter: drain everything that can freeze; follow your agency on antifreeze.
