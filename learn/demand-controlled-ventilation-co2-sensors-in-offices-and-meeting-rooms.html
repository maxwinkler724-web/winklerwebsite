Demand-Controlled Ventilation: CO2 Sensors in Offices and Meeting Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A meeting room that holds 20 people is empty most of the day. Conditioning outdoor air for 20 people around the clock wastes energy, so many buildings let a CO2 sensor decide how much outdoor air to bring in. This lesson covers how that works, the math behind it, and how it fails.

Check the standard and code edition

Check the edition. ASHRAE standards and energy codes are revised on a cycle, and the adopted edition and the local authority decide what applies. This page summarizes public descriptions and maker literature. It is not the standard. For the basics of the residential standard and outdoor air in general, see Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics and Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics (ASHRAE 62.2).

What DCV is

Demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) automatically cuts outdoor air below the design rate when a space has fewer people than it was designed for. The Alberta Infrastructure design series quotes the ASHRAE 90.1 definition in that sense. ASHRAE 62.1-2016 section 6.2.7, as a trade article describes it, treats DCV as an optional way to reset ventilation. Design outdoor air is for a full room. DCV gives the room less when it is not full and ramps back up when people arrive.

Why CO2, and why it is only a proxy

People exhale CO2, so in a room whose only CO2 source is its occupants, the CO2 level tracks how many people are there compared with how much outdoor air is arriving. That makes it a cheap occupancy signal. It is not treated as a poison at these levels. ASHRAE's 2025 position document says indoor CO2 is not an overall indicator of air quality, that the old 1000 ppm benchmark traces to body-odor perception rather than a health threshold, and that evidence for direct health effects at typical indoor concentrations is inconsistent. For scale, the NIOSH Pocket Guide lists a recommended exposure limit of 5,000 ppm as an 8-hour average for CO2, and OSHA's limit is also 5,000 ppm.

Because CO2 only reflects people, it misses pollutants that do not follow occupancy, such as emissions from furnishings. ASHRAE notes that CO2-removing air cleaners can fool DCV, and the Ebtron presentation cites 62.1-2019 as barring CO2-based DCV in zones with CO2 sources other than people.

The 62.1 recipe: people plus area

For the breathing zone, 62.1 adds a people part and a floor-area part. A trade article writes it as Vbz = Rp x Pz + Ra x Az, where Rp is cfm per person, Pz is the number of people, Ra is cfm per square foot and Az is floor area. For a conference or meeting room, a copy of the 62.1-2022 Table 6-1 on ashrae.org lists Rp = 5 cfm per person, Ra = 0.06 cfm per square foot, and a default density of 50 people per 1,000 square feet. The area part never goes away. Even with nobody in the room, DCV should not drop below it (the Trane example shows 60 cfm for an empty lecture room, the area part only).

From ppm to cfm: the steady-state relation

The HPAC article by Trane engineers uses a mass balance: outdoor air per person Vo = N / (Cs - Co), where N is CO2 generated per person in cfm, Cs is room CO2 and Co is outdoor CO2. It uses N = 0.0105 cfm per person and Co of 350 ppm, and says generation varies with activity and the individual. Rearranged, the CO2 rise above outdoors equals N divided by the airflow per person. The word steady state matters: the room must sit long enough for CO2 to settle, which is why a sensor lags behind a crowd walking in.

That article also warns that holding a fixed rise based on design occupancy would underventilate at partial occupancy, because the area part is shared among fewer people. A controller must therefore compute the rise for the current airflow, not hold one number.

Worked example: a 400 square foot meeting room

  1. Design people. 50 per 1,000 ft² x 400 ft² = 20 people.
  2. Area part. 0.06 x 400 = 24 cfm.
  3. Design airflow. 5 x 20 = 100 cfm for people, plus 24 = 124 cfm, or 6.2 cfm per person.
  4. Design CO2 rise. 20 people x 0.0105 = 0.21 cfm of CO2. 0.21 / 124 = 0.001694, about 1,694 ppm above outdoors. With outdoors near 420 ppm (the Alberta document allows a fixed assumption of 400 ppm), the room settles near 2,100 ppm. The Trane slides reach the same ballpark for a conference room: 6 cfm per person gives 0.0105 / 6 = 1,750 ppm above 350 ppm outdoors, a 2,100 ppm target.
  5. Four people. 5 x 4 = 20 cfm, plus 24 = 44 cfm, or 11 cfm per person. CO2 rise = 0.042 / 44 = about 955 ppm.
  6. Same four people, no DCV. Damper stuck at 124 cfm: 0.042 / 124 = about 339 ppm rise.

So DCV trades a 339 ppm room for a 955 ppm room and brings in 124 - 44 = 80 cfm less outdoor air to heat or cool. Both rooms get the same required rate per the formula. Note these are the breathing-zone numbers; the standard adjusts for how well the supply air reaches the occupants, which this example leaves out.

Where codes require it

A Trane continuing-education deck summarizes ASHRAE 90.1-2013 and 2016 (section 6.4.3.8) as requiring DCV in spaces over 500 ft² with design occupancy of 25 or more people per 1,000 ft², when served by a system with an air economizer, automatic modulating outdoor air damper control, or design outdoor airflow above 3,000 cfm, with exemptions such as systems under 750 cfm. A software help page summarizes IECC 2012 section C403.2.5.1 with the same 500 ft² and 25 per 1,000 ft² idea. Older 90.1 editions used 40 per 1,000 ft², so the numbers move with the edition. Our 400 ft² room is under the area threshold; a 600 ft² boardroom at 50 per 1,000 would not be.

Sensor placement and drift

The Trane slides put the sensor on the wall in the space, with the return duct as an alternative. The return duct reads an average, so the busy room can end up under-ventilated. Vaisala's user guide says to mount at 4 to 6 feet, and to avoid doors, windows, sun, heat sources, blocked airflow and spots near supply outlets. It also says to seal wall openings behind the sensor, because air from the wall cavity changes the reading.

Drift is the quiet problem. Telaire's literature says drift is typically a few ppm per month, mostly from infrared source aging. Its ABC self-calibration assumes the lowest daily reading is outdoor level, about 400 ppm, which needs roughly 4 hours a day of low occupancy. Spaces occupied around the clock are not suitable, and with the feature off the maker says calibration may be needed every 2 to 3 years. A California-funded Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report found 47 percent of field sensors off by more than 75 ppm at about 760 ppm, some by over 300 ppm, and four of nine faulty sensors had an output that did not change with CO2. No facility manager in the authors' field studies reported calibrating after installation.

What moves: dampers and ERVs

On a rooftop unit with an economizer, the controller moves the outdoor air damper between a minimum position and open as CO2 climbs; the Trane example sets 60 cfm at 350 ppm and 550 cfm at 1,600 ppm for a lecture room. See Economizers Basics and Building Automation Basics. On an ERV, a controls maker's note describes a 0 to 10 VDC signal to the supply fan speed controller: at or below the CO2 threshold the fan runs at its minimum, and above it speed rises. The recovery core still works at lower flow; see ERV vs. HRV. On 100 percent outdoor air systems, the Alberta document warns that fan and VAV box turndown limits can set the lowest achievable airflow.

Common failures

  • Stuck or binding dampers and bad actuators. Ebtron's presentation says fixed damper positions do not hold outdoor airflow because of wind, stack pressure, hysteresis, binding and deterioration. A stuck-closed damper starves the room; stuck-open wastes energy. Check linkage and feedback; see RTU Economizer Fault Diagnosis.
  • Out-of-calibration or dead sensors. A sensor reading low makes the system under-ventilate. Compare to a trusted meter, and check readings overnight when the room should sit near outdoor level.
  • Bad placement. A sensor in a supply stream or by an open door reads wrong all day.
  • Wrong setpoint. The Alberta document says an old 700 ppm sample calculation got applied to every space type, which is why ASHRAE removed it. Use the design values for the specific space.
  • Damage. One trade article calls physical destruction the most common sensor problem.

Field card: Demand-Controlled Ventilation

CO2 counts people. It does not measure air quality.

  • DCV lowers outdoor air when the room is under design occupancy.
  • 62.1 breathing zone: Rp x people + Ra x area. Meeting room: 5 cfm per person + 0.06 cfm per ft².
  • CO2 rise above outdoors = 0.0105 cfm per person / airflow per person (steady state).
  • Never reset below the area part.
  • Sensor on a wall, 4 to 6 ft up, away from doors, windows and diffusers.
  • Check drift, damper travel and feedback. Dead sensors still output a signal.
  • Confirm code edition and thresholds locally.
