Adding AC to a House Without Ducts: Mini-Splits vs High-Velocity vs Central

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many older New Jersey houses heat with steam or hot water radiators and have no ducts at all. That is great for quiet, even heat and awkward for air conditioning. This lesson compares the four ways people usually add cooling to a house without ductwork, and why the size of the equipment should come from a load calculation instead of a guess.

Check the sources

This page is general education built from federal and manufacturer pages read in 2026. Models and ratings change, so confirm details with the literature for the exact model and a licensed New Jersey contractor.

Why radiators cannot cool

A boiler sends hot water or steam to radiators or baseboards. Nothing in that piping moves cool air, so cooling needs its own distribution. Four options are common.

Option 1: Ductless mini-split

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a ductless minisplit as an outdoor compressor and condenser connected to an indoor air handler by a conduit that carries power, refrigerant tubing and a condensate drain. It lists suitable uses as home retrofits, room additions and small apartments, and says only a small wall opening is needed. ENERGY STAR makes the same point: no duct work is needed, and the indoor heads mount on a wall or ceiling. See Mini-Split Basics.

Single zone means one outdoor unit and one indoor head. Multi-zone means one outdoor unit feeds several heads. DOE says one outdoor unit can serve up to four indoor units. Manufacturers differ: Trane's data sheet for its 23 Series multi-zone outdoor units says up to five indoor units, with total indoor capacity allowed up to 150 percent of the outdoor unit rating, though the system is then limited by the outdoor unit. Trane gives 82 feet as the longest line length to any indoor unit. Mitsubishi's MXZ-SM-TL page says up to 12 residential and light commercial indoor units. The limit depends on the model.

Pros (DOE): no ductwork and so no duct losses, room-by-room zoning, and heat pump models also heat. Cons (DOE): minisplits generally cost more than central systems if you leave out the cost of ducts, indoor units may not look appealing, and their filters are lower-grade, so a separate air cleaner may be wanted. DOE also warns that an oversized or poorly placed indoor unit can short cycle and fail to control temperature and humidity.

Option 2: Ducted mini-split air handlers

Some mini-split systems use a low-profile indoor unit hidden in a ceiling or soffit, with short ducts to a few rooms. A Mitsubishi spec sheet for its SEZ-KD18 horizontal-ducted indoor unit calls it a unit "for concealed applications" with a 7-7/8 inch tall body and fan charts up to 0.20 inches of water gauge of external static pressure. The charts stop at that low pressure, so ask the installer how long the duct runs are and what static pressure the design assumes. For background, see Ductwork Basics. ENERGY STAR notes that homes with existing ductwork may fit a ducted air source heat pump better than a ductless one.

Option 3: Small-duct high-velocity

SpacePak's guide describes a central system for 2 to 5 tons that uses flexible, pre-insulated 2 inch diameter tubing which runs through walls and around obstructions, aimed at older homes without central air, especially those with hot water, steam or radiant electric heat. It recommends six to seven outlets per ton, each designed for roughly 35 to 40 CFM, and about 250 CFM per ton versus 400 CFM for a conventional system. It says blower units are small enough for attics, basements, crawl spaces and closets, and that the coil removes more moisture than conventional systems (up to 30 percent more by SpacePak's claim). Unico's literature also calls its system suited to retrofitting older homes. These are manufacturer claims and not independent tests.

A trade-magazine article on SpacePak notes the trade-offs: the system moves about half the air volume of conventional ones, bedroom and bathroom doors need a gap at the bottom for airflow, and the design needs a return-air path. The outlet is small, but the air is fast, so placement matters, and the article says the airstream is felt for the first two to three feet.

Option 4: Conventional central air with new ducts in the attic

This is the familiar air handler and branch ducts, installed where there is room, often in an attic. Ducts in an attic are the weak spot. A DOE and NREL homeowner brochure from 2004 says attic and crawl-space duct systems typically lose 25 to 40 percent of the heating or cooling energy that passes through them, and that in summer attic ducts pick up heat from the attic. A separate DOE page puts estimated losses for ducts in unconditioned attics at 10 to 45 percent. ENERGY STAR says that in a typical house about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through the duct system is lost to leaks, holes and poor connections, and recommends sealing with mastic or metal tape and insulating accessible ducts, and not using ordinary duct tape. The DOE brochure suggests R-8 added to uninsulated attic ducts. For the attic itself, see Attic Insulation and Air Sealing and Sealing Duct Leaks. The 20 to 30 percent figure is lost air and the 25 to 40 percent figure is lost energy, so do not mix them.

Size by load, not square feet

ENERGY STAR says an oversized or undersized system will struggle to meet comfort needs and that a contractor should verify size with a Manual J calculation of the home's heating and cooling loads. Same-size houses can need different capacity because insulation, windows, shading and leakage differ. See Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J. A mini-split has an extra reason to size by room: each indoor head serves its own space, and DOE says each indoor unit must be correctly sized and placed.

Worked example (hypothetical numbers)

These numbers are invented for practice. A real house needs its own Manual J. Suppose a load calculation gave these peak cooling loads:

  1. Add the rooms: 6,500 + 4,000 + 3,200 + 3,000 + 2,800 + 2,500 = 22,000 Btu/h.
  2. Convert to tons, using 12,000 Btu/h per ton: 22,000 / 12,000 = 1.83 tons.
  3. Mini-split zones: the open living room and kitchen total 6,500 + 4,000 = 10,500 Btu/h, so the head for that space should be chosen near that figure, from the sizes the manufacturer actually lists. Each bedroom head would be near 3,200, 3,000 and 2,800 Btu/h. Outdoor capacity must be checked against the manufacturer's multi-zone tables, not just the sum.
  4. High-velocity check using SpacePak's figures: 1.83 tons x 6 to 7 outlets per ton = 11 to 13 outlets. Airflow at 250 CFM per ton = 1.83 x 250 = about 458 CFM. Divided over 12 outlets that is 458 / 12 = about 38 CFM each, which fits the guide's 35 to 40 CFM per outlet.
  5. Attic-duct penalty: if an attic duct system loses 25 to 40 percent (DOE brochure), the system must deliver more than the load. Delivery of 75 percent means 22,000 / 0.75 = 29,333 Btu/h; delivery of 60 percent means 22,000 / 0.60 = 36,667 Btu/h. This is only an illustration; Manual J has its own duct-loss method, and the better fix is sealed, insulated ducts or ducts inside the conditioned space.

Efficiency ratings

Cooling efficiency is rated in SEER2 and EER2, and heat pump heating in HSPF2. What Does a SEER2 Rating Mean? explains them. For heat, NEEP's cold-climate air-source heat pump list identifies equipment best suited to heat efficiently in cold climates (IECC climate zone 4 and higher), and ENERGY STAR advises choosing certified cold climate models in cold regions. See Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Noise and placement

For ductless units, ENERGY STAR says to put the outdoor unit where airflow is unobstructed and away from doors and windows because defrost water can form icy patches, to check roof runoff, and to plan for service access. DOE says the outdoor unit can sit up to 50 feet from the indoor unit, and ENERGY STAR says the line set can run through closets, stairways, garages, basements, crawlspaces or attics to limit patching. Install details are in Mini-Split Installation Basics. SpacePak calls its system ultra quiet and recommends sound attenuators for the last 3 feet of each run; Unico calls its system whisper quiet. Neither gives decibel figures, so listen to a running unit first.

Keeping the boiler for heat

Adding cooling does not require replacing a steam or hot water boiler. The boiler and radiators keep heating; the new system only cools. A heat pump model can also heat, and ENERGY STAR suggests discussing backup heating with your contractor in cold climates. Whether to use one for some of the heating is a separate decision from adding cooling. See Steam System Fundamentals for how the boiler side works, and leave boiler work to a licensed professional.

Field card: Cooling a House Without Ducts

Pick the distribution first, then size by load.

  • Four options: ductless, ducted mini-split, high-velocity, attic central air.
  • Ductless avoids duct losses; the model sets how many heads one outdoor unit takes.
  • Attic ducts typically lose 25 to 40 percent of energy (DOE/NREL 2004 brochure).
  • Size from a Manual J, not square feet.
  • High-velocity guide figures: 6 to 7 outlets and about 250 CFM per ton.
  • The boiler can stay in place for heat.
