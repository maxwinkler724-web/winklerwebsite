Millivolt Gas Valves and Thermopiles on Fireplaces and Wall Heaters: Open and Closed Circuit Readings, Switch Wire Drop and Pilot Problems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many gas fireplaces, wall heaters and some small heaters need no 120 volt or 24 volt power at all. The pilot flame heats a thermopile, the thermopile makes a few hundred millivolts, and that tiny voltage opens the gas valve. This lesson shows how to read that circuit with a meter, and why a weak pilot or a long, tired switch wire can look like a bad valve.

Safety first

Owners: do not open the gas control. The gas valve, pilot assembly and burner are for a qualified gas technician. If a pilot will not stay lit or the heater will not start, turn the appliance off, and call a qualified technician. If you smell gas, leave the building and call from outside. The federal Consumer Product Safety Commission tells households to have fuel-burning heating equipment professionally inspected every year and to keep working carbon monoxide alarms outside sleeping areas. The rest of this page is written for technicians.

Techs: follow the maker's instructions for the exact model, shut off the gas before changing any part, and leak-test every gas joint you disturb with the method the maker and your local code call for. A millivolt circuit is harmless to touch, but the gas side is not, and the same fireplace may also have a blower or lights on line voltage.

How a thermopile makes power

Two different metals joined at a point make a small voltage when that joint is hotter than the other ends of the wires. A university guide to thermocouple measurement puts the size at about tens of microvolts for each degree of temperature difference, and explains that the voltage depends on the temperature difference, not on absolute temperature. One junction pair therefore gives only a few tens of millivolts at pilot-flame temperatures. A thermocouple is one such pair. A thermopile is many junction pairs wired in series, so their voltages add. That is how a pilot flame can make hundreds of millivolts.

  • Thermocouple, the roughly 30 millivolt class. A training-site page we read gives a working thermocouple as roughly 25 to 30 mV open circuit, and about half that when loaded. It holds a safety magnet open and does nothing else. Our existing lesson on pilot lights that will not stay lit covers that case.
  • Thermopile, the hundreds of millivolts class. Maker installation data for millivolt gas controls describes thermopiles with outputs in the hundreds of millivolts. That is enough to hold the safety magnet and also pull in the main valve operator. No transformer is needed, which is why these systems keep working in a power outage.

Either way it is a very weak source. Its internal resistance is only a few ohms, so a bit of extra resistance anywhere in the loop eats a large share of the voltage.

The valve and the switch loop

A millivolt gas valve has a pilot operator, which is the manual pilot and safety magnet side, and a main operator, which opens the main burner gas. The thermopile feeds both through its leads. The wall switch, or a millivolt thermostat, is wired in series with the main operator, so closing the switch completes the loop and the main burner lights. Valve terminals are usually marked with names such as TP, TH and TH/TP, but the meaning and layout differ by maker. Read the wiring diagram for the model in front of you rather than guessing from the letters. A maker installation manual we read also warns that a safety magnet lead not connected to the correct terminal can make a pilot refuse to stay lit.

Open circuit and closed circuit readings

Set a DC meter to a millivolt range. Use a trusted meter with clean probe tips, because you are measuring tenths of a volt in a loop that tolerates almost no extra resistance.

  1. Open-circuit reading. With the pilot burning for a few minutes and the thermopile lead taken off the valve, read across the thermopile leads (point A in the diagram). This tells you what the thermopile can make with no load. It is a quick check of the flame and thermopile, but it cannot show a resistance problem, because nothing is drawing current.
  2. Closed-circuit reading. Put the leads back, then read at the valve terminals with the circuit complete and the switch closed (point B). Current now flows, the thermopile voltage sags because of its own internal resistance, and what is left must still be enough to pull in the main operator. A maker procedure we read lists a loaded minimum and a lower hold-in value for its valve. These minimums vary a lot between makers and models. Use the maker's table for the valve in front of you, not a number from this page.
  3. Drop across the switch and wires. Read across the closed switch contacts (point C), and across the two ends of a single wire in the run. A good switch shows close to zero. A wire reading shows the resistance of the run. Maker data we read says a thermostat-circuit reading that is too high should be fixed by cleaning and tightening leads, shortening the run, or using heavier wire, and gives a length limit for each wire gauge. Another maker procedure treats each switch as dropping only a few millivolts. Look up the limits for your valve.
  4. Jump the controls out. If the closed-circuit reading is poor, briefly bridge the switch loop at the valve terminals. If the main burner then behaves, the valve and thermopile are fine and the trouble is in the switch loop. If not, the problem is the pilot, thermopile or valve.

The method is the same half-splitting idea we describe in half-splitting a control circuit, and the arithmetic is the one in voltage drop on HVAC circuits, only at a much smaller scale.

Pilot problems that look like valve problems

  • Dirty or weak pilot. A sooty or wandering flame heats the thermopile less. Maker service data we read calls for the flame to cover the thermopile tip by a stated amount, and tells the tech to clean carbon off the tip and adjust the pilot before replacing anything. Low inlet gas pressure, a partly blocked pilot orifice or a drafty cabinet can also thin the flame.
  • Poor connections. Because the signal is so small, loose, corroded or oxidized terminals are a common cause of low output. Clean and tighten before you condemn the thermopile.
  • Long or aging switch wire. Thin wire over a long run adds resistance. Corrosion in old splices, wire nuts or a worn wall switch adds more. Remember the return leg: the run counts twice.
  • Safety shutoffs in the loop. Some millivolt appliances put a thermal cutoff or a roll-out switch in series, and each one is another place for a bad contact.

For how fireplaces vent and what owners should check, see gas fireplaces for owners. Wall heaters are covered in gas wall furnaces and direct vent wall heaters.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own example. Every number below is an assumption chosen to show the method, not a reading from a real appliance or a maker limit. We model the loop as a simple series circuit, which is good enough to see the pattern. Assume:

  • Thermopile open-circuit reading: 600 mV.
  • Thermopile internal resistance: 1.8 ohms.
  • Main operator coil: 3.0 ohms.
  • Switch contacts: 0.2 ohm.
  • Wire run to the wall switch and back, out and return together: 1.0 ohm.
  • Valve pull-in value: 250 mV across the coil.

Step 1, current. Total loop resistance = 1.8 + 3.0 + 0.2 + 1.0 = 6.0 ohms. Current = 600 mV / 6.0 ohms = 100 mA.

Step 2, closed-circuit reading at the thermopile. Internal drop = 100 mA x 1.8 ohms = 180 mV. Loaded output = 600 - 180 = 420 mV.

Step 3, drops. Wire run = 100 mA x 1.0 ohm = 100 mV. Switch = 100 mA x 0.2 ohm = 20 mV.

Step 4, what reaches the coil. 420 - 100 - 20 = 300 mV. Check: 100 mA x 3.0 ohms = 300 mV. That clears the assumed 250 mV pull-in with a 50 mV margin.

Now the same loop after the wire corrodes, with the wire run at 2.5 ohms. Total = 1.8 + 3.0 + 0.2 + 2.5 = 7.5 ohms, so current = 600 / 7.5 = 80 mA. The coil now sees 80 mA x 3.0 ohms = 240 mV. That is below 250 mV, so the main burner will not light, even though the open-circuit reading is still a healthy 600 mV. The wire drop is 80 mA x 2.5 ohms = 200 mV, and it is the number that gives it away.

And a weak pilot instead, with the wire back at 1.0 ohm but open-circuit output down to 450 mV. Current = 450 / 6.0 = 75 mA, and the coil sees 75 mA x 3.0 ohms = 225 mV. Again below 250 mV. The lesson: open-circuit output alone does not clear a system. Read it closed circuit, then read the drops to see where the voltage went.

Field card: Millivolt gas valves and thermopiles

Open circuit shows the source. Closed circuit shows the system.

  • Owners do not open gas controls. Call a qualified technician.
  • Thermocouple is the roughly 30 mV class and holds a safety magnet. Thermopile is the hundreds of mV class and runs the main valve too.
  • Use a DC millivolt range and clean probe contact.
  • Open circuit at the thermopile leads, then closed circuit at the valve terminals.
  • Read the drop across the closed switch and along the wire run. Suspect long, thin or corroded wire.
  • Jump out the switch loop to split the problem in half.
  • Clean the pilot and the thermopile tip, and tighten connections, before replacing parts.
  • Use the maker's table for pass and fail millivolts. Do not borrow numbers.
