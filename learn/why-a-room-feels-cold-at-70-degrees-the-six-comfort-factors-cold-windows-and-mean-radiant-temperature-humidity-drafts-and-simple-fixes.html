Why a Room Feels Cold at 70 F: The Six Comfort Factors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostat reads the air. Your body reads the air, the surfaces around you, the moisture on your skin, the air moving past you, what you are wearing and what you are doing. That is why one room can feel cold at 70 F while another feels fine at 68 F, and why a muggy room at 76 F can feel worse than a dry one at 78 F.

The six comfort factors

Berkeley's Center for the Built Environment documents its comfort calculator around six primary factors. Four belong to the room: dry-bulb air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air speed and relative humidity. Two belong to the person: clothing and metabolic rate, which is a fancy name for how active you are. The U.S. comfort standard usually called Standard 55 is the formal reference for judging indoor comfort, and we name it here in plain text only; we use the Berkeley documentation for the factor list. A thermostat sees one of the six. The other five decide whether 70 F actually feels like 70 F.

  • Air temperature. What the thermostat measures, in the spot where the thermostat sits. See where to put a thermostat for why that spot can mislead.
  • Mean radiant temperature. The average temperature of the surfaces that can "see" you: walls, ceiling, floor and glass. Your body trades heat with them whether or not the air moves.
  • Humidity. It controls how easily sweat and moisture leave your skin.
  • Air speed. Moving air strips heat from skin, which is why a fan cools you without cooling the room.
  • Clothing. A sweater is portable insulation.
  • Activity. Sitting still makes about as little body heat as you ever make; cooking or cleaning makes much more.

How your body loses heat

A person is a small heater that has to dump its heat at a steady rate. A CDC chapter on thermal standards (Hertig, hosted on CDC Stacks) lays out the three paths. Radiant exchange depends mainly on the gap between your skin and the mean radiant temperature of the surroundings. Convective exchange depends on the gap between your skin and the air, and on how fast the air moves past you. Evaporative loss depends on air speed and on the vapor-pressure gap between your skin and the air, so humid air slows it down.

For a seated person indoors, radiation is the biggest path. A Berkeley study of office buildings states that the body releases about 58 to 60 percent of its sensible heat as radiation under typical office conditions. That figure is for offices, not houses, but it explains the main point of this lesson: surfaces matter about as much as air.

Why a cold window wall stays cold at 70 F

Glass is the thinnest, least insulated part of most walls, so on a cold day its inside surface sits well below room temperature. Berkeley researchers note that air and radiant temperatures can differ near surfaces that are unusually cold, and they name a spot "next to a window on a cold day" as a case where the difference matters. Two things happen there. Your body radiates toward the cold glass faster than the glass radiates back. And the air touching the glass cools, gets heavy and falls, creating the cold draft along the floor that people notice at the ankles. The DOE fact sheet on cellular shades lists "cold drafts near windows" among the problems shades help reduce.

Raising the thermostat does not fix this well. The thermostat is across the house in a hallway, and it only knows the air temperature. To make the window spot comfortable you would have to heat every other spot past comfortable. The worked example below shows the numbers.

Worked example (our own): the cold window seat

Our own example, with assumptions. Operative temperature is a single number that blends air temperature and radiant temperature into how the room feels to the body. A Berkeley paper states that operative temperature can be calculated as the simple mean of air temperature and mean radiant temperature when air speed is below 0.2 m/s (about 40 feet per minute, our conversion). That is a simplification: it ignores humidity, clothing and which surfaces face you most. It is fine for a seated person in still air, which is what we assume.

Assumptions (ours): air is 70 F everywhere, air is still, and the visible surfaces average 60 F near the window (cold glass plus a cool exterior wall) or 69 F in the interior spot.

  1. Window spot: (70 + 60) / 2 = 65 F operative temperature.
  2. Interior spot: (70 + 69) / 2 = 69.5 F operative temperature.
  3. The window seat feels like about 65 F even though the thermostat reads 70 F, a gap of about 5 F against the interior spot.

Now try the thermostat fix. To bring the window spot to 70 F operative, the air would need to be 80 F, since (80 + 60) / 2 = 70. At 80 F air, the interior spot works out to (80 + 69) / 2 = 74.5 F, which is warm for most people, and it costs more fuel. Better to warm the surface than the whole room.

Humidity and air speed: the muggy and drafty sides

Humidity works the other way around. When air holds a lot of moisture, sweat evaporates slowly and you lose less heat that way. A DOE case study on whole-house dehumidification found that at a 75 F set point, humidity can change the perceived temperature by 11 F, and it set a comfort target of below 60 percent relative humidity. That is how a 76 F room can feel sticky: the thermometer is fine but the evaporation path is blocked. Our lesson on a house that feels humid with the AC running covers causes and fixes.

Air speed is the factor you control most cheaply. DOE says a ceiling fan lets you set the thermostat roughly 4 F higher without losing comfort, because moving air makes you feel cooler. That same effect is the downside in winter: a fast draft across bare skin feels cold at any air temperature. Sealing leaks is the cure, covered in drafty doors and windows.

Practical fixes

  • Close insulating window coverings at night. DOE advises keeping drapes and shades closed overnight to block chilly air and opening them in the day for sun. Cellular shades are one option; see window shades, blinds and cellular shades. A DOE fact sheet reports cellular shades saved up to 20 percent on heating energy against no shades in its tests.
  • Add a rug where you sit. Our own practical note, not a sourced figure: a rug adds a layer between your feet and a cold floor, and it cuts the chilly feeling at the ankles. Floor coverings are covered further in our radiant floor covering lesson.
  • Set the ceiling fan to the season. DOE says counterclockwise in summer for a cooling breeze, and clockwise at low speed in winter to push warm air collected near the ceiling back down. See ceiling fans: direction and real savings.
  • Control humidity. If a room feels sticky at a normal temperature, look at the AC running time and consider dehumidification before lowering the setpoint.
  • Dress the person, not the room. A sweater or slippers changes the clothing factor at no energy cost.
  • Look at the whole house. If rooms differ by floor, read upstairs too hot. If a house is slow to recover after a setback, thermal mass may be part of the reason.

Field card: Why a Room Feels Cold at 70 F

The thermostat reads the air. You feel air plus surfaces.

  • Six factors: air temperature, mean radiant temperature, humidity, air speed, clothing, activity.
  • The body loses heat by radiation, convection and evaporation.
  • Simplified operative temperature = (air + mean radiant) / 2 in still air.
  • Our own example: 70 F air with 60 F surfaces feels like about 65 F; with 69 F surfaces, about 69.5 F.
  • Cold glass: close insulating coverings at night, seal drafts, add a rug.
  • Sticky at 76 F: check humidity first. Fan season: counterclockwise summer, clockwise low speed winter.
