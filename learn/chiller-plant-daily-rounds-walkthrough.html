Chiller Plant Daily Rounds: A Walk-Through

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

A chiller plant rarely fails without warning. Most trouble shows up first as a slow drift in a number or a small change in sound, which is why a careful walk-through, done the same way every day, is one of the most useful habits in a plant.

Why a fixed route matters

The point of rounds is to catch drift. A log only helps if readings are taken the same way, in the same place, in the same units, and compared with earlier days. Walk the plant in the same order every time. Look, listen and smell before you write anything down, and note anything odd even if you cannot explain it yet.

Safety on the walk

Rounds are observation. Keep clear of couplings, belts and open guards, and never reach past a guard to read or feel something. Do not open energized panels unless you are qualified and authorized. Know where the machine room refrigerant monitor, ventilation controls and exits are, and if a refrigerant alarm sounds, leave first and report from outside. If you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, leave and call for help from outside. Do not loosen fittings or open drains on a pressurized system on a round. If a task needs more than normal operating controls, stop and follow your site’s lockout/tagout procedure.

Start with the context

Write down the date, time, your name, outdoor conditions and which equipment is running. The same chiller at light load and at heavy load looks very different, so a reading with no operating context cannot be trended. Record units, and write what you actually read, never what you expect. If a gauge is stuck or a sensor looks wrong, say so rather than copying yesterday’s number.

Stop by stop

  • Control room or screen first. Review alarms and faults since the last round, check that setpoints match the schedule, and check that the number of machines running makes sense for the load.
  • Each chiller. Entering and leaving chilled-water and condenser-water temperatures, refrigerant pressures and their saturation temperatures, motor current or percent load, and oil level, pressure and temperature on machines that have an oil system. Compute the approaches (see Chiller Approach Temperatures).
  • Pumps. Suction and discharge pressure, motor current or drive speed, seal condition, bearing temperature by a proper thermometer, noise, and strainer pressure drop.
  • Tower. Basin level, make-up operation, fans running as commanded, visible slime or scale, and treatment readings. See Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks.
  • System side. Supply and return chilled-water temperatures and their difference, differential pressure, bypass position, expansion tank, and the make-up water meter, which can reveal a hidden leak.
  • The room. Temperature, ventilation, refrigerant monitor status, and housekeeping: oil on the floor, drips, stored materials.

Normal-looking versus a flag

The honest answer is that “normal” belongs to your plant. Your commissioning records, design data and nameplates are the real reference, and a good habit is to write “normal for this plant” beside each reading once you know it. Published examples exist: one maintenance guide lists a chilled-water delta-T within about a degree of design as typical, and flags a gap of more than a couple of degrees. Treat figures like that as examples, not your thresholds. Instead of relying on a magic number, look for these patterns over the last two weeks:

  • Approach temperatures creeping up at similar load and weather, which often points to fouling.
  • Delta-T falling while flow rises, which usually means a system-side problem rather than a chiller problem. See Low Delta-T Syndrome Explained.
  • Oil level or pressure drifting, or motor current creeping up at the same load.
  • Pump pressure difference dropping or strainer pressure drop climbing.
  • Make-up water use rising with no known reason.
  • Two sensors that should agree but do not, which usually means one is failing or poorly located.
  • New noise, vibration or smell. Treat these as real until shown otherwise.

When to report

Report to your lead or supervisor, per site procedure, for any safety or refrigerant alarm, a sudden step change in a reading, a multi-day trend in the wrong direction, a leak of any size from a refrigerant or oil system, or anything you do not understand. Never silence an alarm without understanding it, and never override a safety or bypass an interlock.

Printable checklist

  • Walk the route in the same order every time: look, listen, smell
  • Record date, time, outdoor conditions and which equipment is running
  • Review alarm and fault history since the last round
  • Each chiller: water temperatures, refrigerant pressures, load, oil readings
  • Each pump: pressures, current or speed, seal, noise, strainer pressure drop
  • Tower: basin level, fans, visible slime or scale, treatment readings
  • System: supply and return temperature, differential pressure, bypass position, make-up meter
  • Machine room: temperature, ventilation, refrigerant monitor status, housekeeping
  • Compare each reading with your plant’s own normal and the last two weeks
  • Report anything unexplained; never silence an alarm or bypass a safety

Catch the drift before it becomes a failure.

  • Same route, same order, same units, every round.
  • Log operating context or the reading cannot be trended.
  • Normal belongs to your plant, not to a textbook.
  • Trends over two weeks matter more than one reading.
  • Report what you do not understand; never silence an alarm.
