CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems: Pressures, Gas Coolers and Safety

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most heat pumps use refrigerants that condense at a few hundred psi. CO2, written R-744, works differently: on a hot day, or when making hot water, it never condenses at all. This lesson explains why, what that does to pressures and equipment, and the cautions that come with it.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. Pressures, ratings and service steps differ by model and change over time. This lesson is general education built from public sources read in 2026. It does not replace the installation and service instructions for the unit in front of you.

What R-744 is

R-744 is carbon dioxide used as a refrigerant. The EPA SNAP program lists it as acceptable for new equipment in refrigerated food processing and dispensing equipment, with a global warming potential (GWP) of 1, zero ozone depletion and ASHRAE safety class A1 (lower toxicity, no flame propagation) on the page we read. That page is an archived 2019 snapshot and says the Federal Register takes precedence, so check the current rule for any specific use. Copeland's CO2 hazards article adds that it is odorless and heavier than air, with an 8-hour occupational exposure limit of 5,000 ppm, so leaks in small closed rooms still matter. See Refrigerant Safety for breathing hazards in general.

The critical point and "transcritical"

Every refrigerant has a critical point, a temperature and pressure above which liquid and vapor become one dense fluid. For most refrigerants that temperature is far above any outdoor temperature. For CO2 it is about 88 F (31 C) at roughly 73.8 bar, as Danfoss's CO2 fundamentals guide gives it. A summer day or a hot water target sits above that line.

So a CO2 system is run transcritical. The evaporator side stays below the critical point (liquid boils, as always). The high side runs above it. A review in the journal Energies describes the cycle this way: the evaporator is subcritical while the gas cooler works in the supercritical region.

Gas cooler vs condenser

A condenser rejects heat while vapor turns to liquid at nearly one temperature. A gas cooler cools a dense, hot fluid that never changes phase, so its temperature falls steadily from inlet to outlet. That sloping profile is called temperature glide. The Energies review states that in the gas cooler the refrigerant temperature decreases continuously without a phase change. Danfoss notes that the gas cooler outlet should be kept as cold as possible for efficiency, and that when water cools the gas cooler, water return temperature strongly governs the high-side pressure.

Why this suits hot water

Water heating needs a large temperature rise, and the sloped refrigerant line can follow the water line. The Energies review says a CO2 ground-source heat pump can supply water at 90 C (194 F) or higher where conventional refrigerants reach about 60 C (140 F), and credits the glide for better heating performance. Colder water returning to the gas cooler also helps: Danfoss says water return temperature strongly governs gas cooler pressure, and a field report on a CO2 split unit linked larger water draws (colder water back to the outdoor unit) to better COP, though it found no clean trend with inlet temperature across its sites. A published modeling study (OSTI abstract, Vol. 85 of the International Journal of Refrigeration) calls tank stratification essential for higher CO2 water heater performance.

Pressures and what they mean for tools

Sources state high-side pressures that would be alarming on an R-410A system. The Energies review gives 8.0 to 11.0 MPa and compressor discharge of 90 to 130 bar, against 10 to 40 bar for HFC heat pumps. A Danfoss small-system guide gives 80 to 120 bar discharge, a 130 bar maximum allowable pressure with a burst disc, and gas cooler parts good for up to 137 bar. Sanden's technical information for its water heater gives an operating range of 600 to 1,600 psi and a high-pressure switch that opens above 2,000 psig. Even at 0 C the evaporating pressure is about 35 bar, where common refrigerants are under 10 bar.

What that implies: ordinary R-410A gauge sets and hoses are not the right tools. Components, piping and tools must be rated for these pressures, as Copeland states. Use CO2-rated gauges, hoses and recovery equipment, and follow the maker's service procedure before you connect anything. Our manifold gauge lesson covers normal hose handling.

CO2 heat pump water heaters

A common residential layout is a split system: an outdoor unit holds the compressor, gas cooler and evaporator fan coil, and a separate indoor tank stores the water. The Ecotope laboratory report on one such unit describes a 315 L (83 gal) indoor tank with no resistance elements and an inverter-driven outdoor unit, and the maker's literature lists set points from 130 F up to 175 F with a mixing valve required to limit delivered temperature. The same literature rates the outdoor unit to operate down to -20 F without a backup element, with COP above 2.0 at 5 F ambient and above 4.0 at 67 F. Refrigerant circuit piping must not be altered, and the refrigerant charge is small (22 oz in that model). For how HPWH tanks fit into a house, see Heat Pump Water Heaters: How They Work, and for low-ambient behavior see Cold-Climate Heat Pumps.

Supermarkets in brief

Large stores use the same idea at much bigger scale, with transcritical racks and a gas cooler in place of a condenser. The rack basics are in Supermarket Refrigeration Racks; the CO2 differences are the gas cooler, the pressures above and the safety items below.

Relief valves, standstill and trapped liquid

When a CO2 system stops, it warms toward room temperature and pressure rises. Copeland says standstill pressure on some systems is higher than the maximum rated suction pressure, so pressure relief valves are needed and may discharge during a fault such as power failure. Danfoss's small-system guide recommends burst discs or relief valves, one for each section that can be isolated, and a pressure switch set so the compressor stops before the burst disc opens. Copeland also warns that trapped liquid is severe: its example shows a 20 C rise from -10 C taking pressure from about 44 bar to about 240 bar. Never close in liquid with no relief path.

Dry ice and venting

Below the triple point, CO2 cannot be liquid. Copeland puts it at 60.9 psig (4.2 bar) and -68.8 F (-56 C). If pressure falls below that, as in venting vapor, a relief discharge or charging liquid into an empty system, solid dry ice forms. It can block a vent line and then sublimate with a big pressure rise. Dry ice surfaces are about -109 F, so contact causes freeze burns; wear gloves and eye protection. Copeland advises venting outdoors, away from people and occupied spaces. Follow the maker's recovery and charging steps, and charge slowly where cylinder pressure exceeds the system's maximum operating pressure.

Worked example: heating water from 50 F to 150 F at 1.5 gpm

Stated inputs for this example: inlet water 50 F, outlet 150 F, flow 1.5 gpm, COP 3.5 at those conditions. The COP is a stated assumption, not a rating.

  1. Our own: temperature rise = 150 - 50 = 100 F.
  2. Our own: heat output = 500 x 1.5 gpm x 100 F = 75,000 Btu/h. (The 500 factor is about 8.33 lb per gallon x 60 min per hour for water.)
  3. Our own: electric input = 75,000 / 3.5 = 21,429 Btu/h.
  4. Our own: 21,429 / 3,412 Btu per kWh = about 6.28 kW.
  5. Our own: resistance heater at COP 1 needs 75,000 / 3,412 = about 21.98 kW.
  6. Our own: difference = 21.98 - 6.28 = about 15.7 kW less, or about 71 percent.

Our own sanity check: the maker's literature above rates one residential outdoor unit at about 15,400 Btu/h, so it could heat only about 15,400 / (500 x 100) = 0.31 gpm through a 100 F rise. A flow of 1.5 gpm for long periods would call for a much larger system, which is why a storage tank is used.

Field card: CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps

Hot side never condenses: gas cooler, high pressure, CO2-rated tools.

  • R-744: GWP 1, class A1; still an asphyxiation concern in closed rooms.
  • Critical point is about 88 F (31 C), so the high side runs transcritical.
  • Gas cooler = sloped temperature, good match for heating water.
  • Sources cite 80 to 130 bar discharge. Use CO2-rated gauges, hoses and tools.
  • Standstill and trapped liquid raise pressure. Relief paths required.
  • Venting below about 60.9 psig can form dry ice. Vent outdoors, wear gloves and eye protection.
  • Heat output (Btu/h) = 500 x gpm x temperature rise. kW = Btu/h / 3,412.
