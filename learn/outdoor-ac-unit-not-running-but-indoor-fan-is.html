Outdoor AC Unit Not Running but the Indoor Fan Is

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You hear air from the vents, but the big box outside is silent. That pattern is a clue: the indoor blower and the outdoor unit are started by different wires. Something is stopping the cooling request, or the power, from reaching the outdoor unit.

What the symptom points to

The thermostat asks for the blower with the G wire and for cooling with the Y wire. Y feeds the coil of the contactor, the power switch in the outdoor unit. Terminal meanings are in Thermostat Terminal Letters, and the contactor itself is in Capacitors and Contactors. So an indoor fan with a dead outdoor unit usually means one of these:

  • The Y signal never arrives. Causes include thermostat settings or dead batteries, a tripped condensate float switch, a blown low-voltage fuse or a failed control transformer (see Low-Voltage Short, Blown Fuse and Transformer Troubleshooting). If the transformer were fully dead, the indoor fan would usually stop too, so a blown fuse or open safety on the Y path is likelier.
  • The unit has no line power. A tripped breaker or a pulled outdoor disconnect leaves the unit dark even though the coil is signaled.
  • The contactor coil or contacts failed. The coil may be open, or the contacts may be burned.
  • A capacitor failed. Lennox says a bad capacitor can stop the fan and compressor from starting, and a buzzing or humming outdoor unit may signal it. This one hums rather than sits silent.
  • A delay or safety is holding it off. Some boards and kits add a compressor delay, and a high-pressure or low-pressure switch can open the control circuit. A Rheem-hosted kit sheet says opening its high-pressure switch starts a 5 minute compressor delay.
  • Heat pump defrost. Trane calls it normal for a heat pump outdoor fan to stop during defrost, usually for 3 to 5 minutes. That is a pause, not a fault. See Heat Pump Defrost Control Boards and Sensors.

Homeowner checks that are safe

  1. Thermostat. Carrier says to set the mode to Cool (or Auto) and the fan to Auto, and to check that a schedule is not overriding you. Trane suggests setting it 2 to 4 degrees below the room, in case it is still in heating mode. Goodman notes dead thermostat batteries can keep a unit from starting.
  2. Breaker and outdoor disconnect. Check the panel for a breaker that is off or sitting in the middle position, and look at the disconnect box near the outdoor unit. Carrier and Lennox both say that if a breaker trips again after a reset, stop and call a professional. Do not pull or push a disconnect you cannot see clearly.
  3. Wait. If the system just shut off or lost power, give it several minutes before deciding it is dead. Some units hold the compressor off for a delay.
  4. Look for water, without opening anything. A condensate float switch shuts cooling off when the drain backs up. Lennox lists a tripped float switch among the reasons an outdoor unit will not start. Look at the floor or secondary pan near the indoor unit and at the drain outlet. If you see standing water, switch the system off at the thermostat and see Water Leaking from the AC or Furnace. Carrier, Lennox and Trane all keep capacitor, contactor and wiring work for professionals, and so does this page. Do not open the unit or jumper anything.

The broader checklist for a system that runs but is warm is in Air Conditioner Running but Not Cooling. Call a pro if the checks do not help, if you hear humming from the outdoor unit, if you smell burning, or if you see ice.

The tech section: a measured sequence

This is education, not a repair procedure. The live checks need training, a rated meter ( Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings ) and lockout habits ( Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead ). ACHR News describes a similar order for condensers: confirm supply voltage at the unit, then control voltage, then the contactor.

  1. Is the call real? With the thermostat calling for cooling, read Y to C at the air handler. No 24 V there means the problem is at the thermostat side. First read R to C to prove the transformer and fuse.
  2. Is 24 V reaching the contactor coil? Read across the two coil terminals. About 24 V with a contactor that does not pull in means a failed coil, as in an ACHR News case where 230 V sat at the contactor and nothing pulled in. Zero volts means the fault is upstream.
  3. Walk the Y wire. ACHR News says that with no voltage at the coil, you read across each safety, and a reading across a safety shows the open one. The same article notes a low-voltage wire can run through a high-pressure switch inside the unit.
  4. Line voltage. Read L1 to L2 at the bottom of the contactor and compare it to the nameplate. Then read the load side with the contactor closed. Power on one side only points to the contactor.
  5. Capacitor and motor. If the contactor is in and the unit hums, lock out and test the capacitor against its label. Details are in Testing Capacitors, Contactors and Relays with a Meter and Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures.
  6. Delays and pressure switches. If everything upstream is good but the coil is not powered, find the delay board or switch in that path and ask why it opened. Never bridge a safety. See Compressor Hums and Won't Start for the hum case.

Worked example: isolating a float switch

This is a constructed teaching case. A pattern like it appears in an ACHR News piece where the Y wire ran through a float switch in a secondary pan. The indoor fan runs, the thermostat calls for cooling, and the outdoor unit is silent.

  1. Coil. The meter across the contactor coil terminals reads 0 V. The coil is not being asked to pull in, so the contactor is not yet suspect.
  2. Transformer. R to C at the air handler reads 24 V. The transformer and fuse are fine, so the control side has power.
  3. Before the switch. With cooling called, Y to C at the air handler board reads 24 V. The call is real.
  4. After the switch. Y to C on the far side of the float switch reads 0 V. The signal enters the switch but does not leave.
  5. Across the switch. Place the leads on the two float switch terminals: 24 V - 0 V = 24 V. In a series circuit, an open part drops all of the available voltage, so this reading shows the switch is open.
  6. Cause. The pan holds water from a clogged drain. Clear the drain and the switch closes. Y to C after the switch then reads 24 V, the coil sees 24 V, and the contactor pulls in. In the ACHR case, clearing the pan closed the switch and the condenser started.

The logic is "voltage before, none after, full voltage across." That pattern points to the one open device, and it keeps you from replacing a contactor that is fine. Do not jumper the switch to prove it; the water is the real fault. Why the fan still runs: the RectorSeal sheet says a switch in the yellow circuit leaves the fan running, while one in the red circuit shuts down the entire unit. A trade training site adds that breaking Y keeps the blower on while the compressor and condenser fan stop.

RectorSeal says its switch will not detect clogs upstream of its Tee, and it should be inspected, cleaned and tested at least every 30 days and after drain cleaning.

Field card: Outdoor Unit Dead, Indoor Fan Running

G runs the indoor fan. Y runs the outdoor unit. Find where Y stops.

  • Homeowner: thermostat on Cool, fan on Auto, batteries good.
  • Homeowner: breaker and outdoor disconnect, then wait several minutes.
  • Homeowner: look for standing water by the indoor unit. Do not open panels.
  • Tech: R-C, then Y-C, then voltage across the contactor coil.
  • Tech: 24 V across an open series part is the clue. Do not jumper safeties.
  • Humming outdoors points to a capacitor. A fan that stops in heat pump defrost is normal.
