Anaerobic Digester Heating: Heat Loops, Fouling, H2S and Gas Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A digester on a dairy farm or at a wastewater plant is a heated tank full of living bacteria, and the bacteria only work well when the tank stays warm. Keeping it warm is a boiler and hot-water job. This lesson covers how that heating is done, what goes wrong with it, and the gas hazards you walk into when you service the equipment.

Why the tank is held warm

The bacteria that make biogas are grouped by the temperature they like. A university extension fact sheet on manure biogas describes the mesophilic group as thriving around 95°F (35°C) and the thermophilic group as most active at 120°F to 140°F. It adds that going to the higher range often is not worth the energy needed to get there. A federal environmental agency operator guidebook for farm digesters gives the mesophilic range as 86°F to 104°F, and a second extension handbook gives 95°F to 104°F. A federal agency fact sheet on sludge digestion gives 85°F to 100°F for wastewater plants. The ranges differ a little by source, but they all land near 95°F, so that is the number we use below.

For the heating system, the point is steady temperature. The guidebook notes that a falling digester temperature can be a sign that heating coils have caked over, so the heat input is part of how the plant is monitored, not just a comfort setting.

How the heat gets in

The federal sludge fact sheet lists three common ways to add heat: steam injection, internal heat exchangers and external heat exchangers. It calls external exchangers the most common because they are flexible and easier to maintain, and says steam injection dilutes the contents and can waste energy. The farm guidebook says high-rate digesters need internal or external heat exchangers.

  • Internal coils. Hot water runs through pipe inside the tank. The extension fact sheet describes one example with 188 feet of three-quarter-inch steel pipe giving 72 square feet of heat exchange surface. Coils can cake over with slurry, and the sludge fact sheet says caked internal coils may have to be pulled or the digester taken out of service.
  • External heat exchangers. Slurry is pumped out of the tank, through a tube-in-tube or spiral exchanger with hot water on the other side, and back in. You can isolate and open the exchanger without emptying the tank.

The hot-water side is an ordinary closed loop from a boiler, so everything in the related lessons on circulators, expansion and water treatment applies. The slurry side is the unusual part.

Sludge fouling and approach temperature

Approach temperature here means how close the two streams get at the exchanger: for instance, the hot water leaving the exchanger compared with the slurry entering it. When the exchanger is clean, a small approach is enough to move the heat. Slurry solids bake or cake onto the surface and act like insulation, so the same job needs a bigger temperature difference. Typical signs are a growing approach, a hot-water return that stays hot, a rising pressure drop on the slurry side (the guidebook lists an increased pressure drop across a heat exchanger as an early warning) and a digester that will not hold temperature.

You cannot simply raise the hot-water temperature to compensate. The extension fact sheet warns that slurry will bake onto the heating surface if that surface goes above 150°F (77°C), which makes the fouling worse. Keep supply temperatures modest, keep slurry velocity up, and clean on a schedule. For the math behind approach and fouling, see the log mean temperature difference lesson.

Biogas-fired boilers and engine heat

The farm guidebook says biogas can replace natural gas as boiler or furnace fuel. In the extension fact sheet's example, a biogas boiler held a 95°F digester all year and, after startup, used about 30 percent of the biogas produced. Biogas is weaker fuel than pipeline gas: the extension fact sheet gives about 600 Btu per cubic foot, and another extension fact sheet gives 537 to 700 Btu per cubic foot against 1,028 for commercial natural gas. A burner or gas train set for natural gas will not pass the same heat at the same pressure, so settings and orifices need to match the fuel.

Where an engine-generator runs on the biogas, its jacket and exhaust heat can warm the digester. The guidebook and the handbook both say recovered engine heat is typically used for digester heating, with the boiler as the backup or startup heat source. The engine side is covered in the combined heat and power lesson, so we do not repeat it here.

Hydrogen sulfide, moisture and corrosion

The farm guidebook says biogas carries trace hydrogen sulfide, anywhere from 0 to 15,000 ppm, and that it forms highly corrosive sulfuric acid when it meets water. Raw biogas is also saturated with water vapor, and the guidebook says untreated vapor can condense and damage piping and equipment. The same guidebook says the acid formed from burning H2S with water vapor creates corrosive conditions that can quickly degrade combustion equipment, and that biogas moisture, H2S and possible siloxanes can cause problems inside a boiler. An extension fact sheet adds that H2S can wear burners down until they need replacement.

What this means for you:

  • Expect pitting at burners, flue connections, the heat exchanger and any cold spot where the flue gas can drop below its dew point.
  • Mild steel corrodes anywhere H2S, carbon dioxide or water is present, according to the guidebook, so inspect often.
  • Gas piping should slope, and the handbook says all low points need condensate traps and gives a slope of one quarter inch per foot of run. The guidebook calls for self-dumping traps and says to check freeze protection in cold weather. Cooling 1,000 cubic feet of biogas to 60°F yields about one and a half pints of condensate, per the handbook, which shows why drains matter.
  • Gas cleanup (removing H2S and moisture) is recommended before biogas is burned on site, according to the handbook. Ask which cleanup is installed before you set combustion.

Pressure relief

The guidebook says an automatic pressure relief valve should be part of every digester gas system, piped to an automatically igniting flare so released gas is burned. Its causes of high pressure include condensate blockage, foam carry-over and frozen valves or fittings. It says relief valves should be checked so they do not stick from corrosion or deposits. Do not cap, plug or adjust these devices. Treat any protective device you did not install as the designer's, and ask before touching it.

Explosive atmosphere, confined spaces and gas detection

A federal agency safety document for on-farm digesters gives the explosive range of methane as 5 percent to 15 percent by volume in air. It lists hydrogen sulfide above 100 ppm as immediately dangerous to life and health. A university extension safety page lists a safety limit of 10 ppm for H2S and notes that asphyxiation is a risk in confined spaces connected to a digester.

  • Ignition control. The safety document names light switches, electric motors, pilot flames and cell phones as possible ignition sources and suggests a 25 to 50 foot setback and keeping smoking at least 50 feet away. It says wiring near combustible gas has to meet the hazardous-location standard.
  • Test before entry. Use a multi-gas detector that reads oxygen, carbon monoxide, H2S and the lower explosive limit. The safety document says to enter only with oxygen above 19.5 percent, H2S below 20 ppm and methane below 5 percent, test from outside, and keep the space ventilated.
  • Buddy and retrieval. A second person watches from a safe distance and the entrant wears a harness tied to a retrieval device.
  • Fixed detectors. The safety document suggests wall-mounted sensors in places such as pump chambers and engine-generator rooms.

The confined spaces lesson covers the entry sequence. Hot work on gas piping or a boiler room near a digester needs the plant's permit system and a gas-free check first; this page is education, not a procedure.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a published case. Assumptions we chose: a feed of 20,000 gallons per day of manure slurry, treated as water (8.34 lb per gallon, specific heat 1.0 Btu/lb°F), arriving at 50°F. The digester target is 95°F, from the extension fact sheet. Boiler efficiency is 80 percent (assumed), and the biogas is 600 Btu per cubic foot (extension fact sheet). We ignore tank wall losses, pump heat and heat released by the biology, so a real design needs more.

  1. Temperature rise: 95 − 50 = 45°F.
  2. Mass per day: 20,000 gal × 8.34 lb/gal = 166,800 lb/day.
  3. Heat per day: 166,800 × 1.0 × 45 = 7,506,000 Btu/day.
  4. Per hour: 7,506,000 ÷ 24 = 312,750 Btu/h.
  5. Fuel input at 80 percent: 7,506,000 ÷ 0.80 = 9,382,500 Btu/day.
  6. Biogas: 9,382,500 ÷ 600 = 15,637.5, about 15,600 cubic feet per day.

For a quick check against 312,750 Btu/h of load, a 400,000 Btu/h input boiler at 80 percent gives 320,000 Btu/h output (our own arithmetic). Winter feed colder than 50°F raises every number in the list.

Field card: Digester heating

Warm tank, clean exchanger, dry gas, tested air.

  • Mesophilic digesters run near 95°F; sources give ranges from about 85°F to 104°F.
  • Heat comes from internal coils or external exchangers on a hot-water loop; engine heat first, boiler as backup.
  • Growing approach or pressure drop means fouling; surface above 150°F bakes slurry on.
  • Biogas is about 600 Btu per cubic foot, so burners and orifices must suit it.
  • H2S plus water makes acid: watch burners, flues and exchangers; keep condensate traps working.
  • Methane explosive range is 5 to 15 percent; test oxygen, H2S, CO and methane before entry.
  • Never defeat relief or protective devices.
