Reading an HVAC Nameplate: MCA, MOCP, RLA and LRA

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every outdoor condensing unit has a data plate, and two numbers on it decide what wire and what breaker go in the wall: the minimum circuit ampacity (MCA) and the maximum overcurrent protection (MOCP). They look alike, they are not the same job, and mixing them up is a classic apprentice mistake. This lesson reads the plate line by line and checks the math.

Reading a plate is not wiring a circuit. This lesson teaches you to read and check a plate. It does not authorize you to run or change a circuit. In New Jersey, the HVACR board's rule limits a master HVACR contractor's electrical work to 30 volts, apart from listed replacement hookups from an existing disconnect, and new circuits generally belong to a licensed electrical contractor; see NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers for where that line sits.

The plate, line by line

  • Voltage, phase, hertz. The supply the unit was built for. Makers also publish an operating voltage range. In one Rheem installation manual, the table for 208/230 volt single-phase units gives 187 to 253 volts, and the troubleshooting section says the voltage must stay within plus or minus 10 percent of the nameplate marking while running. Check your own unit's manual rather than assuming a range.
  • RLA (rated-load amps). The manufacturer's current rating for the compressor at its rated load, voltage and frequency. It is the starting number for the MCA and MOCP math, according to IAEI Magazine.
  • LRA (locked-rotor amps). The current a motor draws when the rotor is stalled, which is also what it pulls for an instant at start. Designers use it to check that the disconnect and controls can handle it.
  • FLA (full-load amps). Here, the outdoor fan motor's own rated current. The sample plate lists it separately because the fan is a second, smaller load on the same circuit.
  • MCA. The smallest supply-circuit conductor ampacity the unit needs.
  • MOCP. The largest fuse or breaker the maker allows. Some plates also list a minimum, the smallest device that will still let the compressor start.

Why wire follows MCA and breaker follows MOCP

The wire has one job: carry the unit's running current without overheating. The MCA already includes a safety margin, so you check that the conductor's ampacity is at least that number. IAEI Magazine puts it this way: the manufacturer has already applied the 125 percent factor, so the installer verifies the conductor against the plate.

The breaker or fuse has a different job: open on a short circuit or ground fault, while still letting a compressor ride through its start-up surge. That is why the plate gives a ceiling, not a target. The marked maximum can look too big for the wire. In the IAEI sample, a 40 amp maximum goes with a conductor that is rated for less than that, and the article explains that the maximum device together with the unit's own overload protection provides the required protection. Mike Holt's EC&M article makes the same point: the wire and the device are sized as two separate limits taken from the nameplate under Article 440 of the National Electrical Code, which is the part of the code written for air-conditioning and refrigerating equipment.

The practical rule: the device may be smaller than the maximum if it still handles starting and running current, but never larger. And if the plate lists a minimum device too, stay between the two.

Worked example: rebuild the plate numbers

Use the sample plate above: compressor RLA 18.0 A, outdoor fan FLA 1.3 A, single phase. The compressor is the largest motor.

Step 1, MCA. IAEI Magazine gives the formula as 1.25 times the largest motor plus the other loads.

  1. 1.25 x 18.0 A = 22.5 A
  2. 22.5 A + 1.3 A (fan) = 23.8 A
  3. Round up to 24 A. The plate says 24 A. It matches.

Step 2, MOCP. The same article shows the maximum device as up to 2.25 times the largest motor plus the other loads, a figure it traces to the product safety standard UL 1995.

  1. 2.25 x 18.0 A = 40.5 A
  2. 40.5 A + 1.3 A = 41.8 A
  3. 41.8 A is not a standard device size, and the article says the rule takes the next lower standard size, which is 40 A. The plate says 40 A. It matches.

Notice the two roundings go opposite ways: the MCA rounds up, so the wire is never too small; the MOCP rounds down, so the protection is never above the limit. Always trust the plate over your own arithmetic; the maker's published figure is the one that counts. Use the math to understand the plate and to catch a typo, not to replace it.

Fuse, breaker and HACR

HACR stands for Heating, Air Conditioning and Refrigeration. IAEI Magazine explains that an HACR breaker has been tested for this kind of equipment, including the smaller fan motor on the same circuit. Read the plate wording like this:

  • "Fuse only" means a circuit breaker is not allowed. Mike Holt's article says it plainly: with a fuse-only plate, a circuit breaker cannot be used.
  • "Fuse or HACR circuit breaker" means either is acceptable, up to the maximum.
  • "HACR breaker" means the breaker itself must carry the HACR marking.

Daikin's and Goodman's condensing-unit installation references both list time delay fuses and HACR type circuit breakers as the approved devices, because the delay lets the compressor start. The Daikin manual tells you to use the same amperage when you replace a device, and to use copper conductors. IAEI Magazine adds that most current breakers are HACR rated, but older ones in existing panels may not be, so check the marking, not the brand.

Where this meets your daily work

A service call involving a new or replaced unit can start with the plate: does the breaker in the panel match the MOCP, and does the wire at least meet the MCA? If either looks wrong, write down what you saw and report it. Do not swap a breaker or wire on your own. Who may replace a device or change a circuit is set by state licensing rules, and the NJ lesson linked above explains where that line sits.

Your meter work on the same unit still follows Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC and Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings. For what the contactor and run capacitor on the plate's loads actually do, see Capacitors and Contactors. Amps, volts and ohms are covered in Electrical Basics for HVAC.

Field card: Reading an HVAC Nameplate

Wire follows MCA. Breaker follows MOCP. The plate beats your math.

  • RLA = compressor rated-load amps. LRA = locked-rotor (start-up surge) amps. FLA = fan motor full-load amps.
  • MCA = 1.25 x largest motor + other loads, rounded up. Example: 1.25 x 18.0 + 1.3 = 23.8, so 24 A.
  • MOCP = up to 2.25 x largest motor + other loads, rounded down to a standard size. Example: 2.25 x 18.0 + 1.3 = 41.8, so 40 A.
  • The maximum is a ceiling. Go smaller only if it still starts the compressor, and honor any listed minimum.
  • Fuse-only plate: no breaker. HACR plate: HACR-marked breaker.
  • Check supply voltage against the maker's range while the unit runs.
  • NJ: new circuits and work above 30 V generally belong to a licensed electrical contractor. Report, do not improvise.
