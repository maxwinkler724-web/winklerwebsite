Drafty Doors and Windows: Finding Air Leaks, Weatherstripping, Door Sweeps, Caulk and When to Get a Blower Door Test

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A cold draft at your ankles in January does not mean the furnace is failing. More often the house is leaking at the doors and windows. This lesson shows how homeowners find those leaks, which seals fit which gap, and where a do-it-yourself fix stops and a professional test starts.

Why a draft is a comfort problem even when the furnace is fine

A furnace can hold the thermostat at 70 degrees while you still feel chilly. A Missouri extension guide says drafts or cold spots may point to some of the larger leaks in a home, and that gaps can make the house drafty and chilly in winter. A moving stream of cold air across skin feels colder than still air at the same temperature. It also pulls the room unevenly: the spot by the door is cold while the thermostat in the hall reads fine. Sealing helps both the bill and the feel. The Energy Saver guide from the U.S. Department of Energy says that sealing uncontrolled air leaks can save 10% to 20% on heating and cooling bills, and calls air leaks one of the greatest sources of energy loss in a home. DOE's building science pages add that air sealing can improve comfort, indoor air quality, noise control and durability.

Finding leaks

Pick a cold, windy day if you can. The Energy Saver guide says to check around walls, ceilings, windows, doors and lighting and plumbing fixtures, and to hold a lit incense stick or smoke pen near windows, doors and electrical boxes. If the smoke stream travels sideways, you have found a leak. A North Carolina State extension guide adds more low-tech tests:

  • Damp hand. It says wetting your hand makes it more sensitive to moving air.
  • Incense. Smoke that moves sideways shows airflow. The same guide says closing windows and running exhaust fans first makes leaks easier to see. Read the safety section below before you do that.
  • Daylight. With the room dark, shine a flashlight along the door edge while a helper looks from the other side. If light shows through, air can pass too. A Virginia extension guide calls this the flashlight test and also describes a dollar-bill test: if a bill pinched in the closed door slides out easily, the weatherstripping needs repair or replacement.

Use an open flame carefully indoors; that same NC State guide gives only that caution, so keep candles and incense away from curtains and clear of any gas appliance.

Choosing the seal

A Maryland state energy office page lists the common types and where each goes. The Virginia and Arkansas extension guides cover the door bottom.

  • Foam tape. Closed-cell or open-cell foam tape goes along a door jamb or at the top and bottom of a window sash. The Maryland page says that for air leakage, closed-cell foam with rubber tapes and strips with a tight-sealing, rigid gasket worked well.
  • V-strip (tension seal). Plastic or bronze, it sits inside the track of a double-hung window or between a door and its jamb. Virginia's guide says V-channel belongs in window tracks and between sashes. It may have a self-adhesive back but still needs nails or staples to stay put.
  • Door sweep. Virginia and Arkansas describe it as a strip of metal, often with a flexible rubber or plastic edge, for a door with no threshold. It mounts on the bottom of the door, inside or outside depending on the swing.
  • Door shoe. Rubber or plastic gaskets set into a metal bracket that fits the door bottom. Both guides say it works with any threshold that is not worn in the middle. A floor-mounted gasket threshold, they warn, wears quickly in high-traffic areas.

Arkansas also says to put weatherstripping on the sides and top of a door with a sweep or similar device on the bottom, and to replace pressed rubber or foam that does not spring back or shows dents or tears. Missouri adds that well-installed weatherstripping is slightly compressed when the door is closed. Compare that with the sketch below.

Caulk, foam and gaskets

Caulk window trim. Caulk is for fixed seams, such as between window or door frames and siding or trim, not for parts that move. Virginia says caulk can seal openings up to 1/4 inch wide, and Missouri puts the limit at about 3/8 inch. For larger gaps, Missouri says foam sealant is commonly used up to 1 inch, and Virginia says to put in foam backing material before caulk. Apply it to a clean, dry surface. Missouri says to apply caulk only when the outdoor temperature is above 45 degrees F.

Outlet gaskets. The Energy Saver guide says to install foam gaskets behind outlet and switch plates on walls. Virginia says the gaskets are made for this and go behind the plates. Switch off the circuit before you remove a cover plate. Do not push gasket foam past the plate into the box.

Interior window film. The Energy Saver guide lists low-e film on the inside of windows to keep heat from radiating out in cold weather. Seasonal kits that stretch clear plastic over the inside of the frame are a related idea, but we did not find a government or university source that tests them, so treat them as a short-term way to cut drafts at a leaky window, not as a replacement for sealing the frame.

How much do doors and windows matter next to the attic?

Do not expect the door to be the main event. A Virginia extension figure, credited there to the U.S. Department of Energy, splits a typical home's air leaks by source: ceilings, walls and floors 31%, ducts 15%, fireplace 14%, plumbing penetrations 13%, doors 11%, windows 10%, fans and vents 4% and electrical outlets 2%. Those are typical-home shares and your house will differ. The EPA and DOE ENERGY STAR attic page says the attic is usually where you can find some of the largest opportunities to save energy, and Missouri points to the stack effect: warm air rises and leaves through openings in the attic floor. So work in order. Weatherstrip the doors and windows that you feel, because that is where comfort changes fastest. For the bigger savings, see Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for New Jersey Homes. If the window glass fogs after you tighten up, read Condensation on Windows in Winter.

Worked example: a 1/8 inch gap under a 36 inch door

This is our own example, with assumed inputs. Assume a door with a uniform 1/8 inch gap (0.125 in.) along the full 36 inch width of the bottom. Nothing here is a measurement from a real house.

  1. Open area = gap height x width = 0.125 in. x 36 in. = 4.5 square inches.
  2. Compare to a square hole of the same area: side = square root of 4.5 = about 2.12 in., so a hole roughly 2 1/8 inches on a side.
  3. Compare to a round hole: area = pi x d squared / 4, so d = square root of (4 x 4.5 / 3.1416) = square root of 5.73 = about 2.4 inches across.

So the thin, easy-to-ignore slit leaks like a 2.4 inch round hole punched in the wall. For scale, Missouri's guide estimates the gaps in an average home add up to a hole about 14 inches square, or about 196 square inches. Our 4.5 square inches is about 2.3% of that (4.5 / 196). One door gap is small but cheap to close, and a sweep or door shoe closes it. Virginia and Arkansas give another sense of scale: a 1/4 inch gap along a pair of 6 ft 8 in. doors adds up to about 20 square inches.

Safety: do not seal vents or starve combustion appliances

  • Combustion air. ENERGY STAR says sealing can cause naturally venting combustion appliances to back-draft gases into the home and may trap pollutants such as carbon monoxide. Missouri says cutting off the air supply could let carbon monoxide build up, and that combustion air should be sized properly and drawn from outside the house.
  • Test when you have gas or oil. For oil- or gas-fired appliances, ENERGY STAR recommends a home performance contractor test that they are drafting properly before and after sealing. It also suggests a radon test after air sealing.
  • Do not seal flue and vent paths. The Energy Saver guide says to use fire-resistant materials around fireplace chimneys, furnaces and gas water heater vents. Never caulk over vents, louvers or fresh-air openings for appliances.
  • Fans and moisture. Kitchen and bath exhaust still need to work; see Bathroom Exhaust Fans. Working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms matter more in a tighter house.

When to call for a blower door test

A blower door is a large fan set in an exterior door frame that pulls air out of a house. Missouri calls it the usual starting point for finding leaks, and DOE says air tightness can be measured using a blower door test. Consider one if the house is still drafty after you seal the doors and windows, if rooms are uneven (see Upstairs Too Hot), if you have gas or oil appliances and plan major sealing, or before insulating. The Energy Saver guide says a detailed assessment includes diagnostic tests such as a house air leakage test, a duct leakage test or an infrared scan. See What Happens in a Home Energy Audit for what the visit looks like.

Field card: Drafty Doors and Windows

Find it, seal it, then keep the air supply.

  • Test with incense, a damp hand, or a flashlight and a helper.
  • Door sides and top: foam tape or V-strip. Bottom: sweep or door shoe.
  • Caulk fixed trim seams only; foam for bigger gaps.
  • Our example: 1/8 in. x 36 in. = 4.5 sq in., like a 2.4 in. round hole.
  • Attic leaks usually matter more than a door gap.
  • Never seal vents or fresh-air openings; test combustion appliances.
  • Still drafty after the easy fixes: ask about a blower door test.
