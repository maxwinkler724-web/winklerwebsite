Plastic Vent Pipe for Furnaces and Boilers: PVC, CPVC and Polypropylene

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "PVC vs CPVC vs polypropylene boiler venting," the short answer is that the appliance maker picks, not the installer and not the pipe supplier. This lesson shows what the makers' own manuals say about each plastic, how temperature limits and a few special rules show up, and how to add up vent length with a maker's table.

The manual decides

The manual decides. This is general education, not a ruling on any job. Vent material, length, fittings, glue and terminations come from the installation manual for the exact appliance and the fuel gas code where you work. The numbers below come from the manuals named in the text and are examples. Your manual may say something different. We did not read the vent standards themselves, only manufacturer documents and a federal recall notice.

Why condensing appliances use plastic

A condensing furnace, boiler or water heater pulls so much heat out of the flue gas that the gas is cool and wet. Dura-Vent's instructions say condensing appliances have much lower exhaust temperatures that create significant amounts of acidic condensate, which is why metal chimney parts built for hot gas are not the right tool. These are the appliances in Category IV, and often Category II; see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained for the sorting, and Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat for what happens to the old chimney. This page is only about the plastic itself.

The three plastics

PVC. Trane's installer's guide for its condensing furnaces lists PVC pipe made to ASTM D1785 (Schedule 40, 80 and 120), D2665 drain-waste-vent pipe and D2241 SDR pipe, with a maximum of 158°F. Lochinvar's SB-1300 boiler manual lists Schedule 40 or 80 under ASTM D1785 and fittings under ASTM D2466 or D2467.

CPVC. The same Trane table gives CPVC (ASTM F441 and others) a higher limit of 212°F. Lochinvar's boiler manual adds a practical rule: the first 10 equivalent feet of vent off the boiler must be CPVC or stainless steel. Some boilers ask for a CPVC first section like this because the gas is hottest right at the appliance. Read the manual for the length it wants.

Polypropylene. Trane's table gives polypropylene a limit of 230°F and names PolyPro, Z-Dens, InnoFlue and PolyFlue. Centrotherm describes InnoFlue as rated for 230°F sustained flue gas, and DuraVent describes PolyPro as limited to 230°F continuous. Polypropylene joins by push-together gasketed sockets, not by glue. Centrotherm's guide describes a push-and-twist joint with a factory gasket, a water-based lubricant and a connector ring, and says to use InnoFlue parts throughout rather than mixing makers. Trane says not to use cement on polypropylene.

Temperature limits are the maker's numbers

Do not memorize one set of limits. DuraVent's PolyPro page compares polypropylene at 230°F with PVC at 149°F and CPVC at 194°F, which are lower than Trane's 158°F and 212°F. Both are real documents from real makers; they rate different products and test them differently. What matters is that the appliance manual states a maximum vent temperature or a list of allowed materials, and the vent you install must match it. If an appliance can produce flue gas hotter than a plastic can take, the manual will steer you to CPVC, polypropylene or stainless steel. Do not insulate plastic vent pipe unless the manual says so. Lochinvar says insulation raises wall temperature and can cause failure, and also says not to insulate polypropylene.

Listed systems: UL 1738 and ULC S636

Centrotherm lists InnoFlue to UL 1738 in the United States and ULC S636 in Canada. DuraVent says PolyPro is listed to ULC S636 in Canada and that U.S. listing depends on the appliance, so check the appliance maker. Trane and Lochinvar say Canadian installations must use ULC S636 vent. The takeaway is that a listed vent system is a tested set of pipe, fittings and terminations from one maker. Mixing a polypropylene pipe from one company with fittings from another breaks the listing, and the makers say so.

Cellular core PVC

Cellular (foam) core pipe has a foamed center layer. Makers disagree about it, which is the point. Lochinvar's SB-1300 manual says plainly not to use it. Trane's Table 9 lists cellular core PVC (ASTM F891) and cellular core ABS (ASTM F628) among its materials. So cellular core is not banned everywhere and not allowed everywhere; it is allowed only where the manual for that appliance allows it. Check the pipe markings against the list in the manual before you glue anything.

Primer, cement and joints

Trane's guide says to clean the pipe and socket, apply primer to the socket and the pipe, apply cement, and hold the joint for 30 seconds, then wait 15 minutes before handling. It also says primer, cement and plastic parts must come from a single system maker. Lochinvar names ASTM D2564 cement for PVC and ASTM F493 for CPVC, and says to use only cleaners, primers and solvents approved for the materials being joined. Where PVC meets CPVC, it asks for an all-purpose cement suited to both. Pitch the vent back toward the appliance at least 1/4 inch per foot so condensate drains instead of pooling.

Terminations

Clearances to windows, doors and corners come from the maker's table and the gas code, not from this page. Many makers also set a height above snow; see High-Efficiency Furnace Vent Pipes Blocked by Snow or Ice. If a combustion air pipe is part of the job, see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters.

The old gray vent recall

Before today's listed systems, a gray or black plastic called high-temperature plastic vent (HTPV) was sold under names such as Plexvent and Ultravent. The Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall on February 24, 1998 of about 250,000 systems on gas and propane furnaces and boilers, saying the pipes could crack or separate at joints and leak carbon monoxide. In 2009 the agency announced changes to the remedy and noted that white PVC is not part of the recall. If you meet gray or black plastic stamped Plexvent or Ultravent, it is a safety issue to report to the owner. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Worked example: add up the equivalent length

Every elbow slows the flow like extra straight pipe, so makers give each fitting an equivalent length in feet. Use Trane's guide, Table 10, for model UC1B080A9421A. For 3-inch pipe it allows 100 equivalent feet per pipe in direct vent, and 80 feet in nondirect vent where only the exhaust is piped. The guide says a short-radius 90 equals 10 feet of 3-inch pipe, a long-radius 90 equals 7 feet, two 45s equal one long 90, and the termination tee counts toward the elbow total. The layout below is made up for the exercise. We count the termination tee as a short-radius 90, which is our assumption; follow your manual for the kit you use.

  1. Straight pipe: 35 ft
  2. Two short-radius 90s: 2 × 10 = 20 ft
  3. One long-radius 90: 7 ft
  4. Two 45s (count as one long 90): 7 ft
  5. Termination tee, as a short-radius 90: 10 ft

Total: 35 + 20 + 7 + 7 + 10 = 79 equivalent feet for the exhaust pipe. Against the 3-inch direct vent limit of 100 ft, that leaves 21 ft to spare. If the same appliance is nondirect vent, the limit is 80 ft and the layout passes by only 1 ft. Count the intake pipe separately against its own limit.

Now try the same layout in 2-inch pipe. Short-radius 90s are 8 ft and long-radius 90s are 5 ft in that size: 35 + 16 + 5 + 5 + 8 = 69 ft. The 2-inch direct vent limit is 50 ft, so it fails by 19 ft. Pipe size is a real design choice, not a detail.

Field card: Plastic Vent Pipe for Furnaces and Boilers

The appliance maker picks the pipe, the glue and the length.

  • Allowed materials and temperature limits come from the appliance manual; makers differ.
  • Trane example: PVC 158°F, CPVC 212°F, polypropylene 230°F.
  • Some boilers need CPVC or stainless for the first section off the appliance.
  • Cellular core pipe: allowed in one maker's table, banned in another's. Read the manual.
  • Polypropylene is not glued. Use the same maker's system, gaskets and parts.
  • Prime, cement, hold 30 seconds, wait before handling (per Trane).
  • Add equivalent feet for every fitting and compare to the manual's limit for that pipe size.
  • Gray or black Plexvent or Ultravent pipe was recalled in 1998.
