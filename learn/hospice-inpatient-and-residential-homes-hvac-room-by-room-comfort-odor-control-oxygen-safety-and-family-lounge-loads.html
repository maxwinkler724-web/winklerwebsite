Hospice and Residential Home HVAC: Room Comfort, Odor Control, Oxygen Safety and Family Lounges

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hospice unit or small residential home is a place where people are often frail, often cold, and sometimes on oxygen, while family members sit up through the night. The HVAC job is quiet, steady and gentle. This lesson covers what a facility tech should check room by room, with one worked example for oxygen concentrator heat and soiled utility exhaust.

What the federal hospice rule says about the environment

The federal rule for hospices that provide inpatient care in their own facility is 42 CFR 418.110. As we read it on eCFR, it requires the hospice to keep patients comfortable, clean and protected, and to maintain a safe physical environment free of hazards for patients, staff and visitors. It requires procedures for controlling light, temperature and ventilation or air exchanges throughout the hospice. It does not give a numeric temperature range in the text we read. It also asks for a home-like atmosphere, private space for patient and family visiting, accommodations for family to stay overnight, and visitors at any hour. Patient rooms must be at or above grade, hold no more than two patients, and give at least 100 square feet per patient in a single room (80 in a double). That is why the family lounge and a room that works at 3 a.m. are part of the HVAC job, not extras.

For ventilation numbers, designers often use ASHRAE Standard 170 (Ventilation of Health Care Facilities). We read a public addendum to the 2017 edition, which lists hospice facilities as a residential health care type. Its resident room row shows 2 outdoor and 2 total air changes per hour with a 70 to 75 F design range, and its soiled utility row shows negative pressure and 10 total air changes per hour. The standard itself is copyrighted, so we name it in plain text only. Your state may have adopted a different edition, so confirm with the engineer of record.

Room by room: each patient gets a say

Patients feel cold at temperatures that suit staff, and two family members in the same room may not agree. That is why we like a thermostat or local control in every patient room rather than one sensor for a whole wing. This is our own field practice, not a code rule. In practice:

  • Confirm each room's sensor sits on an interior wall, away from the bed headwall, sunny glass and the supply air stream.
  • Use gentle moves. Make small setpoint changes and avoid dumping cold air across a bed. Check diffuser throw and any draft with your hand or a smoke pencil.
  • Check the quiet side: loose panels, worn fan bearings and rattling duct covers are noticeable when a room is silent. See HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings for how noise ratings work.
  • Ask staff which rooms get complaints before you pick a room to test. Nurses know.

Soiled utility and odor control

Odor control is about where air goes. CDC's Appendix B on air (a reprint of older hospital design tables) and an Oregon state hospital table both show the soiled workroom or soiled utility row as 10 total air changes per hour, air moving into the room, exhausted directly outdoors, and not recirculated by room units. The Oregon table also carries a note that air from areas with contamination or odor problems is exhausted outdoors. The ASHRAE 170 addendum we read agrees on 10 total, with 2 of those outdoor. The idea is simple: the room stays slightly negative to the corridor so smells flow in, not out.

  • Check that the exhaust fan runs and that the backdraft damper opens fully.
  • Check the door closes tightly and that a transfer grille or door undercut lets air in. A tissue at the gap should lean into the room.
  • Check that the exhaust discharge is far from any outdoor air intake and window. (Our own practice.)
  • Do not add a recirculating fan or air purifier as a fix for a dead exhaust fan.

For how pressure monitors and checks work in hospital rooms, see Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare.

Oxygen: heat, flame and clearance

Oxygen does not burn, but it makes other things burn faster and hotter. CDC reported 38 deaths from fires caused by smoking during long-term oxygen therapy in four states from 2000 to 2007, and the featured fire started when a visitor smoked while a concentrator was running. Oxygen can saturate clothing, fabric and hair. The report states it acts as an accelerant. A facility tech cannot fix smoking, but can keep heat away from the bed.

New York State Department of Health patient guidance says to stay at least five feet from gas stoves, candles, lighted fireplaces and other heat sources, and to keep oxygen cylinders at least eight feet from heaters and heat producing or electrical appliances. It also says cylinders should be upright, secured to a fixed object or stand, and kept in a well-ventilated area, not in closets or behind curtains. Applying that to HVAC:

  • Keep portable heaters, heat lamps and warming devices out of the oxygen area. Do not leave a loose heater beside a bed to answer a cold complaint.
  • Keep baseboard, fan coil and PTAC heat outputs from blowing straight onto a cylinder rack.
  • No open flame, torch, or spark work in a room where oxygen is in use. Follow the facility's hot work procedure and move oxygen first. (Our own practice.)
  • Concentrators need free air around them. Read the maker's manual for the required clearance rather than guessing.

Family lounges and kitchens

A family lounge is a small gathering room with a kitchenette, often used at odd hours and sometimes by many people at once. Give it its own thermostat so it does not drag a wing off setpoint. A kitchenette used for coffee and microwave meals still needs a working range hood or exhaust if it has cooking appliances, and a closed door should not leave smells in the corridor. See Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics for how outdoor air relates to the number of people in a room.

After-hours calls, handled gently

Night calls are often a comfort complaint, such as a cold room or a noisy unit. Call ahead, do not knock on a door of an occupied room unless asked, and speak quietly. Ask the nurse for a safe time to enter. Fix what you can from the corridor or ceiling space and return for the rest in daylight. This is our own advice, and the facility's plan comes first.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions, not from a source: a 12-room wing, each room 220 sq ft, one oxygen concentrator per room drawing an example 350 W (check the maker's nameplate for real values), and we assume all of that power ends up as heat in the room. EIA states 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu, so 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h.

  1. One concentrator: 350 W x 3.412 = 1,194 Btu/h.
  2. Twelve rooms: 12 x 350 W = 4,200 W; 4,200 x 3.412 = 14,330 Btu/h, about 14,300 Btu/h of heat across the wing.
  3. Soiled utility, 80 sq ft with a 9 ft ceiling: volume = 80 x 9 = 720 cu ft.
  4. At the cited 10 total air changes per hour: 720 x 10 = 7,200 cu ft/h.
  5. Divide by 60 min/h: 7,200 / 60 = 120 cfm of exhaust.
  6. Outdoor air share at 2 ACH: 720 x 2 / 60 = 24 cfm.

Reading it: about 1,200 Btu/h is a small load per room, but it is steady and it is added to what the room already gains. The 120 cfm is a minimum to check against a measured value. If a measured flow reads far less, suspect a closed damper, a failed fan or a clogged grille.

Field card: Hospice and Residential Home HVAC

Quiet, steady, one thermostat per room, exhaust that works, heat kept off the oxygen.

  • 42 CFR 418.110: control light, temperature and ventilation; keep patients comfortable; home-like setting; family may stay overnight.
  • Soiled utility: negative, 10 total ACH in the tables we read, exhausted outdoors, no recirculation.
  • Our worked example: 80 sq ft x 9 ft x 10 ACH / 60 = 120 cfm.
  • Oxygen: 5 ft from heat sources and flames, cylinders 8 ft from heaters, upright and secured (NY DOH guidance).
  • Twelve 350 W concentrators (assumed) = about 14,300 Btu/h.
  • Night calls: quiet, ask staff first, minimal disturbance.
