Steam Traps Explained

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Steam does its useful work by turning back into water. A steam trap helps make sure that the water leaves, and the steam stays. This lesson explains the job and the common types in general terms.

The problem a trap solves

When steam gives up its heat in a heating coil, process equipment or a pipe, some of it turns back into liquid water called condensate. If condensate stays in the line, it blocks heat transfer, can cause noise and damage, and can reduce performance. But if steam simply blows out with the water, the plant wastes the fuel it spent making that steam.

What a trap does

A steam trap is an automatic valve that lets condensate and air out of a steam system while holding live steam back. Each trap senses something about what is reaching it and opens or closes in response. A well-working trap quietly drains condensate and then stays out of the way.

Common families of traps

Manufacturers describe traps by how they sense the difference between steam and condensate. In general terms:

  • Mechanical traps use the difference in density. A float or bucket rises or falls with the liquid and moves the valve.
  • Thermostatic traps use temperature. An element reacts when the fluid cools slightly below steam temperature, which tells the valve that condensate has arrived.
  • Thermodynamic traps use differences in how steam and condensate flow, and the resulting pressure effects, to move a disc.

Each family has strengths. The right choice depends on the application, the load, and the manufacturer's selection guidance. This lesson does not pick one for you.

Failing open and failing closed

  • A trap that fails open lets steam blow through. It wastes energy, can overload the condensate return, and can cause pressure problems downstream.
  • A trap that fails closed or plugs holds condensate back. Equipment may heat poorly, and standing water can cause water hammer.

Signs of trouble

Technicians and plant staff watch for clues such as cold spots where equipment should be hot, unusual hammering or banging, visible steam venting at a discharge, equipment that struggles to reach its target, and a trap that is hot on both sides but never seems to cycle. Surveys of traps often use listening devices, temperature tools or other test methods. How to test a given trap safely is a job for trained people following site procedures and the manufacturer's directions.

Safety around traps

Traps live in hot, pressurized systems. Steam and hot condensate can cause serious burns, often with little warning. Never open, disassemble or test a trap on a live line unless you are trained and authorized, and the proper isolation and lockout steps have been followed. Wear the protective gear your site requires. Hot discharge points and condensate lines are hazards even when no steam is visible.

Why upkeep pays off

The U.S. Department of Energy points to inspecting and repairing steam traps as a basic way for plants to save energy. DOE Steam Tip Sheet #1 says a system left unmaintained for three to five years may have 15 to 30 percent of its traps failed, while a regularly maintained one should have under 5 percent leaking. Its example: one trap stuck open with a 1/8-inch opening on a 150 psig line loses about 75.8 pounds of steam an hour, which at $10 per 1,000 pounds is roughly $6,640 a year. The tip sheet suggests checking high-pressure traps (150 psig and up) weekly to monthly, medium-pressure (30 to 150 psig) monthly to quarterly, and low-pressure (under 30 psig) annually. Those are the sheet's suggestions, so follow your own plant's schedule. A routine inspection and repair program is both a safety measure and a cost saver.

Field card: Steam Traps Explained

A trap drains condensate and holds back live steam.

  • Condensate must leave; live steam should stay in.
  • Traps sense by density, temperature or flow behavior.
  • Failed-open traps waste energy; failed-closed traps cause cold spots and hammer.
  • Hot steam and condensate cause serious burns. Follow lockout and site rules.
  • Regular inspection and repair saves fuel.
