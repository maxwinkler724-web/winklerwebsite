Makeup Water Meter on a Closed Loop: Catching Hidden Leaks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A closed hot-water or chilled-water loop is supposed to hold the same water for years. When it keeps asking for more, water is going somewhere, and the fresh water coming in is doing damage of its own. A simple meter on the fill line turns that quiet problem into a number you can read on a Monday.

Why a closed loop needs almost no makeup

Once a loop is filled and purged, the water just circulates. Temperature swings make it expand and contract, which the expansion tank absorbs, but nothing is consumed. A federal health agency's facilities bulletin on closed-loop treatment says these systems should need little makeup water, typically under 5 percent of total system volume per year. A state university's facility water-treatment specification sets a tighter target of 1 percent of system volume per year or less. Treat those as two published yardsticks, not a code requirement. Either way, a healthy loop uses a small fraction of its own volume in twelve months.

What fresh water brings in

Every gallon added is a gallon of untreated water. The facilities bulletin lists minerals, dissolved gases, and other impurities as things makeup water adds, and says they can worsen corrosion, scaling, and fouling. It also notes that dissolved oxygen can enter either through makeup water or through system leaks, and that in steel pipe oxygen reacts with iron to form rust. A second bulletin in the same series adds that oxygen in makeup water can cause flash rusting on carbon steel. The same bulletin says a loop's water quality can be compared with the makeup water: iron or copper readings higher than the makeup water's point to active corrosion in the loop.

In plain terms: a leak costs you the water, then the replacement water costs you the pipes, the heat exchanger surfaces, and the treatment chemicals that were diluted. Our own note: scale from the added minerals is worst on the hottest surfaces, which is why a leaking hot-water loop hurts the boiler. For the chemistry and the dosing side, see the related lesson on closed-loop water treatment.

Put a meter on the fill line

A federal energy department best-practice page for boiler systems says to install meters on makeup lines to recirculating closed-loop heating systems "so that leaks can be accounted for." The university specification goes further and asks that chilled-water and hot-water makeup lines each have a water meter. A basic cold-water meter downstream of the backflow device and upstream of the fill valve does the job. We cover the valve and backflow device in the related lesson on the boiler fill valve, so we do not repeat them here.

Read and log it weekly

  1. Pick one day and time each week and read the meter at the same point in the routine.
  2. Write the date, the reading, and the gallons used since the last entry.
  3. Note anything unusual that week: a repair, a drain-down, a zone that was opened up, or a cold snap.
  4. Look at the trend, not one number. A flat log is good. A staircase that climbs every week is a leak.

Our own tip: write down the system volume on the log sheet too, so anyone can turn gallons into a percent at a glance. A fill that happens right after a repair is expected and should be noted so it is not mistaken for a leak.

What a rising reading points to

These are our own field-reasoning starting points, not a sourced ranking. The facilities bulletin itself names minor leaks in mechanical seals and expansion tank overflow as common causes.

  • Relief valve weeping. Look at the discharge pipe for staining, moisture, or drips. A relief valve that drips often points to pressure that is too high or a valve that is worn; see the related relief valve lesson.
  • Leaking fittings and valve packings. Check unions, flanges, air vents, drain valves, and packing nuts for crust, rust, or damp insulation.
  • Pump seals. Look under the pump for drips or mineral trails at the seal.
  • Buried or in-slab piping. No visible drip anywhere, steady makeup, and perhaps a wet spot or warm floor patch. These are the hardest leaks to find and the most costly to ignore.
  • Coil leaks. Water getting into an air handler pan, a damp duct, or a coil with a stained header. In a chilled-water loop, a coil leak may show as extra condensate-pan water that is not condensate.
  • Expansion tank overflow. Per the facilities bulletin, a common cause of loss in some systems.

Isolating zones to narrow it down

If the total is high, split the loop. Close the valves that isolate one zone or branch, leave the rest running, and compare the meter over a day or two. If the makeup rate drops to normal, the leak is in the zone you closed. Repeat, closing different zones, until you have a short list. Our own cautions: never isolate a boiler or heat source from its relief valve and expansion tank, make sure every closed section still has a way to relieve thermal expansion, and follow the site's lockout rules. A pressure drop test on one isolated zone, done by a qualified person, can confirm the finding.

The auto-fill trade-off

An automatic fill valve keeps pressure up so the system keeps running. That is its job. The trade-off is that it also hides a leak: the pressure gauge looks normal, the zones stay warm, and the only evidence is the meter. Our own view: if a system has an auto-fill, the meter is the only way to know it is working overtime. Some sites add a high-flow alarm or a manual fill for this reason. That is a design decision for the people responsible for the building, not something to add on your own.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. Assume a loop holds 300 gallons and the log shows 45 gallons of makeup over 30 days. All three values are assumptions made up for teaching.

  1. Gallons per day: 45 gal ÷ 30 days = 1.5 gal/day.
  2. Percent of system volume per month: 45 ÷ 300 = 0.15, or 15 percent per 30 days.
  3. Annual makeup at that rate: 1.5 gal/day × 365 days = 547.5 gal.
  4. Full system turnovers per year: 547.5 ÷ 300 = about 1.8 turnovers per year.

Compare that with the published yardsticks above. Under 5 percent per year would be 0.05 × 300 = 15 gallons a year, and 1 percent would be 3 gallons a year. This loop is replacing its water about 1.8 times a year, roughly 36 times the 5 percent figure (547.5 ÷ 15 = 36.5). That is a leak worth chasing, with fresh oxygen and minerals arriving daily. The facilities bulletin says losing 25 percent of volume per month is not uncommon, so this example is not extreme, which is exactly the point.

Field card: Makeup water meter on a closed loop

A closed loop should barely drink.

  • Published yardsticks: under 5 percent of volume per year, or 1 percent or less.
  • Fresh water adds oxygen and minerals, so leaks also cause corrosion and scale.
  • Read the fill-line meter weekly at the same time and log the gallons.
  • Convert to gallons per day and percent of volume per month.
  • Check relief valve, fittings, pump seals, coils, and expansion tank; suspect buried piping if nothing is visible.
  • Isolate zones one at a time, never cutting off the relief valve or expansion tank.
  • An auto-fill hides leaks from the gauge, not from the meter.
