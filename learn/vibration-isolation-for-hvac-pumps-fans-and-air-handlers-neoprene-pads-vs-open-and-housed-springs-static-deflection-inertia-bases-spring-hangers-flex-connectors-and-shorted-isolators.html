Vibration Isolation for HVAC Pumps, Fans and Air Handlers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every fan and pump pushes on whatever carries it, once per turn of the shaft. Isolation is how we keep that push from becoming noise and shaking in the rooms below. This lesson compares pads, open springs and housed springs, shows the one formula that sets the choice, and lists what we check in the field.

Check the drawings first

Check the drawings first. The isolator selection, deflection and adjustment come from the job specification, the equipment instructions and the isolator drawings. This page is general education built from public specifications and university handouts read in 2026.

What isolation is, and what it is not

Isolation is a soft support under the machine. A different lesson covers how we measure vibration at the bearings; see Vibration Analysis Basics for Fans and Pumps. Noise targets and silencers are in HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings, and small refrigeration compressors have their own mounts, covered in Compressor Mounts. Here we focus on pumps, fans and air handlers, and on the support types and connections that make isolation work. A shorter overview with a fan example is in Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors for Pumps and Fans; this lesson adds restrained springs, inertia bases, hangers and a pad comparison.

Three kinds of support

Neoprene pads. Flat or ribbed rubber layers. A university master specification we read lists a ribbed pad type sized for 20 to 40 psi that deflects only 0.12 to 0.16 in, and uses it for a small packaged pump. Pads are cheap and quiet at high frequency, but they sag very little.

Open spring isolators. A free-standing steel coil on a base plate, often with a thin rubber pad under it. The same specification describes a type that is adjustable and welded for stability, and requires the horizontal stiffness to be at least 1.2 times the static deflection divided by the working height, so the spring does not tip. Coils touching in operation are not acceptable.

Housed or restrained springs. The spring sits inside a steel housing or enclosure. The specification describes a version whose restraining bolts stay disengaged in normal operation but stop the equipment from rising when it is drained of water, such as a cooling tower, and a telescoping housed type that limits sideways motion. Restraint is for wind, seismic and drain-down loads. It is not meant to touch while the machine runs.

Static deflection sets the isolation

Static deflection is how far the support squashes under the weight it carries. For a spring and a weight, the natural frequency is the square root of stiffness over mass, and the static deflection is the weight over the stiffness (university vibrations handout). Combine them and the weight cancels: natural frequency is proportional to one over the square root of deflection. With the deflection in inches and gravity at 386 in/s², that works out to about 188 divided by the square root of the deflection, in cycles per minute. That arithmetic is ours (see the worked example); the weight does not matter, only how far the support sags.

Isolation only begins when the shaft frequency is more than the square root of 2 (about 1.41) times the natural frequency. At exactly 1.0 we get resonance. The handout shows transmitted force equals the input force at a ratio of 1.41 and drops after that. So we want the ratio well above 1.41, and the way to raise it is to pick a larger static deflection, which means a softer support.

Our own worked example: a 900 rpm fan

Our own example with stated assumptions. Assume a fan at 900 rpm, so it pushes 900 times per minute (900 cpm). Assume two choices: open springs with 1.00 in static deflection, or a pad with 0.15 in deflection (inside the 0.12 to 0.16 in range above). Assume an undamped single-mass model, the simple form shown in a university optics tutorial: transmitted fraction T = 1 divided by (r² minus 1), where r is shaft frequency over natural frequency.

  1. Springs: natural frequency = 188 / √1.00 = 188 cpm.
  2. Check against the 1.41 rule: 1.41 × 188 = 265 cpm. The fan at 900 cpm is far above that.
  3. Ratio r = 900 / 188 = 4.79. Then r² = 22.9, and T = 1 / (22.9 − 1) = 1 / 21.9 = 0.046. About 5 percent of the force is transmitted.
  4. Pad: natural frequency = 188 / √0.15 = 188 / 0.387 = 485 cpm.
  5. Ratio r = 900 / 485 = 1.86, which is above 1.41 but not by much. Then r² = 3.44, and T = 1 / (3.44 − 1) = 1 / 2.44 = 0.41. About 41 percent is transmitted, roughly nine times the spring case.

Our own check: the pad passes the 1.41 test but leaves little margin, and a slower fan would drop toward resonance. At 600 rpm the same pad has r = 600 / 485 = 1.24, which is below 1.41, so it would amplify. Real machines have damping and flexible floors, so this is a classroom comparison, not a design.

Inertia bases and housekeeping pads

A base-mounted pump has a motor and pump on one steel frame. A federal design guide calls for concrete inertia bases under all pumps, axial fans above 300 rpm and centrifugal fans above 50 hp. The extra mass is a steel form filled with concrete and rebar, with the isolators attached to it. The base ties the pump, motor and suction elbow into one rigid block, lowers the center of gravity, and keeps the machine from rocking at startup. Details from the university specification: the form is at least 6 in deep and at least one twelfth of the longest span, it extends at least 4 in beyond the equipment base, and it leaves at least 2 in of clearance below when loaded. It sits over a concrete housekeeping pad. For floor-mounted pumps it calls for 1.0 in deflection springs up to 5 hp and 2.0 in at 7.5 hp and over.

Spring hangers for suspended fans and piping

Suspended air handlers and fans hang from rods with a spring hanger: a steel box holding a coil, sometimes with a rubber element in series. The specification lists hanger types from rubber only (about 0.5 in) to a coil plus rubber element (about 2.5 in total). For piping, the first two hangers next to the equipment match the equipment deflection (capped at 2.0 in), and the rest use at least 0.75 in. A federal design guide says piping 2 in and larger hung below noise-sensitive spaces should get spring or rubber hangers, and hangers nearest the equipment should match the equipment isolators.

Flexible connections on pipe and duct

A pipe that runs from a floating pump to a fixed wall carries vibration right around the springs. We use flexible pipe connectors close to the pump on suction and discharge. The specification rated one flanged type to 220 psi and 220°F and called for control units on larger sizes to limit thrust elongation, so do not remove the control rods. For duct, the federal guide asks for 6 in flexible connectors between ductwork and rotating equipment. Pipe thermal growth can load these too; see Pipe Thermal Expansion.

Why a shorted isolator passes vibration

A spring is soft only if nothing stiff bridges it. A bolt that runs from the base to the slab, a rigid conduit, a pipe resting on steel, a leftover shipping block or debris under the base each gives the vibration a hard path past the springs. The specification we read says units must not be short-circuited by other contacts or bearing points, and that temporary space blocks must be removed after installation. We think of the machine as a boat: any line to the dock that is tight carries the motion across.

Field checks

  • Spring height. Compare each spring to the drawing. All springs on one machine should sit at about the same height with the machine floating.
  • Level. A tilted base means uneven loading. The specification allows shimming where leveling devices cannot spread the load.
  • Shorting. Look and feel for gaps around the base, bolts that touch, conduit without a flexible section, pipes on rigid rests, and blocks left in place. Coils touching is a defect.
  • Restraint bolts. Restraining bolts should show clearance while the machine runs.
  • Worn pads. Cracked, flattened or oil-soaked rubber has lost its deflection.
  • Flex connectors. Cracks, bulges, collapsed duct fabric, or connectors stretched tight.
  • After a speed change. A new drive speed changes the ratio; redo the arithmetic.

Field card: Vibration Isolation for HVAC Pumps, Fans and Air Handlers

Soft support, no hard path, big gap between natural and shaft frequency.

  • Natural frequency in cpm is about 188 divided by the square root of static deflection in inches.
  • Isolation starts above a ratio of 1.41; aim well above it.
  • 1.00 in springs give 188 cpm; a 0.15 in pad gives about 485 cpm.
  • Inertia bases go under pumps; spring hangers carry suspended fans and nearby pipe.
  • Flexible connectors on pipe and duct at the machine.
  • Check spring height, level, shorting, restraint clearance and worn pads.
