Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Walk a steam plant and you will see several kinds of traps. They are not interchangeable. Each senses condensate differently, and each fits some jobs better than others.

Three families

Standards group steam traps by what they sense, and a trap-sizing overview uses the same three families:

  • Mechanical traps sense the density difference between steam and condensate. Float and inverted bucket designs belong here.
  • Thermostatic traps sense temperature: condensate cooler than steam, or air. Bimetallic and balanced-pressure types belong here.
  • Thermodynamic traps sense the speed and pressure behavior of flash steam at an orifice. Disc and piston types belong here.

A federal technology review gives the same plain split and adds that failures come two ways. A trap that fails open blows steam. One that fails closed floods the equipment.

Mechanical: float and inverted bucket

A manufacturer describes a float trap that discharges condensate as soon as it arrives, keeping the heat transfer surface in contact with steam and keeping water hammer risk down. It is that maker's first choice for steam-using equipment, distribution lines and high-temperature tracing. TLV makes float traps, so read its rankings as one maker's view, not a neutral standard. The same source says inverted bucket traps bleed some steam through a small vent hole, remove only limited air and can start up slowly. It says they cycle intermittently and can be damaged by freezing.

Thermostatic

These are valued for air venting. A thermostatic element is open at startup and lets air out. They are commonly used as air vents in steam systems. On tracer lines, a thermostatic trap holds back some condensate in the line, which extracts more heat from it before discharge. That holding back is a drawback for heat exchangers, where the backed-up condensate covers heat transfer area. The sizing overview says thermostatic traps hold condensate back, which reduces heat transfer area, and says float-thermostatic traps that drain continuously at steam temperature are usually preferred there. A manufacturer does not recommend thermostatic traps as a first choice for drainage.

Thermodynamic

The disc trap is compact and works over a wide pressure range. The sizing overview says thermodynamic traps suit small-capacity drip points, frequent pressure changes and low-to-high pressure mains. Cautions from the sources:

  • Oversizing shortens the cycle time and increases wear.
  • The manufacturer says vertical mounting can shorten disc trap life through extra cycling, while allowing freeze risk to drop.
  • Rapid pressure rise can cause air binding, so a separate thermostatic air vent may be needed.

Matching the job

  • Steam main drip points: thermodynamic is common for small loads. One float-trap maker ranks its float trap first and the disc second on ordinary mains, but ranks the disc first for extreme-pressure mains.
  • Heat exchangers and process equipment: mechanical, usually float with a built-in thermostatic vent, for large loads where efficiency matters.
  • Tracer lines: bimetallic traps extract more heat from condensate. Thermodynamic traps are favored where reliability without overheating matters.
  • Air venting: thermostatic.

These are the sources' general guides, and some come from makers with a product to sell. Your design may differ.

Selection cautions from the sizing overview

  • Size on actual conditions. Traps sized on the running load can waterlog at startup, and traps sized on startup load can waste steam when running.
  • Avoid group trapping. The overview says it is rarely successful and normally causes waterlogging and loss of output. Give each steam space its own trap.
  • Plan for zero or negative pressure difference, common in control-valve-fed heat exchangers. A pump trap may be needed, and check valves help stop reverse flow in common returns.
  • Most trouble is from installation rather than trap choice: pipe sizing, air venting, strainers, slope and condensate lift.

Why it matters for maintenance

Knowing the type tells you what a normal sound and temperature look like, and what failure to expect. A cold bucket trap and a cold disc trap can have different causes. For test methods, see Steam Trap Testing.

Replace traps only with the type and size the system designer or manufacturer specifies. Steam is hot and under pressure, so follow lockout, isolation and permit rules before opening anything.

Field card: Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits

Match the trap to the job, then keep it clean and sized right.

  • Mechanical senses density, thermostatic senses temperature, thermodynamic senses flash steam.
  • Float-thermostatic is common on heat exchangers; thermostatic excels at air venting.
  • Disc traps are compact and cope with wide pressure ranges; do not oversize them.
  • Do not group-trap. One steam space, one trap.
  • Most trapping trouble is installation, not trap choice.
