Thread Sealant on Water and Hydronic Threaded Joints

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most small hydronic piping ends in a threaded joint: a boiler drain, a valve, a union, a gauge tapping. These joints leak when the sealant is wrong, the wrap is backwards, or someone tries to crank a bad joint tight. This lesson covers how a tapered thread seals, what the sealant does, how to wrap it, and when to take the joint apart and start over.

Why tapered threads need sealant

Tapered pipe threads get narrower toward the end of the pipe, so the threads wedge into the fitting as you turn. A federal standards handbook table lists a taper of 1 in 16 (0.75 inch per foot of length) and a 60 degree thread angle for the American standard taper pipe thread. The same table lists 14 threads per inch for both 1/2 inch and 3/4 inch pipe sizes. Even a wedged thread leaves a thin spiral gap along the flanks, and a university open textbook for building maintenance says that pipe threads by themselves are not enough to seal a pressurized system, so a sealant should always be used (except on the dry-seal type of thread). The sealant fills that spiral gap and lubricates the joint so the threads can pull tight. It does not hold pressure by itself: the wedged metal does the work and the sealant closes the leak path.

Tape, paste, or both

PTFE tape. The textbook says tape goes on the male threads, and notes that standard white tape is not for gas lines. A different, gas-rated tape is used there; see our gas pipe thread lesson. For water and hydronic joints, use the tape or paste that the fitting maker and the fluid allow, which matters with glycol mixes and plastic parts.

Paste (pipe compound). It goes on the male threads with a brush. The same textbook warns that some compounds are not for plastic pipe and says to check the maker's directions.

Both. The textbook says many makers claim there is little or no benefit to using both at once. We agree with the simple rule: pick one, and keep the other off unless the maker of the fitting says otherwise. A state university's utility plant design standard simply says to apply appropriate tape or thread compound to external pipe threads, unless dry-seal threading is specified. Sealant goes on the male thread only, which is also how a state building-codes document words it for threaded joints in medical gas piping.

Wrapping the tape

  • Direction. Look at the pipe end and wrap in the direction the fitting will turn on, so tightening pulls the tape into the threads instead of unwinding it. A public-health guidance sheet describes wrapping clockwise on a shower arm, which is the same idea seen from the pipe end for a right-hand thread.
  • How many wraps. The two public sources we read differ: the textbook gives three to four wraps, stretched and overlapped evenly across the threads, and the health guidance gives two or three. Follow the tape maker's instructions if they print a count. Our own rule is to start with the lower number on small pipe and add a wrap only if the joint weeps on test.
  • Clean first. Remove old tape and dry the threads, as the health sheet says, before wrapping new tape.
  • Our practice: keep the first thread bare. Start the tape one thread back from the pipe end. Tape that overhangs the end can shred and ride into the system, where it fouls strainers, valve seats and small orifices.

What never gets sealant

A flare, a compression fitting and a union face seal by contact: metal to metal, a gasket or a cone. Their threads only pull the sealing parts together. Sealant on those faces can hold them apart or carry grit, and it can turn a good seal into a leak. Wrap and dope tapered pipe threads only. See flare fittings for how a flare differs. This is our reasoning from how those joints work, and the fitting maker's instructions come first.

Hand-tight plus turns, and the backup wrench

Thread the male end in by hand until it stops. The federal handbook table lists the hand-tight engagement length as 0.320 inch for 1/2 inch pipe and 0.339 inch for 3/4 inch pipe, and lists a wrench makeup length of 3 threads for the internal thread. A "hand-tight plus turns" rule uses that idea: count turns past hand-tight with a wrench, rather than going by feel. Which number of turns suits which fitting depends on the fitting and its maker, so use the maker's number when there is one.

Hold the fitting you are not turning with a second wrench, so the force does not twist a valve body, a union or an existing joint behind it. Wrench choice and the backup rule are in our pipe wrench lesson.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed inputs. Assumptions: a 1/2 inch tapered thread with 14 threads per inch and a hand-tight engagement of 0.320 inch (both from the federal handbook table), and our own assumed shop rule of hand-tight plus 2 turns.

  1. Thread pitch: 1 / 14 = 0.0714 inch per turn.
  2. Threads engaged at hand-tight: 0.320 / 0.0714 = 4.48 threads.
  3. Advance from 2 turns: 2 x 0.0714 = 0.143 inch.
  4. Engagement after the rule: 0.320 + 0.143 = 0.463 inch, or 0.463 / 0.0714 = 6.5 threads.
  5. Compare to 3 turns: 3 x 0.0714 = 0.2143 inch, the same as the handbook's 3-thread wrench makeup length. Engagement would be 0.320 + 0.214 = 0.534 inch, about 7.5 threads.

The 2-turn rule stops about one turn short of the handbook's 3-thread wrench makeup. If it still weeps on test, the next step is to check the joint, not to keep turning past the maker's limit. Each extra turn adds only 0.0714 inch of engagement but a lot of stress in the fitting wall.

Leak testing a fill

Test before you insulate or close walls. A university design standard for utility plant hydronic piping says to hydrostatically test at not less than 1.5 times the working pressure without exceeding the rating of any component, hold the pressure at least 10 minutes before checking joints, fix leaks and repeat, and leave joints uncovered during the test. For small residential loops, fill and bleed air, pressurize to the maker's stated test pressure, and look at every threaded joint with a dry paper towel. A weep shows as a dark spot. Pressure testing a new loop is covered in our new hydronic piping lesson, and the fill valve in the boiler fill valve lesson.

When to remake the joint

  • It weeps after the planned turns: stop, depressurize, drain below the joint, and take it apart.
  • The threads are chewed, corroded or cross-threaded. The university design standard says not to use pipe or fittings with corroded or damaged threads.
  • The tape is bunched or the paste is lumpy: clean both threads, then reapply.
  • The fitting is cracked or flared out at the mouth. Replace it. More force only cracks it.

Never solve a threaded leak with extra sealant on top of the bad joint. Soldered joints near threads need the fitting free of sealant and water; see our soft soldering lesson. The International Plumbing Code and the International Mechanical Code are model codes that your local jurisdiction may adopt in some edition; check the adopted edition and the local inspector for the threaded joint and test rules that apply to your job.

Field card: Thread Sealant on Water and Hydronic Threaded Joints

One sealant, male thread only, wrapped with the tightening direction.

  • Tapered pipe threads wedge tight, and sealant fills the spiral gap.
  • Pick tape or paste. Do not stack them unless the maker says to.
  • Wrap with the way the fitting turns on. Follow the tape maker's wrap count.
  • Keep the first thread bare so tape does not shred into the system.
  • No sealant on flare, compression or union faces.
  • Hand-tight, then a counted number of turns. Back up the other fitting.
  • Our own 1/2 in example: 14 threads per inch, 0.0714 in per turn, 2 turns adds 0.143 in.
  • Leak test with the joints uncovered. If it weeps, remake the joint.
