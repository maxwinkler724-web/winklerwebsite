Compressor Discharge Line Temperature as a Diagnostic

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Gauges tell you about pressure. A thermometer on the discharge line tells you how hard the compressor is working and how hot its valves and oil are getting. This lesson covers where to read discharge line temperature, what pushes it up or down, how to figure compression ratio in absolute pressure, and what the protection on modern scroll and inverter units does.

Check the maker's limits

Check the maker's limits. Every temperature limit below comes from a source we name, and limits differ by compressor model, refrigerant and application. Use the nameplate, the unit's service literature and the compressor maker's application data for the equipment in front of you. This page is general education.

Where to measure it

Discharge gas is superheated vapor, so there is no pressure-to-temperature chart that gives its temperature. You read it with a probe. Two trade-magazine articles by a retired HVACR instructor say to clamp an insulated thermistor or thermocouple on the bare discharge line a few inches from the compressor: one says about 1 to 3 inches, the other about 3 to 4 inches. A compressor maker's application guide for scroll heat pump compressors, in a protection design context, puts a fixed sensor 120 mm (about 4.7 inches, our conversion) from the compressor shell along the pipe, with the pipe and sensor insulated and the sensor held in a brazed copper sleeve with thermal compound. That guide gives a reason that applies to your clamp too: insulation and good contact keep room air from changing the reading.

Because spots differ, a single number is only useful if you read it the same way every time. Clean the pipe, press the probe firmly, wrap it, and note the distance. Then compare the reading with the same unit at known conditions, or with the maker's published limit measured at the maker's sensor location.

Why heat on the discharge side hurts

A conference paper from a compressor maker's chemist, hosted by Purdue, called the discharge valve leaf the hottest part of the compressor. It described a coke-like carbon deposit forming near the valve seats, and said that deposit can keep the valve from seating. A compressor maker's application guide says overheated oil "cooks," leaving carbon on valves, oil passages and filters, which lowers lubrication and leads to wear. The same guide says chemical reaction rates roughly double for every 10°C rise, so heat speeds up reactions among oil, refrigerant and any contaminants, and acids can form. A Purdue conference paper on an R-32 oil study found shaft roughness and bearing wear got worse in endurance tests run at higher discharge temperatures.

The line reading is lower than what the valve plate sees. The trade articles say the valve area can run 50 to 75°F hotter than the line, and they put a limit of 225°F on the discharge line. They also say oil can carbonize near the valve plate at those temperatures. Treat 225°F as the figure those authors teach, not as a rule for every compressor. A 1990 Purdue conference paper said gas in the cylinders should not exceed about 300°F for long life in the low-temperature R-22 units it studied. Oak Ridge National Laboratory reports say typical compressors tolerate discharge temperatures of about 230°F, while compressors built with heating-application features allow up to 280°F. The maker's guide lists maximum discharge gas temperatures of 121 to 140°C at the sensor (about 250 to 284°F, our conversion), depending on model and refrigerant. These numbers differ because they describe different compressors, locations and applications, which is why the maker's data for your unit wins.

What drives it up

The trade articles group the causes the same way, and so do the research papers once you read them closely:

  • High compression ratio. Either high condensing pressure, low suction pressure, or both. Oak Ridge reports name low suction pressure and a high compression ratio at low outdoor temperatures as the cause of very high discharge temperatures in cold-climate heat pumps.
  • High suction superheat. A starved evaporator from undercharge, a restricted liquid line, a plugged drier or a weak metering device sends hot, thin vapor to the compressor. A Purdue conference paper on compressor tests reported that higher superheat raises discharge temperature almost directly. Long or uninsulated suction lines through hot spaces add superheat too, per the trade articles.
  • Dirty condenser or poor airflow. The trade articles list dirty coils, failed fans, recirculated air and high ambient as head pressure causes. See Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.
  • Non-condensables. A 2021 Purdue conference paper says non-condensable gas adds its own partial pressure to the condenser pressure, which raises compressor work.
  • Overcharge in heating mode. Oak Ridge notes that heat pump charge is usually set for cooling, and overcharge in heating raises discharge temperature further.

What keeps it low

Cooler gas entering the compressor leaves cooler. Our reasoning, from the superheat result above, is that a very low superheat or liquid reaching the suction lowers the discharge line temperature. That is why a cool discharge line is not automatically good news. It can mean floodback, which has its own damage path; see Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging. A low compression ratio at mild conditions also keeps the line cooler. Some compressors are also cooled on purpose: the 1990 Purdue paper describes a demand cooling method that uses a discharge sensor and a small solenoid valve to inject liquid.

Compression ratio math

Compression ratio is absolute discharge pressure divided by absolute suction pressure. Absolute pressure is gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure, and we use 14.7 psi, the sea-level value, as an assumption. A Purdue university engineering text works its compressor examples in psia for this reason. If you divide gauge readings you get the wrong answer, and the error is worst at low suction pressure.

Worked example (our own numbers)

These are our own example readings for an R-410A system, not measurements. The discharge line temperatures are assumed so you can see the comparison; they are not from a source.

Case A, normal. Suction 118 psig, discharge 420 psig.

  1. Suction absolute: 118 + 14.7 = 132.7 psia.
  2. Discharge absolute: 420 + 14.7 = 434.7 psia.
  3. Ratio: 434.7 / 132.7 = 3.28.
  4. Assumed discharge line reading: 175°F, well under the 225°F figure the trade articles teach.

Case B, dirty condenser. Same suction, 118 psig. We assume the dirty coil pushes discharge to 520 psig.

  1. Suction absolute: 132.7 psia.
  2. Discharge absolute: 520 + 14.7 = 534.7 psia.
  3. Ratio: 534.7 / 132.7 = 4.03.
  4. Assumed discharge line reading: 205°F.

The ratio rose from 3.28 to 4.03, about 23 percent (4.03 / 3.28 = 1.23). Discharge gauge pressure rose only about 24 percent (520 / 420 = 1.24), but the line is now 30°F hotter in our example and closer to the trade limit. The lesson is the pattern: high head pressure with normal suction points to the condenser side, while a high line temperature with normal ratio points to superheat. A reading that is hot with a normal ratio sends you to the suction side: charge, restriction or metering device.

Discharge protection on scroll and inverter units

A compressor maker's application guide for scroll heat pump compressors says some models have no internal protection and need an external discharge sensor or thermostat, while others have internal sensors wired to a motor protection device, the drive or the system controller. The guide says the sensor trip point must allow for sensor tolerance and the temperature difference between gas and sensor, and that some models use fixed internal set points that cannot be adjusted. One example thermostat in the guide opens at 120°C and closes at 95°C. The guide stresses that this protection is a fallback for control failure, not a substitute for proper control of pressures and superheat, and that relying on it causes short cycling and poor performance. For drive-based units, see Inverter Drives and Variable Speed Compressors. If you see a discharge temperature fault code, find the cause before resetting; the oil side is covered in Compressor Oils, and general failure patterns in Compressor Basics and Failure Modes.

A 2014 conference paper from the University of Dayton, hosted by Purdue, notes that published performance data for compressors usually omits discharge line temperature, and that common thermodynamic models match real readings only modestly. That is another reason a measured reading on your unit, compared with the maker's guidance, beats a calculated one.

Field card: Discharge Line Temperature

Read it with a probe, a few inches out, insulated, every time the same way.

  • No P/T chart for discharge gas; use a probe on the bare line, insulated.
  • Trade articles: limit 225°F on the line; valve plate 50 to 75°F hotter. Maker limits vary.
  • Compression ratio = (discharge psig + 14.7) / (suction psig + 14.7).
  • Hot line: check ratio, then superheat, condenser, non-condensables, charge.
  • Cold line: check for floodback before relaxing.
  • Discharge protection is a backup, not a control.
