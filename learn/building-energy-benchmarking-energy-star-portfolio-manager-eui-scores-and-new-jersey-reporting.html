Building Energy Benchmarking: Portfolio Manager, EUI and NJ Reporting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A building's utility bills tell you what it spent. Benchmarking tells you whether that is a lot or a little for a building like it. This lesson covers the EPA ENERGY STAR terms (site energy, source energy, EUI and the 1 to 100 score), what New Jersey's benchmarking page says, and how a facility tech can use the numbers.

Check the current rules

Confirm with the BPU. Reporting rules, lists and deadlines change. Everything about New Jersey below comes from state web pages as we read them in 2026. Confirm the current requirements and your building's deadline with the NJ Board of Public Utilities (BPU) and New Jersey's Clean Energy Program before you rely on this page.

What benchmarking is

EPA's ENERGY STAR site describes benchmarking as measuring and comparing a building's energy use to similar buildings, to its own past use, or to a reference level. It says benchmarking turns the information on a utility bill into knowledge you can act on, and that tracking use over time helps you verify that savings stick and spot "snapback" when they fade. The tool for this is Portfolio Manager, which EPA offers. EPA's page says all you need are your bills and some basic information about the building.

Site energy, source energy and EUI

EPA defines site energy as the heat and electricity a building consumes as reflected in its utility bills. Source energy is the total raw fuel needed to operate the building, which adds back the losses from generating and delivering the energy. Portfolio Manager applies one national ratio per fuel to turn site into source. In EPA's Source Energy technical reference (August 2023), the U.S. ratios are 2.80 for grid-purchased electricity and 1.05 for natural gas. The reference says the ratios were last fully revised in 2018.

Energy use intensity (EUI) expresses energy use relative to size. EPA says it is expressed as energy per square foot per year, in kBtu per square foot, and is found by dividing the annual energy total by the gross floor area. A low EUI generally means good performance, although some building types are naturally more energy intensive. Portfolio Manager reports both site and source EUI, and EPA says source EUI is the basis for the ENERGY STAR score.

The 1 to 100 ENERGY STAR score

EPA says the score compares a building with similar buildings nationwide, based on how the building is used. A score of 50 represents median energy performance. A score of 75 or higher means the building is a top performer, and EPA says it may be eligible for ENERGY STAR certification, though a high score does not guarantee it. EPA also notes not every property type can receive a score. A university facilities page we read says classroom and laboratory buildings cannot, so that campus compares their EUI against national data instead.

What goes into Portfolio Manager

EPA's Get Started page says to collect the data first, using its data collection worksheet, which lists the information required for your specific building. It points to "add property" and "add meters" wizards. In plain terms, that means basic property facts including floor area and use, plus energy from your meters. We did not find the field-by-field list on the pages we read, so use EPA's worksheet. Accurate floor area matters because it sits in the denominator of every EUI.

Worked example: a 60,000 sq ft office

EPA's Thermal Energy Conversions reference (August 2015) lists 3.412 kBtu per kWh and 100 kBtu per therm of natural gas. The building and its yearly use are our own made-up numbers, and all arithmetic below is Our own.

  1. Electricity: 900,000 kWh × 3.412 = 3,070,800 kBtu. (Our own)
  2. Gas: 30,000 therms × 100 = 3,000,000 kBtu. (Our own)
  3. Total site energy: 3,070,800 + 3,000,000 = 6,070,800 kBtu. (Our own)
  4. Site EUI: 6,070,800 ÷ 60,000 sq ft = 101.18, about 101 kBtu/sq ft/yr. (Our own)
  5. Source electricity: 3,070,800 × 2.80 = 8,598,240 kBtu. (Our own)
  6. Source gas: 3,000,000 × 1.05 = 3,150,000 kBtu. (Our own)
  7. Total source energy: 8,598,240 + 3,150,000 = 11,748,240 kBtu. (Our own)
  8. Source EUI: 11,748,240 ÷ 60,000 = 195.8, about 196 kBtu/sq ft/yr. (Our own)

Notice that electricity is about half of site energy but about 73 percent of source energy (8,598,240 of 11,748,240; our own). That is the effect of the 2.80 ratio. This example does not produce an ENERGY STAR score, because the score also depends on building details and EPA's peer data, which we did not model.

New Jersey's benchmarking requirement

The NJ Clean Energy Program's benchmarking page says the program is mandatory for commercial buildings in New Jersey larger than 25,000 square feet, and that owners benchmark through EPA's Portfolio Manager. It says covered owners report energy and water use for the prior calendar year. For the 2026 season, the page showed data from January 1 through December 31, 2025, with a deadline of July 1, 2026. The BPU's 2022 news release, announcing approval of the program, said the owner or operator of each such building must report, with first submissions due October 1, 2023, for 2022 use. The Clean Energy Program page also says covered owners should receive an email or mailed notice containing a New Jersey Unique Building Identification (UBID).

The covered buildings page adds details: it says buildings classed 4A (commercial) or 4C for tax assessment are covered, condos and co-ops are not, and the 25,000 square foot test applies to the building as a whole. It lists exemption cases such as new buildings, demolitions and a building vacant for a full 365 days. It does not give a calendar deadline, only the deadline for each program year. The pages we read did not describe penalties. Because the program year and deadline can change, check with the BPU.

How facility techs use EUI trends

A single EUI is a snapshot. A trend is a diagnostic. Some ways techs can use it:

  • Compare year to year. If weather was similar and EUI rose, something changed: a schedule, a stuck valve, a failed economizer, a new load. (Our own reasoning.)
  • Watch for snapback. EPA's page names it as the loss of savings after a project. A rising EUI after a retrofit is a cue to recheck controls.
  • Split the fuels. Electric and gas are separate meters. A jump in gas points toward boilers, reheat or simultaneous heating and cooling, and a jump in electricity toward chillers, fans or after-hours loads. (Our own reasoning.)
  • Look at leaks and waste. The UNCG energy management page we read tied a gas increase to extra makeup water in the steam plant from leaks, and noted that without building-level steam and chilled water meters, costs get allocated by square footage and per-building problems cannot be isolated.
  • Compare to peers. DOE's Building Performance Database lets users build peer groups by building type, location, size, age and equipment and compare them statistically.

An EUI trend tells you where to look, not what is wrong. Field card: Energy Benchmarking

Bills in, kBtu, divide by square feet, watch the trend.

  • Site energy is what the bills show. Source energy adds generation and delivery losses.
  • EUI is annual kBtu divided by gross floor area, in kBtu/sq ft/yr.
  • 1 kWh = 3.412 kBtu. 1 therm = 100 kBtu (EPA).
  • ENERGY STAR score: 50 is median, 75 or higher is a top performer (EPA).
