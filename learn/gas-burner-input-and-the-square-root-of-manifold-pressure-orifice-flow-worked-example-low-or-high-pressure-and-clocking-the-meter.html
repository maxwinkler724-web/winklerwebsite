Why Burner Input Follows the Square Root of Manifold Pressure

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Turn a manifold pressure screw by what looks like a small amount and the input barely moves. Turn it by a lot and the input still moves less than you would guess. The reason is the square root, and once you see it you can predict the change before you touch anything. This lesson explains the flow idea, works an example of our own with the pressure set 0.5 in. w.c. low and high, and shows how to confirm input by clocking the meter.

Check the model's manual

Check the model's manual. Manifold pressure and orifice size belong to one model, one gas and one rating plate. The numbers below are teaching numbers. Set and verify pressure only as the installation instructions for the appliance in front of you say, and never use this page to choose a setting.

Orifice flow in plain terms

A burner orifice is a small, sharp-edged hole that meters gas into the burner. Two things decide how much gas passes: how big the hole is, and how hard the pressure behind it pushes. A university lecture page on flow through a sharp-edged opening gives the flow as a coefficient times the opening area times the speed of the jet, and the jet speed grows with the square root of the pressure difference divided by the fluid density. Lecture notes on flow measurement from another university say the same thing for orifice meters: the flow rate depends on the square root of the pressure drop, with a discharge coefficient to correct for losses. That page says the coefficient is around 0.61 for most commercial orifice flowmeters, and that it varies with the opening and the flow.

In words, for one gas and one hole:

  • Input goes up in direct proportion to orifice area. Twice the area, twice the gas.
  • Input goes up with the square root of the pressure drop across the orifice. Four times the pressure, twice the gas.

The area of a round hole depends on its diameter squared, so a drill size that looks only a little bigger can add noticeably to the area. A federal aerospace research center's beginner page explains why the pressure and speed are tied together this way: for steady flow that is much slower than the speed of sound, density can be treated as constant, and pressure plus the speed term stays fixed along the flow. Burner gas at inches of water column is far below that limit. We treat the gas as a constant-density fluid, which is a simplification, and it is a good one at these pressures.

Why a small pressure change moves input less

Because of the square root, the percent change in input is roughly half the percent change in pressure for small changes. Here is the arithmetic, using the ratio of new input to old input equal to the square root of new pressure over old pressure.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. These are assumptions for teaching, not a real model:

  • Natural gas furnace, rated input 80,000 Btu/h on the rating plate.
  • Rated manifold pressure 3.5 in. w.c. at that input.
  • Orifice size is correct and unchanged. Only pressure changes. Gas heating value, supply and burner condition stay the same.

Manifold 0.5 in. w.c. low (3.0 in. w.c.).

  1. Pressure ratio: 3.0 / 3.5 = 0.857.
  2. Square root of 0.857 = 0.926.
  3. Input = 80,000 x 0.926 = about 74,100 Btu/h.
  4. Change: about 5,900 Btu/h less, or 7.4 percent low.

Manifold 0.5 in. w.c. high (4.0 in. w.c.).

  1. Pressure ratio: 4.0 / 3.5 = 1.143.
  2. Square root of 1.143 = 1.069.
  3. Input = 80,000 x 1.069 = about 85,500 Btu/h.
  4. Change: about 5,500 Btu/h more, or 6.9 percent over the rating.

Notice two things. The pressure moved by 14 percent low or high, but input moved by only about 7 percent. And the effect is not symmetric: the same 0.5 in. w.c. costs a little more on the low side than it adds on the high side. The high case is the one to respect, because an input above the rating plate value is overfiring. Our own sense check: if the orifice were instead 3 percent too large in diameter (our assumption), area would be 1.03 x 1.03 = 1.061, so input would be about 6.1 percent high at the correct pressure, a similar size of error from a very small drill-size difference.

Why the maker sets both the orifice and the pressure

Look at the two controls together. Input depends on orifice area and on the square root of pressure, and the maker picks a pair that delivers the rating plate input. The same pair also has to give a good mix of gas and air, stable flames and lightoff that works with the burner design. Changing only one control throws that tested pairing off. If a wrong orifice is installed, turning the pressure to chase the right Btu number can leave the flame, the air mix and the safety limits outside what the maker designed for. That is why we match the orifice to the model and the gas, and why pressure adjustment is limited to the range the instructions give. For related symptoms see the linked lessons on lightoff trouble and on gas conversion.

Gas type matters too. Density is in the flow relation, so a denser or lighter gas passes a different amount through the same hole at the same pressure. Different gases therefore use different orifices and different manifold pressures, and the conversion lesson linked below covers how makers handle that.

Confirming input by clocking the meter

Pressure and orifice size give a prediction. Clocking the meter measures what the appliance actually burns. With every other gas appliance off and the burners running at full rate, time one turn of the small test dial. Cubic feet per hour is 3,600 times the dial size in cubic feet divided by the seconds for one turn. Btu per hour is cubic feet per hour times the heating value of the gas. The step-by-step method is in the clocking lesson linked below.

The heating value is not fixed. A federal energy statistics agency reports that the national average heat content of gas delivered to consumers was about 1,036 Btu per cubic foot in 2025, but its data show state averages ranging from 917 to 1,096, and its notes say the value may vary by location and over time and that exact figures come from the local gas supplier. For field math, use the value from the supplier.

Our own clocking example. Same assumed furnace, 80,000 Btu/h rating, and an assumed heating value of 1,036 Btu per cubic foot:

  1. Expected flow at rating: 80,000 / 1,036 = 77.2 cubic feet per hour.
  2. With a one-half cubic foot test dial, expected time per turn: 3,600 x 0.5 / 77.2 = 23.3 seconds.
  3. Suppose we clock 25.0 seconds. Flow = 1,800 / 25.0 = 72.0 cubic feet per hour.
  4. Input = 72.0 x 1,036 = about 74,600 Btu/h, which is 93 percent of the rating.

That result is close to the 3.0 in. w.c. prediction above (about 74,100 Btu/h). A slow clocked time means low input; a fast time means high input. If the clock and the manometer disagree by more than the reading error, look for a wrong orifice, a blocked orifice, a wrong heating value or a meter dial that was misread.

Safety

Work on gas appliances only as trained and as the instructions allow. After any pressure tap work, close the tap and leak check it before leaving. This page is background for understanding, not a procedure.

Field card: Why Burner Input Follows the Square Root of Manifold Pressure

Input goes with area, and with the square root of pressure.

  • Double the orifice area, double the input. Four times the pressure, double the input.
  • Our own example: 3.5 in. w.c. rated, 80,000 Btu/h. At 3.0 in. w.c. input is about 74,100 (7.4 percent low); at 4.0 in. w.c. about 85,500 (6.9 percent high).
  • The maker sets orifice and pressure as a pair. Do not trade one for the other.
  • Confirm by clocking: cfh = 3,600 x dial size / seconds; Btu/h = cfh x heating value.
  • Get the heating value from the gas supplier.
