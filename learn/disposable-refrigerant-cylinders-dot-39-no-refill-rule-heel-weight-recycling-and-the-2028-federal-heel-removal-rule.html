Disposable Refrigerant Cylinders: DOT 39, No Refilling, Heels and Disposal

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The light, cheap cylinder that new refrigerant comes in is built for one trip. It is not a recovery tank, it is not a refill, and it is not trash until the refrigerant is out of it. This lesson covers what the federal text says, how to check an empty by weight, and where the federal rules stand today.

Check the current rule

Check the current rule. Federal rules change, and this page is general education, not legal advice. We read the federal texts named in the Sources list in 2026. Confirm dates with EPA and the Department of Transportation before you rely on them.

What DOT 39 means

DOT 39 is a federal packaging specification for non-reusable cylinders. The specification text, 49 CFR 178.65, caps the water capacity of these cylinders at 55 pounds when service pressure is 500 psig or less. It sets the minimum test pressure as the contents pressure at 130 °F or 180 psig, whichever is greater. The marking section requires durable, waterproof markings, including the DOT-39 specification, service and test pressures, a maker registration number and a lot number.

The marking section also carries the refill warning. Cylinders made on or after October 1, 1996 must bear a statement that federal law forbids transportation if the cylinder has been refilled, with a penalty of up to $500,000 and five years in prison (49 U.S.C. 5124). So the rule against refilling is on the cylinder itself. If you see that wording, the cylinder is single use. Read the section on the official 2024 annual edition of 178.65.

Why they must not be used for recovery

  • It would be a refill. A tank that takes recovered refrigerant is being filled a second time. The cylinder is marked as non-reusable, so recovery breaks the rule on the label.
  • No float switch, no liquid/vapor split. A recovery tank is built with separate ports so a recovery machine can be protected from overfilling (see the picture below). A disposable cylinder has a single valve.
  • Recovered refrigerant is an unknown. Our own note: it may be mixed or carry oil, and it belongs in a labeled recovery container. See Recover, Recycle and Reclaim and Recovery Machine Care for the fill limits that apply to recovery cylinders.

Our own plain-language summary: a recovery cylinder is a refillable pressure vessel with a test date. A disposable one is a one-trip package. Do not swap them.

Pressure relief and heat

The pressure relief rules are in 49 CFR 173.301(f). Paragraph (f)(4)(i) says a DOT 39 cylinder must have a pressure relief device regardless of size or filled pressure. Fusible devices are not authorized on a DOT 39 cylinder holding a liquefied gas (f)(4)(iii). The device must be able to prevent rupture of a normally filled cylinder in a fire test (f)(1). The purpose is to vent safely if the cylinder gets too hot instead of rupturing.

The same section, paragraph (a)(8), says the contents pressure at 131 °F (55 °C) may not exceed 5/4 of the service pressure, and that the cylinder must have enough outage so it is not liquid full at 131 °F. Section 173.304(b) repeats the not-liquid-full idea for liquefied gas. Those are filling rules for the people who fill and ship, not a storage temperature limit for a service van. We did not find a federal storage temperature rule in the texts we read, so use the cylinder label and the supplier's instructions. Our own habit: keep cylinders out of sun and away from heat, and never warm one with a flame.

Rust and damage

Section 173.301(a)(2) says a person filling a cylinder must inspect the outside, and a cylinder that has a crack, a leak, is bulged or has a defective valve may not be filled or offered for transport. That is a filling rule, but it shows how the federal text treats damage. Our own advice: a cylinder with deep rust, pitting, dents, a damaged valve or a wet, rusting base is a poor candidate for anything but careful emptying and disposal. Rust on a thin disposable shell can hide wall loss, and it also hides the stamped markings.

Emptying before disposal

Disposable cylinders still hold a heel, the refrigerant left when the cylinder no longer feeds the manifold. An empty looks empty at 0 psig on the gauge, but a heel of liquid or vapor can remain. Do not vent it; see Refrigerant Cylinders and Labels for handling basics.

The EPA rule at 40 CFR 84.116 sets what happens to a used disposable cylinder. The EPA published it in October 2024 and its disposal requirements start January 1, 2028. From that date a person who uses a disposable cylinder for HFCs to service, repair or install refrigerant or fire suppression equipment, and does not plan to reuse it, must send it for heel removal to one of these: a certified reclaimer, a fire suppressant recycler (for fire suppression use), a final processor such as a landfill operator or scrap metal recycler that can remove heels, or a refrigerant supplier such as a distributor that can remove heels. The rule has an alternative: a certified technician evacuates the heel to a vacuum of 15 in. Hg, signs a certification statement (name, address, date, evacuation to 15 in. Hg), and hands it to the final processor, who keeps it for three years. Cans of two pounds or less that qualify for a separate exemption are not covered. Note that 15 in. Hg of vacuum is below 0 psig, so a gauge that merely reads zero does not meet that route. That is our reading of the units; confirm with EPA.

Before 2028 the same rule is not yet in force for this step, so follow your employer's procedure and any state or local rules. Once the cylinder is empty, ask the recycler how they want it handled. Some want the valve removed or the shell punctured so no one reuses it, and some do not allow it. Our own advice: do not cut or puncture a cylinder yourself unless the processor tells you to, and recycle the steel through the channel they name. Under 49 CFR 173.301(a)(7), a cylinder past its service life that has been emptied must be condemned under 180.205.

Worked example (our own numbers)

These weights are assumptions for practice, not real data for any product. A disposable cylinder is stamped with a tare weight of 6.0 lb. We assume the tare includes the valve and no cap, and that the scale reads to 0.1 lb.

  1. Stand the cylinder on the scale with the same valve fitting you assumed. The scale reads 6.4 lb.
  2. Subtract the tare: 6.4 lb − 6.0 lb = 0.4 lb of refrigerant left.
  3. Convert to ounces: 0.4 lb × 16 oz/lb = 6.4 oz. Because the scale rounds to 0.1 lb (1.6 oz), the heel is about 6.4 oz, give or take a couple of ounces.

What to do with the heel: it is not empty and not trash. Recover it with a recovery machine into a refillable recovery cylinder, down to the vacuum your procedure and the rule require, or send the cylinder to a reclaimer or supplier that removes heels. If the scale reads 6.0 lb or less, the weight check passes, but you still have to reach the evacuation level, since a scale cannot see a few ounces of vapor. If the reading is far above the tare, you may have water, oil or the wrong tare; stop and ask. Record the weights.

The federal move toward refillable cylinders and tracking

EPA first set up an HFC allocation program under the AIM Act in 2021, and that rule (86 FR 55116) included a ban on filling and selling disposable cylinders and a cylinder tracking system with QR codes. In June 2023 the D.C. Circuit held that EPA had not identified authority for those two parts and vacated them. EPA then removed them from the CFR in a rule published September 11, 2024 (89 FR 73588), which cites 40 CFR 84.5(h) for the ban and 84.23 for tracking. EPA's frequent questions page says the agency is not implementing or enforcing the prohibition on filling and selling disposable cylinders.

What replaced it is the Emissions Reduction and Reclamation rule, published October 11, 2024 (89 FR 82682) and effective December 10, 2024. It handles disposable cylinders by heel removal at disposal (above), not by requiring refillable cylinders. In that final rule EPA said it was not finalizing its proposed provisions for container tracking. As of the EPA pages we read in 2026, the only later action listed for this rule was a May 26, 2026 proposal about transport refrigeration unit leak repair, which does not concern cylinders. Our reading: the federal approach today is disposal control, not a refillable-only mandate. Check EPA's page for new actions.

Field card: Disposable Refrigerant Cylinders: DOT 39, No Refilling, Heels and Disposal

One trip, then empty it right.

  • DOT 39 means non-reusable. The cylinder is marked: federal law forbids transportation if refilled.
  • Never use a disposable cylinder for recovery. Use a refillable recovery cylinder with a test date.
  • Heel check (our example): gross weight minus stamped tare.
  • EPA 40 CFR 84.116 disposal duties start January 1, 2028: reclaimer, final processor, supplier, or technician evacuation to 15 in. Hg with a signed statement.
  • The disposable cylinder ban and QR tracking were vacated in 2023 and removed in 2024.
  • Keep cylinders cool, upright and away from rust and damage.
