Anti-Sweat Heaters on Glass-Door Cases and Freezers: Why Doors Fog, Wattage and Humidity Controls

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A glass door on a cooler or freezer case is the coldest, wettest surface in a warm store. Without help it fogs or frosts over. Anti-sweat heaters keep the glass and frame warm enough to stay clear. They cost money twice: as electricity and as heat the refrigeration system must remove.

Why doors fog or frost

When a surface is colder than the dew point of the air touching it, vapor condenses on it, and below freezing it frosts. The dew point depends only on the moisture in the store air, so a humid day raises it even if the thermostat has not moved. The frame and the seam at the door edge are usually colder than the middle of the glass, so they sweat first. For moisture basics, see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

What anti-sweat heaters are

A glass heater is a conductive coating or edge wire. A door frame heater is a wire inside the frame. A mullion heater sits in the case frame between doors. A utility information sheet says that in standard cases these heaters generally run 24 hours a day. The heater only needs to lift the surface above the dew point. Better glass helps too: federal rules for walk-ins with transparent reach-in doors require triple-pane glass with heat-reflective treatment or gas fill for freezers, and double-pane with both for coolers.

How much power a heater draws

Use the nameplate when you have it. A utility technical document gives averages of 0.105 kW per door for medium-temperature display cases and 0.191 kW per door for low-temperature cases, without controls, for 8,760 hours a year. A regional efficiency alliance page reports a whole case at 0.54 kW, or 4,730 kWh a year run continuously. Treat these as examples.

The heat comes back as refrigeration load

Heater heat that reaches the case must be removed by the compressor, and sources differ on how much. The utility document uses 35 percent of heater energy as refrigeration load. The 2014 DOE walk-in rule assumes 75 percent of the power of a device on the inside face of a door. For the 0.191 kW door at 35 percent, 0.191 x 0.35 = 0.067 kW, about 228 Btu/h (Our own arithmetic, 3,412 Btu/h per kW).

Controllers: dew point, humidity and pulse width

A controller runs the heater only when needed. Most read store humidity and temperature to work out dew point; some sense condensation or door conductivity. Many pulse the heater: on for a fraction of a repeating cycle, short at low humidity and 100 percent at high humidity. The alliance page reports a case falling from 0.54 kW to 0.25 kW with pulse control, and says no savings occur on glass doors above about 55 percent relative humidity.

Claimed savings vary. That page assumes 30 percent (range 25 to 55). A utility sheet says more than 90 percent in most instances, and that Colorado door heaters are needed less than 5 percent of the year. A 2022 study of supermarket frozen-food doors modeled dew point regulation and estimated about 84.6 percent savings for frame heaters and 90.1 percent for glass heaters (abstract).

Low-heat and no-heater doors

Some doors stay above the dew point with little or no heater, using more panes, coatings, gas fill and frames that resist conducting cold. The 2024 DOE rule lists design options such as reduced anti-sweat power and heat only at the door perimeter. Even a no-heater door sweats if the store is humid enough.

Federal rules in 10 CFR 431

For walk-ins with transparent reach-in doors, 10 CFR 431.306(b) caps door rail, glass and frame heater power at 7.1 watts per square foot of door opening for freezers and 3.0 for coolers. Heaters over the cap need controls that cut energy with outside relative humidity or inner-glass condensation. For a 30 by 78 inch opening, 2.5 x 6.5 = 16.25 square feet (Our own assumed size), so the caps are 16.25 x 7.1 = 115 W and 16.25 x 3.0 = 49 W (Our own arithmetic). Self-contained cases fall under 10 CFR 431.66, which sets daily kWh limits by class; it does not name heaters, but anti-condensate energy counts in the total. In 2024 DOE did not amend walk-in display door standards. See the walk-in DOE rules lesson.

Worked example: will this door sweat?

Assumed conditions (Our own): store air 24 °C (75.2 °F), 55 percent relative humidity, standard pressure 101.325 kPa. Table values: 24 °C is 2.9858 kPa; 14 °C is 1.5990 kPa; 15 °C is 1.7058 kPa (Urieli, Engineering Thermodynamics, saturation table).

  1. Vapor pressure: pw = 0.55 x 2.9858 = 1.642 kPa.
  2. Humidity ratio: W = 0.622 x pw / (p - pw) = 0.622 x 1.642 / (101.325 - 1.642) = 0.01025 kg of water per kg of dry air.
  3. Dew point: the saturation temperature at 1.642 kPa. It falls between 14 °C (1.5990) and 15 °C (1.7058). Interpolating: 14 + (1.642 - 1.5990) / (1.7058 - 1.5990) = 14.4 °C, or about 57.9 °F.
  4. Decision: if the unheated door surface is 12 °C (53.6 °F, Our own assumed value), it is below 14.4 °C, so the door sweats. With the heater lifting the surface to 16 °C (60.8 °F, also assumed), it is above the dew point and stays clear.
  5. Drier day: at 45 percent the dew point is about 11.3 °C, so the same 12 °C surface stays dry.

Worked example: annual kWh, full time versus controlled

Use the low-temperature door draw of 0.191 kW (utility deemed value, no controls, 8,760 h per year).

  1. Full time: 0.191 kW x 8,760 h = 1,673 kWh per door per year.
  2. Controlled at a 10 percent duty cycle (Our own assumption, in the spirit of the over-90-percent savings claim above, for a dry climate): 1,673 x 0.10 = 167 kWh. Saved: 1,506 kWh.
  3. Refrigeration penalty, using the utility 35 percent share and a compressor COP of 1.5 (Our own assumption): full time 1,673 x 0.35 / 1.5 = 390 kWh; controlled 167 x 0.35 / 1.5 = 39 kWh.
  4. Totals: about 2,063 kWh full time versus about 206 kWh at 10 percent duty, before electric rates.

Troubleshooting

  • All doors fogged. Check store humidity first. Fix it (see humid building causes) before replacing parts.
  • One door or frame sweats. Suspect an open or burned heater. With power locked out, read its resistance and compare to the nameplate.
  • Controller set wrong. Look for jumpers or overrides, a sensor in a draft, or an offset set too far toward off. Compare with a separate humidity meter.
  • Frame frosts, glass clear. Frame or mullion heaters may be on a circuit the controller does not switch.

Many heater circuits are line voltage, so lock out and test first. See Reach-In and Display Case Defrost and Walk-In Freezer Door Heaters.

Field card: anti-sweat heaters

Keep the surface above the dew point, no more.

  • A door sweats when its surface is below the store dew point.
  • W = 0.622 pw / (p - pw).
  • Example: 24 °C and 55 percent gives a 14.4 °C dew point.
  • Example door: 0.191 kW, 1,673 kWh per year uncontrolled.
  • Heater heat returns as refrigeration load; sources assume 35 to 75 percent.
  • Walk-in glass door caps: 7.1 W/ft² freezer, 3.0 W/ft² cooler, unless humidity or condensation controls.
