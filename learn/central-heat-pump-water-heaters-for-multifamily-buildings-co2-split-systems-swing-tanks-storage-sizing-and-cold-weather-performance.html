Central Heat Pump Water Heaters for Multifamily: CO2 Split Systems, Swing Tanks, Storage Sizing and Cold-Weather Performance

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Apartment buildings used to make hot water with a big boiler or a row of tank heaters. A central heat pump water heater plant makes it in one mechanical room or on a roof instead, with outdoor heat pumps, a bank of storage tanks and a way to cover the heat the recirculation loop loses. This lesson is about how those plants are laid out and why they are sized so differently from a boiler.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual and the design documents. This page is general education built from public design guides and field studies read in 2026. Each plant has its own sequence of operation, setpoints and clearances. Follow the manufacturer and the engineer of record, not this page.

What a central plant is

The Building America Solution Center guide describes central systems as keeping all the equipment outside the dwelling units, which makes maintenance simpler, and says they need a hot water circulation loop. It also says a well designed and working plant can be about three to four times as efficient as gas or electric resistance water heating. For a single unit on one tank, see Heat Pump Water Heaters: How They Work.

Two loads, not one

The same guide splits the job in two. The first is the primary load: heating cold city water up to storage temperature. Heat pumps do this well. The second is temperature maintenance: making up the heat that the circulation loop loses, with the loop held at about 115 to 125 F. The guide says heat pumps struggle with that tepid return water. Much of the design work is deciding how to keep these two loads apart.

Why CO2 suits the primary load

Single-pass heat pumps heat water to the storage setpoint in one trip through the unit. The Energy Trust of Oregon design guide says single-pass lift is typically 70 to 100 F, while multi-pass units raise the water about 10 F per pass and circulate it many times. It says single-pass plants need less storage for the same capacity because they recover faster, and reach higher temperatures that help with Legionella control. CO2 (R-744) is the refrigerant that fits this well. The same guide says CO2 units can reach 180 F or more and operate below 0 F ambient, and that their high pressure limits the piping distance between the compressor and the condenser. Pressures, gas coolers and safety are in CO2 (R-744) Heat Pumps and Transcritical Systems.

The opposite is also true. A CO2 design guide from the California Energy Commission project team says its example CO2 unit heats city water at about 50 to 70 F up to storage at 140 to 160 F very efficiently, but does not work well on a temperature-maintenance job of heating water from 115 to 125 F. Single-pass wants cold water in and hot water out.

Primary storage and why stratification matters

Tanks do three jobs. The Solution Center guide says primary storage limits short cycling, separates the heat pump hydraulically from the building, and lets the plant run off peak. It adds that storage is cheaper than heat pump capacity and that heat pumps can run 16 to 20 hours a day to recharge large tanks.

A single-pass heat pump pulls water from the bottom and returns it hot at the top, so it needs a cool bottom. The Energy Trust guide says single-pass systems depend on stratification, with the bottom of the tank staying cool while hot water leaves the top. The Solution Center guide recommends vertical tanks for single-pass systems to maximize stratification. The Ecotope field paper on one retrofit describes tanks piped in series so the warmest water is at the end, with cold city water entering the first tank at the bottom, which keeps incoming water cooler and helps heat pump performance. It also says power draw rises quickly once inlet water goes above about 100 F. The same idea in chilled water is in Stratified Chilled Water Storage Tanks.

Controls use sensors at several heights. The CEC quick guide has three sensors at roughly 25, 50 and 75 percent of tank volume to switch the heat pumps, and the Energy Trust guide says the aquastat is about 30 percent of the way up. When a plant misbehaves, sensor position and what is happening to the water at the tank bottom are the first things to look at.

Swing tanks: keeping the recirculation return out

If the warm loop return enters the heat pump tank bottom, it ruins the cool layer. The Energy Trust guide says that for single-pass systems, mixing returning circulation water into primary storage reduces stratification and suits multi-pass equipment better. The usual single-pass answer is a swing tank, also called a temperature maintenance tank. The Energy Trust guide describes it as an unstratified tank in series with primary storage, passively heated by primary water during heavy use, with an electric element that switches on if the tank falls too far. The Solution Center guide notes the approach relies on primary storage at 150 F or higher, well above delivery temperature. The alternative is a parallel loop tank with its own multi-pass heat pump, which allows lower primary storage temperature but adds equipment and cost. See Hot Water Recirculation Pumps for what the loop itself does.

The weakness is the element. The Solution Center guide warns that many systems have run mostly on electric backup without the owners noticing, and the CEC quick guide sizes the swing tank for the longest limited-draw period, typically four to six hours. A CalNEXT study of lab results found swing-tank system COP falling as loop loss rises: 3.8 at 50 W per apartment, 2.8 at 150 W for one CO2 unit tested at 140 F. The same study says median loop loss in retrofits was 93 W per apartment. In the Seattle retrofit, losses were low and the swing tank worked well.

Sizing: smaller heat pump, bigger tanks

The Solution Center guide says properly sized plants have much lower recovery and higher storage than gas systems, and should not be sized like boilers. Oversizing the heat pumps raises first cost. The Energy Trust guide says the free Ecosizer tool picks, by default, the point where the heat pump runs 16 hours on a peak day, and the designer can trade capacity against storage. The CEC guide says space-limited sites may need a higher heat pump to storage ratio, and electrically limited sites a lower one. CalNEXT interviewees also warn against oversized tanks. For recirculation loss in new construction the Energy Trust guide gives 100 W per apartment with a 1.75 safety factor, and says retrofits should measure loop flow and temperature drop.

Outdoors, cold weather and backup

The Solution Center guide says central plants suit every U.S. climate zone with the right equipment, and that CO2 units can run far below 0 F. Frost builds on the outdoor coil at roughly 20 to 45 F ambient, which triggers defrost and cuts capacity and efficiency. It advises asking the maker for capacity (with defrost derating) and COP at record low, design cold and design hot air temperatures. Protected spaces such as a parking garage temper the intake air; a Seattle study cited by the Energy Trust guide kept inlet air above 50 F all year in a garage. Elevate units above snow, keep the maker's clearance from walls, and watch noise toward tenant windows.

Backup can be full redundancy, supplemental heat for undersized plants, or heat below the machines' operating range. The guide says controls should run backup only when the heat pumps cannot meet the load. For where an air-to-water plant fits in a hybrid system, see Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Commercial Hot Water and Heating.

What techs check

  • Thermowell sensor depths and the set points that match the sequence (aquastat, element on point, swing tank setpoint).
  • Where the return enters, and whether any warm return water reaches the heat pump tank bottom (CalNEXT lists return location and stratification as key checks).
  • Incoming strainers. The Seattle retrofit had a heat pump shut down on a high-temperature error from a clogged strainer.
  • Fan and flow sensor faults, and backup setpoints changed and never reset. The same site once had two units down with the backup inactive.
  • Mixing valve discharge near 120 F. The CEC guide expects about 120 F and the Energy Trust guide recommends a digitally controlled valve.
  • Run hours and element hours, and who gets the alarm. The Solution Center guide says alerts only help if a person is assigned to act on them.

Worked example: a 60-apartment plant (our own)

Assumptions (ours, for practice). 60 apartments at 20 gallons per apartment per day (the average measured at the Seattle senior building in the BPA study; your building will differ). Cold water 50 F, delivered at 120 F, so dT = 70 F. A 16-hour heat pump run, the Ecosizer default for a peak day. System COP 3.3, the annual figure measured at that same site. Loop loss 100 W per apartment, without the 1.75 safety factor.

Daily use. 60 x 20 = 1,200 gal/day.

Heat per day. Q = 1,200 x 8.33 x 70 = 699,720 Btu/day, about 700,000 Btu.

Heat pump output. 699,720 / 16 h = 43,733 Btu/h, which is 43,733 / 3,412 = 12.8 kW of heat. Our own arithmetic. For a peak day at 35 gal per apartment (the maximum in the BPA study), 2,100 gal makes 1,224,510 Btu/day, or 76,532 Btu/h over 16 hours. That is why the tanks and a ratio of capacity to storage matter more than one average-day number.

Electric input. 699,720 / 3,412 = 205.1 kWh of heat per day. At COP 3.3: 205.1 / 3.3 = 62.1 kWh/day, or about 3.9 kW while running. At an assumed cold-weather COP of 2.0: 102.5 kWh/day. The 3.3 is one plant's measured annual figure, not a promise.

Storage idea. With 149 F storage mixed down to 120 F with 50 F water, the hot share is (120 - 50) / (149 - 50) = 0.71, so each gallon of storage makes about 1.41 gallons at the tap. About 850 gallons of storage water covers a 1,200 gallon day if it is drawn down fully, which it never is; real tanks keep a cool layer, so plan for more.

Loop loss. 60 x 100 W = 6.0 kW, which is 144 kWh/day, or 20,472 Btu/h. If the element covered all of it, it would use more electricity than the whole primary plant above. That is why the swing tank must be heated mostly by primary water.

Field card: Central Heat Pump Water Heaters for Multifamily Buildings

Cool bottom in, hot top out, keep the loop return away.

  • Two loads: primary (cold to hot) and temperature maintenance (loop losses).
  • CO2 single-pass suits high lift; it is poor at heating 115 F water.
  • Vertical tanks and sensors at several heights protect the cool bottom layer.
  • Swing tank takes the recirculation return; its element is backup, not the main source.
  • Size smaller heat pumps with more storage; the Ecosizer default is a 16-hour peak-day run.
  • Ask the maker for capacity and COP at record low, design cold and design hot air.
  • Check strainers, sensor depths, mixing valve near 120 F, and alarms.
