Commissary Kitchen HVAC: Cook-Chill, Makeup Air, Room Pressure

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A central commissary kitchen cooks food in bulk, chills it fast, and ships it to many other sites. That puts four jobs under one roof: big cooking lines with big hoods, rapid cooling, cold storage and loading docks, and a clear split between raw and ready-to-eat rooms. This lesson shows how the air and refrigeration side fits together, with one heating calculation of our own.

The cooking line and its makeup air

Every cubic foot of air a hood throws out of the roof has to come back in somewhere. On a large cooking line that can be thousands of cfm. A state energy commission page for commercial kitchens sets hood exhaust limits per linear foot of hood, and they climb with the duty of the appliances under it: a wall-mounted canopy over light-duty gear is limited to 140 cfm per foot, while the same canopy over extra-heavy-duty gear is limited to 385 cfm per foot. That page also says replacement air delivered straight into the hood cavity cannot exceed 10 percent of the hood exhaust rate, and in tests of local makeup air devices the capture of smoke often failed at modest shares of supply. Takeaway: heavy appliances need more exhaust, and where the replacement air enters matters as much as how much.

The same page lists ways a large kitchen can reduce the burden, including using transfer air that would otherwise be exhausted, modulating hood fans with cooking sensors while keeping capture, and a path that uses mostly unheated makeup air at no more than 60°F. Those are code-level choices for the designer. For the tech, the point is simple: whatever is exhausted must be replaced, and in winter that replacement air needs heat. See Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics for the equipment and the heat transfer formula lesson for where 1.08 comes from.

Worked example (our own): heating the makeup air

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design. Assumptions: the hoods exhaust 8,000 cfm in total; all of it is replaced by outdoor air through a makeup air heater (no transfer air); the winter outdoor temperature is 10°F; and we want to deliver the air at 60°F. The 1.08 factor is the usual sensible-heat constant for standard air.

  1. Temperature rise: 60 − 10 = 50°F.
  2. Heat needed: 1.08 × 8,000 cfm × 50°F = 8,640 × 50 = 432,000 Btu/h.
  3. Check in kilowatts: 432,000 ÷ 3,412 = about 127 kW of heat into the airstream.
  4. If the heater is 80 percent efficient (also an assumption), fuel input is 432,000 ÷ 0.80 = 540,000 Btu/h.
  5. Sensitivity: each extra 1,000 cfm adds 1.08 × 1,000 × 50 = 54,000 Btu/h. Each extra 10°F of rise on the 8,000 cfm adds 1.08 × 8,000 × 10 = 86,400 Btu/h.

The math ignores moisture and duct losses. It also shows why a hood that cycles to full speed during a rush is a heating event: the heater must follow the exhaust. Interlocks between hood fans and makeup air are covered in the hood interlock lesson.

Cook-chill: why cooling speed sets the refrigeration load

Commissary food is cooked, then chilled quickly so it can be held and shipped. The federal model food code cooling rule, as summarized in a federal food agency document and a university extension page, says cooked food must drop from 135°F to 70°F within two hours, then from 70°F to 41°F or less within the following four hours, six hours in all. The same federal document lists shallow pans, thinner portions, rapid cooling equipment and ice baths as methods, and says to arrange containers for maximum heat transfer through the container walls and to cover them loosely or leave them uncovered if protected from overhead contamination.

For meat and poultry plants, the federal meat and poultry inspection agency publishes its own stabilization guidance. One option allows 130°F to 80°F in no more than 1.5 hours and 80°F to 40°F in no more than 5 hours. That guidance says cooling rate depends on product size, shape and weight, how the cooler is stacked and loaded, air velocity and the cooler's starting temperature, and it recommends checking worst-case conditions such as the largest product and the fullest cooler. It gives no equipment capacity figures, so those come from the maker's data.

For the refrigeration tech, the first two hours are the hard part: a blast chiller sees the heat of a whole batch dumped in a short window, and tumble chillers (chilled-water drums used for bagged product) shift that load onto a chilled-water plant. Pull-down loads, airflow across pans and defrost are covered in the blast chiller lesson, so we do not repeat them here. If a batch is loaded warm into a walk-in instead, it warms everything already inside, which is one reason walk-in banks are kept for holding, not chilling.

Walk-in banks and refrigerated docks

A commissary usually has several walk-ins grouped by use: raw, cooked and chilled, and staging for shipping. A refrigerated dock lets trucks load without opening cold rooms to the outdoors, but every open door lets warm humid air in, and that moisture ends up as frost on coils and ice or condensation near the door. Seals, shelters and building pressure effects are discussed in the loading dock lesson. For bakery-specific ovens, proofers and dust, see the bakery lesson.

Raw and ready-to-eat rooms: air pressure

A federal meat and poultry sanitation training document warns that air flowing from a raw product area into a ready-to-eat area could carry Listeria, and it recommends positive air pressure movement out of the ready-to-eat room into the raw processing areas. It adds that air flow can be influenced by the location and operation of refrigeration units. In practice, that means supply, return and exhaust are balanced so doors open toward the dirtier side, and a fan-coil or evaporator blowing across a door opening is checked with a smoke pencil, not assumed.

Condensation in cold prep rooms

Condensation forms when a surface is colder than the dew point of the air touching it. Our own example: room air at 75°F and 60 percent relative humidity has a dew point near 60°F (our own psychrometric estimate), so any bare duct, pipe or ceiling panel below that will sweat. The same federal sanitation document says design should include ventilation to prevent condensation and control humidity, lists overhead condensation as a contamination source, and advises plumbing drip pans so moisture drains away and installing cooling units so they can be cleaned. Insulate and vapor-seal cold lines, keep door gaskets tight, and avoid dumping humid kitchen air into cold rooms.

How much heat the kitchen gives off

A kitchen is a heat source, not just a cooling load. Cooking appliances, hot wash water, steam, lighting and people all add heat, and the hoods carry part of it out with the exhaust. We did not find a federal figure we could cite for a specific appliance, so use the equipment nameplate input and the hood maker's data for a real job. What matters for the tech is the interaction: more exhaust means more makeup air, which in summer is a cooling load and in winter a heating load.

Field card: commissary kitchen HVAC

Replace what you exhaust, chill fast, keep air moving from clean to raw.

  • Makeup heat, Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature rise. Our example: 8,000 cfm × 50°F = 432,000 Btu/h.
  • Cooked food cooling: 135°F to 70°F in 2 hours, then to 41°F within 4 more hours.
  • Air should move from the ready-to-eat room toward the raw area.
  • Surfaces below the dew point sweat; fix insulation and humidity.
  • Do not load warm batches into a holding walk-in.
