Asphalt Plant Heating and HVAC: Tank Heat Loss, Hot-Oil Coils, Heat-Traced Lines and Worker Heat Stress

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A hot-mix asphalt plant is mostly a heating job. Liquid asphalt has to stay hot and pumpable in storage, stay hot in the lines, and arrive at the mixer at the right temperature, while the people who run the plant work next to hot tanks, burner exhaust and trucks that give off fumes. This lesson walks through the heating and air side of that job and ends with a heat-loss example we worked ourselves.

Check your site rules first

Check your site rules first. Tank temperatures, heater settings and entry procedures come from the asphalt supplier, the equipment maker and the plant's own safety program. This page is general education, built from federal and university pages we read in 2026. It is not a procedure.

What a plant does with heat

The federal emission-factor chapter for hot-mix asphalt plants describes the process. Aggregate is dried and heated in a rotary dryer, then mixed with liquid asphalt. In a drum-mix plant the asphalt is injected inside the drum; in a parallel-flow drum the material moves the same way as the exhaust gas, and in a counterflow drum it moves against it. The same chapter says most plants can burn natural gas or fuel oil, and it estimates that 70 to 90 percent of the mix is made on natural gas. For the HVAC and plant tech, that means the dryer burner, the tank heaters and the exhaust path are all combustion equipment, so the basics in gas and CO detection in boiler and mechanical rooms carry over.

Keeping liquid asphalt hot in storage

Asphalt in a storage tank loses heat to the air all the time, so something has to put heat back. Three common approaches:

  • Hot-oil coils. A heater warms a thermal fluid, a pump sends it through a coil near the bottom of the tank, and the asphalt picks up heat from the coil. The federal chapter lists hot-oil systems fired with natural gas or No. 2 fuel oil as part of the plant's emission sources. The heater, expansion tank and fluid care are covered in thermal-fluid hot-oil heating systems in plants; we do not repeat them here.
  • Direct-fired tank heaters. A burner fires into a tube that runs through the asphalt. The tube wall is the hot surface, so the asphalt near it sees a much higher temperature than the bulk liquid. The usual caution is to follow the heater maker's limits and the asphalt supplier's maximum temperature.
  • Electric heaters. Elements in a tube or immersion housing do the same job without a flame or stack. They avoid combustion, but they still have a hot-surface limit and need the tank to stay covered with liquid.

The federal chapter notes that emissions from heated storage tanks include organic compounds in gas and aerosol form, plus combustion products from the tank heater. So a tank heater is both a heat source and an exhaust source, and a tank vent is a place fumes can leave. We did not find a typical storage temperature in that chapter, so we use an assumed one in the example below.

Insulation on tanks and line runs

Heat flow through a wall is the temperature difference divided by the total thermal resistance in the path. A university set of heat transfer notes writes it as Q = U × A × (inside temperature minus outside temperature), where 1/(UA) is the sum of the individual resistances in series, and notes that fiberglass has a thermal conductivity of about 0.038 W/(m·K), versus several hundred for copper. In US units, R-values add: a layer of R-6 and a layer of R-4 make R-10. Three practical points follow:

  • Heat loss rises with surface area, so large tank shells and long line runs dominate the heater load.
  • Wet or crushed insulation loses most of its resistance, and a missing section of jacket is a hot spot. Fix jackets and seams before adding heater capacity.
  • On small pipes there is a limit to how much insulation helps; see the critical radius lesson for why this rarely matters on larger line sizes.

Heat-traced asphalt lines

Asphalt that sits in a cold line stiffens, and a line that cools below its pumping temperature can plug. Lines are insulated and then kept warm either by a hot-oil jacket or tracer, or by electric heat trace cable under the insulation. Electric trace puts a set number of watts per foot into the pipe; the insulation then has to keep that heat from simply going to the air. Sizing, self-regulating versus constant-wattage cable and ground-fault protection are in the heat trace cable lesson. For asphalt, the extra points are to check the cable's temperature rating for the line temperature and to keep valves, pump casings and flanges inside the insulated, traced envelope, because those are the usual cold spots.

Control house cooling and dust

The control house sits next to a dryer and a baghouse, in an air stream with fine dust. The federal chapter describes dryer particulate controls as dry mechanical collectors, scrubbers and fabric filters, so the plant is built around moving dusty air. For the control house, the HVAC goals are the same as in any plant control room: filtered outside air, enough cooling for the electronics, and a slight positive pressure so that leaks go outward rather than pulling dust in. Door seals, sealed cable entries and clean filters matter more than any one setpoint. The plant control room lesson, plant control room HVAC, covers intake siting and filtration. At an asphalt plant, put the fresh-air intake away from the stack, the loadout lane and the tank vents. For dust that can burn, see dust collectors and combustible dust risk.

Burner and dryer exhaust basics

The dryer burner sends combustion gas and moisture-laden dust through the drum to the collectors. The federal chapter says electrostatic precipitators have had little success on dryers and that dryer controls are usually a primary collector followed by a fabric filter or venturi scrubber, while parallel-flow drum mixers often use primary collection only. It also describes a visible "blue smoke" plume from organic compounds cooling after a parallel-flow stack, which plants reduce with flame shields and other design changes. A tech working on the burner should expect a plant that holds a permit for its exhaust, so changes to fuel, burner or collectors are not field decisions.

Worker heat stress and fume exposure

Around tanks and the loadout lane, workers have hot surfaces, hot air, sun and often a hard physical load. The federal workplace safety agency's heat pages say heat illness happens when the body cannot shed heat fast enough, and that road construction and indoor jobs near furnaces or other hot sources are both affected. Its prevention pages give these points:

  • Provide cool water close to the work and tell people to drink often, not only when thirsty. For jobs over two hours, add drinks with electrolytes.
  • Give hourly breaks whenever heat stress is over the limits on the agency's heat hazard page, and a cool place to rest: shade, an air-conditioned vehicle or room, or a fan and mist area.
  • Start new workers at 20 percent of a normal workload and add 20 percent per day, reaching a full schedule by the end of the first week. Some workers need up to 14 days.
  • A wet bulb globe temperature meter is the most accurate way to measure heat stress, because it combines temperature, humidity, sunlight and air movement.

For fumes, the federal occupational research institute's pocket guide says asphalt fumes can irritate the eyes and airways, and it lists a recommended ceiling of 5 mg/m³ over 15 minutes and treats the fumes as a potential occupational carcinogen. It says to prevent skin and eye contact, and it lists inhalation, skin absorption and contact as exposure routes. The federal emission chapter adds that loadout is a fugitive source and that vapors keep coming off a loaded truck until it leaves the plant. Practical steps: stay upwind of loadout and tank vents, keep gauge and sample work short and out of the vapor, and wear what the site's hazard assessment requires. Entry into a tank is confined-space work under the site's own permit program. For heat on a rooftop or attic job, see heat stress in attics and on roofs.

Worked example (our own): holding an assumed tank hot

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design for any real tank. We estimate how much heat an insulated tank loses and the hot-oil heater output needed to hold it.

Assumptions (all ours): a vertical cylinder 12 ft in diameter and 30 ft tall (about 25,000 gallons); asphalt held at 300°F; winter air at 20°F; insulation on the shell and roof with a total effective resistance of R-10 (we ignore the steel and air films, which is slightly conservative); no heat lost through the floor; 20 percent extra for supports, nozzles and weather; and 10 percent of heater output lost in the oil piping.

  1. Area. Shell: π × 12 × 30 = 1,131 ft². Roof: π × 6² = 113 ft². Total A = 1,244 ft².
  2. Temperature difference. 300 − 20 = 280°F.
  3. U-value. U = 1 / R = 1 / 10 = 0.10 Btu/(h·ft²·°F).
  4. Heat loss. Q = U × A × ΔT = 0.10 × 1,244 × 280 = 34,832 Btu/h.
  5. Allowance. 34,832 × 1.20 = 41,798 Btu/h, about 41,800 Btu/h that the coil must deliver.
  6. Heater output. 41,798 ÷ 0.90 = 46,443 Btu/h, about 46,400 Btu/h, or 13.6 kW (46,443 ÷ 3,412 Btu/h per kW).
  7. Fuel input, if gas-fired. At an assumed 80 percent efficiency, 46,443 ÷ 0.80 = 58,053 Btu/h input.

Check. Doubling the insulation to R-20 halves step 4 to 17,416 Btu/h, which is what the formula says. The result is a holding load only. Heating the tank contents up from cold takes far more heat than holding does, and real heaters are sized for that job. Real tanks also lose heat through the floor and have wind effects, so treat 41,800 Btu/h as a floor for the estimate, not a design value.

Field card: Asphalt Plant Heating and HVAC

Insulate first, then size the heater.

  • Hold load = U x A x temperature difference, with U = 1 / R.
  • Our example: 1,244 ft², R-10, 280°F difference gives 34,832 Btu/h before allowances.
  • Hot-oil, direct-fired and electric tank heaters all need the maker's temperature limits.
  • Keep valves, pumps and flanges inside the insulated, traced envelope.
  • Control house: filtered air, slight positive pressure, intake away from stack and loadout.
  • Heat: water, rest, shade, and a 20 percent a day ramp for new workers.
  • Fumes: stay upwind, avoid skin contact, follow the site hazard assessment.
