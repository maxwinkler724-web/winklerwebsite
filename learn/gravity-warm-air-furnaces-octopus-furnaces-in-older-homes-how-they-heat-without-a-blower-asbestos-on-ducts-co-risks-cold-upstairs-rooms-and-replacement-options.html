Gravity Warm-Air Furnaces (Octopus Furnaces) in Older Homes: How They Heat Without a Blower, Asbestos on Ducts, CO Risks, Cold Upstairs Rooms and Replacement Options

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Many older basements hold a big round furnace with large pipes branching off the top like arms. People call it an octopus furnace. It is a gravity warm-air furnace: it moves heat through the house with no blower at all. This lesson explains how that works, what to watch for (asbestos, carbon monoxide, cold upstairs rooms) and what replacing one involves. For the safer way to think about asbestos around any old heating system, see Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems.

How it moves air with no blower

Air heated inside the furnace is lighter than the cooler air around it, so it rises. In a gravity system the furnace sits low, usually in the basement, under a dome-shaped top section called the bonnet. Large round ducts leave the bonnet and climb to floor registers in the rooms above. Cooler air in those rooms is heavier, so it sinks back through a big return grille in the floor, usually near the middle of the house, and drops to the bottom of the furnace to be heated again. That loop is natural convection, and the whole system depends on it. This is our own plain-language description of the principle; the numbers below are our own example.

Because nothing pushes the air, the ducts are big and short, with few bends, and the registers are large. Anything that adds resistance, such as a dirty register, a closed damper, a furniture-blocked return or a duct that was altered, hurts flow much more than it would in a blower system.

Why upper floors run cold

The driving force is small and it depends on height and temperature difference (see the worked example). The first floor, close to the furnace, gets the most flow. A second-floor run is longer, loses heat to the air around the duct and rises into a house where the rooms are already warm, so the difference that drives it is smaller. Drafty, uninsulated old walls make it worse. Our own rule of thumb: if the upstairs is cold, look first for blocked returns and closed registers, then for duct changes. Never solve it by closing the basement door or covering the floor return, because the loop needs that return.

Coal, oil and gas conversions

Many of these furnaces were built to burn coal and later changed over to oil and then to gas. A gas conversion typically means a gas burner and controls were fitted to an old cast-iron or steel heat exchanger. The Department of Energy guide on home heating and cooling points out that older furnaces often rely on a natural draft to carry exhaust gases out through the chimney, and that they can backdraft when the chimney is blocked, when wind blows, or when the house is under negative pressure. Those are the venting problems a converted gravity furnace can inherit. Gas appliance venting has changed since the originals were built, so a converted unit is something to have a qualified technician evaluate, not assume safe.

Possible asbestos on the ducts and cabinet

Treat insulation on an old furnace as suspect until a professional says otherwise. An EPA homeowner guide to asbestos in the home says oil and coal furnaces and door gaskets may have asbestos insulation, and that furnace ducts insulated with an asbestos blanket or asbestos paper tape can release fibers if they are damaged or handled improperly. The same guide says it is usually best to leave asbestos material in good condition alone, not to saw, sand, scrape or drill into it, and that repairs and removal belong to people trained in handling asbestos. For octopus furnaces that often means white or gray wrap on the large round ducts and a lining or wrap on the cabinet.

What to do: do not tear off, rip, brush or vacuum wrap, even if it is flaking. Do not cut into old duct insulation to run new pipe. Keep children and pets away from damaged material. Have a trained, licensed asbestos professional test and, if needed, deal with it before any work starts. We cannot tell asbestos from other wrap by looking, and neither can you; only lab testing can. Anyone who works on a system like this should expect asbestos to be handled first by the right people.

CO and venting concerns with old heat exchangers

A heat exchanger separates burner flue gases from the air that goes to the rooms. If a very old exchanger rusts through or cracks, or if the flue or chimney is blocked, loose or leaking, carbon monoxide can end up in the house. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends that a professional inspect every fuel-burning heating system once a year, including furnaces, chimneys, flues and vents, and its furnace inspection guidance says inspectors should look for blockages and leaks in the flues and vents, loose connections, and whether the appliance gets enough fresh air. It adds that dark staining on a chimney or flue, or unusual odors, can mean combustion products are escaping. Install working CO alarms; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do. For how exchangers are built and how far an inspection can see, see Gas Furnace Heat Exchanger Types.

Why adding air conditioning is hard

Central cooling needs a blower. A gravity system has none, and cold air sinks rather than rises, so cold air delivered from the high registers does not travel up through ducts built for warm air. The usual route is to add a blower-driven air handler and a new duct system, or a ductless approach. The DOE guide says existing ducts can often be reused for central AC only after inspection, since they may be poorly installed, damaged or undersized, and that new ducts should be sized with an accepted method such as Manual D. Old gravity trunks are also bulky and may be wrapped in suspect insulation, which complicates any change.

Replacement options

  • Modern furnace with new ductwork. The DOE guide presents replacing an old furnace with a modern high-efficiency model as the simple fix when it is worn out or oversized, and says repair can make sense on a furnace under 15 to 20 years old when repair costs stay under half of replacement. A gravity furnace is far older than that, and new blower-driven ducts also make AC possible. Ducts outside the conditioned space should be sealed with mastic and insulated; the guide cites R-8 on supply runs and R-6 on returns.
  • Hot-water heating. A boiler with radiators, baseboard or floor tubing avoids ducts. The DOE guide says hydronic systems need maintenance and radiant floors suit low-temperature water.
  • Heat pumps. A ducted heat pump can heat and cool, but the DOE guide says heat pumps may need larger ducts than a furnace because the air is cooler and moves in greater volume. Standard central units lose heating efficiency below roughly 35°F and may need backup heat in cold climates; ductless units avoid duct losses. See Replacing a Gas Furnace With a Ducted Heat Pump and Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Jersey Home.

Any option that removes the old furnace or its duct wrap starts with the asbestos question above.

Worked example (our own): how small is the gravity push?

This is our own estimate with assumed inputs, not measured data. We use the stack-effect form: pressure difference = (density of cool air minus density of warm air) × g × height.

  • Assumed duct height from furnace bonnet to the first-floor register: 6 ft = 1.83 m.
  • Assumed warm air in the duct: 150°F. Assumed cool return air: 70°F.
  • Densities from the ideal gas law at sea-level pressure (101,325 Pa), using 287 J/(kg·K): 70°F (294 K) gives about 1.20 kg/m³; 150°F (339 K) gives about 1.04 kg/m³.

Step 1: density difference = 1.20 − 1.04 = 0.16 kg/m³.
Step 2: pressure = 0.16 × 9.81 × 1.83 = about 2.9 Pa.
Step 3: convert, using 249 Pa per inch of water column: 2.9 ÷ 249 = about 0.012 in. w.c.

For comparison, we assume a typical modern blower furnace is set up for about 0.5 in. w.c. of total external static pressure (an assumption we chose for this example; equipment data plates vary). That is about 125 Pa, so the blower can push roughly 40 times harder than the gravity column. This is why gravity ducts must be so large, and why a few added elbows, a dirty register or a long upper-floor run can slow the flow a lot. Real systems differ, because duct temperatures drop along the run and heights vary.

Field card: Gravity warm-air furnaces

No blower means no margin: leave the loop open and leave the wrap alone.

  • Warm air rises in big round ducts; cool air falls to a large floor return.
  • Our example: about 3 Pa of drive vs about 125 Pa for a blower (assumed).
  • Keep returns and registers clear; cold upstairs usually means weak flow.
  • Old wrap and gaskets may be asbestos. Do not disturb; call trained professionals.
  • Get a professional to inspect the unit, flue and vent yearly; use CO alarms.
  • AC needs a blower and new ducts. Replacement starts with the asbestos question.
