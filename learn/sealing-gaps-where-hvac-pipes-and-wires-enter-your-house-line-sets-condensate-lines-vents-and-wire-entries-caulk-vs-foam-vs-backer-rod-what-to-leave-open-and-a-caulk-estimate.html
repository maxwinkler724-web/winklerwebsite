Sealing Gaps Where HVAC Pipes and Wires Enter Your House

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Every HVAC system that serves your house has to pass through the wall somewhere: refrigerant lines, a condensate drain, a vent, a power or control cable. The hole an installer drills is almost always bigger than the pipe, and the leftover gap lets in air, water and animals. This lesson shows how to find those gaps, pick caulk, foam or backer rod, and know what must never be sealed.

Where the gaps hide

Federal and university extension guides all give the same checklist: look anywhere plumbing, ductwork, fans or electrical wiring pass through exterior walls, floors and ceilings. For HVAC that translates into five spots we check on every walk-around:

  • Line set entry. The pair of refrigerant lines for a central air conditioner, heat pump or ductless unit, usually in a sleeve or a drilled hole near the outdoor unit.
  • Condensate line exit. The small drain pipe from the coil or a high-efficiency furnace, where it leaves the house or goes through a sill.
  • Vent and exhaust pipes. Furnace and water heater flue pipes, intake pipes, bath fan and dryer hoods, where the pipe meets the siding.
  • Wire entries. The service cable, the cable to the outdoor disconnect, and thermostat or control wire.
  • Old holes. Abandoned pipes or cable from a prior system, often left open behind a shrub.

To find them, walk the outside on a calm day and look for dark smudges, spider webs, light staining, loose trim or gaps you can fit a pencil into. Inside, hold a thin strip of tissue near the spot on a windy or heated day and watch it flutter. Our drafty doors and windows lesson covers the tissue and incense checks in more detail.

Why the size of the gap picks the material

A federal energy department job aid for sealing penetrations gives a simple size ladder. Gaps of a quarter inch or less: caulk. Quarter inch to 2 inches: one-part foam or mastic. Two to 3 inches: two-part foam. Three inches or more: a solid infill with support. The same job aid says holes for electrical, plumbing and HVAC runs are often oversized, and that holes over a quarter inch may need support for the sealant. Its answer is backer rod, a foam rope pushed into the gap as infill, then sealed over so the whole opening, including the rod, is covered.

University extension factsheets agree on the main points. One says caulk handles openings up to about a quarter inch and aerosol (can) foam suits holes up to about half an inch. Another says cracks more than half an inch wide or deep need backer rod or expanding foam, and describes backer rod as foam rope in sizes from a quarter inch to an inch and a quarter. It adds a useful line: expanding foam is a sealer, not a filler, so do not use it to stuff a hole. Do not caulk a gap wider than the cut nozzle can bridge.

Caulk, foam or backer rod, in plain terms

  • Outdoor-rated caulk for narrow joints where the pipe or cable meets siding, trim or masonry. One extension guide says to use exterior caulk on the outside so it stands up to weather. Read the label: it should say it is made for outdoor use and for the material it touches. If caulk shrinks as it dries, one extension source says to apply more so it still bridges the gap.
  • Backer rod plus caulk when the gap is deep or wider than a quarter inch. The rod sets the depth so the bead does not sink into the hole.
  • Foam for hidden cavities or inside a larger hole, not as the outer face. It is easy to apply and then easy to forget, and the outside still needs a caulk bead or a cover.

Application tips from the extension sources: clean and dry the surface first, cut the nozzle at a slant, hold the gun at about 45 degrees, make sure the bead touches both sides of the gap, and smooth it with a tool. One guide suggests an opening of about 3/8 inch at the cut tip. Caulk when the air is above about 40 degrees F, because some products will not cure well in colder weather.

What stays unsealed

This is our own rule set, and it matters as much as the sealing.

  • Vent and exhaust terminals. Seal the joint between the vent hood or pipe and the wall, not the opening the exhaust flows through. A blocked flue, dryer exhaust or bath fan terminal is a safety problem. A rodent guide says to close gaps around exhaust vents and ducts, not the vents themselves. For dryer hoods see our dryer vent lesson.
  • Moving parts. Do not glue a line set sleeve, a pump, a damper or a hinged flap shut so it cannot move or be serviced. A flexible bead around a pipe can stretch a little; a hard plug can crack when the line vibrates.
  • Weep holes and drain paths. Extension guidance on caulking windows says to leave weep holes open. The same idea applies to the small drain slots in outdoor unit bases and to any drain or overflow outlet: if water cannot leave, it finds another way. Our condensate drain lesson shows where it should go.
  • Anything hot. Do not push foam or caulk against a flue or vent pipe. Follow the equipment maker's clearance instructions, or ask a pro.

Pests: caulk alone is not a barrier

A state university extension guide on structure-invading rats says gaps of a quarter inch or larger let young rats in and gaps of half an inch or larger let adults in. It advises sealing gaps around electrical and plumbing lines on the outside, and around vents, ducts and air conditioners inside. For the fill, it lists galvanized mesh, fine steel wool (caulked over so it stays in place and does not rust through), copper mesh and thin galvanized sheet metal. It says expanding foam is not recommended because rats chew through it, and foam alone is not effective. So, at a pipe or wire gap larger than a quarter inch, we push a metal or mesh filler in first, then caulk over it. Our pests in ducts lesson covers signs and safe cleanup.

Our own worked example: how much caulk?

Our own example, with assumptions. We want to reseal four openings with outdoor-rated caulk over backer rod. We assume each gap is a ring around a round pipe or a flat edge, with a sealed cross section of 1/4 inch wide by 1/2 inch deep. The lengths are assumed too, and we assume a 10 fluid ounce cartridge (the extension sources describe tubes of about 10 to 11 ounces). We also assume 25 percent of the caulk is lost to nozzle waste and trimming.

  1. Gap lengths (assumed). Line set sleeve ring 12 in, condensate line 4 in, dryer hood flange joint 14 in, cable entry 8 in. Total: 12 + 4 + 14 + 8 = 38 in.
  2. Volume per inch. 0.25 in x 0.5 in x 1 in = 0.125 cubic inch per inch.
  3. Volume needed. 38 in x 0.125 = 4.75 cubic inches.
  4. Add waste. 4.75 x 1.25 = 5.94 cubic inches.
  5. Cartridge volume. One US fluid ounce is about 1.805 cubic inches, so 10 fl oz is 10 x 1.805 = 18.05 cubic inches.
  6. Cartridges. 5.94 / 18.05 = 0.33 cartridge, so one cartridge is plenty with two thirds left over (it must be used before it cures, so plan the job in one visit).

Sanity check. If the bead were only 1/4 by 1/4 inch, one cartridge would cover 18.05 / 0.0625 = 289 in, about 24 ft. That sits inside the 20 to 30 linear feet that one extension factsheet gives for a 10 to 11 ounce tube, so the arithmetic is reasonable. Deeper beads cover less: at 1/4 by 1/2 inch the same cartridge covers about 12 ft.

When a pro should reseal a sleeve

Handy owners can recaulk narrow joints at siding. Call a pro when:

  • the sleeve or hole is cracked, has rotted wood or crumbling masonry around it, or is wide enough that you cannot reach back to place filler,
  • the gap is near a flue or vent, or the opening goes through a fire-rated wall or ceiling,
  • you find chewed insulation, chewed wire, droppings or nesting material at the opening,
  • water stains show inside, or the line set slopes toward the house,
  • you would need to move a line, cable or drain to reseal it.

Pros see this at installs and service calls. Our mini-split wall penetration lesson covers line set sleeves and slope in depth.

Field card: Sealing Gaps Where HVAC Pipes and Wires Enter

Seal the gap, never the exhaust.

  • Check line sets, condensate lines, vents, wire entries and old holes.
  • Quarter inch or less: caulk. Over a quarter inch: backer rod or foam first, then seal over it.
  • Use outdoor-rated caulk outside; clean, dry surface; warmer than about 40 degrees F.
  • Leave vent and exhaust terminals, weep holes and drain paths open.
  • Do not glue moving parts or press sealant against hot flue pipes.
  • For rodents, use metal or mesh filler under caulk; foam alone is chewed through.
  • Volume check: width x depth x length, plus waste, divided by cartridge volume.
