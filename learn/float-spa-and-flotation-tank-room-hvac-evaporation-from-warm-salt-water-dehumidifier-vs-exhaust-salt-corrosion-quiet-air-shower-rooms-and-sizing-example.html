Float Spa HVAC: Humid Tank Rooms, Salt Corrosion and Dehumidifier Sizing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A float room is a small, quiet, warm and wet space. The tank holds very warm salt water, the client lies in it for an hour or more, and the air around it has to stay comfortable without blowing across a wet person. For the tech, the three jobs are moisture control, corrosion control and keeping the room quiet and warm enough.

How a float room works, in HVAC terms

Clients float in a pod or an open tank filled with a dense Epsom salt solution. A university medical-center web page describes the water as skin-temperature water about 10 inches deep, in tanks about 5 by 10 feet. Because the water is held near body temperature, it gives off water vapor all day, and the room around it is kept warm so the client does not chill when stepping out. The HVAC system has to carry that vapor away, keep the air still and quiet, and survive the salt.

Where the moisture goes

Warm water evaporates faster than cool water, and still, humid air slows it down. Each pound of water that evaporates takes latent heat from the room, and the same pound has to be condensed or exhausted again. Indoor pool rooms follow the same physics; see Indoor Pool Dehumidification rather than repeating it here. The hot-room side of the business is covered in the hot yoga and sauna lesson.

Dehumidifier or exhaust

Exhaust removes humid air and brings in outdoor air that must then be heated and, in summer, dried. In winter that outdoor air is dry, so exhaust can work, but it throws away the heat you paid to put into the room. In humid weather it can import more moisture than it removes. A dehumidifier recycles the room air: it cools it below dew point on a coil, collects the water and returns the air slightly warmer, since the compressor heat stays in the room. This is general practice, not a sourced rule, and many rooms use a dehumidifier for moisture plus a small exhaust fan for odors and the shower area. Check the local mechanical and plumbing requirements for the specific building.

Our own worked example

This is our own example with assumed inputs. Treat the assumed numbers as classroom values, not field data.

  • Room A (assumption): one pod with 25 sq ft of exposed water surface.
  • Room B (assumption): one open tank 5 ft by 10 ft, so 50 sq ft of exposed water surface (the size matches the university page above).
  • Evaporation rate (our assumption, not a sourced figure): 0.08 lb of water per hour per square foot of open surface, with water near 94 F and room air near 80 F, running all 24 hours.
  • Latent heat: 1,048 Btu per pound of water evaporated near 80 F. We interpolated this from a university engineering table of saturated water in SI units: 2,441.7 kJ/kg at 25 C and 2,429.8 kJ/kg at 30 C. At 26.7 C (80 F) that is about 2,437.7 kJ/kg, and dividing by the standard conversion of 2.326 kJ/kg per Btu/lb gives about 1,048. Water at 94 F has a slightly lower value, so using the 80 F value is a simple approximation.
  • Pints: 1 pint is about 1.04 lb (our arithmetic: 8.34 lb per gallon divided by 8 pints).

Room A. 25 sq ft x 0.08 = 2.0 lb/h. Latent load = 2.0 x 1,048 = 2,096 Btu/h. Per day: 2.0 x 24 = 48 lb, and 48 / 1.04 = 46.2 pints per day.

Room B. 50 sq ft x 0.08 = 4.0 lb/h. Latent load = 4.0 x 1,048 = 4,192 Btu/h. Per day: 4.0 x 24 = 96 lb, and 96 / 1.04 = 92.3 pints per day.

Derating. A federal laboratory report on dehumidifier operation rates units at 80 F and 60 percent relative humidity, and in its test the largest unit measured 121.5 pints per day against a rating of 150, about 81 percent. We use 0.81 as an assumed derate for the example: Room A needs a rated capacity of about 46.2 / 0.81 = 57 pints per day, and Room B about 92.3 / 0.81 = 114 pints per day. Round up to the next available size and add the shower and wet-floor load, which this example leaves out.

Corrosion from salt spray and residue

Salt dries into a crust that holds moisture against metal. A federal defense research report on air-conditioning coils says that in humid coastal areas severe corrosion has reduced expected equipment life by up to 50 percent, and it recommends copper over aluminum for highly corrosive settings. A university thesis that ran two air conditioners for about a year in a coastal city found latent capacity fell about 20.9 percent and sensible capacity about 4.6 percent, from galvanic corrosion where copper tubes meet aluminum fins. Those were outdoor coils in coastal air, not float rooms, so we cite them only as evidence of how salt attacks coils. In a float room, the general-practice steps are: stainless or coated fasteners, coated or plastic grilles and diffusers, a sealed or coated coil, a sealed cabinet for any fan in the air path, and a wipe-down routine. Place the supply away from the tank so droplets are not blown into it.

Quiet, low-velocity air

Float rooms are sound-sensitive, so use a larger diffuser at low throw speed and keep fans and compressors in a closet with a solid door rather than in the room. Avoid dumping air over the tank surface, which also speeds evaporation. The same quiet-air ideas appear in the day spa lesson. These are general practice.

Keeping clients from getting chilled

Wet skin cools quickly in moving air. The usual practice is to keep the float room and shower room warm, near the water temperature range, with no draft on the tank, and to avoid a supply grille aimed at the exit path. Because the water is near skin temperature, a client in a cold room will feel it most at the moment they step out.

Shower rooms between floats

Clients shower before and after a float, so the shower room adds steam and wet surfaces. Give it its own exhaust or a dedicated share of the dehumidifier, keep its door undercut or transfer path from pulling wet air into the tank room, and treat grilles there like any wet-area grille. This is general practice.

Equipment heat, drain pans and condensate

Heaters, pumps and filtration equipment give off heat in the mechanical closet, so the closet needs ventilation or its own cooling or it will cook its own gear. The dehumidifier makes liquid water all day, so the drain pan and trap must slope to the drain, stay clear of salt scale, and be checked for overflow. For related salt-air and evaporation problems, see Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air and Aquarium and Fish Room HVAC.

Field card: Float Spa HVAC: Humid Tank Rooms, Salt Corrosion and Dehumidifier Sizing

Warm water in, vapor out, salt on everything.

  • Open water gives off vapor all day; each pound evaporated carries about 1,048 Btu near 80 F.
  • Pints per day is about pounds per day divided by 1.04.
  • Rate our example used (0.08 lb/h per sq ft) is an assumption, not a measurement.
  • Derate the dehumidifier: rated at one condition, it delivers less at others.
  • Use coated or stainless parts and keep grilles and pans clear of salt crust.
  • Keep air low-velocity, quiet and warm; no draft on the tank or exit path.
  • Give the shower area its own exhaust or dehumidifier share.
