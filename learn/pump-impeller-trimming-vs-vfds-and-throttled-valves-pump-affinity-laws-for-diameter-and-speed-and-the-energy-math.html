Pump Impeller Trimming vs VFDs and Throttled Valves: Affinity Laws and Energy Math

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hydronic balance often ends with the main balancing valve half closed because the pump was sized with a cushion. That works, but the pump keeps making head the system then burns off across the valve. This lesson compares three fixes (leave it throttled, trim the impeller, slow the motor) using the affinity laws and one worked energy example.

Why a balanced system can still waste energy

The U.S. Department of Energy's Pumping Systems Tip Sheets say pumps end up oversized through conservative design, rounding up, allowance for rising pipe roughness, anticipated expansion and poorly defined requirements. Tip Sheet #6 lists throttled operation, high bypass flow and running more than 30 percent away from best efficiency point flow as signs a pump does not match its system.

After a proportional balance (see the proportional balancing lesson), a valve at the pump that has to stay mostly closed to hold design flow is that same warning sign. To see where the pump sits, review finding the operating point.

The three options

  • Leave it throttled. Zero cost, highest running cost. DOE Tip Sheet #10 notes that the power needed to push flow through a pressure drop rises with both the flow and the size of the drop.
  • Trim the impeller. A machined smaller diameter lowers tip speed, so the pump curve shifts down and to the left. The Hydraulic Institute's article says it lets a constant-speed motor stay in place, and DOE says trimming is typically used to avoid the throttling losses of control valves.
  • Slow the motor with a VFD. The drive sets a lower speed. Tip Sheet #12 says adjustable speed drives save energy by varying rotational speed, and the cube law applies in systems with no static lift. For general VFD behavior see VFDs on pumps and fans.

The affinity laws, two ways

For a change in speed N (same impeller) the standard relationships are flow proportional to N, head to N squared and power to N cubed. They are the same ones used in the fan laws lesson. The Hydraulic Institute article gives the same form for diameter D: flow scales with D, head with D squared and power with D cubed, where subscript 1 is the original impeller and 2 the trimmed one. DOE's trimming tip sheet gives the same set.

The diameter version is an approximation. It gets looser as the trim grows, because the added clearance causes more internal recirculation. The Hydraulic Institute article says its guidance allows a 5 percent reduction when using the affinity rules, and the maker's trim curves or correction charts cover bigger cuts.

Limits on trimming

  • Efficiency falls. The Hydraulic Institute article says trimming typically makes the pump less efficient. DOE says the same, but the lower head and flow usually still beat throttling or bypassing on power.
  • Do not go below the published minimum. DOE: the impeller should not be trimmed smaller than the minimum diameter shown on the performance curve. Both sources warn against trimming outside the maker's recommendations, and both give about 75 percent of the maximum diameter as a default ceiling on the trim.
  • NPSH can change. DOE says NPSH required usually rises with a smaller impeller. The Hydraulic Institute article says it usually drops at lower flow but can climb near shutoff. Either way, check it with the maker; see NPSH available vs required.
  • Pump type matters. The Hydraulic Institute article says trimming works best on radial-flow impellers, axial-flow impellers are rarely trimmed, and for multistage pumps removing stages is often better when the correction is large.
  • It is a one-way cut. Metal removed stays removed; a VFD can be turned back up.

Who trims

Neither source describes a field procedure for a technician. DOE and the Hydraulic Institute both direct engineers to the pump manufacturer for trim limits, correction charts and NPSH effects. In practice that means the pump maker or a pump repair shop machines the impeller to a diameter the maker approves, and the engineer or balancer re-verifies flow afterward.

Worked example: 300 gpm pump, 250 gpm needed

Our own example. Assumptions: a 10 in impeller pump delivers 300 gpm at 80 ft on a system curve where head scales with flow squared. Pump efficiency is 70 percent and held constant (a trim would lower it, so these savings are optimistic). Motor efficiency is 92 percent. Run time is 6,000 hours a year. Brake horsepower uses the DOE form: bhp = gpm x ft / (3,960 x efficiency).

  1. Before balancing, point A: bhp = 300 x 80 / (3,960 x 0.70) = 24,000 / 2,772 = 8.66 bhp.
  2. Balancing shows 250 gpm is enough. On the open system curve, 250 gpm needs 80 x (250/300)2 = 80 x 0.694 = 55.6 ft.
  3. Throttled, point B: we assume the original pump curve reads 88 ft at 250 gpm (an assumption, read from a curve like the sketch). bhp = 250 x 88 / 2,772 = 7.94 bhp. The valve burns 88 - 55.6 = 32.4 ft.
  4. Trimmed, point C: diameter ratio about 250/300 = 0.833, so the impeller is about 10 x 0.833 = 8.3 in (a 17 percent cut, far past the 5 percent affinity guidance, so the real diameter must come from the maker's curve). Head = 55.6 ft. bhp = 8.66 x 0.8333 = 8.66 x 0.579 = 5.01 bhp. Check: 250 x 55.6 / 2,772 = 5.01.
  5. Annual energy, throttled: 7.94 x 0.746 / 0.92 = 6.44 kW; 6.44 x 6,000 = about 38,600 kWh.
  6. Annual energy, trimmed: 5.01 x 0.746 / 0.92 = 4.06 kW; 4.06 x 6,000 = about 24,400 kWh.
  7. Saved: about 14,200 kWh a year, roughly 37 percent of the throttled figure.
  8. VFD instead: speed ratio 0.833 gives the same 55.6 ft and 5.01 bhp at the pump (on a 1,750 rpm motor, about 1,460 rpm). We assume 96 percent drive efficiency, so input is 4.06 / 0.96 = 4.23 kW, or about 25,400 kWh. Tip Sheet #12's example shows drive efficiency from 53.7 percent at its lowest flow to 95.9 percent at 1,200 gpm, so 96 is a best case.

Sensitivity: if the trimmed pump runs at 66 percent instead of 70, the trimmed bhp is 5.01 x 70/66 = 5.31, and input is about 25,900 kWh. The trim still wins over throttling, but the margin shrinks.

Choosing between them

A fixed trim fits a constant-flow system that needs a permanent correction. A VFD suits loads that vary; DOE says to consider one when a pump runs at least 2,000 hours a year and flow needs vary by 30 percent or more, and it does not save energy near full load most of the time. DOE's pump facts page also warns that VFDs fit friction-dominated systems poorly when static head is high, because low speed can push the pump toward shutoff head. In a closed hydronic loop there is little static lift, so the cube law applies.

Field card: Trim vs VFD vs throttle

A closed balancing valve is wasted pump head.

  • Affinity (diameter, approx.): Q scales with D, H with D squared, power with D cubed.
  • Speed: Q with N, H with N squared, power with N cubed (no static lift).
  • bhp = gpm x ft / (3,960 x efficiency).
  • Never trim below the minimum diameter on the maker's curve; check NPSH.
  • Trimming lowers efficiency; the maker or a pump shop does the cut.
  • Our example: 7.94 bhp throttled vs 5.01 bhp trimmed, about 14,200 kWh a year.
