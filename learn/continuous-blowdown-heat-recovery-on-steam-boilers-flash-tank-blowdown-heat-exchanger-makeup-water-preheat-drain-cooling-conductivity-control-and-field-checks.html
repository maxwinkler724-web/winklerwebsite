Continuous Blowdown Heat Recovery on Steam Boilers

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steam boiler that blows down continuously is sending a steady stream of water at boiler temperature down a drain. That water is hot, treated and already paid for. This lesson shows where that heat can be recovered with a flash tank and a small heat exchanger, and how to check that the equipment is still doing its job.

Why the blowdown water is so hot

Continuous (surface) blowdown takes water from near the top of the boiler, where dissolved solids are most concentrated. That water is at the boiler's saturation temperature, which depends on boiler pressure. At 150 psig, saturation is about 366 degrees F (our own steam-table value, rounded). A federal energy tip sheet on blowdown notes that the blowdown is at steam temperature, and that heat recovery from it is worth evaluating where blowdown is large. The same tip sheet suggests that boilers with continuous blowdown above 5% of steam flow, and higher-pressure boilers, are good candidates. For the reasons boilers blow down at all, and the difference between surface and mud blowdown, read Why Boilers Blow Down. For how much to blow down, see Calculating Steam Boiler Blowdown Rate. This lesson starts after those numbers are set.

What sets the continuous blowdown rate

Most continuous systems use a conductivity probe in the boiler water or in a sample line. Conductivity rises as dissolved solids build up. A federal energy tip sheet on steam system controls says high- or low-pressure probes measure conductivity, and that a controller compares the reading with a set point and drives a modulating blowdown valve. If conductivity climbs above the set point, the valve opens more. If it falls below, the valve closes down. A federal facility-management page on steam boilers also recommends an automatic blowdown system based on boiler water quality, because too little blowdown risks deposits and carryover while too much wastes water, energy and chemicals.

The key point for heat recovery is that the flow is steady and predictable. A flow that is continuous, and not a short manual burst, is what makes a heat recovery system worthwhile. See Boiler Water Tests for how conductivity relates to the other tests on the log sheet.

Step 1: the flash tank

Piped from the modulating valve, the blowdown goes into a flash tank, also called a flash vessel. The tank is at low pressure. When the 366-degree water enters, it is hotter than the saturation temperature at the new low pressure, so part of it boils off at once. That part is flash steam. The federal tip sheet on blowdown heat recovery describes lowering the pressure in a flash tank so a portion of the blowdown becomes low-pressure steam, usually used in the deaerator. A low-pressure header can also take it if it has a steady demand. For the general idea, see Flash Steam and Flash Tanks; the deaerator side is covered in Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics.

The same facility-management page recommends an expansion flash tank to temper blowdown instead of mixing in cold water, which throws the heat away. The steam leaves the top of the tank. The remaining hot liquid leaves from the bottom through a trap or level-controlled drain.

Step 2: the blowdown heat exchanger

The leftover liquid is still hot. A blowdown heat exchanger passes it on one side while cold makeup water flows on the other, preheating the makeup before it reaches the deaerator or feedwater system. The tip sheet describes this arrangement, with flash tank drain water going through the exchanger. The tip sheet on controls adds that these systems are not 100% efficient, and that an exchanger may pay off for boilers with blowdown as low as about 500 lb/h.

Step 3: cooling before the sewer

After the exchanger, the water is cooler, but check it before it goes down a drain. The blowdown tip sheet notes that cooling blowdown helps meet local codes that limit the temperature of liquids discharged to the sewer. As general practice, local drain temperature limits exist, the limit varies by place, and the discharge point must stay within it. Never use a temperature in this lesson as your local limit. Find out the number for your site from the local authority or the plant's own documents.

Our own worked example

Assumptions (ours): boiler at 150 psig; flash tank at 5 psig; 1,000 lb/h of continuous blowdown; water properties from a standard steam table, rounded; makeup and drain temperatures chosen for illustration.

  1. Liquid enthalpy of saturated water at 150 psig: hf high = 338.5 Btu/lb.
  2. At 5 psig: hf low = 196 Btu/lb, and heat of vaporization hfg low = 960 Btu/lb.
  3. Percent flash = (hf high minus hf low) divided by hfg low = (338.5 minus 196) divided by 960 = 142.5 divided by 960 = 0.148, or about 14.8%.
  4. Flash steam = 1,000 lb/h times 0.148 = about 148 lb/h.
  5. Liquid remaining = 1,000 minus 148 = about 852 lb/h, at roughly 227 degrees F (saturation at 5 psig).

Check: 852 lb/h of liquid plus 148 lb/h of steam gives back the 1,000 lb/h that entered, and the heat balance holds because the flash fraction is built from the enthalpy difference. Now the heat exchanger. Assume the drain water is cooled from 227 to 100 degrees F and that water holds about 1 Btu per pound per degree F. Heat recovered = 852 lb/h times 1 times (227 minus 100) = about 108,000 Btu/h passed to the makeup water. The 100-degree figure is only an assumed exit temperature, not a sewer limit and not a design target.

Field checks

  • Flash tank trap or drain. A trap stuck open blows live steam to the drain or lets the tank pressure rise. A trap stuck closed floods the tank and carries water into the steam outlet. See Steam Trap Testing Methods.
  • Tank pressure. Pressure rising above the design value means the flash steam has no outlet or demand, or the vent or relief path is blocked. Report it and follow the site procedure. Never plug or alter a relief device.
  • Heat exchanger fouling. Blowdown carries dissolved solids, and scale and sludge build up on the blowdown side. Watch for a falling makeup outlet temperature or a rising drain temperature at the same flow. Cleaning needs the exchanger isolated, drained and cooled first.
  • Sewer temperature. Read the drain temperature at the point where it leaves the plant, against the local limit for your site.
  • Probe and valve. A fouled conductivity probe reads wrong and drives the blowdown valve wrong. Compare the controller reading with a hand sample test on the schedule your water treatment plan sets.
  • Hot surfaces and discharge. Treat every line in this system as hot enough to burn. Use lockout, isolation and the site's procedure before opening anything.

Field card: Continuous Blowdown Heat Recovery

Valve, flash, exchange, cool, then drain.

  • Continuous blowdown leaves at boiler saturation temperature.
  • A flash tank drops the pressure; part of the water becomes low-pressure steam.
  • Percent flash = (hf high minus hf low) divided by hfg low.
  • Leftover hot water preheats makeup water in a heat exchanger.
  • Check the drain temperature against your local limit.
  • A conductivity probe and modulating valve set the blowdown rate.
  • Check the trap, exchanger fouling and sewer temperature.
