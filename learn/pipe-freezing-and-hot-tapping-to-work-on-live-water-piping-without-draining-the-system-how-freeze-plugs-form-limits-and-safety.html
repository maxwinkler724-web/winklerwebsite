Pipe Freezing and Hot Tapping on Live Water Piping

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A big chilled or hot water loop is expensive to empty. Pipe freezing and hot tapping are two ways specialists work on a pipe while the rest of the system stays full. This lesson explains how a freeze plug forms, what defeats it, where the hazards are, and when draining is the better call.

Why plants avoid draining

A plant pays several ways to drain a loop. The glycol must be caught and reused or disposed of, the treatment chemicals go with it and must be dosed again, and refilling takes time plus venting out every pocket of air. Yale's published standards show how owners think: they ask that isolation valves be placed so equipment can be serviced "without draining complete or substantial portions of the system," and they ask the designer to flag any job that would need a pipe freeze, wet tap or hot tap.

The first choice is a working isolation valve. If one is seized or missing, the choices are drain, freeze or tap. See exercising isolation valves.

How a freeze plug works

Pipe freezing builds a temporary plug of ice inside the pipe by chilling the outside of it. A maker's guide describes the idea: the fluid in the pipe freezes into a solid barrier, the isolated section is worked on while the rest of the system runs, and the plug is thawed in a controlled way afterward. A cold source is wrapped around the pipe in a jacket or bag. Small kits use an electric refrigeration unit or a CO2 cylinder. A maker FAQ lists CO2 kits for pipe from 2 up to 8 inches depending on the model, and a liquid nitrogen kit up to 12 inches. Yale's utility guide says a chilled water wet tap by freezing "has been successfully performed on 24" piping," but that is specialist work.

Time matters. A 2024 peer-reviewed study froze water in cast iron pipe using a liquid nitrogen jacket in a lab, in 1.5 meter lengths with no flow. It took about 21 minutes in 75 mm (3 inch) pipe, about 46 minutes in 100 mm (4 inch) and about 60 minutes in 150 mm (6 inch). Each is one test, so treat them as an order of magnitude. The authors also say the method cannot be used on plastic pipe such as PE and PVC, because heat moves through it too slowly.

What defeats a freeze

  • Flow. A maker FAQ says all CO2 systems require that the water not be moving. A maker article says static fluids freeze more predictably and moving fluid can prevent a plug from forming. Stop flow first.
  • Hot water. The same FAQ says water that is too warm may never freeze, and warmer lines take longer and use more CO2.
  • Glycol. Solutions with glycols or other additives have lower freezing points and may need more cooling, per a maker article. The CO2 FAQ adds that glycol concentration is hard to know, so sample the line and run a test freeze.
  • Pipe size and wall. Bigger pipe needs more coolant, and thick or insulated walls slow heat transfer. In the study, the 150 mm pipe took nearly three times longer than the 75 mm pipe.
  • Plastic pipe. The CO2 FAQ says plastic takes 3 to 4 times as long and uses 3 to 4 times as much CO2, and warns never to freeze thin wall plastic pipe.

A maker article calls confirming a fully formed plug critical before anyone relies on it.

Safety

Trapped pressure

Water expands as it freezes. If liquid is trapped between the ice and a closed valve, cap or fitting, it has nowhere to go and pressure can climb. A maker article warns of dangerous pressure buildup in isolated sections and calls for relief or venting. In the lab study, pressure in the trapped section rose to about 30 kg/cm2 (about 430 psi, our own conversion) in the 75 mm pipe and about 12 kg/cm2 (about 170 psi) in the 150 mm pipe. That is the diagram's warning zone. Do not freeze beside a closed valve or fitting unless the written procedure provides a relief path.

Nitrogen and confined spaces

Liquid nitrogen boils at -320 F (-196 C) and expands about 700 to 1 as it turns to gas, per a college safety guideline. In a pit, vault, mechanical room or trench, that gas pushes out air. OSHA defines an oxygen deficient atmosphere as below 19.5 percent oxygen (29 CFR 1910.134). The Chemical Safety Board's nitrogen bulletin says 4 to 6 percent oxygen causes coma in less than 40 seconds, nitrogen has no smell, and from 1992 to 2002 it found 85 workplace nitrogen asphyxiation incidents with 80 deaths. Over 60 percent of the 130 deaths and injuries involved someone in or next to a confined space. It recommends continuous monitoring and forced ventilation. Treat a pit or vault freeze as a permit-required confined space job and read confined spaces in HVAC work.

Frostbite and PPE

The same college guideline says cryogenic fluids cause burns, frostbite and eye damage. It calls for a face shield plus unvented goggles, loose-fitting insulated cryogenic gloves that can be shed fast, long non-absorbent sleeves, and closed shoes with cuffless pants over the tops. A maker article says frostbite can occur within seconds. Lock out pumps and valves that could change pressure; see lockout and verify dead.

Hot tapping basics

A hot tap adds a connection to a live pressurized pipe without shutting it down. In general terms (our own plain-language summary), a saddle, boss or welded fitting goes on the main, a valve goes on the fitting, and a tapping machine bolted to the valve drills through the open valve, then backs out so the valve can close. The fitting is pressure tested before drilling, per a maker article. A hot tap makes a permanent branch; a freeze is temporary isolation.

Both are specialist jobs. Yale's utility guide says hot or wet taps "must be performed by qualified personnel who have equipment and experience." One maker article calls for careful planning and a risk assessment. Expect written procedures and a pressure test.

Worked example: drain or freeze

Question: what glycol is lost draining one section?

Table value. The Copper Development Association tube table lists Type L copper at 1.39 gal per foot for 6 inch nominal. We use that as a stand-in for 6 inch pipe. Steel pipe has different inside diameters, so check your pipe.

Assumptions (ours, not from a source): section length 300 ft; glycol 30 percent by volume; glycol price $12 per gallon; all glycol is lost; no labor, water or treatment cost counted.

  1. Water volume: 1.39 gal/ft x 300 ft = 417 gal.
  2. Glycol in that volume: 417 x 0.30 = 125 gal.
  3. Glycol cost: 125 x $12 = $1,500.

Reusing the glycol shrinks the loss but needs tanks, pumps and time. Refilling 417 gal at an assumed 50 gpm is about 8 minutes of fill (417 / 50 = 8.3), but air removal and re-dosing add much more. A freeze job costs a specialist's day, a quote you must get yourself, and the risks above. If the loop is glycol, hot, flowing, very large or has no safe relief path, the freeze may not hold, and draining is the right answer.

When to just drain

Drain when the pipe is plastic, the fluid is glycol-rich or hot, flow cannot be stopped, a closed valve would trap liquid beside the plug, the space has no monitored ventilation, or the section is short and the fluid cheap.

After any refill, flush and test as in hydrostatic testing and flushing.

Field card: Pipe Freezing and Hot Tapping

Freeze needs still water, no trapped liquid and clean air.

  • No flow, no hot water, little or no glycol, metal pipe: those favor a freeze.
  • Trapped water between ice and a closed valve can build pressure.
  • Nitrogen gas pushes out air: monitor oxygen, ventilate, 19.5 percent is the OSHA floor.
  • Freezes and hot taps are specialist work with written procedures.
  • Example: 1.39 gal/ft x 300 ft = 417 gal; 30 percent glycol = 125 gal.
