Retail Store and Mall Tenant HVAC: Display Lighting Heat, LED Retrofits, Storefront Doors, Fitting Rooms and Mall Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-30

A tenant space in a mall or strip center looks simple: a box with a front door, a sales floor, a stockroom and a rooftop unit. The service calls are not. Display lights change the load, the front door fights the weather, fitting rooms bake, and the air balance with the mall decides whether the door opens easily. This lesson walks a tech through each piece.

How to read this page

Every store has its own drawings, lease and landlord rules, and the equipment maker's manual always wins. Where a number comes from a public source we name it. Where we describe ordinary field practice without a source, we say it is our own description. Nothing here is legal advice about any lease.

What the equipment usually looks like

A DOE national laboratory design guide for small retail assumed one packaged rooftop unit (RTU) per store in a strip mall, and a standalone store split into three RTUs, each controlling one thermal zone. That matches what techs see: a single-zone packaged unit with direct-expansion cooling and gas heat, one thermostat, and ductwork across the ceiling. Enclosed-mall stores may instead take air from a landlord system, so check which one serves your store before you touch anything (our own advice). See our rooftop unit PM lesson for upkeep.

Display lighting heat and what LEDs changed

Stores light merchandise hard. A DOE page says LEDs emit very little heat, while incandescent bulbs release about 90 percent of their energy as heat. For the building, the useful number is the connected watts, because nearly all electric power in a lit store ends up as heat in the space (our own physics note). Fewer watts means a smaller internal load.

Three public data points show how far retail lighting density has fallen. A DOE lab design guide used a 1999-standard baseline of 2.10 W/ft² of general lighting on the sales area, before accent lighting. An Oregon code office training deck lists the retail sales area allowance in Standard 90.1 as 1.22 W/ft² in the 2016 edition and 1.05 W/ft² in the 2019 edition. A national laboratory report on stock-wide LED measures lists modeled retail lighting power density falling from 1.17 W/ft² in its first lighting generation to 0.54 in its third and 0.42 in its fifth.

The same report found that an LED measure across the U.S. commercial stock cut interior lighting electricity 37.0 percent and cooling electricity 4.3 percent, but raised heating use: heating electricity by 6.4 percent and heating gas by 5.0 percent. Those are stock-wide averages, not your store. A 1998 lab study reached the same direction: less lighting energy lowers cooling and raises heating, and the net depends on building type and climate. A DOE retrofit guide adds a practical point: after loads drop, a smaller rooftop unit may suit the building better. Size any replacement unit from a fresh load calculation, not the old nameplate.

Worked example: lighting drop to tons freed

This is our own example. The inputs are assumptions, chosen from the public ranges above, not measurements.

  1. Assume 8,000 ft² of sales floor, existing lighting at 1.17 W/ft² and an LED retrofit at 0.54 W/ft².
  2. Density drop: 1.17 - 0.54 = 0.63 W/ft².
  3. Power drop: 0.63 x 8,000 = 5,040 W.
  4. Convert to heat. NIST lists 0.2930711 W per Btu/h (International Table), so 1 W is 1 / 0.2930711 = 3.412 Btu/h. 5,040 x 3.412 = 17,196 Btu/h, about 17,200.
  5. Tons. A DOE lab guide says one ton equals 12,000 Btu/h. 17,196 / 12,000 = 1.43 tons.

So about 1.4 tons of the cooling load goes away when the lights are on. If we assume the store has a 20-ton RTU, that is 1.43 / 20 = about 7 percent. Caveats: this treats all of the lighting heat as hitting the space at the design hour, and real loads lag and split between space and return air, so use a load calculation before you resize anything. In winter the same 17,196 Btu/h is heat the furnace now has to make while the lights are on.

Storefront door, vestibules and air curtains

Every opening is a leak. A PNNL report on Standard 90.1 vestibules modeled infiltration through retail doors. Peak infiltration for a standalone retail store was 1,986 cfm with a vestibule and 3,006 cfm without. For a large store in a strip mall it was 511 cfm with and 824 cfm without; for a small strip store, 285 and 471. Door traffic drives it: the report counted a peak of 153 openings per hour for standalone retail and 34 and 16 for large and small strip stores. The Standard 90.1 vestibule rule it quotes needs self-closing doors at least 7 ft apart with exceptions by climate and size. Doors that open from a conditioned mall to a conditioned store are usually not an outdoor entrance (our own note), but a store with an exterior entrance is. For air curtain sizing and the energy code alternative, see our air curtain lesson, and for a different big-door building see the car dealership lesson.

Pressure to the mall

EPA's building air quality guide explains the rule: air moves from higher to lower pressure through any opening, and a room becomes positive when it gets more supply air than it exhausts. It says lobbies are designed to run positive to limit unconditioned air entering. Whether a tenant is held slightly positive or slightly negative to the mall is set by the landlord's tenant criteria and the balance report. We found no public source for a mall rule, so confirm it. A tech's clues: a door that is hard to open or whistles points to a strongly positive store; a door that pulls open and brings in mall odor points to negative, often from stockroom or restroom exhaust with too little make-up air.

Fitting rooms, stockrooms and zoning

This part is our own field description. Fitting rooms are small, lit, closed and full of people for a few minutes at a time, with one thermostat on the sales floor deciding for all of them. Check for a closed or missing supply, no way for air to leave (undercut door, transfer grille), and heavy display lighting. Stockrooms are mostly storage with few people, so they get less supply and often serve as the store's exhaust path; ask where the air goes. A stockroom with its own thermostat or zone damper is nicer than letting the sales floor sensor overcool the back.

Outdoor air and holiday crowds

Ventilation is set by ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (named in plain text; we do not link copies). A student report that quoted the 2007 edition lists retail sales at 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.12 cfm per ft², with a default of 15 people per 1,000 ft², and mall walkways at 7.5 cfm per person, 0.06 cfm per ft² and 40 people per 1,000 ft². Later editions may differ, so use your local code's current table.

Our own arithmetic for the 8,000 ft² floor: people = 15 x 8,000 / 1,000 = 120. Per person: 7.5 x 120 = 900 cfm. Per area: 0.12 x 8,000 = 960 cfm. Total about 1,860 cfm of breathing-zone outdoor air, before any zone adjustments. The DOE guide recommends CO2 control for demand-controlled ventilation and motorized outdoor-air dampers; see our DCV lesson. On holiday weekends the crowd is above the default density, the door opens more, and the load rises. A DOE-lab study found door-opening frequency swings infiltration by about plus or minus 25 percent for a 30 percent change (a restaurant case, so only a hint here).

After-hours setbacks and early start

A national laboratory scheduling guide says setback limits are typically 55 to 62°F for heating and 78 to 85°F for cooling, warns wide limits slow morning recovery, and says optimal start typically begins 0 to 30 minutes before occupancy, with 120 minutes as a recommended maximum. It also says to schedule minimum outdoor air to zero when unoccupied. A Berkeley lab guide says to review schedules quarterly and notes that thermostats can hold special-event and holiday schedules. For holiday extended hours, change the schedule rather than overriding it and then forgetting.

Who maintains what (general)

Leases differ, so read yours. This is a general description, not legal advice. Often the tenant handles the RTU that serves only its store, thermostat, ductwork, diffusers and store lighting, while the landlord handles the roof, mall common-area air and base-building services. Roof access and penetrations are commonly shared. A DOE retrofit guide advises including tenants early and scheduling work outside business hours.

Field card: Retail Tenant HVAC

Lights, door, balance, schedule.

  • Watts x 3.412 = Btu/h; Btu/h / 12,000 = tons.
  • Example: 8,000 ft² at 1.17 down to 0.54 W/ft² frees 17,196 Btu/h, about 1.4 tons.
  • LEDs lower cooling load and raise heating load.
  • Door traffic drives infiltration; vestibules and air curtains cut it.
  • Air moves high to low pressure; check door effort and mall odor.
  • Fitting rooms: check supply, air exit and lights.
