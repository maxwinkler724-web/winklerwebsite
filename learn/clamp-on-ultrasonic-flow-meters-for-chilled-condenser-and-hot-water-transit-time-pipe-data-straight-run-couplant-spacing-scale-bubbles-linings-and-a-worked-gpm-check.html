Clamp-On Ultrasonic Flow Meters on Water Pipes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Sometimes you need to know how much water a pump is really moving, and there is no flow meter in the pipe and no way to cut one in. A clamp-on ultrasonic meter straps to the outside of the pipe and reads flow without a pipe penetration. It is a handy tool for chilled water, condenser water and hot water loops, but only if you feed it the right pipe data and put it on a good spot. This lesson covers how it works, how to set it up, what ruins a reading, and how to check the answer.

How it measures, in plain words

Two transducers sit on the outside of the pipe. Each one sends a short sound pulse through the pipe wall and the water to the other. A pulse traveling with the flow arrives slightly sooner than one traveling against it. That is the transit-time principle. The meter measures the tiny time difference and converts it to an average water velocity. A federal water agency manual chapter on transit-time flowmeters describes the same idea with pulses sent along a diagonal path, and notes the meter must be programmed with the pipe material, diameter and wall thickness. A national laboratory report prepared for the federal energy department describes transit-time meters as commonly found in non-invasive, clamp-on units that are easy to install on the outside of the pipe.

Do not confuse this with the other common type. A Doppler-style ultrasonic meter depends on bubbles or particles in the fluid to reflect sound. A national laboratory field-test guide describes that type as measuring fluids containing bubbles or particulates. Transit-time meters are the usual choice for clean water loops, so check which type you are holding before you trust the instructions.

Pipe data you must enter

The meter does not measure flow directly. It measures velocity, then multiplies by the cross-sectional area it calculates from your inputs. So the numbers you type matter as much as the sensors do.

  • Pipe material. Sound travels at different speeds in steel, copper, plastic and cast iron, so the meter needs the material to plan its sound path.
  • Outside diameter and wall thickness. These give the inside diameter. A state university extension fact sheet on portable ultrasonic meters calls wall thickness a critical input and says a wrong value can throw flow readings off by a significant margin. It also reports that the meter software uses the material and wall thickness to calculate transducer spacing.
  • Liner and coating. If the pipe has an internal liner, its thickness and type are separate inputs. Paint can sometimes be added into the lining entry, according to that same fact sheet.

Use the real wall thickness if you can measure it, not just a table value. The same fact sheet reports one pipe whose measured wall ranged from about 0.20 to 0.33 inch, which it attributes partly to pitting, rust and scale.

Where to put the meter: straight pipe

Elbows, valves, strainers and pumps twist the water and skew the velocity profile. The meter assumes a smooth, symmetrical profile. A university extension guide on irrigation ultrasonic meters gives common guidance of 5 to 10 pipe diameters of straight, unobstructed pipe upstream of the meter and at least 1 diameter downstream, and recommends at least 10 diameters upstream where possible. The state university fact sheet says manufacturers generally suggest at least 10 diameters from fittings, and that some disturbances need more for 5 percent accuracy.

Both sources show how fast errors grow when you crowd a fitting. For a 90-degree elbow, the fact sheet lists a correction factor of 1.15 with plus or minus 20 percent at 2 diameters, 1.05 with plus or minus 3 percent at 6 diameters, and 1.02 at 10 diameters. Those figures came from tests on specific devices, so treat them as a warning of the trend, not as a correction you apply to a real job. On a plant room with tight piping, the practical answer is to find the longest straight section you can, and to follow the meter maker's instructions for the fitting types nearby. Mounting also matters: pick a spot where the pipe is full, which usually means a vertical rise with upward flow or a horizontal run away from the top of the pipe where air collects.

Couplant and transducer spacing

Sound does not cross an air gap well, so each transducer needs couplant, a gel or grease, between its face and the pipe. The state university fact sheet says the bead should spread evenly with full contact, with no air bubbles or drying, and that over-tightening a transducer can warp the contact area and cause erratic readings. Clean the pipe to bare metal where the sensors sit if it is painted or rough, and remove any fibrous coating or metal mesh.

Spacing is the distance between the two transducers along the pipe. The meter calculates it from the pipe data you entered, and you set the sensors to that distance. The fact sheet describes direct (Z), single-reflection (V) and multiple-reflection (W) mounting, with the direct style often used on larger pipes and the reflected styles on smaller ones. It also reports that in lab tests, a 1 millimeter shift in spacing changed the measured flow by about 1 percent. Measure the spacing with a tape, not by eye, and keep the two sensors in line with each other.

Why readings go bad

  • Scale and corrosion. Layers on the pipe wall or inside it absorb and scatter the signal. The fact sheet says severe corrosion can overestimate the inside diameter, rough interiors can cause up to about 4 percent error if not accounted for, and a direct (Z) layout is often better on corroded pipe. It also advises caution if readings swing by 25 percent or the meter has trouble finding the wall signal.
  • Air bubbles. A federal national laboratory water-metering guide advises against using these meters on water that contains air bubbles, because they affect accuracy, and the federal water agency chapter says entrained gas or suspended sediment weakens the signal. The fact sheet suggests bleeding air where possible and checking that the top of a full pipe feels as cold as the bottom. Fresh fills, open tower basins, pumps with suction air leaks and unbled hot water loops are the usual suspects.
  • Lined pipe. The sound has to cross the liner, which may not carry it well, and the liner changes the effective wall. Enter the liner data, try a different mounting style, or pick a spot where the pipe is unlined.
  • Wrong inputs. A typo in outside diameter or wall thickness quietly scales every reading.

Check the reading against something

A clamp-on number is a measurement with an error band, so compare it with other evidence. Look up the pump's flow at the operating condition on its published curve (see Reading Pump Curves and System Curves), compare it with the coil or loop design flow on the schedule, and cross-check with the temperature difference across the load, as in Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T. If the meter and the curve disagree by a lot, suspect your inputs and mounting spot before you blame the pump. If you want to build flow from gauge readings instead, see Estimating Circulator Flow from Gauge Readings. Permanent in-line flow meters used for energy billing are covered in BTU Meters and HVAC Submetering.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a clamp-on meter on a 3-inch steel hot water branch, correctly mounted with good couplant and enough straight pipe. The meter reports a velocity of 4.2 ft/s. We assume the pipe inside diameter is 3.068 inches and the design flow on the schedule is 100 gpm. The conversion factor 448.8 is the number of gallons per minute in one cubic foot per second (7.48 gallons per cubic foot times 60 seconds).

  1. Convert the inside diameter to feet: 3.068 in / 12 = 0.2557 ft.
  2. Area = 3.1416 / 4 x 0.2557 x 0.2557 = 0.05134 square feet.
  3. Flow in cubic feet per second = 0.05134 x 4.2 = 0.2156.
  4. Flow in gpm = 0.2156 x 448.8 = 96.8 gpm.
  5. Compare: 96.8 / 100 = 0.968, so the loop is moving about 96.8 percent of design, or about 3.2 percent below it.

That is close enough that, with the meter's error band, we would call it on design. Now see why inputs matter. If the wall thickness had been entered wrong so the meter used a 3.168 inch inside diameter, the area would be larger by (3.168 / 3.068) squared, about 1.066, so the same velocity would be reported as about 103 gpm. A 0.1 inch input slip produces a 6.6 percent flow error, which is larger than the gap we were trying to judge.

Field card: Clamp-On Ultrasonic Flow Meters on Water Pipes

Good data in, good spot, good couplant, then compare.

  • Transit-time meters time a pulse with the flow and against it; the time difference gives velocity.
  • Enter material, outside diameter, wall thickness and any liner.
  • Find the longest straight pipe you can; follow the maker's run lengths.
  • Bead of couplant, full contact, no air, spacing from the meter's calculation.
  • Scale, bubbles and liners weaken the signal; bleed air and clean the pipe.
  • Gpm = area (sq ft) x velocity (ft/s) x 448.8.
  • Compare with the pump curve and the design flow before you act.
