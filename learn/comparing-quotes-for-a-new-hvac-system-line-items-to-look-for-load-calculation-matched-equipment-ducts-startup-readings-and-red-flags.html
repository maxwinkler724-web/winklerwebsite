Comparing Quotes for a New HVAC System: Line Items, Load Calculation, Matched Equipment, Ducts, Startup Readings and Red Flags

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Two quotes for the same house can look alike and describe very different jobs. One lists every step in plain terms. The other says "replace unit" and leaves the rest to trust. This lesson shows the line items a good written quote carries, how to lay two quotes side by side, which red flags matter, and what to ask when something is missing.

General information only. This page is education, not legal advice and not a recommendation of any contractor. Quote contents and local rules vary. Ask your local building department what permits apply to your job.

Why the paper matters

The U.S. Department of Energy's building science pages list proper sizing and component matching, sealed ducts, correct airflow and correct refrigerant charge as parts of a quality installation, and say savings from better installations may range from 18% to 36% for air conditioners and heat pumps. Those are results of work done in your house, so they should show up on the quote as work, not as adjectives. The FTC's consumer advice adds that a written estimate should describe the work, the materials, the completion date and the price, that you should get several, and that you should ask for an explanation when they differ a lot.

Line items to look for

  • Load calculation. A written heating and cooling load for your house, ideally room by room, using the national residential load method (see Heat Load Calculation Intro). A national-lab replacement checklist says a quote that never mentions a load calculation or equipment selection after it may not follow good practice. Equipment size is then chosen from that result, as explained in picking equipment size after the load calculation.
  • Equipment model numbers and matched rating. Every piece named by full model number: outdoor unit, indoor coil, furnace or air handler, and thermostat. For air conditioners and heat pumps, ask for the paperwork showing that exact indoor and outdoor combination has a published rating. See Matched systems: why indoor and outdoor units must match.
  • Efficiency ratings. The heating and cooling efficiency ratings for the combination being installed (for example SEER2, HSPF2 or AFUE, whichever apply), not just the outdoor unit's best case.
  • Duct changes. Whether ducts are sealed, repaired, resized or left alone, and whether the existing ducts can carry the new equipment's airflow. A federal installation bid sheet asks contractors to check the maximum system size the existing ductwork can support and to test leakage. Our lesson on duct leakage testing shows what that test means.
  • Line set. Replaced or reused. The same federal bid sheet asks for new refrigerant lines rather than reusing old ones. If a contractor proposes reuse, the quote should say how the lines will be cleaned and pressure tested.
  • Electrical. Disconnect, breaker and wire size checked against the new equipment's nameplate. The national-lab checklist notes that electrical scope is not covered by the checklist, so ask each contractor to state it, because it is a common place for quotes to differ.
  • Condensate. Where the drain goes, the trap, the slope, and any safety switch or secondary pan.
  • Permits and inspection. Who pulls the permit and who is present at the inspection. The FTC lists a contractor asking you to get required permits yourself as a warning sign.
  • Startup and commissioning readings. A written record of measured values at startup: electrical readings, airflow or static pressure, temperature rise or split, refrigerant charge and, for new refrigerant lines, vacuum. The national-lab checklist calls for charge verified against the maker's instructions and airflow across the coil tested. See recording startup readings for a sample sheet.
  • Removal and refrigerant recovery. Old equipment removed, and the refrigerant recovered instead of vented. EPA's rules prohibit intentionally venting refrigerants when servicing or disposing of equipment, with narrow exceptions for tiny losses during a genuine recovery effort.
  • Warranty terms in writing. What is covered, for how long, whether labor is included, and what registration or maintenance the maker requires. "Manufacturer warranty" alone is not a term.

Making it apples to apples

Build one grid with the line items down the side and one column per quote. Mark each cell as shown, vague or missing. A missing item is not a zero; it is a question. Where one quote includes duct sealing and another does not, you are not comparing the same job until you ask the second contractor to quote that work too. Ask each contractor to revise to the same scope and send it back in writing, then compare. The FTC also says not to pick a bid automatically because it is lowest, and to ask for an explanation when bids differ.

Red flags

  • Sizing by square foot. "Your house is this many square feet so you need this many tons" skips insulation, windows, leakage, sun exposure and ducts. DOE names oversizing as a common installation problem.
  • Sizing by the old unit only. The old unit may have been wrong, and the house may have changed. If short cycling or humid rooms were your complaint, see AC short cycling.
  • No model numbers, or only a brand-free description such as "high efficiency unit".
  • Duct work described only as "inspect".
  • Pressure to sign today, full payment up front or cash only (all listed by the FTC as warning signs).
  • Verbal promises that are not in the written scope.

Worked example, our own

Our own example. The house and both quotes are invented. Assumptions: a two-story house with a central furnace and a split air conditioner, the same house quoted by two contractors, and no prices shown because the point is scope. Quote A and Quote B each come back as one page.

Follow-up questions we would send to Contractor B:

  1. Please send the room-by-room load calculation, or tell us when you will do one, and show how the equipment size follows from it.
  2. Please list full model numbers for the outdoor unit, coil, furnace and thermostat, with the matched-rating listing for the combination.
  3. What ducts will be sealed or changed, and will you test leakage and airflow afterward?
  4. If you plan to reuse the line set, how will you clean and pressure test it?
  5. What electrical work is included: disconnect, breaker, wire?
  6. Who pulls the permit, and who meets the inspector?
  7. Will we receive a written startup sheet with your measured readings?
  8. Who recovers the old refrigerant, and is that written on the quote?
  9. Please attach the full warranty terms, including labor.

For Quote A, our follow-up is shorter: confirm the warranty registration steps and ask for the startup sheet to be signed and dated. If B answers every question in writing, the two quotes become comparable. If B does not, that is useful information too.

Questions to ask in the meeting

Ask who will actually do the work, how long it takes, how the house will be protected, and what happens if the startup readings are off. Our lesson what to ask an HVAC contractor before hiring covers the contractor side. If the old system is still repairable, read how long HVAC systems last first.

Field card: Comparing HVAC quotes

If it is not in writing, it is a question, not a feature.

  • Load calculation attached, equipment sized from it.
  • Full model numbers and a matched rating for the combination.
  • Ducts, line set, electrical and condensate each addressed.
  • Permit, inspection, startup readings and refrigerant recovery named.
  • Warranty terms attached, labor included or excluded stated.
  • Red flags: sizing by square foot or by the old unit, no model numbers, pressure to sign.
