Busy and Slow Seasons in HVAC Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

HVAC work does not arrive evenly. A new tech can go from a week of waiting for the phone to ring to a week of twelve-hour days, and the weather decides which one it is. This lesson explains why, how to stay safe and rested in the rush, and what to do with the slow weeks.

Why demand spikes with the weather

A system that has been idle for months is asked to run flat out on the first hot or cold day, and weak parts tend to show themselves then: a tired capacitor, a dirty coil, a failed igniter. That is why the first heat wave and the first cold snap bring a burst of no-cool and no-heat calls all at once. The federal occupational outlook page for HVAC mechanics and installers says technicians may be on call for emergencies and sometimes work overtime or irregular schedules during peak heating and cooling seasons. It also says most work full time, with some evening or weekend shifts. The same page puts most jobs, 68 percent, with plumbing, heating and air-conditioning contractors, which is where this swing is felt most.

A first heat wave is also a trap for people. A hot day after a mild stretch is exactly when bodies are least ready, which is covered below.

Long days and overtime

During a surge, the schedule fills from both ends: early starts, calls added at the end of the day, and on-call weekends. Extra hours can mean a bigger paycheck, but we will not discuss pay here. The point for a new tech is that long days change how you work. The federal worker-health research institute lists extended work hours, stress, physically demanding tasks and hot environments among the causes of fatigue. Its fatigue page says fatigue slows reaction time and weakens attention, memory and judgment. A related article says fatigued workers may take more risks that lead to injury or errors, and gives estimates that close to 1 in 8 workplace injuries may relate to fatigue and that more than 1 in 5 fatal vehicle crashes may involve a drowsy driver. Those are research estimates, not exact counts, but they explain why the drive home after a long day deserves as much respect as the roof. Our lesson on fatigue, long shifts and on-call work goes deeper.

Heat on rooftops and in attics

The federal worker safety agency says hazardous heat can occur indoors or outdoors and can occur in any season if conditions are right, not only in a heat wave. Risk factors include heavy physical activity, hot conditions, lack of acclimatization and clothing that traps heat. Attics and rooftop units combine several of these at once.

Acclimatization matters most in the first rush. The agency's page for new workers says a new worker should do only 20 percent of a normal day in the heat on the first day and add 20 percent each day after that. Most fit workers reach a full schedule by the end of the first week, though some need up to 14 days. It adds that the same care applies to people returning after an absence of a week or more, to workers in the first spring or early-summer warm-up, and to anyone facing a day much hotter than the days before it. The worker-health institute's heat recommendations add that cool water should be kept nearby, that workers should use a buddy system to watch each other for signs of heat illness, and that new employees should be closely supervised for the first 14 days or until acclimatized. In real life a new tech cannot dictate the schedule, so speak up early, drink water on the clock and take shade breaks. See heat stress in attics and on roofs for warning signs.

Shoulder seasons: spring and fall

Between the extremes, the weather is mild and systems rest. These shoulder weeks are not empty. Spring is when cooling systems get checked before they are needed, and fall is when heating systems get the same. Work in these weeks is more often scheduled than emergency: tune-ups, planned replacements, and installs that were sold earlier. Hours usually sit between the two extremes. Shoulder weeks are also the best time to take on longer jobs that would be hard to fit in a rush.

How shops fill slow weeks

This part is our own general description of common practice, not a sourced statistic, and shops differ.

  • Maintenance agreements and tune-ups. A shop with a list of customers on yearly visits can schedule that work in slow weeks, which smooths out the year. Our tune-up checklist shows what a visit covers.
  • Installs and replacements. Planned changeouts are often booked for mild weather, when a home can be without a system for a day without hardship.
  • Training. Slow weeks are when a shop can put techs through product and safety training without losing a day of emergency calls.
  • Shop and fleet work. Restocking, cleaning, and equipment repair that was put off in the rush.

What a new tech can do in slow weeks

  • Study. Review the basics you rushed past, and work through an exam study guide at your own pace.
  • Organize the van. Restock, label bins, and check meters, leads and tools so the next surge starts clean. See getting ready before a service call.
  • Ride along. Ask to go with an experienced tech on installs or diagnostic calls. Our lesson on learning from an experienced tech has habits that help.
  • Log your own times. Slow weeks are a calm time to practice the timing habits in tracking your own job times.

Planning your own time and money, in general terms

This is not financial advice. The idea is only that hours are uneven, so a plan built on the best weeks will feel wrong in the slow ones. Many people think in yearly averages rather than weekly ones, keep a cushion for slow stretches, and protect rest during busy weeks: fixed sleep time, no stacking of social plans on top of an on-call weekend, and one real day off when the shop allows it. Ask your employer how slow weeks are handled before you assume anything.

Commercial and plant work: a different rhythm

Residential service follows outdoor temperature closely, so it swings hard. Commercial and plant work has the same weather pulses, but more of its work is planned: seasonal startups of chillers and boilers, scheduled preventive maintenance, shutdown windows, and controls projects. Buildings that must stay conditioned, such as hospitals and data rooms, also call for round-the-clock coverage, so on-call duty can continue in a mild week. Plant and facilities staff often work steadier shifts than residential service techs, though they can still see a rush in the first cold snap when heating plants come online. Worked example: a year of uneven hours (our own example)

These numbers are our own assumptions, not data for any shop. Assume a 52-week year with three kinds of weeks:

  • Busy weeks: 12 weeks at 55 hours (assumed).
  • Shoulder weeks: 22 weeks at 42 hours (assumed).
  • Slow weeks: 18 weeks at 34 hours (assumed).

Check the weeks: 12 + 22 + 18 = 52.

Hours in each group: 12 × 55 = 660. 22 × 42 = 924. 18 × 34 = 612.

Total hours: 660 + 924 + 612 = 2,196 hours.

Average week: 2,196 ÷ 52 = 42.23 hours, or about 42.2.

What it shows: the busy weeks are 12 of 52, about 23 percent of the weeks (12 ÷ 52 = 0.231), but they hold 660 of 2,196 hours, about 30 percent (660 ÷ 2,196 = 0.301). A busy week is 21 hours longer than a slow one (55 − 34 = 21). So an average of 42 hours hides weeks you will feel as very different, which is why rest and planning matter in the surge, and why the slow weeks are a good place to study.

Field card: Busy and Slow Seasons in HVAC Work

Expect surges, protect your rest, use the slow weeks.

  • First heat wave and first cold snap bring a burst of calls at once.
  • Long days raise fatigue risk; the drive home counts.
  • Ease into heat: 20 percent of a normal day first, adding 20 percent a day, up to 14 days for some.
  • Drink water on the clock and use a buddy to watch for heat illness.
  • Shoulder weeks: tune-ups and planned installs.
  • Slow weeks: study, organize the van, ride along.
  • Our assumed year: 2,196 hours over 52 weeks is about 42.2 hours per week.
