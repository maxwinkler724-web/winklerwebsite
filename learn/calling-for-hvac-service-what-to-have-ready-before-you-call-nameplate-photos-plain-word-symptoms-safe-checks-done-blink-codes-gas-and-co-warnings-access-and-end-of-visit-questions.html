Calling for HVAC Service: What to Have Ready Before You Call

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A good service call starts before the phone rings. When you can read the equipment label, describe what the system is doing in plain words, and say what you already checked, the person on the other end can book the right visit and the technician arrives better prepared. This lesson is a call-ready list, plus a filled-in sample sheet you can copy.

Safety first: gas smell or a carbon monoxide alarm

If you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, do not start a checklist. Leave the building and call for help from outside. The federal consumer product safety agency says that when a carbon monoxide alarm sounds you should move outside to fresh air right away, call emergency services or 911, make sure everyone is accounted for, and not go back in until responders say it is safe. It also says not to try to find the source yourself. A federal pipeline safety page gives the same pattern for natural gas: leave on foot, avoid anything that could spark such as light switches and doorbells, and call from a safe location once you are clear. Carbon monoxide has no smell, so an alarm is the warning, not your nose. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Is anyone at risk from heat or cold?

Tell the dispatcher at the start if anyone in the home is more vulnerable. The federal public health agency lists people 65 and older, infants and young children, pregnant women, and people with asthma or heart disease as higher risk in heat, and notes that older adults may take medicines that affect how the body controls temperature. That page also says a fan alone is not a good main cooling method in very hot weather, and that people without cooling can look for a cooling center through local health or emergency offices. The same thinking applies in cold: an infant, an elderly relative or someone with a medical condition makes a no-heat call more urgent. If anyone feels ill, get medical help first. The equipment can wait.

What to gather before you call

  • Nameplate photos. Take a clear, straight-on photo of the label on the indoor unit (furnace, air handler or boiler) and the label on the outdoor unit. Labels give the model and serial numbers, and the electrical ratings. Our own tip: take one wide photo of the whole unit too, so the person knows which equipment you mean. See How Old Is My Furnace or AC?.
  • What it is doing, in plain words. "The air from the vents is warm," "the outdoor fan is not spinning," "it runs for a few minutes and quits." Skip guesses about the part that failed.
  • When it started. Suddenly or slowly, after a storm, after a power flicker, after a filter change.
  • Smells and sounds. Burning, hot dust, a rotten-egg or gas odor (leave, see above), buzzing, grinding, clicking, a hiss, or silence.
  • Any blinking light pattern. Some indoor controls have a small light behind a window. Write down the count and rhythm, such as "three quick blinks, pause, repeat," or take a short video. Do not remove covers to look for it.
  • Access. Say where the indoor and outdoor equipment are, whether there are stairs, an attic hatch or a locked gate, and clear a path. Put pets in a closed room before the visit.

The safe checks, and where to stop

Most no-cooling and no-heat calls begin with the same simple items. The federal energy department's home guide says to clean or replace filters monthly during seasons of peak use, and a university extension page notes a dirty filter restricts airflow and makes the system work harder. Beyond that, we keep homeowner checks to things that need no tools and no opened panels:

  • Thermostat. Is it set to the right mode (cool or heat), with the set point past the room temperature? If the screen is blank or dim, replace the batteries if it uses them.
  • Filter. Slide it out. If it is packed with dust, put in a clean one of the same size, with the airflow arrow pointing the way the unit's label shows.
  • Breaker and service switch. Look at the breaker panel for a tripped breaker for the system. If one is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and tell the technician. The wall switch near an indoor unit that looks like a light switch is a service switch; make sure it is on. Do not open any disconnect box near the outdoor unit.
  • Condensate float. Cooling systems with a safety float switch in the drain pan or drain line shut the system off when water backs up. You may look for standing water in a visible pan or around the indoor unit, and note it. Do not take the float out, bypass it or open the unit. For the full picture, see No-Heat First Checks, which covers the heating side so we do not repeat it here.

Not for homeowners: opening cabinets or control panels, touching wires, bypassing any safety switch, adding refrigerant, or working on a gas line. Those steps belong to a trained technician.

Worked example (our own): a sample call sheet for no cooling

Assumptions (our own, made up for teaching): a two-story house with an attic air handler and an outdoor unit; it is a hot afternoon; nobody in the home is elderly or an infant; no gas smell; no alarm. The system "stopped cooling" yesterday evening. Each line below is marked Homeowner OK or Technician only.

  • Problem in plain words: "Vents blow room-temperature air; outdoor fan is not spinning; indoor fan runs." Homeowner OK (look and listen from the floor and the yard).
  • Started: "Yesterday evening, after a short power flicker. It cooled fine in the morning." Homeowner OK.
  • Thermostat: "Set to Cool, set point below room temperature, display on, batteries replaced." Homeowner OK.
  • Filter: "Was very dirty; replaced with a clean one, same size." Homeowner OK.
  • Breaker and service switch: "Outdoor unit breaker was tripped; reset once; stayed on. Attic switch is on." Homeowner OK. If it tripped again: "leave off, tell the technician."
  • Condensate: "No water seen under the attic unit or in the visible pan." Homeowner OK to look. Removing the float or opening the cabinet: Technician only.
  • Blinking light: "Light on the indoor control seen through the small window: two blinks, pause, repeat." Homeowner OK to read through the window. Removing the door: Technician only.
  • Smells or sounds: "No burning smell. A faint hum from the outdoor unit when it tries to start." Homeowner OK to note. Touching the fan or wiring: Technician only.
  • Photos: "Indoor label, outdoor label, one wide shot of each unit." Homeowner OK.
  • Access: "Attic hatch in the hall closet; ladder access; side gate unlocked; dog in the bedroom." Homeowner OK.
  • Checking inside the outdoor unit or its electrical parts: Technician only.

A sheet like this takes only a few minutes to fill in. The caller can read it off, and the technician learns three useful things before arriving: the symptom, the timing, and that the easy items are already done.

Questions to ask at the end of the visit

  • What did you find? Ask for it in plain words, with the failed part pointed out if it is safe to see.
  • What readings did you take? Ask which measurements were taken and what they were. Write them on your sheet. Reading an HVAC Service Report explains what the common ones mean.
  • What are my options, in writing? Ask for the repair choices, and what happens if you wait, written down before work starts. Talking with Customers on HVAC Service Calls shows the same conversation from the technician's side.
  • Is there anything unsafe I should know about? Ask directly, and keep a copy of what you are told.

The federal energy department's guide also recommends having a professional inspect your cooling system in spring and your furnace in fall, so a planned visit is better than an emergency one when you can manage it.

Field card: Calling for HVAC Service

Danger first, facts second, safe checks only.

  • Gas smell or CO alarm: leave, call from outside, do not re-enter.
  • Tell the dispatcher if an infant, older adult or person with a medical condition is in the home.
  • Photograph the indoor and outdoor labels.
  • Say what it is doing and when it started, in plain words.
  • Safe checks: thermostat mode and batteries, filter, breaker and service switch, look for water.
  • Write down any blink pattern, smell or sound. Do not open panels.
  • Clear access and put pets away.
  • At the end: what was found, what readings, what options in writing.
