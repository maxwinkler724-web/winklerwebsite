Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Water expands when it heats. In a closed hot-water system, the expansion tank gives that extra water somewhere to go. If the tank is wrong, the pressure relief valve ends up doing the tank's job.

What the tank does

A closed hydronic system has no air space for expanding water. The expansion tank has a cushion of air or gas on one side of a rubber diaphragm or bladder and system water on the other. As the water heats and expands, it pushes into the tank and compresses the air. As the system cools, the air pushes the water back. A tank that cannot do this lets pressure climb until the relief valve opens.

Precharge and fill pressure

The tank's air side has a precharge, set at the factory and checked with a tire-type valve. One tank maker's installation instructions say to set the precharge equal to the system fill pressure, at room temperature, with the tank isolated from the system or the system drained. The same instructions advise against recharging a tank after the initial installation, especially a corroded one, and warn that a lost precharge fails the tank, so the valve cap should be sealed. Many tanks ship at a set factory pressure, and one trade article warns not to assume that matches your system, because taller buildings need more static pressure at the top. The boiler manual and system design give the target, and a low-pressure gauge shows what you really have.

The reason is simple. If precharge is lower than fill pressure, the tank starts out partly full of water and has less room left to absorb expansion. If it is higher, the system may sit below the tank's air pressure and the tank does nothing at all until pressure climbs.

Signs of trouble

  • Pressure swings widely from cold to hot, or the relief valve drips after the system heats.
  • The tank feels heavy or full of water all over, rather than hollow on the air side.
  • Pressing the air valve stem releases water instead of air. That points to a failed diaphragm, and the tank needs replacing.
  • Repeated need to add water, or the opposite, pressure that climbs fast.
  • Rusted or leaking tank shell.

This lesson does not give a sizing formula. The sources used here name improper precharge, a waterlogged tank and wrong size as troubleshooting causes but give no formula, so size the tank from the tank maker's manual.

Install and service notes

  • Many installers put the tank near the circulator inlet, mounted so that air can be removed, as the tank maker describes. A Caleffi guide explains why: a tank on the discharge side can leave parts of the system below atmospheric pressure, so vents and packings draw air in, and moving the tank to the inlet side has cured many older systems. When air keeps coming back, check the tank location first.
  • Use an isolation valve so the tank can be serviced without draining the system, if the design includes one.
  • Keep it away from very hot water and from places where dirt can collect and reach the diaphragm.
  • Do not connect several tanks at different points in the piping.
  • Support the tank per the manufacturer.

Relieve system pressure and let the system cool before opening anything. Hot water under pressure causes burns. Never plug or replace a relief valve to stop dripping; find the cause.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the exact equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints or wiring and piping steps.

Field card: Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure

Precharge matches fill. A wet air valve means a dead tank.

  • Water expands when it heats. The tank absorbs it.
  • Check and set precharge with the tank isolated from system pressure.
  • Fill pressure and precharge should agree. Verify against the manual.
  • Water from the air valve means a failed diaphragm.
  • A dripping relief valve is a symptom. Look at the tank first.
