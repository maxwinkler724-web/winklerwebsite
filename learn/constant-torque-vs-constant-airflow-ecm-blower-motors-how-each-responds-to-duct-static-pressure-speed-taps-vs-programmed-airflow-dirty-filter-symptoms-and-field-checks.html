Constant-Torque vs Constant-Airflow ECM Blowers: Static Pressure, Taps and Dirty Filters

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Two furnaces can both have an ECM blower and still behave very differently when the ductwork or filter gets restrictive. One kind lets airflow fall and keeps its power about the same. The other kind speeds up, pulls more watts and holds the airflow. Knowing which one you are standing in front of changes how you read a dirty filter and how you check the system.

Two ways to control the same motor

The motors in question are electronically commutated, brushless permanent-magnet motors with a small control board built in. A DOE market assessment for consumer furnace fans describes two control styles. A constant-torque motor keeps a set torque in each airflow-control setting as operating conditions change, and it is built to mimic the speed-tap interface of older split-capacitor motors. A constant-airflow motor tries to hold the same airflow in each setting as conditions change. The same DOE material says the older tap-style motors give distinct, selectable settings, and each tap corresponds to one airflow-control setting.

What happens when static pressure rises

Static pressure is the resistance the duct system, coil and filter put in the airstream. A DOE rulemaking document quotes industry comments on the difference: a constant-torque motor gives reduced airflow as static pressure increases, while a constant-airflow motor increases speed and power to maintain airflow, up to the limit of the motor. The same comments note that at one specific pressure and airflow the two types use about the same energy, so the difference only shows when the pressure moves away from the design point.

The reason the watts climb is basic fan behavior. The DOE assessment notes that the static pressure needed to move air is roughly the square of the airflow, and that fan input power is roughly the cube of airflow. If you ask for the same airflow against a tighter system, the fan has to make more pressure, so air power goes up. See the fan laws lesson for the full relations.

Speed taps versus programmed airflow

On a tap-style setup you pick a speed or setting from a wire or board selection. On a programmed setup you set a target such as cooling airflow, heating airflow and continuous-fan airflow, often through a board menu or dip switches, and the control works toward it. Constant-torque motors usually look like the first kind from the outside, and constant-airflow motors like the second. Do not assume it from appearance. Read the installation manual for the exact model, because the label "ECM" alone does not tell you the control style.

Why a dirty filter looks different on each

A loaded filter adds static pressure. On a constant-torque blower the result is lower airflow: weaker supply at the registers, a larger temperature rise across a furnace, or a colder coil in cooling. The watts barely tell the story. On a constant-airflow blower the airflow is held, so the symptom moves into the watts and into noise or heat, until the motor runs out of capability and then airflow falls too. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study of filters in homes saw the same split: older split-capacitor blowers lost both power and airflow as filters loaded, while variable-speed permanent-magnet blowers tried to hold airflow and increased their power to do it. Airflow stayed steady until the blower reached its maximum output, after which it dropped at rates similar to the older motors. In a couple of cases the blower could not hold airflow at all with a very restrictive filter.

A separate Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory paper on these motors in gas furnaces found that blower electricity use depends on the static pressure across the blower, and that real household ducts usually run at much higher static pressure than the test conditions, so the savings are smaller than the test procedure predicts. That is a reminder to measure the real system instead of trusting a nameplate.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not data from a product or study. Assumptions: a 1200 CFM system with clean-filter total external static pressure of 0.50 in. w.c.; a dirty filter raises system resistance so the same airflow would need 0.70 in. w.c.; resistance follows the square law above; overall blower and motor efficiency is 35 percent and does not change; the constant-torque fan can deliver 0.90 in. w.c. at zero airflow, falling in a straight line to 0.50 in. w.c. at 1200 CFM. Air power in watts is CFM times in. w.c. times 0.1175, which is just the unit conversion.

  1. Clean filter, both motors: 1200 x 0.50 x 0.1175 = 70.5 air watts. At 35 percent, input is 70.5 / 0.35 = about 201 W.
  2. Constant-airflow motor, dirty filter: it holds 1200 CFM at 0.70. Air power is 1200 x 0.70 x 0.1175 = 98.7 W, input 98.7 / 0.35 = about 282 W. That is about 40 percent more watts (0.70 / 0.50), with airflow unchanged, as long as the motor has headroom.
  3. Constant-torque motor, dirty filter: the dirty system curve is 0.70 / 1200 squared times CFM squared. Setting the fan line (0.90 minus CFM / 3000) equal to that curve gives about 1060 CFM at about 0.55 in. w.c. Air power is 1060 x 0.55 x 0.1175 = about 68 W, input about 195 W.
  4. Result: the torque motor lost about 12 percent of its airflow and its watts changed by only a few percent. The airflow motor lost no airflow and used about 40 percent more watts. The same dirty filter shows as lower CFM on one and higher watts on the other.

Real motors have efficiency that changes with speed and load, so treat the numbers as a way to see the direction of change, not a prediction.

Field checks

  • Identify the control style from the installation or service manual for the exact model, and find the airflow setting that was chosen.
  • Measure total external static pressure at the unit and compare it to what the manufacturer allows. Our static pressure measurement lesson covers test ports and readings.
  • Check the filter, its rack and any bypass gaps before blaming the motor. See filter racks and bypass gaps.
  • On a torque motor, confirm airflow by measurement, such as a blower table at the measured pressure or the temperature rise method, since the watts will not warn you.
  • On an airflow motor, compare measured pressure and any displayed airflow or watts to the manual, and treat unusually high watts or noise as a sign of restriction. If the control reports it is at its limit, airflow is no longer held.
  • Do not change speed settings to hide a restrictive duct. Fix the restriction, then set the airflow to the equipment's rated range. Follow the manual and lockout procedures before opening panels.

For the motor basics, see ECM Blower Motors, and for the pressure basics Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Field card: Constant-torque vs constant-airflow ECM

Torque motors lose airflow. Airflow motors spend watts.

  • Constant torque: more static pressure means less airflow, watts about flat.
  • Constant airflow: more static pressure means more speed and watts, until the motor limit.
  • Taps pick a fixed setting; programmed airflow sets a target.
  • Dirty filter: low CFM or high temperature rise on one; high watts or noise on the other.
  • Measure total external static pressure, then confirm airflow by a second method.
  • Fix the restriction instead of turning up the setting.
