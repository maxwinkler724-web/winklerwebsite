Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors for Pumps and Fans: Static Deflection and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Big fans and pumps shake. Springs, pads and flexible connectors keep that shake out of the structure, and the math behind them comes down to one square root.

Check the maker first

Check the maker first. This lesson explains the idea and the arithmetic. The isolator maker's drawings, the equipment manufacturer's instructions and the job specification decide the actual deflection, mounting and adjustment. This page was built from maker guides and a public specification read in 2026 and is general education.

Why we isolate rotating equipment

A fan or pump always has some unbalance, and it pushes on whatever carries it at the shaft speed. If that push goes into a floor or roof, the structure carries it as noise and shaking to rooms far away. An isolator is a soft support that lets the machine move a little so the structure moves much less. Kinetics Noise Control's selection guide describes its flexible connectors as isolating against the transfer of noise and vibration, and says flexible duct connectors should be installed at the intake and discharge of all fans and air-handling equipment. An Ohio public-building specification goes further and says rigid connections between vibrating equipment and the building are not permitted.

Three common supports

  • Neoprene pads and mounts. Mason Industries' technical lecture puts neoprene mountings in the 0.20 to 0.50 in deflection range and says pads deflect only about 10 to 20 percent of their thickness, so it limits them to basement or non-critical work. Kinetics describes its pads as isolating noise, shock and high frequency vibration.
  • Steel spring isolators. Mason calls steel springs by far the most widely used choice on critical jobs and says they are practical through 5 in of static deflection. It adds that neoprene elements are often put in series with springs to reduce high frequency noise.
  • Inertia bases. A heavy concrete-filled frame under the equipment. Kinetics says the extra mass allows stiffer springs, which limit heaving movement. Mason gives about 1 to 3 times the equipment weight for high-pressure fans and 5 to 7 times for compressors.

Static deflection and natural frequency

Static deflection is how far the isolator squashes under the dead weight of the equipment. Vibration Eliminator Co.'s theory page defines it that way and says the natural frequency is a function of it, with d in inches. The Vibrationdata isolation tutorial writes the relationship as fn = 3.13 / sqrt(d) Hz, with d in inches. Mason's lecture states the idea in cycles per minute (cpm): an isolation system's frequency depends on static deflection, and about 1 in of deflection gives a base frequency of 188 cpm. Our own check: 60 cpm per Hz times 3.13 is 187.8, which matches 188. Weight does not appear in the answer. A soft spring and a stiff spring that sag the same distance under their loads have the same natural frequency.

The ratio rule

Compare the disturbing frequency (the shaft speed in cpm; the theory page says the lowest rpm in the system is generally used) with the natural frequency. When the two are equal you have resonance, which the theory page says produces excessive violent movement. The Vibrationdata tutorial says isolation, meaning transmitted force smaller than the input, only occurs above about 1.4 times fn, and that for maximum isolation the disturbing frequency should be as far above fn as possible. The theory page adds that the gains diminish. Mason mentions a ratio of about 3 to 1 as the textbook case for removing 90 percent, but says that single-mass theory does not hold up on real structures. Kinetics' fan guidance is worded the other way around: pick the deflection so the isolator natural frequency is 40 percent or less of the fan speed. That is a ratio of 2.5 or more.

For an undamped system the Vibrationdata tutorial gives transmissibility as T = 1 / (1 - r squared), where r is the disturbing frequency over the natural frequency. Above resonance that comes out negative, which only flips the phase, so we use the size, 1 / (r squared - 1). The tutorial also notes that damping slightly raises transmission well above resonance, so treat the number as a best case.

Worked example: a 1,200 rpm fan

A fan runs at 1,200 rpm. The disturbing frequency is 1,200 cpm, because it pushes once per revolution.

On springs with a stated 1 in static deflection.

  1. Natural frequency: about 188 cpm for 1 in, from Mason's lecture (188 / sqrt(1) = 188).
  2. Our own: ratio = 1,200 / 188 = 6.38.
  3. Our own, using the undamped formula above: r squared = 6.38 x 6.38 = 40.7, so T = 1 / (40.7 - 1) = 1 / 39.7 = 0.025. About 2.5 percent of the force passes into the structure.
  4. Our own check against Kinetics' 40 percent guidance: 0.40 x 1,200 = 480 cpm. 188 is below 480, so it passes.

The same fan on a pad that deflects 0.25 in under load (0.25 in is the deflection, not the pad thickness).

  1. Our own: fn = 188 / sqrt(0.25) = 188 / 0.5 = 376 cpm.
  2. Our own: ratio = 1,200 / 376 = 3.19.
  3. Our own: r squared = 10.2, so T = 1 / (10.2 - 1) = 1 / 9.2 = 0.11. About 11 percent passes, roughly four times the spring case.
  4. Our own: 376 is below 480, so it still meets the 40 percent guidance, but with far less margin. If a drive later slows this fan, the ratio shrinks and the pad gets worse; the fan laws lesson shows how speed changes things.

Our own note: Mason says pads deflect only 10 to 20 percent of their thickness, so a pad that deflects 0.25 in would need to be roughly 1.25 to 2.5 in thick. These numbers are a classroom comparison, not a design. Only the "about 1 in gives 188 cpm" point comes straight from a maker's lecture; the rest is our own arithmetic on the sourced formula.

Where it sits: pad or roof

Kinetics' guide recommends that rooftop equipment over 300 lb be isolated externally, as if internal isolation was not used, because internal isolation is not always reliable due to internal short-circuiting. It also says that on roof spans over 20 ft, if additional roof deflection is over 0.25 in, supplemental roof stiffening should be installed, and that its roof rail product is installed between roof-mounted equipment and the supporting curb. A roof flexes, so its own deflection adds to the isolator's. A slab or basement floor is stiffer, which fits Mason placing neoprene pads in basement or non-critical work.

Flexible connectors

The nyb fan guide recommends flexible connections at both the inlet and outlet. It says they let the fan move freely on its isolators, limit vibration passing in both directions, and prevent thermal expansion from loading the fan. It also says ductwork and stacks should be supported on their own, because their weight can distort the housing. Mason says flexible hose should be installed horizontal and parallel to the axis of rotating equipment, and that hoses are not complete protection against pipe-borne noise. Ohio's specification lists two piping connector types: molded reinforced neoprene with steel flanges and control rods, and flexible steel braid with steel flanges.

Field faults to look for

  • Springs collapsed or uneven. The nyb guide has the installer adjust the isolators in alternating steps so no single spring collapses, until the fan floats free above the blocks. A 30XW chiller spring kit sets a 1/4 to 1/2 in gap between the upper and lower mount housings on every isolator, adjusts them one turn at a time in sequence, and requires base plates within 1/4 in of the same elevation. An isolator that is collapsed or locked down tight cannot isolate.
  • A rigid path around the springs. The Ohio specification does not permit rigid connections between vibrating equipment and the building, and Kinetics warns of short-circuiting. In the field, look for anything stiff that touches both the machine and the structure, and let the maker's drawings tell you what must be flexible.
  • Added weight. Unsupported duct hanging on a fan changes the load on the isolators, per nyb.
  • Wrong speed. A drive change moves the disturbing frequency, as the example shows.

Field card: Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors

Soft support, flexible connections, and a big gap between fn and fan speed.

  • Isolators let equipment move so the structure moves less.
  • fn = 3.13 / sqrt(d) Hz, or about 188 / sqrt(d) cpm, d in inches of static deflection.
  • About 1 in on springs is about 188 cpm.
  • Higher ratio of fan speed to fn isolates better; Kinetics fan guidance: fn at 40 percent of fan speed or lower.
  • Neoprene is 0.20 to 0.50 in deflection; springs go to 5 in.
  • Flexible connection at fan inlet and outlet; no rigid path to the structure.
  • Check isolator gaps and levelness against the maker's drawing.
