Recording Closet or Podcast Booth: Heat, Stale Air and Safe Airflow

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A closet lined with foam or blankets makes a great recording space and a poor place to breathe. With the door shut, a person, a laptop and a couple of lights turn it into a small, sealed heater with no fresh air. This lesson explains why it gets hot, stuffy and humid, how to move air without adding noise, and what to keep out of there.

Why a padded closet heats up fast

A closet is small and padding is insulation. Every watt that goes in stays in. A resting adult gives off roughly the heat of a 100 W bulb, a laptop under load draws 40 to 60 W or more, and even a couple of LED strips add something. These are our own round numbers for the example below, not measured values. The same idea shows up in the coworking phone booth lesson, and the home office lesson covers the open-room version.

Breathing and talking also add moisture. Humid, warm air feels worse than dry air at the same temperature, and moisture is the thing that mold needs (more on that below).

Stale air and carbon dioxide

People breathe out carbon dioxide. A federal environmental agency fact sheet says it can build up when the outdoor air supply is too low, that high levels can make people drowsy or cause headaches, and that excess indoors points to inadequate ventilation. A federal workplace-safety research agency suggests that readings under about 800 parts per million are one possible target for good ventilation in a space, and notes that a single number does not suit every room. The same agency lists 5,000 ppm as an eight-hour workplace average limit, and describes the gas as colorless and odorless, so you cannot count on smelling a problem. A closet with the door shut can pass these numbers faster than most people expect, as the example shows.

Worked example (our own)

Heat. Assumptions, all our own: one person at rest about 100 W, a laptop 60 W, LED lights 20 W. Total heat 100 + 60 + 20 = 180 W. Over one hour that is 180 Wh. One watt-hour is 3.412 BTU, so 180 × 3.412 = about 614 BTU. That is the same heat as a 180 W heater running for the hour, about 12 percent of a 1,500 W space heater (180 / 1,500 = 0.12). Next, a 4 ft by 3 ft by 8 ft closet holds 96 cubic feet. At 0.075 lb per cubic foot, that is 7.2 lb of air, and air takes 0.24 BTU per pound per degree F, so the air alone holds 7.2 × 0.24 = 1.73 BTU per degree. If the air took every BTU, it would rise 614 / 1.73 = about 355 degrees F. It cannot, because foam, walls, the door and your body soak up and leak heat. The number shows only that the air is a tiny heat sink, so the closet warms noticeably in the first few minutes while the padding slowly takes up the rest.

Carbon dioxide. Assumptions, all our own: a seated adult makes about 0.31 liters of carbon dioxide per minute (18.6 liters per hour), outdoor air starts at about 420 ppm, the closet is sealed, and the 96 cubic feet equals 96 × 28.317 = 2,718 liters. The rise is 18.6 / 2,718 = 0.00684, or about 6,840 ppm per hour. After one hour the reading would be near 420 + 6,840 = 7,260 ppm. The time to reach 800 ppm is (800 - 420) / 6,840 × 60 = about 3 minutes. The time to reach 5,000 ppm is (5,000 - 420) / 6,840 × 60 = about 40 minutes. Real closets leak a little and foam and gear take up volume, so treat these as upper-bound estimates, not predictions.

Airflow to hold a target. With steady fresh air, the indoor rise is the generation rate divided by airflow. To hold 1,000 ppm (580 ppm above outdoors): 0.31 / 0.00058 = about 534 liters per minute, or 534 / 28.317 = about 19 CFM per person. To hold 800 ppm: 0.31 / 0.00038 = about 816 liters per minute, about 29 CFM. These are our own arithmetic from the stated assumptions. A small quiet fan moves that much, but only if it has a path in and a path out.

Do not block the register or return with foam

If the closet or small room has a supply register, stuffing it with foam starves the room of air and can push the system out of balance. A blocked return does the same to the whole house: the blocked vents lesson explains why. Keep foam and blankets off the register, off the grille and well away from any heat source. If you need the sound to stop, ask an HVAC contractor about a lined path, not a plug.

Quiet ways to get air in and out

  • Door undercut or transfer path. A gap at the bottom of the door lets room air in. A transfer grille or a lined duct elbow does the same job. Lining the path with absorbent material and bending it (a baffle) stops much of the noise while air still passes. This is general design thinking, our own; the recording studio lesson covers silencers and sealed rooms in detail.
  • A quiet fan. A small low-speed fan in or on the exhaust path, placed away from the microphone and well clear of padding. Use the airflow example above to see what is needed.
  • Short breaks with the door open. Record in blocks and open the door between takes. This is the simplest fix, and extension fact sheets also note that air movement is good at removing moisture.

Space heaters and portable AC in a tiny closet

Do not put a space heater in a small padded closet. A federal consumer product safety agency says to keep a heater at least three feet from bedding, drapes, furniture or anything flammable, and never to leave one on when you sleep. A federal fire administration page says to keep anything that can burn three feet from heat sources, to turn space heaters off when you leave the room, and not to use extension cords with appliances. It also reports that in a recent three-year period, portable heaters were 3 percent of annual home heating fires but 41 percent of fatal home heating fires. In a booth lined with foam or blankets, three feet of clearance is not possible. The space heater lesson has the full safety list.

A portable AC is a poor fit for the same reason (our own reasoning): it needs room to breathe, a hose to the outdoors for its hot exhaust, and a way to handle condensate water, which can leak or overflow in a closet. If the room is too hot to work in, record somewhere else or take breaks.

Foam, lights and clearances

Lights and gear make heat, and foam and blankets are flammable (our own general caution). Keep padding off lamps, off power strips and off chargers, and give lights room. The same federal fire administration page's three-foot rule applies to any heat source.

Moisture and mildew behind padding

If the closet is on an outside wall, that wall can be the coldest surface in winter. Warm, humid air from people can condense there, behind the foam, where nobody sees it. A federal environmental agency says that controlling moisture controls mold, suggests keeping indoor humidity between 30 and 60 percent, advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, and recommends insulating cold surfaces such as exterior walls to prevent condensation. A state university extension fact sheet on mildew advises opening closet doors or adding a fan in poorly ventilated closets. Pull panels away from the wall a little, leave a gap, and check behind them now and then. The cold closets lesson works through the dew point side. If you find a musty smell or mold behind padding, remove the cause (moisture) first, and ask a professional for anything big.

Field card: Recording Closet or Podcast Booth: Heat, Stale Air and Safe Airflow

Small, padded and shut means hot, stuffy and damp.

  • One person plus a laptop and lights is about 180 W in our own example, about 614 BTU per hour.
  • Our own CO2 example: about 6,840 ppm rise per hour in a sealed 96 cubic foot closet.
  • About 19 to 29 CFM of fresh air per person holds 800 to 1,000 ppm (our own arithmetic).
  • Never plug the register with foam. Keep foam and blankets off heat and lights.
  • No space heater or portable AC in a padded closet. Keep three feet from anything that can burn.
  • Open the door between takes. Leave a gap behind padding on outside walls.
