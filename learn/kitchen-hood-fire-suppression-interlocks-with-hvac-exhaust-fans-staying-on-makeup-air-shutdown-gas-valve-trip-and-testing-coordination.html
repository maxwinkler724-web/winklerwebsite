Kitchen Hood Fire Suppression Interlocks: What HVAC Must Not Touch

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A wet-chemical hood suppression system is a fire system, but it reaches into HVAC: it decides what the exhaust fan, the makeup air and the gas valve do in an emergency. This lesson explains that wiring in plain terms, shows what happens to the room if the pieces disagree, and says what an HVAC tech must leave alone.

Check the local rules

Check with the fire official. The sources below are public checklists and design standards from fire departments and a university, plus New Jersey state pages. They quote the national codes but are not the codes. Your local fire marshal decides what applies at a given building. This is general education, not legal advice.

What the system is

A restaurant hood suppression system holds a wet-chemical agent and discharges it through nozzles aimed at the appliances, the plenum and the duct. A fusible link (a heat-sensitive link) or a detector releases it automatically, and a manual pull station lets a person trigger it. A university design standard says these systems must meet UL 300, the test standard for this kind of system. The university standard and a city fire checklist call for a pull station in the path of egress, 42 to 48 inches above the floor, and 10 to 20 feet from the kitchen exhaust system.

What the building does when it discharges

  • Fuel and power to the cooking line shut off. A city fire department checklist cites the fire code and NFPA 17A and requires automatic shutdown of fuel or electric power to the cooking equipment, including lights under the hood. A university standard adds that shutoff valves and switches must need a manual action to reset, so nothing restarts by itself.
  • Makeup air supplied inside the hood shuts off. The same checklist quotes NFPA 96 as requiring that makeup air supplied internally to a hood be shut off when the extinguishing system activates. The university standard says makeup air shall cease flow.
  • The hood exhaust fan keeps running. The checklist quotes NFPA 96 as saying the hood exhaust fan continues to operate after activation. Keeping it on pulls smoke and heat into the duct instead of the dining room.
  • The fire alarm is told. The university standard says the suppression system must be interconnected to the building fire alarm so activation sounds the alarm.

Rules differ. The same university standard says activation shuts down fans, which is the opposite of the NFPA 96 line quoted by the city checklist. Different authorities set different requirements, so what a given building does is whatever its approved drawings and the fire official say. Never assume.

Worked example: makeup air drops out, exhaust keeps running

These numbers are our own, with assumptions called out.

  • Assumption: hood exhaust is 4,000 cfm, and makeup air is 80 percent of that.
  • Makeup air = 4,000 × 0.80 = 3,200 cfm.
  • Normal shortfall = 4,000 − 3,200 = 800 cfm, drawn from the dining room as transfer air.
  • If makeup air shuts down and exhaust stays on, the full 4,000 cfm must come from somewhere, mostly through doors and leaks.
  • Assumption: the doors and gaps that actually leak add up to 20 ft² of open area.
  • Velocity = cfm ÷ area = 4,000 ÷ 20 = 200 fpm, about 2.3 mph (200 × 60 ÷ 5,280). For comparison, the normal 800 cfm is 800 ÷ 20 = 40 fpm.

Why it matters, in our own reasoning: air rushing toward the hood fights anyone pushing a door open to leave, and it pulls room air across the cooking line. That draft also disturbs open flames and pilot lights, which is why the fuel shutoff must work, and it can pull smoke toward the exits. The code accepts that imbalance for a short emergency, because the hood fan is the thing clearing smoke. In normal operation, a building that drifts this far from balance has a fault to find.

Heat load of the makeup air

Our own sensible-heat check, using the standard factor 1.08 for air at standard density. Heating 3,200 cfm from 0 F outdoors to 60 F:

  • 1.08 × 3,200 = 3,456
  • 3,456 × 60 F = 207,360 Btu/h

That is a big load, which is one reason makeup air units are sized and controlled carefully; see make-up air units.

Inspection and who may do the work

A county fire department checklist requires the last service tag and inspection report to be less than six months old, and monthly owner checks logged for the six-month period between dealer inspections. In New Jersey, the Department of Community Affairs, Division of Fire Safety, certifies fire protection contractors through its Contractor Certification and Emblems Unit. Its requirements page says the Kitchen Fire Suppression System contractor classification requires passing a restaurant fire suppression examination, and the state rule text says an all-fire-protection-equipment contractor must also pass it. That is the work of recharging, resetting and testing the system itself. It is not HVAC work.

What the HVAC tech must not do

  • Do not bypass, jumper or remove an interlock to get a fan or makeup air unit to run for troubleshooting. If a relay seems wrong, tell the building owner and the suppression contractor.
  • Do not reset a tripped gas valve or a discharged system. The university standard says resets must be a manual action, and the system itself must be recharged by the certified contractor.
  • Do not move, block or paint over fusible links, nozzles or pull stations while cleaning ducts or replacing fans.
  • Do not change fan or makeup air airflow without checking the approved drawings; a rebalance changes the pressure picture above.
  • Do not re-energize equipment after a fire event until the fire official and contractors release it.

Coordinating PM with the hood service

  • Ask when the semiannual suppression visit is, and book fan and makeup air work on a different day or with the contractor present.
  • Ask them to watch your fan stop and start and to confirm the interlock still drops makeup air. The university standard calls for gas and power tripping on activation at acceptance testing, so ask whose job each test step is.
  • After any control or fan change, tell the suppression contractor so that the interlock test is repeated.
  • Share findings in writing: belt, rotation, damper position, makeup air status.

Field card: Hood Suppression Interlocks

Fire system owns the interlock. HVAC works around it, never through it.

  • On discharge: fuel and power to cooking drop, internal makeup air drops, alarm signals.
  • NFPA 96 as quoted by a city checklist: hood exhaust fan keeps running. Some local standards differ; follow the approved drawings.
  • Gas and power resets are manual.
  • Semiannual tag from the suppression contractor; NJ certifies those contractors through the Division of Fire Safety.
  • Example: 4,000 cfm exhaust, 20 ft² leaks, 200 fpm.
  • Never jumper or bypass an interlock.
