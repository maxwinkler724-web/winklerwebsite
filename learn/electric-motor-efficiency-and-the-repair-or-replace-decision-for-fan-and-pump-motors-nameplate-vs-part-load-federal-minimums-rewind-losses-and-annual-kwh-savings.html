Electric Motor Efficiency and the Repair-or-Replace Decision for Fan and Pump Motors: Nameplate vs Part Load, Federal Minimums, Rewind Losses and Annual kWh Savings

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A fan or pump motor that runs thousands of hours a year uses far more money in electricity than it cost to buy. That is why a small gap in efficiency, a point or two, can decide whether a failed motor gets rewound or replaced. This lesson shows what the efficiency number on a nameplate really means, how it changes at part load, what federal rules set the floor for new motors, and how to turn an efficiency gap into kilowatt-hours.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. Federal efficiency tables and compliance dates can be amended. This page was built from federal energy-program documents and the federal regulation text read in 2026. It is general education. Confirm the current regulation and your site's procedures before you buy or specify a motor, and have a qualified person handle any work on energized equipment.

What nameplate efficiency means

Efficiency is output power divided by input power. A motor's shaft output is the horsepower it delivers; the input is the electricity it draws. The difference becomes heat in the windings, the core, the bearings and the cooling fan. The U.S. Department of Energy's motor handbook describes efficiency as the ratio of mechanical power output to electrical power input, and explains that some losses rise with load (winding resistance losses and stray load losses) while core and friction and windage losses stay about the same regardless of load.

The efficiency printed on a nameplate is a nominal full-load value. The federal regulation defines a nominal efficiency as a value not greater than the average full-load efficiency of a population of motors of the same design. So it describes a typical motor of that model at 100 percent load, not your motor at the load it actually carries. For the rest of the nameplate, including service factor and design letter, see Reading a Three-Phase Motor Nameplate.

Input kilowatts from horsepower

One horsepower is 0.746 kilowatts of output (the DOE load-and-efficiency fact sheet uses 0.7457 in its body text and the rounded 0.746 in an attachment). To find what the motor draws from the line:

Input kW = hp × 0.746 × load fraction ÷ efficiency

Here the load fraction is the shaft load as a share of nameplate horsepower (0.75 for 75 percent), and efficiency is a decimal (0.93 for 93 percent). Use the efficiency that applies at that load. The same fact sheet gives the full-load form, input kW = hp × 0.7457 ÷ full-load efficiency. This is real power, so it is not the same as the kVA a utility meter or a power-factor reading shows; see Power Factor on HVAC Motors for the difference.

Part-load efficiency

Fan and pump motors rarely run at exactly 100 percent. The DOE fact sheet says most motors are designed to run at 50 to 100 percent of rated load, that maximum efficiency is usually near 75 percent of rated load, and that efficiency tends to decrease dramatically below about 50 percent load. It adds that larger motors show a relatively flat efficiency curve down to 25 percent of full load, and that power factor falls off sooner than efficiency, though less steeply. Another DOE guide suggests sizing motors to run mainly in the 65 to 100 percent range and treats motors running below 40 percent load as candidates for a properly sized replacement.

The practical lesson: a big efficiency gap on paper shrinks or grows with load, so check what load the motor really carries. An oversized motor on a lightly loaded fan wastes energy even if it is a good motor. Right-sizing, or slowing the load with a drive, may beat any motor swap. See Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans and Fan Laws for how speed changes power.

Federal minimum efficiency rules

Federal energy conservation standards for general purpose electric motors are in the Code of Federal Regulations, 10 CFR Part 431, Subpart B, section 431.25. Reading the current text of that section:

  • From June 1, 2016, Table 5 sets nominal full-load minimums for Design A and Design B motors (and the matching international designs) from 1 to 500 hp. Table 6 covers Design C motors from 1 to 200 hp.
  • Starting June 1, 2027, Table 8 applies to Design A and B motors from 1 to 750 hp, with separate tables for air-over motors.
  • The rules cover single-speed, continuous-duty, squirrel-cage induction motors on 60 Hz polyphase power at 600 volts or less, with 2, 4, 6 or 8 poles. Fire pump motors have their own table, and some types are excluded, such as submersible motors and definite purpose motors.
  • For a 20 hp, 4-pole motor, Table 5 lists 93.0 percent for both open and enclosed construction. The 2027 Table 8 row for 20 hp is the same, 93.0 percent.

These rules govern motors that manufacturers make and import for sale. The DOE motor handbook notes that repaired motors were excluded from the original federal standards, so a rewound motor is not held to the table. The eCFR page also notes that some amendments were removed and others reverted in 2024 and 2025, so read the current section yourself before you rely on a number.

What rewinding does

A rewind strips the old windings and installs new ones. Done well, it can preserve efficiency. A DOE repair guide says a quality rewind can maintain the original efficiency, while heat damage to the core, a wrong wire size or turn count, or a poor winding redesign can lower it. A 2009 industrial energy-efficiency conference paper summarizes older DOE guidance that took two points off efficiency for motors under 40 hp and one point for larger motors as a planning penalty when shop quality is unknown, and reports that later guidance used 1 point for smaller motors and 0.5 point for larger ones. The same paper says shops with the best quality-control practices can often rewind with no significant degradation. So a rewind penalty is a planning assumption, not a law of nature. Ask the shop for a no-load or core-loss test and watch for a changed winding resistance.

Repair or replace: rules of thumb from DOE

  • Non-specialty motors under 15 hp should almost always be replaced rather than rewound (DOE motor-system guide).
  • Motors under 40 hp that are over 15 years old, especially ones already rewound, are often much less efficient than current models; replacement is usually best (same guide).
  • If the rewind cost is more than 50 to 65 percent of the price of a new energy-efficient motor, buy the new one (same guide).
  • The DOE motor handbook says repair of motors under 20 hp often costs more than a new motor, while repair of motors over 50 hp is generally under 60 percent of the cost of a new premium efficiency motor, so those tend to be repaired.
  • A DOE technical brief gives the rule: rewind when it is cheaper than a new motor, but for standard off-the-shelf motors repair rarely wins unless original efficiency is high, is kept through the repair, and reliability matches a new motor.

Judge availability, downtime and lead time too. A critical pump with no spare may be replaced from stock even when the arithmetic is close. Before deciding, test the winding; see Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing.

Worked example (our own): 20 hp fan motor at 75 percent load

Our own example with assumed inputs. A building exhaust fan has a 20 hp, 4-pole motor that fails. Assumptions: it carries 75 percent of rated load; it runs 6,000 hours a year; the existing motor, if rewound, would have an efficiency of 90.0 percent at that load; a new premium motor is 93.0 percent at that load (the Table 5 minimum for this size, assumed to hold at 75 percent load, which a real data sheet should confirm).

  1. Shaft output: 20 hp × 0.746 × 0.75 = 11.19 kW.
  2. Input, rewound motor: 11.19 ÷ 0.900 = 12.4333 kW.
  3. Input, new motor: 11.19 ÷ 0.930 = 12.0323 kW.
  4. Demand difference: 12.4333 − 12.0323 = 0.4010 kW.
  5. Annual energy difference: 0.4010 kW × 6,000 h = 2,406 kWh a year.
  6. Check against total use: rewound 12.4333 × 6,000 = 74,600 kWh; new 12.0323 × 6,000 = 72,194 kWh. The gap is 2,406 kWh, about 3.2 percent of the rewound motor's use.

Now suppose a poor rewind costs one point, so the existing motor ends at 89.0 percent: input = 11.19 ÷ 0.890 = 12.5730 kW; difference = 12.5730 − 12.0323 = 0.5407 kW; annual = 0.5407 × 6,000 = 3,244 kWh a year, about 4.3 percent more than the new motor would use.

The DOE technical brief's version is the same math written as kW saved = hp × load × 0.746 × (1/Eexisting − 1/Enew). To price it, multiply kWh by your electric rate. As a made-up example only, at a rate of 0.10 dollars per kWh, 2,406 kWh would be 240.60 dollars a year. Use your own rate, any demand charge, and real quotes. A simple payback is the extra cost of the new motor over the repair, divided by the yearly savings.

The savings are modest here, which is the point. At 6,000 hours the gap is worth a look; at 500 hours a year it is small, and repair or a spare may make more sense. Whichever motor you pick, the biggest saving often comes from not running oversized or throttled.

Field card: Electric Motor Efficiency and Repair or Replace

Efficiency is a full-load average; your savings depend on your load and your hours.

  • Input kW = hp × 0.746 × load fraction ÷ efficiency.
  • Annual kWh saved = hp × 0.746 × load × (1/E old − 1/E new) × hours.
  • Efficiency peaks near 75 percent load and drops fast below about 50 percent.
  • Federal tables (10 CFR 431.25) set minimums for new motors; 20 hp, 4-pole is 93.0 percent.
  • Rewinds can hold efficiency or lose 0.5 to 2 points; ask about shop quality control.
  • DOE rules of thumb: replace under 15 hp; replace when rewind is over 50 to 65 percent of new cost.
  • Our example: 90.0 vs 93.0 percent, 20 hp, 75 percent load, 6,000 h = 2,406 kWh a year.
