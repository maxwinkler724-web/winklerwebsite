Smokehouse and Jerky Drying Room HVAC for Farms and Butcher Shops

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A small smokehouse looks simple: a box, a fire or smoke generator, and a stack. In practice it is a controlled oven, a humidifier, a dryer and a chimney in one room, and each of those has an air-handling job. This lesson walks through the cycle, the humidity control, the smoke and exhaust side, the room around it, and the carbon monoxide and fire hazards a service tech should know.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. This page is general education from public sources read in 2026. It is not legal advice and not a food-safety plan. Product temperatures, process records and plan review are set by the food-safety inspector, the fire official and the equipment maker for the site. Ask them before you change anything.

The cycle in three stages

Most small smokehouses and jerky rooms run a program of stages. We describe them here as a general pattern, not a recipe. Names and settings vary by product.

  • Drying. Warm, moving air takes surface moisture off the product so smoke will stick and the surface sets.
  • Smoking. A smoke generator feeds smoke into the chamber. Smoke has to flow through, so the chamber is not sealed: some air enters and some leaves through the exhaust duct.
  • Cooking. The chamber is raised to cook the product to a safe internal temperature, with moisture controlled so the surface does not dry out too early.

A jerky room often combines these into a heating step followed by a long drying step. A federal compliance guideline for jerky says drying should closely follow heating and that humidity has to be applied during the first part of heating, before any drying occurs.

Humidity and wet-bulb control

Humidity in a smokehouse is controlled by adding steam or a fine water mist, or by opening dampers to let vapor out. The sensor you use is the wet-bulb: a thermometer whose bulb is covered with a wetted wick. A psychrometrics text from a land-grant university notes that the wet-bulb reading is very nearly the temperature of adiabatic saturation when air moves past the bulb at about 1,000 ft/min and radiant heat is kept small. That is why airflow past the wet-bulb sensor matters, and why a dry wick or a wick fouled by smoke and grease reads wrong.

For jerky, the federal guideline says relative humidity must stay above 90 percent throughout the heating process, and that wet-bulb and dry-bulb temperatures should not differ by more than 4.5°F. Its example: a 2°F difference points to about 94 percent relative humidity, while a difference above 4.5°F points to about 86 percent, which is below the minimum. In plain terms: if the two sensors drift apart during the cook, the room is too dry for the process, and the first things to check are the wick, the water supply, the steam or mist valve and the sensor airflow.

After heating, the same guideline calls for drying to reach a water activity of 0.85 or lower, checked by laboratory testing. A dryer cannot be judged by clock time alone.

Safe product temperatures, in general

The federal food-safety agency recommends heating meat to 160°F (poultry to 165°F) before dehydrating, so that wet heat destroys bacteria; it notes that a household dehydrator may not reach that. It also says a dehydrator should hold 130°F to 140°F throughout drying, and a state extension publication repeats the 160°F and 165°F figures. These are consumer-level numbers. A commercial or farm processor follows the inspector and the written process, not this page. For an HVAC tech the lesson is simple: the temperature and humidity the controller shows must be the temperature and humidity the product sees, so sensor placement, airflow and heat distribution are your job.

Smoke generators, exhaust ducts, creosote and grease

Smoke is incomplete combustion. A university extension bulletin on chimneys explains that creosote is a dark, tar-like residue that forms from incomplete burning and condenses on surfaces below about 250°F; below 150°F it becomes thick and sticky. It lists incomplete combustion, cool surfaces and wet wood as the causes. A smokehouse exhaust duct runs cool compared with a stove flue, and it also carries fat and moisture, so it collects a mix of creosote and grease. That bulletin calls the deposit very flammable and says chimney fires can reach 2,000 to 3,000°F.

What this means for the service tech:

  • Keep the exhaust duct short, with few bends, so the smoke does not cool and condense in it. Dampers and sample ports must stay accessible for cleaning.
  • Do not combine the smokehouse exhaust with a kitchen hood or other duct. See the solid-fuel exhaust lesson for the separate-duct and spark-arrestor reasoning.
  • Cleaning is a scheduled job, not a repair. The same bulletin says to check stove pipes at least every two or three months of operation, and to clean at least once a year or whenever you see heavy soot or creosote. That is written for home stoves; a smokehouse should use the maker's schedule and the fire official's requirements, and smoke-heavy use may call for more.
  • Fan failure, a stuck damper or a blocked duct means smoke and heat back into the room. Interlock the generator to airflow so it shuts down when exhaust is lost, if the maker allows it.

Makeup air for the room

Whatever the exhaust fan removes, something must replace. In a tight building the smokehouse stack can run weakly or backdraft if the room is under negative pressure, pushing smoke and combustion gases into the work area. Bring makeup air into the room, not directly into the chamber, near the generator or fuel-fired unit if you can, and heat it in winter so cold air does not freeze the plumbing or condense on the product. Our own rule of thumb is to treat the smokehouse like any other exhaust appliance and check pressure with the door open and closed, and with other fans running. For the general method, see the industrial drying oven lesson.

Carbon monoxide

Any gas, propane, oil, wood or charcoal fire makes carbon monoxide. A federal public-health page lists fuel-burning devices such as stoves, generators and lanterns as sources, says never to run them in enclosed spaces even with doors open, and lists headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea and confusion as common signs. It adds that sleeping people may die without symptoms. For a smokehouse, that means a fuel-fired heater or smoke generator needs a proper flue and room makeup air, a working carbon monoxide detector in the room, and a rule that a headache in the building is a reason to leave and ventilate first. For combustion basics, see Combustion and CO Safety Basics.

Cooling after the cook

The cook is not the end. A federal cooling guideline for heat-treated meat gives validated schedules. One option: cool from 130°F to 80°F within 1.5 hours, then from 80°F to 40°F within 5 hours, 6.5 hours total. The guideline says the purpose is to limit growth of spore-forming bacteria. Other options exist with other conditions, so the inspector decides which applies. For the tech, that is a refrigeration capacity and airflow question: a cooler loaded with hot product can swamp its evaporator, and a product moved from a 100 percent humid smokehouse into a cooler adds a lot of moisture to the coil. See the butcher shop lesson and the meat processing plant lesson for cooler and wet-room issues.

Our own worked example: water removed from a jerky batch

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume a 200 lb batch of trimmed raw meat. A federal food-safety page says about 1 lb of meat weighs about 4 oz after being made into jerky, so we assume a 25 percent yield. We also assume all of the weight lost is water, and that the drying takes 10 hours. This is a teaching estimate, not a design value. We use about 1,000 Btu per pound of water evaporated; an Oregon State University paper on kiln drying energy says about 1000 Btu/lb is good enough as an average over a drying temperature range (it gives 970 Btu/lb at 212°F and about 1070 Btu/lb at 50°F).

  1. Finished jerky: 200 lb × 0.25 = 50 lb.
  2. Weight lost: 200 lb − 50 lb = 150 lb of water.
  3. Latent energy: 150 lb × 1,000 Btu/lb = 150,000 Btu.
  4. Average over 10 hours: 150,000 Btu ÷ 10 h = 15,000 Btu/h, or about 15 lb of water per hour.

The 15,000 Btu/h is about 1.25 tons of cooling-equivalent latent load (12,000 Btu/h per ton). It is not a refrigeration load: here the heat source supplies it, and the exhaust or a dehumidifier has to carry the vapor away. Real loads peak early, when the surface is wet, so the first hours run higher than the average. If a room uses a dehumidifier instead of an exhaust stack, size it for the peak, not this average.

Field card: Smokehouse and Jerky Room HVAC

Humidity in, smoke out, air back in, and watch the CO.

  • Stages: drying, smoking, cooking. Jerky: heating with humidity first, then drying.
  • Federal jerky guideline: above 90 percent RH while heating; wet and dry bulb within 4.5°F.
  • Drying target is water activity 0.85 or lower, checked by lab testing.
  • Creosote forms on cool surfaces below about 250°F and burns hot. Keep exhaust short and clean.
  • Replace what the exhaust takes with makeup air; test pressure with doors open and closed.
  • Fuel-fired units make carbon monoxide: flue them, add a detector.
  • Cooling example: 130°F to 80°F in 1.5 h, then to 40°F in 5 h.
  • Worked example: 150 lb of water at about 1,000 Btu/lb is 150,000 Btu, or 15,000 Btu/h over 10 h.
