Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise: How to Measure and What They Mean

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Two simple subtractions tell you a lot about a system. In heating, supply air minus return air is the temperature rise. In cooling, return air minus supply air is the temperature split (also called delta T). Both are quick to take, and both point at airflow. This lesson shows how to measure them cleanly and what the numbers mean.

Same idea, opposite direction

Temperature rise on a furnace is the supply air temperature minus the return air temperature. Temperature split on an air conditioner is the return minus the supply. Either way you are measuring how much the air changed as it crossed the heat exchanger or the coil. If a lot of air moves across, each pound of air changes only a little. If little air moves across, each pound changes a lot. That is why these two readings are the first airflow check most technicians make. For the full picture of blowers and duct pressure, see Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Furnace temperature rise

Where the target comes from. The furnace rating plate lists an allowed rise range. Goodman's installation manual for its 96 percent class furnaces says to install the furnace so it runs within its intended temperature rise range, and says that range is on the rating plate. Lennox's installation manual for its ML193DF furnace says the furnace must be adjusted for a rise within the range on the nameplate, and warns that failing to do so can cause erratic limit operation and early heat exchanger failure. The range differs by model, so always read the plate on the unit in front of you. For a feel for the size of the numbers, Carrier's product data for its 59TN7A two-stage furnace lists a certified rise of 40 to 70°F on high heat for every size, and 25 to 55°F (smaller sizes) or 30 to 60°F (larger sizes) on low heat.

How to measure it. Carrier's 58TUA installation instructions tell the installer to put duct thermometers in the return and supply ducts as near the furnace as possible, and to make sure the thermometers cannot see the heat exchangers, so radiant heat does not affect the readings. Wait for the readings to settle, then subtract return from supply. The same manual says to check both high and low fire on a two-stage furnace. One training site's guide suggests letting the furnace run about 15 to 20 minutes first so it stabilizes, and taking the supply reading a few feet from the furnace.

Probe placement and stable readings

An ACHR News article on supply plenum temperature points out that radiant heat from a coil, heat exchanger or heat strips will make a probe read wrong, so pick a spot those parts cannot reach. It also warns that poor air mixing is a common cause of bad readings, and gives an example of a gas furnace with air about 20°F warmer in one trunk than another. Its advice is to use a digital thermometer that reads to 0.1°F, let the probe settle fully before you write a number down, and take more than one reading when the air is not mixed.

AC temperature split, and why a fixed number is only a rough guide

You will hear "a good split is 20 degrees." It is a reasonable first look and a poor rule. ACCA's HVAC blog calls the typical 15 to 20 degree split notoriously lousy, and says the correct number depends on indoor and outdoor conditions and on the manufacturer's data. In its worked example the required split is 16.5°F, found by dividing the unit's listed sensible capacity (17,900 Btu/h) by 1.08 and by the airflow (1,000 CFM).

The reason is moisture. The coil also pulls water out of the air, and that work (latent cooling) does not show up on a thermometer. John Tomczyk's ACHR News article explains that at a given return dry-bulb temperature, the required split gets bigger as return air humidity falls, because less of the coil's capacity goes to removing moisture. His example, 80°F return air at about 60 percent humidity, needs 17.5°F. A measured 20°F there is close but a hint of low airflow. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for wet-bulb and humidity basics, and use the manufacturer's performance data for the real target.

Estimating airflow from the numbers

For air, the sensible heat equation is Btu/h = 1.08 × CFM × temperature change. The 1.08 comes from air density (0.075 lb per cubic foot), 60 minutes per hour, and the specific heat of air (0.24 Btu per pound per degree): 0.075 × 60 × 0.24 = 1.08. Solving for airflow gives CFM = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × rise). The constant assumes air at about 70°F and sea level, so treat the answer as an estimate.

Worked example (hypothetical furnace)

  1. Input: 80,000 Btu/h, rated 96 percent AFUE. Carrier explains that an AFUE rating is a seasonal average, so 96 percent output is only an approximation of what the furnace delivers on a given day.
  2. Estimated output: 80,000 × 0.96 = 76,800 Btu/h.
  3. Measured: return air 70°F, supply air 120°F, thermometers out of sight of the heat exchanger, readings steady.
  4. Rise: 120 − 70 = 50°F.
  5. Check the range. Say the plate reads 40 to 70°F (the Carrier 59TN7A high-heat range quoted above; your plate rules). 50 is inside it, and near the middle.
  6. CFM = 76,800 ÷ (1.08 × 50) = 76,800 ÷ 54 = about 1,422 CFM.

Compare with the end points: at a 40°F rise the same furnace would be moving about 76,800 ÷ (1.08 × 40) = 1,778 CFM, and at 70°F about 76,800 ÷ (1.08 × 70) = 1,016 CFM. This is an estimate only. Static pressure and the manufacturer's blower tables give a firmer answer.

What a low or high reading points to

  • Furnace rise too low. Usually too much air for the heat. One training site says air feels cool at the registers, and below the minimum rise condensation can form in the heat exchanger. Slowing the blower usually fixes it.
  • Furnace rise too high. Usually too little air: a dirty filter, closed or restricted registers, a dirty blower wheel. The limit may begin cycling the burner. Carrier's 58TUA manual says to raise blower speed to reduce rise, but first check gas input, altitude derate and duct restrictions (it warns against static pressure above 0.50 inch w.c.). Never bypass or swap a limit; see High-Limit Trips and Airflow and Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input.
  • AC split larger than the target. Tomczyk says a split larger than required points to airflow, since slow air spends longer on the coil. Check filter, coil, blower and ducts.
  • AC split smaller than the target. He says this points to refrigerant flow rather than air, so go on to Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Field card: Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise

Read the plate, hide the probe, let it settle.

  • Furnace rise = supply minus return; compare to the range on the rating plate.
  • Probes out of the heat exchanger's line of sight; wait for steady readings.
  • AC split = return minus supply; 15 to 20 is a rough guide, not a rule.
  • Target split depends on return humidity and the manufacturer's data.
  • CFM is about Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × rise). Estimate only.
  • High rise or big split: look at airflow. Low AC split: look at refrigerant flow.
