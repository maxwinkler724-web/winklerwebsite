Purging Air From a Hot-Water Heating System: Purge Stations, Zone by Zone

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Vents and an air separator remove the small amounts of air a working system collects. They are not built to clear a loop that was just drained or opened. For that job, techs flush the air out with fast-moving water, one zone at a time. This lesson walks through that purge and the numbers behind it.

Follow the boiler maker first

Follow the boiler maker first. This page is general education. The installation manual for the boiler in front of you sets the fill pressure, the allowed water temperature and any purge steps. If it disagrees with this page, the manual wins.

Why a purge is different from venting

Federal energy guidance for homes says hot-water systems should be bled at the start of each heating season, and a federal report on balancing multifamily hot-water systems describes the normal ongoing job as the air eliminator catching microbubbles that come out of the warming water. That is the slow, everyday path covered in Hydronic Air Separators and Automatic Air Vents. After a drain-down, a new zone or a pump or valve swap, the pipes hold air in bulk, and a small vent can take a long time to clear it. A purge uses the water itself: push enough flow through the pipe that the air is swept out of the system through an open drain instead of waiting for it to rise to a vent.

The hardware: a purge station on each zone return

A purge station is two valves on the zone return near the boiler: a shutoff valve, then a drain valve just after it, usually with a hose-thread outlet. Closing the shutoff directs fill water through the zone and out the drain instead of back into the boiler. If the system has no purge stations, a boiler drain on each zone return and a separate shutoff can do the same job. Adding them is a good habit on any new zone, because they also make flushing and draining simple later.

The steps, in order (our general outline)

  1. Set up. Turn the boiler and circulators off, and let a hot boiler cool first (see the shock section below). Run a hose from the zone drain to a bucket or floor drain.
  2. Close the main return. Shut the valve on the main return line so the fill water cannot take the short way back to the boiler. All incoming water now has to go through a zone.
  3. Isolate one zone. Open the zone you want to purge and close the other zone valves or shutoffs. Purging one zone at a time keeps the flow in that pipe high.
  4. Open the drain and feed the system. Open the drain valve, then open the fill valve (many have a fast-fill lever or bypass). Watch the boiler pressure gauge the whole time. If the drain cannot carry away what the fill valve supplies, pressure climbs, and the relief valve is the thing that will open. Throttle the fill or open the drain more.
  5. Flow until clear. Water leaves the hose full of bubbles and spitting. Keep flowing until it runs steadily with no bubbles. Then close the drain, open the next zone and repeat.
  6. Finish at normal pressure. Close the drains, reopen the main return and all zones, and bring the system to the fill pressure the maker lists. Restart the pumps, then walk the loop and bleed any radiator or vent that still hisses.

Worked example (our own numbers and assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a manual. Assumptions: one zone is 150 ft of 1-inch copper tube, inside diameter 0.995 in; the emitters and fittings add another 4 gal (our guess); we aim for a water velocity of 2 ft per second in the pipe (our assumed target for sweeping bubbles along; check the boiler or fitting maker for any other figure); and we flush three volumes of water to be safe (our habit, not a rule).

  1. Pipe flow area: 3.1416 ÷ 4 × 0.9952 = 0.7776 sq in, or 0.7776 ÷ 144 = 0.00540 sq ft.
  2. Flow at 2 ft/s: 0.00540 × 2 = 0.0108 cu ft per second. One cu ft per second is 448.8 gpm, so 0.0108 × 448.8 = 4.85 gpm. The fill supply and the drain both need to pass about 4.8 gpm.
  3. Quick field check: a 5-gal bucket should fill in 5 ÷ 4.85 × 60 = about 62 seconds or less. If the fill supply is slower, the velocity is lower and a small bubble can sit or ride back.
  4. Water in the pipe: 0.7776 sq in × 12 in/ft = 9.33 cu in per ft, and 9.33 ÷ 231 = 0.0404 gal per ft. Over 150 ft that is 6.06 gal. With the 4 gal of emitters, the zone holds about 10 gal.
  5. One pass through the pipe: 6.06 gal ÷ 4.85 gpm = 1.25 min, which matches 150 ft ÷ 2 ft/s = 75 s.
  6. Three volumes: 3 × 10 gal = 30 gal, and 30 ÷ 4.85 = about 6.2 min for this zone.

The gallons-per-foot figure comes straight from pipe geometry. For other pipe sizes and emitters, see How Many Gallons Are in a Hot-Water Heating System. Note that a larger pipe needs much more flow for the same velocity, and many home fill valves cannot supply it. That is why a long purge on big pipe can leave air behind, and why a zone with high spots may still need a vent.

Cold-water shock on a hot boiler

Feeding cold water into a hot boiler can stress it. Cast iron sections are the usual worry, because a sharp temperature difference makes parts expand unevenly. We have not found a federal or university page that gives a number, so we will not give one. The practical rule: let the boiler cool, and use the maker's stated limits for water temperature and fill. If a boiler was running hot when the system lost water, do not rush cold water into it.

What the separators and vents still do

After the purge, the air separator and automatic vents take over. Cold fill water holds dissolved air that comes out as it heats, so a few days of small hissing and bleeding can be normal after a refill. Federal guidance says to confirm the expansion tank holds air rather than water, because a waterlogged tank causes pressure swings that open the relief valve and invite air back in.

When the air keeps coming back

  • A leak. A federal operations guide advises meters on make-up lines of closed hot-water loops so leaks show up as extra fill. If you keep adding water, find the weep at a valve packing, a flange or a fitting.
  • Low pressure. If the gauge sits too low at the highest point, the system can draw air in at vents or packings. See Boiler Pressure Too High or Too Low and Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer.
  • Cold spots. If one emitter stays cold after a purge, try Some Radiators or Baseboards Cold: Air in a Hot-Water Heating System.

Field card: Purging Air From a Hot-Water Heating System

Fast flow sweeps air out; vents only handle the leftovers.

  • Purge station: shutoff valve, then drain valve, on each zone return.
  • Close the main return, open one zone, run a hose to a bucket or drain.
  • Feed with the fill valve and watch boiler pressure so the relief valve stays shut.
  • Flow until the water runs clear of bubbles, then finish at normal fill pressure.
  • Our example: 1-inch copper at 2 ft/s needs about 4.8 gpm; 10 gal zone, about 6 min for three volumes.
  • Let a hot boiler cool before adding cold water.
  • Air keeps returning: look for a leak or low pressure.
