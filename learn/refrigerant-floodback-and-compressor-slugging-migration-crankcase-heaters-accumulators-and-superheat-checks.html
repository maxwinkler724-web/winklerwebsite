Refrigerant Floodback and Compressor Slugging: Migration, Crankcase Heaters, Accumulators and Superheat Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Technicians use "floodback," "migration" and "slugging" as if they meant one thing. Trade and maker literature separates them by when the liquid arrives and what it does. This lesson sorts the terms, the protection each one calls for, and three field checks you can do with a clamp meter and a thermometer.

Three words, three moments

Retired Ferris State professor John Tomczyk, writing in ACHR News, ties each term to the compressor's state. Flooding (often called floodback) is liquid refrigerant entering the crankcase while the compressor runs. Migration happens only while the compressor is off: refrigerant moves to the coldest spot, often the crankcase, and dissolves in the oil. Slugging is liquid, or liquid and oil, entering the cylinders, and it can follow either of the first two. His crankcase heater article draws the line the same way: slugging at start-up from migrated refrigerant, floodback while running.

About "oil slugging": the sources we read do not define it as a separate event. Tomczyk describes liquid refrigerant and oil together reaching the cylinder, and Bitzer's literature warns about poor oil return from too much superheat. We keep the two ideas apart: a liquid slug is a hydraulic problem, and oil starvation is a lubrication problem. See Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return for the second one.

Common causes, as the sources state them

For floodback, Tomczyk lists a loose expansion valve bulb, an oversized valve, a wrong valve setting that leaves no compressor superheat, overcharge, evaporator fan failure, low evaporator load, capillary overfeeding, a failed defrost clock or heater leaving an iced coil, and a dirty or blocked evaporator. For migration he names no automatic pump-down or a leaking liquid line solenoid, and says cold ambient temperatures and short off cycles make it worse. He lists slugging causes as the flooding ones plus migration and a hunting valve. Tecumseh's charge limitation bulletin is a reminder that charge matters: small self-contained units are "vulnerable to damage from excessive liquid refrigerant." Walk-in defrost problems are covered in Walk-In Cooler and Freezer Defrost, Evaporator Fan Delay and Icing.

Protection, as makers describe it

  • Crankcase heater. Copeland's scroll heater document says it keeps refrigerant out of the shell during standstill and prevents oil dilution and bearing stress at start-up. It must stay energized during off cycles and be on 12 hours before the first start. Heaters are not required below certain charge limits, though they are always required for CO2 models. Tomczyk says a heater can be overpowered near 0 F or in cold wind, and that it does not stop floodback or start-up slugging.
  • Pump-down. Both Tomczyk and Bitzer call for it. Tomczyk: "the only sure remedy" for migration. Bitzer recommends it before any off cycle. Copeland's refrigeration scroll guidelines say a pump-down system should be used to control migration, and that cold air constantly drawn over the compressor can make a heater ineffective. See Pump-Down Cycle and Liquid Line Solenoid Valves.
  • Suction accumulator. Copeland says an accumulator should reduce floodback to a level the compressor can handle, and warns that repeated floodback dilutes oil. Danfoss's scroll instructions call for a pump-down cycle or suction line accumulator when charge is above its limits. Tomczyk notes accumulators "can also flood" in severe cases.
  • Superheat. Bitzer says low suction superheat may cause liquid slugging and bearing washout but that too much causes high discharge temperature and poor oil return, and sends the reader to its selection software for the number. Copeland's guidelines require a minimum stable superheat of at least 5 K for a typical evaporator and expansion valve setup; Danfoss's envelope shows continuous running at 5 K to 20 K for its scroll model.

Damage signs

Tomczyk lists for flooding: a cold, frosted or sweaty crankcase, foaming oil with a low sight-glass level, high current, and broken valves. For migration: oil foaming at start-up, scored parts, high current and broken valves. For slugging: broken valves, head gaskets or connecting rods, and damaged pistons and bearings. Bitzer names bearing washout. Copeland adds that liquid can wash oil off the bearings, leading to overheating.

Field checks

  1. Superheat at the compressor. Read suction pressure, convert it to saturated temperature on the refrigerant's PT chart, measure the suction line temperature near the compressor, and subtract.
  2. Crankcase heater current. With the heater energized, a clamp meter reading times line voltage gives watts to compare with the nameplate. The Copeland heater document gives no amp figure or method, so this is a standard electrical calculation.
  3. Look and feel. Tomczyk's checks are qualitative: a cold or frosted crankcase, oil foam in the sight glass, and current compared to normal. Discharge temperature limits depend on the compressor; Copeland's guidelines list protector trips by model (for example, an external discharge thermostat cut-out of 130 C plus or minus 4 K on some scrolls), so use the maker's data, not a general number.

Worked example

Setup (assumed for illustration). A refrigeration system on R-404A. The suction gauge reading, converted on the refrigerant's PT chart (the conversion comes from a chart, not a formula), gives a saturated temperature of 40 F. The suction line thermometer at the compressor reads 44 F.

Superheat. Our own: 44 F minus 40 F = 4 F.

Compare to a maker minimum. Copeland's guidelines give the minimum in kelvin. Our own: a temperature difference of 5 K is 5 x 1.8 = 9 F. Our own: 4 F is about 4 / 1.8 = 2.2 K. Four degrees is below the 5 K (9 F) minimum stable superheat Copeland gives for a typical evaporator and expansion valve setup, so this reading points to too much liquid arriving. That is a reason to check the causes listed above before touching the valve. The minimum for your compressor is in its own maker data.

Heater watts. A 240 V heater draws 0.29 A. Our own: 240 V x 0.29 A = 69.6 W, roughly 70 W. For reference, Copeland's heater table lists 70 W at 240 V for some scroll models, but the heater on a given compressor should be matched to its own data, and a reading far below nameplate suggests an open or failed heater.

Field card: Floodback, Migration and Slugging

Off cycle is migration, start is slugging risk, run is floodback.

  • Heater on during off cycles; Copeland says 12 hours before first start.
  • Heaters limit migration but do not stop floodback or start-up slugging.
  • Pump-down is the sure remedy for migration, per Tomczyk.
  • Superheat = suction line temperature minus saturated temperature from the PT chart.
  • Copeland: at least 5 K (9 F) stable superheat, typical setup.
  • Heater watts = volts x amps. 240 V x 0.29 A = 69.6 W.
  • Cold, frosted crankcase or foaming oil: look for liquid return.
