Bipolar Ionization and Other In-Duct Electronic Air Cleaners: What EPA and CDC Say

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A box or bar mounted in the duct that promises cleaner, safer air without a filter change is an easy sell. Whether it works is a harder question. This lesson covers what these devices are, what EPA, CDC and ASHRAE say about them, why ozone and other byproducts matter, and what a tech checks on install and maintenance. It does not recommend or rate any brand.

What counts as an in-duct electronic air cleaner

  • Bipolar or needlepoint ionization. Electrodes in the airstream release positive and negative ions. EPA describes it as a technology that generates charged particles in HVAC systems or portable cleaners.
  • Electrostatic precipitators (ESPs). EPA's technical summary says a high-voltage wire charges particles, which collect on oppositely charged plates. The plates load up and must be cleaned.
  • Photocatalytic oxidation (PCO). A UV-lit catalyst is meant to break down gases.
  • Plasma. An electric arc ionizes gases and breaks chemical bonds.

All four put electrical energy or reactive chemistry into the air that then flows to the rooms. A mechanical filter only catches particles. That difference is behind most of the safety questions below.

What the evidence shows

EPA's COVID-19 air cleaner page calls bipolar ionization an emerging technology and says little research evaluates it outside lab conditions. It also says the evidence for safety and effectiveness is less documented than for established options such as filtration. ASHRAE's public filtration and disinfection guidance says ion-based cleaners range from ineffective to very effective and that convincing, rigorous, peer-reviewed studies on these emerging technologies do not currently exist. It also notes that ASHRAE's filter test method can be used on many of these devices but does not officially cover them.

Published tests look the same way. A 2025 study in PubMed Central tested a portable needlepoint unit in sealed and small test rooms and reported no detectable ozone or formaldehyde, with lab reductions in airborne virus and bacteria. The authors list limits: few repetitions, one device, and no health effects studied. The text we read did not include a funding statement, so check the full paper for that. A California Air Resources Board (CARB) white paper summarizes in-duct tests, including a chamber test where particle loss rates barely changed with the ionizer on (about 1.27 per hour off and 1.28 on for PM2.5), and VOC changes where some compounds fell and others rose. Evidence for one device in one setting cannot be assumed for another.

Byproducts: ozone, formaldehyde and others

EPA's technical summary says ionizers generate ozone, and that ESPs sometimes have high ozone and nitrogen oxide generation rates. Ozone is a lung irritant. Even below health limits it reacts with indoor chemicals and can form ultrafine particles, formaldehyde and other aldehydes. For PCO it lists formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide and ozone as possible byproducts. For plasma it lists particles, ozone, formaldehyde, carbon monoxide and other gases. EPA says bipolar ionization has the potential to generate ozone and other harmful byproducts unless precautions are taken in design and maintenance. Results vary by product: the studies above found little or no ozone from the needlepoint devices tested, while the CARB white paper reports in-duct ozone emissions across devices from 8.4 to 347 mg/hr, with the highest from a unit containing UV.

What EPA, CDC and ASHRAE tell you to do

  • Ask for data. ASHRAE says to request testing data that shows a clear benefit under intended-use conditions, ideally from several sources including independent third parties, and to question claims based on one device in one room.
  • Ask about ozone. EPA recommends a device that meets UL 2998. ASHRAE says to verify UL 867 at minimum, and preferably UL 2998. CARB's white paper notes that UL 2998 does not guarantee literally zero ozone, despite its title.
  • Do not use ozone generators in occupied spaces. EPA is firm on this.
  • Start with proven steps. CDC's ventilation guidance (archived page) recommends MERV 13 or higher filters when possible, bringing in outdoor air, and a goal of 5 or more air changes per hour of clean air. The page we read does not mention ionization or electronic air cleaners, so we cannot say what CDC thinks of them.

California's ozone limit

CARB's consumer page says electronic air cleaners sold in California must meet an ozone emission limit of 0.050 ppm (50 ppb), carry the label "Meets California ozone emissions limit: CARB certified," and be listed by CARB. It says that as of October 2020, electronic in-duct air cleaning devices are also subject to the rule. A different CARB fact sheet we also read lists in-duct cleaners as exempt, so confirm with CARB's current list. The testing standard is UL 867 (EPA's summary says CARB uses that test method). The tighter UL 2998 limit is 5 ppb, ten times lower, according to CARB's white paper. Those are standards named here in plain text only; we do not link copies.

Worked example: equivalent clean air, filter vs unverified claim

Our own arithmetic. Assumptions: a 3-ton system moving 1,200 cfm through the duct (our assumption of about 400 cfm per ton), running continuously, particles in the 0.3 to 1 micrometer range. EPA's technical summary says MERV 13 and above filters have at least 50 percent removal efficiency for 0.3 to 1 micrometer particles. We use that 50 percent floor for a single pass, which understates many real MERV 13 filters.

  1. Filter: 1,200 cfm x 0.50 = 600 cfm of equivalent particle-free air per pass.
  2. A vendor sheet claims an ionizer removes "90 percent" of particles. The 90 percent is our invented number, standing in for any claim without third-party data. If true: 1,200 x 0.90 = 1,080 cfm.
  3. With no test report, the claimed 1,080 cfm cannot be counted. The data-backed number is 600 cfm. Counting the claim as zero would be unfair, but so would counting it as 1,080.

The same math shows why EPA says ionizers usually have very low CADRs, since they move no air themselves. Also note that the 600 cfm applies only while the blower runs, and neither figure accounts for bypass around the filter or the byproducts. A device with documented particle results and a documented ozone test is a different case, and then you can do this math with its tested efficiency.

Compared with higher-MERV filtration and outdoor air

EPA's summary says medium-high efficiency filters such as most MERV 13 filters are likely to be almost as effective as HEPA filters for most particle sizes, though field studies have not confirmed this under real system runtimes. Filters also come with published rating methods and no electrical byproducts. Outdoor air dilutes everything, including gases and CO2, which no particle device can do. Filter upgrades have limits: higher resistance can cut airflow, so check the equipment. See our lessons on filters and MERV and ventilation basics.

What techs check on install and maintenance

This comes from one manufacturer's installation instructions for an in-duct electronic air cleaner (AIRA series, hosted on shareddocs.com). Always follow the instructions for the unit in front of you.

  • Power off first. Turn off all incoming power. The sheet says to discharge residual charge in the secondary with an insulated-handle screwdriver after de-energizing.
  • Airflow range. Stay inside the listed range (350 to 1,200 cfm for one model). Using it outside the range is not recommended.
  • Prefilters in place. They keep lint and pet hair off the collector cells.
  • Power indication. A green light shows input power and a red light shows output voltage. A red light that dims or goes out may mean shorted cells.
  • Cleaning. The sheet calls for washing cells about every 2 months for most homes, and wiping ionizing wires. ASHRAE also warns that silicone buildup on ESP wires reduces efficiency.
  • Odor or damage. The sheet says damaged or dirty cells, bent plates or loose wires can raise ozone. Check them if you smell ozone.

Ionizer bars and PCO cells have their own service steps, and we did not find a public maker document we could cite for them. For UV lamps see UV lights in HVAC systems. For portable units see air purifiers and whole-house air cleaners, and for the ozone background see ozone rules and the Montreal Protocol.

Field card: In-Duct Electronic Air Cleaners

Particle filter first. Ask for third-party data on anything that charges the air.

  • Types: bipolar ionization, ESP, PCO, plasma.
  • EPA: bipolar ionization is emerging, with little research outside the lab.
  • Byproducts to ask about: ozone, formaldehyde, other aldehydes.
  • Ozone: CARB limit is 50 ppb; EPA points to UL 2998; ASHRAE says UL 867 at minimum.
  • MERV 13 filter: at least 50 percent at 0.3 to 1 micrometer (EPA summary).
  • Install: power off, airflow range, prefilters, indicator lights.
  • Maintain: clean cells and wires on the maker's schedule.
