Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing on Motors and Compressors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An ohmmeter tells you whether a winding is open. A megohmmeter (often called a megger) asks a different question: is the varnish and paper between the copper and the compressor shell still holding? This lesson covers what the reading means, how to take it safely, and how to judge a number you get back.

Check the manufacturer first

Check the manufacturer first. This page is general education built from published material read in 2026. Maker limits differ by model and by compressor type, so the compressor maker's data for your exact unit wins over anything here.

What the test measures

The meter pushes a steady DC voltage, far higher than a multimeter uses, between the winding and the compressor shell. A small leakage current flows through and across the insulation, and the meter shows voltage divided by current as megohms. Fieldpiece's megohmmeter manual says the same test works for insulation "from winding to ground in a motor, compressor, or transformer," and that contaminants in a refrigeration system can attack winding insulation. An ACHR News article on megohmmeters adds that a regular ohmmeter cannot replace a megger, because it does not generate enough voltage to find a high-resistance fault. If you need to find the terminals and check for a hard short first, see Checking Compressor Windings.

Safety first

  • Lock out and prove dead. Follow Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. The megger is used on a dead, isolated motor only. A multimeter has its own limits; see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.
  • Never test under vacuum. Fluke's compressor application note says never to perform insulation resistance testing, or operate a compressor, when the system is under a vacuum. ACHR News, Fieldpiece and Fluke all give the same warning. Copeland's scroll literature says never to hipot a compressor in a vacuum and that internal arcing may occur if any compressor is started in one, and a Trane CenTraVac manual says not to apply electrical power to a motor in a vacuum. Evacuation steps are in Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.
  • Isolate the motor. Remove every wire from the compressor terminals, as Fluke and Fieldpiece both instruct. Fluke also says to disconnect motor drives, PLCs and transmitters before insulation testing, because electronics can be damaged by a higher-than-normal test voltage. A Daikin Applied starter manual lists a megger check of motor and cabling and says to disconnect from the starter before testing. A variable-speed compressor drive counts: see Inverter Drives and Variable-Speed Compressors.
  • Do not touch the leads or terminals during the test, and expect stored charge afterward. Megger's guide says to discharge the capacitance after the test before handling the leads, for about four times as long as the voltage was applied.

Choosing the test voltage

For compressor motors, Fluke's application note uses 500 volts DC, and ACHR News says standard meggers put out about 500 volts DC. Megger's guide pairs 440 to 550 volt AC equipment with 500 or 1,000 volts DC. Fluke's testing guide gives a rule of thumb of about twice the equipment rating (so 1,000 volts for 460 to 600 volt gear) but also says that when in doubt, use the lower voltage. Fieldpiece's SMG5 is a 1,000 volt tester. ACHR News warns that a higher-output meter should be used only by an experienced tech, since too much voltage for too long can weaken a winding. Pick the voltage the compressor maker names; if none is given for your unit, 500 volts DC is the figure in the sources above. Hold the test about 60 seconds, as Fluke's note does.

Temperature changes the number

Insulation resistance falls as it warms. Megger's guide gives the rule of thumb: for every 10°C rise, halve the resistance. Fluke's note expresses it as a factor, KT = 0.5^((TR − TA)/10), with a reference temperature TR of 40°C (104°F), and Fluke cites IEEE 43 for correcting readings to 40°C. Multiply the measured value by KT. Fluke uses the compressor terminal temperature as the best stand-in for winding temperature, and wants terminals above the dew point so moisture on them does not skew the reading. Other makers pick other bases (a Megger article suggests 20°C), so correct every reading to the same one and write down which.

Worked example: one reading, then a trend

This is an invented 460 volt compressor, and the numbers are for practice, not a maker spec.

  1. Convert temperature. 104°F is (104 − 32) × 5/9 = 40°C.
  2. Correct to 40°C. KT = 0.5^((40 − 40)/10) = 0.5^0 = 1.00. Corrected value = 40 × 1.00 = 40 megohms. The reading was already at the reference.
  3. Compare with minimums. Fluke describes the IEEE 43 minimum as 1 megohm plus 1 megohm per kV: 1 + 0.46 = 1.46 megohms. Trane's rule for hermetic motors, 1,000 ohms per applied volt, gives 460,000 ohms, or 0.46 megohm. Forty megohms clears both. But Fluke warns modern insulation may be unacceptable below 100 megohms, ACHR News calls 100 megohms and up good, and Fieldpiece's chart puts 20 to 50 megohms at "contamination/moisture." So the motor passes the bare minimums yet is not healthy.
  4. Correct the older readings. A year ago: 120 megohms at 95°F = 35°C. KT = 0.5^((40 − 35)/10) = 0.5^0.5 = 0.7071. Corrected = 120 × 0.7071 = 84.9 megohms. Six months ago: 50 megohms at 113°F = 45°C. KT = 0.5^((40 − 45)/10) = 0.5^(−0.5) = 1.4142. Corrected = 50 × 1.4142 = 70.7 megohms.
  5. Percent drop. 84.9 to 70.7: (84.9 − 70.7) / 84.9 = 16.7%. 70.7 to 40.0: 30.7 / 70.7 = 43.4%. Whole year: (84.9 − 40.0) / 84.9 = 52.9%.

Uncorrected, 120 to 40 looks like a 66.7% drop, which overstates it, and 120 to 50 looks gentler than it really was. The corrected curve shows steady decline that is speeding up. Trane's support page says readings should hold steady year to year without significant change, so a 53% fall is a reason to look for moisture, acid or contamination, check the filter drier (see Compressor Basics and Failure Modes), and retest, not to wait for a ground fault.

Trending habits

Fluke says to log each reading with terminal temperature and test voltage, and that testing should start at installation and continue through the equipment's life. Fluke also notes that a first reading with the compressor off is more likely to show oil or refrigerant contamination, and suggests a second one after it has run 5 to 10 minutes. Off-scale readings (above 2,000 megohms on Fluke's chart) are logged but cannot show a trend. Fieldpiece says megohm readings work best when the same equipment is measured over time.

Polarization index in brief

Polarization index (PI) is the 10-minute reading divided by the 1-minute reading, per Fluke and Megger. In healthy insulation the reading keeps climbing as the test runs; in wet or dirty insulation it stays flat. Megger's older guide lists PI below 1 as dangerous, 1 to 2 questionable, 2 to 4 good and above 4 excellent, and marks the table tentative. Trane's page says a PI test should also be done on chiller motors.

Field card: Megohmmeter Testing

Dead, wires off, not in vacuum, corrected to one temperature, logged.

  • The megger measures leakage from winding to shell, not winding continuity.
  • Never test or power a compressor under vacuum.
  • Remove wires and disconnect drives and electronics first.
  • 500 V DC is the figure in Fluke's compressor note; follow the maker's value.
  • Fluke: KT = 0.5^((40 − Tactual)/10); multiply the reading by it.
  • Passing a bare minimum is not the same as healthy; watch the trend.
  • Discharge the winding after the test before touching leads.
