Bakery HVAC: Oven Heat Loads, Proofer Humidity, Flour Dust and Retarder-Cooler Service

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A bakery stacks four problems in one room: an oven that dumps heat, proofers that make their own humid air, flour dust that is both a lung hazard and a fire hazard, and refrigerated dough boxes that run in a dusty, warm space. This lesson walks through each, with one worked cooling example, and points to other lessons for the general kitchen, fan, dust collector and walk-in topics.

Check the rules where you work

Check with your local authority. Hood type, fire protection and dust rules are set by the local building, mechanical and fire officials. This page is general education built from public agency pages and is not a design or code opinion.

Oven heat and the hood question

A deck or rack oven is a large, hot box. Some of its input goes into the product and up the flue or hood, and the rest leaks into the room through walls, doors and the floor. An ASHRAE Journal article on kitchen cooling loads says appliance heat gain comes from published tables for hooded and unhooded appliances, so the room share depends on the oven, the hood and the capture. We assume a fraction below.

Which hood goes over the oven is a call for the local authority. Merced County Environmental Health describes a Type I hood as the one for grease or smoke and a Type II hood as the one for steam, vapor, heat or odors. It says oversized bakery ovens with rack-size doors need a canopy or eyebrow hood, with at least 6 inches of overhang on open sides and 36 inches past the door openings. It does not name a hood type for them. A Minnesota Department of Health guideline uses a flowchart: if the process makes smoke or grease-laden vapor, Type I; if it makes only fumes, steam or heat that the room HVAC cannot handle, Type II may be enough. The same guideline says wood-fired hearth ovens need separate welded Type I ducts. A port authority standard says a convection oven used for cooking generally needs Type I, a warming-only use may allow Type II, and equipment that is not listed is decided by the authority from the data sheet and the menu. In short, bring the oven data sheet and the product list to plan review and ask.

For hood and make-up air basics see Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics, and for the roof fan side see Restaurant Upblast Exhaust Fans.

Worked example: how much cooling the oven room needs

Every input here is our own assumption, not a measurement. Change them for a real job.

  • Assumed oven input: 200,000 Btu/h.
  • Assumed fraction released to the room (the rest leaves through the flue and hood): 10 percent.
  • Assumed spot-cooling air: 62 F supply into a 82 F work zone, so a 20 F difference.

Step 1, heat to the room: 200,000 × 0.10 = 20,000 Btu/h.

Step 2, convert to tons using 12,000 Btu/h per ton: 20,000 ÷ 12,000 = 1.67 tons.

Step 3, spot-cooling airflow with the sensible-heat formula, cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × temperature difference). The 1.08 is our own arithmetic for standard air: 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb-F. So cfm = 20,000 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 20,000 ÷ 21.6 = about 926 cfm.

That 1.67 tons covers only the oven. Add people, mixers, lights, the proofer and the building load. If the true room share were 20 percent, the same math gives 3.3 tons and about 1,850 cfm, so measure rather than trust an assumption. See Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers for spot-cooling gear and its limits.

Proofer temperature and humidity

Utah State University Extension, writing for home bakers, says to keep first-rise dough at 85 to 90 F and shaped loaves at 80 to 85 F, covered, away from drafts, with a damp cloth to stop the surface drying. Commercial practice runs warmer. A trade-press piece on the classic baking text says final proof is held at 95 to 120 F depending on the product, that yeast is most active at 95 to 104 F, and that humidity should be as high as possible so the surface does not dry and split. It gives dough entering the proofer at 82 to 86 F.

A maker manual for proofer and retarder-proofer cabinets (distributor-hosted copy, form 36709) allows 65 to 115 F and 25 to 95 percent humidity. It gives 95 F and 85 percent as a typical setting and 75 F for croissants. The humidity is deliberate, and it is a load on the room. Our own tip: keep proofer doors closed and watch for condensation on cold ducts and walls nearby.

Retarder-proofer and dough cooler service basics

The same trade-press piece says dough is retarded at 35 to 40 F and about 85 percent humidity, and stays stable for up to 48 hours. The cabinet manual lists a retarding range of 34 to 50 F, with defrost defaults of every 6 hours for 10 minutes, and says service personnel should check the refrigeration coils and the air-duct drain every 6 months. It also calls for disconnecting power and following lockout/tagout before maintenance.

  • Condenser coil and filter: in a bakery the coil collects flour. Our own caution: flour can mat a coil and raise head pressure in hot weather, so clean it with a dust-rated vacuum and avoid compressed air that throws dust into the room.
  • Drain and defrost: a plugged drain or heater fault shows up as ice or water on the floor.
  • Door gaskets: a leaking gasket pulls humid room air into a 36 F box and ices the coil.

The refrigeration side is the same as other reach-in and walk-in boxes; see Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics.

Flour dust: fire and lungs

Fire. OSHA says any combustible material can burn rapidly in finely divided form and lists flour, sugar, starch and spice among food dusts. Its dust explosion pentagon has five elements: oxygen, heat, fuel, dispersion and confinement, and without one of them there is no explosion. A first blast can shake loose settled dust and cause a worse second explosion. OSHA advises using only vacuums approved for dust collection and avoiding cleaning methods that make dust clouds near ignition sources. Federal rule 29 CFR 1910.22 requires workplaces to be kept clean and workroom floors clean and, where feasible, dry. OSHA also points to NFPA 61 and NFPA 654 for food dust. We name those standards in plain text because we do not link copies; ask the fire authority which applies.

Lungs. A Massachusetts health department summary hosted by CDC says 7 to 15 percent of bakers are estimated to have baker's asthma, that short peak exposures may matter even if the shift average looks low, and that wheat flour and added enzymes such as amylase are causes. A NIOSH study at a large commercial bakery measured average inhalable flour dust of 0.235 mg/m³ in a lower-exposure group and 3.01 mg/m³ in a higher-exposure group, and the higher group had more wheezing and runny nose. A NIOSH health hazard evaluation (a poultry breading plant, not a bakery, but the same dust) recommended the ACGIH limit of 0.5 mg/m³ for inhalable flour dust and said disconnected or uncapped local exhaust and open conveyors let dust build up.

Ventilation and filter loading

Our own guidance, based on those findings: put exhaust at the dump station and mixer first, keep ducts connected, and keep supply diffusers from blowing across the bench, because jets stir settled dust back into the air. Flour loads filters fast, so use a differential pressure gauge and a change schedule, and keep spare filters on hand. As with welding dust collectors in Welding Fume Extraction and Dust Collectors, a dust-loaded filter is both a pressure-drop problem and a fuel store. Ask the fire authority before returning collector air into the building system.

Summer comfort in production

Treat the oven line as the problem. Capture it at the hood, put cool air on people at the stations they work, keep the proofer doors closed, and keep the mixer area on its own exhaust. Ask the crew where it feels worst and measure there.

Field card: Bakery HVAC

Heat, steam and flour in one room.

  • Hood type (I or II) is the local authority's call; bring the oven data sheet and product list.
  • Our example: 200,000 Btu/h input x 10% assumed to the room = 20,000 Btu/h = 1.67 tons.
  • Spot cooling: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x temperature difference); 20,000 / 21.6 = about 926 cfm.
  • Proofer: maker manual typical 95 F and 85% humidity; retarding about 35 to 40 F per trade press.
  • Coils and drains: check every 6 months per the maker; lockout/tagout first.
  • Flour: fire hazard and baker's asthma hazard. Dust-rated vacuums, no dust clouds, filters on a schedule.
