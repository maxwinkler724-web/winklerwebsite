Pet Grooming Salon and Dog Daycare HVAC: Dryer Heat, Bath Humidity, Hair on Filters and Coils, Exhaust and Odor

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A grooming room turns electricity and tap water into warm, wet, hairy air. Tubs add moisture, dryers add heat, and every dog sheds into the return. This lesson covers what that does to equipment, how to size the extra cooling, and why the lobby should stay slightly positive.

Check the rules that apply to your site

Check the rules that apply to your site. Local health, animal-care and mechanical codes differ, and this page is general education. The federal standards we quote are a reference point, not the rule for a typical groomer. Confirm requirements with the local authority and the equipment instructions.

Where the heat and moisture come from

Almost every watt a dryer draws ends up as heat in the room unless it is ducted out. The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatthour as 3,412 Btu, so each kilowatt running is 3,412 Btu per hour of cooling load.

Water is the second load. Drying turns the water in a coat into vapor, which takes latent heat and leaves moisture in the room air. Using NIST enthalpies of formation for liquid and gaseous water, we get about 1,050 Btu per pound evaporated (arithmetic below). The EPA suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and venting moisture-producing appliances outdoors where possible.

Hair, filters and coils

The U.S. Department of Energy says clogged filters cut airflow and efficiency, and that obstructed airflow lets air bypass the filter and deposit dirt on the evaporator coil. It suggests changing filters about every month or two in cooling season, and more often with fur-bearing pets. A grooming room is the extreme case, so we treat monthly as the ceiling and set the interval by inspecting the filter.

  • Return grilles. Hair mats on the grille face first and chokes the return before the filter sees it. Staff can clear it weekly.
  • Filter choice. The EPA says a higher MERV traps smaller particles and suggests MERV 13 or as high as the fan and filter slot can accommodate. We think a coarse pre-filter ahead of the main filter is easier to swap often. Confirm the fan can still move the air; see the filter rack lesson below.
  • Evaporator coil. Hair that bypasses the filter insulates the coil and cuts airflow, the setup for ice. See the frozen coil lesson below.
  • Condenser coil. DOE notes outdoor coils clog with dirt and nearby plants and advises keeping about 2 feet clear. A dryer or door exhaust blowing lint toward the condenser will do the same, so moving the exhaust beats monthly cleaning.

Exhaust for the bath and drying area

The cleanest fix is to take moisture and odor out at the source: an exhaust fan near the tubs and dryers, with makeup air coming from the lobby side or a dedicated opening. Exhaust larger than supply makes the grooming room slightly negative, which pulls lobby air toward the work area. Exhaust costs cooling energy because the replacement air must be cooled; the worked example puts a number on that. The ventilation standard for acceptable indoor air quality (ASHRAE 62.1) sets outdoor air rates by space type, and the code edition your jurisdiction adopted decides which applies.

Odor and ammonia

Urine, wet hair and shampoo all add odor. Ammonia has exposure limits: NIOSH lists 25 ppm over a workday with a 35 ppm short-term limit, and OSHA lists 50 ppm over a workday. For odor complaints, check exhaust flow, drain traps and cleaning before blaming the air handler. The kennel and shelter lessons below go deeper on air changes and washdown.

Lobby at slight positive pressure

A small positive pressure in the lobby sends air toward the grooming room each time the door opens, which keeps hair, humidity and odor in the work area. We usually get it by supplying slightly more air to the lobby than is returned there and letting the grooming exhaust pull the difference. A door that closes, or a transfer grille, helps; a door propped open defeats the design.

Heat stress risk to dogs in cage dryers

UC Davis defines heat stroke in dogs as a body temperature above 105.8 F, against a normal range of about 99.5 to 102.5 F, and says above 106 F permanent brain damage may occur. It lists confinement with poor ventilation and warm, humid air as risk factors, along with short flat faces, obesity and thick coats. Cornell puts the threshold at 105 F or higher and notes dogs rely on panting to cool down. Neither page names dryers, so the link to cage dryers is our own reasoning: a small box of heated air and wet fur works against the evaporation and panting a dog depends on.

Some governments act on this. A Los Angeles County grooming rule bans enclosed cage dryers with heating elements and no safety vents, requires dryers be placed where staff can monitor the animals, and requires checks at least every 15 minutes. A New York bill signed in 2012 bars using a cage or box dryer with its heating element on; read the final chaptered text for details. The federal standard for regulated dog facilities (9 CFR 3.2) requires auxiliary ventilation at 85 F and says temperature must not stay above 85 F for more than 4 consecutive hours. It does not govern a typical groomer, so we use it only as a yardstick: a room in the mid 80s is a room where dryers should stop.

Dryer electrical loads

A heater-equipped cage dryer or large blower can use much of a branch circuit, and several in one room can trip breakers or overheat cords. Read the nameplates, count what runs at the same time, and follow the electrical code for circuit sizing instead of chaining power strips. That same power is added to the cooling load below.

Worked example (our own): one busy hour

Every input here is an assumption for illustration, not a typical figure from a source. Use your own nameplates and dog counts.

  1. Dryers (assumed): 2 high-velocity dryers at 1.5 kW plus 2 cage dryers at 1.2 kW, all running the full hour. 3.0 + 2.4 = 5.4 kW.
  2. Heat released: 5.4 x 3,412 (EIA) = 18,425 Btu per hour.
  3. Water (assumed): 3 dogs per hour, about 1.0 pound of water each. 3.0 lb per hour.
  4. Latent heat: NIST lists -285.830 kJ/mol (liquid) and -241.826 kJ/mol (gas), a 44.004 kJ/mol difference. Divide by 18.015 g/mol for 2.443 kJ/g, or 2,443 kJ/kg, and multiply by 0.4299 for about 1,050 Btu per pound.
  5. Latent load: 3.0 x 1,050 = 3,150 Btu per hour. That energy comes out of the warm air, so it shifts from sensible to latent and does not add to the total.
  6. Sensible load: 18,425 - 3,150 = 15,275 Btu per hour.
  7. Tons: OSU Extension states one ton is 12,000 Btu per hour. 18,425 / 12,000 = about 1.5 tons for the dryers alone.
  8. Exhaust makeup air (assumed 300 cfm, 15 F hotter outside): 1.08 comes from 0.075 lb per cubic foot x 60 minutes x 0.24 Btu per pound per F (standard air). 1.08 x 300 x 15 = 4,860 Btu per hour, about 0.4 ton. The makeup air latent load is not included.
  9. Total: 18,425 + 4,860 = 23,285 Btu per hour, about 1.9 tons.
  10. Humidity rise: 300 x 0.075 x 60 = 1,350 lb of air per hour. 3.0 / 1,350 = 0.0022 lb per lb, about 16 grains per pound (7,000 grains per pound), if the vapor is carried out rather than recirculated.

Not counted: the dogs' own heat, hot bath water, lights, and the building envelope.

Common service calls

  • Frozen coil. Return grille and filter matted with hair. Clear them, thaw the coil, then check airflow.
  • Hair-matted condenser near a dryer exhaust. Clean the fins and redirect the exhaust.
  • Drain clog. Hair and slime in the pan and line trip the overflow switch. Clear the line and pan.

Field card: Grooming salon and dog daycare HVAC

Dryers are heaters, dogs are filters, the lobby stays positive.

  • Dryer kW x 3,412 = Btu per hour into the room (EIA); 12,000 Btu per hour is one ton.
  • Evaporating water takes about 1,050 Btu per pound (our arithmetic from NIST data).
  • Check filter and return grille by eye; monthly is the ceiling, not the target (DOE says more often with fur-bearing pets).
  • Keep the condenser clear and away from dryer exhaust lint.
  • Dogs: heat stroke is above about 105 to 106 F body temperature (UC Davis, Cornell); watch cage dryers closely.
  • Exhaust the tub and dryer area; keep the lobby slightly positive.
