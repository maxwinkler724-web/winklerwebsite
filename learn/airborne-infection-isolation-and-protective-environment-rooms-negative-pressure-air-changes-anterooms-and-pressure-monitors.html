Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms: Pressure, Air Changes, Anterooms and Monitors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hospital isolation room is a normal-looking room with one strict rule: air must cross the door in one direction, all the time. This lesson is the room-level detail behind that rule: the two room types, how the numbers fit together, why the anteroom and the door gap matter, and what a tech checks on a service call. For the CDC pressure table itself, start with our companion lesson linked below.

Two rooms, two jobs

An airborne infection isolation (AII) room protects the building from the patient. CDC's guideline page says it is held at negative pressure, with air flowing into the room, so airborne germs stay inside. A protective environment (PE) room protects the patient from the building. CDC says it is held at positive pressure, with air flowing out to the adjacent area, and its supply air goes through a filter rated 99.97 percent at 0.3 micron. A bed can need both jobs at once. CDC says that when a patient needs both PE and AII, return air should be HEPA filtered or exhausted outside. A 2021 NIH technical bulletin lists exhaust grille placement for each type: for AII rooms, directly above the bed or at the headwall; for PE rooms, near the patient room door.

The numbers that tie together

The NIH bulletin gives the baseline: AII, PE and combined rooms need at least 12 total air changes per hour (ACH), with 2 ACH from outdoor air. The pressure floor is 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 Pa), negative for AII and positive for PE, measured against adjacent non-isolation rooms. A CDC and NIOSH measles checklist repeats the 0.01 in. w.c. negative minimum and the 12 total ACH with at least 2 ACH outdoor air, unless local authorities set something different. Where a state licenses the hospital, that state's rules and plan review decide what applies. In New Jersey, for example, the rules we read (N.J.A.C. 8:43G-2.3) send plans for new construction or renovation to the Health Facilities Construction Service for review, so the design documents for the job are the final word. We also name ASHRAE Standard 170 and the FGI Guidelines because designers use them, but we did not read them for this page, so we give no values from them.

Exhaust: outdoors or through HEPA

CDC says air from rooms housing TB patients is exhausted outside if possible, or passed through a HEPA filter before recirculation. It also says HEPA filtering of AII exhaust should not be required when the exhaust is properly located to prevent re-entry into the building. The NIOSH checklist says exhaust either goes directly outdoors or passes completely through HEPA filtration before mixing with other exhaust. It puts outdoor discharge at least 25 ft horizontally from air intakes, openable windows and doors, and public areas, and at least 10 ft above the adjoining roof. The NIH bulletin adds that suite, toilet and anteroom exhaust is discharged directly outdoors. For a tech, that means a stuck exhaust damper or a loaded HEPA filter is not a comfort problem. It is a pressure problem and a containment problem.

Portable HEPA units are a different tool. CDC and NIOSH describe them as an emergency option when no proper room exists, and say verification of inward airflow should be shown with a handheld smoke generator or smoke tube and documented before each occupancy. A recirculating unit cleans room air but does not by itself make a room negative to the corridor.

The anteroom: a pressure airlock

CDC says AII rooms can be built with or without an anteroom. When one is present, the NIOSH checklist calls for at least 10 total ACH and a pressure that is positive to the AII room and negative to the corridor. Read that as a staircase: corridor, then anteroom, then patient room, each step a little more negative. Air walks down the stairs toward the patient. The NIH bulletin says anterooms need their own supply and exhaust terminal units so their pressure can be controlled reliably. Two doors, never open together, keep the staircase intact.

Worked example: one room, step by step

All inputs here are Our own assumptions, used to show the method. They are not a design. The room is 12 ft x 14 ft x 9 ft, and we assume the 12 ACH design count is taken on exhaust. Your project documents say which side they count, so confirm that first.

  1. Volume = 12 x 14 x 9 = 1,512 ft³.
  2. Exhaust for 12 ACH = 12 x 1,512 / 60 = 18,144 / 60 = 302.4 cfm.
  3. Assume the balancer sets supply 75 cfm below exhaust. Supply = 302.4 - 75 = 227.4 cfm, which is 227.4 x 60 / 1,512 = 9.0 ACH on supply. The offset is 75 / 302.4 = 25 percent of exhaust.
  4. Does 75 cfm hold 0.01 in. w.c.? Assume a 36 in. wide door with a 0.75 in. undercut: 36 x 0.75 = 27 in² = 0.1875 ft². Ideal velocity at 0.01 in. w.c. = 4005 x √0.01 = 400.5 fpm, using the same 4005 relation as in our duct traverse lesson. Ideal leakage = 0.1875 x 400.5 = 75.1 cfm.

So in this ideal case the 75 cfm offset and the door gap agree at about 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 Pa). Real gaps pass less air than the ideal figure, so the same offset gives a bit more pressure, and a bigger gap or an open door gives less. The check is a balance: offset in, leakage out. If exhaust drops, the pressure drops with it. The sources we read do not set an offset or an undercut size, so treat those as design values to read from the drawings. For an anteroom of 6 x 7 x 9 ft (378 ft³) and the NIOSH 10 ACH minimum, we get 10 x 378 / 60 = 63 cfm.

Monitors and alarms

The NIOSH checklist calls for a calibrated differential pressure gauge to verify negative pressure and a permanently installed visual monitor for continuous watching, with daily pressure logs whenever an isolated infectious patient is present, and airflow and ACH measurements recorded and reviewed at least annually. The NIH bulletin says pressure is monitored with a visual display at each door, either a magnehelic gauge or a digital readout. Maker pages show what electronics add. One maker lists audible and visual alarms with adjustable delays to cut nuisance alarms, BACnet or Modbus connection to a building automation system, a key switch that puts the room in a neutral mode, and monitoring of up to two isolation rooms and one anteroom. Another maker lists alarms for pressure, temperature, humidity and a digital door input. Neither page we read gives alarm setpoints, so use the facility's. A neutral mode switch is a facility decision, not a tech's.

What a tech checks

CDC's air page prefers a hand-held calibrated device that gives a numerical reading, and lists smoke tubes, flutter strips, ping-pong balls or tissue as qualitative checks of direction. A sound routine uses both: a number from the gauge and a picture from smoke at the door gap.

  • Door: self-closing doors are mandatory per CDC. Check the closer, latch and gap with the door shut.
  • Monitor against a reference gauge: compare readings, and check that the sensing port and tubing are not blocked.
  • Smoke at the door gap: smoke should be pulled into the room.
  • Exhaust: fan running, dampers open, HEPA filter condition if fitted, and nothing blocking the grille.
  • Airflow: measure supply and exhaust, not just pressure.
  • Alarm test: only with the facility's approval, and restore everything after.
  • Log it: record readings and what you changed.

OSHA's technical manual says air pressure in isolation rooms should be negative, and that is all it says on the point; the detail comes from CDC and NIOSH.

Field card: Isolation Room Pressure

Exhaust more than supply for AII. Prove it with a gauge and smoke.

  • AII: negative, air flows in. PE: positive, air flows out.
  • NIH bulletin: 12 total ACH, 2 outdoor; 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 Pa) either way.
  • Anteroom (NIOSH checklist): 10 ACH minimum, between corridor and room in pressure.
  • Exhaust outdoors or fully through HEPA. Self-closing doors.
  • cfm = ACH x volume / 60. Door leakage ideal cfm = area (ft²) x 4005 x √(in. w.c.).
  • Check: calibrated gauge, monitor, smoke at the door gap, exhaust path, log.
