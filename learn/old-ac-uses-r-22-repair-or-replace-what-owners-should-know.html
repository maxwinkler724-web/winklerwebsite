My AC Uses R-22: Repair or Replace? What Owners Should Know

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If your air conditioner or heat pump was installed before about 2010, it may run on R-22, a refrigerant that has been phased out in the United States. That does not mean the system is illegal or about to fail. This lesson helps you confirm what you have, understand what the EPA actually says, and ask better questions when someone tells you to repair or replace.

Check the sources

Check the sources. Rules and supply change. This page is general education built from EPA web pages and fact sheets read in 2026. It is not legal advice and it does not set prices. Confirm current rules with the EPA and your licensed contractor.

Step 1: confirm what refrigerant you have

Do not guess from the age of the house or the look of the unit. EPA's residential guide says the unit's nameplate lists the refrigerant it contains, along with its safety listings and electrical ratings. The nameplate is a metal or printed label on the outdoor unit, usually near the service panel. Look for "R-22," "HCFC-22" or "refrigerant 22." If you see "R-410A," you have a different refrigerant and this phaseout does not apply to your system. The electrical lines on that same plate are covered in Reading an HVAC Nameplate.

Step 2: what the EPA says about R-22

  • Production and import ended. EPA's residential fact sheet says U.S. production and import of HCFC-22 ended January 1, 2020.
  • You may keep using your system. The same sheet says you may continue to use HCFC-22 in your existing system for as long as necessary. EPA's consumer page says EPA does not require replacing or converting existing systems.
  • Servicing still has a supply. After 2020, refrigerant for repairs comes from recovered and reclaimed HCFC-22 and from stock produced before 2020. EPA's consumer FAQ expects recovered and reclaimed supplies to serve existing systems well beyond the phaseout date, and EPA's technician FAQ says price and availability may change.
  • Repairs include parts. EPA's technician FAQ says servicing includes replacing failed components.
  • Only certified technicians add it. The EPA consumer FAQ says Section 608 certification is required to recharge appliances and that refrigerant for stationary equipment is sold only to certified technicians or their employers. Venting it on purpose is prohibited.

Step 3: the "drop-in" question

You may hear that a cheaper "drop-in" refrigerant can replace R-22. EPA's consumer FAQ states there are no drop-in replacements for HCFC-22. A retrofit is allowed only with a substitute EPA has approved for that use under its SNAP program. EPA's residential guide says the technician should recover the remaining HCFC-22, follow the substitute maker's guidelines, and explain how performance and energy use may change. EPA also notes that equipment generally runs most efficiently on its designed refrigerant, and that alternatives will not work well without changes to system components.

Three cautions follow:

  • Ask for the approved name. A legitimate retrofit uses a named refrigerant on EPA's SNAP list for residential air conditioning. The list is by substitute and use, so your technician should be able to tell you which entry applies.
  • Do not accept a flammable product. EPA warns that "R-22a," marketed as if it were R-22 or a replacement, is a hydrocarbon blend containing propane and butane, is highly flammable, and is not an acceptable alternative. The SNAP table also lists flammable hydrocarbon refrigerants such as R-290 as unacceptable for retrofitting split air conditioners and heat pumps.
  • Oil and mixing matter. In EPA's description the old refrigerant is recovered before a retrofit, not topped up with something new. Compressor oil also has to suit the refrigerant; see Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE.

Why leaks drive the decision

Refrigerant is not a consumable. A system that is low has lost refrigerant somewhere. EPA's guide says a technician can check for leaks and do preventive maintenance, and its consumer FAQ says to ask a technician to find and fix leaks rather than add refrigerant to a leaking system. With R-22 supply limited to recovered, reclaimed and older stock, a system that keeps leaking is where repeated top-offs make the least sense. Leaks and Leak Repair Basics covers how leaks are found.

Worked example: how much leaked?

These numbers are made up for practice. Say the nameplate lists a factory charge of 7 lb 8 oz. A technician recovers everything from a low system and weighs it at 5 lb 12 oz.

  1. Convert to ounces: 7 lb 8 oz = 7 × 16 + 8 = 120 oz. 5 lb 12 oz = 5 × 16 + 12 = 92 oz.
  2. Missing refrigerant: 120 − 92 = 28 oz.
  3. Share of the charge: 28 ÷ 120 = 0.233, or about 23 percent.

That is not a rounding error. The nameplate charge assumes a standard line set, and longer lines hold more, so the real charge can differ. The point is that "just add a pound" leaves a leak in place.

Repair or replace: what R-22 changes

The general thinking (age, repeated repairs, which part failed) is in How Long Do HVAC Systems Last? Repair or Replace. R-22 adds a few points. EPA's consumer FAQ says you can no longer buy a new central air conditioner that uses HCFC-22, and that a broken HCFC-22 condensing unit may be replaced, but the replacement must meet regional efficiency standards. For a large repair, ask for the leak location, the failed part and the refrigerant the quote assumes, and get a second opinion if you want one.

If you replace: the newer refrigerant shift

Under the AIM Act, EPA's Technology Transitions fact sheet (December 2024) sets a global warming potential limit of 700 for residential and light commercial air conditioners and heat pumps. It applies to manufacture and import from January 1, 2025, and to installation from January 1, 2026, with an allowance to install systems whose components were made or imported before 2025. The same fact sheet says the rule does not restrict continued use of existing systems, so your R-22 unit is not affected by it. EPA's SNAP table lists mildly flammable A2L refrigerants such as R-32 and R-454B as acceptable with use conditions for new equipment only, not retrofits. See the A2L Refrigerants Guide for what that means for service. Compliance dates can change, so confirm them with EPA.

For technicians: recovery and labels

  • Recover before opening. 40 CFR 82.156 requires evacuating to the Table 1 vacuum level for the appliance type, charge size and age before opening it, using certified recovery equipment, and verifying the level is reached. See Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step.
  • Know where recovered R-22 can go. EPA's technician FAQ says recycled refrigerant may only go back into the owner's own equipment, reclaimed refrigerant may be sold for any existing equipment, and recovered HCFCs for disposal should go to an EPA-certified reclaimer. Compare Recover, Recycle and Reclaim.
  • Retrofit training. EPA says technicians should be trained in retrofit installation and servicing, and that some alternatives are not allowed in retrofits.
  • Labels on new parts. EPA's labeling fact sheet says components such as condensing units and compressors sold for servicing existing high-GWP equipment must be labeled "For servicing existing equipment only." Check the sheet and the manufacturer for any other label to apply.
  • Certification. See EPA 608 Certification Basics.

Field card: R-22 Systems

R-22 is phased out for new supply, not banned from use.

  • Find the refrigerant on the outdoor unit nameplate.
  • US production and import of R-22 ended January 1, 2020; recovered and reclaimed R-22 services existing systems.
  • EPA does not require replacing a working R-22 system.
  • No drop-in replacements for R-22; a retrofit needs a refrigerant on EPA's SNAP list.
  • "R-22a" is a flammable hydrocarbon blend. Do not accept it.
  • Low charge means a leak. Ask for it to be found, not just topped off.
  • New residential equipment moves to GWP 700 or lower refrigerants, including mildly flammable A2L types.
