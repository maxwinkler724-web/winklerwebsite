Farm Stand Walk-In Coolers: Packaged Unit vs Room AC Conversion

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A grower with a truck full of warm produce wants a cold room by Saturday. Two builds show up again and again: an insulated box with a packaged walk-in condensing unit, and an insulated box cooled by a room air conditioner with an add-on controller. This lesson explains how each works, where the second one gets into trouble, and how to size either one from the heat in the product and the heat leaking through the walls.

Two ways to cool the same box

A packaged walk-in condensing unit is built for refrigeration. It runs an evaporator that is meant to operate below freezing on its coil, with a defrost control, and its controls hold the box near the setpoint. The related lessons on walk-in defrost and florist coolers cover those parts in detail.

A room air conditioner conversion uses an ordinary window or wall unit mounted through the box wall. Its own thermostat will not call for cooling below its comfort range, so an add-on controller overrides the thermostat. Generically, the controller reads the box temperature, keeps the unit running well below the thermostat's floor, and adds its own protection against coil freezing. University of Kentucky Extension describes this as a control unit that adds heaters, a thermostat and a timer to create a defrost cycle that alternates between the heaters and the compressor. University of Minnesota Extension (a blog post whose address names a brand, so we cite it as plain text without a link) says these conversions suit well-insulated boxes that are rarely opened and do not receive large loads of warm product at once.

Why a room air conditioner ices up near 35 F

A room air conditioner is designed to cool a room that stays in the 70s. Its evaporator coil normally runs a few degrees above freezing while it cools that air. Kentucky Extension says a standard room unit's coils freeze if the room is cooled below about 65 F. Our own explanation: the coil runs well below the air it is cooling, so when box air falls toward 35 F the coil is far below 32 F, moisture from the air freezes on the fins, airflow drops, and the coil gets colder still. Without defrost, the coil becomes a block of ice and the box warms up. The add-on controller exists to manage that, but it is working against the unit's design, so the unit needs a drain tilt, clean filters and a close look at the coil on every visit. Because a room unit is not rated for this duty, treat it as a compromise, not a refrigeration system.

Insulation and vapor barriers

Kentucky Extension calls R-19 a standard value for a market grower's cooler, with the vapor barrier on the warm outside of the wall because this is a cooler (the reverse of a heated house). University of Vermont Extension's calculator page lists per-inch values of about 5.6 for foil-faced polyisocyanurate and 5 for extruded polystyrene, and notes that layers add. Using that 5.6 figure, about 4.5 inches of foil-faced polyisocyanurate gives roughly R-25 (4.5 x 5.6 = 25.2). Seal foam seams with tape, avoid compressed batts and gaps in corners, and insulate the floor, because Kentucky Extension notes that floor losses are significant even on concrete.

Door gaskets and strip curtains

Wisconsin Extension lists leaks at doors and seams as a refrigeration load, and warns that opening the door lets in warm, humid air. A worn gasket lets that air in all day. See door gaskets and closers for the paper test, and strip curtains for the doorway.

Humidity for produce

Wisconsin Extension groups crops by what they need. Leafy greens, carrots, beets, sweet corn and broccoli want about 32 F and 95 percent relative humidity or more. Snap beans, cantaloupe and tomatoes want 40 to 50 F and 90 to 95 percent. Onions and garlic want cold and dry, 65 to 70 percent. The same page ties minimum humidity to the temperature drop across the evaporator: at 32 F storage, a 2 F air drop supports about 91 percent minimum and a 5 F drop about 79 percent. A conversion that holds its coil very cold therefore also pulls moisture out of the room, which dries produce. Do not mix crops with different needs without thinking it through; see produce cold storage.

Field heat and pre-cooling

Wisconsin Extension says field heat is the largest part of the refrigeration requirement, gives the formula as temperature change times pounds times specific heat, and says quality drops when field heat is not removed fast enough. It also reports that forced-air cooling is 75 to 90 percent faster than a plain cooling room and that hydro-cooling is about five times faster than air cooling. A cooling room alone is called too slow for crops that need quick cooling. Kentucky Extension adds that a smaller air conditioner can bring a room to 55 to 60 F, which removes field heat for crops that like those temperatures, such as tomatoes (colder makes them mealy).

Our own worked example

These are our own assumptions, not a design for a real farm. Product: 1,000 lb of sweet corn at 85 F field temperature, cooled to 35 F. Specific heat above freezing: 0.87 Btu/lb F (Wisconsin Extension's table). Box: 8 ft by 10 ft by 8 ft inside, walls and ceiling at R-25, outside air 90 F. We ignore floor, door openings, lights, fans and respiration, all of which add load in a real box. Net cooling capacity after losses at the box temperature: 12,000 Btu/h for a packaged unit.

  1. Field heat: 1,000 lb x 0.87 x (85 - 35) = 43,500 Btu.
  2. Wall and ceiling area: walls 2 x (8 + 10) x 8 = 288 ft2, ceiling 80 ft2, total 368 ft2.
  3. Steady heat gain: Q = area x temperature difference / R = 368 x (90 - 35) / 25 = 810 Btu/h.
  4. Capacity left for the product: 12,000 - 810 = 11,190 Btu/h.
  5. Time: 43,500 / 11,190 = about 3.9 hours.

Now assume a room air conditioner conversion that only delivers 8,000 Btu/h at box temperature (our assumption; Vermont Extension says such units need further derating). Then 8,000 - 810 = 7,190 Btu/h, and 43,500 / 7,190 = about 6.1 hours. Vermont Extension also suggests sizing for a 16-hour run in 24 hours to allow for defrost. Daily load is 43,500 + (810 x 24 = 19,440) = 62,940 Btu, and 62,940 / 16 = about 3,930 Btu/h of average capacity, before respiration and door openings. Every extra load makes that number grow, so batch sizes matter: a big truckload at once is the usual way these builds fail.

Refrigerant handling and electrical

A packaged condensing unit and a room air conditioner both hold refrigerant. The EPA says technicians who maintain, service, repair or dispose of equipment that could release refrigerant must be certified under Section 608, with apprentices exempt only when closely and continually supervised, and that intentionally venting refrigerant while servicing equipment is prohibited. In practice, anyone who attaches gauges, adds or removes refrigerant, or opens the system needs EPA 608 certification and recovery equipment. A farm owner can clean filters, keep the drain clear and watch for ice, but should call a certified technician for anything past that. For new packaged equipment we expect a nameplate refrigerant other than R-410A; read the nameplate and follow the installation instructions.

On electrical, University of Minnesota Extension notes that smaller room units run on 120 V and larger ones need 220 V units, which may need an electrician. Our own guidance: read the nameplate for the minimum circuit ampacity and maximum overcurrent device, give the unit a circuit of its own, use fittings rated for damp locations, and let a licensed electrician do new circuits. Also our own: seal wire entries so warm air does not enter the box.

Field card: Farm Stand Walk-In Coolers

Pull the field heat first, then hold the box.

  • Room air conditioners ice up when box air nears 35 F; a controller is a workaround, not a rated refrigeration system.
  • Field heat = lb x specific heat x temperature change (sweet corn: 0.87 Btu/lb F above freezing).
  • Wall heat gain = area x temperature difference / R.
  • Our example: 43,500 Btu of field heat, 810 Btu/h wall gain, about 3.9 h at 12,000 Btu/h net.
  • Vapor barrier on the warm side; insulate the floor; seal seams and the door.
  • Greens and sweet corn need 95 percent humidity or more; onions and garlic want it dry.
  • Opening the refrigerant circuit takes EPA 608 certification.
