Wrenches on HVAC Equipment: Right Size, Flare-Nut and Backup Wrenches

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rounded flare nut or a bent service valve turns a ten-minute install into a callback. Most of that damage comes from one habit: grabbing whatever wrench is nearest. This lesson covers matching the wrench to the fastener, which tool belongs on which nut, and how to hold and pull so the wrench stays on and the fitting stays round.

Match the wrench to the nut first

A hand tool safety fact sheet from a university extension service says to match the wrench to the nut and check whether the nut is English (inch) or metric. A near fit rounds the corners and then slips. A federal hand tool booklet says wrenches must not be used once the jaws are sprung enough to slip, and that you should examine each tool before use and not use a damaged one. It also says most hand tool hazards come from misuse and poor maintenance. On HVAC equipment, inch and metric hardware often share one unit, so try the wrench on the nut by hand before you commit force to it. If it rocks, it is wrong.

The wrench families and where each one fits

  • Box-end (ring) and socket. These wrap all six corners of a hex. A university facilities safety sheet recommends box or socket wrenches for heavy pulls because they grip the whole fastener and are less likely to slip. A ratcheting wrench or ratchet with a socket lets you work in tight spots without lifting the tool off the nut each stroke.
  • Open-end. It touches only two flats, so use it where a ring will not fit over the fitting or you cannot get above the nut. The same sheet says an open-end wrench for medium-duty work must fit snugly.
  • Adjustable. That sheet treats an adjustable open-end wrench as light-duty work when the correct size is not on hand. Snug the jaws to the flats so there is no play.
  • Flare-nut (line) wrench. This is a box-end wrench with a slot cut in the ring so it can slide over refrigerant tubing, then seat on all six corners. In our practice it is the right tool on brass flare and tubing nuts because it spreads the load. An open-end wrench there is the usual way flare nuts get rounded.

Pipe wrenches and locking pliers do not belong on hex nuts; the extension fact sheet says they can break off the corner of a nut. Pipe wrenches have their own lesson, linked below.

Backup wrench: hold one part, turn the other

Tightening or loosening a flare nut on a service valve or fitting twists everything behind it unless something holds that part still. Put a second wrench on the flats of the valve body or fitting, and turn the nut with the first. Set the two handles so you squeeze them toward each other: the loads cancel and the line, valve and brazed joints see little twisting. Never lean on the tubing for leverage. The backup rule for pipe fittings is covered in the pipe wrench lesson; the idea is the same here. Seat the nut by hand before any wrench goes on, so you are not forcing a cross-threaded flare.

Pull, do not push, and face the jaw to you

Place yourself so you pull the wrench toward you. A university facilities sheet says this reduces the chance the wrench slips off, and a state university safety manual says to pull rather than push a hand tool because a slip when pushing can cut or bruise your hand. With an adjustable or open-end wrench, that same facilities sheet says to keep the open end facing you as you pull. If you cannot pull, push with the open palm and keep fingers off the handle's far side. Keep your body clear of any edge, panel corner or fan behind the tool: when a wrench slips, your hand goes where the wrench was pointing.

Service valve stem and cap wrenches

Many outdoor-unit service valves have a sealing cap you remove with a wrench, and a stem you move with a hex key or a square-drive valve wrench, depending on the maker. The size varies by valve, so read the valve itself and the maker's installation instructions before choosing; do not substitute pliers on a stem. Open and close the stem slowly with a tool that fits it fully, and put the caps back, since they are the main seal.

When you need a torque wrench

When the maker gives a tightening value for a flare nut, a torque wrench is how you meet it; feel is not a measurement. We do not print torque values here. Follow the equipment maker's instructions, and see Flare Fittings on Mini-Splits for how torque fits into making the flare joint.

No cheater bars

A university facilities sheet says not to raise torque by striking a wrench with a hammer or by adding a cheater bar to the handle, because either can break or damage the tool, and the extension fact sheet says it is unsafe to add leverage to any tool with an extension. If a nut will not move, apply penetrating oil as the facilities sheet advises, use a longer, correctly rated wrench, a six-point socket or a box wrench, and check that you have the right size. Do not use a pipe on the handle. A fractured flare or a bent valve stem costs far more than the minutes you saved.

Worked example (our own): the minimum wrench set

This is our own example with assumed sizes. Real hex sizes vary by maker, so treat every size below as an assumption. The job: a wall-mount mini-split and a furnace gas connection, done by one tech.

Assumed fasteners:

  • Mini-split, flare nuts: 2 liquid line nuts at 11/16 in and 2 suction line nuts at 7/8 in (one indoor and one outdoor of each).
  • Mini-split, valve bodies for backup: liquid at 3/4 in hex, suction at 1 in hex.
  • Two service valve caps at 3/4 in. Four outdoor-unit mounting bolts at 1/2 in. One ground-lug nut at 3/8 in.
  • Furnace: 1 gas connector nut at 7/8 in, mating fitting body at 3/4 in for the backup, and 3 inducer-motor bolts at 5/16 in.

Step 1: count the fasteners. Mini-split: 4 flare nuts + 2 caps + 4 bolts + 1 ground nut = 11. Furnace: 1 connector nut + 3 bolts = 4. Total = 11 + 4 = 15 fasteners (not counting the backup positions).

Step 2: list distinct sizes, including backup sizes. 11/16, 7/8, 3/4, 1 in, 1/2, 3/8 and 5/16. That is 7 sizes, so at most 7 tools.

Step 3: assign tools. Flare nuts get flare-nut wrenches: 11/16 and 7/8 (2 tools). The 3/4 in size covers the liquid valve body, the caps and the furnace backup, so one box-end or open-end wrench does all three (1 tool). The 1 in suction valve body backup can be an adjustable wrench opened to the flats, jaw facing the pull (1 tool), because the 7/8 flare-nut wrench is already in use on that nut. The 1/2 in and 3/8 in sizes take a box-end wrench each (2 tools). The 5/16 in bolts take a socket on a ratchet (1 tool).

Step 4: total. 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 7 tools for 15 fasteners. Under our assumptions, 7 is the minimum. In practice, a second 3/4 in wrench is handy when you hold one body while turning a cap. Torque the flare nuts with a torque wrench per the maker, which is one more tool, not counted above.

Field card: Wrenches on HVAC equipment

Right size, right type, pull toward you.

  • Try the wrench by hand: if it rocks, it is the wrong size or system (inch vs metric).
  • Flare-nut wrench on flare and tubing nuts; box, socket or ratcheting for heavy pulls; open-end only where a ring will not fit.
  • Backup wrench on the valve body or fitting, handles squeezed together.
  • Pull, do not push; open jaw faces you.
  • Pipe wrenches and locking pliers stay off hex nuts.
  • Torque wrench where the maker gives a value. No cheater bars or hammering.
  • Replace sprung or damaged wrenches.
