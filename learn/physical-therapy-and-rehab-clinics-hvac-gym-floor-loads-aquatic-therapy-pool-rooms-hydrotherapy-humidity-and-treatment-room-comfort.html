Physical Therapy and Rehab Clinics HVAC: Gym Floor Loads, Pool Rooms and Treatment Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A physical therapy clinic is several buildings in one. A busy exercise floor sheds sweat and breath like a gym. Quiet treatment rooms hold people who are lying still, partly undressed and easily chilled. A warm therapy pool behaves like a small natatorium, and the locker rooms need exhaust. This lesson sorts out which room drives which load, then works one moisture example with numbers.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. Ventilation tables, healthcare standards and state licensing rules differ by jurisdiction and edition. Everything here is general education from public pages read in 2026. Confirm every rate against the design documents and the code your inspector uses.

What the ventilation tables say for these rooms

ASHRAE Standard 62.1 is a copyrighted standard, so we name it in plain text only. A public ASHRAE addendum to the 2019 edition (Addendum i, approved April 2022) prints the outpatient physical therapy rows. We read these:

  • Physical therapy exercise area: 20 cfm per person plus 0.18 cfm per square foot, default density 7 people per 1,000 square feet, air class 2.
  • Physical therapy individual room: 10 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, default density 20 people per 1,000 square feet, air class 1.
  • Physical therapeutic pool area: 0.48 cfm per square foot, no people rate, air class 2.

Compare the model code. Washington State's adopted International Mechanical Code table has no physical therapy row at all. It lists a health club weight room at 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot at 10 people per 1,000 square feet, an aerobics room at 40 people per 1,000 square feet, and a pool and deck area at 0.48 cfm per square foot. Its text says to use the most similar listed row or an approved engineering analysis for unlisted spaces. So the same exercise floor can carry a different minimum depending on which table the jurisdiction adopted. Ask which one applies before you judge a unit.

For healthcare air changes, ANSI/ASHRAE/ASHE Standard 170 is the reference. A public addendum to the 2021 edition (Addendum c) shows a physical therapy row in its nursing home table: negative pressure, 2 minimum outdoor air changes, 6 total, and a design temperature of 70 to 78 F. We did not find a public outpatient physical therapy or hydrotherapy row, and a New York State Department of Health checklist for outpatient rehabilitation facilities gave no values for those rooms. Use the project's own design criteria for hydrotherapy rooms.

Why the rooms feel different

Exercise floor. People working hard give off far more moisture than people sitting. The addendum's exercise-area area rate (0.18) is three times the weight-room area rate in the Washington table (0.06), while the people rate is the same 20 cfm.

Treatment rooms. A patient lying on a table in a gown is losing heat to the room, not gaining it. In our experience that is why treatment rooms draw cold complaints at the same setpoint where the gym floor draws hot ones. Individual-room rates are small, so the fix is usually zoning and a local thermostat, not more air. Our own field note, not a code rule.

Warm therapy pool and hydrotherapy tubs. The CDC Model Aquatic Health Code defines "hot water" as above 90 F and asks indoor facility design documents to record water temperature, space temperature, relative humidity, dew point and a vapor barrier in walls and ceiling. Warm water gives more evaporation, so the room needs real dehumidification, not just the 0.48 cfm per square foot ventilation minimum. Our indoor pool lesson covers the evaporation math, so we do not repeat it. A hydrotherapy tub room is the same problem at small scale: keep surfaces above dew point and exhaust the room.

Chloramines

CDC says chloramines form when chlorine binds to body waste from swimmers, escape into the air, irritate the skin, eyes and airways, and settle over the water because they are heavier than air. It lists limited fresh air and restricted exhaust, often set back in winter to save heat, as a cause. In a CDC and NIOSH report on an indoor waterpark, air trichloramine ran as high as 1.06 mg per cubic meter, above the 0.5 mg per cubic meter provisional guideline, and the system recirculated up to a third of the air in cold weather. Ductwork moved closer to the deck helped. A therapy pool has far fewer swimmers, but check that setback never shuts off needed outdoor air and exhaust.

Locker room exhaust

The Washington table lists showers at 50 or 20 cfm per showerhead and public toilets at 50 or 70 cfm per fixture, with the lower number only for continuous exhaust and no recirculation back to other spaces. Transfer air from the gym can serve as make-up. Our locker room lesson works through that balance.

Worked example: our own gym floor

Assumptions, all our own: a 1,500 square foot exercise floor, 15 people exercising, and a zone air distribution effectiveness of 1.0 so breathing-zone airflow equals outdoor airflow.

  1. Ventilation, 62.1 exercise-area rates. People: 15 × 20 = 300 cfm. Area: 1,500 × 0.18 = 270 cfm. Vbz = 570 cfm.
  2. Compare. With the weight-room rates (20 and 0.06): 300 + 90 = 390 cfm. With the default 7 people per 1,000 square feet (10.5 people): 210 + 270 = 480 cfm. Our 15 people is above the default, so the people part is higher.
  3. Exercising latent gain. The Engineering ToolBox human heat gain table, which attributes the data to itself and names no original source, lists 510 Btu/h sensible and 940 Btu/h latent per person for heavy work and gymnasium at 78 F. For 15 people: latent 15 × 940 = 14,100 Btu/h, sensible 15 × 510 = 7,650 Btu/h.
  4. Convert to moisture. We use about 1,060 Btu per pound of water, our own rounding. 14,100 ÷ 1,060 = 13.3 lb/h of water vapor.
  5. Resting room. The same table gives 215 sensible and 185 latent for an office. For 15 people: 15 × 185 = 2,775 Btu/h, or 2,775 ÷ 1,060 = 2.6 lb/h.

The exercise floor adds about five times the moisture of a resting room of the same size and headcount (13.3 ÷ 2.6 = 5.1). In coil terms, 4.5 × cfm × change in humidity ratio gives lb/h (our own algebra: 60 minutes times 0.075 lb/ft³). With an assumed 2,000 cfm supply, 13.3 ÷ (4.5 × 2,000) = 0.0015 lb per lb, about 10 grains per pound, that supply must absorb. The resting room needs about 2. Using 4,840 instead of 4.5 × 1,060 changes the pounds by under two percent, so the answer does not hinge on the rounding. See our latent load lesson for why that low sensible heat ratio matters.

The 570 cfm is a minimum outdoor airflow, not the cooling airflow.

What a tech checks

  • Which table the jurisdiction adopted for the exercise floor, and what the design says.
  • Pool room: setpoints, dehumidifier running, surfaces above dew point, exhaust running during setback.
  • Treatment room thermostats and whether the cold rooms share a zone with the hot gym.
  • Locker exhaust running, no recirculation, door pulling slightly negative.

Field card: Physical Therapy Clinic HVAC

Hot gym, cool tables, wet pool, exhausted lockers.

  • 62.1 addendum rows: exercise area 20 cfm/person + 0.18 cfm/ft2; individual room 10 + 0.06; pool area 0.48 cfm/ft2.
  • Some model codes have no physical therapy row. Ask which table applies.
  • Exercise latent gain was about five times a resting room in our example.
  • Moisture lb/h = latent Btu/h divided by about 1,060.
  • Warm therapy pools need dehumidification and outdoor air. Chloramines settle low.
  • Locker exhaust is not recirculated.
