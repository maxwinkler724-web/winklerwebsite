Phase Loss and Single-Phasing on Three-Phase HVAC Motors

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A three-phase motor that loses one leg does not always stop. It may keep turning, draw far more current on the two legs it still has, and cook its windings while the equipment appears to be working. This lesson covers what happens, what protects the motor, and how to troubleshoot a trip safely.

What single-phasing is

Single-phasing means one of the three supply conductors to a three-phase load is open. The load is then fed from only two legs. A fuse manufacturer's technical note we read lists the usual causes. On the utility side: storm damage, a blown primary fuse, or a failed switch or recloser. On the equipment side: worn motor starter contacts, which that note calls the most common cause, plus open fuses, burned overload heaters, loose lugs and open windings. The Department of Energy tip sheet on voltage unbalance also lists an open circuit on the utility primary as a cause.

Why a running motor keeps going and a stopped one hums

A running induction motor can keep turning on two legs because its rotor already has momentum. It still has to deliver the same load torque from less magnetic drive, so the current rises. A power-monitoring maker's article says torque falls away and current climbs. A stopped motor behaves differently. With no rotating field, it will generally refuse to start, hum and draw heavy current, per the same article. Shut it off at once if it hums.

How much does current rise? The fuse maker's note says that for a secondary-side single-phasing, the current in the two remaining phases theoretically rises to 1.73 (173 percent) of normal, and can reach 200 percent because of power factor changes. The power-monitoring article gives a lighter case: at 50 percent load the healthy phases carry roughly 86 percent of full-load current. That is below nameplate, which matters in a moment.

The 1.73 figure is the square root of 3, a rough rule. If the loss is upstream of a transformer bank, the split changes: the same note says one secondary phase can reach 230 percent and the others about 115 percent.

Phase monitor relays

A phase monitor (phase failure) relay watches the three supply voltages and opens a contact in the control circuit when something is wrong. A maker's white paper we read lists the functions in general terms: phase loss, phase sequence (reversal), unbalance, under-voltage, and over-voltage. It says the set point is adjustable and depends on the equipment being protected, that under-voltage relays can trip at once, and that over-voltage relays can be set instant or time-delayed. A time delay lets a brief sag ride through without a nuisance trip. Settings vary by model, so read the label and the relay documentation.

Two cautions. First, the monitor reads voltage at the point where it is wired, so it can miss an open on the load side of that point; a different maker's article says such relays usually monitor a single point. Second, it protects only if its contact is in the control circuit. A jumpered contact means no protection. See phase rotation and voltage imbalance.

Overload relays and classes

An overload relay protects the motor from heat, not the wiring from a short. A Class 10 overload relay has to trip in 10 seconds or less at 600 percent of full-load amps, according to two motor-control maker documents we read. Classes 20 and 30 allow longer starts; the same documents say Class 30 suits high-inertia loads. Choose the class to match the load starting time and follow the equipment documentation.

Overload type matters for single-phasing. Maker documents say mechanical overloads lack phase loss detection, while electronic types can offer it. A fuse maker's note adds that the code calls for an overload device in each phase of a three-phase motor, and that two devices do not guarantee protection against primary-side single-phasing.

The overload rule in plain text

NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code, sets the maximum overload trip setting from the motor's nameplate current. A trade association presentation based on the 2023 edition reads it this way: a separate overload device is based on the nameplate current, and its trip rating is no more than 125 percent for a motor with a marked service factor of 1.15 or more, or a marked temperature rise of 40 degrees C or less. For all other motors the limit is 115 percent. It is a ceiling, not a target. The fuse maker's note says to set overloads at nameplate current for a fully loaded motor, and to measure actual running amps with a clamp meter for an oversized or lightly loaded one. See Reading a Three-Phase Motor Nameplate and Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter. Confirm the edition adopted where you work. Hermetic compressors have their own rules, so check the equipment label.

Worked example

Assumptions (Our own): a three-phase fan motor with nameplate current of 18.0 A and a marked service factor of 1.15. We use the 1.73 factor from the fuse maker's note and the 125 percent limit above.

  1. Maximum overload setting: 18.0 A x 1.25 = 22.5 A. Say the relay is set at nameplate, 18.0 A.
  2. Loaded motor, one leg lost: 18.0 A x 1.73 = 31.1 A on each remaining leg. That is well above 18.0 A, so the overload trips on heat in a short time.
  3. Same motor at 50 percent load (Our own assumption): it normally draws about 9.0 A. After the loss: 9.0 A x 1.73 = 15.6 A. That is below the 18.0 A setting, so a mechanical overload may never trip while the motor runs hot.

The third step is why a phase monitor or an electronic overload with phase loss detection is worth having. The fuse maker's note makes a similar point: a motor reading 12 A against a 10 A nameplate is overloaded, but a lightly loaded one can single-phase without exceeding nameplate.

What a trip tells you

  • Overload tripped, all three voltages normal: look at the load (locked bearing, dirty coil, failing winding) and for an open leg below where you measured.
  • Phase monitor tripped: it saw loss, reversal, unbalance or voltage out of band. Measure first, then reset.
  • Hum and no start: shut it off and look for an open leg. Do not keep resetting; repeated tries can finish a hot motor.

Troubleshooting after a trip, safely

  1. Follow your site lockout procedure and read Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. OSHA requires that a qualified person use test equipment to confirm circuit parts are de-energized before treating them as dead, and that interlocks not be a substitute for lockout.
  2. Before opening anything, with power on and only if you are qualified and equipped to do so, measure line voltage at the disconnect line side between each pair of legs.
  3. De-energize, lock out, and verify dead with your meter on all three legs and ground, proving the meter on a known source before and after.
  4. Check all three fuses with the meter, not by eye. A fuse can look fine and be open.
  5. Inspect contactor poles for pitting, burn marks or a loose lug.
  6. Check the overload setting against the nameplate.
  7. Restore power, start, and clamp each leg. Compare the three readings; they should be close to each other.

Field card: Phase loss and single-phasing

Hum or heat with a low-load pump: look for a lost leg.

  • Single-phasing: one leg open from a blown fuse, burned contactor pole, or utility.
  • Running motor: keeps going, healthy legs near 1.73 times pre-fault current (fuse maker note).
  • Never jumper a phase monitor contact.
  • Class 10 overload trips within 10 s at 600 percent of full-load amps.
  • Overload ceiling: 125 percent of nameplate (SF 1.15 or more), else 115 percent.
  • Lock out, verify dead on all three legs, test every fuse with a meter.
