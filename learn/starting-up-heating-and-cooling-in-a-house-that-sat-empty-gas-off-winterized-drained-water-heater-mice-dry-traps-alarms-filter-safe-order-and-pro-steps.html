Starting Up Heat and Cooling in a House That Sat Empty

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A house that sat empty for months is not just a cold house. Gas may be off, water may be drained, mice may have moved in, and the safety alarms may be dead. This lesson gives the look-first order for waking a house up, and marks the steps that belong to a licensed pro.

Look first, switch on last

Walk the whole house before you touch a thermostat. Start the furnace, boiler or water heater into a problem and a small issue becomes a fire, a flood or a burned-out part. This is general education. It is not legal, real-estate or insurance advice, and it cannot replace the manufacturer instructions on the equipment or the codes where you live.

Step 1: Alarms and air

Check the carbon monoxide and smoke alarms before anything burns fuel. A federal fire-administration page says to test smoke alarms monthly and to replace the whole alarm every 10 years from its manufacture date. A federal consumer product safety page says to place carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, and notes the test button checks the circuitry, not the sensor accuracy. It also names a recommended replacement age in the product literature. If an alarm is missing, dead or past its date, replace it first. Our own rule of thumb: if you cannot tell how old an alarm is, treat it as expired.

See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do for what a real alarm means.

Step 2: Gas, and who restores it

If the gas was shut off at the meter, do not turn it back on yourself. Our own advice: the gas supplier or a licensed gas fitter restores service, checks the piping for leaks, and relights pilots. If you smell gas at any time, leave the house and call from outside. Do not flip switches on the way out.

Step 3: Winterization signs

Look for tags on the water heater, furnace, boiler, sinks or main valve. Someone who winterized a house may have drained the pipes, poured antifreeze into drain traps and toilets, or put antifreeze in the heating loop. Our own advice: if you find tags, ask whoever winterized the house what they did and in what order, and have a licensed plumber or heating pro restore the system. Do not run antifreeze-filled drains or a glycol loop as if they were ordinary water systems without knowing what is in them. Do not fill a drained heating loop and fire the boiler on a guess.

Step 4: Water heater: full before power or gas

A drained tank must be completely full before the power or burner is on. A maker installation manual for an electric tank says not to restore power until the tank is full: open a hot faucet, let it run at full stream for three minutes or longer so the air is out, then reconnect power. The same manual says that powering the elements before the tank is full can burn out the upper element (a dry fire) and that this is not covered by the warranty. For a gas water heater, the same fill-first idea applies, and the pilot relight is a pro step if you are not trained. See Boiler Startup, Shutdown and Layup Basics for the plant version.

Step 5: Mice, nests and chewed wires

Open the furnace door, air handler and outdoor unit cabinet only with power off at the disconnect, and look for droppings, shredded insulation, nests and gnawed wire coatings. A federal disease-control page on rodent cleanup gives the safe handling: ventilate the area by opening doors and windows for about 30 minutes, wear rubber or plastic gloves, and never sweep or vacuum droppings because that can spread virus-carrying particles into the air. Soak droppings and nests with a disinfectant for about 5 minutes, wipe them up with paper towels, and double-bag nests. The same page says a heavy infestation calls for extra protection, including a fitted respirator, so call a cleanup pro in that case.

Chewed wire insulation is a fire and shock risk. Our own rule: a licensed tech repairs or replaces damaged wiring and checks the unit before it runs. Details and sealing tips are in Mice and Other Pests in Home Ducts and HVAC Equipment.

Step 6: Dry traps, water stains and mold

Every sink, tub and floor drain has a trap that holds a plug of water to block sewer odor. In an empty house that water can evaporate. Our own advice: after a long vacancy, run water into every drain, including the basement floor drain and a rarely used shower, and then check that odors are gone. If a smell persists, call a plumber.

Look at ceilings, walls and around the equipment for water stains, soft spots and fuzzy growth. A federal environmental protection page says to fix water problems as soon as possible and dry everything completely, and to scrub mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water. It says most people can handle an area under about 10 square feet, but a lot of water damage, mold over more than 10 square feet, or sewage-contaminated water calls for a professional. Do not run ducts through a moldy area; fix the moisture source first.

Step 7: Filter

Install a new filter of the size printed on the old one before the blower runs, because dust, nests and debris will have settled in the duct system. Our own tip: tape the install date on the frame. See First Heat of the Season for the burning-dust smell that follows a first start.

The safe order, short version

  1. Alarms checked or replaced.
  2. Gas restored by the supplier or a licensed pro, if it was off.
  3. Winterization sorted out and the plumbing and heating loop restored by someone who knows what was done.
  4. Water heater full before power or gas.
  5. Pests cleaned up, wiring checked, power off while you look.
  6. Drains run, moisture and mold dealt with.
  7. New filter in, then a first short run of heat or cooling while you watch and listen.

Then have a pro do a full inspection. A federal consumer safety page recommends an annual professional inspection of the heating system, including the chimney and flue for blockages, corrosion and loose connections, and after a long vacancy that visit is worth booking right away.

Worked example (our own): refilling a drained hot-water heating loop

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume a drained loop of 3/4 inch copper tube with an inside diameter of about 0.785 inch, 150 feet of baseboard loop, and a boiler and expansion tank that are counted separately. We want the water volume to expect before you start, so you know when the fill is done.

  1. Area of the bore: pi / 4 x 0.785 squared = 0.7854 x 0.616 = 0.484 square inch.
  2. Volume per foot: 0.484 square inch x 12 inches = 5.81 cubic inches.
  3. Convert to gallons: 5.81 / 231 cubic inches per gallon = about 0.025 gallon per foot.
  4. For 150 feet: 150 x 0.025 = about 3.8 gallons, before the boiler and any tank are counted.

So if a loop of that size takes far more than about 4 gallons plus the boiler volume, water is going somewhere it should not, such as a leak or an open drain. If it takes much less, air is trapped. Our own guidance: the actual fill, venting and pressure setting is a heating pro's job on an unfamiliar system. See Boiler Pressure Too High or Too Low: Homeowner Guide for reading the gauge afterward.

Field card: Starting Up Heat and Cooling in a House That Sat Empty

Look first, fill first, light last.

  • Test or replace carbon monoxide and smoke alarms before anything burns fuel.
  • Gas off at the meter: a pro restores it and relights pilots.
  • Winterization tags or antifreeze: find out what was done first.
  • Water heater full, air out, then power or gas.
  • Droppings: ventilate, wet down, never sweep or vacuum.
  • Run every drain; deal with stains and mold.
  • Loop volume, our own: about 0.025 gal per foot of 3/4 in copper.
