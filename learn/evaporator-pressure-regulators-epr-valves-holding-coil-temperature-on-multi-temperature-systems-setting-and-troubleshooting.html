Evaporator Pressure Regulators (EPR Valves): Holding Coil Temperature on Multi-Temperature Systems, Setting and Troubleshooting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

One suction header, two boxes that want different temperatures. If both coils see the header pressure, the warm box freezes its product. An evaporator pressure regulator (EPR) stops that. This lesson covers what it does, how to set one, what a bad one looks like, and the energy cost.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Setpoints and adjustment direction come from the valve maker's instructions. A rack is a live, pressurized system, and handling refrigerant takes EPA 608 certification. The numbers here come from the trade articles and chart named in Sources.

What an EPR does

An EPR keeps evaporator pressure from falling below a set value. ACHR News calls it a flow control for an evaporator designed to run warmer than the coldest one in the system. It is also called an inlet, upstream or back-pressure regulator. It sits in the suction line just after the evaporator outlet and opens as inlet pressure rises; outlet pressure does not move it. It does not cap maximum pressure, and it is selected from the maker's table (refrigerant, coil capacity, available drop), not by suction line size.

Why multi-temperature systems use them

A store usually has a low-temperature group for frozen food and a medium-temperature group for coolers, as in our supermarket rack lesson. The EPA GreenChill refrigeration training deck says an EPR allows evaporators at different temperatures to share one suction group. An ORNL fault-detection study adds that the target suction pressure is set by the coldest temperature the cases on that group need.

The compressors pull the header down for the coldest coil, and every warmer coil would follow it down. The EPR throttles the warm coil's outlet so that coil stays at its own pressure while the header sits lower.

Keeping a coil above freezing

HPAC explains the goal for high-water-content products such as fresh meat, produce and flowers: a large temperature difference between coil and air pulls moisture out of the product. The EPR holds a constant saturated suction temperature (SST) in that coil. HPAC's example: an R-404A circuit that needs 28 F SST has its valve set at 66 psi. For where saturation pressure comes from, see our glide lesson.

The same logic fits any coil that must not ice, such as a produce box or a chiller barrel: the valve sets a floor on coil pressure, chosen so saturation stays above 32 F with some margin. We found no source that gives the margin.

Per HPAC, the EPR is a suction-line restriction and can push header pressure outside the compressor's safe range, so check the compressor data.

Direct-acting and pilot-operated

ACHR News explains it. A direct-operated valve is moved only by a diaphragm against a range spring, with a stroke of 0 to 0.015 in., so it suits only small capacities. Pilot-operated valves suit commercial work: one type is piloted from a discharge-gas line, which the article says adds high-side joints in the machine room; the other pilots from inlet pressure inside the valve. A solenoid option lets the valve act as a suction stop for reverse-gas defrost.

A refrigeration supplier's blog lists typical drops: as little as 2 psi for a direct-acting valve on a single evaporator, about 1 psi for an internally piloted valve at full capacity. Contracting Business says stepper-motor and electronic EPRs are used where tight temperature control is needed, such as fresh meat cases.

Setting one with gauges at the inlet

  1. Put a gauge on the valve's inlet side so you can see each adjustment. ACHR News says the same, and that turning the screw clockwise normally raises the setpoint. Check the maker's direction for your valve.
  2. Make sure the header is pulling below the target, or the valve sits open and you read header pressure (supplier blog).
  3. Adjust with the coil at minimum load (Contracting Business, for frost protection).
  4. Set the EPR first, with the TXV at its preset, then adjust superheat at design conditions if needed (ACHR News). Our TXV lesson covers superheat.
  5. Wait: ACHR News says rebalancing can take up to 30 minutes, though a lower setting responds right away.

Stuck, undersized or hunting

We found no trade article listing EPR faults, so these come from a supplier's blog plus our reading of how the valve works. Compare the inlet gauge to the setpoint.

  • Box too cold, inlet below setpoint: stuck open, set too low, or debris holding a pilot open.
  • Box too warm, inlet well above setpoint: undersized, stuck closed, set too high, or the suction-stop solenoid is not opening.
  • Inlet pressure swinging: the supplier says an oversized EPR hunts, and a hunting TXV can do the same. Check the TXV first; see our defrost lesson for iced coils.
  • Warm only after defrost: check that the solenoid re-energized first (supplier). Contracting Business adds that a heat-damaged EPR may act erratically.

The energy cost of running coils warmer than suction

The coil needs its warmer pressure, so the cost is on the compressor side: the whole group runs at the pressure the coldest coil needs. ACHR News says a lower suction pressure raises compression ratio and lowers volumetric efficiency, and cites about a 15 percent capacity loss on a low-temperature R-404A system for a 3.5 psi drop (-25 F to -20 F SST at 105 F condensing). A trade-training article warns that raising suction too far can warm the cases. One utility presentation puts typical medium-temperature SSTs at 15 to 20 F. A mechanical EPR also throttles at its setpoint regardless of air temperature, which slows pulldown after defrost (ACHR News).

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions: R-448A. The rack suction group runs at 20 F saturated. An EPA modeling study uses 20 F for a medium-temperature group, but here it is our assumption. The produce box coil must stay at a minimum of 32 F saturated. We use the vapor (dew) column of a published R-448A chart, in 5 F steps, because the coil outlet is vapor. The chart has no 32 F row, so we interpolate.

Chart values used (psig): 20 F vapor 44.6; 30 F vapor 57.3; 35 F vapor 64.4.

  1. Header at 20 F: 44.6 psig, read directly.
  2. Slope from 30 F to 35 F: (64.4 - 57.3) / 5 = 7.1 / 5 = 1.42 psi per F.
  3. Coil floor at 32 F: 57.3 + 2 x 1.42 = 57.3 + 2.84 = 60.1 psig.
  4. Pressure the EPR holds: 60.1 - 44.6 = 15.5 psi.

Reading it: set the EPR so gauge 1 holds about 60 psig at minimum load while gauge 2 shows about 45 psig. If gauge 1 settled at 51 psig instead, the chart's 25 F vapor value is 50.7, so the coil would sit near 25 F saturated, well below freezing. That setting ices the coil.

Glide check: the liquid column gives 72.3 at 30 F and 80.4 at 35 F, so 32 F is 72.3 + 0.4 x 8.1 = 75.5 psig. The wrong column would be off by 75.5 - 60.1 = 15.4 psi.

Compression ratio, assuming a 200 psig discharge: absolute pressures add 14.7. Header at 20 F: 214.7 / (44.6 + 14.7) = 214.7 / 59.3 = 3.62. If that box ran alone at 32 F: 214.7 / (60.1 + 14.7) = 214.7 / 74.8 = 2.87. The ratio is 3.62 / 2.87 = 1.26, about 26 percent higher. That is a ratio, not a power bill.

Field card: EPR valves

Coil pressure on the inlet gauge, header pressure on the outlet side.

  • EPR holds a minimum coil pressure; it opens as inlet pressure rises.
  • Gauge the inlet, set at minimum load, wait up to 30 minutes.
  • Use the dew column for a coil outlet on a glide blend.
  • Too warm: stuck closed, undersized, set high, solenoid. Too cold: stuck open, set low.
  • Pressure swinging: check the TXV, then valve size.
