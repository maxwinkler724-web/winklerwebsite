Heating Armories and Drill Halls With Hot Water: Boiler Sizing, Warm-Up and Freeze Protection

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A drill hall is a huge box that is empty most of the week and full of people and trucks on a few weekends a month. The heating job is not a steady load. It is a long cold soak, then a hard push to get the floor comfortable by Saturday morning. This lesson walks through how the pieces fit, with one worked warm-up calculation of our own.

Why these buildings are different

A big hall has a large air volume, a big roof, tall walls and large doors, but few people. Internal gains are small, so almost all heat comes from the boiler. Because the use is intermittent, the design question is less "how much heat holds 65°F" and more "how fast can we get there from the setback temperature." If you have not read the general lessons on halls used a few days a week, start with heating and cooling halls used a few days a week and the one on houses of worship and intermittent occupancy. We do not repeat them here.

Choosing the heat emitter

  • Hot-water unit heaters. Cheap, fast and simple, and an earlier edition of federal facility HVAC design criteria names hot-water unit heaters for space heating in garages, warehouses and hangars, with outlets high above the floor. Their weak points are throw, noise and coil freeze risk; see steam and hot-water unit heaters in warehouses.
  • Radiant panels or in-floor heat. They warm people and the slab rather than the air, which helps under a high roof. The same design criteria treat floor-slab radiant heat as a part-load choice in hangars in very cold weather. The trade-off is slow response. A cold slab takes many hours to come up, so radiant works best with a longer, gentler warm-up or a lower setback.
  • Air handlers with ductwork. Good for even delivery, filtered air and ventilation, but they need duct space and a planned return. A floor-type unit that pulls warm air from the top of the space moves heat back down, which is useful in tall rooms.

Many halls end up with a mix: unit heaters or an air handler for fast recovery, plus radiant where a slab is already there. Pick the system whose recovery time matches how the building is really scheduled. For more on tall, door-heavy spaces see aircraft hangar heating and ventilation.

Setback and warm-up

Letting a hall coast cold during the week saves fuel, but only if the plant can bring it back. A federal hydronic setback case study, written for houses, makes a point that applies here: a system sized exactly to the design load cannot recover from setback, so setback needs extra boiler and emitter capacity. A federal small-building scheduling guide suggests a 65°F unoccupied and 70°F occupied heating pair as a starting point for offices; a big hall can drop lower. An earlier edition of the federal HVAC design criteria limits unoccupied heating setpoints to no more than 55°F and allows a safety factor of up to 1.25 on equipment for morning warm-up. Use those as reference points, not rules for your building, and check the current edition.

Do not set the weekday temperature so low that the water in a wet sprinkler line, floor drain trap or coil could freeze. The same criteria use a 40°F indoor design for freeze-protection zones. Our own habit is to hold a floor of 45°F in a hall like this, which is an assumption in the example below.

Worked example (our own): warm-up load and boiler output

This is our own teaching example. Every input is an assumption, not a measurement. Check real halls with real surveys.

  • Hall 100 ft wide, 150 ft long, average height 25 ft, so volume = 100 × 150 × 25 = 375,000 ft³.
  • Walls: 500 ft perimeter × 22 ft = 11,000 ft² gross, assumed U = 0.20 (doors and windows lumped in). UA = 11,000 × 0.20 = 2,200 Btu/h·°F.
  • Roof: 15,000 ft², assumed U = 0.05. UA = 750 Btu/h·°F.
  • Air leakage: assumed 0.3 air changes per hour with the doors closed and ventilation off. Conductance = 0.018 × 375,000 × 0.3 = 2,025 Btu/h·°F. (Check: 0.3 ACH is 1,875 cfm, and 1.08 × 1,875 = 2,025.)
  • Total conductance = 2,200 + 750 + 2,025 = 4,975 Btu/h·°F.
  • Outdoor design 15°F, setback 45°F, occupied 65°F, warm-up time 6 hours.
  • Stored heat: air = 0.018 × 375,000 × 20 = 135,000 Btu. Interior mass (assume 150,000 lb of slab and wall surface warmed an average 8°F at 0.2 Btu/lb·°F) = 150,000 × 0.2 × 8 = 240,000 Btu. Total = 375,000 Btu.

Step 1, the ramp. 375,000 Btu over 6 hours = 62,500 Btu/h.

Step 2, the loss at the end of warm-up. At 65°F indoors and 15°F outdoors the difference is 50°F. Loss = 4,975 × 50 = 248,750 Btu/h.

Step 3, peak output. 248,750 + 62,500 = 311,250 Btu/h. Add 10 percent for piping and distribution losses (assumed): 311,250 × 1.10 = about 342,000 Btu/h of boiler output.

Step 4, input. At an assumed 85 percent efficiency, input = 342,000 / 0.85 = about 402,000 Btu/h.

Step 5, water flow. With a 20°F water temperature drop, gpm = 342,000 / (500 × 20) = 34.2 gpm.

Check. Holding 65°F needs only 248,750 Btu/h, so the warm-up adds about 25 percent. If we cut warm-up to 3 hours the ramp doubles to 125,000 Btu/h and the output goes to about 411,000 Btu/h, so a shorter warm-up costs boiler size. This is a lumped estimate: the real slab lags, the air is not one temperature, and stratification raises roof loss. A real design uses a proper load calculation.

Stratification under a high roof

Warm air rises. A university extension page on poultry buildings, another tall single-volume space, describes ceilings as much as 20°F warmer than the floor when little mixing is happening, and reports that mixing fans cut that spread to a few degrees in the cases it reviewed. Hot air under the roof also leaks heat through the roof faster, so the warm-up load above is on the low side if the hall stratifies. Slow, high-mounted mixing fans, or heaters aimed downward, reduce the spread. Run fans only when the hall is being heated, and check that they do not blow cold air onto people.

Vehicle bays and exhaust capture

Trucks and generators bring in carbon monoxide and diesel exhaust. A federal occupational health report on a fire station bay found tailpipe-capture hoses worked when they fit the vehicle and activated, but one tailpipe did not fit its hose, airflow differed hose to hose, one hose did not release cleanly, and air moved from the bay toward living space unless doors were closed. Takeaways for a drill hall: match the hoods to the vehicles, test activation, balance airflow, and run general exhaust if the capture system is down. Never rely on heaters to move exhaust. Makeup air for the exhaust must be heated by your plant, so include it in the boiler load. Do not run vehicles inside during warm-up weekends with the doors open and the heaters off.

Freeze protection for coils near big doors

A door that opens to 10°F air can drop coil air temperature fast. Keep supply water flowing through any coil near a door, use a low-limit freezestat on air handlers, and make sure outside air dampers close tight. The federal criteria mention automatic pump circulation below 35°F as one freeze method, or antifreeze solutions where needed. For related details see cabinet unit heaters, low limits and coil freeze risk and car wash heating and freeze protection.

Glycol choices

Glycol protects against freezing but costs performance. A national laboratory abstract on glycols in HVAC says ethylene and propylene glycol lower heat transfer and raise pumping requirements compared with water, and that careful coil selection can reduce those effects. The earlier federal design criteria say to avoid antifreeze if possible, and where it is needed to use propylene glycol at the lowest workable concentration. Use inhibited glycol made for heating systems, never automotive antifreeze, and test the mix on a schedule, since a diluted loop stops protecting. Most halls only need glycol in the coil branches that sit near doors or outside air, not in the whole plant. Ask the equipment maker for corrected capacity at your mix.

Simple controls a building operator can follow

  • A weekday schedule at the setback temperature and a weekend schedule that starts the warm-up the number of hours the calculation shows, plus a spare hour for cold snaps.
  • An outdoor sensor that raises the water temperature as it gets colder, with a cut-off in mild weather. The earlier federal criteria describe an outdoor sensor with a positive cut-off above 65°F.
  • A low-limit freezestat on every air handler and a freeze alarm in the hall.
  • A boiler log: a federal operations guide recommends daily logs and notes that frequent boiler cycling points to too little load, so a boiler sized for a hard warm-up may short-cycle on a mild week. Staging or two smaller boilers help; see boiler aquastats and cutoffs and boiler return temperature and condensing.

Write the plan on one page by the boiler: schedule, setpoints, warm-up hours, who to call. Operators change; the page stays.

Field card: Heating a weekend-use hall

Size for the warm-up, not the hold.

  • Warm-up load = hold load + stored heat divided by warm-up hours.
  • Shorter warm-up means a larger boiler.
  • Radiant is slow; unit heaters and air handlers are fast.
  • Mix the air under a high roof, and count the extra roof loss.
  • Coils near doors: flow, low limit, tight dampers, glycol only where needed.
  • Exhaust capture needs the right hood, activation and makeup air.
