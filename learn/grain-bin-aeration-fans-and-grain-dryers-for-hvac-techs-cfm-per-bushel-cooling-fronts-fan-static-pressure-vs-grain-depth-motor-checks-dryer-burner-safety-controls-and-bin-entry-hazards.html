Grain Bin Aeration Fans and Grain Dryers for HVAC Techs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A grain bin is a storage building that needs a fan, a heat source in some cases, sensors and a controller, so farm calls reach HVAC and refrigeration techs. This lesson covers how aeration fans are sized and run, what a cooling front is, how static pressure grows with grain depth, what to check on the fan motor, how heated-air dryers are protected, and why the bin itself is a place you do not enter without the site's written procedure.

Aeration is cooling, not drying

An aeration fan pushes outdoor air up through stored grain to even out and lower its temperature so it keeps. A university extension guide is blunt about the limit: aeration is not a grain-drying system. The same guide recommends a minimum of 0.1 cfm per bushel for on-farm storage and notes that commercial storage often runs lower, around 0.02 to 0.03 cfm per bushel. Grain that is wetter, such as 15.5 to 18 percent moisture held for feeding or blending, is given 0.2 to 0.5 cfm per bushel. Another extension fact sheet says at least 0.1 cfm per bushel is needed for successful aeration, and a third gives 0.1 as the usual sizing rate for dry grain in bins.

Why that number? The airflow only has to carry heat in and out of a mass of grain that is a good insulator. Slow, steady air is enough to move the whole bin to the target temperature, as long as the fan runs long enough. Higher airflow shortens the run time but needs a bigger fan and more static pressure.

Cooling and warming fronts

Temperature does not change everywhere at once. The extension guide describes a cooling or warming front that moves through the grain, and the job is not done until the front has passed completely through the bin. Picture a flat layer of cool grain growing from the floor upward (fan pushing up) while the warm grain above it shrinks.

How long does a cycle take? Two extension sources give figures at 0.1 cfm per bushel: one says 100 to 200 hours, another says around 150 hours to fully cool, and a fact sheet gives 150 hours as the minimum cooling time. The same sources say fan time is proportional to airflow, so halving the airflow doubles the time and a fan at 1 cfm per bushel takes about 15 hours. Those data points fit hours = 15 divided by cfm per bushel. That formula is our own fit to the sourced points, not a quoted rule, and the fact sheet notes it does not hold for hot corn cooling in silos at higher airflow, so treat it as a planning estimate.

Static pressure grows with depth

Grain resists airflow, and deeper grain resists more. The extension guide lists fan static pressure in inches of water column by grain depth and airflow rate. For corn at 0.1 cfm per bushel, up to 20 ft deep it lists 1.0 inch, 20 to 30 ft also 1.0, 30 to 40 ft 1.5 and 40 to 50 ft 2.0. At 0.2 cfm per bushel the values for corn are 1.0, 1.5, 2.5 and 4.0 for the same depth bands. Wheat is tighter and needs more: 7.5 inches at 0.2 cfm per bushel and 30 to 40 ft deep. Remember that these are the guide's design values, so a fan only delivers its rated cfm at the static pressure shown on its curve. If you read much higher or lower static than the bin design suggests, think blocked floor, fines packed in the grain, or a leaking duct. Our fan laws lesson and the airflow and static pressure basics lesson cover the reasoning.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers, not a real bin. Assume a 20,000 bushel bin filled with dry corn about 25 ft deep.

  1. Choose the airflow rate. Assume 0.1 cfm per bushel, the extension minimum for on-farm storage.
  2. Total fan cfm = 20,000 bu x 0.1 cfm per bu = 2,000 cfm.
  3. Static pressure the fan must overcome: 25 ft falls in the 20 to 30 ft band, so the guide's corn value at 0.1 cfm per bushel is 1.0 inch of water. A fan used here should be able to deliver 2,000 cfm at 1.0 inch.
  4. Hours for the front to pass: 15 divided by 0.1 = 150 hours of fan run time, which matches the 150 hours the sources give at that rate. That is 150 / 24, about 6.3 days of nonstop running, but fan time only counts while outdoor air is cool enough, so the calendar time is longer.
  5. What if we doubled the airflow to 0.2 cfm per bushel? Total cfm becomes 4,000 and the rule gives 15 / 0.2 = 75 hours. The guide's corn static value for 25 ft at 0.2 is 1.5 inches, so the bigger flow also costs more pressure and a larger motor.

Fan motor electrical checks

Aeration fans are often large motors that sit idle for months, then run for days. The checks are the same ones used on any fan and are covered in our other lessons: lock out and verify dead first (see the confined spaces lesson for the permit mindset), compare running amps with the nameplate, check voltage on all legs, and test insulation resistance after a long idle period. Three-phase fans can lose a phase on long farm feeder lines; the phase loss lesson shows what that looks like. Also check rotation, since a backward fan moves much less air. One extension fact sheet advises making sure nothing obstructs airflow in the fan housing, and another says fans should be covered or sealed when not running so warm air does not enter through the floor. Both are cheap checks that save a call back.

Moisture and temperature cables, controllers

The extension guide says cable sensors are valuable in larger bins, in the 20,000 to 25,000 bushel range and up, while smaller bins can be checked with a probe. Another fact sheet mentions finding hot spots with a cable system or a portable probe. A controller reads those temperatures and an outdoor sensor, then starts or stops the fan.

The manual rules those controllers copy are in the extension guide: run the fan continuously during cool-down when the exhaust temperature is at or above the daily maximum, treat cooling as adequate when the 24-hour average outside temperature is 10 degrees F or more below the exhaust temperature, and avoid long fan runs in rainy weather once the grain is within 5 to 10 degrees F of the average outdoor air temperature. The guide's cool-down target is below 35 F but not below 20 F. From a service view, a controller is a set of sensors and a relay. The usual faults are a bad or wet outdoor sensor, a cable with an open or shorted point, and wrong setpoints. Check those inputs with a meter the way you would on a building control loop, and see the fan laws lesson before you consider changing fan speed. We could not source moisture cable specifics, so this lesson sticks to temperature.

Heated-air dryers and their safety controls

A dryer is a different machine from an aeration fan. It heats the air with a propane or natural gas burner and moves far more air per bushel than aeration does. The extension fact sheet we read says flame color is a good indicator of proper burner operation and that uneven drying air temperature can show up as an uneven flame around the burner ring. It also advises a fire extinguisher by the dryer, a clean area around it and emergency numbers posted on a nearby phone.

OSHA's grain handling standard, 29 CFR 1910.272, describes protective controls for direct-heat dryers at grain elevators. Under paragraph (p), the fuel supply must automatically shut off on power or flame failure or if exhaust fan airflow stops, and automatic controls must stop grain feed if the drying section exhaust temperature gets excessive. Those match controls HVAC techs already know from burners: flame safeguard, airflow proving and a temperature limit. Our industrial drying oven lesson covers the same logic. Never jumper a safety to keep a dryer running. Gas train and burner work should follow the maker's instructions and local gas codes.

Dust explosion and entrapment hazards

OSHA lists grain dust as the main fuel for explosions, and ignition sources such as hot bearings or welding can set it off. For priority areas, including enclosed areas with interior dryers, OSHA says fugitive grain dust must be removed immediately once it exceeds one-eighth inch, and hot work needs a permit in most cases. Bring no torch or grinder near grain dust unless the site has issued that permit.

The bin is the other hazard. OSHA says suffocation is a leading cause of death in grain bins and that moving grain acts like quicksand. An OSHA hazard alert estimates a 6-foot worker can be covered in 11 seconds. Its list of controls includes lockout of powered equipment, an entry permit for each entry, a body harness and lifeline, an observer outside with communication, rescue equipment, and air testing, with no walking down grain and no entry under bridged grain. HVAC techs should never enter a bin on their own. If a job needs bin entry, ask the site for its written procedure and permit first. If the site has none, stop and call the owner or a supervisor.

Field card: Grain Bin Aeration Fans and Grain Dryers for HVAC Techs

Aeration cools, dryers dry, and nobody enters the bin alone.

  • Aeration minimum on farm: 0.1 cfm per bushel; it is not a drying system.
  • Cooling front time, planning estimate: hours = 15 / cfm per bushel (150 hours at 0.1).
  • Deeper grain and wheat need more static pressure; check the fan curve at that pressure.
  • Fan motor: lockout, volts on all legs, amps vs nameplate, rotation, insulation test after idle months.
  • Dryer: flame, airflow and exhaust temperature controls must stay in service.
  • Dust over 1/8 inch in priority areas is an explosion fuel. Hot work needs a permit.
  • Bin entry only under the site's written procedure with permit, lifeline and observer.
