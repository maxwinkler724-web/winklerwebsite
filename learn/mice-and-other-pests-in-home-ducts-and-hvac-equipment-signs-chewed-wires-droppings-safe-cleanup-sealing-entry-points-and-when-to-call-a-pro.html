Mice and Other Pests in Home Ducts and HVAC Equipment

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A furnace, a duct run and an outdoor unit are warm, dry, quiet and full of hiding places. To a mouse or a colony of ants, that is a home. This lesson is for homeowners: how to tell pests have moved in, why it matters, what you can safely do yourself, and what to leave to a professional.

Check your own situation first

Check first. This is general education, not medical advice or a repair procedure. Turn the equipment off at the switch before you open any panel, and do not touch wiring you cannot see clearly. If you or someone in the house feels sick after exposure to droppings, tell a health care provider about the exposure. For the technician side of this topic, see Pests in Attics, Crawlspaces and Rooftop Units.

Signs something has moved in

  • Odor from the registers. A musty, urine-like or dead-animal smell when the blower starts can mean a nest or a dead animal in a duct or at the furnace. Our odor lesson covers the other causes, since not every smell is a pest.
  • Droppings near the furnace, water heater or duct boots. Fresh droppings, shredded insulation, paper or fabric bits mean mice are active nearby.
  • Chewed thermostat or low-voltage wire. Thin control wire has soft insulation. A system that suddenly will not call for heat or cooling, or a thermostat that blanks, can be a chewed wire.
  • Rustling or scratching in the ductwork, mostly at night.
  • Outdoor unit problems. Leaves, grass and nesting material packed into the base or around the coil, or a unit that trips or will not start after a quiet season. Ants nesting in the electrical section can foul switching parts.
  • Weak airflow from one register, which can be a nest or debris blocking a return or a duct section.

Why it matters

Health. The CDC says the deer mouse is the most common U.S. carrier of the hantavirus that causes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome. People are mostly infected by contact with urine, droppings or saliva, and stirring up fresh droppings or nesting material can put the virus in the air. Symptoms usually start 1 to 8 weeks after contact, and the CDC says 38 percent of people who develop respiratory symptoms may die from the disease. Most house mice are not deer mice, but you cannot tell by looking at droppings, so treat all of it with care. A running blower can also carry odor and dust from a contaminated duct into living spaces.

Equipment. University pest guidance notes that house mice gnaw wiring and that many fires of unknown origin are suspected to be caused by rodents chewing wires. A chewed control wire can strand the system, and a chewed power or igniter wire is a safety problem. Texas A&M entomologists describe ants in electrical equipment chewing insulation, which can cause short circuits or interfere with switching parts, and hauling in soil and moisture that corrode components.

What a homeowner can safely do

Seal the outside gaps

The CDC says mice can fit through a gap the width of a pencil, about 1/4 inch. UC IPM says house mice can pass under gaps 1/4 inch tall and through openings 3/8 inch wide. The CDC suggests steel wool for small openings, held in with caulk or spray foam, and metal sheeting, hardware cloth or cement for bigger holes. The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory building guide adds that foam alone is not enough because rodents chew it, that copper or stainless mesh should go into larger gaps first, and that the final patch should be smooth. UC IPM says plastic screening, rubber, vinyl and foam are ineffective because mice chew through them, and that steel wool is only a temporary plug because it can rust. Our own plain-language takeaway: use metal behind the sealant, and recheck it each season.

Good targets are the hole where a refrigerant line set enters the house (our wall penetration lesson shows sleeves and covers), gaps around duct boots and plumbing, and gaps where the outdoor unit's lines or wiring pass through a wall or pad.

Screens and caps without choking airflow

The same national lab guide recommends 1/4-inch metal screening on crawlspace vents and notes that filters on HVAC intakes also help. For dryer vents it says to screen carefully and clean often, because lint buildup creates a fire risk. A clogged screen on any exhaust is a hazard, so if you are not sure a screen leaves the opening free, leave it off and ask a pro. Grilles on the inside are covered in registers and return grilles.

Trap and seal, do not poison

UC IPM prefers trapping in homes with only a few mice, partly because poisoned mice often die inside the structure and cause odor and sanitation problems. That is the main reason not to put poison in or near ducts: a dead animal in a return plenum is a smell you will notice for weeks. Trap first, then seal the entry so new mice do not follow.

Clean up droppings the CDC way

Follow these steps from the CDC cleanup page, for small amounts in an open area:

  1. Open doors and windows for 30 minutes to air the space out, and leave while it ventilates.
  2. Put on rubber or plastic gloves.
  3. Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings, because that can send particles into the air.
  4. Spray droppings and urine with an EPA-registered household disinfectant, or a fresh mix of 1 part bleach to 9 parts water (about 1.5 cups of bleach per gallon), until they are very wet.
  5. Let it sit 5 minutes, or as the product label says.
  6. Wipe up with paper towels and put them in a covered garbage can.
  7. Wipe hard surfaces with disinfectant, wash your gloved hands, remove the gloves, then wash with soap and warm water.

What to leave to a professional

  • Anything inside the ducts. The CDC says that if there is evidence of rodents in heating or cooling ducts, call a professional rodent control service, and that duct-cleaning companies can also help. The same CDC page calls for coveralls, goggles and a HEPA-filter respirator in heavy infestations. A confined crawl space or plenum is not a place to improvise.
  • Blower and coil cleaning. See when a blower and coil cleaning is needed.
  • Chewed or burned wiring. Do not tape over it and run the system. Have a qualified tech repair and test it.
  • Ants in the outdoor unit. Texas A&M advises against spraying liquid drenches around electrical parts or treating the equipment yourself; turn the power off and have an electrician or licensed pest provider handle it. Baiting nearby mounds with labeled products is something an owner can do.
  • Gas appliance vents and combustion air. Do not add screens, caps or patches to a furnace or water heater vent or air intake. Chimney and flue work belongs to a qualified pro.

Worked example (our own): a 10-minute walk-around checklist

This is our own example, not a published procedure. It assumes a single-family house with a crawl space or basement, an outdoor condenser and a gas furnace. The size rule comes from the sources above: treat any gap of about 1/4 inch (6 mm) or more as open to a mouse, and 3/8 inch as plainly open.

Where to lookWhat a gap looks likeSimple owner fix
Line set hole in the wallGap around the pipes wider than a pencilMetal mesh packed in, then sealant; smooth finish
Dryer ventLoose cap, torn flap or screenRepair the cap; keep it clear of lint
Return duct in crawl space or basementOpen seams, torn boots, missing insulation wrapNote it and call a pro for duct repair
Outdoor unit base and padDebris piled in, gap between unit and wallClear it, trim plants back, seal wall gaps
Furnace room pipes and wiresLoose pipe collars, holes in floorSteel wool or mesh and sealant

Walk the outside first, then the basement or crawl space. Hold a pencil up to each gap: if the pencil fits, a mouse can. Photograph anything you cannot fix, which helps a technician quote the job.

Field card: Mice and Other Pests in Home Ducts and HVAC Equipment

Seal with metal, trap do not poison, wet before you wipe.

  • Mice fit through about 1/4 inch (a pencil width); 3/8 inch is clearly open.
  • Signs: odor at registers, droppings, chewed thermostat wire, nests in the outdoor unit.
  • Never sweep or vacuum dry droppings. Ventilate 30 minutes, glove up, soak with disinfectant 5 minutes, wipe.
  • Foam alone is not a seal. Put mesh or steel wool behind sealant.
  • Pest inside ducts, damaged wiring, gas vents, ants in the outdoor unit: call a pro.
