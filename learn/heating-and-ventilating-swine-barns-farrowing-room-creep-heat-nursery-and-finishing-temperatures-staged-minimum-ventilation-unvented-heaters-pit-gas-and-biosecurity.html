Heating and Ventilating Swine Barns: Farrowing Rooms, Minimum Ventilation and Pit Gas

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A farrowing room has two customers in the same space. The sow wants cool air, and the newborn piglets want a warm patch of floor. Add manure gas, dust and a strict entry routine, and a swine barn becomes a very different job from a house. This lesson covers the heat and air side. It does not repeat the general barn lesson linked below.

Check the sources and the site

Check the sources and the site. The figures here come from university extension pages and one federal agency page, read in 2026. Treat them as study numbers. On a real job the producer, the barn designer and the equipment instructions decide the settings.

Two temperatures in one room

Extension tables put a lactating sow at about 55 to 65°F, and a newborn litter near 95°F (a range of 90 to 100°F) in an older extension worksheet. The answer is to heat only the piglets' zone. A university hover study kept the creep area at 85°F for the first two or three days, then reduced the added heat steadily, with none needed once piglets were about one to two weeks old. In that study the heat source was a 250 watt heat lamp over a creep area beside each crate, or a 100 watt bulb or an electric heat mat in a covered hover. The worksheet adds that creep heat for farrowing and nursery rooms is about half of the room's space heat need, and that a hover needs at least one half square foot per piglet.

Here is why the zones matter to us. If a tech or an owner turns the whole room up to please the piglets, the sow overheats and eats less. If the room is cold, the creep heat has to work harder and the piglets pile up. Our own reading: when you see a sow panting while piglets are huddled, the layout of heat is the fault, not the thermostat setting. Heat mats and lamps run on their own circuits and controls. See the electrical lessons before you touch them.

Targets by pig weight

Pigs lose their need for heat as they grow. The table lists a university extension table of preferred room temperatures and cold-weather ventilation rates per pig.

Pig weightPreferred room temperatureCold-weather airflow per pig
10 to 30 lb80 to 90°F2 cfm (12 to 30 lb)
30 to 75 lb65 to 80°F3 cfm
75 to 150 lb60 to 75°F7 cfm (75 to 100 lb)
150 to 260 lb50 to 75°F10 cfm

Another extension page lists a thermoneutral target of 77 to 85°F for a 15 pound weaned pig and 50 to 60°F for a 200 pound finisher. The same page says to keep air in the pig's comfort zone for its age and size. For newly weaned pigs, a university extension publication on heat sources gives an ideal air temperature of 83°F and a mat surface of 95°F, both stepped down starting seven days after placement, with at least 2 cfm per pig and relative humidity not above 65 percent. It also says huddled pigs are cold, pigs lying side by side are comfortable and pigs spread apart are too warm. That is a fast check a tech can make from the doorway.

Staged minimum ventilation versus heater cycling

In winter the fans must still run to remove moisture and gas. A university extension farrowing building plan sizes fans in stages. For a 24 crate room with no pit ventilation, stage one is a variable speed fan moving 480 to 1,000 cfm, and the later stages are larger fans that come on as the room warms. The plan sizes heaters at 3,000 to 4,000 Btu/h per crate, which is 72,000 to 96,000 Btu/h for 24 crates. Extension guidance says to keep the building tight enough to hold a static pressure target of 0.05 inch of water, with 0.02 inch as the minimum while the minimum fan runs.

The fight to avoid is the heater and the second-stage fan running together. One extension page says to set the offset from heater off to the next fan stage on at 2°F or more, so a heater that overshoots does not trigger the fan. An older energy publication says fan thermostats should be at least 4°F above the heater thermostat, or the two should be interlocked. Both say the same thing in different words: do not heat air and then throw it out the fan. If a tech sees a heater cycling hard while fan stage two chatters, check the offset before blaming the heater.

Our own worked example: heat to warm minimum ventilation air

This is our own example, not from a source. The assumptions are ours: a farrowing room with 24 crates, an outdoor temperature of 10°F, and a room temperature of 60°F. The 60°F is inside the sow range above and matches the worksheet's optimum for a lactating sow. The ventilation rate is sourced. An older extension energy publication gives 20 cfm per sow and litter for cold weather, and the extension worksheet lists the same 20 cfm for a solid floor and 10 cfm for a fully slotted floor. We use 20 cfm. We also ignore building heat loss and creep heat. This covers only the outdoor air.

  1. Airflow: 24 crates x 20 cfm = 480 cfm. This also matches the stage one fan range in the 24 crate plan.
  2. Temperature difference: 60°F − 10°F = 50°F.
  3. Sensible heat: 1.08 x 480 cfm x 50°F = 25,920 Btu/h.
  4. Per crate: 25,920 / 24 = 1,080 Btu/h.

The worksheet says unvented heaters add 4 cfm per sow and litter to the moisture control rate. Redo it with 24 cfm per crate: 24 x 24 = 576 cfm, then 1.08 x 576 x 50 = 31,104 Btu/h. That is 5,184 Btu/h more, just to move the extra air. The plan's 72,000 to 96,000 Btu/h heater range is much larger than 25,920 because a real room also loses heat through walls, ceiling and floor and must recover from cold snaps. We do not calculate those here. See where the 1.08 comes from in the linked lesson.

Unvented and vented gas heaters, carbon monoxide and moisture

A university extension publication says many forced air heaters in pig buildings are unvented, so combustion products stay in the room, and poor maintenance can build up carbon monoxide. It gives the same warning for gas-fired brooders kept dirty. Another extension page says clean and adjust gas heaters so the fuel-air mixture is correct. Burning gas also adds water vapor, and the energy publication calls 50 to 70 percent relative humidity desirable. That is why the worksheet adds extra airflow for unvented heaters. A vented heater sends its flue gases outside through a flue, so it avoids both problems when the flue is sound. Another extension page recommends carbon monoxide alarms in any building with fuel-burning equipment, and says opening windows does not make engine exhaust safe. If you suspect carbon monoxide in a barn, get people and animals into fresh air and call the proper responders. Read the general barn lesson for the poultry side of the same risk.

Manure pits and gas

Under slatted floors the pit stores manure, and a pit fan pulls gas from below the slats. An extension gas publication gives these levels. Ammonia above 100 ppm causes considerable discomfort to people and animals. Hydrogen sulfide above 10 ppm causes considerable discomfort, and above 500 ppm is likely lethal. Carbon dioxide above 4 percent is a concern because it displaces oxygen. The same page says agitating stored manure can release dangerous gas even when ventilation is working, and that the most common cause of critically high gas is ventilation failure. It recommends a backup power supply for the fans. Another extension page says no one should be in the building during pumping or agitation, that fans should be at 100 percent, and that dust masks and cartridge respirators do not filter out these gases.

Our rule: a tech never enters a pit. The extension page says never enter unless it has been ventilated. We go further. Pit entry is a trained, permitted confined space job for others. See the confined space lesson linked below. If a pit fan is down, report it to the owner. Do not wait to see how bad it is.

Dust, sensors and alarms

Dust takes airflow away quietly. One extension page says dirty, rusted or stuck shutters can cut a direct-drive fan's airflow by 40 percent, and a loose belt on a large winter fan can cost 50 percent or more. Inlets should not have excessive dirt either. Dust also coats heater burners and controller probes. Our own practice: clean and check the sensor at the start of every visit, and compare its reading to a handheld thermometer in the animal zone. We do not have a sourced figure for how much a dusty sensor drifts, so we give none. Check that the high and low temperature alarms and the backup power transfer still work. Ask the owner how they test the alarm, then test it with them.

Biosecurity before you enter

A federal agriculture page for pork producers describes a line of separation between outside (dirty) and inside (clean) areas. People crossing it arrive showered and in clean clothing and footwear. Visitors sign an entry log, only essential people go in, and vehicles and equipment are cleaned and disinfected at each crossing. Tools are part of this. Plan what you bring, clean it before it goes in, and ask the owner where to park and where to change. Do not eat in animal areas. Our own advice: call ahead, ask for the entry routine, and never enter a second barn the same day without asking. The farm service call lesson linked below covers scheduling and parts planning.

Field card: Heating and Ventilating Swine Barns: Farrowing Rooms, Minimum Ventilation and Pit Gas

Warm the piglets, not the whole room. Never enter a pit.

  • Sow about 55 to 65°F. Creep 85°F for the first days, then stepped down.
  • Cold-weather airflow: 20 cfm per sow and litter, 3 cfm per 30 to 75 lb pig, 10 cfm per 150 to 260 lb pig.
  • Sensible heat: 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.
  • Heater off to next fan stage on: at least 2°F offset, or interlock.
  • Unvented heaters: carbon monoxide and moisture. Add airflow, keep burners clean.
  • Hydrogen sulfide over 500 ppm is likely lethal. Dust masks do not stop gas.
  • Dirty shutters can cut fan airflow 40 percent.
  • Shower or change, sign in, clean tools, then enter.
