Reading an HVAC Service Report: A Homeowner Decoder

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A service report full of readings can look like a foreign language. Each line is a measurement that a technician compares to a target. This lesson explains what eight common readings mean in plain words, what a normal result looks like, and which questions help you understand the report. It is written for homeowners; the tech-facing view is in the lessons linked below.

The one idea behind every line

A reading by itself says little. It means something only next to a target: a number printed on the equipment label, in the maker's instructions, or on a charging chart. When a report shows a number, a good habit is to look for the target next to it. If the report has no target, that is the first question to ask. Several targets here come from the equipment maker, so we give general ranges and say so, not one rule for every system.

Refrigerant readings: superheat and subcooling

These two numbers describe the state of the refrigerant in a cooling system. Superheat is how far the refrigerant vapor has been warmed above its boiling point on the way back to the compressor. A university extension text puts it at roughly 10 to 15 degrees F above the saturated temperature in a basic cycle, and a valve maker's installation bulletin says the required superheat comes from the system manufacturer's specifications. Subcooling is how far the liquid has been cooled below its condensing point. We found no single typical subcooling figure that applies to every system, so the target is the one on the unit's label or chart. In plain words: low superheat can mean too much refrigerant or too little airflow, and high superheat can mean too little refrigerant. Neither is a diagnosis alone. For the details, see Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Temperature split and static pressure

The temperature split is return-air temperature minus supply-air temperature at the indoor unit. A trade training article calls 18 to 22 degrees F the typical rule of thumb for a cooling coil, and also says the right figure depends on the temperature and moisture of the air entering the coil, so a humid day can legitimately change it. A split far from the usual range points to airflow or charge questions. Furnace heating has its own number, the temperature rise, covered in Temperature Split and Furnace Temperature Rise.

Static pressure is the resistance the blower pushes against in the ducts, filter and coil, measured in inches of water column. A national-lab example installation report states that an efficiently running system has a total external static pressure of 0.5 inches of water column or less. Our own plain-words version: high static is like a clogged straw; the blower works harder and moves less air. Total static is the supply-side reading plus the return-side reading, added as plain magnitudes (the return side reads as suction, a negative number). See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Electrical readings: amps and capacitor

Amp draw is how much current a motor pulls while running. The label on the equipment lists rated load amps for the compressor and fan motors. Our own rule of thumb for reading a report: the measured number should sit at or below the label figure, and the tech should say which label line they compared it to. A reading above the label figure is worth a question. We did not find a universal tolerance, so we do not give one here.

A capacitor is a small part that gives motors a push to start and run. Its size is printed in microfarads (the symbol is µF or MFD), along with a tolerance. A trade training article suggests replacement when a measured value is more than 10 percent off the rating, while the label tolerance on your part may be tighter, so compare against the label. Capacitors store charge, so only a trained person should handle one. See Capacitors and Contactors.

Gas readings: manifold pressure and CO

Manifold pressure is the gas pressure at the furnace burners, in inches of water column. A furnace maker's test-kit instruction sheet lists 3.5 inches for natural gas and 10.0 inches for propane at high fire for several models, and lists lower values for the low stage of two-stage models, such as 1.7 for natural gas on one model. The furnace label controls, and altitude can change it. Our own plain words: it is the pressure of the gas pushing the flames, so too low gives weak heat and too high is a safety concern.

CO (carbon monoxide) appears two ways. A flue reading is taken in the exhaust of the appliance. The common reference limit is 400 ppm of flue CO, and the same trade article says below 100 ppm air-free is generally possible. "Air-free" means the reading is mathematically corrected for extra air that dilutes the sample: measured CO times 20.9, divided by (20.9 minus the O2 percent). A room reading is something else. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says about 1 to 70 ppm usually causes no symptoms for most people, above 70 ppm symptoms become more noticeable, and sustained levels of 150 to 200 ppm can be life-threatening. The Environmental Protection Agency says fuel-burning appliances should be inspected at the beginning of every heating season and that detectors are a backup, not a replacement for maintenance. If a CO alarm sounds, leave the building and call emergency services, as the commission advises. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Worked example (our own): one sample report, line by line

Our own example, not a real job. Assumptions: a split cooling system and a natural-gas furnace; the unit label and charging chart give these targets: superheat 8 to 14 degrees F, subcooling 8 to 12 degrees F, static limit 0.50 in. w.c., compressor rated load 17.9 A, capacitor 45 µF with a printed tolerance of plus or minus 6 percent, furnace manifold 3.5 in. w.c. All readings are invented for teaching.

  1. Superheat 11 F. Inside 8 to 14. Reads normal.
  2. Subcooling 9 F. Inside 8 to 12. Reads normal.
  3. Split. Return 78 F, supply 59 F. 78 minus 59 = 19 F. Inside the usual 18 to 22 range.
  4. Static. Supply 0.30 plus return 0.25 = 0.55 in. w.c. That is 0.05 above the 0.50 limit. Mildly high; the worked question is the filter and duct restriction.
  5. Amps. Compressor 14.2 A against 17.9 A rated. Below the label figure, which reads fine.
  6. Capacitor. Measured 42.5 µF against 45. 45 minus 42.5 = 2.5; 2.5 divided by 45 = 0.056, or 5.6 percent low. Inside the assumed 6 percent tolerance, but close to the edge; a tech may recommend watching it.
  7. Manifold. 3.4 in. w.c. against 3.5. Within 0.1, which reads normal.
  8. Flue CO. Measured 24 ppm with 7 percent O2. Air-free = 24 x 20.9 / (20.9 minus 7) = 24 x 20.9 / 13.9 = about 36 ppm. Under the 100 ppm mark and far under 400. Reads normal.

Summary: the only flags are static slightly over its limit and a capacitor near its tolerance edge, so those are the lines to ask about.

Questions to ask the tech

  • What target did you compare each reading to, and where did it come from?
  • Which readings were outside the target, and what does the tech think causes it?
  • Was the filter clean and the airflow normal when you took the superheat, subcooling and split?
  • Were the CO readings taken in the flue, in the room, or both?
  • Can I have the readings and targets written on the report?
  • What should I watch for before the next visit?

Field card: Reading an HVAC Service Report

No target, no meaning.

  • Superheat and subcooling: compare to the maker's chart or label.
  • Split: return minus supply; about 18 to 22 F is the usual rule of thumb, but humidity matters.
  • Static: total of both sides; 0.5 in. w.c. or less is the general goal.
  • Amps: compare to rated amps on the label.
  • Capacitor: compare µF to the label rating and tolerance.
  • Manifold: about 3.5 in. w.c. natural gas, 10 propane at high fire on several models; label controls.
  • CO: flue under 100 ppm air-free is a good result; any alarm means leave and call.
