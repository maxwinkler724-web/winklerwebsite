Condensate Neutralizers for High-Efficiency Furnaces and Boilers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condensing furnace or boiler turns steam in its exhaust back into water, and that water is acidic. A condensate neutralizer is a small canister of limestone-type media that raises the pH before the water reaches a drain. This lesson covers why, when a maker or code asks for one, how to size it, and how to test it.

Why the condensate is acidic

Burning natural gas makes water vapor. A condensing appliance pulls extra heat out of the exhaust until that vapor turns to liquid; Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers explains the temperature side. Sources agree on the result but give different ranges. Oak Ridge National Laboratory describes furnace condensate as having a pH of about 3, more acidic than typical acid rain, with corrosion and fouling problems. Lochinvar's Knight boiler manual says its condensate is slightly acidic, typically pH 3 to 5, and Navien's boiler manual gives 3 to 5 as well. A hydronics article from HTP gives 3.2 to 4.5. Neutrasafe, a neutralizer maker, gives a wider 1.8 to 4.5 and says the actual value depends on the gas, the combustion adjustment and contaminants in the combustion air. Treat these as typical ranges, not guarantees. None of the pages we read explained the chemistry, so this lesson does not either.

pH is a log scale. Each whole number is a factor of ten, so water at pH 3 is 100 times more acidic than water at pH 5 in hydrogen-ion terms.

When a neutralizer is called for

The answer depends on the maker and on your local rules. Read both.

  • Lochinvar says to install a neutralizing filter if local codes require one, and sells a factory kit. It also says to use only plastic tubing or pipe for the drain.
  • Navien calls its neutralizer kit optional but recommended for one of the disposal options, says to follow all local codes for disposal, and says not to connect more than one appliance to one neutralizer.
  • Viessmann's neutralization unit page says it is not mandatory in all regions but is highly recommended where local rules cover acidic waste. We read a summary page, not the full manual.
  • Weil-McLain sells a neutralizer kit, but the listing we read gave no requirement wording. Goodman's GMVC96 manual, in the pages we could read, says the drain must meet local and national codes and says nothing about neutralizers. Check the manual for your model.
  • Codes. An ACHR News guest column by an Ariston executive summarizes the model codes this way: the International Plumbing Code (sections 803.1 and 1003.3), the Uniform Plumbing Code (814.0) and the International Mechanical Code (307.2.1 and 307.2.2) all aim to keep condensate from harming drains, buildings and the environment, and NFPA 54 defers to the maker's instructions. It adds that many municipalities require neutralizers. That is a trade-press summary, not the code text, and the adopted edition varies. HTP likewise says some municipalities require neutralizers when condensate goes into home plumbing. Ask the local plumbing or building office.

Why drains and septic tanks matter

Neutrasafe says untreated condensate can damage cast iron, galvanized and copper pipe, and can harm the bacteria a septic system depends on. HTP says it can eat away cast iron drains. The ACHR column flags older cast iron and copper as especially exposed. These are maker and trade claims, not test results we read. Neutrasafe puts the damage line at a pH below about 5 to 5.5 for untreated condensate.

How a neutralizer kit works

The kit is a sealed tube or tank. Condensate flows in at one end, passes over the media and leaves at the other. The media dissolves slowly and raises the pH. Viessmann says its media is typically calcium carbonate. Neutrasafe says calcium carbonate with magnesium oxide, and says marble chips work only briefly. HTP uses half-inch marble chips. JJM says only its own pellets may go in its units, and not limestone or marble. The makers disagree, so use the media your kit maker names. The media is used up as it works, which is why every maker says to check it.

Sizing by Btu/h

Kit makers rate kits by the appliance's input. Axiom lists its NC-1W at up to 400,000 Btu/h input (at 93 percent efficiency) and 1.6 gallons per hour of flow, with a media capsule of about 0.26 gallon. AERCO's CN-1 data sheet lists a one-capsule kit for up to 400,000 Btu/h at 1.6 gal/hr and a two-capsule kit for up to 1,000,000 Btu/h at 4.0 gal/hr, with kits in series above that. Neutrasafe and HTP both estimate condensate at up to one gallon per hour for every 100,000 Btu/h of input in full condensing mode. One appliance per kit is the rule Navien states. The JJM sheet we read gave no Btu/h rating.

Worked example: does the kit fit?

A condensing boiler has a 120,000 Btu/h input. Choose the Axiom NC-1W as the candidate kit.

  1. Condensate estimate: 120,000 Btu/h ÷ 100,000 = 1.2, and 1.2 × 1 gal/h = 1.2 gal/h at most, in full condensing mode.
  2. Btu/h check: the kit is rated to 400,000 Btu/h. 120,000 is less than 400,000, so it passes.
  3. Flow check: the kit is rated 1.6 gal/h. 1.2 is less than 1.6, so it passes.

Now watch the edge. A 400,000 Btu/h boiler at the same rule gives 400,000 ÷ 100,000 = 4 gal/h, more than the kit's 1.6 gal/h flow rating, even though the kit's Btu/h rating allows it. The two makers' numbers do not line up at the top. We could not find a source that reconciles them. So the kit maker's Btu/h chart is the pass or fail test, and the gallons figure is only a sanity check. Near the limit, pick the next size up.

Testing with pH strips and replacing media

Axiom, Navien and JJM all say to test at the neutralizer's outlet with pH strips or a meter. Axiom and Navien say to do it annually and to replace the media when the pH falls below the minimum set by the local water authority. Navien's kit sheet also recommends replacement below pH 6.0, and JJM says to replace its pellets annually or when outlet pH drops below 5.0. Viessmann says to inspect every 6 to 12 months depending on use. Neutrasafe says most units should be recharged annually at the yearly service. Keep each maker's threshold with its own kit.

Example reading over three yearly visits at the outlet: 6.5, then 5.5, then 4.5. Compare to raw condensate near 3.2 to 4.5 (HTP's range). The first reading is far better than raw. Under Navien's 6.0 guidance, the second visit is the replace point. Under JJM's 5.0 rule on a JJM unit, the third visit is. The drop from 6.5 to 4.5 is two units, 10 × 10 = 100 times more hydrogen ions, and the media is nearly spent. If the local authority sets a higher minimum, it wins. Note that a visible media level is not proof either; the strip is.

Installing without breaking the trap

  • Keep the appliance's trap. Navien and Axiom state that every condensate drain must have a trap so flue gas does not leak, and Lochinvar says its trap must hold water whenever the boiler runs. Goodman says to prime both sides of its supplied trap before the first start. The neutralizer goes downstream of the trap.
  • Place the kit lower than the appliance's drain. Axiom, Navien and JJM all say the inlet and outlet must sit below the appliance drain. Mount horizontally with the outlet at the high point (Axiom, Navien), or if vertical, the outlet above the inlet.
  • Axiom recommends a Y or T fitting as an overflow if the drain clogs. Lochinvar says to leave the top of its tee open as a vacuum break.
  • Use plastic or other corrosion-resistant pipe, support it and keep it out of freezing areas.
  • If no gravity drain exists, Axiom says to use a condensate pump built for condensing equipment with an overflow switch; see Condensate Pump Not Working.
  • JJM says to fill the neutralizer and traps with water before running and never to run with them dry.

Sizing, trap care and the neutralizer check all belong to the yearly visit described in Condensing Boiler Annual Maintenance. For furnace vent types see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV.

Field card: Condensate Neutralizers

Trap first, neutralizer low, strip at the outlet.

  • Condensate is acidic; sources give typical ranges near pH 3 to 5, so read your maker.
  • Neutralizer required or not: your manual plus local code. Ask the local office.
  • Size by the kit maker's Btu/h chart; 1 gal/h per 100,000 Btu/h is a rough upper estimate.
  • Kit sits below the appliance drain; the appliance trap stays and stays full.
  • Test outlet pH yearly; use the maker's replace threshold and the local minimum.
  • One appliance per kit (Navien); pump with overflow switch if no gravity drain.
