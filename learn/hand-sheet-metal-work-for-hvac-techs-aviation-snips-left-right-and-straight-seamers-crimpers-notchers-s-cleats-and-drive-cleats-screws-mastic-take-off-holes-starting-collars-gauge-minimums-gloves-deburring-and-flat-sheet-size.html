Hand Sheet Metal Work for HVAC Techs: Snips, Cleats, Collars and Flat Sheet Size

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most duct work on a new system is bought as fittings, but every job still needs a few fields cuts: a take-off hole, a trimmed branch, a patched joint. This lesson covers the hand tools and joints behind that work, the safety habits that keep your hands intact, and the arithmetic for the flat sheet a piece needs.

Choosing aviation snips

Aviation snips use compound leverage, so the same hand force makes a bigger cut than with plain tin snips (source 1). The names tell you where the cut goes. A university shop page says a left cut tool cuts left and straight, a right cut tool cuts right and straight, and a straight cut tool cuts straight lines and shallow curves either way (source 1). So choose by the line you need: a long straight run takes the straight pattern, and a hole or an inside curve takes left or right depending on which way the waste should curl away from you.

The same page gives rules that apply to every pair: match the snips to the cut and to the maximum thickness the maker lists, keep them sharp, use ordinary hand pressure and step up to a larger tool instead of adding force, never lengthen the handles or hammer the blades, and wear eye protection and gloves (source 1). Padded grips are for comfort, not electrical insulation (source 1). Our own practice: snips are for thin sheet only. Anything thicker or harder goes to a power tool.

Seamers, crimpers and notchers

A university equipment list describes a duct crimper as a tool that crimps round ductwork so one piece can slip into a fitting (source 2). The other two are described here from our own field practice, not from a cited rule:

  • Hand seamer. Wide, flat jaws that grip sheet metal so you can fold an edge or close a seam without a brake. A bend made this way is only as straight as your hand, so use it for short folds and touch-ups.
  • Hand notcher. Cuts a clean corner or tab out of an edge so that a flange or cleat can fold over without bunching. A neat notch beats a ragged snip cut because the joint closes tighter.

Joining sections: S-cleats and drive cleats

Rectangular duct sections are often joined with a slip joint. A state-hosted duct construction handout shows flat and standing S-cleats, and S-and-drive and drive cleat joints, as ways to connect sections, but it gives no dimensions (source 3). In plain terms (our own description): the S-cleat is a short strip bent into an S that slips over the ends of two duct edges on the wide sides. The drive cleat is a strip that slides onto the narrow sides and is tapped over the joint. The two together lock all four sides of the joint. The rules for which joint a given duct needs come from the duct construction standard and the project documents, so follow those, not our summary.

Screws and sealing joints with mastic

Fasten first, then seal. For round metal duct, the model mechanical code requires at least three sheet metal screws or rivets spaced equally around the joint, and three on the exposed part if the joint is partly inaccessible (source 4). A federal installation guide gives the same count for rigid duct connections (source 5).

The sealant is mastic, or mastic with mesh. A federal guide says to wrap the seam with mesh and cover the mesh with a layer of mastic (source 5). A federal buildings-research information sheet on duct sealing says to seal the longitudinal seams and the transverse joints of sheet metal ducts, to use fiberglass mesh with mastic where gaps are larger than 3/8 inch, and that fabric-and-rubber duct tape is not recommended (source 6). The model code agrees on the last point: unlisted duct tape is not permitted as a sealant on any duct (source 4). For more on materials and where leaks hide, see the related sealing lesson below.

Cutting a take-off hole and installing a starting collar

A starting collar is a short round fitting with tabs that connects a round branch to a rectangular trunk. A state duct handout shows beaded collars among the take-off types, with no dimensions (source 3). The steps below are our own field practice:

  1. Hold the collar against the trunk, trace the round part of it, and mark the center.
  2. Drill a pilot hole inside the line, large enough to start the snips.
  3. Cut the hole with the left or right snips, curling the waste away from your hand, and stay just inside the line. A hole that is a little small can be trimmed. One that is too big cannot.
  4. Deburr the edge (see below), push the collar in, and bend the tabs flat against the trunk wall.
  5. Fasten the tabs with sheet metal screws, then seal around the collar and over the tab edges with mastic.

Gauge minimums at a general level

Thickness depends on duct size, shape and pressure, so a hand tech should not guess. At a general level, the model mechanical code, section 603.4, sets minimum metal thickness for ducts in a single dwelling unit. One state's adopted rules print that table: for galvanized steel, round and enclosed rectangular duct 14 inches or smaller is 30 gauge (0.013 inch), and over 14 inches is 28 gauge (0.016 inch). Exposed rectangular duct is 28 gauge up to 14 inches and 26 gauge above that (source 4). The same rules require ducts in a private garage, and ducts passing between a dwelling and a garage, to be continuous 26 gauge galvanized with no openings into the garage (source 4). A higher gauge number means thinner metal. Editions and local amendments differ, so check the code your jurisdiction has adopted before you buy sheet.

Sharp edges, gloves and deburring

Federal rules require employers to choose hand protection based on the hazards, and list severe cuts and lacerations among them (source 7). A university sharp-objects fact sheet says to wear cut-resistant gloves, especially on the hand holding the item being cut, and to take gloves off near powered equipment with moving parts because they can get caught (source 8). Both ideas fit sheet metal: cut gloves to carry and trim duct, bare or snug gloves off at the drill and grinder. Our own addition: pick a cut-resistant glove that still lets you feel a screw head. A glove too thick to work in ends up in the pocket.

A national laboratory process guide defines a burr as a thin ridge or roughness produced in cutting or shaping metal, and notes burrs hurt fit and that burr tools themselves can cut skin (source 9). It names files among the hand tools and suggests dragging a fingernail or a cotton glove across the edge to find leftover burrs (source 9). On duct, that means filing or scraping the cut edge, then checking it with a light touch through a glove. Deburr before you assemble, because a burr left in a joint also keeps it from seating flat for sealing.

Worked example (our own): flat sheet size

Everything below is our own example with assumed inputs, in inches. Real jobs use the allowances in the project documents.

Round pipe. Assumptions: 7 inch diameter, 36 inch length, 1 inch seam allowance, no end allowance.

  1. Circumference = 3.1416 x 7 = 21.99 inches.
  2. Add the seam: 21.99 + 1 = 22.99 inches, which we round to 23 inches.
  3. Cut sheet: 23 inches wide by 36 inches long.

Rectangular duct. Assumptions: 14 inch by 8 inch section, 48 inches long, one wrapped piece with one seam, 1 inch seam allowance, and 3/4 inch cleat allowance at each end.

  1. Perimeter = 2 x (14 + 8) = 44 inches.
  2. Add the seam: 44 + 1 = 45 inches wide.
  3. Length = 48 + (2 x 0.75) = 49.5 inches.
  4. Cut sheet: 45 inches wide by 49.5 inches long.

Check the result against the corner marks: the four bend lines on the rectangle sit 14, 8, 14 and 8 inches apart, which sum to 44, and the extra inch is the seam. For tape-measure fractions and take-off allowances along a run, see the layout math lesson linked below instead of repeating it here.

Field card: Hand Sheet Metal Work

Right tool, three screws, mesh and mastic, deburr, then glove up.

  • Left cut curls left, right cut curls right, straight cut for straight lines and shallow curves.
  • Do not push a snip past the maker's thickness rating.
  • Round metal joints: at least three screws spaced equally, then seal.
  • Seal with mastic, with mesh over wide gaps. Unlisted duct tape is not a code sealant.
  • Round flat size = pi x diameter + seam allowance.
  • Rectangular flat size = perimeter + seam allowance, with cleat allowance on length.
  • Gauge: a bigger number is thinner metal. Check the adopted code.
  • Cut gloves for carrying and trimming, off near rotating tools.
  • Deburr every cut edge before assembly.
