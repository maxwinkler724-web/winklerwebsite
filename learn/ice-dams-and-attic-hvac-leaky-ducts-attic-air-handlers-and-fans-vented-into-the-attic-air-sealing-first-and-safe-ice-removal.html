Ice Dams and Attic HVAC: Leaky Ducts, Attic Air Handlers, Fans Vented Into the Attic, Air Sealing First and Safe Ice Removal

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An ice dam is a ridge of ice at the roof edge, and the heat that builds it usually comes from the house, not from the weather. When HVAC ducts, an air handler or a fan share that attic, they can be part of the problem. This lesson explains how a dam forms, which attic HVAC items add heat, the fix order that government guides give, why heat cables do not fix the cause, and how to deal with ice without getting hurt. We keep it general. It is not insurance, legal or roofing advice, and a local pro should look at your roof.

How an ice dam forms

A University of Minnesota Extension page lists the ingredients: snow on the roof and a roof surface at uneven temperatures. The higher part of the roof has to sit above 32 F while the lower part stays below 32 F, averaged over a sustained period. Snow melts on the warm upper roof, the water runs down and refreezes at the cold edge, and the dam grows only over areas that stay below freezing. Meltwater then backs up behind it. The Building America Solution Center (U.S. DOE, run by a national laboratory) adds that dams need only a poorly air-sealed or poorly insulated attic, freezing weather and as little as 2 inches of snow, and that trapped water can be drawn under shingles by capillary action.

The point of the picture: the dam is the symptom, and the warm roof deck is the cause. Eaves are cold because they stick out past the heated walls. Anything that warms the deck above them feeds the cycle.

Where attic HVAC adds heat

The Building America Solution Center says heat loss into the attic through air leakage that warms the deck is one of the greatest sources of ice dams, and that any system able to put heat in the attic is a potential problem. The items below are the HVAC-related ones the sources name.

  • Leaky or poorly insulated ducts. The University of Minnesota page says inadequately insulated or leaky duct work in the attic is a source of heat. The Building America page says ducts in a vented attic are not recommended. If they must stay, it suggests sealing them at the ceiling penetrations and burying them in spray foam and insulation.
  • Air handlers and furnaces in the attic. The same page says not to put HVAC equipment in the attic and, if it must be there, to move the air barrier and insulation up to the roofline above it. That is a design choice for a pro. For the equipment side, see Air Handlers and Ducts in Unconditioned Attics.
  • Bath and kitchen fans that end in the attic. ENERGY STAR (EPA and DOE) lists kitchen, bathroom or clothes dryer vents that exhaust moist air straight into the attic, instead of outdoors, and advises hiring a professional to correct this before air sealing. The University of Minnesota page also notes that exhaust vents ending just above the roof can melt snow around them.
  • Recessed lights. The Building America page lists recessed can lights among the common leak points, and ENERGY STAR says to caulk around electrical junction boxes and fill holes in the boxes.
  • The attic hatch or pull-down stairs. Both ENERGY STAR and the Building America access-panel guide list them as common leaks. The guide says escaping heat can contribute to snow melt and ice dams, and describes weatherstripping the opening and insulating the panel or stair cover.
  • Other paths. The sources also name plumbing stacks, wiring holes, furnace flues and chimneys, dropped ceilings, open soffits, top plates and knee walls.

The Minnesota Department of Commerce guide puts the main cause plainly: the primary cause of ice dams is attic air leaks.

The fix order

The Building America pages give three steps in order: air seal the ceiling plane, insulate the attic thoroughly, then ventilate the roof. The University of Minnesota page also says to air seal first and consider more insulation after. Here is how the HVAC items fit that order. The mapping is our own reading, not a quote.

  1. Air seal the ceiling plane first. Seal gaps around wiring, plumbing, recessed lights, duct boots at the ceiling, chaseways and the hatch. ENERGY STAR says the biggest savings come from plugging the large openings, and that dirty insulation is a sign air is moving through it.
  2. Send fans fully outdoors. A fan that blows into the attic adds heat and moisture where you are trying to remove both. Our bathroom exhaust fan lesson covers the duct run and the outdoor cap, so we do not repeat it here.
  3. Seal and insulate the ducts. Seal seams and joints first; see Sealing Duct Leaks, then add insulation; see Duct Insulation R-Values.
  4. Insulate the attic floor. Insulation needs a continuous air barrier below it to work. For R-values and the sequence in one climate, read Attic Insulation and Air Sealing.
  5. Then ventilate. The Building America page says baffles and insulation dams keep insulation out of the soffit vents, with an air gap of at least 2 inches between the insulation and the roof deck. It also says each rafter or truss bay should be vented at the intake side. The University of Minnesota page says mechanical attic ventilation is not recommended in Minnesota and that natural ventilation needs only small amounts once heat loss is controlled. Ventilation helps, but it cannot make up for a leaky ceiling.

One caution from the Minnesota page: once a house is tightened, the combustion devices and exhaust systems still need enough air to run safely. Ask the installer to check combustion appliances after the work.

Why heat cables are a band-aid

A heat cable melts a path through the ice at the edge. It does not stop the warm deck above the heated house, so the same melting happens the next storm. The Minnesota Department of Commerce guide does not recommend heating cables, saying they shorten roof life and cost money to run. The cause stays in the attic. If a pro installs cables anyway as a short-term measure, treat them as a stopgap while the air sealing and insulation get done.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

This is our own example to show scale. Every input is an assumption, not a measurement or a figure from a source. We use the usual conduction relation: heat loss in Btu/h equals U times surface area times the temperature difference.

  • Assumed duct: 8 inch round supply duct, 20 feet long, in a cold attic.
  • Assumed air in the duct: 120 F. Assumed attic air: 25 F. Temperature difference: 120 - 25 = 95 F.
  • Assumed U for bare sheet metal: 1.0 Btu/h per square foot per F (that is about R-1, mostly the air films). With R-8 duct insulation added: total R = 1 + 8 = 9, so U = 1 / 9 = 0.111.

Step 1, surface area. Diameter 8 / 12 = 0.667 ft. Area = 3.1416 x 0.667 x 20 = 41.9 square feet.

Step 2, bare duct. 1.0 x 41.9 x 95 = 3,979 Btu/h. For scale, 3,979 / 3,412 = 1.17 kW, about the output of a small space heater, running inside the attic whenever the blower runs.

Step 3, with R-8. 0.111 x 41.9 x 95 = 442 Btu/h. The insulation cuts the loss by 3,979 - 442 = 3,537 Btu/h, about 89 percent.

Step 4, rough monthly cost. Assume the blower runs 240 hours in the month (8 hours a day for 30 days), gas costs $1.50 per therm (100,000 Btu), and the furnace is 90 percent efficient.

  • Bare: 3,979 x 240 = 955,000 Btu = 9.55 therms of heat. Divided by 0.9, that is 10.6 therms of gas, about $15.92.
  • R-8: 442 x 240 = 106,100 Btu = 1.06 therms. Divided by 0.9, that is 1.18 therms, about $1.77.
  • Difference: about $14 per month.

What this tells us. The dollars are small, and our numbers are rough: the air cools as it moves down the duct, so we overstate the loss, and we ignored leaks and the larger outside surface of insulation. But the lost heat does not vanish. It goes into the attic, and a leaky duct adds air on top. Sealing and insulating shrink that heat source, which is why the duct step belongs in the ice dam fix.

Clearing ice safely

The safest rule is to keep people off icy roofs and ladders. The University of Minnesota page warns that anyone on the roof in winter risks injury, that working from below can also damage the roof and house, and that it is best to contact professionals. It also says a roof rake or push broom can remove snow but may damage roofing. The Minnesota Commerce guide says not to remove ice with chippers, chemicals or heat, because they can damage shingles, gutters and other parts.

The Alaska Department of Labor snow removal page gives the rules a pro crew follows, and they apply to anyone:

  • Falls cause the most deaths and injuries in rooftop snow removal, including from roof edges, through skylights hidden under snow, and from ladders.
  • Do not use a snow rake or shovel while standing on a ladder. Check the rungs for ice, clean snow from boots, extend the ladder 3 feet above the upper level, and keep three points of contact.
  • Where possible, use rakes or drag lines from the ground, with extendable nonconductive poles, and remove small amounts at a time.
  • Keep people back 10 feet from where snow or ice is expected to fall, and wear eye and head protection when removing ice. People below can be buried by snow sliding from a roof.
  • Stay at least 10 feet from power lines and treat all lines as energized.

If water is coming in, call a qualified roofer, and for the cause call a pro who can air seal and insulate. HVAC techs have their own cold-weather risks on roofs; see our cold stress lesson.

Field card: Ice dams and attic HVAC

The dam is the symptom. The warm roof deck is the cause.

  • Dam forms when the upper roof is above 32 F and the eave is below it.
  • Heat sources: leaky or thin-insulated ducts, attic air handlers, fans that end in the attic, recessed lights, hatches.
  • Order: air seal ceiling, insulate, then ventilate. Fans go fully outdoors; seal and insulate ducts.
  • Keep at least a 2 inch gap between insulation and roof deck.
  • Heat cables treat the edge, not the heat source.
  • Never climb an icy roof. No rake on a ladder. Stay 10 feet from falling ice and power lines.
