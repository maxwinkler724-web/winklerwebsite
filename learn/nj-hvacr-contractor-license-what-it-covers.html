NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers

People say "my HVAC license" as if there were one. In New Jersey there are several, issued by different agencies for different jobs. This lesson is about one of them: the Master HVACR contractor license from the State Board of Examiners of Heating, Ventilating, Air Conditioning and Refrigeration Contractors.

Check the issuing board

Check the issuing board. Laws, fees and rules change. This page was built from state web pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal advice. Confirm every requirement with the agency that issues the license before you rely on it.

Who the board is

The 2007 law that created the license places the board within the state Division of Consumer Affairs, in the Department of Law and Public Safety. It describes a nine-member board that includes practicing master contractors, mechanical inspectors, a journeyperson and public members. Its web FAQ says the board approves each application at a monthly meeting and that the license is issued only after the exam has been scheduled and passed. That is different from the Mechanical Inspection Bureau of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Compliance in the Department of Labor, which handles boiler and refrigeration plant operator licenses such as the Black Seal. Black Seal and Blue Seal operator licenses come from that Department of Labor bureau, not from this board. Those are separate agencies with separate licenses; see the pathway page linked below.

What the license covers

The statute defines HVACR contracting broadly: for payment, planning, installing, maintaining, servicing, repairing, altering or modifying heating, ventilating, air conditioning and refrigeration systems, equipment and related parts. The board's rule, N.J.A.C. 13:32A-1.2, defines HVACR work as including the responsible handling, collection and cleaning of refrigerants in stationary equipment, and lists items such as boilers, furnaces, ductwork, fans, compressors, chillers, cooling towers and controls. Its boiler item covers power boilers, hydronic heating systems, fire tube and water tube boilers and pressure steam and hot water boilers, but leaves out parts used only to heat potable water. Offering or advertising to do such work also counts. The state FAQ adds that only licensees may use the title Master HVACR contractor. Designing systems within professional engineering practice is carved out.

What it does not replace

  • Electrical. The Board's rule N.J.A.C. 13:32A-1.3 says a master HVACR contractor shall not do electrical work above 30 volts, apart from the replacement cases in the HVACR definition. The rule was amended in 2022; before that it used a lower 10 volt line, so older articles may say 10. Ordinary 24 volt thermostat and control wiring is under 30 volts, so it falls inside what a master HVACR contractor may do. Electrical contracting has its own carve-out: the Electrical Contractors Licensing Act says work with a potential of less than 10 volts is not electrical contracting. That 10 volt figure belongs to electrical contractors, not to the HVACR license. The replacement cases are limited to wiring from the equipment's service disconnect, following the Uniform Construction Code. The rule text we read still showed limits of 25 tons and 240 volts for those hookups, but the Board proposed dropping them in 2021, so confirm the current wording. New circuits and wiring above 30 volts belong to an electrical contractor. The FAQ adds that a master HVACR contractor may not replace a disconnect box or switch, and may replace a surge protector only on the load side of a disconnect. Whether someone who is not a master (an employee or helper) may do the same control wiring is not answered by the sources we read, so ask the Board.
  • Plumbing. The law exempts certain master plumber work, and the FAQ says domestic water connections cannot be made on a combination unit swap. Piping may connect to an ice machine or humidifier only if the unit has a backflow connector.
  • Federal refrigerant certification. EPA Section 608 is a separate federal credential. See EPA 608 Basics.
  • Home improvement registration. A separate state registration may apply to residential work; see the pathway page.
  • Boiler and refrigeration plant operation. Operator licensing sits with the Department of Labor bureau.

Getting licensed, in general terms

Under N.J.A.C. 13:32A-2.2 there are three experience paths: (1) five years in total, made of at least four years in a U.S. Department of Labor approved HVACR apprenticeship or training program (steamfitter, pipefitter or sheet metal programs count) that includes refrigerant management and propane education, plus at least one year as an HVACR journeyperson; (2) a bachelor's degree in HVACR from a regionally accredited school, with the same refrigerant and propane content, plus one year of practical experience; or (3) a bachelor's degree in a related field, with the same content, plus three years of practical experience. Finishing an HVACR program at a technical school, trade school, county or community college counts as two of the four apprenticeship years in path 1. The propane education has to cover substantially the same material as the rule's named training manuals. The applicant must also show proof of being at least 21 years old (N.J.A.C. 13:32A-2.1). The Board's FAQ lists the school-credit route as a fourth path; it is the same school credit, counted separately. The FAQ also says the old grandfathering period ended in 2014 and that apprenticeships must be employer-sponsored and registered. Because rules change, confirm the current text with the Board before you plan around it.

The exam

A third-party vendor prepares and gives the exam, and only the Board grants the license. N.J.A.C. 13:32A-2.3 says it is offered at least four times a year. It has two written portions: practice issues in HVACR contracting, and the laws and rules governing HVACR contracting in New Jersey. You need a score of 70 or higher on each portion. If you fail, you may not retake it until six months have passed.

The law also requires a master to carry general liability insurance of at least $500,000 per occurrence (or approved self-insurance) and to file a $3,000 surety bond in favor of the State, renewed annually. The FAQ lists an application fee, a renewal fee and continuing education, but fees change, so confirm them with the Board.

Business rules technicians should know

The FAQ describes a bona fide representative, a licensed master contractor who acts as the business's agent. Vehicles, invoices, websites and advertising must show the license number and the words HVACR Contractor, and some advertising needs a subcontracting statement if a large share of work is subcontracted. If you are an employee working under a licensed contractor, those rules are the business's duty, but they explain why the license number is on every van. Municipalities cannot require a separate local HVACR license, though they still inspect work.

Why this matters for study

A person can hold federal 608 certification, a Black Seal, an apprenticeship card and still need a state contractor license to run an HVACR business. The reverse also holds. Keep each credential straight when you read a job ad, so you do not assume one covers the others. See NJ Black Seal Basics and NJ Black Seal License Paths and Plant Size for the operator side.

Field card: NJ HVACR Contractor License: What It Covers

Contractor license is not operator license is not 608.

  • The state HVACR board licenses Master HVACR contractors; check that board for current rules.
  • The license covers planning, installing, servicing and repairing HVACR systems for pay.
  • Electrical work above 30 V and plumbing work have their own licenses and limits.
  • Boiler operator Black Seal licensing is a different agency and a different license.
  • Electrical: a master HVACR contractor may do work up to 30 V (so 24 V controls); the under-10 V exemption belongs to electrical contractors.
  • Path: three experience routes, age 21; exam has two written parts, 70 or higher on each, six-month wait to retake.
  • Fees and continuing education change. Confirm with the issuing board.
