Movie Theater HVAC: CO2 Demand Ventilation, Booth Heat and Pull-Down

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A multiplex auditorium can hold almost nobody at 2 p.m. and a sold-out crowd at 7 p.m., then empty out and refill every two or three hours. This lesson covers the parts that are special to a cinema: sizing outdoor air for a swinging crowd, CO2 control, recovering the room between shows, the projector booth and the lobby. For under-seat supply and noise basics we do not repeat ourselves; read Theater and Auditorium HVAC first.

Check the standard and code edition

Check the edition. Standards and codes are revised, and the adopted edition and local authority decide what applies. This page is not the standard.

Why a cinema is a hard room

The federal Whole Building Design Guide says auditorium HVAC is sized and zoned for varying internal loads that depend on audience size, performance lighting and projection equipment. It also says the auditorium should be zoned separately, with extra cooling capacity.

Outdoor air: design crowd versus real crowd

The ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation table, as shown in a copy of the 2022 edition we read, lists an auditorium seating area at 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 150 people per 1,000 square feet. The same table lists lobbies in public assembly spaces at the same three numbers. The people part follows the head count; the area part is always there. That is why demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) fits a cinema: the Department of Energy's federal DCV guide names movie theaters and auditoriums as likely cost-effective, and says the best payback is in high-density spaces where occupancy varies in a way nobody can predict. It reports that Purdue put CO2 sensors in 12 auditoriums and lecture halls of 100 to 500 seats.

For how a CO2 sensor turns ppm into a damper position, see Demand-Controlled Ventilation. Two cinema-specific cautions:

  • Sensor accuracy. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study of 208 field sensors in 34 buildings found average absolute errors of 118 ppm at 760 ppm, and 47 percent of sensors off by more than 75 ppm at that level. Its authors say many CO2 systems will likely fail to save energy while meeting code rates, and suggest calibration or occupant-counting sensors.
  • Placement. The DOE guide prefers wall sensors over duct sensors, which average several spaces, and says every CO2 zone needs its own sensor. It also says calibration can be checked against outdoor air after the building has been empty about 5 hours. A sensor near a supply outlet reads the supply, not the crowd.

Worked example: design crowd versus a partial house

Our own example. Every input is an assumption, not a measurement.

  • Auditorium floor area: 3,000 square feet (assumed).
  • Design occupancy from the default density: 150 per 1,000 square feet × 3 = 450 people.
  • Rates from the 62.1 table above: 5 cfm per person and 0.06 cfm per square foot.
  • We ignore the standard's zone air distribution adjustment to keep the arithmetic short.

Outdoor air at design occupancy. People part: 5 × 450 = 2,250 cfm. Area part: 0.06 × 3,000 = 180 cfm. Total: 2,250 + 180 = 2,430 cfm.

Outdoor air with DCV at a partial house of 90 people. People part: 5 × 90 = 450 cfm. Area part: 180 cfm. Total: 450 + 180 = 630 cfm. That is 2,430 − 630 = 1,800 cfm less outdoor air to condition.

What that is worth. Take an assumed 20°F difference between outdoor and room air. Sensible load of the outdoor air = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT (the 1.08 is our own rounding of 60 min × 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb°F). Saved: 1.08 × 1,800 × 20 = 38,880 Btu/h, about 3.2 tons, for those hours.

People heat. The EnergyPlus documentation lists 108 W per seated, quiet person (from an ASHRAE Handbook table). We convert at 3.412 Btu/h per watt: 108 × 3.412 = about 368 Btu/h each. For the split we use our own assumption of two-thirds sensible, in line with a public reference table that lists 67 W sensible and 33 W latent per 100 W at 24°C. Sensible is about 247 Btu/h per person and latent about 121.

At 450 people: sensible 450 × 247 = 111,150 Btu/h (about 9.3 tons) and latent 450 × 121 = 54,450 Btu/h. At 90 people: sensible 90 × 247 = 22,230 Btu/h. The swing from a quarter-full to a full house is 111,150 − 22,230 = 88,920 Btu/h of sensible heat that was not there 20 minutes earlier. Compare that with the air in the room: assuming 60,000 cubic feet, the air stores 60,000 × 0.075 × 0.24 = 1,080 Btu per degree F. If nothing removed the crowd's heat, the air alone would climb about 111,150 ÷ 1,080 = 103°F per hour. Seats, walls and finishes soak up part of that, but the point stands: the crowd needs cooling fast.

CO2 check. Using the 0.0105 cfm of CO2 per person from the DCV lesson: at design, 450 × 0.0105 = 4.725 cfm of CO2, divided by 2,430 cfm = about 1,944 ppm above outdoor at steady state. At 90 people and 630 cfm: 0.945 ÷ 630 = 1,500 ppm above outdoor. A 2-hour movie may not reach steady state, so the real curve is gentler.

Between shows: pull-down and pre-cooling

Because the crowd arrives in a lump, reacting to temperature or CO2 after the doors open is late. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory field study of two commercial buildings found that pre-cooling during unoccupied hours can reduce cooling load during peak hours, but that releasing the stored cool too quickly can create high demand. For a screen, the practical version is a schedule tied to the show times: bring the room down to setpoint during the cleaning gap, run the air handler before the doors open, and let CO2 or ticket counts take outdoor air up as people arrive. Do not park the fan on low if the next crowd is minutes away.

Projection booth: equipment heat and its own exhaust

The booth holds the heat source that the audience never sees. A projector maker's installation FAQ says a 20,000 lumen laser phosphor model draws 2.9 kW including its cooling units, and its brightest laser model about 6.5 kW plus 3 kW for two chillers. Almost all of that electric input ends up as heat in the booth (our own energy-balance reasoning). Converting at 3.412: 2.9 kW is about 9,900 Btu/h, and 6.5 kW is about 22,200 Btu/h before the chillers. That FAQ also says laser cooling air moves at a higher flow but a lower temperature than lamp cooling, and gives two options: follow the higher cfm and exhaust the warm air directly, or keep the lamp-era exhaust and let the booth's own cooling absorb the lower Btu/h load. The choice depends on which is easier to provide in that booth.

A second maker installation page warns that heat building up in an enclosed space can shut a projector down, that warm exhaust must not reach another projector's intake, and that dust can clog the cooling and exhaust path. Another maker guide cautions against installing a projector near an air-conditioning vent. We found no public figure for exhaust cfm we could cite, so take the number from the projector's installation manual and the booth designer.

Quiet supply, briefly

The Whole Building Design Guide gives a recommended range of NC-20 to NC-30 for performance and general presentation spaces. A university facilities standard we read leaves the assembly target to be set with an acoustical consultant. The field lesson is large, slow outlets and silencers. See HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings.

Lobby and concession loads

The 62.1 table lists a public-assembly lobby at the same 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, and on a lobby the peak is short. Concession cooking gear, such as popcorn machines and fryers, may need an exhaust hood, and which hood type and make-up air the local authority requires depends on the appliances. Start with Commercial Kitchen Ventilation. Exhaust air has to be replaced with make-up air. Where lobby exhaust and auditorium ventilation share an air-handling system, energy recovery can reclaim part of the outdoor air load.

Field card: Movie Theater HVAC

Size for the crowd, run for the crowd you have.

  • 62.1 table row for an auditorium seating area: 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, default 150 people per 1,000 square feet.
  • Our example: 2,430 cfm at design, 630 cfm at 90 people, 1,800 cfm saved.
  • Seated person: about 108 W (368 Btu/h); we assume about two-thirds sensible.
  • CO2 sensors drift. Check them against outdoor air after a long closed period and ask about ticket counts.
  • Pre-cool in the gap before doors open, not after the room fills.
  • Projector heat is mostly its electric input; get exhaust cfm and Btu/h from the installation manual.
  • Booth exhaust stays out of the house; keep exhaust from entering a projector intake.
  • Hoods in the concession area need make-up air; follow local rules.
