Chiller Start-Up and Shutdown: Sequence and Safeties

A chiller is not a switch you flip. A good start proves water is moving on both sides, checks that the oil and safeties are ready, and only then lets the compressor run. A good stop does the reverse in a controlled way. This lesson explains the order and the reasons, not one machine's settings.

Why the order matters

A water chiller moves heat between two water loops. If the chilled-water side is not flowing, the cold refrigerant can freeze the water in the evaporator tubes. If the condenser side is not flowing, the machine cannot dump heat and pressure climbs. So the controls are built to prove flow first and run the compressor second.

A typical start, in general terms

One published plant checklist describes the first start of the season in this order. Treat it as an example of the logic, not as your machine's procedure.

  • Warm the oil ahead of time. The checklist has the oil heater energized for many hours the day before, and says never to start the compressor on an estimated oil temperature, because oil diluted with refrigerant can harm bearings quickly.
  • Check the water side: strainers, expansion tank, glycol concentration if used, isolation valves and the pressure-difference switches on both loops.
  • Make sure the cooling tower is ready before condenser water circulates through the chiller.
  • Start the chilled-water pump and confirm flow, then the condenser-water pump and confirm flow. The checklist says both flow switches must prove flow to the chiller controller before the compressor start sequence is permitted.
  • Start the chiller and log the first-run readings: entering and leaving water temperatures, pressures, oil pressure, compressor amps and approach temperatures. Keep them as a baseline.

Your machine's manual may add steps such as pre-lube, guide-vane or slide-valve position checks and minimum off-time before restart. Those details are the maker's to give.

The safeties and what each one protects

A water-chiller maker's overview lists the common cut-outs and what usually trips them:

  • **Flow switch.** Protects the evaporator from freezing and the machine from running with too little water. Common causes of a trip include a clogged strainer, a closed valve, low fluid, a failed pump or air in the loop.
  • **Freeze protection (low leaving-water or fluid temperature).** Guards against water or glycol freezing in the evaporator. Causes include low flow, a low temperature setting or the wrong glycol mix.
  • **High-pressure cut-out.** Guards against excess discharge pressure. Causes include a dirty condenser, a failed condenser fan, hot ambient air, low condenser-water flow or a restriction.
  • **Low-pressure cut-out.** Guards against low suction pressure, for example from low charge, a restriction, low water flow, evaporator icing or a very low load.
  • **Low oil pressure.** On compressors that use pressurized oil, protects against lubrication failure. Causes include low oil, a failed oil pump or a blocked passage.

All of these stop the compressor or keep it from starting. The source says to find and fix the cause before resetting, and not to bypass any of them. That is the rule here too.

A typical stop

The same checklist describes a controlled stop at season end: unload the compressor gradually and soft-stop it per the maker's sequence, and keep the pumps running until oil pressure drops and the coast-down is complete. It cautions against hard-tripping the chiller for a seasonal shutdown. Afterward it suggests recording end-of-season readings, cleaning the tubes while the plant is offline and servicing the tower.

Two off-season points stand out. Keep the oil heater energized unless the maker says otherwise, and tag the disconnect so nobody turns it off. And protect against freezing, either by draining the water sides or by verifying glycol protection, because tube freeze damage is expensive.

Staging and surge

When more than one chiller serves a plant, a trade article on chilled-water plant control notes that staging on load alone can push centrifugal machines toward surge, and that staging should consider lift as well. Low-load behavior differs by compressor type, so follow the plant's sequence of operations.

Reading a trip

A safety trip is information. Note which device tripped, what the readings were just before it, and what changed (a pump cycled, a valve closed, the weather turned hot). Fixing the cause is the job. Resetting and hoping is not. Write the trip, the cause and the fix in the log so the next shift sees the pattern.

Field card: Chiller Start-Up and Shutdown: Sequence and Safeties

Prove the water, warm the oil, respect every safety.

  • Start order in the example: oil ready, tower ready, chilled-water flow, condenser-water flow, then the compressor.
  • Flow, freeze, high and low pressure, and oil safeties each protect something specific. Find the cause before reset.
  • Use the controlled soft-stop for shutdown, not a hard trip.
  • Keep oil heaters energized in the off-season unless the maker says otherwise.
  • Never bypass a safety. Your machine's manual overrides this lesson.
