PRV Station Startup and Shutdown Concepts

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pressure-reducing station only looks simple. The real work is in how you bring steam to it and how you take it out of service, because both are moments when condensate, trapped pressure and stored heat can hurt people.

Read this first

This lesson explains the logic of startup and shutdown. It is not a procedure. Your site’s written steps, the valve manufacturer’s instructions and the rules in your jurisdiction decide what you actually do and who is allowed to do it. If you do not know the station, ask the person who does.

What is in the station

Stations differ, so trace yours against the drawing. A typical one has an isolation valve upstream, a strainer, a separator or trapped drip point so the valve sees dry steam, the pressure-reducing valve itself, gauges on both sides, a relief valve downstream, a downstream isolation valve and a bypass. A manufacturer learning module notes that pilot-operated valves need both a strainer and a separator upstream, because clean, dry steam protects their small clearances. See PRV Station Basics for the parts.

Why startup is slow

Cold pipe turns the first steam into condensate. If steam rushes in, it can sweep that water along as a slug, and since water does not compress, the impact can fracture piping and release live steam. The steam-distribution reference recommends opening isolation valves slowly so condensate can drain through the traps before fast steam pushes it along, and it describes opening valves too quickly on cold pipe as a classic cause of water hammer. For the mechanism, see Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.

The logic of startup

  • Confirm everyone and everything is clear. Locks and tags removed by their owners, people clear, downstream equipment ready for steam.
  • Look before you open anything. Missing gauges, open drains, loose bolting, a blocked relief discharge or a closed sensing line are all reasons to stop.
  • Warm the upstream piping gradually and let the condensate drain away through traps or drains.
  • Let the valve prove itself with the downstream side closed or nearly so. The downstream gauge should hold near setpoint with no flow. If it creeps upward, the seat is leaking and the problem should be solved before load is added.
  • Bring load on slowly and watch for hammer, steady pressure, and a relief valve that stays seated and quiet.
  • Write it down. Upstream and downstream pressure, time, and anything odd.

On a two-stage station, one manufacturer’s guide describes setting the low-pressure valve first while it passes a small flow, then setting the high-pressure valve to the intermediate pressure, and finally setting the low-pressure valve to the final pressure. Follow your own station’s procedure.

The logic of shutdown

  • Warn the people affected and the operator in charge.
  • Close the downstream isolation, then the upstream isolation.
  • Open downstream vents and drains slowly, standing clear of the discharge, to let pressure and condensate out.
  • Confirm zero pressure at a vent or drain, not only on a gauge, because a plugged gauge line can read zero while pressure is present.
  • Apply lockout/tagout per site procedure. Many sites require double isolation, because steam valves can leak by.
  • Some stations also have a strainer blow-off and a way to bleed trapped pressure from the high-pressure side; whether and how to use them is set by the station's procedure and the manufacturer's manual, not by this lesson.
  • Only then open a flange, a strainer cover or the valve.

Safety rules that do not change

Never stand in line with a relief outlet, drain, vent or flange you are loosening. Crack flanges slowly with the far side first. Never cap, gag, plug or adjust a relief valve unless you are qualified and authorized, and never block its discharge. Each person working applies their own lock and tag. The bypass can send full supply pressure downstream, so it is for trained staff following a procedure, not a shortcut.

Printable checklist

  • Site procedure and manufacturer instructions in hand
  • Permit or authorization obtained if required
  • Upstream line warmed slowly, condensate drained before full steam
  • Relief discharge clear and routed to a safe place
  • Station holds setpoint with no load before load is added
  • Load brought on gradually; no hammer, relief valve quiet
  • Shutdown: close downstream then upstream isolation, vent slowly, confirm zero at a drain
  • Lockout/tagout applied by each worker; double isolation if site requires
  • Readings and anomalies recorded
