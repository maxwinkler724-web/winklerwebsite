Dropped Screws and Parts Inside HVAC Equipment: Find It Before Startup

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A screw that slips out of a panel and falls into a cabinet seems like a small thing. Inside a blower housing, an inducer, a condenser fan or a control board, it can turn into a jammed wheel, a bent blade or a shorted trace. This lesson covers how we keep parts from getting lost, how we find one that did, and when we hand the problem to the lead.

Why a loose screw is a real hazard

Equipment that moves or carries current does not forgive loose metal. Here is the reasoning we use on the job (this is our own shop reasoning, not a quoted rule):

  • Blower wheel. A screw in the housing can be picked up by the wheel blades, strike the scroll, bend a blade, throw the wheel out of balance or lock it. A struck wheel can also load the motor.
  • Inducer. The housing is small and the wheel is light. A screw can jam it or rattle against the housing, and the unit may then lock out on a pressure switch fault that looks like a vent problem.
  • Condenser fan. A screw dropped through the top can bend a blade or knock the fan out of balance. A bent blade can also strike the guard.
  • Control board. A metal screw resting on a powered board can bridge two traces or pins. That can burn a trace or open a fuse, and it can also send a wrong signal to the equipment.

The common thread is that the damage often shows up on startup, with people close to the equipment and the unit running unattended afterward. Finding the part first is cheaper than finding the damage later.

Power off before you reach in

Retrieving a part is servicing, and it needs the same energy control as any other work inside the cabinet. The federal workplace safety agency's overview page lists electrocution, burns, crushing, cutting, amputation and fractures among the harms from failing to control hazardous energy during servicing and maintenance. The general industry lockout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, sets the order: prepare, shut the equipment down, isolate it, apply the lock or tag, relieve stored energy, then verify that isolation and deenergization have been accomplished before work begins. A blower or fan that has just been stopped may still coast, and a capacitor may still hold charge, so a reach-in right after the disconnect is thrown is not safe. Do not reach in until you have verified with a meter. For the full routine, see our lockout and verify-dead lesson. We do not repeat it here.

Prevent it first

  • Catch screws. Set a shallow tray, or a folded towel, under the panel before the first screw comes out, and drop every screw into it. Keep the screws for one panel in one spot. Tool choice and magnetic trays are covered in the screwdriver and nut driver lesson.
  • Support the panel. The last screw on a panel is the one that tends to drop, because the panel shifts. Hold the panel with your free hand while you take it out.
  • Cover openings. If a part has to come off over an open housing, lay a towel over the opening below your hands.
  • Count out, count back. Write the number of screws removed from each panel on a piece of tape on the panel or in your notes, and check it when you put the panel on. That gives you a stated number to test against.
  • Watch your pockets. Washers, clips and wire nuts can fall from a shirt pocket when you lean in.

If a part drops: find it

  1. Stop and keep the power off. Do not try to run the unit to see whether the noise goes away.
  2. Note where it fell. Which panel, which direction, and did you hear it hit metal, plastic or nothing.
  3. Light it. Use a bright flashlight held low and at a slant so shadows show edges. A telescoping inspection mirror helps for gaps you cannot look straight into. See the inspection mirror and light lesson.
  4. Pick it up the right way. A telescoping magnetic pickup works for steel screws. A claw grabber works for non-magnetic parts such as brass or aluminum hardware, or for screws in tight spots. A magnet can also drag other small steel parts, so watch what comes along.
  5. Check low spots. Look in the bottom of the cabinet, in the blower housing, under the motor, in the fan shroud and along the drain pan edge. With power still off and verified, turn a blower wheel by hand to see whether anything rattles.
  6. Look at the board. Check around the control board, relays and terminals for anything metal lodged against pins or traces.

Never start it up until it is found

The same lockout standard speaks to this in its restoration steps. Before lockout devices are removed and energy is restored, it requires that the work area be inspected to ensure nonessential items have been removed and that the machine or equipment components are operationally intact. A state university's lockout program that we read says the same in its own words: inspect the area for unneeded items, check the area for people, and notify affected employees before the equipment starts. A missing screw is an unaccounted-for item. If we cannot account for it, we do not re-energize.

When you cannot find it

Tell the lead. This is not something to hide. Say which panel, how many screws were counted out and back, and where you looked. The lead may decide to open more panels, use a camera, pull the blower or fan assembly, or keep the equipment locked out and tagged until the part is found. Silence is the risky choice: a shorted board or a damaged wheel later costs more than the extra minutes now. Keep the equipment locked out in the meantime and note the issue in the service record.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a source. Assumptions: a service call on one furnace and its outdoor unit. We removed four panels, counted each panel's screws as we took them out, and counted the screws as we put each one back. The numbers are made up for practice.

PanelScrews removedScrews replacedDifference
Blower door440
Control board cover330
Inducer cover660
Condenser fan guard871

Step 1, total removed: 4 + 3 + 6 + 8 = 21.

Step 2, total replaced: 4 + 3 + 6 + 7 = 20.

Step 3, missing: 21 − 20 = 1 screw.

Step 4, where: only the condenser fan guard row has a difference, so that is where the screw most likely went, probably onto the fan blades or the base pan. We would make sure the outdoor unit is locked out and verified, check the base, shroud and blades with a light, use the pickup if we see it, and not close the disconnect until it is found or the lead has been told. This check only works because we wrote the counts down. With no counts, we could not know whether 20 was right.

Field card: Dropped Screws and Parts

Count out, count back, and never start up with a part missing.

  • Power off, lock out and verify with a meter before reaching in.
  • Tray or towel under the panel before the first screw.
  • Light, mirror, magnetic pickup, claw grabber.
  • Check low spots, the blower housing, fan and board.
  • Missing part: do not energize, tell the lead.
