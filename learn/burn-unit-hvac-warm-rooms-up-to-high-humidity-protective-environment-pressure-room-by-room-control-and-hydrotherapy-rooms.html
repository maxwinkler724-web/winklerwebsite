Burn Unit HVAC: Warm Rooms, High Humidity, Protective Pressure, Room-by-Room Control and Hydrotherapy Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most hospital rooms are kept cool and dry. A burn unit patient room is often the opposite: warm enough that staff sweat, and sometimes humid. For a facility tech that flips the usual worries. The problem is not only comfort. Warm, moist room air meets cold duct and window surfaces, and that is where water forms. This lesson covers why the rooms run warm, what the public ASHRAE 170 addenda list, and a worked condensation check.

Check the design documents first

Do not change setpoints on your own. A burn room is set for a medical reason. The numbers on this page are general education from public sources. The unit's design documents, infection control team and clinical staff decide what a room must hold. Standards are named in plain text only; we are not linking or copying them.

Why burn rooms run warm

Burned skin no longer holds heat in. A nursing journal article on hypothermia in major burns says heat escapes from burned skin by conduction and evaporation, and that large IV fluid volumes and wet dressings add to the cooling. It recommends keeping ambient temperature up to 33 C (about 91 F) to reduce hypermetabolism, and lists raising ambient temperature to 32 C (about 90 F) as a passive rewarming step. A review of perioperative temperature management says guidelines often call for a warmer room, but the evidence is limited. It also says burn patients lose heat mainly by convection and radiation, with evaporation and fluid resuscitation adding to it, and that a warmer operating room of roughly 30 to 40 C helps keep normal body temperature.

A 1985 engineering study used a water-surface phantom as stand-in for burned tissue. It found that radiant heat or warm air over the surface cut heat loss without changing evaporation much. That is one reason radiant sources show up in these rooms. The International Health Facility Guidelines (iHFG), a non-US design guide, sets an ambient target of 26 to 28 C (about 79 to 82 F, our conversion) for the burns bathroom. The unit studied in the nursing article used a room temperature of 28 C (about 82 F) as part of its protocol.

What the ASHRAE 170 addenda list

Addendum p to the 2017 edition of ASHRAE/ASHE Standard 170 is public. Its Table 7.1 row for wound intensive care (burn unit) reads: pressure not required, 2 outdoor air changes per hour (ACH), 6 total ACH, 40 to 60 percent design RH, 70 to 75 F design temperature. The same table's hydrotherapy row reads: negative pressure, 2 outdoor and 6 total ACH, no RH value, 72 to 80 F.

Footnote l of that table says a lower or higher temperature is permitted when patients' comfort or medical conditions require it. That is how a burn room can run at 85 F and still be inside the standard. A 2008 edition table we also read shows the same burn row, with pressure listed as not required. A 2021-edition summary on a consulting blog lists the burn row as positive pressure with HEPA filtration. We could not confirm that against the standard, so treat pressure as a design-document question.

Humidity, steam and what CDC says

CDC's environmental infection control guideline says to engineer humidity controls into the HVAC system, monitor them so moisture is removed adequately, prefer steam humidifiers over cool-mist, and keep a water-removal mechanism in the system. It also says not to use large-volume room humidifiers that make aerosols in protective environment rooms. For humidifier types and water quality, see our adiabatic humidifier lesson.

Pressure and the protective environment

CDC defines a protective environment (PE) room as a patient care area with positive airflow relative to the corridor, with HEPA supply air, more than 12 ACH and at least 2.5 Pa (0.01 in. w.c.) positive to the corridor. CDC does not name burn units as PE rooms in the parts we read. The iHFG burns unit part says positive pressure isolation rooms with anterooms and HEPA filtered air are required, with at least one negative pressure room for infected patients. So a burn unit may mix room types, and each door sign matters. The pressure, anteroom and monitor details are in our protective environment lesson; we do not repeat them here.

Room-by-room control and fast response

iHFG calls for a local thermostat in each patient room that the occupant can adjust, and says the burns bathroom needs its own temperature control and monitoring. As a design principle (ours, not a cited rule), that means a reheat coil, radiant panel or terminal unit per room, and a heating source fast enough that a patient is not cold during a dressing change. See our VAV box lesson for how reheat and minimum airflow settings interact. Remember that warm rooms with positive pressure push warm, moist air out through every door gap, so corridor walls and door frames can sweat too.

Worked example: will this room sweat?

Inputs. Room at 85 F and 40 percent RH. Assumptions (ours): sea level pressure p = 1013.25 mb, a 55 F duct surface, a 60 F window inner surface. Saturation vapor pressure comes from a university table that lists 41.1 mb at 85 F (Colorado atmospheric science department, svp table).

  1. Water vapor pressure: pw = 0.40 x 41.1 = 16.44 mb.
  2. Humidity ratio: W = 0.622 x pw / (p - pw) = 0.622 x 16.44 / (1013.25 - 16.44) = 10.226 / 996.81 = 0.01026 lb per lb dry air, about 72 grains (7,000 grains per lb).
  3. Dew point: find the temperature whose saturation pressure matches 16.44 mb. The same table lists 16.4 mb at 58 F and 17.0 mb at 59 F. The dew point is about 58 F.
  4. Compare. A 55 F surface has saturation pressure 14.8 mb, below 16.44, so water condenses on it. A 60 F surface has 17.7 mb, above 16.44, so it stays dry, but with a margin of only about 2 F above the dew point.

Result. An uninsulated 55 F supply duct or chilled surface in this room sweats. A 60 F window stays dry until it drops to about 58 F, which a cold winter night can do. Raising the room to 60 percent RH makes pw 24.66 mb and W 0.01552, about 109 grains. That is above every row we read (62 F is 19.0 mb), so the dew point would be above 62 F and even more surfaces sweat. That is why this lesson pairs with insulation and vapor retarder work.

Humidifier load (assumed supply). Assume 200 cfm of supply at W = 0.0050 (our assumption). Delta W = 0.01026 - 0.0050 = 0.00526. Moisture: 4.5 x 200 x 0.00526 = 4.7 lb/h. Latent Btu/h: 4,840 x 200 x 0.00526 = about 5,090 Btu/h.

Hydrotherapy and debridement rooms

The ASHRAE addendum row for hydrotherapy is negative pressure, 6 total ACH and 72 to 80 F, with no RH range given. That means moisture from tubs and showers is pulled toward exhaust. Watch for exhaust duct sweating, fogged lights and rusting grilles. Our physical therapy lesson covers pool-room humidity; the same ideas apply to a tub room. The iHFG says warm water in patient-accessible areas should be 38 C and must not exceed 43 C; that is a plumbing matter, so report scalding concerns, do not adjust mixing valves.

Staff comfort

A PLOS ONE study of burn surgery theatres compared 24.0 C and 45 percent RH with 30.8 C and 39 percent RH. The warmer room raised staff heart rate, core temperature and fluid loss, and workload and fatigue ratings were higher. Cognitive function and manual dexterity did not differ in that small study of 10 staff. Expect complaints, and route them to the clinical lead rather than cooling the room.

Field card: Burn Unit HVAC

Warm is the design, so look for sweating, not for a setpoint to fix.

  • Burn rooms run warm to limit patient heat loss; get approval before touching a setpoint.
  • ASHRAE 170 addendum p burn row: 70 to 75 F, 40 to 60 percent RH, 6 total ACH; footnote l allows higher temperatures for medical need.
  • Dew point at 85 F and 40 percent RH is about 58 F (our math). Surfaces colder than that sweat.
  • Check duct insulation, window frames, door frames and exhaust ducts from hydrotherapy rooms.
  • Moisture lb/h = 4.5 x cfm x delta W; Btu/h = 4,840 x cfm x delta W.
  • Verify pressure signs and monitors against the design documents.
