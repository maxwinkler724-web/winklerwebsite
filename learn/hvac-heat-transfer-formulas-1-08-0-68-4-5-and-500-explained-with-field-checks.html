HVAC Heat Transfer Formulas: 1.08, 0.68, 4.5 and 500 Explained With Field Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Four numbers show up on almost every tech's cheat sheet: 1.08, 0.68, 4.5 and 500. They are not magic. Each one is a few unit conversions multiplied together, and knowing what is inside them tells you when they can be trusted and when they drift.

Where the constants come from

1.08 (sensible heat in air). MEP Academy shows the build: 0.075 lb/ft³ (air density) times 60 min/hr times 0.24 Btu/lb·°F (specific heat of air) equals 1.08. So Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature change. Cfm is cubic feet per minute, so the 60 turns minutes into hours.

4.5 (total heat in air). Trane's Engineers Newsletter on psychrometrics says the 4.5 equals standard air density times 60 min/hr, which is 0.075 × 60 = 4.5. Total Btu/h = 4.5 × cfm × change in enthalpy, where enthalpy (Btu per lb of dry air) is read from a psychrometric chart or table. Enthalpy already includes both the temperature part and the moisture part, so no specific heat appears.

0.68 (latent heat in air). Trane builds its latent constant from air density, the latent heat of vaporization of water (it uses 1,076 Btu/lb), 60 min/hr and 7,000 grains per pound, and gets 0.69. A grain is a tiny unit of weight, and 7,000 of them make a pound. Our own arithmetic: 0.075 × 60 × 1,076 ÷ 7,000 = 0.692. With a latent heat near 1,061 Btu/lb, the figure PDHonline's psychrometrics overview uses in its enthalpy equation, the result is 0.075 × 60 × 1,061 ÷ 7,000 = 0.682. That is why sources print either 0.68 or 0.69, and Belimo's formula guide uses 0.69. The difference is under 2 percent. Engineering Toolbox goes another way and prints 1.08, 0.68 and 4.7, with its 4.7 coming from metric property values for 20°C air. Different bases give slightly different constants, and that is normal.

500 (water). Bell & Gossett's hydronic design manual and Taco's cheat sheet both describe it as the weight of a gallon of water (about 8.33 lb) times 60 minutes times the specific heat of water (1.0 Btu/lb·°F). Our own: 8.33 × 60 × 1.0 = 499.8, which rounds to 500. John Siegenthaler, writing in Plumbing & Mechanical, shows the same constant from water at 60°F as 500.02 and notes it is rounded to 500.

Tons. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h, so tons = Btu/h ÷ 12,000. Belimo's guide states it the same way.

When the constants are accurate

Each constant assumes a particular fluid in a particular state.

  • Air constants assume standard air. Trane's newsletter says ASHRAE defines standard air at 0.075 lb/ft³, which is sea-level air at 29.92 in. Hg. It also says the 1.085 it prints (it uses 0.241 for specific heat) "is not a constant," and neither is the 0.69 or the 4.5: all three shift when density changes with altitude.
  • Altitude. Trane's Figure 1 pairs elevations with barometric pressure: 29.92 in. Hg at sea level, 27.82 at 3,000 ft and 24.89 at 6,000 ft. Thinner air means each cfm carries fewer pounds of air, so the true constants are lower than the printed ones. We did not find a source that prints corrected constants for each elevation, so use a density ratio from the maker's or Trane's method rather than guessing.
  • Water constant assumes plain water. The 500 holds near ordinary hydronic temperatures; Deppmann says that for water between 40°F and 200°F the default specific heat and gravity are both 1.0. Glycol lowers the specific heat, so the same gpm moves less heat. Belimo's guide says designers often use about 485 for a 30 percent propylene glycol mix. Siegenthaler's example for 50 percent propylene glycol at 120°F needs about 9.3 percent more flow for the same heat. Deppmann's examples show correction factors such as 0.871 for a 40 percent inhibited ethylene glycol solution in a chilled water case, and says the factor depends on both temperature and concentration. Use the glycol maker's chart for your mix. See our glycol lesson.
  • The sensible-only trap. 1.08 sees only dry-bulb change. A cooling coil that removes moisture takes out more heat than 1.08 shows, which is why the total needs enthalpy.

Worked example 1: the air side

This is made-up data. An air handler moves 4,000 cfm. Return air is 75°F dry bulb and 62.5°F wet bulb; supply air is 55°F dry bulb and 53.5°F wet bulb.

Enthalpies. PDHonline's psychrometrics overview lists 28.2 Btu/lb for 75°F dry bulb and 62.5°F wet bulb. For the supply air we used Engineering Toolbox's saturated-air table at 29.92 inHg, which prints 20.27 Btu/lb at 50°F and 26.41 at 60°F. Our own straight-line reading for 53.5°F: 20.27 + 0.35 × (26.41 − 20.27) = 22.4 Btu/lb. At wet bulb the air's enthalpy is close to the saturated value at that temperature, but reading a chart this way is good to only about 0.2 Btu/lb, and the curve means the real figure is a hair under our straight-line one.

Our own arithmetic:

  1. Sensible = 1.08 × 4,000 × (75 − 55) = 1.08 × 4,000 × 20 = 86,400 Btu/h.
  2. Enthalpy change = 28.2 − 22.4 = 5.8 Btu/lb.
  3. Total = 4.5 × 4,000 × 5.8 = 104,400 Btu/h.
  4. Latent = total − sensible = 104,400 − 86,400 = 18,000 Btu/h.
  5. Tons = 104,400 ÷ 12,000 = 8.7 tons.
  6. Sensible heat ratio = 86,400 ÷ 104,400 = 0.83.

A ratio of 0.83 means about 83 percent of the work cools the air and 17 percent removes moisture. The sensible heat ratio lesson shows how airflow shifts that split, and psychrometrics for technicians covers reading the chart.

Worked example 2: the water side

The coil in example 1 is fed by chilled water: 24 gpm in at 44°F, out at 56°F, so delta T is 12°F. Plain water assumed.

  1. Btu/h = 500 × 24 × (56 − 44) = 500 × 24 × 12 = 144,000 Btu/h.
  2. Tons = 144,000 ÷ 12,000 = 12.0 tons.

The mismatch. The water says 144,000 Btu/h and the air says 104,400, so the water side is about 38 percent higher (144,000 ÷ 104,400 = 1.38). That is a lot. Because the data are invented, nothing is wrong with any real coil, but in the field a gap this big would send us looking. Our own arithmetic on two ways to close it: the air would need 144,000 ÷ (4.5 × 5.8) = 5,517 cfm instead of 4,000 to match, or the water would need 104,400 ÷ (500 × 12) = 17.4 gpm instead of 24. Either the airflow reading, the gpm reading, a thermometer, the wet-bulb reading, glycol in the loop, or heat picked up elsewhere could explain it. A real check is also never exact; field temperature and flow readings carry error, and a gap of 10 percent or so is easier to live with than 38.

Three field checks

  1. Coil capacity from air. Get a cfm reading (see measuring airflow), take dry bulb and wet bulb on both sides of the coil, and use 4.5 with the enthalpy change. Wait for steady conditions first.
  2. Boiler or chiller load from water. With a flow reading or the balancing valve's gpm, and supply and return temperatures, Btu/h = 500 × gpm × delta T. Our own: 30 gpm with a 20°F drop is 500 × 30 × 20 = 300,000 Btu/h. Compare with the nameplate input times efficiency. See chilled water pumps and delta T for why low delta T matters.
  3. Airflow from heat input. Rearrange: cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × delta T). Our own: a furnace with 60,000 Btu/h output and a 45°F temperature rise gives 60,000 ÷ (1.08 × 45) = 60,000 ÷ 48.6 = about 1,235 cfm. Only sensible heat is involved, so this works for heating and for dry cooling but not for a wet coil.

Field card: HVAC heat transfer formulas

Know what is inside the constant.

  • Sensible air: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × delta T (0.075 × 60 × 0.24).
  • Total air: Btu/h = 4.5 × cfm × delta h (0.075 × 60), enthalpy from a chart or table.
  • Latent air: Btu/h = 0.68 (some sources 0.69) × cfm × delta grains, or total minus sensible.
  • Water: Btu/h = 500 × gpm × delta T (8.33 × 60 × 1.0).
  • One ton = 12,000 Btu/h. Sensible heat ratio = sensible ÷ total.
  • Air constants assume standard air at sea level; they fall with altitude.
  • 500 assumes plain water; glycol lowers it, so use the maker's chart.
  • If air-side and water-side loads disagree a lot, recheck the readings before blaming the coil.
