PID Control Loops in Building Automation: Proportional, Integral, Derivative, Tuning and Hunting

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A discharge air temperature that wanders up and down all afternoon, or a chilled water valve that never stops moving, is usually a control loop problem before it is an equipment problem. This lesson explains what a loop is, what the P, I and D settings do, what hunting looks like on a trend, and how the sources we read say to approach tuning carefully.

Check the settings in your own system

Check your controller's own documentation. Controller makers use different words and units for the same ideas, and this page is general education built from public sources read in 2026. Do not change live loop settings without permission and the maker's instructions for that controller.

What a control loop is

A loop has five parts. A sensor measures the thing you care about, such as discharge air temperature. A setpoint is the value you want. The controller compares the two and calculates an output, which drives an actuator on a valve or damper. Schneider Electric's building automation article describes the same idea as a process variable compared with a setpoint, with the output adjusted to keep them close. For the sensor side, see BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits; for the overall system, see Building Automation Basics.

Proportional action, gain and proportional band

Proportional control moves the output in proportion to the error. Automated Logic's educator handout says the output is proportional to the error and that it responds fast but can leave a steady error. Two settings describe the same thing from opposite sides. Gain is how much output you get per degree of error. Proportional band (also called throttling range) is how far the measured value must travel to move the output across its full range. Forest Rock's training page defines throttling range as how much the measured value changes when the output goes from 0% to 100%, and gain as the output range divided by that range. A narrow band is a high gain, and a high gain is more aggressive.

Offset, also called droop

With proportional action alone, the output only exists while there is an error. So when the load needs some steady output, the loop settles away from setpoint. Automated Logic calls the sustained error at equilibrium the offset, and Forest Rock calls it offset or droop. The worked example below puts numbers on it.

Integral (reset) action and windup

Integral action adds up the error over time. Forest Rock says it eliminates the offset by accumulating error, and Automated Logic says the output keeps building as long as an error persists. In plain terms, as long as the temperature is off setpoint, the integral term keeps nudging the output until the error is gone. Integral too fast causes trouble: Forest Rock's trend guide shows a large overshoot followed by a swing back below setpoint.

Windup is integral action that keeps accumulating when the output cannot respond. Forest Rock describes it as the integral building to extreme values while the output is maxed out and says output limits help prevent it. The ACHR News boiler article describes a related case: the integral keeps accumulating while the output is in manual, which can cause a sudden jump when the loop returns to automatic, and it names track mode as a way to avoid that. Check how your controller handles manual overrides before you release one.

Derivative, and why HVAC rarely uses it

Derivative responds to how fast the error is changing, a braking action in Forest Rock's words. Automated Logic says very few HVAC applications actually need it, that most use PI control, and that HVAC applications are usually slow and can tolerate a little overshoot. Schneider Electric's tuning note calls derivative usually unnecessary, and ACHR News warns that it can give worse control than none if set badly. Most of the loops you will tune are PI loops.

Our own worked example: a 55 F discharge air loop

Our own setup, for illustration: a cooling loop with setpoint 55 F, a proportional band of 10 F, and an output of 0% at setpoint rising to 100% at 65 F. (Real controllers may add a bias or use other conventions, so check yours.)

  1. Our own: the gain is 100% ÷ 10 F = 10% of output per degree F of error.
  2. Our own: at 58 F, the error is 58 − 55 = 3 F. Output = 3 F × 10%/F = 30%.
  3. Our own: at 60 F, the error is 60 − 55 = 5 F. Output = 5 F × 10%/F = 50%.
  4. Our own: suppose the load needs 40% output to hold steady. A proportional-only controller makes 40% only when the error is 40% ÷ 10%/F = 4 F. The loop therefore settles at 55 + 4 = 59 F. The offset is 0.40 × 10 F = 4 F.
  5. Our own: at 59 F, a proportional-only controller is satisfied, since its 40% output matches the load. Nothing pushes the temperature further. That is why the loop stalls short of setpoint.
  6. With integral action added, the 4 F error is still there, so the integral term keeps adding output. The extra output cools the air below 59 F, the error shrinks, and the loop settles when the error reaches 0 F, with the integral term supplying the 40% the load needs. We do not give a settling time, because it depends on your controller's integral setting.

What hunting looks like on a trend

Hunting is the loop cycling above and below setpoint, in Automated Logic's wording. Building Commissioning Association guidance describes an underdamped loop as one that crosses setpoint repeatedly or needs more than three cycles to settle, and hunting as continuous adjustment over hours or days that never holds setpoint. The same page warns that an overdamped loop is the opposite problem: it changes so slowly that it never reaches setpoint. It also says trend intervals of 1 to 2 minutes are suggested for seeing hunting, and that long intervals can hide poor control. See BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them for setting up trends.

Forest Rock's trend guide gives a quick read. A slow, sluggish approach with no oscillation suggests gain too low. A fast rise followed by persistent swinging that does not die out suggests gain too high. A loop that approaches setpoint and stalls just short of it suggests too little integral, or proportional-only control. Overshoot followed by a swing back below setpoint suggests too much integral. Plot the output next to the temperature: a valve or damper command swinging end to end is a stronger clue than the temperature alone.

Common causes of hunting, as the sources state them

  • Too much gain. Automated Logic lists excessive P or I gain, and ACHR News says too much proportional and integral together can cause a slow oscillation lasting 5 to 10 minutes or more.
  • Delay between output and response. Automated Logic says the longer the delay, the easier it is to go unstable. Schneider Electric's valve article says a controller that reacts faster than the process can begins chasing the temperature.
  • Oversized valves. That article says an oversized valve often operates near closed, so small actuator moves cause large flow changes. ACHR News says oversized or fast-opening valves and dampers make tuning harder. See Two-Way vs Three-Way Control Valves for valve authority.
  • Mechanical problems. Schneider Electric lists excessive deadband, backlash, hysteresis, sticking, linkage wear and degraded feedback as reasons an actuator cannot hold a steady intermediate position.
  • Changing conditions. Automated Logic notes a loop tuned on one day can go unstable when conditions change.

Wild output swings can also shorten actuator and valve life, according to ACHR News, and a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory paper calls hunting one of the most common faults in commercial buildings and links it to improper PID parameters and early equipment failure.

A cautious field approach

  1. Our own advice: look at the mechanical causes listed above first, because a sticking actuator is not something a gain change is documented to fix.
  2. Trend the loop at a short interval so you can see the problem.
  3. Change one setting at a time and let the loop settle before the next change; Automated Logic says to be patient.
  4. Schneider Electric's knowledge base article starts with proportional only, adjusting until the loop is stable, checks with a modest setpoint change, and only then adds integral. It also says that if proportional alone cannot stabilize the loop, adding integral or derivative will make it worse.
  5. Automated Logic says to err toward slow response rather than risk instability, and to tune near typical load.
  6. Building Commissioning Association guidance says to tune the primary loop first, then the loops that feed it, and to retest at different loads and occupancy modes, expecting several rounds. See BAS Commissioning: Checklists and Functional Testing.
  7. ACHR News says a newly tuned loop should be watched tracking load for a long period before it is left alone, and that tighter control carries more risk of oscillation. Our own habit: write down the old values before you change anything.

Field card: PID Control Loops and Hunting

Fix the hardware first, tune one setting at a time.

  • Loop parts: sensor, setpoint, controller, output, actuator.
  • Gain is output per degree; proportional band is the degrees for 0 to 100% output.
  • P-only control leaves an offset (droop); integral removes it.
  • Windup: integral piles up while the output cannot respond.
  • HVAC loops are mostly PI; derivative is rarely needed.
  • Hunting: continuous cycling above and below setpoint. Trend the output too.
  • Check sticking actuators, oversized valves and slow sensors before tuning.
  • Our own example: 10 F band, 40% load, offset 0.40 × 10 = 4 F.
