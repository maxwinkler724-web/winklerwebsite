Dry Cleaner HVAC: Perchloroethylene Vapor Rules, Vapor Barrier Rooms, Exhaust, Steam Boilers and Heat Loads

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dry cleaning shop is a small chemical plant, a steam plant and a retail counter in one storefront, often with neighbors or apartments next door. The HVAC job is to keep solvent vapor where it belongs, keep the shop pulling air in rather than pushing it out, and keep the pressing area livable. This lesson covers the rules and the airflow ideas, with one worked heat-load example.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Federal, state and local rules change, and this page is general education, not legal advice or an engineering design. The shop owner and the environmental agency decide what applies. We read the sources listed below in 2026.

What perc is and why the room matters

Perchloroethylene, also called perc or tetrachloroethylene, is the solvent in traditional dry cleaning. OSHA's perc guide for dry cleaners says breathing it can cause dizziness, drowsiness, headache and nausea, and that it irritates the eyes, skin and airways and may affect the nervous system, liver and kidneys. It also calls perc a potential occupational carcinogen (NIOSH), reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen (National Toxicology Program) and a probable human carcinogen (IARC). The same guide gives a relative vapor density of 5.8 (air is 1), so perc vapor is heavier than air and sinks to the floor. That matters when you place a return grille or a floor exhaust.

The exposure numbers, from the NIOSH Pocket Guide and OSHA's guide: the OSHA limit is 100 ppm as an eight-hour average, with a 200 ppm ceiling for five minutes in any three hours and a 300 ppm peak. NIOSH treats perc as a carcinogen, recommends keeping exposure as low as possible, and lists 150 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health. OSHA's guide also lists a lower, voluntary ACGIH value of 25 ppm. In other words, the legal limit is not a comfort target. Ventilation is how a shop stays well below it.

People who live above or near a dry cleaner can also be exposed, according to the Delaware Division of Public Health. That is the reason the federal rule treats buildings with homes differently.

The federal rule in plain terms

EPA's dry cleaning rule is the National Emission Standard for Hazardous Air Pollutants for perchloroethylene dry cleaning, 40 CFR Part 63 Subpart M, on eCFR. Three parts matter to an HVAC tech.

  • No perc machines in buildings with a residence. Section 63.322(o) requires eliminating perc emissions from machines in a building with a residence. Machines installed after December 21, 2005 had to meet it at startup, and the remaining older ones had to eliminate emissions after December 21, 2020. In practice, a perc machine in a mixed-use building with apartments is no longer an allowed arrangement. If you are asked to size ventilation for one, stop and ask what permit and what machine it is.
  • Vapor barrier room. Subpart M describes an enclosure built from perc-impermeable material such as glass, plexiglass, PVC, a 22-mil PVC sheet, sheet metal or metal foil composite board, with sealed joints except for makeup air, exhaust and the entry door. Its exhaust must be separate from the building's other ventilation, discharge outdoors, hold the room at negative pressure, and provide at least one air change per five minutes. It runs whenever the machine runs and during maintenance, and the door may stay open only while someone is going in or out. This is the version of the rule tied to the residential-building provisions. Check the current text for which provision applies to a given machine and building.
  • Machine controls. The rule also requires leak inspections (weekly for perceptible leaks, monthly for vapor leaks), repair of leaks within 24 hours, and a refrigerated condenser with its outlet at or below 45°F (7.2°C) on dry-to-dry machines. The condenser temperature is checked and logged weekly. A leak is defined as perc above 25 ppm.

These are federal minimums. State air permits can add more, as the next section shows.

What New Jersey adds

New Jersey's DEP offers a general permit, GP-012, for perc dry cleaners using 150 gallons or less of perc in 12 months. Its permitted equipment is a dry-to-dry machine with both a refrigerated condenser and a carbon adsorber, and it does not allow transfer machines or machines with only one of the two controls. The operating requirements repeat the 45°F condenser limit with weekly logs, a diverter valve, a closed machine door except during loading and unloading, weekly leak checks and covered waste containers. Records are kept five years. The NJDEP Small Business Assistance checklist adds that every dry cleaning machine needs an air permit whatever the solvent, and it tells operators to keep lint screens clean so condensers do not clog. That last item is squarely HVAC: a lint-choked condenser coil is a service call. We did not find the general permit page naming a vapor barrier room, so ask the DEP about a specific shop.

The refrigerated condenser is a refrigeration system

Inside a dry-to-dry machine, warm air carrying solvent vapor passes over a cold coil, the perc condenses to liquid and is recovered, and the air is recirculated. The coil must be cold enough: the rule's 45°F outlet is the check, and the federal text allows watching refrigerant high and low pressures against the maker's range instead. A tech sees the usual refrigeration faults: low charge, fouled condenser, a failed fan, a stuck diverter. Because the 45°F reading is a compliance number, fixing the unit quickly and telling the owner what the log should show is part of the job. Handle the refrigerant under EPA 608 rules as always.

Alternatives, in plain terms

A Massachusetts state fact sheet compares the options. Professional wet cleaning uses water, detergents and computer-controlled machines, is not flammable, and is generally the lowest-hazard option, though it struggles with some delicate items. Liquid carbon dioxide cleaning is not flammable but uses expensive high-pressure equipment, and CO2 at high concentration can displace air. High-flashpoint hydrocarbon solvents are the most widely used alternative; they are combustible, so the shop needs fire and explosion protection. The fact sheet says n-propyl bromide is marketed as a drop-in for perc but is not considered safer.

For ventilation this changes the question. Perc is a toxic-vapor problem. Hydrocarbon is a fire and explosion problem, so electrical classification and ignition sources come into play, and the design owner decides that, not the HVAC tech alone. Wet cleaning is mostly a heat and humidity problem. New Jersey's checklist notes an air permit is needed regardless of solvent.

General exhaust, local exhaust and staying negative

OSHA's guide separates two ideas. General ventilation, such as overhead fans, dilutes vapor and removes heat and humidity. Local exhaust captures vapor at the source, such as a hood at the machine door. In one OSHA case study, a simple hood over a dry-to-dry machine's door, exhausting only when the door was open, cut average full-shift exposure from 4.7 ppm to 3.0 ppm.

The airflow rule for the whole shop is simple: air should flow from clean spaces toward the machine. The vapor barrier room is the most negative space, the shop is negative to the tenant next door and to any apartments above, and makeup air comes from outdoors. A shop that is positive pushes perc-laden air through wall gaps, shared ceilings and shafts. That is the same pressure logic as in stack effect and door problems: in a multistory building, warm air rising in winter tends to pull air up through shafts, which can carry odors from the shop to the units above even when the shop fan seems fine. Two exhaust streams must stay separate: the barrier room exhaust must not tie into the building's other exhaust, and the dryer and lint exhaust in a laundry setup is handled as in commercial laundry rooms. For measuring and holding a small negative pressure, the ideas in negative-pressure isolation rooms carry over: a pressure indicator you can read, a closing door and a makeup path that does not short circuit.

Steam boiler and pressing heat

Presses, a steam-heated finishing area and the boiler itself release heat and humidity into the shop. The boiler needs combustion air and a proper flue (see boiler feed pumps and condensate receivers for the condensate side). NJ's general permit covers boilers and heaters totaling 3 million Btu/hour or less, so a small shop plant can sit under that permit while a bigger one may not. Failed steam traps and bare piping add heat and waste fuel; Energy Trust of Oregon describes two dry cleaners that replaced steam traps and added pipe insulation, with incentives covering the cost and savings estimated at ,700 and ,400 a year.

Worked example: our own pressing area

All inputs here are our own assumptions, not measured values. Real presses vary widely, so use the nameplate or the boiler maker's data on a job.

  1. Assumption: 6 steam presses and finishing units in the pressing area.
  2. Assumption: 15 lb/h of steam per press while in use.
  3. Total steam: 6 x 15 = 90 lb/h.
  4. Heat given up per pound: Armstrong's steam table lists latent heat of 945.3 Btu/lb at about 15 psig (970.3 at 0 psig, 880 at 100 psig). Many people round to 1,000; we use the table value.
  5. Heat released: 90 x 945.3 = 85,077 Btu/h.
  6. Assumption: half of that, 50 percent, ends up in the room (the rest leaves with garments, condensate and vents).
  7. Room heat: 85,077 x 0.5 = about 42,500 Btu/h.
  8. Tons: 42,500 / 12,000 Btu/h per ton = about 3.5 tons of cooling, before people, lights, the boiler jacket and the building envelope.

The same steam also adds moisture, so the cooling coil sees a latent load too. And because this shop is held negative, makeup air arrives hot and humid in summer. Exhausting hot air near the presses is cheaper than cooling it.

Quick check: the barrier room exhaust

Assumption: a 10 ft x 12 ft x 9 ft room is 1,080 cubic feet. One air change per five minutes is 1,080 / 5 = 216 cfm, the minimum under Subpart M. Build the exhaust and its makeup path so that rate is met when filters and duct runs are dirty.

Field card: Dry Cleaner HVAC

Perc vapor sinks, the shop stays negative, steam heats the room.

  • OSHA perc limit: 100 ppm 8-hour average; NIOSH calls it a potential carcinogen.
  • Subpart M: no perc emissions from machines in buildings with a residence.
  • Vapor barrier room: sealed, separate exhaust, negative, one air change per five minutes.
  • Condenser outlet at or below 45 degrees F, logged weekly.
  • Alternatives (wet cleaning, hydrocarbon) shift the hazard, not remove the need for ventilation and permits.
  • Pressing heat example: 6 presses, 15 lb/h each, 945 Btu/lb, 50 percent to room, about 3.5 tons (our assumptions).
