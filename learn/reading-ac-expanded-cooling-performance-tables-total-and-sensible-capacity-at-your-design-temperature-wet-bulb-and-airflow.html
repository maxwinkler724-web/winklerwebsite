Reading AC Expanded Cooling Performance Tables: Total and Sensible Capacity at Your Design Temperature, Wet Bulb and Airflow

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The nameplate says 3 tons. The table behind it says something more useful: how many Btu/h that outdoor unit and indoor coil will really move on a 92 F afternoon, at your airflow, and how much of it is dry-air cooling versus moisture removal. This lesson shows how to read that table before you order equipment.

Why nameplate tons describe one test point

One ton is 12,000 Btu/h by definition, so a 3-ton label means about 36,000 Btu/h. That figure is tied to a single rating condition. The DOE Building America measure guideline on air conditioner diagnostics describes the standard test point as 95 F outdoor dry bulb, 80 F indoor dry bulb and 67 F indoor wet bulb, and notes that these conditions are rare in field testing. Your job site is almost never at that point, so the label is a name for the size class, not a promise about your house.

The public rating directory for matched systems has a second limit. The Building America sizing strategy guideline points out that those certification listings show total capacity only, not sensible or latent capacity, and that their test conditions are not the same as the indoor design conditions used in a load calculation. It says plainly that the manufacturer's own performance data should be used to select equipment. Those data are the expanded performance tables in the maker's engineering literature (we cite them here only as a kind of document, not by name).

What is in an expanded cooling table

Most tables are a grid for one airflow at a time. Rows are outdoor air temperature entering the condenser, usually in 10 F steps. Columns are indoor wet bulb, often with indoor dry bulb as a sub-column. Each cell gives total capacity and sensible capacity, and many also list power draw. A new table starts for each airflow (for example 1,000, 1,200 and 1,400 CFM) and sometimes for each indoor coil or blower setting. Read the header first: the table says what airflow, what dry bulb and which indoor unit it applies to.

How each input moves the numbers

  • Outdoor temperature. Hotter outdoor air raises condensing temperature, so capacity falls as you move down the rows. The Building America sizing guideline stresses using the same outdoor design temperature for the load calculation and for the equipment selection.
  • Indoor wet bulb. A higher wet bulb means more moisture in the return air. The same guideline says higher wet bulb raises total capacity but lowers sensible capacity, leaving more room for latent (moisture) removal.
  • Indoor dry bulb. Warmer return air raises sensible capacity. Total capacity barely changes, so the extra goes to the sensible side. Our own arithmetic with the standard sensible relation (about 1.08 times CFM times the temperature change): at 1,200 CFM, each 1 F of extra entering dry bulb is worth roughly 1,300 Btu/h of sensible capacity. That is why a table sensible number at 80 F entering air cannot be used for a 75 F design.
  • Airflow. The sizing guideline says more airflow raises both total and sensible capacity while the latent capacity falls as CFM rises. The measure guideline summarizes a maker test on a typical 3-ton system: at about 250 CFM per ton, sensible capacity fell to 75 percent and total to 85 percent of the values at 400 CFM per ton, and it warns that very low airflow can ice the coil. Its design guidance is 350 to 400 CFM per ton, with 350 suggested for humid climates and 500 for hot-dry ones. For how airflow shifts moisture removal, see Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.

Check the load at the local design temperature

Take the design outdoor temperature and indoor conditions from your load calculation (see Heat Load Calculation Intro), not the rating point. The Building America sizing guideline shows the indoor wet bulb is read off a psychrometric chart from the indoor design dry bulb and humidity, using 75 F and 50 percent relative humidity in its examples, which lands near a 63 F wet bulb. It also covers the case where the design temperature falls between table rows: interpolation between the rows is acceptable, but extrapolating beyond the table is not. Its Florida example interpolated a 92 F design point, the same case we practice below.

Then compare two numbers, not one. Total capacity must at least cover the total load. Sensible capacity must at least cover the sensible load. The guideline's goal is to meet the peak load without grossly oversizing, because oversized equipment short-cycles (see AC Short Cycling).

Worked example (our own, invented table)

This table is invented for teaching. It is not any maker's data and must not be used to size a real system. Assumptions: a nominal 3-ton split system, 1,200 CFM across the coil, 75 F entering indoor dry bulb, capacities in thousands of Btu/h (MBh), shown as total / sensible. The load calculation gave 31,000 Btu/h total and 25,000 Btu/h sensible at a 92 F outdoor design with 75 F, 50 percent indoor air (wet bulb about 63 F).

Outdoor63 F WB67 F WB71 F WB
85 F35.6 / 31.038.0 / 27.240.4 / 22.8
95 F33.6 / 29.636.0 / 25.838.4 / 21.4
105 F31.6 / 28.234.0 / 24.436.4 / 20.0

Note the 95 F, 67 F cell: 36.0 total, matching the 36,000 Btu/h nameplate. Now read 92 F at 63 F wet bulb.

  1. Stay in the 63 F column. 92 F is 7 F above the 85 F row, in a 10 F step, so the fraction is 7 / 10 = 0.7.
  2. Total: 35.6 + 0.7 x (33.6 - 35.6) = 35.6 - 1.4 = 34.2 MBh.
  3. Sensible: 31.0 + 0.7 x (29.6 - 31.0) = 31.0 - 0.98 = 30.02, about 30.0 MBh.
  4. Compare. Total 34.2 versus 31.0 load is 34.2 / 31.0 = 1.10, so 10 percent extra. Sensible 30.0 versus 25.0 is 1.20, so 20 percent extra.
  5. Latent by subtraction: the unit gives 34.2 - 30.0 = 4.2 MBh, while the load needs 31.0 - 25.0 = 6.0 MBh.

What the answer says

On total and sensible capacity, this invented unit clears the load: a total margin of 3,200 Btu/h and a sensible margin of 5,000 Btu/h. That looks like a pass. But the latent side is short at the 63 F design wet bulb: 4.2 MBh available against 6.0 MBh needed. In real life the room humidity would settle above design until the return wet bulb rises and the coil removes more moisture. Read the 67 F column to see the trade. Interpolating at 92 F gives total 38.0 + 0.7 x (36.0 - 38.0) = 36.6 MBh and sensible 27.2 + 0.7 x (25.8 - 27.2) = 26.2 MBh, so latent is 36.6 - 26.2 = 10.4 MBh. Sensible is then only just above the 25.0 load, which is the same pattern in every row: wetter air gives more latent and less sensible.

Why sensible capacity matters in humid climates

A unit with a high sensible heat ratio (here 30.0 / 34.2, about 0.88, against a load ratio of 25.0 / 31.0, about 0.81) cools the air faster than it dries it. In a humid climate, the air feels clammy even though the thermostat is satisfied. The Building America measure guideline notes that a larger indoor coil improves efficiency but raises the sensible heat ratio, which may not suit humid climates. The sizing guideline's Florida example chose a lower airflow target for the same reason. Lower airflow lowers the ratio, though the measure guideline's numbers show how far you can push it before capacity and coil safety suffer.

Matching the indoor coil

A table is valid only for the outdoor unit and indoor coil combination it lists. The sizing guideline notes that the indoor equipment has to fit the coil dimensionally, and the measure guideline adds that some local codes require matched ratings listed in the public rating directory. Do not read the outdoor unit's row from one combination and assume a different coil gives the same result. Duct and blower capacity must also deliver the table airflow; see Duct Sizing Basics, and for field verification see Measuring Air-Side Cooling Capacity.

Field card: Reading AC Expanded Cooling Tables

Nameplate tons are one test point. Your design point is the one that counts.

  • Rating point: 95 F outdoor, 80 F and 67 F wet bulb indoors. Rare in the field.
  • Outdoor heat lowers capacity. Higher wet bulb raises total, lowers sensible.
  • More airflow raises total and sensible, less latent.
  • Use the load calculation's outdoor and indoor design conditions.
  • Interpolate between rows. Never extrapolate.
  • Check total, sensible and latent (total minus sensible) against the load.
  • Match the indoor coil and confirm the ducts can move the table airflow.
