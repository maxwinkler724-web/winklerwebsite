Fan Arrays and Fan Walls in Air Handlers: Redundancy, Backdraft Dampers, EC Motors and Airflow Measurement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a newer air handler and you may not find one big belted blower. You may find a wall of small fans, each on its own motor, all pushing into the same plenum. When one stops, the others keep going. This lesson covers how that works, what has to be in place when a fan fails, and how to read the airflow.

What a fan array is

A fan array, often called a fan wall, is a group of fans mounted side by side, each connected straight to its own motor shaft. Instead of blowing through a scroll housing, the fans pressurize the plenum around them. An NIH technical bulletin from June 2023 describes it this way and says the benefits are shorter air handling units, more redundancy and easier fan or motor replacement without shutting down the whole system. It also says NIH typically limits an array to eight fans, because more fans give diminishing returns in cost, redundancy and maintenance. The fans in an array should be in parallel with identical motors, speeds and curves. For fan types and curve shapes, see Centrifugal Fan Types and Fan Curves.

Why makers use them instead of one big fan

  • Footprint. Trane's motorized impeller array brochure says its array can ship as short as 30 inches in length and fit through tight access points, which it says can lower retrofit cost.
  • No belts. Greenheck's retrofit article says belt-driven single fans lose energy in the drive and need frequent realignment and inspection, and that its EC fan arrays have no belts, pulleys, bearings or VFDs.
  • Redundancy. If one fan fails, the rest are meant to carry the load. Trane's brochure says the remaining fans continue to deliver the required airflow.
  • Easier swaps. A Continental Fan guide points out that a big fan may not fit through the doors and may need a crane, while a small array fan can be carried in.

Redundancy is not automatic. The NIH bulletin says the array must still meet design airflow with one fan out, and that the project decides how much redundancy is required. It also warns that if the remaining-fan operating point falls in an unstable region of the fan curve, the array risks uneven airflow and pressure, extra noise and mechanical damage. Greenheck's parallel-fan guidance, reprinted by ACHR News, gives a rule of thumb: the operating point with all fans running should be no higher than the lowest pressure in the curve's dip.

What happens when one fan fails

The plenum is at higher pressure than the air entering the unit. A dead fan is an open hole between the two. Without something to stop it, air from the running fans can flow backward through the idle fan, so the array wastes part of its output. The ACHR News article says parallel fans need isolation dampers so air from a running fan cannot flow back through an idle one, and that a stopped fan can windmill. Stopping and restarting a windmilling fan causes a shock the article calls harmful to the fan, motor and system.

The NIH bulletin says each fan needs an isolation damper that prevents backward rotation at startup and closes when a fan fails or is serviced. The damper can close by gravity or by a linkage driven by an actuator. Makers test such dampers to ANSI/AMCA Standard 500-D for leakage, pressure drop, closure and torque (we name the standard in text only). Trane says backdraft dampers are available for all its array combinations. Where a damper is not fitted, a Continental Fan guide advises that a failed fan's inlet be covered to prevent backflow until the problem is fixed. That is the blank-off idea: a temporary plate over the dead fan.

EC motors versus one VFD on several motors

Two control approaches show up. An EC fan has the motor, impeller and electronics packaged together, so each fan controls itself. Trane's brochure describes its array as an electronically commutated motor array with motor, fan and inverter packaged together, wired back to a single control box.

The other approach is a conventional AC motor on each fan driven from a VFD. Here the NIH bulletin is strict for its labs: it requires a VFD for each primary and standby motor and says a single VFD must not control multiple fans in an AHU. Not every job follows that rule. A Yaskawa article in Consulting-Specifying Engineer (2014) says one drive per motor is ideal but often costly in panel space and conduit, and that one drive can power several motors. The catch is overload protection. A VFD's built-in overload sees only one combined load, so each motor needs its own protection. KEB makes the same point: the drive measures the total current and cannot detect one motor overloading. Yaskawa compares three schemes. Overloads wired to a drive fault input take down the whole array on one trip. Overloads with contactors drop only the faulted motor. Manual motor protectors do the same with fewer parts, and the article cites NEC 430.32 for overload sizing.

If you service these, the practical point is that a tripped motor protector looks like a failed fan to the airflow, even when the fan and motor are fine. Check the protector before the motor.

Worked example: six fans, one fails (Our own)

Assumptions, all ours: six identical fans share a plenum. At full speed each moves 5,000 cfm at 2.0 in. wg, so the array delivers 30,000 cfm at 2.0 in. wg. The ductwork, coils and filters do not change, so system resistance is unchanged and the unit still needs 30,000 cfm at 2.0 in. wg. Backdraft dampers work perfectly, so no air leaks back. Each fan's curve near its rated point is a straight line that drops 0.0002 in. wg for every extra cfm (a 1.0 in. wg drop over 5,000 cfm). Real curves differ, so use the maker's data on a real job.

  1. Five fans must now share 30,000 cfm: 30,000 / 5 = 6,000 cfm each. That is 20 percent more flow per fan.
  2. The shortcut of speeding each fan up 20 percent is wrong here. The fan laws (see Fan Laws) say pressure rises with the ratio squared, so 2.0 x 1.22 = 2.88 in. wg, but the system only needs 2.0 in. wg at 6,000 cfm per fan. Following the shortcut also gives power of 1.23 = 1.73 times per fan.
  3. Apply the laws to the curve instead. At speed ratio r, a point on the old curve at flow q and pressure P moves to flow rq and pressure r2P. Our line is P = 3.0 - 0.0002q at the old speed. At the new speed the curve is P = 3.0r2 - 0.0002rq.
  4. Set q = 6,000 cfm and P = 2.0: 3.0r2 - 1.2r - 2.0 = 0. The positive root is r = (1.2 + 5.04) / 6 = 1.04.
  5. Each remaining fan speeds up about 4 percent.
  6. Power: air power is flow times pressure. Per fan it goes from 5,000 x 2.0 = 10,000 to 6,000 x 2.0 = 12,000 units, a 1.2 times increase. If efficiency stays the same (assumption), each fan draws about 20 percent more, and the array total is 5 x 1.2 / 6 = 1.0, about the same as before.

The lesson is that speed rises modestly, but each surviving motor carries a fifth more load, which matters if the motors were sized with little margin. Continental Fan's guide has a related example: four fans at 1,595 rpm meet 15,000 cfm at 2.75 in. wg, and if one fails, the other three go to their maximum of 1,800 rpm. Check the motor and rpm limit of each fan before you promise N-1.

Measuring array airflow

Most makers measure flow with the fan's inlet cone acting as a flow nozzle. Aerovent's piezometer ring flyer describes a ring at the cone throat and a static tap on the cone face, with a differential pressure sensor between them, and claims accuracy within plus or minus 5 percent in its lab tests. Continental Fan describes a constant-airflow setpoint as a differential pressure measured upstream of the fan and at the inlet cone, set by testing (its recommendation) or by calculation. Q-PAC's guide says its fan performance is calculated from the pressure drop across the inlet cone. In an array that means one reading per fan. The sources we read do not say how an air handler totals them, so ask the maker for the method and the factor each fan uses. Also note that a dead fan with a closed damper reads near zero, which is correct.

Sound and vibration

Continental Fan gives a rule that sound rises about 10 log(n) dBA for n identical fans, so nine fans add about 10 dBA to one, and says smaller impellers make noise at higher frequencies that are easier to attenuate. The NIH bulletin warns that poor selection causes noise and duct pressurization, that stacked fans need structural supports that limit vibration, and that the operating frequency must stay out of the isolator's resonant band. See Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors and Vibration Analysis Basics. A fan-wall unit also needs its speed command from the building controls; see VAV Supply Fan Static Pressure Reset.

Retrofitting into an old unit

Greenheck says old single fans can be hard to replace, since a like-for-like unit may be scarce and the old fan may have to be cut apart to get it out, while modular EC fans fit through standard doors and elevators. A Q-PAC guide cautions that array designs often need wiring to each motor, field assembly and fabrication, and may need significant disassembly to replace a motor or impeller. Greenheck also says evenly spaced fans pull air more uniformly across the coil and filter face than a single big fan, which peaks at the center. These are maker claims, so confirm the fit, the coil face velocity and the control wiring before you commit.

Field card: Fan Arrays and Fan Walls

Every fan needs a way to stop backflow, and every motor needs its own protection.

  • Array: several direct-drive fans, each on its own motor, all feeding one plenum.
  • A dead fan is a hole in the plenum. Closed backdraft dampers or a blank-off plate stop backflow.
  • Redundancy means the remaining fans can carry design airflow; confirm rpm and motor limits.
  • One VFD on several motors loses per-motor overload sensing. Add protection per motor.
  • Airflow readings come from each fan's inlet cone pressure drop.
  • Check a tripped motor protector before condemning a fan.
  • Our own example: six fans, one out, speed up about 4 percent, each fan about 20 percent more power.
