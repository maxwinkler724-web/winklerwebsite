Walk-In Defrost: Termination, Fan Delay and Icing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A walk-in freezer coil that has turned into a block of ice is usually a defrost problem before it is a refrigerant problem. Knowing the sequence tells you what to measure.

Why the coil ices

Frost forms because the evaporator runs colder than the air and the air carries moisture. Frost blocks airflow, so the box warms. A defrost system melts it on a schedule. When a freezer coil is a block of ice, ask a defrost question first: did defrost start, did the heat reach the frost, and did it end the right way? The parts are in Walk-In Cooler and Commercial Refrigeration Basics, and the frost basics are in Reach-In and Display Case Defrost, Gaskets, Drains and Coil Care.

The sequence in plain steps

An ACHR News article on electric defrost describes a common walk-in setup. A timer starts defrost, from once a day to several times a day. During defrost the compressor and evaporator fan stop and heaters on the coil warm it. Heaters are rated in watts per foot and are usually made for one specific coil, so the maker's part is the safer choice. Walk-ins are usually designed for one or two defrosts a day. Humid locations may need more, and the article advises adding one period at a time and watching the result, because extra defrosts do not help by themselves.

Termination: ending on temperature

Defrost most often ends on temperature. A sensing bulb sits high on the coil where frost clears last. When that spot warms, defrost ends early instead of running the full period. The article gives 60 degrees F as one typical example and says the setting depends on the design, so use the maker's value. Pressure and time can also end defrost, but time alone is less popular because the time needed swings with frost load. A second ACHR piece gives an example of a freezer defrosting four times a day for 40 minutes: if frost clears in about 10 minutes, ending early skips 30 minutes of idle, warming time.

The fail-safe

If the termination switch fails, the timer ends defrost anyway. The article says this fail-safe is usually 35 to 45 minutes, long enough for a normal defrost but short enough to avoid constant heater operation. A coil that always ends defrost at the fail-safe time is a clue that heat is not reaching the sensor, or the sensor is wrong.

Fan delay

After defrost the coil is warm and wet. A fan delay holds the evaporator fans off until the coil has cooled. The ACHR piece gives roughly 20 to 30 degrees F as an adjustable example. Without it, the fans start at once and blow warm, moist air over the product, and dense vapor reaches the compressor, which can mean high amps and an overload trip. A Copeland defrost-board sheet says that on freezers the fans stop when defrost starts and restart after a programmed delay, while on coolers the fans normally keep running. It mentions a drip time but gives no value. Fan delay is protection for the compressor and the food. It is not something to jumper.

Boards instead of clocks

Many units now use a controller board in place of a mechanical clock. The Copeland sheet says the termination signal comes from an evaporator-mounted temperature sensor, and the old termination switch is removed. Fan delay and defrost length are set in the controller, and the sheet asks you to power down the condensing unit before removing the clock, to wire the evaporator for wet locations, and to cycle power after changing certain switch settings. Settings live in that board's instructions, so this lesson gives none.

Working an iced coil

  • Lock out first. See Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Heaters are high energy and the coil can be hot.
  • Note where the ice is. A fully iced coil and ice only along the bottom point to different causes.
  • With power off and the heater isolated, measure each heater against the maker's data. An open heater leaves a patch of frost.
  • Check the termination sensor: position, mounting, wiring and whether it has been jumpered or moved.
  • Confirm the timer or controller actually starts defrost, and look for a frozen drain line or a dead drain heater.
  • Look for the moisture source: door left open, torn gasket, wet product.
  • Melt remaining ice by the maker's method. Do not chip or pry at fins or tubing.

Safety

Never bypass a termination control, fan delay or fail-safe to get a box cold. Check that the inside door release works before entering. Product that warmed during the fault is for the operator to evaluate. See Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return for the next checks once defrost is sound.

Field card: Walk-In Defrost: Termination, Fan Delay and Icing

Defrost has to start, heat the frost, and end on the right signal.

  • Frost forms because the coil is colder than the air. Defrost melts it on a timer or controller.
  • Termination is usually by coil temperature; the fail-safe timer (35 to 45 minutes is one cited example) is the backup.
  • Fan delay holds the fans off after defrost to protect the product and the compressor.
  • Iced coil: lock out, find where the ice is, test heaters off-circuit, check the termination sensor, timer and drain.
  • Never jumper a termination control, fan delay or fail-safe. Settings come from the maker.
  • Check the door release, and leave product decisions to the operator.
