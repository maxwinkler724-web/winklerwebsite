Pharmaceutical Warehouse HVAC: 68 to 77 F, Mapping, Hot Spots and Alarms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A distribution warehouse for medicines is mostly a big, tall box with rooftop units and unit heaters, but the owner has to prove the whole space stays inside a set band. This lesson covers the band, how temperature mapping finds the hot and cold spots, and what the HVAC tech should expect to hear about it.

The band and the excursions

Federal rules for wholesale drug distribution (21 CFR 205.50 on eCFR) say storage areas must be designed to provide adequate ventilation and temperature, that drugs be stored at appropriate temperatures and conditions following the label or the current USP/NF, and that recording equipment be used to document proper storage. The general warehousing rule for drug products (21 CFR 211.142) likewise calls for storage under appropriate temperature, humidity and light.

USP is a standard we name in plain text. A USP stimuli article on mean kinetic temperature restates the current definition: controlled room temperature (CRT) is 20 to 25 °C, with excursions permitted between 15 and 30 °C. In Fahrenheit that is 68 to 77 F, with excursions between 59 and 86 F (our own conversion). The same article says mean kinetic temperature (MKT) is used to evaluate short excursions above 25 °C, and that USP is proposing changes, so confirm the current text with the quality department. Quality staff, not the HVAC tech, judge an excursion from the logger record. Our job is to keep the space well inside the band.

Do not mix this up with vaccines. CDC lists refrigerator, freezer and ultra-cold ranges of 2 to 8 °C, -50 to -15 °C and -90 to -60 °C in its storage toolkit; those belong to cold units and their data loggers and to refrigerated warehouses. A room-temperature warehouse is a different problem: lots of volume and roof.

What a mapping study is

The WHO technical supplement on temperature mapping of storage areas (TRS 961 Annex 9, Supplement 8) describes mapping as documenting how temperature is distributed in a storage area. Key points from it:

  • When: before a new area is used; after significant changes such as a changed set point or refrigeration change; and when records show unexplained variation. It gives every three years as an example of periodic re-mapping.
  • Seasons: areas that follow the weather need at least two studies, one in the warmest season and one in the coldest.
  • Length: at least seven consecutive days for ambient warehouses, including five working days and two weekend days, so truck traffic and quiet days are both captured.
  • Empty and loaded: it asks for the air profile both empty and under normal loaded conditions.
  • Recording: loggers record every 1 to 15 minutes, with the same interval and start time on every unit, and calibration points that bracket the range with error no greater than 0.5 °C.
  • Duplicate equipment: where there are two units, map with each one running alone.

Where the loggers go

The supplement suggests a grid of loggers every 5 to 10 m (about 16 to 33 ft), and up to 20 to 30 m in very large facilities. It sets no fixed count. For ceilings 3.6 m (about 12 ft) or lower it uses three heights: floor, 1.2 m and 3.0 m. For taller buildings it asks for bottom, several middle and top positions; its example for a 6 m (about 20 ft) ceiling is 0.3, 1.8, 3.6 and 5.4 m. It does not set strict rules for corners, doors or air outlets, but it asks the team to record the outlets and fans during a site survey and to compare readings near and far from heating and cooling units. Teams commonly add loggers near doors and at pallet centers (our own note).

Hot spots: the roof and the dock

Hot air rises, so a tall box stratifies. A U.S. Department of Energy field report on warehouse heaters says warm air collects at the ceiling and can raise average ceiling air temperature 10 to 20 °F above the thermostat set point, and notes that the roof is typically the largest heat-transfer surface in a warehouse. That report is about winter heating, but the same layering happens in summer when a sun-heated roof puts heat into the air under the deck. Top-rack pallets sit in that layer. Dock doors are the other likely spot: an open door lets outside air into the bay, so we expect the widest swings near doors, and a mapping study is how that gets confirmed (our own expectation).

Fixes the mapping may lead to

  • Destratification: big slow fans or heater fans that push the layer down evens out vertical differences. See HVLS fans and destratification. The DOE report notes that direct-fired high-throw heaters spread heat more evenly but use more fan power, about 1.41 kW per unit versus 0.44 kW for the older unit heaters it replaced.
  • Unit heaters versus rooftop units: unit heaters cover winter only, while rooftop units can do both seasons. Our own rule of thumb: pick equipment that can hold the band in both seasons with one unit down. For radiant options see infrared tube heaters, which warm objects and floors rather than air, and for heated-air options see unit heaters in warehouses.
  • Door control: good seals, short open times and air curtains cut infiltration at the dock.

Alarms, redundancy and call-outs

CDC's vaccine toolkit lists useful features for a monitoring device: an out-of-range alarm, a detachable probe, a low-battery indicator, a display of current, minimum and maximum, accuracy of plus or minus 0.5 °C (1 F), and a programmable logging interval. It also says to keep at least one backup device, and to calibrate every two to three years or per the maker. Those rules are written for vaccine storage, but the same habits make sense in a room-temperature warehouse. CDC's excursion steps are a good model for any site: tell the responsible person right away, label and set aside affected product, document the event with readings and times, restore conditions, and ask the manufacturer or the right authority whether product is usable.

For call-outs we suggest (our own practice, not from a cited rule): set the first alarm well inside the band so there is time to act, send it to a person who can reach the site, and have a second level that goes to a manager. If the alarms run through a building automation system, see BAS alarms and trend logs. Test alarms after any controls work, and keep a plan for temporary heating or cooling. The WHO supplement says a power-failure test can show how long temperatures take to exceed limits.

Documenting HVAC changes

Because the mapping study is tied to how the building was set up, an HVAC change can invalidate it. The WHO supplement lists set point changes and refrigeration changes among reasons to re-map. Before swapping an RTU, moving a fan, changing a set point or re-balancing air, tell the quality team and write down what changed, the date, the old and new settings, and who signed off.

Worked example (our own assumptions)

Assumptions, not measured values: roof area 50,000 ft²; effective roof U-value 0.05 Btu/h·ft²·F (an R-20 type roof); effective outdoor-to-hot-layer temperature difference 50 F on a hot sunny day; rooftop units supply air 20 F cooler than the warehouse air.

  1. Roof heat gain: Q = U × A × ΔT = 0.05 × 50,000 × 50 = 125,000 Btu/h.
  2. Tons: 125,000 ÷ 12,000 = about 10.4 tons, just for the roof. Doors, lighting, forklifts, walls and outside air come on top of that.
  3. Air to carry it: cfm = Q ÷ (1.08 × ΔT) = 125,000 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 125,000 ÷ 21.6 = about 5,790 cfm.

Logger count. 50,000 ft² is about 4,645 m². With a 20 m grid (400 m² per position, within the WHO range for very large buildings) that is 4,645 ÷ 400 = about 12 plan positions. At three heights each, that is 36 loggers, before extras near doors and at pallet centers. With a 10 m grid (100 m² per position) it would be 46 positions and 138 loggers. The quality team picks the grid; the point is that the count climbs fast, so ask early.

Field card: Pharmaceutical Warehouse HVAC: 68 to 77 F, Mapping, Hot Spots and Alarms

Keep the whole box in band, not just the thermostat.

  • Controlled room temperature per USP: 20 to 25 C (68 to 77 F), excursions 15 to 30 C (59 to 86 F). Quality decides what an excursion means.
  • WHO mapping: at least 7 days, warm and cold season, empty and loaded, loggers at several heights.
  • Expect the hot spot under the roof and near dock doors; check loggers there first.
  • Destratification fans and good door control help; changes need a record and may need re-mapping.
  • Alarms need a person who can respond, and a backup plan.
