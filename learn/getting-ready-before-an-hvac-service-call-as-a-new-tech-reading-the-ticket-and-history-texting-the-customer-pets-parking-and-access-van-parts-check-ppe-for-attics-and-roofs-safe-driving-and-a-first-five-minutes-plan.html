Before the Service Call: A New Tech's Pre-Arrival Routine

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most of a good service call is decided before you knock. A few minutes with the ticket, a text to the customer, a quick look in the van and a plan for what you will wear and do first can save time, keep you safer and make you look like you know your job. This lesson gives a new tech a routine to follow.

Read the ticket and the equipment history

Start with what the customer reported and what the office wrote down. Then look for history on the same address or the same equipment: earlier visits, parts already replaced, readings someone recorded, notes about access or pets. A repeat complaint after a recent repair points you somewhere different than a first-time complaint does. If the ticket is thin, ask dispatch one or two questions before you leave rather than after you arrive. For how a good ticket is written in the first place, see the service ticket lesson; this page is about using one.

Write down, in our own shorthand, three guesses about likely causes and what each would need in parts and tools. They are guesses, not conclusions. You will test them on site, not assume them.

Contact the customer when you are on the way

The Federal labor statistics agency describes HVAC technicians as often working in customers' homes and offices, and says they must be friendly, polite and punctual. A short call or text when you are leaving is the easiest way to be punctual. Say who you are, that you are on the way, and your honest arrival estimate. Ask the questions that change how you work: Is there a pet that needs to be put in another room? Where should we park, and is there a gate code, a permit or a narrow driveway? Who will let us in, and where is the equipment (attic, closet, roof, crawl space)? Is anyone in the home who is ill or sleeping?

Do the call or text before you start the van or while parked, never while driving. More on that below. For pets, see the pets on house calls lesson.

Check the van against the likely job

Use your guesses from the ticket. For each one, ask: do we have the part, the filter size, the meter, the leads, the ladder and the access tools? A short look in the bins before you leave is faster than a supply trip in the middle of the job. Stocking and restocking are covered in the van inventory lesson, so here we only say: match the check to this ticket, not to a generic list.

Plan your PPE for the place, not the trade

The federal labor statistics agency notes that technicians often work in cramped spaces and in extreme temperatures, and that some technicians service equipment outdoors in all types of weather. The federal workplace safety agency's PPE page says protective equipment should address the hazards actually present, and that poor fit can leave a worker exposed. So choose gear for the location you are about to enter.

  • Attic. Plan for heat, dust and insulation fibers, low light and limited footing. Bring a headlamp, gloves, eye protection, a respirator if your employer's program calls for one, and a plan for water and breaks.
  • Roof. Plan for ladder setup, edge protection and weather. Check the roof surface and wind before you climb, and decide who else will know where you are.
  • Crawl space. Plan for tight movement, moisture, pests and poor air. Knee protection, a light and a way to tell someone you are going in are our own habits.

The federal workplace safety agency's heat guidance for new workers says almost half of heat-related deaths occur on a worker's first day on the job and over 70 percent occur in the first week, and it suggests a new worker start at about 20 percent of a normal heat exposure, adding 20 percent a day. Your employer decides how that applies to you, but the lesson is for you: if you are new or just back from time off, tell your lead before you head to a hot attic. Long hot days and tiredness are covered in the fatigue lesson.

Drive, do not read

The federal highway traffic safety agency says reading or sending a text takes your eyes off the road for 5 seconds, which at 55 mph is like driving the length of a football field with your eyes closed. It also says distracted driving claimed 3,208 lives in 2024. The federal workplace safety agency's motor vehicle safety page reports that on average 39 percent of all occupational fatalities are due to transportation incidents. A university workplace policy page recommends barring texting, email and handheld device use while driving for work, and cites a national safety group that would ban hands-free use too. State laws differ, so know the rule where you drive and your employer's rule.

Our own routine: read the ticket and history while the van is parked, send the on-the-way text before you pull out or once you have stopped, and let navigation give directions by voice only if your employer allows it. If dispatch calls with changes, pull over at a safe place and take them there. Fatigue makes this worse; see the fatigue lesson linked above.

Plan the first five minutes on site

  1. Minute 1: park and look. Park where you will not block anyone or the van's side door. Look at the outdoor unit, roofline, meter and any obvious hazards before you go to the door.
  2. Minute 2: greet and confirm. Introduce yourself, confirm the complaint in the customer's words, confirm the pets are secured and ask what has already been tried.
  3. Minute 3: walk to the equipment. Check the path, lighting and footing. Note the model and serial, and anything that does not look right.
  4. Minute 4: safe first look. Use your PPE, look before you touch, and check power and obvious safety conditions before panels come off.
  5. Minute 5: say the plan. Tell the customer what you will check first and about how long, and write the start time on the ticket.

This is our own sequence, not a rule. Adjust it for your employer's procedures.

Worked example (our own): prepping during the break

These numbers are our own assumptions, not measured data. A tech has four calls. Drive times between stops are 25, 20, 30 and 15 minutes, so total driving is 25 + 20 + 30 + 15 = 90 minutes. Reading the ticket, checking the van and PPE and planning the first five minutes takes 12 minutes if done on arrival at each site. If the same prep is done earlier at a desk, the on-arrival step shrinks to 4 minutes (a quick look and the plan). Doing that prep earlier takes 8 minutes per call, from a 45-minute break after call 2.

Without break prep. Every call spends 12 minutes on prep on arrival: 4 × 12 = 48 minutes on site. With break prep for calls 3 and 4. Calls 1 and 2 still take 12 minutes each, 2 × 12 = 24. Calls 3 and 4 take 4 minutes each, 2 × 4 = 8. Total on arrival is 24 + 8 = 32 minutes. The break holds 2 × 8 = 16 minutes of prep, leaving 45 − 16 = 29 minutes to eat and rest.

Time saved on site is 48 − 32 = 16 minutes, or 8 minutes on each of calls 3 and 4. Prepping in the van at the start of the day for calls 1 and 2 in the same way would save another 16 minutes. The value is also in the customer's wait and in the calmer start, not only in minutes. Compare your own prep times with the logging advice in the job times lesson.

Field card: Before the Service Call

Prep parked, text parked, drive focused, plan five minutes.

  • Read the ticket and equipment history; write down a few likely causes.
  • Text or call the customer: on the way, ETA, pets, parking, access.
  • Check the van for the parts, filters, meter and tools those causes need.
  • Pick PPE for the attic, roof or crawl space you will enter.
  • Never read tickets or text while driving; pull over for dispatch calls.
  • First five minutes: park, greet, walk to the unit, safe look, say the plan.
