Push-Pull Liquid Refrigerant Recovery: Setup, Limits and Finishing with Vapor

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Pulling a big charge out as vapor can take a long time. Push-pull recovery moves the liquid first, using the recovery machine to push it out of the system and into the cylinder. This lesson covers when that makes sense, how the hoses go, when it will not work, and why you always finish with ordinary vapor recovery.

Check the current rules

Check the current rules. This page was built from federal regulation text and trade articles read in 2026. It is general education. Your recovery machine's instructions, the cylinder markings and the current EPA rules come first.

What push-pull actually does

In normal vapor recovery the machine draws vapor out of the system, so the system pressure falls and the liquid has to boil off before it can leave. Push-pull works differently. A trade magazine article on the method says the machine pulls vapor from the recovery cylinder and sends it, at higher pressure, into the system. That pressure difference pushes liquid out of the system and into the cylinder. The same article calls it a two-step process: it moves the liquid fast, and then you switch to vapor recovery because push-pull "cannot be used to completely recover all the refrigerant."

When it makes sense

Two trade magazine articles agree on the basics. Push-pull is for larger charges, and both give a floor of about 10 pounds. One says to use it only when there is more than 10 pounds in the system. It also needs a place where a solid column of liquid can form and be isolated, such as a liquid receiver or a liquid line you can reach on a split or commercial system. A system with a receiver and service valves on the liquid line is the classic fit. If liquid is scattered through coils and you cannot tell where it sits, the method has nothing to push.

The hose and valve setup

The figure shows the connections described in those articles. Follow your machine's manual for the exact valve order.

  1. Connect the system's liquid side, through a sight glass, to the liquid valve of the recovery cylinder.
  2. Connect the cylinder's vapor valve to the recovery machine inlet.
  3. Connect the machine outlet to the system's vapor side.
  4. Put the cylinder on a scale and purge the hoses of air before you start.
  5. Run the machine and watch the sight glass and the scale. When liquid stops moving, the liquid stage is done.

One article adds that the cylinder should hold no more than about 5 pounds of refrigerant when you begin, so there is vapor space to work with, and that the sight glass must be rated for the refrigerant's pressure. Use only hoses and gauges rated for it too.

When it will not work

  • Small charges. Under about 10 pounds, vapor recovery is usually as quick and simpler.
  • Heat pumps and systems with a reversing valve. Both articles list these, because flow can reverse and you cannot isolate a liquid column.
  • An accumulator between the service ports you would use for liquid recovery.
  • No place for liquid to pool. If the piping cannot form a solid column of liquid, or you do not know where the liquid has migrated, there is nothing to push.

Finish with vapor recovery

Once no liquid shows in the sight glass and the scale stops rising, close the cylinder vapor valve and let the machine clear the lines as its manual directs. Then reconnect for ordinary vapor recovery and run until the machine shuts off or you reach the required level. Purge again before opening the system.

How low you have to go

The federal rule, 40 CFR 82.156, says to evacuate the refrigerant, including all the liquid, to the levels in its Table 1, using certified recovery equipment. EPA's service practice page says this applies to appliances with more than five pounds of charge. Values are inches of mercury vacuum and depend on the appliance's pressure category and full charge. For equipment made on or after November 15, 1993, the table lists:

  • High-pressure appliances with a full charge under 200 pounds: 0 inches.
  • High-pressure, 200 pounds or more: 10 inches.
  • Medium-pressure, under 200 pounds: 10 inches. Medium-pressure, 200 pounds or more: 15 inches.
  • Low-pressure: 25 mm Hg absolute.

Those levels are tied to when your recovery machine was made (older machines have different values), and the regulation defines which refrigerants fall in each category. Read the table itself on the job.

Cylinder fill limit: the 80 percent rule

A trade magazine article says not to fill a recovery cylinder to more than 80 percent of its total capacity. The federal cylinder rule, 49 CFR 173.304, gives no percentage for refrigerant: it says the liquid may not completely fill the container at 131 °F (55 °C). So the 80 percent figure is trade practice. It is a limit on liquid volume. To turn it into a weight on the scale, use the cylinder's marked water capacity and the refrigerant's liquid density.

Worked example

Assumptions (ours, for practice only): the cylinder is marked with a water capacity of 30 lb; the refrigerant's liquid specific gravity is 1.2 (a round number, roughly the heavy end for common HFCs; your refrigerant's safety data sheet gives the real value); the empty cylinder weighs 14 lb; it already holds 3 lb of the same refrigerant.

  1. Volume limit, in water-weight terms: 30 lb × 0.80 = 24 lb of water-equivalent volume.
  2. Convert to refrigerant weight: 24 × 1.2 = 28.8 lb maximum refrigerant.
  3. Maximum gross weight: 14 + 28.8 = 42.8 lb on the scale.
  4. Room left: 28.8 − 3 = 25.8 lb.

If the cylinder's own markings give a lower limit, use the lower one. Stop filling at the limit even if more refrigerant remains in the system, and use another cylinder.

Cylinder ratings, mixing and A2L compatibility

  • Ratings. The cylinder, hoses and sight glass must all handle the refrigerant's pressure. Check the stamped service pressure on the cylinder (our own advice) and follow the machine manufacturer's directions, which 40 CFR 82.156 requires you to do.
  • Never mix refrigerants. One cylinder, one refrigerant. A mixed cylinder may be unusable. Our cylinder lesson covers labels and identification.
  • A2L refrigerants. EPA says recovery equipment made on or after January 1, 2017 is certified either for non-flammable refrigerants (appendix B3) or for flammable ones (appendix B4). Do not assume a machine, cylinder or hose set is suitable for a mildly flammable refrigerant. Look for the machine's certification label and its manual's statement, and use cylinders and equipment marked for that refrigerant. See A2L and A3 refrigerants.

Also check that the machine has not been altered. The regulation treats altered equipment as uncertified if the change affects its ability to meet the standard.

Field card: Push-Pull Liquid Recovery

Liquid first, vapor to finish, scale always on.

  • Use push-pull for larger charges (about 10 lb or more) with reachable liquid.
  • Skip it for heat pumps, reversing valves, accumulators between ports and small charges.
  • Liquid side to cylinder liquid valve through a sight glass; cylinder vapor to machine inlet; machine outlet to system.
  • When liquid stops, switch to vapor recovery to reach the required vacuum.
  • Fill limit: trade practice is 80 percent; weight = water capacity × 0.80 × liquid specific gravity.
  • One refrigerant per cylinder. Match machine and cylinder to the refrigerant, flammables included.
