Infrared Thermography for HVAC and Electrical Gear: Emissivity, Delta-T and Safe Scanning

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An infrared camera lets you see heat on a panel, a motor or a duct without touching it. That is useful, but the picture is only as honest as the settings and the conditions behind it. This lesson covers what the camera really measures, how to read hot lugs on electrical gear, what published severity bands look like, and how to stay safe around energized equipment.

Check your own rules first

Check your own rules first. This page is general education built from public sources we read in 2026. Your employer, the equipment maker and the electrical safety rules where you work come first. Thermography on energized equipment is for qualified people only.

What the camera measures

Every object above absolute zero gives off infrared energy. The DOE Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) operations and maintenance guide describes thermography as making images of variations in the infrared radiance of surfaces. Testo's pocket guide adds that the camera is passive and non-contact and shows surface temperature only, so you cannot see inside an object. An enclosed lug behind a closed door is invisible to it. Fluke makes the same point: the camera cannot see through electrical cabinets or solid metal bus trays, so enclosures have to be opened where it is safe to do so (see the safety section below).

Emissivity and reflections

Emissivity is how well a surface gives off infrared energy. The camera needs a value for it to turn radiation into a temperature. Testo explains that emissivity and reflectance add up to about 1, so a low-emissivity surface mostly reflects whatever is around it. Its table lists polished copper at 0.03 and oxidized copper at 0.76, and black matte paint at 0.97, as guideline values only. In plain terms: shiny bare copper and bus bar can fool the camera badly, and painted or dirty surfaces read much better.

Two practical checks come from the sources. Reclamation's thermal analysis manual (FIST 4-13) says to walk around the target: if a hot spot goes away or fades when you move, it was probably a reflection. Testo describes measuring a reference spot with a contact thermometer or putting heat-resistant emission tape on the bare surface, then adjusting emissivity until the camera agrees. Testo's electrical guide also warns that glass or Plexiglas covers block accurate readings. For quick qualitative scans, Reclamation says a starting emissivity of 0.9 to 0.95 may be estimated, but it also warns to use emissivity tables sparingly and set the best value in the field.

Scanning electrical gear under load

Fluke explains that heat comes from current through a contact with high resistance, and that the heat from a loose connection rises as the square of the load. So a bad lug can look normal at light load. Testo's guide asks for at least 30 percent of nominal load and says to record the load in amps or as a percentage. Fluke cites NFPA 70B guidance of at least 40 percent load, with equipment warmed up to steady state. Reclamation says to wait at least 45 minutes after a load change and to consider wind, since moving air cools the part. Where the sources differ, follow your site procedure and the maker of your camera.

What you look for:

  • Loose or corroded connections. The FEMP guide says a loose or corroded connection results in abnormally elevated connection temperature. Fluke notes a fuse hot at only one end points to high resistance at that end.
  • Unbalanced phases. Fluke says equal load should give equal temperatures, but an unbalanced load, an overload, a bad connection or harmonics can all make a similar pattern, so you have to measure the actual current to know which. See Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance.
  • Overloaded conductors and breakers. Fluke recommends checking conductor ratings against the real load; the usual fix it names is redistributing loads.

What severity bands look like

Teams usually compare a suspect part with the ambient air (over ambient) or with a similar part under a similar load (over similar). Reclamation's manual reproduces a table based on the NETA acceptance testing specification, 2005 edition, and says that without consensus standards these values give reasonable guidelines. We have not seen the NETA document itself, so check the current NETA and NFPA 70B text your employer uses.

DifferenceMeaning in that table
1 to 10 C over ambient, or 1 to 3 C over similarPossible deficiency; monitor
11 to 20 C over ambient, or 4 to 15 C over similarProbable deficiency; investigate
21 to 40 C over ambientDeficiency; repair at next opportunity
Over 40 C over ambient, or over 15 C over similarMajor deficiency; repair immediately

Fluke states the same two immediate-action limits as 15 C (27 F) over similar parts and 40 C (72 F) over ambient. Reclamation also says the same rise is worse at 50 percent load than at 90 percent. Testo adds that its guide gives no numeric thresholds and says assessment criteria should be agreed in advance with the client.

Worked example (Our own arithmetic)

Three phase lugs on a disconnect read 98 F, 101 F and 131 F. Ambient is 77 F. We do not know the load, so treat this as a screening result.

Step 1, rise over ambient (Our own): L1 98 minus 77 is 21 F. L2 101 minus 77 is 24 F. L3 131 minus 77 is 54 F.

Step 2, rise over similar (Our own): average of the other two lugs is (98 + 101) divided by 2, which is 199 divided by 2, or 99.5 F. The hot lug is 131 minus 99.5, which is 31.5 F above its siblings.

Step 3, convert to C (Our own): multiply a temperature difference in F by 5/9. 21 F is 11.7 C, 24 F is 13.3 C, 54 F is 30.0 C, and 31.5 F is 17.5 C.

Step 4, compare with the table above (Our own): L3 at 30.0 C over ambient lands in the 21 to 40 C row (deficiency). L3 at 17.5 C over similar is above 15 C, which is the major deficiency row and also above Fluke's 27 F limit (31.5 F is larger). The table's two columns can disagree: L1 and L2 at 11.7 and 13.3 C over ambient fall in the over-ambient probable-deficiency row, but they are within 3 F of each other, which is why comparing similar parts is the more useful test. Because the load is unknown, we cannot say whether this is a loose lug or a heavily loaded phase. The next step is to measure the current on each leg and, with the equipment properly de-energized and verified, check the lug.

Arc flash and safety when panels open

Reclamation says arc flash and shock hazards apply, calls for a job hazard analysis before a survey, and recommends a "freeze and leave" habit: capture the image and leave the area. It also prefers labels, heat-sensitive paint or inspection ports over opening panels. Fluke's article on NFPA 70E says opening hinged doors or covers that expose bare energized conductors calls for arc flash PPE, while scanning from outside the restricted approach boundary without opening anything does not. Teledyne FLIR's article describes infrared windows as a way to collect data without opening the equipment. Testo says to observe safety distances, open cabinets carefully and use only a qualified electrician from the plant. None of this replaces your employer's electrical safety program. Read Arc Flash and Shock Safety for HVAC Techs and Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC, and see Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing for the de-energized side of motor checks.

Mechanical uses

  • Bearings and rotating equipment. The FEMP guide says rotating equipment problems normally cause a frictional change that shows as a temperature increase. Reclamation lists bearing bands over ambient: 10 to 24 C normal, 25 to 39 C probable deficiency, 40 to 69 C deficiency, 70 C or more major.
  • Motors. Reclamation lists unbalanced loads, shorted or open windings and blocked cooling passages among the things heat patterns can point to.
  • Steam traps. The FEMP steam trap assessment says infrared guns allow safe non-contact checks but calls temperature the least reliable of the three basic methods, because steam and condensate share a saturation temperature. A cold trap suggests flooding, and temperature should be paired with another method. See Steam Trap Testing.
  • Ducts. Fluke says thermal imaging can show whether ductwork is leaking conditioned air.

Documenting and trending

Testo asks for reports that name the object, date, imager and load, with a visual photo from the same angle as each thermal image. Fluke says to save every image, build a baseline, and re-scan after a repair to confirm it worked. Reclamation says to record observations and trend them over the equipment's life, and to issue a work order when a problem is found.

Field card: Infrared Thermography

Under load, compare like with like, and do not open a panel on a hunch.

  • The camera shows surface temperature only, and shiny bare metal reads wrong.
  • Confirm a hot spot is not a reflection by changing your viewing angle.
  • Scan at steady, meaningful load; record the amps.
  • Compare a part with a similar part, then with ambient.
  • A rise only tells you where to look; find the cause with a meter.
  • Opening a cover over bare energized parts is an arc flash task.
  • Photograph, log and trend every scan.
