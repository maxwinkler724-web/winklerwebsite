Home Improvement and Hardware Store HVAC: High-Bay Sales Floors, Garden Centers, Forklift CO and Big Entrances

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A home-improvement store looks like a big empty box, but it is really several different spaces under one roof: a tall racked sales floor, a paint counter, a lumber yard indoors, a garden center that is half outdoors, and forklifts running in the aisles. This lesson is about how those pieces change the HVAC job. It covers generic big-box and neighborhood hardware stores only.

Check your local rules

Check the authority having jurisdiction. Fire codes, mechanical codes and workplace rules change and differ by town. This page is general education built from federal workplace-safety pages and one university paper read in 2026. It is not design advice. Confirm distances, ventilation rates and equipment listings with the local code official and the building's design documents.

The tall sales floor and stratification

Warm air is lighter than cool air, so in a space with a high roof the warm air collects up near the ceiling. That layering is called stratification. In a store with racking that reaches well up toward the roof, the thermostat may sit at head height while the roof zone is much warmer. In winter the heating system then works to fill the whole volume while the people stay in the cooler layer at the bottom. In summer the same layering is partly helpful, because the heat sits above the shoppers.

A university paper from Oregon State, presented at a 2018 building-performance conference, puts it this way: removing stratification can reduce heating fuel cost, while cooling benefits from stratification, and ceiling fans are the main way to de-stratify. The same paper notes that there was little data on how long the fans have to run to keep a space mixed, so it builds a numerical model and checks it against a large retail distribution center and a small classroom. The page we read gives no savings percentages, so we do not quote any. Treat any savings number you are handed as unverified until you see its source.

Rooftop units and fans

Most stores of this type are served by many rooftop units (RTUs), each handling a zone. Zoning matters more here than in a small shop: the front end near the doors, the garden center, the paint desk and the back stock area each see different loads. When you walk a roof, note which unit serves which zone, where each outside-air intake sits, and whether any intake is near an exhaust, a dust collector outlet or the propane cage described below. Our own checkout habits are in RTU Startup and Checkout Basics.

A destratification fan hangs from the roof structure and pushes warm air from the ceiling zone down toward the floor. Look at the figure: the fan drives air down an aisle, that air spreads along the floor, rises again along the walls and rack faces, and returns to the ceiling zone to be pulled back toward the fan. Large slow fans and air-turnover units do similar jobs in different ways. See HVLS fans and destratification for the sprinkler and heat-recovery questions, and air turnover units for the heater-style alternative. One practical point for us: a fan can mix air only where the racking leaves a path. Full-height racking with no gaps can block the loop shown in the figure.

Entrances, dock doors and air curtains

Customer entrances open constantly, and the front vestibule of a big store moves a lot of air. An air curtain blows a flat stream of air down across the opening to slow the exchange of inside and outside air, as shown at the left of the figure. The jet must reach the floor and the unit must match the door width, or the stream breaks up. Air curtains over commercial doors covers sizing, door switches and code use. Loading dock doors are the same problem with a different load: when a dock door is open the building can lose or gain a large amount of air, so stores often tie dock-area units, door switches and pressure control together. Stores are normally run slightly positive so that unconditioned air does not leak in; the retail lesson linked below explains the pressure relationships to a mall.

Garden center

The garden center is where the building stops being a building. A canopy with open sides gets no real conditioning, and a glazed seasonal section behaves like a small greenhouse: strong sun load, humidity from watering, and its own heating and venting. Keep the equipment for these areas separate in your mind from the main RTUs. Greenhouse heating and ventilation covers unit heaters, exhaust fans, evaporative pads and the CO risk from fuel-burning heaters in such spaces.

Paint mixing and solvent vapors

A paint-mixing area releases vapors when cans are opened and tinted. We did not confirm a ventilation rate for it from a government or university page, so we give no number here. What we can say is where to look: read the product safety data sheet for the vapors involved, find out how the area is exhausted and whether its air is kept out of the shared return, and ask the code official or the original design documents what the exhaust and make-up air are supposed to be. Never add a recirculating fan or reroute ducts in that area without that information.

Lumber and wood dust

Cutting and sanding lumber puts wood particles in the air. OSHA's wood dust page says breathing wood particles from processes like sanding and cutting may cause allergic respiratory symptoms, other respiratory symptoms and cancer, while noting that the extent of these hazards and the wood types involved are not clearly established. The page also points to OSHA's combustible dust inspection program. For us the practical effects are loaded filters and dirty coils on nearby units, so ask whether a cutting station has its own dust collection and keep return air pickup away from it.

Propane cylinder exchange cages

Customer exchange cages are kept outdoors. OSHA's LP-gas standard says containers must be located outside of buildings, with listed exceptions, and that portable containers shall not be taken into buildings except as allowed under that rule (paragraphs 1910.110(b)(6)(i) and (b)(13)(iii)). The exact cage distances, fencing and signs come from the local fire code, which many towns base on the International Fire Code and the national LP-gas code. Our own advice: before you place or change a fresh-air intake, exhaust outlet or rooftop unit, ask the fire official how far it must be from the cage, and treat any ignition source near it as a question to ask first.

Forklifts, CO and battery charging

OSHA's powered industrial truck standard says carbon monoxide created by truck operations shall not exceed the levels in 29 CFR 1910.1000 (paragraph 1910.178(i)(1)). OSHA's table of limits lists a carbon monoxide limit of 50 ppm, also written as 55 mg/m3; the page does not label it as an 8-hour average but it follows the table's default of an 8-hour time-weighted average. The CDC's NIOSH lists a recommended limit of 35 ppm as a time-weighted average with a 200 ppm ceiling, and an immediately-dangerous level of 1,200 ppm. OSHA's forklift pages say internal combustion trucks can cause high carbon monoxide levels in enclosed work areas, advise against operating a combustion engine in a warehouse without adequate ventilation, note that cold weather can lead people to close doors and windows, and recommend CO monitors and signs, or battery-powered trucks where much of the work is in poorly ventilated spaces. Electric trucks have a different issue: the standard requires adequate ventilation for dispersal of fumes from gassing batteries in charging areas (1910.178(g)(2)).

Worked example: CO from propane forklifts (our own example)

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen for easy arithmetic, not a measured or published value.

  • Assumed sales-floor air volume: 100,000 ft2 times 20 ft = 2,000,000 ft3.
  • Assumed forklifts running at the same time: 3.
  • Assumed CO emission rate: 5 g/min each, so 3 × 5 = 15 g/min, or 15 × 60 = 900 g/h.
  • Assumed outdoor air change rate: 0.5 air changes per hour, with outdoor CO taken as zero and the air perfectly mixed.

Step 1, airflow. 2,000,000 ft3 × 0.5 = 1,000,000 ft3/h. Using 1 ft3 = 0.0283 m3, that is about 28,300 m3/h.

Step 2, steady-state concentration. Concentration = generation divided by airflow = 900 g/h ÷ 28,300 m3/h = 0.0318 g/m3, or about 31.8 mg/m3.

Step 3, convert to ppm. OSHA's table gives 50 ppm = 55 mg/m3, so 1 mg/m3 = 50 ÷ 55 = 0.909 ppm. Then 31.8 × 0.909 = about 29 ppm.

Compare. 29 ppm is below OSHA's 50 ppm limit and below NIOSH's 35 ppm recommended limit. Now halve the ventilation to 0.25 air changes per hour: the airflow halves, so the concentration doubles to about 58 ppm, which is above the 50 ppm limit. Two things follow. First, this is a room average; the air in a tight aisle with a running truck will be higher. Second, the room takes time to get there. The time constant is 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2 hours, so after 1 hour at 0.5 air changes per hour the rise is 29 × (1 − e−0.5) = 29 × 0.393 = about 11 ppm. Real emission rates vary with the engine, tune and load, so the useful lesson is the sensitivity: halve the outdoor air and the CO doubles. If you are asked why a store smells of exhaust in January, check whether outside-air dampers were closed down, whether exhaust fans are off and whether the dock doors are shut.

Field card: Home Improvement and Hardware Store HVAC

Tall box, many zones, and combustion engines in the aisles.

  • Warm air collects under a high roof; a destratification fan only mixes where racking leaves a path.
  • Stratification helps cooling and hurts heating, so check the season before you change fan settings.
  • Air curtains need the right width and a jet that reaches the floor; dock doors are a separate load.
  • Paint mixing and lumber cutting need their own exhaust or dust collection; get the design documents, not a guess.
  • Propane cylinders for exchange stay outdoors; ask the fire official for distances to intakes.
  • OSHA CO limit: 50 ppm. NIOSH recommended: 35 ppm average, 200 ppm ceiling, 1,200 ppm immediately dangerous.
  • Example: 3 assumed trucks at 5 g/min each in 2,000,000 ft3 at 0.5 air changes per hour gives about 29 ppm; at 0.25 it is about 58 ppm.
