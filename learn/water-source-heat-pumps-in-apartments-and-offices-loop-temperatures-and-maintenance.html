Water-Source Heat Pump Loops: Temperatures and Maintenance

Last updated: 2026-10-16

In many apartment buildings and offices, the unit in the ceiling or closet has no outdoor condenser. Instead it is wired into a shared loop of water that runs through the whole building. This lesson explains how that loop works, what temperatures to expect, what the unit needs from the water, and how to check a unit's water-side numbers.

The idea: one loop, many heat pumps

A water-source heat pump (WSHP) is a heat pump whose outdoor heat exchanger is a coil of water instead of air. Each apartment or office zone has its own unit. A unit that is cooling pushes heat into the loop water. A unit that is heating pulls heat out of it. A Trane engineering newsletter on the topic puts it this way: in cooling mode, the heat rejected to the water loop equals the heat removed from the space plus the heat of compression, and zones in cooling reject heat to the shared loop, which heating zones then use as their heat source.

That sharing is the point. On a mild day, some units cool the sunny side while others heat the shaded side, and heat moves around the building instead of being made or thrown away. Only when most units need the same thing does the loop need help. A boiler adds heat when the loop gets too cold. A cooling tower or fluid cooler removes heat when it gets too warm. Trane's newsletter says the boiler is sized for the heat the units extract and the tower for the heat they reject, with a diversity factor applied, and notes that ASHRAE 90.1 calls for a temperature deadband between starting heat addition and starting heat rejection so the boiler and tower do not fight each other.

Loop temperatures: what makers say

Different makers publish different normal bands, so read the manual for the unit in front of you. ClimateMaster's Tranquility 16 compact installation manual says water-loop (tower and boiler) systems are typically maintained between 60 and 90 degrees F. A Bosch installation manual for its units says the tower and boiler loop is usually maintained between 50 and 100 degrees F. Trane's newsletter describes a traditional WSHP system as adding heat at about 60 degrees F and rejecting heat at about 90 degrees F. These are design bands, not alarms. A loop reading 95 on a hot afternoon is not automatically a fault, but it does make every unit work harder.

The loop is a closed circuit in most buildings, so the water is treated and sealed. If a fluid cooler or an open tower is used, the water-quality problems in Condenser Water Treatment apply to that part of the system; Bosch's manual says chemical water treatment is mandatory for open cooling towers. The tower side is covered in Cooling Tower Fan VFD Staging and Condenser Water Reset.

Flow per ton and the hose kit

Each unit needs its own share of loop water. ClimateMaster's manual says water-loop flow is usually set between 2.25 and 3.5 gpm per ton, and that the maker recommends 3 gpm per ton for most water-loop applications. Units are often connected with a hose kit. ClimateMaster says kits come in several configurations and may include shut-off valves, pressure and temperature test plugs, braided hose, a Y strainer and balancing valves. ClimateMaster's Tranquility TR manual and Bosch both say to place strainers at the inlet of each circulating pump. The balancing valve is what lets a technician set the flow once the loop is flushed and the air is out. Never stretch or twist hoses; Bosch says not to use a hose with external wear or damage.

Worked example: heat of rejection from water readings

Water carries heat according to a simple rule: Btu/h = 500 x GPM x change in temperature (in degrees F). The 500 is not magic. A MEP Academy article derives it as 8.33 pounds per gallon, times a specific heat of 1 Btu per pound per degree F, times 60 minutes per hour, which is 499.8, rounded to 500.

Take a ClimateMaster TC-036, which the maker's cut sheet rates at 34,500 Btu/h of cooling with an 86 degree F water loop. Treat it as a 3-ton unit (our assumption from the 036 size in the model number) and set flow at the maker's suggested 3 gpm per ton:

  1. Flow = 3 tons x 3 gpm per ton = 9 GPM.
  2. A technician reads 86 degrees F entering water and 95.5 degrees F leaving water, so the change is 95.5 - 86 = 9.5 degrees F.
  3. Water-side heat = 500 x 9 x 9.5 = 42,750 Btu/h.

That is more than the 34,500 Btu/h cooling rating, and it should be. The water carries away the heat taken from the room and also the heat added by the compressor. The Trane newsletter says exactly that, and notes that product data sheets label the two values heat of rejection and heat of absorption. The gap here is 42,750 - 34,500 = 8,250 Btu/h. The maker's submittal data lists the heat of rejection for each model, so compare your number to that table, not only to the capacity rating.

Now see why flow matters. If the balancing valve is closed too far and flow drops to 6 GPM (2 gpm per ton) while the unit moves the same heat, the rise becomes 42,750 / (500 x 6) = 14.25 degrees F. ClimateMaster's service tables for its TRC units show a water temperature rise of 12 to 14 degrees F at 2 gpm per ton and 90 degree F entering water, so that rise would look normal at that lower flow. The point is that a temperature difference only means something when you know the flow. Less flow makes a bigger rise and a hotter refrigerant side, which leads to the high-pressure trips below.

Filters, coils and condensate

These are the jobs that keep most units running. ClimateMaster's TRC manual says to inspect filters monthly and replace as needed, never run without a filter, check the air coil yearly and brush or vacuum it if dirty, and check compressor and fan amp draw yearly. It also says to inspect the condensate drain twice a year to avoid plugging and overflow, treating with algaecide about every three months where slime forms. It says closed loops generally need no water-coil maintenance. Bosch gives a rule of thumb of changing filters about every 60 days in a house and as often as every two weeks in settings with heavy lint. Condensate lines should be pitched toward the drain (ClimateMaster specifies 1/8 inch per foot, and a trap at least 1-1/2 inches deep) and the drain cleaned annually. A full pan can lock out a unit: both makers list a condensate fault. Related reading on a similar through-wall system: PTAC Units.

Common faults and what trips them

ClimateMaster's TRC manual lists faults by code. A high-pressure fault points to reduced water or air flow, water or air temperature out of range, overcharge or a bad switch. A low-pressure or loss-of-charge fault points to low charge. A water-coil low-temperature (freeze) fault points to reduced water flow, inadequate antifreeze, a wrong limit setting, out-of-range water temperature or a bad thermistor. Bosch says periodic lockouts almost always come from air or water flow problems, and warns the freeze sensor may not protect against loss of flow in heating mode, so it recommends a flow switch. Bosch's default freeze limit is 30 degrees F, and a unit that hits it for 30 seconds goes into a soft lockout. On the pressure side, see AC High and Low Pressure Switches.

Notice the pattern: nearly every lockout begins with flow, filters or loop temperature. Check those before you suspect the refrigerant circuit. Heat pump basics are in Heat Pump Basics; a buried-loop cousin is in Geothermal Ground-Source Heat Pump Basics. Refrigerant work needs EPA 608 certification.

What tenants should report

Our own advice: report these to building management.

  • Water around or under the unit, or a gurgling or overflowing drain pan.
  • The unit stops after a short run and restarts later, or shows a fault light.
  • Weak airflow, or a filter you cannot reach or have not seen changed.
  • Cooling or heating that fades on very hot or cold days while neighbors are fine.
  • Unusual noise, ice on the unit, or a musty smell.

Tenants should not open water valves, hose kits or the electrical panel.

Field card: Water-Source Heat Pump Loops

Most lockouts start with flow, filters or loop temperature.

  • Units share one loop: cooling units dump heat in, heating units pull it out.
  • Boiler adds heat when the loop is cold; tower or fluid cooler rejects it when hot.
  • Typical bands: 60 to 90 F (ClimateMaster), 50 to 100 F (Bosch). Check the unit's manual.
  • Flow: about 3 gpm per ton recommended by ClimateMaster (range 2.25 to 3.5).
  • Btu/h = 500 x GPM x change in F.
  • Water-side heat is cooling capacity plus compressor heat.
  • Check filters, drain and strainer first; then flow, then refrigerant.
