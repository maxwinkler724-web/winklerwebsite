Pianos, Guitars and Wood Instruments at Home: Dry Winter Air, Target Humidity and Placement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A piano, guitar, violin or dulcimer is built from thin pieces of wood glued together under tension. Heating season quietly pulls moisture out of that wood. This lesson explains why winter air gets so dry, what humidity range conservation guidance recommends, where in a room to put an instrument, and how to add moisture without fogging your windows or growing mold.

Why dry air hurts wood instruments

Wood takes up and gives off moisture with the air around it. When it dries it shrinks, mostly across the grain, and when it takes moisture back it swells. The National Park Service guidance on wooden objects says wood loses moisture quickly below about 35 percent relative humidity (RH) and splits more easily, and below about 30 percent the glue can dry out, joints can loosen and finishes can turn brittle. It also notes that a rise from 30 to 70 percent RH can swell wood by about 2 percent across the grain, enough to grow a 2-foot panel by nearly half an inch. A Canadian government conservation page says damage comes mainly from fluctuations, that winter heating without humidification can pull indoor RH down to about 5 percent, and that musical instruments fall in its high-sensitivity group because stress concentrates at their joints and thin panels.

For a homeowner the symptoms are familiar: a hairline crack in a soundboard or guitar top, a seam that opens, a bridge or brace that lets go, fret ends that poke out at the edge of the neck. Tuning drift is the symptom people notice first. This part is our own reasoning, not a quote from a source: a soundboard that shrinks and swells changes the tension on the strings it carries, so the pitch wanders with the seasons even when nothing has cracked.

Why heating makes the air so dry

Relative humidity is the water vapor actually in the air divided by the most vapor the air could hold at that temperature. Warm air can hold much more. When cold outdoor air leaks in and is heated, the amount of vapor stays the same but the amount it could hold jumps, so RH falls. The worked example below puts numbers on this. Forced-air systems add to the problem by moving a lot of dry air past the wood, and by blowing it hard where a register aims at an instrument.

A target range to aim for

Museum guidance gives a good starting point. The National Park Service Museum Handbook sets most collection spaces at 45 to 55 percent RH, and the park service preventive conservation note recommends 40 to 55 percent for organic materials such as wood, with less than 5 points of change in 24 hours and less than 15 points of seasonal change. Its wood-objects appendix gives 50 percent plus or minus 5 as the general figure, accepts 35 to 40 percent in dry climates, and says to keep RH between 45 and 55 percent against fungi and never above 65 percent. Separately, the EPA says to keep indoor RH below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, to limit mold.

Our own working target: try to keep the instrument room between about 40 and 55 percent RH, and keep it steady. In the coldest weather, if the windows start to fog before you reach 40 percent, settle a little lower rather than push moisture into the walls. Steadiness matters as much as the number: a room that holds 38 percent all winter is kinder than one that swings between 20 and 50.

Where to put the instrument

  • Away from supply registers and radiators. The park service note lists nearby heat registers and radiators, along with direct sun, as heat sources that dry organic material, and the wood appendix describes a tabletop beside a radiator drying faster on its top surface and cupping. Moving a register is a job for the heating contractor; at minimum keep the instrument out of the airstream.
  • Away from sunny windows. The wood appendix calls sunlight through unshaded windows the most common light damage in historic buildings and lists shades, blinds and window filters as the fix. Sun also heats one side of a case and dries it unevenly.
  • Away from outside walls where you can. This is our own reasoning: an exterior wall is the coldest surface in winter, so the air next to it is cooler, nearer to saturation and more prone to swings and drafts than the middle of the room. An interior wall is steadier. For a piano that must sit against an exterior wall, pull it a few inches from the surface so air can move behind it.
  • Keep the room on its normal schedule. The wood appendix advises against turning heat or air conditioning off at night and against short bursts of heating, because the point is steady conditions.
  • Cases help. A closed case slows how fast an instrument follows the room. Small case humidifiers are a common idea for guitars, but we have no government source for them, so follow the instrument maker's own care guidance.

Whole-house or room humidifier?

A single room unit is the simplest way to protect one room. It needs regular cleaning and water, and it only helps while the door is closed. A whole-house unit on the furnace raises RH everywhere and follows a humidistat, but it should be sized and set for the house. See Whole-House Humidifiers for how bypass, fan-powered and steam types differ.

Over-humidifying is the real risk. Indoor air at 70 F can hold far more moisture than the cold outer wall of the house can handle. Moisture shows up first as fog or water on windows, then as damp in the wall cavity. The EPA says condensation on windows, walls or pipes is a sign of high humidity, that you should dry the surface quickly and remove the moisture source, and that RH below 60 percent is the mold-control ceiling. If the windows are running with water, turn the humidifier down. Condensation on Windows in Winter covers causes and fixes.

Measure it: hygrometers and loggers

You cannot manage humidity you do not measure. The EPA says a humidity meter costs about 10 to 50 dollars at many hardware stores. Put one in the room, near the instrument but out of the airstream and sun, not on an outside wall. Cheap meters can read several points off, so compare two side by side; Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes shows a salt test for checking one. A small data logger records the swings you would otherwise miss; a Smithsonian archives page says it uses digital data loggers and web-based software to follow humidity trends. Read the trend over a few days, not one afternoon.

Summer dampness

The same wood swells in humid weather. The Canadian conservation page puts the damp danger zone above 75 percent RH, and the park service appendix warns of mold, weaker glue and corroding hardware above 70 percent. Keep the room below the 60 percent EPA ceiling with air conditioning, a dehumidifier, or both. For a damp basement or lower level, Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage walks through it. In winter, do not let a dehumidifier undo your humidifier.

Worked example (our own): why the room reads 10 percent

Question: outdoor air is 20 F at 70 percent RH. It leaks in and is heated to 70 F with no added moisture. What is the indoor RH?

Method. RH equals actual vapor pressure divided by saturation vapor pressure at the air temperature. Heating does not change the actual vapor pressure, only the saturation value. The National Wildfire Coordinating Group (federal) atmospheric-moisture table lists saturation vapor pressure of water:

Air temperatureSaturation vapor pressure (inches of mercury)
20 F0.110
70 F0.739
  1. Actual vapor pressure outdoors = 0.70 × 0.110 = 0.077 in. Hg.
  2. Heated to 70 F, that same 0.077 in. Hg is still in the air (assumption: no moisture added or removed).
  3. Indoor RH = 0.077 ÷ 0.739 = 0.104, or about 10 percent.

Reading the answer. The bare arithmetic gives about 10 percent. Cooking, bathing and breathing add some moisture in a real house, so a home without a humidifier often lands a little higher, which is why we say roughly 10 to 15 percent. This is our own estimate under stated assumptions, not a measurement, and the 10 to 15 range is our judgment, not a cited figure. It is far below the 40 to 55 percent target and below the 30 percent where the wood appendix warns about glue. The outdoor 70 percent RH looks humid, but it is little help once the air is heated 50 degrees.

Field card: Wood instruments and indoor air

Steady, moderate and out of the airstream.

  • Target about 40 to 55 percent RH (park service range for organic material), steady day to day.
  • Below about 30 percent, wood splits more and glue and joints are at risk.
  • Keep instruments away from registers, radiators, sunny windows and cold outside walls.
  • Fog on windows means you are over-humidifying; the EPA mold-control ceiling is 60 percent.
  • Put a meter in the room; use a logger to see swings.
  • 20 F at 70 percent RH heated to 70 F gives about 10 percent RH (our own example).
