Gymnasium and Recreation Center HVAC: Tall Courts, Crowds, CO2 and Wood Floors

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gymnasium is a tall box that is nearly empty on a Tuesday morning and packed on Friday night. The air system has to handle both without heating the roof, chilling the players or cooking the wood floor. This lesson walks through the big ideas for gymnasiums, fieldhouses and community recreation centers with basketball and volleyball courts.

A tall room and a warm ceiling

Warm air is lighter than cool air, so in a room with a high ceiling it drifts upward and collects under the roof. A court room can be 25 feet tall or more, so the people on the floor sit in the cool layer while the heater burns fuel to keep the warm layer warm. The result is high heating bills and cold feet. The usual fix is a destratification fan: a slow, large fan that pushes the warm layer back down gently. The fan must not blow so hard that players feel a draft or that a volleyball curves in flight. Fans, sprinklers and lights all share the ceiling, so the installer checks clearances and fire-protection rules before choosing a spot. Our lesson on HVLS fans and destratification covers the details.

Heating choices: radiant, unit heaters or air handlers

  • Gas-fired radiant tube or panel heaters warm the floor and people directly instead of heating all the air. That suits a tall room, because less heat is wasted at the roof. They hang high, need clearance from combustibles, and need venting and combustion air as the maker specifies.
  • Gas unit heaters are simple and cheap to run in a mostly open room. They blow warmed air from a fixed spot, so throw and placement matter, and they add no fresh air by themselves.
  • Air handlers or rooftop units can heat, cool, filter and bring in outdoor air. That matters when a crowd arrives. Many gyms use a rooftop unit for ventilation and cooling and a radiant or unit heater for the cold-weather base load.

No choice is right everywhere. Radiant heat does not ventilate and an air handler alone may leave a stratified room. Follow the maker's installation instructions and local code for every gas appliance.

People are the big load, and they come and go

Players and spectators give off heat, and the number swings widely. A university design report gives an exercise-load table that lists basketball at 710 Btu/h sensible and 1,090 Btu/h latent per person, a modified version of a standard handbook table. Sensible heat warms the air. Latent heat is moisture from breathing and sweat, which the cooling coil has to remove. On a game night the crowd can be several times the load of an empty practice. A system sized only for practice will lag in the first period, and one sized only for the crowd will cycle and run inefficiently most of the week.

Outdoor air by occupancy

A state energy code ventilation table that we read lists three rows that matter here. A play area needs 0.30 cfm per square foot and no per-person rate. Spectator areas need 7.5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot, with a default density of 150 people per 1,000 square feet. A health club or aerobics room needs 20 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot. Which row applies to a given room is a design decision, so confirm it against the code your jurisdiction has adopted.

Demand-controlled ventilation with CO2 sensors

People breathe out CO2, and outdoor air is fairly steady at about 400 ppm. A federal demand-controlled ventilation guide says the difference between indoor and outdoor CO2 shows how much fresh air each person is getting: a 700 ppm difference (about 1,100 ppm indoors) matches roughly 15 cfm per person, and a 500 ppm difference matches roughly 20 cfm per person. The controls raise outdoor air as CO2 rises and let it fall when the room empties. The same guide says systems usually keep a minimum base rate while the building is occupied, often 20 to 30 percent of design, and that the idea fits spaces where occupancy varies and peaks high. A federal measurement laboratory report reviews the method and gives recommendations for applying it. It also says questions remain about energy savings and air quality effects, so do not treat a sensor as a replacement for judgment.

Sensor tips from the federal guide: keep sensors away from spots where people breathe directly on them, cover every zone, and check calibration against outdoor air during an unoccupied stretch. In a gym, add one more rule: put sensors where a basketball cannot hit them, or behind a guard. Our lesson on CO2 sensors in offices and meeting rooms shows the basics.

Wood floor humidity

A hardwood court floor takes on and gives off moisture as the room air changes. The A federal forest research laboratory wood handbook gives equilibrium moisture content at 70°F: about 6.2 percent at 30 percent relative humidity, 9.2 percent at 50 percent and 12.0 percent at 65 percent. It also says wood shrinks as it dries, which can cause gaps in flooring, and swells as it gains moisture. The handbook does not give a target humidity for a court floor, so we state none. As general practice, keep humidity steady within the band the floor maker or installer gives in writing, and avoid big swings, such as a heater that dries the room in January and a cooling-off period that lets humid air in during a wet summer. Dehumidification and humidification, if used, need their own controls and a trained tech.

Condensation on a cold roof

A roof deck can be well below indoor dew point on a winter night. A national laboratory paper abstract on roof condensation says clear-sky radiation can make a flat roof surface much colder than outdoor air, and that air leaking into the roof assembly carries moisture that condenses on cold surfaces. In a gym, a crowd adds moisture to the air, so drips on a cold roof show up after big events. Fixes are building-envelope and air-sealing jobs, plus ventilation that carries moisture out. Destratification can help a little by keeping the roof surface from sitting in a pool of cold, wet air, but it does not replace insulation and air sealing.

Ball guards, noise and setbacks

  • Ball guards. Supply and return grilles, thermostats, CO2 sensors and any low controls need guards or recessed mounting. A gym ball can break a sensor or bend a grille. Choose guards that do not choke airflow.
  • Noise. A hard-surface room is loud, and a loud fan or a roaring heater spoils a game and covers whistles. Check the maker's sound data, use slower and larger fans when possible, and keep supply velocities modest near seating.
  • Scheduling setbacks. When the court is empty overnight, set the temperature back and drop outdoor air to the minimum. Schedule recovery early enough that the floor and the room are ready before the first practice, and add an override button for after-hours events. A setback that goes too far can leave a cold floor and wet air.

Our own worked example

This is our own example, not a real building. We assume a gymnasium with a 6,000 square foot play area, a 2,000 square foot spectator area, 10 players on the court and 300 spectators.

  1. Player heat (sourced rate). 10 players × 710 Btu/h sensible = 7,100 Btu/h.
  2. Spectator heat (our assumption). We assume 250 Btu/h sensible for a seated spectator, which is our planning value and not sourced. 300 × 250 = 75,000 Btu/h.
  3. Total people sensible heat. 7,100 + 75,000 = 82,100 Btu/h. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h, so 82,100 ÷ 12,000 = about 6.8 tons. This leaves out latent heat, lights, sun and envelope loads.
  4. Outdoor air at the game (state table rates). Play area: 6,000 × 0.30 = 1,800 cfm. Spectators: 300 × 7.5 = 2,250 cfm, plus 2,000 × 0.06 = 120 cfm. Total: 1,800 + 2,250 + 120 = 4,170 cfm.
  5. Outdoor air at an empty practice. With no spectators, the people part drops out. Play area 1,800 cfm plus the spectator area rate of 120 cfm = 1,920 cfm.

Game-night outdoor air is 4,170 ÷ 1,920 = about 2.2 times the practice rate. CO2 control lets the unit swing between those two numbers without paying for 4,170 cfm all week.

Field card: Gymnasium and Recreation Center HVAC

Tall room, swinging crowd, wood floor, flying balls.

  • Warm air stratifies under a high roof. A slow destratification fan or radiant heat helps.
  • Radiant heaters warm people and floor. Unit heaters and air handlers heat the air. Follow maker and code rules for gas.
  • Game-night people heat and outdoor air can be about double an empty practice.
  • CO2 sensors let outdoor air follow the crowd. Keep sensors guarded and away from direct breath.
  • Keep wood floor humidity steady within the floor maker's written band.
  • Cold roofs sweat. Air sealing and ventilation matter more than fans.
  • Guard grilles and controls. Set back when empty, with early recovery and an override.
