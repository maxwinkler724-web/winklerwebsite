Flash Steam and Flash Tanks: Percent Flash From Condensate

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Hot condensate drops in pressure at a steam trap, and part of it boils right there. That is flash steam. It explains the white cloud at a return vent, it can be calculated with one short formula, and it can be put to work. This lesson walks the formula with real numbers.

What flash steam is

TLV describes flash steam as steam formed from hot condensate when its pressure drops, and says it is no different from normal steam. Condensate leaving a coil at 100 psig is water at about 338 °F. Water at that temperature can only stay liquid if the pressure holds it there. Let the pressure fall and the water is suddenly hotter than it can be at the new pressure. The extra heat has to go somewhere, so some of the water boils off and the rest cools to the new saturation temperature. Spirax Sarco puts the same idea this way: water at 170 °C boils at any pressure below 6.9 bar g.

The percent flash formula

TLV gives the formula as: percent flash = (hf1 − hf2) / hfg2. Here hf1 is the heat in a pound of saturated water at the higher (trap inlet) pressure, hf2 is the same at the lower (flash) pressure, and hfg2 is the latent heat of steam at the lower pressure. In plain words: the heat the water has to give up, divided by the heat it takes to turn one pound into steam at the low pressure. Multiply by the condensate flow to get pounds per hour of flash steam. TLV also notes that a trap built to hold condensate and cool it first will arrive with less heat than the saturated value, so the real flash is lower than the textbook number.

Worked example: 2,000 lb/h from a 100 psig coil

The coil discharges 2,000 lb/h of saturated condensate at 100 psig. Steam table values come from the Engineering ToolBox saturated steam table (imperial units). The table rounds to whole Btu/lb.

  • hf at 100 psig: 309 Btu/lb (338 °F)
  • hf at 5 psig: 195 Btu/lb (227 °F)
  • hfg at 5 psig: 960 Btu/lb
  • hf at 0 psig (atmospheric, 14.7 psia): 180 Btu/lb (212 °F)
  • hfg at 0 psig: 970 Btu/lb

Flash tank at 5 psig (our own arithmetic):

  1. Heat released: 309 − 195 = 114 Btu/lb.
  2. Percent flash: 114 / 960 = 0.1188, about 11.9 percent.
  3. Flash steam: 2,000 lb/h × 0.1188 = 237.5 lb/h.
  4. Leftover condensate: 2,000 − 237.5 = 1,762.5 lb/h.

As a cross-check, DOE Steam Tip Sheet 12 has a table of flash percentages. For 100 psig condensate flashed to 5 psig it lists 11.8 percent, which agrees with ours within table rounding.

Same condensate to a 0 psig atmospheric vent (our own arithmetic):

  1. Heat released: 309 − 180 = 129 Btu/lb.
  2. Percent flash: 129 / 970 = 0.1330, about 13.3 percent.
  3. Flash steam: 2,000 × 0.1330 = 266 lb/h.
  4. Leftover condensate: 2,000 − 266 = 1,734 lb/h.

Dropping to atmosphere flashes about 29 lb/h more than the 5 psig tank, and all of it is steam you can see at the vent. The lower the flash pressure, the more flashes, because there is more heat to shed. The DOE vent condenser tip sheet says flash steam typically holds roughly 10 to 40 percent of the condensate's original energy, and for the vented case that energy is simply lost.

Why a return line looks like it is blowing live steam

TLV notes that a steam cloud at a vent is usually flash steam, not a leaking trap, and that its volume can be more than 1,500 times that of the condensate it came from. A small mass of steam makes a big plume. A thermostatic trap that is working can still show a cloud at the end of the line. Spirax Sarco adds that flash steam and boiler steam cannot be told apart once they mix. So a cloud alone does not condemn a trap. See Steam Trap Testing Methods for how to tell the two apart, and Steam Traps Explained for what a trap does.

Flash tanks, vents and relief

A flash tank is a pressure vessel that separates condensate from flash steam. A National Institutes of Health technical bulletin says the percent that flashes comes from the condensate line pressure, the load in pounds per hour, and the tank design pressure, and that vertical tanks separate better. It gives sizing figures from its own design manual: inside diameter large enough to slow the steam to about 10 ft/s so liquid does not carry over, and an outlet sized for about 60 ft/s. It calls for an air vent, relief valves piped to a safe location above the roof line, and a back-pressure regulator set a few psi above the system design pressure on the steam outlet. It also says the low-pressure load should be able to use more steam than flashes, or the extra must go somewhere. Spirax Sarco describes the same idea as a surplussing valve that opens slightly above normal pressure, with a safety valve as backup. Treat all tank sizing as the maker's job; those numbers are examples, not a design rule.

Putting flash steam to work

DOE Steam Tip Sheet 12 says flashing high-pressure condensate can supply part of a low-pressure steam need at low cost, especially where returning the high-pressure condensate to the boiler is not practical. It adds that the condensate source should be fairly close to the low-pressure header. Its example flashes 5,000 lb/h of 150 psig condensate to 30 psig, giving 515 lb/h of 30 psig steam. Where there is no good use, DOE Tip Sheet 13 describes a vent condenser, a heat exchanger that recovers heat from the vent to warm makeup water.

Return line size and water hammer

Because flash steam takes far more room than water, return lines carry both liquid and a lot of vapor. The NIH bulletin warns that undersized lines build back pressure that can flood the trap, and that high steam velocity can push condensate along as waves that fill the pipe and damage it, which is water hammer. Yale's steam safety guide lists several kinds of steam water hammer and says that most steam system failures are caused by it. Pitching lines and low-point drains help; see Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics and Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits.

Safety

  • Condensate at 100 psig is 338 °F water, and a plume of flash steam hides how hot the spot is. Yale warns that 140 °F water can cause a third-degree burn in five seconds.
  • Do not open a return line, tank or fitting that may be under pressure. Isolate it and follow your site's lockout and tagout procedure, which the Yale guide calls for before work.
  • Pipe vents and reliefs away from people, as the NIH bulletin says for relief valves.
  • Wear the PPE your site requires; wet or wrong-rated gear can fail to protect.

Field card: Flash Steam and Flash Tanks

Pressure drops, water boils off, the rest cools.

  • Percent flash = (hf in − hf out) / hfg out.
  • 100 psig to 5 psig: 114 / 960 = 11.9 percent.
  • 2,000 lb/h gives 237.5 lb/h flash, 1,762.5 lb/h condensate left.
  • 100 psig to 0 psig: 129 / 970 = 13.3 percent, 266 lb/h.
  • A cloud at the vent is usually flash, not a failed trap.
  • Flash tank needs a vent, relief to a safe place, and a load that can use the steam.
  • Isolate and lock out before opening anything.
