Heat Loss From Slabs on Grade and Basement Walls: Perimeter F-Factor, Ground Temperature, Edge Insulation and Heating Loads

Last updated: 2026-11-03

A slab floor looks like a big uninsulated surface, so it is natural to assume it loses heat in proportion to its area. It does not. In winter the loss is concentrated where the slab meets cold outdoor conditions, along the edge, and basement walls lose most of their heat near the top. This lesson explains why, what a perimeter F-factor is, and how those numbers enter a heating load.

Why the edge does the damage

A Building America code brief on slab insulation says slabs lose energy mainly through heat conducted outward and through the perimeter of the slab. A national-lab study of slab floors in two test houses in Florida (the data are from a cooling-dominated climate, but the physics is the same) reports that losses are greatest at the slab edge and lowest at the center. A state document that reprints the energy standard's F-factor table says the bulk of slab loss goes to the perimeter, because that is where the slab is close to soil and air that swing the most in temperature.

Think of two paths. From the middle of the slab, heat has to travel a long way through soil to reach any cold surface, and the soil it passes through is deep and fairly steady. From the edge, the path to the cold ground surface and to outdoor air is only a few inches to a couple of feet. A short path with a big temperature difference carries a lot of heat. That is why the diagram below shows arrows crowded near the foundation and only small ones under the middle.

Why deep ground is steadier than air

Soil is heavy and conducts heat slowly, so the surface swings take a long time to reach down. A Building America basement guide says that in most regions ground temperatures several feet below grade stay near the average annual air temperature of the region, and that winter ground temperatures are often warmer than winter air temperatures. The Florida slab study measured 20 ft deep ground and found it varied only a few degrees through the year, with a much smaller swing than the surface and about one month behind it. The exact numbers there belong to Florida, so do not carry them to New Jersey. The direction is what matters: the deeper the soil, the smaller and later the swing.

So the temperature difference that drives loss through a below-grade surface is smaller than the indoor-to-outdoor-air difference, and it shrinks further with depth. The same guide says the top portion of a basement wall has the highest potential for heat loss, because it sits next to outdoor air and the first few feet of earth, which get much colder than the earth near the basement floor. A university housing research brief on basement walls makes the related point that wall temperature swings are smaller below grade because of the relatively constant soil temperature at depth.

What the F-factor is

A wall is rated by U-factor, which is heat flow per square foot. A slab edge is rated differently because the loss is tied to length, not area. The F-factor, as a federal efficiency program guide describes it, is the perimeter heat loss factor for slab-on-grade floors: an approximation of the total heat passing through the slab, expressed per unit length of slab perimeter. In the table we use, the unit is Btu/h per linear foot per degree F.

That gives a simple load term (our own arithmetic from those units):

Slab heat loss (Btu/h) = F × perimeter (ft) × (indoor temperature − design outdoor temperature)

Table values for an unheated slab, from the standard's F-factor table as reproduced in two government documents we read:

  • No edge insulation: 0.73 (the state document notes this assumes a soil conductivity of 0.75 Btu/h per linear ft per F).
  • R-10 vertical insulation, 12 in. deep: 0.58. At 24 in.: 0.54. At 36 in.: 0.51. At 48 in.: 0.48 (federal efficiency program guide, citing the 2019 edition of the standard).

Two lessons from the numbers. First, more depth helps, but with diminishing returns: 12 in. to 24 in. drops the factor by 0.04, and 24 in. to 48 in. drops it by 0.06 more. Second, placement matters. The state document lists 24-in. horizontal insulation as barely better than none, at about 0.69 for R-15, while 24-in. vertical R-15 insulation is 0.52. Vertical insulation sits right on the short, hot-to-cold path. One caution: the same document says that residential values for no insulation are higher, about 1.04, so a real load calculation must use the table its method names, not our example values.

Edge insulation in practice

A Building America brief on slab edges says exterior insulation lets the inside surface of the slab perimeter track indoor conditions more closely. In climates where the coldest month averages below 45 F, the perimeter can fall below the indoor dew point for long periods, which risks condensation, mold and dust mites. So edge insulation helps comfort and moisture control, not only energy. For a monolithic slab with a grade beam, insulation goes on the outside edge down to the bottom of the grade beam. For a slab that is independent of the foundation wall, it goes on the wall's exterior or between the wall and the slab, with a bond break. The same brief lists rigid foam, rigid fiberglass and rock wool as suitable for ground contact, and says to use insect control where insulation meets the ground.

Model codes set minimums by climate zone. A Building America code brief describing the 2015 IECC and IRC lists slab edge R-10 at 2 ft in zones 4 and 5, and R-10 at 4 ft in zones 6 to 8, with none in zones 1 to 3. A companion page summarizing the 2021 edition lists R-10 continuous to 4 ft in zones 4 and up, and says R-5 goes under the full area of a heated slab. Check which edition your jurisdiction has adopted, and which climate zone the building is in, before you rely on any table. See Radiant Floor Heating Basics for heated slabs.

Basement walls

Basement walls are treated as a wall with a temperature difference that changes by depth. The Building America basement guide says insulating only the below-grade part is not acceptable, since the top lost the most, and recommends full-height insulation. It also notes that exterior insulation is usually practical only in new construction and tends to leave the wall assembly drier, while interior insulation is often the only practical choice in existing homes. For basement walls the same guide tabulates 2009 IECC values from none in zones 1 and 2 to R-10 continuous or R-13 cavity in zones 4 and 5 and R-15 continuous or R-19 cavity in zones 6 to 8. The 2021 summary page lists newer values; use the adopted edition.

How this enters a heating load

In the national residential load method, a slab-on-grade floor is a perimeter term, in the form of F times perimeter times the temperature difference. Below-grade walls are listed separately from above-grade walls because the temperature difference outside them is smaller and changes with depth, so use the method's own tables for them. The pieces add up with the walls, ceiling, windows and air leakage. See Heat Loss Through Walls and Ceilings for the above-grade terms and Attic Insulation and Air Sealing for the ceiling side. Slabs also store heat; Thermal Mass in Homes covers that separate effect.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a source. Assumptions: a rectangular slab 40 ft by 30 ft; indoor 70 F; design outdoor 10 F; uninsulated edge F = 0.73 and insulated edge F = 0.54 Btu/h per ft per F (the table values above for no insulation and for R-10 vertical at 24 in.); steady conditions; no heated slab; floor covering ignored.

  1. Perimeter = 2 × (40 + 30) = 140 ft.
  2. Temperature difference = 70 − 10 = 60 F.
  3. Uninsulated edge: 0.73 × 140 = 102.2 Btu/h per F; × 60 = 6,132 Btu/h.
  4. Insulated edge: 0.54 × 140 = 75.6 Btu/h per F; × 60 = 4,536 Btu/h.
  5. Savings = 6,132 − 4,536 = 1,596 Btu/h, which is 1,596 ÷ 6,132 = 26% less (matching 0.54 ÷ 0.73 = 0.74).

Per foot of edge that is 43.8 Btu/h uninsulated versus 32.4 Btu/h insulated. Spread over the 1,200 sq ft floor, the average is 5.1 versus 3.8 Btu/h per sq ft, but that average is misleading, because the loss sits along the 140 ft perimeter. The savings lower the peak heating load by 1,596 Btu/h, and they also raise the slab edge surface temperature, which is the comfort and condensation benefit above.

Field card: slab and basement wall heat loss

Slab loss runs along the edge, not across the area.

  • Slab loss = F × perimeter × temperature difference, with F in Btu/h per ft per F.
  • Heat takes the short path from the slab edge to cold ground and air; the middle loses little.
  • Deep soil is steadier than air, so loss through a below-grade surface falls with depth.
  • The top of a basement wall loses the most; insulate full height.
  • Vertical edge insulation beats horizontal; more depth lowers F with diminishing returns.
  • Our example: 140 ft edge, 60 F difference, 0.73 to 0.54 saves 1,596 Btu/h (26%).
  • Use the F values and ground temperatures that your load method and adopted code name.
