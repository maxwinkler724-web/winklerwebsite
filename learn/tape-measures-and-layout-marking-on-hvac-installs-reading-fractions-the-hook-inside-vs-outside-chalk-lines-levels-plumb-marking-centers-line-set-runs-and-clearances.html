Tape Measures and Layout Marking on HVAC Installs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most install mistakes that cost a second trip start with a number written down wrong: a hole in the wrong stud bay, a hanger off center, a line set cut short. This lesson is about the plain habits that prevent them: reading the tape, trusting the hook, and marking where the work goes.

Reading fractions without guessing

A U.S. customary tape marks each inch in halves, quarters, eighths and usually sixteenths. The reliable way to read it is to count the small marks past the last whole inch and say them as sixteenths, then reduce. Seven marks past the inch is 7/16. Six marks is 6/16, which reduces to 3/8, as a federal military training manual chapter on measuring tools says to do: state the reading in inches and fractions, reduced to lowest terms. A federal education research document on measuring and layout tools does not teach fraction reading, so we are giving you our own method. Practice with a tape until you stop counting and just see the 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8 marks.

How close is close enough? A federal measurement laboratory page says a 6 ft tape sold for commercial use should be accurate to within 1/32 inch under a federal weights-and-measures handbook. Our own takeaway: the tape is better than your eye, so most error comes from how you hold it and write it down, not from the tape.

The hook and the true zero

The first inch of a tape is the reference for everything after it. The military training chapter says the hook is normally part of the first measured inch, and that on an outside measurement the hook locates the tape against the surface. If the hook is bent, or the rivets are worn, every reading is off by the same amount. Test your own tape: hook it on the edge of a board and read a mark, then butt the hook against the board end and read the same mark. If the numbers differ, learn your tape or use the 1-inch mark as a start and subtract 1 inch. A federal measurement blog post also notes you lose accuracy when you transfer a measurement from one tape to another, so if two people share a job, share one tape for the critical dimensions.

Inside versus outside

An outside measurement is across something: a board, a duct, a pad. Hook the edge and read at the far edge. An inside measurement is between two surfaces, like a closet or a stud bay. The military training chapter says you extend the tape between the surfaces, read where it enters the case, and add the case length, which is printed on the case. The same chapter says an extension-type rule is best for inside dimensions. Forgetting the add is the classic error: a closet that reads 30 on the tape is not 30 inches wide. Write "IN" or "OUT" next to every number so the next person knows what it means.

Measure twice, and measure from the same place

The old rule about measuring twice works best when the second measurement is different: measure from the other end, or from another reference. If both numbers agree, mark. If they disagree, find out why before you drill. Long runs need more care. The military training chapter says long flat tapes must be supported, because sag causes reading errors, and that you should keep the tape parallel to the surface and stretch out the slack. Do not pull a tape around a corner and read it as one run; measure each straight leg and add the legs.

Chalk lines, levels and plumb

A chalk line gives you one long straight mark, such as a hanger line along a joist or a pad edge. The federal research document says to fix the chalked cord at both layout points, pull the middle up like a bow, and let it snap down to leave a straight line. It also notes the chalk is a temporary mark that is easy to remove. A level line is horizontal and a plumb line is vertical; the same document says the bubble must sit between the two marks on the vial, and that you check a level on surfaces known to be level and plumb, since dropping it can ruin its accuracy. A pointed weight on a string hangs plumb when it is free to swing, which helps for a vertical line set or pipe drop. For a condensate drain, which must slope, take the slope from the equipment manual and measure it on the actual pipe instead of trusting the eye.

Marking centers for holes and hangers

Mark the center of a hole, not the edge. For a wall sleeve, find the center on the inside, then drill a small pilot through to the outside and measure from the same reference on both faces. For hanger spots on a strut or a duct, measure from the first support and mark each center with a short cross, so the spot is where the lines meet. Mark with a pencil or a fine marker, not a wide stripe, because a wide mark hides the error. Where a line set must be supported, a federal permitting checklist for heat pump space heating cites a model residential code requirement for refrigerant piping to be fastened to a permanent support within 6 feet of the condensing unit; check that your local rule is the same.

Duct and line set runs, and clearances

Duct and tubing are measured along the path, not in a straight line. A string or a flexible tape laid along the route, leg by leg, is better than a guess. Make a short sketch and write each leg beside it. Bends use extra length that a straight tape reading will not show, and a service loop is extra slack on purpose. Both are allowances, not tape readings, so write them separately and state what you assumed. For equipment, do not rely on memory for clearances. The federal checklist says outdoor equipment must be installed to the maker's instructions, that a permit package must show the location and length of the refrigerant lines, and it cites a model residential code for a 30 inch by 30 inch level working space on the service side. The manual may ask for more, so measure from the manual's reference point, such as the coil face or the cabinet, and read the notes. See the lessons below on the installation manual and the site survey.

Writing it down

Write the number, the unit, IN or OUT, and what it was measured from, for example "closet width, IN, wall to wall, floor level". Use feet and inches, not decimals, until the final arithmetic. Sketch the leg order. Photograph the sketch so it does not get lost in the truck. Read the numbers back to your partner aloud before cutting.

Worked example (our own): a line set route

This is our own example with stated assumptions. Assume a crew measured a route in four straight legs with a tape, using outside measurements along the path, with these readings: 4 ft 7 3/8 in, 6 ft 2 1/2 in, 3 ft 10 7/8 in and 5 ft 0 3/4 in. Assume the route has 4 bends and that the crew allows 2 1/2 in extra for each bend. Assume one service loop of 18 in. These allowances are our assumptions, not manufacturer numbers; use your own plan or the maker's text.

  1. Add the feet: 4 + 6 + 3 + 5 = 18 ft.
  2. Add the inches: 7 3/8 + 2 1/2 + 10 7/8 + 0 3/4. In eighths: 59/8 + 20/8 + 87/8 + 6/8 = 172/8 = 21 1/2 in.
  3. Combine: 18 ft + 21 1/2 in = 19 ft 9 1/2 in, which is 237 1/2 in (19 x 12 = 228, plus 9 1/2).
  4. Bend allowance: 4 x 2 1/2 in = 10 in.
  5. Add the service loop: 10 in + 18 in = 28 in of allowances.
  6. Total: 237 1/2 in + 28 in = 265 1/2 in.
  7. Convert to feet: 265.5 / 12 = 22.125 ft, or 22 ft 1 1/2 in.

So the estimated run is about 22 ft 1 1/2 in. Whether that length is allowed, and what it does to the charge, comes from the installation manual; see the line set length lesson linked below.

Field card: Tape Measures and Layout Marking on HVAC Installs

Same tape, same start, written down twice.

  • Read the small marks as sixteenths, then reduce.
  • Test the hook against a board edge and an end before you trust it.
  • Outside: hook the edge. Inside: read, then add the case length.
  • Write IN or OUT, the unit and the reference point next to every number.
  • Measure again from the other end or another reference.
  • Measure line set and duct leg by leg, then add bends and loops as stated allowances.
  • Take clearances from the manual, not memory.
