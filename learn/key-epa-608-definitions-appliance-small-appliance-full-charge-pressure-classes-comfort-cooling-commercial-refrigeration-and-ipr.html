Key EPA 608 Definitions: Appliance, Small Appliance, Pressure Classes and End-Use Categories

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Many EPA 608 exam questions are really vocabulary questions. Is this unit a small appliance? Which pressure class is it? Is that a recover, a recycle or a reclaim? This lesson walks through the definitions in the federal rule and sorts five invented machines with them.

Check the current rule text

Check the current rule text. The definitions below come from the Section 608 refrigerant management rule, 40 CFR 82.152, as we read it on the eCFR site, and from EPA web pages. Rules get amended, and the exam you sit may be built on a particular version. This page is general education, not legal advice. Short phrases in quotation marks are from the rule; everything else is our own wording.

Appliance and small appliance

The rule defines an appliance as "any device which contains and uses a class I or class II substance or substitute as a refrigerant" for household or commercial use. Each independent circuit in a multi-circuit system counts as its own appliance, which matters for leak-rate and charge questions.

A small appliance is narrower. As we read it, it is an appliance "fully manufactured, charged, and hermetically sealed in a factory" with five pounds or less of refrigerant. The rule lists home and commercial refrigerators and freezers, room air conditioners, dehumidifiers and drinking water coolers as examples. Two tests must both pass: the weight (5 lb or less) and the factory-sealed construction. A split system with a field-connected line set is not factory sealed, even if the charge is small, so it is not a small appliance under that wording. The rule also describes a process stub, a tube that gives access to the refrigerant in a small appliance or room air conditioner and can be resealed after service.

Full charge

Full charge is "the amount of refrigerant required for normal operating characteristics and conditions of the appliance." The rule lets you work it out from the manufacturer's specification, from calculations based on component and piping sizes, from measured additions or removals (including seasonal variances), or from the midpoint of an established range. It matters because leak-rate math compares the refrigerant added against the full charge. See our leak-rate lesson.

The four pressure classes

The rule sorts appliances by the saturation pressure of the refrigerant in them, using the liquid phase at 104 °F. These are the thresholds as stated in the text we read:

  • Low-pressure: liquid phase saturation pressure below 45 psia at 104 °F.
  • Medium-pressure: between 45 psia and 170 psia at 104 °F.
  • High-pressure: between 170 psia and 355 psia at 104 °F.
  • Very high-pressure: a refrigerant with a critical temperature below 104 °F, or a liquid phase saturation pressure above 355 psia at 104 °F.

Note the units are psia (absolute), not psig. The class follows the refrigerant in the machine, so you need a pressure-temperature chart or a reference table for the refrigerant to place an appliance. We do not list refrigerants here; use the current rule text and a reliable pressure table.

End-use categories

  • Comfort cooling: air-conditioning used to control heat or humidity in occupied facilities. The rule's examples include chillers, commercial split systems and packaged rooftop units.
  • Commercial refrigeration: refrigeration used in the retail food and cold storage warehouse sectors, such as supermarkets, restaurants and cold storage facilities.
  • Industrial process refrigeration (IPR): "complex customized appliances that are directly linked to the processes used in" sectors such as chemicals, pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals and manufacturing. Industrial ice machines, power-generation appliances and ice rinks are covered too. A dual-use appliance counts as IPR if 50 percent or more of its operating capacity is used for industrial process refrigeration.

These categories matter for exam questions about leak-repair triggers, which differ by category. We keep that detail out of this lesson; check the current rule text for the numbers.

Technician and the work verbs

A technician is a person who, while maintaining, servicing or repairing an appliance (MVACs excepted), could reasonably be expected to break the integrity of the refrigerant circuit and so release refrigerant. The definition also reaches certain disposal work, but not disposal of small appliances, MVACs or MVAC-like appliances. As we read it, jobs like painting or tightening bolts do not count.

  • Recover: "to remove refrigerant in any condition from an appliance and to store it in an external container." No testing or processing is required.
  • Recycle: clean recovered refrigerant for reuse, typically by oil separation and passes through filter-driers, by the same owner, without meeting full reclamation requirements.
  • Reclaim: reprocess recovered refrigerant to the specifications in appendix A of the subpart and verify it with the analytical methods there. See Refrigerant Recovery, Recycling and Reclaiming.
  • Disposal: the process leading to and including discharge or dumping of a discarded appliance onto land or water, and also taking it apart for discharge of parts, taking it apart for reuse of parts, or recycling it for scrap.

Memory hook: recover is moving it out, recycle is cleaning it on the spot, reclaim is bringing it back to a tested standard.

How the definitions connect to certification

EPA's certification page lists Type I for servicing small appliances, Type II for servicing or disposing of high- or very high-pressure appliances (not small appliances or MVACs), Type III for low-pressure appliances, and Universal for all types. It says an open-book core test cannot be used toward Universal. For the full breakdown see Type I, II and III Explained. EPA's refrigerant management page says anyone who buys refrigerant for stationary appliances or handles it must be Section 608 certified; see the sales restriction lesson. Medium-pressure appliances are defined in the rule, but the certification page we read does not name them in any type, so check the current rule text for where they fall.

Worked example (our own): sort five invented appliances

This is our own example. The machines are made up, and so are the refrigerants. We state each assumption because the pressure class depends on the refrigerant.

Assumptions: every unit is factory built unless noted; the 4 lb window AC is factory charged and sealed; the window AC, split heat pump, rooftop unit and freezer system use a refrigerant whose liquid saturation pressure at 104 °F sits between 170 and 355 psia (high-pressure band); the chiller uses a refrigerant below 45 psia at 104 °F. You would confirm both with a pressure table. The 2,000 lb freezer system is assumed to be directly linked to a manufacturing process.

  1. 4 lb window AC. 4 is 5 or less and it is factory sealed, so it is a small appliance. High-pressure band. Room air conditioning in occupied space is comfort cooling. Type I covers it (Universal also).
  2. 7 lb split heat pump. 7 is over 5, and the line set is field connected, so not a small appliance. High-pressure band. Comfort cooling. Type II.
  3. 30 lb rooftop unit. Over 5 lb, not a small appliance. High-pressure band. Packaged rooftop units are a named comfort cooling example. Type II.
  4. 300 lb low-pressure chiller. Not small. Low-pressure by our assumption. Chillers are a named comfort cooling example, assuming it cools an occupied building. Type III.
  5. 2,000 lb industrial freezer system. Not small. High-pressure band by assumption. Linked to a manufacturing process, so industrial process refrigeration; if it were a retail or warehouse cold store it would be commercial refrigeration instead. Type II.

The takeaway: weight alone does not make something a small appliance, the refrigerant sets the pressure class, and the job the machine does sets the end use.

Field card: Key EPA 608 definitions

Weight and seal, then pressure, then use.

  • Small appliance: factory made, charged and hermetically sealed, 5 lb or less.
  • Low: below 45 psia at 104 °F. Medium: 45 to 170. High: 170 to 355. Very high: above 355 or critical temperature below 104 °F.
  • Full charge is the amount for normal operation.
  • Comfort cooling, commercial refrigeration and industrial process refrigeration are separate end uses.
  • Recover is out, recycle is cleaned for reuse, reclaim meets the standard.
  • Check the current rule text before relying on any number.
