Hot-Water Heat in Older Apartment Buildings: Hot Top Floors, Cold Units and Riser Balancing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

In an older apartment building with one central boiler, it is common for one unit to be too hot while the unit two floors away is cold. Tenants then open windows to cool off, and the boiler keeps burning fuel. This lesson explains why that happens and what a technician or building super can do about it.

Check your local rules

General education only. Heat requirements, complaint procedures and tenant rights differ by place and by building type. Nothing here is legal advice. Follow the building owner's procedures, the equipment maker's instructions and the rules where you work.

Why heat is uneven

A federal energy department report on overheating in hot-water and steam-heated multifamily buildings looked at more than 100 apartments in 18 older buildings (the OSTI abstract is linked in Sources). It found overheating was significant with both steam and hot water. In the copy of the report we read, apartment averages were above 70°F in every building, and units inside the same building often differed by about 10°F. The same report described residents using the window as the thermostat.

The common causes it lists are plain: one zone for the whole building, no thermostat or valve at each apartment, old or damaged emitters, stuck zone valves and badly set balancing valves. A separate federal guide on balancing hydronic systems adds that in a two-pipe direct return layout the water path is longer to the far emitters, so they get less flow. In one of that guide's buildings, a unit on the first floor reached 83°F with its own thermostat set to 60°F, probably from heat rising from units above. So "top floors overheat" is a pattern, not a rule. The overheating report found no steady link between floor level and temperature in these low-rise buildings, so measure before you assume.

Risers and branches

A riser is the vertical pipe that feeds radiators on every floor. Branches run off it to each unit. The boiler pump pushes water to the risers by the easiest path. A riser that is short, large or close to the boiler takes more flow than a long, thin or far one. The near riser gets too much and its apartments overheat. The far riser is starved and its apartments stay cold.

Balancing means setting valves so each riser gets about its design flow. The balancing guide says to use planned calculations instead of trial and error when you adjust valves in a direct return layout. It also says a reverse return layout is self-balancing in theory but still needs balancing valves, because real resistances differ. Our direct and reverse return lesson, linked below, covers the piping side.

Worked example: trimming an overheated riser (our own example)

Everything here is our own assumed example, not data from any building. Heat from water is Btu/h = 500 x gpm x temperature drop. The 500 comes from the sourced 8.33 lb per gallon (the federal residential hydronic study states 8.33 as the constant converting gpm to pounds per minute) x 60 minutes x 1 Btu/lb·°F.

  1. Assumptions: a riser carries 6 gpm, supply water 170°F, return 150°F, room 70°F. We want to cut that riser's output by 20 percent.
  2. Heat now: drop = 170 - 150 = 20°F. Output = 500 x 6 x 20 = 60,000 Btu/h.
  3. Target: 60,000 x 0.80 = 48,000 Btu/h.
  4. The tempting shortcut: cut flow 20 percent to 4.8 gpm. That only gives 48,000 Btu/h if the drop stays at 20°F, and it will not stay there.
  5. Check with a simple emitter model (assumption): treat the radiators as one emitter whose output is a constant times (average water temperature minus room temperature). Now: average 160°F, so 90°F above room, constant = 60,000 / 90 = 666.7 Btu/h per °F.
  6. At 4.8 gpm with supply held at 170°F, solve 500 x 4.8 x (170 - R) = 666.7 x ((170 + R)/2 - 70). That gives return R = 145.6°F, drop 24.4°F, output 2,400 x 24.4 = about 58,500 Btu/h. The cut is only about 2.4 percent.
  7. Flow needed for a true 20 percent cut: 48,000 / 666.7 = 72°F above room, so average water 142°F, return 114°F, drop 56°F. Flow = 48,000 / (500 x 56) = 1.71 gpm. That is a flow cut of 71 percent, from 6 to about 1.7 gpm.
  8. Another lever: keep 6 gpm and lower the supply instead. Drop must be 48,000 / (500 x 6) = 16°F, average water 70 + 72 = 142°F, so supply 150°F and return 134°F. Check: 500 x 6 x 16 = 48,000 Btu/h.

Lesson: with supply temperature fixed, a modest flow trim barely changes output, and a big trim risks starving the riser, noise and air problems. Lowering supply temperature (reset) is usually the stronger lever. Real radiators are not perfectly linear, so treat these numbers as a method, trim in small steps and re-measure unit temperatures after each change.

Outdoor reset and night setback

Outdoor reset lowers boiler supply temperature as the weather warms, so mild days do not push 170°F water through every riser. Our outdoor reset lesson explains the curve. The overheating report notes that reset settings often do not match outdoor conditions and suggests tuning them per building. In one monitored building in the balancing guide, seven of eleven units sat above 75°F median temperature, which the authors read as a reset set too high. A reset controller can also use a low outdoor cutout; the guide mentions one set at 55°F.

Night setback lowers the target overnight. The overheating report says nighttime setback could add savings. A federal residential hydronic study cautions that setback only works if the system can recover in time: one house with a 7°F night setback could not recover within the daytime window. Large old risers have lots of water and iron to reheat, so check recovery time in your building before you trust a setback schedule. Cold-morning complaints often start here (our own note).

Tenants and windows

Overheated tenants open windows, and underheated ones use ovens, according to the balancing guide, which linked these habits to temperature swings of up to 48°F between units in one building. The overheating report also warns that window opening hides how much overheating exists, because the open window lowers the reading. Treat an open window in January as a symptom to log, not as a tenant mistake.

Thermostatic radiator valves

A thermostatic radiator valve throttles the flow through one radiator when the room is warm. That is the apartment-level control many older single-zone buildings lack. The overheating report says full savings likely need apartment or zone level control. Valves add resistance and change riser flow (our own note), so rebalance after installing them and make sure the pump can still deliver enough head. See the two-pipe versus series loop details in our TRV lesson.

Air in high risers

Air is lighter than water, so it collects at the top of risers and at the highest radiators (our own note). The balancing guide says air creates noise, contributes to balancing problems and can reduce heat transfer and circulator efficiency. It recommends purging with bleed valves at installation and annually, and using an air eliminator to remove microbubbles during operation. A top-floor unit that is cold while the floors below are hot is a classic air suspect, so check the vent before you adjust any balancing valve. See our air separator lesson below.

Circulator sizing and boiler cycling

A circulator is sized from two numbers: the flow needed and the head loss at that flow. The balancing guide says the pump must supply the required head at the design flow. Flow comes from the heat load: gpm = Btu/h / (500 x drop). For example, a 600,000 Btu/h building load with a 20°F drop needs 600,000 / (500 x 20) = 60 gpm (our own arithmetic). Oversizing tends to flood near risers (our own note). Variable-speed pumps hold pressure steady as TRVs close, the guide notes.

A boiler that is big for the load will cycle on mild days. The federal hydronic study reports severe short cycling in similar studies, with the boiler going to high fire, supply temperature rising quickly, and then shutting off. It found that thermal mass, input limits and reset can reduce cycling. A building super can watch for this by counting burner starts per day.

What a building super can log

These are our own suggestions for a simple daily log:

  • Outdoor temperature, boiler supply and return temperature, and pump status.
  • Burner starts or run hours, if the control shows them.
  • Temperature in a few sample apartments (top floor, bottom floor, far end), with the date and time.
  • Complaints with unit number, date, what the tenant said, and what was found (air, closed valve, bleed-through, window open).
  • Every valve or reset change you make, so you can undo it.

Our boiler rounds lesson in Related lessons has a full operator-style log. Log first, change one thing, then log again.

Heat complaints

A federal housing agency inspection standard for assisted housing, which we read, treats heat as a life-safety item in the heating season. It sets an indoor floor of 64°F, measured three feet above the floor and two feet from an exterior wall, and treats a heat source that is not working as urgent. It sets no maximum temperature. That is one agency's inspection yardstick, not a rule for your building. Take complaints in writing, respond fast to no-heat calls and keep records. Field card: Hot-Water Heat in Older Apartment Buildings

Measure first, change one thing, measure again.

  • Big unit-to-unit gaps usually mean flow and control, not just weather.
  • Heat (Btu/h) = 500 x gpm x temperature drop.
  • With supply fixed, a small flow trim barely cuts output; lower supply temperature (reset) instead.
  • Check the air vent before touching balancing valves on a cold top floor.
  • Open windows in winter mean overheating somewhere. Log it.
  • TRVs give apartment-level control; rebalance after adding them.
  • Log supply, return, outdoor temperature, burner starts and complaints.
