Hydronic Kick-Space Heaters and Fan Convectors: Toe-Kick Units in Kitchens and Baths

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A kitchen full of cabinets has no wall for baseboard, and a small bath may have none either. The usual answer on a hot-water system is a kick-space (toe-kick) heater: a small hot-water coil and a fan, tucked into the dead space under a cabinet. This lesson covers how they work, how the fan is controlled, how to pipe them, what they put out at different water temperatures, and the upkeep that keeps them quiet and warm.

Check the maker's manual

Check the maker's manual. The details below come from three manufacturer installation manuals for different kick-space models. Switch settings, flow limits and output tables differ by model, so the label and manual on the unit in front of you win over anything here.

Where they are used

Fin-tube baseboard needs a run of open wall. Kitchens and small baths often have no such wall, but they do have a few inches of space at the floor under a cabinet. A kick-space unit fits there and blows warm air across the floor. One maker's manual lists one-pipe, two-pipe and series-loop systems as suitable, with a note that the pressure and temperature drop of the loop must be tolerable. One maker says the unit is for circulating hot-water systems with at least 1 gpm expected flow, and not for steam.

How one works

Inside the housing there is a small copper-tube, aluminum-fin coil, a small fan and a switch. Room air is drawn through the coil, picks up heat from the hot water, and leaves through a grille at the toe-kick. The fan is what lets such a small coil put out useful heat, which is why these are sometimes called fan convectors. Figure 1 shows the parts.

How the fan is controlled

There are two jobs: do not blow cold air, and give the room a way to say when it wants heat.

  • Pipe-temperature (aquastat) switch. A bimetal or strap-on switch sits on the coil or supply pipe and only passes power to the fan when the water is hot. One manual sets it to close at 120 F (plus or minus 5 F) and open again at 105 F. A second keeps the fan off until water is above 115 F. A third cuts the fan at 90 F and restores it at 110 F, and says the fan may take several minutes to start for that reason. A cooler-water option (110 F to 95 F) is offered by the first maker.
  • Thermostat or zone call. The room thermostat or zone control calls for heat. One manual shows a thermostat that controls only the fan, which will not run unless the circulator is running, and an optional circulator switch that stops the blower the moment the circulator stops.
  • Speed switch. All three manuals offer a high/low (or MAX/MIN) speed switch. One says to run on low for best results and keep high for quick heat-up.

For the 24-volt zone call and end-switch side, see zone valve end switches and zone control relay panels.

Piping: series loop or its own zone

Putting the unit in a series loop is cheap, but it takes water that earlier emitters have already cooled, so output falls (the example below shows how much). One maker states a minimum expected flow of 1 gpm and says that adding a circulator and a line-voltage thermostat gives the unit its own zone. Its standard connection puts the return to the circulator inlet side. Another maker shows two-pipe reverse-return and one-pipe series piping diagrams. Our own take: for a big heat-loss room, or a unit at the end of a long series loop, a small separate zone is easier to balance. See heat emitters and output versus water temperature for why the last emitter in a series loop runs cooler.

Output, water temperature and fan speed

Every one of these units loses output as water cools. A rating is only meaningful at its stated entering water temperature, entering air temperature and flow. The manuals we read tabulate output from about 120 or 130 F up to 200 or 210 F, with different entering-air conditions (65 F in one, 68 F in another). One maker's low-speed rating factors range from 0.65 to 0.85 depending on model, so low fan speed gives noticeably less than the full table. Output does not fall in a straight line with temperature, so read the table, and interpolate between listed temperatures rather than extrapolating past them.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions (ours, not from any source): a kitchen with a design heat loss of 10,000 Btu/h and no other heat source; a 1 gpm flow; fan on high. Rating data are from the capacity table in one maker's manual for a mid-size kick-space model with a table of output by entering water temperature, at 1 gpm: 140 F = 4,050; 150 F = 4,655; 160 F = 5,265; 180 F = 6,330 Btu/h per unit. Check the table's entering-air condition in the real manual.

Step 1, units needed at each supply temperature. Divide load by unit output.

  • 180 F: 10,000 / 6,330 = 1.58, so 2 units (12,660 Btu/h).
  • 160 F: 10,000 / 5,265 = 1.90, so 2 units (10,530 Btu/h).
  • 140 F: 10,000 / 4,050 = 2.47, so 3 units (12,150 Btu/h). Two units would give only 8,100, or 81 percent of the load.

Going from 160 F to 140 F cut each unit's output by 4,050 / 5,265 = 0.77, a 23 percent loss.

Step 2, the minimum supply for two units on separate branches. Each unit must give 5,000. Between 150 F (4,655) and 160 F (5,265), the table rises 610 per 10 F. Needed above 150 F: (5,000 - 4,655) / 610 x 10 = 5.7 F. So about 156 F entering water at each unit.

Step 3, the same two units in series. Water gives up heat along the way. At 1 gpm the standard hydronic relation is Btu/h = 500 x gpm x temperature drop. With a 160 F supply, the first unit gives 5,265, so the water drops 5,265 / 500 = 10.5 F and the second unit sees 149.5 F. Its output: 4,050 + (9.5 / 10) x 605 = 4,625. Total: 5,265 + 4,625 = 9,890 Btu/h, which is about 1 percent short. Raising the supply to 162 F gives 5,384 plus 4,728 = 10,112, which meets the load.

Step 4, low fan speed. The same maker gives a low-speed factor of 0.75 for this model: 5,265 x 0.75 = 3,949 per unit, or 7,898 for two (79 percent of the load). Low speed suits mild days, not the design day.

Decision: two units on about 160 to 165 F supply water, either on a small zone or in series with a supply of 162 F or more. If the boiler reset or the condensing-boiler settings hold supply near 140 F, plan on three units or a different heat source. This is an illustration, not a design.

Air, noise and lint

  • Bleeding. One maker builds in a bleeder valve reached through the front grille and says an air-bound coil prevents fan operation. Another has a bleed valve on the coil. When the fan will not start, one manual's troubleshooting calls for hot water of 130 F or more in both supply and return, so check for air and flow before blaming the switch. See cold air in a hot-water system.
  • Lint and pet hair. Dust collects on the blades and the fins. One manual calls for inspection and cleaning once a year, more often in dusty conditions, with a vacuum and cleaning tool; another says to vacuum through the grille, especially where there is pet hair. Our own advice: shut the switch off and let the fan stop first. Fin care is covered in baseboard cleaning and bent fins.
  • Noise. None of the three manuals gives a sound rating. Our own experience: noise grows with lint on the blades, high speed and a loose grille. Try the low setting first.

Freeze risk on outside walls

None of the three manuals addresses freeze protection. The cabinet base on an outside wall is cold, and the small pipes behind the unit sit in that space. Water in the pipe moves only when the circulator runs, and the fan does nothing to protect pipes. Two university extension pages say to open cabinet doors under sinks so warm room air reaches the pipes, and one says to keep indoor temperatures from falling below 55 F. Our own rule: insulate pipes on the outside side, keep the zone live, and do not let the room go unheated in a cold snap. The same logic applies to the hot-water coil in an air handler; see hydro-air coils.

Field card: Kick-space heaters

Small coil plus fan. The fan runs only when the pipe is hot.

  • Fan switch closes on hot water (115 to 120 F in two manuals, 110 F on restart in a third) and opens lower.
  • Output falls about 23 percent when 160 F water drops to 140 F, in our worked example.
  • Series loops hand the last unit cooler water; a small zone avoids that.
  • Bleed air first. Air-bound coils stop the fan.
  • Vacuum lint and pet hair about once a year, more in dusty homes.
  • No manual we read covers freeze protection: keep the room heated.
