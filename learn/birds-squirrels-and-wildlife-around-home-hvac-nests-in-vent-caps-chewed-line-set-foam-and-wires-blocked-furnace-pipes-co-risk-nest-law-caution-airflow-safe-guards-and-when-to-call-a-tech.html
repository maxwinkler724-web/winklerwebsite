Wildlife and Home HVAC: Bird Nests in Vents, Chewed Line Sets and Wires

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The outside of a house is where an HVAC system meets the animals. Birds look for a warm, dry hole to nest in, and a vent cap is exactly that. Squirrels and other chewers find foam and wire interesting. This lesson covers what to look for, why a blocked furnace pipe is more than a comfort problem, what the wildlife rules say in plain words, and when to stop and call a technician.

Where animals show up

Look at every place air or exhaust leaves or enters the house through a small wall cap.

  • Clothes dryer cap. Warm, lint-lined and sheltered, so birds like it. See Clothes Dryer Vents for the fire side of a blocked dryer duct.
  • Bath fan and kitchen hood caps. Same story, and the flap often hangs open when a nest holds it. See Bathroom Exhaust Fans.
  • High-efficiency furnace and water heater pipes. Many newer furnaces use plastic pipes through the wall, one for exhaust and often one for intake. Both ends are small round openings in plain view. This is the one that matters most for safety.
  • The outdoor unit. Squirrels and other animals may chew the foam insulation on the refrigerant line set and the thin low-voltage control wires. A university extension guide says squirrels gnaw on wires and can damage wiring and insulation after getting into buildings. Chewing is not limited to attics.

Signs you can spot from the ground

  • No airflow at a cap. Hold a hand or a tissue near the dryer or fan cap while the appliance runs. A weak or absent breeze, or a flap that does not move, points to a blockage.
  • Twigs, grass, feathers or string poking out of or around a cap. Another university extension page describes house sparrow nests as messy piles of grass, string and other debris stuffed into small cavities.
  • Chewed or shredded foam on the line set, with bare copper showing. Bare cold copper sweats in summer and loses efficiency. See Refrigerant Line Set Insulation, UV Damage and Sweating Lines.
  • Frayed or cut thin wires near the unit, or a thermostat that goes blank or a system that will not start. Do not touch frayed wires near the unit. Call a technician.
  • Droppings, nesting material or gnaw marks around the unit pad, or a furnace that keeps shutting off.

Why a blocked furnace vent pipe matters

A gas or oil furnace sends combustion gases out through its vent pipe. Many modern furnaces have a pressure switch and a draft fan that check the exhaust path before and during burning. If a nest or debris plugs the pipe, the switch may refuse to prove airflow and the furnace shuts down or will not start. That is the safety system doing its job; see Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis and Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories. Do not bypass a switch to keep the heat on.

The bigger worry is carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell. The federal consumer product safety agency names blocked appliance vents and chimneys as a CO concern and tells homeowners to make sure vents are not blocked by debris. Any fuel-burning appliance with a plugged flue could let combustion gases into the house. A blocked intake pipe on a sealed-combustion furnace starves the burner of air instead, which also causes trouble.

CO alarms

That agency advises CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, ideally interconnected, and not in kitchens or directly above fuel-burning appliances. Its test button checks the circuitry, not the sensor accuracy, and the alarm has a replacement age in its instructions. If an alarm sounds, do not hunt for the cause. Go outside to fresh air, call 911 or the fire department, count heads, and do not go back in until responders say so. If it sounds again within 24 hours after you return, repeat these steps and have a qualified technician inspect all fuel-burning equipment. More in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Wildlife law caution

Plain words, not legal advice. The federal wildlife agency says most bird nests are protected under a federal migratory bird law, and that it is illegal to destroy a nest that has eggs or chicks in it, or young birds still depending on it. The agency says an empty, inactive nest is not covered the same way, and that permits for active nests are issued only in rare cases, usually where a nest causes a health or safety concern. Rules also vary by state and by species. Before you remove anything that looks like an active nest, check with your state wildlife agency. If a nest is plugging a furnace exhaust pipe, shut the furnace off at the thermostat and call a technician and your wildlife agency rather than guessing.

Keeping animals out without choking the airflow

  • Act before nesting season. The federal wildlife agency advises placing barriers and clearing old nests before birds return, and notes that old nests can be reused.
  • Dryer, bath fan and hood caps: use the cap's own flap or a cap built for that use. One university extension page suggests half-inch mesh screens on bathroom and exhaust vents but cautions that lint can build up and catch fire in dryer vents, so a plain screen on a dryer cap is a bad idea.
  • Furnace intake and exhaust pipes: follow the furnace maker's installation instructions for any terminal guard. Do not stuff a rag or fine mesh into a pipe. A guard has to be set off from the pipe end so air can still move.
  • Line set and wires: replace chewed foam, and consider a rigid cover over the line set. Trim branches that give squirrels a route to the unit, as a university extension guide suggests. Hardware cloth with half-inch holes is the mesh the extension guides use for vent openings.

Worked example: how much airflow area does a screen take?

Our own example, with assumptions. Assume a round 3 inch inside-diameter vent pipe and a screen over its end that is 50 percent open area (half the face is wire, half is hole). Assume the screen is clean. These numbers are our assumptions for teaching, not a product rating.

  1. Radius = 3 / 2 = 1.5 in.
  2. Pipe area = π × r² = 3.1416 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 3.1416 × 2.25 = 7.07 sq in.
  3. Open area left = 7.07 × 0.50 = 3.53 sq in.
  4. Check: 3.53 / 7.07 = 0.50, so half the area is gone even when the screen is spotless.

Now assume dust and cobwebs plug half of the remaining holes. Then 3.53 × 0.50 = 1.77 sq in, about one quarter of the pipe area. Fine mesh clogs fast, which is why we do not put one on a furnace pipe.

Field card: Wildlife and Home HVAC

Check the caps, check the foam, and never touch an active nest without checking the rules.

  • Look at dryer, fan, hood and furnace pipe caps for twigs, feathers and dead airflow.
  • Chewed foam and frayed low-voltage wire at the outdoor unit mean call a tech.
  • A blocked furnace vent can shut the furnace down or risk CO. Never bypass a safety switch.
  • CO alarms: every level and outside sleeping areas. If it sounds, go outside and call 911.
  • Active nests with eggs or chicks are protected. Check with your state wildlife agency first.
  • Our example: 3 inch pipe is 7.07 sq in; a 50 percent open screen leaves 3.53 sq in.
