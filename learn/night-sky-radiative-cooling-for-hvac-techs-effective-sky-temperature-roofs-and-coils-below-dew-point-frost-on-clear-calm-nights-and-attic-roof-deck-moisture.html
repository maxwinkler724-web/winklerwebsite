Night-Sky Radiative Cooling: Why Roofs and Coils Run Below Air Temperature

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a clear, calm night a car windshield can frost over while the thermometer on the porch still reads above freezing. The same thing happens to roofs, metal buildings, rooftop duct and idle outdoor coils. The surfaces are not cooled by the air. They are cooled by looking up at a sky that acts like a very cold wall. This lesson shows why, puts numbers on it with our own example, and ties it to heat pump frost and to moisture on attic roof decks.

The sky as a cold surface

Every surface sends out long-wave (infrared) radiation, and it also receives radiation from whatever it can "see." If you want the basics first, read radiation heat transfer for HVAC. A roof that faces the open sky sees the atmosphere and, through gaps in it, outer space. A federal weather service glossary defines radiational cooling as the cooling of the earth's surface when it loses more heat to space than it receives, and says the loss is greater under clear skies. A second weather service page says the clear sky has a very cold effective temperature, so exposed surfaces such as car roofs lose more energy than they receive and cool off.

A university-hosted review paper on radiative sky cooling explains why: the atmosphere is fairly transparent to long-wave radiation between about 8 and 13 micrometers, so surfaces can dump heat to space through that window, and it notes the mean tropospheric temperature is around 250 K. It also says the sky's transparency is quite sensitive to the amount of water vapor, so a humid sky blocks more of the window. The sky has no single temperature, so engineers use an effective sky temperature: the temperature of a black wall that would return the same long-wave radiation. On a dry, clear night it is well below air temperature. On a cloudy night it is close to air temperature.

What cools, and what does not

The weather service page on dew and frost makes several points that matter on a roof:

  • Only objects under the open sky cool this way. Objects under trees do not.
  • Metal and glass radiate efficiently, so they are good collectors of dew and frost. Concrete holds daytime heat and collects little.
  • Clouds limit the cooling because they absorb long-wave radiation and send it back down.
  • Dew starts when a surface reaches the dew point. It becomes frost when the surface is at or below 32°F.

Another weather service page gives a plain example: air at 37°F with a dew point of 35°F. A car roof under open sky condenses water once it cools below 35°F and freezes it below 32°F, even though the air never reached freezing. A regional office page adds that near-ground readings can run several degrees below the standard thermometer height on calm, clear nights, and that metal, glass, cars and well-exposed rooftops cool fastest. Look at that last point from the HVAC side: a thin metal roof or duct wrap has very little mass to hold heat, so it follows the sky closely.

Wind and cloud change the answer

Wind brings warm air across the surface, which adds convective heat to the roof. A regional office page says even a light wind of 3 to 5 mph can keep the air mixed and prevent widespread frost. Cloud cover raises the effective sky temperature, so the radiated loss shrinks. The best nights for roof frost are therefore clear, calm and humid enough that the dew point sits only a few degrees below the air temperature. Check the sky and the wind before you trust the air temperature alone.

Our own worked example

This is our own example, with assumed inputs. The assumptions are not measurements of any real roof.

  • Air temperature 40°F, which is 4.44°C or 277.6 K (K = °C + 273.15).
  • Effective clear-sky temperature 255 K (assumption, chosen to sit in the neighborhood of the roughly 250 K tropospheric mean given above).
  • Roof emissivity 0.90 (assumption for a typical non-metallic roof surface).
  • Stefan-Boltzmann constant 5.670 × 10-8 W/(m²·K4), from a federal measurement agency table of fundamental constants.
  • The roof is a thin skin with no heat arriving from below (assumption, so this is an upper-end case).

Step 1, absolute temperatures to the fourth power. Radiation math needs kelvin, never °F or °C. 277.64 = 5.94 × 109. 2554 = 4.23 × 109. The difference is 1.71 × 109.

Step 2, net loss if the roof were at air temperature. q = 0.90 × 5.670 × 10-8 × 1.71 × 109 = about 87 W/m². To change units, multiply by 0.317 (our conversion from W/m² to Btu/h per square foot): about 27.7 Btu/h per square foot. For comparison, if clouds raised the sky to an assumed 268 K, the same math gives about 40 W/m² (12.6 Btu/h per square foot). With an assumed 273 K cloud base it falls to about 20 W/m² (6.2 Btu/h per square foot).

Step 3, how cold does the roof get? The roof cools until the air gives back as much heat by convection as radiation takes away. Assume a calm-air film coefficient of 2 W/(m²·K) (assumption). Trying roof temperatures, 263.1 K balances: radiation net loss = 0.90 × 5.670 × 10-8 × (263.14 − 2554) = about 28.9 W/m², and convection gain = 2 × (277.6 − 263.1) = about 28.9 W/m². The two match. The roof sits about 14.5 K, or 26°F, below the air, near 14°F.

Step 4, add a light breeze. With an assumed film coefficient of 5 W/(m²·K), the balance moves to 268.05 K. Radiation: 0.90 × 5.670 × 10-8 × (268.054 − 2554) = about 47.7 W/m²; convection: 5 × (277.59 − 268.05) = about 47.7 W/m². The roof is only about 9.5 K (17°F) below the air. Wind cut the gap by a third in this example.

What this means. A real roof is not an isolated skin. Heat flowing from the attic, thermal mass, trees, neighbors and a humid sky all shrink the gap, which is why the weather pages above report a few degrees, not 26. The point of the example is the direction and the sensitivity: a clear sky and calm air let a roof fall well below air temperature and below dew point, while wind or cloud pull it back toward air temperature. Compare this with the daytime story in sol-air temperature and roof heat gain.

Link to heat pump coil frost

A national laboratory review of defrost technology says frost forms on an outdoor heat pump coil when the coil surface is below the dew point of the air and well below freezing for the water to freeze, with outdoor air roughly from −7 to 5°C (about 19 to 41°F) and humidity above 65 percent. It also says frost blocks airflow, raises pressure drop and cuts evaporator capacity. The full defrost story is in heat pump frost and defrost losses.

Our own reasoning, not a figure from those sources: a running coil is cold mainly because refrigerant flows through it, so sky radiation is a secondary effect there. It matters most for surfaces that are not refrigerant-cooled: the top of the cabinet, a coil sitting off between cycles, or a unit under open sky on a clear night where fins and sheet metal can sit below the air temperature and collect dew or frost first. If you see frost on the top or shoulder of an idle outdoor unit on a clear, still morning with air above freezing, think sky radiation before you think refrigerant fault. A heavy, even frost across a running coil is the ordinary frost-and-defrost case described in the related lesson and in defrost cycle and auxiliary heat.

Link to attic and roof deck moisture

A national laboratory field report on sealed attics says the night sky radiation effect exists mainly on a night when the sky is clear and the winds are calm, and that it may lower the roof surface temperature to a point favorable for condensation. In its monitoring from May 20 to December 6, 2013, the south roof had at least 3 hours below the outdoor dew point on 180 of 201 days. The report also describes water vapor from the attic air being reabsorbed into the cool roof deck at night. It gives no figure for how far the deck itself fell below air temperature, so we do not state one here. For the ventilation side, see attic ventilation basics, and for dew point math see psychrometrics for technicians.

For a tech, the practical lesson is that air leaks from the house into the attic carry vapor that finds the coldest surface, and under a clear sky the underside of the roof deck can be that surface. Sealing air leaks and keeping duct leakage out of the attic reduce the vapor supply.

Field card: Night-sky radiative cooling

Clear and calm means colder than the thermometer says.

  • Radiation math always uses kelvin: K = °C + 273.15.
  • The effective sky temperature is far below air on clear dry nights and near air under cloud.
  • Exposed metal, glass and thin roofs follow the sky; surfaces under trees or eaves do not.
  • Surface below dew point gives dew; at or below 32°F it gives frost, even with air above freezing.
  • Wind and clouds shrink the gap; calm, clear, humid nights widen frost risk.
  • Our example: air 277.6 K, sky 255 K, emissivity 0.90 gives about 87 W/m² (27.7 Btu/h per square foot) at air temperature, and a calm-air roof near 26°F below air in the idealized case.
  • Frost on an idle unit's top on a clear still morning may be sky radiation, not a fault.
