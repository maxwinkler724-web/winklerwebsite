Lifting Condensate After Steam Traps: Back Pressure and Overhead Returns

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A steam trap is happiest when the condensate leaves by gravity. Sometimes the only return line is overhead, and the trap has to push condensate uphill. That lift is not free: every foot of rise pushes back on the trap, and the push can quietly cut drainage until a coil floods. This lesson shows how to count that back pressure and what to do about it.

Why lift creates back pressure

Condensate in a riser is a column of water, and a column of water weighs on whatever is under it. A standard water-head relation puts one foot of water at about 0.433 psi, which is the same as saying about 2.31 feet of rise per psi (see the university extension note in Sources). So the rule of thumb is roughly 1 psi of back pressure for every 2.3 feet of lift. Hot condensate is slightly lighter than cold water, so the rule runs a little high, which is the safe direction for a quick check.

The trap does not see only the lift. It also sees whatever pressure is already in the return line. Add the two:

Back pressure at the trap outlet = lift in feet / 2.31 + return line pressure (psig).

The trap can only pass condensate when the steam-side pressure ahead of it is higher than that number. What matters is the pressure difference across the trap, not the supply pressure alone.

How back pressure cuts capacity and stalls a coil

A trap's capacity depends on the pressure difference across it. Less difference means less condensate through the same orifice. A maker's bulletin we read says to size the trap on the actual differential and to apply a safety factor of 1.5 to the full-load condensate rate. Look up the differential, not the supply pressure, on the capacity chart.

The bigger problem shows up on a heat exchanger or coil with a modulating steam valve. When the valve closes down, the pressure inside the coil falls. If it falls below the back pressure, the trap has nothing to push with. The same bulletin notes that any lift after the trap needs a positive pressure to build in the shell to drain condensate, and advises avoiding lift on units with a modulating control valve. Condensate backs up in the coil, the heat output drops, the controller opens the valve, pressure rises, the coil dumps, and the cycle repeats. We call that stall, and it often looks like poor temperature control with an occasional bang.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example. The numbers are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic. Steam at 15 psig is supplied to a coil with a trap on its outlet. The trap lifts condensate 18 ft into an overhead return held at 2 psig.

  1. Lift back pressure: 18 ft / 2.31 ft per psi = 7.79 psi (check: 7.79 x 2.31 = 18.0).
  2. Add the return pressure: 7.79 + 2 = 9.79 psig of back pressure at the trap outlet.
  3. Pressure left across the trap at full load: 15 - 9.79 = 5.21 psi. Always use the pressure at the trap inlet, not the main, and assume no valve or strainer drop.
  4. Stall point: if the modulating valve throttles the coil to 9.79 psig or less, the difference is zero or negative and the coil will not drain by itself. That is far above atmospheric pressure, so a coil that is fine at full load can flood at part load.

Compared with a gravity drain into the same 2 psig return, the available difference falls from 13 psi (15 - 2) to 5.21 psi, so the trap has about 40 percent of the difference it would have had (5.21 / 13 = 0.40). That is a difference ratio only. How much capacity the trap loses depends on its type and size, so read the maker's chart at 5.2 psi, not at 13.

Which traps tolerate lift

We did not find a trustworthy source that ranks trap types by how well they handle lift, so we do not rank them here. What we can say from the arithmetic is that every type must be sized for the real pressure difference, and every type needs the supply pressure to stay above the back pressure. Pick the trap from the maker's capacity chart at the actual differential, and compare it with the types covered in Steam Trap Types and Where Each Fits. If the lift is large compared with the coil pressure at part load, no trap type fixes that; a different approach is needed.

Check valve after the trap

When the steam shuts off or the system cools, the riser holds a column of water above the trap. Without a check valve after the trap, that water can run back toward the coil and the trap during an off cycle. A check valve after the trap keeps the column in the riser. In our reasoning this is standard practice, but it also adds a small pressure drop and its own opening pressure, so count it as extra back pressure and use a type the maker lists for condensate. It is a second job for the trap's inspection list: a stuck check valve looks like a bad trap.

Sizing the lift line: flash steam and two-phase flow

Condensate leaving a trap at steam temperature drops to a much lower pressure in the riser and return. Part of it flashes to steam. A state university facilities report we read describes this: condensate holds more energy than it can keep as liquid at the lower pressure, so some boils off as steam. A bulletin for heat exchangers lists flash losses of 0.6 percent for 2 psig steam, 3.9 percent for 15 psig and 14.8 percent for 125 psig, so the share climbs fast with pressure.

The mix in the riser is water and steam. Steam has far more volume than the water it came from, so a lift line sized for liquid only will run at high velocity and push slugs. A riser and return should be sized larger than the trap outlet, with the pitch continuous and no low pockets, and the return line must be sized for the flash steam it carries. Use a published two-phase sizing table from the maker, and confirm the flash share for the real pressures. We keep that table off this page because we did not read one we could cite.

Water hammer where flash and cold condensate meet

Water hammer starts when steam meets cooler water and collapses. In a return, flash steam can meet cold condensate in a low spot or at a tee, and the steam bubble collapses suddenly. The maker bulletin we read warns that a lift or back pressure after a trap can flood a heat exchanger shell and cause severe water hammer. Steady slope, no pockets, and a riser that rises straight all help. See Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water for the causes.

When a pump or pump trap is needed instead

If the lift is large, if the coil modulates widely, or if the numbers above show the stall point near the normal operating pressure, stop trying to make the trap do the lifting. The same bulletin says to install a condensate pump when no gravity return exists, and describes pressure-powered pumps for the job. A pump or pump trap moves condensate out of the shell on its own power, so the coil drains even at low pressure. See Pressure-Powered Condensate Pumps and Pump Traps for how they work and how to check them. When a trap discharges after a pump, the trap is sized from the pump discharge pressure minus the return back pressure.

Field card: Lifting Condensate After Steam Traps

Count the lift, then count what is left across the trap.

  • Back pressure = lift in feet / 2.31 + return pressure (psig).
  • Pressure across the trap = inlet pressure at the trap minus that back pressure.
  • A modulating coil stalls when its pressure drops to the back pressure.
  • Put a check valve after the trap and count its drop as back pressure.
  • Expect flash steam in the riser; size for steam and water together.
  • Large lift or wide modulation: use a pump or pump trap.
