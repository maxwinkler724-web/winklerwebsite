Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Home: Cost per Million Btu, COP vs AFUE, Climate, Venting and Dual Fuel

Last updated: 2026-11-03

When a home is being designed, nothing is installed yet, so the choice between a heat pump, a gas furnace or both is open. The comparison has more than a price tag: heat delivered per dollar, how the climate affects the heat pump, whether gas service and a vent are needed, whether cooling comes along, and combustion safety. This lesson works each one and ends with the numbers.

Put both on the same scale: cost per million Btu delivered

Gas is sold in therms and electricity in kilowatt-hours, so the prices cannot be compared directly. The U.S. Energy Information Administration lists sample conversion factors of 1 kWh = 3,412 Btu and 1 therm = 100,000 Btu, and says they are recalculated each year, so treat them as close, not exact. The method is the one in comparing heating fuel costs: turn each price into dollars per million Btu of fuel, then divide by the efficiency of the equipment.

Two different efficiency numbers appear. DOE defines AFUE for a furnace as annual heat output divided by the fuel energy it consumes, so 0.95 means 95 percent of the fuel energy becomes heat. A heat pump is described by COP: heat delivered divided by electrical energy used. DOE says a properly installed air-source heat pump can deliver up to about three times as much heat as the electricity it uses, because it moves heat instead of making it from a fuel. The reason a COP above 1 is possible, and the reason a condensing furnace can reach 95 percent, are covered in higher vs lower heating value. For a new home, COP and AFUE both end up in one formula: delivered cost = fuel cost per million Btu divided by efficiency.

Worked example (our own, with invented prices)

Assumptions. These prices are made up for the math and are not real or current: electricity at $0.20 per kWh and natural gas at $1.50 per therm. We use 3,412 Btu per kWh and 100,000 Btu per therm (EIA). Duct and piping losses are ignored for both systems, and COP is treated as one seasonal average.

  1. Electricity per million Btu: 1,000,000 / 3,412 = 293.08 kWh. At $0.20 that is 293.08 x 0.20 = $58.62.
  2. Gas per million Btu: 1,000,000 / 100,000 = 10 therms. At $1.50 that is $15.00.
  3. Furnace, AFUE 0.95: 15.00 / 0.95 = $15.79. Furnace, AFUE 0.80: 15.00 / 0.80 = $18.75.
  4. Heat pump, COP 3.0: 58.62 / 3.0 = $19.54. COP 2.0: 58.62 / 2.0 = $29.31. COP 1.5: 58.62 / 1.5 = $39.08.

Break-even COP. Set the two delivered costs equal: 58.62 / COP = 15.00 / AFUE, so COP = (58.62 / 15.00) x AFUE = 3.908 x AFUE. Against the 95 percent furnace, COP = 3.908 x 0.95 = 3.71. Check: 58.62 / 3.71 = 15.80, matching $15.79 within rounding. Against the 80 percent furnace, COP = 3.908 x 0.80 = 3.13.

What the numbers say. With these invented prices, electricity costs 3.9 times as much as gas per Btu, so a seasonal COP of 3.0 loses to both furnaces: $19.54 against $15.79 is about 24 percent more than the 95 percent furnace and about 4 percent more than the 80 percent one. COP 2.0 and 1.5 lose by more. Change the prices and the answer moves. At the same $0.20 electricity, a COP of 3.0 would break even with the 95 percent furnace only if gas cost about $1.86 per therm (19.54 / 10 x 0.95). Run the formula with your own local rates, not ours.

Climate and cold-weather capacity

COP is not one fixed number. It is higher in mild weather and lower in deep cold, so the seasonal average that matters depends on how many hours the home spends at each temperature, which is a climate question. Capacity also falls as it gets colder while the house needs more heat. DOE says older heat pumps were generally unsuitable for long subfreezing periods, and newer models are a legitimate heating option in colder regions. One field study from Oak Ridge National Laboratory of a cold-climate unit reported about 75 percent of rated heating capacity at -13 F, operation down to that temperature without resistance heat, and a measured seasonal COP of 3.16; that is one machine in one home, not a promise for every unit. How to read a manufacturer's capacity table is in cold-climate heat pumps, and how to match equipment to the design load is in sizing a heat pump for a new home.

Gas service and venting

A furnace needs a gas supply: a service line, a meter and piping to the equipment, and a way to remove flue gases. DOE explains that high-efficiency sealed-combustion furnaces draw air from outside and exhaust directly outdoors, and that condensing furnaces can use plastic vent pipe, while the acidic exhaust is unsuitable for an old unlined chimney. A non-sealed furnace takes room air for combustion, which is where combustion air questions come from. In a new home the vent route is a design decision: a wall or roof penetration, and a place for the condensate to drain. A heat pump needs none of this. It needs electrical service with enough capacity, an outdoor pad, refrigerant lines and a condensate path. If the lot has no gas service, the furnace option also means the cost of getting it there, or a different fuel; ask the utility, because we cannot say what that costs for your site.

Cooling comes with the heat pump

DOE notes that one heat pump heats and cools by reversing the refrigerant flow. A gas furnace only heats, so a new home with a furnace also needs an air conditioner or similar cooling equipment. Counting both pieces gives a fairer comparison: furnace plus air conditioner against one heat pump. The cooling efficiency is rated separately (SEER); the heating rating is HSPF.

Combustion safety

CDC lists furnaces among the fuel-burning appliances that can produce carbon monoxide, and advises yearly inspection, correct venting and an alarm near every sleeping area. DOE adds that a cracked heat exchanger can leak combustion gases into the house. A heat pump does not burn fuel, so it has no flue and cannot make carbon monoxide itself. A sealed-combustion furnace lowers some risks, such as backdrafting from room air, but it still burns gas and needs a clear vent, so alarms matter in any home that has combustion equipment (see carbon monoxide alarms). A gas-free home removes gas leak and flue concerns from the list; it does not remove the need for good electrical work.

When dual fuel makes sense

A dual-fuel system pairs a heat pump with a gas furnace, usually with the heat pump running in mild weather and the furnace taking over in the cold. It makes the most sense when (a) the climate has long cold stretches where heat pump COP falls, (b) the gas price per Btu is low compared with electricity, so the furnace wins on cost at low outdoor temperature, (c) gas service is already planned or available, and (d) the owner wants a second heat source. The trade-offs are two sets of equipment, the vent and gas piping anyway, and more controls to set. Setting the changeover point is in the dual-fuel changeover lesson. For the same decision in an existing house, see heat pump or gas furnace for an existing home.

Field card: Heat Pump or Gas Furnace for a New Home

Same units first, then compare.

  • Delivered cost per million Btu = fuel cost per million Btu / efficiency (COP or AFUE).
  • EIA factors: 3,412 Btu per kWh, 100,000 Btu per therm.
  • Our own example (invented prices $0.20 per kWh, $1.50 per therm): furnace 0.95 = $15.79; furnace 0.80 = $18.75; heat pump COP 3.0 = $19.54, 2.0 = $29.31, 1.5 = $39.08.
  • Break-even COP = (electric $ per million Btu / gas $ per million Btu) x AFUE = 3.71 vs 0.95, 3.13 vs 0.80.
  • Furnace needs gas, a vent and a cooling system; heat pump needs electrical capacity and does both jobs.
