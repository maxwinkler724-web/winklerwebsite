Water Leaking Into a Building From a Rooftop Unit Itself: Hood and Economizer Rain Entry, Curb Gaps, Cabinet Gaskets, Plugged or Frozen Traps, and a Step-by-Step Leak Trace

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A brown stain on a ceiling tile under a rooftop unit gets blamed on the roof, and sometimes it is the roof. But the unit is also a big sheet-metal box full of openings, gaskets, a wet coil and a drain. This lesson is about water that gets in because of the unit itself, and how to trace it before anyone calls a roofer.

Roof leak or unit leak?

Flashing, pitch pockets and boots around the curb are a roofing-side topic, covered in Roof Penetrations for Rooftop HVAC Equipment. A small residential-style drain leak is covered in Water Leaking From an AC or Furnace Condensate Drain. Here we stay on the commercial unit: air that comes in, air that goes out, and water that the coil makes.

The unit sits on a curb, a raised frame that is flashed into the roof. The space inside the curb is the opening where supply and return air pass through the roof. Anything that lets water into the unit cabinet, or into the curb opening, can show up as a ceiling leak below even when the roof membrane is perfect.

The five unit-side water paths

1. Rain through the outdoor air hood or economizer

The outdoor air opening has to let air in and keep water out. A university master specification for large rooftop units calls for an outdoor-air intake hood with a moisture eliminator where needed to keep moisture from entering, plus a bird or insect screen. Economizer dampers are in the same path. Damper blades that do not close, bent blades, broken seals or a failed actuator leave a gap, and wind-driven rain or snow can ride in through it. A national laboratory retuning guide lists loose linkages and broken damper seals among the causes of a damper that will not behave, and says to check for something obstructing the damper or a failed actuator. Cycle the damper fully open, fully closed and to minimum to see that it moves at each position. See Economizers Basics for how the damper is supposed to work.

Look for water marks or stains on the return side of the mixing box, rust streaks below the hood, a clogged or missing moisture eliminator, and snow drifted inside the hood. Drain water from the hood should have somewhere to go, not sit against the casing.

2. Open supply and return openings and gaps at the curb

The curb opening is the part people forget. The unit and curb are meant to be joined with gaskets and sealant, and the same university roofing-accessory specification asks for curb joints to be sealed with sealant as the accessory maker requires and for the installation to be weathertight. If the unit was set crooked, the curb was swapped, or an adapter was used, there can be a gap between the unit base and the curb top, or between a duct and the curb wall. Air leaks, and any water that gets under the unit travels along the curb and drips into the duct or ceiling below. Open knockouts, unsealed duct passthroughs and loose duct collars inside the curb have the same effect. See Replacing a Rooftop Unit: Curb Adapters, Crane Lifts and What Owners Should Plan for why fit matters.

3. Cabinet panel gaskets

The master specification for large rooftop units asks for neoprene gasket around the perimeter of panel frames, sealed casing joints, and grommet seals where wiring and pipes go through the cabinet. Gaskets that are flattened, torn, missing after a service call, or left unlatched leave rain a path into the cabinet. Look at the latch side and the bottom edge of each access door, and at any panel taken off recently. A door left open an inch is a very common cause that no gasket can fix.

4. Condensate pan overflow

A cooling coil removes water from the air. That water drops into a drain pan and leaves by a drain connection. A national laboratory maintenance summary for unitary equipment tells staff to check the drain and the pan and to flush them, treating with an anti-algae product as needed. If the drain plugs with slime, debris or a dead insect, the pan fills and spills over the edge. A cracked or rusted-through pan can leak directly, with no overflow at all. In a big unit, the spill then runs down into the curb opening.

5. Plugged, missing or frozen traps

On a draw-through unit the fan pulls air across the coil, so the pan sits under negative pressure. Without a trap of enough depth, the fan can pull air up the drain line and hold water back in the pan. That is why the master specification asks for a deep trap at the pan connection and cleanouts at changes of direction. A trap that is missing or too shallow can let water pile up. See AC Coil Condensate Traps: Why Draw-Through Units Need Them and How Deep. In cold weather, a drain line and trap that run outside can freeze solid, which dams up the pan even when the trap is built correctly. Our own rule of thumb (not from a cited source): if the leak shows up only after a freeze and clears up in a warm spell, suspect the trap first.

How much water is the coil making?

It helps to know what the drain must carry, because a drain that handles a light load can still spill on the hottest, wettest day. Here is our own worked example. The inputs are assumptions, not measurements.

  • Assumed airflow: 4,000 cfm across the coil.
  • Assumed moisture removed: 30 grains of water per pound of dry air (the difference between entering and leaving air).
  • Constants: 4.5 converts cfm to pounds of air per hour (it is 60 minutes per hour times about 0.075 pound of air per cubic foot at standard conditions); 7,000 grains make 1 pound; water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon.

Step 1: pounds of water per hour = cfm x 4.5 x grain difference / 7,000 = 4,000 x 4.5 x 30 / 7,000 = 540,000 / 7,000 = about 77.1 lb/h.

Step 2: gallons per hour = 77.1 / 8.34 = about 9.2 gal/h.

Step 3: that is about 0.15 gallons per minute, or about 222 gallons over 24 hours of those same conditions. A drain that stops carrying it sends all of that water to the pan and then over the edge. If the grain difference is half as large (15 grains), the same math gives about 4.6 gal/h, so the load changes a lot with weather. Real air is not exactly at standard density, so treat the result as a close estimate.

Check yourself: 4.5 x 4,000 = 18,000 lb of air per hour; 18,000 x 30 = 540,000 grains per hour; 540,000 / 7,000 = 77.1 lb. The arithmetic holds.

A step-by-step way to trace the source

  1. Note the weather pattern. Does it leak only during rain, only when the unit is cooling in humid weather, only after a freeze, or even on dry days with the unit off? Rain-only points to the hood, damper, gaskets, curb or roof. Cooling-only on a dry day points to the pan, drain or trap.
  2. Compare dry weather to rain. Run the unit in cooling on a dry day. If water appears, it is condensate. If it stays dry until it rains, keep going.
  3. Look inside first. With power locked out, open the access doors. Look for standing water in the pan, a slimy drain, rust, stains on the floor of the cabinet and wet insulation. Check the pan for cracks.
  4. Check the trap and drain. Pour clean water into the pan and watch it leave. Slow or no drain means a plug. In freezing weather check the trap for ice. Clear a plug using the maker's instructions and your site procedures.
  5. Hose test, one zone at a time. Use a garden hose with low pressure, never a pressure washer. Start low and work up so you know which zone leaks: first the curb and base, then the lower panels, then the upper panels, then the hood. Keep one person inside the building or the curb space watching for water, with a radio. Wet each zone for several minutes before moving on.
  6. Inspect the curb opening. From inside the building, look up through the duct for light and watch for drips along the curb wall. Note whether water follows the duct or comes from the unit base.
  7. Document before you repair. Take photos and record which test produced the leak. Then fix the cause: replace gaskets, reseal the curb joint, clear or rebuild the trap, repair the damper, or hand the roof finding back to the roofing contractor.

Work on the roof carries its own hazards: edges, hatches and wet surfaces. Follow your site fall-protection and lockout rules every time.

Field card: Water leaking in from a rooftop unit

Match the weather to the path.

  • Rain only: hood, economizer dampers, panel gaskets, curb joints.
  • Humid cooling only: pan, drain, trap.
  • Leaks after a freeze: frozen or plugged trap.
  • Pour water in the pan to test the drain.
  • Hose test low to high, one zone at a time.
  • Draw-through units need a trap of enough depth.
  • Our example: 4,000 cfm and 30 grains is about 77 lb/h, or 9.2 gal/h.
