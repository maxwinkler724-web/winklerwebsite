Electrical Basics for HVAC

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Almost every HVAC problem eventually becomes an electrical question. Before you can chase a fault, you need three words, one simple rule, and a set of habits that keep you alive.

Three words that run everything

Think of a water system. Voltage is the push, like water pressure, measured in volts. Current is the flow, like gallons moving through a pipe, measured in amperes (amps). Resistance is anything that holds the flow back, like a narrow pipe, measured in ohms. Voltage pushes, resistance resists, and current is what you get.

The rule that ties them together

Ohm's law says that for many simple parts, current rises in step with voltage and falls as resistance rises. Written out it is V = I x R. As a labeled teaching example only: a 12 volt push across a 6 ohm resistance gives 2 amps. You will not do this sum on every job, but it explains why a shorted part draws too much current and why a loose, corroded connection makes heat: resistance where there should be almost none.

Power, in one line

For a resistive heater, electrical power in watts is volts times amps; for motors and compressors, real power in watts is volts times amps times the power factor, so volts times amps alone gives volt-amps. That is why a motor that draws more amps than normal is working harder, or failing, and why wire and breaker sizes matter.

Line voltage and low voltage

HVAC equipment usually has two worlds. Line voltage runs motors, compressors and heaters and can seriously hurt or kill. Low voltage, commonly 24 volts AC, runs the controls. Low voltage is much gentler, but never assume. Treat every wire as live until you have proven otherwise. More in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

What a meter does

A multimeter measures voltage (AC and DC), resistance, and often continuity (a beep when there is a complete path), capacitance and temperature. A clamp meter measures current around a wire without opening the circuit. Published meter references stress these habits:

  • Use a meter rated for the place you are working. Meters carry safety category ratings. Higher categories are built for higher-energy locations such as panels and motor circuits.
  • Use good leads and the right jacks. Damaged leads or the wrong jack can turn a reading into an accident. Do not set the meter to current or resistance while its probes are across a live voltage source.
  • Do not measure resistance on a live circuit. Resistance and continuity checks are done with power off and the part isolated.
  • Respect the meter's range limits.

Lockout and test before you touch

Lockout/tagout is a set of procedures for shutting down and isolating equipment so that stored or residual energy cannot be released while someone is working on it. Electrical energy is one kind. The idea in the field is simple, and the exact steps are set by your employer and the rules where you work:

  • Shut the equipment off and open the disconnect. Apply your own lock and tag where required.
  • Test before touch. With your meter, prove the circuit is dead. First check the meter on a known live source, test the circuit, then check the meter again.
  • Remember stored energy. Capacitors can hold a charge after power is off. See Capacitors and Contactors.
  • Never defeat a safety device, fuse or switch to get a reading.

Some diagnosis has to be done live. That is work for trained people with proper protective equipment, following their site rules. If you are a student, ask your instructor first.

Field card: Electrical Basics for HVAC

Voltage pushes, current flows, resistance resists. Prove it dead before you touch it.

  • Volts = push, amps = flow, ohms = resistance. Ohm's law: V = I x R.
  • Line voltage is dangerous. 24 V control is gentler but not to be assumed safe.
  • Use a meter rated for the job, with undamaged leads in the correct jacks.
  • Resistance and continuity: power off, part isolated.
  • Lock out, then test your meter, test the circuit, test your meter again.
  • Never bypass a fuse, switch or safety device.
