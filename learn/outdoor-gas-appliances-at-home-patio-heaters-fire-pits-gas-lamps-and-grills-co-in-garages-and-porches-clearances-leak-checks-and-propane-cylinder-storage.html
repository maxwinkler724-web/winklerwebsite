Outdoor Gas Appliances at Home: Patio Heaters, Fire Pits, Gas Lamps and Grills

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A patio heater, a fire table, a gas lamp and a grill all burn fuel, and every one of them makes carbon monoxide (CO) when it runs. The rules for the whole group start from one fact: they were built for open air. This lesson covers where they can and cannot go, how to check a propane connection, how to store cylinders and what a 20 lb cylinder really gives you.

Outdoors means outdoors

A federal safety release on grills tells owners to use a grill outside only, in a well-ventilated area, and never indoors or in a garage, breezeway, carport or porch, or under a surface that will burn. It also says gas and charcoal grills carry a risk of fire and CO poisoning. The same thinking applies to a patio heater or fire table, which also burns fuel with no flue to carry exhaust away. A federal test report on portable propane radiant heaters found that models without an oxygen depletion sensor are meant for outdoor use only. In its small-room tests those heaters pushed CO past the 100 ppm test limit, and one radiant heater and cooker peaked at 2,124 ppm. The report also warns that any portable gas camp heater can be carried into a tent or other enclosed space, which is the foreseeable misuse to avoid.

So the practical list is short. No outdoor gas appliance goes in a garage, a basement, a tent, a camper or an enclosed or screened-in porch, even with a door cracked. A roof over a porch that is walled in on several sides traps exhaust the same way a garage does. If the appliance is not clearly rated for the space by its own instructions, treat the space as off limits. A working CO alarm in the house is still a good idea; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Clearances come from the instructions

There is no single number that fits every appliance. A heater's required distance to siding, eaves, overhangs, trees and furniture is printed in the maker's installation and use instructions, and the label on the unit is the first place to look. We treat those numbers as the rule for that model. One federal fire-administration flyer gives a general grill figure: keep a grill at least 3 feet from siding, deck rails and eaves. That is a floor for grills only, not a substitute for a heater's manual. Vinyl siding, fabric awnings, wooden pergolas and low branches are the usual trouble spots, because radiant heat from a heater head reaches upward and outward farther than most people expect.

Leak checks with soapy water

On propane, the federal grill guidance describes this check: open the gas supply valve fully, brush a soapy solution on the connection point, and watch for bubbles. Bubbles mean a leak. Try tightening the tank connection once; if the bubbles continue, close the gas valve and have a qualified person repair the appliance. An older federal grill notice adds that you should not light a grill if you smell gas, and should not use it until every leak is fixed, and that hoses and fittings may need to be replaced. Check hoses for cracking, brittleness and holes at the start of each season. Never use a flame to hunt for a leak. For the same idea on indoor appliance connectors, see Flexible Gas Appliance Connectors.

Natural gas or propane: why conversion needs the kit

Many fire tables, lamps and heaters come set up for one fuel. Natural gas and propane burn at different supply pressures and need different orifice sizes, so the same burner cannot simply be hooked to the other fuel. Switching takes the maker's conversion kit and instructions for that exact model, not a drilled-out orifice or a guess. Our conversion kit lesson explains the idea for furnaces and boilers, and the logic is the same outdoors: the kit is matched to the appliance, and the appliance label states the fuel it was built for. Natural gas hookups to a patio line also depend on the local gas piping rules and a qualified installer, so we do not describe a do-it-yourself hookup here.

Tip-over, wind and spider webs

Patio heaters stand tall and top-heavy, so a gust, a pet or a child can knock one over. Set the base on a firm, level surface, follow the maker's instructions on weighting the base, and shut the unit off in strong wind. Do not place a heater where wind can blow the flame toward siding or a canopy. Spiders and insects like gas burners too. A federal grill notice says the venturi tube, the short tube that mixes air with gas, is often a nesting place when a grill is stored for winter. It warns that a blockage can cause a fire behind the controls. Following the owner's manual, the tube is removed, checked and cleared, then seated back correctly on the valve outlet. Lamps, fire tables and heaters have similar mixing tubes or burner ports, so a hissing, uneven or yellow flame after storage is a reason to shut off and inspect, not to keep running it.

Storing propane cylinders

A propane trade association safety sheet, reposted on a state code-office alert page, says not to bring propane cylinders indoors or into an enclosed space such as a garage or basement. It says to use and store cylinders upright, outdoors and away from ignition sources, and not where they would be exposed to high temperatures. We follow that: spare cylinders live outside, upright, in the shade, away from the grill and from doors and windows. Cylinders are not carried in a hot car trunk for long. For how large tanks behave in the cold, see Propane Tanks for Home Heating.

Winter shutoff and covers

At the end of the season, close the cylinder valve, disconnect it and store it outside as above. Clean the burner and venturi, then fit a cover that the maker approves. Allow a unit to cool fully before covering it. In spring, redo the visual hose check, the venturi check and the soapy-water test before the first lighting, and look for new spider webs. On a natural gas line, close the appliance shutoff valve and leave other work to a qualified tech. During winter outages, never bring a heater or grill indoors for warmth; see Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages.

Worked example (our own): how long does a 20 lb cylinder last?

This is our own example, with assumed inputs, to show the arithmetic. It is not a measurement of any product.

  • Assumption: the patio heater is rated at 40,000 Btu/h input and runs on high the whole time.
  • Fill: a federal measurement article says most 20 lb cylinders are filled and labeled to a net weight of 15 lb of propane.
  • Assumption: about 4.2 lb of liquid propane per gallon (our rounded figure).
  • Energy content: about 91,500 Btu per gallon, rounded from the 91,452 Btu per gallon federal energy-statistics figure.

Step 1, gallons in the cylinder: 15 lb ÷ 4.2 lb per gallon = 3.57 gallons.
Step 2, energy: 3.57 gal × 91,500 Btu per gallon = about 326,800 Btu.
Step 3, time: 326,800 Btu ÷ 40,000 Btu/h = about 8.2 hours.

At a lower setting of 30,000 Btu/h the same arithmetic gives 326,800 ÷ 30,000 = about 10.9 hours. Real runtime is shorter, because the last bit of liquid is hard to use and heaters vary, so keep a spare cylinder outside and check the maker's rating plate for the true input.

Field card: Outdoor Gas Appliances at Home

Outside, open, clear and checked.

  • Never use outdoor heaters, fire tables or grills in garages, tents, campers or enclosed porches (CO).
  • Clearance to siding, eaves and overhangs comes from the maker's label and manual.
  • Soapy water on propane connections; bubbles mean a leak. Do not use a flame.
  • Natural gas versus propane: conversion needs the maker's kit for that model.
  • Level base, away from wind; shut off if it tips.
  • Check the venturi or burner for spider webs after storage.
  • Spare cylinders upright, outdoors, shaded. Never indoors.
  • Our estimate: 15 lb of propane at 40,000 Btu/h lasts about 8 hours.
