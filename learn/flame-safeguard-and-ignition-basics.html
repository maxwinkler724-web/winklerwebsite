Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics

Part of the Gas Heat Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Lighting gas is easy. Lighting it safely, and proving it is burning before letting more gas flow, is the real job of the ignition system.

Why gas needs a safeguard

Gas that flows without burning can collect and later ignite, or can be a fuel for something worse than a cold house. So modern equipment follows a rule: open the gas only when ready to light it, and close it quickly if flame is not proven. The piece that does this watching is called a flame safeguard or flame proving system, and often it is part of the main control board.

Ways to light the burner

  • Standing pilot. An older design with a small flame that burns all the time, with a thermocouple that holds the gas valve open only while the pilot is hot.
  • Hot surface igniter. A ceramic element that glows when electricity passes through it. It is fragile, so handle it gently and never touch it with bare skin, since oils can shorten its life.
  • Spark ignition. A high-voltage spark lights a pilot or main burner. It is quick and common on some furnaces, boilers and appliances. High voltage means treat it with respect.
  • Direct spark, intermittent pilot. Variants of the above that use a spark only at the start of each cycle.

Proving flame: the flame sensor

Most modern equipment proves flame with a flame sensor, often a thin metal rod sitting in the burner flame. It uses a trick called flame rectification. A flame is full of charged particles, so it conducts electricity, and it does so unevenly. Applied alternating current passes more easily one direction than the other, which leaves a small direct current that the board can measure. The board treats that tiny signal as proof that a real flame is there. Technician references note that the same measurement can also hint at flame quality.

What goes wrong

  • Dirty sensor. A film on the rod weakens the signal, so the flame lights, then the board shuts the gas after a few seconds. Cleaning it the way the maker says is a very common fix.
  • Poor ground. Rectification needs a good return path through the burner, so a loose or corroded ground connection can hide a good flame.
  • Weak or lifting flame. A poor flame can fail to touch the sensor properly.
  • Cracked or worn igniter. It may glow dim, or not at all, or fail after a short life.
  • Lockout. After repeated failed attempts, a board stops trying. Many boards need a reset after a wait or a power cycle, and some also reset on their own after a long timeout; the reset is not a repair, so find out why first.

Safe habits

Treat gas, high voltage and hot parts with care. Power off and lock out before reaching in, and let hot surfaces cool. Do not hold a flame or a jumper wire where a sensor belongs, and never bypass a flame safeguard, because it is the device that closes the gas when something goes wrong. If gas is smelled or ignition is delayed or rough, stop and have a qualified tech check the unit.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the installation and service instructions for the specific unit in front of you always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It does not give setpoints, timings, wiring steps or test values, and those vary by manufacturer, model and local rules.

Field card: Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics

Light it, then prove it.

  • Igniter types: standing pilot, hot surface, spark.
  • A flame sensor rod uses rectification to prove a real flame.
  • Flame that lights then drops out often means a dirty sensor, weak ground or poor flame.
  • Handle igniters gently and respect high-voltage spark.
  • Never bypass the flame safeguard or reset a lockout without finding the cause.
