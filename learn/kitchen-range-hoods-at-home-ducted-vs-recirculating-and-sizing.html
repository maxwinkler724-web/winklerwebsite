Kitchen Range Hoods at Home: Ducted vs Recirculating and Sizing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Homeowners ask whether a range hood really has to vent outside, or whether the cheaper kind that blows air back into the room is good enough. Short answer from the sources below: a hood that vents outdoors is the option the EPA points to over a gas stove. This lesson explains why, how to tell a good hood from a weak one, and how a pro sizes one.

Check the local rules

Check the local rules. Codes, product specs and adopted editions change. This page is general education built from sources read in 2026. Follow the hood's installation manual and ask your local building department what applies to your house.

Why cooking air matters

Cooking puts gases and tiny particles into the air. The EPA lists unvented combustion appliances, such as gas stoves, among the main indoor sources of nitrogen dioxide (NO2). Its page says NO2 mainly irritates the eyes, nose, throat and airways, that low-level exposure may increase airway reactivity in some people with asthma, and that it is linked with more respiratory infections in young children. The EPA also says homes without combustion appliances average about half the outdoor NO2 level, while homes with gas stoves often run above outdoor levels.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) chair said in a 2023 statement that research indicates gas stove emissions can be hazardous, and that the agency was studying ways to reduce the risk. The same statement said there was no proceeding to ban gas stoves. Treat that as a reason to ventilate sensibly, not a reason for alarm. None of these sources tell a particular household what its own health risk is. If someone in your home has asthma or another lung condition, ask their clinician.

The EPA's general guidance says source control is usually the most effective approach, and that ventilation matters most during short, high-pollutant activities, cooking included. It says kitchen fans that vent outdoors pull contaminants out of the room. For a gas stove, it advises "an exhaust fan vented to outdoors."

Ducted vs recirculating

A ducted hood pulls air through a filter and pushes it through a duct to the outdoors. A recirculating (ductless) hood filters the air and returns it to the kitchen. Zephyr's hood manual says ducted fans must vent to the outdoors, not into a wall, ceiling, attic, crawl space or garage cavity, and says to use metal ductwork only. The same manual lists a ductless recirculating option, but the section did not load for us, so we are not quoting its details.

Why does venting outside matter? A grease filter catches grease, not gases. The EPA says air cleaners are generally not designed to remove gases. That is not a test of any recirculating hood, and none of the sources we read measured one. It does explain why the EPA language says "vented to outdoors," and why a recirculating hood is the fallback when no duct path exists, such as in some apartments, not the first choice.

A related warning from the bathroom side: a duct that ends in an attic or wall just moves the problem. See Bathroom Exhaust Fans: Sizing, Ducting and Moisture Control for the same idea with moisture.

Capture matters as much as airflow

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) tested seven residential range hoods and reported in Environmental Science & Technology (Delp and Singer). The LBNL news page for the study says:

  • Capture efficiency, the share of cooking pollutants the hood actually pulls out, ranged from under 15% to over 98% depending on the hood.
  • Every hood captured more from the back burners than from the front ones. Using the back burners first lowers exposure further.
  • Hoods that met the airflow recommendation in the Home Ventilating Institute (HVI) HV1 standard captured roughly 80% or more from the back burners, 60% or more from the oven and 50% or more from the front burners.
  • Open-bottom hoods did better than ones with grease screens or metal bottoms.
  • Hoods meeting ENERGY STAR criteria captured under 30% from the front burners and oven.
  • The best front-burner performer was too loud for normal conversation.

That last item is the practical catch. A hood that is so loud nobody turns it on does nothing. LBNL also noted that even a moderately effective hood can reduce a cook's exposure, so running a modest hood beats running none.

Sizing: a worked example

Broan's guidance for gas cooktops is a minimum of 100 cfm for every 10,000 BTU per hour of cooktop output. Broan's own example is that a 50,000 BTU stove calls for 500 cfm. Here is the arithmetic for a hypothetical four-burner gas range with burners rated 18,000, 12,000, 9,000 and 6,500 BTU/h:

  1. Add the burners: 18,000 + 12,000 + 9,000 + 6,500 = 45,500 BTU/h.
  2. Divide by 10,000: 45,500 / 10,000 = 4.55.
  3. Multiply by 100 cfm: 4.55 x 100 = 455 cfm minimum.
  4. Choose the next size up. Broan also recommends 500 cfm for gas cooking in general, so a 500 cfm hood fits this range.

Broan says electric and induction cooktops typically need 350 to 500 cfm, more if you often run several burners. It also says the duct must be at least 3 in by 10 in (about the same as a 6 in round duct), and that the cfm you need goes up as the duct run gets longer, so match the hood to its manufacturer's duct specs. This is one manufacturer's rule. The HVI recommendation that LBNL used is a separate figure that we could not confirm, so ask the hood maker which rating a model meets.

Make-up air for big hoods

Whatever air a hood exhausts must come in somewhere. Zephyr's make-up air kit sheet says a hood that exhausts outside leaves the house under negative pressure unless outdoor air replaces it, and describes a damper that opens automatically while the hood runs. It says the kit is meant for Zephyr hoods rated above 300 cfm, and states that it meets International Residential Code section M1503.4. A building-forum quotation of the 2009 IRC text says hood systems able to exhaust more than 400 cfm need make-up air at about the same rate, with a way to close it and automatic control with the hood. Local adoption varies, so a 455 cfm hood like the example may trigger the rule where you live. Ask your building department and read the hood's installation manual before you buy.

Short tech section: what the installer checks

  • Duct path. Straight and short beats long with elbows, because Broan says required cfm rises with length.
  • Duct material. Zephyr's manual says metal only.
  • Termination. Outdoors, never into an attic, wall or crawl space, per Zephyr's manual.
  • Filters. Keep grease filters in place, per Zephyr.
  • Make-up air. Check code and the hood's manual if the rated cfm is high.

Field card: Kitchen Range Hoods at Home

Vent it outside, capture the back burners, and size to the burners.

  • EPA lists gas stoves as an indoor NO2 source and advises an exhaust fan vented to outdoors over them.
  • Ducted hoods go outdoors through metal duct; never into an attic, wall or crawl space.
  • Recirculating hoods are a fallback; EPA says air cleaners generally do not remove gases.
  • LBNL: capture varied from under 15% to over 98%; back burners were captured better than front ones.
  • Broan rule for gas: 100 cfm per 10,000 BTU/h. 45,500 BTU/h gives 455 cfm, so 500 cfm.
  • Large hoods (over about 400 cfm in some IRC editions) may need make-up air. Ask the building department.
  • Pick a hood quiet enough that people will actually run it.
