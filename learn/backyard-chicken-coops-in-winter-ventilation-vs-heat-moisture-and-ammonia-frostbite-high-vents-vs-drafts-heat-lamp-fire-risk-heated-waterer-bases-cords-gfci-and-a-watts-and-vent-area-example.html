Backyard Chicken Coops in Winter: Ventilation, Heat Lamps and Cords

Last updated: 2026-11-04

When a cold snap hits, the first instinct is to add heat to the chicken coop. University extension poultry guidance points the other way: keep the coop dry and drafts off the birds, let moist air escape high up, and treat any electrical heat as a fire and shock risk that needs careful setup. This lesson explains why, and puts numbers on the electricity.

Why moisture, not cold, is the problem

Hens give off water in breath and droppings. A university extension page on cold-weather chicken care notes that chicken manure is about 70 percent water, so a coop with wet litter keeps adding moisture to the air. Moisture plus cold can condense on surfaces, and the same page links that damp cold to frostbite on combs, wattles and feet. A university extension fact sheet on winter backyard chickens says humid air creates more frostbite risk than drier air and warns that ammonia building up from litter in a tightly sealed coop can cause respiratory problems over time. A university extension fact sheet on winter weather and small flocks adds that damp air feels much colder than dry air and that moisture raises ammonia levels.

The practical lesson for a homeowner: if you smell ammonia, or see frost or wet beads on walls and windows, the coop needs more air exchange and cleaner litter, not a hotter lamp. A university small-scale poultry housing page says dampness and ammonia build-up are signs there is not enough ventilation, and that birds handle cold well when they are dry and fully feathered. The same ideas show up in our winter condensation lesson for houses.

Vents high, drafts low

The small-flocks fact sheet recommends a vent or opening near the roof or ceiling so ammonia and stale air can leave, and warns that holes letting air blow directly on birds can chill them. Fresh air should mix with warmer inside air before it reaches the birds. A university backyard chickens guide says a good coop has enough air exchange without drafts. The backyard chickens fact sheet suggests checking doors and windows for drafts and inspecting for holes. Several extension pages also say wooden roosts are better than metal or plastic, because those hold cold, and that frostbite shows up as gray or black, brittle tissue on combs, wattles and feet.

Do healthy birds need extra heat?

Sources differ in tone. A university extension article on coop heat lamps says chickens do not need a heat source in winter if they are acclimated, kept without drafts and given dry bedding. The small-flocks fact sheet says adult birds protected from wind and wet should not need added heat, though young birds do. The backyard chickens fact sheet says supplemental heat may or may not be necessary and that hardy breeds in a dry, insulated, well-ventilated coop usually do not need it. The cold-weather care page and a university article on coop ventilation both say supplemental heat is advised below about 35 degrees F, without tying that to a breed. Small-bodied bantams and birds with large combs may need more care, so the decision depends on your flock.

Heat lamp fire risk and safer choices

The heat lamp article notes that chickens can knock a lamp into bedding. The cold-weather care page says never to hang a heat lamp by its cord, to follow the fire warnings and installation directions, and that a guard can help keep a bulb from touching bedding if a lamp falls. A university youth livestock page on heat lamp safety says to remove dust, cobwebs and dead insects from lamps, keep the lamp at least 20 inches from combustible bedding, secure it with a metal chain or clamp rather than twine, and never leave one unattended overnight. Straw and shavings, dust and a loose fixture are the usual fire path.

Safer choices named in the sources: a wall-mounted coop heater made for safety (the heat lamp article), radiant heat panels placed at nest-box or low-roost height (the coop ventilation article), or simply skipping heat for hardy birds. A university extension page on winter chicken care also says to use low-heat or LED light for extra daylight, since heat can start a fire. These pages do not rank products and we do not either.

Heated waterer bases

Liquid water matters in freezing weather. The cold-weather care page lists heated bases for metal waterers, heated plastic fonts and partly covered heated dog dishes, and says to keep the heating element away from bedding, set the water on a stand so birds do not scratch litter into it, and keep a spare waterer. The small-flocks fact sheet says a base must not damage the container or start a fire if it touches litter, and the backyard chickens fact sheet notes such devices can malfunction, so use them with caution. New Hampshire warns of fire and electrical shock risks. A heated base draws far less power than a lamp, as the example below shows.

Cords and GFCI

The cold-weather care page says to use extension cords only short term and to have a professional electrician do coop wiring; The backyard chickens fact sheet says the feed to the coop should be sturdy, safe and out of the birds' reach. The federal consumer product safety agency says an extension cord is for outdoor use only if marked for it, must be rated for the total wattage plugged in, is a short-term fix, and should be unplugged if it feels warm. The same agency explains a ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) watches the current out and back and cuts power if they differ by as little as 0.006 ampere, and says to test it monthly with the test button. Our own extension cord and GFCI lesson goes further. For a permanent coop, ask a qualified electrician; this page is not wiring instructions.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with our own assumptions: six hens, a 250 W lamp running 24 hours a day, versus a 125 W heated waterer base running 12 hours a day. Electric heat turns every watt into heat at 3.412 Btu/h per watt (a standard conversion). Wattages and run times are assumptions, not measurements.

  • Lamp energy: 250 W x 24 h = 6,000 Wh = 6.0 kWh per day.
  • Lamp heat rate: 250 W x 3.412 = about 853 Btu/h while on, and the same averaged over 24 hours.
  • Waterer energy: 125 W x 12 h = 1,500 Wh = 1.5 kWh per day.
  • Waterer heat rate: 125 W x 3.412 = about 427 Btu/h while on, or about 213 Btu/h averaged over 24 hours (427 x 12 / 24).
  • Comparison: 6.0 / 1.5 = 4, so the lamp uses four times the energy per day, and the lamp's heat is aimed at the air, not at the water. Daily heat: 6.0 kWh x 3,412 = about 20,500 Btu versus 1.5 kWh x 3,412 = about 5,100 Btu.

Vent area check. None of the extension pages we read gave a vent area per bird rule, so we state our own assumption: 1 square foot of vent opening per 10 birds. For 6 birds: 6 / 10 x 1 sq ft = 0.6 sq ft = 0.6 x 144 = 86.4 square inches, for example a 6 in by 14.4 in opening, placed high and covered with predator-proof wire. Treat it as a starting point; if the coop still smells of ammonia or shows moisture, add opening and clean the litter.

Field card: Backyard Chicken Coops in Winter: Ventilation, Heat Lamps and Cords

Dry air and no drafts first, heat last.

  • Wet litter makes ammonia and frostbite risk; fix moisture first.
  • Vents high above the roost; seal gaps at bird level.
  • Wood roosts, not metal or plastic.
  • Many healthy, hardy adults do not need extra heat; young birds do.
  • Heat lamp: never hang by the cord, keep it clean and clear of bedding, guard it, never leave it unattended.
  • Our example: 250 W for 24 h = 6.0 kWh and 853 Btu/h; 125 W for 12 h = 1.5 kWh and 427 Btu/h while on.
  • Cords: outdoor-marked, short term, rated for the load; GFCI tested monthly.
