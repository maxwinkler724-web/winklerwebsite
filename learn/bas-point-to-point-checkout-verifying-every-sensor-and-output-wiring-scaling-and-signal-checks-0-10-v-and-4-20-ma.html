BAS Point-to-Point Checkout: Verifying Every Sensor and Output

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before anyone tests a sequence of operations, someone has to prove that every wire lands where the drawings say and that the number on the screen means what it claims. That job is point-to-point checkout. This lesson covers what it is, what gets checked on each point, how to check analog inputs and outputs with a meter, and the faults it usually turns up.

What it is and how it differs from functional testing

A point is one input or output on a controller: a duct temperature, a damper command, a fan status. Point-to-point checkout (also called I/O checkout) follows each point from the field device, through the wiring, to the controller, to the value shown on the operator screen, and back out for outputs. Owner standards treat it as a required deliverable. One state university lists a "Point to Point Checkout" among the required as-built software documents and says each point "shall be tested for both hardware and software functionality" (Illinois State University BAS exhibit). A New Mexico state facilities authority checklist asks for seven checks on "each and every point," and says they do not replace the maker's own start-up procedures (NMPSFA BAS checklist).

Functional testing is the next layer. It asks whether the system does the right thing: does the economizer open, does the supply air reset follow the sequence. That only means something if the points feeding the sequence are trustworthy. A federal guide specification written for GSA in 1991 already said installation testing "shall not substitute" for final functional testing (NISTIR 4606). The two are separate steps, and checkout comes first. For the functional side, see BAS Commissioning Checklists and Functional Testing.

The checklist for each point

The seven items on the New Mexico checklist are wire, actuator movement, software address, program load, device calibration, sensor location and sensor calibration. Putting that together with the other standards we read, a practical per-point list looks like this (the grouping is ours):

  • Label. The device tag, the wire label at both ends and the point name in the controller should all match the drawings. One university standard requires wires to be labeled at both ends and landed on terminal blocks, and requires the owner to approve a nameplate and labeling schedule before labels are made (American University Division 25).
  • Wiring. Right terminal, right polarity, no unplanned splices, shield grounded at one end (the controller end in the American University standard). See BAS Analog Signals: 4-20 mA and 0-10 V for loop wiring.
  • Range and scaling. The low and high end of the signal must be entered in the controller as the sensor's real span. The American University standard says each point needs its offset, scaling factor, engineering units and output range programmed.
  • Sign (direction). Our own term: does a higher signal mean more of the thing? A reverse-acting actuator or a swapped scaling entry gives a reading or motion that is backward but smooth, so it hides well.
  • Failure state. What the point does when its signal or power is lost. The American University standard asks that an actuator have the correct fail position and stroke,.
  • Result and initials. The New Mexico form says to fill every field, using N/A if not involved.

Checking an analog input with a meter

The method is a three-way comparison: what a trusted reference says about the real condition, what the meter says about the signal, and what the controller displays. The New Mexico checklist records exactly this: BAS reading, instrument reading, and any offset. The American University standard puts the calibrated reference instrument within 6 inches of the site sensor, with readings taken at several points across the range.

  1. Note the real condition with a reference instrument, such as a calibrated thermometer or manometer.
  2. Measure the signal. For a 4-20 mA sensor, put the meter in series on the milliamp setting; for a voltage sensor, measure across the signal and common. Our analog signals lesson covers meter setup and the 250 ohm resistor method.
  3. Convert the signal to engineering units by hand and compare with the screen.
  4. If the screen and the hand math agree but the reference differs, the sensor is off, so record an offset or replace it. If the screen and the hand math disagree, the scaling in the controller is wrong.

That last split is the main time saver. The American University standard allows a sensor offset of no more than 5 percent of sensor span for temperature, humidity and CO2, and sends pressure sensors to a calibrated device connected in parallel. Project specifications set the real tolerance, and the New Mexico form says to use the accuracy in the specification.

Commanding outputs and watching the device move

For outputs, override the point from the operator screen and look at the device itself. The American University standard forces each analog output to 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent and checks the final element across its range. It checks valve position at closed, full open and several steps between, and replaces an actuator that does not match. The New Mexico checklist asks for full free movement and for simulated minimum and maximum signals to confirm the end positions.:

  • Does it travel the full stroke, or stop short?
  • Does it fail the right way when the signal is pulled?
  • Does the feedback value agree with what you see?

The American University standard warns against driving a 2-10 V actuator from a 0-10 V signal, so match the actuator's input range to the output type.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

Assumptions, ours and not from a product: a duct temperature transmitter is a 4-20 mA device ranged 0 to 100 F, a static pressure transmitter is 4-20 mA ranged 0 to 2 in. w.c., and a damper actuator takes 0-10 V with 0 V closed and 10 V open. The scaling rule is value = low + (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × span.

Temperature. The meter in series reads 12.8 mA. 12.8 − 4 = 8.8. 8.8 ÷ 16 = 0.55. 0.55 × 100 F = 55 F, plus a low of 0, so 55.0 F. The screen should show 55.0 F. If a reference thermometer reads 54.2 F, the sensor or its reading is 0.8 F high, which is a candidate offset of −0.8 F. If the screen shows something far from 55.0 F, check the scaling entered in the controller.

Pressure. The meter reads 7.2 mA. 7.2 − 4 = 3.2. 3.2 ÷ 16 = 0.20. 0.20 × 2 in. w.c. = 0.40 in. w.c.

Damper output. For a 0-10 V command, volts = percent ÷ 100 × 10. At 50 percent the meter should read 5 V, since 0.50 × 10 = 5 V. The test points are 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5 and 10 V for 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent. At the 50 percent step, the damper blades should be about half open; measure the real position if you can, not just the actuator pointer.

Sensor offsets and documentation

The American University standard lists an offset field for each point and a Point Name Summary Table. The New Mexico form shows a worked row: BAS 70.2 F, instrument 71.4 F, programmed offset +1.2 F. Record the instrument you used too: the American University standard requires NIST-traceable instruments calibrated within one year, with the calibration reports filed with the checkout sheets, and another of its sections wants test equipment at least twice as accurate as the device under test. Write down before and after readings, who did it, and the date. Later troubleshooting, trend review and retro-commissioning lean on that sheet.

Common faults checkout finds

  • Wrong point on the wrong terminal, so the screen shows a value that belongs to another device.
  • Scaling entered for the wrong range, such as 0-5 in. w.c. when the transmitter is ranged 0-2.
  • Reversed action, so 100 percent closes the device.
  • Input type set to current for a voltage device, or the reverse.
  • A signal below 4 mA, which usually means an open or unpowered loop rather than a low reading (see our analog signals lesson).
  • Actuator stroke that stops short, so the command says 100 percent while the device sits partly open.
  • A sensor in a poor location; the New Mexico checklist has a separate sensor-location check for this. Sensor drift is covered in BAS Sensor Types, Calibration Drift and Setpoint Habits.

Field card: BAS Point-to-Point Checkout

Reference, meter, screen: three readings must agree.

  • Checkout proves each point; functional testing proves the sequence. Checkout first.
  • Per point: label, wiring, range and scaling, sign, failure state, result.
  • 4-20 mA: value = low + (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × span.
  • 0-10 V command: 5 V is 50 percent; test at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent.
  • Screen matches hand math but not reference: sensor offset. Screen differs from hand math: scaling.
  • Use calibrated instruments and keep the sheet.
