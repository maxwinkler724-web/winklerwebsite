Shaft Keys, Keyways and Tapered Bushings on Fan and Pump Drives

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A sheave, fan hub or coupling half has to turn with its shaft without slipping, wobbling or working loose. On many fan and pump drives that job is split between a small steel key and a clamping fit. This lesson covers how a key works, what a good fit feels like, how a split tapered bushing grips, and how to take a hub off without hurting the shaft.

Stop and isolate first

Lock out before you touch any drive. Federal workplace safety rules treat adjusting, inspecting and cleaning machines as servicing work, and require stored or residual energy to be relieved or restrained after the locks go on, then isolation to be verified before work starts. A fan wheel can windmill on its own from airflow, and a belt, coupling or wheel can hold motion after the motor is off. Follow your site procedure and see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. Guards come back on before the unit is released.

What a key does

A key is a small bar that sits half in a slot cut in the shaft (the keyway) and half in a slot in the hub. The motor torque passes from the shaft, through the side of the key, into the hub. The key is not a clamp. It carries the turning force, and a hub that is also clamped or set down keeps the key from rolling and from sliding sideways.

A university shaft-design lecture note says keys fail in two ways: shear straight across the key, or crushing along the bearing face where the key side presses on the slot wall. The same note says to design for both and see which governs, and that the key should be the cheap weak link rather than something over-built. Treat that as a clue to what you find on the floor: a sheared key usually means the drive was overloaded or jammed, so find out why before you fit a new one.

Square keys and key length

Most fan and pump drives use a square key, whose width and height match, with the width matched to the keyway the shaft was cut with. The shaft diameter and the bore determine the key size, so read it off the bore, shaft and drive data rather than guessing from a drawer of loose key stock. Square stock sold by the foot is cut to length. Do not swap in a different cross-section just because it fits loosely.

Key length is a balance. The key should cover the hub's full bearing length for good support, but the same lecture note suggests the key length should not exceed about 1.5 times the shaft diameter to avoid twisting problems. For a 2 in shaft that guideline works out to 2 x 1.5 = 3 in. If the hub is longer than that, follow the drive maker's data for the key rather than running a long key by habit.

Checking key fit

A paper on coupling hub installation, written by a coupling engineer and presented at a university turbomachinery symposium, describes the fit in plain terms: the key should fit tightly in the shaft keyway, slide into the hub keyway, and leave radial clearance at the top. Use that as your check:

  • Snug side to side. The key should seat in the shaft keyway with no rocking and no visible gap at the sides. If you can wiggle it, the keyway or key is worn.
  • Small clearance on top. The key must not bottom against the top of the hub keyway. The same paper warns that a key set too high can split a hub, and that a sloppy key can roll or shear.
  • Not too tight. A key that must be hammered in builds stress and makes assembly hard.
  • Corners. A square-cornered key sitting on the rounded corner of a keyway can cut in and start a crack. The paper says to chamfer key corners so they do not ride on the keyway radii.

Use a proper key, not a piece cut from a bolt or a flat bar. The paper lists damaged keys replaced with square bar as a common problem because the corners can cut into keyway radii and shear.

Why a setscrew sits over the key

Many hubs have a setscrew threaded in directly over the key. Its job is to lock the hub axially so it cannot creep along the shaft and to take up the backlash between key and slot, not to carry the torque. The shaft-design note says a setscrew can be added to locate the key axially and reduce rotational backlash. A university mechatronics page adds that a setscrew has to be clamped hard to hold much torque, which may damage the shaft. That is a reason to treat the key as the driver and the setscrew as the locator, and to read the lesson on hex keys and setscrews for seating and thread detail instead of repeating it here.

Worn or rolled keyways and fretting

If the key or slot is loose, the hub rocks a tiny amount every time torque reverses or the load pulses. A university tribology page defines fretting as damage from small oscillating movement at a loaded contact, often driven by vibration, with wear debris trapped in the contact area. In a keyed hub that shows up as polished or dark powdery patches at the keyway edges, a keyway that is wider at the mouth, and a key whose edges look rolled over. The same page says fretting can start fatigue cracks, which is why a worn shaft keyway is more than a nuisance.

When you find worn keyways, a new key will not fix a wallowed slot. Replace or repair the hub or shaft per the equipment maker, and check alignment and vibration so the cause does not return.

Split tapered bushings

A split tapered bushing is a sleeve with a tapered outside and a split along its length. It sits inside a matching tapered bore in the hub. Cap screws that pass through the hub and thread into the bushing, or the reverse, pull the two tapers together. The bushing closes onto the shaft and wedges against the hub, so the grip is friction, with a key still used in the usual way. Because the clamp is only as even as your tightening, the screws must be brought up together.

The steps below are our own general sequence. The bushing maker's instructions, torque chart and gap values for your exact size always rule.

  1. Lock out and verify. Clean the shaft, bore, bushing taper and hub taper of oil, paint and burrs, and check that the key fits as above.
  2. Fit the bushing in the hub, align the holes, and set the hub on the shaft at its marked position with the key in place.
  3. Start the cap screws by hand, then snug them evenly.
  4. Tighten in passes, moving in rotation around the hub each time, to one third, then two thirds, then full of the maker's torque.
  5. Measure the remaining gap between the hub and the bushing flange at each screw. It should be even, and it should not be closed to zero.
  6. Recheck torque, then recheck again after a short run-in, as the maker directs.

Our own example: three cap screws, three passes

This is our own example, with assumed numbers. The values are not from any maker's chart. Always use the real torque and gap data for the bushing you have.

Assumptions: three cap screws; assumed full torque 90 in-lb per screw; assumed rule that the gap between the hub and bushing flange must stay at least 1.5 mm and that the three gaps must agree within 0.5 mm.

Torque in passes:

  • Pass 1, one third: 90 / 3 = 30 in-lb on each screw (30 / 12 = 2.5 ft-lb).
  • Pass 2, two thirds: 90 x 2 / 3 = 60 in-lb on each screw (5 ft-lb).
  • Pass 3, full: 90 in-lb on each screw (90 / 12 = 7.5 ft-lb).

Go around screw 1, 2, 3 in order on each pass, then repeat the full pass once more until the wrench stops turning the screw.

Gap check (assumed readings after pass 3): 2.6 mm, 2.4 mm, 2.5 mm.

  • Average: (2.6 + 2.4 + 2.5) / 3 = 7.5 / 3 = 2.5 mm.
  • Spread: 2.6 - 2.4 = 0.2 mm, which is under the 0.5 mm limit.
  • Margin above the minimum: 2.5 - 1.5 = 1.0 mm remaining, so the bushing has not bottomed out.

Result for this example: pass. If one gap read 1.2 mm while another read 2.8 mm, the spread of 1.6 mm would fail. Back the screws off and start again with a fresh sequence, because the hub is cocked on the taper.

Pulling a sheave or coupling hub without hammering

The hub-installation paper says plainly that hammering will always damage the coupling and can also bend shafts or damage bearings and seals. Its advice follows this order:

  • Remove keys, setscrews and bushing fasteners first, and loosen any retaining parts.
  • Use the puller holes in the hub, if it has them. Pull on the hub body, not a thin sleeve or rim.
  • On a straight shaft, put a spacer between the shaft end and the puller screw to protect the shaft end.
  • Apply steady force. If the hub still will not move, the paper gives a light soft-face tap as the limit before looking at heat, and heat is something to get the equipment maker's direction on first.

For a tapered bushing, back out the cap screws and use them in the removal holes as the maker shows, so the screws jack the bushing loose. For the full sheave swap, belts and speed math, use Changing a Fan Sheave and check belt tension afterward.

Field card: Shaft Keys, Keyways and Tapered Bushings

The key drives, the setscrew or bushing holds.

  • Lock out and verify before touching any drive; release stored energy.
  • Key snug side to side, a small gap on top, chamfered corners.
  • Use proper square key stock, not a cut bolt.
  • Length: stay near the hub length, and under about 1.5 times shaft diameter as a design guide.
  • Rolled corners, loose rocking or dark powder at the keyway mean wear or fretting.
  • Bushing screws: even passes at 1/3, 2/3 and full torque from the maker's chart.
  • Gaps equal at every screw and not closed to zero.
  • Pull hubs with a puller, never a hammer.
