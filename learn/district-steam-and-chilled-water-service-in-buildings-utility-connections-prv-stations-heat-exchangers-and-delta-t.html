District Steam and Chilled Water Service in Buildings: Utility Connections, PRV Stations, Heat Exchangers and Delta T

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some buildings have no boiler or chiller of their own. They buy steam or chilled water from a central plant down the street, and the mechanical room is built around that connection. This lesson walks through what a building does with district steam and chilled water, and why the utility cares so much about the temperature of what comes back.

What district energy is

The U.S. Department of Energy describes a district energy system as one or more central plants producing hot water, steam and/or chilled water, with insulated pipes carrying it to nearby buildings. Each building ties in through what DOE calls building interconnections, such as meters, valves and pumps. DOE says most U.S. systems use steam for heating, while chilled water is sent through closed loops for air conditioning. Steam can also run chillers inside a building. The building buys heat or cooling, not fuel or electricity for its own plant.

Who owns what at the entrance

The split is set by each utility, so always get the local rules. Con Edison's steam FAQ gives one clear example: the utility is responsible for traps upstream of its steam meters, and the building owner for traps downstream. The same page lists the shut-off points in order: the street service valve, the inside service valve (the first valve in the building), the meter stop valves, the trap isolation valves, and the pressure reducing station stop valves. A chilled water provider's FAQ (Emicool) draws the line differently: the provider maintains its primary network up to the energy transfer station or the building main valve, and the customer maintains everything inside its premises. Treat the meter and the first service valve as the usual dividing area, but do not assume.

District steam: from main to PRV station

District steam arrives hot and high. Con Edison says its steam is saturated at about 165 psig and 358 F. Nashville's district system describes roughly 145 to 150 psig at customers. Almost no building equipment is rated for that, so the steam passes through a pressure reducing valve (PRV) station. Nashville says its steam flow meter sits upstream of the PRV at every customer connection, and that drip legs with traps sit between the wall penetration and the PRV to catch condensate and reduce water hammer risk.

A typical station detail from Virginia Tech's design standards shows what is in one: an inlet valve, a strainer with a blowdown, two PRVs sharing the load (about one-third and two-thirds), traps that drain to a flash tank or receiver, a relief valve set at 15 psig that vents to the roof, and gauges and sensors tied to the building automation system. A University of Michigan guideline adds that the relief downstream of the PRV must vent to the outdoors and that the PRV should be insulated. For station basics, see PRV Station Basics.

Using the steam: direct or through a heat exchanger

Low-pressure steam can feed coils, radiators and process loads directly. For a hydronic heating loop, a steam-to-hot-water converter keeps utility steam and building water separate; see Steam to Hot Water Converters. Either way, the steam gives up its latent heat and becomes condensate at about the same temperature.

Condensate: return it or cool it

Some utilities take condensate back. Nashville's guide says condensate collects in a receiver and pumps return it to the plant. It notes that, unlike chilled water, there is no direct surcharge for poor condensate return, but that hot returns improve plant efficiency. Where condensate is not returned, Con Edison says it is tempered with city water before it goes to the sewer, and it lists ways to use the heat first, such as preheating domestic water through a heat exchanger or serving as cooling tower makeup. Con Edison's page does not state a drain temperature. As one general reference point, Minnesota's plumbing rule says water above 140 F may not be discharged under pressure directly into a drainage system. That is a state plumbing rule for water in general, not a district steam tariff, so the local utility and plumbing code decide the real limit. Reuse and return are covered in Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

Metering

Steam is sold by the thousand pounds, so the meter is the cash register. Con Edison says its vortex meters are calibrated every five years and others annually. Michigan's guideline prefers metering by gravity-type condensate meters, with flash tanks placed upstream of them. Chilled water is metered in energy: Emicool describes a BTU meter and billing in refrigeration ton-hours.

District chilled water: heat exchanger or direct

A building can connect to chilled water through a plate heat exchanger, which separates the utility loop from the building loop, or directly, with the utility water running through the building's coils. We did not find a source that ranks one over the other, so check the utility's connection standard. Texas A&M's campus standard shows the direct style in practice: supply is 43 to 47 F, coils must be designed for at least a 14 F temperature rise at 43 F supply, two-way control valves are required, and three-way bypass valves are not allowed in new work. Plate exchanger behavior is in Plate Heat Exchanger Fouling and Approach.

Why delta T is money

A chilled water plant is sized and billed around moving heat, and the heat moved equals flow times temperature rise. If water returns too cold, the plant pumps more water for the same cooling. Emicool's FAQ requires customers to keep an 8.9 C (about 16 F) difference between supply and return, and adds a 10 percent surcharge on the consumption charge for each degree C the building falls short. It names fouled heat exchangers as one cause. We could not confirm a comparable penalty at a U.S. steam or chilled water utility, so this is one provider's rule, not a universal one.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Our own example. Assumptions we chose: a heating load of 3,000,000 Btu/h; steam delivered to the equipment at 15 psig after the PRV; all the load is met by latent heat only, with no credit for cooling the condensate; no losses. A saturated steam table (Copper Development Association) lists latent heat of 945 Btu/lb at 15 psig.

  • Steam flow = 3,000,000 Btu/h divided by 945 Btu/lb = 3,175 lb/h, about 3.2 thousand pounds per hour.

Now chilled water. Assumptions: a 300 ton load; one ton is 12,000 Btu/h (ACHR News); the relation is Btu/h = 500 x gpm x delta T for water (MEP Academy shows 500 = 8.33 lb/gal x 60 min/h x 1 Btu/lb-F).

  • Load = 300 x 12,000 = 3,600,000 Btu/h.
  • At a 12 F delta T: gpm = 3,600,000 / (500 x 12) = 600 gpm.
  • At an 8 F delta T: gpm = 3,600,000 / (500 x 8) = 900 gpm.

The same 300 tons needs 50 percent more water when delta T falls from 12 F to 8 F. That means bigger pumps, bigger pipes and a plant that runs at lower efficiency, which is why the utility writes delta T into its rules.

Safety and lockout at the service valves

Do not touch the utility's valves on your own. Con Edison says its inside service valve should be closed only in an emergency, is wire-sealed, and must be reopened by the utility, and that you should call before changing PRVs or welding. It also notes condensate can build up behind a closed valve, so reopening takes care. Isolation, venting and lockout steps follow your site procedures and the utility's, and the building side valves listed above are the points a plan should name. Steam fundamentals are in Steam System Fundamentals.

Field card: District Steam and Chilled Water Service

Meter in, PRV down, exchanger out, watch the return.

  • District energy sells steam, hot water or chilled water from a central plant.
  • Typical steam order: service valve, meter, strainer, PRV station, equipment, traps.
  • Ask the utility who owns what; one utility's split is not another's.
  • Condensate is returned or cooled before drain; the drain limit is local.
  • Chilled water: Btu/h = 500 x gpm x delta T; low delta T means more gpm.
  • Utility valves are wire-sealed or utility-operated; call first.
