Running a Heat Pump on a Generator: Starting Surge, LRA, Strip Lockout and Safe Hookup

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump looks like a modest load until the power goes out in January and someone asks whether the portable generator in the garage can run it. The answer depends on three nameplate numbers, one big resistance heater, and how the generator is connected. This lesson walks through all three and ends with an estimate for a made-up 3-ton system.

Check the plate and the maker

Check the equipment. The numbers in the worked example are our own assumptions, not data for any real unit. Your outdoor unit, air handler and generator each have their own data plate and instructions. Use those, and have a licensed electrician do any generator wiring. This page is general education, not a design.

Step 1: Read RLA, LRA and MCA

The outdoor unit plate lists the compressor in amps. RLA (rated load amps) is the current the compressor draws at its rated running condition. LRA (locked rotor amps) is the current when the rotor is not yet turning, which is the moment of starting. MCA (minimum circuit ampacity) is a wire-sizing number for the whole unit, built from the compressor, fan and other loads; it is not a measure of running or starting watts. Our lesson on reading an HVAC nameplate shows how those plate numbers fit together, so we will not repeat it here.

For generator work you mostly want two things: RLA plus the fan and blower amps for the running load, and LRA for the starting load. Multiply amps by the supply voltage (about 230 V for most single-phase residential equipment) to get volt-amps, which is close enough to watts for a rough estimate. Because RLA is a rating, not a measurement, this overstates a lightly loaded compressor a little. That is the safe direction for sizing.

Step 2: Starting surge versus running watts

A motor that is starting draws far more than it does at speed. A university extension bulletin on motor loads reports that an induction motor generally draws five to eight times its running current while starting, and that the surge lasts only a few seconds at most. A separate agricultural extension publication reports that starting amperage runs from two to seven times the nameplate running current, and gives a rule of thumb that a generator should be about three times the wattage of the largest motor it must start. A third extension publication on portable generators gives about three times the operating current as a typical figure and warns that inverter generators are especially sensitive to startup overloads. Those three sources do not agree exactly, which is a reminder that the multiple depends on the motor. For a compressor, the plate LRA is the better number than any rule of thumb, because it is written on the equipment.

Two practical points follow. First, the surge is brief, but a generator that cannot supply it will sag in voltage, and a compressor on low voltage starts slowly and keeps drawing the high current longer, as the first source notes. Second, generators carry two ratings: a continuous (running) rating and a higher short-term surge rating. Compare your running load to the first and your starting load to the second, and read the generator maker's own surge number rather than assuming one.

Step 3: The strips are the big load

If the heat pump has electric backup heat, the strip kit is usually the largest single load in the house. Resistance heat converts every watt to heat, and the U.S. Energy Information Administration lists 1 kilowatt-hour as 3,412 Btu. A 10 kW strip kit therefore makes about 34,120 Btu/h (10 x 3,412) and draws about 10,000 W, or roughly 41.7 A at 240 V (10,000 / 240). That is more than the entire compressor, fan and blower combined. Our lesson on backup heat strips covers the sizing and staging of those kits.

On a generator the strips are a problem because on many systems they can come on automatically on a call from the thermostat or controls, and the controls do not know the generator is small. For that reason we suggest locking them out for the duration of the outage. Options we have seen include a thermostat setting that disables auxiliary heat, a dedicated strip breaker left off and clearly tagged, or a lockout built into the transfer arrangement by the electrician. Which method is right depends on how your system is wired, so ask the installer; do not pull wires to defeat it. The tradeoff is real: with strips locked out, a heat pump in very cold weather may not hold the house temperature, and you will need to plan for that.

Inverter-driven units and soft starters

The U.S. Department of Energy notes that inverter-driven systems can modulate their speed and capacity at near-infinite degrees. Our understanding is that these drives typically bring the compressor up to speed gradually instead of connecting it straight across the line, so the starting surge can be far smaller than the LRA of a single-speed compressor, but that is our own reading and the maker's data decides. The maker's electrical data, not the outdoor-unit size alone, tells you what the starting current actually is. Still, an inverter unit also has control electronics that may not like poor-quality generator power, so check the installation manual for generator guidance before you connect it.

A single-speed unit can be given a gentler start with a soft starter or a hard-start kit that is approved for that equipment. Either approach lowers the peak current at startup, but neither changes the running load. The reduction depends on the device, so use the device maker's published figure for your RLA range. Our lesson on hard start kits and potential relays covers the components and their limits. A time-delay relay that staggers motor starts is the extension publications' related tip: do not start motors together, and start the largest first.

Worked example (our own assumptions)

These inputs are assumptions made up for the lesson, not data for a real unit: a 3-ton single-speed heat pump on 230 V single-phase; compressor RLA 16.0 A and LRA 82 A; outdoor fan 1.5 A; indoor blower 4.0 A; a 10 kW strip kit; and a portable generator with a 7,500 W continuous rating. The generator's surge rating is not given because we are not assuming one.

  1. Running load, strips locked out: 16.0 + 1.5 + 4.0 = 21.5 A. 21.5 A x 230 V = 4,945 VA, call it 4,950. That is about 66 percent of 7,500 W (4,950 / 7,500 = 0.66), so it fits with room for a refrigerator and lights.
  2. Running load, strips also on: 4,950 + 10,000 = 14,950 VA. That is about twice the generator rating (14,950 / 7,500 = 1.99). Locking the strips out is not optional with this generator.
  3. Starting load at full LRA: the blower and fan are already running, so add their 5.5 A to the compressor LRA: 82 + 5.5 = 87.5 A. 87.5 A x 230 V = 20,125 VA. That is about 2.7 times the 7,500 W rating (20,125 / 7,500 = 2.68). Even if the surge lasts only a moment, we would not expect this generator to start the compressor this way.
  4. Starting load with a soft starter (assumed): suppose the starter holds peak current to 2 x RLA, that is 32 A (an assumption; use the maker's figure). 32 + 5.5 = 37.5 A. 37.5 A x 230 V = 8,625 VA. That is above the continuous rating but could be within a generator's short-term surge rating, which you must confirm from the generator maker.

Result of the estimate: with the strips locked out and a soft starter, the 7,500 W generator might handle this heat pump, but only with the surge rating confirmed and with little else running during the start. As a plain line-start unit it likely would not. A larger generator, or an inverter-driven heat pump with a documented low starting current, changes the answer.

Connect it safely: transfer switch or interlock only

Never plug a generator into a wall outlet to feed house wiring. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission calls this extremely dangerous to utility workers and neighbors, and says it bypasses household circuit protection. The Illinois extension page explains that the purpose of a transfer switch is to stop generator power from flowing back to utility lines, where it can endanger line workers, and says a qualified electrician should install the generator and the switch. The Florida extension publication adds that the switch should be sized for the maximum current the circuit will carry. A heat pump is a hardwired 240 V load, so it needs a properly installed transfer switch or a listed interlock kit installed to the manufacturer's instructions, with the generator inlet and cord rated for the current. A heat pump should never be powered through a homemade cord with two male plugs.

Carbon monoxide: outdoors and away from openings

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says to keep a generator outside at least 20 feet from any doors, windows or vents, and never to use one inside the home or garage even if doors and windows are open. The Consumer Product Safety Commission adds that carbon monoxide can linger in a garage for hours after the generator shuts off, and recommends battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms. Our lesson on carbon monoxide in winter outages goes further.

What to run first

  1. Start the generator with nothing connected, then add loads one at a time. The Illinois extension page gives the same advice to avoid overloading the unit.
  2. Put the refrigerator, freezer, lights and phone chargers on first. They are small, and they protect food and communication.
  3. Confirm the strip lockout is in place.
  4. Bring on the heat pump last, with the biggest motor starting first and nothing else starting at the same moment.
  5. If the generator bogs down, voltage sags or the breaker trips, shut the heat pump off and reduce other loads before another try.

If your furnace or boiler is the heat source rather than a heat pump, see running a furnace or boiler on a generator.

Field card: Running a Heat Pump on a Generator

Strips off first, soft start second, switch installed third.

  • RLA plus fan and blower amps gives running load; LRA gives the starting peak.
  • Volts x amps gives volt-amps, close to watts for a rough estimate.
  • 1 kW of resistance heat is 3,412 Btu/h; a 10 kW kit draws about 10,000 W.
  • Lock out the strips so a generator never sees them.
  • Soft starters and inverter drives cut the start peak; use the maker's figures.
  • Use an installed transfer switch or listed interlock, never a wall-outlet backfeed.
  • Generator outside, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents.
  • Add loads one at a time; heat pump last.
