Winter Storage in a Home Garage: Car, Motorcycle or Boat Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Putting a car, motorcycle or boat away for the winter looks like a parking job, but a home garage shares air, ductwork and burners with the house. This lesson covers the heating, air and safety side: no warm-up idling, battery charging, fuel near burners, moisture under a cover, rodents and carbon monoxide alarms.

Never idle or warm up an engine in the garage

A federal consumer product safety agency says plainly: never leave a car running in an attached garage, even with the garage door open. A federal public health agency gives the same warning for any garage attached to a house, and says that only for a detached garage should you open the door for fresh air when you run the vehicle. A consumer safety booklet from the first agency adds that running a car or generator in an attached garage can cause fatal poisoning inside the home. Carbon monoxide has no smell, so a person cannot tell when the level is climbing.

For storage, that means you pull the vehicle out onto the driveway before you start it, and you back it in only after the engine is off. A motorcycle and a boat on a trailer count too. See attached garages and your home heating and cooling for how fumes travel through the shared wall, garage ducts and return leaks.

Why short winter start-ups do more harm than good

Our own view, not from a source: a few minutes of running in cold weather does not bring the engine oil and exhaust system up to full temperature. Moisture and unburned fuel from those short runs can stay in the crankcase and the exhaust instead of cooking off. The run also drains the battery a little and adds carbon monoxide risk if it happens in the garage. If you want to check on a stored vehicle, we think a better plan is to prepare it properly in the fall (follow the owner's manual) and leave it alone until a full outdoor drive in spring.

Battery maintainers and chargers

A battery maintainer (sometimes called a float or trickle charger) supplies a small charge so a stored battery does not sag over months. Three safety points matter at home.

  • Hydrogen. A federal workplace safety page on battery charging says hydrogen from charging is potentially explosive if it builds up in a closed area. It calls for adequate ventilation and says to prevent open flames, sparks and electrical arcs in the charging area. A federal workplace safety rule for construction work also says ventilation must be provided to diffuse gases from batteries, and puts unsealed batteries in vented enclosures or well-ventilated rooms. Those are workplace rules, and applying them to a garage is our own reading. Lead-acid batteries can give off hydrogen while charging, so charge in a ventilated spot away from flames, sparks and gas appliance burners, including a water heater or furnace pilot or burner.
  • Use the right charger. Use the charger the battery maker calls for. Lead-acid, sealed and lithium types charge differently, so we do not mix them. Check cords and clamps before use and connect them in the order the maker gives.
  • Do not hide it. Keep a maintainer where you can see it, off flammable clutter and away from the fuel can shelf. A garage with a bigger battery bank is a different case; see battery room ventilation.

Keep fuel-filled vehicles away from water heater and furnace burners

A federal consumer product safety agency says never to store gas cans inside a house or basement or near ignition sources such as fuel-burning appliances, open flames and pilot lights, and explains that fuel vapor escaping a container can ignite if it meets a spark or flame. A staff report from the same agency on gas appliance vapor fires says gasoline vapor is more than twice as dense as air, so it settles low and drifts toward a burner, and that about 1.3 percent vapor in air is enough to reach the flammable limit. That report also describes the garage rule that treats the space up to 18 inches above the floor as a hazardous zone.

For a stored vehicle, a full tank, a boat with portable tanks and a motorcycle can all leak or vent a little. Park them as far as you can from a gas water heater or furnace, and keep the burners raised or shielded per the maker. Our own tip: do not store spare fuel with the vehicle. See flammable vapors and gas appliances at home for the longer version.

Garage humidity and condensation under a cover

A federal environmental agency says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent and ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and explains that condensation forms when moisture in the air meets a chilled surface. Its home guide says condensation is a sign of high humidity and suggests lowering humidity, improving airflow and using dehumidifiers when needed. A cold metal body or boat hull in a damp garage is exactly that chilled surface.

  • Breathable cover. A cover that lets moisture escape is our own pick over a plastic tarp, which can trap damp air against the paint. Keep the cover clean and dry when it goes on.
  • Dehumidifier. Our own rule of thumb: pick a unit rated for a space at least as large as the garage and check the maker's lowest operating temperature, because many units ice up in a cold garage. Plan a drain hose or a bucket you will empty.
  • Measure. A cheap humidity meter shows whether the garage stays under that 60 percent line. For the basement version of this idea, see basement dehumidifier sizing.

Rodents in stored vehicles and near duct openings

An extension pest-management page says rats nest in stored materials in garages, gnaw engine wiring, and that a weighted car cover can help keep them out of the engine area. It says to seal holes larger than 1/4 inch, and a federal public health agency says mice can fit through a hole about the width of a pencil (1/4 inch), recommending steel wool with caulk or spray foam for small holes and hardware cloth or sheet metal for larger ones. The extension page lists 1/2-inch mesh screening for vent grills.

Our own application: garage floor registers, return openings and cracks around duct boots are also entry points, because rodents that move into a vehicle can move on to the house. Look at them before storing the vehicle, and keep food, pet feed and bird seed out of the garage. Do not seal over combustion air openings or return paths without a pro; ask a technician first.

CO alarms by the house door

A federal consumer product safety agency recommends alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, not near heating vents or where furniture or drapes could cover them. Alarms also have a replacement age from the maker, and the test button checks the circuit, not the sensor. Our own addition for garage storage: place an alarm on the house side near the door to the garage, in addition to one outside bedrooms. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.

Worked example (our own)

These are our own assumptions, not sourced figures, so swap in the numbers from your own labels.

Maintainer energy. Assume a maintainer averages 4 watts and stays plugged in for 150 days.

  1. Hours: 150 days x 24 hours = 3,600 hours.
  2. Energy: 4 W x 3,600 h = 14,400 watt-hours.
  3. Convert: 14,400 / 1,000 = 14.4 kilowatt-hours for the winter.

Dehumidifier water. Assume a 20 ft x 20 ft x 8 ft garage (20 x 20 x 8 = 3,200 cubic feet), and a unit whose label says 35 pints per day when running at its rated condition. A cold garage will pull less, so treat this as an upper figure. Assume it runs 25 percent of the day for 150 days.

  1. Daily water: 35 pints x 0.25 = 8.75 pints per day.
  2. Season: 8.75 x 150 = 1,312.5 pints.
  3. Gallons: 1,312.5 / 8 pints per gallon = about 164 gallons.

That is a lot of water, which is why a drain hose to a floor drain or sump beats a bucket. If the humidity meter stays under 60 percent, the unit is doing its job.

Field card: Winter Storage in a Home Garage

Engine off in the garage, charge in fresh air, fuel away from burners.

  • Never idle or warm up an engine in a garage, even with the door open.
  • Skip short winter start-ups; prepare in the fall and drive it out in spring (our own).
  • Charge lead-acid batteries in a ventilated spot, away from flames, sparks and burners, with the maker's charger.
  • Keep fuel and fuel-filled vehicles away from water heater and furnace burners.
  • Aim for indoor humidity below 60 percent; use a breathable cover and a dehumidifier sized for the space.
  • Seal gaps over 1/4 inch and check duct openings for rodents.
  • CO alarm by the house door and outside bedrooms.
