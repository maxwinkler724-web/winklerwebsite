Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems (DOAS): Decoupling Ventilation, Dehumidification and Energy Recovery

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dedicated outdoor air system, or DOAS, is a unit whose only job is to treat the fresh air a building needs: filter it, dry it, cool or warm it, and hand it to the rooms. Everything else, the day-to-day room temperature, is left to other equipment. This lesson explains why designers split the work that way, what the numbers look like, and what to check when one misbehaves.

Check the design documents

Check the design documents. This page is general education built from maker literature, university and national laboratory material read in 2026. Setpoints, sequences and code limits differ by project and by code edition. Follow the engineer's drawings and the maker's manual for the unit in front of you.

What a DOAS is

Trane's white paper describes a DOAS as a unit that can dry 100 percent outdoor air to a dew point drier than the space it serves. A 2009 HPAC Engineering article by Wolf and Smith describes the same idea from the design side: the unit conditions outside air, cooling and drying it in summer and warming it in winter, so the building can meet ASHRAE Standard 62.1 ventilation requirements in a way that is easy to verify. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) report on very high efficiency DOAS describes the split as separating heating and cooling from the outdoor air ventilation system so each can be controlled on its own.

Why split ventilation from room cooling

Outdoor air in summer carries a lot of moisture, and that moisture (the latent load) is stubborn. Wolf and Smith say a DOAS is sized for the space latent load, which decouples space sensible load from latent load and lets the building hold humidity even when the temperature load is light. Trane's white paper puts the failure side bluntly: it warns that condensate problems typically show up in systems that fail to decouple the space sensible and latent loads. The local equipment, whether fan coils, heat pumps or beams, can then be sized and run for sensible cooling only. John Murphy of Trane, writing for ASHRAE's IAQ Applications, lists fan coils, water source heat pumps, small packaged units and chilled ceilings or beams as typical local equipment that treats recirculated room air while the DOAS handles outdoor air.

Wolf and Smith add that the DOAS runs whenever the building is occupied, while local units run only on a call for conditioning. PNNL's very high efficiency DOAS program pairs the unit with a VRF or heat pump system and a high efficiency heat or energy recovery ventilator. Chilled beams are a good partner because they cannot dry the air, which is covered in our chilled beams lesson.

Dew point targets as sources state them

There is no single number. Here is what each source we read says:

  • Wolf and Smith (2009): supply air is dried to a dew point below the room dew point, typically 45 to 52 °F.
  • Trane white paper (2025): the AHRI 920 rating test requires a leaving dew point of no more than 55 °F at its first test condition, and the paper's example compares a 55 °F dew point conventional rooftop with a 45 °F dew point DOAS.
  • Yale's DOAS requirements: a discharge dew point in dehumidification mode that does not exceed 55 °F, and for active chilled beams a dry bulb of 50 to 65 °F. This is one owner's standard, not a universal rule.

Neutral air or cold, dry air

After the cooling coil dries the air, it leaves cold. A designer then picks one of two paths, and both Murphy and Wolf and Smith describe them.

  • Neutral delivery. Reheat brings the air back to about room temperature. Murphy says this wastes the sensible cooling the DOAS already did, and the local units must carry more.
  • Cold, dry delivery. The air goes out without reheat, so its low temperature offsets part of the room sensible load. Wolf and Smith say this can cut local unit size by up to 40 percent in a typical classroom wing. Trane's white paper example shows terminal capacity falling from 1.55 to 0.87 tons (44 percent) with cold delivery.

The tradeoff is overcooling at light load. Murphy says reheat is used only when needed to prevent that, and the control section below shows how.

Energy recovery in a DOAS

Wolf and Smith say that in non-arid climates a total energy wheel can reduce mechanical cooling by 3 to 4 tons per 1,000 cfm of outdoor air, and that plate exchangers mainly provide sensible preconditioning. Murphy recommends an air-to-air energy recovery device to precondition outdoor air. PNNL's program modeled recovery ventilators with a 70 percent sensible effectiveness baseline and 82 percent in the best practice package, with electric preheat for defrost when leaving exhaust air falls below 35 °F. The mechanics of wheels, purge and frost are in our energy recovery wheel lesson.

Worked example: 2,000 cfm in July

This is an invented case. Outdoor air is 95 °F and 120 grains of moisture per pound of dry air. The coil must deliver 52 °F and 55 grains. A 55-grain air at 52 °F is close to saturated, so this is roughly a 52 °F dew point, inside the ranges above. (Our own check of that dew point is approximate.) The constants are the usual rule-of-thumb factors: 1.08 for sensible and 0.68 for latent. See Psychrometrics for Technicians for where they come from.

Our own arithmetic:

  1. Sensible temperature drop: 95 − 52 = 43 °F.
  2. Sensible load: 1.08 × 2,000 × 43 = 92,880 Btu/h.
  3. Moisture removed: 120 − 55 = 65 grains per pound.
  4. Latent load: 0.68 × 2,000 × 65 = 88,400 Btu/h.
  5. Total: 92,880 + 88,400 = 181,280 Btu/h.
  6. Tons: 181,280 ÷ 12,000 = 15.1 tons.

Notice the latent part is almost half the load. A room cooling unit would have to carry that moisture too, which is the case for decoupling.

Now add a 70 percent effective wheel. Assume the wheel's 70 percent is a sensible effectiveness and equal airflows, and return air is 75 °F. Our own estimate:

  1. Maximum change: 95 − 75 = 20 °F.
  2. Actual change: 0.70 × 20 = 14 °F.
  3. Outdoor air after the wheel: 95 − 14 = 81 °F.
  4. Sensible coil load becomes 1.08 × 2,000 × (81 − 52) = 1.08 × 2,000 × 29 = 62,640 Btu/h, a drop of 30,240 Btu/h (2.5 tons).

An enthalpy wheel would also reduce the latent load, but we did not use a latent effectiveness here because none is stated. Use the maker's rated data for a real unit.

For cold delivery, our own comparison: air leaving at 52 °F into a 75 °F room can absorb 1.08 × 2,000 × (75 − 52) = 49,680 Btu/h of room sensible load before it warms to room temperature, which is the credit the local units receive.

Controls

  • Dew point and dehumidistat. Wolf and Smith describe a room humidity signal that lowers the DOAS discharge dew point when humidity is high and returns it to normal when humidity is acceptable. Murphy says dew point is controlled independently of dry bulb.
  • Discharge temperature reset. Murphy says a building automation system can find the critical space, the one closest to overcooling, and raise the DOAS leaving temperature only enough to protect it. Yale's standard resets discharge on thermostat votes and returns to neutral 70 °F heating and 75 °F cooling air when votes are satisfied.
  • CO2 reset. PNNL's program opens zone dampers toward full ventilation as zone CO2 rises toward a setpoint, with CO2 sensors required in zones with 15 or more people per 1,000 square feet. Yale controls airflow to hold CO2 on the exhaust duct. See demand-controlled ventilation.

Service checks

  • Wheel. Airxchange's manual recommends inspecting the wheel once a year while running and stopped. Check that the belt tracks near the rim center and is not twisted or badly worn, that the motor draws no more than nameplate full load amps, that the wheel turns clockwise from the pulley side and spins freely, and that diameter seals just touch the media. Cleaning intervals run from 8 to 10 years in clean air to 1 to 2 years in dirty air. Lock out the power first.
  • Filters. Trane lists filtration options from MERV 8 to 14, and PNNL's program calls for MERV 13.
  • Coil and reheat. Yale lists hot gas reheat, a wrap-around coil or a hot water or steam coil as dehumidification options. Our own advice: check the coil is clean, the reheat actually fires when called, and condensate drains freely. Condensate and moisture trouble is what Trane's paper ties to poor decoupling.
  • Sensors. Our own advice: because dew point control is the whole point, compare the unit's dew point and temperature readings against a trusted handheld on a regular schedule. Yale also requires an outdoor air monitor, so airflow readings deserve checking.

Field card: Dedicated Outdoor Air Systems

The DOAS dries and ventilates; local units only cool the room.

  • Sensible Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature change.
  • Latent Btu/h = 0.68 × cfm × grains-per-pound change.
  • 12,000 Btu/h = 1 ton. Example: 2,000 cfm, 95 °F/120 gr to 52 °F/55 gr = about 15.1 tons.
  • Sources give leaving dew points of about 45 to 55 °F; the design documents decide.
  • Neutral air costs reheat; cold, dry air shrinks local units but risks overcooling.
  • Service: wheel belt, motor, seals and rotation, filters, coil, reheat, dew point and CO2 sensors.
