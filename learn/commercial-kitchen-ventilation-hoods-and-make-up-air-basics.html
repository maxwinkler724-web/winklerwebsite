Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A kitchen hood is only half of a system. Every bit of air the exhaust fan removes has to come back in from somewhere, and when it does not, the whole kitchen starts to misbehave.

The three working parts

A commercial kitchen ventilation system has a hood over the cooking line, ductwork and an exhaust fan that carries the air outside, and a make-up air supply that replaces what was removed. A vendor guide describes the fan as creating negative pressure that pulls contaminated air through the ductwork and out through the roof. Fan size depends on the cooking equipment below it.

Type I and Type II hoods

Mechanical code summaries describe two hood types. Type I hoods go over equipment that makes grease or smoke, such as fryers, griddles and charbroilers. They carry grease filters and need grease-rated ductwork. Type II hoods go over equipment that makes steam, heat or combustion products, such as dishwashers and ovens, and have less strict requirements. Which hood belongs over which appliance is set by the code and the appliance maker, not by guesswork.

Make-up air

One vendor guide puts the idea simply: what the fan removes must be replaced. Make-up air brings in outside air, often tempered or conditioned, to balance the kitchen. A trade-magazine code overview says make-up air replaces exhausted air, helps cool hot exhaust gases and keeps the kitchen slightly negative relative to nearby spaces so cooking odors do not drift into the dining room. Note the word slightly. A kitchen pulled far negative is a problem, not a feature.

Balance and negative pressure symptoms

A vendor article lists what an unbalanced kitchen can cause: back-drafting, doors that are hard to open, disrupted pilot lights and reduced exhaust effectiveness. A hood that cannot capture smoke may be starved for air rather than too weak. This is why a tech called for a smoky hood also looks at the make-up air unit, its filters, dampers and interlocks. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics and Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics.

Numbers vary by code and appliance

Hood exhaust rates depend on the appliance type, hood type and duty level (light, medium, heavy or extra heavy). The trade-magazine overview, summarizing a model mechanical code, gives as labeled examples a minimum general exhaust rate of 0.70 cfm per square foot and a limit of 0.02 inch of water column for building pressure from make-up air. These are not design values for your job. Your adopted local code, the hood listing and the engineer's drawings set the real ones, and a tech should not change fan speeds or dampers to chase a number without that paperwork.

Grease is the fire hazard

Grease filters catch grease before it reaches the duct, and the grease cup collects drips. One vendor says a full or missing grease cup is a fire hazard. NFPA 96 is the standard for ventilation control and fire protection of commercial cooking operations. A fire-protection company describes it as covering design, installation, inspection, testing and maintenance, with the aim of preventing grease buildup. A hood-equipment vendor summarizes cleaning intervals that depend on cooking volume, from monthly for solid-fuel cooking to annually for low-volume kitchens, and says cleaning is done by a qualified hood-cleaning company. That is a vendor summary, so the current NFPA 96 edition and the local fire official have the final word.

Fire suppression is a separate trade

A hood-mounted fire-suppression system is a code requirement, but installing, testing and servicing it is its own licensed work. An HVAC tech should not adjust, discharge or disable it. If a suppression system or its fuel shutoff interlocks need attention, the owner calls the licensed fire-protection company. Never bypass a safety interlock to get a fan running.

Working safely around hoods

Lock out and tag the fan and make-up unit before opening anything (Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC). Greasy surfaces, roof edges and hot cooking equipment are all hazards on this job.

Field card: Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics

A kitchen hood is only half of a system.

  • Type I = grease or smoke hoods (fryers, griddles, charbroilers); Type II = steam and heat hoods (dishwashers, ovens).
  • Exhaust out means make-up air in; the kitchen should be only slightly negative.
  • Warning signs of imbalance: back-drafting, hard-to-open doors, disturbed pilots, weak hood capture.
  • Check the make-up air unit when a hood will not capture smoke.
  • Grease filters and cups must be kept clean; cleaning is by a qualified hood-cleaning company under NFPA 96 and local rules.
  • Fire suppression belongs to licensed fire-protection trades; never bypass or disturb it.
