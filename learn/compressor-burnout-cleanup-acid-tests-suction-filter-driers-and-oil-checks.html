Compressor Burnout Cleanup: Acid Tests, Suction Filter-Driers and Oil Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a hermetic compressor motor burns, the winding insulation breaks down and leaves acid, sludge and carbon in the oil. If you only swap the compressor, the new one inherits that mess. This lesson walks through how Sporlan and Emerson describe the cleanup: decide how bad it is, protect the new compressor with driers, and prove the oil is clean before you leave.

Follow the maker first

Check the equipment maker. Both makers read for this page say the unit manufacturer's own cleanup procedure comes first when one exists. Sporlan's bulletin says to use it "wherever possible." What follows is general education built from Sporlan and Emerson literature, not a replacement for the instructions on your job.

Confirm it really burned

Sporlan's first step is to confirm a burnout with proper electrical tests. A compressor that will not run is not automatically burned. Use the winding checks in Checking Compressor Windings before you commit to a full cleanup, because cleanup costs time and parts.

Mild or severe

Sporlan sorts burnouts by testing the compressor's oil. If the acid is within the limits of its test kit, it calls the burnout mild. Its words for the rest: "All other cases should be considered a severe burnout." Emerson's instruction sheet describes a severe burnout through what you can see and smell: discolored oil, an acid odor, and contamination products on both the high and low sides. Emerson also warns you to avoid breathing acid vapors and to avoid contact with contaminated liquid, so treat a burned system as hazardous and follow the maker's safety notes.

Acid and oil testing

Sporlan lists two test kits. The TA-1 is a one-time pass or fail kit. The AK-3 is reusable and can give pass or fail or a measured acid level. Sporlan says both work on mineral, alkylbenzene and POE oils. Emerson uses its own Acid Alert kit for the follow-up tests. Emerson also shows a way to take an oil sample without opening the system: a horizontal suction line with a Schrader fitting, the male fitting on the bottom of the line.

Whichever kit you use, read its directions. Kits differ in how much oil they need and how they show a result.

Recover the refrigerant, and decide if it is worth keeping

Sporlan says that after a severe burnout the refrigerant is usually not worth saving. Emerson says a mild burnout may allow saving the charge if the compressor has service valves. Either way the refrigerant must be recovered into a proper cylinder, not vented. See Refrigerant Recovery Step by Step for the how-to.

The cleanup sequence

Emerson's sheet lists these steps for a suction-line drier cleanup, shown here in plain terms:

  1. Judge how bad the burnout is.
  2. Clean and replace system controls and remove old strainers and driers.
  3. Install the replacement compressor and complete an electrical check.
  4. Clean the suction line near the compressor and install a suction line drier.
  5. Pressure test and leak test per the unit maker.
  6. Evacuate to at least 500 microns, then break the vacuum with clean, dry nitrogen at 0 psig.
  7. Charge through a liquid line drier, per the equipment maker.
  8. Start the compressor and record the pressure drop across the suction drier; replace it if the drop becomes excessive. Watch for the first 4 hours.
  9. After 48 hours, test the oil. If it shows acid, replace both the liquid and suction line driers.
  10. After about 2 weeks, if the oil is still discolored, replace both driers.

Emerson calls the cleanup complete when the oil is clean, odor-free and passes the acid test. For the evacuation steps see Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics. For the nitrogen you use while brazing new fittings, see Nitrogen Purge Flow While Brazing.

Sporlan's version for a severe burnout is similar. It puts a suction line filter-drier directly ahead of the new compressor and also an oversized liquid line filter-drier, then asks you to evacuate and charge per the maker, check the suction drier's pressure drop in the first few hours, add a moisture indicator, and take a second oil sample after about 24 hours. For a mild burnout Sporlan calls for an oversized liquid line Catch-All. Note the different timing: Sporlan says about 24 hours and Emerson says 48 hours. Use the one in the maker's procedure for your equipment.

Why a suction drier

Compressor makers want a drier on the suction side because that is the last place to catch contaminants before they reach the new compressor. Sporlan says a suction line drier must be larger than a liquid line one, since refrigerant moves faster in the suction line. Sporlan recommends its HH charcoal core for burnout cleanup. Bryan Orr, writing on a trade blog, adds a practical point: pick a drier with test ports on both sides, so you can measure the drop at every visit. Read Filter Driers and Sight Glasses for the basics on liquid line driers.

Pressure drop and when to take it out

A suction drier that clogs starves the compressor and lowers suction pressure. So you measure inlet and outlet pressure with the same gauge and subtract. The limit depends on the drier and the application. Examples we read:

  • Danfoss's fitter's guide gives suction-line limits in bar: 0.50 for air conditioning, 0.25 for refrigeration and 0.15 for freezing. It says to replace a suction filter before the drop passes them.
  • Danfoss's guide for its burnout drier (type DAS) gives lower, temperature-based numbers in psi. For R-22 at a 40°F evaporator it says to replace the drier if the drop across the core exceeds 3.00 psi, after you record the first reading and check again at 72 hours.
  • Emerson tells you to record the drop at startup, then replace the drier if the drop becomes excessive. It gives no psi figure in its sheet.

Those numbers do not match, so find the figure for the exact drier. Sporlan's bulletin has its own table of maximum recommended drop, which we could not read clearly here; check it at the source.

As for removal: Sporlan says the cores may need several changes. Once cleanup is done, it says to swap to its low pressure drop element so the permanent drop is as small as possible. Danfoss says that once the oil check shows no acid, the liquid line burnout filter can be swapped for an ordinary drier and the suction burnout core can be removed. In the same spirit, trade podcast notes on a Copeland episode say a suction drier used for cleanup must come out within a few days.

Worked example: drop and temperature penalty

An R-22 air conditioning system has a new suction filter-drier. You read 71.5 psig at the inlet port and 68.6 psig at the outlet port.

  1. Pressure drop = inlet minus outlet = 71.5 − 68.6 = 2.9 psi.
  2. Convert the Danfoss air conditioning limit: 0.50 bar × 14.504 psi per bar = 7.25 psi. 2.9 is under 7.25, so it passes. It is also under the 3.00 psi that Danfoss gives for its own DAS drier at 40°F, though only by a little, so recheck it.
  3. Convert to temperature with the Chemours R-22 pressure-temperature guide. 71.5 psig is listed at 42°F. 68.6 psig is listed at 40°F.
  4. Penalty = 42 − 40 = 2°F. The compressor sees a suction that is 2°F colder than the coil.

Now suppose the outlet had read 62.9 psig. The drop is 71.5 − 62.9 = 8.6 psi, which is over 7.25. The Chemours chart lists 62.9 psig at 36°F, so the penalty is 42 − 36 = 6°F. That drier is restricted and should be replaced. Because gauges read gauge pressure at both ports, you subtract directly with no atmospheric correction. You can review the pressure and temperature link in Pressure-Temperature Basics.

Flushing and oil type

Sporlan's bulletin and Emerson's sheet do not describe a flushing procedure, so this page does not teach one. Bryan Orr, writing on a trade blog, says to remove or flush as much contaminated oil as the application allows, including from traps, separators and accumulators. If your unit maker gives a flush procedure, follow it.

POE oils take on moisture, so keep the system closed and use the driers the maker names. Sporlan makes RCW high water capacity cores designed for use with POE oils, and also suggests them for a ruptured water-cooled condenser or other high-moisture cases. Emerson lists POE among approved fluids and says anything else needs written approval from its engineers. See Refrigerant Oils.

Field card: Compressor Burnout Cleanup

Prove the burn, protect the new compressor, then prove the oil.

  • Confirm a burnout with electrical tests first.
  • Use the unit maker's cleanup procedure if there is one.
  • Test the old oil for acid. Acid within limits: mild. Otherwise: severe (Sporlan).
  • Severe: suction drier next to the compressor plus a liquid line drier.
  • Evacuate to 500 microns or lower and break with dry nitrogen (Emerson).
  • Pressure drop = inlet minus outlet psig. Check at startup and in the first hours.
  • Retest oil at 24 or 48 hours as the maker says. Acid: replace driers.
  • Done when oil is clean, odor-free and passes the acid test.
