Suction Line Accumulators and Liquid Receivers: What They Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Both parts are steel tanks in the refrigerant piping, and both get mixed up. They do opposite jobs on opposite sides of the system, and each one changes how you judge a charge. This lesson sorts out which is which.

Two tanks, two jobs

Parker describes the two this way in its product literature. An accumulator is there to catch and hold any unused portion of the system charge and to protect the compressor from liquid entering it. A receiver is a reservoir for refrigerant while the system runs, a spare supply for heavy load, and a place to store the charge during pump-down.

The suction line accumulator

Liquid refrigerant in the compressor is a problem because it washes oil off the bearings and compressors do not pump liquid well. Copeland's instruction sheet for its accumulators says the part protects against liquid arriving during reversing flow in a heat pump, after a defrost in refrigeration, and during temporary flooding back from the system.

Where it sits. Copeland says to mount it as close to the compressor as possible in the main suction line, upright, and ahead of any vibration absorber. On a heat pump, Copeland and Refrigeration Research both place it between the reversing valve and the compressor. It sweats in operation, so Copeland says to insulate it after start-up.

Oil return hole. ACHR News describes a vessel with a U-shaped outlet tube. A small orifice at the bottom bend picks up a little oil and some liquid and meters it back into the gas stream. A second small hole near the top of the tube is a pressure equalizer, so the liquid column is not pulled into the compressor at start-up. Refrigeration Research says heat pump models use smaller metering orifices, and ACHR News warns that a standard unit on a heat pump can return too much liquid in winter. If sludge or wax clogs the orifice, the oil stays in the tank and the compressor starves, so a clogged accumulator is replaced with the cleanup. For that cleanup, see Compressor Burnout Cleanup: Acid Tests, Suction Filter-Driers and Oil Checks.

Heat pumps in defrost. ACHR News notes that defrost often sends a surge of liquid down the suction line. That surge is the reason heat pump accumulators exist. The defrost sequence itself is covered in Defrost Cycle and Auxiliary Heat.

It is a safety margin, not a repair. One ACHR News article says an accumulator should not be used to cover for a system that keeps returning liquid. Find the cause, such as an overcharge, a dirty evaporator or a bad metering device.

The liquid receiver

The receiver goes on the liquid line after the condenser. It holds extra charge so the system can handle changing loads and so the charge has a place to go during pump-down. A trade blog's discussion of subcooling says receivers should be no more than about 80 percent full, even when pumped down. Which systems get one is a design choice, so check the maker's data for the unit in front of you.

How each changes charging

Receiver. On a plain system, extra charge backs up in the condenser and subcooling climbs. With a receiver, that trade blog says the extra refrigerant goes into the tank, so subcooling stays fairly flat until the receiver is nearly full, and by then the system may be overcharged. A subcooling number alone can mislead. The usual alternative is the receiver level, read through a sight glass with the system running, since a glass can also look clear when the system is off. Always follow the maker's charging label or manual for the unit, because the sight glass method differs between makers. Subcooling basics are in Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Accumulator. A trade blog says liquid added through the suction side collects in the accumulator and leaves only as it boils off, so readings respond slowly and it is easy to overcharge. Its advice is to meter in vapor, add charge in small steps, and let the system settle. On piston (fixed orifice) systems there is no sensing bulb to hold superheat steady, which is why those are touchier than a TXV system; see TXV Bulb Placement, Equalizers and Adjusting Superheat. For commercial boxes, Refrigeration Superheat Checks and Oil Return covers oil return.

Worked example: a 50 percent sizing check

ACHR News says the liquid-holding capacity should normally be at least 50 percent of the system charge. Refrigeration Research's catalog gives a similar floor of 50 percent (it words it as system capacity) and says to confirm by test where possible. Suppose a nameplate shows 9 lb 8 oz of R-410A, and you want to compare against a maker's liquid-holding rating given in volume. The charge and unit are an example, not a specific product.

  1. Charge: 9 lb 8 oz = 9.5 lb.
  2. Minimum liquid to hold: 9.5 x 0.50 = 4.75 lb.
  3. Liquid density of R-410A at 77°F from Arkema's property table: 66.09 lb/ft³.
  4. Volume: 4.75 / 66.09 = 0.0719 ft³.
  5. In inches: 0.0719 x 1728 = 124 in³. In liters: 0.0719 x 28.317 = 2.04 L.
  6. For comparison, holding the full charge: 9.5 / 66.09 = 0.1437 ft³ = 4.07 L.

So an accumulator rated for less than about 2 L of liquid would fail the 50 percent rule on this system. Compare against the maker's stated liquid capacity, not the shell size, and let the maker's selection table decide, since it also limits pressure drop and oil return.

Rust-through leaks

ACHR News says accumulators are usually steel and can run below the dew point, so rust leading to a leak is a common problem. Welding or brazing can burn off the rust-preventive paint near the connections, and that article suggests recoating exposed steel. A trade site, High Performance HVAC, says a rusted-out accumulator was the most common heat pump leak the author repaired. That is one tech's experience, not a statistic. The repair is to recover the refrigerant, cut out the old tank and pipe in a new one, then leak test the whole system, as that author advises.

When they are replaced

  • The shell or fusible plug leaks. Copeland says a leaking fusible plug means replacement, not repair.
  • The orifice is clogged by sludge or wax (ACHR News).
  • A standard accumulator was fitted on a heat pump (ACHR News).
  • It is too small for the charge (ACHR News).

Before opening any system, recover refrigerant per your EPA 608 certification and company procedure.

Field card: Accumulators and Receivers

Accumulator guards the compressor. Receiver stores charge.

  • Accumulator: suction line, near the compressor; on a heat pump, between reversing valve and compressor.
  • Receiver: liquid line after the condenser; keep it under about 80 percent full.
  • Accumulator has an oil return orifice; a clogged one starves the compressor.
  • Rule: liquid capacity at least 50 percent of charge (example: 9.5 lb gives 4.75 lb, about 2.04 L of R-410A).
  • Receiver systems: subcooling stays flat as you add charge; follow the maker's method.
  • Accumulator systems: add charge in small steps and let it settle.
  • Rusted or leaking shell: replace, do not patch.
