Spray Paint Booth HVAC: Airflow, Make-Up Air, Filters and Interlocks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A spray booth is a box with a fan on one end, a pile of filters, and a long list of safety rules. For an HVAC tech the job is mostly air: how much leaves, how much comes back in, how warm it is, and what stops the gun from firing if the fan quits. Auto body shops are the common New Jersey setting, so we use one for the example.

Check the rules that apply to you

Check the issuing agency. Rules and permits change. This page was built from federal and state pages read in 2026 and is general education, not legal or fire-code advice. NFPA 33 (the national standard for spray application) is named here in plain text only; we have not quoted it. The local fire official and the booth maker's manual decide what your booth must meet.

Booth types in plain words

The name tells you which way the air travels past the work. In a cross-draft booth, air enters through filters in one end wall (or the open door), flows horizontally past the vehicle and leaves through filters in the far wall. In a side-draft booth the air crosses from one side wall to the other. In a downdraft booth, air drops from a filtered ceiling plenum, passes the vehicle and leaves through a grated pit or floor below it. Those are our own plain descriptions. NIOSH recommends downdraft booths over cross-draft or semi-downdraft ones, because overspray is carried away from the painter; in its study overspray concentrations were 15 times lower in a downdraft booth. NIOSH adds that a downdraft booth only works well when it is maintained, and that painters still need respiratory protection.

What OSHA says about booth airflow

OSHA has two standards that touch spray booths. 29 CFR 1910.94(c) covers the ventilation numbers, and 29 CFR 1910.107 covers the fire and operating rules for spray finishing.

  • Velocity. Under 1910.94(c)(6)(i), unless the booth has an adequate air replacement system, the velocity into every booth opening has to meet Table G-10. For air-operated guns with cross-drafts up to 50 feet per minute (fpm), the design velocity is 100 fpm for a large booth and 150 fpm for a small one. The table also gives a compliance range around each design value, and says cross-drafts over 100 fpm should not be permitted. We do not repeat the table here; read the full table and its notes at the OSHA link below for your gun type and booth size.
  • Where it is measured. The page says velocity is measured in the booth cross-section upstream, or above, the object being sprayed.
  • Solvent dilution. 1910.94(c)(6)(ii) also requires the total exhaust to dilute solvent vapor to at most 25 percent of its lower explosive limit. That is a second check on the exhaust number, and the material being sprayed decides it.
  • Fan running. 1910.107(d)(2) says mechanical ventilation must stay on at all times while spraying is going on, and keep running after spraying long enough to clear vapors from drying work.
  • Exhaust path. Each booth gets its own exhaust duct to the outdoors (with limited exceptions), the terminal must keep clear of combustible walls and roofs (1910.107(d)(8)), and spray exhaust must not contaminate make-up air or other intakes (1910.107(d)(9)).

Make-up air: replace what the fan removes

1910.94(c)(7)(i) says clean fresh air, free of contamination from nearby exhaust systems, must be supplied in quantities equal to the volume exhausted through the booth. Self-closing doors, dampers or louvers have to be fully open during spraying, and the air through them may not exceed 200 fpm (1910.94(c)(7)(ii)). The general idea matches what we cover in make-up air units and kitchen hoods, so we will not repeat the unit types here. What is special about a booth is the pressure target. For full-vehicle booths the EPA auto body rule (40 CFR 63, Subpart HHHHHH, known as 6H) requires ventilation at negative pressure, and it allows a fully enclosed booth with sealed openings and an automatic pressure balancing system to run at up to 0.05 inch of water column positive. So in practice a technician trims the supply a little under the exhaust so the booth stays slightly negative, and does not push overspray into the shop.

Heat comes next. 1910.94(c)(7)(iv) says make-up air must be heated where outdoor temperatures may stay below 55°F for appreciable periods, and the replacement air has to be kept at not less than 65°F at the point it enters the booth or room. A directly-fired or indirect unit can do this; the lesson linked above explains how each is controlled.

Filters and the pressure gauge

There are two filter banks and they do different jobs. Intake filters clean the incoming air so dust does not land in wet paint. OSHA says that if the supply is filtered, fan static pressure has to be figured with the filters dirty, and that a pressure gauge must show the pressure drop across them, marked at the point where the filters need cleaning or replacing (1910.94(c)(7)(iii)). Filters should be changed when the pressure drop gets excessive or when airflow falls below the Table G-10 values. A manometer or a dial gauge on a tap before and after the bank does that job; the static pressure basics lesson shows how to read one. Our practical habit: write the clean-filter reading on the gauge at startup, so a loading filter bank is obvious later. Do not invent your own change-out number; use the one the maker marks.

Exhaust filters catch overspray. For auto body, 6H requires filter technology shown to capture at least 98 percent of paint overspray in every spray booth and prep station, with the test method named in the rule (water wash booths run per the maker's specifications are exempt). OSHA also requires sprinkler protection on both sides of the filters and noncombustible or low-combustibility filters (1910.107(b)(5)). Used filters have to go to a safe place or a water-filled metal container.

A loaded exhaust bank raises resistance and the fan moves less air, so booth velocity falls. That is why the gauge and a periodic velocity check belong together. For measuring, see the pitot traverse lesson.

Interlocks: no fan, no spraying

This part needs care. The OSHA text we read has explicit interlock wording in specific cases: electrostatic hand spray equipment must not operate unless the ventilation fans are running (1910.107(i)(8)); fixed electrostatic apparatus must cut high voltage with no time delay if the fans stop (1910.107(h)(9)); and drying units with open flame or spark-producing heat need a purge of the space before the heater starts and must shut down if ventilation fails (1910.107(j)(3)). Infrared dryers inside a spray enclosure need interlocks including a purge of at least 3 minutes (1910.107(j)(4)(iii)). We did not find a line in OSHA's text that wires every spray-gun air supply to the fan. The general rule is the 1910.107(d)(2) requirement that the fan stay on while spraying. Many shops and local fire officials ask for the interlock anyway under NFPA 33 and their local fire code, so ask your fire official what is required and wire to the booth maker's diagram. A common method, which is our own description and not an OSHA quote, is an airflow proving switch or differential pressure switch in the exhaust that must close before the spray air solenoid or the burner can run.

Heated cure cycles

Many booths switch between a spray mode and a heated cure or bake mode. OSHA's 1910.107(j)(1) says drying, curing and fusion ovens must also meet NFPA 86A-1969 where it applies (an older edition, so ask the fire official which edition governs), and 1910.107(d)(12) says freshly sprayed articles may dry only in spaces with adequate ventilation, otherwise the drying space is treated as a spray area. Hot surfaces and heaters must not sit where combustible residue can pile up (1910.107(c)(3)). The purge-before-heat and shutdown-on-fan-failure rules above apply to the burner in this mode.

Worked example: our own cross-draft booth

Every input here is an assumption unless a source is named.

  1. Cross-section (our own assumption): booth 14 ft wide by 9 ft high, so 14 × 9 = 126 sq ft.
  2. Velocity (cited): 100 fpm, the Table G-10 design value for air-operated guns with up to 50 fpm cross-draft, large booth. Our own assumption: this booth counts as a large booth under the table's notes; confirm that against the table.
  3. Exhaust: 126 sq ft × 100 fpm = 12,600 cfm.
  4. Make-up air: equal to the exhaust per OSHA, so 12,600 cfm, trimmed slightly lower in the field to keep the booth a little negative.
  5. Winter design temperature (our own assumption): 14°F outdoors, a stand-in for a northern New Jersey winter condition; use your local design value.
  6. Leaving-air temperature (cited): 65°F, the OSHA minimum. Temperature rise = 65 − 14 = 51°F.
  7. Load: Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT = 1.08 × 12,600 × 51 = 1.08 × 642,600 = 694,008, about 694,000 Btu/h output. (The 1.08 factor is explained in our formulas lesson; it assumes standard air.)

Two checks. First, if the unit is about 80 percent efficient (our own assumption), input would be 694,008 / 0.80, about 867,500 Btu/h. That matters in New Jersey because the NJDEP auto body exemption form is for shops that keep coating use at or under half a gallon an hour and have no heating device rated 1,000,000 Btu/h or more in the spray booth; a larger heater means the shop has to list air permits. The form does not say whether a separate make-up unit counts, so confirm with NJDEP. Second, this ignores the solvent dilution check, filter loading, and heat for curing, which usually set a bigger number.

Field card: Spray Paint Booth HVAC

Exhaust sets the number; make-up air matches it; the gauge tells you when it drifts.

  • Exhaust cfm = booth cross-section (sq ft) × Table G-10 velocity (fpm).
  • Make-up air equals exhaust, trimmed so the booth stays slightly negative.
  • Heat make-up air where it may stay below 55°F outdoors; keep it at 65°F or more entering.
  • Heating load Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT (standard air).
  • Fan stays on while spraying (1910.107(d)(2)); interlock requirements come from OSHA's specific cases, NFPA 33 and the local fire official.
  • Gauge across the filters, marked by the maker at the change point; log the clean reading.
  • 6H for auto body: 98 percent overspray capture filters, negative pressure for vehicle booths.
