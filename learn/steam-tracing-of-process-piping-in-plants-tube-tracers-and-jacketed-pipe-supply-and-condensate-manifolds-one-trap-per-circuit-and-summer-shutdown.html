Steam Tracing of Process Piping in Plants

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Some pipes carry fluids that must stay above a certain temperature, and the plant already has steam. Steam tracing runs small steam lines along the pipe to replace the heat it loses. This lesson covers why lines are traced, the main tracer types, the supply and condensate manifolds, why every tracer gets its own trap, how long a circuit can be, and what it costs when tracers run for no reason.

Why lines are traced

A U.S. Army cold-regions laboratory digest on heat tracing says steam tracing is used primarily for freeze and pour-point protection. A technical sheet from a steam-equipment trade association lists three jobs: maintaining a process temperature, protecting against freezing, and controlling viscosity. Fluids that get thick and hard to pump when cold need this, and so do fluids that must stay molten (the sheet gives sulfur lines kept at 270 to 300°F). That sheet adds that steam tracing suits low flash point fluids because it makes no spark, and that it is practical where a steam system already exists.

Safety. Tracers and traps run at steam temperature (about 293°F in our example). Do not open, test or work on a live tracer circuit unless you are trained and authorized, and follow your site's lockout and hot-work rules.

Tracer types

  • Tube tracer. A small copper or stainless tube, commonly in the 3/8 to 3/4 inch range, strapped to the pipe. With no added filler it heats mostly through the air gap, so the trade-association sheet calls it convection tracing and says it mostly serves seasonal freeze protection, not tight temperature control.
  • Tube with heat-transfer cement. The same tube is bedded in a conductive cement or compound that adds metal-to-metal contact, which raises the heat that reaches the pipe. A maker's specification guide says compounds differ, so check their conductance data, and that they must be cured and kept dry before insulation goes on. That guide favors compound tracers for fast heat-up after a shutdown and for most places where jacketed pipe is normally used.
  • Channel tracer. A partial jacket, bolted on, that increases the contact area.
  • Jacketed pipe. A steam jacket around the full circumference gives the most heat transfer of the four, according to the trade-association sheet.

Manifolds, and one trap for every circuit

A steam supply manifold takes one steam line and splits it into several tracer circuits, each with its own isolation valve. A maker's specification guide asks for prefabricated manifolds where three or more tracers share a header, puts a strainer and isolation valve on each, adds spare connections (15 percent of the total), and keeps manifolds reachable. At the other end, a condensate collection manifold lets several tracers drain in a small footprint, according to the trade-association sheet. The key point is that the manifold collects condensate after each tracer has been trapped. It does not replace the traps.

Each tracer circuit gets its own trap at the end, and jacketed pipe drains each jacket to its own trap. Three separate sources we read say the same thing: a trap-maker's steam conservation guide says unit trapping is required to prevent short circuiting; a maker's specification guide says each tracer is served by a single trap at the end of the circuit; and the trade-association sheet warns that tying several tracers to one trap will almost always cause drainage problems. The reason is that circuits with different heat demands have different pressure drops. The trade-association sheet says low-demand lines short-circuit steam while others back up with condensate and go cold.

Circuit length and tracer pressure

A tracer loses pressure as steam flows, and its steam condenses along the way, so a tracer can only be so long before its far end runs cold. A maker's specification guide sets a limit of 30 percent of the supply pressure as the allowed drop, with circuits generally designed for 10 percent. It says that if a pipe run is longer than the maximum trapping length, a new tracer is started to continue the tracing. As one example of an actual cap, an engineering project specification we read allows 3/8 or 1/2 inch tracers with a maximum traced length of 37 meters, about 120 feet. Other specifications set other lengths, so use the design guide for your tube size and pressure, not a single number.

Pressure is a trade. The maker's guide notes that low-pressure steam carries the most latent heat per pound, but the trap pressure has to be high enough to push condensate to the collection system. It also says low-pressure systems are more prone to freezing where ambient temperature drops below -20°F, and advises supplying the highest available pressure and reducing it at a pressure-reducing valve. A steam table shows the latent heat effect: about 915 Btu/lb at 60 psia and about 889 Btu/lb at 100 psia.

Insulation over the tracer

The pipe and tracer are insulated together. The maker's guide says to pick the next larger insulation size that fits both the pipe and the tracer, and it applies a 10 percent minimum safety factor on heat loss. The U.S. Department of Energy says insulation can cut losses by about 90 percent and that damaged or wet insulation should be repaired or replaced. For general insulation background, see Pipe Insulation: Steam Heat Loss, Chilled Water Condensation and Vapor Retarders.

Worked example (our own example)

Everything marked assumed is our own choice for the example, not plant data. We find the heat loss of 100 ft of insulated 2 inch pipe, then the steam a tracer must supply.

  1. DOE bare-pipe table. DOE Steam Tip Sheet 2 lists 235 MMBtu per year lost from 100 ft of bare 2 inch steam line at 15 psig, for horizontal pipe in 75°F air with no wind, running 8,760 h a year.
  2. Per hour. 235,000,000 Btu ÷ 8,760 h = 26,826 Btu/h per 100 ft, or 268 Btu/h per foot.
  3. Temperature difference in that table. A steam table gives 250.3°F saturation at 30 psia (15 psig is about 29.7 psia). We take the bare pipe surface as about 250°F, so the difference is 250 − 75 = 175°F (our reading, since the sheet does not print it).
  4. Our assumed pipe. The pipe holds fluid at 60°F (assumed) in 0°F air (assumed): a difference of 60°F.
  5. Scale to 60°F. We scale the loss in proportion to temperature difference, a rough approximation: 268.3 × 60 ÷ 175 = 92.0 Btu/h per foot bare.
  6. Insulated. Using DOE's 90 percent reduction: 92.0 × (1 − 0.90) = 9.2 Btu/h per foot. With the 10 percent safety factor from the maker's guide: 9.2 × 1.10 = 10.1 Btu/h per foot, or about 1,012 Btu/h for 100 ft.
  7. Tracer steam pressure. We pick about 45 psig, or 60 psia (assumed). The steam table lists latent heat (hfg) of 915.4 Btu/lb at 60 psia, with saturation at 292.7°F.
  8. Steam flow. 1,012 Btu/h ÷ 915.4 Btu/lb = about 1.1 lb/h for the 100 ft circuit, assuming the trap passes condensate at steam temperature and ignoring the tracer's own losses.

Run all year, that is 1.1 × 8,760 = about 9,700 lb of steam. Real designs add the tracer tube's own losses and other heat paths, so treat 1.1 lb/h as a floor, not a design value.

Energy waste: summer and failed traps

The maker's guide says to avoid keeping a tracing system live when there is no heat requirement. A tracer left on in July keeps condensing steam (and passing it to the trap) for no benefit. The CRREL digest adds that condensate in traps can freeze during a shutdown unless the traps are self-draining or drained by hand, so winter shutdowns need a drain-down plan.

A failed-open trap is the bigger leak. DOE Steam Tip Sheet 1 says plants that go three to five years without trap maintenance may have 15 to 30 percent of traps failed, and under 5 percent when maintained. Its orifice table lists a 1/16 inch hole at 100 psig losing 13.2 lb/h (discharge to atmosphere assumed). Our own comparison: 13.2 ÷ 1.1 = about 12 times the steam our whole example circuit needs. Over 8,760 h that is 115,600 lb, which is about $1,160 at the DOE example's assumed $10 per 1,000 lb. Back pressure in a real condensate manifold would reduce the leak, so this is an upper estimate. Learn the checks in Steam Trap Testing: Sight, Sound and Temperature.

Steam tracing versus electric heat trace

The CRREL digest says steam is generally more expensive to install and maintain than electric resistance tape, though its energy cost is lower. It also notes traps are among the costliest maintenance items. The trade-association sheet adds that steam is practical where a steam system already exists. For sizing and ground-fault protection of electric cable, see Heat Trace Cable on Pipes.

Field card: Steam Tracing of Process Piping in Plants

One tracer, one trap.

  • Traced for freeze protection, pumpable viscosity and temperature holding.
  • Types: tube, tube with heat-transfer cement, channel, jacketed pipe.
  • Supply manifold splits the steam; condensate manifold collects after each trap.
  • Never group tracers on one trap: unequal pressure drops back up condensate.
  • Keep circuits within the design pressure drop; start a new tracer when the run is too long.
  • Insulate pipe and tracer together; fix wet insulation.
  • Shut off unneeded tracers in summer; test traps on a schedule.
  • Our example: about 1,012 Btu/h and 1.1 lb/h of steam per 100 ft at a 60°F difference.
