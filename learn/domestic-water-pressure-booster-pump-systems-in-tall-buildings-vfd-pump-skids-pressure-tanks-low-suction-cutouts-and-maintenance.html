Domestic Water Booster Pump Systems in Tall Buildings: VFD Skids, Pressure Tanks, Low Suction Cutouts and Maintenance

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The city main can only push water so high. In a tall building the top floors need help, and that help is a booster pump skid in the basement. This lesson explains why buildings need one, how a multi-pump variable speed skid decides what to do, why the low suction cutout exists, and what a tech checks during maintenance. It is about domestic cold water, not heating or chilled water loops.

Why a building needs a booster

Water pressure falls as water goes uphill. A high-rise design article from PHCP Pros says that with floors 10 feet apart you lose or gain roughly 4.33 psi per floor, which works out to 0.433 psi per foot of height. On top of that, every foot of pipe, every fitting, the water meter and the backflow assembly at the service eat pressure through friction. The PHCP Pros article says the pressure available at the main is typically not enough for the upper floors, so the pressure is raised at the building entry. Whatever is left after those losses has to still meet the minimum the top fixture needs to work.

What a multi-pump skid is

A booster skid is a factory-built frame carrying two or three pumps, a common suction header, a discharge header, isolation valves, check valves, gauges, a pressure transmitter and a control panel. One university design standard we read calls for at least two pumps, a pressure transmitter with a 4 to 20 mA signal on the discharge header, panel gauges on suction and discharge, and isolation valves so a pump can be serviced while the skid keeps running. Maker literature describes the common arrangement: a variable speed lead pump holds a pressure setpoint, and when the lead pump cannot do it alone a lag pump starts after a time delay. When demand falls, pumps stage off in reverse.

Lead, lag and standby

Lead is the pump the controller uses first, lag pumps join as load rises, and standby is a spare that is ready if another pump fails. A Bell & Gossett submittal says a lag pump auto-starts if the lead pump fails. An Armstrong sequence of operation says the lead pump and staging order rotate on an adjustable timer (default 24 hours, adjustable 1 to 168 hours), and that a standby pump is rotated in as well so all pumps wear evenly. Rotation matters to a tech because an idle pump with a dead motor or a stuck check valve would otherwise be found only on the day it is needed.

Constant pressure control

The controller reads the discharge pressure transmitter and changes pump speed to hold the setpoint no matter whether one fixture or the whole building is drawing water. The Armstrong document also describes a lower setpoint at light load that rises as pump power rises, so the pumps do not overshoot at low flow. It describes what happens if the main sensor fails: the controller can switch to a remote sensor, or run at a default speed. A bad transmitter is therefore a real cause of strange pressure, and the sensor is the first thing to check before blaming a pump. For how VFDs behave on pumps in general, see the related VFD lesson.

The pressure tank on the skid

Many skids include a hydropneumatic tank. The Bell & Gossett document says the pumps shut off automatically at low demand when the tank can supply it, with no external flow switch needed. The university standard describes the lead pump speeding up to recharge the system and tank before shutting down. Armstrong describes a no-flow shutdown that waits for a delay, trims speed, and shuts the lead pump down if pressure shows no demand; the skid restarts when pressure falls a set amount (5 psi in that document) below setpoint.

A diaphragm or bladder tank holds air on one side and water on the other. The precharge is the air pressure in the tank with no water in it. A tank maker's manual we read says to set precharge about 2 psi below the pump cut-in pressure, to make adjustments only with the tank empty of water and the system off, and to do so at ambient temperature. Another tank maker's manual says to shut off power, drain the tank and read the air valve with a standard tire gauge, and to check the air charge every six months. The skid's own manual sets its precharge number, so use that, not the example.

The low suction pressure cutout

Pumps take water from the building service. If a main break or heavy demand drops the city pressure, the pumps could run with too little pressure at their suction. The Bell & Gossett document describes the low suction cutout as protection against pumps running with insufficient suction pressure, and utilities can require one on the booster. A Virginia American Water commercial requirements poster says facilities with booster pumps must have a low suction pressure cutoff that shuts the pump off when pressure in the distribution system drops to a minimum of 20 psi, and that the sensor must not be set lower than 10 psi gauge per the Virginia Department of Health. That is one Virginia utility, not a New Jersey rule. Your own water utility sets its own number, so ask it. The Armstrong document describes how a cutout behaves: the skid alarms and shuts down when suction pressure stays at or below setpoint for 10 seconds and resets automatically 2 psi above it. The university standard also asks for an auto-reset low suction shutdown circuit with a delay timer.

Worked example: how much boost does a 12-story building need

All numbers below marked as assumptions are Our own for illustration; they are not code values or design values for any real building.

  1. Height: 12 floors at an assumed 10 ft per floor is 120 ft from the booster to the top fixture. (Assumption: the top fixture is 12 floors above the skid.)
  2. Static head: 120 ft × 0.433 psi/ft = 51.96 psi, call it 52.0 psi.
  3. Fixture pressure: assume 25 psi needed at the top fixture. (Assumption.)
  4. Friction and device losses: assume 12 psi total for piping, meter, backflow assembly and valves at design flow. (Assumption.)
  5. Pressure needed at the skid discharge: 52.0 + 25 + 12 = 89.0 psi.
  6. Minimum city pressure at the service: assume 30 psi on the worst day. (Assumption.)
  7. Boost the pumps must add: 89.0 − 30 = 59.0 psi at design flow.

Two side effects follow. First, 89 psi at the skid is more than the 80 psi limit the PHCP Pros article uses for building zones, so lower floors need pressure reducing valve stations. The article notes that zones usually span four to eight floors and that many contractors put the zone on the lower levels. Second, a low suction cutout set near a utility's 20 psi leaves only 10 psi of margin below our assumed 30 psi minimum, so a main problem would shut the skid quickly. A real design would also add margin and be sized by an engineer.

PRVs, backflow and check valves

PRVs on the lower floors keep fixture pressure under the zone limit; the PHCP Pros article gives a PRV outlet setting between 40 and 75 psi in its examples. A backflow assembly at the service keeps booster-side water from returning to the main. The same American Water poster says backflow assemblies must be ASSE approved and recommends an expansion tank when one is installed, to prevent pressure buildup inside the premises. See the backflow and water heater expansion tank lessons below. The university standard calls for a pilot-operated, non-slam combination pressure regulating and check valve on each pump, and Bell & Gossett calls for a check valve on the variable speed lead pump. Our own explanation, not a quoted source: a check valve that does not seat lets pressure bleed back through an idle pump, so a skid may start for no visible demand, and a tech should suspect the check valves.

Common faults

  • Short cycling. A tank maker's manual says a reduction in pump cycle time can result from lost tank air. Check precharge against the skid's specified value, tank empty, system off.
  • Waterlogged tank. A second manual says a missing air valve cap may allow air loss and eventually waterlogging, and that a leaking bladder shows as falling pressure after the cell is charged; if so, the air cell is replaced. Check the air charge every six months.
  • Bad sensor or setpoint. Armstrong describes pumps falling back to a default speed when sensors fail, so odd pressure with steady pumps points at the transmitter.
  • Low suction trips. Our own advice: check the city side and the inlet strainer and backflow assembly before touching the setting. The Virginia poster sets a floor of 10 psi gauge for the sensor, so do not lower a cutout to hide a supply problem.

Maintenance checklist

  • Log suction and discharge pressure at several flows, and compare with the startup report. The university standard asks for a startup report with tests and sequence of operation.
  • Check that lead rotation is working and that each pump runs. The standard asks for elapsed time meters, which show run hours per pump.
  • Test the low suction cutout by safe, utility-approved means and record where it trips and resets.
  • Check tank precharge on schedule, with power off and the tank drained. Follow the lockout and pressure release steps in the maker's manual.
  • Look at seals, vibration and strainers; see the related seal lesson.
  • Exercise isolation valves so a pump can be pulled while the skid runs.

Who may install one in New Jersey

We did not find a New Jersey page that says in plain words that a booster pump installation needs a plumbing license. N.J.A.C. 13:32-1.3 defines plumbing to include public and private water supply systems within the property line of a building, and a booster pump would seem to fall near that, but that is our reading, not the Board's statement. Check with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers and your local construction official before you quote or do the work. The electrical side of the skid may need its own licensed electrician. Also confirm backflow testing requirements with the water utility.

Field card: Domestic water booster systems

Static head is 0.433 psi per foot; booster adds what the city cannot.

  • 12 floors at 10 ft: 120 ft x 0.433 = about 52 psi static (Our own example).
  • Add fixture pressure and friction, subtract minimum city pressure for the boost.
  • Lead pump holds the setpoint; lag starts if lead cannot; rotation shares wear.
  • Low suction cutout protects pumps and the main. One utility example: 20 psi. Ask yours.
  • Precharge about 2 psi below cut-in per one tank maker's manual, tank empty and system off.
  • Zones over 80 psi get PRVs on the lower floors.
  • Check licensing with the NJ Board; do not assume.
