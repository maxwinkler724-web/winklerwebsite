Houses of Worship HVAC: Intermittent Occupancy, Pre-Conditioning, Tall Naves, Organ Humidity and Setbacks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A sanctuary may be empty for most of the week and then hold hundreds of people for ninety minutes. That swing, plus a tall volume, heavy walls and sometimes a pipe organ, is why the schedule matters as much as the equipment. This lesson covers people loads, lead times, ventilation, stratification and humidity in that kind of room.

Check your building

Check your building. Every sanctuary is different. The numbers below come from general guidance and one worked example with assumed inputs. This is education, not a design. Confirm settings with the equipment maker, a controls contractor and the organ caretaker.

What is different about this room

Smaller halls are covered in Heating and Cooling Halls Used a Few Days a Week. A sanctuary adds three problems. The crowd is a large heat source that arrives in minutes. The ceiling is high, so air sorts itself by temperature. And the contents, such as wood and an organ, can be damaged by fast changes.

How much heat 600 people bring

People give off sensible heat (raises air temperature) and latent heat (moisture from breathing and skin). A building-modeling software help page, citing the ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals, lists a person seated in a theater in the evening at 245 Btu/h sensible and 105 Btu/h latent. We use that as a stand-in for a seated congregation. It is a secondary listing; check the Handbook for a real design.

Worked example: a 600-seat sanctuary

Assumptions (ours): every seat filled with 600 people, all seated; the floor area follows the default density in the ventilation table below; the 245 and 105 Btu/h figures above. Choir, clergy and lighting are left out.

  1. Sensible. 600 × 245 = 147,000 Btu/h.
  2. Latent. 600 × 105 = 63,000 Btu/h.
  3. Total. 147,000 + 63,000 = 210,000 Btu/h. One ton of cooling is 12,000 Btu/h, so 210,000 ÷ 12,000 = 17.5 tons from people alone (12.25 sensible, 5.25 latent).
  4. Outdoor air, people part. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (named only; we do not link copies of standards) lists places of religious worship at 5 cfm per person, 0.06 cfm per square foot and a default density of 120 people per 1,000 square feet. 600 × 5 = 3,000 cfm.
  5. Outdoor air, area part. 600 people at 120 per 1,000 ft² means 600 ÷ 120 × 1,000 = 5,000 ft². Then 5,000 × 0.06 = 300 cfm.
  6. Total breathing-zone outdoor air. 3,000 + 300 = 3,300 cfm, or 5.5 cfm per person. This is the table value only; the standard also adjusts for how well supply air reaches the occupants, which we leave out.
  7. A typical weekday group of 60. People load: 60 × 350 = 21,000 Btu/h, which is 1.75 tons. Outdoor air: 60 × 5 = 300, plus the 300 area part = 600 cfm. That is 2,700 cfm less than the full-house number.

A full house needs 10 times the cooling and 5.5 times the outdoor air of a group of 60.

Demand-controlled ventilation for big swings

Outdoor air has to be heated or cooled, so delivering 3,300 cfm to a half-empty room costs money. Demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) uses CO2 sensors as a stand-in for headcount and lowers outdoor air when fewer people are present. The method, the sensor placement and the drift problems are covered in Demand-Controlled Ventilation: CO2 Sensors in Offices and Meeting Rooms, so we do not repeat them. One point matters here: a crowd can arrive within minutes while a CO2 reading in a tall room lags, so ask the controls contractor how the schedule opens the outdoor air path before the doors open, and how outdoor air is handled during pre-conditioning.

Why a big room needs a long lead time

You are not only heating or cooling the air. Thick masonry, floors and pews hold heat, and they must change temperature too, or people will feel cold walls and drafts even when the air reads right. The U.S. Department of Energy says slow systems such as steam and radiant floors need an early start, and suggests returning to normal two or three hours before people arrive for a typical programmable thermostat; a large mass takes longer. The ENERGY STAR checklist for worship facilities says smart thermostats can pre-heat or pre-cool "an hour prior to occupation". That suits a small, light room, not a heavy nave.

The faster limit often comes from the contents, not the equipment. The Diocese of Norwich heating checklist states that more than 2°C (3.6°F) temperature rise per hour is considered damaging to fabric. A guide from the Institute of British Organ Building (cited as plain text; the copy we read is hosted by a diocese) calls heating a cold church to about 20°C (68°F) in a few hours "thermal shock," and gives an unoccupied weekday temperature of no more than 10°C (50°F) and a Sunday temperature of no more than 20°C (68°F).

Worked example: start time

Assumptions (ours): weekday setback 50°F, Sunday target 68°F (the organ builders' figures above), a rise rate held to the 2°C per hour limit, service at 10:00 a.m.

  1. Rise needed: 68 − 50 = 18°F.
  2. Limit: 2°C per hour × 1.8 = 3.6°F per hour.
  3. Lead time: 18 ÷ 3.6 = 5 hours. Start at 5:00 a.m.
  4. Shut-off: ENERGY STAR says to turn HVAC off 15 to 30 minutes before use ends. If the service ends at 11:30, stop at 11:00 for the 30-minute case.

If your measured rate is slower than 3.6°F per hour, the lead time grows. Log a cold morning, then adjust the start by 15 to 30 minutes at a time. In summer the same logic runs backward: pre-cool slowly so the structure absorbs part of the 17.5 tons the crowd brings, instead of the air handler catching up after the doors open. Our own view: watch humidity then too.

Stratification in tall naves

Warm air rises, so heat from a blown-air system collects at the top while people sit at the bottom. The IBO guide says warm air stratifies and that forced-air systems raise temperature quickly and also cool quickly. It reports about a 2°C floor-to-ceiling difference in a continuously heated cathedral, against up to 15°C between floor and organ in one other case. A conservation paper by Camuffo and della Valle (Getty Conservation Institute site) says warm-air systems overheat the upper part of the church, where artworks and organs often sit, and create very low humidity there. Gentle background heat and slow rates reduce the problem. For fan-based destratification and how ceiling fans move the warm layer, see HVLS Fans and Destratification. Our own view: ask the organ caretaker before any new air stream is aimed near the pipes.

Humidity for organs and wood

The IBO guide describes a case where relative humidity fell from 60 to 30 percent so quickly that the recorder trace turned nearly vertical over half an hour. It says to keep humidity within roughly 55 to 75 percent in the heating season and treats readings below 50 percent as potentially dangerous. The Norwich checklist gives 40 to 70 percent, ideally 45 to 55 percent. The guides differ, so ask your instrument's builder. Camuffo and della Valle say heating only for services produces sharp temperature peaks and humidity drops, and that organs are most at risk. For setpoints and drift, see Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control.

Setback energy savings

The Department of Energy says that turning a thermostat back 7 to 10°F for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling, with higher percentages in milder climates. A sanctuary sits empty far longer, so treat 10 percent as an anchor, not a forecast. DOE also says standard programmable thermostats are generally not recommended for heat pumps in heating mode, because backup resistance heat can erase the savings, and that raising the cooling setting does save energy. Setback depth for a room with an organ must respect the contents: the IBO figure of 50°F on weekdays is an example, and the organ builder sets the floor. For schedules and optimal-start logic, see Setpoint Scheduling and Optimal Start Basics.

Practical checklist

  • Set the start from the slowest allowed rate and the measured response.
  • Ventilate for the actual headcount.
  • Log temperature and humidity at the organ and the pews for several weeks.
  • Keep supply air and heaters away from the pipes.

Field card: Houses of Worship HVAC

Size the start time by the slowest safe rate, not the clock.

  • Seated person: about 245 sensible plus 105 latent Btu/h. 600 people is about 210,000 Btu/h, 17.5 tons.
  • Worship space ventilation table (ASHRAE 62.1): 5 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot.
  • Lead time = degrees to recover divided by allowed rate. 18 F at 3.6 F per hour = 5 hours.
  • Warm air stratifies; forced warm air can dry the organ.
  • Organ guides differ on humidity (45 to 55 or 55 to 75 percent); ask the builder.
  • DOE: setback 7 to 10 F for 8 hours can save as much as 10 percent a year.
