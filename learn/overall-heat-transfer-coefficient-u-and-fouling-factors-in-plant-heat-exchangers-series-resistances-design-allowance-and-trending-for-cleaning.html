Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient U and Fouling Factors in Plant Heat Exchangers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every heat exchanger in a plant has one number that sums up how easily heat crosses from one fluid to the other: the overall heat transfer coefficient, U. It is built from layers, and the layers you cannot see (scale, biofilm, oil) are the ones that change over the years. This lesson shows how those layers add up, why a brand new exchanger can look too big, and how techs trend U to decide when to clean.

U is a stack of resistances in series

Heat leaving the hot fluid has to cross several layers in a row: the moving film of fluid on the hot side, any deposit on that surface, the metal wall, any deposit on the other surface, and the film on the cold side. When layers are in series, their resistances add, just as resistors do in a circuit. Each convection film has a resistance of 1/h, so the sum is:

1/U = 1/hinside + Rfouling, inside + Rwall + Rfouling, outside + 1/houtside

The University of Oklahoma design notes on heat transfer equipment state the principle as overall resistance equal to the sum of the resistances, with the film coefficients, the wall and the fouling layers each as a term. The notes' tube version also scales the inside terms by the tube diameter ratio because the inside and outside surfaces have different areas. The U.S. Department of Energy fundamentals handbook builds U from the two film coefficients and the wall (its equations 2-10 and 2-11) but does not carry a fouling term, so the fouling terms are the extra step plant designers add. In a thin metal plate exchanger the wall term is tiny and is often ignored. For the three ways heat moves through each layer, see conduction, convection and radiation.

Units matter. U is in Btu/h per square foot per degree F. Each resistance, including a fouling factor, is in the reciprocal unit: h-ft²-F/Btu. That is why a fouling factor is not a temperature or a percent; it is one more term in the sum.

What the layers are in plant equipment

The film on each side depends on flow, fluid and surface. Moving water gives a high h; oil, glycol mixes at low temperature, and slow or laminar flow give a lower h. The fouling layers are different: they grow with time. A 2023 power-industry research report on condenser tubes (hosted on the OSTI site) lists four internal fouling types: mineral scale, microbiological film, particles that stick to the surface, and corrosion products. A laboratory study of biofilm on condenser tubes, also on OSTI, found films that were 95 to 98 percent water, with a thermal conductivity close to that of water, so the film resists heat mostly through its thickness. Hard scale conducts heat poorly compared with the metal. We also treat an oil film on a refrigerant or process surface as one more low-conductivity layer. That is our own reasoning from the series model, not a figure from a source. For the water-chemistry side of scale and growth, see condenser water treatment.

Why a new exchanger looks oversized

A designer does not size for the clean surface. Standards for shell-and-tube exchangers publish suggested fouling resistances for common services, and the designer adds a chosen fouling resistance to the sum so the unit still makes its duty after it gets dirty. The OSTI record on the cost of heat exchanger fouling in U.S. industry says designers usually add a fouling factor and that this raises capital cost; its 1982 estimate counted extra surface area bought to offset lost heat transfer.

The result on day one is a clean exchanger with more surface than the job needs. It runs with a bigger margin: the leaving temperatures sit closer to the entering ones than the design sheet shows, and a valve may throttle more than expected. That is not a mistake. It is the allowance, and it is meant to be used up as deposit builds. The practical consequence for a tech is that a healthy new unit can look excellent, and the first sign of trouble may not show until the allowance is gone.

Our own worked example: clean U versus fouled U

Our own example, not from a maker or a job. Assumptions: a water-to-water exchanger, clean film coefficients of 1,000 Btu/h-ft²-F on one side and 800 on the other, a negligible wall, and a fouling resistance of 0.001 h-ft²-F/Btu on one side only. For the duty comparison we also assume 100 ft² of surface and a clean LMTD of 20 F (see log mean temperature difference).

  1. Clean resistance. 1/U = 1/1,000 + 1/800 = 0.00100 + 0.00125 = 0.00225 h-ft²-F/Btu.
  2. Clean U. 1 / 0.00225 = about 444 Btu/h-ft²-F.
  3. Fouled resistance. 0.00225 + 0.00100 = 0.00325.
  4. Fouled U. 1 / 0.00325 = about 308 Btu/h-ft²-F.
  5. Percent drop. (444 − 308) / 444 = about 31 percent.
  6. Clean duty. Q = U × A × LMTD = 444 × 100 × 20 = about 889,000 Btu/h.
  7. Fouled duty, same area and LMTD. 308 × 100 × 20 = about 615,000 Btu/h, or 69 percent of the clean duty.
  8. Extra area to keep 889,000 Btu/h at 20 F. 889,000 / (308 × 20) = about 144 ft², which is 44 percent more than 100.
  9. Or a higher LMTD with the same 100 ft². 889,000 / (308 × 100) = about 28.9 F, which is 44 percent higher than 20 F. In a real plant that means hotter supply or colder return water than the process may allow.

Notice that a fouling layer of only 0.001 cost about a third of U, because the clean sum was so small. High-h water-to-water service is more sensitive to fouling than a low-h service, where the films already dominate. The 44 percent also shows what a design allowance of this size looks like: a new unit sized for the fouled case has about 44 percent more area than the clean case needs.

How fouling shows up as approach

Approach is how close one stream gets to the other fluid's temperature. With fixed flows and entering temperatures, a lower U x A means less heat moves, so leaving temperatures drift away from where they used to sit and the approach widens. On a chiller condenser, the gap between leaving condenser water and the refrigerant condensing temperature grows. On a plate exchanger, the cold side leaves cooler than before. See plate heat exchanger fouling and approach for the plate view. Pressure drop rising across the exchanger is a second clue, especially with debris or scale that narrows flow passages.

Trending U or approach to time cleaning

  • Get a clean baseline. Record the four temperatures, the flow and the date right after a cleaning or startup. Record at similar load.
  • Compute U x A, not just U. You rarely know area exactly, but it does not change. Q divided by LMTD, using Q of about 500 × gpm × temperature change for water (our own rule of thumb from 8.33 lb per gallon times 60 minutes), gives U x A. Compare it to the baseline.
  • Or just trend approach. It needs fewer readings and techs can log it on rounds; see chiller condenser tube cleaning for what happens when the trend says clean.
  • Correct for load. Compare like with like. A part-load reading will look better than full load.
  • Set an action line. A common plant practice is to pick a percent drop from baseline (our own suggestion: whatever the owner or chiller maker documents) and plan the cleaning before the allowance runs out. That 2023 condenser study measured heat transfer coefficient about every two days over test runs of 30 days or more, which shows how continuous the monitoring can be when instruments allow.

One reading proves little. A trend line that bends away from the baseline, with sensors checked against each other, is the evidence. Steam-to-hot-water converters have a similar signature; see steam converters.

Field card: Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient U and Fouling Factors in Plant Heat Exchangers

Add the layers, then watch the sum grow.

  • 1/U = 1/h inside + R fouling inside + R wall + R fouling outside + 1/h outside.
  • Fouling factors are resistances in h-ft²-F/Btu; they add to the sum.
  • Design allowance makes a clean unit look oversized by design.
  • Our own example: U drops from about 444 to 308 (31 percent) with 0.001 fouling.
  • Same duty then needs about 44 percent more area or LMTD.
  • Scale, biofilm and oil films each add a layer and widen approach.
  • Log temperatures, flow and date at a clean baseline; trend U x A or approach; clean before the allowance is gone.
