How the Human Body Exchanges Heat and Why People Add Cooling Loads

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Every person in a room is a small heater and a small humidifier. A seated adult gives off roughly as much heat as a 100 watt bulb, and some of it leaves as moisture that the cooling coil must condense. Fill a conference room and those two effects add up fast. This lesson shows where the heat comes from, how it leaves the body, how the sensible and latent split changes with room temperature and activity, and how to turn it into Btu/h, tons and pints of water for a 40-person room.

Scope. The six comfort factors and cold-window effects are in Why a Room Feels Cold at 70 Degrees. Sensible versus latent heat in general is in Sensible vs Latent Heat. Call centers, computers and ventilation per person are in Call Center and Dense Open Office HVAC. This page is only about the body as a heat source.

Metabolic rate and the met

The body turns food into work and heat, and nearly all of it ends up as heat in the room. A met is a unit for that rate per unit of skin area. A U.S. Department of Energy building simulation program's input reference (plain text, not linked) states that one met equals 58.1 W/m² and uses a standard adult body surface area of 1.8 m². The CDC NIOSH heat criteria document defines 1 met as 50 kcal per square meter per hour and says 1 kcal/h is 1.16 W, which is about 58 W/m²; it also uses a standard person of 70 kg and 1.8 m². Our arithmetic: 58.1 W/m² x 1.8 m² = 104.6 W per person, and 104.6 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = about 357 Btu/h.

The same simulation reference lists typical values by activity. We converted its W per person to Btu/h with 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h:

How the heat leaves the body

The NIOSH heat criteria document writes the body's heat balance as storage S = (M - W) +/- C +/- R +/- K - E, where M is metabolism, W is external work, C is convection, R is radiation, K is conduction and E is evaporation. In a steady comfortable room storage is near zero, so everything the body makes has to leave by those four paths. See the three ways heat moves for the basics.

  • Radiation. Infrared leaves the warm skin and clothing and lands on cooler walls, windows and ceilings. The NIH StatPearls heat loss article gives about 60 percent of total loss at rest, and says radiation works only while the skin is warmer than its surroundings.
  • Evaporation. About 22 percent. The StatPearls temperature regulation article says water evaporates from skin and lungs even when a person is not sweating, at roughly 600 to 700 mL per day, and that sweat removes about 0.58 kcal per gram evaporated. Our arithmetic: 600 to 700 mL per day is 25 to 29 mL per hour, which carries away about 17 to 20 W (58 to 67 Btu/h) at rest. Sweating adds to that when the room is warm or people are active.
  • Convection and conduction through air. About 15 percent combined: air touching the skin warms and rises, and moving air carries more away.
  • Direct contact. About 3 percent, to a chair, floor or tool.

Treat the percentages as rough resting figures. A PubMed study of local and whole-body heat loss at operative temperatures of 23, 28, 33 and 37 °C found that as the temperature rose, whole-body radiation and convection losses fell and evaporative loss rose, while conduction, respiration and diffusion stayed roughly constant. When the surroundings are warmer than the skin, the StatPearls heat loss article says the body depends on evaporation.

Sensible and latent: what the HVAC system sees

Radiation, convection and conduction warm the room and its surfaces without changing the moisture in the air: that is sensible heat. Evaporation puts water vapor in the air, and removing that vapor takes energy at the coil: that is latent heat (see the sensible and latent lesson linked above). Breath counts as evaporation, so it lands on the latent side.

The NIH percentages and the HVAC split are not the same bookkeeping. Radiation plus convection plus conduction is about 78 percent at rest, yet the typical published HVAC value for a seated person below is 70 percent sensible. Office workers are a bit more active than resting, and the two sets of numbers come from different sources, so do not expect them to match.

A Purdue University load calculation lecture gives these typical published values per person. The page does not state the room temperature behind them:

Look at the pattern: sensible gain barely moves from rest to heavy office work, but latent gain climbs steeply. More activity mostly means more sweat and breath. A Canadian government building digest gives a similar picture in watts: about 75 W sensible and about 60 W latent for a sedentary person in a comfortable environment, and it notes that latent rises substantially if the room is too warm or people are very active. That is 256 Btu/h and 205 Btu/h by our conversion.

Room temperature moves the split too

The DOE simulation program's engineering reference publishes a polynomial that divides a person's total heat into sensible and latent by room air temperature and metabolic rate, and defines latent as the total minus sensible. It was fit to 350 to 1,450 Btu/h and 70 to 82 °F. We ran it for two metabolic rates (our arithmetic, rounded):

Total heat stays the same; only the route changes. A warmer room pushes people from dry heat toward wet heat, because the skin has less temperature difference to shed heat by radiation and convection. That is why a space run warm to save energy can feel clammy and still need dehumidification.

Why crowded rooms need more cooling and dehumidification

  • Sensible load adds up linearly. Every person is another 245 Btu/h or so of heat the cooling coil has to remove, on top of the walls, windows and lights.
  • Latent load adds up faster than people expect. Each person adds water vapor. In a crowded or active room, the share of the load that is moisture grows.
  • The coil sees a lower sensible heat ratio. People alone are about 61 percent sensible at light office work, a wetter load than the sensible-heavy loads from sun and walls. See Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load. Our reasoning: if the airflow is too high or the coil is not cold enough, the unit may satisfy the thermostat while leaving the room humid.
  • Occupancy is bursty (our reasoning). A meeting room may be empty for hours and then take 40 people in ten minutes. Moisture arrives right away, while the thermostat only reacts after the air warms.

Worked example (our own): a 40-person meeting room

This is our own example. The room is made up. Assumptions: 40 people, all seated doing light office work, so we use the Purdue values of 245 Btu/h sensible and 155 Btu/h latent per person (400 Btu/h total). The room is held near 75 °F. We ignore lights, equipment, walls, windows and fresh air, which are separate loads. The latent heat of water is taken as 1,050 Btu/lb; NIST reference data give about 1,052 Btu/lb at 75 °F (vapor enthalpy 1,094.7 minus liquid 43.1).

  1. Sensible: 40 x 245 = 9,800 Btu/h.
  2. Latent: 40 x 155 = 6,200 Btu/h.
  3. Total people load: 9,800 + 6,200 = 16,000 Btu/h (check: 40 x 400 = 16,000). In watts, 16,000 / 3.412 = about 4,690 W.
  4. Tons: 16,000 / 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 1.33 tons, from people alone.
  5. Latent share: 6,200 / 16,000 = 39 percent. Sensible heat ratio for people only: 9,800 / 16,000 = 0.61.
  6. Moisture: 6,200 Btu/h / 1,050 Btu/lb = 5.9 lb/h of water vapor.
  7. Pints: using our conversion of 8.34 lb per gallon and 8 pints per gallon (1.04 lb per pint), 5.9 / 1.04 = about 5.7 pints per hour.
  8. Over an 8-hour day: 5.9 x 8 = 47 lb, or about 5.7 gallons, that the system must condense out of the air if it holds the humidity steady.

Variation: if the same 40 people were in a lively working session at moderate office work (250 sensible and 200 latent per person), the totals become 10,000 + 8,000 = 18,000 Btu/h, which is 1.5 tons. Moisture is 8,000 / 1,050 = 7.6 lb/h, about 7.3 pints per hour. A 12.5 percent larger total load comes with a 29 percent larger moisture load (8,000 vs 6,200 Btu/h latent).

This is a people-only calculation to show the method. A full room load adds the envelope, lights, equipment and ventilation air; see the call center lesson linked above. If you size equipment from a table of people loads, check which room temperature and activity the table assumes, since the table above does not say.

Field card: People as a heat and moisture source

  • 1 met = 58.1 W/m²; adult area 1.8 m², so 1 met is about 105 W or 357 Btu/h per person.
  • Four paths out: radiation (about 60% at rest), evaporation (22%), convection with air conduction (15%), contact (3%).
  • Radiation, convection and conduction are sensible. Evaporation, including breath, is latent.
  • Typical published values, seated light office work: 245 sensible + 155 latent = 400 Btu/h.
  • More activity or a warmer room shifts the split toward latent.
  • Convert: 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h; 1 ton = 12,000 Btu/h; water vapor lb/h = latent Btu/h / about 1,050.
  • Our 40-person room: 9,800 sensible + 6,200 latent = 16,000 Btu/h = 1.33 tons; 5.9 lb/h = about 5.7 pints/h of moisture.
