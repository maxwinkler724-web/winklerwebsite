Heat Pump Frost and Defrost: Why Average Heat Falls Near 35 F

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Between 47 F and 17 F, a heat pump makes less heat as it gets colder. That part is a smooth slope. Frost adds a dip on top of the slope, centered in the damp, near-freezing range, because the unit must stop heating the house and melt the coil. This lesson covers why the dip exists, why test results show it, and how to work out the average heat a house really gets.

How frost grows

In heating mode the outdoor coil runs colder than the air so it can absorb heat. When the coil surface is below freezing and the air carries moisture, water vapor turns to frost on the fins. A national laboratory literature review on defrost describes frost forming when the coil is well below freezing and the air is humid. It cites a frosting range of about -7 C to 5 C (roughly 19 F to 41 F) with humidity above 65 percent. Another national laboratory review notes that humidity strongly affects how much frost forms.

Frost hurts twice. It is a poor conductor, so it insulates the coil from the air, and it fills the gaps between fins, which cuts airflow. The same review says both effects lower heating capacity and efficiency. It also cites one field study in which more than 60 percent of the outdoor coil was frosted after five days and capacity fell to 43.4 percent. That was a severe case without good defrost control, so it shows the direction, not a typical day. A separate review of frost modeling cites seasonal heating efficiency losses of up to 13 percent from frosting and defrosting cycles. For the heat transfer details, see the related frost lesson below.

Humid cold versus dry cold

Very cold air holds little water, so a 10 F day can be cold and still make less frost than a damp 35 F day. That is our own reading of the humidity trend in the sources above, not a measured number. An older federal test report chose its frost test point at 32 F dry bulb and 30 F wet bulb, which it said matches about 80 percent relative humidity, and called that typical of regions with many heating hours below 35 F. Current federal test conditions use 35 F dry bulb and 33 F wet bulb for the frost test, per the U.S. Department of Energy cold-climate product specification. Its other test points are 17 F dry bulb and 15 F wet bulb, and a federal rule notice gives 47 F dry bulb and 43 F wet bulb. In plain terms, a dry cold day, a wet snowy day and a foggy morning at 36 F all frost differently, and the controls can only react to what they sense.

What defrost does to the house

Most residential heat pumps defrost by reversing to cooling mode for a few minutes. The outdoor coil gets hot refrigerant, the frost melts and the outdoor fan usually pauses. The heat has to come from somewhere, and in a reversing system part of it comes from the indoor air stream. One study reviewed by a national laboratory found that indoor air supplied 71.8 percent of the heat used for defrosting. That is one unit under one set of conditions, so treat it as an example. The same review says pulling heat from indoors can hurt comfort.

That is the reason backup heat often comes on during defrost: the electric strips offset the cool draft. A university extension article points out that a model energy code excepts defrost mode from the usual lockout of supplemental heat, and says strip heat helps the heat pump when it is below about 35 F. The related defrost and backup lesson covers how that is wired; we do not repeat it here.

Why test results sag around 35 F

The federal test procedure reports a capacity for the frost test that is an average over the heating period and the defrost period together. The cold-climate product specification describes it as the heating and defrost interval used to evaluate average space heating capacity, with energy measured from one defrost end to the next. An older federal report on the same idea defines net heating as heat delivered minus heat removed from the indoor air. A federal rule notice explains why: leaving out the defrost energy would overstate the heating capacity.

Now the straight line. Draw a line between capacity at 47 F and capacity at 17 F and read off 35 F. That line assumes the loss grows evenly as it gets colder. Frost does not behave that way. At 47 F the coil is mostly above the frosting range, and at 17 F the air carries so little water that frost grows slowly. The damp middle is where frost and defrost cost the most. So the true cycle average at 35 F lands below the line. This explanation is our own reasoning from the sources above, and the size of the gap varies by model and by test.

Worked example (our own)

These numbers are our own assumptions for practice. They are not data for any real unit.

  • Steady capacity at 47 F is 36,000 Btu/h and at 17 F is 22,000 Btu/h.
  • Average capacity while heating at 35 F, with frost slowly building, is 27,600 Btu/h.
  • A defrost starts every 90 minutes of heating and lasts 6 minutes.
  • During defrost the system removes a net 18,000 Btu/h from the indoor air stream.
  • Strip heat is 8 kW and runs for the whole defrost. Using the standard conversion of 0.2930711 W per Btu/h, 8,000 W is about 27,300 Btu/h.

Step 1, the straight line. The change from 17 F to 47 F is 36,000 - 22,000 = 14,000 Btu/h over 30 F. At 35 F we are 18 F above 17 F: 22,000 + (18 / 30) x 14,000 = 22,000 + 8,400 = 30,400 Btu/h.

Step 2, one full cycle without strip heat. Heating: 27,600 x 1.5 h = 41,400 Btu. Defrost: -18,000 x 0.1 h = -1,800 Btu. Net = 39,600 Btu over 1.6 h = 24,750 Btu/h.

Step 3, compare. 24,750 / 30,400 = 0.81, so the line overstates the cycle average by about 19 percent in this example (5,650 Btu/h).

Step 4, add strip heat. Strip output during defrost: 27,300 x 0.1 h = 2,730 Btu. Net in the defrost period becomes -1,800 + 2,730 = +930 Btu. Cycle total = 41,400 + 930 = 42,330 Btu over 1.6 h = 26,456, about 26,460 Btu/h.

Step 5, strip energy. 8 kW x 0.1 h = 0.8 kWh per cycle. Averaged over the 1.6 h cycle, that is 0.5 kWh each hour (0.5 kW average). The strips gave back about 1,700 Btu/h on average (26,460 - 24,750) for that electricity.

If your house needed 28,000 Btu/h at 35 F, the heat pump alone would fall short in this example. The cycle is the reason, not a fault. Real units have different defrost timing, and demand-based controls change the interval to match conditions.

What homeowners see

Steam from the outdoor unit, a fan that stops for a few minutes, and a brief burst of cooler air from the vents are normal during defrost, and the strips may add a little warmth. A long, thick sheet of ice that stays is not normal; see the iced-up lesson. A higher electric bill in cold, damp weather is also a clue that strips are running more. We do not give cost numbers here.

Field card: Heat Pump Frost and Defrost: Why Average Heat Falls Near 35 F

Output drops with temperature, then dips again from frost.

  • Frost forms on a cold coil in humid air, roughly 19 F to 41 F per one review.
  • Frost insulates the coil and blocks airflow; defrost then takes heat back from the house.
  • Test capacity near 35 F is a cycle average that includes the defrost period.
  • A straight line between 17 F and 47 F overstates the average at 35 F.
  • Our example: 30,400 on the line, 24,750 net, 26,460 with 8 kW strips during defrost.
  • Strip heat in the example: 0.5 kWh per hour averaged.
