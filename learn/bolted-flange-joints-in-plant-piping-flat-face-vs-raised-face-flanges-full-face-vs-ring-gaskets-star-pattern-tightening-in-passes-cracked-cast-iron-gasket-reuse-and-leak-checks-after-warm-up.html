Bolted Flange Joints in Plant Piping: Flat Face, Raised Face, Gaskets and Star-Pattern Tightening

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A bolted flange looks simple: two faces, a gasket, a ring of bolts. Most flange leaks, and most cracked cast iron flanges, come from three choices made at the bench: matching the faces, picking the right gasket shape, and tightening the bolts evenly. This lesson walks through each one in plain terms, with a worked tightening order for an 8-bolt flange.

Check your site rules. This is general education. Always follow the piping specification, the gasket maker's instructions and your site's lockout and pressure-release procedures. Never open or tighten a flange on a line that is pressurized or hot.

Flat face and raised face: what the difference is

A flat face flange has a gasket surface level with the bolting face. A raised face flange has a slightly raised ring around the bore, so the gasket sits on that ring and the outer part of the flange stands clear. Steel plant flanges are commonly raised face. A university steam piping specification we read calls for Class 150 raised face weld neck steel flanges on medium and low pressure steam. Older cast iron valves, pumps and fittings are commonly flat face.

Why a raised face steel flange should not be pulled onto a flat face cast iron flange

Cast iron is strong in compression and weak in bending. If a raised face steel flange is bolted to a flat face cast iron flange, the contact is only at the raised ring. As the bolts draw the flanges together, the outer rim of the cast iron flange is pulled toward the steel one while the ring acts as a pivot. That bending load is what snaps the edge off cast iron flanges. The remedy in the federal marine piping rules we read is direct: in 46 CFR 56.25-10, a class 150 steel flange bolted to a flat face cast iron flange must be furnished with a flat face. In plain terms, the faces are matched so the whole flange face is supported, and a full face gasket fills the space between them. A related federal rule says that for combinations it does not list, calculations must show the bolt load and flange moment are no higher than for the accepted combinations. We do not take that as permission to improvise in the field: ask for the matching flat face flange or a written procedure.

This is not a small matter. A federal energy department operating experience summary describes a blind flange in a steam line that fractured during a water hammer event at a national laboratory. It was an older cast iron flange with a maximum pressure failure rating of 125 psi, and the site's own standards call for steel steam flanges rated to 150 psi. The lab surveyed the system to find and replace nonconforming flanges.

Full face and ring gaskets

The gasket shape follows the flange face. The university specification says gaskets fit within the bolt circle on raised face flanges, and are full face on flat face flanges. A ring gasket stays inside the bolts. A full face gasket covers the whole face and has a hole for every bolt, so it also supports the flange rim and keeps the bolt load from bending it. Gasket material must be suitable for the fluid and temperature; the federal marine rules say a gasket must not be injuriously affected by either. Pick the material from the piping specification and the gasket maker's data, not from what is on the shelf.

Pressure classes in general terms

Flanges are sorted into pressure classes. A class number is a label for a family of flanges and their allowed pressure at a given temperature, not a direct psi rating, and the allowed pressure drops as the temperature rises. The sources we read mention steel classes 150 and 300, and cast iron classes 125 and 250. Mixing classes is where mistakes happen: matching bolt circles do not mean matching ratings. Read the markings on the flange and check the pressure and temperature tables in the governing piping standard (named here in plain text only) before you reuse or mix parts.

Bolting: even and in a star pattern

The aim is to compress the gasket evenly. If one side gets tight first, the flange tilts, the gasket is squeezed unevenly, and the loose side leaks. On cast iron the same tilt adds bending stress. So bolts are snugged and tightened in a cross or star pattern, in several passes, rather than around the circle or all the way down one bolt at a time. A study by researchers at a UK university tested an 8-bolt flange with a star sequence of 1, 5, 3, 7, 2, 6, 4, 8 and a plain sequential order; it tightened gasketed joints in four increments of roughly 42, 61, 79 and 100 percent of final torque (our calculation from the stated values) and recommended at least five passes. Our worked example below uses three passes, which is a common plan, but follow your procedure if it calls for more.

Overtightening is not the cure for a leak. Too much bolt load can crush the gasket or crack a cast iron flange. Use the torque value from the procedure or gasket maker, a calibrated wrench, and clean, lightly lubricated threads if the procedure says so, since friction changes how much bolt load a given torque gives.

Worked example (our own): 8-bolt flange, three passes

Our own example with assumed inputs. We assume a target of 100 ft-lb per bolt (an assumed number for teaching, not a recommendation for any real joint), 8 bolts, and the bolts numbered clockwise 1 through 8 from the top.

  1. Snug: run all nuts up by hand so the flanges are parallel and the gasket is centered.
  2. Star order: 1, 5, 3, 7, 2, 6, 4, 8. Each bolt is roughly opposite or at a right angle to the one before it.
  3. Pass 1 at 30 percent: 0.30 × 100 = 30 ft-lb, in star order.
  4. Pass 2 at 60 percent: 0.60 × 100 = 60 ft-lb, in star order.
  5. Pass 3 at 100 percent: 100 ft-lb, in star order.
  6. Final check pass: go around the circle 1 through 8 at 100 ft-lb. Bolts tightened later in a pass relax their neighbors a little, so the nuts should turn very little or not at all if the joint is even.

Total wrench steps: 3 passes × 8 bolts = 24, plus 8 for the check pass, 32 in all. For a rough sense of scale, the first pass applies only 30 of the final 100 ft-lb, so a bolt that is off by a few turns after pass 1 is easy to see and correct before the gasket takes its full load.

Gasket reuse

None of the federal, laboratory or university documents we read addressed reusing gaskets, so we do not give a rule from them. A used gasket has already taken a set and may be scored or crushed, so many sites require a new gasket whenever a flange is opened. Follow the maker's instruction and your site procedure.

Leak checks after warm-up

A joint that is dry when cold can weep once it is hot, because the pipe, flange, bolts and gasket do not expand together. A federal energy department operating experience notice about a failed plastic-pipe flange gasket recommended re-torquing flange bolts at least 24 hours after gasket installation, because that material relaxes under load. That case is plastic, not steel or cast iron, so we cite it only to show that some gaskets need a planned re-torque; ask the gasket maker whether yours does. Our own practice for any joint: bring the system up to temperature and pressure in stages, look at each new or disturbed flange from a safe position, and treat any weep as a reason to isolate and depressurize before going near it. A national laboratory steam training outline lists flanges and gasketed joints among the common places steam leaks occur, so they deserve a look on every walk-through. Never use your hand to find a steam or hot-water leak.

Field card: Bolted Flange Joints in Plant Piping: Flat Face, Raised Face, Gaskets and Star-Pattern Tightening

Match the faces, match the gasket, tighten in a star, in passes.

  • Raised face steel to flat face cast iron: use a flat face steel flange and a full face gasket.
  • Ring gasket stays inside the bolts; full face gasket covers the whole face.
  • Star order for 8 bolts: 1, 5, 3, 7, 2, 6, 4, 8.
  • Our example passes: 30, 60, 100 percent of an assumed target, then a circular check.
  • Overtight or uneven bolting leaks the gasket or cracks cast iron.
  • Check gasket reuse and re-torque with the maker. Check for leaks after warm-up from a safe position.
