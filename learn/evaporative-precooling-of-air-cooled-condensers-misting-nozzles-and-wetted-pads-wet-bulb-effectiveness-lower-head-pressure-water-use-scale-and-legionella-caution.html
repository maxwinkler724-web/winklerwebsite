Evaporative Precooling of Air-Cooled Condensers: Misting Nozzles and Wetted Pads

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a hot afternoon an air-cooled condenser is working against the outdoor dry-bulb temperature. Evaporating a little water into the air before it reaches the coil can pull that air temperature part of the way down toward the wet bulb. A cooler air stream means a lower condensing temperature, lower head pressure and less compressor work. It also means water, scale and a bacteria risk to manage. This lesson covers the math and the trade-offs.

How evaporation cools the air

Turning liquid water into vapor takes a lot of heat. A U.S. Geological Survey report gives the latent heat of vaporization of water as about 580 calories per gram, which converts to roughly 1,044 Btu per pound. When water evaporates into passing air, that heat comes out of the air itself. The air gets cooler and wetter, and its total heat content stays about the same. The lowest temperature it can reach this way is the wet-bulb temperature, which is why the process works best on hot, dry days and poorly on humid ones. A national laboratory report on direct evaporative precooling says the same: the dry-bulb drop is large when the air is hot and dry and much smaller when humidity is high. For reading wet bulb on site, see Measuring Wet-Bulb Temperature in the Field.

Two ways to wet the air

  • Wetted pads. A pad of wetted media is mounted in front of the coil, and all the entering air is pulled through it. Water is fed to the top and drains out of the bottom. Pads give a repeatable effectiveness because the air path is forced through a wet surface. They add airflow resistance, which the condenser fan has to overcome. That is our own caution; check the equipment maker before adding any pad.
  • Misting nozzles. Fine spray nozzles put droplets into the air stream ahead of the coil. They need little space and little added resistance, but the droplets must evaporate before they reach the fins. In one experimental study of a small split system, a pad averaged an effectiveness of 0.55, a mist system averaged 0.43, and a pad plus mist averaged 0.49. That is one small, fixed-speed unit in one climate, so treat the numbers as an example, not a promise.

The effectiveness math

Effectiveness (wet-bulb effectiveness) tells you what fraction of the available cooling you actually get. The available cooling is the wet-bulb depression, which is dry bulb minus wet bulb.

Leaving dry bulb = entering dry bulb − effectiveness × (entering dry bulb − entering wet bulb)

An effectiveness of 1.0 would reach the wet bulb exactly; real systems do not. The national laboratory report above modeled values of 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9, and cited a tested pad at about 0.82. See Evaporative Coolers on Commercial and Industrial Buildings for direct versus indirect media and pad types.

What it does to condensing temperature and head pressure

An air-cooled condenser runs at a condensing temperature some number of degrees above the entering air temperature. If the entering air gets 14 degrees cooler, the condensing temperature falls by roughly the same amount, because that temperature difference is set by coil size and airflow (see Air-Cooled Condenser TD and Total Heat of Rejection). The saturation pressure for the refrigerant drops with it. Lower head pressure means a lower compression ratio and less compressor power. In the small experimental study, condensing temperature without precooling was 44 C, and with a pad it was 39 C, a 5 degree C drop. The national laboratory simulations found average peak power reductions of roughly 19 to 22 percent for a modern refrigerant, and annual energy savings averaging 10 to 13 percent, across 16 cities. Those are simulated results and varied widely by city, from single digits to nearly 40 percent at peak. Remember that dirt can cancel these gains; see Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil.

Worked example (our own)

These are our own numbers with assumed inputs, not measured data. Assumptions: outdoor air at 95 F dry bulb and 75 F wet bulb; a wetted pad with an assumed effectiveness of 0.70; condenser airflow of 10,000 cfm; condenser TD (condensing temperature minus entering air) of 25 F; standard air, so sensible heat in Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × temperature change (1.08 comes from 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h × 0.24 Btu/lb-F).

  1. Wet-bulb depression: 95 − 75 = 20 F.
  2. Temperature drop: 0.70 × 20 = 14 F.
  3. Leaving air: 95 − 14 = 81 F.
  4. Condensing temperature, assuming the TD holds: before 95 + 25 = 120 F; after 81 + 25 = 106 F, about 14 F lower.
  5. Heat taken out of the air: 1.08 × 10,000 × 14 = 151,200 Btu/h.
  6. Water evaporated: 151,200 ÷ 1,044 Btu/lb = about 145 lb/h.
  7. In gallons at 8.34 lb/gal: 145 ÷ 8.34 = about 17 gal/h.

So in this example, about 17 gallons of water per hour buys a 14 F lower condensing temperature, while the unit is running at that airflow. A pad or nozzle system also needs extra water for bleed-off or for pad flushing, so the supply must cover more than the evaporated amount.

Downsides to plan for

  • Mineral scale. When water evaporates, the minerals stay behind. A university research center that tested evaporatively cooled condensers found that these minerals can foul the coil, pumps, piping and nozzles, and that scale cut airflow and heat transfer. In its full-scale test with no bleed, the coil gained an estimated 27.7 lb of scale; with low bleed, about 0.7 lb. Scale still formed on nozzles, the pump basket and the drift eliminator. Hard water makes it worse, and hardness varies a lot from place to place, so get a water quality report first.
  • Corrosion. Constant wetting, plus dissolved minerals drying on aluminum fins and copper tubes, can attack the coil and its coating. This is our own caution, not a sourced figure. Ask the equipment maker whether the coil is rated for wetting before you add precooling.
  • Water treatment and bleed. Bleeding off part of the recirculated water holds down mineral concentration but raises water use. The same university study found that cleaning the pump basket, nozzles, sump and drift eliminator restored much of the lost efficiency, and recommended a regular maintenance program. See Condenser Water Treatment: Scale, Corrosion and Biological Growth.
  • Legionella. A federal public health toolkit lists misters, atomizers and cooling towers among devices that can spread Legionella through aerosols. It gives 77 F to 113 F as the range where Legionella grows best, and says stagnant water encourages biofilm and lowers disinfectant levels. A warm, standing sump or a pad that stays wet while the unit is off is a concern. Do not let water stand, follow the owner's water management program, and do not start a mist system that has sat idle without checking with the person responsible for it.
  • Drift. Droplets can be carried past the coil by the airstream. Besides wasting water and spotting nearby surfaces, drift is a concern because the toolkit above describes aerosols as a way Legionella spreads. A drift eliminator on the system and keeping nozzles and the air path away from air intakes and walkways are sensible measures. Aim and pressure settings come from the maker.

Is it worth it?

The benefit depends on how often your site has a large wet-bulb depression while the condenser is working hard. In our own example, on a muggy day with a 5 F depression and 0.70 effectiveness, you would only gain 3.5 F. Many sites run the system only when the dry bulb is above a set point, which also saves water. In one simulation set, spraying only above 90 F cut water use for one refrigerant and gave slightly better annual cost savings, but spraying at all temperatures saved more for another. The national laboratory report also did not discuss scale or maintenance, so factor those in on your own.

Field card: Evaporative precooling of air-cooled condensers

Leaving dry bulb = entering dry bulb minus effectiveness times (dry bulb minus wet bulb).

  • Cooling is limited by the wet bulb: best on hot, dry days.
  • Our example: 95 F dry, 75 F wet, effectiveness 0.70 gives 81 F air.
  • Condensing temperature falls by about the same number of degrees if the TD holds.
  • Water evaporated = sensible heat removed (1.08 × cfm × degrees) divided by about 1,044 Btu/lb.
  • Our example at 10,000 cfm: about 145 lb/h, about 17 gal/h, before any bleed.
  • Scale, nozzle clogging, corrosion and drift need a maintenance plan.
  • Legionella: avoid standing or warm stagnant water, follow the owner's water management program.
