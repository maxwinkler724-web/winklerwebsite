Upstairs Too Hot: Uneven Temperatures in a Two-Story House

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The thermostat reads 74 downstairs and the bedrooms are at 80. That is one of the most common comfort complaints in two-story houses, and it usually has several causes stacked together. This lesson sorts them out, then separates what a homeowner can try from what needs a technician's gauges.

Why the second floor runs hot

Heat rises, and the roof is right above you. A manufacturer's homeowner guide puts it plainly: hot air rises, the roof absorbs heat that seeps into the attic, and a poorly insulated attic makes the top floor worse (Trane). Air leaks matter too. Building Science Corporation described a test where hot air from a vented attic was pulled into a wall and the top of an exterior wall in summer. ENERGY STAR notes that sealing the attic is one of the bigger opportunities in a house and lists uneven temperatures among the symptoms it can help.

Stack effect is real but mostly a winter story. In a tall building, warm air leaves near the top and outside air enters low down. Green Building Advisor, a trade publication, gives about 4 pascals per story in winter and about 1.5 in summer, with the flow reversed. So in July the pressure effect is small. The bigger summer factors are the sun, the attic and the layering of warm air near the ceiling.

One thermostat, one temperature. Trane notes that a standard system is built to bring the whole house to a single temperature. If the only thermostat is downstairs, the system shuts off when the downstairs is satisfied. Building Science Corporation described a house where the thermostat sat on the cool side of hot-air layering, so the second floor stayed "sweltering" while the system kept cycling. Green Building Advisor makes a related point about bedrooms: with supply air and no return path, the main floor can be satisfied before the bedrooms are comfortable.

Ducts in the attic. The University of Florida extension service (UF/IFAS) says most ductwork sits in unconditioned space such as attics, and leaks there mean supply air is lost and uneven distribution. ENERGY STAR lists ducts in an attic, crawlspace or garage, and rooms that are hard to heat and cool, as signs that a duct system may be performing poorly.

No easy way for air to get back. The Building America Solution Center, funded by the U.S. Department of Energy, explains that when a bedroom door is shut and the room has no return, pressure builds in the room, causing drafts and temperature differences. Fixes include a ducted return, a jump duct or a transfer grille. Return and supply sizing are part of duct design, and ACCA says an undersized or poorly designed duct system can bring high static pressure and inadequate comfort.

What a homeowner can try

Block the sun and seal the attic. Trane suggests shades or UV film on sun-facing windows, and attic insulation and sealing. ENERGY STAR calls attic air sealing "generally a challenging DIY project." It advises calling a professional first if you find damp insulation, mold, little ventilation or old knob and tube wiring. It also warns that sealing can cause naturally venting gas or oil appliances to back-draft, and that vermiculite insulation may contain asbestos and should not be disturbed until tested. If any of that applies, stop and get a pro.

Keep returns and registers clear. UF/IFAS tells homeowners to keep furniture away from registers and return vents, change the filter regularly, and give closed rooms an air path such as a door vent, jumper duct or transfer vent.

Fan ON versus AUTO has a trade-off. Trane says setting the fan to ON in summer can mix the air more evenly but that constant running can add wear. The humidity side comes from a 1987 Florida Solar Energy Center report: with the fan running continuously, net moisture removal dropped, and in one 20-minute cycle about 19 percent of the water the coil had removed evaporated back into the house. That was one test setup from 1987, so treat it as a caution, not a rule. If you try ON, watch for clammy air and ask your technician about it. We found no sourced figure here for the energy cost of a constantly running blower.

Closing downstairs registers is a weak fix. Trane says it does not recommend closing vents downstairs to cool the upstairs. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study (Iain Walker, 2003) of heating systems found that duct leakage rose as registers were closed and discouraged closing more than 60 percent. A Department of Energy case study measured higher duct pressure when a register was restricted. The mechanism applies to cooling too, but those studies did not measure a two-story cooling case. If a technician wants a damper partly closed, they should measure the result.

What the pros check

  • Static pressure and airflow at registers. High static pressure from restrictive ducts or returns is a classic cause of weak flow to far rooms. See Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.
  • Duct leakage testing. UF/IFAS lists blower door, duct blaster and pressure pan tests as ways to find leaks you cannot see, and notes return leaks are hard to detect. See Ductwork Basics and Common Problems.
  • Return sizing and pathways for each floor and each closed-door room.
  • Manual J, S and D. ACCA describes Manual J as the load calculation, Manual S as equipment selection and Manual D as duct design, and warns against rules of thumb for airflow. See Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J.
  • Zoning or a second system. Trane lists motorized dampers with a zone controller, or adding a separate system for the upstairs. Zoning has its own airflow traps; see Zoning and Zone Dampers Basics. A ductless unit avoids the attic ducts; see Mini-Split Basics.

Worked example: what duct leakage can mean

ENERGY STAR says about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through a typical duct system is lost to leaks. Take a hypothetical system moving 1,200 cfm. This is an illustration, not a measurement of your house.

  1. Middle of the range: 25 percent = 0.25.
  2. 1,200 cfm x 0.25 = 300 cfm lost.
  3. Low end: 1,200 x 0.20 = 240 cfm. High end: 1,200 x 0.30 = 360 cfm.
  4. Delivered at the registers, if the loss is all supply side: 1,200 - 300 = 900 cfm.

If attic supply ducts leak, that lost air goes into the attic, not the bedrooms. The statement is general, so only a test (such as the duct blaster mentioned above) gives the real figure. UF/IFAS gives a different number, 25 to 40 percent of heating or cooling energy lost in typical systems. That is energy, not airflow, so the two are not the same measure.

Plain-language takeaways

A hot second floor is rarely one defect. Reduce sun and attic heat first, make sure air can return, and ask for duct leakage and static pressure measurements before anyone sells you a bigger unit. Related lessons also cover AC running but not cooling.

Field card: Upstairs Too Hot: Uneven Temperatures in a Two-Story House

Heat, attic, ducts, returns, one thermostat.

  • Roof and attic heat plus warm-air layering hit the top floor first.
  • Summer stack effect is weak; do not blame it alone.
  • ENERGY STAR: about 20 to 30 percent of duct air lost to leaks; 1,200 cfm x 0.25 = 300 cfm.
  • Do not close many downstairs registers; leakage and pressure rise.
  • Fan ON mixes air but can raise humidity; watch it.
  • Check static pressure, duct leakage, returns, Manual J/D before zoning or a second system.
