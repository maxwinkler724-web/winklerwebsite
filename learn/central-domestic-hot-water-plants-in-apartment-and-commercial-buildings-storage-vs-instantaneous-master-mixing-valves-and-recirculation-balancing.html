Central Hot Water Plants: Master Mixing Valves and Recirc Balancing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house has one heater and a short run of pipe. An apartment building has one plant that feeds dozens of units through risers, and the hot water has to be safe at a tap on the top floor and also safe from bacteria while it sits in a pipe. This lesson covers how those central plants are built, what a master mixing valve does, and how a recirculation loop is balanced and checked.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Plumbing codes, health rules and the building's water management plan decide the actual temperatures. This page is general education from public sources read in 2026. Follow the equipment maker's instructions and the engineer's design.

Three ways to make hot water for a whole building

The Budlong engineering firm's design article groups central plants into storage, semi-instantaneous and instantaneous. A storage plant keeps a tank of hot water that covers the morning peak. The article lists gas storage tanks from 30 to more than 500 gallons and warns that recovery capacity must refill the tank between peaks. The tank can have its own burner or be heated by a boiler coil; see Indirect Water Heaters on a Boiler. A semi-instantaneous plant uses a plate heat exchanger fed by a boiler or other primary plant. The article says it holds only about 1 to 5 gallons, so it is compact, but the primary side has to respond fast when a shower starts. Instantaneous heaters have almost no storage.

The trade-off, per that article: storage buffers peaks but needs recovery capacity, while the heat exchanger plant is compact but depends on the primary plant answering quickly. The temperature rules below apply either way.

Why store hot and mix down

CDC's Legionella toolkit says to store hot water above 140 F, to keep circulating hot water from falling below 120 F, and to remove dead legs, which are sections of pipe with no or low flow. Hot enough to hold bacteria back is also hot enough to scald (see Water Heater Temperature Setting for the burn-time table). So the usual building answer is to store at 140 F and temper the water on its way out. CDC adds that thermostatic mixing valves belong as close to fixtures as possible, which also allows the loop to run above 120 F.

Some designs put one master mixing valve at the plant, so the whole loop runs tempered. The ASPE Pipeline article on this question reports that a federal facilities design standard (UFC 3-420-01) calls for distribution at 131 F through a master valve, and that others prefer a hotter loop with a valve at each fixture. It also notes a master valve adds cold water back into the system. Which design a building has is an engineering and code decision.

What the master valve can and cannot do

  • It is not the last line of scald defense. Watts' guide names the standards plainly: ASSE 1017 covers the hot water source, and it says 1017 devices should never deliver water directly to the user. Fixture-level protection is a separate standard (ASSE 1016 for showers and tubs, 1070 for sinks, per ASPE).
  • Minimum flow. Valves have a listed minimum. Watts lists 0.5 gpm for its small mixing valves and 3 to 7 gpm for its larger master models, depending on size, and warns that flow below the minimum can increase outlet deviation. A large valve in a building with a small night load can hunt, so tempered temperature drifts when only one tap is open.
  • Inlet temperature margin. In the ASPE article, a Caleffi engineer says a valve whose hot inlet is close to its set point sits nearly wide open on the hot side, leaving little room to regulate, and may hunt. A reader question there says these valves typically want a 15 to 20 F difference between inlet and outlet. Storing at 140 F and delivering 120 F gives 20 F.
  • Checks and service. Watts says check valves are required on ASSE 1017 applications and the supply should be flushed before the valves go into service.
  • Recirculating tempered piping. Watts says very little flow is needed to hold a minimum temperature in tempered piping, suggests setting the circulator's aquastat 5 to 10 F below the valve's mixed outlet, and notes that a circulator that runs continuously lets the pipe temperature climb toward the heater's. It also warns that one of its small valves must not be used to recirculate elevated-temperature water. Where the return piping connects depends on the design and the maker's drawing.

The recirculation loop

Water leaves the plant, climbs the risers, and a return line brings it back so a distant tap is not a long wait. The cost is that the loop loses heat all day. An NREL report on control strategies says distribution losses in multifamily buildings can be 30 to 50 percent of the energy input, and cites a California study in which only 35 percent of the input reached the user and 33 percent was lost in the recirculation loop. A DOE Building America presentation recommends at least R-4 on all loop piping and says R-4 on 3/4 inch pipe triples the cool-down time.

Balancing: getting flow to every riser

A pump pushes the most flow through the shortest, easiest path. The riser nearest the pump gets hot water and the farthest riser stays lukewarm. A balancing valve at the bottom of each riser adds resistance to the easy paths so every riser gets a share. The Steven Winter Associates design deck for Connecticut Green Bank says to put balancing specs in the drawings and a detail for riser balancing valves with a check valve.

A manual valve is set once, with a measuring step. A thermostatic balancing valve sets itself by return temperature. Caleffi's data sheet for its thermal balancing valve describes a cartridge that gradually closes as the water arriving at it reaches the set point, to a minimum position that never fully shuts, so the pump is not dead-headed and the valve still senses temperature. Its setting range is 95 to 140 F, with a factory setting of 130 F, and it says not to set above 120 F where no anti-scald valves are at the fixtures. Some models add a bypass for thermal disinfection. Read the maker's sheet for the model on site.

Return temperature checks

The return line tells you how the loop is behaving. Using CDC's number, circulating water should not drop below 120 F. In the NREL buildings with pumps running all the time, average supply was about 159 F and 131 F while average return was 153 F and 129 F, a drop of only 6 F and 2 F. Our own reading: the return is almost as hot as the supply, so the loop is moving more water than the heat loss needs. DOE's presentation puts the temperature sensor on the return line upstream of the pump and suggests the pump's set point sit at least 5 F below the heater set point. As a rule of thumb from these numbers (our own), a cold far riser points to balancing, and a return almost as hot as the supply points to over-pumping.

Worked example: loop flow and yearly heat loss

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption, not a measurement. Suppose a loop loses 12,000 Btu/h through its piping, and we want the return to be about 10 F cooler than the supply at the plant.

Step 1, the constant. One gallon of water weighs about 8.33 lb, and a pump rated in gpm runs 60 minutes each hour. Water takes about 1 Btu to warm one pound by 1 F. So 8.33 × 60 = 499.8, which we round to 500. That gives gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). The Caleffi data sheet states the same formula for sizing a recirculation loop with a 10 F design drop.

Step 2, the flow. gpm = 12,000 ÷ (500 × 10) = 12,000 ÷ 5,000 = 2.4 gpm. Check: 2.4 × 60 × 8.33 × 10 = 11,995 Btu/h, about 12,000. With a 20 F allowed drop the flow halves, 12,000 ÷ (500 × 20) = 1.2 gpm, but the far end of the loop runs cooler.

Step 3, the yearly heat. Assume the loop runs 24 hours a day, 8,760 hours. 12,000 Btu/h × 8,760 h = 105,120,000 Btu, or about 105 million Btu. Assume the heater is 80 percent efficient. Fuel = 105,120,000 ÷ 0.80 = 131,400,000 Btu, which is 1,314 therms at 100,000 Btu per therm.

So a modest, well-insulated loop still burns about 1,300 therms a year just to stay warm, before any shower is taken. The NREL report found demand-based pump control cut water-heating fuel use by about 6 to 7 percent in two Brooklyn test buildings. See Hot Water Recirculation Pumps for control types.

Field card: Central Hot Water Plants: Master Mixing Valves and Recirc Balancing

Store hot, mix down, circulate warm, and balance every riser.

  • CDC: store hot water above 140 F, keep circulating water at or above 120 F, remove dead legs.
  • Plant types: storage tank, semi-instantaneous heat exchanger (about 1 to 5 gallons), instantaneous.
  • ASSE 1017 is the source valve standard. Do not use it as the only scald protection.
  • Master valves have a minimum flow and want a margin between inlet and outlet; flow below the listed minimum increases outlet deviation.
  • Recirc flow: gpm = Btu/h ÷ (500 × ΔT); 500 is 8.33 lb/gal × 60 min.
  • Our example: 12,000 Btu/h and 10 F drop is 2.4 gpm; about 1,314 therms a year at 80 percent (assumed).
  • Return nearly as hot as supply means over-pumping. A cold far riser means check balancing.
