Maple Sugar House Ventilation: Arches, Steam Cupolas, Condensation and Carbon Monoxide

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A maple sugar house is a small steam plant with a roof on it. A fuel-fired evaporator boils off hundreds of pounds of water an hour, and all of it has to leave the building as steam while the fire gets the air it needs and its smoke goes up a stack. This lesson explains why the building is vented the way it is, and puts numbers on the steam.

The arch, the stack and draft

The evaporator sits on an arch, which is the firebox. It can be fired with wood, oil or gas. Whatever the fuel, the flue gases leave through a stack, and the stack works only if the building gives the fire enough air and a clear path up. A state university extension bulletin on small-scale processing says the evaporator fire has to be vented so smoke and ashes do not contaminate the syrup, which is the food-safety reason for a tight, well-drawing stack. For the mechanical reasons a chimney pulls and what weak draft looks like, see our lesson on combustion air for furnaces, boilers and water heaters. The same ideas apply to an arch, only bigger.

Steam: the load that surprises people

The fire is not the main ventilation problem. The steam is. A state health department's guidance for maple producers says the evaporation process makes "copious amounts of steam or condensate water," and that ventilation should be enough to minimize odors and vapors, with fans, screened windows or other effective means. A university extension sizing table for backyard and small commercial pans lists capacity from about 1 gallon per hour for a tiny pan up to 70 gallons per hour for a 3 by 8 foot evaporator. Bigger pans boil off more. Every one of those gallons becomes steam.

Cupolas and steam hoods

Steam is light and rises, so the usual answer is an opening at the highest point of the roof: a roof cupola over the pans, or a hood and vent stack that catches steam right above the evaporator. The health department guidance mentions a screen on the steam cupola, if there is one, to keep birds from roosting above an open evaporator. A university agricultural experiment station bulletin from 1978 on sap preheaters describes the hood approach for the preheater section: a close-fitted hood, because cold room air leaking in lowers heat transfer, and a damper in the hood stack that the operator closes until steam just whiffs out at the base of the hood. That keeps a slight positive pressure under the hood so steam goes up the stack instead of into the room.

Notice what that means for the building. A cupola or hood only moves steam if replacement air can come in somewhere. A sealed shed with a big cupola cannot exhaust much. Low openings for makeup air feed both the steam exhaust and the fire.

Condensation and dripping on cold roof framing

Steam that hits cold rafters, sheathing or the underside of a roof turns back into water and drips. The extension bulletin above says the plant must have a roof that protects the evaporator from the elements and from condensation, so dripping onto the evaporator is treated as a problem, not a nuisance. The 1978 bulletin makes the same point for the preheater: it says to catch condensate in a drip pan and drain it away instead of letting it fall back into the pan, because reboiling it wastes fuel. A steep roof, a continuous path from the pans to the vent, and warm surfaces near the cupola give condensate somewhere to go other than the pans.

Carbon monoxide from a fuel-fired arch in a closed building

The federal public health agency says carbon monoxide is an odorless, colorless gas found in fumes produced any time fuel is burned, that it can build up indoors, and that wood, oil and gas appliances that are not properly vented or maintained can release it. It lists blocked chimneys and improperly vented appliances as ways it collects inside a space. An arch is a fuel-burning appliance. A closed sugar house with the doors shut against a cold night, a weak stack, a bad gasket or a downdraft from a strong wind is exactly the setup that agency warns about. The agency also says to have fuel-burning appliances and chimneys serviced and inspected yearly and to use working battery-operated detectors. Its occupational page adds that CO can accumulate even in areas that appear well ventilated, and that symptoms include headache, dizziness and confusion. If a worker in a sugar house gets a headache and feels dizzy, get them to fresh air and seek medical help. Our carbon monoxide alarms lesson covers what to do when an alarm sounds.

Combustion air for the arch

A fuel-fired arch burns air as well as fuel, and the room has to supply it. If the cupola and any exhaust fan pull the building under negative pressure, they can compete with the stack and cause spillage of smoke into the room. Treat the fire and the steam exhaust as one air balance: provide makeup air through a dedicated low opening near the arch, and do not close it up in cold weather. Exact opening sizes depend on fuel, firing rate and local rules, so we do not give a figure here.

Reverse osmosis rooms and freezing

A maple extension guide notes that reverse osmosis can remove much of the water from sap before it reaches the evaporator, raising its sugar content from 1 to 3 percent up to as high as 10 percent. That shortens boiling time, so it also cuts the steam per gallon of syrup. The RO unit holds water and membranes, so the room it sits in has to stay above freezing when the evaporator is cold. That usually means a separate heated space, not the steam-filled boiling room, because the steam would condense on cold pumps and panels. Check the maker's instructions for the lowest temperature the unit tolerates, and drain or flush as they say before a cold stretch.

Steam heat recovery as a general idea

A preheater uses steam heat that would otherwise leave the building. The 1978 university bulletin describes heating incoming sap, which is typically 35 to 45 F, to about 190 F before it enters the pan. In their tests, average evaporator efficiency was 72.8 percent without a preheater and 84.3 percent with one. The authors also note that a preheater can raise production or cut fuel, but not both at once. It also moves some of the steam condensing inside the preheater instead of in the room. See our lesson on flash steam and low-pressure recovery for a similar idea in a plant.

Worked example (our own): the steam from one evaporator

Our own example with our own assumptions. We assume a 3 by 8 foot evaporator boiling off 70 gallons of water per hour, which matches the size-table entry above but is our assumption about what that table means. We assume the steam leaves at 212 F and atmospheric pressure.

  1. Mass of water. Water weighs 8.34 lb per gallon, so 70 gal/h × 8.34 lb/gal = 583.8 lb/h of steam.
  2. Heat leaving with the steam. A university engineering text puts the latent heat of vaporization at 212 F at 970.11 Btu per pound, so we use about 970. 583.8 lb/h × 970 Btu/lb = 566,286 Btu/h, or roughly 566,000 Btu/h.
  3. Steam per minute. 583.8 lb/h ÷ 60 = 9.73 lb/min.
  4. Volume of steam. A 1914 university engineering bulletin gives the specific volume of saturated steam at 212 F as about 26.8 cubic feet per pound. 9.73 lb/min × 26.8 ft³/lb = 260.8, so about 261 cubic feet per minute.

So one mid-size evaporator makes about 261 cubic feet of pure steam a minute. The real air stream in a cupola is larger, because steam mixes with room air and has to stay hot enough not to condense on the way out. A building of 7,200 cubic feet (our assumption: 20 by 30 by 12 feet) would be filled with 261 cfm of steam in about 28 minutes if nothing left (7,200 ÷ 261 = 27.6). The roughly 566,000 Btu/h also goes up the cupola, which is why the heat recovery in the section above is worth thinking about.

Field card: Maple Sugar House Ventilation

The fire needs air in, the steam needs a way out, and the building has to do both.

  • Steam is the big load: our example is about 584 lb/h and 261 cfm for 70 gal/h.
  • Vent steam high, through a cupola or hood, and give it makeup air low.
  • Cold framing drips. Keep condensate off the pans and drain it away.
  • Fuel-fired arch in a closed building means a carbon monoxide risk. Keep the stack and vents clear and use working detectors.
  • Keep RO equipment in a space that stays above freezing, away from the steam.
  • A steam preheater reuses heat that would leave through the roof.
