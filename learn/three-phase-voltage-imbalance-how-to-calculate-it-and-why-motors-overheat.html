Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance: How to Calculate It and Why Motors Overheat

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A three-phase motor or compressor wants three equal voltages. When one leg runs a little high or low, the windings do not share the load evenly and the extra heat shortens motor life. This lesson shows how to measure the three voltages, do the percent imbalance math, and read what published sources say about heat, limits and derating.

Measure the three line-to-line voltages

With the unit running, read the voltage between L1 and L2, L2 and L3, and L3 and L1. A trade article by John Tomczyk in ACHR News says you can take these at the motor terminals, the disconnect or the contactor. A Trane service manual says to take the readings at the compressor terminals, and that if the imbalance is 2.0 percent or less at the disconnect but higher at the terminals, the cause is faulty wiring inside the unit. Measuring live voltage on a commercial unit is energized work. Use a meter with the right category rating and follow your site procedure; see Multimeter Safety and CAT Ratings.

The percent imbalance formula

NEMA's definition, as the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) motor tip sheet states it, is 100 times the largest deviation of any line voltage from the average voltage, divided by the average. In steps:

  1. Add the three readings and divide by 3 to get the average.
  2. Subtract the average from each reading and ignore the sign.
  3. Take the biggest of those three differences.
  4. Divide it by the average and multiply by 100.

You compare against the average of your three readings, not against the nameplate voltage. Nameplate tolerance is a separate question. One ACHR News article gives plus or minus 10 percent of nameplate as the allowed range, so a 230 volt unit would be expected between 207 and 253 volts. Supply can sit inside that range and still be out of balance.

Worked example

Say a 460 volt rooftop compressor reads 456 V (L1-L2), 468 V (L2-L3) and 480 V (L3-L1).

  • Sum: 456 + 468 + 480 = 1,404 V.
  • Average: 1,404 / 3 = 468 V.
  • Deviations: 468 - 456 = 12 V, 468 - 468 = 0 V, 480 - 468 = 12 V.
  • Largest deviation: 12 V.
  • Imbalance: 12 / 468 = 0.0256, times 100 = about 2.56 percent.

For comparison, Tomczyk's published example uses 216, 223 and 225 volts: average 221.33, largest deviation 5.33, imbalance 2.4 percent.

What does 2.56 percent mean for the motor? Using the DOE and Power Monitors figures in the next sections, current imbalance could run 6 to 10 times as large, about 15 to 26 percent (2.56 x 6 = 15.4 and 2.56 x 10 = 25.6). The DOE heat rule gives 2 x 2.56 x 2.56 = 13.1 percent extra temperature rise, about 13 degrees C on a motor that normally rises 100 degrees C. The reading is also over the 2.0 percent maximum in the Trane manual.

Why a small voltage difference makes a bigger current difference

The DOE tip sheet says current imbalance can be 6 to 10 times the voltage imbalance. Its own example: 2.5 percent voltage imbalance at full load produced a 27.7 percent line current imbalance. A Pumps & Systems article adds that losses rise with the square of current, so a phase carrying 10 percent more current than the others has at least 21 percent more loss (1.10 x 1.10 = 1.21).

Heat and winding life

The DOE tip sheet gives a rule: extra temperature rise in percent equals 2 times the square of the percent voltage imbalance. At 2 percent that is 8 percent more rise, about 8 degrees C on a motor with a 100 degree C rise. Tomczyk's ACHR articles use the same formula. DOE also says winding insulation life is cut in half for every 10 degrees C of added operating temperature, and that imbalance lowers efficiency. It names 120 Hz vibration as a warning sign that calls for an immediate voltage balance check.

Limits and derating: what each source says

The sources do not all agree, so say whose number you are quoting.

  • DOE tip sheet: keep imbalance at the motor terminals at 1 percent or less. Above 1 percent the motor should be derated using the NEMA MG-1 derating figure. DOE adds that most manufacturers' warranties are void above that level.
  • Trane service manual: maximum allowable imbalance is 2.0 percent, measured at the compressor terminals.
  • ACHR News (Tomczyk): more than 2 percent is a problem on three-phase compressor motors.
  • Power Monitors white paper: under 1 percent is ideal, over 2 percent is unacceptable, and 5 percent or more means a motor should not be run on that system.

For derating, the text of these sources gives points rather than a full table. The Power Monitors page reads the NEMA MG-1 curve at 3.13 percent imbalance as a derating factor of 0.88, so a 10 hp motor would be limited to 8.8 hp. Pumps & Systems says that at the 5 percent limit a motor must be derated to about 75 percent of nameplate horsepower. Our 2.56 percent example is above the DOE 1 percent target and the Trane 2 percent maximum, and the derating factor would fall somewhere above 0.88. Read the exact factor from the NEMA MG-1 curve. Always use the equipment maker's limit for the unit in front of you.

Common causes

The DOE tip sheet lists faulty power factor correction equipment, an unbalanced or unstable utility supply, a transformer bank too small for its three-phase load, unevenly spread single-phase loads, unidentified single-phase-to-ground faults and an open circuit on the distribution primary. An EC&M article names unevenly distributed single-phase loads and advises checking the building's own distribution before blaming the power company. Trane's advice to compare readings at the disconnect and at the compressor terminals helps separate supply trouble from wiring inside the unit. Tomczyk advises telling the building owner and the power company when imbalance is found.

Check the currents too

Voltage tells you about the supply; current shows what the motor is doing. Clamp each leg (see Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter) and compare. The same average-and-largest-deviation arithmetic can be run on the three amp readings; the sources we read give no maker limit for it, so use it as a trend and a comparison to the 6 to 10 times figure above. For background see Electrical Basics for HVAC, and for where three-phase motors show up see Rooftop Unit Basics and Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans.

Field card: Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance

Average the three, find the farthest one, divide, times 100.

  • Read L1-L2, L2-L3, L3-L1 with the unit running.
  • Imbalance % = largest deviation from average / average x 100.
  • Example: 456, 468, 480 V gives 468 V average, 12 V deviation, 2.56 %.
  • DOE: current imbalance can be 6 to 10 times voltage imbalance.
  • DOE heat rule: extra rise % = 2 x (imbalance %) squared; insulation life halves per 10 C.
  • DOE target 1 % or less; Trane manual maximum 2.0 %. Use the maker's number for your unit.
  • Compare readings at the disconnect and at the terminals to find bad connections.
