Pump NPSH Available vs Required: Calculating Suction Margin on Open and Closed Systems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every pump data sheet gives a number called NPSH required, and every system has a number called NPSH available. If the second is not comfortably bigger than the first, the pump cavitates. This lesson shows where each number comes from and how to do the arithmetic, once for a cooling tower sump and once for a hot condensate tank.

What the two terms mean

NPSH stands for net positive suction head. NPSH required (NPSHr) belongs to the pump. It is the inlet head the pump needs, at a given flow, to avoid cavitation. Grundfos describes it as the lowest inlet pressure the specific pump needs at a given flow, and says makers publish it in their pump data. On a typical curve sheet it is a separate line, usually near the bottom, as the article in The Driller notes. NPSH available (NPSHa) belongs to the system. It is what the piping, tank and water temperature actually deliver at the pump inlet. The rule is simple: NPSHa must be greater than NPSHr. Cavitation itself is covered in Pump Cavitation Basics; this page is about the numbers.

Where does the pump's number come from? The U.S. Department of Energy's pumping sourcebook says the Hydraulic Institute defines NPSHr as the point where the pump's total head falls 3 percent because of cavitation, a criterion now called NPSH3. An article in the Pumps & Systems NPSH e-book says the same thing in other words, that the pump's performance is degraded by 3 percent. So NPSHr is not the point where bubbles first appear. It is a test point, and bubbles can start before it. The data comes from the pump maker, not from a field measurement. Read the pump curve with Reading Pump Curves and System Curves, because NPSHr changes with flow and rises as you run farther out on the curve.

The terms of NPSHa

An R. L. Deppmann article, drawing on Bell & Gossett material, lists four terms for the simple case with no velocity head: HA, HZ, HF and HVP. Put into words:

  • Atmospheric or system pressure head (HA). The absolute pressure on the water surface, in feet of water. For a cooling tower basin or a vented tank, that is atmospheric pressure. For a closed hydronic loop, it is the pressure set by the expansion tank.
  • Static head (HZ). How far the water surface is above (plus) or below (minus) the pump suction centerline.
  • Friction loss (HF). Head lost in the pipe, fittings, valves and strainer between the surface and the pump. It only ever subtracts.
  • Vapor pressure head (HVP). The absolute vapor pressure of the water at the pump suction temperature, in feet. It also only subtracts.

So, as The Driller writes it: NPSHa = static head + atmospheric pressure − friction head − vapor pressure. Some texts add a small velocity-head term; the Pumps & Systems e-book includes one, and we leave it out below to match the simpler version. Check which version your pump maker uses.

Pressure has to be turned into feet before you add terms. The Pumps & Systems material gives h = 2.31 × p / SG, where h is feet of head, p is psi and SG is the liquid's specific gravity. In the example we skip the specific gravity and divide by a cited specific weight instead.

Why hot water and open tanks are the risky cases

Vapor pressure depends only on temperature, according to the Pumps & Systems material, and it climbs fast. One author in that e-book gives about 0.41 ft at 50 F and about 11.2 ft at 160 F. Atmospheric pressure is nothing to lean on either: it is fixed, so an open tank cannot be pressurized to help. The Deppmann article says NPSHa is a function of the pan elevation, friction and temperature, and that combinations of these can hurt you. It stresses checking the tower's minimum basin level against the pump centerline and avoiding undersized reducers at the suction. Condensate and feed tanks are a harder version: the water sits near its boiling point, so vapor pressure eats nearly all of the atmospheric term, and the tank is often only a few feet above the pump. For the plant side, see Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

Worked example: cooling tower sump at 85 F

Our own arithmetic. A sump pump at sea level draws from an open cooling tower basin. Water is 85 F, the basin level is 4 ft above the pump centerline, suction friction is 2.5 ft, and the curve says NPSHr is 12 ft.

  1. Atmospheric pressure: 14.7 psia at sea level (Engineering Toolbox altitude table). Convert to psf: 14.7 × 144 = 2,116.8 lb/ft².
  2. Specific weight of 85 F water: 62.15 lb/ft³ (Engineering Toolbox, imperial water table). Atmospheric head = 2,116.8 / 62.15 = 34.06 ft.
  3. Vapor pressure head at 85 F: 1.38 ft of water, from the same table.
  4. NPSHa = 34.06 + 4.0 − 2.5 − 1.38 = 34.18 ft.
  5. Margin = NPSHa − NPSHr = 34.18 − 12 = 22.18 ft.
  6. Margin ratio = 34.18 / 12 = 2.85.

Comfortable. For comparison, the DOE sourcebook cites a common design rule of NPSHa at least 25 percent above NPSHr (a ratio of 1.25, here 15 ft), and a Pumps & Systems author reports that the Hydraulic Institute recommends a ratio of 1.1 to 2.5 depending on pump design, adding that some experts want more and that you should check the maker's requirement. We name the Hydraulic Institute guidance but do not link to copies of it.

Same pump, 180 F vented condensate tank

Our own arithmetic. Same pump, same piping, same 4 ft of water above the centerline, but now the tank holds 180 F condensate and is vented to the atmosphere.

  1. Specific weight at 180 F: 60.57 lb/ft³. Atmospheric head = 2,116.8 / 60.57 = 34.95 ft.
  2. Vapor pressure head at 180 F: 17.85 ft.
  3. NPSHa = 34.95 + 4.0 − 2.5 − 17.85 = 18.60 ft.
  4. Margin = 18.60 − 12 = 6.60 ft. Ratio = 18.60 / 12 = 1.55.

Heating the water by 95 F cut NPSHa nearly in half, from 34.18 to 18.60 ft, and the margin from 22.18 to 6.60 ft. It still passes, but now the tank level matters. Suppose the tank runs low, so the level is only 1 ft above the centerline:

  1. NPSHa = 34.95 + 1.0 − 2.5 − 17.85 = 15.60 ft. Margin = 3.60 ft, ratio = 1.30. That is near the DOE 25 percent rule of thumb (15.0 ft).
  2. Now let the suction strainer load up and add 4 ft of friction, so friction is 6.5 ft: NPSHa = 34.95 + 1.0 − 6.5 − 17.85 = 11.60 ft. Margin = −0.40 ft, ratio = 0.97. NPSHa is below NPSHr, so expect cavitation.

The 4 ft strainer loss is an assumed number for the example, not a published value. The Engineering Toolbox vapor pressure heads are given at each temperature, so the same head conversion is used throughout.

What techs see and hear

Grundfos says too little inlet pressure can bring the pump's lowest internal pressure below the liquid's evaporation pressure, causing noise and possibly failure. In the field that means a gravel or rattling sound, vibration, low flow and pressure, and pitted impellers; see Pump Cavitation Basics for the symptom list and its sources. Worn seals can follow, as in Pump Seal Leaks and Strainer Habits. Report it and follow your site procedure; lock out and relieve pressure before you open anything, and let hot water cool.

Field fixes

  • Raise suction pressure. Raise the water level or the tank, or in a closed loop raise the pressure set by the expansion tank (Deppmann, citing the closed-system case).
  • Lower the water temperature. Vapor pressure depends only on temperature, so cooler water helps right away.
  • Shorten or upsize the suction piping. In The Driller's example, going to a 1¼ inch suction pipe cut friction to 4 ft and raised NPSHa from 9.5 to 11.5 ft, ahead of an NPSHr of about 11 ft. Deppmann's example shows an undersized reducer at the suction dropping NPSHa from 25.7 to 18.2 ft.
  • Clean strainers and open valves. A Pumps & Systems author lists clogged suction piping, a partly closed suction valve and clogged strainers as common causes, and says comparing an upstream gauge with the pump suction gauge shows strainer blockage.

If the numbers still do not work, the pump selection may be wrong for the duty. That is a call for the engineer or the pump maker, not a field patch. New to the site? Start Here lays out the path.

Field card: Pump NPSH Available vs Required: Calculating Suction Margin on Open and Closed Systems

NPSHa has to beat NPSHr, with room to spare.

  • NPSHa = atmospheric (or system) head + static head − friction − vapor pressure head.
  • NPSHr comes from the pump curve; it is the 3 percent head drop point (NPSH3).
  • Feet of head = 2.31 × psi / specific gravity.
  • Example: 85 F, 4 ft level, 2.5 ft friction: NPSHa 34.18 ft vs NPSHr 12 ft, ratio 2.85.
  • Same at 180 F: NPSHa 18.60 ft, ratio 1.55. Low tank and dirty strainer can erase the rest.
  • DOE cites a 25 percent margin rule of thumb; Hydraulic Institute guidance runs 1.1 to 2.5 by pump design.
  • Fixes: more suction pressure, cooler water, short and large suction pipe, clean strainer.
