Condenser Fan Blades: Rotation, Pitch, Matching a Replacement, and Blade Height

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condenser fan blade looks like a simple stamped part, but four small details decide whether it moves the right air: which way it turns, how steeply it is pitched, how it matches the motor, and how high it sits in the orifice ring. This lesson covers all four, plus the wear and safety points that come with changing one.

What the blade has to do

A condenser fan pulls air through the coil so the refrigerant can dump its heat. A trade-press article on condenser blades describes them as blades built to work against static pressure, because the coil is a restriction. It warns that too little airflow removes less heat and cuts efficiency, while too much airflow can overload the motor. For the electrical side of the same fan, see Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures. For what a dirty coil does to pressures, see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure.

Rotation direction

Blades are made clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW). A parts maker's how-to article stresses that rotation is referenced from the shaft end of the motor. The same article says that if the blade pushes air forward, the blade rotation matches the motor rotation, and if it pulls air back over the motor, the blade is the opposite of the motor. A trade-press article puts it another way: the blade direction is judged from the air-discharge side, and the motor nameplate may list rotation. Because the two sources use different viewpoints, do not trust a blade by name alone. Read the old blade, the motor label and the unit's airflow direction, then confirm after install that air leaves the unit the way it did before.

Pitch, blade count and size

Pitch is the twist of the blade. The trade-press article says it is measured with a pitch gauge at the spider or lobe, not out on the blade, and gives an example: an 18 inch blade at 33 degrees moves about 10 percent more air than an 18 inch blade at 27 degrees. A steeper blade also loads the motor harder. The same article says more blades at the same pitch and diameter give more airflow, and that a three-blade fan can be swapped for a four-blade fan if the airflow matches. A fan maker's performance guide agrees in general terms that blade count changes both free-air volume and pressure ability. It says more blades usually lower free-air volume and raise the ability to work against pressure.

Matching checklist

  • Diameter. Measure from the center to a tip and double it, as the parts maker's article describes. A trade-training tech tip says the diameter cannot change because the gap between the blade tip and the shroud is usually 1/2 to 1 inch and affects air movement.
  • Number of blades and pitch. Copy the old blade, or use the unit maker's part number.
  • Rotation. CW or CCW, checked as above.
  • Bore. Must fit the motor shaft diameter. Check the shaft, not the old hub, since a worn hub can mislead.
  • Motor rpm and horsepower. Find both on the nameplate. The parts maker's article selects a blade by matching rpm and then picking a pitch whose rated horsepower is just above the motor's. Its example is an 18 inch blade on a 1/10 hp, 825 rpm motor.

Speed matters most. The trade-press article says not to put a 1,100 rpm motor's blade on a 1,725 rpm motor, and notes that a 20 inch, 24 degree blade needs about a 1/6 hp motor at 1,140 rpm but at least a 1/2 hp motor at 1,725 rpm.

Blade height in the orifice ring

The orifice ring (shroud) guides air through the blade. If the blade sits too low or too high in it, air can swirl and recirculate instead of leaving in a clean column. One trade-training tech tip says the blade and shroud are designed so the middle of the blade lines up with the bottom of the shroud, and that if they do not match, airflow can decrease. It adds that a replacement motor that is deeper than the original can leave the blade lower than designed, which matters most on small units with motors under 1/4 hp. A second tech tip says the usual maker guidance is about one third of the blade above the shroud and two thirds below, warns that a blade hanging too low can make turbulent air with poor throw out of the cabinet, and suggests measuring from the old blade hub to the fan guard rather than to the motor casing. These two descriptions are worded differently, so we do not teach one number as law. Measure the old blade's position before you pull it, and follow the unit maker's drawing when there is one.

Bent, cracked and out-of-balance blades

A bent or cracked blade, or one with a missing balance weight, shakes the motor, the cabinet and the refrigerant lines. Replace a cracked or bent blade rather than straightening it. After the swap, spin it by hand with power locked out and confirm the tips clear the ring all the way around, then run it and look for shake. A wobbling blade tip also means the hub is loose or the shaft is bent. For how vibration is measured and what imbalance looks like, see Vibration Analysis Basics for Fans and Pumps.

Set screws, hub keys and shaft rust

This part is general shop practice, not taken from a source we read, so apply the unit maker's instructions first. Many blades lock on the shaft with a set screw that seats on the shaft flat, sometimes with a key. A loose set screw lets the blade creep up or down the shaft, which changes blade height and can make the blade rub the ring or walk off. Check the screw seats on the flat and re-check it after the first run. A rusty shaft makes the old hub hard to remove and the new one hard to seat, so clean off surface rust and confirm the shaft is straight and the correct size. Heavy pitting or a bent shaft means the motor needs replacing. The parts maker's article also notes interchangeable hubs exist, so a hub can be matched to a motor shaft.

Removing a blade safely

A condenser blade can start on a call for cooling without warning, and a capacitor can hold charge. Use lockout and verify dead; see Lockout/Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. OSHA's lockout/tagout material (29 CFR 1910.147) describes the order: prepare, shut down, isolate, apply your own lock or tag, relieve stored energy, then verify isolation before work. Also stay clear of the fan's path when you restore power, since a loose blade can fly.

Worked example: what a temperature rise tells you

You can estimate condenser airflow from how much the air warms as it passes through the coil. The relation is heat rejected equals 1.08 times airflow (cfm) times air temperature rise (°F). The 1.08 is our own arithmetic from standard air: 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F × 60 min/h = 1.08. It is only close at normal conditions.

Assumptions (our own, for teaching): a 3-ton system doing 36,000 Btu/h of cooling, with compressor input adding about 25 percent, so about 45,000 Btu/h is rejected; standard air.

  1. If the air rises 20°F: cfm = 45,000 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 45,000 ÷ 21.6 ≈ 2,083 cfm.
  2. If a wrong blade or a low blade height lets the same heat leave with a 25°F rise: cfm = 45,000 ÷ (1.08 × 25) = 45,000 ÷ 27 ≈ 1,667 cfm.
  3. Ratio: 1,667 ÷ 2,083 ≈ 0.80, so about 20 percent less air for a rise 5°F higher.

The real rise varies across the coil face, so average several readings, and the arithmetic is only a screening tool. In practice a lower pitch or wrong height trends this way: with the trade-press example, the 27 degree blade moves about 10 percent less than the 33 degree one at the same size, so 1 ÷ 1.10 ≈ 0.91 of the air.

Speed check. Using the cube relation in a U.S. Department of Energy motor tip sheet, power varies with the cube of the speed ratio. Going from 1,100 to 1,725 rpm is a ratio of 1,725 ÷ 1,100 ≈ 1.568, and 1.568³ ≈ 3.86. That is why the same blade on the faster motor needs a much larger motor. See Fan Laws.

Checking the result

  • Temperature rise. Compare the rise across the coil, at the same load and outdoor air, before and after the change. A bigger rise means less air.
  • Amp draw. Compare running amps against the motor nameplate. The trade-press article notes that too much air can overload the motor, so a blade that pushes the motor over nameplate amps is the wrong match.
  • Head pressure. If head pressure runs higher than expected, the tech tip says to check that the blade is the right size and sitting at the right height.
  • Look and feel. Air should leave the top evenly, with no rub, shake or strange noise.

Field card: Condenser Fan Blades

Right size, right spin, right height, locked off.

  • Match diameter, blade count, pitch, rotation, bore and motor rpm.
  • Rotation is judged from a stated viewpoint; confirm airflow direction after install.
  • Pitch changes airflow: 33 degrees moves about 10 percent more than 27 degrees (trade-press example).
  • Never run a blade on a faster motor than it was made for.
  • Match blade height to the old blade or the maker's drawing; a deeper motor can drop it.
  • Cracked, bent or loose blades shake the unit. Replace, then check the set screw.
  • Lock out and verify dead before touching a blade.
  • Screening estimate: cfm = Btu/h rejected ÷ (1.08 × temperature rise).
