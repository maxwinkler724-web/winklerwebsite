Duct Traverse with a Pitot Tube: Velocity Pressure to FPM and Total CFM

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A flow hood only works at a register, and a blower table only works if the conditions match the chart. When someone needs to know what a fan is really moving, the reference answer is a duct traverse: slide a pitot tube through a row of small holes in a straight run of duct, read the velocity pressure at each spot, and turn the readings into cfm. This lesson walks through it, including one trap that changes the answer.

Check your own spec first

Check your own spec first. The sources below do not agree on every number, such as point counts and straight-duct lengths. They are written for different jobs. On a real job, follow the procedure your project specification or test-and-balance standard names, such as ASHRAE Standard 111, AMCA Standard 203 or ISO 3966 (we name these in plain text only and do not link copies). This page is general education.

Why a traverse is the reference method

Fluke calls a duct traverse "the most precise method" of getting airflow data, and a register hood or a table lookup does not sample the whole fan duct that way. A traverse is a set of regularly spaced velocity and pressure measurements across the cross section of a straight duct. A paper by Hall, hosted on commissioning.org, also calls the duct traverse the primary, preferred airflow method. The reason is simple: air does not move at one speed across a duct. It is slow along the walls and fastest near the middle, so one reading would mislead you. A traverse samples the whole cross section. measureQuick says a properly done traverse is typically good to about 3 to 5 percent, while Aerovent, a fan maker, says a typical field fan test lands around 7 to 11 percent uncertainty once everything is counted. For quicker spot checks, see Measuring Airflow (CFM).

The pitot tube and its ports

A pitot tube is a thin double-wall probe. Dwyer's instruction manual describes a total pressure opening that faces upstream into the flow, and a static pressure opening that senses the pressure on the duct side. Hall's paper says the tube senses total and static pressure and that the difference between them is the velocity pressure. Fluke states it as a formula: total pressure minus static pressure equals velocity pressure. Static pressure is what you already know from Airflow and Static Pressure Basics. Velocity pressure is the extra push you feel only when you face moving air.

Connect the total pressure port to the high side of the manometer and the static port to the low side. The manometer then shows velocity pressure directly, in inches of water column (in. w.c.). Dwyer's manual adds two details: the faces of the openings must be perpendicular to the airflow, and if the reading comes up negative, reverse the connections. Fluke adds that the tube should be kept parallel to the duct wall with its nose facing the air.

From velocity pressure to fpm

For standard air, velocity in feet per minute equals 4005 times the square root of velocity pressure in in. w.c. measureQuick gives this form, with an example of 0.03 in. w.c. working out to about 693 fpm, and notes that it assumes standard density of 0.075 lb/ft³. Aerovent writes the same relationship with a different constant, 1096 times the square root of velocity pressure divided by density. Hall says a density correction is needed when the air temperature is more than 30°F from the 70°F standard or the site is more than 2,000 ft above sea level, and Aerovent says to measure density at the same plane as the traverse. Fluke notes its meter assumes 29.92 in. Hg and 70°F.

Low readings are hard. measureQuick says pitot readings below roughly 500 fpm (under about 0.016 in. w.c.) are unreliable with most field manometers, and Hall says traverse accuracy is better at 1,000 fpm or above.

Where to read: points, holes and straight duct

Pick a long, straight section of duct. Fluke prefers 10 equivalent diameters of straight duct upstream of the traverse and 3 downstream, and says 5 upstream and 1 downstream may still give adequate results. measureQuick cites ASHRAE 111 for at least 7.5 diameters after a disturbance and 3 before the next. Hall gives round ducts 2.5 diameters from a disturbance up to 2,500 fpm and one more diameter for each extra 100 fpm. Our own arithmetic for the example duct: the equivalent diameter is the square root of (4 × 24 × 12 ÷ π), about 19 in., so Fluke's 5-diameter minimum is about 96 in. (8 ft) and its 10-diameter preference is about 16 ft.

For a rectangular duct, divide the cross section into equal areas and read at each center. measureQuick lists minimum grids of 3 x 3 for up to 12 x 12 in., 3 x 4 for 12 x 24 in. and 4 x 4 for 24 x 24 in. or larger, and says going from 9 to 16 points typically changes the average by less than 3 percent. Fluke gives a different rule, at least 25 points, with log-Tchebycheff positions (spacing that puts points closer to the walls, such as 0.074, 0.288, 0.5, 0.712 and 0.926 of the duct width for 5 lines) and credits the rule to ISO 3966. For a round duct, measureQuick has you read along two perpendicular diameters, with points bunched near the wall, and Fluke calls for three diameters of at least 6 points each. Use the count in your spec; we use 16 so the arithmetic fits on one page.

Test holes: for a round duct, measureQuick has two small holes drilled 90 degrees apart in the same plane; for a rectangular duct, holes along one or two sides, with positions marked on the outside first. Its equipment list includes a drill and hole plugs for sealing the holes afterward. Macquarie University's procedure says to confirm every point is accessible first.

Why we average velocities, not velocity pressures

Velocity pressure follows the square root of velocity, not a straight line. Doubling the speed quadruples the pressure. Averaging the pressures first and converting once does not give the average speed. Hall, Fluke and measureQuick all describe converting each reading to velocity and then averaging. Aerovent explains the same thing from the other side: its test method averages the square roots of the velocity pressures (a root-mean-square style calculation) because velocity depends on the square root. Convert each point, average the fpm, then multiply by area.

Step 1. Add the 16 velocities: 17,367 fpm. Step 2. Average: 17,367 ÷ 16 = 1,085 fpm. Step 3. Area: 24 in. × 12 in. = 288 in.², and 288 ÷ 144 = 2.00 ft². Step 4. Airflow: 1,085 fpm × 2.00 ft² = about 2,171 cfm (2,170.9 using the unrounded average).

The wrong way. Add the 16 velocity pressures: 1.20 in. w.c. Average: 1.20 ÷ 16 = 0.075 in. w.c. Convert once: 4005 × √0.075 = 4005 × 0.2739 = about 1,097 fpm. Then 1,097 × 2.00 = about 2,194 cfm. That reads about 23 cfm, or 1 percent, high. The error is small here because the made-up readings are fairly even. It is always on the high side, since the square root curve bends, and it grows when the readings are more uneven, for example near an elbow.

Common errors

Turbulence. Reading too near a fitting is the classic error. measureQuick says poor point placement, too few points or measuring close to a disturbance can cause 10 to 20 percent error, and Aerovent warns that a collapsed flex connector or an unstable fan creates turbulence. Misalignment. Aerovent says to point the tube nose parallel to the duct axis, and Fluke says to keep it parallel to the wall, so check the tube is not angled on the way in. Negative readings. First swap the hose connections, as Dwyer says. If a few points still read negative or very low while others are high, Macquarie's procedure says to choose another traverse location. For its fan test method, Aerovent says to record negative readings as zero. Outliers. measureQuick says to check readings far above or below the average for an obstruction or damage, not to throw them out.

Once you have a cfm number, you compare it to design as in Reading a Test and Balance Report. If you change fan speed, the fan laws predict how the numbers move.

Field card: Pitot Tube Duct Traverse

Read many points, convert each, then average the speeds.

  • Total port to the high side, static port to the low side: the manometer shows velocity pressure.
  • Standard air: fpm = 4005 × √VP (in. w.c.). Correct for density if your standard says so.
  • Convert every point to fpm first, then average the fpm.
  • cfm = average fpm × duct area in ft² (in.² ÷ 144).
  • Use a straight run, read at the centers of equal areas, and keep the tube parallel to the flow.
  • Our made-up example: 1,085 fpm × 2.00 ft² = about 2,171 cfm. Averaging VP first gave about 2,194.
  • Follow the point count and straight-duct rule in your spec.
