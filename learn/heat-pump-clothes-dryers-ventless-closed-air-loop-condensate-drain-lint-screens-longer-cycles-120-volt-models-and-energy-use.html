Heat Pump Clothes Dryers: Ventless Drying, Condensate, Lint Screens and Energy Use

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump dryer has no outdoor vent. Instead of throwing warm, damp air outside, it dries that same air and sends it around again. This lesson explains the loop, what the owner has to empty and clean, and how the energy use compares with a vented electric dryer, using federal numbers.

Check the label and the manual

Check the label and the manual. Models differ in voltage, drain options, filter layout and cleaning schedules. The sources below are federal and national-lab pages, and they disagree with each other on a few points (noted where they do). The installation guide for the exact model controls the job. This page is general education.

How the closed loop dries clothes

Two loops run side by side, shown in the diagram. In the air loop, a fan pulls warm, damp air out of the drum and pushes it across the evaporator, a cold coil. The coil cools the air below its dew point, so water vapor condenses into liquid and the air leaves cool and dry. That air then passes over the condenser, a hot coil, which reheats it. The hot, dry air goes back into the drum and picks up more moisture from the clothes. The cycle repeats until the load is dry.

In the refrigerant loop, the compressor raises the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant vapor and sends it to the condenser, where it gives up heat to the air and turns to liquid. The liquid passes through an expansion valve (a metering device), gets cold, and flows through the evaporator, where it absorbs heat and moisture from the air and boils back to vapor. Then the vapor returns to the compressor. So one refrigerant loop does two jobs: the cold coil removes water and the hot coil adds heat. If you have studied a dehumidifier or a heat pump water heater, this is the same idea pointed at a laundry load.

An ENERGY STAR scoping report from the program's early work on dryers described a typical heat pump dryer as moving about 2 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity it uses. That is the reason for the energy savings covered below.

Why no outdoor vent is needed

The Building America guide from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) says these dryers recirculate hot air in the drum instead of exhausting it, so they need no outside vent. ENERGY STAR makes the same point: because the loop is closed, there is no need for a dryer vent, which can cut installation cost in a home that has no duct. The PNNL guide adds that when a vented dryer is replaced, the old duct should be capped with foil mastic tape and the gaps sealed, and that the dryer saves some heating and cooling energy because it does not blow conditioned air outdoors. The fire risk from a packed exhaust duct, covered in our dryer vent lesson, does not apply to a ventless dryer, but lint still has to be managed (next sections).

Condensate: where the water goes

All the water that left the clothes ends up as liquid in the pan under the evaporator. PNNL says the water must be drained or emptied after each load. There are two layouts:

  • Drain hose. A pump moves the water through a drain hose to the washer's drain, a utility sink or a floor drain. The guide says to stay within the maximum height and length in the manual, keep the hose from kinking, secure it, and use a non-return valve if it goes into a standpipe so water cannot flow back.
  • Water tank. Some models store the water in a drawer or tank that is emptied by hand into a sink after every load or every few loads, as the model instructs. ENERGY STAR says the tank must be emptied after each load if no drain is practical.

When a dryer reports a drain or tank problem, check the hose route, kinks and the tank before looking at the sealed system, and read the model's fault list.

Lint: three places to clean

Lint does not go out a duct, so it stays in the cabinet and must be removed by hand. PNNL lists the routine this way:

  • Clean the main lint filter after every load. The guide calls this very important for performance and the life of the dryer.
  • Empty the secondary lint trap or screen monthly, or when the control panel prompts.
  • Rinse and clean the condenser (and wash the filter) about every 30 to 40 loads, since fabric softener and dryer sheet residue can coat them; one section of the guide says every few months. Follow the manual for how to reach the coil.

The scoping report notes that one maker said blocked airflow can cut dryer efficiency by more than 30%. Cleaning is the first check on a dryer that has become slow to finish. Unplug the dryer before opening panels.

Cycle time and drum temperature

Expect the cycle time to depend on the model. ENERGY STAR gives typical dry times of 37 to 70 minutes for its certified heat pump dryers, but the PNNL pages disagree with each other: one says condensing dryers can take about twice as long, another says heat pump dryers take the same time as conventional electric ones. We could not settle this from federal pages, so check the cycle times of the specific model. PNNL also notes that some heat pump dryers have a smaller drum than conventional ones. We did not find a federal figure for drum air temperature, so we do not state one; the maker's literature has it. Time and energy trade against each other on any dryer: in one test cited by the scoping report, a lower heat setting cut energy use by 13% at the cost of 14 more minutes.

Electrical supply

Conventional full-size electric dryers need a 240 volt circuit. PNNL says all-in-one heat pump washer-dryers can be plugged into a standard 120 volt circuit instead of a 240 volt line, which makes gas-to-electric swaps easier. The PNNL pages we read do not state the voltage for stand-alone heat pump dryers, so read the nameplate and manual and match the circuit and receptacle to them. PNNL's guide says to use a grounded three-prong outlet and never remove the ground prong. The federal dryer standard also lists a 120 volt class, but it is for compact vented dryers, so confirm the voltage for the exact model.

Room heat, humidity and placement

The dryer is not a heat sink for free. PNNL says a condensing dryer releases heat into the room it sits in, so do not place it next to a refrigerator, whose compressor would then work harder. The room needs airflow: a small closet should have a louvered door or a door left open while it runs. Leave at least 3/8 inch under the dryer for air, and do not block that gap with a toe-kick panel. The room should stay at or above 40 degrees Fahrenheit for the condensing to work properly, which rules out an unheated garage in winter. The federal pages we read give no humidity figure, so we cannot give a number for room moisture. For a way to measure it yourself, see humidity meters and the salt test.

Worked example: yearly kWh and cost (our own example)

This is our own example, not a federal result. We take the current federal minimum for a standard vented electric dryer and apply ENERGY STAR's "around 70% less energy" claim for heat pump dryers. The result is an estimate, not a measured value for any model.

  1. Federal figure used. The Department of Energy minimum combined energy factor (CEF) for a standard vented electric dryer is 3.73 pounds of clothes per kWh. The test load is 8.45 pounds (ENERGY STAR criteria page).
  2. Assumption: 300 loads a year. ENERGY STAR says the average family washes about 300 loads a year. The federal test procedure uses 283 loads instead, so your results will move with your own laundry habits.
  3. Assumption: 17 cents per kWh. This is our own assumed electric rate. Use your bill's rate.
  4. Vented electric, per load. 8.45 lb divided by 3.73 lb/kWh = about 2.27 kWh per load.
  5. Vented electric, per year. 2.27 kWh times 300 loads = about 680 kWh. At $0.17 per kWh, 680 × 0.17 = about $115.60.
  6. Heat pump, per year. 70% less leaves 30%: 680 × 0.30 = about 204 kWh. At $0.17, 204 × 0.17 = about $34.70.
  7. Difference. 680 − 204 = about 476 kWh a year, or about $81 a year.

At a lower rate or fewer loads, the savings fall in proportion. Per the ENERGY STAR page, savings are largest with high usage and high electricity rates, and heat pump dryers cost more to buy. The 2011 scoping report cited a 2010 paper that found a benefit only for households running more than 700 cycles a year, or more than 500 where rates are high. Prices have changed since then, so we do not give a payback figure.

For comparison, a 2011 DOE-based table in the scoping report listed about 684 kWh a year for a standard vented electric dryer at 283 cycles, which is close to our 680 kWh figure.

Where a laundry closet shares a wall with living space, the dryer's room heat and fan sound matter too. If you can hear a new rattle, hum or gurgle, matching sound to part is the same habit taught in our noise lesson. For how heat pump capacity changes with outdoor temperature, see cold-climate heat pumps; a dryer's 40 degree room limit is a separate, simpler rule.

Field card: Heat Pump Clothes Dryers

Closed loop: cold coil removes the water, hot coil puts the heat back.

  • No outdoor vent. The same air circulates through the drum, evaporator, condenser and fan.
  • Water ends up in a pan: drain hose or tank. Empty the tank after each load if there is no drain.
  • Clean the main lint filter every load, the secondary screen monthly, and the condenser every 30 to 40 loads (PNNL).
  • Room at 40 degrees F or warmer, airflow gap under the unit, not beside a refrigerator.
  • Check the nameplate for voltage. Some all-in-one units use 120 V; many dryers need 240 V.
  • Our example: about 680 kWh vs 204 kWh a year at 300 loads. About $81 saved at $0.17 per kWh.
