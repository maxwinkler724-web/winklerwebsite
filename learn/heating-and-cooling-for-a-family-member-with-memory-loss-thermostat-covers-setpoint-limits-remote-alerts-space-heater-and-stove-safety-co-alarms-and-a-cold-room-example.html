Home Heating and Cooling for a Family Member With Memory Loss

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A person living with memory loss may turn the heat way up, switch it off, or sit in a cold or overheated room without noticing or saying so. This lesson is about the house, not the person: thermostat settings, simple alerts, heaters and alarms that make the home more forgiving. It is home safety information, not medical advice. For any health question, follow the care team.

Why the house setup matters

A federal aging-research page on cold weather safety says memory problems can lead a person to forget the usual precautions, and that even mildly cool homes, 60 to 65 F, can lead to hypothermia in older adults. The same page advises setting the heat to at least 68 F. Heat works the other way: a room that is too warm is a risk too, and the person may not open a window or change clothes. Our own take: a person who cannot be relied on to adjust the system is better served by a system that stays in a safe range without being touched. For the broader weather picture, see the older adults heat wave and cold snap lesson.

Keep the thermostat in a safe range

  • Covers. A lockable clear cover over the thermostat stops the dial or buttons from being changed. It is cheap to try and easy to remove. Our own suggestion: make sure a caregiver or the person can still read the display through it.
  • Setpoint limits. Many programmable and smart thermostats let an installer or owner set a lowest and highest allowed setpoint, often in a hidden or installer settings menu. Check your model's instructions. Our own example limits: 68 F low and 76 F high.
  • Know the gaps. A limit does not help if the system is switched off at a breaker or switch, the batteries die, the furnace locks out, or the Wi-Fi drops. That is why limits and alerts work best together. See the thermostat batteries lesson for the battery side.

Remote temperature alerts

Many smart thermostats and stand-alone Wi-Fi sensors can send a phone alert when a room goes above or below a number you choose. Set the low alert a few degrees under the low limit, so you hear about real trouble and not every small dip. Our own suggestions: pick one or two people to receive alerts, test the alert once, and also set a high alert. Remember that a sensor only reports where it sits, so a bedroom far from the thermostat can be colder than the hallway reading. For how home and away modes can misfire with several people in the house, see the smart thermostat lesson. Our own note: a remote alert does not replace a person checking in.

Space heaters, stoves and gas fireplaces

The federal aging-research home safety page for Alzheimer's caregiving suggests removing portable space heaters, keeping controls for electric blankets and heating pads out of reach, and adding safety knobs and an automatic shut-off switch to the stove. It also says people with Alzheimer's may not be able to smell smoke or an unlit gas stove, and suggests smoke and natural gas detectors checked often. If a heater must stay, a federal consumer product safety release says to keep it at least three feet from drapes, furniture and bedding, plug it directly into a wall outlet and not an extension cord or power strip, and never leave it running while you sleep. A federal fire administration heating handout says the same three-foot distance and to switch portable heaters off when leaving the room or going to bed. For fireplaces, that handout recommends a glass or metal screen. Our own suggestion for a gas fireplace: have the wall switch or key valve controlled by the caregiver, and see the gas fireplace lesson and the space heater lesson.

Carbon monoxide and smoke alarms

The consumer product safety release says to put carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, and smoke alarms on every level and inside each bedroom, preferably interconnected. It says to test them monthly and replace batteries at least yearly, or use sealed ten-year batteries. A federal fire administration checklist for caregivers adds a question worth asking: can everyone hear the alarm from any room? Our own note: an alarm only helps if the person responds, so tell neighbors or family who can be reached when it sounds.

Cold or overheated rooms the person may not report

Our own checklist of places to look: rooms with the door shut, a bedroom over a garage, a sunroom, a room whose vent was closed, a window left open, a basement bathroom, and a room where a heater was moved. Look for a person wearing a coat indoors, no coat on a hot day, a window open in winter, or heavy blankets in summer. These are cues to check on them and call the care team, not a diagnosis.

Worked example (our own): the room that went cold overnight

Assumptions, all our own: the thermostat has a 68 F low limit and a 76 F high limit; someone turns the system fully off at the wall; the room holds 70 F for one hour, then falls about 1 F per hour; the low-temperature alert is set at 65 F; the next check is in the morning, nine hours after the evening check.

  1. Hour 0 to 1: the room stays at 70 F.
  2. After hour 1, the temperature is 70 minus (hours minus 1). At hour 9: 70 - (9 - 1) = 70 - 8 = 62 F. This matches the drift from 70 F to 62 F.
  3. The room reaches 65 F when 70 - (h - 1) = 65, so h - 1 = 5 and h = 6.
  4. Time below 65 F by hour 9: 9 - 6 = 3 hours.
  5. The 65 F alert crosses at hour 6, so the caregiver is told 3 hours before the 62 F morning reading, as long as the sensor, Wi-Fi and phone settings work.

The 68 F limit would have prevented a lower setting in the menu, but not a full off, which is why the 65 F alert is the second line of defense. A real room will cool faster in wind or slower in a mild house, so our own 1 F per hour rate is only a planning number. Setting the alert a bit higher, such as the 68 F the federal aging page advises as a minimum, gives more warning but more false alerts.

A simple daily room check

  1. Read the thermostat and the remote sensor. Both should be inside your chosen limits.
  2. Look at the cover, the display and the batteries.
  3. Check that any space heater is off, unplugged or removed, and the stove knobs and gas fireplace are off.
  4. Walk through the main rooms and bedroom, feeling for cold or hot spots and closed doors.
  5. Press the test button on one alarm, rotating through them so each is tested monthly.
  6. Note anything odd and tell the care team about any health concern.

Field card: Memory loss and home heating

Limits, alerts, no loose heaters, working alarms, one daily walk-through.

  • Heat to at least 68 F; 60 to 65 F homes can lead to hypothermia in older adults.
  • Cover and limit the thermostat (our own examples: 68 F low, 76 F high).
  • Set a remote low alert, such as 65 F, and a high alert; test it.
  • Remove portable heaters if you can; else 3 feet clear, wall outlet, off at night.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas; smoke alarms in each bedroom; test monthly.
  • Health questions go to the care team.
