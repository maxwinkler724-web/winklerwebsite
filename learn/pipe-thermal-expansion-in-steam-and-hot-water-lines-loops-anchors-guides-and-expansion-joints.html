Pipe Thermal Expansion in Steam and Hot Water Lines

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A steel steam main is installed cold and then asked to run at 250°F. It does not stay the length it was. This lesson shows how to estimate that growth with simple arithmetic and what the pipe fitter does with it: anchors, guides, loops, offsets and expansion joints.

Why pipe grows

Almost every solid gets a little longer when it warms up, because the atoms vibrate harder and take up more room. Metal pipe is no different. The change is small per foot, but a long run adds up. If the pipe is held at both ends and nothing is built in to take up the growth, the pipe has nowhere to go, so it pushes on whatever holds it: hangers, fittings, equipment connections and branch lines. DOE's steam sourcebook makes the same point in a sentence: distribution piping has to accommodate thermal reactions during start-ups and shutdowns.

The formula and the coefficients

Victaulic's thermal-movement bulletin writes the standard relationship as change in length = original length × coefficient × temperature change (ΔL = L × α × ΔT). In its Table 1 the coefficients are 8.0 × 10-5 inches per foot per °F for carbon steel and 11.2 × 10-5 inches per foot per °F for copper. Notice that copper grows about 40 percent more than steel for the same length and temperature change.

Temperature change means installed temperature to operating temperature. For a steam main, the operating temperature is the saturation temperature of the steam. TLV's steam table lists 250°F at 15.129 psig, and an engineering reference table lists 248°F at 14 psig and 252°F at 16 psig, so about 250°F is a fair number for 15 psig. Shutdown matters too: the pipe shrinks back, so the movement happens twice a day on a system that cycles.

Worked example: steel steam main versus copper hot water line

All arithmetic below is Our own, using the cited coefficients and the cited steam temperature.

A. 150 ft of carbon steel steam main, installed at 60°F, run at 15 psig (about 250°F).

  1. Temperature change: 250 − 60 = 190°F.
  2. Coefficient (Victaulic, carbon steel): 0.00008 in per ft per °F.
  3. Growth = 150 ft × 190°F × 0.00008 = 28,500 × 0.00008 = 2.28 in.
  4. Per 100 ft: 2.28 ÷ 1.5 = 1.52 in.

B. 80 ft of type L copper hot water line, from 60°F to 180°F.

  1. Temperature change: 180 − 60 = 120°F.
  2. Coefficient (Victaulic, copper): 0.000112 in per ft per °F.
  3. Growth = 80 ft × 120°F × 0.000112 = 9,600 × 0.000112 = 1.08 in.
  4. Per 100 ft: 1.08 ÷ 0.8 = 1.34 in.

Cross-check against a published table. Victaulic's copper data sheet lists growth per 100 ft of copper measured from 0°F: 0.684 in at 60°F and 2.051 in at 180°F. Our own subtraction: 2.051 − 0.684 = 1.367 in per 100 ft, and 1.367 × 0.8 = 1.09 in for 80 ft. That agrees with the coefficient result within about 0.01 in. For steel, Metraflex's guide uses 2.52 in per 100 ft for a 50°F to 360°F example (a 310°F rise); our own check with the Victaulic coefficient gives 100 × 310 × 0.00008 = 2.48 in, close enough to show the numbers agree in size. (The Metraflex page does not name the material for its figure.)

Compare. The steel main moves 2.28 in against 1.08 in for the copper line, about twice as much (2.28 ÷ 1.08 = 2.1). Copper has the larger coefficient, and per 100 ft it is nearly as lively (1.34 in versus 1.52 in), but the steel run is nearly twice as long and sees a larger temperature change. Length and temperature change often matter more than the metal. In both cases, more than an inch of movement cannot be ignored.

Anchors, guides and loops

Anchors fix the pipe at one point so growth has a known direction. Victaulic says main anchors go at terminal points, major branch connections, or changes of piping direction. Senior Flexonics adds that anchors must be designed for the test pressure thrust loads when expansion joints are used. Guides let the pipe slide along its length but stop it from bowing sideways. Senior Flexonics describes a support (hanger, U-bolt, roller) as only holding the pipe up, while a guide controls its line of motion. Metraflex puts the first guide within four pipe diameters of a bellows joint and the second within fourteen of the first. Deppmann, a distributor writing about those joints, calls guiding the real story: bowing happens when a pipe lacks proper guiding or anchors.

Loops and offsets. The cheapest expansion device is often just the piping itself. University of Colorado's steam piping standard prefers expansion loops over mechanical expansion devices, and the UNC steam design guideline calls loops, Z bends and L bends the required method for building flexibility into its steam piping. In all of these, the long legs of the pipe flex like a spring when the run grows. A U-loop sits between two anchors; a Z or L offset uses a change of direction that already exists. Victaulic gives loop and offset formulas, and its copper data sheet puts an expansion loop between two anchors and says to guide the pipe so movement is directed into the loop. Sizing a loop takes the pipe size and the growth; use the maker's charts or the engineer's drawings, not guesswork.

Expansion joints: slip and bellows

When there is no room for a loop, a mechanical joint can absorb the movement. A slip (packed) joint slides inside a sleeve sealed with packing. Deppmann describes packed joints as needing regular maintenance by a knowledgeable fitter, warns that packing can "set up" and raise anchor loads, and notes very large anchoring loads. A bellows joint flexes a corrugated metal element instead. Senior Flexonics lists its handling rules: do not lift by the bellows, keep shipping bars on until installation is done, do not force flanges to line up, and avoid wire brushes or abrasives on the bellows. Metraflex adds that shipping restraints come off before hydrotest and that unit lengths are not changed during installation, apart from cold pull. UNC's steam standard prohibits stretching an expansion joint to fix misalignment. Bellows have a limit on unrestrained length under pressure (called squirm), and Deppmann identifies squirm as a failure where the bellows moves sideways. Without a liner, high-velocity steam can erode bellows.

Maintenance in plain terms: the sources we read tie maintenance to the joint type. Packed joints need regular attention from a knowledgeable fitter because the packing can set up. Bellows joints need care to keep abrasives, weld spatter and wrong lifting loads off the bellows, and the maker's page asks for chloride-free water in hydrotest. Follow the maker's instructions and your site's lockout and steam procedures.

What poor expansion handling looks like

The sources we read are mostly design documents, not troubleshooting guides, so we state this part carefully. Heating Help describes a pipe rubbing on wood where it passes through a floor as a "clack-clack" or dull knocking sound, different from a metallic radiator ping. Deppmann says expansion in long runs can cause scraping noises "at best and damage at worst." University of Colorado says piping must expand and contract without stressing pipe, joints or connected equipment. So the family of symptoms is: scraping or knocking as the pipe moves, bowing where guiding is missing, and stress or damage at fittings and connections. We could not source a list of specific failures such as cracked fittings, so ask the engineer or maker if you see them. Do not confuse expansion noise with water hammer; see Water Hammer in Steam and Hot Water.

Branches off a riser

Victaulic says branch lines can be treated as a system offset or change of direction in the same formulas, and that major branch connections are common anchor locations. The practical lesson for a tech: a branch tied rigidly to a main or riser that is moving has to flex somewhere, and the weak point is the connection. A branch that includes an offset (a swing joint made with elbows and nipples, or a loop in the branch) lets the main move without dragging the takeoff along. Where the branch meets a pressure reducing station, University of Colorado requires a swing joint to allow components to be removed. Check the engineer's drawing for where anchors and swings are intended.

Where this fits in the system

Hot water systems also expand the water itself; that is a different problem handled by the tank, see Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure. For hangers and saddles on copper lines, see Supporting Refrigerant and Copper Piping. Equipment connections use flexible connectors for a different reason (vibration), covered in Vibration Isolators and Flexible Connectors. The pressure piping codes (ASME B31.1 power piping and ASME B31.9 building services piping) govern design stress; those are for the engineer, and we do not link copies.

Field card: Pipe Thermal Expansion

Length times coefficient times temperature change.

  • Growth (in) = length (ft) × coefficient × temperature change (°F).
  • Victaulic coefficients: carbon steel 0.00008, copper 0.000112 in per ft per °F.
  • Our own example: 150 ft steel, 60 to 250°F, grows 2.28 in; 80 ft copper, 60 to 180°F, grows 1.08 in.
  • Anchors set the direction, guides keep the line, loops and joints take the growth.
  • Do not stretch a joint to fix misalignment; keep shipping bars on until installed, remove before hydrotest.
  • Branches off moving mains need an offset or swing.
