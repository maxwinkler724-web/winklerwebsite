Candy and Chocolate Room HVAC: Sugar Bloom, Cooling Tunnels and Packing Rooms

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A candy shop or small confectionery plant is a room full of sugar, and sugar loves water. The HVAC job is to keep the air dry enough, cool enough and steady enough that chocolate stays glossy and hard candy stays dry. This lesson follows the product from tempering through the enrober, the cooling tunnel and the packing room.

Check the maker and the product spec

Check the maker and the product spec. Setpoints differ by recipe, wrapper and shelf life. The numbers below come from university and extension pages read in 2026 and from our own labeled assumptions. Use the confectionery plant's quality targets and the equipment makers' instructions for the real design.

Why humidity ruins sugar products

Iowa State University Extension describes sugar bloom as what happens when chocolate meets humidity or moisture: the sugar takes up water, and when the water leaves, sugar crystals stay behind as a blotchy, dusty, rough surface. Purdue Extension says the same thing from the plant side: sugar bloom shows up when water condenses on the surface, dissolves some sugar, and the sugar recrystallizes as the water evaporates. A thesis from the University of Illinois adds that sugar bloom feels dry and does not melt, unlike fat bloom, and lists high humidity without proper packaging and too little time for gradual cooling among the causes.

Hard candy and other sugar glasses have a related problem. A review of moisture in sugar confections (Ergun, Lietha and Hartel, Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2010) explains that a candy gains water when the room relative humidity is above the candy's own equilibrium humidity and loses water when the room is below it. Moisture uptake often makes a sticky surface layer first, and storing hard candy at 75 to 80 percent RH speeds moisture movement through the package. So the room that holds unwrapped product has a humidity ceiling, and the packing room is where the product is most exposed.

Cool, dry and steady: the targets

  • Purdue Extension's tempering fact sheet says to keep the tempering and storage area at 18 to 20 C (64 to 68 F) and 50 to 60 percent RH, typically with air conditioning and a dehumidifier.
  • Iowa State Extension and Clemson Cooperative Extension both give about 65 to 70 F and relative humidity below roughly 50 to 55 percent for storing chocolate.
  • The University of Illinois thesis cites work concluding that storage below 50 percent RH, without temperature swings, is best. The same thesis puts the stable cocoa butter form (form V) melting at 32 to 34 C, which is why warm, swinging rooms cause fat bloom instead.

We use 68 F and 45 percent RH in the worked example below as our own assumption, a little drier than the quoted ceilings to leave margin.

Enrober and cooling tunnel

An enrober coats centers with tempered chocolate, and the belt then runs through a cooling tunnel so the coating sets. Purdue's tempering steps show why the tunnel profile matters: the mass is cooled in stages around 28 to 32 C so the right crystals form, and the Illinois thesis lists too little time for gradual cooling as a cause of sugar bloom, so the tunnel profile and exit temperature matter as much as the room. The room's job is to supply the tunnel with dry, stable air and to take the heat the tunnel rejects.

The tunnel is a refrigerated box. Its coil removes heat from the product and the belt, and the refrigeration system rejects that heat plus the compressor work at its condenser. In our own reasoning, if that condenser is in or near the plant, it adds sensible heat to the space and can raise the room load, so locate it where the rejected heat does not recirculate into the packing or tempering areas. A tunnel that is poorly sealed leaks cold air out and humid room air in, and the humid air then condenses on the coil and the belt as frost or water. Oklahoma State University Extension makes the general point for food plants: warm, humid air leaking into cold spaces is a common source of condensation, and sealing openings and controlling air movement come first.

Cold product meets warm humid air

Oklahoma State Extension defines the condition plainly: water vapor condenses on any surface at or below the air's dew point, and it uses a cold container taken from a refrigerator on a humid day as the everyday example. In a plant, the cold surface is a tray or a bar of product just out of the tunnel, entering a packing room that is more humid than the tunnel exit.

The test is simple. Find the room air dew point from its temperature and RH (see Psychrometrics for Technicians), then compare it with the product surface temperature. If the product is warmer than the dew point, nothing condenses. If it is at or below, it sweats.

Packing rooms: keep the dew point low

The packing room is where unwrapped product waits for the wrapper, so it needs the lowest dew point in the building and the least air exchange with outside. Pressurize it slightly with dry conditioned air, keep outside doors closed, and give workers a vestibule or a short door-open time.

Desiccant or DX dehumidification

Oklahoma State describes the two families this way. Chiller-type (cooling coil) dehumidifiers cool air below its dew point to condense water out, and the air leaving the coil can be above 90 percent RH, so it may condense on slightly cooler surfaces. Desiccant systems remove water directly, can save money, and are often more complex to operate and maintain. For a deeper look at wheels and reactivation air, see Desiccant Dehumidifiers. For the way airflow changes how much water a coil removes, see Sensible Heat Ratio and Latent Load.

Worked example: dew point and moisture removal

Assumptions (ours, not sourced): packing room 68 F (20 C) and 45 percent RH target; product leaves the tunnel at 54 F (12 C); outdoor air 86 F (30 C) and 60 percent RH; 200 cfm of outdoor air gets in by leakage and door openings; six people work in the room, each adding 0.2 lb/h of water vapor; sea level pressure, 101.325 kPa. Saturation pressures come from the NIST water saturation table: 2.3393 kPa at 20 C and 4.2470 kPa at 30 C, and the table rows give 1.0730 kPa at 8 C and 0.8726 kPa at 5 C.

Step 1: room vapor pressure. pw = 0.45 × 2.3393 = 1.0527 kPa.

Step 2: room dew point. 1.0527 kPa falls between the 5 C and 8 C rows, close to 8 C, so the dew point is about 7.8 C (46 F). Product at 12 C is 4 C (about 8 F) above it. No condensation. If the room drifts to 60 percent RH, pw = 1.4036 kPa, and the 12 C table row is 1.4028 kPa, so the dew point is about 12 C and the product sits right at it: expect sweating.

Step 3: humidity ratios. W = 0.622 pw / (p − pw).
Room: W = 0.622 × 1.0527 / (101.325 − 1.0527) = 0.00653 lb/lb.
Outdoor: pw = 0.60 × 4.2470 = 2.5482 kPa; W = 0.622 × 2.5482 / (101.325 − 2.5482) = 0.01605 lb/lb. Delta W = 0.01605 − 0.00653 = 0.00952.

Step 4: moisture sources. The rule lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × delta W follows from 60 minutes times 0.075 lb/ft³ air density.
Outdoor air: 4.5 × 200 × 0.00952 = 8.56 lb/h.
People: 6 × 0.2 = 1.20 lb/h.
Total to remove: 9.76 lb/h, about 234 lb (28 gallons) per day.

Not counted: open product, wet floors, wash-down, steam from the kitchen and the cooling tunnel's own leakage. The outdoor air alone is 88 percent of the load, which is why sealing the door matters more than adding capacity. The same 200 cfm has a latent load of about 4,840 × 200 × 0.00952 = 9,200 Btu/h, which shows what a coil must also handle as cooling.

Field card checks

  • Log room temperature and RH where product is exposed, and compute the dew point. Compare it with the coldest product surface.
  • Look for sweating on tunnel exit belts, trays and cold metal near the packing tables.
  • Check tunnel door seals and the packing room door, and keep the room slightly positive.
  • For a desiccant unit, check filters, reactivation exhaust and the dry-air discharge per the maker.
  • Keep the condenser heat out of the packing room.

Field card: Candy and Chocolate Room HVAC

Keep the product warmer than the room dew point, and the room cool, dry and steady.

  • Sugar bloom is moisture dissolving surface sugar; fat bloom is warmth and temperature swings (Iowa State, Purdue).
  • Extension targets: about 65 to 70 F, RH below roughly 50 to 55 percent.
  • Condensation happens when a surface is at or below the air dew point.
  • Cooling coils leave air near saturation; desiccant units dry directly but need more upkeep (Oklahoma State).
  • Our example: 68 F, 45 percent RH gives a dew point near 46 F; 200 cfm of 86 F, 60 percent air adds 8.6 lb/h.
