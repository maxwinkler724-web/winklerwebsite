Absorption Chillers: Lithium Bromide Cycle, Heat Sources, Crystallization and Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An absorption chiller makes chilled water with heat instead of a big compressor motor. Most commercial units use water as the refrigerant and lithium bromide salt as the absorbent. This lesson explains the cycle, the heat sources, why the salt solution can crystallize, and what a tech looks at. It does not recommend any brand.

The cycle in plain words

The U.S. Department of Energy's CHP fact sheet describes the absorption chiller as a "thermal compressor." The mechanical compressor is replaced by an absorbent that chemically bonds with refrigerant vapor, and heat does the work. For water and lithium bromide, the fact sheet says the machine serves chilled fluid at 40°F or higher, which fits comfort cooling but not freezing.

  1. Generator. Heat boils water out of the weak lithium bromide solution. The solution left behind is stronger.
  2. Condenser. The water vapor condenses against tower water.
  3. Evaporator. The liquid water is sprayed over the chilled-water tubes. Carrier's 16DF manual notes that at 0.25 in. Hg absolute, water boils at only about 40°F, which is why the shell is held in deep vacuum. The boiling water pulls heat out of the building loop.
  4. Absorber. Strong solution soaks up that vapor, and the absorbing is exothermic. DOE says this heat goes to the condenser water and tower loop.
  5. Solution heat exchanger. Hot strong solution going to the absorber warms the cooler weak solution going to the generator, so less heat is wasted. Carrier's manual lists it among the solution circuit parts.

Carrier's manual says non-condensable gases collect in the absorber, the lowest-pressure part of the machine. That is why vacuum is central to these machines.

Single-effect and double-effect

A double-effect (two-stage) unit adds a second, hotter generator that reuses the first generator's vapor heat. DOE's fact sheet gives single-stage COP at 0.70 to 0.79 in its examples and two-stage at 1.35 to 1.42. A York article lists 0.77 and 1.37 for steam units. Both note COP leaves out pump and fan energy. DOE says a two-stage unit needs a generator temperature about 150°F higher than a single-stage unit. Compare that with the electric centrifugal COP of 6.5 the York article uses for reference; absorption trades electricity for heat.

Heat sources

  • Steam. The York article gives 4.5 to 45 psig for single-stage and 30 to 145 psig for two-stage.
  • Hot water. DOE shows 190 to 240°F for single-stage and about 350°F for two-stage. York says single-stage hot water can be as low as 158°F.
  • Direct-fired. A burner is built into the chiller, usually on natural gas, per DOE.
  • Waste heat and CHP. DOE says absorption chillers are often exhaust-fired with heat recovered from combined heat and power, so no extra fuel is burned for the cooling.

Why solution crystallizes

Lithium bromide dissolves only so far, and less so as it cools. Carrier's manual says the solution "will begin to crystallize" and restrict flow when temperature and concentration reach the crystallization line on the equilibrium diagram. Strong, cool solution is the danger, for example when:

  • cooling water is very cold (the Thermax brochure ties a low-temperature safety to a dilution cycle) or the load is high (Carrier's overflow pipe limits concentration then);
  • power is lost with no safe shutdown. Carrier warns that no emergency power source could result in crystallization.

Makers build in defenses. Carrier uses an evaporator overflow pipe, a strong-solution overflow pipe, and a dilution cycle at stop that drains refrigerant into the solution; its solution pump stops 15 minutes after the stop sequence starts. A direct-fired brochure on trane.com for a Thermax-built machine says its controller keeps a margin of 18°F from the crystallization line. Do not try to clear a crystallized machine without the maker's procedure.

Purge, vacuum and inhibitor

Carrier's manual defines non-condensables as gases that will not condense at normal operating conditions; they cut vacuum and capacity. Hydrogen from corrosion is vented through a heated palladium cell, and other gases go to a purge tank that is normally emptied once or twice a year on a tight machine. The York article says modern units use an automatic purge. Corrosion inhibitor controls hydrogen; York says to analyze a solution sample once or twice a year to set the inhibitor top-up, and the Thermax brochure names lithium molybdate. Carrier says to wear goggles and protective clothing around inhibitor and lithium bromide, and never to use oxygen or air to pressure test, only nitrogen. Compare this with the electric low-pressure machine in Low-Pressure Chiller Purge Units and Non-Condensables.

Worked example: a 500-ton single-effect steam machine

Our own arithmetic, using a COP of 0.7 (DOE gives 0.70 to 0.79 for single-stage) and low-pressure steam at 15 psig. The steam table we used gives 947 Btu/lb at 14 psig and 944 at 16 psig, so we take about 945 Btu/lb at 15 psig.

  1. Cooling. Our own: 500 tons × 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 6,000,000 Btu/h.
  2. Heat input. Our own: COP = cooling ÷ heat input, so 6,000,000 ÷ 0.7 = 8,571,429 Btu/h.
  3. Steam. Our own: 8,571,429 ÷ 945 = about 9,070 lb/h, assuming all the latent heat is used and ignoring condensate sensible heat.
  4. Tower heat. Our own: cooling + heat input = 6,000,000 + 8,571,429 = 14,571,429 Btu/h, or 29,143 Btu/h per ton.
  5. Electric comparison. Our own, with a teaching value: FEMP lists 0.541 kW/ton full load for a water-cooled centrifugal of 400 to 599 tons. Compressor heat: 500 × 0.541 = 270.5 kW × 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 922,946 Btu/h. Tower load = 6,000,000 + 922,946 = 6,922,946 Btu/h, or 13,846 Btu/h per ton.
  6. Ratio. Our own: 29,143 ÷ 13,846 = about 2.1 times the tower load per ton.

The 29,143 figure agrees with the 29 MBh per ton York gives for single-stage. Same arithmetic at COP 1.37 gives about 20,800 Btu/h per ton, close to York's 21. That is why DOE says an absorption chiller needs a larger cooling tower. See Cooling Tower Basics.

What a tech checks

  • Solution concentration. Carrier says measure specific gravity with a hydrometer and read solution temperature, then plot on the equilibrium diagram. Its full-load cooling example shows 59.0 percent weak and 64.5 percent strong.
  • Vacuum and non-condensables. Use an absolute gauge or wet-bulb vacuum indicator, never mercury.
  • Cooling water temperature and flow. York gives 68 to 99°F inlet and 3 to 8 gpm/ton single-stage; hot inlet water derates capacity. Carrier's controls turn the tower fan off below 77°F absorber solution and trip on high solution at 113°F, or on flow under 50 percent of design.
  • Chilled water. Carrier says never let leaving water fall below 41°F.
  • Log sheets. Carrier lists daily operator duties and log sheets in its manual; compare each day's readings with the maker's design values.

Field card: Absorption Chillers

Heat drives it, vacuum protects it, tower carries about twice the heat.

  • Water is the refrigerant, lithium bromide is the absorbent, and the shells run in deep vacuum.
  • Single-effect COP is about 0.7 to 0.8, double-effect about 1.35 to 1.4 (DOE, York).
  • Heat sources: steam, hot water, direct fire, CHP or waste heat.
  • Strong, cool solution crystallizes; let the dilution cycle finish and never cut power mid-stop.
  • Non-condensables collect in the absorber; check purge, vacuum, inhibitor.
  • 500 tons, COP 0.7: 6.0 MMBtu/h cooling, 8.57 MMBtu/h heat, 14.57 MMBtu/h to tower.
