Water Heater Temperature: Scald Risk, Legionella and Mixing Valves

Last updated: 2026-10-16

What temperature should a water heater be set at? The two federal answers most homeowners find, from the Department of Energy and the Consumer Product Safety Commission, are both 120 F. Building-water guidance for Legionella points higher. This lesson lays out what each source says, where it applies, and how a mixing valve lets a tank run hot while the tap stays safe.

What DOE and CPSC say

A U.S. Department of Energy Energy Saver page on adjusting the water heater says most households only need the tank set at 120 F, and that a lower setting also slows mineral buildup and corrosion in the tank and pipes. It adds that turning the temperature down saves energy by cutting standby losses, which is heat leaking from the tank into the surrounding space. DOE puts a number on the cost of running hot: a heater set at 140 F can waste roughly $36 to $61 a year in standby heat loss. That page did not give a per-10-degree figure, so this lesson does not either.

The CPSC safety alert on tap water scalds urges people to lower their water heaters to 120 F, and says lower settings are fine if they still cover laundry and dishwashing. CPSC also states plainly who gets hurt: the majority of tap water scald injuries and deaths are to the elderly and to children under five.

How fast hot water burns

CPSC lists approximate times for an adult to suffer a third-degree burn. Caleffi, a valve maker, publishes its own wording in a MixCal 521 data sheet, describing partial burning, and adds that children and elderly people may be affected in half that time or less. The two lists use different burn definitions, so compare them as rough guides, not as one measurement.

Water temperatureCPSC: adult, third-degree burnCaleffi: partial burning
120 Fabout 5 minutesnot given as a time
130 Fabout 30 secondsabout 30 seconds
140 Fabout 6 secondsabout 5 seconds
150 Fabout 2 secondsnot given

The pattern is steep. Between 120 F and 140 F the time drops from minutes to seconds, which is why DOE also calls water heated to 140 F a scalding hazard.

What Legionella guidance says, and where it applies

Legionella is a bacterium that can grow in building water systems. The sources below are mostly about building water management, so read each for its scope.

  • CDC. On its potable water systems page, CDC says to store hot water above 140 F (60 C) and to make sure circulating hot water does not fall below 120 F (49 C). It says cold water should be kept below the range favorable to Legionella, given as 77 to 113 F (25 to 45 C), and notes growth can occur as low as 68 F (20 C). The page covers hot and cold potable water in a facility from where water enters to where it leaves at a fixture, and says it supports a water management program that follows ASHRAE Guideline 12. It also recommends thermostatic mixing valves near fixtures so circulating water can stay above 120 F while scalding is prevented. The page is written for facility water systems, not as a rule for one house.
  • ASHRAE. Guideline 12 is titled "Managing the Risk of Legionellosis Associated with Building Water Systems." A published addendum to it says alternate heat sources can help maintain hot water consistently at or above 120 F (49 C) throughout a building hot-water system. The excerpt I read does not say whether the guideline covers single-family homes, so this lesson makes no claim either way.
  • WHO. Its legionellosis fact sheet suggests keeping hot water above 50 C (122 F) and water leaving the heating unit at or above 60 C (140 F), and says these controls matter particularly in hospitals, other health care settings and aged-care facilities.

Back to the house: DOE says there is a very slight risk of promoting Legionella when tanks are held at 120 F, still considers 120 F safe for most people, and says people with a suppressed immune system or chronic respiratory disease may consider 140 F, which significantly raises scald risk. DOE's suggested answer for taps used for washing or bathing is a mixing valve. If someone in your home is in that higher-risk group, that is a question for their doctor and a licensed plumber, not a guess.

The compromise: store hot, deliver warm

Some makers describe a middle path. Caleffi's data sheet says that in systems with hot water storage, water must be stored at 140 F or higher to prevent Legionella, and that at that temperature a thermostatic mixing valve is needed to lower the temperature at the point of use. Caleffi's Kevin Freidt wrote in Plumbing & Mechanical that commercial designers commonly suggest producing or storing around 150 to 160 F and distributing at 130 to 140 F, which requires scald protection at every point of use. That is commercial practice, not a residential code citation.

The same article separates two valve types, using ASSE standard numbers. An ASSE 1017 valve at the heater controls the temperature sent into the whole piping system and can be set at 120 F, but he says these valves should not be considered scald-protection valves because they can be set above 120. An ASSE 1070 valve is a point-of-use valve with a 120 F maximum setpoint, check valves on both inlets and quick shutdown of hot water if the cold supply fails; it goes in the supply pipes of a fixture. Honeywell's AM-1 installation sheet lists ASSE 1017 for its 70 to 120 F ("C") and 70 to 145 F models and warns that water above 120 F can cause serious injury. Caleffi says its 521 series is not designed for scald or chill protection service and should not be used where ASSE 1070 devices are required. Follow the data sheet for the exact valve, and have a licensed plumber set it under local code.

Dial markings and the tap thermometer

CPSC's advice is to check the water with a candy or meat thermometer held under the faucet, to test by hand before using it for infants and young children, and, for a gas heater, to take the reading first thing in the morning or at least two hours after water use. Honeywell's valve sheet likewise says to use a water thermometer at the faucet outlet when setting a mixing valve. A reading at the tap tells you what actually reaches you; the dial only tells you where the control is set.

Worked example: tap check and mixing ratio

Part 1: tap check. Suppose the dial is at a middle mark and, following CPSC's method, a thermometer under the bathroom faucet reads 134 F after the water runs hot. (The 134 F is a made-up reading for practice.) In the table, 134 F falls between 130 F (about 30 seconds) and 140 F (about 6 seconds) for an adult. That is well short of the 120 F (about 5 minutes) that CPSC and DOE point to. Turn the setting down, wait for the tank to reheat, and measure again.

Part 2: blending 140 F storage to 120 F. Let x be the fraction of the delivered water that comes from the tank. The temperature of a blend is the weighted average of the two streams (my arithmetic, assuming no heat loss in the pipes):

140x + 50(1 − x) = 120
140x + 50 − 50x = 120
90x = 70
x = 70 ÷ 90 = 0.78

So about 78 percent hot and 22 percent cold: 7.8 gallons from the tank and 2.2 from the cold line for every 10 gallons at the tap. Each gallon of 140 F water also stretches to 1 ÷ 0.78 = about 1.29 gallons at 120 F. Check: 0.78 × 140 = 109.2, plus 0.22 × 50 = 11.0, total 120.2 F, close enough given the rounding.

Which setting is right for your house?

The federal home advice is 120 F. Storing at 140 F with a mixing valve is a design some makers describe for buildings and for households with specific health concerns, and it adds a part that has to be installed and set correctly. If hot water is weak or missing instead, start with the tank checks lesson linked below.

Field card: Water Heater Temperature

Measure at the tap, not the dial.

  • DOE and CPSC both point to 120 F for most homes.
  • CPSC adult burn times: about 5 min at 120 F, 30 s at 130 F, 6 s at 140 F, 2 s at 150 F.
  • Children under five and older adults are hurt most often, says CPSC.
  • CDC building guidance: store hot water above 140 F, keep circulating water at or above 120 F, with mixing valves near fixtures.
  • Hot storage needs a mixing valve; read the valve's own ASSE listing.
  • Blend 140 F and 50 F to 120 F: about 78 percent hot, 22 percent cold.
