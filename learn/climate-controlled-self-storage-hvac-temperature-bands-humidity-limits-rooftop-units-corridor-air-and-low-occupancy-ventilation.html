Climate-Controlled Self-Storage HVAC: Temperature Bands, Humidity Limits, Rooftop Units, Corridor Air and Low-Occupancy Ventilation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A tenant who searches "climate controlled storage humidity" wants to know if the stuff will mold. A tech who gets the service call wants to know why a rooftop unit that runs all day still leaves the hallway sticky. This lesson ties the two together for an interior-corridor storage building.

What "climate controlled" means

It is not a standardized term. A trade magazine article on the topic quotes an industry veteran saying standardization of the terms "hasn't really been established," that the meaning shifts by region, and that "temperature controlled" once meant only that a building had heat. The same article says operators hold a range, "typically between 50 and 80 degrees or 54 to 78 degrees," and it quotes the Self Storage Association saying that only an HVAC system with a dehumidifier gives true climate control. A consumer guide lists company ranges from 50 to 85 degrees. Neither gave a humidity number; another trade article suggests 45 to 50 percent for most belongings. Treat the label as marketing. Ask the owner for the temperature band and humidity ceiling, and write them down before touching the controls.

Humidity limits that matter

  • Mold. The EPA guide says to keep indoor humidity below 60 percent relative humidity, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. The Canadian Conservation Institute is more cautious, noting that mold can grow at an RH as low as 65 percent.
  • Paper and books. The Library of Congress recommends 30 to 40 percent RH for storing paper.
  • Wood, leather, textiles. A National Park Service conservation note gives 40 to 55 percent RH for organic objects, with less than 5 percent swing in a day and less than 15 percent over the seasons.

Notice the gap. A target that keeps mold away (under 60) is not the same as a target that is gentle on a family archive (30 to 40) or on wood furniture. Conservation guidance also says no single ideal fits everything. For the full museum and archive picture, see Museum, Library and Archive Climate Control. Here we only need a ceiling the owner can hold in July.

Conditioning a corridor that feeds sealed units

In many interior-corridor buildings the supply air goes into the hallway, not into each unit. Unit walls are partitions and the doors are metal roll-up doors. A trade article on self-storage HVAC notes that these walls "are not airtight." In that design the units get their air by leaking from the corridor, through door edges and gaps (our reading of the article). Another trade article describes limiting air loss by conditioning only the inner units that tenants reach through a corridor.

What that means for the tech:

  • Corridor supply and return grilles control what the units see. A blocked return or a closed damper can leave the far end of the hall warm and damp while the sensor near the unit sits happy.
  • Put the humidity sensor where the problem is, away from a supply register and out of the sun, and compare it to a second meter before trusting it. Our lesson on sensible heat ratio and latent load explains why more airflow can make the air feel cool while the humidity climbs.
  • Exterior doors and the office door are the big moisture entry points.

Low occupancy and minimal outdoor air

Few people are inside, and they stay a short time. ASHRAE Standard 62.1 sets ventilation by adding a per-person rate and a per-area rate for each space type, so a lightly occupied storage corridor needs far less outdoor air than an office. We could not open the standard itself. A public summary of the 2024 International Mechanical Code table, which carries the same kind of rates, lists warehouses at 10 cfm per person plus 0.06 cfm per square foot. A separate summary of the 62.1 table lists storage rooms at 0.12 cfm per square foot. A California code summary lists warehouses at 0.15 cfm per square foot, so the number varies; confirm which rate and edition apply with the local code official.

Why it matters for moisture: every cfm of outdoor air in a humid summer is a latent load. Keep the outdoor air damper at its minimum, and confirm it is closed when the unit is off. If the unit has an economizer, a humid day is not a free-cooling day; see our rooftop unit basics for how the damper section fits in.

Dehumidifying in mild weather

This is the classic trap. On a 65 to 72 degree rainy day there is almost no cooling demand, so the compressor runs briefly, satisfies the thermostat, and shuts off. Humidity is removed only while the coil is cold and the air passes over it. A big, oversized unit makes it worse, because it reaches setpoint fast; compare AC short cycling and oversizing. Options to raise with the owner: a hot gas reheat style dehumidification mode, a separate dehumidifier, or a humidity control that holds the compressor on. The tech's job is to report what the humidity does through the day.

Worked example (our own, assumptions stated)

All inputs below are assumptions we picked for the arithmetic; none comes from a real building.

  • Indoor air: 72°F and 50 percent RH, which reads near 0.0083 lb of water per lb of dry air on a psychrometric chart (our own calculation).
  • Humid outdoor day: 70°F dew point, about 0.0158 lb/lb (our own calculation).
  • Assumed infiltration from door openings plus leakage through the envelope: 200 cfm.

Step 1, humidity ratio difference: 0.0158 − 0.0083 = 0.0075 lb/lb.

Step 2, moisture entering: 4.5 × 200 cfm × 0.0075 = 6.8 lb/h. (The 4.5 is our own conversion: 60 min per hour times about 0.075 lb of air per cubic foot.)

Step 3, latent load: 4,840 × 200 × 0.0075 = 7,260 Btu/h, about 0.6 ton, just from the leaks.

Step 4, per day: 6.8 lb/h × 24 h = 163.2 lb/day. A pint of water weighs about 1.04 lb (8.34 lb per gallon divided by 8), so 163.2 ÷ 1.04 = about 157 pints per day. That is the load the equipment must remove, not a rating to buy. A dehumidifier rating in pints per day depends on its test conditions; ENERGY STAR notes that its portable-unit test uses 65°F air, so a cool corridor will not match a rating taken at a warmer temperature.

Double the assumed leakage to 400 cfm and the moisture doubles to 13.6 lb/h, so sealing doors often beats adding equipment.

Rooftop unit service for a storage building

The checklist in our rooftop unit maintenance lesson still applies. Storage buildings add a few habits: check the condensate drain and trap every visit, because a plugged drain pans water into a building nobody walks through daily; look at filters, since a loaded filter cuts airflow and moves the coil toward freezing; verify the minimum outdoor air damper position; and test the humidity sensor against a second meter. Follow roof-access rules and lockout procedures.

Field card: Climate-controlled storage humidity

The label is not a standard. Ask for the temperature band and the humidity ceiling.

  • EPA: keep indoor RH below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent. CCI: mold possible at 65 percent.
  • Paper: 30 to 40 percent RH (Library of Congress). Organic objects: 40 to 55 percent (NPS).
  • Corridor supply reaches sealed units by leakage.
  • Outdoor air: keep at minimum and closed when off. Confirm the local rate.
  • Moisture lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × delta W. Latent Btu/h = 4,840 × cfm × delta W.
  • Our example: 200 cfm and 0.0075 delta W gives 6.8 lb/h, about 157 pints/day.
