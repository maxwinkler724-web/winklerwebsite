DIY Box Fan Air Cleaners: One-Filter and Four-Filter Cube Designs

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A square furnace filter taped to a box fan can clean a room's air for a few dollars, and EPA and university labs have measured how well. It is a stopgap with real limits, not a permanent appliance. This lesson covers the two common builds, what the numbers say, the safety rules that matter most, and when to buy a commercial unit or upgrade the furnace filter instead.

What it is and when people use it

EPA describes a do-it-yourself air cleaner as one or more standard 20 by 20 inch HVAC filters attached to a box fan with tape, brackets or bungee cords. People build them for wildfire smoke days, for crowded rooms during cold and flu season, and for dusty or allergy-heavy rooms. EPA's position is that these are for times when products of known performance are not available or affordable, and that they are not a permanent alternative. For the commercial side, see Air Purifiers and Whole-House Air Cleaners: What Actually Works; for the full smoke-day routine, see Wildfire Smoke Days: How to Set Your HVAC and Filters. We do not repeat those here.

The two designs

One filter. One filter is held flat against the fan with the filter's airflow arrows pointing the way the air travels, so the air passes through the filter first. EPA's instructions add a cardboard shroud: a circle cut to the blade area and taped over the front so air can only move through the blades, not around them. This is the cheapest and easiest build, and the easiest to re-filter.

Four-filter cube. Four filters stand upright as the sides of a cube, arrows pointing inward, edges taped. The fan sits on top with its blowing side up and is taped in place. The bottom is closed with cardboard. A five-filter version swaps the cardboard for a fifth filter, but then the whole unit must stand on sturdy supports so air can enter from below. The cube is bulkier, but it has far more filter area.

What the testing found

EPA ran a chamber test with wildfire smoke and reported clean air delivery rate (CADR), the volume of smoke-free air the device produces per minute. The results, in cubic feet per minute:

  • One 1 inch MERV 13 filter on a fan: 111.
  • The same with a cardboard shroud: 156, about 40 percent higher at no cost.
  • One 4 inch MERV 13 filter with shroud: 248.
  • Two 1 inch filters with shroud: 263.
  • Four-filter cube with shroud: 401.

A University of California, Davis group measured a five-filter version in a small home office at about 600 to 850 cubic feet per minute depending on fan speed, against about 285 and 129 for two commercial HEPA units, and reported the box costs roughly a tenth as much per unit of CADR. Their measured power was 67 to 98 watts and their measured noise was 58 to 67 dB, a bit above the commercial units. A separate UC Davis energy-center case study of a four-filter build estimated CADR of 165 to 239 across three fan speeds (an estimate, not a certified rating), at 70 to 102 watts and 53 to 61 dB. EPA says well-built units can perform like commercial ones, but results vary with design, build quality and maintenance, and it notes that DIY units are typically noisier.

Why MERV 13

CDC describes a MERV 13 filter as at least 50 percent efficient on 0.3 to 1.0 micron particles and at least 85 percent efficient on 1 to 3 micron particles, which covers much smoke and many virus-carrying droplets. EPA recommends MERV 13 or higher for DIY units, and says thicker filters (2 to 4 inches) beat 1 inch. The UC Davis paper found the real single-pass efficiency of its filters, at 41 to 58 percent, was lower than the rating suggests, possibly because of low airflow speed through the media. Treat the rating as a floor to shop for, not a guarantee.

Direction, gaps and speed

  • Arrows. Align the filter's airflow arrows with the direction the air travels. In the cube, they point inward.
  • Fan orientation. Keep the cube's fan blowing up. In UC Davis testing, turning the unit over so the fan blew toward the floor cut CADR at low speed from about 600 to about 481, which the authors tied to air short-circuiting and dust being stirred back up.
  • Gaps. CDC says to seal all gaps with tape because leaks reduce effectiveness, and EPA says to seal the filter-to-fan joint. Cover the fan's outer corners with the shroud. Keep the unit clear of walls so air can reach it.
  • Speed and noise. EPA advises the highest speed whenever the room is occupied. CDC points out that noise tempts people to turn the fan down, which lowers the benefit. Measured CADR rose with speed in both UC Davis tests.

Fan and fire safety

This is the part not to skip. A box fan was built to move air through open space, not through a stack of filters, and the filters cut airflow.

  • EPA says to use a fan built in 2012 or later with a safety listing mark from a recognized testing lab. Newer fans have fused plugs and thermal cutoffs. Older fans were not tested and EPA says they pose known fire risks. If you must use one, EPA says never leave it unattended and never run it while you sleep.
  • A testing lab tested five newer box fan models with clean, smoke-loaded and dust-loaded filters, and with both fan sides blocked for seven hours. None caught fire and temperatures stayed under the limits of the fan standard, though internal parts ran about 60 degrees C warmer in the worst case. EPA cautions that five models do not represent every fan.
  • Keep the motor from overheating: do not block the motor or cover its housing vents with tape or cardboard, and keep dust off the fan. The safety parts are a backup, and we would not rely on them.
  • No extension cords, no damaged or malfunctioning fan, and sturdy supports so it cannot tip. Keep working smoke detectors in the home and keep children away from a running fan.
  • Used filters may hold dust, smoke particles or microbes. EPA says to remove them gently without shaking, bag them and wash your hands.

EPA gives no separate rule for overnight use of a newer fan. If that matters to you, we suggest a commercial unit with a listed safety mark designed for continuous use.

When to change filters

EPA says a dirty filter makes a DIY cleaner almost completely ineffective. In smoke, check the filter daily and replace it when it looks dark brown or gray or smells of smoke, which can mean every few days or weeks. Replace them again when the event ends and keep spares on hand. In ordinary use, replace them when they look dirty. See How Often to Change Your Air Filter and MERV for the furnace side.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

These inputs are assumptions chosen for the arithmetic, not measurements. The formula is the volume form given by CDC: ACH = CADR x 60 / room volume.

  • Assumed room: 20 ft by 25 ft with an 8 ft ceiling, so volume = 20 x 25 x 8 = 4,000 cubic feet.
  • Assumed one-filter fan with shroud: CADR 156 (the EPA figure above).
  • Assumed target: 5 ACH, the figure CDC uses for clean air in occupied spaces.
  • Assumed fan power 90 W (inside the 67 to 102 W measured above), 12 hours a day, 30 days, at $0.18 per kWh.

Step 1, one fan: ACH = 156 x 60 / 4,000 = 9,360 / 4,000 = 2.34 air changes per hour.

Step 2, CADR needed for 5 ACH: 5 x 4,000 / 60 = 333 CADR.

Step 3, how many fans: 333 / 156 = 2.14, so round up to 3 fans (3 x 156 = 468 CADR, or 7.0 ACH). CDC says to compute each device's ACH separately and add them, which gives the same answer here: 3 x 2.34 = 7.0.

Step 4, electricity for three fans: 3 x 90 W = 270 W = 0.27 kW. 0.27 x 12 h x 30 days = 97.2 kWh. 97.2 x $0.18 = about $17.50 a month.

Compare one four-filter cube at EPA's 401 CADR: 401 x 60 / 4,000 = 6.0 ACH, already above the target. At the same 90 W: 0.09 x 12 x 30 = 32.4 kWh, about $5.83 a month. In this example the cube meets the target on a third of the power, which fits EPA's finding that multi-filter designs cost less to run per CADR. The catch is the bulk and the cost of four filters per change. Real results will be lower than these round numbers if the filters are loaded or seams leak.

Versus a commercial air cleaner or a better furnace filter

  • Commercial portable unit. EPA says units with a measured CADR are generally more reliable, because the label tells you the room size they serve. They are usually quieter. Choose smoke CADR and avoid ozone-generating units.
  • Central filter upgrade. Both CDC and EPA recommend MERV 13 or the highest efficiency the system can take, with the fan set to On. Check first that the system can handle the higher pressure drop, which can cut airflow. It treats the whole house but not one room's peak load. See Air Handler Filter Racks and Filter Bypass Gaps.
  • Any of these. Allergy and cold-and-flu help is a supplement; see Home HVAC for Allergies and Asthma. None replaces source control or ventilation.

Field card: DIY Box Fan Air Cleaners

Newer fan, MERV 13, sealed seams, fan blowing up, clean filters.

  • Fan built 2012 or later with a testing-lab listing mark; no extension cord; never unattended or asleep with an old fan.
  • Filter arrows follow the airflow; the cube's arrows point inward.
  • Shroud and tape: the shroud took one-filter CADR from 111 to 156 in EPA tests.
  • ACH = CADR x 60 / room volume (cubic feet).
  • Change dirty filters; in smoke, check daily.
  • Stopgap use only; a commercial unit or a MERV 13 furnace filter is the longer-term path.
