Zoo Animal House HVAC: Reptile, Tropical and Primate Buildings and Aviaries

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A zoo animal building has two sets of people to protect: the animals, who live in the air all day, and the visitors and keepers who pass through it. The hard parts are heat and humidity that can be extreme, a heating failure that can kill animals, and keeping animal air away from the public. This lesson covers the air side only.

Check the rules that apply

Check the rules that apply. Exhibit species, permits and veterinary direction decide the real targets. This page is general education built from federal rules and public guidance read in 2026. Confirm every setpoint with the facility veterinarian and the design engineer.

What the federal rules actually say

The USDA Animal Welfare Act regulations are the only hard numbers we found, and they are short. For nonhuman primates, 9 CFR 3.76 says indoor facilities must be heated and cooled so temperatures do not fall below 45 °F or rise above 85 °F for more than 4 consecutive hours while primates are present. It also says the building must be ventilated at all times to control odors, drafts, ammonia and moisture condensation, and that auxiliary ventilation is required at 85 °F or higher. Humidity has no number: it is set as directed by the attending veterinarian. Other warmblooded animals fall under a different subpart, 9 CFR 3.126, which asks only that indoor temperatures stay compatible with the species and that ventilation be adequate. Those are floors, not design targets. A tropical house sits far above 45 °F, so the species list sets the real range.

Tropical and reptile houses

Hot, wet rooms behave like the rooms in the indoor pool lesson. Warm water, plants, pools and misting put moisture into the air faster than people or equipment would. The cold surfaces become the problem: glass, skylights and exterior walls drop below the room dew point and sweat. A zoo engineering article from ASME describing a polar bear habitat notes that summer outdoor air with high humidity means condensation has to be designed out, and that winter outdoor air, being drier, can be used to dehumidify a viewing area. The same logic applies to a tropical house: dry cold air brought in during winter is a free dehumidifier, but it must be heated, which is why the energy bill is large.

  • Glass and framing. Keep interior glass surfaces above the room dew point with warm supply air washing the glass, thermal breaks and a warm perimeter. Our own rule of thumb from psychrometrics: if the room is 80 °F at 70% RH, the dew point is near 69 °F, so any surface colder than that sweats. See psychrometrics for technicians.
  • Corrosion. Wet air plus fertilizer, chlorine, salt or animal waste attacks galvanized duct, fasteners and coils. Specify coated or stainless parts and keep fan motors out of the humid stream. See the aquarium and fish room lesson.
  • Hot-spot heating. Our own note: reptile enclosures often use local heat lamps or pads, on separate circuits from the room air handler, so check both.

Primate buildings

The National Academies workshop chapter on environmental controls says US ventilation rates have historically followed 10 to 15 air changes per hour of fresh air, that higher rates are allowed with justification, and that calculations should be made to remove the thermal and moisture loads. It lists pressure practice too: quarantine, isolation, biohazard and nonhuman primate spaces should be kept under negative pressure, while pathogen-free and clean spaces are positive. For primates the same chapter gives European temperature suggestions of 16 to 25 °C for macaques and vervets and 23 to 28 °C for marmosets and tamarins, with 40 to 70% humidity for macaques and squirrel monkeys.

Keeper areas matter because of disease. CDC says people who are regularly exposed to macaques have a higher risk of B virus, that the virus can be in saliva, feces and urine and can survive on wet surfaces for hours, and that bites and scratches are the main route. Our own design note: build keeper corridors negative to the public side so air moves from visitors toward the animals and out through exhaust. NIOSH, studying primate caretakers at four facilities, found many incidents went unreported and that only 37% of respondents reported respirator training. Do not rely on air alone: train before assigning respirators.

Section 3.84 of the same subpart calls for sanitizing methods such as live steam or hot water of at least 180 °F, so these rooms are wet, and the air system has to survive that.

Aviaries

An aviary is a large volume where birds shed dust, feathers and droppings. CDC says psittacosis spreads among bird owners and workers, and advises dampening cages and surfaces before cleaning and avoiding dry sweeping or vacuuming, which puts dust in the air. The page does not give any ventilation rate. Our own HVAC note: choose filters that can be changed without stirring up dust, and keep netting and screens at intakes clean. This is the same dust problem as in the animal shelter lesson and the veterinary and kennel lesson.

Separating animal air from visitor air

The ASME article on the polar bear habitat describes the arrangement we want: visitor areas and off-exhibit dens have separate air handling so den odors do not reach visitors, and the quarantine area uses negative pressure and higher filtration like a hospital isolation room. A simple building plan: the visitor hall is neutral to slightly positive so entry doors do not pull in unconditioned air, the exhibit itself is held warm and wet, and the keeper corridor is the lowest pressure so air flows from public toward the animals and then out. Our own rule: never recirculate keeper or holding air to the public.

Heat backup and alarms

A cold failure kills animals faster than a warm one is noticed, so the heating system needs a second way to produce heat and a way to tell people. The federal primate rule gives a 4-hour limit outside 45 to 85 °F, which tells you how fast you have to respond. Our own suggestions, not from a cited code: separate heat sources (for example boiler plus electric or a second boiler), standby power for fans and controls, low and high temperature and high humidity alarms that send a message to a person who is on duty, and a logged daily temperature check. The National Academies chapter notes the European guidance asks that temperature be measured and logged daily. See BAS alarms and trend logs.

Worked example: our own, with assumed inputs

All inputs here are assumptions for a small tropical house, not a design. Room: 80 °F, 70% RH. Winter outdoor air: 20 °F, 50% RH. Outdoor air brought in: 2,000 cfm. Pressure: 14.696 psia.

Humidity ratios, W = 0.622 pw / (p − pw). Saturation pressure at 80 °F is about 0.507 psia (psychrometric table value), so pw = 0.70 × 0.507 = 0.355 psia. Wroom = 0.622 × 0.355 / (14.696 − 0.355) = 0.01540. At 20 °F saturation is about 0.063 psia (over ice), pw = 0.0315, Wout = 0.622 × 0.0315 / (14.696 − 0.0315) = 0.00134.

Moisture leaving with exhaust: ΔW = 0.01540 − 0.00134 = 0.01406. lb/h = 4.5 × 2,000 × 0.01406 = about 127 lb/h, roughly 15 gallons per hour. That is the water the pools, plants and misting must put back in winter to hold 70%, and the humidifier or the exhibit's own evaporation supplies it. In summer the sign flips: if outdoor W is higher than the room, the same airflow brings moisture in and the coil must remove it. The latent heat is 4,840 × 2,000 × 0.01406 = about 136,000 Btu/h.

Heat backup. Suppose the failed main heat is replaced by a backup that holds 70 °F. Assume a building UA of 2,500 Btu/h per °F (assumed) and outdoor air cut to 500 cfm. ΔT = 70 − 20 = 50 °F. Envelope: 2,500 × 50 = 125,000 Btu/h. Ventilation: 1.08 × 500 × 50 = 27,000 Btu/h. Total about 152,000 Btu/h, or about 44.5 kW (152,000 / 3,412).

Field card: Zoo Animal House HVAC

Warm and wet inside, separate air, and a second heat source.

  • Federal primate rule: not below 45 °F or above 85 °F for over 4 hours; humidity per the veterinarian.
  • Primate rooms: historically 10 to 15 air changes per hour; negative pressure for primate and isolation spaces.
  • Keep glass and cold surfaces above the room dew point.
  • Moisture lb/h = 4.5 × cfm × ΔW; sensible Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT.
  • Keeper corridors negative to the public side; do not recirculate animal air to visitors.
  • Dampen before sweeping aviaries; respirators need fit-testing and training.
  • Backup heat plus an alarm that reaches a person, tested on a schedule.
