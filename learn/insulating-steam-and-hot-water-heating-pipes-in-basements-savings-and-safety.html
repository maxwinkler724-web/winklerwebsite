Insulating Steam and Hot-Water Heating Pipes in Basements

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Walk into an older basement and the heating pipes may be bare, hot to the touch and warming the whole room. Is that wasted fuel, or free heat for the floor above? This lesson shows how to tell, which insulation fits steam and which fits hot water, and what to check before you touch old wrap.

Check the sources

Check the sources. This page was built from U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) sheets and maker data sheets read in 2026. It is general education, not a design or an inspection. Follow the insulation maker's data sheet and the boiler maker's instructions for the exact products you use.

Why bare pipe loses heat

Any surface that is hotter than the room gives heat to the room. The hotter the pipe and the more surface it has, the more it gives off. A steam main is both hot and large, so it is the classic case. DOE's steam tip sheet on insulating distribution and condensate lines says to insulate any surface above 120 F, including piping and fittings, and it publishes a table of bare-pipe losses. Those tables are written for industrial plants, but the physics is the same in a house: a bare pipe keeps radiating all season, whether or not that room needs heat.

When insulating helps and when the heat is wanted

Heat lost in a basement is not always lost. If the basement is lived in, or if the floor above is cold and the pipes sit under it, some of that heat is useful. The trouble is that it is uncontrolled. It does not follow a thermostat, it heats the part of the house you care least about, and it keeps coming in spring and fall while the upstairs is already warm.

  • Insulation usually pays on long runs through unheated basements, crawl spaces and garages, on mains that feed radiators far from the boiler, and on any pipe that passes through a space that is too hot in winter.
  • It pays less where the pipe sits in a finished, heated room, or runs short distances next to the boiler.
  • Look for the symptom. If the basement is warm while upstairs rooms at the end of a steam main stay cold, steam is condensing in the main before it reaches them. Insulation moves that heat to where it is meant to go.

Steam: the mains and the returns

On a steam job, two things change once you insulate. First, more steam reaches the far radiators, because less of it condenses on the way. Second, the system can behave differently. DOE's tip sheet warns that heat flow in other parts of the steam system changes after insulating. In practice, a system that was balanced by accident, with a hot basement helping some rooms, may need its venting reviewed. See Steam Main Venting Basics, because a faster-filling main pushes air out faster.

Condensate return lines matter too. Returned condensate is hot water that took fuel to make. DOE's tip sheet on returning condensate says the energy left in condensate can exceed 10 percent of the steam's total energy in a typical system, so a bare return line is giving away heat you already paid for. Read Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics for the full picture, and Steam Traps Explained before wrapping near traps. DOE also advises following the maker's instructions for traps and using removable jackets on valves, flanges and fittings, so you can reach them for service.

Hot-water systems

A hot-water system loses less per foot than steam, because the pipe is cooler. Baseboard systems were traditionally designed for water in roughly the 180 to 220 F range (see Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature), so supply pipes still run hot. The same rule applies: insulate the runs that pass through spaces you do not mean to heat, and leave the emitters themselves bare, since the emitter's job is to give off heat. DOE's Energy Saver page on water-heater piping lists 3 to 4 percent annual savings for insulating domestic hot-water pipes and says it can allow water 2 to 4 F hotter. That page covers domestic hot water, not a heating loop, so use it only as a sense of scale.

Check for asbestos before you touch old wrap

This comes first. EPA lists hot water and steam pipes coated with asbestos material, or covered with an asbestos blanket or tape, among the places asbestos may be found, and says exposure may occur when the material is disturbed or damaged. You cannot identify it by looking. If a house has white or gray wrap on its pipes, do not cut, sand, scrape or sweep it. Read Asbestos and Lead Paint Around Old Heating Systems and have a trained professional sample it before any work that would disturb it. Intact wrap that nobody touches is a different situation from wrap that is crumbling or in the way of a repair.

Which insulation for which pipe

Match the product's temperature rating to the pipe, not to what is on the shelf.

  • Fiberglass with a jacket. Johns Manville lists its Micro-Lok pipe insulation, a preformed fiberglass with an ASJ paper-finish jacket, for application temperatures from 0 to 850 F. Owens Corning's SSL II with ASJ Max data sheet lists 0 to 1,000 F, with a heat-up schedule required above 850 F. Both ranges are well above steam heating temperatures, which is why jacketed fiberglass is the usual choice on steam mains. Owens Corning also lists a separate limit for the jacket itself, -20 to 150 F, so read the maker's sheet for how the jacket is meant to be used.
  • Elastomeric foam tube. Armacell's AP Armaflex data sheet lists a maximum service temperature of 220 F for its NBR/PVC tubes, and 300 F for 1-1/2 and 2 inch wall EPDM tubes. That sheet does not mention steam. Because steam pipe at ordinary pressure runs near or above 212 F, foam is a poor match there. On hot water, it is fine only when the water stays under the maker's number, with margin.

DOE's Energy Saver page adds one more rule for domestic piping near a gas water heater flue: use unfaced fiberglass pipe wrap within 8 inches of the flue, not foam. The idea carries over, keep combustible or low-rated materials away from flues and hot parts.

Sizes and fittings

Preformed insulation is made to fit a pipe's nominal size, so a 2 inch pipe takes insulation made for 2 inch pipe. Owens Corning's installation sheet sends installers to its separate sizing manual for proper sizing, so check the maker's table instead of guessing. Seal the lap and butt joints as the maker directs. On fittings, DOE recommends removable jackets on valves, flanges and fittings so you can service them. Follow the trap maker's instructions near steam traps, as DOE says.

Worked example: 60 feet of bare steam main

DOE's steam tip sheet lists bare-pipe loss in million Btu per year per 100 feet of horizontal steel line, with 75 F room air, no wind and 8,760 operating hours. For a 2 inch line at 15 psig it gives 235 MMBtu per year per 100 ft. The tip sheet's own example assumes insulation that is 90 percent effective, 80 percent boiler efficiency and fuel at $8.00 per MMBtu. We borrow those values and add our own assumptions for a basement.

Assumptions: 60 ft of bare 2 inch main, 3,000 hours per year with steam in the line. Residential steam is usually at a few psi, below the table's 15 psig, so real losses would be somewhat lower. Treat this as an upper estimate.

  1. Per foot at full time: 235 MMBtu / 100 ft = 2.35 MMBtu per ft per year. At 8,760 hours that is 2,350,000 Btu / 8,760 h = about 268 Btu per hour per foot.
  2. 60 ft at 8,760 h: 235 x 0.60 = 141 MMBtu per year.
  3. Scale to 3,000 h: 141 x 3,000 / 8,760 = 48.29 MMBtu per year lost from the bare pipe.
  4. Insulation recovers 90 percent: 48.29 x 0.90 = 43.46 MMBtu per year of heat kept.
  5. Boiler input to make that heat: 43.46 / 0.80 = 54.32 MMBtu per year of fuel.
  6. Cost at the example price: 54.32 x $8.00 = about $435 per year.

Your fuel price will differ, so use your own bill. The method stays the same. Note too that this is heat that did not reach the radiators, not only heat that warmed the basement.

Field card: Insulating Steam and Hot-Water Heating Pipes in Basements

Test old wrap first. Match the rating to the pipe.

  • Bare hot pipe heats the room all season. DOE says to insulate surfaces above 120 F.
  • Worth it on long runs through unheated spaces; less so where the heat is wanted.
  • Asbestos may be in old pipe wrap. Do not disturb it; have a pro sample it first.
  • Jacketed fiberglass suits steam (JM 0 to 850 F; OC to 1,000 F). Foam is rated about 220 F.
  • Leave traps, gauges and valves reachable; use removable jackets.
  • Example: 60 ft of 2 in bare main, 3,000 h, 90% effective, 80% boiler: 54.32 MMBtu fuel, about $435 per year at $8.00 per MMBtu.
