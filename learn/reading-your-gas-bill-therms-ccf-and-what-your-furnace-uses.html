Reading Your Gas Bill: Therms, CCF and What Your Furnace Uses

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Your gas meter counts volume. Your bill charges for energy. The unit in between is the therm, and once you see how a meter reading becomes therms, you can also estimate how much gas a furnace uses from the rating on its nameplate.

What a therm is

The U.S. Energy Information Administration glossary defines a therm as 100,000 Btu. A Btu is a small amount of heat, so a therm is a convenient bundle for a household-sized energy bill. EIA's Btu page also lists a sample figure of 1,036 Btu per cubic foot of natural gas and notes that such factors are recalculated every year because fuel quality and composition change. That is the key idea for the rest of this lesson: a cubic foot of gas does not carry one fixed amount of energy, so the utility applies a conversion factor.

CCF: what the meter and the bill count

CCF means hundreds of cubic feet. New Jersey Natural Gas (NJNG) explains on its sample bill that usage is shown "in 100's of cubic feet" and is the current meter read minus the previous read. In its sample, a reading of 5960 minus 5895 gives 65 CCF. PSE&G's bill guide says the same thing in its own words: it measures the volume of gas in cubic feet (CCF) and then converts it to therms. The state's Division of Rate Counsel also publishes a gas bill guide that says therms are found by multiplying the cubic feet used by the BTU content.

The factor on your bill

Utilities label the conversion differently, so look for a line with a number near 1. NJNG's sample bill calls it "BTU Content" and shows 1.071; its guide says that figure is used to convert cubic feet of gas into therms. PSE&G's sample shows two steps: a first factor of 1.012 applied to the meter difference (175 became 177.1 CCF), then a factor of 1.038 (reaching 183.830 therms) to reach therms. Both of those are only the numbers on those sample bills. Your own factor will differ by utility, by month and by location, so use the one printed on your bill. Do not assume it is the same year to year.

Reading the meter yourself

The difference between two readings is your usage for that stretch. Take a reading on the same day each week and you will see where the gas goes. For the technician method, where you time the small test dial to check a furnace's actual input, see Clocking a Gas Meter and Checking Furnace Input. Remember that meters can read in cubic feet or in CCF; your bill states which unit it uses, so match the unit before you do any math.

Supply versus delivery

PSE&G's sample bill splits the gas portion into delivery charges, which include a monthly service charge and a per-therm rate plus a seasonal balancing charge, and a supply line for the cost of the gas itself, labeled as default supply service (BGSS). NJNG's guide uses the same split, with a supply charge for the gas bought on the wholesale market and a delivery charge for moving gas, maintaining the system and state programs, then a customer charge for the account. The New Jersey Board of Public Utilities (BPU) explains that a 1999 law separated supply from delivery. You may buy supply from a licensed third-party supplier, but the local utility still delivers the gas and responds to emergencies. The BPU says it regulates the utility's delivery service and its default supply rates, but does not regulate third-party supplier rates. PSE&G adds that if you buy from a third party, the supply charge will not appear on its bill. Rates change often and are set through the regulatory process, so this lesson quotes none; read the rate lines on your own bill.

Worked example: CCF to therms

Use NJNG's sample numbers, which are labeled as an example on its bill. The meter difference is 65 CCF and the BTU content factor is 1.071.

  1. Therms = CCF x factor.
  2. 65 x 1.071 = 69.615.
  3. Rounded, that is about 69.62 therms, which matches the sample bill.

Check it by energy: 69.62 therms x 100,000 Btu per therm = about 6,962,000 Btu. Divide by 6500 cubic feet and you get about 1,071 Btu per cubic foot, a little above EIA's 1,036 sample, which fits the point that the factor varies.

Estimating what a furnace uses

A furnace nameplate lists a gas input rating in Btu per hour. While the burners are on, the furnace draws roughly that much fuel. The arithmetic below uses a hypothetical furnace and hypothetical run hours, chosen only for illustration.

  1. Rated input (hypothetical): 80,000 Btu/h.
  2. Convert to therms per hour: 80,000 / 100,000 = 0.8 therm per hour.
  3. Assumed burner run time (hypothetical): 6 hours a day for 30 days = 180 hours.
  4. Therms = 0.8 x 180 = 144 therms.
  5. Back to bill units with the 1.071 factor from above: 144 / 1.071 = about 134.5 CCF.

A scale reference from PSE&G's bill guide: it says a standard gas clothes dryer takes about six hours to equal one therm. That puts the 0.8 therm per hour furnace at roughly five times the dryer's hourly appetite, since 6 hours per therm is about 0.17 therm per hour. Treat this as a rough comparison.

Real use differs from this estimate. The rating is for full firing. Two-stage and modulating furnaces often run below it (see Two-Stage and Modulating Gas Furnaces), the actual input can be off the label (the clocking lesson shows how to measure it), and the fuel burned is not the same as heat delivered to rooms, since efficiency is a separate number (see What SEER2, HSPF2, AFUE and EER2 Mean). Your bill also includes gas for water heating, cooking and dryers if those are gas.

Using this to find bill problems

Compare therms, not dollars, from month to month. Dollars move with rates; therms show what the house actually burned. If therms climb with no change in weather or habits, work through Why Is My Heating or Cooling Bill So High? What to Check. If therms are fine and dollars are not, the rate lines are the place to look. To see what lowering the thermostat can do, read Thermostat Setback Schedules: How Much You Really Save. If you suspect a billing error, ask the utility and, if needed, the BPU.

Field card: Reading Your Gas Bill

Meter counts volume, bill charges energy.

  • 1 therm = 100,000 Btu (EIA).
  • CCF = hundreds of cubic feet; therms = CCF x the factor on your bill.
  • The factor changes by utility and over time. Use your own bill.
  • Supply is the gas; delivery is the pipes and service. A third-party supplier replaces only supply.
  • Furnace therms per hour = input Btu/h / 100,000.
  • Estimated therms = therms per hour x burner run hours.
  • Rated input is full firing. Actual use varies.
