Pressure Relief Valves, Fusible Plugs and Rupture Discs on Refrigeration Systems

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A relief device is the last thing standing between a vessel and a burst. It does nothing for months, then it matters more than anything else on the rack. This lesson covers the devices you will meet on receivers and chillers, why their vent piping goes outdoors, how long they are meant to stay in service, and what to do when one lifts or weeps.

Check the rules where you work

Check the rules where you work. Relief requirements come from ASHRAE Standard 15, the model mechanical code and the local code in force, plus the maker's data on the nameplate. We name them in plain text because we cannot reproduce them. This page is general education, not a code interpretation. Never change a set pressure, cap a vent or bypass a relief device.

The four devices

Spring-loaded relief valve. A spring holds a seat closed until refrigerant pressure under the seat overcomes it. A 1972 state refrigeration code archived online defines a pressure-relief valve as a pressure-actuated valve held closed by a spring or other means. One trade article adds that the valve is flow-rated at 10 percent above its setting, and that debris on the seat after a discharge can keep it from reseating tightly. This is the standard device on liquid receivers, condensers with built-in receivers and many chillers.

Fusible plug. A plug of metal with a set melting point, so it opens on temperature (a fire) and not on pressure. The same archived state code defines it as a device with a predetermined-temperature fusible member for relief of pressure, and allowed it only on the high side of a system, with limits on vessel size. Once it melts it is gone, so it cannot be tested by lifting it.

Rupture disc. A thin metal disc that bursts at a set pressure. A centrifugal chiller maker's installation manual (a distributor-hosted copy, so cited as plain text) says its low-pressure machines carry a rupture disc that breaks if evaporator pressure exceeds 15 psig. Low-pressure chillers run near or below atmospheric pressure on the evaporator side, so a modest-pressure disc is the main protection. A disc cannot reseat; after it goes, the charge goes with it.

Disc in series with a valve. A trade article on relief devices says ASHRAE Standard 15 allows a rupture member between a pop-type valve and the vessel, provided a gauge or vent watches the space between them for leakage. The idea is a tight seal under normal pressure while the valve still does the relieving.

Dual relief manifold with a three-way valve

A relief valve should be swapped on a schedule, but you cannot leave a charged vessel unprotected while you do it. The answer is one three-way shutoff valve feeding two relief valves. The valve body makes it impossible to isolate both at once, so one valve always protects the vessel while you remove the other. Two trade articles recommend this layout where the charge is large and valuable, and tie it to code language for vessels of 10 cubic feet or more gross volume. The older archived state code even required two devices in parallel on vessels that size. Figure 1 shows the arrangement.

Why the vent goes outdoors, and why length matters

When a relief device opens it dumps refrigerant, and for some refrigerants that is a suffocation or toxicity problem in an enclosed room. The 1972 state code we read required discharge piping to run unobstructed to the outside atmosphere, with a drain leg at the low point and a rain-proof termination. The chiller maker's manual still says the same today: both the purge and rupture-disc vent lines must be routed to outside atmosphere. It also asks for the termination at least 15 feet above grade and at least 20 feet from any building opening, and says to keep rainwater and debris out. Those are one maker's figures for one line of machines. Your code sets the numbers for your job.

Back pressure is the other limit. A trade article warns that correct pipe size and length are required to prevent back pressure building in the discharge line, and that a shared header must stay clear of back pressure even if every device discharges together. A pop-type valve is affected by back pressure, and a diaphragm-type valve is not appreciably affected, according to the same article. Long runs, elbows and small pipe all raise the pressure at the valve outlet while it is flowing, which can cut its capacity or change when it closes. The chiller manual measures vent length in equivalent feet (straight pipe plus the equivalent length of fittings) and sizes it from a table. The archived state code limits discharge length with a formula and a table. Do not extend or re-route a vent without the sizing data.

Replacement intervals

A trade article recommends inspecting relief devices every six to 12 months and replacing a valve after it discharges, because debris can keep it from reseating at its stamped pressure. It also reports that ANSI K61.1 calls for replacement no later than five years after manufacture when the maker gives no date, and cites an industrial refrigeration bulletin that says to replace, or inspect, clean and test, every five years. Those two are ammonia-plant documents, so treat five years as a reference point, not a rule for your equipment. The maker's literature and local code decide the interval, and local rules may be shorter. Never discharge a new valve to test it or leave it in place during a system pressure test, since that article notes debris can then stop it from reseating.

What a lifted or weeping valve means

A valve that lifts has seen pressure at or above its setting. Do not just cycle the pressure and move on. Find the cause: a dirty or failed condenser, a fan out, noncondensables, overcharge, a closed valve that trapped liquid, or a tripped high-pressure control that did not do its job. A valve that weeps (leaks past the seat) is telling you it no longer holds its set pressure, often because debris or wear kept it from reseating after an earlier lift. The trade article says a valve that has discharged may relieve at much lower pressure than its stamped setting, and that valve leakage is a condition to watch for at inspections. Either way, plan to replace it, and do not stack a second valve on top of a leaking one; the same article says stacking is not permissible by code and changes the effective setting. If a valve is on a three-way manifold, switch to the good side and replace the other.

EPA rules on refrigerant released through relief

EPA's Section 608 pages say the rules prohibit individuals from intentionally venting ozone-depleting substances or their substitutes while servicing or disposing of equipment. The agency lists exceptions: de minimis quantities released while making good-faith attempts to recapture and recycle or safely dispose of refrigerant, certain emissions from normal operation, and substitutes EPA has found do not pose an environmental threat (some hydrocarbons are exempt in set uses). The pages we read do not mention relief valves, so they do not say how to classify a discharge. The safe approach is to treat any release as something to prevent, find the cause, and look at your leak records. Never vent a charge to be rid of it or to "test" a valve; recover it. For how a discharge feeds the leak-rate math and repair deadlines, see the linked leak-rate lesson.

Worked example (ours)

This is our own illustration with assumed inputs. We assume an R-410A receiver with a stamped maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) of 600 psig, and a relief valve set at 600 psig. The archived state code requires settings not to exceed design working pressure, and one trade article says the setting cannot exceed the stamped MAWP and that the vessel should allow a setting at least 25 percent above the maximum system pressure. Check your own nameplate; 600 is an assumption.

Saturation pressures come from a refrigerant maker's pressure-temperature chart (cited as plain text): 366.4 psig at 110°F, 419.4 psig at 120°F and 477.9 psig at 130°F, bubble-point values. A government energy agency's R-410A chart agrees: it lists about 418 to 420 psig near 120°F.

  1. Normal hot day, 120°F condensing: 419.4 ÷ 600 = 0.70, so the receiver sits at about 70 percent of the set pressure.
  2. Headroom: 600 − 419.4 = 180.6 psi between normal operation and the lift point.
  3. 25 percent rule: the highest system pressure the valve should see is 600 ÷ 1.25 = 480 psig. That corresponds to about 130°F (477.9 psig).
  4. Dirty-condenser day, 130°F: 477.9 psig leaves only 600 − 477.9 = 122.1 psi, or 80 percent of set.

The lesson: the system's normal high-side pressure has to sit well below the relief setting, so the valve only ever opens in a real emergency. Condensing temperature is the lever. A fouled coil or failed fan can push a system from the 110°F row to the 130°F row, and the valve then has little room for error. That is why the high-pressure cutout must be set below the relief setting (the archived code capped it at 90 percent), and why you clean condensers and fix airflow first. The pressure rise toward the relief setting is also what makes a weeping valve more likely over time.

Field card: Pressure Relief Devices on Refrigeration Systems

Keep the system pressure well under the set pressure, and never block the vent.

  • Spring valve: pressure opens it. Fusible plug: heat melts it. Rupture disc: pressure bursts it once.
  • Dual relief on a three-way valve: one valve always protects the vessel while you swap the other.
  • Vent piping goes outdoors, drained, rain-proof, sized for low back pressure. Do not alter it without the sizing data.
  • One trade source: inspect every 6 to 12 months and replace after any discharge. Follow maker and local code.
  • Set pressure must not exceed the MAWP on the nameplate.
  • A lifted valve means find the cause. A weeping valve means replace it.
  • Do not vent refrigerant to "test" anything; recover it.
