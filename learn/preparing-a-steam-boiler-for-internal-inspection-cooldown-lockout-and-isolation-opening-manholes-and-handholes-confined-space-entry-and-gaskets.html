Preparing a Steam Boiler for Internal Inspection: Cooldown, Lockout and Isolation, Opening Manholes and Handholes, Confined Space Entry and Gaskets

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An internal inspection is the one time an inspector looks at the water side of the boiler with their own eyes. Everything before that moment is yours: cool it slowly, drain it, cut off every energy and material source, open it, wash it, test the air and only then let anyone climb in. This lesson walks through that order and why each step comes where it does.

Check the rules that apply to your site

Check the rules that apply to your site. Boiler rules differ by state and city, and this page is general education built from government pages we read in 2026. Your jurisdiction's inspector, your written plant procedures and the boiler maker's instructions come first. The ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code and the National Board Inspection Code are the model documents behind most of these rules; we name them in plain text and do not reproduce them.

Step 1: Shut down and cool slowly

Ohio's boiler rule lists the owner's job in order, and its first item is to shut down and cool the boiler slowly. Connecticut's rule adds that the furnace and combustion chambers must cool fully before they are cleaned. Neither rule gives a degrees-per-hour figure, so we do not invent one for you. The reason for slow cooling is our own reasoning: thick shell plates and tube sheets cool at different speeds than thin tubes, and forcing the pace with cold water or open doors stresses the joints.

A federal facilities criteria document (UFC 3-430-07) gives one number for taking a boiler out of service: cool it until the water is below the atmospheric boiling point, but not below 180 F, then empty and flush it. Treat that as one published practice, not a universal rule, and follow the boiler maker's instructions for your unit.

Step 2: Lock out fuel, power, feedwater, blowdown and steam

OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 29 CFR 1910.147, applies to servicing and maintenance in general industry. It lays out the order: prepare, shut down, isolate, apply lockout or tagout devices to each energy-isolating device, relieve stored energy, then verify the equipment is isolated before work begins. It also expects written procedures and an inspection of them at least annually, and it allows group lockout where each worker adds a personal lock.

For a boiler, the sources we read add the material side. Ohio requires that feedwater and blowoff valves be closed and secured before anyone enters a boiler or drum. Connecticut says to stop any steam or hot water leaking into the boiler by disconnecting the pipe or valve at a convenient point. Washington's rule says to disconnect piping or install isolation blinds, and requires an energy isolation program so nothing can start up or release stored energy.

Two valves with a free-blowing drain between

OSHA's confined space standard, 29 CFR 1910.146, defines isolation as removing the space from service and protecting it from release of energy and material, for example by blanking or blinding, or by double block and bleed. It defines double block and bleed as closing and locking or tagging two in-line valves and opening a drain or vent valve between them. That is the arrangement in the drawing below on both the steam outlet and the blowdown line. If the drain between the valves keeps running steam or water, one of the valves is passing and the line is not isolated. That check is our own reading of the definition.

Step 3: Open, wash and clean

With the boiler isolated, Ohio and Washington both call for draining, then removing the manhole and handhole plates and washout plugs, plus the plugs in the water column connections. Ohio adds that the fire side of the boiler and setting be cleaned thoroughly, and that the water side be cleaned of loose scale and mud and washed out. North Dakota's boiler program puts the goal plainly: the inspector needs access to the fire side and the water side, the tubes brushed clean, and water, mud and loose scale removed so the metal can be seen. Its slides also want the float bowls of the low-water cutoff opened.

Draining needs air to replace the water, so we open a vent at the top first. That is our own practical reasoning, not a quote from a rule.

Step 4: Confined space entry

A boiler drum or shell usually meets the OSHA definition of a confined space: big enough to enter, limited ways in and out, not built for people to stay in. OSHA's confined space page describes a permit space as one that has or may have a hazardous atmosphere, material that could engulf an entrant, a shape that could trap someone, or another recognized serious hazard. Under 1910.146 for general industry the permit lists the entrants, the isolation and hazard controls used, and any other permits such as hot work. At least one attendant stays outside for the whole entry. Construction work falls under a separate OSHA rule; see the related lesson on confined spaces.

Test the air before entry in the order the standard gives: oxygen first, then combustible gases and vapors, then toxic gases and vapors. A hazardous atmosphere includes oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent. Washington's rule also makes the inspector confirm a safe atmosphere before anyone enters.

What the inspector looks at

  • Tubes and tube sheets: North Dakota's slides list leaking tubes or tube rolls, the tube sheet condition, and scale and pitting on tubes and shell.
  • Corrosion low in the boiler: the bottom of mud legs, and stays checked for thinning.
  • Distortion: any part, plus firebox metal, leaking stays and refractory condition.
  • Low-water cutoff: Washington lets the inspector require it to be taken apart. New York City's review list wants a float-type cutoff to have a drain at the low point of its equalizing piping so it can be flushed and tested.
  • Safety valves: the same city list says there must be no means of shutoff between the boiler and its safety or relief valve. Washington lets the inspector require valves removed for testing, and the facilities criteria document wants them sealed and tagged before a power boiler is certified.
  • Fusible plug: where the boiler has one, ask the inspector what they need. A Minnesota inspector's report on an antique boiler failure said the plug showed slight signs of overheating but did not melt, and that rusted handhole plug threads showed the plug had not been removed recently.

New gaskets and seating the plates

The facilities criteria document says manhole and handhole gaskets must be replaced after the strength test unless they are the non-compressible steel type. We read that as a firm habit: do not reuse a crushed gasket on a plate you just took off. Cleaning the seat, centering the plate and tightening it follow the boiler maker's procedure, which the sources we read do not spell out, so get it from the maker.

Refill and warm up slowly

After the internal inspection the same document has the boiler brought up to operating pressure and temperature as part of the external inspection, where leaks at the plates show up. We give no fill rate or warm-up rate here; the maker's instructions set them. The reason to go slowly is the same as for cooling: even heating keeps the joints from being stressed unevenly.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

Take a fire-tube boiler we are calling an example. Assumptions, all our own: 400 gallons of water; water at 250 F when the fire goes off; natural cooling at 8 F per hour; a drain that moves 20 gallons per minute; 30 minutes to pull plates; 2 hours of washout; 30 minutes to ventilate, test and sign the permit. Real boilers cool at other rates, so measure your own.

  1. Cooling from 250 F down to 180 F is 70 F. At 8 F per hour, 70 / 8 = 8.75 hours, or 8 h 45 min.
  2. Draining 400 gallons at 20 gallons per minute takes 400 / 20 = 20 minutes.
  3. Total from fire off to permitted entry: 8 h 45 + 0 h 20 + 0 h 30 + 2 h 00 + 0 h 30 = 12 h 05 min (the valve and lockout work at the start happens while the boiler cools).

Checklist of isolation points (our own)

  • Main fuel shutoff and pilot or igniter fuel: closed, locked, tagged.
  • Burner, control and feed pump electrical disconnects: locked, tried to confirm they will not start.
  • Boiler stop valve and header stop valve: closed, locked, tagged, with the drain between them open and free-blowing.
  • Feedwater stop valve and check valve: stop valve closed, locked, tagged.
  • Blowdown valve 1 and valve 2: closed after the boiler is drained, locked, tagged, with the bleed between them open.
  • Chemical feed, water column drains, deaerator or heater connections and any other pipe touching the boiler: closed and locked, or disconnected or blinded.
  • Verification: nothing flowing from either bleed, and the entry permit lists every point above.

Field card: Preparing a Steam Boiler for Internal Inspection

Cool slowly, isolate twice, test the air, new gasket.

  • Cool slowly (Ohio rule); drain and wash; open all plates and plugs.
  • Lockout order: prepare, shut down, isolate, lock, relieve stored energy, verify (29 CFR 1910.147).
  • Double block and bleed: two valves closed and locked, free drain open between (29 CFR 1910.146).
  • Close and secure feed and blowoff valves before entry (Ohio rule).
  • Air test order: oxygen, flammables, toxics; oxygen 19.5 to 23.5 percent.
  • Inspector wants tubes brushed, scale and mud out, float bowls open.
  • Replace manhole and handhole gaskets after the strength test unless steel.
  • Warm up as the maker directs; check the plates for leaks.
