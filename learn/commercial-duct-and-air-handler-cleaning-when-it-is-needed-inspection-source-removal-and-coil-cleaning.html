Commercial Duct and Air Handler Cleaning: When It Is Needed

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A customer in a commercial building asks whether the ducts need cleaning. The honest answer is "it depends on what we find." This lesson covers what EPA says about when cleaning is warranted, why we inspect first, how a source removal job works with negative air and HEPA collection, and what changes for coils, drain pans and fiberglass-lined duct.

Check the standard and the spec

Check the standard and the project spec. This page is general education built from public agency pages we read in 2026, not a work procedure. The industry standard is named ACR, The NADCA Standard for the Assessment, Cleaning and Restoration of HVAC Systems; we name it here but do not reproduce it, so get the current edition and the project specification before you bid or work.

When EPA says cleaning is warranted

EPA's duct page is written for homes, and it says so, but its triggers are a sound starting point for any building. EPA makes no blanket recommendation for routine cleaning. It lists three conditions: substantial visible mold growth inside hard-surface ducts or other system components, ducts infested with vermin, and ducts clogged with excessive dust and debris or releasing particles from supply registers. Normal dust on return registers is not, by itself, a sign of contamination. For larger buildings, EPA's Building Air Quality guide (Appendix B) agrees in spirit: look into contamination where there are specific problems such as water damage, biological growth, debris that restricts airflow, or dust discharging from diffusers, because dust in a duct does not by itself mean a current microbiological problem. Both pages add the same rule: fix the cause, such as a leak or a wet coil, or the problem will return.

What evidence exists

EPA says duct cleaning has never been shown to prevent health problems, and that studies do not conclusively show dirty ducts raise particle levels in living spaces or that cleaning lowers them. EPA also says some research suggests cleaning coils, fans and heat exchangers may improve efficiency, while little evidence shows cleaning only the ducts does. The Appendix B discussion admits there is less agreement about when cleanup is appropriate and how well techniques work, and calls for more research.

The industry side offers one demonstration. NADCA summarizes a University of Colorado Boulder study in four public elementary buildings near Denver. After cleaning done under the ACR standard with containment, negative pressure and HEPA equipment, median PM2.5 fell 36% to 46% in the month after, and PM10 fell 27% to 54%. Treat that carefully: it covers four buildings, one month, and the summary comes from a trade association whose members do this work. It is a reason to ask questions, not a promise.

Inspect first

NADCA's public commercial checklist puts inspection ahead of cleaning: inspect the components, evaluate and prepare the site, check the inspector's qualifications, and verify cleanliness afterward by visual inspection and testing under the standard. In practice we start with what we can see without opening anything: the drain pan, the coil face, the filter bank, and any supply diffuser that is dirty. Then we open access doors to look at the plenum, the first stretch of supply and return duct, and any liner. A borescope or duct camera extends the view where doors are far apart. We photograph findings, because a photo is the evidence that justifies the work. EPA also suggests having a provider show any claimed mold, and notes that lab confirmation is possible, and that settling-plate tests are not appropriate for deciding on biocides. Before cutting new openings, EPA says to check for asbestos-containing materials, which need specialized handling. Service openings must be closed airtight afterward; EPA says use more than tape, such as screws, rivets or mastic.

Source removal with negative air and HEPA

Source removal means we loosen debris with agitation tools and pull it out to a collection device, rather than just blowing it around. NADCA describes the whole system as held under continuous negative pressure, so loosened particles are captured instead of escaping into occupied space. EPA's Appendix B says negative air pressure that draws pollutants to a vacuum collection system should be maintained at all times in the cleaning area. It says high-velocity airflow alone (it mentions more than 6,000 cfm) should be paired with controlled brushing, because airflow by itself is unlikely to achieve satisfactory results. Two cautions from the same page: use HEPA-filtered vacuums when the collector sits inside the occupied space, and do not use the building air handler as the air mover during cleaning. EPA also warns that high negative pressure with limited airflow can collapse ducts, so we watch the duct as the machine ramps up. On fiberglass duct board and fiberglass-lined duct, EPA says use only soft-bristled brushes.

EPA describes a HEPA filter as one that can theoretically remove at least 99.97% of 0.3 micron particles, the most penetrating size. A damaged or poorly seated HEPA stage defeats the point, so we check seals before each shift.

Containment in an occupied building

EPA's Appendix B says to clean while the building is unoccupied, to avoid exposure to chemicals and loosened particles. Its Section 7 adds that ventilation changes can isolate a work area: temporary local exhaust, sealing off returns in the work zone, and scheduling noisy or dusty work for unoccupied periods with ventilation kept on. After cleaning, the guide reports NADCA recommends running the system for at least eight air changes in the occupied space. Workers who may breathe duct contaminants or biocides need suitable respiratory protection; OSHA's respiratory protection rule, 29 CFR 1910.134, requires a written program, a medical evaluation and a fit test before a tight-fitting respirator is used, and a fit test at least annually.

AHU coils and drain pans

Cleaning only the duct and leaving a dirty coil can re-contaminate it. EPA's duct page says to clean all components, and NADCA's checklist lists coils, evaporator fins, internal surfaces, condensate collectors and drains. Before wash-down starts, NADCA says an operative drainage system must be in place. EPA's Appendix B says careful cleaning and sanitizing of coils and drip pans can reduce microbial contaminants, and that parts should be rinsed and dried so occupants are not exposed to cleaning chemicals. It also warns that pans that are not sloped or trapped correctly hold standing water. See Air Handler Cooling Coils for the coil and pan design side.

Fiberglass-lined duct

EPA says experts disagree whether porous fiberglass grows mold more readily than bare metal, but once liner is contaminated with mold, cleaning cannot prevent regrowth. EPA, NADCA and NAIMA recommend replacing wet or moldy fiberglass. Steam cleaning and other moisture-based methods should not be used on any ductwork. NADCA's checklist notes that replacing damaged fibrous glass insulation is not part of its cleaning specification, so that is a separate scope item. The mold side is covered in Mold on the AC Coil or in Ducts.

Biocides and sealants

EPA registers biocides under federal pesticide law, and they must be used strictly as the label directs. EPA says none is registered for fiberglass duct board or fiberglass-lined duct, and only a small number are registered for bare sheet metal. Appendix B says registered HVAC antimicrobials are sanitizers, not disinfectants or sterilizers, and that deodorizers only mask odor. NADCA's checklist says antimicrobials go on only if active fungal growth is reasonably suspected. EPA adds that spraying into an operating system can carry material through the building. On sealants, EPA, NADCA, NAIMA and SMACNA do not recommend routine encapsulation in any duct type, and sealant should never go on wet liner or growing mold. Do not confuse encapsulant with sealing duct leaks, which EPA says can save energy.

Worked example: sizing negative air for a duct section

We could not find a minimum velocity in a free public source, so the target below is Our own planning figure. Replace it with the number in the project specification or the ACR standard. Our own readings: a 24 in by 16 in branch, and a 48 in by 24 in trunk.

Step 1, branch area. 24 × 16 = 384 sq in. 384 ÷ 144 = 2.67 sq ft.

Step 2, branch airflow. Our own example target of 150 fpm (we found no public minimum; use the figure in your project spec): 2.67 × 150 = 400 cfm.

Step 3, trunk. 48 × 24 = 1,152 sq in. 1,152 ÷ 144 = 8.0 sq ft. 8.0 × 150 = 1,200 cfm.

Step 4, sanity check. EPA's Appendix B mentions airflow above 6,000 cfm as the high-velocity range. Our trunk figure is 1,200 ÷ 6,000 = 0.2, or one fifth of that, so it is a collection airflow, not a blow-out. If our own example machine is rated 2,000 cfm at its working point, 2,000 − 1,200 = 800 cfm of margin for leaks and filter loading. Verify the actual velocity with a traverse; see Duct Traverse with a Pitot Tube.

Coil trend check, Our own readings: pressure drop across the coil of 0.62 in. w.c. before cleaning and 0.48 in. w.c. after at the same airflow. 0.62 − 0.48 = 0.14. 0.14 ÷ 0.62 = 0.226, about a 23% drop. Same airflow matters; a different fan speed makes the comparison meaningless.

Field card: Commercial Duct and Air Handler Cleaning: When It Is Needed

Inspect, prove the problem, fix the cause, then clean.

  • EPA triggers: substantial visible mold, vermin, or clogging dust and debris. No blanket routine cleaning.
  • Evidence is thin: EPA says benefit to health has never been shown.
  • Inspect through access doors and with a camera, and photograph findings.
  • Source removal: agitation plus continuous negative air to a HEPA-filtered collector.
  • Do not use the building air handler as the air mover. Watch for duct collapse.
  • Clean coils, pans and drains too, with drainage working first.
  • Wet or moldy fiberglass liner gets replaced, not cleaned.
  • No EPA-registered biocide for fiberglass duct; no routine sealants.
  • Our own: area (sq ft) × fpm = cfm.
