Operating Room HVAC: Air Changes, Pressure, Diffusers, Humidity and Setbacks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An operating room is a room where the air system is part of the patient's protection. If you service the air handlers, controls or filters that feed one, you need to know what the room is supposed to do, which numbers matter, and why you never change them on your own. This lesson answers the common search "operating room air changes and humidity requirements" in plain terms.

The facility's rules come first

The facility's infection control team and design criteria govern. The numbers below come from public sources and are general education. The hospital's infection control group, the engineer of record and the commissioning documents decide what a specific room must do, and local code can be stricter. Never adjust airflow, pressure, setpoints or filters in an operating room without permission from facility engineering and a plan for infection control. Standards named here are named in plain text only; we are not linking or copying them. They are ASHRAE/ASHE Standard 170, which covers ventilation of health care facilities, and the FGI Guidelines. FGI says on its own site that Standard 170 is included in its Guidelines documents and that the two groups have worked together since 2008.

What the air system does

Five jobs show up again and again in the sources.

  • Dilute and remove. The room gets a lot of filtered air every hour. The CDC's 2003 environmental infection control guideline recommended at least 15 total air changes per hour with at least 3 of them outdoor air. A summary on the Price Industries blog (a maker of air distribution products) describes Standard 170 as asking for at least 20 total air changes with at least 4 outdoor. An article on the American Society for Health Care Engineering (ASHE) site says occupied rooms typically run 20 to 25 air changes with 4 outdoor, and many older designs exceed 30.
  • Positive pressure. The CDC says to keep operating rooms positive to corridors and adjacent areas. ASHE's article says Standard 170 requires positive pressure to all adjoining spaces at all times, at least +0.01 in. wc (2.5 Pa). See Negative and Positive Pressure Rooms in Healthcare for how pressure is checked.
  • Clean air over the table. The Price summary says Standard 170 calls for a primary diffuser array that covers the table and extends at least 12 in. beyond it on each side, with downward unidirectional flow averaging 25 to 35 cfm per square foot.
  • Low returns. The same summary says at least two low sidewall return or exhaust openings are required, with four recommended, spaced symmetrically. The CDC's older guidance, for rooms not built for horizontal laminar flow, was supply at the ceiling and exhaust near the floor.
  • Final filtration. The CDC 2003 text called for 90 percent dust-spot efficiency on all recirculated and fresh air. The Price summary says the latest Standard 170 edition requires a minimum of MERV 16 (up from MERV 14), with HEPA filters in the terminal device for orthopedic, transplant, neurosurgery or dedicated burn rooms. See Cleanroom HVAC for how HEPA filters and cascades work.

The CDC also says to keep doors closed except for moving staff, patients and equipment, and to keep backup fans or filters available in case the fixed system fails. It says not to use UV lights to prevent surgical site infections; for what UV does in HVAC, see UV Lights in HVAC Systems.

Temperature and humidity, and how they changed

An ASHRAE Journal excerpt gives Standard 170 ranges of 68 to 75 degrees F dry bulb and 20 to 60 percent relative humidity, and calls the temperatures minimum design values. The humidity floor has moved. An American Hospital Association advisory from January 2015 says some older codes required at least 30 percent, and that a 2010 Standard 170 addendum lowered the minimum to 20 percent with the 60 percent upper limit kept. An ASHE issue brief says the 1999 edition of NFPA 99, which the Medicare Life Safety Code reference pulled in, set a 35 percent minimum, originally meant to reduce static discharge when flammable anesthetics were used.

In April 2013 a CMS survey memo issued a categorical waiver letting hospitals and critical access hospitals run anesthetizing locations at 20 percent or higher instead of 35 percent. The memo recommends staying at or below 60 percent. It does not apply where state or local rules are stricter or where lower humidity would hurt ventilation system performance. Ambulatory surgery centers are told to follow nationally accepted guidelines instead.

On temperature, a Joint Commission FAQ says deviations from the required range must follow the organization's policy, limited to cases based on the surgeon, patient or procedure. It calls this "not a blanket exception," says the room should return to the normal range afterward, and says relative humidity should stay below 60 percent even then. It is not acceptable to run consistently outside the ranges.

Unoccupied setbacks

Operating rooms sit idle a lot. A healthdesign.org summary says they are often unused upwards of 40 percent of the time, which is why setbacks save energy. The catch is the pressure rule. ASHE's setback article says Standard 170 lets air changes drop when the room is unoccupied, but pressure must still be held at all times, and the minimum must be restored when the room is occupied. Its example sets back from 20 to 5 air changes, with the real rate to be found by testing with infection control, and it generally recommends reducing airflow only while holding temperature and humidity so the room returns to ready quickly. A U.S. Department of Energy webinar transcript on ventilation setbacks has one speaker saying the unoccupied requirement comes down to maintaining the pressure relationship, with a 400 cfm example holding a 0.01 inch setpoint. One health system plan in that transcript returned rooms to 20 air changes 30 minutes before the first case and added a 45 minute purge after the last. Those are one plan, not a rule. California and New York have higher local requirements, so verify locally. The CDC adds that offices may be shut down for energy savings, but not in a way that upsets pressure in critical areas such as operating rooms.

Worked example (Our own)

Our own. Assumptions: a room 20 ft by 20 ft with a 10 ft ceiling, a design of 20 total air changes per hour and 4 outdoor air changes per hour (the Standard 170 minimums as summarized by Price Industries and ASHE), and an assumed diffuser array of 8 ft by 4 ft. A real room may be designed higher, and the design drawings govern.

  1. Volume = 20 x 20 x 10 = 4,000 cubic feet.
  2. Total supply = 4,000 x 20 / 60 min = 1,333 cfm.
  3. Outdoor air = 4,000 x 4 / 60 = 267 cfm, about 20 percent of the supply (4 / 20).
  4. Array at 25 to 35 cfm per square foot: 8 x 4 = 32 sq ft, so 32 x 25 = 800 cfm up to 32 x 35 = 1,120 cfm through the array.
  5. Setback at 5 air changes (ASHE example): 4,000 x 5 / 60 = 333 cfm, one quarter of the occupied flow.

The array can pull most of the total flow, so the rest of the supply goes to other diffusers. With the old CDC figures, 15 and 3 changes would give 1,000 cfm total and 200 cfm outdoor. Same room, different era.

Humidity excursions: what techs check

The 2015 advisory says humidity recovers slowly after weather changes, so short dips are expected, and it suggests reviewing data over a full year to see when and how long humidity falls below 30 percent. Its concern was low humidity affecting static discharge and some supplies. On a call, our own general checklist is below.

  • Read the room humidity and temperature trend and the pressure reading, and note the time the problem began, before touching anything.
  • Look at the outdoor conditions. Hot, humid days and cold, dry days stress the system in opposite ways. Our psychrometrics lesson shows how to read the air state.
  • Check the cooling coil, drain pan and carryover; see Air Handler Cooling Coils.
  • Check humidifier and reheat operation, sensor condition and the control sequence, and compare with setback and ready modes.
  • Confirm doors are closed and pressure is still positive. Report anything out of range through the facility's process; do not just reset it.

Documentation and surveyors

The CMS memo says facilities using the humidity waiver are expected to have written documentation of that decision, to tell the survey team at the entrance conference, and to monitor humidity and correct it to stay at or above 20 percent. The ASHE brief warns that failing to show that documentation could mean a citation. The Joint Commission FAQ says it follows NFPA 99 and Standard 170-2008. Keep logs, set point records and work orders clean so facility staff can show them.

Pressure never goes away, even in setback.

  • Cited Standard 170 minimums: 20 total and 4 outdoor air changes per hour; older CDC text: 15 and 3.
  • Positive to adjoining spaces at all times, at least +0.01 in. wc.
  • Downward flow over the table at 25 to 35 cfm per sq ft; at least two low returns.
