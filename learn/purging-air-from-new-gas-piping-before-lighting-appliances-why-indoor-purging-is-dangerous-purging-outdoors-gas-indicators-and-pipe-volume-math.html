Purging Air From New Gas Piping Before Lighting Appliances

Last updated: 2026-11-04

New or repaired gas piping is full of air. Before an appliance can light, that air has to be pushed out by gas. How you do that matters a great deal: federal investigators have documented deaths and explosions when the air and gas were vented into a building. This lesson explains why air causes problems, why purging indoors is dangerous, how a purge to the outdoors is set up, and when the job belongs to the utility or a licensed fitter.

Safety first. This is education, not a procedure to follow on a live job. Gas piping work is regulated and the rules differ by place. Use the approved procedure and the permits that apply where you work, and stop and call the gas utility if you are unsure.

Why air in the line is a problem

After a gas line is built or opened for repair, it is tested with air or an inert gas, and then holds air when the gas is first turned on. The CSB safety bulletin on this subject notes that the model fuel gas codes require new piping to be tested this way before first use, and that introducing natural gas afterward requires purging. Our own explanation of what air does to an appliance: a burner needs the right mix of gas and air at the flame, and a stream that is mostly air will not light. A furnace or water heater control makes a set number of ignition tries, so if air is still arriving it can fail to light and lock out. Techs often chase this as a bad igniter or gas valve. See Gas Furnace Sequence of Operation for how a control decides to retry or lock out. Purging well solves the problem at its source, and purging badly can be far worse than a lockout.

Why gas and air together are dangerous

Gas burns only when its share of the air falls between two limits. The CDC (the federal public health agency) reports methane, the main component of natural gas, as explosive in air between 5 percent and 15 percent by volume. The federal occupational health guide lists propane at 2.1 percent to 9.5 percent. Below the lower limit a mixture cannot ignite, and above the upper limit it is too rich to burn. Our own reasoning: when gas pushes air out of a pipe, the front between them is a mixed zone, and mixtures in that zone pass through the flammable range on the way. Any gas released into a room also drifts through that range as it mixes with room air. An ignition source in that room, such as a spark from a switch or a pilot flame, can set it off. The code definition of purging itself speaks of freeing a conduit of "air or gas, or a mixture of gas and air."

Smell is not a safe backup. The CSB bulletin warns that people differ in how well they smell the odorant, that noses tire during exposure, and that new steel pipe can remove odorant from the gas, called odor fade. Methane itself has no color and no odor. Our home gas detector lesson covers odorant fade further.

What federal investigators found

In June 2009 a natural gas explosion at a food plant in North Carolina killed three workers and burned four critically; 71 people went to hospitals. The CSB reports that a contractor was purging a newly tied-in 3 inch line to start a water heater, venting the gas into an interior utility room. The room had an exhaust fan that no one checked for capacity, no combustible gas detector was used, workers relied on smell, and the work went on intermittently for up to two and a half hours. The gas rose above the lower explosive limit and found an ignition source. The same bulletin lists earlier explosions linked to indoor purging at a fitness center, hotels and others, including burned plumbers. These were not exotic jobs. They were routine commissioning work done in an unsafe way.

A related event in February 2010 at a power plant in Connecticut killed six workers. That was a "gas blow," in which high-pressure natural gas is blown through new piping to clear debris, which is a different operation from a purge. OSHA's 2010 memo on it reports about fifty injured and urges non-flammable cleaning methods. It says that if gas must be used, hazards must be controlled by venting vertically above all structures, removing ignition sources, removing nonessential workers and monitoring the atmosphere. Those same controls are a good checklist for any purge.

Purge to the outdoors

The CSB recommendation is plain: wherever practicable, vent purged gas directly to a safe place outdoors, using a temporary hose or pipe or a permanent vent pipe. Only when that cannot be done should indoor purging be considered, and then with nonessential people out, ignition sources controlled, ventilation that keeps the gas concentration well below the lower explosive limit, and gas detectors in use. Our reading for field work: if the only way to purge is into the room, stop and hand the job to the utility or a licensed fitter.

  • Plan the purge point first. Choose the point where the hose will attach and the outdoor spot where it ends before any gas is turned on.
  • Run the hose to open air. Secure the outlet so it cannot whip. Keep it away from doors, windows, air intakes, vents, meters with electrical parts, and anyone standing nearby.
  • Remove ignition sources in the building and at the outlet. That includes pilot flames, running appliances that could ignite gas, power tools, and switching of electrical devices near a leak.
  • Keep people clear. Only the people doing the work stay near the line.
  • Test first. The line should already have passed its pressure test. See threaded gas pipe joints and pressure testing.

Using a combustible gas indicator

The CSB calls for sampling frequently or continuously with a combustible gas detector, placed with attention to where the purge happens, whether the gas is lighter or heavier than air, how it mixes or layers, and how the room is ventilated. In practice an indicator does two jobs. At the outdoor end of the hose, it shows when what comes out has changed from air to gas, so the purge is judged by instrument, not a clock or a nose. Around the building and the work area, it warns if gas is leaking where it should not be. Instruments differ in their scales, some read percent of the lower explosive limit and some percent gas, and many can read off scale in pure gas, so use the maker's instructions and keep the instrument in good calibration and checked before use.

What the model code says about larger piping

The CSB bulletin cites the IFGC and another model fuel gas code and says that for piping beyond specified lengths, set by nominal pipe size, the codes require purging with an inert gas rather than fuel gas. We are not repeating the exact lengths here, because adopted editions and local amendments differ. The code your inspector enforces governs. The practical point for a tech: long or large-diameter piping is not a job for a quick improvised purge.

Our own worked example: how much air is in the pipe

This example is our own, with assumptions stated so you can check it. Take 60 ft of 3/4 inch Schedule 40 black pipe plus 20 ft of 1/2 inch Schedule 40. The Seattle fuel gas code sizing table lists actual inside diameters of 0.824 in for 3/4 inch and 0.622 in for 1/2 inch Schedule 40 pipe.

  1. 3/4 inch area: 0.7854 x 0.824 x 0.824 = 0.5333 square inches. Per foot: 0.5333 x 12 = 6.40 cubic inches. For 60 ft: 6.399 x 60 = 383.9 cubic inches, which is 383.9 / 1728 = 0.2222 cubic feet.
  2. 1/2 inch area: 0.7854 x 0.622 x 0.622 = 0.3039 square inches. Per foot: 0.3039 x 12 = 3.646 cubic inches. For 20 ft: 72.9 cubic inches = 0.0422 cubic feet.
  3. Total: 0.2222 + 0.0422 = 0.2644 cubic feet, about 0.26 cubic feet or roughly 2.0 gallons.

Assumptions: the pipe holds air at close to atmospheric pressure; pipe volume only, with no appliance, valve, regulator or fittings counted; gas pushes air in a clean front with no mixing; purge flow is a steady 10 cubic feet per hour (CFH), a small flow we chose for illustration; and we want to move 5 pipe volumes through, a number we picked, not a code requirement.

  1. Gas to vent: 5 x 0.2644 = 1.32 cubic feet.
  2. Time at 10 CFH: 1.32 / 10 = 0.132 hour, which is 0.132 x 60 = about 7.9 minutes.
  3. At 5 CFH the same job takes about 15.9 minutes. Moving only 3 volumes at 10 CFH would take about 4.8 minutes.

The lesson in the math: the air volume is small, only about 2 gallons, yet a purge can still release over a cubic foot of fuel gas, and a leak or an indoor purge puts it right in the room. Real lines mix at the front and have extra volume in fittings and the appliance, so this is an estimate of order only. Always end the purge by indicator reading at the outlet, never by the timer alone. For how pipe size and length limit flow, see sizing natural gas piping.

When the utility or a licensed fitter must do it

Hand off the purge, or at least stop and ask, in any of these cases: the piping is large or long enough that the code calls for inert-gas purging; there is no safe way to reach open air with a hose; the system is commercial or in an occupied building; propane or natural gas supply controls such as the meter or tank regulator are involved; or the job is outside what you are licensed to do. Who may purge or turn on gas varies by jurisdiction and utility, so check with your local utility and the authority that licenses gas work where you are. The meter and service regulator are the utility side; see gas meter and service regulator basics.

Field card: Purging New Gas Piping

Air out, outdoors only, by instrument.

  • Air in the line can cause failed ignition and lockouts.
  • Methane explodes in air at about 5 to 15 percent; propane about 2.1 to 9.5 percent.
  • Do not vent purge gas into the building. Hose it to open air, away from people and ignition sources.
  • Never trust smell; new pipe can strip odorant.
  • Use a combustible gas indicator at the outlet and around the work.
  • Our example: about 0.26 cubic feet of air in 60 ft of 3/4 inch plus 20 ft of 1/2 inch Schedule 40.
  • Larger piping: the model code requires inert-gas purging beyond set lengths. Follow the adopted code.
  • Unsure or no outdoor path: stop and call the utility or a licensed fitter.
