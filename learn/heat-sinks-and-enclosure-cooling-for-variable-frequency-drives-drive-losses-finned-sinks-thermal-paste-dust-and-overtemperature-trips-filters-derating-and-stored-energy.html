Heat Sinks and Enclosure Cooling for Variable-Frequency Drives

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A variable-frequency drive wastes only a few percent of the power it passes, but in a small cabinet a few percent is a lot of heat. This lesson follows that heat from the power semiconductors to the room, and shows why a dusty heat sink is one of the most common reasons a drive trips on overtemperature.

Where the heat comes from

No drive is perfectly efficient. Switching losses and conduction losses in the power semiconductors, plus smaller losses in the DC bus parts, the chokes and the control board, all end up as heat. A federal energy department tip sheet on drive part-load efficiency gives typical values for pulse-width-modulated drives of about 94 to 97 percent at full load for sizes from 5 to 200 hp. Efficiency falls at light load, and the sheet notes the drop is steeper in smaller drives. It also warns that its values are representative and that there is no widely accepted test method for comparing drives. Put simply, the missing 3 to 6 percent has to go somewhere, and it goes into the drive and its enclosure as heat. The tip sheet does not state heat figures itself; the loss is our arithmetic from its efficiency numbers.

For the basics of what a drive does, see Variable-Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans. Here we only care about the heat.

How a finned heat sink and fan move the heat

The power semiconductors are bolted to a metal heat sink, usually aluminum with many thin fins. Heat conducts from the silicon through the case into the base of the sink, spreads into the fins, and is carried away by air moving across them. University lecture notes on thermal design describe this as a chain of thermal resistances in series, each measured in degrees per watt, so the temperature rise is the power times the total resistance. Notes from another university engineering department add that forced convection with a fan is more efficient than natural convection, and that a sink's rating improves as airflow rises.

Fin spacing is a design compromise. The same lecture notes explain that closely spaced fins add area but can stall the flow, so spacing is chosen for the airflow the fan provides. That is why a drive's fins are fine and narrow, and why they are so sensitive to lint. Fine fins that were tuned for clean air have almost no margin when a mat of dust bridges the gaps.

Thermal paste and contact

Two metal surfaces that look flat are not. The university notes point out that real surfaces have pits, scratches and roughness, and that extruded sink material is never perfectly flat. A thin layer of thermal grease fills those gaps so heat crosses the joint. The same notes warn that more is not better: a thick layer of viscous grease can stay thick and add resistance, so apply it sparingly and wipe off the excess. A filled grease conducts much better than a plain silicone one. If a power block has been removed, the mounting screws must be tightened evenly and in the maker's sequence, and the maker's thermal material and torque must be used. Do not guess, because a poor joint can overheat the semiconductor while the sink itself still feels only warm.

Dust, lint and blocked fins

Dust acts as insulation on the fins and as a plug in the gaps. Airflow falls, the sink runs hotter, and the drive protects itself by tripping on overtemperature or by lowering its switching frequency to cut losses. A drive specification we read from one university facilities office says its drive control lowers the carrier frequency based on the drive temperature, which is a typical protective behavior. The result in the field is a drive that faults on hot afternoons or at high speed, then resets when it cools, which looks like an intermittent problem until someone opens the cabinet.

Common causes are a clogged intake filter, a fan that has slowed or stopped, a filter mat that has been installed backward or doubled, lint from textile or paper operations, and an enclosure door left open or a panel missing so air short-circuits around the heat sink. Our own advice: after any overtemperature trip, check the fan spin, the filters and the fins before touching settings.

Enclosure intake and exhaust filters

Enclosures with fans typically draw room air in low through a filter and exhaust it high, as in the figure. Keep the intake and exhaust clear of boxes and wall clearance clear, replace or clean the filters on a schedule that matches the dust in the room, and never run the cabinet with the filter removed, because dust then goes straight to the fins. Filters that are plugged hurt the same way as dirty fins. Follow the drive maker's manual for clearances and filter service, because the allowed clearances above and below the drive are model specific.

Ambient limits and derating in general terms

Drives are rated for a range of surrounding air temperature. The university specification we opened lists full-load operation from 5 to 104 F, with operation up to 122 F allowed only with a 10 percent reduction in full-load current, and it lists a similar reduction at higher altitude. Treat those as an example of how derating works, not as a rule: every maker publishes its own curves. The practical point is simple. Hotter surrounding air, thin air at altitude, a clogged filter or a high switching frequency all leave less margin, so the drive carries less current before it trips. Read the nameplate and the maker's manual for the limits of the drive in front of you.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, with assumed numbers. Assume a drive carrying 15 kW of output at 97 percent efficiency inside a cabinet cooled by a fan, and a design rise of 15 F between the air entering and leaving the enclosure. We use the standard sensible-heat relation for air near sea level, cfm = Btu/h divided by (1.08 times the temperature difference).

  1. Loss fraction: 100 percent minus 97 percent = 3 percent.
  2. Heat: 15 kW × 0.03 = 0.45 kW = 450 W.
  3. Convert: 450 W × 3.412 Btu/h per watt = about 1,535 Btu/h.
  4. Airflow: cfm = 1,535 / (1.08 × 15) = 1,535 / 16.2 = about 95 cfm.

So the cabinet fan must actually deliver about 95 cfm through the filters, not just be rated for it. Now suppose clogged filters cut the real flow to half, about 47 cfm. Rearranging gives a temperature rise of 1,535 / (1.08 × 47) = about 30 F, double the design rise. If the room is already warm, the air at the drive can approach its limit, and the drive trips. Note too that the 3 percent figure is an assumption; the tip sheet range of 94 to 97 percent means the real loss could be up to 6 percent, which would double the airflow need.

Lockout and stored energy before cleaning

Cleaning a drive means putting your hands near live parts that can hold energy after the supply is off. The federal workplace safety agency says the unexpected startup or release of stored energy during servicing can seriously injure or kill workers. A university safety office notes that capacitors hold a charge for some time after power is turned off, that a bleeder resistor should not be assumed to be working until it is tested, and that you should confirm with a suitable voltmeter that no residual charge remains. For a drive, that means: open and lock out the supply per Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead, wait the time on the drive label, then measure the DC bus voltage at the drive's test points with a meter rated for the job before you clean or reach in. Use a vacuum or low-pressure dry air only as the maker allows, and keep fins and the circuit boards dry and undamaged. Never use water. If you do not know how to verify, stop and get a qualified electrician.

Field card: Heat Sinks and Enclosure Cooling for Drives

Few percent of the power becomes heat; dust takes the margin.

  • Typical drive efficiency is about 94 to 97 percent at full load, so 3 to 6 percent becomes heat.
  • Heat in Btu/h = watts lost × 3.412.
  • Enclosure airflow: cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 × temperature rise in F).
  • Our example: 450 W = about 1,535 Btu/h = about 95 cfm at a 15 F rise.
  • Clogged filters or fins cut flow and raise the rise; overtemperature trips follow.
  • Thermal paste: thin, even, wiped clean; follow the maker's mounting steps.
  • Lock out, wait, then measure the DC bus before cleaning.
