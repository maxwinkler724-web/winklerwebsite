Heat Pumps and Air Conditioners at High Altitude: Thin Air, Capacity per CFM, Strip Heat Rise and Gauge Readings

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A heat pump or air conditioner that works well near sea level does less in thin mountain air, and the loss shows up in places a tech might not expect: capacity per cfm, strip heater temperature rise, motor load and what the gauges read. This lesson walks through each one, then works a full example with our own assumed numbers.

What thin air changes

A blower moves volume. Cubic feet per minute is the same whether the air is thick or thin, but the pounds of air inside each cubic foot are not. The relation is plain: at the same temperature, air density falls in step with pressure. A federal aerospace page gives an atmosphere model that fits the lower atmosphere with straight-line temperature and an exponential pressure curve, and a fan maker's engineering bulletin lists a density factor of 1.20 for 5,000 feet. That means the thin air is about 1/1.20, or 83 percent, as dense as sea-level air at the same temperature. Heat moves by mass, so the same cfm now carries about 17 percent less heat for the same temperature change. Our companion lesson on where the 1.08 comes from builds the factor from density, time and specific heat; here we apply it to heat pumps.

Sensible capacity per cfm

The sensible heat formula, Btu/h = 1.08 × cfm × ΔT, assumes air at sea-level density. At altitude, replace 1.08 with 1.08 times the density ratio. With a coil that is otherwise unchanged and the same cfm, each degree of temperature split moves fewer Btu. So either the split grows, or the capacity falls, or both. A heat pump maker's engineering bulletin for ductless units publishes one capacity correction for heating and cooling, from 1.00 at sea level down to 0.83 at 5,000 feet and 0.69 at 10,000 feet, and states that the reduction is due to decreased air density. A university paper on heat pumps for high homes estimates a loss of about 3 to 4 percent of heating capacity for each 1,000 feet, which is the same size as that table. Treat the paper figure as one study's estimate, not a rating.

Condenser and outdoor coil

The outdoor fan also moves the same cfm of thinner air. Less air mass crosses the coil, so the coil rejects heat (in cooling) or absorbs it (in heating) less effectively for the same refrigerant temperatures. In cooling, our reasoning is that the condensing temperature has to climb to push the same heat out, which is the reason makers publish a derate instead of letting the unit simply run harder. We did not find a source that quantifies the condenser share by itself, so we do not put a number on it. For the work behind coil resistances, see the lesson on air-side and refrigerant-side heat transfer.

Fan motor load drops

A fan at fixed speed moves the same cfm, but its static pressure and brake horsepower scale with density. The fan engineering bulletin says that for altitude, standard-air values are divided by the altitude factor: its example at 5,500 feet (factor 1.22) turns 3.05 inches of static pressure into 2.5 and 5.28 brake horsepower into 4.33. So a motor on a thin-air job draws less power for the same speed. Do not read a lower amp draw as proof of a healthy system; it is partly just thin air. A constant-airflow blower will lower its own power the same way. See constant torque vs constant airflow ECM blowers.

Heat strips and temperature rise

An electric strip heater turns every kilowatt into 3,412 Btu/h (our unit conversion), whatever the altitude. The heat goes into less air mass, so supply air runs hotter than at sea level for the same cfm. Strip kits and furnaces carry limits on supply temperature rise on the data plate. If the rise at altitude comes out near that limit, say so to the maker before changing staging or airflow. Never bypass a limit control to fix a rise problem. The related lesson on heat strip kW, breakers and staging covers sizing and wiring.

Gauge readings: psig vs psia and boiling points

A gauge reads the difference between the pressure inside and the local air, and a university engineering page defines it that way: gauge pressure is absolute pressure minus local atmospheric pressure, and it reads zero in open air at any elevation. Refrigerant boiling temperature depends on absolute pressure. At sea level the standard value is 14.7 psia. At 5,000 feet our calculation from the federal atmosphere equations gives about 12.2 psia, a drop of about 2.5 psi. So a gauge at 5,000 feet reads about 2.5 psi lower than at sea level for the same absolute pressure and the same saturation temperature. Pressure-temperature charts printed in psig assume sea-level air, so a reading near a target should be corrected, or you can lean on temperature-based checks (subcooling and superheat) that the maker prints for the unit. The same physics lowers the boiling point of water: a federal food-safety page says water boils at 212 degrees F at sea level and that each 500 feet of elevation lowers it by just under 1 degree F, citing 208 degrees F at 2,000 feet and about 198 degrees F at 7,500 feet. By our interpolation, boiling is near 203 degrees F at 5,000 feet. For gauge pointers on boilers, see the lesson on boiler gauges and altitude pointers.

Defrost and dry cold air

A national laboratory literature review says frost forms when the coil surface is below the dew point of the air and that ambient humidity greatly affects how much frost builds. It says frost raises thermal resistance and air pressure drop, and that reverse-cycle defrost sends hot gas to the outdoor coil to melt it. It does not mention altitude. Our reasoning: thin, cold mountain air is often dry, so some days frost less, but snow, fog and wet spells still frost the coil. Defrost timing and termination come from the maker's control; do not assume a sea-level setting is right. See the defrost cycle and auxiliary heat and balance point and low-temperature capacity.

Worked example (our own)

Assumptions, all ours: a home at 5,000 feet elevation; 1,200 cfm of 70 degree F return air; a 20 degree F temperature split across the coil; a 10 kW strip heater; the blower holds 1,200 cfm at both elevations.

  1. Atmosphere (federal equations). Temperature T = 59 − 0.00356 × h gives 59 degrees F at sea level and 41.2 degrees F at 5,000 feet. Pressure p = 2116 × [(T + 459.7) / 518.6]5.256 lb/ft² gives about 2,118 at sea level (14.7 psia) and 1,763 at 5,000 feet (12.2 psia). Density from the same page, p / [1718 × (T + 459.7)], then times 32.174 to convert slugs to pounds, gives 0.0765 lb/ft³ at sea level and 0.0659 at 5,000 feet.
  2. Density ratio at the same air temperature. The 1.08 is built for indoor air near 70 degrees F, so compare at the same temperature; the ratio is then the pressure ratio: 1,763 ÷ 2,118 = 0.832. This matches the fan bulletin (1 ÷ 1.20 = 0.833).
  3. Corrected factor. 1.08 × 0.832 = 0.899, about 0.90.
  4. Sensible capacity. Sea level: 1.08 × 1,200 × 20 = 25,920 Btu/h. At 5,000 feet with the same 20 degree split: 0.899 × 1,200 × 20 = 21,574 Btu/h, about 17 percent lower. (The coil would need a 24 degree split to move the sea-level amount, which a real coil may not give.)
  5. Strip heater rise. 10 kW × 3,412 = 34,120 Btu/h. Sea level: 34,120 ÷ (1.08 × 1,200) = 34,120 ÷ 1,296 = 26.3 degrees F. At 5,000 feet: 34,120 ÷ (0.899 × 1,200) = 34,120 ÷ 1,079 = 31.6 degrees F, about 5.3 degrees F more.
  6. Gauge. A sea-level reading of 250 psig is 264.7 psia. At 5,000 feet the same absolute pressure reads 264.7 − 12.2 = 252.5 psig, so 2.5 psi higher on the gauge face is the same saturation temperature. Our check: 70 psig at sea level (84.7 psia) reads 72.5 psig at altitude.

What this means for homeowners and techs

A homeowner who moves from a low area to a mountain area should expect that a unit sized at sea level has less cooling and heating capacity per cfm, that warm air from strips will feel hotter, and that the unit may need a different size choice. Ask the installer which altitude correction the maker publishes for that model. Techs reading performance data should check the elevation used in the data, apply the maker's correction, and treat gauge readings and amp draws with the density shift in mind. For gas heat at altitude, see derating gas furnaces, boilers and water heaters.

Field card: Heat Pumps and ACs at High Altitude

Same cfm, less air mass, less heat per cfm.

  • Density ratio at 5,000 feet is about 0.83, so 1.08 becomes about 0.90 (our calculation).
  • Sensible capacity at the same cfm and split falls about 17 percent in our example.
  • Strip heater rise goes up: 26.3 to 31.6 degrees F for 10 kW at 1,200 cfm in our example.
  • Fan power drops with density at fixed speed; do not read low amps as healthy.
  • Gauge pressure is absolute minus local air: psig reads lower at altitude for the same saturation temperature.
  • Use the maker's published altitude correction and defrost settings.
