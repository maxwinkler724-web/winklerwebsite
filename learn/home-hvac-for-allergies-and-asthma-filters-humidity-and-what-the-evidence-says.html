Home HVAC for Allergies and Asthma: Filters, Humidity, and What the Evidence Says

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you search for the best HVAC filter for allergies, you will find a lot of confident promises. This lesson sticks to what federal agencies and a few other public sources actually say: what a higher-MERV filter can do, what it cannot do, and which other steps matter as much. It is general education, not medical advice.

Not medical advice. Allergies and asthma are medical conditions. Nothing here diagnoses or treats anything. A doctor decides what is right for you or your child.

What triggers allergies and asthma indoors

The EPA's asthma trigger page names the usual indoor suspects. Dust mites are too small to see and live in mattresses, pillows, carpets and upholstered furniture; their body parts and droppings can trigger asthma in people allergic to them. Proteins in a pet's skin flakes, urine, saliva and hair can trigger asthma. Inhaled mold spores can trigger an attack in people sensitive to mold. Pollen is mostly an outdoor problem, and the EPA suggests keeping windows closed on high-pollen days. The CDC's asthma page lists the same family of triggers and adds that mold can set off an attack whether or not you are allergic to it.

Notice that most of those sources sit in soft furnishings, on pets, or in damp spots. A furnace filter only deals with the air that moves through it, so it is one tool among several.

What a higher-MERV filter can do

The EPA says MERV ratings describe how well a filter captures particles from 0.3 to 10 microns, and that for all rating methods a higher rating means higher removal for most particle sizes. For a whole home it recommends a filter rated at least MERV 13, or as high as your system will accommodate, and says most systems can take MERV 13 if the filter is changed often. A technician can confirm what your equipment allows. Advice from the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (ACAAI) is similar: a disposable filter rated about MERV 11 to 13 is the cost-efficient choice, and cheap fiberglass furnace filters are ineffective. For schedules and sizing, see How Often Should I Change My Air Filter? MERV Basics.

What it cannot do

  • It works only while the air moves. The EPA says furnace and HVAC filters filter air only when the system is operating, and that systems usually run less than 25% of the time during heating and cooling seasons.
  • It cannot remove everything. The EPA states that no air cleaner or filter will eliminate all pollutants in your home. Our own view: allergen that has already settled into a mattress or carpet is not in the airstream, so a filter does not reach it. That is why the EPA and CDC still list washing bedding, covers and vacuuming. (ACAAI adds that vacuuming stirs dust up for a couple of hours before it settles again.)
  • It is not a moisture fix. Filters do not stop mold or mites from growing; humidity does that.

Running the fan for more filtering

Because the filter only works when air flows, some people run the blower continuously. The EPA notes that more filtration needs longer run time, which raises electricity cost and may reduce humidity control in cooling season. Fan power draw differs a lot by blower type, so we give no dollar figure here. Look at your equipment's label or ask a technician before choosing a continuous-fan setting.

Humidity: the lever people miss

The EPA's mold guidance says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and that the key to mold control is moisture control. The CDC gives 30 to 50 percent for dust mites and under 50 percent for mold. A University of Kentucky entomology publication says dust mites cannot survive well below 50 percent relative humidity, and that a dehumidifier and better ventilation can help. A hygrometer costs roughly 0 to 0 according to the EPA. If your basement or whole house runs damp, see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage. The EPA also says to keep air conditioner drip pans clean and drain lines flowing.

Ozone-making devices: avoid

The EPA advises avoiding air cleaners and furnace filters that intentionally make ozone. Ozone is a lung irritant that can cause chest pain, coughing and shortness of breath, and may worsen asthma. The EPA says ozone has little potential to remove indoor contaminants at safe levels, does not remove particles like dust or pollen, and that no federal agency has approved these devices for occupied spaces. Ionizers and some electrostatic or plasma devices can emit ozone too. ACAAI also warns ionic cleaners can backfire. More in Air Purifiers and Whole-House Air Cleaners.

Duct cleaning

The EPA does not recommend routine duct cleaning and says it has not been shown to prevent health problems. The University of Kentucky page also calls duct cleaning secondary for mites. See Should You Get Your Air Ducts Cleaned? for when the EPA says it does make sense.

Worked example: MERV 8 versus MERV 13

The EPA's MERV table lists minimum average capture by particle size band under the standard test (ASHRAE 52.2). Bands: E1 is 0.3 to 1.0 micron, E2 is 1.0 to 3.0, E3 is 3.0 to 10.

EPA minimum average efficiency
FilterE1 (0.3-1.0)E2 (1.0-3.0)E3 (3.0-10)
MERV 8not specifiedat least 20%at least 70%
MERV 13at least 50%at least 85%at least 90%

Suppose 1,000 particles in each band reach the filter in one pass.

  1. E2 band, MERV 8: 1,000 x 20% = 200 caught, so up to 800 pass through.
  2. E2 band, MERV 13: 1,000 x 85% = 850 caught, so up to 150 pass through.
  3. Ratio: 800 / 150 = about 5.3, so MERV 13 passes roughly one fifth as many 1 to 3 micron particles at the minimums.
  4. E3 band, MERV 8: 1,000 x 70% = 700 caught, 300 pass. MERV 13: 900 caught, 100 pass. Ratio: 300 / 100 = 3.
  5. E1 band: MERV 13 catches at least 500 of 1,000, so up to 500 pass. The EPA table gives no MERV 8 figure here.

These are minimums for one pass of air, not a whole-house result. Real filters can beat their minimums. And because the system runs under 25% of the time, even a MERV 13 filter sees only a fraction of the day's air. At 25%, that is 6 of 24 hours.

When to talk to a doctor

The EPA says to work with a doctor to identify triggers and build a treatment plan. ACAAI says that if you have questions about how air filters affect your symptoms, it is time to speak with an allergist. Do not change medication or skip treatment because you bought a better filter.

Field card: Home HVAC for Allergies and Asthma

Filter the air, control the moisture, clean the surfaces.

  • EPA: choose MERV 13 or the highest your system accommodates; confirm with a technician.
  • Filters work only while the fan runs; systems often run under 25% of the time.
  • Humidity: below 60%, ideally 30 to 50% (EPA); under 50% for mites (CDC, Kentucky).
  • Avoid ozone-making cleaners and filters.
  • Routine duct cleaning is not recommended by EPA.
  • Questions about symptoms go to a doctor or allergist.
