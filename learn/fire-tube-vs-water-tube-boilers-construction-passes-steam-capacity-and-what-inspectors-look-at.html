Fire-Tube vs Water-Tube Boilers: Construction, Passes and What Inspectors Check

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Both kinds of boiler make steam by putting fire next to water. The difference is which one is inside the tubes. That one choice shapes how the boiler is built, how it behaves when the load jumps, and where an inspector spends the most time. This lesson uses maker and government literature to lay it out.

The one-sentence difference

The American Boiler Manufacturers Association paper "Boiler Types for Steam Applications" (ABMA) puts it simply: in a fire-tube boiler the combustion gases run inside the tubes, and in a water-tube boiler the water and steam run inside the tubes, which connect drums and are bent into furnace and convection sections. Everything else on this page follows from that.

Fire-tube construction: passes and the back end

A pass is one trip of the hot gas through a bank of tubes. The ABMA paper says Scotch marine units run from one pass up to four, with two and three passes common, and that turnarounds at the ends of the boiler create the passes. Cleaver-Brooks lists its CBJT fire-tube line as offered in 2-, 3- or 4-pass designs. Hurst's catalog sorts its Scotch marine boilers the same way, as two-pass, three-pass and four-pass series, some dry back and some wet back.

The back end is where wet back and dry back differ. ABMA describes a wet back turnaround as a water-cooled chamber inside the vessel that needs little routine maintenance but is hard to inspect and repair. A dry back turnaround is a separate refractory-lined chamber on the back of the vessel; access is easy, but gaskets and refractory need more frequent upkeep. Hurst's wet back literature makes the matching claim from the maker side: the only rear refractory is a manway plug for looking into the furnace, and it contrasts this with dry back designs it describes as subject to deteriorating rear refractory, leaking baffles and leaking door seals. Treat the maker comparison as a sales position and the ABMA wording as the neutral one.

Two sizing numbers show up in maker pages. Hurst cites 5 square feet of heating surface per boiler horsepower for its wet back designs. ABMA says Scotch marine fire-tube boilers are usually limited to about 300 psi (its table lists 350 psi) and now exceed 100 MMBtu/h, or about 2,500 horsepower. Maker pages we read give ranges such as Hurst's 30 to 1,500 BHP on one series and up to 2,500 BHP on another.

Water-tube construction: D, A, O and bent tube

Babcock & Wilcox describes its package water-tube layouts with a steam drum above a lower drum. In a D-type, the drums sit over each other with the furnace offset to one side and one generating bank. In an O-type, the furnace sits in the center with two generating banks. B&W says all of its package boilers use membrane (gas-tight) wall construction, which it says removes the need for furnace refractory. ABMA adds that industrial water-tube units are usually the D style, that A and O styles can be packaged in larger capacities and reach very high pressures and superheat, and that bent-tube boilers form their gas passes with bent tube shapes. We did not find A-type details in the maker pages we read, so we leave that layout at ABMA's one-line mention.

Where each fits, as the sources state it

  • Pressure and size. ABMA says packaged industrial water-tube units typically reach about 300,000 lb/h of steam. B&W lists package boilers from 8,200 to 1,200,000 lb/h with design pressures of 250 to 2,400 psig, and steam temperatures up to 1,005 degrees F on its largest model.
  • Steam quality. ABMA says the large shell of a fire-tube boiler gives a big steam space that delivers good steam quality without mechanical separation, while a small water-tube steam drum often needs mechanical separation, more so at lower pressures. B&W says its drum internals give low moisture and high steam purity.
  • Load swings. ABMA says the large water and steam content of a fire-tube boiler keeps pressure and level steady under fluctuating loads, but that multi-pass units are prone to thermal shock and warm up over several hours. It says industrial water-tube units, with small water content, show load changes as quick pressure swings and may need two- or three-element level control. B&W says its larger furnace-wall tubes give faster load response. ABMA adds that bent-tube units avoid thermal shock and can reach full input in minutes.
  • Footprint. ABMA calls Scotch marine units large and industrial water-tube furnaces larger, with bent-tube and coil-tube units compact.

These are the sources' statements, not a ranking. Which boiler fits a building depends on the load, pressure and code requirements, and that decision belongs to the engineer of record and the authority having jurisdiction.

Boiler horsepower, defined

A boiler horsepower (BHP) is a capacity unit, not engine power. The ABMA white paper "Boiler Horsepower: History of Definitions in the Firetube Boiler Industry" gives the current definition as equivalent evaporation of 34.5 pounds of water per hour from and at 212 degrees F, which it equates to 33,475 Btu/h. "From and at 212 F" means feedwater already at 212 F is turned into steam at 212 F. The same paper traces the term to steam-engine ratings from the 1870s and says ASME standardized an older version in 1889.

Worked example: a 300 BHP fire-tube boiler

The 82 percent efficiency below is an example, not a nameplate value. All arithmetic is ours.

  1. Output in Btu/h. Our own: 300 BHP x 33,475 Btu/h per BHP = 10,042,500 Btu/h, or 10,042.5 MBH.
  2. Steam in lb/h, from and at 212 F. Our own: 300 BHP x 34.5 lb/h per BHP = 10,350 lb/h.
  3. Fuel input at 82 percent (example). Our own: 10,042.5 MBH / 0.82 = about 12,247 MBH input.
  4. Heating surface check. Our own: at the 5 sq ft per BHP Hurst cites, 300 x 5 = 1,500 sq ft.
  5. Sanity check against a maker table. Hurst lists 30 to 1,500 BHP as 1,004 to 50,213 MBTU/HR. Our own: 30 x 33.475 = 1,004 and 1,500 x 33.475 = 50,213, so our factor matches the maker's table.

Real steam output depends on feedwater temperature and operating pressure, so the from-and-at figure is a rating, not what the boiler makes on a given day.

What inspectors and operators look at

The most detailed public list we found is by Luis Ponce of the National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors, based on the National Board Inspection Code, in Today's Boiler. It is written for water-tube power boilers, so we use it for that boiler type and note where an item is general.

  • Tubes. Exteriors for corrosion, warping or sagging, cracking, pitting and scaling, plus tube welds. Erosion from ash and soot blowers is a main concern. Overheating can show as blisters, bulges or tubes touching.
  • Drums and joints. Tube-to-drum joints and the ligaments between tube holes for cracks, leaks, corrosion and pitting.
  • Refractory. It should be intact, not broken, unfastened or damaged, with clips and fasteners working.
  • Low-water cutoff. Piping free of scale that could stop it working, float chambers free of sludge, and a slow response during testing may mean an obstruction. See Low-Water Cutoff Testing and Maintenance.
  • Manholes, handholes and gaskets. Clean seating areas and gasket surfaces with no steam cuts.

We did not find a public National Board page that spells out the equivalent list for fire-tube boilers or lists low-water damage as its own item, so we do not claim one. On the maker side, Hurst says its wet back boilers let one technician remove rear cover plates to reach the tubes, which ties back to the ABMA point that dry back access is easy and wet back access is harder.

Water side and fire side upkeep

The Department of Energy's Federal Energy Management Program says to inspect both the water side and the fire side of the boiler regularly, clean tube surfaces where needed, check water chemistry as part of a treatment program, and complete a boiler tuning program at least once per operating year. The DOE Steam Tip Sheet 7 on waterside surfaces adds that even thin scale insulates and slows heat transfer, that scale can raise fuel use by as much as 5 percent in fire-tube boilers, that scale shows up as higher flue gas temperature at a steady firing rate, and that tubes should be visually inspected when the unit is shut down. Water treatment is covered in Boiler Water Treatment and Blowdown Basics.

Field card: Fire-Tube vs Water-Tube Boilers

Fire-tube: fire in the tubes. Water-tube: water in the tubes.

  • A pass is one trip of hot gas through a tube bank; Scotch marine units run 1 to 4 passes.
  • Wet back: water-cooled turnaround, little upkeep, harder to inspect. Dry back: refractory chamber, easy access, more gasket and refractory upkeep (ABMA).
  • 1 BHP = 34.5 lb/h from and at 212 F = 33,475 Btu/h (ABMA).
  • Our own: 300 BHP = 10,042,500 Btu/h = 10,350 lb/h; at an example 82 percent, about 12,247 MBH input.
  • Inspect both water side and fire side; thin scale can raise fire-tube fuel use up to 5 percent (DOE).
