Refrigerant Safety Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the EPA 608 Guide

Refrigerants are useful because they change easily between liquid and gas, and that same behavior creates hazards. This lesson covers the main general safety ideas for anyone who works around refrigerant.

Start with the rules that apply to you

Everything here is general. Your equipment manufacturer's instructions, the safety data sheet for the specific refrigerant, and your employer's safety program always come first. If anything on this page differs from them, follow them instead.

Protective gear

Two basics come up again and again. Eye protection matters because liquid refrigerant sprays and can injure eyes in an instant. Gloves matter because refrigerant and cold metal surfaces can injure skin. Your workplace may require more, such as long sleeves, protective footwear or a face shield. Ask what your site requires, and make sure gear fits and is in good condition.

Frostbite from liquid refrigerant

When liquid refrigerant escapes, it boils and absorbs a lot of heat in a very short time. On skin, that can freeze tissue almost immediately. The injury looks like a burn or frostbite. Keep your face and hands out of the path of any release, open connections slowly, and never point a hose or fitting at yourself or another person. If skin is exposed to liquid refrigerant, follow your site's first aid procedure and seek medical advice.

Breathing hazards in enclosed spaces

Most refrigerant vapors are heavier than air. In a small room, pit or low area, a leak can push out the air you need to breathe. This is called asphyxiation, and it can happen without any strong smell or warning. Some refrigerants also have toxic effects at higher concentrations. Know where the exits and ventilation controls are, never enter a space where you suspect a leak, and treat confined spaces as serious places that may need permits and monitoring. OSHA has general information on confined spaces linked below.

Cylinders: never heat, never overfill

  • Do not heat a cylinder with a flame or an uncontrolled heat source. Heat raises pressure, and a container that is too hot or too full can rupture.
  • Do not overfill. Liquid refrigerant expands as it warms. Cylinders need empty space (called headspace) so that expansion has somewhere to go. Fill limits are set by rules and by the cylinder's markings, and a scale is the usual way to track what goes in.
  • Use the right container. Do not refill a disposable cylinder. Do not mix different refrigerants in the same cylinder.
  • Secure cylinders upright where required, protect valves, and keep them out of hot vehicles and direct sun.
  • Check labels and test dates. A damaged or out-of-date cylinder should be taken out of use.

Cylinder color codes

Many refrigerant cylinders follow a color convention so people can tell them apart at a glance. A few older examples: light green for R-22, light blue for R-134a, rose or pink for R-410A. AHRI's guideline now recommends a single light green-gray for all new refrigerant containers, so do not count on these colors. Recovery cylinders are commonly gray with yellow on the top or collar. Treat color as a hint, never as proof. Colors can fade, be painted over, or differ by manufacturer. Always read the label before you connect anything.

Flammable refrigerants: A2L and A3

Refrigerants are given safety classifications that combine toxicity and flammability. A common system uses a letter for toxicity and a number for flammability. Here are the groups you will hear most about:

  • A1: lower toxicity, no flame spread under test conditions.
  • A2L: lower toxicity and mildly flammable. They are hard to ignite and burn slowly, but they can burn.
  • A3: lower toxicity and highly flammable. Hydrocarbons such as propane fall here and are used in small, sealed equipment.

The practical lessons are the same ones every time: control ignition sources, ventilate the work area, and use tools and recovery equipment that are rated for the refrigerant being handled. Many newer products use A2L refrigerants, so expect to see them more. Training and manufacturer instructions for these products matter even more than for older ones.

Ventilation and leak awareness

Good airflow reduces risk. Open the area up where possible, know how any mechanical ventilation or refrigerant monitor works, and leave and report if an alarm sounds. Never silence a safety device just to keep working.

Do not vent

Deliberately releasing refrigerant to make a job quicker is not only a safety problem, it is prohibited in most cases. Recover refrigerant with proper equipment instead. The next lesson on recovery, recycling and reclaiming explains how.

Heat and open flame

Refrigerants can break down into harmful substances when they meet very hot surfaces or flame. This is one more reason to recover refrigerant before brazing or welding, and to follow your site's hot-work rules.

Field card: Refrigerant Safety Basics

Handle refrigerant like the hazard it is: eyes, skin, air and cylinders.

  • Wear eye protection and gloves; liquid refrigerant can cause frostbite.
  • Refrigerant can displace air in enclosed spaces. Ventilate.
  • Never heat a cylinder or fill past its limit.
  • Check the cylinder label and color code before use.
  • Flammable (A2L/A3) refrigerants need ignition-source control. Follow the manufacturer.
