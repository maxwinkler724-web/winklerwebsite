Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

Oxygen in boiler feedwater eats steel from the inside. The deaerator is the plant's main defense, and it works with ordinary physics: hot water holds less gas.

The problem

Dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide corrode piping and boilers. Carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, and oxygen causes pitting. A deaerator removes most of both so the chemical program only has to mop up the rest. For where the feedwater comes from, see Condensate Return and Feedwater Basics.

The physics

Gases dissolve less well as water heats toward boiling. A water-treatment company explains the effect with an example: oxygen falling from about 5 ppm at 150 F to about 2 ppm at 190 F in water, and nearly all free carbon dioxide gone above about 170 F. Those are the article's example figures. A deaerator pushes much further, heating the water close to boiling with steam so the gases come out and can be vented.

Spray and tray designs

A general reference describes two layouts.

  • Tray type. A vertical dome sits on a horizontal storage tank. Water sprays onto perforated trays and cascades down while steam rises up through the holes. Long contact gives high performance. Some use packed beds instead.
  • Spray type. In a horizontal vessel, a baffle separates a preheater zone from a deaerating zone. Water is sprayed in, heated by steam from a sparger, then deaerated in the next section.

The water-treatment article lists related names (spray-scrubber, spray-tray, atomizing) with the same idea: spray into a steam atmosphere, then scrub and heat further as the water falls toward the storage section.

How good is good?

The same article distinguishes a true deaerator, which can reduce oxygen to about 7 parts per billion or less, from a deaerating heater designed to reach under about 40 ppb. The Bryan manual and the general reference also quote about 7 ppb as the design goal. These are design goals, and your unit's data sheet is the source for your equipment.

Typical operating range

A spray-type deaerator manual from Bryan Boilers says steam holds the unit at 5 to 15 psig, regulated by a pressure-reducing valve, and its vessel is built for a maximum of 50 psig with a relief valve to match. The water-treatment bulletin from Chemaqua gives saturation temperatures of about 227 F at 5 psig and about 250 F at 15 psig, and says outlet water should come within roughly 1 to 4 F of saturation at the operating pressure when steam and water mix well. The Bryan manual also routes returns by temperature: cooler condensate (below about 230 F) goes to the deaerating inlet, and hotter condensate goes straight to storage. Check your own unit's nameplate and manual, because pressures and layouts differ.

Chemical scavengers finish the job

Trace oxygen remains, so plants add an oxygen scavenger. The general reference says sodium sulfite is common in lower-pressure systems and forms a non-scaling sulfate, while other chemicals are used in higher-pressure or special programs. Your treatment company chooses, and the boiler water tests then confirm the residual. See Boiler Water Tests.

What operators watch

  • The vent. Both sources stress that gas must have somewhere to go. A general reference says a vent valve that is too closed gives poor performance and sends high-oxygen water to the boiler, and a small visible steam plume at the vent suggests venting is working. It adds that without an oxygen analyzer, high boiler chloride may hint that the vent is not open enough. The Bryan manual describes throttling the vent until a steam plume is visible and checking the plume daily; its vent valve has a small drilled orifice. The exact setting comes from your deaerator's maker.
  • Steam supply. The unit relies on steam for heating and scrubbing. A starved supply means a cooler tank and less gas removal.
  • Makeup water location. The water-treatment article warns that cold makeup mixing with hot condensate releases oxygen, which can cause pitting and fast failure of downstream piping unless it is stainless steel. It says ideally makeup water goes directly to the deaerator, with cold makeup and hot condensate entering the deaerating section on separate lines.
  • Tank temperature. Low-pressure steam keeps the stored feedwater warm in an insulated tank.
  • Level control. The tank is a buffer. Sudden swings of level mean feed or condensate changes upstream.

Where oxygen sneaks in

Check these first when oxygen or scavenger readings change:

  • A condensate receiver open to the air.
  • Cold makeup poured in the wrong place.
  • A failed vent or float.
  • Leaks on pump suction that draw in air. This is general plant knowledge and is not from the fetched pages.

Federal note on wasted steam

A federal steam trap review lists, among losses from failed traps, steam that bypasses the traps and is vented at the deaerator, adding makeup water and treatment cost. So a busy deaerator vent can be a clue about traps. See Steam Trap Testing.

Safety

Deaerators operate hot and sometimes under pressure. Do not open vents, manways or safety valves except by procedure. Relief devices exist for a reason, and many jurisdictions limit who may work on pressure vessels. Follow the maker's manual and your site's rules.

Field card: Feedwater Deaerators and Oxygen Basics

Hot water holds less gas, so heat it, scrub it and vent it.

  • Oxygen causes pitting and carbon dioxide forms acid, so both must go.
  • Heating feedwater with steam drives gases out; the vent lets them leave.
  • Cold makeup mixing with hot condensate can release oxygen in the wrong place.
  • Scavenger chemical handles the last traces; tests confirm the residual.
  • Do not adjust vents or open vessels except by procedure.
