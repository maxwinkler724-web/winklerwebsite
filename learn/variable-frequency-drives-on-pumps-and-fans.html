Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans

A pump or fan that always runs flat out and then gets choked down with a valve or damper wastes energy. A variable frequency drive, or VFD, lets the motor simply run slower when less flow is needed.

What a VFD does

An AC induction motor turns at a speed set mostly by the frequency of the power feeding it. A VFD takes incoming power and produces output at an adjustable frequency, so the motor speed can follow a command from a controller or building system. The drive also gives soft starts and has its own protections and fault codes. It is an electronic device with high voltage inside, so lockout/tagout and the manufacturer's discharge-wait instructions apply before opening one.

Why slowing down saves so much

For centrifugal pumps and fans, the affinity laws describe the relationships. Flow changes in step with speed, pressure or head changes with the square of speed, and power changes with the cube of speed. The Department of Energy motor tip sheet gives a labeled example: cutting speed and flow by 20 percent can cut input power by about 50 percent. In its fan example, a half-speed fan needs about one eighth of the full-speed power before drive and motor losses, and about 83 percent less after including them.

Compare that with throttling. A balancing or control valve leaves the pump running at full speed against added resistance, which wastes energy and can add stress on seals and bearings. A drive moves the pump to a different point on its curve instead.

Where the savings stop

The cube law is clean only for loads with no static lift, where the system resistance is all friction. A pump that must lift water to a height, or a loop that needs minimum pressure to reach the farthest coil, cannot slow down forever. Real systems have a minimum speed set by the manufacturer for motor cooling, lubrication and pump or fan stability, so keep the limits the maker specifies. Drive efficiency also falls at very light loads, and the tip sheet notes that motor efficiency can drop with the nonsinusoidal waveform a drive produces. Where long cable runs or older motors are involved, ask the drive and motor makers about inverter-duty motors and filters rather than assuming.

Typical HVAC uses

On hydronic systems, a drive can hold a differential pressure while two-way valves open and close, as in chilled-water and hot-water loops. On air systems, it can hold duct static pressure for a variable air volume system. Either way, the drive takes a signal from a sensor and controller, so many callbacks trace to a sensor, a setpoint or a minimum-speed limit rather than to the drive itself.

Service habits

Never bypass a drive's safety trips or the bypass contactor's interlocks. Read the fault history before resetting. Check cooling air to the drive, clean heat sinks and filters, and verify the controller's command signal before blaming the drive. Parameter changes should be recorded and made only with the manual. Confirm rotation after any wiring work.

Field card: Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans

Slower is cheaper, but only down to the limits the manufacturer sets.

  • A VFD changes motor speed by changing output frequency.
  • Affinity laws: flow follows speed, head follows speed squared, power follows speed cubed.
  • Savings shrink with static lift, minimum flow needs and light-load drive losses.
  • Lock out, wait for the drive's discharge time, and use the manual before opening it.
  • Do not bypass trips or interlocks; read the fault history first.
