Clicking, Buzzing and Humming Sounds From Home Heating and Cooling

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Heating and cooling equipment makes noise on purpose. Relays click, valves snap open and motors hum. The skill is telling a normal single sound from a repeating or stuck one. This lesson sorts the common sounds, lists checks a homeowner can do safely, and says when to stop and call a professional.

Sounds that are normal

A single click when the system starts or stops is usually a switch doing its job. In our own plain-language summary, three sounds are common and fine when they happen once per cycle:

  • Thermostat or control-board relay. A small click when the thermostat calls for heat or cooling.
  • Contactor pulling in. A louder clack at the outdoor unit as the power switch closes, followed by the fan and compressor starting. See Capacitors and Contactors.
  • Gas valve opening. A soft click or thump just before the burners light.

The key word is once. A sound that happens one time at the start of a cycle and is followed by normal operation is rarely a concern. A sound that repeats, continues after the equipment should be running, or comes with a failure to start is the clue to act on.

Repeated clicking at furnace startup

If you hear clicking over and over before the furnace lights, the control board is usually trying to light the burner and not succeeding. With a spark ignition system the clicks are the spark. With a hot-surface igniter the board waits for the part to glow before opening the gas valve. After a few failed tries, most furnaces shut the burner down and stop trying for a while. This is a safety lockout. It is working as designed: the board will not keep sending gas to a burner that is not lighting. Many furnaces show a blinking light code through a small window in the door. Write the code down; it helps the technician. Hot-Surface Igniter Care and Testing explains the igniter side, and Furnace No Heat: From LED Code to Part covers how technicians read the codes.

Do not try to light a furnace by hand or bypass any safety switch. The federal consumer product safety agency advises a professional inspection of fuel-burning heating equipment every year and carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, so a furnace that will not light cleanly is a reason to call, not to experiment.

Buzzing or humming outdoor unit

This is the sound that costs the most when it is ignored. If the outdoor unit buzzes or hums but the fan is not turning, or the compressor hums and never settles into a steady run, the usual suspects are a failed run capacitor or a stuck motor. A capacitor gives a single-phase motor the push it needs to start and helps it run. When it weakens, the motor tries to start, cannot, and hums. Our own explanation: a motor that is trying to turn but is held still draws far more current than one that is running, which heats the windings quickly (see the worked example below). Condenser Fan Motor and Capacitor Failures goes into the causes.

What to do right away: switch the system off at the thermostat, then turn off the outdoor unit's disconnect or breaker so the motor is not left humming. Leaving a stalled motor energized can burn it out, and a burned compressor is a much larger repair than a capacitor. Then call a professional. Keep hands and tools out of the unit. Do not poke the fan blade to get it going; if the system comes back on it can start without warning.

Buzzing or chattering contactor

A contactor that buzzes or chatters, rattling on and off, is usually not getting a steady control signal. Low control voltage, a failing transformer, a loose thermostat wire or worn contacts can all be involved. Each chatter can arc the contacts, so this is worth a service call before it damages the compressor. The mechanism is in Contactor and Relay Coil Pickup and Dropout Voltage.

Humming transformer

Control transformers hum faintly at the power frequency; that is normal. A hum that has grown louder, comes with a hot or burning smell, or comes with a system that has stopped responding should be treated as a fault. A transformer is a closed, energized electrical part, so this is for a technician.

Humming boiler circulator

A hot-water boiler has a pump (the circulator) that moves heated water to the radiators or baseboard. A quiet hum while it runs is normal. A hum with no flow, where pipes stay cold while the boiler is hot, can mean a stuck pump shaft or a failed motor. See Boiler Circulator Pump Not Working: Humming, Seized or No Power.

Safe homeowner checks

  1. When does it happen? Only at startup, the whole cycle, or only at shutdown? Note the time and the thermostat setting.
  2. Is the outdoor fan turning? Look from a distance. Do not reach in, and do not push the blades.
  3. Any burning smell, smoke or visible scorching? If yes, turn the system off at the thermostat and the breaker and call a pro. For smoke or a fire, leave and call emergency services.
  4. Check the breaker, but do not keep resetting it. One reset after a trip is a fair check. A breaker that trips again means something in the circuit is overloaded or faulty. The same agency treats unusual smells, sparks or shocks from electrical equipment as signs of an unsafe condition that need an electrician before further use.
  5. Never open an electrical panel or equipment cover with power on. Leave the covers on the outdoor unit and furnace.
  6. Write down any lockout or blink codes and what the thermostat shows.

When to call a pro

  • Capacitor suspected. Capacitors can hold an electrical charge after power is off, so opening a unit to look is a shock hazard. Our own summary: only a trained person should discharge and test one. The federal workplace lockout rule requires stored energy to be relieved or made safe before servicing; it covers workplaces, not your home, but the same idea applies.
  • The breaker trips again after one reset.
  • Any burning smell, scorch marks or melted wire insulation.
  • Repeated lockouts or a furnace that clicks and never lights.
  • A hum from a motor, pump or transformer that does not go away when it should.

Worked example (our own): why a stalled compressor heats up so fast

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not from a real unit. Assume an outdoor unit nameplate shows a compressor rated load amps (RLA) of 10.3 A and locked rotor amps (LRA) of 52 A, on a 240 V supply. Assume the line voltage stays at 240 V and treat current times voltage as apparent power in volt-amps (VA).

  1. Current ratio. 52 A / 10.3 A = 5.05, or about 5.0 times the running current.
  2. Apparent power while running. 240 V x 10.3 A = 2,472 VA, about 2,470 VA.
  3. Apparent power while stalled. 240 V x 52 A = 12,480 VA.
  4. Compare. 12,480 / 2,472 = 5.05, the same ratio, because voltage is the same in both cases.

Why this matters: the heat made in motor windings rises with the square of the current, so about 5 times the current means roughly 25 times the winding heating (5.05 squared is about 25.5). A running compressor also gets cooling from refrigerant flow, while a stalled one does not. That is why a humming, stalled compressor gets hot fast and why its internal overload device trips. It is also why you should shut it off at the thermostat and the disconnect. Note that VA is not the same as real power in watts, so these numbers show current stress, not the exact heat. Real units vary.

Field card: Clicking, Buzzing and Humming Sounds

One click per cycle is normal. Repeating, stuck or smelly is not.

  • Single clicks: thermostat relay, contactor, gas valve opening.
  • Repeated clicks at furnace startup: igniter not lighting, then safety lockout.
  • Outdoor unit humming with no fan: shut it off at the thermostat and disconnect.
  • Stalled compressor draws about 5 times running current (our own example).
  • Check the breaker once; do not keep resetting it.
  • Hands out of the unit; covers on; never open panels with power on.
  • Capacitors hold a charge: call a pro.
