How Long Should an Air Conditioner Take to Cool a Hot House?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You come home from a week away, the house is 85 F inside, and you set the thermostat to 75 F. Two hours later it still feels warm and sticky. Is the system broken? Usually not. Cooling a house is not like cooling a cup of air. This lesson shows what the system has to remove, why the first hours are slow, and why a bigger unit is not the fix.

The house is more than the air

The air inside a house holds very little heat. The walls, ceilings, floors, cabinets, furniture, books and bedding hold far more, and after days of heat they have all warmed up with the air. When the system pulls the air down to 75 F, those surfaces are still warmer than the air, so they keep handing heat back to it. The thermostat reads air, but the system is really cooling the whole contents of the house.

A building-science paper from a national laboratory modeled a 1,180 square foot house with 7,000 lb of interior furnishings. It treated the furnishings as wood with a specific heat of 0.29 Btu per pound per degree F. That works out to roughly 2,000 Btu for each degree the furnishings change (our arithmetic: 7,000 times 0.29 is 2,030). The same paper used half-inch gypsum board (drywall) on both sides of the interior partitions. Drywall, framing, flooring and ceilings add more on top of that. The exact total depends on the house, which is why we use stated assumptions below.

Moisture is stored in the house too

Materials take up water vapor when the air is humid and give it back when the air dries out. A national laboratory simulation of a small room in July found that about 3 kg of water accumulated in the structure over the month, and the authors noted that the heat released by that moisture could warm the room by 3 C if it all went into the air. Another laboratory paper measured a fully furnished test room against an empty one and found that furnishings smoothed out the humidity swings, with the furnished room peaking about 10 percentage points of relative humidity lower. In plain words, furniture, fabric, paper and wood act like sponges.

That matters after a vacation. If the house was closed up and humid, the cooling coil has to remove moisture from the incoming air and also from the materials as they release it. Removing moisture takes cooling capacity too. A university extension publication explains the split with a sensible heat ratio: a ratio of 0.7 means about 70 percent of the cooling goes to lowering temperature and 30 percent to removing moisture.

The simple rule: net capacity

The system does not cool at its full rating, because heat keeps leaking in while it runs. Sunlight through windows, hot attic air above the ceiling, outdoor air leaking in, people, lights and appliances all add heat. What is left over to cool the stored heat is the capacity minus the ongoing heat gain:

Time to cool = heat to remove ÷ (capacity − ongoing heat gain)

One ton of cooling equals 12,000 Btu per hour, according to a university extension publication. If the heat gain on a hot afternoon is close to the capacity, the net is small and the pull-down is slow. That is also why the same unit that holds 75 F easily on a mild day may struggle to get there on the hottest day of the year.

Worked example (our own, with stated assumptions)

This is our own example, not a measurement of any real house. Every input below is an assumption we chose to keep the arithmetic readable.

  • Assumed effective stored mass in the house: 6,000 Btu per degree F, counting the indoor air, furnishings, and the part of the drywall and framing that follows the room temperature within a few hours.
  • Assumed sensible cooling capacity of the 3-ton system at these hot conditions: 27,000 Btu per hour (a 36,000 Btu per hour rating, with about 9,000 going to moisture).
  • Assumed heat gain through the building on a hot afternoon: 15,000 Btu per hour.
  • Assumed stored moisture to remove: 10 lb of water, at about 1,050 Btu per pound to condense it, and an ongoing moisture gain of 3,000 Btu per hour.

Step 1, heat to remove. 85 F to 75 F is 10 degrees, so 6,000 × 10 = 60,000 Btu.

Step 2, net sensible capacity. 27,000 − 15,000 = 12,000 Btu per hour.

Step 3, time. 60,000 ÷ 12,000 = 5 hours.

Step 4, moisture check. 10 lb × 1,050 = 10,500 Btu. Net moisture capacity is 9,000 − 3,000 = 6,000 Btu per hour, so 10,500 ÷ 6,000 is about 1.75 hours. The moisture comes out sooner than the temperature falls, which matches the usual experience: the air feels less sticky first, then slowly cools.

So in this example, five hours of nonstop running is normal, not a fault. If your own house takes two or three times longer, or the air from the registers is not clearly cool, that is a reason for a service visit; see AC Running but Not Cooling.

Why it looks slow after a vacation

  • More stored heat. A week of heat soaks deep into walls, ceilings and contents, so the effective mass is at its largest.
  • Moisture to remove. A closed-up house can be humid, and the coil spends part of its capacity on water instead of temperature.
  • Peak heat gain. People tend to come home on the hottest days, when the net capacity is smallest.
  • Surface warmth lingers. Even after the air reads 75 F, warm furniture and walls reheat it, so the thermostat can satisfy, the system rests, and the air drifts up a degree or two. That can feel like the unit is not keeping up when it is simply finishing the job.

A university extension publication recommends a summer thermostat setting of 78 F or higher and says each degree below that can raise cooling cost by up to 8 percent. A smaller drop is a smaller job. For a trip, a modest setback is a better choice than turning the system off or setting it very low on return.

Why a bigger unit is not the answer

A larger unit would shorten the one-time pull-down. In our example a 4-ton system with an assumed 36,000 Btu per hour of sensible capacity would have a net of 21,000 and finish the temperature drop in about 2.9 hours (60,000 ÷ 21,000). But the pull-down happens a few times a year. The other thousands of hours are spent holding the temperature, and there a bigger unit has to cycle on and off in short bursts.

  • A university extension publication says an oversized system cools the house too quickly to remove moisture well, leaving it cool but clammy.
  • A federal guide for homeowners notes that many older systems were oversized because contractors used rules of thumb instead of a load calculation.
  • A university extension publication says close sizing gives better humidity control and fewer start-stop cycles, and that oversizing can hide problems such as leaky ducts, poor insulation or excess air leakage.
  • A university solar energy research center paper measured that sensible capacity reaches steady state about 30 seconds after startup, but moisture removal begins after about 20 seconds and takes about 3 minutes to settle. Short runs therefore remove proportionally less moisture.
  • The same paper found that running the fan continuously lets some of the water on the coil evaporate back into the house. In one test about 19 percent of it did, and at about a quarter run time the humidity rose from 55 to 65 percent. Oversizing plus a fan left on makes that worse.

A better path is to find out why the load is high: air leaks, a hot attic, leaky ducts, sun on west windows. See Duct Leakage Testing and AC Short Cycling for the symptoms of an oversized system.

What to expect, in plain terms

  • A properly sized system is meant to run most of a design-hot day. Long runs are normal; a system that never reaches the setpoint on a moderate day is the concern.
  • Expect the air to feel drier before it feels colder after a long absence.
  • Do not judge the system by the first two hours. Judge it by the next morning, with the house settled.
  • Close blinds on sunny windows and keep doors and windows shut during the pull-down to cut heat gain.

Field card: How Long Should an Air Conditioner Take to Cool a Hot House?

Time = heat to remove ÷ (capacity − heat gain).

  • The house stores heat in walls, drywall and contents, not only in the air.
  • It also stores moisture, and removing moisture uses part of the capacity.
  • One ton is 12,000 Btu per hour.
  • Our own example: 60,000 Btu ÷ 12,000 Btu per hour is about 5 hours. Assumptions, not measurements.
  • After a vacation the stored heat and moisture are at their highest, so the first hours look slow.
  • A bigger unit shortens the pull-down but short-cycles the rest of the year and removes less moisture.
