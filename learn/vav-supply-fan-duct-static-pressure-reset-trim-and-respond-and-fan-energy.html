VAV Duct Static Pressure Reset: Trim and Respond and Fan Energy

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A VAV system has one big supply fan feeding dozens of boxes. The fan has to hold enough duct pressure for the hungriest box, but most of the day that is far more than the other boxes need. Duct static pressure reset, often built as "trim and respond," lets the building automation system lower the target when nobody is asking for more. This lesson covers how it works, what it saves, and how to spot the zone that ruins it.

How the fan holds a setpoint

A pressure sensor sits in the supply duct and reports duct static pressure to the controller. The controller compares it to a setpoint and tells the variable frequency drive (VFD) to speed the fan up or slow it down. As VAV box dampers close, duct pressure rises, so the fan slows. As dampers open, pressure falls, so the fan speeds up. For the parts, see VAV Boxes Basics, Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans and Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Trane's engineers newsletter says that in many systems the sensor is located approximately two-thirds of the distance down the main supply duct. Trane's ASHRAE Journal article says the same, and adds that this conventional approach usually produces more static pressure than necessary. Treat two-thirds as a common starting rule, not a law. The design drawings and the controls submittal say where your sensor actually is.

Why a fixed setpoint wastes energy

With a fixed setpoint, the fan is told to hold the pressure the design needed at peak load, all day. At part load most boxes are partly closed and their dampers are throttling away pressure the fan worked to make. Trane's newsletter says the system usually generates more static pressure at part load than necessary. Its ASHRAE Journal article says lowering part-load pressure means the fan uses less energy and makes less noise, and wide-open dampers regenerate less noise at the boxes.

Reset from damper positions

The idea is simple: the box with the most-open damper tells you whether the fan is making enough pressure. Trane describes resetting the setpoint so that at least one damper is nearly wide open. Johnson Controls documents a version in which the setpoint changes by 0.1 in. w.c. when the neediest zone's damper leaves a band of 85 to 95 percent open, with 10 minutes between changes. The DOE and PNNL re-tuning report models a similar reset: the average of the most-open 10 percent of boxes is compared to 70 and 90 percent, and the setpoint steps by 0.1 in. w.c. In that report the primary savings come from lower fan energy, and it gives 5 to 7 percent savings from static pressure reset in its modeled buildings. Those are modeling results, not a promise for your building.

Trim and respond

Trim and respond is a popular way to build the reset. A slide deck titled "Taylor VAV Design Tips," hosted by AMCA, describes it this way: each zone generates requests, for example one request when its damper position exceeds 95 percent. Every time cycle, the setpoint is reduced a little (the trim). But it is increased (the respond) in proportion to the number of requests, up to a maximum change. Zones can be given an importance multiplier, where 0 ignores a zone entirely. An "ignore" count lets the setpoint skip a few requests so one or two fussy zones do not drive the whole system. ASHRAE Guideline 36 is the best-known document that defines this logic; we name it but do not link a copy. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's open-source Modelica library of Guideline 36 controls lists example starting values: initial 120 Pa, minimum 25 Pa, trim -12 Pa, respond 15 Pa, two ignored requests, a 600 second delay and a 120 second sample period. Those are in pascals, with a maximum that you must supply. Real jobs are tuned to the building.

Worked example 1: what lower pressure is worth

DOE's fan sourcebook gives fan efficiency as total pressure times airflow divided by brake horsepower times 6,362, with pressure in inches of water and airflow in cfm. Rearranged, brake horsepower equals total pressure times airflow divided by efficiency times 6,362. So at a fixed efficiency, power varies with flow times pressure. Our own arithmetic, with made-up numbers for teaching: 20,000 cfm at 2.5 in. w.c. total static, and reset lowers it to 2.0 in. w.c. at the same airflow.

  1. Pressure ratio: 2.0 divided by 2.5 = 0.80.
  2. Flow ratio: 20,000 divided by 20,000 = 1.00.
  3. Power ratio at the same efficiency: 1.00 times 0.80 = 0.80, so brake horsepower falls by 20 percent.
  4. Check with an assumed 65 percent efficiency: 6,362 times 0.65 = 4,135. Before: 20,000 times 2.5 = 50,000, divided by 4,135 = about 12.1 bhp. After: 20,000 times 2.0 = 40,000, divided by 4,135 = about 9.7 bhp. That is 2.4 bhp less, and 2.4 divided by 12.1 is about 20 percent.

Real fans do not hold the same efficiency at every speed, so treat 20 percent as an estimate. Our fan laws lesson covers why speed changes matter so much.

Worked example 2: five time steps of trim and respond

Our own simplified rules and made-up request counts, for teaching only. Start at 1.50 in. Each step: if requests are at or below the ignore count of 2, trim by 0.04 in. If requests are above 2, respond by +0.06 in. for each request above 2 and skip the trim. Keep the result between the minimum 0.5 in. and the maximum 1.5 in.

  1. Requests 0. Not above 2, so trim: 1.50 - 0.04 = 1.46 in.
  2. Requests 0. Trim: 1.46 - 0.04 = 1.42 in.
  3. Requests 1. Still ignored, so trim: 1.42 - 0.04 = 1.38 in.
  4. Requests 6. Above 2 by 4, so respond: 1.38 + (4 times 0.06 = 0.24) = 1.62 in. That passes the 1.50 maximum, so the setpoint is held at 1.50 in.
  5. Requests 0. Trim: 1.50 - 0.04 = 1.46 in.

Real implementations also cap the change per step (the LBNL library lists a maximum response), so one burst of requests cannot slam the setpoint. We left that out to keep the arithmetic short.

Rogue zones

A rogue zone keeps asking for more. The slides define it as one that is always requesting more, which drives the reset to extremes and prevents savings. Causes the sources list include loads larger than design, poor duct design or construction, extreme setpoint changes and failed equipment such as a broken damper. Trane adds an undersized terminal, a duct restriction, a zone setpoint set too low, or a zone sensor in sunlight or near a heat source. To find one, the Taylor slides suggest the BAS total request-hours for each zone and alarm on high percentages. Fix the cause first. If the zone is not critical, the importance multiplier can lock it out, and the slides say that decision belongs to the operator, not automatic logic.

What to trend

These come from the logic above, not from a single source. See Building Automation Basics for trending. Trend, on the same time axis: the duct static setpoint, the measured duct static, fan speed or VFD output, total zone requests, and the damper position of each box (or at least the most-open one). A healthy reset shows the setpoint moving through the day with the load. A setpoint pinned at its maximum with one damper at 100 percent points to a rogue zone. A measured pressure that cannot reach the setpoint points to the fan, the sensor or the VFD.

Field card: Duct Static Pressure Reset

Lower the target until one damper is nearly open.

  • Sensor and VFD hold duct static; dampers closing raises pressure and slows the fan.
  • Fixed setpoint at part load makes extra pressure, energy, and damper noise.
  • Trim lowers the setpoint each step; respond raises it for requests above the ignore count.
  • Min and max limits bound the setpoint.
  • Same flow, 2.5 to 2.0 in. w.c.: power ratio 2.0/2.5 = 0.80, about 20 percent less (estimate).
  • A zone that always requests is a rogue zone; fix it before locking it out.
  • Trend setpoint, measured static, fan speed, requests and damper positions together.
