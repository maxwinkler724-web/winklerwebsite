Chlorine and Halogen Fumes in Combustion Air: Why Burners Corrode

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A gas flame burns whatever air it is given. If that air carries chlorine or other halogen vapors, the flame turns them into acids, and the acids attack the metal that the flame heats. This lesson explains where those vapors come from, why some rooms are worse than others, what fixes the problem, and what a tech checks.

What a halogen is, and why a flame matters

Chlorine and fluorine are halogens. Many household and shop products contain them: laundry bleach, pool sanitizers, some paint strippers, some aerosol cans, dry-cleaning fluids and many refrigerants. Sitting in a closed container, most are not a problem for the appliance. The trouble starts when the vapor is pulled into a burner. A state hazardous substance fact sheet for a common dry-cleaning solvent says it decomposes slowly with heating into hydrogen chloride and phosgene. The same kind of fact sheet for a refrigerant-type compound says heat, flames or red-hot metal break it down into hydrogen fluoride and phosgene. A federal chemical datasheet says chlorine gas dissolves in water to form hydrochloric and hypochlorous acids, a mixture that is corrosive. A water heater maker's technical bulletin puts it in appliance terms: burned chlorine and fluorine compounds form acids that corrode the heat exchanger and the vent system.

Why does the metal suffer? A university chapter on halogens in combustion explains that halogens leave a flame mainly as acid gases such as hydrogen chloride and hydrogen fluoride, and that below roughly 100 degrees C the hydrogen chloride combines with water vapor and condenses into strong liquid acid. The cooler parts of an appliance, such as a heat exchanger wall, a flue connector or a vent, are where that can happen. The same chapter notes that at much higher metal temperatures chlorine attacks iron directly, starting around 200 degrees C for chlorine and 400 degrees C for hydrogen chloride. So there is risk at both ends of the temperature range.

Where the vapors come from

  • Laundry bleach and cleaners. The maker's bulletin lists laundry bleaches, detergents, adhesives, paints, varnishes, paint strippers, waxes and plastics as sources of contaminants. A state fact sheet says bleach-type sodium hypochlorite releases chlorine and other toxic gases when mixed with acids or with ammonia compounds.
  • Pool chemicals. A federal public health page on pool chemical safety tells operators to keep storage areas and pump rooms well ventilated, dry and cool, and to keep incompatible chemicals apart. The same page says indoor venues should have separate air handling for these areas.
  • Paint strippers. A federal consumer product safety agency fact sheet warns that vapor from methylene chloride strippers can be deadly and that the products should not be used in enclosed areas such as bathrooms, basements or closets. That is the same kind of room a furnace may sit in. We found no source in our reading that says what that solvent does in a flame, so we do not claim it here.
  • Aerosols. A federal environmental agency fact sheet lists the propellants used in aerosol cans. Some are halogenated, such as HFC-134a and HFC-152a, while others are hydrocarbons or compressed gases. The maker's bulletin adds that halogen-treated aerosol propellants are curtailed but still in use, and it singles out shops that use aerosol sprays all day.
  • Dry-cleaning fluids. The federal environmental agency says the main end use of perchloroethylene is as a dry cleaning solvent.
  • Refrigerant leaks. The refrigerant-type fact sheet above is the reason a leak near a burner is more than a refrigerant loss. See the related lesson on how a cracked heat exchanger is inspected for another route by which gases move around an appliance.

Rooms that put appliances at risk

The risk is about where the appliance gets its air. An atmospheric furnace or water heater that takes combustion air from the room it sits in will burn whatever that room holds. That makes three spots worth special attention: a laundry room, where bleach and cleaners are stored and used; a pool equipment room, where sanitizer products are stored near the heater; and a hobby workshop, where strippers, solvents, adhesives and spray cans are used. The maker's bulletin says the way to avoid heat exchanger and vent corrosion is to isolate the appliance so it takes only outdoor air for combustion, ventilation and draft hood dilution. Our own field note: an equipment closet that opens to a utility room full of chemicals is just as exposed as one inside the room, because the air moves through the louver or door gap.

Fixes

  • Sealed combustion. A federal indoor air page says sealed combustion equipment draws its combustion air directly from outside the home. A university extension publication likewise says to look for sealed combustion units that use outside air for combustion, so combustion pollutants are never mixed with room air. The maker's bulletin adds that a direct-vent unit must still not take its air from a contaminated place, and gives a swimming pool area as an example of one.
  • Outdoor combustion air. A state university extension publication restates the model-code idea that outdoor combustion air is provided through openings to the outdoors. Sizing and layout belong to the code and the maker's instructions; see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters in Closets and Basements.
  • Separate exhaust for fumes. The maker's bulletin says corrosive process fumes need their own exhaust system and an adequate supply of fresh makeup air.
  • Storage. Store bleach, pool products, strippers, solvents and spray cans in a cool, dry, ventilated spot away from the appliance room. This follows the pool chemical page: dry, ventilated, below 95 degrees F, each product in its own place, incompatible products kept apart. Also follow the labels.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, built from assumed numbers, not measured data. How much chlorine vapor could a small appliance draw in over a heating season if the room air held some?

  • Assumed room concentration: 0.5 ppm chlorine by volume.
  • Assumed combustion air flow: 30 cubic feet per minute.
  • Assumed run time: 1,200 hours in the season.
  • Chlorine molar mass: 70.91 g/mol (federal chemical datasheet). Molar volume of air at 20 degrees C and 1 atm: 24.06 L/mol (standard value).

Step 1, airflow in metric. 30 cfm x 0.0283168 m³/ft³ = 0.8495 m³/min. Times 60 min/h = 50.97 m³/h.

Step 2, air volume over the season. 50.97 m³/h x 1,200 h = 61,164 m³.

Step 3, concentration as mass. 0.5 ppm x 70.91 g/mol / 24.06 L/mol = 1.474 mg/m³.

Step 4, mass drawn in. 61,164 m³ x 1.474 mg/m³ = 90,150 mg, about 90 g of chlorine.

Step 5, acid ceiling. 90.15 g / 70.91 g/mol = 1.27 mol Cl₂, which is 2.54 mol of chlorine atoms. If every atom ended up as hydrogen chloride (36.46 g/mol), that is at most about 93 g of acid gas. At one tenth the concentration (0.05 ppm) every figure is one tenth: about 9 g.

The units check: ppm is volume per volume, so it scales with moles, and cfm and hours multiply to cubic feet. A modest amount spread over a season still passes through the same heat exchanger and vent. We did not look up a safe or typical concentration for this example, so treat 0.5 ppm as an assumption only.

What a tech checks

  • Where the appliance gets its combustion air, and whether that room or space holds bleach, pool products, strippers, solvents or sprays.
  • Whether the unit is sealed combustion or direct vent, and whether its outdoor intake sits near a contaminated spot such as a pool area.
  • Corrosion on the heat exchanger, burner, controls and vent connectors. The maker's bulletin names thermocouples, controls, flue baffles and burner assemblies as parts that degrade.
  • Our own field checklist, not from a source: look for crusty deposits, pinholes and a sharp smell near the appliance, and ask the customer what has been used or stored nearby.
  • Whether refrigerant is leaking anywhere the burner can reach it, and where chemical containers are kept.

Do not try to light or test an appliance in a room where you smell strong chemical fumes. Ventilate, tell the customer, and follow your employer's and the maker's procedures.

Field card: Chlorine and Halogen Fumes in Combustion Air

The flame turns room fumes into acids; give it clean air.

  • Halogen vapors burned in a flame form acids that attack heat exchangers, burners and vents.
  • Sources: bleach, pool chemicals, strippers, some aerosols, dry-cleaning fluids and refrigerant leaks.
  • Laundry rooms, pool equipment rooms and hobby shops are high-risk spots.
  • Fixes: sealed combustion, ducted outdoor air, separate exhaust, chemicals stored elsewhere.
  • Check the air source, the intake location, corrosion, and what is stored nearby.
  • Our worked example: 0.5 ppm, 30 cfm, 1,200 h gives about 90 g chlorine (assumed inputs).
