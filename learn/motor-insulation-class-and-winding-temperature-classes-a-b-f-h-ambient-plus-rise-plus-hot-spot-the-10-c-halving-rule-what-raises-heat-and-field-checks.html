Motor Insulation Class and Winding Temperature: A, B, F, H and the 10 C Rule

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most motors that fail in a building do not fail all at once. Heat slowly cooks the varnish and film on the copper until a winding shorts or grounds. This lesson explains the insulation letter classes, how the winding temperature adds up, the rule of thumb that ties heat to motor life, what pushes windings hotter, and a few field checks that catch the trend early.

What an insulation class is

The insulation class on a motor nameplate is a letter that names the highest temperature the winding insulation system is rated to live at. A university extension tech note on reading motor nameplates gives approximate values of about 105 C for Class A, 130 C for Class B, 155 C for Class F and 180 C for Class H. It adds that Class A and B are common on single-phase motors, Class F is common on three-phase motors, and Class H is used where a motor sees high ambient temperatures. A university lecture on three-phase induction motors shows the same idea on a nameplate: Class F, rated for 155 C, with continuous duty at a 40 C ambient. A later letter means the insulation can stand more heat. Motor standards define these classes, and the nameplate is where you find the one for your motor.

A higher class does not make a motor run cooler. A federal tip sheet on extending motor life notes that a rewind to a higher class, for example from Class B (130 C) to Class H (180 C), can increase winding life, but warns that higher-rated new motors may actually operate at higher internal temperatures. The class is the allowed ceiling, not the operating temperature.

How winding temperature adds up

Think of the hottest spot inside the winding as three pieces stacked together: the temperature of the air around the motor (ambient), plus the temperature rise caused by the motor's own electrical and friction losses, plus a small hot-spot allowance because the center of a coil runs hotter than the average the test measures. The sum is what the insulation actually feels. Motor makers pick the nameplate ambient and the design rise so that this sum stays at or under the class limit when the motor is loaded as rated.

The useful part for a technician is simple. Raise any one block and the whole stack moves up. A hot mechanical room raises the ambient block. A clogged cooling path or an overload raises the rise block. The class limit does not move, so the margin shrinks.

The 10 C halving rule

Insulation ages chemically, and heat speeds that up. A federal motor tip sheet states the rule of thumb this way: for every 10 C rise in operating temperature, insulation life is reduced by half. A federal motor-system sourcebook says the same: as a rule of thumb, every 10 C rise in winding temperature reduces insulation life by half. It is a rule of thumb, not an exact law, and it describes a motor already near its rating. The direction matters more than the exact figure: a little extra heat costs a lot of life, and a cooler motor lasts longer.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. A Class F motor (rated 155 C) on a plant exhaust fan runs with its winding hot spot 20 C above that rating for long periods. We assume the 10 C halving rule holds and that the motor would last its normal design life if it ran at the rating.

  1. Excess temperature: 20 C over the rating.
  2. Number of 10 C steps: 20 / 10 = 2.
  3. Each step halves life, so the life factor is (1/2) to the power 2 = 1/4.
  4. Equivalent statement: life is shorter by a factor of 2 to the power 2 = 4.

So by the halving rule, that motor's insulation life is about one quarter of normal, or about 4 times shorter. A motor that would have gone twelve years might last about three. The numbers are only an illustration, but they show why a modest, steady overheat is worth fixing.

What raises winding temperature

  • High ambient. A motor in a hot mechanical room, in direct sun, or enclosed with other heat sources starts the stack higher. The federal tip sheet lists high ambient temperature as a cause of overheating.
  • Blocked or weak cooling. Dirt on the frame and fins, plugged fan guards, poor ventilation, or a broken cooling fan keep heat in. The same tip sheet names dirt and poor ventilation.
  • Low, high or unbalanced voltage. The tip sheet lists undervoltage, overvoltage and voltage unbalance among the causes. See Three-Phase Voltage Imbalance for the math. Single-phasing is the extreme case; see the phase-loss lesson linked below.
  • Frequent starts. Starting current is several times running current, so repeated starts heat the winding. The federal sourcebook notes that frequent starting and stopping can cause motors to run at high temperatures.
  • Running in the service factor. The tip sheet counts overload operation even within the service factor. The service factor is reserve capacity for brief use, not a place to run all day. The sourcebook says the main consequence of running above rated load is higher winding temperature, which shortens motor life.

Field checks

You rarely measure winding temperature directly. These checks work as stand-ins, and each one is most useful as a trend:

  • Amps versus nameplate. Measure running current on each leg and compare with the full-load amps on the nameplate, with the service factor in mind. A motor pulling more than its nameplate is making more heat. See Measuring Amp Draw With a Clamp Meter.
  • Surface temperature trend. Take frame temperature at the same spot, same load and similar ambient, and log it. A frame that creeps up over months points to dirt, a failing fan or rising load. The reading is a trend tool; the winding inside is hotter than the frame.
  • Megohm trend. Insulation resistance to ground that keeps falling from one test to the next is the sign that heat or moisture is wearing the insulation down. Always lock out first and follow the meter maker's instructions. The details are in Megohmmeter Insulation Resistance Testing.

When a check looks wrong, find the cause before you reset an overload or swap the motor. A new motor in the same hot, dirty, overloaded setup will cook the same way.

Field card: Motor Insulation Class and Winding Temperature: A, B, F, H and the 10 C Rule

Heat is what ages a winding. Watch the stack: ambient + rise + hot spot.

  • Class limits, approximate: A 105 C, B 130 C, F 155 C, H 180 C.
  • Winding hot spot = ambient + rise + hot-spot allowance.
  • Rule of thumb: each 10 C over rating halves insulation life.
  • Heat sources: hot ambient, blocked cooling, bad voltage, frequent starts, living in the service factor.
  • Checks: amps vs nameplate, frame temperature trend, megohm trend.
