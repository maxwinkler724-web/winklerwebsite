Smoke and Cooking Odors From a Neighbor's Unit: How Air Moves Between Attached Homes, What Sealing and Fans Can Do, and What an Air Cleaner Can and Cannot Do

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You close your windows, yet tobacco smoke or last night's fried fish still shows up in your bedroom. In an apartment, condo or townhouse the air does not stay on its own side of the wall. This lesson explains the paths, the pressure that drives air along them, the simple fixes you control, and the honest limits of portable air cleaners. It is general education, not legal advice.

Why attached homes share air

A federal environmental agency page on indoor air says smoke can travel between rooms in a building, including from one unit to another, and that no exposure level to secondhand smoke is known to be safe. Another page from the same agency lists light fixtures, electrical outlets, ceiling crawl spaces and doorways as routes smoke can take from a neighbor's apartment. A federal housing agency page on air leakage adds that sealing leaks can limit odors and contaminated air coming in from other units.

The same housing page explains the driving force in the heating season. Warm air rises and pushes on the ceiling, so air leaves through the upper part of a building and cold air is drawn in through the lower part. That is the stack effect. Pressure differences are typically strongest on the upper and lower floors and in basements. In our own plain reading, the leaks you cannot see do the moving, and the pressure difference decides which way the air goes. Which way it goes in your particular building is something we cannot predict from a page like this.

The usual leak paths

  • Shared walls and floors. Gaps around the wall where it meets the floor, ceiling or exterior wall are common routes (our own observation, consistent with the federal page that lists floors, ceilings and walls among leakage areas).
  • Penetrations. A university extension fact sheet on caulking says to check wherever plumbing, ductwork, fans or wiring pass through walls, floors or ceilings. In the home it describes, plumbing penetrations account for roughly 13 percent of the leakage and electrical outlets about 2 percent. Those shares are for a single-family example, so treat them as a rough guide only.
  • Outlets and switches. The same fact sheet says foam gaskets behind outlet plates address leaks at outlets.
  • Chases, shafts and ducts. A national laboratory guide on multifamily ventilation (cited as plain text below) says vertical service shafts should be sealed at each floor, and that each unit should be sealed from the ones above, below and beside it. Shared exhaust or supply ductwork is the building's equipment, not yours to modify.
  • Entry doors. A gap under your entry door can let in corridor air. The door sweep and weatherstripping in the next section address that, though the sources we read do not give corridor-specific numbers.

Why your own fans can pull neighbor air in

A national laboratory literature review says exhaust fans are among the most important causes of a house being depressurized, that the effect grows with fan airflow, and that the same fan makes a tighter dwelling more negative than a leaky one. It lists dryers at up to 200 cubic feet per minute, basic range hoods at about 150, and bath fans at 50 or more. The multifamily guide above says exhaust fans without makeup air can depressurize a unit and pull air from corridors and adjacent dwellings, carrying odors with it, and that one unit can end up strongly negative while its neighbors are not.

Plain-language meaning: when your bath fan, range hood and dryer run together, your unit tends toward lower pressure than its surroundings, and the air that replaces what you exhausted comes through the nearest gaps. If the nearest gap leads to the neighbor's unit, that is where it comes from. Our own advice: run the fan you need, for the time you need it, and avoid running several large fans at once.

The related lesson on bathroom exhaust fans covers sizing and ducting. Never block or disconnect a fan to stop the draw. A housing agency page also says combustion appliances should be checked for proper venting before and after air sealing, because changing pressures can affect them. If you have a gas furnace, water heater or other fuel-burning appliance inside your unit, a technician should check it before you make large changes.

What you can do inside your own unit

  • Seal outlets and switches on shared walls. Foam gaskets behind the cover plates are the fact sheet's remedy for outlet leaks. Turn the circuit off first, and if you are not comfortable working at an outlet, ask someone qualified (our own safety note).
  • Caulk small gaps. The fact sheet says caulk generally works for openings up to about one quarter inch, aerosol sealants for holes up to about one half inch, and larger gaps need foam backer material before caulking. Common spots are baseboards, around pipes under sinks and where trim meets the wall.
  • Add a door sweep and weatherstripping to your entry door. Keep fire-door hardware and building rules in mind, and ask management before changing a door (our own caution).
  • Balance your own exhaust. Crack a window while a range hood runs if that is allowed in your building, and avoid running the dryer, hood and bath fan together. The multifamily guide recommends makeup air or passive inlets so exhaust does not pull from neighbors.
  • Do not seal vents that bring in air for your appliances or cover the building's grilles. A housing page notes that tightening a building without enough ventilation can harm occupants.

A federal environmental agency multifamily preface warns that tightening buildings can create or worsen indoor air problems unless sources, ventilation and moisture are managed. Sealing helps, but it is one piece of the fix. The related lesson on condos and townhouses covers the shared equipment, bath and dryer vents and checks to do before calling service.

What a portable air cleaner can and cannot do

A federal agency guide to home air cleaners says most portable units are designed to filter either particles or gases, and many have one filter for each. Look for a high clean air delivery rate (CADR) for particles. Those cleaners often reach a high rate using a HEPA filter. For gases, the guide points to an activated carbon or other gas-removing filter, ideally with a large amount of material, and notes there is no widely used rating for gas removal. CADR applies only to particles. For tobacco smoke, the guide says the smoke CADR reflects the smallest particles, so a high smoke rating means good performance on fine particles.

The same guide says cleaners cannot remove all pollutants and do not replace reducing the source and bringing in clean outdoor air. A federal environmental agency page on secondhand smoke says ventilation, filtration and air cleaning can lower secondhand smoke but do not eliminate it, and only keeping smoking out entirely does. In other words, no cleaner removes all the health risk from smoke. Cooking odors are mostly gases and vapors, so a particle filter alone may leave the smell.

The guide's sizing chart lists minimum CADRs of 65 cfm for 100 square feet, 130 cfm for 200, 195 cfm for 300, 260 cfm for 400, 325 cfm for 500 and 390 cfm for 600, assuming 8-foot ceilings. Higher ceilings need a higher rate, and higher fan speeds and longer run times filter more air. Check the label for your room size and avoid obstructing the unit's airflow.

Worked example (our own): air changes per hour for a bedroom

An air change per hour (ACH) is the number of times the unit's clean air flow equals the room's volume in one hour. A university medical center page gives the relation as clean air delivery rate times 60, divided by room volume. The multiplier 60 converts minutes to hours. This example is our own, and every input is an assumption.

Assumptions (our own): bedroom 12 ft by 14 ft with an 8 ft ceiling; label CADR of 150 cfm for smoke at the setting you will run overnight; door closed.

  1. Floor area: 12 × 14 = 168 square feet.
  2. Volume: 168 × 8 = 1,344 cubic feet.
  3. Cubic feet of clean air per hour: 150 × 60 = 9,000.
  4. ACH: 9,000 ÷ 1,344 = 6.696, about 6.7 changes per hour.

Check by another route: one room volume takes 1,344 ÷ 150 = 8.96 minutes to process, and 60 ÷ 8.96 = 6.70. The two match. Check against the sizing chart: 168 square feet is 1.68 times the 100 square foot row, so the chart suggests a minimum of about 65 × 1.68 = 109 cfm. The 150 cfm unit is above that. These are label-based numbers. They are not a measurement of your room, they assume the unit is the only air source for the room, and the real result will be lower with the fan on a low speed, leaks bringing in smoke, or the door open.

Never use an ozone generator

A federal environmental agency page says that at levels within public health standards, ozone generators have little ability to remove indoor pollutants, are not effective at removing most odors, and can create irritating byproducts such as aldehydes and formic acid. It calls ozone a toxic gas that can damage the lungs and worsen asthma, and says some studies found indoor ozone levels five to ten times above health limits even when instructions were followed. Our advice follows that page: do not use one, and be cautious of any cleaner or "ionizer" that makes ozone on purpose. The related lesson on electronic air cleaners covers this further.

Keep a simple log and talk to management

Our own suggestion: write down the date, time, what you smelled, how long it lasted, which room, what the wind and your fans were doing, and any symptoms. A log helps you see patterns, such as smoke arriving when your range hood runs. It also gives a building manager something specific to check.

Then ask building management or the condo association, in writing, about shared ventilation and any smoke-free policy that applies. A federal environmental agency page suggests reading your lease or condo agreement for any smoke or vapor policy, and asking local health or indoor air contacts about building codes or ordinances. That page also says to consult a legal professional about your rights. We do not give legal advice and cannot say what applies in your building.

Field card: Smoke and Odors From a Neighbor's Unit

Seal the leaks, balance your fans, filter what you can, skip ozone.

  • Air moves between attached units through outlets, pipe gaps, chases, floors, ceilings and door gaps.
  • Winter stack effect pulls cold air in low and pushes warm air out high.
  • Running exhaust fans lowers your unit's pressure and can pull neighbor air in.
  • Foam gaskets behind outlet plates and caulk for gaps up to about 1/4 inch; ask before changing doors.
  • HEPA cleaners handle particles; gases need carbon and no rating exists. None removes all smoke risk.
  • ACH = CADR × 60 ÷ room volume (150 cfm, 1,344 cubic feet, about 6.7 ACH; our own example).
  • Never use ozone generators.
  • Keep a log; ask management in writing; no legal advice here.
