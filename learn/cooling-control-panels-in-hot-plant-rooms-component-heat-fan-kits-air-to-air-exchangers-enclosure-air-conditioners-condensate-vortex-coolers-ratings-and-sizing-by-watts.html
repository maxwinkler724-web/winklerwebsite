Cooling Control Panels in Hot Plant Rooms: Sizing by Watts

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Boiler rooms, chiller plants and rooftop mechanical rooms are hot, and the control panels in them make their own heat on top of it. This lesson covers why that heat shortens component life, the four common ways to cool an enclosure, and how to size the cooling in watts and Btu/h.

Why heat kills control components

Drives, power supplies, transformers and contactor coils all waste some of the power they handle as heat. That heat has to leave the enclosure, and in a hot room it has less far to fall. A university thesis on power electronics says temperature is closely associated with operating efficiency and failure rates, and reprints a table where the failure rate of a power transistor chip rises from 5,000 at 25 °C to 270,000 at 125 °C in that table's units. Capacitors, which age quickly when warm, show the same pattern. You do not need the exact figures to get the lesson: a part running hot fails sooner, and repeated heating and cooling swings add to the wear.

The symptoms show up in the field as drive overtemperature trips, nuisance resets, controllers that lock up on afternoon calls, and power supplies that fail every second summer. For the drive side of this, including finned sinks and derating, see Heat Sinks and Enclosure Cooling for Variable Frequency Drives. This lesson is about the enclosure as a whole.

Heat in: parts plus walls

Two things heat an enclosure. First, the parts inside, which you can total from the watts each one dissipates (drive loss, power supply loss, transformer loss, relay and contactor coils). Second, the walls: when the room air is hotter than the temperature you want inside, heat leaks in through the enclosure surface, and sunlit or hot-wall locations make it worse. The room heat gain follows the usual relation, heat flow equals U times area times temperature difference. One watt equals 3.412 Btu/h, so you can work in either unit as long as you do not mix them.

Four ways to cool a panel

  • Filtered fan kit. A fan pulls room air through a filter, across the parts and out a louvered exhaust. It is cheap and simple, but it can only bring the inside down to the room temperature plus a rise. If the room is 115 °F, the panel will be warmer than 115 °F inside. It also pulls dust and moisture through the filter, so the filter must be checked and replaced on a schedule, and the enclosure is no longer sealed.
  • Air-to-air heat exchanger. Hot inside air and room air run through separate passages in a core, so heat moves across without the two air streams mixing. The enclosure stays sealed. Like the fan kit, it can only cool toward room temperature, never below it, and it needs clear air paths on both sides.
  • Closed-loop enclosure air conditioner. A small refrigeration circuit cools the inside air and rejects heat to the room. It is the only choice here that can hold the inside below room temperature, and it keeps the enclosure sealed. Because the coil is cold, moisture in the inside air condenses on it. Condensate has to go somewhere: some units evaporate it with a heated pan, others need a drain line. Any drain needs a slope and a safe discharge point, away from live parts. If a door is left open, humid plant air floods in and the unit makes much more condensate. Its condenser side also needs clean airflow, so it needs the same filter care as any other condenser coil. A unit that is set too cold can drip on parts, so use the manufacturer's recommended setpoint for the installation.
  • Compressed-air vortex cooler. A fitting uses compressed air to blow a stream of cold air into the panel. It has no moving parts and fits in a small hole, but it burns compressed air continuously. A national-lab energy guide says as much as 80 to 93 percent of the electricity used by an industrial air compressor ends up as heat, that compressed air is probably the most expensive form of energy in a plant, and that for cooling electrical cabinets, air conditioning fans are the better choice than compressed-air vortex tubes. A federal industrial tip sheet agrees that compressed air is expensive to produce. Use a vortex cooler only as a short-term or last-resort fix. See Compressed Air Systems for Facility Techs for the supply side.

Keep the enclosure rating intact

An enclosure carries a type rating on its nameplate for dust, splash or oil. Any cooling kit that opens it can defeat that rating. A fan kit with an open louver may not be suitable where the panel was bought for washdown or dust. A sealed exchanger or air conditioner should be rated for the same exposure as the enclosure, or the result is a weaker enclosure than the one you started with. Rules for cutting a new opening are in Clean Openings in HVAC Disconnects, Control Cabinets and Panels. Also keep gaskets intact, close doors and plug every unused opening. A cooling kit that arrives with its own cutout template and gasket should be installed exactly as its instructions say, with the panel de-energized and locked out before you cut or drill.

Worked example: sizing by watts (our own example)

This is our own example with assumed numbers. Do not treat it as a rule for your panel; use the real nameplate and data sheet losses.

Assumptions: the parts inside dissipate a drive at 450 W, power supplies at 120 W, relays and contactor coils at 60 W and other items at 70 W. The enclosure surface area is 30 ft². We assume a U-value of 1.5 Btu/h·ft²·°F for a painted sheet-steel wall. The room air is 115 °F and we want 95 °F inside.

  1. Part heat in watts: 450 + 120 + 60 + 70 = 700 W.
  2. Convert: 700 W × 3.412 = 2,388.4 Btu/h.
  3. Temperature difference: 115 − 95 = 20 °F.
  4. Wall gain: 1.5 × 30 × 20 = 900 Btu/h.
  5. Total heat load: 2,388.4 + 900 = 3,288.4 Btu/h, about 964 W (3,288.4 ÷ 3.412).
  6. Add a 20 percent margin (our own choice) for dirty filters and warm days: 3,288.4 × 1.2 = 3,946.1 Btu/h, so choose a unit rated at about 3,950 Btu/h or more at the stated conditions.

Check: a fan kit cannot do this job. Holding 95 °F inside when the room is 115 °F would mean cooling below room temperature, which only an air conditioner can do. If the target were 125 °F inside with the same 115 °F room, only the 700 W of parts would count, since the walls would lose heat to the room instead. Heat flow is 2,388 Btu/h. With the standard sensible-heat air factor of 1.08, the airflow for a 10 °F rise is 2,388.4 ÷ (1.08 × 10) = 221 cfm. That is the size of fan you would need before accounting for filter resistance, which is why a fan kit works only when the allowed inside temperature is above the room.

Also check the component limits: many parts have a maximum ambient temperature on the data sheet, which can be lower than 125 °F. The data sheet beats any rule of thumb.

Service checks

  • Read panel temperature at the hottest point, not near the door.
  • Replace fan-kit filters on a schedule and keep intake and exhaust clear.
  • Check air-to-air core passages and air conditioner condenser coils for dust and lint.
  • Check air conditioner condensate drains or pans, and look for water below the unit.
  • Find and fix open knockouts, missing gaskets and doors left ajar.
  • Lock out and verify dead before opening a panel to clean or replace a unit.

Field card: Cooling Control Panels in Hot Plant Rooms: Sizing by Watts

Total the watts, add the wall gain, then pick a cooling method that can reach the target.

  • Heat shortens component life; failure rates rise with temperature.
  • Watts × 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Wall gain = U × area × temperature difference.
  • Fan kits and air-to-air exchangers cannot go below room temperature.
  • Enclosure air conditioners can, but they make condensate that needs a path.
  • Vortex coolers burn expensive compressed air; use them as a last resort.
  • Keep the type rating: gaskets, filters, sealed cutouts.
