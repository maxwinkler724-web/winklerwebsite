Pneumatic HVAC Controls: Compressors, Dryers, Thermostats and Actuators in Older Buildings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Open a ceiling in a 1970s office building and you may find small plastic tubes running to every damper and valve. That is a pneumatic control system: compressed air is the signal. Nothing about it is magic, and once you see how one thermostat turns a room temperature into an air pressure, the rest follows.

Why these systems are still around

Pneumatic controls were the standard in commercial buildings for decades. A GSA guide on wireless pneumatic thermostats describes pneumatic systems as common in multi-story buildings over 20,000 square feet built before 1999. Converting a whole building to direct digital control (DDC) is a big job. An article on a university retrofit says it means rewiring, opening walls and ceilings, and disrupting occupants, and that it was usually done for an entire building at once. So many owners keep the old system running, replace it in stages, or add digital pieces to it. If you service older buildings, you will meet it. For the digital side, see Building Automation Basics.

The control air system

Everything starts at a small air plant, usually in the mechanical room. Figure 1 shows the order of the parts.

  • Compressor and receiver. One maker's air station overview (NECC) says the receiver tank should be big enough that the compressor cycles fewer than 10 times an hour, which also gives the air time to cool and drop water. It suggests sizing compressor capacity at two to three times the system need, often as a duplex pair.
  • Refrigerated dryer. It cools the air sharply so water vapor condenses out. NECC gives a 35 degree F pipeline dew point as the normal target. Schneider Electric's air quality sheet describes dryer outlet air cooled to 30 to 40 degrees F.
  • Filters. A particle filter ahead of the dryer and an oil-removal (coalescing) filter after it if the compressor is lubricated. NECC adds that this filter protects a new system but cannot clean one that is already contaminated.
  • Pressure reducing station. Tank pressure is higher than the controls can take. Schneider's sheet describes a regulator holding 15 to 25 psig for the system, with a relief valve set at 30 psig downstream.

Main air and branch pressure

The air leaving the pressure reducing station is main air (also called supply air). Maker literature mostly shows it near 20 psi: a Johnson Controls bulletin lists a 20 psig nominal supply (13 to 25 psig range) for one thermostat series, and a Kele article uses 20 psi main air as its example. Siemens states 18 to 25 psi normal supply for one of its thermostats. Always read the label on the device in front of you.

The branch line is the signal. A thermostat uses a restrictor and a small bleed nozzle to turn main air into a branch pressure that rises or falls with temperature. Honeywell's literature, for example, describes a thermostat sensor with a 3 to 15 psi signal. Hold that picture: main air is the fuel, branch pressure is the message.

Direct acting and reverse acting

Honeywell's thermostat literature says a direct-acting thermostat's signal pressure increases as temperature increases; a reverse-acting one's increases as temperature decreases. Siemens words it the same way. Which one is used depends on what the actuator does with the pressure. The thermostat also has a sensitivity, in psi of branch change per degree F. Honeywell lists examples from 1 psi to 5 psi per degree F, and a Johnson Controls bulletin shows one adjustable thermostat with a factory setting near 2.5 psi per degree F.

Actuators: spring ranges and normally open or closed

A valve or damper actuator is a diaphragm pushing against a spring. Schneider Electric's knowledge-base article describes the spring range as the air pressure needed to fully stroke an actuator with no external load. Kele's article gives 8 to 13 psi as an example range. The device sits at one end of travel until branch pressure reaches the bottom of its range, then moves across its stroke, and reaches the other end at the top.

Normally open means the spring leaves the valve or damper open when there is no air. Normally closed means the spring closes it. Schneider's article describes a sequencing example with a normally open heating valve and a normally closed cooling valve on one thermostat, and notes that load can shift their ranges so they may overlap. Different spring ranges let one thermostat sequence several devices, such as 3 to 8 psi for one and 8 to 13 psi for the next. That is why checking the label on the actuator matters. For a related damper check on the electric side, see RTU Economizer Fault Diagnosis and Damper Actuator Checks; for terminal units see VAV Boxes Basics.

Worked example

All arithmetic below is Our own, using figures from the sources above. We assume the actuator moves in a straight line across its spring range; real devices have friction and load shift, so treat the result as a bench estimate.

Part 1: 50 percent stroke. The valve actuator has a stated spring range of 8 to 13 psi.

  1. Span = 13 − 8 = 5 psi.
  2. Half of the span = 0.50 × 5 = 2.5 psi.
  3. Branch pressure for 50 percent stroke = 8 + 2.5 = 10.5 psi.

Part 2: the thermostat. Take a direct-acting thermostat with a stated sensitivity of 2.5 psi per degree F, giving a branch of 8 psi at a 72 F setpoint. The room is at 73.5 F.

  1. Temperature above setpoint = 73.5 − 72 = 1.5 F.
  2. Pressure change = 1.5 × 2.5 = 3.75 psi.
  3. Direct acting, so branch rises: 8 + 3.75 = 11.75 psi.
  4. Position in the 8 to 13 range: (11.75 − 8) ÷ 5 = 0.75, so about 75 percent of stroke.

A reverse-acting thermostat with the same numbers would move the other way (8 − 3.75 = 4.25 psi), which is below this actuator's range, so it would sit at its spring end of travel. Real thermostats are calibrated and limited by supply pressure, so this is only a reasoning exercise.

Why oil and water ruin the system

The restrictor and bleed nozzle have tiny openings. Schneider's air quality sheet says oil in concentrations greater than 0.1 PPM should never be tolerated in control air, that moisture corrodes untreated metal parts and blocks passageways, and that moisture can freeze and block flow in lines on outside walls in cold climates. It also says particles above 0.03 microns can plug passageways and restrictors, and that synthetic or paraffin base compressor oils should never be used. NECC says pneumatic controls are supersensitive to these contaminants. A failed dryer is therefore not a minor issue: it can leave water to travel through the whole building. If a system is already contaminated, Schneider says to find and correct the source, then replace or clean the affected parts.

Common faults and checks

A troubleshooting page from Control Trends gives a simple order for thermostat problems:

  • No bleed noise, no output. Check that supply air reaches the thermostat. If it does, the restrictor may be plugged or the bleed nozzle dirty.
  • Bleed noise but no output. Suspect a leak behind the mounting bracket, tired hoses on the base, or broken plastic nipples.
  • Isolating the branch. A jumper hose from supply to the branch port, with a gauge on the branch, tells whether the thermostat or the controlled device's branch line leaks.
  • Squeeze bulb and gauge. The page uses a squeeze bulb with a gauge to test whether the controlled device's diaphragm holds. If it does, the branch hose is the leak.

Siemens technical instructions for one thermostat say to measure control pressure at the thermostat test port with a test gauge. Follow the maker's procedure for the gauge and test port.

Moving to digital without a full rip-out

Two common paths appear in the sources. One is a wireless pneumatic thermostat that replaces the old thermostat, senses room temperature and branch pressure, and reports to the building system. Cypress Envirosystems' university case describes this, and a GSA guide reports that each unit takes about 30 minutes to install but says the pneumatic system should already be working, with dry air and no leaks. A Johnson Controls pneumatic-to-DDC thermostat similarly mounts at the existing location and uses the existing air lines. The other path is an electric-to-pneumatic (E/P) transducer, which Kele describes as converting an electronic signal into air pressure to drive pneumatic loads such as actuators. Either way, a leaky or wet pneumatic system must be fixed first, and the signal side then connects to protocols covered in BACnet, Modbus and LonWorks in Plain English.

Field card: Pneumatic HVAC Controls

Main air is the fuel; branch pressure is the message.

  • Control air path: compressor and receiver, dryer, filters, pressure reducing station, main air.
  • Main air is often near 20 psi in maker literature; read the device label.
  • Direct acting: branch rises as temperature rises. Reverse acting: branch falls.
  • Spring range is the pressure that strokes the actuator; example 8 to 13 psi.
  • Linear estimate: pressure = start + fraction × span (Our own).
  • Oil and water plug restrictors; fix the air source first.
  • Check with a gauge and squeeze bulb, following the maker's procedure.
